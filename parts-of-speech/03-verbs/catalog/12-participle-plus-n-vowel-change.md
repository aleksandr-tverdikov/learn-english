# Participle = base + -n, with vowel change

> The participle is formed with -n, and the vowels shift across the three forms.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Every verb in this group ends its participle in **-n** or **-en**, but the stem it attaches to has shifted its vowel along the way: *write — wrote — written*, *take — took — taken*, *grow — grew — grown* (3-я = 1-я + "n", в формах меняются звуки). That is what separates these verbs from the group where the participle is simply the base form plus an ending — here the third form is a changed stem *plus* the ending, so you have to learn all three together. The standing trap is letting the past do the participle's job: *I have wrote*, *I have took*, and *I have drove* are all wrong, and the fix is always the -n form. A large share of the list is prefixed verbs — *overtake*, *underwrite*, *befall* — which simply copy the base verb they are built on.

### arise

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraɪz/ &middot; *uh-RYZE*
**Русский:** подняться; возникнуть
**Forms:** *arise* /əˈraɪz/ &middot; *arose* /əˈroʊz/ &middot; *arisen* /əˈrɪzən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing, less common in speech
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *arises* &middot; **-ing form:** *arising* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** *Arise* takes no object: situations *arise*, but you *raise* a question and a person *rises* from a chair. It follows [rise](#rise) exactly, and the same trap applies — the past *arose* cannot do participle duty: not *a problem has arose* but *a problem has arisen*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, but with an abstract subject: problems, questions, and needs *arise*. No linking or stative use.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** only, in every sense — *a dispute arose*, *claims arise from the accident*. You *raise* a question; ✗ *he arose the issue*.
**Passive:** **no** — there is no object to promote: ✗ *a dispute was arisen over the fence*. Only the active *a dispute arose* is possible.

To come up or come into being — of problems, questions, and opportunities; in formal or older use, to get up.

**1. Come up, occur (of a situation).** The everyday sense: questions, problems, needs, and opportunities *arise*. It never takes an object — you *raise* a question, but a question *arises*. (возникать, появляться)

1. Questions *arise* every time the budget changes midyear.
2. A dispute *arose* over who owned the fence.
3. New evidence *has arisen* since the trial began.
4. If the need *arises*, call me at home.

**2. Arise from, arise out of.** With *from* or *out of*: to have something as its cause or origin. Standard in formal, legal, and academic writing. (проистекать, быть следствием)

5. Most complaints *arise* from a single billing error.
6. The lawsuit *arose* out of a contract signed in 1998.
7. Several claims *have arisen* from the same accident.

**3. Get up, rise (formal).** Literary and elevated — standing up, or getting out of bed. Ordinary American speech uses *get up*. (вставать, подниматься)

8. The congregation *arises* for the final hymn.
9. He *arose* before dawn and made coffee in the dark.
10. She *had arisen* early every day of that long summer.

**See also:** [rise](#rise), [befall](#befall)

### befall

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈfɔl/ &middot; *bih-FAWL*
**Русский:** случаться; приключаться; выпадать на долю
**Forms:** *befall* /bɪˈfɔl/ &middot; *befell* /bɪˈfɛl/ &middot; *befallen* /bɪˈfɔlən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — literary and journalistic; ordinary speech says *happened to*
**Pattern:** a — e — a, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *befalls* &middot; **-ing form:** *befalling*
**Contrast:** Built on [fall](#fall) and inflected exactly like it. Unlike plain *fall*, it takes a person as its object — *misfortune befell them* — so not *he befell down the stairs*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the subject is an event or a misfortune, not a person who acts.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in the living sense, with a person or place as the object — *a string of disasters befell the company*. The archaic sense 2 is **intransitive**: *it befell on the third night*.
**Passive:** **no** in practice — transitive but it resists promotion: ✗ *the company was befallen by disasters*. Keep the active, or switch to *was struck by*.

To happen to someone, nearly always something unwelcome.

**1. Happen to someone (something bad).** Formal and literary. Unlike plain *fall* it takes a person or a place as its object, and what befalls is misfortune — trouble, disaster, harm. (случаться с кем-л., приключаться)

1. Whatever troubles *befall* us, we handle them together.
2. A string of disasters *befell* the company that winter.
3. Nothing like this *has befallen* the town in living memory.
4. He wondered what *had befallen* the crew of the second boat.
5. Misfortune *befalls* the careless traveler.

**2. Come to pass (archaic).** The older objectless use, meaning simply *to happen*. Found in scripture and folktale, not in current American speech. (происходить, статься)

6. It *befell* on the third night that the gate stood open.
7. Strange things *have befallen* since the comet passed.

**See also:** [fall](#fall), [arise](#arise)

### bequeath

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkwið/ &middot; *bih-KWEETH*
**Русский:** завещать
**Forms:** *bequeath* /bɪˈkwið/ &middot; *bequoth* /bɪˈkwoʊθ/ &middot; *bequothen* /bɪˈkwoʊðən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — wills and legacies; the strong *bequoth/bequothen* are archaic
**Pattern:** ea — o — o, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *bequeaths* &middot; **-ing form:** *bequeathing*
**Contrast:** In modern American English *bequeath* is a regular verb: *bequeathed*, *have bequeathed*. The strong forms *bequoth* and *bequothen* listed here are archaic and turn up only in old wills and literary imitations of them — do not use them in anything you write today. Note the voiced ending, /bɪˈkwið/ like *breathe*, not like *teeth*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, a verb of transfer like *give*.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive** — *that century bequeathed us the vaccine*, or with *to*: *her will bequeathed the house to her sister*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the house was bequeathed to her sister*, and three paintings *have been bequeathed* to the museum.

To leave property to someone in a will, and by extension to pass anything on to those who come after you.

**1. Leave in a will.** The legal sense, used of money and personal property. In modern American English the verb is regular: *bequeathed*, *have bequeathed*. (завещать, оставлять по завещанию)

1. Wealthy donors often *bequeath* their libraries to a university.
2. Her will *bequeathed* the house to her sister, not to her son.
3. The family *has bequeathed* three paintings to the museum since 1980.
4. He *bequeaths* nothing to anyone who contests the estate.

**2. Hand down to later generations.** Figurative: to leave something non-material — a legacy, a problem, a body of work — to whoever comes next. (передавать по наследству (традицию, наследие))

5. Each administration *bequeaths* its unfinished business to the next.
6. That century *bequeathed* us both the vaccine and the bomb.
7. Their teachers *have bequeathed* them a whole vocabulary for it.

**3. Strong forms bequoth, bequothen (archaic).** The strong inflection listed for this verb survives only in old wills and literary imitations of them. Do not use it in anything you write today. (завещать (устаревшие формы))

8. The parish record says the merchant *bequoth* his lands to the abbey.
9. Scholars still argue over what the founder *had bequothen* to the town.

**See also:** [give](#give), [forsake](#forsake)

### bestride

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈstraɪd/ &middot; *bih-STRYDE*
**Русский:** сидеть верхом; стоять расставив ноги; перешагивать
**Forms:** *bestride* /bɪˈstraɪd/ &middot; *bestrode* /bɪˈstroʊd/ &middot; *bestridden* /bɪˈstrɪdən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — literary and headline use; Americans say *sat astride* or *dominated*
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -den &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *bestrides* &middot; **-ing form:** *bestriding* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Built on [stride](#stride) and inflected exactly like it, never *bestrided*. It is literary; in ordinary American speech you would say *sat astride* or *dominated*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the literal *bestride a log*; closer to stative in the dominating sense, where it describes a standing position rather than an act.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in every sense, and the object is obligatory — *one firm bestrides the market*; ✗ *the general bestrode for years*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but rare and stiff: *the industry has been bestridden by one company* is possible in literary writing, though the active is far more natural.

To sit or stand with a leg on either side of something; figuratively, to dominate an entire field.

**1. Dominate, tower over.** The sense still in use, and always literary or journalistic: one firm or figure *bestrides* an industry or an era. (господствовать, довлеть над)

1. That one company *has bestridden* the industry for twenty years.
2. A single firm now *bestrides* the entire market.
3. By 1900 the railroads *had bestridden* the continent.

**2. Sit or stand astride.** The literal sense — a leg on each side of a horse, a log, or a wall. Ordinary American speech says *sit astride* instead. (сидеть верхом; стоять расставив ноги)

4. Bronze generals *bestride* half the squares in Europe.
5. He *bestrode* the fallen log and pulled the rope tight.
6. The rider *bestrode* a gray mare and led the parade.

**3. Step across, span.** Rare and old: to cross something in a single stride, or of a structure to span it. (перешагивать; перекрывать собой)

7. A stone causeway lets you *bestride* the stream without wading.
8. The arch *bestrode* the whole road until 1912.

**See also:** [stride](#stride), [ride](#ride), [override](#override)

### bid

**Pronunciation:** /bɪd/ &middot; *BID*
**Русский:** предлагать цену; велеть
**Forms:** *bid* /bɪd/ &middot; *bade* /bæd/ (also /beɪd/) &middot; *bidden* /ˈbɪdən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — auctions, contracts, and *bid farewell*; the forms *bade/bidden* are dated
**Pattern:** i — a — i, participle in -den &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *bids* &middot; **-ing form:** *bidding* (the d doubles)
**Contrast:** *Bid* splits by meaning. In the auction sense it never changes — *bid, bid, bid*: not *he bade forty dollars* but *he bid forty dollars*. The forms *bade* and *bidden* belong to the older sense of commanding or greeting: *she bade him good night*, *the guests had been bidden to arrive at seven*. Most Americans say *bade* as /bæd/, rhyming with *had*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, from offering a price to commanding.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive with a preposition in business (*they bid on federal jobs*), transitive with an amount (*she bid nine hundred dollars*), and ditransitive in the command and greeting senses (*she bade us good night*, *he bids his students think for themselves*).
**Passive:** **yes**, but sense by sense — *that lamp has been bid on twice*, *the guests had been bidden to arrive at seven*. The frozen *bid farewell* stays active: ✗ *farewell was bidden to us*.

To offer a price, at auction or for a contract; in an older and more formal sense, to command, to greet, or to invite.

**1. Offer a price at auction.** In this sense the verb never changes shape: *bid, bid, bid*. Not *he bade forty dollars*. (предлагать цену (на аукционе), торговаться)

1. Two collectors *bid* against each other for the manuscript.
2. She *bid* nine hundred dollars and got the table.
3. Three people *have bid* on that lamp already.

**2. Bid on / bid for a contract.** Business use with *on* or *for*: to submit a formal offer to do work at a stated price. The forms stay invariant here too. (подавать заявку, участвовать в тендере)

4. Small contractors rarely *bid* on federal jobs.
5. They *bid* too low and lost money on the project.
6. The city *had bid* for the tournament twice before.

**3. Command, tell someone to do.** Formal and literary — this is the sense that carries the strong forms *bade* and *bidden*. It takes a bare infinitive: *bid them come in*. (велеть, приказывать)

7. He *bids* his students think for themselves.
8. The sergeant *bade* them wait outside the tent.
9. The captain *has bidden* the crew to stand down.

**4. Bid farewell, bid good night.** A fixed formula for greetings and farewells: *bid someone welcome, farewell, good night*. Most Americans say *bade* as /bæd/, rhyming with *had*. (прощаться; желать (доброй ночи))

10. She *bade* us good night and went upstairs.
11. We *bade* farewell at the gate and drove home.
12. They *have bidden* their guests welcome in three languages.

**5. Invite (archaic).** Old use, alive today mostly in the phrase *the bidden guests*. Modern English uses *invite*. (приглашать, звать)

13. The steward *bade* the whole village to the wedding.
14. The host *had bidden* the guests to arrive at seven.
15. Only those *bidden* to the feast came through the gate.

**See also:** [forbid](#forbid), [underbid](#underbid)

### blow

**Pronunciation:** /bloʊ/ &middot; *BLOH*
**Русский:** 1) дуть 2) цвести, расцветать
**Forms:** *blow* /bloʊ/ &middot; *blew* /blu/ &middot; *blown* /bloʊn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ow — ew — own &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *blows* &middot; **-ing form:** *blowing*
**Contrast:** The past *blew* is a homophone of *blue*. Not *I blowed the fuse* and not *I have blew it* — the participle needs *have*: *I have blown it*. In the phrasal senses (*blow up*, *blow out*, *blow off*) the same three forms apply.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, whether the subject is wind, a person, or a fuse.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive of wind and of things that fail (*cold winds blow*, *the rear tire blew*), transitive when something is moved, sounded, or wasted (*the storm blew three shingles off*, *he blew a paycheck*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the umbrella has been blown into the pool*, *the whistle was blown on the play*. No passive of the intransitive *the fuse blew*.

Of air, to move or to move something; of a person, to force air out of the mouth — plus a large family of idioms from *blow up* to *blow it*.

**1. Of wind: move, be in motion.** The core intransitive sense, with wind or a draft as the subject. (дуть (о ветре))

1. Cold winds *blow* off the lake all winter.
2. It *blew* hard all night.
3. The wind *has blown* from the south since Tuesday.

**2. Move something with air.** Transitive: air moves an object, usually with a particle — *blow off, blow over, blow away*. (сдувать, уносить (ветром))

4. Papers *blow* everywhere when that door opens.
5. The storm *blew* three shingles off the roof.
6. A gust *has blown* the umbrella into the pool.

**3. Breathe out forcefully.** Of a person directing breath at something. This covers the fixed *blow out the candles* and *blow your nose*. (дуть, выдыхать; сморкаться)

7. She *blows* on her coffee to cool it.
8. He *blew* out all the candles at once.
9. *Blow* your nose before you come to the table.
10. The nurse asked whether he *had blown* into the tube yet.

**4. Sound a horn or a whistle.** Used of wind instruments, whistles, and car horns. The related noun *whistleblower* comes from this sense. (трубить, дуть в свисток)

11. Conductors *blow* the whistle before the doors close.
12. The referee *blew* the whistle on the play.
13. Someone *has blown* the horn three times.

**5. Blow up — explode; destroy.** Both transitive and intransitive. *Blow up* also means to lose your temper, and, recently, for something to become suddenly popular. (взрывать(ся), взлетать на воздух)

14. Engineers plan to *blow up* the old bridge in March.
15. The transformer *blew up* during the storm.
16. The video *has blown up* on every platform.

**6. Burn out, burst (a fuse, a tire).** Of something that fails suddenly under pressure or current. Works in both directions: *the fuse blew*, *he blew the fuse*. (перегорать (о пробке); лопнуть (о шине))

17. Cheap bulbs *blow* within a month.
18. The rear tire *blew* on the interstate.
19. Somebody *has blown* the fuse again.

**7. Waste, squander (money).** Informal, with money or time as the object, often with *on* or *through*. (спустить, промотать (деньги))

20. Do not *blow* your whole budget in the first week.
21. He *blew* an entire paycheck on concert tickets.
22. They *have blown* through the grant already.

**8. Blow it — ruin a chance.** Informal. The object is an opportunity: a game, an interview, a lead. (провалить, упустить, испортить)

23. Do not *blow* this.
24. He *blew* the interview by showing up late.
25. We *have blown* two chances at the title.

**9. Blow off — skip, brush aside.** Informal: to skip an obligation, or to dismiss a person or a concern without taking it seriously. (забить на, продинамить; отмахнуться)

26. He *blows off* class whenever the surf is good.
27. She *blew off* the whole warning.
28. He *has blown off* three appointments this month.

**10. Blow over — pass without consequence.** Of a storm, a scandal, or an argument that ends without lasting damage. (утихнуть, сойти на нет)

29. Stories like this usually *blow over*.
30. The whole thing *blew over* in a week.
31. The controversy *had blown over* long before he got back.


**Phrasal verbs:** [blow away](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-away) &middot; [blow in](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-in) &middot; [blow off](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-off) &middot; [blow out](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-out) &middot; [blow over](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-over) &middot; [blow through](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-through) &middot; [blow up](14-phrasal-b.md#blow-up)

### co-write

**Pronunciation:** /koʊˈraɪt/ &middot; *koh-RYTE*
**Русский:** писать, написать совместно
**Forms:** *co-write* /koʊˈraɪt/ &middot; *co-wrote* /koʊˈroʊt/ &middot; *co-written* /koʊˈrɪtən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — standard in music, film, and publishing credits
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -ten &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *co-writes* &middot; **-ing form:** *co-writing* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Built on [write](#write) and inflected exactly like it. American style keeps the hyphen, and the stress falls on the second part: koh-RYTE, koh-ROHT, koh-RIT-n.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, a verb of creation.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the work is the object and the collaborator follows *with*: *she co-wrote the screenplay with her brother*. The object is normally obligatory.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the usual form in credits — *the report had been co-written by two agencies*.

To write something jointly with another person.

**1. Write jointly with someone.** Standard for songs, screenplays, books, and papers; the collaborator follows *with*. American style keeps the hyphen and stresses the second part. (писать, написать совместно)

1. The two friends *co-write* every song on the album.
2. She *co-wrote* the screenplay with her brother.
3. He *has co-written* four textbooks with the same colleague.
4. The report *had been co-written* by two agencies.
5. He *co-writes* a column with a retired judge.
6. They *co-wrote* the grant proposal in a single weekend.

### crow

**Pronunciation:** /kroʊ/ &middot; *KROH*
**Русский:** кричать кукареку, петь (о петухе)
**Forms:** *crow* /kroʊ/ &middot; *crew* /kru/ &middot; *crown* /kroʊn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — the verb itself is ordinary and regular in American English; the strong *crew* is biblical and *crown* obsolete
**Pattern:** ow — ew — own &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *crows* &middot; **-ing form:** *crowing*
**Contrast:** In American English *crow* is a regular verb: *crowed*, *have crowed*. The strong past *crew* survives only in older and biblical English (*the cock crew*), and the participle *crown* is obsolete — it is not the noun *crown* /kraʊn/, which is an unrelated word. Use *crowed* in your own writing, especially for the boasting sense.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a verb of noise-making, both for the rooster and for the person gloating.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in all three senses; the thing boasted about follows *about* or *over*: *he crowed about the promotion*. ✗ *he crowed his win*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, so there is nothing to promote: ✗ *the promotion was crowed about* is at best marginal, and ✗ *the win was crowed*.

Of a rooster, to make its loud call; of a person, to boast about a win.

**1. Boast, gloat.** The everyday sense, mildly disapproving, usually *crow about* or *crow over*. Regular in modern American English: *crowed*, *have crowed*. (хвастаться, злорадствовать)

1. She *crows* whenever her team wins.
2. He *crowed* about the promotion for a solid week.
3. They *have crowed* about that one game for years.

**2. Of a rooster: call.** The literal sense. The strong past *crew* survives only in older and biblical English, and the participle *crown* is obsolete — use *crowed* today. (кукарекать, петь (о петухе))

4. The roosters *crow* before the sun clears the ridge.
5. Their rooster *has crowed* every morning since April.
6. The old translation reads that the cock *crew* three times.
7. Antique printings of the ballad say the cock *had crown* twice before daybreak.

**3. Of a baby: squeal with delight.** A small, affectionate use: the happy noise an infant makes. (гулить, радостно вскрикивать)

8. Babies *crow* when you lift them overhead.
9. The baby *crowed* and reached for the balloon.
10. He *has crowed* and giggled all morning.

**See also:** [blow](#blow), [grow](#grow), [throw](#throw)

### do

**Pronunciation:** /du/ &middot; *DOO*
**Русский:** делать
**Forms:** *do* /du/ &middot; *did* /dɪd/ &middot; *done* /dʌn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** oo — i — u, participle in -ne &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *does* /dʌz/ (irregular in both spelling and sound) &middot; **-ing form:** *doing*
**Contrast:** Not *I done it*: *done* always needs *have*, so use *I did it* or *I have done it*. As an auxiliary, *do* carries the tense for the main verb, which is why *did you went* is wrong and *did you go* is right. The third person is spelled *does* and said /dʌz/, never /duz/.
**Verb type:** **both lexical and auxiliary** — an action verb in *do the dishes*, and the tense-carrying **auxiliary** of questions, negatives, and emphasis (*do you take cream?*, *I do remember*), plus a pro-verb standing in for an earlier verb (*she reads more than I do*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the lexical senses (*do the dishes*, *do thirty miles to the gallon*), intransitive in *that will do* and *she has done well*. The auxiliary takes no object at all.
**Passive:** **yes** in the lexical transitive senses — *the upstairs has been done already*, *the essay had to be done over*. The auxiliary and pro-verb uses have no passive: ✗ *cream is done taken by you*.

To perform an action or a task — and, as an auxiliary, the verb that carries tense in questions, negatives, and emphasis.

**1. Perform, carry out.** The core lexical sense, with a task, a job, or a chore as the object. Not *I done it*: the participle always needs *have*. (делать, выполнять)

1. We *do* the dishes right after dinner.
2. She *did* the whole route on foot.
3. He *has done* more than anyone asked him to.
4. By noon they *had done* half the packing.

**2. Auxiliary in questions and negatives.** *Do* carries the tense so the main verb stays in the base form. That is why *did you went* is wrong and *did you go* is right. (вспомогательный глагол (вопрос, отрицание))

5. *Do* you take cream?
6. She *did* not answer the second email.
7. *Does* he know about the meeting?

**3. Emphatic do.** Stressed *do* in an affirmative sentence insists on it against doubt or contradiction. (усилительное do (действительно, всё же))

8. I *do* remember her name — give me a second.
9. He *did* apologize, eventually.
10. She *does* care, whatever she says.

**4. Stand in for another verb.** *Do* substitutes for a verb already mentioned, especially in comparisons, short answers, and tag questions. (заменяет ранее названный глагол)

11. She reads more than I *do*.
12. He said he would call, and he *did*.
13. Nobody *has done* more for this town than she has.

**5. Tidy, arrange, put in order.** *Do* a room, *do* your hair, *do* the yard — to put something into the state it is supposed to be in. (приводить в порядок, убирать; делать (причёску))

14. She *does* her hair before anyone else is awake.
15. They *did* the whole kitchen over a weekend.
16. The cleaners *have done* the upstairs already.

**6. Be enough, suffice.** Intransitive, usually with *will* or *would*: *that will do*, *it will do for now*. (годиться, подойти, хватить)

17. Two chairs will *do* for now.
18. A shorter rope *did* just fine.
19. Cold pizza *has done* the job before.

**7. Fare, get along.** Always with an adverb — *doing well*, *doing badly*, *how are you doing*. Standard in talk about health and business alike. (поживать, справляться)

20. How *did* she *do* on the exam?
21. The store *did* badly its first year.
22. She *has done* well for herself.

**8. Cover a distance or a speed.** With a measurement as the object: miles covered, miles per gallon, speed on the highway. (проходить, проезжать (расстояние); ехать со скоростью)

23. This car *does* thirty miles to the gallon.
24. We *did* four hundred miles before dark.
25. They *had done* the whole loop by noon.

**9. Serve a prison sentence.** Informal but widespread: *do time*, *do five years*. (отсидеть (срок))

26. Nobody wants to *do* a day in that place.
27. He *did* eight years upstate.
28. She *has done* time for fraud.

**10. Do over — redo from the start.** American English keeps *do over* where British English says *do again*; a *do-over* is also a noun. (переделать заново)

29. The teacher made him *do* the essay *over*.
30. We *did* the bathroom *over* last spring.
31. They *have done* the whole website *over* twice.

**11. Do without / could do with.** *Do without* means to manage in the absence of something; *could do with* means to need or want it. (обходиться без; не помешало бы)

32. We can *do* without a second car.
33. They *did* without heat for three days.
34. She *has done* without help all year.
35. I could *do* with a nap.

**12. Do away with — abolish.** To get rid of a rule, a practice, or an institution for good. (покончить с, отменить)

36. Nobody wants to *do away with* the program.
37. The district *did away with* letter grades.
38. They *have done away with* the paper form entirely.

### draw

**Pronunciation:** /drɔ/ &middot; *DRAW*
**Русский:** рисовать, чертить; тащить, тянуть, волочить
**Forms:** *draw* /drɔ/ &middot; *drew* /dru/ &middot; *drawn* /drɔn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** aw — ew — awn &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *draws* &middot; **-ing form:** *drawing*
**Contrast:** The past *drew* /dru/ rhymes with *do* and *true*, not with *draw*; the participle is *drawn*, never *drawed*. *Drawn* doubles as an adjective for a tired face: *she looked drawn*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, from making lines to attracting a crowd.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in most senses (*she drew a map*, *free food draws a crowd*, *you can draw cash*), intransitive in *architects still draw by hand*, in the sports *United drew with City*, and in *winter draws near*.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the curtains had been drawn*, *the exhibit's crowds were drawn by the reviews*, *new rules have been drawn up*. No passive of *winter draws near*.

To make a picture with lines; to pull; and, by extension, to attract, to take out, and to end level.

**1. Make a picture, sketch.** The most common sense, of lines made by hand rather than paint or color. (рисовать, чертить)

1. Architects still *draw* by hand for the first sketches.
2. She *drew* a map on the back of the receipt.
3. He *has drawn* the same barn a hundred times.

**2. Pull, drag.** Somewhat formal for plain pulling, but ordinary in fixed uses: *draw the curtains*, *draw a chair up*, *horses draw a wagon*. (тянуть, тащить, волочить)

4. Two horses *draw* the wagon up the hill.
5. He *drew* the chair closer to the fire.
6. The curtains *had been drawn* before we arrived.

**3. Attract.** Of crowds, attention, criticism, or interest — what the subject pulls toward itself. (привлекать, притягивать)

7. Free food *draws* a crowd every time.
8. Her first novel *drew* attention from three publishers.
9. The exhibit *has drawn* record crowds.

**4. Take out, withdraw.** Of money from an account, a card from a deck, a weapon from a holster, or water from a well. (снимать (деньги); вытаскивать, доставать)

10. You can *draw* cash at any branch.
11. He *drew* a card from the top of the deck.
12. She *has drawn* her salary from the same account for years.

**5. Draw a conclusion, a comparison, a line.** A fixed set of abstract objects: conclusions, distinctions, parallels, and the line you will not cross. (делать (вывод); проводить (сравнение, черту))

13. Do not *draw* conclusions from one bad week.
14. The report *drew* a sharp comparison with 2008.
15. Critics *have drawn* the obvious parallel.

**6. Draw blood, draw a breath.** Two set collocations: taking blood for a test or a wound, and taking in air. (брать кровь; вдохнуть)

16. A nurse will *draw* blood before the appointment.
17. He *drew* a long breath and started over.
18. They *have drawn* blood twice this week.

**7. End level, tie (sports, chess).** Standard in soccer and chess; in most American sports the ordinary verb is *tie*, though the noun *a draw* is used everywhere. (сыграть вничью)

19. The two grandmasters usually *draw* when they meet.
20. United *drew* with City on Saturday.
21. They *have drawn* three matches in a row.

**8. Draw up — compose a document.** Of contracts, wills, plans, and lists put together in written form. (составить (документ, план, список))

22. We should *draw up* a list before we shop.
23. The lawyer *drew up* the contract overnight.
24. The committee *has drawn up* new rules.

**9. Draw on / draw upon — use as a resource.** To take material, money, or experience from a source you already have. (черпать, использовать (опыт, средства))

25. Good teachers *draw on* their own mistakes.
26. The novel *drew* heavily *on* her childhood.
27. He *has drawn on* that fund twice.

**10. Draw out — prolong; get someone talking.** Two related uses: making something last too long, and coaxing a quiet person into conversation. (затягивать; разговорить)

28. Do not *draw* the meeting *out*.
29. She *drew* him *out* with a few easy questions.
30. The negotiations *have been drawn out* for months.

**11. Draw near, draw to a close.** Of time approaching. Slightly formal, common in writing and speeches. (приближаться; подходить к концу)

31. Winter *draws* near.
32. The year *drew* to a close without a deal.
33. The season *has drawn* to a close.

### drive

**Pronunciation:** /draɪv/ &middot; *DRYVE*
**Русский:** водить машину
**Forms:** *drive* /draɪv/ &middot; *drove* /droʊv/ &middot; *driven* /ˈdrɪvən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *drives* &middot; **-ing form:** *driving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Not *I have drove* — the participle is *driven*. *Drove* is also a noun (*a drove of cattle*), and *driven* works as an adjective for an ambitious person: *a driven student*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the subject is either an operator or a force.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in *he drove nine hours*, transitive with a vehicle, a person, a nail, or a herd (*drive the stake in*, *I can drive you to the airport*), and with an object plus a resultant complement in *the noise drives me crazy*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the pumps have been driven by solar panels since June*, *young families have been driven out of the city*. No passive of the intransitive *he drove nine hours*.

To operate a vehicle; to force someone or something into a state; and to power, push, or propel.

**1. Operate a vehicle.** The core sense, transitive or intransitive. Not *I have drove* — the participle is *driven*. (водить машину, ехать за рулём)

1. I *drive* to the coast every August.
2. He *drove* nine hours without stopping.
3. She *has driven* that truck since college.

**2. Take someone somewhere by car.** With a person as the object: you drive them, and the destination follows. (подвозить, отвозить)

4. I can *drive* you to the airport.
5. My neighbor *drove* us home after the game.
6. He *has driven* her to every appointment.

**3. Force into a state or an action.** *Drive someone crazy*, *drive prices up*, *drive families out* — the subject pushes the object into a condition it did not choose. (доводить до, вынуждать)

7. The noise *drives* me crazy.
8. Debt *drove* him to sell the shop.
9. High rents *have driven* young families out of the city.

**4. Power, propel (a machine).** Of the energy source behind machinery. Often passive: *the pump is driven by a small motor*. (приводить в движение, питать)

10. A small motor *drives* the belt.
11. Steam *drove* the first looms.
12. The pumps *have been driven* by solar panels since June.

**5. Motivate, be the force behind.** With an abstract subject — curiosity, fear, cheap credit. The participle is a common adjective for an ambitious person: *a driven student*. (двигать, быть движущей силой)

13. Curiosity *drives* most of her work.
14. Fear *drove* the whole campaign.
15. Cheap credit *has driven* the boom.

**6. Hammer in, force in.** Of nails, stakes, posts, and screws forced into place by repeated blows. (вбивать, забивать (гвоздь, кол))

16. *Drive* the stake in at an angle.
17. He *drove* the last nail at sunset.
18. They *have driven* posts along the whole fence line.

**7. Herd animals.** The old sense that gives us *cattle drive* and *drover*. (гнать (скот))

19. Ranchers still *drive* cattle to summer pasture.
20. The crew *drove* the herd across the shallow ford.
21. Cowboys *had driven* the steers north for weeks.

**8. Hit hard (golf, baseball).** Sports use: a long, hard hit. *Drive in a run* is standard baseball, and the noun *RBI* comes from it. (сильно бить (по мячу))

22. He can *drive* a ball three hundred yards.
23. She *drove* a single into right field.
24. He *has driven* in four runs tonight.

**9. Drive at — mean, be getting at.** Almost always in the progressive and almost always in a question about what someone really means. (клонить к, иметь в виду)

25. What are you *driving at*?
26. I could not tell what he *was driving at*.
27. Nobody knew what she *had been driving at* until later.

### eat

**Pronunciation:** /it/ &middot; *EET*
**Русский:** есть
**Forms:** *eat* /it/ &middot; *ate* /eɪt/ &middot; *eaten* /ˈitən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ea — a — ea, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *eats* &middot; **-ing form:** *eating*
**Contrast:** Americans say *ate* as /eɪt/, rhyming with *late*; the British /ɛt/ sounds out of place here. The participle never stands alone: not *I eaten already* but *I have eaten already*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, including the figurative *rust ate through the pan*.
**Transitivity:** **both**, with an optional object — *we eat dinner around seven* and plain *the kids have eaten already* are equally good. Transitive in *rent eats up half her paycheck*.
**Passive:** **yes** where there is an object — *every roll had been eaten by the time I sat down*, *the settlement has been eaten up by legal fees*. No passive of *they ate out*.

To take food into the mouth and swallow it; also, to wear away, and the base of several everyday idioms.

**1. Consume food.** The core sense, transitive or intransitive. The participle never stands alone: not *I eaten already* but *I have eaten already*. (есть, кушать)

1. We *eat* dinner around seven.
2. He *ate* two bowls of chili and asked for more.
3. The kids *have eaten* already.
4. By the time I sat down, they *had eaten* every roll.

**2. Eat out / eat in — take a meal.** *Eat out* means at a restaurant, *eat in* means at home. Both are fixed and cannot be reversed. (питаться (в ресторане или дома))

5. We *eat out* twice a week.
6. They *ate in* every night that month.
7. She *has eaten* lunch at her desk since March.

**3. Eat up — consume entirely.** Literal with food, and very common with time and money as the object. (съедать, поглощать (время, деньги))

8. Rent *eats up* half her paycheck.
9. The delay *ate up* the whole morning.
10. Legal fees *have eaten up* the settlement.

**4. Eat into, eat away — corrode.** Of acid, rust, salt, or water gradually destroying a surface, and of costs gradually destroying a margin. (разъедать, подтачивать)

11. Salt *eats into* the underside of the car.
12. The river *ate away* four feet of bank.
13. Rust *has eaten* through the pan.

**5. Bother, trouble (what is eating you).** Informal, usually progressive or with *at*: something is quietly bothering a person. (мучить, донимать, грызть)

14. Something *is eating* him and he will not say what.
15. What *ate* at her all week was the silence.
16. Guilt *has eaten* at him since the trial.

**6. Eat one's words.** A fixed phrase: to be forced to admit that what you said confidently was wrong. (взять свои слова обратно)

17. He had to *eat* his words by Friday.
18. She *ate* her words on live television.
19. They *have eaten* those words more than once.

### fall

**Pronunciation:** /fɔl/ &middot; *FAWL*
**Русский:** падать
**Forms:** *fall* /fɔl/ &middot; *fell* /fɛl/ &middot; *fallen* /ˈfɔlən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** a — e — a, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *falls* &middot; **-ing form:** *falling*
**Contrast:** *Fall* takes no object: not *he fell the glass* but *he dropped the glass*. The separate verb *fell* meaning to cut down a tree is regular (*felled, felled*), which is why the past *fell* and that verb look alike. And not *I have fell* — the participle is *fallen*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; also **linking (copular)** in the fixed *fall asleep, fall ill, fall silent, fall in love*, where an adjective or phrase follows instead of an object.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense, and this is the trap: ✗ *he fell the glass* — you *drop* a glass, or *fell* a tree with the separate regular verb *fell, felled*.
**Passive:** **no** — nothing can be promoted: ✗ *the capital was fallen in three days*. *The fallen* is a noun from the participle, not a passive.

To drop toward the ground; to decrease; to pass into a state; and, of cities and governments, to be defeated.

**1. Drop toward the ground.** The core sense, and it never takes an object: not *he fell the glass* but *he dropped the glass*. (падать)

1. Leaves *fall* early up in the mountains.
2. He *fell* off the ladder and broke his wrist.
3. Snow *had fallen* all night on the pass.
4. The old barn *has fallen* in on itself.

**2. Decrease, go down.** Of prices, temperatures, numbers, and rates. The opposite is *rise*, not *raise*. (падать, снижаться (о цене, температуре))

5. Prices *fall* after the holidays.
6. The temperature *fell* twenty degrees in an hour.
7. Enrollment *has fallen* for three straight years.

**3. Pass into a state — fall asleep, fall ill.** A fixed pattern with a short list of complements: *asleep, ill, silent, in love, behind on payments*. (впадать в состояние: заснуть, заболеть, влюбиться)

8. She *falls* asleep on every flight.
9. He *fell* in love with the town on the first visit.
10. Half the crew *has fallen* ill.

**4. Be captured or defeated.** Of cities, fortresses, and governments that lose power or are taken by an enemy. (пасть, быть захваченным (о городе, режиме))

11. Cities like that rarely *fall* without a siege.
12. The capital *fell* in three days.
13. The government *had fallen* before the vote was counted.

**5. Die in battle.** Formal and commemorative. *The fallen* is the standard collective noun for soldiers killed in war. (погибнуть (в бою))

14. Soldiers *fall* and towns forget them.
15. Thousands *fell* on that ridge.
16. Men *have fallen* here in two wars.

**6. Occur on a date.** Of holidays, deadlines, and anniversaries landing on a particular day. (приходиться на (дату, день недели))

17. Thanksgiving *falls* late this year.
18. My birthday *fell* on a Monday.
19. The deadline *has fallen* on a holiday twice now.

**7. Fall apart — break down, collapse.** Literal for objects, figurative for deals, plans, and people who stop coping. (разваливаться, рассыпаться; сорваться)

20. Cheap furniture *falls apart* in a year.
21. The deal *fell apart* over money.
22. He *has fallen apart* since the diagnosis.

**8. Fall behind — lag.** With *on* or *with*: to be later or further back than you should be, often about work or payments. (отставать (в учёбе, в платежах))

23. Try not to *fall behind* on the reading.
24. They *fell behind* on the rent in March.
25. She *has fallen behind* two chapters.

**9. Fall through — fail to happen.** Of an arrangement that collapses before it is completed. (сорваться, провалиться (о плане, сделке))

26. Plans like that often *fall through*.
27. The sale *fell through* at the last minute.
28. Two offers *have fallen through* already.

**10. Fall for — be taken in by.** Informal: to be fooled by a trick or a lie you should have seen through. (попасться на, повестись на)

29. People still *fall for* that email.
30. He *fell for* the oldest trick in the book.
31. I *have fallen for* worse scams.

**11. Fall back on — resort to.** Of the reserve you turn to when the first choice fails: savings, an old job, a familiar excuse. (прибегать к, опираться на (запасной вариант))

32. It helps to have savings to *fall back on*.
33. She *fell back on* her old job.
34. They *have fallen back on* the same excuse all year.

### fly

**Pronunciation:** /flaɪ/ &middot; *FLY*
**Русский:** летать
**Forms:** *fly* /flaɪ/ &middot; *flew* /flu/ &middot; *flown* /floʊn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** y — ew — own &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *flies* (y becomes ies) &middot; **-ing form:** *flying*
**Contrast:** In baseball the verb is regular: a batter *flied out*, never *flew out*. Everywhere else it is *flew* and *flown*, so not *I have flew*. The past *flew* is a homophone of *flu* and *flue*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, of birds, aircraft, time, and tempers.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive of motion (*geese fly south*, *we flew to Denver*, *time flies*), transitive when you pilot or hoist something (*she flies cargo runs*, *ships fly the flag*) or flee a place (*he flew the country*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the flag has been flown at half staff all week*, *the last leg was flown by the copilot*. No passive of *we flew to Denver*.

To move through the air; to travel in or pilot an aircraft; and, of time and people, to move very fast.

**1. Move through the air.** The core sense, of birds, insects, and anything airborne. (летать, лететь)

1. Geese *fly* south along the river every October.
2. A hawk *flew* low over the field.
3. Sparks *have flown* into the dry grass.

**2. Travel by plane.** Of the passenger rather than the pilot. Not *I have flew* — the participle is *flown*. (летать самолётом, лететь)

4. I *fly* out Sunday night.
5. We *flew* to Denver on Tuesday morning.
6. She *has flown* this route a hundred times.

**3. Pilot an aircraft.** Transitive, with the aircraft as the object: *fly a plane*, *fly cargo*. (пилотировать, управлять самолётом)

7. She *flies* cargo runs to Alaska.
8. The copilot *flew* the last leg.
9. He *has flown* small planes since he was nineteen.

**4. Move fast, rush by.** Very common with time as the subject: *time flies*, *the week flew by*. (мчаться, лететь (о времени))

10. Time *flies* when the work is interesting.
11. The morning *flew* by.
12. The whole week *has flown* past.

**5. Fly a flag, fly a kite.** Transitive, of something held up in the wind — a flag, a kite, a banner. (поднимать флаг; запускать змея)

13. Ships *fly* the flag of the country they are registered in.
14. Kids *flew* kites all afternoon out on the point.
15. The embassy *has flown* the flag at half staff all week.

**6. Flee, get away.** Literary and legal, and old-fashioned in speech; ordinary English uses *flee, fled*. (спасаться бегством, бежать)

16. Families *fly* the city at the first sign of trouble.
17. He *flew* the country before the indictment.
18. Many *have flown* to the hills for safety.

**7. Fly off the handle — lose your temper.** Informal and fixed; the *handle* never varies. (вспылить, сорваться)

19. He *flies off the handle* over nothing.
20. She *flew off the handle* at the dispatcher.
21. He *has flown off the handle* twice this week.

**8. Baseball: hit a fly ball.** In this sense only, the verb is regular: *flied out*, never *flew out*. (выбить мяч свечой (бейсбол))

22. He tends to *fly* out to left.
23. She *flied* out to end the inning.
24. The cleanup hitter *has flied* out three times tonight.

4. Most parks *forbid* open fires in August.
5. State law *forbade* the sale outright.

### give

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɪv/ &middot; *GIV*
**Русский:** дать
**Forms:** *give* /ɡɪv/ &middot; *gave* /ɡeɪv/ &middot; *given* /ˈɡɪvən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** i — a — i, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *gives* &middot; **-ing form:** *giving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Not *he has gave it to me* — the participle is *given*. The vowel is short in the base and the participle (*giv*, *GIV-n*) and long only in the past (*gave*), which is the whole shape of this group in miniature.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, including the intransitive *the floorboard gave under his weight*. The participle *given* has hardened into a preposition in *given the weather*.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive** in the core sense, either order — *he gave me his number* / *he gave his number to me*. Also plain transitive (*they gave a party*) and intransitive (*alumni give to the fund*, *the old lock gives*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and either object can be promoted — *she was given the keys already* or *the keys were given to her*. No passive of *the floorboard gave*.

To hand something over or provide it — plus a large family of idioms from *give up* to *give birth*.

**1. Hand over, pass to someone.** The core sense, with two objects: *give someone something* or *give something to someone*. (давать, вручать, передавать)

1. They *give* every new hire a week of training.
2. He *gave* me his number on a napkin.
3. She *has given* me the keys already.

**2. Donate, contribute.** Of charity and gifts, often intransitive with *to*: *give to the fund*. (жертвовать, дарить)

4. Alumni *give* to the scholarship fund every spring.
5. They *gave* ten thousand dollars to the shelter.
6. We *have given* away most of the furniture.

**3. Provide, cause someone to have.** *Give someone trouble, a headache, an idea, a hard time* — the subject causes the object to have something, good or bad. (предоставлять; вызывать, доставлять)

7. Cheap tires *give* you nothing but trouble.
8. The noise *gave* me a headache.
9. That trip *has given* her a whole new plan.

**4. Give a talk, a party, a performance.** With an event as the object: the subject is the one putting it on. (выступать с (речью, докладом); устраивать (приём))

10. She *gives* the same talk twice a year.
11. They *gave* a party for the retiring dean.
12. He *has given* four concerts this month.

**5. Yield, bend under pressure.** Intransitive: something bends, stretches, or breaks rather than holding firm. (поддаваться, прогибаться)

13. The old lock *gives* if you push hard.
14. The floorboard *gave* under his weight.
15. The rope *had given* before anyone noticed.

**6. Give up — stop trying; surrender.** Two branches: to quit an effort, and to quit a habit. *Give oneself up* is to surrender to the police. (сдаваться; бросать (дело, привычку))

16. Do not *give up* this early.
17. He *gave up* smoking in his forties.
18. She *has given up* on the whole project.

**7. Give in — yield to pressure.** To stop resisting a demand, usually after holding out for a while. Often with *to*. (уступать, сдаваться под давлением)

19. Parents *give in* faster than they admit.
20. The union *gave in* on the pension question.
21. Management *has given in* twice already.

**8. Give out — run out; distribute; fail.** Three related uses: supplies run out, people hand things out, and bodies or machines quit working. (кончаться; раздавать; отказать (о силах))

22. Volunteers *give out* water at every mile.
23. The batteries *gave out* halfway through.
24. His knees *have given out*.

**9. Give away — donate; reveal.** To hand over for nothing, or to let a secret slip. *Give away the ending* is the standard spoiler warning. (отдавать даром; выдать (секрет))

25. The store *gives away* samples on Saturdays.
26. His face *gave* him *away*.
27. Somebody *has given away* the ending.

**10. Give birth.** A fixed phrase, with *to* before the child. Also figurative: *the era gave birth to a whole style*. (рожать, родить)

28. Cows on this farm *give birth* in early spring.
29. She *gave birth* on the way to the hospital.
30. She *has given birth* to three boys.

**11. Given — considering, in view of.** The participle has hardened into a preposition and conjunction: *given the weather*, *given that we are late*. (учитывая, принимая во внимание)

31. *Given* the weather, the turnout was good.
32. *Given that* nobody objected, we moved on.

**Pronunciation:** /ɡroʊ/ &middot; *GROH*
**Русский:** расти
**Forms:** *grow* /ɡroʊ/ &middot; *grew* /ɡru/ &middot; *grown* /ɡroʊn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ow — ew — own &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *grows* &middot; **-ing form:** *growing*
**Contrast:** Not *it has grew* — use *has grown*. The past *grew* rhymes with *do*, not with *grow*. With crops the verb takes an object (*they grow corn*); in the sense of getting bigger it takes none (*the town grew*).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; also **linking (copular)** in sense 4, where an adjective follows: *grow tired*, *grow dark*, *the crowd has grown quiet*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive when something gets bigger on its own (*the town has grown by half*), transitive when you cultivate or expand something (*she grows herbs*, *he has grown revenue every quarter*, *grow a beard*).

To get bigger over time, to cultivate something that does, and to become — gradually — something else.

**1. Get bigger, increase.** The core intransitive sense, of people, towns, numbers, and anything that gets larger on its own. (расти, увеличиваться)

1. Cities *grow* toward the water first.
2. He *grew* four inches over the summer.
3. The town *has grown* by half since 2010.

**2. Cultivate, raise (plants).** Transitive, with a crop or a plant as the object. (выращивать)

4. Tomatoes *grow* well on that side of the house.
5. She *grows* herbs on the fire escape.
6. They *have grown* the same corn for forty years.

**3. Let hair or a beard get longer.** Transitive, often with *out*: *grow it out*. (отращивать (волосы, бороду))

7. He decided to *grow* a beard in November.
8. Her hair *had grown* past her shoulders.
9. She *has grown* it out twice and cut it both times.

**4. Become, turn gradually.** With an adjective: *grow quiet, grow tired, grow dark*. Slightly more formal and slower-sounding than *get*. (становиться (постепенно))

10. People *grow* impatient in that line.
11. It *grew* dark before we reached the car.
12. The crowd *has grown* quiet.

**5. Expand a business.** Transitive and firmly business register: *grow the company*, *grow revenue*. Some editors still dislike it. (развивать, наращивать (бизнес, выручку))

13. They want to *grow* the company without new debt.
14. She *grew* the client list from six to sixty.
15. He *has grown* revenue every quarter.

**6. Grow up — become an adult.** Both about maturing and about where your childhood happened: *I grew up two towns over*. (взрослеть; расти (где-л.))

16. Kids *grow up* fast around here.
17. I *grew up* two towns over.
18. He *has grown up* a lot since the fall.

**7. Grow on someone — become likable.** Of something you disliked at first and came to enjoy. The subject is the thing, not the person. (начать нравиться со временем)

19. The song *grows on* you.
20. The apartment *grew on* her by August.
21. That painting *has grown on* me.

**8. Grow apart, grow into.** *Grow apart* is of relationships that fade; *grow into* is of a role or a piece of clothing you become the right size for. (отдаляться друг от друга; дорасти до)

22. Old friends sometimes *grow apart*.
23. They *grew apart* after the move.
24. He *has grown into* the job.

### know

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ/ &middot; *NOH*
**Русский:** знать
**Forms:** *know* /noʊ/ &middot; *knew* /nu/ &middot; *known* /noʊn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ow — ew — own &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *knows* &middot; **-ing form:** *knowing*
**Contrast:** The k is silent in every form. Not *I have knew* — the participle is *known*. *Knew* /nu/ is a homophone of *new*, and *know* of *no*.
**Verb type:** **stative** — a verb of cognition, and one of the clearest cases of a verb that rejects the progressive: ✗ *I am knowing the answer*; say *I know the answer*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, with a noun, a clause, or *how to* as the object — *they know the neighborhood*, *she knew how to fix the pump*. Weakened with a preposition in *I know of her*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — stative, so the plain passive is bad: ✗ *the answer is known by me*. It survives in fixed frames with no *by*-phrase: *the street is known for its bakeries*, *he was known around town as Doc*, *the place has been known by three names*.

To have information about something, to be acquainted with someone, and to be able to tell one thing from another.

**1. Have information, be aware.** The core sense, with a clause or a fact as the object. Not a progressive verb: not *I am knowing*. (знать (что-л.))

1. They *know* the neighborhood better than anyone.
2. I *knew* the answer and then forgot it.
3. We *had known* about the layoffs for weeks.

**2. Be acquainted with a person or place.** Of people, cities, and streets you have direct experience of, usually with *since* or *for*. (быть знакомым с, знать (кого-л.))

4. Do you *know* the family on the corner?
5. He *knew* the city well enough to skip the map.
6. She *has known* him since grade school.

**3. Have a skill, know how to.** *Know how to* plus a verb, or *know* a language or a trade. (уметь, владеть (языком, навыком))

7. He *knows* three languages.
8. She *knew* how to fix the pump herself.
9. Nobody here *has known* how to run that machine since he left.

**4. Recognize, tell apart.** Often *know someone by* a detail, or *know A from B*. (узнавать, различать)

10. You would *know* her anywhere by that laugh.
11. I *knew* him from his voice before he turned around.
12. He *has known* good work from bad since he was twenty.

**5. Experience, live through.** Usually in the perfect and often negative: *she has never known hunger*. Formal and a little literary. (испытать, познать, изведать)

13. Few of us *know* what that is like.
14. He *knew* real poverty as a child.
15. That family *has never known* a quiet year.

**6. Know of, know about.** Weaker than plain *know*: to have heard of something without direct acquaintance. (слышать о, быть в курсе)

16. I *know of* her, but we have never met.
17. Nobody *knew about* the second offer.
18. The board *has known about* the deficit since spring.

**7. Be known as, be known for.** Passive only. *Known as* gives a name or role; *known for* gives what something is famous for. (быть известным как; славиться)

19. The street *is known* for its bakeries.
20. He *was known* around town as Doc.
21. The place *has been known* by three names.

### lie

**Pronunciation:** /laɪ/ &middot; *LY*
**Русский:** лежать (лгать правильный глагол)
**Forms:** *lie* /laɪ/ &middot; *lay* /leɪ/ &middot; *lain* /leɪn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking, though *lain* is rare in speech
**Pattern:** ie — ay — ai, participle in -n &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *lies* &middot; **-ing form:** *lying* (the ie becomes y)
**Contrast:** Two traps at once. First, the transitive twin: *lay, laid, laid* means to put something down (*lay the book here*), while *lie, lay, lain* means to recline — and since the past of *lie* is spelled *lay*, the wrong *I laid down for a nap* is everywhere; the right form is *I lay down for a nap*. Second, *lie* meaning to tell an untruth is a regular verb: *lied, lied*.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the situated and remaining senses (*the town lies east of the river*, *the mill lay idle*) — closer to a position than an act; **action** only in the moment of lying down. The *lie idle / lie empty* pattern is effectively **linking**.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense. Its transitive twin is the separate regular *lay, laid, laid*: you *lay* a book down, but ✗ *I laid down for a nap* — the past of *lie* is *lay*: *I lay down for a nap*.
**Passive:** **no** — no object exists to promote: ✗ *the counter was lain on by the keys*. Only the active *the keys have lain on the counter*.

To be, or move into, a flat resting position; also, to be situated, and to consist in something. (The verb meaning *to tell an untruth* is a separate, regular verb: *lied, lied*.)

**1. Recline, lie down.** Takes no object. The transitive twin *lay, laid, laid* means to put something down, which is why *I laid down for a nap* is the most common error in English — the right form is *I lay down*. (лежать, ложиться)

1. I *lie* down for twenty minutes after lunch.
2. She *lay* on the couch all afternoon.
3. The keys *have lain* on the counter since Friday.
4. He *lay* awake until three in the morning.

**2. Be situated (of a place).** Of towns, borders, and land relative to something else. Slightly formal but standard in description. (находиться, располагаться)

5. The town *lies* just east of the river.
6. The border *lay* two hours north of the camp.
7. The ruins *have lain* under that field for centuries.

**3. Remain in a state — lie idle, lie empty.** With an adjective: something stays untouched or unused for a long stretch. (оставаться (без дела, пустым, нетронутым))

8. Half the lots *lie* empty.
9. The mill *lay* idle for a decade.
10. Snow *had lain* on the field for a week.

**4. Lie in, lie with — consist in; rest with.** Formal: *the problem lies in the wiring*, *the decision lies with the board*. (заключаться в; лежать на (об ответственности))

11. The trouble *lies* in the wiring, not the switch.
12. The final say *lay* with the trustees.
13. The answer *has lain* in the data all along.

**5. Lie ahead, lie behind.** Of what is coming and of what caused something, both common in narrative and news writing. (предстоять; стоять за (чем-л.))

14. A hard winter *lies* ahead.
15. Nobody knew what *lay* behind the decision.
16. Whatever *had lain* behind the delay, no one explained it.

To write over something that was already there, replacing it — most often data in a file.

5. Rents *rise* faster here than wages do.

**Contrast:** *I seen it* is the classic nonstandard slip: say *I saw it* or *I have seen it*. After *see*, a second verb takes the bare infinitive or -ing form — *I saw him leave*, *I saw him leaving* — never *I saw him to leave*.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the core perception and understanding senses, so it normally avoids the progressive: ✗ *I am seeing three deer*. The dating sense is the exception and is **dynamic**: *they have been seeing each other since March*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** with a noun, a clause, or a bare infinitive or -ing complement — *I saw him leave*, *I saw him leaving*, never ✗ *I saw him to leave*. Prepositional in *see to it that*.
**Passive:** **yes** — unusually for a stative verb, it passivizes freely: *that comet has not been seen since 1997*, *the film was seen as an apology for the war*.

To perceive with the eyes, and, by extension, to understand, to meet, to experience, or to make sure of something.

**1. Perceive with the eyes.** The base sense. It describes perception, not deliberate attention, so it is normally not used in the continuous: *I see it*, not *I am seeing it*. (видеть, замечать)

1. I *see* three deer at the edge of the field.
2. She *saw* the taillights turn onto the gravel road.
3. Nobody *has seen* that comet since 1997.
4. He *had seen* the letter long before he admitted it.

**2. Understand, grasp.** Very common, from *I see* as an acknowledgment to *see the point*. The object is an idea rather than a thing. (понимать, видеть (смысл))

5. I *see* what you mean about the second paragraph.
6. It took a week before anyone *saw* the flaw in the argument.
7. Now that you explain it, I *have* finally *seen* the logic of it.

**3. Meet with, consult.** Used of appointments and visits — *see a doctor*, *see a client*. The professional you consult is the object. (встречаться с, обращаться к (врачу, юристу))

8. You should *see* a doctor about that cough.
9. I *saw* my lawyer on Tuesday about the lease.
10. She *has seen* three specialists and gotten three answers.

**4. Be seeing someone — date.** Romantic, and one of the few uses of *see* that does take the continuous. *Are you seeing anyone?* is the standard way to ask about someone's relationship status. (встречаться (с кем-л., о романтических отношениях))

11. They *have been seeing* each other since March.
12. She *was seeing* somebody from the office at the time.
13. He does not *see* anyone seriously these days.

**5. Watch a movie, game, or show.** For a one-time event you *see* a film or a game; for regular viewing of television you *watch*. *Have you seen it?* is the standard question about a movie. (смотреть, посмотреть (фильм, спектакль))

14. Let's *see* the late show instead.
15. We *saw* the Cubs play in the rain.
16. I *have seen* that movie four times.

**6. See to it — make sure.** A fixed pattern for taking responsibility that something happens: *see to it that*, *see that*, or *see to* a task. (позаботиться, проследить, чтобы)

17. Please *see* that the doors are locked at closing.
18. The super *saw to* the leak the same afternoon.
19. Someone *has seen to* the paperwork already.

**7. Witness, experience (of a period).** The subject is a period, a place, or a thing rather than a person: *the decade saw enormous change*. Standard in journalism and history writing. (видеть, знавать, быть свидетелем (об эпохе, месте))

20. The next ten years will *see* the whole industry reorganized.
21. The 1990s *saw* a wave of small presses open and close.
22. This town *has seen* three floods since I moved here.

**8. See someone off, see someone home.** To go with a person to their point of departure or destination. *See off* is at a station or airport; *see home* is walking someone back. (проводить (кого-л.))

23. We always *see* the grandparents off at the gate.
24. He *saw* her home through the rain.
25. The whole family *had seen* him off at the pier.

**9. See through — not be fooled.** Two related fixed uses: *see through* a person or a lie means you are not deceived; *see something through* means you stay with a task to the end. (видеть насквозь; довести до конца)

26. Voters *see through* that kind of promise immediately.
27. She *saw* the project *through* despite losing half her staff.
28. He *has seen through* every excuse I have ever offered.

**10. See as, see in — regard.** With *as* it means consider or interpret; with *in* it asks what quality someone finds attractive or promising in another. (считать, воспринимать как; находить (в ком-л.))

29. I *see* the delay *as* a chance to test it properly.
30. Critics *saw* the film *as* an apology for the war.
31. I never *saw* what she *saw* in him.
32. Investors *have seen* real potential *in* the smaller markets.

### shake

**Pronunciation:** /ʃeɪk/ &middot; *SHAYK*
**Русский:** трясти
**Forms:** *shake* /ʃeɪk/ &middot; *shook* /ʃʊk/ &middot; *shaken* /ˈʃeɪkən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** a — oo — a, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *shakes* &middot; **-ing form:** *shaking* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Not *I have shook* — the participle is *shaken*. *Shaken* also serves as an adjective for someone badly upset: *she looked shaken*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, of literal motion and of emotional jolts alike.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive when you move something (*shake the bottle well*, *he shook the sand out*) or unsettle someone (*the accident shook the crew*), intransitive when the subject trembles on its own (*my hands shake*, *the whole house shook*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and the emotional sense is very often passive — *her confidence has been shaken by two rejections*, *the last apples have been shaken loose*. No passive of *the house shook*.

To move quickly back and forth, to tremble, or to unsettle someone badly.

**1. Move something back and forth.** Transitive and literal — bottles, blankets, trees, a fist. This is the sense behind the label on medicine bottles. (трясти, встряхивать, взбалтывать)

1. *Shake* the bottle well before pouring.
2. He *shook* the sand out of his shoes.
3. The wind *has shaken* the last apples loose.
4. Someone *had shaken* the vending machine hard enough to dent it.

**2. Tremble, vibrate.** Intransitive: the subject moves on its own, from cold, fear, laughter, or a passing train. (дрожать, трястись)

5. My hands *shake* whenever I have to speak in public.
6. The whole house *shook* when the truck went by.
7. The windows *have shaken* like that since the mine reopened.

**3. Shake hands.** A fixed social use, alone or with *on it* to mark an agreement: *let's shake on it*. (пожать руку)

8. In this business you *shake* hands and the deal is done.
9. They *shook* hands and never spoke again.
10. We *had shaken* on the price before the paperwork arrived.

**4. Shake one's head.** Another fixed collocation. In American culture shaking the head means no or disbelief, and nodding means yes. (качать головой (в знак несогласия))

11. He just *shakes* his head whenever the topic comes up.
12. She *shook* her head and went back to her book.
13. The doctor *had shaken* his head before saying a word.

**5. Upset, unsettle.** Figurative, about emotions and confidence. Very often passive — *she was badly shaken* — and also used of shaking someone's faith or belief. (потрясти, выбить из колеи)

14. Bad reviews do not *shake* him at all.
15. The accident *shook* everyone on the crew.
16. Her confidence *has been shaken* by two rejections in a row.

**6. Shake off — get rid of.** To free yourself from an illness, a mood, a follower, or a defender. Common in both everyday speech and sports commentary. (избавиться от, стряхнуть)

17. I cannot *shake off* this cold.
18. He *shook off* the tackler and went in for the score.
19. She *has* never quite *shaken off* the reputation she got that year.

**7. Shake up — reorganize; jolt.** Two senses of the same phrasal verb: to reorganize an institution drastically, or to shock a person. The noun *a shake-up* comes from the first. (перетряхнуть, реорганизовать; встряхнуть)

20. New owners always *shake up* the front office.
21. The near miss *shook* him *up* more than he let on.
22. Management *has shaken up* the department twice this year.

### slay

**Pronunciation:** /sleɪ/ &middot; *SLAY*
**Русский:** убивать (поражать - правильный глагол)
**Forms:** *slay* /sleɪ/ &middot; *slew* /slu/ &middot; *slain* /sleɪn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — the strong *slew/slain* is literary and headline usage; the modern slang sense is regular (*slayed*)
**Pattern:** ay — ew — ai, participle in -n &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *slays* &middot; **-ing form:** *slaying*
**Contrast:** The strong forms *slew* and *slain* belong to the killing sense, which survives in literature and in headlines (*four slain in crash*). The slang sense of impressing an audience is regular: *she slayed at the audition*. Do not confuse the past *slew* with the noun *a slew of*, meaning a large number.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the killing and the *you slay me* senses (*he slew the giant*, *her set slew the crowd*), intransitive in the modern slang: *they absolutely slayed*.
**Passive:** **yes** for the killing sense, where the passive is the commonest form — *thousands had been slain before the treaty*, *four slain in crash*. No passive of the intransitive slang *she slays every time*.

To kill violently, in older and literary language; in modern slang, to impress or amuse enormously.

**1. Kill violently.** Literary, biblical, or heroic in tone — dragons, giants, and enemies are *slain*. Modern news writing keeps it mainly in headlines, where it is short for *murdered*. (убивать, сражать, умерщвлять)

1. In the old stories a single knight can *slay* a dragon.
2. He *slew* the giant with a stone from the brook.
3. Thousands *had been slain* before the treaty was signed.
4. Two brothers *have been slain* on the same block this year.

**2. Amuse or impress hugely.** Informal: *you slay me* means you are hilarious, and a performer who *slays* an audience wins it completely. Older slang, but still widely understood. (сражать наповал, покорять)

5. That story *slays* me every time you tell it.
6. Her opening set *slew* the crowd at the small club.
7. The comic *has slain* tougher rooms than this one.

**3. Slay — excel (modern slang).** Recent slang, especially about fashion and performance: to look or perform outstandingly. In this sense the past is normally the regular *slayed*, not *slew* — the strong forms belong to the older senses. (быть на высоте, отжигать (сленг))

8. She *slays* every time she walks a runway.
9. They absolutely *slayed* that dance number.
10. He has *slayed* on that stage before.

### smite

**Pronunciation:** /smaɪt/ &middot; *SMYTE*
**Русский:** ударять; бить; разбивать
**Forms:** *smite* /smaɪt/ &middot; *smote* /smoʊt/ &middot; *smitten* /ˈsmɪtən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** archaic — biblical; the only living form is the adjective *smitten*
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -ten &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *smites* &middot; **-ing form:** *smiting* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Almost all living American use is the participle *smitten* as an adjective meaning infatuated: *he is smitten with her*. The finite forms *smite* and *smote* are biblical or deliberately jokey. The variant participle *smit* exists but is rare.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; archaic in the finite forms, and today alive mainly as the participle *smitten* used as an adjective meaning infatuated.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is a person, an enemy, or a place: *he smote the table with his fist*, *plague can smite a whole village*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and modern use is almost entirely passive — *he was smitten with her from the first rehearsal*, *the region has been smitten by drought three years running*.

To strike a heavy blow, in archaic and biblical language; in living use, mostly as *smitten* — struck by love or by affliction.

**1. Be smitten with — infatuated.** By far the most common modern use, and only in the passive participle: *smitten with* or *smitten by* someone. Light and slightly playful in tone. (быть влюблённым, сражённым (кем-л.))

1. He *was smitten* with her from the first rehearsal.
2. Anyone who meets that puppy *is smitten* immediately.
3. She *had been smitten* long before she admitted it to anyone.

**2. Strike hard.** Archaic and biblical, or used for humorous effect. The object is a person or an enemy, and the striker is often God or fate. (поражать, разить, ударять)

4. The old text warns that the wicked will *smite* one another.
5. He *smote* the table with his fist and walked out.
6. The angel *had smitten* the camp before morning.

**3. Afflict, strike down.** Formal or literary, of disease, guilt, or disaster falling on someone. Nearly always passive: *smitten by plague*, *smitten with remorse*. (поражать (о болезни, о совести))

7. Plague can *smite* a whole village in a season.
8. Conscience *smote* him halfway home.
9. The region *has been smitten* by drought three years running.

### stride

**Pronunciation:** /straɪd/ &middot; *STRYDE*
**Русский:** шагать (большими шагами); наносить удар
**Forms:** *stride* /straɪd/ &middot; *strode* /stroʊd/ &middot; *stridden* /ˈstrɪdən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — common in narrative writing; the participle *stridden* is rare enough that writers rephrase
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -den &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *strides* &middot; **-ing form:** *striding* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** The past *strode* is common and safe; the participle *stridden* is rare and sounds stiff, so most Americans recast the sentence (*he had walked in*) rather than say *he had stridden in*. Never *strided*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, a verb of manner of walking; it carries purpose, so you do not *stride* aimlessly.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in the living senses, usually with a direction or a preposition — *she strode to the front of the room*, *he strode over the fallen branch*. The rare literary sense 3 takes an object: *the colossus strode the harbor mouth*.
**Passive:** **no** in the ordinary walking senses — ✗ *the lobby had been stridden across*. Even the transitive sense 3 resists it, and writers rephrase around the stiff participle *stridden*.

To walk with long, decisive steps.

**1. Walk with long steps.** The main sense, and it carries confidence or purpose — you do not *stride* aimlessly. The participle *stridden* is rare enough that writers often rephrase to avoid it. (шагать (широким шагом), вышагивать)

1. He tends to *stride* rather than walk when he is angry.
2. She *strode* to the front of the room without a note in her hand.
3. The coach *has stridden* up and down that sideline for thirty seasons.
4. By then he *had stridden* halfway across the lobby.

**2. Stride over, stride across.** With a preposition it describes covering ground or stepping over an obstacle in one long step. (перешагнуть, шагать через)

5. Long-legged birds *stride* across the mudflats at low tide.
6. He *strode* over the fallen branch without breaking pace.
7. The hikers *had stridden* across three fields before noon.

**3. Stand astride, dominate.** Literary and now rare: to stand with a leg on either side of something, or figuratively to dominate it. Modern writing usually chooses *bestride* or *straddle* here instead. (стоять расставив ноги; возвышаться над)

8. In the old cartoons the magnate seems to *stride* the whole continent.
9. The colossus *strode* the harbor mouth in the legend.

### strive

**Pronunciation:** /straɪv/ &middot; *STRYVE*
**Русский:** стараться, прилагать усилия; бороться
**Forms:** *strive* /straɪv/ &middot; *strove* /stroʊv/ &middot; *striven* /ˈstrɪvən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — mission statements and edited prose; speech says *try hard*
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *strives* &middot; **-ing form:** *striving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** American English accepts both *strove/striven* and the regular *strived/strived*. In edited prose *strove* is still the more common past, while *strived* is gaining ground as the participle. *Strive* takes *to*, *for*, or *against*, never a plain object.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, of sustained effort over time.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** — it takes *to* plus a verb, *for* plus a goal, or *against* plus an obstacle, never a plain object: ✗ *she strove accuracy*; say *she strove for accuracy*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, so there is nothing to promote: ✗ *accuracy was striven for by her* is at best stilted. Keep the active.

To make great and sustained effort toward something difficult.

**1. Make a great effort, aim at.** Formal, used with *to* plus a verb or with *for* plus a goal. It implies long effort against difficulty, so it does not fit small everyday tasks. Both *strove* and the regular *strived* are accepted in American English; *strove* sounds more literary. (стремиться, стараться изо всех сил)

1. Small clinics *strive* to keep costs down without cutting hours.
2. She *strove* for accuracy even when nobody was checking.
3. He *has striven* for that title since he was a teenager.
4. They *had striven* for years to keep the mill open.

**2. Contend, struggle against.** Older and more literary: to fight or compete with someone, usually with *against* or *with*. Rare in modern speech. (бороться, состязаться (с кем-л., против чего-л.))

5. Reformers had to *strive* against a century of habit.
6. The two houses *strove* with each other for a generation.
7. The settlers *had striven* against that river every spring.

### take

**Pronunciation:** /teɪk/ &middot; *TAYK*
**Русский:** брать
**Forms:** *take* /teɪk/ &middot; *took* /tʊk/ &middot; *taken* /ˈteɪkən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** a — oo — a, participle in -en &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *takes* &middot; **-ing form:** *taking* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Not *I have took it* — the participle is *taken*. Keep *take* (away from the speaker) apart from *bring* (toward the speaker): *take this to the office*, *bring me the file*. Six other verbs in this group are built on it.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic across its whole range, including the light-verb uses (*take a look*, *take a break*) where the meaning sits in the noun.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in nearly every sense, and the object is obligatory — ✗ *she took* alone. **Ditransitive** in the carrying sense (*I take the kids their lunch*), and it takes an object plus a complement in *they took his silence as agreement*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *my umbrella has been taken from the stand*, *the accounts will be taken over in the fall*. The requirement sense resists it: ✗ *twenty minutes is taken to get downtown*.

To get hold of something, carry it, accept it, require it, or use it — the widest-ranging verb in this group.

**1. Get hold of, pick up.** The base physical sense: your hand closes on something. It also covers taking what is not yours, which is why *take* borders on *steal*. (брать, взять)

1. *Take* a napkin from the pile by the door.
2. She *took* the keys off the hook and left.
3. Someone *has taken* my umbrella from the stand.
4. By then the thieves *had taken* everything but the furniture.

**2. Carry or bring somewhere.** Movement away from where the speaker is — you *take* things there and *bring* them here. The direction is what separates the two verbs. (относить, отвозить, брать с собой)

5. I *take* the kids to school on my way in.
6. He *took* the package to the post office.
7. The neighbors *have taken* our mail inside all week.

**3. Require (time, effort, qualities).** Very common with time — *it takes an hour* — and with abstract requirements: *it takes patience*, *it takes two*. The subject is usually the empty *it*. (требовать, занимать (время, силы))

8. It *takes* about twenty minutes to get downtown.
9. The repair *took* longer than the estimate said.
10. It *has taken* three years to get the permits.

**4. Accept, receive.** Used of jobs, offers, advice, payment, and criticism. *We take cards* is the standard sign in an American store. (принимать (предложение, работу, оплату))

11. Most food trucks here *take* cards now.
12. She *took* the job in Seattle after all.
13. He *has* never *taken* advice from anyone.

**5. Consume (medicine, food, drink).** Standard with pills and doses, and with drugs in the negative sense. With food and drink it is a little formal — *take a little soup*. (принимать (лекарство), употреблять)

14. *Take* two of these with food.
15. He *took* his medication late and slept badly.
16. She *has taken* the same prescription for years.

**6. Travel by, use a route.** You *take* a bus, a cab, a flight, a highway, or the next exit. It covers both the vehicle and the road. (ехать на (транспорте), сворачивать на (дорогу))

17. I *take* the express bus when the weather is bad.
18. We *took* the coast road instead of the interstate.
19. They *have taken* that same flight every Monday.

**7. Study a subject; sit an exam.** American academic use: you *take* a class and *take* a test. British English says *sit* an exam and *do* a course. (изучать (предмет); сдавать (экзамен))

20. Freshmen *take* two writing courses their first year.
21. She *took* organic chemistry in the summer session.
22. He *has taken* the bar exam twice.

**8. Light-verb uses: take a break.** *Take* combines with many action nouns to name the action itself: *take a look, a walk, a shower, a break, a picture, a nap, notes, a seat*. The meaning lives in the noun, not the verb. (делать (снимок), сделать (перерыв), взглянуть)

23. Let's *take* a break before the next session.
24. He *took* a picture of the whole crew on the last day.
25. She *has taken* careful notes at every meeting.

**9. Regard, interpret.** Usually with *as* or in fixed frames: *take it as a compliment*, *take her seriously*, *take it that*. It is about how you read something, not about handling it. (воспринимать, расценивать)

26. Please do not *take* this the wrong way.
27. They *took* his silence as agreement.
28. I *had taken* her question for a joke until I saw her face.

**10. Take off.** Four common senses on one phrasal verb: remove clothing, leave the ground, leave abruptly (informal), and suddenly succeed. Context separates them easily. (снимать (одежду); взлетать; уходить; резко пойти вверх)

29. Please *take off* your shoes at the door.
30. The flight *took off* forty minutes late.
31. Sales *have taken off* since the review came out.

**11. Take after — resemble a relative.** Only about family, and about looks or character rather than behavior in a single moment. Never used of things. (быть похожим на (родственника))

32. The younger boy *takes after* his mother's side.
33. She *took after* her grandfather in temperament more than in looks.
34. None of the kids *have taken after* him in music.

**12. Take over, take on, take up.** Three high-frequency phrasal verbs: *take over* means assume control of something someone else ran; *take on* means accept a task or hire someone; *take up* means start a hobby or occupy space or time. (брать на себя, перенимать; нанимать; заняться (чем-л.))

35. A larger firm will *take over* the accounts in the fall.
36. She *took on* two new clients this month.

7. Prosecutors *have withdrawn* two of the charges.

**3. Pull out of, quit.** Intransitive with *from*: a candidate, a student, or a country stops taking part. (выходить (из гонки, конкурса), отказываться от участия)

8. Two candidates *withdraw* from the primary every cycle.
9. He *withdrew* from the tournament with a shoulder injury.
10. She *has withdrawn* from three classes this semester.

**4. Retreat, move back (troops, people).** Both transitive (a general *withdraws* forces) and intransitive (troops *withdraw*). Also used of people leaving a room formally. (отводить (войска); отступать; удаляться)

11. The commander decided to *withdraw* the forward units.
12. The jury *withdrew* to deliberate just after four.
13. Peacekeepers *have withdrawn* from the eastern districts.

**5. Become distant, withdrawn.** Psychological, about a person retreating from contact. Most often met as the adjective *withdrawn* — a quiet, closed-off person. (замыкаться в себе, отдаляться)

14. Teenagers sometimes *withdraw* from family entirely for a while.
15. He *withdrew* into his work after the divorce.
16. She *has withdrawn* from almost everyone she used to see.

**6. Withdraw from a drug.** Medical: to stop taking a substance the body has come to depend on. The noun *withdrawal* names the symptoms that follow. (прекращать приём (вещества), проходить ломку)

17. Patients should not *withdraw* from this medication abruptly.
18. He *withdrew* from nicotine over about six weeks.
19. She *has withdrawn* from the sedatives under supervision.

### write

**Pronunciation:** /raɪt/ &middot; *RYTE*
**Русский:** писать
**Forms:** *write* /raɪt/ &middot; *wrote* /roʊt/ &middot; *written* /ˈrɪtən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** i — o — i, participle in -ten &middot; **Group:** 12
**Third person:** *writes* &middot; **-ing form:** *writing* (drop the e; one t)
**Contrast:** Not *I have wrote* — the participle is *written*, with two t's, while *writing* has one. The w is silent throughout, so *write* is a homophone of *right*, and *wrote* of *rote*. Americans can say *write me* without *to*; British English keeps *write to me*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, a verb of creation and of recording.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in *he writes for two magazines*, transitive with a text or a document (*she wrote her first novel*), and **ditransitive** in American English: *the clinic will write you a prescription*, *write me when you get settled*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the same phrase has been written on every page*, *the remaining balance was written off*, *she has been written up twice for being late*.

To form letters and words, to compose a text, and to communicate or record in writing.

**1. Form letters and words.** The physical act — pen on paper, chalk on a board. The classic learner trap sits here: not *I have wrote* but *I have written*. (писать (буквы, слова))

1. Kids *write* their names before they can read them.
2. He *wrote* the address on the back of a receipt.
3. She *has written* the same phrase on every page.
4. Someone *had written* the date in pencil in the corner.

**2. Compose a text or work.** The authorial sense: books, articles, reports, essays. Used intransitively, *she writes* can mean she is a writer by trade. (писать, сочинять (книгу, статью))

5. He *writes* for two magazines and teaches on the side.
6. She *wrote* her first novel in a rented cabin.
7. They *have written* four reports on the same landfill.

**3. Write to someone, correspond.** American English allows *write me* as well as *write to me*; British English keeps the *to*. Used of letters and email alike. (писать (кому-л.), переписываться)

8. *Write* me when you get settled.
9. She *wrote* to her brother every Sunday for years.
10. He *has written* to the department three times without an answer.

**4. Write a check, write up.** To fill out a formal document: a check, a prescription, a ticket, an invoice. *Write up* also means to prepare a report or to cite someone formally. (выписывать (чек, счёт, штраф))

11. The clinic will *write* you a prescription today.
12. The officer *wrote* him a ticket for the expired tag.
13. Her manager *has written* her up twice for being late.

**5. Compose music.** Standard for songs, scores, and arrangements — you *write* a song the way you write a poem. (писать (музыку), сочинять)

14. She *writes* most of the band's material.
15. He *wrote* that melody in a single afternoon.
16. They *have written* together since high school.

**6. Write code; write data.** Two technical senses: programmers *write* software, and a program *writes* data to a disk or a file. Both are everyday vocabulary in computing. (писать (код); записывать (данные))

17. We *write* most of the tooling in Python.
18. The script *wrote* the results to a temporary file.
19. He *has written* every line of that parser himself.

**7. Write down, write off.** *Write down* means to record on paper. *Write off* has two senses: cancel a debt or reduce an asset's book value, and dismiss a person or thing as hopeless. (записать; списать (долг); поставить крест на)

20. *Write* it *down* before you forget the number.
21. The bank *wrote off* the remaining balance.
22. Critics *had written* him *off* years before his best season.
