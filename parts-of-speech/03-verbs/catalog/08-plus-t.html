Past = participle, add -t#

The past and participle add a -t to the unchanged base.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

Group 8 holds the verbs whose past and participle are identical to each other and differ from the base by nothing but a final -t: dwell — dwelt — dwelt, learn — learnt — learnt, spill — spilt — spilt. It is the -t twin of group 7, which adds -d instead (отличаются от 1-й добавлением "t"). The catch for an American learner is that most of this group is no longer irregular in American English: learned, spelled, smelled, burned, and spilled are the ordinary American spellings, the -t versions are British, and a handful of the -t spellings on the original list — tost, wrapt, blest, vext, dipt — survive only in old poetry. Every entry below gives the form an American writer actually uses first and then says exactly where the -t form still lives, because the two that really do keep it, dwell and indwell, are the exceptions rather than the rule.

bless#

Pronunciation
/blɛs/ · BLESS
Русский
благословлять
Forms
bless /blɛs/ · blessed /blɛst/ · blessed /blɛst/ (archaic blest /blɛst/)
Register
everyday — an American blesses food, sneezes, and hearts without thinking about it
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
blesses · -ing form: blessing
Contrast
The verb form is one syllable, /blɛst/, but the adjective spelled the same way is two: /ˈblɛsɪd/. The priest blessed the boats is /blɛst/; a blessed relief and a blessed event are /ˈblɛsɪd/. The spelling blest is not an error, just archaic — keep it for hymns and leave it out of prose.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense: the priest, the parent, or the speaker does something (blesses the fleet, blessed the ground). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is obligatory, whether a person, an object, or a place: she blesses the children, ✗ her mother blesses is incomplete. The interjection bless you is a fixed fragment, not an ordinary intransitive.
Passive
yes — the ground was blessed before a single shovel went in, that chapel has been blessed by three bishops. Sense 3 exists only in the passive: we have been blessed with patient neighbors, with no active ✗ something blessed us with neighbors.

To ask God's favor on someone or something, to make something holy, or — in the passive — to be lucky enough to have something good.

1. Ask God's favor on. The core religious sense: a priest, a parent, or a speaker calls down protection or good fortune on a person. Neutral in American English and common well outside church. (благословлять)

  1. The priest blessed the fleet on the first Sunday in June.
  2. Her mother blesses the children before every long trip.
  3. The bishop had blessed the building long before the first class met in it.

2. Consecrate, make holy. Used of objects, water, buildings, and ground rather than of people; the thing becomes sacred or set apart. (освящать)

  1. A rabbi came to bless the new sanctuary.
  2. They blessed the ground before a single shovel went in.
  3. That chapel has been blessed by three different bishops.

3. Be blessed with. Passive only, and usually entirely secular: to be lucky enough to have some good quality, person, or circumstance. (быть наделённым, иметь счастье обладать)

  1. We have been blessed with patient neighbors.
  2. He was blessed with a good ear and no patience at all.

4. Bless you; bless your heart. Fixed conversational phrases: bless you after a sneeze or as warm thanks, and Southern bless your heart, which is sincere sympathy or a soft insult depending entirely on tone. (будь здоров; спасибо тебе; (южн.) бедняжка)

  1. Bless you — that is exactly what I needed today.
  2. Bless her heart, she tried to fix the sink herself and made it worse.
See also
vex, toss

burn#

Pronunciation
/bɝn/ · BURN
Русский
жечь
Forms
burn /bɝn/ · burned /bɝnd/ · burned /bɝnd/ (also burnt /bɝnt/)
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base + -ed / base + -t
Group
8
Third person
burns · -ing form: burning
Contrast
American English writes burned for the verb — the toast burned, I have burned my hand. Burnt is the British past and participle, but it does survive in American English as an adjective sitting in front of a noun: burnt toast, burnt orange, burnt sugar. So an American burns the toast and then throws out the burnt toast.
Verb type
action — dynamic, and ergative: the same event runs both ways (the shed burned / vandals burned the shed). Not stative and not linking, though burn with shame takes a prepositional complement rather than an object.
Transitivity
both — intransitive when the thing itself is alight or reddening (dry pine burns fast, I burn in fifteen minutes, the bulb burned out), transitive when someone or something consumes it (they burned the shed, he burned his hand, the furnace burns a gallon an hour).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the shed was burned to the ground, a backup disc has been burned for every project. Sense 9 is essentially passive-only: investors got burned, I have been burned by contractors twice. No passive of the intransitive: ✗ fifteen minutes is burned in by me.

To be on fire or destroy by fire, and by extension to scorch, sting, consume fuel, or be consumed by a strong feeling.

1. Be on fire; destroy by fire. Works in both directions: the thing burns, or someone burns it. The literal sense that every other one grows out of. (гореть; сжигать)

  1. Dry pine burns fast and throws sparks.
  2. Vandals burned the shed to the ground.
  3. The barn has burned twice since the family bought the place.

2. Scorch food, overcook. Of food left too long on heat, and of the cook who left it there. Nothing is on fire — the food is just ruined. (подгорать; сжечь (еду))

  1. I burned the first batch of cookies and started over.
  2. Don't let the sauce burn while you set the table.
  3. She has burned every pot in this kitchen at least once.

3. Injure with heat or chemicals. Of skin damaged by heat, steam, acid, or friction. Often reflexive or with a body part as object — burn yourself, burn your hand. (обжечь(ся))

  1. He burned his hand on the oven rack.
  2. Steam had burned her wrist before she got the lid off.

4. Redden in the sun. Intransitive, of skin or of a person in strong sun. Tan is what happens when you don't burn. (обгорать на солнце)

  1. I burn in about fifteen minutes without sunscreen.
  2. His shoulders burned on the very first day of the trip.

5. Use as fuel; use up. Of fuel, calories, money, and time — the resource is consumed. Burn through stresses how fast it goes. (сжигать (топливо); расходовать)

  1. The old furnace burns about a gallon an hour.
  2. We burned half the budget on the first prototype.
  3. She has burned through three phone chargers this year.

6. Sting, feel hot. Of a body part or a strong taste producing a hot, painful sensation. No injury is implied. (жечь, саднить)

  1. The smoke burned my eyes all the way down the trail.
  2. My throat has burned since the fire started.
  3. That pepper will burn for a good ten minutes.

7. Be consumed by a feeling. Burn with shame, anger, or curiosity; also be burning to do something, meaning impatient. Literary but still current. (гореть (от стыда, гнева, желания))

  1. Her face burned when they read the letter out loud.
  2. Kids burn with curiosity at that age.
  3. He has burned with resentment about that promotion for two years.

8. Burn out. Of a bulb, motor, or fuse: stop working for good. Of a person: collapse from sustained overwork — the source of the noun burnout. (перегореть; выгореть (о человеке))

  1. Nurses burn out fastest in their third year.
  2. The porch light burned out again.
  3. Two of the residents have burned out and left medicine entirely.

9. Get burned (be cheated). Informal and figurative, almost always passive: to be cheated, betrayed, or badly hurt by trusting someone or something. (обжечься, погореть (обмануться))

  1. Investors got burned when the company folded.
  2. I have been burned by contractors twice, so I ask for references now.

10. Copy data to a disc. To write files to a CD or DVD. Peak-2005 vocabulary and fading with the hardware, but still instantly understood. (записывать (диск))

  1. He burned the whole album onto a CD for the drive.
  2. I have burned a backup disc for every project since that crash.
Phrasal verbs
burn down · burn off · burn out · burn through · burn up
See also
sunburn, spoil

chap#

Pronunciation
/tʃæp/ · CHAP
Русский
трескаться; раскалывать
Forms
chap /tʃæp/ · chapped /tʃæpt/ · chapped /tʃæpt/ (archaic chapt /tʃæpt/)
Register
everyday — chapped lips is ordinary winter talk, though the verb rarely appears outside that participle
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
chaps · -ing form: chapping (the p doubles)
Contrast
In modern American English this verb lives mostly in its participle, used as an adjective about skin: chapped lips, chapped hands. The old spelling chapt is dead. Do not confuse the verb with the British noun chap meaning "a man," which has no verb forms at all.
Verb type
action — dynamic, and ergative like burn: skin chaps, or wind chaps it. No stative or linking use; the very common chapped in chapped lips is the participle doing adjective duty.
Transitivity
both — intransitive with the skin or surface as subject (my hands chapped badly, the clay chapped into plates), transitive with the cold or the weather as subject (wind like this chaps your face).
Passive
restricted — grammatical but rare, because the participle is normally an adjective instead: her knuckles were chapped is read as a state, not as an event, and the agentive her face was chapped by the wind is possible but stiff. No passive of the intransitive ✗ it was chapped by my hands.

Of skin, to crack and turn sore in cold or dry air; in older use, to split or crack open generally.

1. Of skin, crack and redden. Both ways: skin chaps, or wind and cold chap it. In modern American English the verb lives mostly in its participle used as an adjective — chapped lips, chapped hands. (трескаться (о коже))

  1. Wind like this chaps your face in ten minutes.
  2. My hands chapped badly the first winter we lived in Minnesota.
  3. His lips have chapped every January since he was a kid.
  4. By March her knuckles had chapped and split open.

2. Split, crack open. The wider old sense, of dry ground, wood, or paint rather than skin. Archaic outside dialect; you will meet it only in older writing. (трескаться, раскалываться)

  1. Drought will chap bare ground faster than anyone expects.
  2. The clay chapped into plates all across the riverbed.
See also
nip, clap

clap#

Pronunciation
/klæp/ · KLAP
Русский
хлопать ладонями, аплодировать
Forms
clap /klæp/ · clapped /klæpt/ · clapped /klæpt/ (archaic clapt /klæpt/)
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
claps · -ing form: clapping (the p doubles)
Contrast
Clapped is the only form in modern American English; the clapt on the original list is a seventeenth-century spelling. The ending is pronounced /t/, which is what put the -t spelling there in the first place, but it is never written that way now.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, and abrupt by definition (clapped a hand over her mouth). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
both — intransitive in the applause sense, alone or with for (the whole room claps, they clapped for her), transitive elsewhere: clapped me on the shoulder, clapped a hand over her mouth, clapped him in jail, clapped eyes on the house.
Passive
restricted — fine where a person is affected: he was clapped in jail before his lawyer heard about it, players get clapped on the back after a bad inning. The applause sense has no passive (intransitive), and the idiom resists one: ✗ eyes were clapped on the house.

To strike your palms together in applause, and by extension to slap someone in friendly greeting or to put something somewhere abruptly.

1. Applaud. The everyday sense. Intransitive, or with for — they clapped for her; you clap a performance, not a person, when the verb is transitive. (хлопать в ладоши, аплодировать)

  1. The whole room claps at the end of every set.
  2. She clapped until her hands stung.
  3. The audience has clapped through six straight curtain calls.

2. Clap someone on the back. Almost always clap someone on the back or on the shoulder — a hearty gesture of congratulation, never aggression. (хлопнуть (по плечу, по спине))

  1. He clapped me on the shoulder and walked off without a word.
  2. Coaches clap their players on the back after a bad inning too.

3. Put abruptly or forcefully. Sudden, forceful placement: clap a hand over someone's mouth, clap on the brakes, or the old clap him in irons. The speed is the point. (рывком приложить; засадить (в тюрьму))

  1. She clapped a hand over her mouth before the laugh got out.
  2. They had clapped him in jail before his lawyer even heard about it.

4. Clap eyes on. Informal fixed phrase about a first or only sighting, and common in the negative: I've never clapped eyes on him. (увидеть, встретить (впервые))

  1. The minute she clapped eyes on the house she wanted it.
  2. I had never clapped eyes on the man before that morning.
See also
chap, nip

dip#

Pronunciation
/dɪp/ · DIP
Русский
погружать; окунать; макать
Forms
dip /dɪp/ · dipped /dɪpt/ · dipped /dɪpt/ (archaic dipt /dɪpt/)
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
dips · -ing form: dipping (the p doubles)
Contrast
Regular in every American register — dipped. The spelling dipt appears only in older verse. And do not reach for a vowel change by analogy with dive/dove: dip has never had one, so ✗ he dope the brush is not a form of anything.
Verb type
action — dynamic. Transitive senses are agentive (dip bread in the oil); the intransitive ones describe a brief downward movement of numbers or terrain (sales dipped, the road dips). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
both — transitive in the immersion and lowering senses (he dips his bread, ships dip their flags, she dipped water from the barrel), intransitive in the decline and slope senses (the temperature dipped below freezing) and in dip into (we dipped into savings).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the whole tray of strawberries has been dipped in chocolate, the flag was dipped as they passed. No passive of the fall or slope senses: ✗ below freezing was dipped by the temperature.

To put something briefly into a liquid, and by extension to drop, to slope downward, or to reach into a supply.

1. Put briefly into liquid. In and straight back out again — that brevity is what separates dip from soak or submerge. (макать, окунать)

  1. He dips his bread in the olive oil before every bite.
  2. She dipped a toe in the lake and decided against swimming.
  3. I have dipped the whole tray of strawberries in chocolate already.

2. Drop, decline briefly. Of numbers, prices, or temperature: down and usually back up. A permanent fall drops or falls; a dip is expected to recover. (снижаться, падать (ненадолго))

  1. The temperature dipped below freezing overnight.
  2. Sales have dipped for three quarters in a row.
  3. Ratings always dip in the second week of a run.

3. Slope downward. Of a road, a piece of land, or a rock layer that angles down; also of a bird or plane losing height for a moment. (уходить вниз, понижаться)

  1. The road dips twice before the bridge.
  2. The trail had dipped into fog before we ever saw the sign.

4. Dip into. Two uses of the same picture, both meaning to take a little: money out of savings, or a few pages out of a book you never read straight through. (запускать руку (в запасы); заглядывать (в книгу))

  1. We dipped into savings to cover the roof.
  2. I have dipped into that biography three times and never finished it.
  3. Feel free to dip into the report wherever it interests you.

5. Lower briefly. Of a flag, a head, a wing, or headlights. Note that headlights turned down are dipped beams in British English but low beams in American. (опускать (ненадолго))

  1. Ships dip their flags when they pass a naval vessel.
  2. He dipped his head and went back to work.

6. Scoop out. To lift liquid out with a container. Old-fashioned and regional in American English, where scoop or ladle is the ordinary word. (черпать)

  1. She dipped water from the barrel with a tin cup.
  2. The boys had dipped the trough dry by noon.
See also
nip, spill

dwell#

Pronunciation
/dwɛl/ · DWELL
Русский
обитать; задерживаться
Forms
dwell /dwɛl/ · dwelt /dwɛlt/ · dwelt /dwɛlt/ (also dwelled /dwɛld/)
Register
everyday — don't dwell on it is ordinary speech, though the "live somewhere" sense is literary
Pattern
base + -t
Group
8
Third person
dwells · -ing form: dwelling
Contrast
This is one of the two verbs in the group where the -t form is genuinely at home in American English: dwelt is normal in edited American prose and dwelled is the accepted alternative, not the default — the reverse of what happens with learn and spell. Watch the two senses: the literal "live somewhere" now sounds biblical or literary, while dwell on something is everyday American speech — Don't dwell on it.
Verb type
action in dwell on, a sustained mental activity that takes the progressive freely (he's dwelling on it again); closer to stative in the literary live, reside and be lodged in senses, which describe a standing situation (they dwell in a cabin, a resentment dwelt in him) and sit awkwardly in the progressive.
Transitivity
intransitive in every sense — the verb never takes a bare object and always leans on a preposition: dwell on one bad review, dwell in that valley, dwell in him. ✗ She dwelt the subject is impossible.
Passive
restricted — no ordinary passive, since there is no object; only the formal prepositional passive of sense 1 is available: the budget was dwelt on far too long. The residence senses have none at all: ✗ the cabin is dwelt in by them.

To keep thinking or talking about something, or — in older and literary use — to live somewhere.

1. Dwell on, dwell upon. Everyday American English and by far the most common use of the verb. Usually about something painful you would be better off letting go. (зацикливаться, останавливаться на (чём-л.))

  1. Don't dwell on one bad review.
  2. She dwelt on the subject for most of the drive home.
  3. He dwells on every mistake he has ever made.
  4. The speech had dwelt so long on the budget that it lost the room.

2. Live, reside. The original sense, now literary or biblical in American English — live is the ordinary word. Perfectly clear, just elevated. (обитать, проживать)

  1. They dwell in a cabin two miles from the nearest paved road.
  2. Her people dwelt in that valley for four generations.
  3. He has dwelt in the same apartment since 1994.

3. Be lodged in, persist within. Figurative extension of the live sense: a feeling or quality stays inside a person or a place. Literary in register. (жить, таиться (в душе))

  1. A quiet resentment dwelt in him for years.
  2. Something like hope has dwelt in that congregation through worse than this.
See also
indwell

enwrap#

Pronunciation
/ɛnˈræp/ · en-RAP
Русский
завёртывать, укутывать
Forms
enwrap /ɛnˈræp/ · enwrapped /ɛnˈræpt/ · enwrapped /ɛnˈræpt/ (archaic enwrapt /ɛnˈræpt/)
Register
rare — a literary prefixed form of wrap; Americans write wrapped or enveloped
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
enwraps · -ing form: enwrapping (the p doubles)
Contrast
Enwrap is wrap with a literary prefix and it inflects exactly like it. It is rare enough in American English that wrapped or enveloped is almost always the better word. The old spelling enwrapt is also the reason people confuse it with rapt "absorbed, entranced," which is a different word entirely and has nothing to do with wrapping.
Verb type
action — dynamic, the literary twin of wrap: fog, cloth, or darkness closes over something (silence has enwrapped the valley). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is obligatory, usually with in: she enwrapped the child in a quilt, ✗ museums enwrap before they ship is incomplete.
Passive
yes, and sense 2 lives in the passive: he was enwrapped in the argument and never heard the bell. Sense 1 passivizes normally too — fragile pieces are enwrapped in acid-free tissue before shipping.

To wrap something completely around or inside something else, or figuratively to absorb someone's whole attention — literary in both senses.

1. Wrap completely, envelop. Literary; in ordinary American prose wrap, envelop, or swaddle is the natural choice. Often of fog, cloth, or darkness covering something entirely. (окутывать, укутывать)

  1. Museums enwrap fragile pieces in acid-free tissue before they ship them.
  2. She enwrapped the child in a quilt and carried her inside.
  3. Silence has enwrapped the valley since the mill closed.

2. Absorb entirely, engross. Usually passive — enwrapped in thought. Rare and old-fashioned, and constantly confused with rapt 'entranced', which is a separate word; enwrapt is only an archaic spelling of this verb. (поглощать, увлекать)

  1. He was enwrapped in the argument and never heard the bell.
  2. The book had enwrapped her so completely that she missed her stop.
See also
wrap

indwell#

Pronunciation
/ɪnˈdwɛl/ · in-DWELL
Русский
обитать, проживать
Forms
indwell /ɪnˈdwɛl/ · indwelt /ɪnˈdwɛlt/ · indwelt /ɪnˈdwɛlt/
Register
rare — theology and medicine only, and almost always as the adjective indwelling
Pattern
base + -t
Group
8
Third person
indwells · -ing form: indwelling
Contrast
Indwell follows dwell exactly — the past and participle are indwelt, not indwelled. It is a narrow word: outside theology and philosophy you will meet it almost only as the adjective indwelling (the indwelling Spirit, an indwelling catheter), and the finite forms are genuinely rare.
Verb type
stative — it reports presence within, not an act, so the progressive is out as a verb (✗ grace is indwelling the believer); the -ing form survives only as an adjective (the indwelling Spirit, an indwelling catheter).
Transitivity
transitive — the thing inhabited is a direct object with no preposition: grace indwells the believer, a stubbornness indwelt every sentence she wrote.
Passive
restricted — the participial passive is standard inside theology (believers indwelt by the Spirit), but a full clausal passive is rare and the extended sense resists it: ✗ every sentence was indwelt by a quiet stubbornness.

To live or be present within something, especially in a spiritual sense — a narrow word met mostly as the adjective indwelling.

1. Be present within (spiritually). Theological vocabulary: the Spirit, grace, or God indwells a believer. The finite forms are genuinely rare, and the past and participle are indwelt, never indwelled. (пребывать, обитать (в ком-л.))

  1. Theologians argue about how grace can indwell a person at all.
  2. The doctrine holds that grace indwells the believer.
  3. Older translations say the Spirit indwelt the apostles.
  4. That conviction has indwelt the community for three centuries.

2. Pervade, be lodged in. Extended literary use for a quality or mood that seems to live inside a person, a text, or a building. (пронизывать, таиться внутри)

  1. A quiet stubbornness indwelt every sentence she wrote.
  2. Something like grief had indwelt the house long before we bought it.

dwell

learn#

Pronunciation
/lɝn/ · LURN
Русский
изучать, учить; узнавать
Forms
learn /lɝn/ · learned /lɝnd/ · learned /lɝnd/ (British learnt /lɝnt/)
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base + -ed (British base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
learns · -ing form: learning
Contrast
Learned is the American form; learnt is British and reads as a typo in American prose. Then watch the second learned: the adjective meaning "scholarly" has two syllables, /ˈlɝnɪd/ — a learned professor, my learned colleague — while the verb always has one, /lɝnd/. And learn cannot mean "teach": ✗ She learned me to drive is nonstandard; the sentence is She taught me to drive.
Verb type
action — dynamic in all senses, including the mental ones, and it takes the progressive freely (she's learning Spanish, we're still learning). Not stative, despite naming a change in what you know.
Transitivity
both — transitive with a subject, skill, text, or that-clause (he learned Spanish, she learned the whole part, they learned that the grant would not be renewed), intransitive when the object is understood (kids learn fast, he never learned). Nonstandard sense 6 is even ditransitive: his uncle learned him to shoot — dialect only, ✗ in standard prose.
Passive
restricted — natural with a text or skill and no agent (the lines were learned in four days, three new pieces have been learned this season), and the find out sense passivizes impersonally in news style (it was learned Friday that the grant would not be renewed). A by-phrase is stilted: ✗ Spanish was learned by him in eight months.

To take in knowledge or a skill, or to find something out.

1. Acquire knowledge or a skill. The central sense, with a subject or skill as object. Note that learn is what the student does; the teacher teaches. (учить, изучать, осваивать)

  1. Kids learn the alphabet before they learn to read.
  2. He learned Spanish in eight months of night classes.
  3. We have learned a lot from the first two rounds of testing.

2. Find out, hear of. Followed by that, of, or about. Slightly more formal than find out, and the standard verb in news writing. (узнавать, выяснять)

  1. I learned about the closure from a neighbor.
  2. We learn of most changes the same week the customers do.
  3. By Friday they had learned that the grant would not be renewed.

3. Learn to do something. With an infinitive: gain an ability, or come to accept a way of living. She learned to say no is about habit as much as skill. (научиться (делать))

  1. He learned to drive on a farm truck.
  2. You learn to live with the noise.
  3. I have learned to keep my mouth shut in those meetings.

4. Memorize. With a text, part, or piece as object, often with by heart. The result is recall, not understanding. (выучить наизусть)

  1. She learned the whole part in four days.
  2. The choir has learned three new pieces this season.
  3. Actors learn their lines before the first rehearsal.

5. Learn from; learn your lesson. To be changed by an experience, usually an unpleasant one. The fixed phrase is learn your lesson, often sarcastic in the negative. (извлечь урок)

  1. He never learned his lesson about lending money to family.
  2. We have learned from every outage this year.

6. Teach (nonstandard). Dialectal and jokey only — that'll learn you, I'll learn him. Never write it in standard prose; the standard verb is teach. (учить (кого-л.) — просторечие)

  1. That'll learn you to leave the gate open.
  2. His uncle learned him to shoot before he was ten.
Phrasal verbs
learn about, learn from · learn of
See also
relearn, unlearn

Passive
yes, though usually agentless — bad form has to be unlearned before anything better goes in, that idea has been unlearned by a whole generation. The active infinitive stays commoner: some things are harder to unlearn than to learn.

To deliberately get rid of a habit, an assumption, or a piece of knowledge you already have.

1. Get rid of a habit or skill. Of trained behavior — form, technique, an accent — that has to come out before anything better goes in. The effort is deliberate; simply forgetting is not unlearning. (разучиться, отучиться)

  1. New coaches spend the first month getting players to unlearn bad form.
  2. She unlearned the accent she grew up with and later wished she hadn't.
  3. By then the team had unlearned the shortcuts that caused the outage.

2. Discard a belief or assumption. Of ideas rather than skills: what you were taught and now reject. Common in modern writing about bias, education, and management. (отказаться от усвоенного, переосмыслить)

  1. I have unlearned almost everything that class taught me about commas.
  2. Some things are harder to unlearn than to learn in the first place.
  3. A whole generation has unlearned the idea that the office is the only place work happens.
See also
learn, relearn

To annoy or trouble someone, especially over a stretch of time.

1. Annoy, irritate. Formal and slightly old-fashioned in American speech, where annoy or bug is the everyday word; vexing and vexed stay current in writing. (досаждать, раздражать)

  1. Slow permits vexed the developer for two full years.
  2. It does not take much to vex him before his first cup of coffee.
  3. Small delays vex her far more than big ones do.

2. Baffle, resist solution. Of a question or problem that will not yield — the sense behind a vexed question and a vexing issue. The problem is the subject and the people working on it are the object. (ставить в тупик, мучить (о задаче))

  1. The same question vexes every committee that looks at it.
  2. That inconsistency has vexed editors since the style guide came out.
  3. The problem had vexed mathematicians for a century before anyone cracked it.

3. Afflict, harass (archaic). The old strong sense — storms, illness, or enemies tormenting a person or a country. You meet it in the King James Bible and older literature, not in modern American prose. (мучить, изводить)

  1. Storms vexed the coast all that winter.
  2. A fever had vexed the camp for a month.

wrap#

Pronunciation
/ræp/ · RAP
Русский
окутывать; завёртывать
Forms
wrap /ræp/ · wrapped /ræpt/ · wrapped /ræpt/ (archaic wrapt /ræpt/)
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base + -ed (archaic base + -t)
Group
8
Third person
wraps · -ing form: wrapping (the p doubles)
Contrast
The w is silent, so wrapped sounds exactly like rapt "completely absorbed" and like rapped "knocked" — all three are /ræpt/ and no two are spelled alike: wrapped in a blanket, rapt attention, rapped on the door. The archaic spelling wrapt is what made that confusion possible; modern American English writes wrapped and nothing else.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, literal or figurative (wrapped the leftovers, we wrapped up the audit). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
both — transitive in the covering, winding, and finishing senses (she wraps every gift, wrap the pipes, we wrapped up the audit), intransitive in the film sense and in dressing warmly (they wrap on Friday, wrap up — it's ten degrees out).
Passive
yes — every gift is wrapped in plain brown paper, the shoot had been wrapped two days early. Sense 6 is passive-only in form: he was wrapped up in the game, she has been wrapped up in that manuscript for months. The idiom in sense 7 refuses it: ✗ the numbers can't be wrapped my head around.

To cover something by folding material around it — and figuratively, to finish, to enfold, or to be absorbed in something.

1. Cover by folding material around. Paper, foil, or cloth folded over an object until it is covered. The everyday sense. (заворачивать, упаковывать)

  1. She wraps every gift in plain brown paper.
  2. He wrapped the leftovers and put them in the fridge.
  3. I have wrapped half the presents and hidden none of them.

2. Wind around. The material goes around the thing rather than over it — a scarf, a bandage, a cord, an arm. (обматывать, обвивать)

  1. Wrap the pipes before the first hard freeze.
  2. She wrapped a towel around her hair.
  3. Vines have wrapped themselves around the whole trellis.

3. Wrap up (finish). To bring a meeting, a job, or a deal to a close. Let's wrap up is standard American office speech. (завершать, заканчивать)

  1. Let's wrap this up before lunch.
  2. We wrapped up the audit two days early.
  3. The committee has wrapped up its work for the year.

4. Finish filming. Film and television: production ends. Hence the announcement that's a wrap and the noun wrap party. (закончить съёмки)

  1. They wrap on Friday no matter where the schedule stands.
  2. The crew had wrapped the shoot two days early.

5. Wrap up (dress warmly). Intransitive or reflexive: wrap up warm, wrapped up in a parka. Heard more in British speech but understood everywhere. (укутаться, одеться потеплее)

  1. Wrap up — it's ten degrees out there.
  2. The kids were wrapped up to the eyes and still cold.

6. Be wrapped up in. Passive only: so absorbed in something that nothing else registers. Also wrapped up in each other. (быть поглощённым (чем-л.))

  1. He was wrapped up in the game and never heard the phone.
  2. She has been wrapped up in that manuscript for months.

7. Wrap your head around. Informal fixed phrase for finally understanding something difficult, and usually negative — I can't wrap my head around it. (уложить в голове, осмыслить)

  1. I still can't wrap my head around the numbers.
  2. Nobody has wrapped their head around what the new rule actually requires.

8. Wrap a car around something. Colloquial American, and only in this frame: wrap a car around a pole or a tree, describing a violent head-on crash. (разбить машину (об столб))

  1. He wrapped his truck around a utility pole on the county road.
  2. Two kids had wrapped a car around that same tree the year before.
