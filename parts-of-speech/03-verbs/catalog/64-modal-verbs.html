Modal, semi-modal and defective verbs#

The verbs that carry mood rather than action — and the reason most of them have no participle.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

A modal is not a verb that does something; it is a verb that says how the speaker stands toward something else being done. He left is a fact. He must have left, he might leave, he would leave — same departure, three different stances. Because modals carry mood instead of action, English stripped them of nearly everything a verb normally has: no -s in the third person (✗ she musts), no -ing, no participle, no infinitive (✗ to must). What is left is one form, sometimes two, and a bare infinitive after it — must go, never ✗ must to go. Where a missing form is genuinely needed, a full verb steps in: be able to for can, have to for must, be allowed to for may.

The second trap is that the "past" forms are barely past. Would, should, could and might began as the past tenses of will, shall, can and may, and they still do that job in reported speech — but in ordinary American English they far more often mark politeness, distance or hypothesis in the present: Could you repeat that? is now, not yesterday. That is why each of them has an entry of its own below rather than a line inside its parent.

Can and may live with the irregular groups they inflect like — can in group 4 and may in group 5 — and the grammar behind all of this is in §2.5 and §5.5 of the verb reference.

Group

Third person
beware — no -s, ✗ he bewares · -ing form: none
Contrast
Not a modal — an ordinary lexical verb that happens to have lost every form but two. There is no ✗ bewares, ✗ bewared, ✗ bewaring, and it cannot follow is or have: ✗ I have bewared. It survives as an imperative (Beware of the dog) and as a bare infinitive after a modal or should (travelers should beware). Anywhere else, English switches to watch out for or be careful of.
Verb type
defective lexical verb — full meaning, almost no forms. It is the clearest non-modal example of the same gap the modals have.
Transitivity
both — with of (beware of unmarked taxis), or with a clause (beware that the lock sticks), or absolutely (Beware!).
Passive
no — it has no participle, so it cannot form one.

A full verb with two forms and no tenses — the shortest answer to why English grammar keeps a category called defective.

1. Warning about a danger. The imperative, and by far the commonest use. Takes of before a noun. (остерегайся, берегись)

  1. Beware of the dog.
  2. Beware of anyone who promises a guaranteed return.
  3. Beware — the bottom step is loose.

2. After a modal or should. The bare infinitive, the only other form it has. (следует остерегаться)

  1. Travelers should beware of unmarked taxis.
  2. Buyers must beware: the sale is final.
  3. You would do well to beware of his advice.

will#

Pronunciation
/wɪl/ · WIL · weak form /wəl/, contracted 'll /l/ in I'll go
Русский
вспомогательный глагол будущего времени; выражает согласие, обещание
Forms
will /wɪl/ · would /wʊd/ · — (no participle)
Verb class
modal
Register
everyday — the most common modal in the language
Pattern
i — ou (a modal, not an ordinary verb)
Group
64
Third person
will — no -s, ✗ he wills · -ing form: none; modals have no -ing and no infinitive
Contrast
English has no future tense — it has will plus a bare infinitive, which is why will is a modal and not an ending. Russian speakers reach for it too often: after when, if, as soon as, until and before, English uses the present, ✗ when I will arrive → when I arrive. A separate and fully regular verb will means "leave by will" (she willed the house to her niece), and the noun will means both "testament" and "willpower".
Verb type
modal auxiliary — takes a bare infinitive (will go), has no -s, no -ing, no participle (✗ he wills go, ✗ I have would, ✗ to will). Be going to and the present progressive fill in where a stance about the future is not wanted.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — a modal takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object. Bare will with a noun (I will it to happen) is the separate regular verb.
Passive
no — the verb it supports carries the voice: the letter will be sent, never will itself.

The modal of future time, willingness and prediction — and the one that contracts to 'll so reliably that learners stop hearing it.

1. Future time. The neutral way to put an event after now. With 'll in speech almost always. (буду, будет)

  1. The package will arrive on Thursday.
  2. She said the meeting would run long, and it did.
  3. I will be at the airport by six.

2. Willingness, offers and promises. Not time at all — consent. The negative won't is a refusal, and it is used of objects too. (соглашаться; обещать)

  1. I 'll carry that for you.
  2. The car won't start in this cold.
  3. He promised he would call the moment he landed.

3. Requests — Will you…?. A direct request. Would you is the softer version, which is why it is far commoner. (не могли бы вы)

  1. Will you hold the elevator?
  2. Would you mind closing the window?
  3. Will you please stop doing that.

4. Characteristic behavior. A habit presented as typical rather than scheduled — often mildly critical when stressed. (бывает, имеет обыкновение)

  1. He will sit there for hours without saying a word.
  2. Accidents will happen.
  3. She would always bring something from her garden.

would#

Pronunciation
/wʊd/ · WUUD · weak form /wəd/, contracted 'd /d/ in I'd go · the l is silent
Русский
бы (условное наклонение); бывало (о прошлой привычке)
Forms
would /wʊd/ · — · — (historically the past of will)
Verb class
modal
Register
everyday — and the single most important modal for sounding polite
Pattern
defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb)
Group
64
Third person
would — no -s, ✗ he woulds · -ing form: none; modals have no -ing and no infinitive
Contrast
Would is the past of will only in reported speech; everywhere else it is present-tense politeness or hypothesis, so a would in front of you is usually not about the past. It is a homophone of wood. Russian's бы covers most of it, but not the past-habit sense, where Russian uses бывало — and English then also allows used to. Never ✗ if I would have time: the if-clause takes the past, if I had time, I would call.
Verb type
modal auxiliary — bare infinitive after it (would go), no -s, no -ing, no participle. For a past hypothetical it stacks with have: would have gone.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — it takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object.
Passive
no — voice sits on the supported verb: it would be finished by Friday.

The workhorse of conditionals, polite requests and remembered habit — and the reason I would like sounds courteous where I want sounds blunt.

1. The hypothetical result. The main clause of a conditional: if + past, then would + bare infinitive. (бы)

  1. If I had the money, I would buy it outright.
  2. She would have told you if she had known.
  3. What would you do in my place?

2. Polite requests and offers. Present time, not past. Would you and I would like are the two politest frames in everyday English. (не могли бы; хотел бы)

  1. Would you pass me the salt?
  2. I would like a table by the window, please.
  3. Would you rather wait outside?

3. Past habit. A repeated action in a finished stretch of the past. Unlike used to, would cannot describe a past state — ✗ I would live in Boston. (бывало, обычно)

  1. Every summer we would drive up to the lake.
  2. My grandfather would read the paper cover to cover.
  3. She would never admit she was tired.

4. The past of will in reported speech. Backshift: will inside a past-tense report becomes would. (что будет — в косвенной речи)

  1. He said he would be late.
  2. They promised the parts would ship on Monday.
  3. I knew she would say that.

Contrast
The old textbook rule that shall marks the first-person future (I shall go) is dead in American speech — use will. What survives is the question Shall I…? / Shall we…?, offering to do something, where will would be wrong: ✗ Will I open the window? asks a prediction, not an offer. In contracts shall means "is required to", which is why drafters now often replace it with must.
Verb type
modal auxiliary — bare infinitive after it, no -s, no -ing, no participle.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — bare-infinitive complement only.
Passive
no — the supported verb carries voice: the fee shall be paid in advance.

Nearly gone from American speech except in one construction — but that construction has no substitute.

1. Offers and suggestions — Shall I…? / Shall we…?. The living use. It asks the listener to decide whether the speaker should act. (давай…?; мне …?)

  1. Shall I open a window?
  2. Shall we start without him?
  3. What shall I tell them?

2. Obligation in legal and formal writing. "Is required to." Common in contracts, statutes and specifications. (обязан, должен)

  1. The tenant shall give thirty days' notice.
  2. Each party shall bear its own costs.
  3. Applications shall be submitted in writing.

3. Dated first-person future. Old-fashioned in America, still current in British English and in set phrases. (буду, будем)

  1. We shall see.
  2. I shall never forget it.
  3. They said they should be glad to help.

/ʃʊd/ · SHUUD · weak form /ʃəd/ · the l is silent

Pattern
defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb)
Group
64
Third person
should — no -s, ✗ he shoulds · -ing form: none; modals have no -ing and no infinitive
Contrast
Should is advice; must and have to are obligation. Russian должен covers both, so learners overuse must and sound commanding — you should see a doctor is advice, you must see a doctor is an order. For the past, should does not become anything: use should have + participle, which always carries regret or reproach (I should have called). Spoken should've /ˈʃʊdəv/ is heard as "should of" — but never written that way.
Verb type
modal auxiliary — bare infinitive after it, no -s, no -ing, no participle. Stacks with have for the past: should have gone.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — bare-infinitive complement only.
Passive
no — voice sits on the supported verb: the form should be signed.

Advice, expectation, and — in should have — one of the few grammatical ways English has of expressing regret.

1. Advice and recommendation. The core sense. Weaker than must, stronger than could. (следует, стоит)

  1. You should get that looked at.
  2. We should have left earlier.
  3. He should apologize, and he knows it.

2. Expectation and probability. Not duty — a reasonable guess about how things will turn out. (должно быть, должен по идее)

  1. The train should be here any minute.
  2. That should be enough flour for two loaves.
  3. She should have landed by now.

3. Regret and reproach — should have. Something that did not happen and ought to have. Always with the participle, never ✗ should had. (надо было бы)

  1. I should have listened to you.
  2. They should never have signed it.
  3. You shouldn't have gone alone.

4. The past of shall in reported speech. Backshift, and in formal conditionals. (— в косвенной речи)

  1. He asked whether he should wait.
  2. Should you need anything, the front desk is open all night.
  3. She wondered what she should say.

/mʌst/ · MUHST · weak form /məst/

Forms
must /mʌst/ · — · — (no past, no participle)
Verb class
modal
Register
everyday — though have to has largely taken over the obligation sense in American speech
Pattern
defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb)
Group

Third person
must — no -s, ✗ he musts · -ing form: none; modals have no -ing and no infinitive
Contrast
The trap is the negative. Mustn't is a prohibition — you mustn't tell anyone — while don't have to is the absence of obligation: you don't have to tell anyone. They are opposites, and Russian не должен sits between them. Must also has no past: for past obligation use had to (I had to leave early), and reserve must have for deduction about the past (he must have left early).
Verb type
modal auxiliary — bare infinitive after it, no -s, no -ing, no participle, no infinitive (✗ to must). Have to supplies every missing form.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — bare-infinitive complement only. The noun a must ("something essential") is a separate word.
Passive
no — voice sits on the supported verb: the door must be locked.

Obligation and deduction in one form — and the negative means something entirely different from the negative of have to.

1. Obligation and necessity. A requirement, usually from a rule or from the speaker. Stronger and more impersonal than should. (должен, обязан)

  1. All passengers must show a boarding pass.
  2. I must remember to water the plants.
  3. You must be eighteen or older to apply.

2. Logical deduction. Not duty at all — the speaker's certainty, reasoned from evidence. The opposite is can't, not mustn't. (должно быть)

  1. She must be exhausted after that flight.
  2. There's no car in the drive — they must have left.
  3. You must be joking.

3. Prohibition — mustn't. Forbidden. Note that this is not the negative of sense 1's must in Russian terms. (нельзя)

  1. You mustn't say a word about this.
  2. Visitors must not feed the animals.
  3. We mustn't be late again.

The hedge: possible but not asserted — and the reason English speakers sound noncommittal to Russian ears.

1. Possibility. Something may or may not be or become true. The workhorse sense. (может быть, возможно)

  1. It might rain later.
  2. She might already know.
  3. I might not make it to dinner.

2. Past possibility — might have. A guess about something already settled. (мог бы; возможно, …)

  1. He might have taken the earlier train.
  2. We might have met before — you look familiar.
  3. It might have been worse.

3. Tentative suggestions and reproach. Softening a proposal, or — with stress — complaining that something was not done. (мог бы)

  1. You might try turning it off and on again.
  2. You might have told me.
  3. I thought we might walk instead.

4. The past of may in reported speech. Backshift. (— в косвенной речи)

  1. She said she might be late.
  2. He asked whether he might leave early.
  3. They warned us the road might be closed.

The politeness modal — and the one whose past-tense form misleads learners most, because three of its four senses are about the present.

1. Polite requests. Present time. The most frequent use of the word, and the safest register for a stranger. (не могли бы вы)

  1. Could you hold this for a second?
  2. Could I ask you something?
  3. Could we move the meeting to Thursday?

2. Possibility and suggestion. Weaker than will, close to might, and the ordinary way to float an idea. (мог бы, можно бы)

  1. We could take the coast road instead.
  2. That could be the mail carrier.
  3. You could always ask her yourself.

3. Past ability. A general capacity in the past, not a one-time success. (мог, умел)

  1. She could read before she started school.
  2. We couldn't see the stage from where we sat.
  3. He could run a mile in under six minutes back then.

4. Unreal past — could have. Something possible that did not happen, often with reproach or relief. (мог бы)

  1. You could have been hurt.
  2. I could have sworn I locked it.
  3. They couldn't have known.

The only modal that keeps to — and a near-twin of should that survives mostly in speech.

1. Duty and advice. What is right or sensible to do. (следует, надо бы)

  1. You ought to tell her the truth.
  2. We ought to leave a tip.
  3. Somebody ought to fix that step.

2. Expectation. A reasonable prediction, exactly like should. (должно быть, по идее)

  1. That ought to be enough for four people.
  2. The paint ought to be dry by morning.
  3. This ought to only take a minute.

3. Unfulfilled duty — ought to have. Regret, identical in force to should have. (надо было бы)

  1. I ought to have said something.
  2. They ought to have warned us.
  3. You oughtn't to have gone alone.

Two different words are spelled used. The semi-modal here is /juːst/ with an s and means "formerly"; the ordinary verb use /juːz/ → used /juːzd/ means "employ". Transitivity

Past habit and past state, both marked as over — the construction that says "and not anymore" without saying it.

1. Discontinued habit. Something done repeatedly then, not now. (раньше, бывало)

  1. We used to eat dinner at six sharp.
  2. She used to walk to work before the office moved.
  3. I used to smoke, but I quit years ago.

2. Discontinued state. A condition that has since changed — where would cannot be substituted. (раньше был, когда-то)

  1. There used to be a bakery on this corner.
  2. He used to be much heavier.
  3. This used to be farmland.

3. Questions and negatives — use to. The -d is carried by did, so the main form loses it. (раньше …? раньше не …)

  1. Did you use to play an instrument?
  2. It didn't use to cost this much.
  3. Didn't she use to work here?

Advice with a threat behind it — the had is not past, and the negative goes in a place learners never guess.

1. Urgent advice or warning. Present or future. The implied clause is "or something bad happens". (лучше бы)

  1. You 'd better leave now if you want a seat.
  2. We 'd better check the gate number again.
  3. He 'd better have a good explanation.

2. The negative — had better not. Not follows better, never had. (лучше не)

  1. You 'd better not be late.
  2. They 'd better not find out from someone else.
  3. I 'd better not say anything.

Bare infinitive, never to: ✗ I'd rather to stay. Comparison uses than, not that: I'd rather walk than drive. The real trap is the two-subject form — when the preference is about someone else, English switches to a past-tense clause: I'd rather you didn't (not ✗ I'd rather you don't), and for the past, I'd rather you hadn't. Would prefer is the close synonym but takes to: I'd prefer to walk.
Verb type
semi-modal — bare infinitive when the subject is the same, a past-tense clause when it differs.
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive — bare-infinitive or clausal complement.
Passive
no — voice sits on the supported verb: I'd rather be told directly.

Preference — and the construction where English suddenly demands a past tense for a present wish.

1. Simple preference. Same subject, bare infinitive, than for the comparison. (предпочёл бы)

  1. I 'd rather stay in tonight.
  2. She 'd rather walk than wait for the bus.
  3. Would you rather sit inside or out?

2. Preference about someone else. Different subject, and the verb goes into the past although the meaning is present. (я бы предпочёл, чтобы…)

  1. I 'd rather you didn't mention it.
  2. She 'd rather we came on Sunday.
  3. They 'd rather nobody knew yet.

3. Preference about the past. Would rather have + participle, or a past-perfect clause. (лучше бы)

  1. I 'd rather have heard it from you.
  2. He 'd rather you hadn't told her.
  3. We 'd rather have paid more and gotten it right.
