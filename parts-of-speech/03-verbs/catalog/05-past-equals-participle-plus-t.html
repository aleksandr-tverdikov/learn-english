Past = participle, vowel change plus -t#

Like the previous group, with a -t added to the changed stem.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

These verbs change the vowel of the stem and then add a -t, and that single form does duty for both the past and the participle (как №3, плюс окончание "t"). Keep shortens to kept, feel to felt, leave to left — one form to memorize instead of two. A large subgroup goes further and rewrites the whole ending as -ought or -aught: bring/brought, teach/taught, think/thought, buy/bought. Watch the handful of verbs whose British forms end in -t but whose American forms are plain -ed — dreamed, leaned, and daydreamed are the American defaults, and wreak takes wreaked, never wrought.

Pattern

Group

Third person
beseeches (adds -es) · -ing form: beseeching
Contrast
The regular beseeched is now the more common past in American writing; besought is literary and turns up mainly in older prose and translations of scripture. Either is correct, so long as you use the same one twice: ✗ he beseeched them, and had besought them again.
Verb type
action — dynamic; a verb of asking, so it reports a speech act rather than a state. No linking or stative use.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is the person begged, and it is obligatory: the families beseech the governor, ✗ the families beseech. Usually completed by an infinitive (beseech him to forgive her) or by for + the thing wanted (besought the judge for one more week).
Passive
yes, though formal like the verb itself — the judge was besought for one more week; the agency has been besought for years to publish the data.

To beg someone urgently and humbly for something; formal and literary in modern American English.

1. Beg urgently, implore. Formal and literary; in ordinary speech Americans beg or plead instead. The regular past beseeched is now more common in American writing than besought. (умолять, упрашивать)

  1. The families beseech the governor to reopen the case.
  2. Her lawyers besought the judge for one more week.
  3. Advocates have besought the agency for years to publish the data.

2. Entreat in prayer or older prose. The scriptural and literary register, where besought is the expected past. You meet it in translations, hymns, and historical fiction rather than in speech. (молить, взывать)

  1. The pilgrims besought heaven for rain.
  2. He had besought his father to forgive him before the old man died.
  3. In the last chapter she beseeches the reader to remember her kindly.

bring#

Pronunciation
/brɪŋ/ · BRING
Русский
приносить
Forms
bring /brɪŋ/ · brought /brɔt/ · brought /brɔt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
-ing → -ought
Group
5
Third person
brings · -ing form: bringing
Contrast
✗ brang and ✗ brung are heard in some American dialects but are never standard. The trap is that sing/sang/sung and ring/rang/rung look like they should set the pattern; bring refuses to join them. Also keep brought (carried) apart from bought (paid for).
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, from carrying (bring your ID) to causing (spring brings the tourists back). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
transitive, and freely ditransitive: bring me the file, bring her a chair. The object is obligatory — ✗ she brought is incomplete. The particle uses keep the object (bring up the layoffs, bring in a consultant).
Passive
yes, and very common — he was brought up by his grandmother; three cases have been brought against the company; the resignation was brought about by their reporting.

To carry or take someone or something to the place where you are or are going; also, to cause a result or a state.

1. Carry or take to this place. The motion is toward the speaker or toward the place being talked about. If the movement is away from you, the verb is take. (приносить, приводить, привозить)

  1. Please bring your ID to the appointment.
  2. She brought dessert to the potluck.
  3. Somebody has brought a dog into the lobby.

2. Cause a state or result. The subject produces the effect, and the object is usually abstract: rain, joy, trouble, publicity, change. (приносить, вызывать, приводить к)

  1. Spring brings the tourists back.
  2. The merger brought three years of chaos.
  3. That decision has brought nothing but lawsuits.

3. Bring about (cause to happen). A fixed phrase for making a change actually happen, often after effort or delay. (осуществить, вызвать, привести к)

  1. It took a strike to bring about a new contract.
  2. Their reporting brought about the resignation.
  3. Technology has brought about changes nobody planned for.

4. Bring up (raise a topic). To introduce a subject into a conversation, often one others would rather avoid. (поднять тему, упомянуть)

  1. Don't bring up the layoffs at dinner.
  2. She brought up the budget again.
  3. Nobody has brought it up since the meeting.

5. Bring up (raise a child). Common in both countries, though Americans often prefer raise. Frequently passive: he was brought up in Ohio. (воспитывать, растить)

  1. He was brought up by his grandmother.
  2. They brought up four kids on one salary.
  3. She has brought up two children alone.

6. Bring in (earn or recruit). Two everyday uses: money that a business takes in, and a person hired or called in from outside. (приносить доход; привлекать со стороны)

  1. The store brings in about six thousand a week.
  2. They brought in a consultant after the second outage.
  3. We have brought in two new engineers this quarter.

7. Bring down (topple or reduce). Used both of removing someone from power and of lowering a number such as a price, a fever, or a rate. (свергнуть; сбить, снизить)

  1. The scandal brought down the mayor.
  2. Nothing will bring down a fever that fast.
  3. Generic drugs have brought prices down sharply.

8. Bring oneself to (force oneself). Almost always negative or with can't: you are unable to make yourself do something painful. (заставить себя)

  1. I can't bring myself to sell the house.
  2. She never brought herself to apologize.
  3. He has never brought himself to watch the tape.

9. Bring charges, bring a suit. The standard legal collocation for starting a case against someone. (выдвинуть обвинения, подать иск)

  1. Prosecutors may still bring charges.
  2. The tenants brought a class action.
  3. The state has brought three cases against the company.

buy#

Pronunciation
/baɪ/ · BY
Русский
купить
Forms
buy /baɪ/ · bought /bɔt/ · bought /bɔt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — the ordinary word for paying money for something
Pattern
-uy → -ought
Group
5
Third person
buys · -ing form: buying
Contrast
Bought and brought are one letter apart and are swapped constantly: ✗ I brought a new laptop when you mean you paid for one. If money changed hands it is bought; if you carried it somewhere it is brought.
Verb type
action — dynamic in the purchase, bribery, and buy out senses. The believe sense (I don't buy it) is mental but still takes the progressive: I'm not buying that story.
Transitivity
transitive, and ditransitive in buy her a coffee, buy us two more weeks. The object is normally obligatory (✗ we bought at the market), though buy can stand alone in shopping contexts: we buy local.
Passive
yes — the truck was bought used; two inspectors were bought off; the chain has been bought out by a private equity firm.

To get something by paying money for it; informally, also to believe something or to gain it at a cost.

1. Purchase, pay money for. The core sense. Keep it apart from brought, the past of bring: money makes it bought. (покупать, приобретать)

  1. We buy our vegetables at the farmers market.
  2. He bought the truck used, with ninety thousand miles on it.
  3. They have bought a house outside Tucson.

2. Believe, accept as true. Informal, and usually negative: I don't buy it. The object is a story, an excuse, or an argument. (верить, купиться на)

  1. I don't buy that excuse.
  2. The jury never bought his story.
  3. Nobody has bought that line since the first press conference.

3. Gain at a cost. What is paid is not always money; the object is often time, silence, safety, or goodwill. (выиграть (время), купить ценой чего-либо)

  1. The delay bought us two more weeks.
  2. A second server would buy some breathing room.
  3. That concession has bought them nothing at all.

4. Bribe, buy off. Informal but unambiguous. With off it means paying someone to drop an objection or a claim. (подкупить)

  1. You cannot buy a federal judge.
  2. The company bought off two inspectors.
  3. Someone had bought his silence long before the hearing.

5. Buy into (accept, or take a stake). Either to be persuaded by an idea or to purchase a share of a business. The context separates them. (принять идею; войти в долю)

  1. Half the team never bought into the new process.
  2. She bought into the partnership in 2019.
  3. Investors have bought into the story completely.

6. Buy out (purchase someone's share). To acquire the whole of another party's stake so they leave the business. (выкупить долю)

  1. His brothers bought him out.
  2. They offered to buy her out at book value.
  3. A private equity firm has bought out the chain.
Phrasal verbs
buy back · buy in · buy into · buy off · buy out · buy up
See also
bring, catch, lose

catch#

Pronunciation
/kætʃ/ · KACH
Русский
ловить, хватать
Forms
catch /kætʃ/ · caught /kɔt/ · caught /kɔt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — constant in speech, from catching a ball to catching a cold
Pattern
-atch → -aught
Group
5
Third person
catches (adds -es) · -ing form: catching
Contrast
✗ catched is not a form. For the many Americans with the cot–caught merger, caught /kɔt/ and cot /kɑt/ sound identical, but the spelling never wavers — and caught is spelled with the same -aught as taught.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense, including the involuntary ones (catch a cold, catch the joke). No linking or stative use.
Transitivity
both — transitive in most senses (caught the glass, caught three fish), intransitive in the zipper catches and in catch on / catch up (go ahead; I'll catch up).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the driver has been caught; two managers have been caught falsifying the logs. Restricted for the illness sense, where the active is the only natural form: ✗ a cold was caught by him.

To take hold of something moving, and by extension to capture, reach in time, notice, or contract something.

1. Seize something moving. The literal sense: stop an object in flight with your hands. (ловить, поймать)

  1. Dogs catch frisbees better than most people do.
  2. She caught the glass before it hit the floor.
  3. The goalie has caught everything they have thrown at him.

2. Capture, trap. Of animals, fish, or people who are running from you. (поймать, задержать)

  1. Traps rarely catch anything this time of year.
  2. We caught three fish and threw them all back.
  3. Police have caught the driver.

3. Discover someone in the act. Normally catch someone doing something, with an -ing form and never an infinitive. (застать, поймать с поличным)

  1. He caught his roommate going through his mail.
  2. Don't let them catch you parking there.
  3. Two managers have been caught falsifying the logs.

4. Be in time for. Of trains, flights, the end of a movie, or a person before they leave. (успеть на, застать)

  1. If I run I can catch the 6:40.
  2. She caught the last train out of the city.
  3. We have caught that flight twice with ten minutes to spare.

5. Contract an illness. Used of infections you pick up from other people, not of chronic conditions. (подхватить, заразиться)

  1. Kids catch everything in their first year of school.
  2. He caught a cold the week before the wedding.
  3. She has caught the flu twice this winter.

6. Hear or understand. Usually negative or in an apology: I didn't catch that. (расслышать, уловить)

  1. I didn't catch your last name.
  2. He caught only half of what she said over the noise.
  3. Nobody has caught the joke yet.

7. Notice, spot a mistake. Of errors, discrepancies, and problems found before they do damage. (заметить, обнаружить)

  1. Spellcheck catches the obvious ones.
  2. She caught the discrepancy on the second read.
  3. An intern has caught two errors the auditors missed.

8. Snag, become stuck. The subject is the thing that snags, and the phrase is normally catch on something. (зацепиться, застрять)

  1. The zipper catches about halfway up.
  2. My sleeve caught on the door handle.
  3. A branch had caught the kite ten feet up.

9. Catch up (reach the same point). Of distance, of work left undone, or of news between friends who have not talked in a while. (догнать, наверстать)

  1. Go ahead; I'll catch up.
  2. He caught up on email over the weekend.
  3. We have caught up on the backlog at last.

10. Catch on (become popular; understand). Two settled meanings: a fashion or a name spreads, or a person finally grasps what is going on. (прижиться, войти в моду; сообразить)

  1. The name never caught on.
  2. She caught on faster than anyone expected.
  3. The style has caught on with younger buyers.
Phrasal verbs
catch at · catch on · catch out · catch up · catch up on · catch up with
See also
teach, seek, buy

creep#

Pronunciation
/krip/ · KREEP
Русский
ползти
Forms
creep /krip/ · crept /krɛpt/ · crept /krɛpt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — ordinary in speech, especially in creep out and creep up on
Pattern
ee → e + t
Group
5
Third person
creeps · -ing form: creeping
Contrast
The past is crept — except in the fixed slang phrase creep someone out, which is regular: that movie creeped me out, ✗ that movie crept me out.
Verb type
action — dynamic, and typically slow or gradual: literal movement (the cat crept along the fence) and figurative advance (costs have crept up).
Transitivity
intransitive in every literal and figurative sense — the direction is carried by a preposition (creep along the rail, creep into her emails). Transitive only in the fixed slang creep someone out.
Passive
no for the movement senses, which have no object at all: ✗ the fence rail was crept along. The one passive is the slang idiom — I was completely creeped out by that mask.

To move slowly, quietly, and low to the ground, or to advance by small degrees.

1. Move slowly and stealthily. Quiet movement meant to go unnoticed, often close to the ground. (красться, ползти)

  1. Don't creep around behind me like that.
  2. The cat crept along the fence rail.
  3. He had crept downstairs before anyone else woke.

2. Advance by small degrees. Figurative, usually with up or in: costs, delays, doubts, and errors creep rather than jump. (расти незаметно, вкрадываться)

  1. Doubt creeps in around three in the morning.
  2. A little sarcasm crept into her emails.
  3. Costs have crept up about four percent this year.

3. Grow along a surface. Of ivy, vines, and moss; the adjective creeping names whole plant families. (стелиться, виться (о растениях))

  1. Ivy will creep up the north wall if you let it.
  2. Moss crept over the stones.
  3. The vine has crept across the whole trellis.

4. Creep up on (approach unnoticed). Of a person sneaking up, and just as often of a date or a season arriving before you noticed. (подкрасться незаметно)

  1. Winter crept up on us.
  2. She creeps up on you with the sad part of the song.
  3. The deadline has crept up on everyone.

5. Creep someone out. Informal, and the one use of this verb with a regular past: that movie creeped me out, never crept me out. (жутко пугать, вызывать мурашки)

  1. That mask creeps me out.
  2. The whole basement creeped us out.
  3. His messages have creeped her out for weeks.
Phrasal verbs
creep in · creep out · creep up · creep up on
See also
sleep, sweep, weep, keep

daydream#

Pronunciation
/ˈdeɪˌdrim/ · DAY-dreem
Русский
грезить наяву; мечтать, фантазировать
Forms
daydream /ˈdeɪˌdrim/ · daydreamed /ˈdeɪˌdrimd/ (also daydreamt /ˈdeɪˌdrɛmt/) · daydreamed /ˈdeɪˌdrimd/ (also daydreamt)
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — the ordinary word for letting your mind wander
Pattern
regular -ed in American English (British ea → e + t)
Group
5
Third person
daydreams · -ing form: daydreaming
Contrast
Built on dream, so it takes the same two pasts — but the split is even sharper here. American writing uses daydreamed almost without exception; daydreamt, which the spreadsheet lists, reads as British or deliberately old-fashioned. See dream.
Verb type
action — dynamic; it names an ongoing mental activity and takes the progressive freely: he was daydreaming through the lecture.
Transitivity
intransitive — no object at all; what is imagined goes into a prepositional phrase with about or of: I daydream about a week with no phone, ✗ I daydream a week with no phone.
Passive
no — there is no object to promote: ✗ a bakery was daydreamed about for years is not how this is said. Only the awkward prepositional passive is even conceivable, and Americans avoid it.

To let your mind wander to pleasant imaginary things while you are awake.

1. Drift off, stop paying attention. No object: the point is the lapse of attention rather than what you imagine. American English uses daydreamed; daydreamt reads as British. (витать в облаках, отвлекаться)

  1. I daydream through most of the Monday status call.
  2. He daydreamed his way through an hour of the lecture.
  3. My son has daydreamed through two years of algebra.

2. Daydream about (imagine what you want). With about or of. What follows is a wish rather than a plan, which is what separates it from plan or intend. (мечтать, грезить о чём-либо)

  1. I daydream about a week with no phone.
  2. She daydreamed about quitting for the whole drive home.
  3. They have daydreamed about opening a bakery for years.

deal /dil/ · dealt /dɛlt/ · dealt /dɛlt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — deal with it and deal the cards are both plain speech
Pattern

5
Third person
deals · -ing form: dealing
Contrast
The spelling keeps the ea but the vowel shortens: dealt rhymes with belt, not with sealed. ✗ dealed is not a form, and ✗ delt is a common misspelling.
Verb type
action — dynamic in all senses. The be about use (chapter four deals with the war years) is closer to a state but still takes the ordinary tenses, not the progressive: ✗ chapter four is dealing with the war years.
Transitivity
both — transitive and ditransitive in deal me in, deal him four bad hands, dealt the plaintiffs a blow; prepositional-intransitive in deal with and deal in; bare intransitive in the drug sense (he got caught dealing).
Passive
yes — the cards were dealt; a blow was dealt to the plaintiffs. The prepositional passive is natural too: the complaint has already been dealt with.

To handle or cope with something, to hand out playing cards, or to trade in goods.

1. Deal with (handle, cope with). The everyday sense, always with with. The object is a problem, a complaint, or a difficult person. (справляться, иметь дело с)

  1. I deal with vendor complaints all morning.
  2. He dealt with the same landlord for six years.
  3. The agency has dealt with worse crises than this one.

2. Hand out playing cards. The card-table sense, and the source of the noun dealer and of a raw deal. (сдавать карты)

  1. Deal me in.
  2. She dealt the cards and we started playing.
  3. The house has dealt him four bad hands in a row.

3. Deal in (trade in, sell). Of a business and the goods it handles. Also figurative: a paper that deals in rumor. (торговать чем-либо)

  1. The shop deals in rare maps.
  2. His family dealt in grain for a century.
  3. They have dealt in used equipment since the eighties.

4. Sell illegal drugs. Informal, and usually with no object at all: he got caught dealing. (торговать наркотиками)

  1. You do not deal on that corner without permission.
  2. Two of them dealt out of the same apartment.
  3. She has never dealt anything harder than weed.

5. Deal a blow, deal out. Somewhat formal. The object is a blow, damage, justice, or the hand that fate gives you. (нанести удар; раздавать, воздавать)

  1. Judges deal out the same sentence for both offenses.
  2. The ruling dealt a blow to the plaintiffs.
  3. Life has dealt him a bad hand this year.

6. Deal with (be about). Of books, chapters, reports, and laws: what the text covers. (быть посвящённым, рассматривать тему)

  1. Chapter four deals with the war years.
  2. The report dealt with only one of the three sites.
  3. Nobody has dealt with the legal side in print.

dream#

Pronunciation
/drim/ · DREEM
Русский
видеть сны; мечтать
Forms
dream /drim/ · dreamed /drimd/ (also dreamt /drɛmt/) · dreamed /drimd/ (also dreamt /drɛmt/)
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — used constantly in speech and writing alike
Pattern
regular -ed in American English (British ea → e + t)
Group
5
Third person
dreams · -ing form: dreaming
Contrast
Both forms are correct, but they split by country: dreamed is the ordinary American past and participle, while dreamt /drɛmt/ is the British default and sounds poetic in American prose. The same split gives British leant, learnt, spelt, and burnt where Americans write leaned, learned, spelled, and burned.
Verb type
action — dynamic; it names an activity or an experience over time and takes the progressive: she was dreaming about the house again. No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
both — normally intransitive with about or of (I dream of a year with no travel), transitive with a content object (he has dreamed the same thing three nights running) and in dream up a slogan.
Passive
restricted — natural only for dream up: the whole plan was dreamed up over one dinner. The ordinary senses have no passive: ✗ her grandmother's house was dreamed about.

To see images and stories while asleep, or to imagine something you badly want.

1. See images while asleep. With about or of. English makes the sleeper the subject, so Russian мне снилось becomes I dreamed. American English writes dreamed; dreamt is British and sounds poetic here. (видеть сон, сниться)

  1. I rarely dream about work.
  2. She dreamed about her grandmother's house again.
  3. He has dreamed the same thing three nights running.

2. Wish for, aspire to. With of or about, and often with since or always to show how long the wish has lasted. (мечтать о)

  1. We dream of a year with no travel.
  2. She dreamed of medical school and got there.
  3. He has dreamed of playing at Carnegie Hall since he was six.

3. Imagine as possible. Almost always negative: never dreamed, wouldn't dream of it. The second is also a polite refusal. (и представить себе (не мог))

  1. I wouldn't dream of asking her to pay.
  2. She never dreamed it would take four years.
  3. Nobody had dreamed the vote would be that close.

4. Dream up (invent). Of a plan, a name, or an excuse, usually with a hint that it was invented too quickly. (выдумать, придумать)

  1. Marketing dreams up a new slogan every quarter.
  2. Who dreamed this policy up?
  3. We had dreamed up the whole plan over one dinner.

5. Dream on (informal dismissal). A fixed imperative used to tell someone their hope is unrealistic. It appears in no other form. (мечтать не вредно, размечтался)

  1. A corner office in year one? Dream on.
  2. He thinks they'll double the offer. Dream on.
Phrasal verbs
dream about, dream of · dream of · dream on · dream up
See also
daydream, sleep, lean

feel#

Pronunciation
/fil/ · FEEL
Русский
чувствовать
Forms
feel /fil/ · felt /fɛlt/ · felt /fɛlt/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — among the most frequent verbs in the language
Pattern
ee → e + t
Group
5
Third person
feels · -ing form: feeling
Contrast
✗ feeled does not exist. Felt is also an everyday noun — the fabric in a felt hat — spelled and pronounced the same; only the sentence tells them apart.
Verb type
stative in the emotion and opinion senses (I feel better, I feel we should wait) — the opinion sense refuses the progressive: ✗ I am feeling we should wait. Linking (copular) in the room feels cold, the meeting felt rehearsed. Action only in the deliberate senses: feel the fabric, feel them out.
Transitivity
both — intransitive in the linking use (this draft has felt wrong), transitive in felt the fabric, felt the tremor, and with a that-clause in the opinion sense. Feel like takes a noun or an -ing form, never an infinitive: ✗ feel like to leave.
Passive
restricted — fine where the act is deliberate or the sensation is shared: the swelling was felt and a scan ordered; the tremor was felt across three counties; the committee has already been felt out. No passive of the linking use (✗ cold is felt by the room) or of the opinion sense (✗ the price was felt too high by her).

To experience an emotion or a sensation, to touch something to learn about it, or to hold an impression or an opinion.

1. Experience an emotion or state. Followed by an adjective, not an adverb: I feel bad, not the adverb form. (чувствовать себя, испытывать)

  1. I feel better after a walk.
  2. They felt ignored by management for months.
  3. He has felt dizzy since the medication changed.

2. Touch to examine. Deliberate touching to find out about texture, temperature, or swelling. (щупать, трогать)

  1. Feel how heavy this is.
  2. She felt the fabric and put it back on the shelf.
  3. The doctor has felt the swelling and ordered a scan.

3. Perceive by sensation. Here the stimulus comes from outside you: wind, a bump, a tremor, pain. (ощущать, почувствовать)

  1. Can you feel the draft from that window?
  2. We felt the tremor an hour before the news reported it.
  3. I have not felt my toes since mile eight.

4. Seem, give an impression. The subject is a thing or a place rather than a person, and it takes an adjective: the room feels cold. (казаться, ощущаться)

  1. The room feels colder than the thermostat says.
  2. The whole meeting felt rehearsed.
  3. This draft has felt wrong from page one.

5. Hold an opinion. Softer than think: a judgment offered as personal rather than argued. (считать, полагать)

  1. I feel we should wait a week.
  2. She felt the price was too high.
  3. The board has felt that way since the audit.

To cause a great deal of damage or trouble; in practice almost always wreak havoc.

1. Wreak havoc, wreak damage. The modern past is wreaked. Wrought is historically the past of work, not of wreak, so American English writes wreaked havoc. (сеять хаос, причинять разрушения)

  1. A late frost can wreak havoc on the orchards.
  2. The hurricane wreaked damage along sixty miles of coast.
  3. Budget cuts have wreaked havoc on the department.

2. Wreak vengeance. Formal and literary. The object is revenge, vengeance, or someone's anger, and it falls on or upon a target. (вершить месть, обрушить гнев)

  1. He wreaked his revenge slowly and in public.
  2. The gods had wreaked their anger on the city.
  3. No rival has wreaked vengeance that publicly in years.
