Participle = base + -n, with vowel change#

The participle is formed with -n, and the vowels shift across the three forms.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

Every verb in this group ends its participle in -n or -en, but the stem it attaches to has shifted its vowel along the way: write — wrote — written, take — took — taken, grow — grew — grown (3-я = 1-я + "n", в формах меняются звуки). That is what separates these verbs from the group where the participle is simply the base form plus an ending — here the third form is a changed stem plus the ending, so you have to learn all three together. The standing trap is letting the past do the participle's job: I have wrote, I have took, and I have drove are all wrong, and the fix is always the -n form. A large share of the list is prefixed verbs — overtake, underwrite, befall — which simply copy the base verb they are built on.

Forms
arise /əˈraɪz/ · arose /əˈroʊz/ · arisen /əˈrɪzən/
Verb class

Register
neutral to formal — ordinary in writing, less common in speech
Pattern
i — o — i, participle in -en
Group
12
Third person
arises · -ing form: arising (drop the e)
Contrast
Arise takes no object: situations arise, but you raise a question and a person rises from a chair. It follows rise exactly, and the same trap applies — the past arose cannot do participle duty: not a problem has arose but a problem has arisen.
Verb type
action — dynamic, but with an abstract subject: problems, questions, and needs arise. No linking or stative use.
Transitivity
intransitive only, in every sense — a dispute arose, claims arise from the accident. You raise a question; ✗ he arose the issue.
Passive
no — there is no object to promote: ✗ a dispute was arisen over the fence. Only the active a dispute arose is possible.

To come up or come into being — of problems, questions, and opportunities; in formal or older use, to get up.

1. Come up, occur (of a situation). The everyday sense: questions, problems, needs, and opportunities arise. It never takes an object — you raise a question, but a question arises. (возникать, появляться)

  1. Questions arise every time the budget changes midyear.
  2. A dispute arose over who owned the fence.
  3. New evidence has arisen since the trial began.
  4. If the need arises, call me at home.

2. Arise from, arise out of. With from or out of: to have something as its cause or origin. Standard in formal, legal, and academic writing. (проистекать, быть следствием)

  1. Most complaints arise from a single billing error.
  2. The lawsuit arose out of a contract signed in 1998.
  3. Several claims have arisen from the same accident.

3. Get up, rise (formal). Literary and elevated — standing up, or getting out of bed. Ordinary American speech uses get up. (вставать, подниматься)

  1. The congregation arises for the final hymn.
  2. He arose before dawn and made coffee in the dark.
  3. She had arisen early every day of that long summer.

Forms
befall /bɪˈfɔl/ · befell /bɪˈfɛl/ · befallen /bɪˈfɔlən/
Verb class
irregular
Register
formal — literary and journalistic; ordinary speech says happened to
Pattern
a — e — a, participle in -en
Group
12
Third person
befalls · -ing form: befalling
Contrast
Built on fall and inflected exactly like it. Unlike plain fall, it takes a person as its object — misfortune befell them — so not he befell down the stairs.
Verb type
action — dynamic; the subject is an event or a misfortune, not a person who acts.
Transitivity
transitive in the living sense, with a person or place as the object — a string of disasters befell the company. The archaic sense 2 is intransitive: it befell on the third night.
Passive
no in practice — transitive but it resists promotion: ✗ the company was befallen by disasters. Keep the active, or switch to was struck by.

To happen to someone, nearly always something unwelcome.

1. Happen to someone (something bad). Formal and literary. Unlike plain fall it takes a person or a place as its object, and what befalls is misfortune — trouble, disaster, harm. (случаться с кем-л., приключаться)

  1. Whatever troubles befall us, we handle them together.
  2. A string of disasters befell the company that winter.
  3. Nothing like this has befallen the town in living memory.
  4. He wondered what had befallen the crew of the second boat.
  5. Misfortune befalls the careless traveler.

2. Come to pass (archaic). The older objectless use, meaning simply to happen. Found in scripture and folktale, not in current American speech. (происходить, статься)

  1. It befell on the third night that the gate stood open.
  2. Strange things have befallen since the comet passed.

Contrast
In modern American English bequeath is a regular verb: bequeathed, have bequeathed. The strong forms bequoth and bequothen listed here are archaic and turn up only in old wills and literary imitations of them — do not use them in anything you write today. Passive

To leave property to someone in a will, and by extension to pass anything on to those who come after you.

1. Leave in a will. The legal sense, used of money and personal property. In modern American English the verb is regular: bequeathed, have bequeathed. (завещать, оставлять по завещанию)

  1. Wealthy donors often bequeath their libraries to a university.
  2. Her will bequeathed the house to her sister, not to her son.
  3. The family has bequeathed three paintings to the museum since 1980.
  4. He bequeaths nothing to anyone who contests the estate.

2. Hand down to later generations. Figurative: to leave something non-material — a legacy, a problem, a body of work — to whoever comes next. (передавать по наследству (традицию, наследие))

  1. Each administration bequeaths its unfinished business to the next.
  2. That century bequeathed us both the vaccine and the bomb.
  3. Their teachers have bequeathed them a whole vocabulary for it.

3. Strong forms bequoth, bequothen (archaic). The strong inflection listed for this verb survives only in old wills and literary imitations of them. Do not use it in anything you write today. (завещать (устаревшие формы))

  1. The parish record says the merchant bequoth his lands to the abbey.
  2. Scholars still argue over what the founder had bequothen to the town.

bestride /bɪˈstraɪd/ · bestrode /bɪˈstroʊd/ · bestridden /bɪˈstrɪdən/

irregular
Register
rare — literary and headline use; Americans say sat astride or dominated
Pattern

12
Third person
bestrides · -ing form: bestriding (drop the e)
Contrast
Built on stride and inflected exactly like it, never bestrided. It is literary; in ordinary American speech you would say sat astride or dominated.
Verb type
action — dynamic in the literal bestride a log; closer to stative in the dominating sense, where it describes a standing position rather than an act.
Transitivity

Passive

To sit or stand with a leg on either side of something; figuratively, to dominate an entire field.

1. Dominate, tower over. The sense still in use, and always literary or journalistic: one firm or figure bestrides an industry or an era. (господствовать, довлеть над)

  1. That one company has bestridden the industry for twenty years.
  2. A single firm now bestrides the entire market.
  3. By 1900 the railroads had bestridden the continent.

2. Sit or stand astride. The literal sense — a leg on each side of a horse, a log, or a wall. Ordinary American speech says sit astride instead. (сидеть верхом; стоять расставив ноги)

  1. Bronze generals bestride half the squares in Europe.
  2. He bestrode the fallen log and pulled the rope tight.
  3. The rider bestrode a gray mare and led the parade.

3. Step across, span. Rare and old: to cross something in a single stride, or of a structure to span it. (перешагивать; перекрывать собой)

  1. A stone causeway lets you bestride the stream without wading.
  2. The arch bestrode the whole road until 1912.

bid#

Pronunciation
/bɪd/ · BID
Русский
предлагать цену; велеть
Forms
bid /bɪd/ · bade /bæd/ (also /beɪd/) · bidden /ˈbɪdən/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — auctions, contracts, and bid farewell; the forms bade/bidden are dated
Pattern
i — a — i, participle in -den
Group
12
Third person
bids · -ing form: bidding (the d doubles)
Contrast
Bid splits by meaning. In the auction sense it never changes — bid, bid, bid: not he bade forty dollars but he bid forty dollars. The forms bade and bidden belong to the older sense of commanding or greeting: she bade him good night, the guests had been bidden to arrive at seven. Most Americans say bade as /bæd/, rhyming with had.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense, from offering a price to commanding.
Transitivity
both — intransitive with a preposition in business (they bid on federal jobs), transitive with an amount (she bid nine hundred dollars), and ditransitive in the command and greeting senses (she bade us good night, he bids his students think for themselves).
Passive
yes, but sense by sense — that lamp has been bid on twice, the guests had been bidden to arrive at seven. The frozen bid farewell stays active: ✗ farewell was bidden to us.

To offer a price, at auction or for a contract; in an older and more formal sense, to command, to greet, or to invite.

1. Offer a price at auction. In this sense the verb never changes shape: bid, bid, bid. Not he bade forty dollars. (предлагать цену (на аукционе), торговаться)

  1. Two collectors bid against each other for the manuscript.
  2. She bid nine hundred dollars and got the table.
  3. Three people have bid on that lamp already.

2. Bid on / bid for a contract. Business use with on or for: to submit a formal offer to do work at a stated price. The forms stay invariant here too. (подавать заявку, участвовать в тендере)

  1. Small contractors rarely bid on federal jobs.
  2. They bid too low and lost money on the project.
  3. The city had bid for the tournament twice before.

3. Command, tell someone to do. Formal and literary — this is the sense that carries the strong forms bade and bidden. It takes a bare infinitive: bid them come in. (велеть, приказывать)

  1. He bids his students think for themselves.
  2. The sergeant bade them wait outside the tent.
  3. The captain has bidden the crew to stand down.

4. Bid farewell, bid good night. A fixed formula for greetings and farewells: bid someone welcome, farewell, good night. Most Americans say bade as /bæd/, rhyming with had. (прощаться; желать (доброй ночи))

  1. She bade us good night and went upstairs.
  2. We bade farewell at the gate and drove home.
  3. They have bidden their guests welcome in three languages.

5. Invite (archaic). Old use, alive today mostly in the phrase the bidden guests. Modern English uses invite. (приглашать, звать)

  1. The steward bade the whole village to the wedding.
  2. The host had bidden the guests to arrive at seven.
  3. Only those bidden to the feast came through the gate.
See also
forbid, underbid

blow#

Pronunciation
/bloʊ/ · BLOH
Русский
1) дуть 2) цвести, расцветать
Forms
blow /bloʊ/ · blew /blu/ · blown /bloʊn/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
ow — ew — own
Group
12
Third person
blows · -ing form: blowing
Contrast
The past blew is a homophone of blue. Not I blowed the fuse and not I have blew it — the participle needs have: I have blown it. In the phrasal senses (blow up, blow out, blow off) the same three forms apply.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, whether the subject is wind, a person, or a fuse.
Transitivity
both — intransitive of wind and of things that fail (cold winds blow, the rear tire blew), transitive when something is moved, sounded, or wasted (the storm blew three shingles off, he blew a paycheck).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the umbrella has been blown into the pool, the whistle was blown on the play. No passive of the intransitive the fuse blew.

Of air, to move or to move something; of a person, to force air out of the mouth — plus a large family of idioms from blow up to blow it.

1. Of wind: move, be in motion. The core intransitive sense, with wind or a draft as the subject. (дуть (о ветре))

  1. Cold winds blow off the lake all winter.
  2. It blew hard all night.
  3. The wind has blown from the south since Tuesday.

2. Move something with air. Transitive: air moves an object, usually with a particle — blow off, blow over, blow away. (сдувать, уносить (ветром))

  1. Papers blow everywhere when that door opens.
  2. The storm blew three shingles off the roof.
  3. A gust has blown the umbrella into the pool.

3. Breathe out forcefully. Of a person directing breath at something. This covers the fixed blow out the candles and blow your nose. (дуть, выдыхать; сморкаться)

  1. She blows on her coffee to cool it.
  2. He blew out all the candles at once.
  3. Blow your nose before you come to the table.
  4. The nurse asked whether he had blown into the tube yet.

4. Sound a horn or a whistle. Used of wind instruments, whistles, and car horns. The related noun whistleblower comes from this sense. (трубить, дуть в свисток)

  1. Conductors blow the whistle before the doors close.
  2. The referee blew the whistle on the play.
  3. Someone has blown the horn three times.

5. Blow up — explode; destroy. Both transitive and intransitive. Blow up also means to lose your temper, and, recently, for something to become suddenly popular. (взрывать(ся), взлетать на воздух)

  1. Engineers plan to blow up the old bridge in March.
  2. The transformer blew up during the storm.
  3. The video has blown up on every platform.

6. Burn out, burst (a fuse, a tire). Of something that fails suddenly under pressure or current. Works in both directions: the fuse blew, he blew the fuse. (перегорать (о пробке); лопнуть (о шине))

  1. Cheap bulbs blow within a month.
  2. The rear tire blew on the interstate.
  3. Somebody has blown the fuse again.

7. Waste, squander (money). Informal, with money or time as the object, often with on or through. (спустить, промотать (деньги))

  1. Do not blow your whole budget in the first week.
  2. He blew an entire paycheck on concert tickets.
  3. They have blown through the grant already.

8. Blow it — ruin a chance. Informal. The object is an opportunity: a game, an interview, a lead. (провалить, упустить, испортить)

  1. Do not blow this.
  2. He blew the interview by showing up late.
  3. We have blown two chances at the title.

9. Blow off — skip, brush aside. Informal: to skip an obligation, or to dismiss a person or a concern without taking it seriously. (забить на, продинамить; отмахнуться)

  1. He blows off class whenever the surf is good.
  2. She blew off the whole warning.
  3. He has blown off three appointments this month.

10. Blow over — pass without consequence. Of a storm, a scandal, or an argument that ends without lasting damage. (утихнуть, сойти на нет)

  1. Stories like this usually blow over.
  2. The whole thing blew over in a week.
  3. The controversy had blown over long before he got back.
Phrasal verbs
blow away · blow in · blow off · blow out · blow over · blow through · blow up
See also
grow, know, throw, fly

crows · -ing form: crowing
Contrast
In American English crow is a regular verb: crowed, have crowed. The strong past crew survives only in older and biblical English (the cock crew), and the participle crown is obsolete — it is not the noun crown /kraʊn/, which is an unrelated word. Use crowed in your own writing, especially for the boasting sense.

Of a rooster, to make its loud call; of a person, to boast about a win.

1. Boast, gloat. The everyday sense, mildly disapproving, usually crow about or crow over. Regular in modern American English: crowed, have crowed. (хвастаться, злорадствовать)

  1. She crows whenever her team wins.
  2. He crowed about the promotion for a solid week.
  3. They have crowed about that one game for years.

2. Of a rooster: call. The literal sense. The strong past crew survives only in older and biblical English, and the participle crown is obsolete — use crowed today. (кукарекать, петь (о петухе))

  1. The roosters crow before the sun clears the ridge.
  2. Their rooster has crowed every morning since April.
  3. The old translation reads that the cock crew three times.
  4. Antique printings of the ballad say the cock had crown twice before daybreak.

3. Of a baby: squeal with delight. A small, affectionate use: the happy noise an infant makes. (гулить, радостно вскрикивать)

  1. Babies crow when you lift them overhead.
  2. The baby crowed and reached for the balloon.
  3. He has crowed and giggled all morning.

do#

Pronunciation
/du/ · DOO
Русский
делать
Forms
do /du/ · did /dɪd/ · done /dʌn/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
oo — i — u, participle in -ne
Group
12
Third person
does /dʌz/ (irregular in both spelling and sound) · -ing form: doing
Contrast
Not I done it: done always needs have, so use I did it or I have done it. As an auxiliary, do carries the tense for the main verb, which is why did you went is wrong and did you go is right. The third person is spelled does and said /dʌz/, never /duz/.
Verb type
both lexical and auxiliary — an action verb in do the dishes, and the tense-carrying auxiliary of questions, negatives, and emphasis (do you take cream?, I do remember), plus a pro-verb standing in for an earlier verb (she reads more than I do).
Transitivity
both — transitive in the lexical senses (do the dishes, do thirty miles to the gallon), intransitive in that will do and she has done well. The auxiliary takes no object at all.
Passive
yes in the lexical transitive senses — the upstairs has been done already, the essay had to be done over. The auxiliary and pro-verb uses have no passive: ✗ cream is done taken by you.

To perform an action or a task — and, as an auxiliary, the verb that carries tense in questions, negatives, and emphasis.

1. Perform, carry out. The core lexical sense, with a task, a job, or a chore as the object. Not I done it: the participle always needs have. (делать, выполнять)

  1. We do the dishes right after dinner.
  2. She did the whole route on foot.
  3. He has done more than anyone asked him to.
  4. By noon they had done half the packing.

2. Auxiliary in questions and negatives. Do carries the tense so the main verb stays in the base form. That is why did you went is wrong and did you go is right. (вспомогательный глагол (вопрос, отрицание))

  1. Do you take cream?
  2. She did not answer the second email.
  3. Does he know about the meeting?

3. Emphatic do. Stressed do in an affirmative sentence insists on it against doubt or contradiction. (усилительное do (действительно, всё же))

  1. I do remember her name — give me a second.
  2. He did apologize, eventually.
  3. She does care, whatever she says.

4. Stand in for another verb. Do substitutes for a verb already mentioned, especially in comparisons, short answers, and tag questions. (заменяет ранее названный глагол)

  1. She reads more than I do.
  2. He said he would call, and he did.
  3. Nobody has done more for this town than she has.

5. Tidy, arrange, put in order. Do a room, do your hair, do the yard — to put something into the state it is supposed to be in. (приводить в порядок, убирать; делать (причёску))

  1. She does her hair before anyone else is awake.
  2. They did the whole kitchen over a weekend.
  3. The cleaners have done the upstairs already.

6. Be enough, suffice. Intransitive, usually with will or would: that will do, it will do for now. (годиться, подойти, хватить)

  1. Two chairs will do for now.
  2. A shorter rope did just fine.
  3. Cold pizza has done the job before.

7. Fare, get along. Always with an adverb — doing well, doing badly, how are you doing. Standard in talk about health and business alike. (поживать, справляться)

  1. How did she do on the exam?
  2. The store did badly its first year.
  3. She has done well for herself.

8. Cover a distance or a speed. With a measurement as the object: miles covered, miles per gallon, speed on the highway. (проходить, проезжать (расстояние); ехать со скоростью)

  1. This car does thirty miles to the gallon.
  2. We did four hundred miles before dark.
  3. They had done the whole loop by noon.

9. Serve a prison sentence. Informal but widespread: do time, do five years. (отсидеть (срок))

  1. Nobody wants to do a day in that place.
  2. He did eight years upstate.
  3. She has done time for fraud.

10. Do over — redo from the start. American English keeps do over where British English says do again; a do-over is also a noun. (переделать заново)

  1. The teacher made him do the essay over.
  2. We did the bathroom over last spring.
  3. They have done the whole website over twice.

11. Do without / could do with. Do without means to manage in the absence of something; could do with means to need or want it. (обходиться без; не помешало бы)

  1. We can do without a second car.
  2. They did without heat for three days.
  3. She has done without help all year.
  4. I could do with a nap.

12. Do away with — abolish. To get rid of a rule, a practice, or an institution for good. (покончить с, отменить)

  1. Nobody wants to do away with the program.
  2. The district did away with letter grades.
  3. They have done away with the paper form entirely.
Phrasal verbs
do away with · do for · do in · do out of · do over · do up · do with · do without
See also
undo, outdo, overdo

Contrast
The past drew /dru/ rhymes with do and true, not with draw; the participle is drawn, never drawed. Drawn doubles as an adjective for a tired face: she looked drawn.
Verb type

  2. He rode bareback across the pasture.
  3. I have ridden that gray mare since she was three.
  4. None of the kids had ridden a horse before that summer.

  1. Most of the staff ride bikes to work.
  2. He rode his motorcycle from Denver to the coast.
  3. She has ridden the same road bike for a decade.

  1. I ride the train into the city three days a week.
  2. We rode the elevator to the top floor in silence.
  3. He has ridden that same commuter line for twenty years.

  1. Gulls ride the updrafts along the cliff without a wingbeat.
  2. The little boat rode the swell better than we expected.
  3. Surfers have ridden this break since the fifties.

To perceive with the eyes, and, by extension, to understand, to meet, to experience, or to make sure of something.

  2. She saw the taillights turn onto the gravel road.
  3. Nobody has seen that comet since 1997.
  4. He had seen the letter long before he admitted it.

  1. I see what you mean about the second paragraph.
  2. It took a week before anyone saw the flaw in the argument.
  3. Now that you explain it, I have finally seen the logic of it.

  1. You should see a doctor about that cough.
  2. I saw my lawyer on Tuesday about the lease.
  3. She has seen three specialists and gotten three answers.

  1. They have been seeing each other since March.
  2. She was seeing somebody from the office at the time.
  3. He does not see anyone seriously these days.

5. Watch a movie, game, or show. For a one-time event you see a film or a game; for regular viewing of television you watch. Have you seen it? is the standard question about a movie. (смотреть, посмотреть (фильм, спектакль))

  1. Let's see the late show instead.
  2. We saw the Cubs play in the rain.
  3. I have seen that movie four times.

  1. Please see that the doors are locked at closing.
  2. The super saw to the leak the same afternoon.
  3. Someone has seen to the paperwork already.

7. Witness, experience (of a period). The subject is a period, a place, or a thing rather than a person: the decade saw enormous change. Standard in journalism and history writing. (видеть, знавать, быть свидетелем (об эпохе, месте))

  1. The next ten years will see the whole industry reorganized.
  2. The 1990s saw a wave of small presses open and close.
  3. This town has seen three floods since I moved here.

  1. We always see the grandparents off at the gate.
  2. He saw her home through the rain.
  3. The whole family had seen him off at the pier.

  1. Voters see through that kind of promise immediately.
  2. She saw the project through despite losing half her staff.
  3. He has seen through every excuse I have ever offered.

  1. I see the delay as a chance to test it properly.
  2. Critics saw the film as an apology for the war.
  3. I never saw what she saw in him.

Contrast
Not I have wrote — the participle is written, with two t's, while writing has one. The w is silent throughout, so write is a homophone of right, and wrote of rote. Americans can say write me without to; British English keeps write to me.
Verb type
action — dynamic, a verb of creation and of recording.
Transitivity
both — intransitive in he writes for two magazines, transitive with a text or a document (she wrote her first novel), and ditransitive in American English: the clinic will write you a prescription, write me when you get settled.
Passive
yes — the same phrase has been written on every page, the remaining balance was written off, she has been written up twice for being late.

To form letters and words, to compose a text, and to communicate or record in writing.

1. Form letters and words. The physical act — pen on paper, chalk on a board. The classic learner trap sits here: not I have wrote but I have written. (писать (буквы, слова))

  1. Kids write their names before they can read them.
  2. He wrote the address on the back of a receipt.
  3. She has written the same phrase on every page.
  4. Someone had written the date in pencil in the corner.

2. Compose a text or work. The authorial sense: books, articles, reports, essays. Used intransitively, she writes can mean she is a writer by trade. (писать, сочинять (книгу, статью))

  1. He writes for two magazines and teaches on the side.
  2. She wrote her first novel in a rented cabin.
  3. They have written four reports on the same landfill.

3. Write to someone, correspond. American English allows write me as well as write to me; British English keeps the to. Used of letters and email alike. (писать (кому-л.), переписываться)

  1. Write me when you get settled.
  2. She wrote to her brother every Sunday for years.
  3. He has written to the department three times without an answer.

4. Write a check, write up. To fill out a formal document: a check, a prescription, a ticket, an invoice. Write up also means to prepare a report or to cite someone formally. (выписывать (чек, счёт, штраф))

  1. The clinic will write you a prescription today.
  2. The officer wrote him a ticket for the expired tag.
  3. Her manager has written her up twice for being late.

5. Compose music. Standard for songs, scores, and arrangements — you write a song the way you write a poem. (писать (музыку), сочинять)

  1. She writes most of the band's material.
  2. He wrote that melody in a single afternoon.
  3. They have written together since high school.

6. Write code; write data. Two technical senses: programmers write software, and a program writes data to a disk or a file. Both are everyday vocabulary in computing. (писать (код); записывать (данные))

  1. We write most of the tooling in Python.
  2. The script wrote the results to a temporary file.
  3. He has written every line of that parser himself.

7. Write down, write off. Write down means to record on paper. Write off has two senses: cancel a debt or reduce an asset's book value, and dismiss a person or thing as hopeless. (записать; списать (долг); поставить крест на)

  1. Write it down before you forget the number.
  2. The bank wrote off the remaining balance.
  3. Critics had written him off years before his best season.
