# Modal, semi-modal and defective verbs

> The verbs that carry mood rather than action — and the reason most of them have no participle.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

A modal is not a verb that does something; it is a verb that says how the speaker stands toward something else being done. *He left* is a fact. *He must have left*, *he might leave*, *he would leave* — same departure, three different stances. Because modals carry mood instead of action, English stripped them of nearly everything a verb normally has: **no -s in the third person** (✗ *she musts*), **no -ing**, **no participle**, **no infinitive** (✗ *to must*). What is left is one form, sometimes two, and a bare infinitive after it — *must go*, never ✗ *must to go*. Where a missing form is genuinely needed, a full verb steps in: *be able to* for *can*, *have to* for *must*, *be allowed to* for *may*.

The second trap is that the "past" forms are barely past. *Would*, *should*, *could* and *might* began as the past tenses of *will*, *shall*, *can* and *may*, and they still do that job in reported speech — but in ordinary American English they far more often mark politeness, distance or hypothesis in the **present**: *Could you repeat that?* is now, not yesterday. That is why each of them has an entry of its own below rather than a line inside its parent.

*Can* and *may* live with the irregular groups they inflect like — [*can*](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#can) in group 4 and [*may*](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#may) in group 5 — and the grammar behind all of this is in [§2.5 and §5.5 of the verb reference](../README.md).

### beware

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈwɛr/ &middot; *bih-WAIR*
**Русский:** остерегаться, беречься
**Forms:** *beware* &middot; — &middot; — (no past, no participle)
**Verb class:** defective
**Register:** everyday, but almost always on a sign or in a warning
**Pattern:** defective (imperative and bare infinitive only) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *beware* — no -s, ✗ *he bewares* &middot; **-ing form:** none
**Contrast:** Not a modal — an ordinary lexical verb that happens to have lost every form but two. There is no ✗ *bewares*, ✗ *bewared*, ✗ *bewaring*, and it cannot follow *is* or *have*: ✗ *I have bewared*. It survives as an imperative (*Beware of the dog*) and as a bare infinitive after a modal or *should* (*travelers should beware*). Anywhere else, English switches to *watch out for* or *be careful of*.
**Verb type:** **defective lexical verb** — full meaning, almost no forms. It is the clearest non-modal example of the same gap the modals have.
**Transitivity:** **both** — with *of* (*beware of unmarked taxis*), or with a clause (*beware that the lock sticks*), or absolutely (*Beware!*).
**Passive:** **no** — it has no participle, so it cannot form one.

A full verb with two forms and no tenses — the shortest answer to why English grammar keeps a category called *defective*.

**1. Warning about a danger.** The imperative, and by far the commonest use. Takes *of* before a noun. (остерегайся, берегись)

1. *Beware* of the dog.
2. *Beware* of anyone who promises a guaranteed return.
3. *Beware* — the bottom step is loose.

**2. After a modal or *should*.** The bare infinitive, the only other form it has. (следует остерегаться)

4. Travelers should *beware* of unmarked taxis.
5. Buyers must *beware*: the sale is final.
6. You would do well to *beware* of his advice.

### will

**Pronunciation:** /wɪl/ &middot; *WIL* &middot; weak form /wəl/, contracted *'ll* /l/ in *I'll go*
**Русский:** вспомогательный глагол будущего времени; выражает согласие, обещание
**Forms:** *will* /wɪl/ &middot; *would* /wʊd/ &middot; — (no participle)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — the most common modal in the language
**Pattern:** i — ou (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *will* — no -s, ✗ *he wills* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** English has no future tense — it has *will* plus a bare infinitive, which is why *will* is a modal and not an ending. Russian speakers reach for it too often: after *when*, *if*, *as soon as*, *until* and *before*, English uses the **present**, ✗ *when I will arrive* → *when I arrive*. A separate and fully regular verb *will* means "leave by will" (*she willed the house to her niece*), and the noun *will* means both "testament" and "willpower".
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — takes a bare infinitive (*will go*), has no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle (✗ *he wills go*, ✗ *I have would*, ✗ *to will*). *Be going to* and the present progressive fill in where a stance about the future is not wanted.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — a modal takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object. Bare *will* with a noun (*I will it to happen*) is the separate regular verb.
**Passive:** **no** — the verb it supports carries the voice: *the letter will be sent*, never *will* itself.

The modal of future time, willingness and prediction — and the one that contracts to *'ll* so reliably that learners stop hearing it.

**1. Future time.** The neutral way to put an event after now. With *'ll* in speech almost always. (буду, будет)

1. The package *will* arrive on Thursday.
2. She said the meeting *would* run long, and it did.
3. I *will* be at the airport by six.

**2. Willingness, offers and promises.** Not time at all — consent. The negative *won't* is a refusal, and it is used of objects too. (соглашаться; обещать)

4. I *'ll* carry that for you.
5. The car *won't* start in this cold.
6. He promised he *would* call the moment he landed.

**3. Requests — *Will you…?*.** A direct request. *Would you* is the softer version, which is why it is far commoner. (не могли бы вы)

7. *Will* you hold the elevator?
8. *Would* you mind closing the window?
9. *Will* you please stop doing that.

**4. Characteristic behavior.** A habit presented as typical rather than scheduled — often mildly critical when stressed. (бывает, имеет обыкновение)

10. He *will* sit there for hours without saying a word.
11. Accidents *will* happen.
12. She *would* always bring something from her garden.

### would

**Pronunciation:** /wʊd/ &middot; *WUUD* &middot; weak form /wəd/, contracted *'d* /d/ in *I'd go* &middot; the l is silent
**Русский:** бы (условное наклонение); бывало (о прошлой привычке)
**Forms:** *would* /wʊd/ &middot; — &middot; — (historically the past of *will*)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — and the single most important modal for sounding polite
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *would* — no -s, ✗ *he woulds* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** *Would* is the past of *will* only in reported speech; everywhere else it is present-tense politeness or hypothesis, so a *would* in front of you is usually **not** about the past. It is a homophone of *wood*. Russian's **бы** covers most of it, but not the past-habit sense, where Russian uses **бывало** — and English then also allows *used to*. Never ✗ *if I would have time*: the *if*-clause takes the past, *if I had time, I would call*.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it (*would go*), no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle. For a past hypothetical it stacks with *have*: *would have gone*.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — it takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *it would be finished by Friday*.

The workhorse of conditionals, polite requests and remembered habit — and the reason *I would like* sounds courteous where *I want* sounds blunt.

**1. The hypothetical result.** The main clause of a conditional: *if* + past, then *would* + bare infinitive. (бы)

1. If I had the money, I *would* buy it outright.
2. She *would* have told you if she had known.
3. What *would* you do in my place?

**2. Polite requests and offers.** Present time, not past. *Would you* and *I would like* are the two politest frames in everyday English. (не могли бы; хотел бы)

4. *Would* you pass me the salt?
5. I *would* like a table by the window, please.
6. *Would* you rather wait outside?

**3. Past habit.** A repeated action in a finished stretch of the past. Unlike *used to*, *would* cannot describe a past **state** — ✗ *I would live in Boston*. (бывало, обычно)

7. Every summer we *would* drive up to the lake.
8. My grandfather *would* read the paper cover to cover.
9. She *would* never admit she was tired.

**4. The past of *will* in reported speech.** Backshift: *will* inside a past-tense report becomes *would*. (что будет — в косвенной речи)

10. He said he *would* be late.
11. They promised the parts *would* ship on Monday.
12. I knew she *would* say that.

### shall

**Pronunciation:** /ʃæl/ &middot; *SHAL* &middot; weak form /ʃəl/
**Русский:** вспомогательный глагол (предложение, обязательство)
**Forms:** *shall* /ʃæl/ &middot; *should* /ʃʊd/ &middot; — (no participle)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** formal or fixed — in American English it survives mainly in *Shall I…?* and in legal writing
**Pattern:** a — ou (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *shall* — no -s, ✗ *he shalls* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** The old textbook rule that *shall* marks the first-person future (*I shall go*) is dead in American speech — use *will*. What survives is the **question** *Shall I…?* / *Shall we…?*, offering to do something, where *will* would be wrong: ✗ *Will I open the window?* asks a prediction, not an offer. In contracts *shall* means "is required to", which is why drafters now often replace it with *must*.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it, no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — the supported verb carries voice: *the fee shall be paid in advance*.

Nearly gone from American speech except in one construction — but that construction has no substitute.

**1. Offers and suggestions — *Shall I…?* / *Shall we…?*.** The living use. It asks the listener to decide whether the speaker should act. (давай…?; мне …?)

1. *Shall* I open a window?
2. *Shall* we start without him?
3. What *shall* I tell them?

**2. Obligation in legal and formal writing.** "Is required to." Common in contracts, statutes and specifications. (обязан, должен)

4. The tenant *shall* give thirty days' notice.
5. Each party *shall* bear its own costs.
6. Applications *shall* be submitted in writing.

**3. Dated first-person future.** Old-fashioned in America, still current in British English and in set phrases. (буду, будем)

7. We *shall* see.
8. I *shall* never forget it.
9. They said they *should* be glad to help.

### should

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʊd/ &middot; *SHUUD* &middot; weak form /ʃəd/ &middot; the l is silent
**Русский:** следует, должен бы; должно быть
**Forms:** *should* /ʃʊd/ &middot; — &middot; — (historically the past of *shall*)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary way to give advice
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *should* — no -s, ✗ *he shoulds* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** *Should* is advice; *must* and *have to* are obligation. Russian **должен** covers both, so learners overuse *must* and sound commanding — *you should see a doctor* is advice, *you must see a doctor* is an order. For the past, *should* does **not** become anything: use *should have* + participle, which always carries regret or reproach (*I should have called*). Spoken *should've* /ˈʃʊdəv/ is heard as "should of" — but never written that way.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it, no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle. Stacks with *have* for the past: *should have gone*.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *the form should be signed*.

Advice, expectation, and — in *should have* — one of the few grammatical ways English has of expressing regret.

**1. Advice and recommendation.** The core sense. Weaker than *must*, stronger than *could*. (следует, стоит)

1. You *should* get that looked at.
2. We *should* have left earlier.
3. He *should* apologize, and he knows it.

**2. Expectation and probability.** Not duty — a reasonable guess about how things will turn out. (должно быть, должен по идее)

4. The train *should* be here any minute.
5. That *should* be enough flour for two loaves.
6. She *should* have landed by now.

**3. Regret and reproach — *should have*.** Something that did not happen and ought to have. Always with the participle, never ✗ *should had*. (надо было бы)

7. I *should* have listened to you.
8. They *should* never have signed it.
9. You *shouldn't* have gone alone.

**4. The past of *shall* in reported speech.** Backshift, and in formal conditionals. (— в косвенной речи)

10. He asked whether he *should* wait.
11. *Should* you need anything, the front desk is open all night.
12. She wondered what she *should* say.

### must

**Pronunciation:** /mʌst/ &middot; *MUHST* &middot; weak form /məst/
**Русский:** должен, обязан; должно быть
**Forms:** *must* /mʌst/ &middot; — &middot; — (no past, no participle)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — though *have to* has largely taken over the obligation sense in American speech
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *must* — no -s, ✗ *he musts* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** The trap is the negative. *Mustn't* is a **prohibition** — *you mustn't tell anyone* — while *don't have to* is the **absence** of obligation: *you don't have to tell anyone*. They are opposites, and Russian **не должен** sits between them. *Must* also has no past: for past obligation use *had to* (*I had to leave early*), and reserve *must have* for deduction about the past (*he must have left early*).
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it, no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle, no infinitive (✗ *to must*). *Have to* supplies every missing form.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only. The noun *a must* ("something essential") is a separate word.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *the door must be locked*.

Obligation and deduction in one form — and the negative means something entirely different from the negative of *have to*.

**1. Obligation and necessity.** A requirement, usually from a rule or from the speaker. Stronger and more impersonal than *should*. (должен, обязан)

1. All passengers *must* show a boarding pass.
2. I *must* remember to water the plants.
3. You *must* be eighteen or older to apply.

**2. Logical deduction.** Not duty at all — the speaker's certainty, reasoned from evidence. The opposite is *can't*, not *mustn't*. (должно быть)

4. She *must* be exhausted after that flight.
5. There's no car in the drive — they *must* have left.
6. You *must* be joking.

**3. Prohibition — *mustn't*.** Forbidden. Note that this is not the negative of sense 1's *must* in Russian terms. (нельзя)

7. You *mustn't* say a word about this.
8. Visitors *must not* feed the animals.
9. We *mustn't* be late again.

### might

**Pronunciation:** /maɪt/ &middot; *MYT*
**Русский:** может быть, возможно; мог бы
**Forms:** *might* /maɪt/ &middot; — &middot; — (historically the past of *may*)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary way to hedge
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *might* — no -s, ✗ *he mights* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** *Might* and [*may*](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#may) are near-identical in American English for possibility; *might* is slightly more tentative and far commoner in speech. It is **not** usually past — *he might come* is about now. For the past, *might have* + participle (*he might have missed the train*). It is a homograph of the noun *might* ("strength"), as in *with all his might*.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it, no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle. Stacks with *have* for the past: *might have gone*.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *it might be canceled*.

The hedge: possible but not asserted — and the reason English speakers sound noncommittal to Russian ears.

**1. Possibility.** Something may or may not be or become true. The workhorse sense. (может быть, возможно)

1. It *might* rain later.
2. She *might* already know.
3. I *might* not make it to dinner.

**2. Past possibility — *might have*.** A guess about something already settled. (мог бы; возможно, …)

4. He *might* have taken the earlier train.
5. We *might* have met before — you look familiar.
6. It *might* have been worse.

**3. Tentative suggestions and reproach.** Softening a proposal, or — with stress — complaining that something was not done. (мог бы)

7. You *might* try turning it off and on again.
8. You *might* have told me.
9. I thought we *might* walk instead.

**4. The past of *may* in reported speech.** Backshift. (— в косвенной речи)

10. She said she *might* be late.
11. He asked whether he *might* leave early.
12. They warned us the road *might* be closed.

### could

**Pronunciation:** /kʊd/ &middot; *KUUD* &middot; weak form /kəd/ &middot; the l is silent
**Русский:** мог бы, мог; не могли бы вы
**Forms:** *could* /kʊd/ &middot; — &middot; — (historically the past of *can*)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday — the standard polite request
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *could* — no -s, ✗ *he coulds* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** *Could* is the past of [*can*](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#can) only for **general** past ability (*I could swim at four*). For a single past success English switches to *was able to* or *managed to*: ✗ *I could catch the train yesterday* → *I managed to catch it*. And most *coulds* are not past at all — *Could you help me?* is a present request. There is no participle: ✗ *I have could*.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — bare infinitive after it, no *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle. Stacks with *have*: *could have gone*.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *it could be repaired*.

The politeness modal — and the one whose past-tense form misleads learners most, because three of its four senses are about the present.

**1. Polite requests.** Present time. The most frequent use of the word, and the safest register for a stranger. (не могли бы вы)

1. *Could* you hold this for a second?
2. *Could* I ask you something?
3. *Could* we move the meeting to Thursday?

**2. Possibility and suggestion.** Weaker than *will*, close to *might*, and the ordinary way to float an idea. (мог бы, можно бы)

4. We *could* take the coast road instead.
5. That *could* be the mail carrier.
6. You *could* always ask her yourself.

**3. Past ability.** A general capacity in the past, not a one-time success. (мог, умел)

7. She *could* read before she started school.
8. We *couldn't* see the stage from where we sat.
9. He *could* run a mile in under six minutes back then.

**4. Unreal past — *could have*.** Something possible that did not happen, often with reproach or relief. (мог бы)

10. You *could* have been hurt.
11. I *could* have sworn I locked it.
12. They *couldn't* have known.

### ought to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔːt tə/ &middot; *AWT-tuh* &middot; often /ˈɔːtə/ in fast speech
**Русский:** следует, должен бы
**Forms:** *ought to* /ˈɔːt tə/ &middot; — &middot; — (no past, no participle)
**Verb class:** modal
**Register:** everyday but slightly old-fashioned — *should* has largely replaced it
**Pattern:** defective (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *ought to* — no -s, ✗ *he oughts* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** The one modal that keeps its *to* — *ought **to** go*, where every other modal takes a bare infinitive. Meaning is all but identical to *should*, and *should* is commoner in American English; *ought to* leans a little more toward moral duty. The negative *oughtn't* and the question *Ought I…?* both sound stiff — Americans switch to *shouldn't* and *Should I…?*
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — but with a *to*-infinitive, not a bare one. No *-s*, no *-ing*, no participle. Stacks with *have*: *ought to have gone*.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — infinitival complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *it ought to be reported*.

The only modal that keeps *to* — and a near-twin of *should* that survives mostly in speech.

**1. Duty and advice.** What is right or sensible to do. (следует, надо бы)

1. You *ought to* tell her the truth.
2. We *ought to* leave a tip.
3. Somebody *ought to* fix that step.

**2. Expectation.** A reasonable prediction, exactly like *should*. (должно быть, по идее)

4. That *ought to* be enough for four people.
5. The paint *ought to* be dry by morning.
6. This *ought to* only take a minute.

**3. Unfulfilled duty — *ought to have*.** Regret, identical in force to *should have*. (надо было бы)

7. I *ought to* have said something.
8. They *ought to* have warned us.
9. You *oughtn't* to have gone alone.

### used to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjuːst tə/ &middot; *YOOST-tuh* &middot; note the /s/, not /z/ — unlike the verb *used* /juːzd/
**Русский:** раньше, бывало (о прошлой привычке или состоянии)
**Forms:** *used to* /ˈjuːst tə/ &middot; — &middot; — (past only; there is no present *use to*)
**Verb class:** semi-modal
**Register:** everyday — the standard way to mark a discontinued past
**Pattern:** defective (a semi-modal: past tense only) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *used to* — no change, ✗ *he uses to* &middot; **-ing form:** none
**Contrast:** Two different words are spelled *used*. The semi-modal here is /juːst/ with an **s** and means "formerly"; the ordinary verb *use* /juːz/ → *used* /juːzd/ means "employ". A third, *be used to* /juːst/ + *-ing*, means "be accustomed to" — *I'm used to waking early* is not the same as *I used to wake early*. In questions and negatives the *-d* disappears: *Did you use to live here?*, *I didn't use to like it*. Unlike *would*, *used to* works with **states**: *I used to have long hair* is fine, ✗ *I would have long hair* is not.
**Verb type:** **semi-modal** — takes a *to*-infinitive and exists only in the past. *Would* covers the repeated-action half of its meaning but not the state half.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — infinitival complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *the building used to be a school*.

Past habit and past state, both marked as over — the construction that says "and not anymore" without saying it.

**1. Discontinued habit.** Something done repeatedly then, not now. (раньше, бывало)

1. We *used to* eat dinner at six sharp.
2. She *used to* walk to work before the office moved.
3. I *used to* smoke, but I quit years ago.

**2. Discontinued state.** A condition that has since changed — where *would* cannot be substituted. (раньше был, когда-то)

4. There *used to* be a bakery on this corner.
5. He *used to* be much heavier.
6. This *used to* be farmland.

**3. Questions and negatives — *use to*.** The *-d* is carried by *did*, so the main form loses it. (раньше …? раньше не …)

7. *Did* you *use to* play an instrument?
8. It *didn't use to* cost this much.
9. *Didn't* she *use to* work here?

### had better

**Pronunciation:** /hæd ˈbɛtər/ &middot; *had-BET-ur* &middot; usually contracted to *'d better* /d ˈbɛtər/, and in fast speech to *better*
**Русский:** лучше бы, следовало бы (с оттенком предупреждения)
**Forms:** *had better* /hæd ˈbɛtər/ &middot; — &middot; — (one form only, despite the *had*)
**Verb class:** semi-modal
**Register:** everyday — but carries a warning, so not neutral advice
**Pattern:** defective (a semi-modal: fixed form) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *had better* — no change, ✗ *he has better* &middot; **-ing form:** none
**Contrast:** The *had* is frozen — it is **not** a past tense, and *had better* refers to the present or future: *you'd better hurry* means now. It takes a **bare** infinitive, never *to*: ✗ *you'd better to go*. The negative puts *not* after *better*: *you'd better not say anything*, not ✗ *you hadn't better*. And it is stronger than *should* — it implies a bad consequence if ignored, which makes it a poor choice for gentle advice.
**Verb type:** **semi-modal** — bare infinitive after it, no inflection at all.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive complement only.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *this had better be fixed by Monday*.

Advice with a threat behind it — the *had* is not past, and the negative goes in a place learners never guess.

**1. Urgent advice or warning.** Present or future. The implied clause is "or something bad happens". (лучше бы)

1. You *'d better* leave now if you want a seat.
2. We *'d better* check the gate number again.
3. He *'d better* have a good explanation.

**2. The negative — *had better not*.** *Not* follows *better*, never *had*. (лучше не)

4. You *'d better not* be late.
5. They *'d better not* find out from someone else.
6. I *'d better not* say anything.

### would rather

**Pronunciation:** /wʊd ˈræðər/ &middot; *wuud-RA-thur* &middot; usually contracted to *'d rather* /d ˈræðər/
**Русский:** предпочёл бы, лучше бы
**Forms:** *would rather* /wʊd ˈræðər/ &middot; — &middot; — (one form only)
**Verb class:** semi-modal
**Register:** everyday — the standard way to state a preference politely
**Pattern:** defective (a semi-modal: fixed form) &middot; **Group:** 64
**Third person:** *would rather* — no change, ✗ *he woulds rather* &middot; **-ing form:** none
**Contrast:** Bare infinitive, never *to*: ✗ *I'd rather to stay*. Comparison uses **than**, not *that*: *I'd rather walk than drive*. The real trap is the two-subject form — when the preference is about **someone else**, English switches to a past-tense clause: *I'd rather you didn't* (not ✗ *I'd rather you don't*), and for the past, *I'd rather you hadn't*. *Would prefer* is the close synonym but takes *to*: *I'd prefer to walk*.
**Verb type:** **semi-modal** — bare infinitive when the subject is the same, a past-tense clause when it differs.
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** — bare-infinitive or clausal complement.
**Passive:** **no** — voice sits on the supported verb: *I'd rather be told directly*.

Preference — and the construction where English suddenly demands a past tense for a present wish.

**1. Simple preference.** Same subject, bare infinitive, *than* for the comparison. (предпочёл бы)

1. I *'d rather* stay in tonight.
2. She *'d rather* walk than wait for the bus.
3. *Would* you *rather* sit inside or out?

**2. Preference about someone else.** Different subject, and the verb goes into the **past** although the meaning is present. (я бы предпочёл, чтобы…)

4. I *'d rather* you didn't mention it.
5. She *'d rather* we came on Sunday.
6. They *'d rather* nobody knew yet.

**3. Preference about the past.** *Would rather have* + participle, or a past-perfect clause. (лучше бы)

7. I *'d rather* have heard it from you.
8. He *'d rather* you hadn't told her.
9. We *'d rather* have paid more and gotten it right.
