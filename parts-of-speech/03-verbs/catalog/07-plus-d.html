Past = participle, add -d#

The past and participle add a -d to the unchanged base.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

These verbs use one form for both the past and the participle, and they build it by adding a -d to the base: pay → paid → paid, lay → laid → laid, have → had → had. The spelling adjusts a little where the base ends in -ay or -ake — the y becomes i (say → said, mislay → mislaid), and the k drops out of make → made — but there is only ever one form to learn past the base. Most of the group is just four verbs (have, say, make, pay, plus lay) wearing a prefix, so repay, overlay, gainsay and unmake cost you nothing extra once the base verb is in place. (In the original Russian list this is the group отличаются от 1-й добавлением "d".)

The one thing to watch is sound, not spelling: said rhymes with bed, not with paid.

belay#

Pronunciation
/bɪˈleɪ/ · bih-LAY
Русский
мор. закрепить (канат); завёртывать (снасть)
Forms
belay /bɪˈleɪ/ · belayed /bɪˈleɪd/ · belayed /bɪˈleɪd/ — the older nautical belaid /bɪˈleɪd/ is the form the source list records
Register
rare — a climbing and sailing term; outside those trades Americans meet it only in the Navy's Belay that!
Pattern
ay — ayed — ayed
Group
7
Third person
belays · -ing form: belaying
Contrast
This is the odd one out among the -lay verbs here. Lay, mislay and overlay all give -laid, but belay went regular centuries ago: modern American English says belayed, and every climbing manual prints it that way. Belaid survives only in old sailing texts. Note also the Navy idiom Belay that!, meaning "cancel that" — it has no past tense at all.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout: managing a rope (belay your partner), making a line fast (belay the halyard), and the Navy imperative Belay that!, which exists only as a command.
Transitivity
both — transitive with a climber or a line as object (she belayed me on every pitch), intransitive when the partner is understood (he has belayed for beginners for six years).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — I was belayed by my partner on every pitch; the halyard is belayed to a pin on the rail. The imperative Belay that! has no passive.

To hold and control a climbing rope for a partner, or, at sea, to make a line fast around a cleat — and in Navy usage, to cancel an order.

1. Belay a climber. The everyday modern sense: to manage the rope so that a partner's fall is caught. Fully regular in American English — belayed, never the old ✗ belaid. (страховать (в альпинизме))

  1. Always belay your partner before they leave the ground.
  2. She belayed me on every pitch of the route.
  3. He has belayed for beginners at the gym for six years.
  4. By noon they had belayed each other up four routes.

2. Make a rope fast (nautical). At sea: to secure a line by winding it around a cleat or a belaying pin. This is the original sense, and the archaic past belaid belongs to it. (закреплять (канат, снасть))

  1. The bosun belays the halyard to a pin on the rail.
  2. They belayed the line and let the sail hold.
  3. The crew had belayed every sheet before the squall hit.

3. Belay that! — cancel an order. Navy and Coast Guard usage, and it exists only as an imperative — there is no past tense in practice. Belay that means disregard what was just said. (отставить!, отменить (приказ))

  1. Belay that — we're taking the other truck.
  2. Belay my last transmission; the coordinates were wrong.
See also
lay, mislay

clip#

Pronunciation
/klɪp/ · KLIP
Русский
стричь; подрезать
Forms
clip /klɪp/ · clipped /klɪpt/ · clipped /klɪpt/ — the archaic participle clipt /klɪpt/ is what the source list records
Register
everyday — clipping coupons, nails, or a curb is ordinary American conversation
Pattern
p — pped — pped
Group
7
Third person
clips · -ing form: clipping (the p doubles)
Contrast
Clip is a fully regular verb in American English: clipped for both the past and the participle. Clipt is an old poetic spelling — Shakespeare and hymn books have it — and it sounds exactly the same, so nothing is lost by ignoring it. Do not write ✗ He has clipt the hedge today.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense: cutting (clip the dog), snipping out of print (clip a coupon), fastening (clip the badge on), striking in passing (clip the curb), and figurative curtailing (clip his wings).
Transitivity
transitive in all five senses, and the object is normally obligatory — ✗ The truck clipped is incomplete. The fastening sense adds a goal phrase: clip A to B.
Passive
yes — his hair has been clipped the same way since 1998; a mic was clipped to his collar; his wings have been clipped since the merger.

To cut something short with scissors or clippers, to cut a piece out of print, to fasten with a clip, or to strike something a glancing blow.

1. Cut with scissors or clippers. The core sense: trimming hair, nails, a hedge, or an animal's coat. Regular in modern usage; the poetic participle clipt is dead. (стричь, подрезать)

  1. I clip the dog myself to save eighty dollars.
  2. She clipped her nails over the trash can.
  3. The barber has clipped his hair the same way since 1998.
  4. We had clipped the hedge back before the storm came through.

2. Cut something out of print. To snip an article, coupon, or photo out of printed material — still alive in clip coupons. Extended now to saving a short piece of video or audio. (вырезать (из газеты, журнала))

  1. My grandmother still clips recipes out of the Sunday paper.
  2. He clipped the obituary and mailed it to me.
  3. Fans have clipped the best thirty seconds of the interview.

3. Fasten with a clip. To attach one thing to another with a clip or clasp. The pattern is clip A to B. (прикреплять, пристёгивать)

  1. Clip the badge to your jacket before you go in.
  2. The sound tech clipped a mic to his collar.
  3. Someone had clipped the pages together in the wrong order.

4. Hit with a glancing blow. To strike something in passing, usually by accident and usually at speed. Common in driving and in contact sports. (задеть, зацепить (вскользь))

  1. If you clip the curb at that angle you'll blow a tire.
  2. The truck clipped our mirror and kept going.
  3. He has clipped the same doorframe twice this month.

5. Cut short, curtail. Figurative: to reduce or limit. Fixed in clip someone's wings, meaning restrict their freedom, and in clipped speech, meaning terse and brisk. (укорачивать, урезать, ограничивать)

  1. The new rules clip the mayor's authority considerably.
  2. Budget cuts clipped the program back to two days a week.
  3. The board has clipped his wings since the merger.
See also
ski

gainsay#

Pronunciation
/ˌɡeɪnˈseɪ/ · gayn-SAY
Русский
отрицать; противоречить
Forms
gainsay /ˌɡeɪnˈseɪ/ · gainsaid /ˌɡeɪnˈsɛd/ · gainsaid /ˌɡeɪnˈsɛd/ — the spelling pronunciation /ˌɡeɪnˈseɪd/ is also accepted for the past and participle
Register
rare — formal and nearly frozen in no one can gainsay it; almost never produced in speech
Pattern
ay — aid — aid
Group
7
Third person
gainsays /ˌɡeɪnˈseɪz/ · -ing form: gainsaying
Contrast
The past follows said, so it is normally /ˌɡeɪnˈsɛd/, rhyming with bed; the spelling pronunciation /ˌɡeɪnˈseɪd/ is also heard and accepted. The third person, though, keeps the /eɪ/ — gainsays is not pronounced like says /sɛz/. Note too that this verb is formal and lives almost entirely in negative frames: no one can gainsay it, not ✗ I gainsay you.
Verb type
action — dynamic, a verb of speech, but so formal and frozen that it barely appears outside negative frames: no one can gainsay it, there is no gainsaying it.
Transitivity
transitive, object obligatory — a statement (gainsay the numbers) or a person (gainsay the bishop). ✗ Nobody gainsaid is incomplete.
Passive
restricted — natural only in the negative frames the verb lives in: the finding has not been gainsaid in twenty years. A plain positive passive sounds wrong: ✗ Every point was gainsaid by the committee.

To deny or contradict something — formal, and almost always in a negative frame.

1. Deny, contradict a statement. Formal and largely written. It lives in negative frames — no one can gainsay it, there is no gainsaying it — and sounds wrong in a plain positive sentence. (отрицать, оспаривать)

  1. No one can gainsay the numbers in her report.
  2. The committee gainsaid every point he made.
  3. Nobody has gainsaid the finding in twenty years.

2. Oppose, speak against someone. With a person or an authority as the object: to speak in open opposition to them. Archaic in tone and rare outside legal or literary prose. (противоречить, перечить (кому-л.))

  1. Few of his officers dared gainsay him.
  2. She gainsaid the bishop in open council.
  3. Until last week no one had gainsaid the old theory.

say, unsay

have#

Pronunciation
/hæv/ · HAV
Русский
иметь, обладать
Forms
have /hæv/ · had /hæd/ · had /hæd/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking, and the auxiliary behind every perfect tense
Pattern
ave — ad — ad
Group
7
Third person
has /hæz/ (not ✗ haves) · -ing form: having (drop the e)
Contrast
Have is both a full verb and the auxiliary that builds every perfect tense, so the two stack: I have had three cups of coffee, she had had enough. In speech the auxiliary weakens to /əv/, which sounds exactly like of — that is why people write ✗ would of gone. It is always would have gone or would've gone. Also watch the contraction 'd, which can be either had or would: I'd finished is had, I'd finish is would.
Verb type
stative in the core senses — possession (we have two cars), experience (I had a headache), obligation (have to) — and there it rejects the progressive: ✗ I am having two cars. Dynamic in the eat/drink, hold-an-event, give-birth and causative senses, where the progressive is fine: we're having lunch, she's having a baby. Also the auxiliary behind every perfect tense (they have finished).
Transitivity
transitive in every full-verb sense, object obligatory — ✗ We have is incomplete. The causative takes object + complement: have the roof replaced, have the office send you the file. Never ditransitive. As auxiliary it takes no object at all, only a participle.
Passive
no for the stative senses — this is the classic learner trap: ✗ Two cars are had by us, ✗ A headache was had by me, ✗ A meeting is had every Monday. The auxiliary has no passive either. The one live passive is the fixed idiom be had 'be cheated': if you paid full price, you have been had.

To possess or hold something; to eat, experience, receive, or arrange something; and, as an auxiliary, to build every perfect tense in English.

1. Possess, own. The basic full-verb sense. In informal American speech it usually appears as have got: I've got two brothers means the same as I have two brothers. (иметь, обладать)

  1. We have two cars and no garage.
  2. They had a house on the lake before the divorce.
  3. She has had that jacket since college.

2. Auxiliary of the perfect tenses. Have/has + participle makes the present perfect, had + participle the past perfect. In speech it weakens to /əv/, which is why people misspell it ✗ would of gone for would have gone. (вспомогательный глагол перфекта)

  1. They have finished the roof already.
  2. By the time we sat down, they had eaten.
  3. She has had three interviews this week.

3. Eat or drink something. Have replaces eat and drink in most ordinary sentences: you have lunch, a coffee, a beer. (есть, пить, принимать (пищу))

  1. I usually have oatmeal before a run.
  2. We had pizza twice last week.
  3. He has had nothing but coffee all day.

4. Experience, undergo. Used of illnesses, feelings, and things that happen to you: have a headache, have a good time, have an accident. (испытывать, переживать)

  1. Did you have a good flight?
  2. I had a headache all afternoon.
  3. She has had a rough year.

5. Hold an event; take part in one. Have a party, a meeting, a conversation, an argument — the subject organizes it or is in it. (устраивать, проводить)

  1. We have a staff meeting every Monday at nine.
  2. They had a barbecue for the whole street.
  3. The club has had the same fundraiser for twelve years.

6. Have to — be obliged. Have to + base verb expresses obligation, and it is what English uses in the past, where must has no form: I had to leave. In fast speech it becomes hafta. (быть должным, приходиться)

  1. You have to show ID at the door.
  2. I had to leave before the second half.
  3. We have had to rewrite the contract twice.

7. Have got — informal possession and obligation. Spoken American English adds got: I've got a meeting (possession) and I've got to go (obligation, reduced to gotta). Present tense only — the past is plain had and had to. (разг.: иметь; быть должным)

  1. I have got a meeting at three I can't move.
  2. You have got to see this.
  3. She has got two brothers in Chicago.

8. Have something done — causative. Have + object + participle means you arranged for someone else to do it: have the car fixed. With a bare infinitive — have someone do it — you are directing the person yourself. (поручать, делать (чужими руками))

  1. I'll have the office send you the file.
  2. We had the roof replaced last spring.
  3. She has had her hair cut short since June.

9. Give birth to. Of people and animals alike: have a baby, have puppies. Neutral, and by far the commonest way to say it. (рожать, родить)

  1. She's going to have a baby in March.
  2. Our cat had five kittens under the porch.
  3. They have had three children in five years.

10. Receive, get. Somewhat formal or written: have news, have word from someone, may I have your attention. (получать)

  1. May I have your attention for a moment?
  2. We had word from the embassy this morning.
  3. I have had no answer from them at all.

11. Allow, tolerate. Negative only, and emphatic: I won't have it, she wouldn't have any of that. It means refuse to permit. (терпеть, допускать)

  1. I will not have shouting in this house.
  2. The principal wouldn't have any of it.
  3. They had enough sense not to argue with her about it.

12. Be had — be cheated. Usually passive: you've been had means you were tricked or overcharged. British have someone on means to tease them; Americans say put someone on. (быть обманутым, попасться на удочку)

  1. If you paid full price for that, you have been had.
  2. The dealer had us on the trade-in value.
  3. They realized they had been had only after the check cleared.
Phrasal verbs
have at · have in · have it in for · have it out · have on · have over
See also
make, get

Pronunciation
/ɪnˈleɪ/ · in-LAY
Русский
вкладывать; выстилать
Forms
inlay /ɪnˈleɪ/ · inlaid /ɪnˈleɪd/ · inlaid /ɪnˈleɪd/
Register
rare — a craft and dental term; most Americans know only the noun an inlay
Pattern
ay — aid — aid
Group
7
Third person
inlays · -ing form: inlaying
Contrast
Stress moves between the verb and the noun: the verb is in-LAY /ɪnˈleɪ/, but the noun — a piece of inlaid work, or a dental filling — is IN-lay /ˈɪnˌleɪ/. Everything else follows lay.
Verb type
action — dynamic: setting one material flush into the surface of another (inlay mother-of-pearl by hand), including the dental sense (inlay gold).
Transitivity
transitive, object obligatory, in two frames — inlay A with B (inlay the wood with brass) or inlay B into A (inlay brass into the floor).
Passive
yes, and the passive is the commoner form — every door in the house had been inlaid with ivory; brass letters have been inlaid into the lobby floor. The participle doubles as an adjective: an inlaid box.

To set pieces of one material into the surface of another so that the finished surface stays flush.

1. Set material into a surface. Woodworking, jewelry, and instrument making: shell, metal, or stone let into wood or silver. The pattern is inlay A with B or inlay B into A. (инкрустировать, вкладывать)

  1. Craftsmen still inlay mother-of-pearl by hand in that shop.
  2. The luthier inlaid tiny stars along the neck of the guitar.
  3. Someone has inlaid brass letters into the lobby floor.
  4. By 1890 the builders had inlaid every door in the house with ivory.

2. Fit a dental inlay. Dentistry: to fit a shaped filling into a prepared cavity. Most people meet the noun an inlay, but the verb is standard in clinical writing. (ставить вкладку (в зуб))

  1. This dentist inlays gold in cases where a crown would be too much.
  2. They inlaid porcelain rather than amalgam.
  3. By the second visit he had inlaid both molars.
See also
lay, overlay, underlay

lay#

Pronunciation
/leɪ/ · LAY
Русский
класть
Forms
lay /leɪ/ · laid /leɪd/ · laid /leɪd/
Register
everyday — you lay something down a dozen times a day without noticing
Pattern
ay — aid — aid
Group
7
Third person
lays · -ing form: laying
Contrast
This is the classic trap in English. Lay takes an object — you lay something down — and goes lay, laid, laid. Lie takes no object — you lie down yourself — and goes lie, lay, lain. The collision is that lay is also the past of lie: ✗ I laid down for a nap should be I lay down for a nap. And the spelling of the past is laid, never ✗ layed.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense, from putting something down flat to installing (lay tile), preparing (lay a trap), imposing (lay blame), and wagering (lay fifty dollars).
Transitivity
transitive — the object is what separates it from intransitive lie: ✗ I laid down for a nap should be I lay down for a nap. The one object-dropping sense is egg-laying: the hens aren't laying.
Passive
yes — the tile had been laid before the inspector arrived; three charges were laid against the company; two hundred workers were laid off in March.

To put something down flat — and from there, to install, prepare, establish, impose, or wager it. Always transitive; the intransitive verb is lie.

1. Put something down flat. The core sense, and it always takes an object. Do not confuse it with intransitive lie: ✗ I laid down for a nap should be I lay down for a nap. (класть, положить)

  1. Please lay the baby in the crib on her back.
  2. He laid his keys on the counter and forgot them there.
  3. She has laid a blanket over him.

2. Lay eggs. Of birds, fish, and insects. It works without an object too: the hens aren't laying. (нести (яйца), откладывать яйца)

  1. That turtle lays her eggs on the same beach every June.
  2. The salmon laid their eggs and died.
  3. The hens have laid six eggs this week.

3. Install, put in place. The building trades: lay tile, lay carpet, lay pipe, lay track, lay brick. (укладывать, прокладывать)

  1. They lay about four hundred feet of pipe a day.
  2. We had laid the tile before the inspector arrived.
  3. The crew has laid new cable down the whole street.

4. Set out, prepare. Lay the table, lay a fire, lay a trap, lay plans. British English says lay the table where Americans usually say set the table. (накрывать (на стол); разводить (огонь); готовить (ловушку))

  1. Lay the table for six, please.
  2. He laid a fire in the grate before the guests arrived.
  3. They had laid a trap and he walked right into it.

5. Lay a foundation, lay the groundwork. Figurative and very common in writing about projects and arguments: lay a foundation, lay the groundwork, lay the basis for. (закладывать (основу, фундамент))

  1. These three chapters lay the foundation for everything after.
  2. That treaty laid the groundwork for forty years of peace.
  3. Her early work has laid the basis for the whole field.

6. Impose, assign (blame, charges, a tax). Formal and legal: lay blame on someone, lay charges, lay claim to, lay a tax on. (возлагать (вину); налагать (обвинение, налог))

  1. Don't lay the blame on the intern.
  2. Prosecutors laid three charges against the company.
  3. Two countries have laid claim to the same reef.

7. Bet, wager. Lay a bet, lay odds, lay money on something. Informal; I'd lay money that… just means the speaker is confident. (ставить (в пари), держать пари)

  1. I'd lay money that he shows up late.
  2. He laid fifty dollars on the underdog.
  3. She has laid a small bet on every Derby since 1990.

8. Lay off — dismiss from a job. To end someone's employment for economic reasons rather than for cause — quite different from fire. Informally, lay off something also means quit doing it: lay off the coffee. (увольнять (по сокращению))

  1. Management may lay off more people after the audit.
  2. The plant laid off two hundred workers in March.
  3. They have laid off a third of the department since spring.

9. Lay out — arrange, or explain in detail. Two everyday uses: spreading things out in order (lay out your clothes), and presenting a plan or an argument point by point (lay out the terms). (раскладывать, размещать; излагать)

  1. She lays out her clothes the night before every workday.
  2. He laid out the whole proposal in fifteen minutes.
  3. The memo has laid out exactly what happens next.

10. Lay down — set a rule, or put aside. Lay down the law means state the rules firmly; lay down arms means surrender. In music, lay down a track means record it. (устанавливать (правила); складывать (оружие))

  1. The coach lays down the same three rules every season.
  2. The rebels laid down their arms at dawn.
  3. The band has laid down four tracks this week.
Phrasal verbs
lay aside · lay away · lay down · lay in · lay into · lay off · lay on · lay out · lay over · lay up
See also
inlay, mislay, overlay, underlay, waylay, lie

make#

Pronunciation
/meɪk/ · MAYK
Русский
делать; производить; заставлять
Forms
make /meɪk/ · made /meɪd/ · made /meɪd/
Register
everyday — one of the handful of verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
ake — ade — ade
Group
7
Third person
makes · -ing form: making (drop the e)
Contrast
In the "force someone to" sense, active make takes a bare infinitive but the passive takes to: They made him apologize becomes He was made to apologize — ✗ He was made apologize. Note also that made is a homophone of maid.
Verb type
action — dynamic in the creating, forcing, earning, and light-verb senses (make a mistake, make a living). The amount to sense is stative and copula-like, joining subject to a complement rather than acting on an object: four quarters make a dollar, that makes nine of us — no progressive, ✗ four quarters are making a dollar.
Transitivity
both, and mostly transitive — plain transitive (make coffee), ditransitive (make her a sandwich), and complex-transitive with a complement (make her chairman, make me happy) or a bare infinitive (they made him apologize). Intransitive only inside the fixed make do and make for.
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the same part has been made there since 1962; she was made chief financial officer. Note the shift in the forcing sense: the passive requires to — he was made to apologize, ✗ he was made apologize. No passive of the amount to sense: ✗ A dollar is made by four quarters.

To create or produce something — and, in its many extended uses, to force, earn, reach, amount to, or manage something.

1. Create, produce, build. The core sense: bringing something into existence, from a sandwich to a spacecraft. (делать, изготавливать, производить)

  1. I make coffee before anyone else in the house is up.
  2. He made the whole dinner out of what was in the fridge.
  3. That factory has made the same part since 1962.

2. Force or cause someone to do something. Active make takes a bare infinitive — they made him apologize — but the passive takes to: he was made to apologize, never ✗ he was made apologize. (заставлять, вынуждать)

  1. That story makes me laugh every single time.
  2. They made him apologize in front of the class.
  3. Nobody has made her change her mind yet.

3. Cause to become; appoint. Make + object + adjective (make me happy) or + noun (make her chairman). The appointing use is formal. (делать (каким-л.), превращать; назначать)

  1. The new lights make the room feel twice as big.
  2. The board made her chief financial officer.
  3. Two years abroad had made him almost unrecognizable.

4. Earn money. Make a living, make money, make sixty thousand a year. Completely neutral in American English, where earn sounds more formal. (зарабатывать)

  1. She makes more than both of us combined.
  2. He made a fortune on that one contract.
  3. They have made nothing on the store in three years.

5. Light verb: make a decision, a mistake, an offer. Make carries dozens of fixed noun combinations where it means little more than 'do': make a decision, a mistake, an effort, a promise, a call, progress, an offer. Learn them as units. (совершать, принимать, делать (в устойчивых сочетаниях))

  1. Try not to make the same mistake twice.
  2. They made three offers on the same house.
  3. The team has made real progress this quarter.

6. Amount to, equal. Arithmetic and totals: two and two make four, that makes eleven of us. (составлять, равняться)

  1. Four quarters make a dollar.
  2. With Dave, that makes nine people in a six-person van.
  3. The last donation made it an even ten thousand.

7. Reach, arrive at, manage to attend. Make the train, make the deadline, make it to the airport. Very often with it: we made it. (успевать, добираться, поспевать)

  1. If we leave now we can make the six o'clock train.
  2. He made it to the hospital with ten minutes to spare.
  3. She has made every practice this season.

8. Make up — invent, reconcile, or constitute. Three unrelated meanings share one phrase: invent a story, end a quarrel, and form the whole of something (volunteers make up most of the staff). Make up for means compensate. (выдумывать; мириться; составлять)

  1. He made up the entire story on the drive over.
  2. They fought for a week and then made up.
  3. Volunteers make up most of the staff.
  4. She has made up for the missed weeks with extra hours.

9. Make out — discern, or manage. To make out a shape or a signature is to perceive it with difficulty; how did you make out? asks how you did. In American slang it also means to kiss at length. (разобрать (разглядеть, расслышать); справляться)

  1. I can barely make out the signature.
  2. We made out fine without the generator.
  3. Nobody has made out what the last line of the letter says.

10. Make do — manage with what's available. A fixed phrase, always make do (with). There is no ✗ make due. (обходиться (тем, что есть))

  1. We'll make do with what's in the pantry.
  2. They made do on one income for years.
  3. He has made do without a car since he moved downtown.

11. Make for; make off with. Make for a place means head toward it, and make for + noun also means produce a result (that makes for a long day). Make off with something means steal it and go. (направляться к; способствовать; удрать (с чем-л.))
