# Past = participle, vowel change plus -t

> Like the previous group, with a -t added to the changed stem.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

These verbs change the vowel of the stem and then add a -t, and that single form does duty for both the past and the participle (как №3, плюс окончание "t"). *Keep* shortens to *kept*, *feel* to *felt*, *leave* to *left* — one form to memorize instead of two. A large subgroup goes further and rewrites the whole ending as *-ought* or *-aught*: *bring/brought*, *teach/taught*, *think/thought*, *buy/bought*. Watch the handful of verbs whose British forms end in -t but whose American forms are plain -ed — *dreamed*, *leaned*, and *daydreamed* are the American defaults, and *wreak* takes *wreaked*, never *wrought*.

### beseech

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsitʃ/ &middot; *bih-SEECH*
**Русский:** умолять; упрашивать
**Forms:** *beseech* /bɪˈsitʃ/ &middot; *besought* /bɪˈsɔt/ &middot; *besought* /bɪˈsɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — literary and scriptural; in ordinary speech Americans *beg* or *plead*
**Pattern:** -eech &rarr; -ought &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *beseeches* (adds -es) &middot; **-ing form:** *beseeching*
**Contrast:** The regular *beseeched* is now the more common past in American writing; *besought* is literary and turns up mainly in older prose and translations of scripture. Either is correct, so long as you use the same one twice: ✗ *he beseeched them, and had besought them again*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a verb of asking, so it reports a speech act rather than a state. No linking or stative use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is the person begged, and it is obligatory: *the families beseech the governor*, ✗ *the families beseech*. Usually completed by an infinitive (*beseech him to forgive her*) or by *for* + the thing wanted (*besought the judge for one more week*).
**Passive:** **yes**, though formal like the verb itself — *the judge was besought for one more week*; *the agency has been besought for years to publish the data*.

To beg someone urgently and humbly for something; formal and literary in modern American English.

**1. Beg urgently, implore.** Formal and literary; in ordinary speech Americans beg or plead instead. The regular past *beseeched* is now more common in American writing than *besought*. (умолять, упрашивать)

1. The families *beseech* the governor to reopen the case.
2. Her lawyers *besought* the judge for one more week.
3. Advocates *have besought* the agency for years to publish the data.

**2. Entreat in prayer or older prose.** The scriptural and literary register, where *besought* is the expected past. You meet it in translations, hymns, and historical fiction rather than in speech. (молить, взывать)

4. The pilgrims *besought* heaven for rain.
5. He *had besought* his father to forgive him before the old man died.
6. In the last chapter she *beseeches* the reader to remember her kindly.

### bring

**Pronunciation:** /brɪŋ/ &middot; *BRING*
**Русский:** приносить
**Forms:** *bring* /brɪŋ/ &middot; *brought* /brɔt/ &middot; *brought* /brɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** -ing &rarr; -ought &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *brings* &middot; **-ing form:** *bringing*
**Contrast:** ✗ *brang* and ✗ *brung* are heard in some American dialects but are never standard. The trap is that *sing/sang/sung* and *ring/rang/rung* look like they should set the pattern; *bring* refuses to join them. Also keep *brought* (carried) apart from *bought* (paid for).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, from carrying (*bring your ID*) to causing (*spring brings the tourists back*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, and freely **ditransitive**: *bring me the file*, *bring her a chair*. The object is obligatory — ✗ *she brought* is incomplete. The particle uses keep the object (*bring up the layoffs*, *bring in a consultant*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and very common — *he was brought up by his grandmother*; *three cases have been brought against the company*; *the resignation was brought about by their reporting*.

To carry or take someone or something to the place where you are or are going; also, to cause a result or a state.

**1. Carry or take to this place.** The motion is toward the speaker or toward the place being talked about. If the movement is away from you, the verb is *take*. (приносить, приводить, привозить)

1. Please *bring* your ID to the appointment.
2. She *brought* dessert to the potluck.
3. Somebody *has brought* a dog into the lobby.

**2. Cause a state or result.** The subject produces the effect, and the object is usually abstract: rain, joy, trouble, publicity, change. (приносить, вызывать, приводить к)

4. Spring *brings* the tourists back.
5. The merger *brought* three years of chaos.
6. That decision *has brought* nothing but lawsuits.

**3. Bring about (cause to happen).** A fixed phrase for making a change actually happen, often after effort or delay. (осуществить, вызвать, привести к)

7. It took a strike to *bring about* a new contract.
8. Their reporting *brought about* the resignation.
9. Technology *has brought about* changes nobody planned for.

**4. Bring up (raise a topic).** To introduce a subject into a conversation, often one others would rather avoid. (поднять тему, упомянуть)

10. Don't *bring up* the layoffs at dinner.
11. She *brought up* the budget again.
12. Nobody *has brought* it *up* since the meeting.

**5. Bring up (raise a child).** Common in both countries, though Americans often prefer *raise*. Frequently passive: *he was brought up in Ohio*. (воспитывать, растить)

13. He was *brought up* by his grandmother.
14. They *brought up* four kids on one salary.
15. She *has brought up* two children alone.

**6. Bring in (earn or recruit).** Two everyday uses: money that a business takes in, and a person hired or called in from outside. (приносить доход; привлекать со стороны)

16. The store *brings in* about six thousand a week.
17. They *brought in* a consultant after the second outage.
18. We *have brought in* two new engineers this quarter.

**7. Bring down (topple or reduce).** Used both of removing someone from power and of lowering a number such as a price, a fever, or a rate. (свергнуть; сбить, снизить)

19. The scandal *brought down* the mayor.
20. Nothing will *bring down* a fever that fast.
21. Generic drugs *have brought* prices *down* sharply.

**8. Bring oneself to (force oneself).** Almost always negative or with *can't*: you are unable to make yourself do something painful. (заставить себя)

22. I can't *bring* myself to sell the house.
23. She never *brought* herself to apologize.
24. He *has* never *brought* himself to watch the tape.

**9. Bring charges, bring a suit.** The standard legal collocation for starting a case against someone. (выдвинуть обвинения, подать иск)

25. Prosecutors may still *bring* charges.
26. The tenants *brought* a class action.
27. The state *has brought* three cases against the company.

### buy

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ/ &middot; *BY*
**Русский:** купить
**Forms:** *buy* /baɪ/ &middot; *bought* /bɔt/ &middot; *bought* /bɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary word for paying money for something
**Pattern:** -uy &rarr; -ought &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *buys* &middot; **-ing form:** *buying*
**Contrast:** *Bought* and *brought* are one letter apart and are swapped constantly: ✗ *I brought a new laptop* when you mean you paid for one. If money changed hands it is *bought*; if you carried it somewhere it is *brought*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the purchase, bribery, and *buy out* senses. The *believe* sense (*I don't buy it*) is mental but still takes the progressive: *I'm not buying that story*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, and **ditransitive** in *buy her a coffee*, *buy us two more weeks*. The object is normally obligatory (✗ *we bought at the market*), though *buy* can stand alone in shopping contexts: *we buy local*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the truck was bought used*; *two inspectors were bought off*; *the chain has been bought out by a private equity firm*.

To get something by paying money for it; informally, also to believe something or to gain it at a cost.

**1. Purchase, pay money for.** The core sense. Keep it apart from *brought*, the past of *bring*: money makes it *bought*. (покупать, приобретать)

1. We *buy* our vegetables at the farmers market.
2. He *bought* the truck used, with ninety thousand miles on it.
3. They *have bought* a house outside Tucson.

**2. Believe, accept as true.** Informal, and usually negative: *I don't buy it*. The object is a story, an excuse, or an argument. (верить, купиться на)

4. I don't *buy* that excuse.
5. The jury never *bought* his story.
6. Nobody *has bought* that line since the first press conference.

**3. Gain at a cost.** What is paid is not always money; the object is often time, silence, safety, or goodwill. (выиграть (время), купить ценой чего-либо)

7. The delay *bought* us two more weeks.
8. A second server would *buy* some breathing room.
9. That concession *has bought* them nothing at all.

**4. Bribe, buy off.** Informal but unambiguous. With *off* it means paying someone to drop an objection or a claim. (подкупить)

10. You cannot *buy* a federal judge.
11. The company *bought off* two inspectors.
12. Someone *had bought* his silence long before the hearing.

**5. Buy into (accept, or take a stake).** Either to be persuaded by an idea or to purchase a share of a business. The context separates them. (принять идею; войти в долю)

13. Half the team never *bought into* the new process.
14. She *bought into* the partnership in 2019.
15. Investors *have bought into* the story completely.

**6. Buy out (purchase someone's share).** To acquire the whole of another party's stake so they leave the business. (выкупить долю)

16. His brothers *bought* him *out*.
17. They offered to *buy* her *out* at book value.
18. A private equity firm *has bought out* the chain.

### catch

**Pronunciation:** /kætʃ/ &middot; *KACH*
**Русский:** ловить, хватать
**Forms:** *catch* /kætʃ/ &middot; *caught* /kɔt/ &middot; *caught* /kɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — constant in speech, from catching a ball to catching a cold
**Pattern:** -atch &rarr; -aught &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *catches* (adds -es) &middot; **-ing form:** *catching*
**Contrast:** ✗ *catched* is not a form. For the many Americans with the cot–caught merger, *caught* /kɔt/ and *cot* /kɑt/ sound identical, but the spelling never wavers — and *caught* is spelled with the same -aught as *taught*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, including the involuntary ones (*catch a cold*, *catch the joke*). No linking or stative use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in most senses (*caught the glass*, *caught three fish*), intransitive in *the zipper catches* and in *catch on* / *catch up* (*go ahead; I'll catch up*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the driver has been caught*; *two managers have been caught falsifying the logs*. Restricted for the illness sense, where the active is the only natural form: ✗ *a cold was caught by him*.

To take hold of something moving, and by extension to capture, reach in time, notice, or contract something.

**1. Seize something moving.** The literal sense: stop an object in flight with your hands. (ловить, поймать)

1. Dogs *catch* frisbees better than most people do.
2. She *caught* the glass before it hit the floor.
3. The goalie *has caught* everything they have thrown at him.

**2. Capture, trap.** Of animals, fish, or people who are running from you. (поймать, задержать)

4. Traps rarely *catch* anything this time of year.
5. We *caught* three fish and threw them all back.
6. Police *have caught* the driver.

**3. Discover someone in the act.** Normally *catch someone doing something*, with an -ing form and never an infinitive. (застать, поймать с поличным)

7. He *caught* his roommate going through his mail.
8. Don't let them *catch* you parking there.
9. Two managers *have* been *caught* falsifying the logs.

**4. Be in time for.** Of trains, flights, the end of a movie, or a person before they leave. (успеть на, застать)

10. If I run I can *catch* the 6:40.
11. She *caught* the last train out of the city.
12. We *have caught* that flight twice with ten minutes to spare.

**5. Contract an illness.** Used of infections you pick up from other people, not of chronic conditions. (подхватить, заразиться)

13. Kids *catch* everything in their first year of school.
14. He *caught* a cold the week before the wedding.
15. She *has caught* the flu twice this winter.

**6. Hear or understand.** Usually negative or in an apology: *I didn't catch that*. (расслышать, уловить)

16. I didn't *catch* your last name.
17. He *caught* only half of what she said over the noise.
18. Nobody *has caught* the joke yet.

**7. Notice, spot a mistake.** Of errors, discrepancies, and problems found before they do damage. (заметить, обнаружить)

19. Spellcheck *catches* the obvious ones.
20. She *caught* the discrepancy on the second read.
21. An intern *has caught* two errors the auditors missed.

**8. Snag, become stuck.** The subject is the thing that snags, and the phrase is normally *catch on* something. (зацепиться, застрять)

22. The zipper *catches* about halfway up.
23. My sleeve *caught* on the door handle.
24. A branch *had caught* the kite ten feet up.

**9. Catch up (reach the same point).** Of distance, of work left undone, or of news between friends who have not talked in a while. (догнать, наверстать)

25. Go ahead; I'll *catch up*.
26. He *caught up* on email over the weekend.
27. We *have caught up* on the backlog at last.

**10. Catch on (become popular; understand).** Two settled meanings: a fashion or a name spreads, or a person finally grasps what is going on. (прижиться, войти в моду; сообразить)

28. The name never *caught on*.
29. She *caught on* faster than anyone expected.
30. The style *has caught on* with younger buyers.

### creep

**Pronunciation:** /krip/ &middot; *KREEP*
**Русский:** ползти
**Forms:** *creep* /krip/ &middot; *crept* /krɛpt/ &middot; *crept* /krɛpt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in speech, especially in *creep out* and *creep up on*
**Pattern:** ee &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *creeps* &middot; **-ing form:** *creeping*
**Contrast:** The past is *crept* — except in the fixed slang phrase *creep someone out*, which is regular: *that movie creeped me out*, ✗ *that movie crept me out*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and typically slow or gradual: literal movement (*the cat crept along the fence*) and figurative advance (*costs have crept up*).
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every literal and figurative sense — the direction is carried by a preposition (*creep along the rail*, *creep into her emails*). Transitive only in the fixed slang *creep someone out*.
**Passive:** **no** for the movement senses, which have no object at all: ✗ *the fence rail was crept along*. The one passive is the slang idiom — *I was completely creeped out by that mask*.

To move slowly, quietly, and low to the ground, or to advance by small degrees.

**1. Move slowly and stealthily.** Quiet movement meant to go unnoticed, often close to the ground. (красться, ползти)

1. Don't *creep* around behind me like that.
2. The cat *crept* along the fence rail.
3. He *had crept* downstairs before anyone else woke.

**2. Advance by small degrees.** Figurative, usually with *up* or *in*: costs, delays, doubts, and errors creep rather than jump. (расти незаметно, вкрадываться)

4. Doubt *creeps* in around three in the morning.
5. A little sarcasm *crept* into her emails.
6. Costs *have crept* up about four percent this year.

**3. Grow along a surface.** Of ivy, vines, and moss; the adjective *creeping* names whole plant families. (стелиться, виться (о растениях))

7. Ivy will *creep* up the north wall if you let it.
8. Moss *crept* over the stones.
9. The vine *has crept* across the whole trellis.

**4. Creep up on (approach unnoticed).** Of a person sneaking up, and just as often of a date or a season arriving before you noticed. (подкрасться незаметно)

10. Winter *crept up on* us.
11. She *creeps up on* you with the sad part of the song.
12. The deadline *has crept up on* everyone.

**5. Creep someone out.** Informal, and the one use of this verb with a regular past: *that movie creeped me out*, never *crept me out*. (жутко пугать, вызывать мурашки)

13. That mask *creeps* me out.
14. The whole basement *creeped* us out.
15. His messages *have creeped* her out for weeks.

### daydream

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdeɪˌdrim/ &middot; *DAY-dreem*
**Русский:** грезить наяву; мечтать, фантазировать
**Forms:** *daydream* /ˈdeɪˌdrim/ &middot; *daydreamed* /ˈdeɪˌdrimd/ (also *daydreamt* /ˈdeɪˌdrɛmt/) &middot; *daydreamed* /ˈdeɪˌdrimd/ (also *daydreamt*)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary word for letting your mind wander
**Pattern:** regular -ed in American English (British ea &rarr; e + t) &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *daydreams* &middot; **-ing form:** *daydreaming*
**Contrast:** Built on *dream*, so it takes the same two pasts — but the split is even sharper here. American writing uses *daydreamed* almost without exception; *daydreamt*, which the spreadsheet lists, reads as British or deliberately old-fashioned. See [dream](#dream).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; it names an ongoing mental activity and takes the progressive freely: *he was daydreaming through the lecture*.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** — no object at all; what is imagined goes into a prepositional phrase with *about* or *of*: *I daydream about a week with no phone*, ✗ *I daydream a week with no phone*.
**Passive:** **no** — there is no object to promote: ✗ *a bakery was daydreamed about for years* is not how this is said. Only the awkward prepositional passive is even conceivable, and Americans avoid it.

To let your mind wander to pleasant imaginary things while you are awake.

**1. Drift off, stop paying attention.** No object: the point is the lapse of attention rather than what you imagine. American English uses *daydreamed*; *daydreamt* reads as British. (витать в облаках, отвлекаться)

1. I *daydream* through most of the Monday status call.
2. He *daydreamed* his way through an hour of the lecture.
3. My son *has daydreamed* through two years of algebra.

**2. Daydream about (imagine what you want).** With *about* or *of*. What follows is a wish rather than a plan, which is what separates it from *plan* or *intend*. (мечтать, грезить о чём-либо)

4. I *daydream* about a week with no phone.
5. She *daydreamed* about quitting for the whole drive home.
6. They *have daydreamed* about opening a bakery for years.

**See also:** [dream](#dream), [sleep](#sleep)

### deal

**Pronunciation:** /dil/ &middot; *DEEL*
**Русский:** иметь дело
**Forms:** *deal* /dil/ &middot; *dealt* /dɛlt/ &middot; *dealt* /dɛlt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — *deal with it* and *deal the cards* are both plain speech
**Pattern:** ea &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *deals* &middot; **-ing form:** *dealing*
**Contrast:** The spelling keeps the ea but the vowel shortens: *dealt* rhymes with *belt*, not with *sealed*. ✗ *dealed* is not a form, and ✗ *delt* is a common misspelling.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all senses. The *be about* use (*chapter four deals with the war years*) is closer to a state but still takes the ordinary tenses, not the progressive: ✗ *chapter four is dealing with the war years*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive and **ditransitive** in *deal me in*, *deal him four bad hands*, *dealt the plaintiffs a blow*; prepositional-intransitive in *deal with* and *deal in*; bare intransitive in the drug sense (*he got caught dealing*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *the cards were dealt*; *a blow was dealt to the plaintiffs*. The prepositional passive is natural too: *the complaint has already been dealt with*.

To handle or cope with something, to hand out playing cards, or to trade in goods.

**1. Deal with (handle, cope with).** The everyday sense, always with *with*. The object is a problem, a complaint, or a difficult person. (справляться, иметь дело с)

1. I *deal with* vendor complaints all morning.
2. He *dealt with* the same landlord for six years.
3. The agency *has dealt with* worse crises than this one.

**2. Hand out playing cards.** The card-table sense, and the source of the noun *dealer* and of *a raw deal*. (сдавать карты)

4. *Deal* me in.
5. She *dealt* the cards and we started playing.
6. The house *has dealt* him four bad hands in a row.

**3. Deal in (trade in, sell).** Of a business and the goods it handles. Also figurative: a paper that *deals in* rumor. (торговать чем-либо)

7. The shop *deals in* rare maps.
8. His family *dealt in* grain for a century.
9. They *have dealt in* used equipment since the eighties.

**4. Sell illegal drugs.** Informal, and usually with no object at all: *he got caught dealing*. (торговать наркотиками)

10. You do not *deal* on that corner without permission.
11. Two of them *dealt* out of the same apartment.
12. She *has* never *dealt* anything harder than weed.

**5. Deal a blow, deal out.** Somewhat formal. The object is a blow, damage, justice, or the hand that fate gives you. (нанести удар; раздавать, воздавать)

13. Judges *deal out* the same sentence for both offenses.
14. The ruling *dealt* a blow to the plaintiffs.
15. Life *has dealt* him a bad hand this year.

**6. Deal with (be about).** Of books, chapters, reports, and laws: what the text covers. (быть посвящённым, рассматривать тему)

16. Chapter four *deals with* the war years.
17. The report *dealt with* only one of the three sites.
18. Nobody *has dealt with* the legal side in print.

### dream

**Pronunciation:** /drim/ &middot; *DREEM*
**Русский:** видеть сны; мечтать
**Forms:** *dream* /drim/ &middot; *dreamed* /drimd/ (also *dreamt* /drɛmt/) &middot; *dreamed* /drimd/ (also *dreamt* /drɛmt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — used constantly in speech and writing alike
**Pattern:** regular -ed in American English (British ea &rarr; e + t) &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *dreams* &middot; **-ing form:** *dreaming*
**Contrast:** Both forms are correct, but they split by country: *dreamed* is the ordinary American past and participle, while *dreamt* /drɛmt/ is the British default and sounds poetic in American prose. The same split gives British *leant*, *learnt*, *spelt*, and *burnt* where Americans write *leaned*, *learned*, *spelled*, and *burned*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; it names an activity or an experience over time and takes the progressive: *she was dreaming about the house again*. No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — normally intransitive with *about* or *of* (*I dream of a year with no travel*), transitive with a content object (*he has dreamed the same thing three nights running*) and in *dream up a slogan*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural only for *dream up*: *the whole plan was dreamed up over one dinner*. The ordinary senses have no passive: ✗ *her grandmother's house was dreamed about*.

To see images and stories while asleep, or to imagine something you badly want.

**1. See images while asleep.** With *about* or *of*. English makes the sleeper the subject, so Russian *мне снилось* becomes *I dreamed*. American English writes *dreamed*; *dreamt* is British and sounds poetic here. (видеть сон, сниться)

1. I rarely *dream* about work.
2. She *dreamed* about her grandmother's house again.
3. He *has dreamed* the same thing three nights running.

**2. Wish for, aspire to.** With *of* or *about*, and often with *since* or *always* to show how long the wish has lasted. (мечтать о)

4. We *dream* of a year with no travel.
5. She *dreamed* of medical school and got there.
6. He *has dreamed* of playing at Carnegie Hall since he was six.

**3. Imagine as possible.** Almost always negative: *never dreamed*, *wouldn't dream of it*. The second is also a polite refusal. (и представить себе (не мог))

7. I wouldn't *dream* of asking her to pay.
8. She never *dreamed* it would take four years.
9. Nobody *had dreamed* the vote would be that close.

**4. Dream up (invent).** Of a plan, a name, or an excuse, usually with a hint that it was invented too quickly. (выдумать, придумать)

10. Marketing *dreams up* a new slogan every quarter.
11. Who *dreamed* this policy *up*?
12. We *had dreamed up* the whole plan over one dinner.

**5. Dream on (informal dismissal).** A fixed imperative used to tell someone their hope is unrealistic. It appears in no other form. (мечтать не вредно, размечтался)

13. A corner office in year one? *Dream on*.
14. He thinks they'll double the offer. *Dream on*.

### feel

**Pronunciation:** /fil/ &middot; *FEEL*
**Русский:** чувствовать
**Forms:** *feel* /fil/ &middot; *felt* /fɛlt/ &middot; *felt* /fɛlt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — among the most frequent verbs in the language
**Pattern:** ee &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *feels* &middot; **-ing form:** *feeling*
**Contrast:** ✗ *feeled* does not exist. *Felt* is also an everyday noun — the fabric in a *felt* hat — spelled and pronounced the same; only the sentence tells them apart.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the emotion and opinion senses (*I feel better*, *I feel we should wait*) — the opinion sense refuses the progressive: ✗ *I am feeling we should wait*. **Linking (copular)** in *the room feels cold*, *the meeting felt rehearsed*. **Action** only in the deliberate senses: *feel the fabric*, *feel them out*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the linking use (*this draft has felt wrong*), transitive in *felt the fabric*, *felt the tremor*, and with a *that*-clause in the opinion sense. *Feel like* takes a noun or an -ing form, never an infinitive: ✗ *feel like to leave*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — fine where the act is deliberate or the sensation is shared: *the swelling was felt and a scan ordered*; *the tremor was felt across three counties*; *the committee has already been felt out*. No passive of the linking use (✗ *cold is felt by the room*) or of the opinion sense (✗ *the price was felt too high by her*).

To experience an emotion or a sensation, to touch something to learn about it, or to hold an impression or an opinion.

**1. Experience an emotion or state.** Followed by an adjective, not an adverb: *I feel bad*, not the adverb form. (чувствовать себя, испытывать)

1. I *feel* better after a walk.
2. They *felt* ignored by management for months.
3. He *has felt* dizzy since the medication changed.

**2. Touch to examine.** Deliberate touching to find out about texture, temperature, or swelling. (щупать, трогать)

4. *Feel* how heavy this is.
5. She *felt* the fabric and put it back on the shelf.
6. The doctor *has felt* the swelling and ordered a scan.

**3. Perceive by sensation.** Here the stimulus comes from outside you: wind, a bump, a tremor, pain. (ощущать, почувствовать)

7. Can you *feel* the draft from that window?
8. We *felt* the tremor an hour before the news reported it.
9. I *have* not *felt* my toes since mile eight.

**4. Seem, give an impression.** The subject is a thing or a place rather than a person, and it takes an adjective: *the room feels cold*. (казаться, ощущаться)

10. The room *feels* colder than the thermostat says.
11. The whole meeting *felt* rehearsed.
12. This draft *has felt* wrong from page one.

**5. Hold an opinion.** Softer than *think*: a judgment offered as personal rather than argued. (считать, полагать)

13. I *feel* we should wait a week.
14. She *felt* the price was too high.
15. The board *has felt* that way since the audit.

**6. Feel like (want, be in the mood for).** Followed by a noun or an -ing form, never an infinitive: *feel like leaving*, not *feel like to leave*. (хотеться)

16. I *feel like* pizza.
17. Nobody *felt like* driving another three hours.
18. She *has* not *felt like* going out since the move.

**7. Feel for (sympathize with).** Fellow feeling for someone in trouble. Do not confuse it with *feel up*, which is something else entirely. (сочувствовать)

19. I *feel for* anyone flying today.
20. We all *felt for* him after the news.
21. She *has felt for* those families from the start.

**8. Feel up to (be well enough for).** Mostly in questions and negatives, about energy or health rather than ability. (быть в состоянии, чувствовать в себе силы)

22. Do you *feel up to* a walk?
23. He didn't *feel up to* the drive.
24. She *has* not *felt up to* company all week.

**9. Feel out (probe cautiously).** To test someone's position quietly before committing to a proposal. (прощупать почву)

25. *Feel* them *out* before you name a number.
26. We *felt out* two other vendors.
27. He *has* already *felt out* the committee.

### keep

**Pronunciation:** /kip/ &middot; *KEEP*
**Русский:** держать; хранить
**Forms:** *keep* /kip/ &middot; *kept* /kɛpt/ &middot; *kept* /kɛpt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ee &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *keeps* &middot; **-ing form:** *keeping*
**Contrast:** ✗ *keeped* is not a form — the long ee shortens to *kept* /kɛpt/, rhyming with *slept* and *crept*. What follows *keep* is an -ing form, never an infinitive: *keep trying*, ✗ *keep to try*. The noun *keep* (*earn your keep*, *the castle keep*) is spelled the same and has no past at all.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, but several senses describe maintaining a state rather than a single act (*keep the shop warm*, *keep asking*). The resultative pattern *keep the door open* is complex-transitive, not linking: the adjective describes the object, not the subject.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in most senses, and complex-transitive with an added adjective, participle, or place (*keep them waiting*, *keep the keys in the drawer*). Intransitive in the freshness sense: *hard cheese keeps for months*. *Keep* + -ing only: *keep trying*, ✗ *keep to try*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *every letter she sent him was kept*; *records have been kept since 1830*; *we were kept awake by the noise*. No passive of the intransitive sense: ✗ *for months is kept by hard cheese*.

To go on having something, to store it somewhere, or to make someone or something stay in a state.

**1. Continue to have, retain.** The opposite of giving something back or throwing it away. (оставлять себе, сохранять)

1. *Keep* the change.
2. He *kept* every letter she sent him.
3. They *have kept* the same phone number since 1998.

**2. Store in a place.** Where a thing lives when it is not in use; usually with a place phrase. (хранить, держать)

4. We *keep* the extra keys in the kitchen drawer.
5. She *kept* the deed in a safe-deposit box.
6. The store *has kept* my measurements on file.

**3. Cause to stay in a state.** Takes an object plus an adjective, a participle, or a place: *keep the door open*, *keep them waiting*. (держать, поддерживать в каком-либо состоянии)

7. *Keep* the door open.
8. The noise *kept* us awake.
9. That heater *has kept* the shop warm all winter.

**4. Keep doing (continue).** Always with an -ing form, never an infinitive: *keep trying*, not *keep to try*. *Keep on* is the emphatic version. (продолжать делать, всё время делать)

10. My landlord *keeps* raising the rent.
11. He *kept* apologizing until it got awkward.
12. She *has kept* asking the same question for a month.

**5. Observe, fulfill, hold to.** The object is a promise, an appointment, a secret, a schedule, or a law. (сдержать, соблюдать, хранить (тайну))

13. He *keeps* his promises.
14. She *kept* the news to herself all week.
15. They *have* not *kept* a single appointment this month.

**6. Look after, support, maintain.** Of animals, gardens, records, or a household you pay for. This is the sense behind *earn your keep*. (содержать, ухаживать за, вести (записи))

16. They *keep* chickens and two goats.
17. He *kept* a family of five on a machinist's wage.
18. The parish *has kept* records since 1830.

**7. Stay fresh, last.** No object, and mostly of food. Figuratively, news that *will keep* can wait until later. (храниться, не портиться)

19. Hard cheese *keeps* for months.
20. The soup *kept* fine in the freezer.
21. Whatever it is *has kept* this long, so it can wait until Monday.

**8. Keep up (maintain a pace or standard).** Either matching someone else's speed or continuing something at the same level. (успевать, поддерживать на прежнем уровне)

22. I can't *keep up* with the reading.
23. He *kept up* with the leaders for ten miles.
24. They *have kept up* the payments without a single miss.

**9. Keep from, keep off (prevent).** *Keep someone from* doing something is preventing it; *keep off* keeps a thing away. (удерживать, не давать, уберечь)

25. *Keep* him *from* signing anything today.
26. She *kept* herself *from* laughing.
27. The tarp *has kept* the rain *off* the woodpile.

**10. Keep to (stick to, stay within).** Of a schedule, a route, a budget, or a routine. *Keep to yourself* is a separate fixed phrase meaning to stay private. (придерживаться, держаться)

28. *Keep to* the schedule.
29. We *kept to* the main road.
30. He *has kept to* the same routine for twenty years.

To go down on your knees, or to stay in that position.

**1. Go down on your knees.** Both pasts are standard American English: *knelt* is usual in edited prose, *kneeled* in sports and news writing. The k is silent in every form. (опуститься на колени, встать на колени)

1. You have to *kneel* to see the bottom shelf.

4. The ushers ask the congregation to *kneel* at that point in the service.
5. The pilgrims *knelt* on the stone steps.

7. Athletes still *kneel* at some events.
8. Several players *kneeled* during the anthem.

### lean

**Pronunciation:** /lin/ &middot; *LEEN*
**Русский:** опираться; прислоняться
**Forms:** *lean* /lin/ &middot; *leaned* /lind/ (British *leant* /lɛnt/) &middot; *leaned* /lind/ (British *leant*)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in speech; only the British past *leant* is marked
**Pattern:** regular -ed in American English (British ea &rarr; e + t) &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *leans* &middot; **-ing form:** *leaning*
**Contrast:** *Leaned* is the American past and participle. *Leant* /lɛnt/, the form the spreadsheet lists, is British — and it is a homophone of *lent*, the past of *lend*, which is one good reason American writing leaves it alone.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the body senses (*he leaned over the counter*). Closer to **stative** where it reports a standing tilt or tendency: *the old barn leans two degrees east*, *the county leans Republican*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in most senses (*don't lean out that far*, *I leaned toward the cheaper option*), transitive when you prop something: *she had leaned her bike against the wrong tree*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — possible only in the propping sense: *the ladder was leaned against the wall*. No passive of the tendency or posture senses (✗ *toward the cheaper option was leaned*), though the prepositional passive works for pressure: *he was leaned on to sell*.

To bend or rest your body or an object at an angle, and by extension to tend toward a position or rely on someone.

**1. Bend the body from upright.** Movement of your own body toward or away from something. American English uses *leaned*; *leant* is British and a homophone of *lent*. (наклоняться)

1. Don't *lean* out that far.
2. He *leaned* over the counter to read the sign.
3. She *has leaned* into the aisle to see the screen all game.

**2. Rest against a support.** Of a person or an object propped at an angle; also of a structure that has tilted and stayed that way. (прислонять, опираться)

4. The old barn *leans* about two degrees to the east.
5. She *had leaned* her bike against the wrong tree.
6. The fence *has leaned* toward the road since the storm.

**3. Tend toward, be inclined.** With *toward*, or with *left* and *right* when the subject is a voter, a county, or a paper. (склоняться к)

7. The county *leans* Republican.
8. I *leaned* toward the cheaper option.
9. The committee *has leaned* that way from the start.

**4. Lean on (rely on, or pressure).** Two meanings held apart by context: depending on someone for support, or, informally, coercing them. (полагаться на; давить на, нажимать)

10. You can *lean on* me for the deposit.
11. His brother *leaned on* him to sell.
12. We *have leaned on* that one vendor too much this quarter.

**5. Lean in (commit, engage fully).** Business slang of the last decade: to embrace something instead of hedging or apologizing for it. (активно включиться, не отступать)

13. We should *lean in* rather than apologize.
14. The brand *leaned in* on the joke.
15. They *have leaned in* hard on video this year.

**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — at home in writing and in fixed phrases (*leap year*, *leap of faith*); plain speech says *jump*

**Third person:** *leaps* &middot; **-ing form:** *leaping*

To jump a long way or high, and by extension to rise sharply or to act suddenly.

2. The cat *leapt* from the counter to the top of the fridge.
3. He *had leapt* out of the way just in time.

4. Rents *leap* every time a new building opens.
5. The stock *leapt* again on no real news.
6. Sales *have leaped* thirty percent since March.

**3. Leap at (act eagerly).** Of an offer or a chance taken without hesitation. *Leap into* something carries a warning about haste. (ухватиться за, броситься)

7. Don't *leap* into a lease you can't break.
8. She *leaped at* the chance to transfer.
9. He *has leaped at* every opening they have posted.

**4. Leap to conclusions.** A fixed phrase, always a criticism: judging before the facts are in. (делать поспешные выводы)

10. She *leaps* to conclusions faster than anyone I know.
11. The press *leapt* to the obvious conclusion.
12. We *have leaped* to the wrong one twice already.

To go away from a place or a person, or to let something stay behind or stay in a condition.

**2. Quit for good.** Of a job, a school, a band, or a marriage, where the departure is permanent. (уйти с работы, бросить (кого-либо))

4. Half the staff will *leave* if that policy stands.
5. She *left* him in the spring.
6. He *has left* the company after eleven years.

**3. Let something stay behind.** Covers both deliberate leaving and simple forgetting; a message left for someone belongs here too. (оставить, забыть где-либо)

7. Don't *leave* your laptop in the car.
8. She *left* her badge on her desk.
9. I *have left* you three voicemails.

**4. Cause to remain in a state.** Takes an object plus an adjective or a participle: *leave the door open*, *left us speechless*. (оставить в каком-либо состоянии)

10. *Leave* the light on.
11. The news *left* everyone speechless.
12. The flood *has left* two blocks without power.

**5. Bequeath.** Of property passed on at death, usually with an indirect object: *left her the cabin*. (завещать, оставить в наследство)

13. People rarely *leave* anything to a second cousin.
14. Her aunt *left* her the cabin.
15. He *has left* everything to the university.

**6. Entrust, delegate.** *Leave it to me*, or *leave something up to* someone: handing over responsibility for a decision. (предоставить, поручить)

16. *Leave* it to me.

**7. Remain, be left over.** Normally the participle with *be*, and the standard way to state a remainder in arithmetic. (оставаться в остатке)

19. There are two cookies *left*.
20. Six from ten *leaves* four.
21. Nothing was *left* of the barn but the foundation.

**8. Leave out (omit).** Of information or people left off a list, whether by accident or on purpose. (пропустить, не включить)

22. *Leave* the middle section *out*.
23. You *left out* the best part.
24. They *have left* two names *out* of the credits.

25. *Leave* her *alone*.

### lose

**Pronunciation:** /luz/ &middot; *LOOZ*
**Русский:** терять
**Forms:** *lose* /luz/ &middot; *lost* /lɔst/ &middot; *lost* /lɔst/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — constant in speech, about games, keys, and jobs alike
**Pattern:** -ose &rarr; -ost &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *loses* &middot; **-ing form:** *losing* (drops the e)
**Contrast:** Three traps in one word. Spelling: *lose* (one o, a verb) is not *loose* (two o's, an adjective meaning not tight). Sound: *lose* ends in /z/, *loose* in /s/. And *lost* is both the past and a common adjective — *I am lost* is not a past tense at all.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, though most senses report a change of state the subject did not choose (*he lost his job*, *she lost her father*). *Lost* in *I am lost* is an adjective, not a tense.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in nearly every sense (*lost her keys*, *lost his temper*), intransitive in the competition sense: *that team loses whenever it rains*.

To stop having something, to be unable to find it, or to fail to win.

**1. Misplace, be unable to find.** Spelling trap: *lose* has one o and ends in /z/; *loose* is the adjective meaning not tight. (терять, потерять)

1. I *lose* my glasses about twice a day.
2. She *lost* her keys somewhere between the car and the door.
3. He *had lost* the receipt, so the store refused the return.

**2. Fail to win.** Of games, elections, bets, and court cases. You lose *to* an opponent and lose *by* a margin. (проигрывать)

4. That team *loses* whenever it rains.
5. We *lost* the game in overtime.
6. They *have lost* four straight.

**3. Cease to have.** Of a job, weight, hair, a signal, or a reputation: something you had that is now gone. (лишиться, утратить)

7. You *lose* the signal in that tunnel.
8. He *lost* his job in the second round of cuts.
9. She *has lost* twenty pounds since January.

**4. Be bereaved of.** The ordinary English way of saying someone died: *she lost her father*. Plain, not euphemistic. (потерять близкого человека)

10. Nobody wants to *lose* a parent that young.
11. She *lost* her father last spring.
12. The town *has lost* three people to that road.

**5. Fail to keep control of.** With *control*, *your temper*, *track*, *patience*, *your nerve*: things that slip away rather than get misplaced. (потерять контроль, самообладание)

13. Try not to *lose* your nerve at the last minute.
14. He *lost* his temper in the meeting.
15. I *have lost* track of how many times we have filed this.

19. *Lose* the jacket; it's ninety degrees.

### may

**Pronunciation:** /meɪ/ &middot; *MAY*
**Русский:** мочь; иметь возможность
**Forms:** *may* /meɪ/ &middot; *might* /maɪt/ &middot; *might* /maɪt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — a core modal, though *might* now carries most of its weight in speech
**Pattern:** ay &rarr; ight &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** no -s form — ✗ *he mays* &middot; **-ing form:** no -ing form — ✗ *maying*
**Contrast:** *May* is a modal, so it breaks nearly every rule in this file: no -s, no -ing, no *to* after it, and no real participle — ✗ *I have might*. *Might* is the historical past and still works that way in reported speech (*She said we might need a second interview*), but in modern American English it mostly stands on its own as a weaker present-tense possibility: *She may come* (a real chance) versus *She might come* (less likely). To talk about a past possibility, use *may have* or *might have* plus a participle.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — never a main verb. It has no -s form (✗ *he mays*), no -ing form (✗ *maying*), and no participle (✗ *I have might*); *might* is its past, used for backshift (*she said we might need a second interview*) and as a weaker present possibility.
**Transitivity:** Neither — a modal takes no object. It is followed by a bare infinitive, never by *to*: *you may leave*, ✗ *you may to leave*. Past possibility uses *may have* / *might have* plus a participle.
**Passive:** **no** — a modal has no passive of its own: ✗ *permission was mayed*. It combines freely with a passive infinitive, which is a different thing: *the form may be signed once it is witnessed*.

A modal verb expressing possibility or permission; *might* is its past form and also a weaker present possibility.

**1. Express possibility.** *May* states a real chance, *might* a slighter one. No -s, no -ing, and no *to* after either. (может быть, возможно)

1. The delay *may* push the launch to April.
2. She *might* come, but I doubt it.
3. The road *might* be closed by then.

**2. May have, might have (past possibility).** To place a possibility in the past, use *may have* or *might have* plus a participle. There is no participle of *may* itself, so *have might* does not exist. (возможно, сделал что-либо)

4. He *might have* taken the earlier flight.
5. The package *may have* gone to the old address.
6. We *may have* met at a conference years ago.

**3. Grant or ask permission.** Formal register. In everyday American speech people use *can*; *may* survives in rules, notices, and polite questions. (можно, разрешается)

7. You *may* leave once the form is signed.
8. *May* I ask who's calling?
9. Students *may* not retake the exam.

**4. Might in reported speech.** This is where *might* still works as a true past: it is the backshifted form of *may* after a past reporting verb. (мог, можно было (в косвенной речи))

10. She said we *might* need a second interview.
11. He told the class they *might* use their notes.
12. The letter said applicants *may* appeal within thirty days.

**5. Might as a tentative suggestion.** A softened recommendation, usually *you might want to* or *might as well*. Only the *might* form works this way. (пожалуй, стоило бы)

13. You *might* want to call ahead.
14. We *might* as well start without him.
15. She *might* try the other entrance.

**6. Concede a point.** Formal. *May* grants something before the sentence turns on it, normally with *but*. (пусть и, хотя и)

16. It *may* be legal, but it is a terrible idea.
17. He *may* have the votes; he does not have the money.
18. The design *may* look odd at first, and then it makes sense.

**7. Formal wish.** Ceremonial and literary, with *may* at the front of the clause and no subject inversion elsewhere. (пусть, да будет)

19. *May* you both be very happy.
20. *May* it please the court.

### mean

**Pronunciation:** /min/ &middot; *MEEN*
**Русский:** означать; иметь в виду
**Forms:** *mean* /min/ &middot; *meant* /mɛnt/ &middot; *meant* /mɛnt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — used constantly, from *what do you mean* to *I meant to call*
**Pattern:** ea &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *means* &middot; **-ing form:** *meaning*
**Contrast:** *Meant* is spelled with ea but rhymes with *sent* — /mɛnt/, not ✗ /mint/ — which is why learners write ✗ *ment*. ✗ *meaned* is not a form.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the core senses — signify (*that symbol means the same thing*), entail (*a second round means another two weeks*), and matter (*your opinion means more to him*). All refuse the progressive: ✗ *that symbol is meaning*. **Action** only in the intention sense: *I meant to call you back*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object may be a noun (*meant nothing to her*), a *that*-clause (*what I mean is that we can't ship*), or an infinitive (*I don't mean to interrupt*). The object is obligatory: ✗ *the red light meant* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **no** in the stative senses — this is one learners get wrong: ✗ *the same thing is meant by that symbol*, ✗ *a great deal has been meant to my family by that*. To have a particular sense, to intend something, or to matter to someone.

**1. Signify, denote.** What a word, a sign, or a gesture stands for. The subject is the thing that carries the sense. (означать, значить)

1. What does this abbreviation *mean*?
2. The red light *meant* the door was locked.
3. That symbol *has meant* the same thing since the 1950s.

**2. Intend to do.** Followed by an infinitive. *I meant to* usually introduces an apology for not doing it. (намереваться, собираться)

4. I don't *mean* to interrupt.
5. I *meant* to call you back yesterday.
6. We *have meant* to repaint this room for years.

**3. Intend a particular sense.** About the speaker's intent rather than the words: what you were trying to say or to do by saying it. (иметь в виду)

7. What I *mean* is that we can't ship on Friday.
8. She *had meant* it as a compliment.
9. He *has* never *meant* anything by it.

**4. Entail, result in.** The subject is an event or a decision and the object is its consequence. (означать (последствие), влечь за собой)

10. A second round *means* another two weeks.
11. The merger *meant* layoffs in both offices.
12. That change *has meant* rewriting half the tests.

**5. Matter, have value to someone.** Always with *to* someone, and usually with a quantity: *means a lot*, *means nothing*. (значить, быть важным для кого-либо)

13. Your opinion *means* more to him than you think.
14. The award *meant* nothing to her.
15. That *has meant* a great deal to my family.

**6. Be meant for, be meant to.** Passive only. *Be meant for* is purpose or destiny; *be meant to* states what the rules or instructions require. (быть предназначенным, полагаться)

16. This chair was *meant* for the hallway.
17. You are *meant* to sign both copies.
18. They were *meant* for each other.

**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in *swept the broken glass into a dustpan*, *swept the series*, *swept the room for bugs*; intransitive with a path phrase in the movement senses: *she sweeps into a room*, *a cold front sweeps down the valley*.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the room was swept for bugs*; *the complaint got swept under the rug*; *the shop floor has not been swept in a week*. No passive of the movement sense: ✗ *the canyon was swept through by the fire*.

To clean a floor with a broom, or to move something (or move yourself) in a broad, forceful arc.

**1. Clean with a broom.** The literal sense. Americans *sweep up* a room; the verb *upsweep* is not used for cleaning. (мести, подметать)

1. I *sweep* the porch every Saturday.
2. He *swept* the broken glass into a dustpan.
3. Nobody *has swept* the shop floor in a week.

**2. Push or clear away in one motion.** One broad arm movement that clears a surface, with no broom involved. (смахнуть, смести)

4. *Sweep* the crumbs onto a plate.
5. She *swept* the papers off the desk into a box.
6. Wind *had swept* the leaves into one corner of the yard.

**3. Move powerfully across a place.** Of fire, storms, disease, rumor, or a political movement covering everything in its path. (пронестись, охватить)

7. A cold front *sweeps* down the valley every March.
8. The fire *swept* through the canyon in an afternoon.
9. Rumors *have swept* the office all week.

**4. Move with grand, unhurried motion.** Of a person entering or leaving a room. Slightly literary and often ironic. (величественно двигаться, шествовать)

10. She *sweeps* into a room like she owns it.
11. He *swept* past the receptionist without a word.
12. They *have swept* out of two meetings this month.

**5. Win everything.** American sports and awards usage: take every game in a series or every category on the night. (выиграть всухую, взять все награды)

13. No one expected them to *sweep* the series.
14. Their film *swept* the technical categories.
15. The team *has swept* all four games.

**6. Search or scan systematically.** Of security teams, searchlights, and radar: covering an area in a steady pattern. (прочесать, обследовать)

16. Searchlights *sweep* the fence line every few minutes.
17. Security *swept* the room for bugs.
18. Crews *have swept* both floors twice.

**7. Sweep under the rug, sweep aside.** Fixed phrases for hiding something embarrassing or brushing off an objection. (замять; отмахнуться)

19. He *sweeps aside* every objection.
20. The complaint got *swept* under the rug.
21. They *have swept* two investigations under the rug.

### teach

**Pronunciation:** /titʃ/ &middot; *TEECH*
**Русский:** учить, обучать, преподавать
**Forms:** *teach* /titʃ/ &middot; *taught* /tɔt/ &middot; *taught* /tɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary verb for what a teacher does
**Pattern:** -each &rarr; -aught &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *teaches* (adds -es) &middot; **-ing form:** *teaching*
**Contrast:** *Taught* and *taut* ("pulled tight") are homophones — /tɔt/ — and *taught* is also one letter from *thought*. English also splits what Russian *учить* covers with one verb: the teacher *teaches*, the student *learns*, so ✗ *She learned me algebra* is wrong for *She taught me algebra*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the subject can be a person or an experience (*that experience has taught me to read the contract first*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive**, and also **both** — two objects in *she taught me algebra*, one in *she taught fourth grade*, none at all in *I teach*. English splits the roles Russian *учить* covers: the teacher teaches, the student learns, so ✗ *she learned me algebra*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and either object can become the subject — *I was taught algebra in one weekend*; *algebra is taught in the fall*; *the department has been taught to file on time*.

To give someone knowledge or a skill.

**1. Instruct someone in something.** English splits what Russian *учить* covers with one verb: the teacher *teaches* and the student *learns*, so *she learned me algebra* is wrong. (учить, обучать кого-либо)

1. Can you *teach* a beginner in a month?
2. She *taught* me algebra in one weekend.
3. He *has taught* himself three languages.

**2. Work as a teacher.** With a subject, a grade, or a place, and often with no object at all: *she teaches*. (преподавать)

4. I *teach* two sections of writing in the fall.
5. She *taught* fourth grade for thirty years.
6. They *had taught* the course together before the merger.

**3. Cause someone to learn from experience.** The subject is an event or an experience rather than a person. (научить (об опыте, событии))

7. One bad winter *teaches* you to keep chains in the trunk.
8. The recall *taught* the company nothing.
9. That experience *has taught* me to read the contract first.

**4. Teach someone a lesson.** Informal and punitive: a threat or a boast about punishment, not real instruction. (проучить кого-либо)

10. That fine will *teach* him a lesson.
11. She *taught* them a lesson they still talk about.
12. Two audits *have taught* the department to file on time.

### think

**Pronunciation:** /θɪŋk/ &middot; *THINK*
**Русский:** думать
**Forms:** *think* /θɪŋk/ &middot; *thought* /θɔt/ &middot; *thought* /θɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — among the most frequent verbs in English
**Pattern:** -ink &rarr; -ought &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *thinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *thinking*
**Contrast:** Other -ink verbs change the vowel — *drink/drank/drunk*, *sink/sank/sunk* — but *think* goes to *-ought* instead: ✗ *thank* is a different word entirely, and ✗ *thunk* is a joke. *Thought* is also a common noun (*a passing thought*).
**Verb type:** **stative** in the belief sense (*I think we should wait*), where the progressive is out: ✗ *I am thinking he's wrong*. **Action** in the reasoning sense, where it is normal: *let me think*, *I was thinking of leaving early*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the reasoning sense (*he thought for a long moment*), transitive with a *that*-clause (*I think we should wait*) or a concrete object (*think it over*, *thought up three names*), and prepositional in *think about* / *think of*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural with a concrete object: *this has been thought through more carefully than we did it*; *three names were thought up and all rejected*. The belief sense passivizes only impersonally (*it is thought that the two are related*) — never personally: ✗ *the meeting was thought on Thursday by her*.

To use your mind to consider something, or to hold an opinion about it.

**1. Hold an opinion, believe.** The commonest use, normally with a *that* clause. In the negative, English moves the *not* onto *think*: *I don't think he's coming*. (думать, считать, полагать)

1. I *think* we should wait a week.
2. She *thought* the meeting was on Thursday.
3. We *had thought* the issue was fixed.

**2. Use the mind, reason.** No object: the activity itself. This is the sense in *let me think* and *think straight*. (думать, мыслить, соображать)

4. Let me *think*.
5. He *thought* for a long moment before answering.
6. I *have* not *thought* clearly since four this morning.

**3. Think about (consider).** Turning something over in your mind, often over a long period. (думать о, обдумывать)

7. *Think about* what you're asking her to do.
8. We *thought about* moving to Denver.
9. He *has thought about* applying for years.

**4. Think of (recall or come up with).** Retrieving a name or producing an idea on the spot. Often negative: *I can't think of his name*. (вспомнить; придумать)

10. I can't *think of* her last name.
11. She *thought of* three objections right away.
12. Nobody *has thought of* a better title.

**5. Intend, be considering.** Usually in the progressive, *be thinking of* plus an -ing form, which makes a plan sound tentative. (подумывать, собираться)

13. I was *thinking* of leaving early.
14. We *think* we'll drive instead of flying.
15. They *had thought* they would be home by now.

**6. Think through, think over.** *Think through* means follow the consequences all the way; *think over* means take time before answering. (обдумать до конца, взвесить)

16. *Think* it *over* and call me Monday.
17. She *thought* the whole plan through in one night.
18. They *have thought* this through more carefully than we have.

**7. Think up (invent).** Of a name, an excuse, or a scheme, with a hint that it was made up quickly. (выдумать, придумать)

19. He *thinks up* a new excuse every week.
20. Who *thought up* this schedule?
21. They *have thought up* three names and hate all of them.

**8. Think of as, think highly of.** How you rate someone or what category you put them in. (относиться как к, быть высокого мнения)

22. Her professors *think* highly of her.
23. We *thought of* him as a friend.
24. The industry *has* always *thought of* the company as a follower.

**9. Think twice, think better of.** Fixed phrases: *think twice* warns before the act, *think better of it* reports a change of mind after it. (подумать дважды; передумать)

25. *Think twice* before you sign that.
26. He *thought better of* it and stayed home.
27. She *has* never *thought twice* about telling the truth.


**Phrasal verbs:** [think about, think of](22-verb-preposition.md#think-about-think-of) &middot; [think back](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-back) &middot; [think of](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-of) &middot; [think out](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-out) &middot; [think over](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-over) &middot; [think through](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-through) &middot; [think up](21-phrasal-t-w.md#think-up)

**See also:** [rethink](#rethink), [outthink](#outthink), [seek](#seek), [mean](#mean)

### upsweep

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌpˈswip/ &middot; *up-SWEEP*
**Русский:** подметать, прибирать(комнату)
**Forms:** *upsweep* /ˌʌpˈswip/ &middot; *upswept* /ˌʌpˈswɛpt/ &middot; *upswept* /ˌʌpˈswɛpt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — met almost only as the adjective *upswept*; for cleaning, Americans say *sweep up*
**Pattern:** ee &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *upsweeps* &middot; **-ing form:** *upsweeping*
**Contrast:** Rare as a verb, and it does not mean cleaning a room. For that, Americans say *sweep up*: *I swept up the kitchen*. You will meet this word almost only as the participle used as an adjective — *upswept hair*, *upswept wingtips* — or as the noun /ˈʌpswip/ for a hairstyle. It follows [sweep](#sweep).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, but rare as a finite verb; you meet it mainly as the participle *upswept* used as an adjective (*upswept hair*, *upswept wingtips*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, object obligatory — *the stylist can upsweep your hair*, *the designers have upswept the tail fins*. It never means cleaning a room: Americans say *sweep up*, ✗ *upsweep the kitchen*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the tail fins have been upswept on the new model*; *her hair was upswept and pinned*. In practice the same participle is far more often a plain adjective than a real passive.

To sweep or curve something upward; you will meet it mostly as the participle *upswept*.

**1. Arrange hair upward.** Rare as a verb; the adjective *upswept* and the noun *an upsweep* are far more common. For cleaning a room Americans say *sweep up*, never *upsweep*. (зачесать наверх, сделать высокую причёску)

1. The stylist can *upsweep* your hair for the ceremony.
2. She *upswept* her hair and pinned it with a tortoiseshell comb.
3. By seven she *had upswept* every loose strand.

**2. Curve upward.** Design and engineering vocabulary: *upswept wingtips*, *upswept tail fins*, an *upswept* shoulder line. (изгибать вверх, поднимать кверху)

4. That silhouette *upsweeps* sharply at the shoulder.
5. The designers *have upswept* the tail fins on the new model.
6. Wind *has upswept* the snow into a ridge along the roofline.

### weep

**Pronunciation:** /wip/ &middot; *WEEP*
**Русский:** плакать
**Forms:** *weep* /wip/ &middot; *wept* /wɛpt/ &middot; *wept* /wɛpt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — literary and journalistic; in ordinary speech Americans *cry*
**Pattern:** ee &rarr; e + t &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *weeps* &middot; **-ing form:** *weeping*
**Contrast:** Literary — in everyday American English people *cry*. The same verb also describes something that leaks slowly, with the same past: *the pipe wept all night*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and literary in American English, where ordinary speech says *cry*. The leaking sense (*the old radiator weeps*) is equally dynamic.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense — no object: ✗ *she wept the letter*. What is mourned takes *for* or *over*: *nobody is going to weep for a bank*; *historians have wept over the loss of that archive*.
**Passive:** **no** — nothing to promote: ✗ *the letter was wept by her*. The prepositional passive exists but is literary and rare: *a loss much wept over*.

To cry, especially quietly and from deep feeling; literary in American English.

**1. Cry from deep feeling.** Literary. In everyday American English people *cry*; *weep* raises the register or the seriousness. (плакать, рыдать)

1. Some people *weep* at weddings and some at airports.
2. She *wept* when she read the letter.
3. He *has wept* only twice in his adult life.

**2. Ooze liquid slowly.** Of a wound, a pipe, a joint, or a valve losing liquid a drop at a time. Same irregular past. (сочиться, подтекать)

4. The old radiator *weeps* a little onto the floor.
5. The cut *wept* for two days.
6. That valve *has wept* since the day they installed it.

**3. Weep for, weep over (mourn).** Formal, with *for* or *over*, and often ironic in the negative: nobody will weep for a bank. (оплакивать, скорбеть о)

7. Nobody is going to *weep for* a bank.
8. The whole family *wept* through the service.
9. Historians *have wept over* the loss of that archive for two centuries.

### wreak

**Pronunciation:** /rik/ &middot; *REEK*
**Русский:** причинять (вред, ущерб)
**Forms:** *wreak* /rik/ &middot; *wreaked* /rikt/ &middot; *wreaked* /rikt/ (archaic *wrought* /rɔt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — outside the fixed phrase *wreak havoc* it barely appears
**Pattern:** regular -ed in modern American English &middot; **Group:** 5
**Third person:** *wreaks* &middot; **-ing form:** *wreaking*
**Contrast:** The trap here is the spreadsheet's own form. *Wrought* is historically the past of *work*, not of *wreak*, and it survives only in fixed phrases — *wrought iron*, *finely wrought*, *what hath God wrought*. Modern American English writes *wreaked havoc*, not ✗ *wrought havoc*. The w is silent and the vowel is long: *wreak* is /rik/ like *reek*, ✗ not /rɛk/ like *wreck*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and in modern American English almost frozen inside *wreak havoc*. Note that the past is *wreaked*, not ✗ *wrought*, which historically belongs to *work*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, object obligatory and nearly fixed — *havoc*, *damage*, *vengeance*, *revenge*: *a late frost can wreak havoc on the orchards*, ✗ *a late frost can wreak*. The target follows with *on* or *upon*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — *havoc was wreaked along sixty miles of coast* is grammatical but stiff; the idiom strongly prefers the active. *Vengeance was wreaked upon the city* survives only in the literary register.

To cause a great deal of damage or trouble; in practice almost always *wreak havoc*.

**1. Wreak havoc, wreak damage.** The modern past is *wreaked*. *Wrought* is historically the past of *work*, not of *wreak*, so American English writes *wreaked havoc*. (сеять хаос, причинять разрушения)

1. A late frost can *wreak* havoc on the orchards.
2. The hurricane *wreaked* damage along sixty miles of coast.
3. Budget cuts *have wreaked* havoc on the department.

**2. Wreak vengeance.** Formal and literary. The object is revenge, vengeance, or someone's anger, and it falls *on* or *upon* a target. (вершить месть, обрушить гнев)

4. He *wreaked* his revenge slowly and in public.
5. The gods *had wreaked* their anger on the city.
6. No rival *has wreaked* vengeance that publicly in years.
