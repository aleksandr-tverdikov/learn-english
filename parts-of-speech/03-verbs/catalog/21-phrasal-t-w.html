Phrasal verbs: take — write#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs take, teach, tear… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle whose meaning you cannot reach by adding the two halves together: take after has nothing to do with following, tell off has nothing to do with counting, and write off has nothing to do with writing. The second thing to know about each one is separability — whether the object may sit between the verb and the particle. Some let it go either way (take your coat off or take off your coat), some force it inside as soon as it is a pronoun (take it off, never ✗ take off it), and prepositional and three-part verbs never let it in at all (take after him, never ✗ take him after). Every entry below spells out which, because that one line is the difference between sounding native and sounding wrong.

take aback#

Pronunciation
/ˌteɪk əˈbæk/ · take-uh-BAK · the particle carries the stress — took me aBACK
Русский
ошеломить, застать врасплох
Base verb
take
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes — and the object almost always sits inside the phrase: her answer took me aback. With a pronoun it must split: ✗ took aback me. A full noun after the particle (took aback the whole room) is possible but rare.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object — and usually in the passive, be taken aback
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic, but a momentary event of surprising someone, so the progressive is odd: ✗ her answer is taking me aback. It has no linking or stative use.
Passive
yes, and the passive is the usual form — I was taken aback by how blunt her answer was; she looked taken aback. The active her answer took me aback is the marked one.

Surprise someone so completely that they are briefly lost for words.

  1. I was taken aback by how blunt her answer was.
  2. His question took me aback, and I just stared at him.
  3. She looked taken aback when I offered to pay for everything.
  4. Don't be taken aback if he turns the offer down.
  5. The size of the repair bill took us aback.
  6. Everyone was taken aback by how quickly he agreed.

take
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — the object always follows the whole phrase: takes after her mother, takes after her. ✗ takes her mother after, ✗ takes her after.
Transitive

Register

Verb type
stative — a resemblance, like resemble itself, not anything the subject does. It rejects the progressive: ✗ she is taking after her mother.

Resemble an older relative in looks, character, or habits. It runs one way only: a child takes after a parent, never the other way around.

  1. She takes after her mother — same laugh, same temper.
  2. He takes after his grandfather more than anyone else in the family.
  3. Both boys take after their dad.
  4. I don't take after either of my parents.
  5. If she takes after him, she'll be tall.
  6. People say the youngest one took after his uncle.

Separate something into its pieces — and, figuratively, do the same to an opponent or an argument.

1. Dismantle. Undo something into the parts it was built from. The opposite is put back together, not take together. (разбирать на части)

  1. He took the carburetor apart on the kitchen table.
  2. She can take apart a laptop in about four minutes.
  3. Don't take it apart unless you know how it goes back together.

  1. Our defense took them apart in the second half.
  2. The visiting team took us apart, 42 to 3.

3. Pull an argument to pieces. Analyze something hostile line by line until nothing is left standing. (раскритиковать, разобрать по косточкам)

  1. The reviewer took the argument apart point by point.
  2. My advisor took apart the whole third chapter.

take away#

Pronunciation
/ˌteɪk əˈweɪ/ · take-uh-WAY · the particle carries the stress, not the verb
Русский
убирать, забирать; отнимать; вычитать
Base verb
take
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes — take away his phone or take his phone away, and a pronoun must split the phrase: take it away, never ✗ take away it. The three-part take away from is a different verb and never splits — nothing may come between away and from: the noise takes away from the meal, ✗ the noise takes the meal away from.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in the remove sense (they're taking his license away). The arithmetic sense is a frozen classroom formula that does not inflect — six take away two is four, ✗ six is taking away two — and take away from (lessen) is closer to stative.
Passive
restricted — full passive in the remove sense: his license was taken away for six months, our plates were taken away. None in the other senses: ✗ two is taken away from six, ✗ the food is taken away from by the loud music.

Remove something or someone; in arithmetic, subtract; and with from, reduce the effect of something.

1. Remove, confiscate, carry off. The everyday sense. The American noun for restaurant food eaten elsewhere is takeout; takeaway in that meaning is British. (забирать, уносить, отнимать)

  1. They took his license away for six months.
  2. The waiter took our plates away before we finished.
  3. Take it away — I can't look at it anymore.

2. Subtract. Classroom arithmetic. Outside school an American says minus or subtract. (вычитать)

  1. Six take away two is four.
  2. If you take away the tax, it's about forty dollars.

3. Take away from — lessen. Three-part and inseparable: the thing diminished always follows from. (умалять, портить впечатление)

  1. The loud music takes away from the food.
  2. Nothing can take away from what she accomplished.

4. Take away from — come out with. What you leave an experience holding: a lesson, an impression. The noun takeaway comes from here. (вынести (урок, впечатление))

  1. What I took away from the meeting was that nobody had a plan.
  2. The main takeaway was that we need more time.

take back, take out, throw away

Retract, return, readmit, or transport someone mentally to an earlier time.

1. Retract what you said. Withdraw a statement, usually an unfair one. (брать слова обратно)

  1. That song takes me back to high school.
  2. The smell of chalk took her back thirty years.

take for#

Pronunciation
/ˈteɪk fɔr/ · TAKE-for · the verb keeps the stress; for is weak, usually /fɚ/
Русский
принимать за (кого-то другого); считать (кем-то)
Base verb
take
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition), with the object fixed in the middle
Separable
yes, and obligatorily so — the object sits between take and for, and there is no joined order at all: I took him for a security guard, ✗ I took for him a security guard. A pronoun goes in the same slot and nowhere else: take it for granted, never ✗ take for granted it.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday
Verb type
stative — a judgment or a mistaken belief about identity, not an act, so the progressive is out: ✗ I am taking him for a security guard. The fixed take for granted is the same.
Passive
yes, unusually for a stative verb — he was taken for a security guard because of the jacket; it was taken for granted that the room would be free; she doesn't want to be taken for granted.

Wrongly believe someone or something to be something else — and, in the fixed phrase take for granted, to stop noticing what you have.

1. Mistake one thing for another. Usually about identity or role. (принимать за)

  1. I took him for a security guard because of the jacket.
  2. Everyone takes her for the boss.

2. What do you take me for?. A challenge to someone's low opinion of you. (за кого ты меня принимаешь?)

  1. Do you take me for an idiot?
  2. What do you take us for?

3. Take for granted. Assume something without checking, or stop appreciating someone. (принимать как должное)

  1. He takes his wife for granted.
  2. We took it for granted that the room would be free.

понимать, усваивать; приютить; обмануть; ушить (одежду); сходить (на фильм)

take

phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes — both orders work with a full noun, take in a movie or take a movie in, take in the waist or take the waist in, and a pronoun must split the phrase: take it in, never ✗ take in it.

Absorb, shelter, deceive, narrow, or go and see — one of the busiest phrasal verbs in the language.

1. Absorb and understand. Register information, or simply look at something at length. (усваивать, воспринимать)

  1. There was too much on the slide to take it all in.
  2. She sat quietly, taking in everything that was said.

  1. They took in two foster kids last spring.

  1. Don't be taken in by the discount — it isn't one.
  2. He took a lot of smart people in.

4. Make a garment narrower. The tailor's sense; the opposite is let out. (ушить)

  1. The tailor took the waist in two inches.

5. Go and see something. A movie, a game, a show. (сходить на, посмотреть)

  1. We took in a movie after dinner.
  2. The tour takes in four cities in six days.

Pronunciation
/ˌteɪk ˈɔf/ · take-AWF · the particle carries the stress, not the verb

take
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes in the transitive senses — take off your coat or take your coat off, and a pronoun must split: take it off, never ✗ take off it. In the intransitive senses (a plane, a business) there is no object to place.
Transitive
either — transitive when you remove something or deduct time, intransitive when a plane or a business takes off

action — dynamic everywhere: removing something worn, leaving the ground, bolting, suddenly succeeding, and deducting time or money.

Remove something worn, leave the ground or the scene, suddenly succeed, or subtract time or money.

1. Remove something worn or attached. Clothes, glasses, a lid, a label. (снимать)

  1. Take your shoes off at the door.
  2. She took off her coat and hung it by the door.
  3. It's hot — take it off.

2. Leave the ground. Of aircraft; the noun is takeoff. (взлетать)

  1. The plane took off forty minutes late.

3. Leave suddenly. Informal: go, fast, often without explaining. (сорваться, умчаться)

  1. He took off down the alley before anyone could stop him.

4. Suddenly succeed. Of a product, a career, an idea. (пойти в гору, набрать популярность)

  1. The business really took off after the article ran.

5. Take time off; deduct. Not work for a stretch, or knock money off a price. (взять отгул; скинуть, вычесть)

  1. I'm taking Friday off.
  2. She took off three days to move.
  3. The manager took ten dollars off the bill.

6. Imitate mockingly. British. Americans say do an impression of. (пародировать)

  1. In Britain they'd say she takes the boss off perfectly; in the US we'd say she does a great impression of him.
