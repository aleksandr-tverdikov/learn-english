Past = participle, one vowel changed#

The past and participle are identical, differing from the base by a single vowel change.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

These verbs ask you to learn two forms instead of three: the past and the participle are the same word, and that word differs from the base by a single vowel. Win becomes won for both, find becomes found for both, stand becomes stood for both — and the consonants sit still while the vowel does all the work. Because the past and the participle never diverge, the trap here is not choosing between them, the way began and begun force you to choose; it is remembering that the vowel changes at all, since ✗ I finded it and ✗ he winned are the mistakes this group actually produces. In the original Russian spreadsheet this is the group described as (2-я и 3-я одинаковы, от 1-й отличаются сменой одного звука).

/əˈbaɪd/ · uh-BIDE
Русский
вытерпеть; обитать, оставаться верным
Forms
abide /əˈbaɪd/ · abode /əˈboʊd/ · abode /əˈboʊd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
formal — legal and literary; abide by belongs to rules and rulings, and the dwelling sense is older still
Pattern
aɪ — oʊ — oʊ
Group
3
Third person
abides · -ing form: abiding (the silent e drops)
Contrast
In everyday American English the past is the regular abided, and it is the only choice in abide by: We abided by the ruling. Abode belongs to older and literary writing, and it survives most healthily as a noun — a humble abode.
Verb type
stative in the living cannot abide sense — an attitude, not an act, so the progressive is odd: ✗ I am abiding the noise. Action — dynamic only in the archaic dwelling sense (her family abode in that valley).
Transitivity
both — transitive in the tolerate sense, where the object is obligatory (I cannot abide people who talk; ✗ I cannot abide); intransitive in the dwelling sense (a strange quiet abides in that house) and in the prepositional abide by the ruling.
Passive
restricted — the stative tolerate sense resists it: ✗ the noise could not be abided by her. The prepositional passive works in legal writing: every term of the settlement was abided by. The dwelling sense is intransitive and has none.

To put up with something; in older usage, to stay or dwell somewhere.

1. Tolerate, put up with. Almost always negative — cannot abide, could not abide. This is the only sense alive in everyday American English, and it takes the regular abided. (терпеть, выносить)

  1. I cannot abide people who talk during a movie.
  2. She could not abide the noise from the construction site.
  3. He has never abided criticism well.

2. Abide by — comply with. Fixed phrasal use, and always regular: abided by, never the strong form. (соблюдать, следовать (правилам))

  1. We abided by the ruling even though we disagreed.
  2. Everyone here abides by the same rules.
  3. The company has abided by every term of the settlement.

3. Dwell, remain. Archaic and literary. This is the sense that takes abode, which survives far more healthily as a noun — a humble abode. (обитать, пребывать)

  1. Her family abode in that valley for four generations.
  2. The shepherds have abode in these hills since before the road was built.
  3. A strange quiet abides in that house.

Contrast
Both alit and the regular alighted are standard; Americans write alighted a little more often. Keep the verb apart from the adjective alight, meaning on fire — the barn was alight — which has no past tense at all.
Verb type
action — dynamic; a verb of motion and coming to rest (a cardinal alit on the railing). Keep it apart from the adjective alight, meaning on fire, which is not a verb form at all.
Transitivity
intransitive in every sense — what is landed on or stepped out of comes after a preposition (alight on the feeder, alights at the Franklin Street stop, alighted on a compromise), never as a bare object: ✗ she alit the railing.
Passive
no — intransitive, so there is no object to promote: ✗ the railing was alit. The barn was alight is the adjective, not a passive.

To come down and settle on something, or to step down out of a vehicle.

1. Land, settle on something. The core sense, used of birds, insects, and anything that comes lightly to rest. Both alit and the regular alighted are standard. (садиться, опускаться (о птице, насекомом))

  1. Sparrows alight on the feeder the moment I go back inside.
  2. A cardinal alit on the railing and stared at us.
  3. The same dragonfly has alit on that reed all afternoon.

2. Step down from a vehicle. Formal, and commonest in transit announcements and British signage; ordinary American speech says get off. (сходить, высаживаться (из транспорта))

  1. She alights at the Franklin Street stop every morning.
  2. Two passengers had alit before the conductor noticed the open door.

3. Alight on — happen upon. Literary, used of a gaze or a thought coming to rest on something. Usually regular here: alighted on. (натолкнуться (на мысль, решение))

  1. Her eye alighted on the one sentence that mattered.
  2. We have alit on a compromise that might actually work.

Contrast
It follows slide. You will also meet the participle backslidden in religious writing, but backslid is the ordinary American form.
Verb type

Passive

To slip back into a bad habit, an abandoned belief, or an earlier and worse condition.

1. Relapse into an old habit. The everyday sense, used of diets, sobriety, and any effort at self-improvement. (срываться, скатываться назад)

  1. Dieters backslide around the holidays.
  2. He backslid the week after he quit smoking.
  3. She has backslid twice since January.

2. Fall away from faith. Religious register, and the original sense. In that writing you will also meet the participle backslidden. (отступаться от веры)

  1. The preacher warned that a believer can backslide without ever noticing it.
  2. Two of the congregation had backslid by the following spring.

3. Regress, lose ground. Of institutions, countries, or measured results. Common in journalism: democratic backsliding. (регрессировать, откатываться назад)

  1. The department backslides every time the budget tightens.
  2. Several districts have backslid on reading scores.
  3. By March the government had backslid on every reform it promised.

Passive

  1. Violence begets violence.
  2. Cheap money can beget speculation.
  3. That one decision has begot a decade of lawsuits.

Contrast
It follows hold. The verb is literary or ceremonial; in ordinary speech it survives mostly in the fixed phrase lo and behold.
Verb type
stative-leaning perception verb, patterning with see rather than watch: the progressive is unnatural — ✗ the crowd is beholding the eclipse. Literary throughout, and imperative-only in Behold the size of that canyon.

Passive

To look at something, especially something striking.

1. Look upon, gaze at. Literary or ceremonial; nobody says it in ordinary conversation. Do not confuse it with the adjective beholden, meaning indebted, which is a different word. (созерцать, взирать)

  1. The crowd beheld the eclipse in complete silence.
  2. Few travelers have beheld that valley under snow.
  3. She beholds the city each morning from the same window.

2. Behold! — presenting something. Imperative only, and today usually playful or ironic when a thing is revealed. (вот!, смотрите!)

  1. Behold the size of that canyon.
  2. He opened the box and — behold — the missing part was inside.

3. Lo and behold. A frozen phrase introducing a surprise. It is the only place most Americans ever use the verb. (и вот, представьте себе)

  1. We drove all that way and, lo and behold, the museum was closed.
  2. I looked again and, lo and behold, the keys were sitting on the counter.
  3. Nobody who had beheld the wreck could describe it calmly.

Archaic. In living American English the verb appears in one frozen phrase, woe betide — a subjunctive, so it takes no ending: Woe betide anyone who is late. The past betid is museum English.

To happen to someone, especially to happen badly.

1. Woe betide — a warning. The one frozen phrase that keeps this verb alive. It is a subjunctive, so it normally takes no ending, though woe betides also turns up. (горе тому, кто...)

  2. Woe betide anyone who touches my coffee.
  3. Woe betides the traveler who ignores that sign.

2. Happen, befall. Archaic outside of the phrase above; the past betid is museum English. (постигать, случаться (с кем-л.))

  1. Whatever may betide, we will meet again in spring.
  2. Great sorrow betid the household that winter.
  3. Stranger things had betid the village before the bridge was built.

bind#

Pronunciation
/baɪnd/ · BYND
Русский
связать, завязать
Forms
bind /baɪnd/ · bound /baʊnd/ · bound /baʊnd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — ordinary in speech and writing, and bound to is pure conversation
Pattern
aɪ — aʊ — aʊ
Group
3
Third person
binds · -ing form: binding
Contrast
Bound is three words at once: this past tense; a regular verb meaning to leap — the dog bounded up the steps; and an adjective meaning headed for — a train bound for Chicago. Only the first is a form of bind.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout. The participle bound has two frozen adjectival lives that are no longer verbal: bound to happen (certain to) and bound for Chicago (headed for).
Transitivity
both — transitive in the tying, obliging, uniting, bookbinding, and dressing senses (she bound the stack of letters, the contract binds both parties), intransitive in the setting and jamming senses (an egg helps the mixture bind, the drawer binds whenever the wood swells).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the letters were bound with kitchen twine, both parties are bound by the contract for five years. No passive of the intransitive the drawer binds, and it was bound to happen is an adjective, not a passive.

To tie things together, to place someone under an obligation, or to make separate things hold fast to each other.

1. Tie, fasten together. The literal core sense: joining things with rope, twine, wire, or tape. (связывать, завязывать)

  1. We bind the reports in black covers.
  2. She bound the stack of letters with kitchen twine.
  3. Someone has bound all the cables with zip ties.

2. Place under an obligation. Legal and contractual, and the reason binding is an adjective in its own right. (обязывать, связывать обязательством)

  1. The contract binds both parties for five years.
  2. He has bound himself to a two-year lease.
  3. The treaty had bound them long before the war started.

3. Be bound to; be bound for. Two frozen adjectival uses of the participle: bound to means certain to, and bound for means headed for. Neither behaves like an ordinary passive. (непременно, обязательно; направляющийся (куда-л.))

  1. Something like this was bound to happen eventually.
  2. We were on a train bound for Chicago.

4. Hold together, unite. Figurative, of what holds a group or a story together. (сплачивать, объединять)

  1. Shared work binds a crew faster than anything else.
  2. Grief had bound the two families together for good.

5. Bind a book. The bookbinding sense; the trade is called binding. (переплетать)

  1. That shop still binds dissertations by hand.
  2. They bound my grandfather's letters into a single volume.

6. Bind a wound; bind up. Somewhat formal or biblical; everyday speech says bandage. (перевязывать (рану))

  1. Medics bind the wound before they move anyone.
  2. She bound up his arm with a torn shirt.

7. Hold fast; stick, jam. Intransitive, used of a mixture that comes together and of a mechanism that seizes. (схватываться; заедать, заклинивать)

  1. An egg helps the mixture bind.
  2. The drawer binds whenever the wood swells.
  3. The brake has bound against the rotor since the last repair.

8. Bind to (chemistry, computing). Technical, but a learner meets it constantly in science writing and in software documentation. (связываться (с рецептором); привязывать (к порту, ключу))

  1. The antibody binds to a protein on the cell surface.
  2. The molecule has bound to the receptor site.
  3. We bound the shortcut to a different key.

Pattern

Third person
bleeds · -ing form: bleeding
Contrast
The vowel shortens and the spelling loses a letter with it: bleed has two e's, bled has one, and ✗ bleeded is never right. The same shortening runs through feed, breed and lead. The transitive sense keeps the strong form too — he bled the brakes, not ✗ he bleeded the brakes.
Verb type
action — dynamic; a process verb literally (his nose bled) and figuratively (the company bleeds cash).
Transitivity
both — intransitive in the losing-blood, spreading, and blurring senses (cheap dye bleeds in hot water, work bleeds into the weekend), transitive in the draining and financial senses (he bled the radiator, the fund bled investors for three years).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — every line on the car has been bled, the brakes were bled after the lines were changed. No passive of the intransitive: ✗ ten minutes were bled by his nose.

To lose blood, or to let a fluid drain slowly out of something.

1. Lose blood. The literal sense. Never the regular form — the past is always bled. (кровоточить, истекать кровью)

  1. Cuts on the scalp bleed more than they should.
  2. His nose bled for ten minutes.
  3. She had bled through two bandages before the nurse came back.

2. Bleed a line, drain fluid. Transitive and technical: bleed the brakes, bleed a radiator. It keeps the strong form too. (спускать (воздух, жидкость), прокачивать)

  1. You have to bleed the brakes after changing the lines.
  2. He bled the radiator to stop the knocking.
  3. The mechanic has bled every line on the car.

3. Lose money steadily. Business writing, either intransitive or with cash as the object. (терять деньги, истекать деньгами)

  1. The company bleeds cash every quarter.
  2. The fund bled investors for three straight years.

4. Run, spread (of color or ink). Of dye in the wash and of ink on paper. (растекаться, расплываться (о краске))

  1. Cheap dye bleeds in hot water.
  2. The red had bled into the collar before she noticed.

5. Bleed into — blur together. Figurative, of boundaries that fail to hold. (перетекать, сливаться (одно в другое))

  1. Work bleeds into the weekend if you let it.
  2. The two chapters have bled into each other.

6. One's heart bleeds. A fixed sympathy phrase, and in American speech usually sarcastic. (сердце обливается кровью, всей душой сочувствовать)

  1. My heart bleeds for anyone driving in this.
  2. We bled for that family when the story came out.

To hold out against force or pressure without giving way.

1. Endure physical force. Of materials and structures — panels, seawalls, roofs. It follows stand. (выдерживать, выносить (нагрузку))

  1. These panels withstand winds over a hundred miles an hour.
  2. The seawall withstood three hurricanes.
  3. The bridge has withstood everything the river has thrown at it.

2. Resist pressure or opposition. Figurative: to hold up against scrutiny, temptation, or attack. (противостоять, устоять перед)

  1. Few arguments withstand that kind of scrutiny.
  2. The company withstood two hostile bids.
  3. Her account has withstood every cross-examination.

To twist something forcefully, especially to squeeze the liquid out of it.

1. Twist to squeeze out liquid. Often with out: wring out a shirt. The w is silent, so wring sounds exactly like ring and wrung like rung — but ring, rang, rung is a bell, a different verb entirely. (выжимать, отжимать)

  1. Wring the shirt out before you hang it.
  2. He wrung the mop over the bucket.
  3. She has wrung out every towel in the house.

2. Extract with difficulty. Figurative, usually with out of: to get something from someone only by sustained pressure. (выбить, вырвать (согласие, деньги))

  1. You will not wring a straight answer out of him.
  2. The union wrung a raise out of management.
  3. They had wrung every dollar out of the budget.

3. wring one's hands. Literally a gesture of distress; figuratively, to fret in public without doing anything. The noun is hand-wringing. (заламывать руки; сокрушаться)

  1. Don't just wring your hands about it.
  2. She wrung her hands the whole time the jury was out.
  3. Commentators have wrung their hands over this for a decade.

4. Twist violently, of a neck. Of killing a bird by twisting its neck, and in the threat I could wring his neck, meaning I am furious with him. (свернуть (шею))

  1. Farmers used to wring a chicken's neck without thinking twice.
  2. He wrung the bird's neck and got on with dinner.
  3. My grandmother had wrung a hundred chickens' necks before she was twenty.
