# Phrasal verbs: cast — cut

> Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs cast, catch, choose… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

[← Irregular verbs](README.md) &middot; [The grammar of verbs](../README.md)

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word — *off*, *up*, *across*, *on to* — and the pair almost never means what its parts mean separately: *come across an old photo* has nothing to do with crossing, and *cut it out* removes no material. The second thing to learn about each one is where the object can stand. Separable verbs let the object sit in the middle — *cut the power off* or *cut off the power* — and when that object is a pronoun it **must** sit there: *cut it off*, never ✗ *cut off it*. Inseparable ones never split: *come across a photo*, never ✗ *come a photo across*. Every entry below states which it is, because that one line is the difference between sounding native and sounding wrong.

### cast about

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *cast-a-BOUT* &middot; the particle carries the stress, which is what fuses the two words into one verb
**Русский:** искать, лихорадочно подыскивать
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle), normally followed by *for*
**Separable:** no — nothing can stand between *cast* and *about*; what you are hunting for goes after *for*. ✗ *cast an excuse about*, ✗ *cast it about for*.
**Transitive:** no — intransitive; the thing sought is introduced by *for*
**Register:** formal — commoner in writing than in speech; in American speech *cast around* has largely replaced it
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; searching is something you do, and it takes the progressive freely: *he was casting about for an excuse*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, and the prepositional passive fails too: ✗ *an excuse was cast about for*.

To search, in the mind or in a drawer, for something you need at once.

1. He *cast about* for an excuse and found none.
2. She *cast about* for the right word for the better part of a minute.
3. The board has *cast about* for a replacement since March.
4. I *cast about* in the junk drawer for a pen that still worked.
5. Investors *cast about* for somewhere safer to park their money.

**See also:** [cast around](#cast-around), [come up with](#come-up-with)

### cast around

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *cast-a-ROUND* &middot; stress on *round*, never on *cast*
**Русский:** искать, оглядываться в поисках
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle), normally followed by *for*
**Separable:** no — the phrase stays whole and the object follows *for*. ✗ *cast a plan around*, ✗ *cast around a plan* without *for*.
**Transitive:** no — intransitive; use *for* to name what you are after
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary American form of *cast about*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the everyday American twin of *cast about*, equally at home in the progressive: *she was casting around for a chair*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive; ✗ *a caterer was cast around for*.

To look around for something or someone, physically or in your memory.

1. She *cast around* for a chair that wasn't covered in laundry.
2. We *cast around* for a caterer who could take a last-minute job.
3. He *cast around* for someone to blame.
4. After the layoff he *cast around* for anything that paid.
5. They have *cast around* for months without finding a buyer.

**See also:** [cast about](#cast-about), [come across](#come-across)

### cast aside

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst əˈsaɪd/ &middot; *cast-a-SIDE* &middot; the stress falls on *side*
**Русский:** отбросить, отвергнуть
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cast aside her doubts* and *cast her doubts aside* are both good, and with a pronoun it must split: *cast them aside*, never ✗ *cast aside them*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** formal — a written word; in speech Americans say *drop*, *ditch*, or *throw out*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a single act of rejection, not a state.
**Passive:** **yes**, and common in writing — *old loyalties were cast aside in a single afternoon*.

To reject something or someone you no longer have any use for.

1. She *cast aside* everything she had been taught about money.
2. He *cast* his notes *aside* and spoke from memory.
3. Once the season ended, the club *cast* him *aside*.
4. Old loyalties were *cast aside* in a single afternoon.
5. Don't *cast* it *aside* just because the cover looks dated.

**See also:** [cast away](#cast-away), [cast off](#cast-off), [cast out](#cast-out)

### cast away

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst əˈweɪ/ &middot; *cast-a-WAY* &middot; stress on *way*; the noun *castaway* keeps the stress on *cast*
**Русский:** выбросить, отбросить; (в пассиве) быть выброшенным на необитаемый берег
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cast away his fears*, *cast his fears away*, *cast them away*. The shipwreck sense is almost always passive, so the question rarely arises: *they were cast away on an island*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** formal — literary and slightly biblical
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses; in sense 2 (shipwreck) the verb survives almost only as a passive participle.
**Passive:** **yes** — *he cast away his savings* → *his savings were cast away*; sense 2 is passive by default: *the crew were cast away on a reef*.

To throw something away; in the passive, to be stranded by a shipwreck.

**1. Throw away, be rid of.** Used of things and, more often, of feelings and chances. (отбрасывать, выбрасывать)

1. He *cast away* the last of his savings on a bad idea.
2. She *cast* her fear *away* and stepped out onto the ice.
3. You don't get chances like that twice — don't *cast* it *away*.

**2. Be shipwrecked.** Nearly always passive, and the source of the noun *castaway*. (быть выброшенным на берег после кораблекрушения)

4. The crew were *cast away* on a reef two hundred miles from land.
5. He wrote as if he had been *cast away* rather than merely transferred to Ohio.

**See also:** [cast aside](#cast-aside), [cast off](#cast-off)

### cast down

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst ˈdaʊn/ &middot; *cast-DOWN* &middot; the particle takes the stress
**Русский:** опустить (глаза); повергнуть в уныние
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cast down her eyes*, *cast her eyes down*, *cast them down*. In the "dishearten" sense the verb survives mainly as the passive participle, where nothing separates: *she was cast down by the news*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** formal — literary and biblical; the modern American equivalents are *look down* and *get someone down*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic when you lower your eyes (sense 1); in sense 2 the participle sits close to an adjective of state — *she looked cast down*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *her eyes were cast down*; sense 2 lives in the passive: *he was cast down by the second rejection*.

To lower the eyes or the head; to leave someone dispirited.

**1. Lower the eyes or the gaze.** A gesture of modesty or shame. (опускать (глаза, взгляд))

1. She *cast down* her eyes when he came into the room.
2. He *cast* his gaze *down* and said nothing.
3. She raised her eyes for a moment, then *cast* them *down* again.

**2. Dishearten, dispirit.** Almost always passive. (повергать в уныние, удручать)

4. He was *cast down* by the second rejection in a week.
5. Nothing *cast* her *down* for long.

**See also:** [cast aside](#cast-aside), [come down](#come-down)

### cast off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst ˈɔf/ &middot; *cast-OFF* &middot; stress on *off*; the noun and adjective *castoff* move it back to *cast*
**Русский:** сбросить, отбросить; отдать швартовы; закрыть петли (в вязании)
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes when there is an object — *cast off the old rules*, *cast the old rules off*, *cast them off*, never ✗ *cast off them*. In the boating sense there is no object at all: *we cast off at dawn*.
**Transitive:** either — transitive when you shed something, intransitive when a boat leaves
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses: shedding something, leaving a dock, closing off stitches.
**Passive:** **restricted** — fine where there is an object (*the old rules were cast off*, *the stitches are cast off loosely*), impossible in the boating sense, which has none: ✗ *was cast off by the crew* for *we cast off at dawn*.

To shed something you no longer want; to untie a boat and leave; in knitting, to close off the stitches.

**1. Shed, get rid of.** Used of clothes, habits, rules, and people. (сбрасывать, отвергать)

1. The town has *cast off* the reputation it had in the eighties.
2. She *cast* her coat *off* and got straight to work.
3. He *cast* them *off* the moment they stopped being useful.

**2. Untie and leave (of a boat).** No object; standard among American boaters. (отдавать швартовы, отчаливать)

4. We *cast off* just after five and were out of the harbor by six.

**3. Finish a knitted piece.** The last row, where the stitches are closed. (закрывать петли)

5. Knit two more rows, then *cast off* loosely.

**See also:** [cast on](#cast-on), [cast aside](#cast-aside), [cut loose](#cut-loose)

### cast on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst ˈɑn/ &middot; *cast-ON* &middot; stress on *on*
**Русский:** набирать петли (в вязании)
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cast on twenty stitches*, *cast twenty stitches on*, *cast them on*, never ✗ *cast on them*.
**Transitive:** either — the stitches may be named or simply understood
**Register:** everyday — but only inside knitting; outside the craft it means nothing at all
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a knitting instruction, most often imperative.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural as a resulting state (*once forty stitches are cast on, join in the round*), odd with an agent: ✗ *eighty stitches were cast on by her*.

To put the first row of stitches onto a needle, which is how a knitted piece begins.

1. *Cast on* forty stitches and join in the round.
2. She *cast* eighty stitches *on* and lost count twice.
3. Once you have *cast* them *on*, knit one row plain.
4. I *cast on* last night and I'm already at the cuff.
5. He *cast on* too tightly, and the brim won't stretch.

**See also:** [cast off](#cast-off)

### cast out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæst ˈaʊt/ &middot; *cast-OUT* &middot; stress on *out*; the noun *outcast* reverses the order and the stress
**Русский:** изгонять, выгонять, извергать
**Base verb:** [cast](01-all-three-same.md#cast) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cast out the moneychangers*, *cast the moneychangers out*, *cast them out*, never ✗ *cast out them*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** formal — biblical in flavor; everyday American is *throw out* or *kick out*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a single act of expulsion.
**Passive:** **yes**, and idiomatic — *she was cast out by her own congregation*.

To drive someone or something out of a place or a community.

1. The sect *cast out* anyone who married an outsider.
2. The village *cast* the whole family *out* over a debt nobody could prove.
3. They *cast* him *out* without so much as a hearing.
4. She was *cast out* by her own congregation.
5. The story turns on a healer who *casts out* what nobody else can see.
6. Once they decide you don't belong, they *cast* you *out* quietly.

**See also:** [cast aside](#cast-aside), [cast off](#cast-off)

### catch at

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkætʃ ət/ &middot; *CATCH-at* &middot; a preposition, not a particle — the verb keeps the stress and *at* reduces to /ət/
**Русский:** хвататься за, цепляться за
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — the object always follows *at*: *catch at a branch*, never ✗ *catch a branch at*. A pronoun stays there too: *catch at it*.
**Transitive:** yes — the object comes after the preposition
**Register:** formal — literary; everyday American is *grab at* or *grab for*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; an attempt rather than a result, which is why the generic present works: *he catches at every rumor that suits him*.
**Passive:** **no** in practice — the prepositional passive is not idiomatic: ✗ *my sleeve was caught at*, ✗ *the railing was caught at by her*.

To make a grab at something, usually without getting hold of it, and so to seize on any chance that offers.

1. She *caught at* the railing as the bus pulled away.
2. He *caught at* my sleeve and asked me to wait.
3. A drowning man will *catch at* anything that floats.
4. They *caught at* the one figure in the report that helped them.
5. He *catches at* every rumor that suits him.

**See also:** [catch on](#catch-on), [cling to](#cling-to)

### catch on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ˈɑn/ &middot; *catch-ON* &middot; the particle takes the stress
**Русский:** входить в моду, прижиться; смекнуть, догадаться
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle); with *to* it becomes a three-part verb
**Separable:** no — there is no object to move. When you name what was understood, add *to* and put it last: *catch on to the trick*, ✗ *catch the trick on*.
**Transitive:** no — intransitive; *catch on to something* reaches its object through a preposition
**Register:** everyday — the "understand" sense is informal
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses: sense 1 is a gradual change (*the name was catching on*), sense 2 a moment of understanding, not a state (*it took me three episodes to catch on*).
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive; even with *to* there is no passive: ✗ *the trick was caught on to*.

To become popular, or to work out what is going on.

**1. Become popular.** Of a word, a product, a habit. (прижиться, войти в моду)

1. The name *caught on* faster than anyone expected.
2. Curbside pickup *caught on* during the pandemic and never went away.
3. His slang never quite *caught on* outside the team.

**2. Understand, figure it out.** Often *catch on to*, and often with a note of "at last." (смекнуть, догадаться, раскусить)

4. It took me three episodes to *catch on*.
5. She *caught on to* what he was really asking within a minute.
6. By the time the bank *caught on to* the scheme, the money was gone.

**See also:** [catch up](#catch-up), [come around](#come-around)

### catch out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ˈaʊt/ &middot; *catch-OUT* &middot; stress on *out*
**Русский:** застать врасплох; поймать на ошибке, уличить
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *catch out the witness*, *catch the witness out*, and with a pronoun it must split: *caught her out*, never ✗ *caught out her*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday in the passive *caught out*; chiefly British in the active sense of exposing a lie, where Americans say *catch someone in a lie* or *trip someone up*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a single event of surprise or exposure.
**Passive:** **yes** — and sense 1 is essentially passive-only in American use: *we were caught out by the storm halfway up the trail*.

To leave someone unprepared, or to expose someone's mistake or lie.

**1. Be caught unprepared.** Almost always passive in American use. (быть застигнутым врасплох)

1. We were *caught out* by the storm halfway up the trail.
2. Half the office was *caught out* when the deadline moved.
3. Don't let the fine print *catch* you *out*.

**2. Expose someone's error or lie.** The active use is chiefly British. (уличить, поймать на ошибке)

4. A single misremembered date *caught* the witness *out*.
5. The follow-up question *caught* him *out*.
6. She *caught* them *out* on the dates in their own memo.

**See also:** [catch up with](#catch-up-with), [come out](#come-out)

### catch up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ˈʌp/ &middot; *catch-UP* &middot; stress on *up*; the noun *catch-up* keeps it there in *play catch-up*
**Русский:** догнать; наверстать; поболтать, обменяться новостями
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** mostly no — in the everyday senses it takes no object at all (*run to catch up*), and the person or work involved goes after *with* or *on*. There is one older transitive use, "snatch up," which does separate: *catch up your coat*, *catch it up*. British speakers also split it in the "reach" sense — *I'll catch you up* — where Americans say *I'll catch up with you*.
**Transitive:** either — intransitive in the common senses, transitive in the dated "snatch up" sense and in the passive *be caught up in*
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense; closing a gap takes time and takes the progressive: *I spent Sunday catching up*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — nothing to passivize in the common intransitive senses (✗ *was caught up by me* for *I'll catch up*), but sense 4 exists only as a passive: *two of the clerks were caught up in the investigation*.

To close a gap — in distance, in work, or in news.

**1. Reach someone ahead of you.** (догнать, нагнать)

1. Go on ahead; I'll *catch up*.
2. She was two laps down and never *caught up*.

**2. Get current on work or sleep.** Usually with *on*. (наверстать)

3. I spent Sunday *catching up* on email.

**3. Exchange news.** A whole American social ritual lives in this sense. (поболтать, узнать новости друг о друге)

4. We should *catch up* sometime — it's been a year.

**4. Be caught up in — absorbed or entangled.** Passive only. (быть втянутым, увлечённым)

5. He got *caught up in* the excitement and bid twice what he meant to.
6. Two of the clerks were *caught up in* the investigation.

**5. Snatch up quickly.** Dated and literary; this is the one use that separates. (подхватить, схватить)

7. She *caught up* her bag and ran for the gate.

**See also:** [catch up on](#catch-up-on), [catch up with](#catch-up-with), [come along](#come-along)

### catch up on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ˈʌp ɑn/ &middot; *catch-UP-on* &middot; the stress sits on *up*, and *on* is unstressed
**Русский:** наверстать, восполнить, доделать накопившееся
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb
**Separable:** no — three-part verbs never split. The object follows the whole phrase: *catch up on sleep*, *catch up on it*. ✗ *catch sleep up on*, ✗ *catch up sleep on*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the work is done over a stretch of time: *she was catching up on six weeks of paperwork*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the prepositional passive is possible but stiff (*the paperwork was finally caught up on*) and collapses with an agent: ✗ *sleep was caught up on by me*.

To do the part of something that has piled up while you were away from it.

1. I need a weekend to *catch up on* sleep.
2. She *caught up on* six weeks of paperwork in two days.
3. We *catch up on* the show every Sunday night.
4. He has never *caught up on* the reading from that first semester.
5. There's a stack of it on my desk and I'll never *catch up on* it.

**See also:** [catch up](#catch-up), [catch up with](#catch-up-with)

### catch up with

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ˈʌp wɪθ/ &middot; *catch-UP-with* &middot; stress on *up*; both *catch* and *with* are unstressed
**Русский:** догнать; настигнуть (о последствиях); встретиться, поговорить
**Base verb:** [catch](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#catch) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb
**Separable:** no — the object always follows the whole phrase: *catch up with her*, ✗ *catch her up with*, ✗ *catch up her with*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; sense 2 personifies the trouble but is still an event: *the lies caught up with him in the end*.
**Passive:** **no** — ✗ *he was caught up with by the leaders*, ✗ *he was caught up with by his lies*. Keep it active.

To reach someone who is ahead of you — and, said of consequences, to reach someone at last.

**1. Reach someone ahead of you.** (догнать)

1. He *caught up with* the leaders on the last hill.
2. Start walking and I'll *catch up with* you at the corner.

**2. (Of consequences) finally affect someone.** The subject is the trouble, not the person. (настигнуть, дать о себе знать)

3. The lies *caught up with* him in the end.
4. Thirty years of smoking *caught up with* her all at once.

**3. Meet and exchange news.** (встретиться, поболтать)

5. I *caught up with* my old roommate over lunch.

**See also:** [catch up](#catch-up), [catch out](#catch-out), [come after](#come-after)

### choose up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʃuz ˈʌp/ &middot; *choose-UP* &middot; stress on *up*
**Русский:** разбиваться на команды, выбирать игроков по очереди
**Base verb:** [choose](13-o-past-participle-plus-n.md#choose) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** no in practice — the only objects it takes are the fixed *sides* and *teams*, and neither moves: *choose up sides*, ✗ *choose sides up*.
**Transitive:** either — with *sides*, with *teams*, or with nothing at all
**Register:** everyday — American; a British speaker says *pick teams*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a one-time act of dividing a group into teams.
**Passive:** **restricted** — *sides were chosen up before anyone thought to argue* passes, but an agent kills it: ✗ *sides were chosen up by the kids*.

To split a group into two teams by taking turns picking players; by extension, to take sides in a quarrel.

1. We *chose up* sides and played until it got dark.
2. The kids *choose up* every recess without an adult anywhere near.
3. By Tuesday the whole department had *chosen up* sides.
4. Somebody has to *choose up* before we can start.
5. They *chose up* teams right there in the parking lot.

**See also:** [come between](#come-between), [cut in](#cut-in)

### cling on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌklɪŋ ˈɑn/ &middot; *cling-ON* &middot; the particle carries the stress
**Русский:** цепляться, держаться из последних сил
**Base verb:** [cling](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#cling) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle); with *to* it becomes a three-part verb
**Separable:** no — nothing goes between the two words, and what you are holding follows *to*: *cling on to the rail*, ✗ *cling the rail on*, ✗ *cling on the rail to*.
**Transitive:** no — intransitive; add *to* to name what is being held
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic but durative, an activity you keep doing: *she was clinging on with both hands*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive; ✗ *the rail was clung on to*.

To hold on tight, especially when you are close to losing your grip — on a rope, on a lead, or on a job.

1. She *clung on* with both hands until the wave passed.
2. *Cling on* — the ladder shifts when you step off it.
3. He *clung on to* the rail all the way down the stairs.
4. The company has *clung on* through three bad quarters.
5. Their lead was one point, and they *clung on* for six more minutes.

**See also:** [cling to](#cling-to), [catch on](#catch-on)

### cling to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklɪŋ tə/ &middot; *CLING-to* &middot; a preposition, not a particle — the verb keeps the stress and *to* reduces to /tə/
**Русский:** цепляться за, держаться за, не расставаться с
**Base verb:** [cling](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#cling) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — the object always follows *to*: *cling to the raft*, never ✗ *cling the raft to*. With a pronoun it still follows: *cling to it*.
**Transitive:** yes — the object comes after the preposition
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and durative in senses 1–2; sense 3 (a smell, a nickname) is closer to **stative**, a condition that simply lasts: *that nickname clung to him for thirty years*.
**Passive:** **no** — the prepositional passive fails: ✗ *the raft was clung to*, ✗ *he was clung to by the nickname*.

To hold on to something and refuse to let go, whether it is a rope, a belief, or a smell in a room.

**1. Hold on physically.** (цепляться за, держаться за)

1. The kid *clung to* his mother's leg the whole time.
2. Two of them *clung to* the overturned hull until morning.

**2. Refuse to give up a hope or a belief.** (цепляться за (надежду, убеждение))

3. She *clings to* the idea that he'll call.

**Separable:** yes when there is an object — *cut the boat loose*, *cut loose the boat*, *cut it loose*, never ✗ *cut loose it*. In the "let go and enjoy yourself" sense there is no object.
**Transitive:** either
**Register:** everyday — the "enjoy yourself" sense is informal, and thoroughly American
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses, freeing or severing (*he cut the trailer loose*) and letting yourself go (*at a wedding he cuts loose*). *Loose* here is a resultative adjective describing the object, not an ordinary particle — compare *cut it free*.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive sense, and it is common of people: *she was cut loose after one album*, *the trailer had been cut loose miles back*. **No** passive of the enjoy-yourself sense, which has no object: ✗ *the kitchen staff was cut loose* would mean they were fired.

To free something by cutting, to sever ties with someone, or to let yourself go.

**1. Free by cutting; sever ties.** (отвязать, обрубить связь)

1. He *cut* the trailer *loose* and drove on.
2. The label *cut* her *loose* after one album.
3. If the deal is that bad, *cut* it *loose*.

**2. Let yourself go, have a wild time.** No object. (оторваться, дать себе волю)

4. Once the last table cleared, the kitchen staff *cut loose*.
5. He hardly drinks, but at a wedding he *cuts loose*.

**See also:** [cut off](#cut-off), [cast off](#cast-off), [cut up](#cut-up)

### cut off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌt ˈɔf/ &middot; *cut-OFF* &middot; stress on *off*; the noun and adjective *cutoff* move it to *cut*
**Русский:** отрезать; отключить (свет, воду); отрезать от мира; перебить; подрезать; лишить наследства
**Base verb:** [cut](01-all-three-same.md#cut) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cut off the power*, *cut the power off*, and with a pronoun it must split: *cut it off*, never ✗ *cut off it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all six senses: severing (*cut off the stems at an angle*), disconnecting a service, isolating, interrupting (*she cut him off mid-sentence*), pulling in front of a driver, and disinheriting (*his father cut him off*).
**Passive:** **yes**, in every sense, and in sense 3 the passive is the normal form: *the valley was cut off for six days*, *the electricity was cut off on a Friday*, *he was cut off without a cent*, *I got cut off on the ramp*.

To sever, disconnect, isolate, interrupt, or disinherit — one verb doing all six jobs.

**1. Sever, remove by cutting.** (отрезать, отсечь)

1. *Cut off* the stems at an angle before you put them in water.
2. He *cut* two inches *off* and it still didn't fit.

**2. Disconnect a service.** (отключить)

3. The power company *cut off* the electricity on a Friday.
4. They *cut* it *off* over a ninety-dollar balance.

**3. Isolate, block access.** Often passive. (отрезать, изолировать)

5. The storm *cut* the valley *off* for six days.

**4. Interrupt someone.** (перебить)

6. She *cut* him *off* mid-sentence.

**5. Pull dangerously in front of a driver.** (подрезать)

7. A van *cut* me *off* on the ramp.

**6. Disinherit or stop supporting.** Often *cut off without a cent*. (лишить наследства, перестать содержать)

8. His father *cut* him *off* the year he dropped out.

**See also:** [cut out](#cut-out), [cut in](#cut-in), [cut loose](#cut-loose), [cut away](#cut-away)

### cut out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *cut-OUT* &middot; stress on *out*; the noun *cutout* moves it to *cut*
**Русский:** вырезать; прекратить; заглохнуть (о двигателе); исключить (из рациона); быть созданным для (be cut out for)
**Base verb:** [cut](01-all-three-same.md#cut) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cut out the coupon*, *cut the coupon out*, and with a pronoun it must split: *cut it out*, never ✗ *cut out it*. That is why the idiom is *cut it out* and never ✗ *cut out it*.
**Transitive:** either — no object in the "stop working" sense
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: removing by cutting (*she cut out the pattern*), stopping (*cut it out*), an engine quitting (*the outboard cut out*), excluding (*they cut them out of the deal*). Sense 5, *be cut out for*, is a fixed **stative** adjectival passive — a standing fitness, not an event: *he wasn't cut out for sales*.
**Passive:** **yes** in senses 1–2 and 4: *the article was cut out and mailed to me*, *soda has been cut out entirely*, *they were cut out of the deal*. Sense 5 exists **only** in the passive, with no active counterpart: ✗ *sales didn't cut him out for*. **No** passive of the engine sense: ✗ *the outboard was cut out*.

To remove by cutting, to stop doing something, to quit working, or — in the passive — to be suited to something.

**1. Remove by cutting.** (вырезать)

1. She *cut out* the pattern and pinned it to the fabric.
2. He *cut* the article *out* and mailed it to me.

**2. Stop doing something.** Usually as a command. (прекратить, бросить)

3. *Cut* it *out* — she's trying to sleep.
4. I *cut out* soda in January and lost eight pounds.

**3. (Of an engine or a motor) stop suddenly.** No object. (заглохнуть, отключиться)

5. The outboard *cut out* half a mile from shore.

**4. Exclude someone.** (исключить, обойти)

6. The distributor *cut* them *out* of the deal entirely.

**5. Be cut out for — be suited to.** Passive only, and always negative or questioning in practice. (быть созданным для)

7. He wasn't *cut out for* sales.
8. I'm not sure she's *cut out for* the night shift.

**See also:** [cut off](#cut-off), [cut down](#cut-down), [cut away](#cut-away)

### cut through

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌt ˈθru/ &middot; *cut-THROO* &middot; stress on *through*
**Русский:** пройти напрямик через; пробиться сквозь (бюрократию, шум)
**Base verb:** [cut](01-all-three-same.md#cut) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — the object always follows *through*: *cut through the alley*, never ✗ *cut the alley through*.
**Transitive:** yes — the object comes after the preposition
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses, taking a shortcut (*we cut through the alley*) and getting past an obstruction (*her one question cut through an hour of hedging*); progressive fine: *the siren was cutting through the noise*.
**Passive:** **no.** ✗ *the alley was cut through* is read as a hole being made in it, not a shortcut, and the figurative sense is barely better: ✗ *the red tape was cut through by somebody*. Use an active subject, or switch to *get past* / *break through*.

To take a shortcut through a place, or to get past something that is slowing everyone down.

1. We *cut through* the alley to save five minutes.
2. Her one question *cut through* an hour of hedging.
3. Somebody has to *cut through* the red tape or the permit will sit there.
4. A siren *cut through* the noise of the crowd.
5. Good writing *cuts through*; clever writing rarely does.

**See also:** [cut across](#cut-across), [cut into](#cut-into), [come through](#come-through)

### cut up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌt ˈʌp/ &middot; *cut-UP* &middot; stress on *up*; the noun *cutup*, meaning a clown, moves it to *cut*
**Русский:** нарезать, разрезать на куски; дурачиться, паясничать
**Base verb:** [cut](01-all-three-same.md#cut) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *cut up the onions*, *cut the onions up*, *cut them up*, never ✗ *cut up them*. The "clown around" sense takes no object.
**Transitive:** either
**Register:** everyday — the "clown around" sense is informal and American; the "upset" sense is British
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the first two senses, dividing (*cut up the onions*) and clowning around (*he cuts up the second a camera appears*). Sense 3, the British *be cut up*, is a **stative** adjectival passive describing a state of distress: *she was terribly cut up about the dog*.
**Passive:** **yes** in sense 1: *the potatoes were washed and cut up*, *the cardboard had been cut up small enough for the bin*. **No** passive of the clown-around sense, which has no object: ✗ *the class was cut up in*. Sense 3 is passive in form only — it has no active: ✗ *the dog cut her up*.

To divide something into pieces, to fool around, or (in British use) to be badly upset.

**1. Divide into pieces.** (нарезать, разрезать)

1. *Cut up* the onions while the pan heats.
2. He *cut* the cardboard *up* small enough for the bin.
3. Wash the potatoes and *cut* them *up*.

**2. Clown around, misbehave.** American and informal; no object. (дурачиться, паясничать)

4. The two of them *cut up* in the back of the class all year.
5. He *cuts up* the second a camera appears.

**3. Be cut up — be badly upset.** British; Americans say *torn up* or *broken up*. (быть расстроенным, убитым)

6. She was terribly *cut up* about the dog.
7. He was still *cut up* about it a year later.

**See also:** [cut down](#cut-down), [cut off](#cut-off), [cut loose](#cut-loose)
