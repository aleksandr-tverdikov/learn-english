# Past = participle, add -t

> The past and participle add a -t to the unchanged base.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Group 8 holds the verbs whose past and participle are identical to each other and differ from the base by nothing but a final -t: *dwell — dwelt — dwelt*, *learn — learnt — learnt*, *spill — spilt — spilt*. It is the -t twin of group 7, which adds -d instead (отличаются от 1-й добавлением "t"). The catch for an American learner is that most of this group is no longer irregular in American English: *learned*, *spelled*, *smelled*, *burned*, and *spilled* are the ordinary American spellings, the -t versions are British, and a handful of the -t spellings on the original list — *tost*, *wrapt*, *blest*, *vext*, *dipt* — survive only in old poetry. Every entry below gives the form an American writer actually uses first and then says exactly where the -t form still lives, because the two that really do keep it, [dwell](#dwell) and [indwell](#indwell), are the exceptions rather than the rule.

---

### bless

**Pronunciation:** /blɛs/ &middot; *BLESS*
**Русский:** благословлять
**Forms:** *bless* /blɛs/ &middot; *blessed* /blɛst/ &middot; *blessed* /blɛst/ (archaic *blest* /blɛst/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — an American blesses food, sneezes, and hearts without thinking about it
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *blesses* &middot; **-ing form:** *blessing*
**Contrast:** The verb form is one syllable, /blɛst/, but the adjective spelled the same way is two: /ˈblɛsɪd/. *The priest blessed the boats* is /blɛst/; *a blessed relief* and *a blessed event* are /ˈblɛsɪd/. The spelling *blest* is not an error, just archaic — keep it for hymns and leave it out of prose.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense: the priest, the parent, or the speaker does something (*blesses the fleet*, *blessed the ground*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory, whether a person, an object, or a place: *she blesses the children*, ✗ *her mother blesses* is incomplete. The interjection *bless you* is a fixed fragment, not an ordinary intransitive.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the ground was blessed before a single shovel went in*, *that chapel has been blessed by three bishops*. Sense 3 exists only in the passive: *we have been blessed with patient neighbors*, with no active ✗ *something blessed us with neighbors*.

To ask God's favor on someone or something, to make something holy, or — in the passive — to be lucky enough to have something good.

**1. Ask God's favor on.** The core religious sense: a priest, a parent, or a speaker calls down protection or good fortune on a person. Neutral in American English and common well outside church. (благословлять)

1. The priest *blessed* the fleet on the first Sunday in June.
2. Her mother *blesses* the children before every long trip.
3. The bishop *had blessed* the building long before the first class met in it.

**2. Consecrate, make holy.** Used of objects, water, buildings, and ground rather than of people; the thing becomes sacred or set apart. (освящать)

4. A rabbi came to *bless* the new sanctuary.
5. They *blessed* the ground before a single shovel went in.
6. That chapel *has been blessed* by three different bishops.

**3. Be blessed with.** Passive only, and usually entirely secular: to be lucky enough to have some good quality, person, or circumstance. (быть наделённым, иметь счастье обладать)

7. We *have been blessed* with patient neighbors.
8. He *was blessed* with a good ear and no patience at all.

**4. Bless you; bless your heart.** Fixed conversational phrases: *bless you* after a sneeze or as warm thanks, and Southern *bless your heart*, which is sincere sympathy or a soft insult depending entirely on tone. (будь здоров; спасибо тебе; (южн.) бедняжка)

9. *Bless* you — that is exactly what I needed today.
10. *Bless* her heart, she tried to fix the sink herself and made it worse.

**See also:** [vex](#vex), [toss](#toss)

### burn

**Pronunciation:** /bɝn/ &middot; *BURN*
**Русский:** жечь
**Forms:** *burn* /bɝn/ &middot; *burned* /bɝnd/ &middot; *burned* /bɝnd/ (also *burnt* /bɝnt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed / base + -t &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *burns* &middot; **-ing form:** *burning*
**Contrast:** American English writes *burned* for the verb — *the toast burned*, *I have burned my hand*. *Burnt* is the British past and participle, but it does survive in American English as an adjective sitting in front of a noun: *burnt toast*, *burnt orange*, *burnt sugar*. So an American burns the toast and then throws out the burnt toast.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and ergative: the same event runs both ways (*the shed burned* / *vandals burned the shed*). Not stative and not linking, though *burn with shame* takes a prepositional complement rather than an object.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive when the thing itself is alight or reddening (*dry pine burns fast*, *I burn in fifteen minutes*, *the bulb burned out*), transitive when someone or something consumes it (*they burned the shed*, *he burned his hand*, *the furnace burns a gallon an hour*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the shed was burned to the ground*, *a backup disc has been burned for every project*. Sense 9 is essentially passive-only: *investors got burned*, *I have been burned by contractors twice*. No passive of the intransitive: ✗ *fifteen minutes is burned in by me*.

To be on fire or destroy by fire, and by extension to scorch, sting, consume fuel, or be consumed by a strong feeling.

**1. Be on fire; destroy by fire.** Works in both directions: the thing burns, or someone burns it. The literal sense that every other one grows out of. (гореть; сжигать)

1. Dry pine *burns* fast and throws sparks.
2. Vandals *burned* the shed to the ground.
3. The barn *has burned* twice since the family bought the place.

**2. Scorch food, overcook.** Of food left too long on heat, and of the cook who left it there. Nothing is on fire — the food is just ruined. (подгорать; сжечь (еду))

4. I *burned* the first batch of cookies and started over.
5. Don't let the sauce *burn* while you set the table.
6. She *has burned* every pot in this kitchen at least once.

**3. Injure with heat or chemicals.** Of skin damaged by heat, steam, acid, or friction. Often reflexive or with a body part as object — *burn yourself*, *burn your hand*. (обжечь(ся))

7. He *burned* his hand on the oven rack.
8. Steam *had burned* her wrist before she got the lid off.

**4. Redden in the sun.** Intransitive, of skin or of a person in strong sun. *Tan* is what happens when you don't burn. (обгорать на солнце)

9. I *burn* in about fifteen minutes without sunscreen.
10. His shoulders *burned* on the very first day of the trip.

**5. Use as fuel; use up.** Of fuel, calories, money, and time — the resource is consumed. *Burn through* stresses how fast it goes. (сжигать (топливо); расходовать)

11. The old furnace *burns* about a gallon an hour.
12. We *burned* half the budget on the first prototype.
13. She *has burned* through three phone chargers this year.

**6. Sting, feel hot.** Of a body part or a strong taste producing a hot, painful sensation. No injury is implied. (жечь, саднить)

14. The smoke *burned* my eyes all the way down the trail.
15. My throat *has burned* since the fire started.
16. That pepper will *burn* for a good ten minutes.

**7. Be consumed by a feeling.** *Burn with* shame, anger, or curiosity; also *be burning to* do something, meaning impatient. Literary but still current. (гореть (от стыда, гнева, желания))

17. Her face *burned* when they read the letter out loud.
18. Kids *burn* with curiosity at that age.
19. He *has burned* with resentment about that promotion for two years.

**8. Burn out.** Of a bulb, motor, or fuse: stop working for good. Of a person: collapse from sustained overwork — the source of the noun *burnout*. (перегореть; выгореть (о человеке))

20. Nurses *burn* out fastest in their third year.
21. The porch light *burned* out again.
22. Two of the residents *have burned* out and left medicine entirely.

**9. Get burned (be cheated).** Informal and figurative, almost always passive: to be cheated, betrayed, or badly hurt by trusting someone or something. (обжечься, погореть (обмануться))

23. Investors *got burned* when the company folded.
24. I *have been burned* by contractors twice, so I ask for references now.

**10. Copy data to a disc.** To write files to a CD or DVD. Peak-2005 vocabulary and fading with the hardware, but still instantly understood. (записывать (диск))

25. He *burned* the whole album onto a CD for the drive.
26. I *have burned* a backup disc for every project since that crash.


**Phrasal verbs:** [burn down](14-phrasal-b.md#burn-down) &middot; [burn off](14-phrasal-b.md#burn-off) &middot; [burn out](14-phrasal-b.md#burn-out) &middot; [burn through](14-phrasal-b.md#burn-through) &middot; [burn up](14-phrasal-b.md#burn-up)

**See also:** [sunburn](#sunburn), [spoil](#spoil)

### chap

**Pronunciation:** /tʃæp/ &middot; *CHAP*
**Русский:** трескаться; раскалывать
**Forms:** *chap* /tʃæp/ &middot; *chapped* /tʃæpt/ &middot; *chapped* /tʃæpt/ (archaic *chapt* /tʃæpt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — *chapped lips* is ordinary winter talk, though the verb rarely appears outside that participle
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *chaps* &middot; **-ing form:** *chapping* (the p doubles)
**Contrast:** In modern American English this verb lives mostly in its participle, used as an adjective about skin: *chapped lips*, *chapped hands*. The old spelling *chapt* is dead. Do not confuse the verb with the British noun *chap* meaning "a man," which has no verb forms at all.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and ergative like *burn*: skin chaps, or wind chaps it. No stative or linking use; the very common *chapped* in *chapped lips* is the participle doing adjective duty.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive with the skin or surface as subject (*my hands chapped badly*, *the clay chapped into plates*), transitive with the cold or the weather as subject (*wind like this chaps your face*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but rare, because the participle is normally an adjective instead: *her knuckles were chapped* is read as a state, not as an event, and the agentive *her face was chapped by the wind* is possible but stiff. No passive of the intransitive ✗ *it was chapped by my hands*.

Of skin, to crack and turn sore in cold or dry air; in older use, to split or crack open generally.

**1. Of skin, crack and redden.** Both ways: skin chaps, or wind and cold chap it. In modern American English the verb lives mostly in its participle used as an adjective — *chapped lips*, *chapped hands*. (трескаться (о коже))

1. Wind like this *chaps* your face in ten minutes.
2. My hands *chapped* badly the first winter we lived in Minnesota.
3. His lips *have chapped* every January since he was a kid.
4. By March her knuckles *had chapped* and split open.

**2. Split, crack open.** The wider old sense, of dry ground, wood, or paint rather than skin. Archaic outside dialect; you will meet it only in older writing. (трескаться, раскалываться)

5. Drought will *chap* bare ground faster than anyone expects.
6. The clay *chapped* into plates all across the riverbed.

**See also:** [nip](#nip), [clap](#clap)

### clap

**Pronunciation:** /klæp/ &middot; *KLAP*
**Русский:** хлопать ладонями, аплодировать
**Forms:** *clap* /klæp/ &middot; *clapped* /klæpt/ &middot; *clapped* /klæpt/ (archaic *clapt* /klæpt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *claps* &middot; **-ing form:** *clapping* (the p doubles)
**Contrast:** *Clapped* is the only form in modern American English; the *clapt* on the original list is a seventeenth-century spelling. The ending is pronounced /t/, which is what put the -t spelling there in the first place, but it is never written that way now.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, and abrupt by definition (*clapped a hand over her mouth*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the applause sense, alone or with *for* (*the whole room claps*, *they clapped for her*), transitive elsewhere: *clapped me on the shoulder*, *clapped a hand over her mouth*, *clapped him in jail*, *clapped eyes on the house*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — fine where a person is affected: *he was clapped in jail before his lawyer heard about it*, *players get clapped on the back after a bad inning*. The applause sense has no passive (intransitive), and the idiom resists one: ✗ *eyes were clapped on the house*.

To strike your palms together in applause, and by extension to slap someone in friendly greeting or to put something somewhere abruptly.

**1. Applaud.** The everyday sense. Intransitive, or with *for* — *they clapped for her*; you clap a performance, not a person, when the verb is transitive. (хлопать в ладоши, аплодировать)

1. The whole room *claps* at the end of every set.
2. She *clapped* until her hands stung.
3. The audience *has clapped* through six straight curtain calls.

**2. Clap someone on the back.** Almost always *clap someone on the back* or *on the shoulder* — a hearty gesture of congratulation, never aggression. (хлопнуть (по плечу, по спине))

4. He *clapped* me on the shoulder and walked off without a word.
5. Coaches *clap* their players on the back after a bad inning too.

**3. Put abruptly or forcefully.** Sudden, forceful placement: *clap a hand over someone's mouth*, *clap on the brakes*, or the old *clap him in irons*. The speed is the point. (рывком приложить; засадить (в тюрьму))

6. She *clapped* a hand over her mouth before the laugh got out.
7. They *had clapped* him in jail before his lawyer even heard about it.

**4. Clap eyes on.** Informal fixed phrase about a first or only sighting, and common in the negative: *I've never clapped eyes on him*. (увидеть, встретить (впервые))

8. The minute she *clapped* eyes on the house she wanted it.
9. I *had* never *clapped* eyes on the man before that morning.

**See also:** [chap](#chap), [nip](#nip)

### dip

**Pronunciation:** /dɪp/ &middot; *DIP*
**Русский:** погружать; окунать; макать
**Forms:** *dip* /dɪp/ &middot; *dipped* /dɪpt/ &middot; *dipped* /dɪpt/ (archaic *dipt* /dɪpt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *dips* &middot; **-ing form:** *dipping* (the p doubles)
**Contrast:** Regular in every American register — *dipped*. The spelling *dipt* appears only in older verse. And do not reach for a vowel change by analogy with *dive/dove*: *dip* has never had one, so ✗ *he dope the brush* is not a form of anything.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic. Transitive senses are agentive (*dip bread in the oil*); the intransitive ones describe a brief downward movement of numbers or terrain (*sales dipped*, *the road dips*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the immersion and lowering senses (*he dips his bread*, *ships dip their flags*, *she dipped water from the barrel*), intransitive in the decline and slope senses (*the temperature dipped below freezing*) and in *dip into* (*we dipped into savings*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the whole tray of strawberries has been dipped in chocolate*, *the flag was dipped as they passed*. No passive of the fall or slope senses: ✗ *below freezing was dipped by the temperature*.

To put something briefly into a liquid, and by extension to drop, to slope downward, or to reach into a supply.

**1. Put briefly into liquid.** In and straight back out again — that brevity is what separates *dip* from *soak* or *submerge*. (макать, окунать)

1. He *dips* his bread in the olive oil before every bite.
2. She *dipped* a toe in the lake and decided against swimming.
3. I *have dipped* the whole tray of strawberries in chocolate already.

**2. Drop, decline briefly.** Of numbers, prices, or temperature: down and usually back up. A permanent fall *drops* or *falls*; a *dip* is expected to recover. (снижаться, падать (ненадолго))

4. The temperature *dipped* below freezing overnight.
5. Sales *have dipped* for three quarters in a row.
6. Ratings always *dip* in the second week of a run.

**3. Slope downward.** Of a road, a piece of land, or a rock layer that angles down; also of a bird or plane losing height for a moment. (уходить вниз, понижаться)

7. The road *dips* twice before the bridge.
8. The trail *had dipped* into fog before we ever saw the sign.

**4. Dip into.** Two uses of the same picture, both meaning to take a little: money out of savings, or a few pages out of a book you never read straight through. (запускать руку (в запасы); заглядывать (в книгу))

9. We *dipped* into savings to cover the roof.
10. I *have dipped* into that biography three times and never finished it.
11. Feel free to *dip* into the report wherever it interests you.

**5. Lower briefly.** Of a flag, a head, a wing, or headlights. Note that headlights turned down are *dipped beams* in British English but *low beams* in American. (опускать (ненадолго))

12. Ships *dip* their flags when they pass a naval vessel.
13. He *dipped* his head and went back to work.

**6. Scoop out.** To lift liquid out with a container. Old-fashioned and regional in American English, where *scoop* or *ladle* is the ordinary word. (черпать)

14. She *dipped* water from the barrel with a tin cup.
15. The boys *had dipped* the trough dry by noon.

**See also:** [nip](#nip), [spill](#spill)

### dwell

**Pronunciation:** /dwɛl/ &middot; *DWELL*
**Русский:** обитать; задерживаться
**Forms:** *dwell* /dwɛl/ &middot; *dwelt* /dwɛlt/ &middot; *dwelt* /dwɛlt/ (also *dwelled* /dwɛld/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — *don't dwell on it* is ordinary speech, though the "live somewhere" sense is literary
**Pattern:** base + -t &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *dwells* &middot; **-ing form:** *dwelling*
**Contrast:** This is one of the two verbs in the group where the -t form is genuinely at home in American English: *dwelt* is normal in edited American prose and *dwelled* is the accepted alternative, not the default — the reverse of what happens with *learn* and *spell*. Watch the two senses: the literal "live somewhere" now sounds biblical or literary, while *dwell on* something is everyday American speech — *Don't dwell on it.*
**Verb type:** action in *dwell on*, a sustained mental activity that takes the progressive freely (*he's dwelling on it again*); closer to **stative** in the literary *live, reside* and *be lodged in* senses, which describe a standing situation (*they dwell in a cabin*, *a resentment dwelt in him*) and sit awkwardly in the progressive.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense — the verb never takes a bare object and always leans on a preposition: *dwell on one bad review*, *dwell in that valley*, *dwell in him*. ✗ *She dwelt the subject* is impossible.
**Passive:** **restricted** — no ordinary passive, since there is no object; only the formal prepositional passive of sense 1 is available: *the budget was dwelt on far too long*. The residence senses have none at all: ✗ *the cabin is dwelt in by them*.

To keep thinking or talking about something, or — in older and literary use — to live somewhere.

**1. Dwell on, dwell upon.** Everyday American English and by far the most common use of the verb. Usually about something painful you would be better off letting go. (зацикливаться, останавливаться на (чём-л.))

1. Don't *dwell* on one bad review.
2. She *dwelt* on the subject for most of the drive home.
3. He *dwells* on every mistake he has ever made.
4. The speech *had dwelt* so long on the budget that it lost the room.

**2. Live, reside.** The original sense, now literary or biblical in American English — *live* is the ordinary word. Perfectly clear, just elevated. (обитать, проживать)

5. They *dwell* in a cabin two miles from the nearest paved road.
6. Her people *dwelt* in that valley for four generations.
7. He *has dwelt* in the same apartment since 1994.

**3. Be lodged in, persist within.** Figurative extension of the *live* sense: a feeling or quality stays inside a person or a place. Literary in register. (жить, таиться (в душе))

8. A quiet resentment *dwelt* in him for years.
9. Something like hope *has dwelt* in that congregation through worse than this.

**See also:** [indwell](#indwell)

### enwrap

**Pronunciation:** /ɛnˈræp/ &middot; *en-RAP*
**Русский:** завёртывать, укутывать
**Forms:** *enwrap* /ɛnˈræp/ &middot; *enwrapped* /ɛnˈræpt/ &middot; *enwrapped* /ɛnˈræpt/ (archaic *enwrapt* /ɛnˈræpt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a literary prefixed form of *wrap*; Americans write *wrapped* or *enveloped*
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *enwraps* &middot; **-ing form:** *enwrapping* (the p doubles)
**Contrast:** *Enwrap* is [wrap](#wrap) with a literary prefix and it inflects exactly like it. It is rare enough in American English that *wrapped* or *enveloped* is almost always the better word. The old spelling *enwrapt* is also the reason people confuse it with *rapt* "absorbed, entranced," which is a different word entirely and has nothing to do with wrapping.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, the literary twin of *wrap*: fog, cloth, or darkness closes over something (*silence has enwrapped the valley*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory, usually with *in*: *she enwrapped the child in a quilt*, ✗ *museums enwrap before they ship* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes**, and sense 2 lives in the passive: *he was enwrapped in the argument and never heard the bell*. Sense 1 passivizes normally too — *fragile pieces are enwrapped in acid-free tissue before shipping*.

To wrap something completely around or inside something else, or figuratively to absorb someone's whole attention — literary in both senses.

**1. Wrap completely, envelop.** Literary; in ordinary American prose *wrap*, *envelop*, or *swaddle* is the natural choice. Often of fog, cloth, or darkness covering something entirely. (окутывать, укутывать)

1. Museums *enwrap* fragile pieces in acid-free tissue before they ship them.
2. She *enwrapped* the child in a quilt and carried her inside.
3. Silence *has enwrapped* the valley since the mill closed.

**2. Absorb entirely, engross.** Usually passive — *enwrapped in thought*. Rare and old-fashioned, and constantly confused with *rapt* 'entranced', which is a separate word; *enwrapt* is only an archaic spelling of this verb. (поглощать, увлекать)

4. He *was enwrapped* in the argument and never heard the bell.
5. The book *had enwrapped* her so completely that she missed her stop.

**See also:** [wrap](#wrap)

### indwell

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈdwɛl/ &middot; *in-DWELL*
**Русский:** обитать, проживать
**Forms:** *indwell* /ɪnˈdwɛl/ &middot; *indwelt* /ɪnˈdwɛlt/ &middot; *indwelt* /ɪnˈdwɛlt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — theology and medicine only, and almost always as the adjective *indwelling*
**Pattern:** base + -t &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *indwells* &middot; **-ing form:** *indwelling*
**Contrast:** *Indwell* follows [dwell](#dwell) exactly — the past and participle are *indwelt*, not *indwelled*. It is a narrow word: outside theology and philosophy you will meet it almost only as the adjective *indwelling* (*the indwelling Spirit*, *an indwelling catheter*), and the finite forms are genuinely rare.
**Verb type:** **stative** — it reports presence within, not an act, so the progressive is out as a verb (✗ *grace is indwelling the believer*); the *-ing* form survives only as an adjective (*the indwelling Spirit*, *an indwelling catheter*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the thing inhabited is a direct object with no preposition: *grace indwells the believer*, *a stubbornness indwelt every sentence she wrote*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the participial passive is standard inside theology (*believers indwelt by the Spirit*), but a full clausal passive is rare and the extended sense resists it: ✗ *every sentence was indwelt by a quiet stubbornness*.

To live or be present within something, especially in a spiritual sense — a narrow word met mostly as the adjective *indwelling*.

**1. Be present within (spiritually).** Theological vocabulary: the Spirit, grace, or God indwells a believer. The finite forms are genuinely rare, and the past and participle are *indwelt*, never *indwelled*. (пребывать, обитать (в ком-л.))

1. Theologians argue about how grace can *indwell* a person at all.
2. The doctrine holds that grace *indwells* the believer.
3. Older translations say the Spirit *indwelt* the apostles.
4. That conviction *has indwelt* the community for three centuries.

**2. Pervade, be lodged in.** Extended literary use for a quality or mood that seems to live inside a person, a text, or a building. (пронизывать, таиться внутри)

5. A quiet stubbornness *indwelt* every sentence she wrote.
6. Something like grief *had indwelt* the house long before we bought it.

**See also:** [dwell](#dwell)

### learn

**Pronunciation:** /lɝn/ &middot; *LURN*
**Русский:** изучать, учить; узнавать
**Forms:** *learn* /lɝn/ &middot; *learned* /lɝnd/ &middot; *learned* /lɝnd/ (British *learnt* /lɝnt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (British base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *learns* &middot; **-ing form:** *learning*
**Contrast:** *Learned* is the American form; *learnt* is British and reads as a typo in American prose. Then watch the second *learned*: the adjective meaning "scholarly" has two syllables, /ˈlɝnɪd/ — *a learned professor*, *my learned colleague* — while the verb always has one, /lɝnd/. And *learn* cannot mean "teach": ✗ *She learned me to drive* is nonstandard; the sentence is *She taught me to drive*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all senses, including the mental ones, and it takes the progressive freely (*she's learning Spanish*, *we're still learning*). Not stative, despite naming a change in what you know.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with a subject, skill, text, or *that*-clause (*he learned Spanish*, *she learned the whole part*, *they learned that the grant would not be renewed*), intransitive when the object is understood (*kids learn fast*, *he never learned*). Nonstandard sense 6 is even ditransitive: *his uncle learned him to shoot* — dialect only, ✗ in standard prose.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural with a text or skill and no agent (*the lines were learned in four days*, *three new pieces have been learned this season*), and the *find out* sense passivizes impersonally in news style (*it was learned Friday that the grant would not be renewed*). A *by*-phrase is stilted: ✗ *Spanish was learned by him in eight months*.

To take in knowledge or a skill, or to find something out.

**1. Acquire knowledge or a skill.** The central sense, with a subject or skill as object. Note that *learn* is what the student does; the teacher *teaches*. (учить, изучать, осваивать)

1. Kids *learn* the alphabet before they learn to read.
2. He *learned* Spanish in eight months of night classes.
3. We *have learned* a lot from the first two rounds of testing.

**2. Find out, hear of.** Followed by *that*, *of*, or *about*. Slightly more formal than *find out*, and the standard verb in news writing. (узнавать, выяснять)

4. I *learned* about the closure from a neighbor.
5. We *learn* of most changes the same week the customers do.
6. By Friday they *had learned* that the grant would not be renewed.

**3. Learn to do something.** With an infinitive: gain an ability, or come to accept a way of living. *She learned to say no* is about habit as much as skill. (научиться (делать))

7. He *learned* to drive on a farm truck.
8. You *learn* to live with the noise.
9. I *have learned* to keep my mouth shut in those meetings.

**4. Memorize.** With a text, part, or piece as object, often with *by heart*. The result is recall, not understanding. (выучить наизусть)

10. She *learned* the whole part in four days.
11. The choir *has learned* three new pieces this season.
12. Actors *learn* their lines before the first rehearsal.

**5. Learn from; learn your lesson.** To be changed by an experience, usually an unpleasant one. The fixed phrase is *learn your lesson*, often sarcastic in the negative. (извлечь урок)

13. He never *learned* his lesson about lending money to family.
14. We *have learned* from every outage this year.

**6. Teach (nonstandard).** Dialectal and jokey only — *that'll learn you*, *I'll learn him*. Never write it in standard prose; the standard verb is *teach*. (учить (кого-л.) — просторечие)

15. That'll *learn* you to leave the gate open.
16. His uncle *learned* him to shoot before he was ten.

### smell

**Pronunciation:** /smɛl/ &middot; *SMELL*
**Русский:** обонять, чуять, нюхать; пахнуть
**Forms:** *smell* /smɛl/ &middot; *smelled* /smɛld/ &middot; *smelled* /smɛld/ (British *smelt* /smɛlt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (British base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *smells* &middot; **-ing form:** *smelling*
**Contrast:** American English uses *smelled*. *Smelt* is the British past, and in American English *smelt* normally means something else entirely: a small silvery fish, or the verb for melting ore to get the metal out (*smelt — smelted — smelted*, perfectly regular). Note also that *smell* faces both ways: *I smell smoke* means you perceive it, *the kitchen smells wonderful* means it gives the odor off.
**Verb type:** Three classes in one entry. **Stative** in the perception sense — a state of the perceiver, so the progressive is out and *can* fills in: *I can smell rain*, ✗ *I am smelling smoke*. **Action** in the deliberate sense 2, which does take the progressive: *she's smelling the milk*. **Linking (copular)** in senses 3–4, joining subject to a complement rather than an object: *the apartment smelled like burnt popcorn*, *fresh basil smells wonderful*, *that cooler smells*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the perception, sniffing, and figurative senses (*he smelled gas*, *she smells the milk*, *I smell a rat*), intransitive or copular in the give-off senses (*the room smells*, *it smells of paint*, *the plan smells of desperation*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — agentless report passives are fine (*smoke was smelled in the stairwell*), but the stative perception resists a *by*-phrase the way other perception verbs do: ✗ *rain is smelled by me before the clouds show*. The linking senses have no passive at all: ✗ *burnt popcorn was smelled like by the apartment*.

To perceive an odor, or to give one off — and figuratively, to sense that something is wrong.

**1. Perceive an odor.** The passive perception, usually with *can*: *I can smell rain*. A state verb here, so the progressive is odd — not *I am smelling smoke*. (чувствовать запах, чуять)

1. I can *smell* rain coming before I see the clouds.
2. He *smelled* gas the second he opened the door.
3. The dogs *have smelled* something under the porch.

**2. Sniff at deliberately.** The active version — you put your nose to something on purpose. Unlike sense 1, this one takes the progressive: *she's smelling the milk*. (нюхать, принюхиваться)

4. She *smells* the milk before she pours it.
5. He *smelled* the cork and nodded.

**3. Give off an odor.** With *like*, *of*, or an adjective — *smells wonderful*, *smells like smoke*. Here the subject is the source of the smell, not the person noticing it. (пахнуть)

6. The whole apartment *smelled* like burnt popcorn.
7. Fresh basil *smells* nothing like the dried kind.
8. The room *had smelled* faintly of paint for weeks.

**4. Stink.** With no complement at all, *smell* means smell bad: *this room smells*. Add an adjective and the negative meaning disappears. (вонять, дурно пахнуть)

9. That cooler *smells*; take it out to the garage.
10. The gym bag *has smelled* like that since June.

**5. Sense that something is wrong.** Figurative and informal but extremely common: *smell trouble*, *smell a rat*, *smell blood*. (чуять, подозревать)

11. I *smell* a rat in the way that contract was awarded.
12. The reporters *smelled* a story and started calling everyone.
13. Investigators *had smelled* trouble long before the audit began.

**6. Reek of, suggest (figurative).** *Smell of* or *smell like* with an abstract noun: the situation gives an impression, and almost always a bad one. (отдавать чем-л., попахивать)

14. The whole plan *smells* of desperation.
15. That settlement *has smelled* like a cover-up from day one.

### spell

**Pronunciation:** /spɛl/ &middot; *SPELL*
**Русский:** писать или произносить по буквам
**Forms:** *spell* /spɛl/ &middot; *spelled* /spɛld/ &middot; *spelled* /spɛld/ (British *spelt* /spɛlt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking

**Third person:** *spells* &middot; **-ing form:** *spelling*
**Contrast:** American *spelled*, British *spelt*. In American English *spelt* is a grain — an old kind of wheat sold in health-food stores — so *He spelt his name* reads as a mistake rather than a variant. The phrasal verb *spell out* means "explain in plain terms": *She spelled out the consequences.*

**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with a word, outcome, or person as object (*she spelled the street name wrong*, *that memo spells doom*, *two neighbors spelled her at the hospital*), intransitive when the ability itself is the point (*he can't spell*, *spell slowly for me*).

To give the letters of a word in order — and, figuratively, to mean or signal something.

**1. Say or write a word's letters.** The central sense, and the one behind *spelling bee* and *can't spell*. (писать или произносить по буквам)

1. He *spells* his last name for people over the phone every day.
2. She *spelled* the street name wrong on the envelope.
3. I *have spelled* it both ways and neither one looks right.
4. *Spell* it slowly for me and I will write it down.

**2. Of letters, form a word.** Here the subject is the letters themselves: *C-A-T spells cat*. Also used of acronyms that accidentally form a word. (составлять слово)

5. Those four letters *spell* her grandmother's name.
6. The initials *spelled* something unfortunate, so they changed the title.

**3. Mean, signal (spell trouble).** Almost always with a bad outcome as object: *spells trouble*, *spells disaster*, *spells the end of*. The subject is a situation, never a person. (означать, сулить)

7. Another dry summer *spells* trouble for the whole valley.
8. The 2008 crash *spelled* the end for a lot of small firms.
9. That memo *has spelled* doom for the project since the day it went out.

**4. Spell out (explain plainly).** To state something in terms nobody can miss, often with an edge of impatience — *do I have to spell it out?* (разъяснять, растолковывать)

10. Let me *spell* it out for you.
11. She *spelled* out exactly what would happen if we missed the date.
12. The contract *had spelled* out the penalty in the first paragraph.

**5. Spell out (write in full).** The editorial sense: give the whole word or number in letters instead of a symbol or an abbreviation. (писать полностью, прописью)

13. Style guides ask you to *spell* out numbers under ten.
14. He *spelled* out the state name instead of using the postal code.

**6. Relieve, take over for.** Chiefly American: to give someone a break by taking their work for a while. Related to the noun *a spell*, meaning a stretch of time. (подменять, сменять)

16. Two neighbors *spelled* her at the hospital so she could sleep.
17. Volunteers *have spelled* the crew every four hours.

12. Overtime costs *have spilled* over into next year's budget.

**Verb class:** irregular

**Third person:** *spoils* &middot; **-ing form:** *spoiling*

**Register:** everyday — *I got sunburned* is ordinary speech, though the finite verb is rare
**Pattern:** base + -ed / base + -t &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *sunburns* &middot; **-ing form:** *sunburning*
**Contrast:** Follows [burn](#burn), with the same American preference for -ed. In practice Americans rarely use the finite verb at all: *I got sunburned* and *I got a sunburn* are far more common than *I sunburned*. *Sunburnt* survives mainly as an adjective, and even there *sunburned* is the more usual American choice.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, ergative like its base *burn*: skin sunburns, or the sun sunburns it. No stative or linking use, and in practice Americans reach for *get sunburned* rather than the finite verb.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive with the person or the skin as subject (*pale skin sunburns in twenty minutes*, *we sunburn easily*), transitive with the body part as object (*he sunburned the back of his neck*, *years of roofing had sunburned his forearms*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — the participle is where the verb actually lives, and it reads as a state: *I got sunburned*, *the farmer's face was sunburned and lined*. An agentive passive is unnatural: ✗ *his neck was sunburned by the sun*.

To get, or to give someone, red sore skin from too much sun.

**1. Burn one's skin in the sun.** Works both ways: a person or a patch of skin sunburns, or the sun sunburns them. In practice Americans prefer *get sunburned* or *get a sunburn* to the plain finite verb. (обгорать на солнце)

1. Pale skin *sunburns* in twenty minutes on that beach.
2. He *sunburned* the back of his neck on the very first day.
3. We *sunburn* easily, so we go out early or late.
4. I *have sunburned* my shoulders every summer of my life.
5. By the third afternoon everyone *had sunburned* except the lifeguard.

**2. Weather, redden permanently.** Almost always the participle used as an adjective, describing skin darkened and roughened by years outdoors rather than one bad afternoon. *Sunburned* is American, *sunburnt* more British. (обветрить, задубить (кожу на солнце))

6. Years of roofing *had sunburned* his forearms to leather.
7. The farmer's face *was sunburned* and lined from forty seasons outdoors.

### toss

**Pronunciation:** /tɔs/ &middot; *TAWSS*
**Русский:** бросать
**Forms:** *toss* /tɔs/ &middot; *tossed* /tɔst/ &middot; *tossed* /tɔst/ (archaic *tost* /tɔst/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *tosses* &middot; **-ing form:** *tossing*
**Contrast:** *Toss* is a regular verb in American English; *tost* is a poetic spelling of *tossed* that a modern reader will simply take for a misprint. Its famous survival is Emma Lazarus's "the homeless, tempest-tost" on the Statue of Liberty, and even there it is doing period work. On the vowel: many Americans say /tɔs/ and many say /tɑs/, and both are standard.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and quick in every sense, from the casual throw to the judge's dismissal (*the judge tossed the case*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both**, and **ditransitive** in the throwing sense — *toss me the keys*, *he tossed her the ball*. Otherwise transitive (*toss the receipt*, *toss the salad*, *she tossed her hair*, *an appeals court has tossed two charges*), intransitive in *toss and turn*, *the boat tossed all night*, and *we tossed for the top bunk*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the case was tossed for lack of evidence*, *the salad should be tossed right before you serve it*, *a jacket has been tossed over the railing*. No passive of the restless-movement sense: ✗ *it was tossed and turned by him all night*.

To throw something lightly, and by extension to discard it, mix it, or move it around restlessly.

**1. Throw lightly.** A short, casual, underhand throw — nothing like the force of *hurl* or the aim of *pitch*. (бросать, кидать)

1. He *tosses* the keys on the counter the second he walks in.
2. She *tossed* the ball back over the fence.
3. Someone *has tossed* a jacket over the railing.

**2. Throw away, get rid of.** *Toss* or *toss out*, both everyday American speech where British English says *chuck*. (выбрасывать)

4. Just *toss* the receipt; we don't need it.
5. He *tossed* the leftovers without asking anyone.
6. I *have tossed* out three boxes of old paperwork this week.

**3. Mix lightly.** Cooking: lift and turn ingredients so a dressing or coating reaches everything without crushing it. (перемешивать (салат))

7. *Toss* the salad right before you serve it.
8. She *tossed* the pasta with oil and lemon.

**4. Flip a coin to decide.** *Toss a coin*, *toss for it*, and in sports *win the toss*. The noun *a toss-up* — an even chance — comes from here. (бросать жребий, подбрасывать монету)

9. They *tossed* a coin for the last seat on the flight.
10. We *have tossed* for the top bunk every summer since we were six.

**5. Move restlessly; be thrown about.** Of a sleeper — the fixed pair is *toss and turn* — and of a boat in rough water. (метаться; качать (о волнах))

11. He *tossed* and turned until the alarm went off.
12. The boat *had tossed* all night, and nobody slept.

**6. Jerk the head or hair.** A quick upward movement of the head, and always a gesture — pride, impatience, or dismissal. (вскинуть (голову), тряхнуть (волосами))

13. She *tossed* her hair and walked out.
14. The horse *has tossed* its head at every car that passes.

**7. Dismiss, throw out (a case).** American news and sports usage: a judge tosses a lawsuit, an umpire tosses a player out of the game. (отклонять, отменять (иск))

15. The judge *tossed* the case for lack of evidence.
16. An appeals court *has tossed* two of the three charges.

**8. Toss around (an idea).** To discuss possibilities loosely, without committing to any of them. (обсуждать, прикидывать)

17. We *tossed* around a few names for the shop.
18. They *have tossed* that idea around for years and never acted on it.

**See also:** [dip](#dip), [wrap](#wrap)

### unlearn

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈlɝn/ &middot; *un-LURN*
**Русский:** разучиться; забыть то, что знал
**Forms:** *unlearn* /ʌnˈlɝn/ &middot; *unlearned* /ʌnˈlɝnd/ &middot; *unlearned* /ʌnˈlɝnd/ (British *unlearnt* /ʌnˈlɝnt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing about habits and training, less common in speech
**Pattern:** base + -ed (British base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *unlearns* &middot; **-ing form:** *unlearning*
**Contrast:** Follows [learn](#learn): American *unlearned*, British *unlearnt*. The adjective trap is worse here than with the base verb, because *unlearned* has two pronunciations and two meanings — /ʌnˈlɝnd/ "not yet learned" (*an unlearned lesson*) and /ʌnˈlɝnɪd/ "uneducated, ignorant" (*an unlearned man*). Only context tells you which one you are reading.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and deliberate; the entry insists the effort is the point, and the progressive is normal (*they're still unlearning the old form*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — a habit, skill, or belief is obligatory as object: *unlearn bad form*, *she unlearned the accent she grew up with*, ✗ *the players spent a month unlearning* is incomplete without one.
**Passive:** **yes**, though usually agentless — *bad form has to be unlearned before anything better goes in*, *that idea has been unlearned by a whole generation*. The active infinitive stays commoner: *some things are harder to unlearn than to learn*.

To deliberately get rid of a habit, an assumption, or a piece of knowledge you already have.

**1. Get rid of a habit or skill.** Of trained behavior — form, technique, an accent — that has to come out before anything better goes in. The effort is deliberate; simply forgetting is not unlearning. (разучиться, отучиться)

1. New coaches spend the first month getting players to *unlearn* bad form.
2. She *unlearned* the accent she grew up with and later wished she hadn't.
3. By then the team *had unlearned* the shortcuts that caused the outage.

**2. Discard a belief or assumption.** Of ideas rather than skills: what you were taught and now reject. Common in modern writing about bias, education, and management. (отказаться от усвоенного, переосмыслить)

4. I *have unlearned* almost everything that class taught me about commas.
5. Some things are harder to *unlearn* than to learn in the first place.
6. A whole generation *has unlearned* the idea that the office is the only place work happens.

**See also:** [learn](#learn), [relearn](#relearn)

### vex

**Pronunciation:** /vɛks/ &middot; *VEKS*
**Русский:** досаждать, надоедать, дразнить
**Forms:** *vex* /vɛks/ &middot; *vexed* /vɛkst/ &middot; *vexed* /vɛkst/ (archaic *vext* /vɛkst/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — literary and legal; an American says *annoy* or *frustrate*, but *a vexed question* survives in writing
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *vexes* &middot; **-ing form:** *vexing*
**Contrast:** Regular: *vexed*. The spelling *vext* belongs to older poetry and nowhere else. The word is formal and a little old-fashioned in American speech — *annoy* or *frustrate* is the everyday choice — but *vexing* and the set phrase *a vexed question* are still current in writing.
**Verb type:** action — a psychological verb with the experiencer as object (*slow permits vexed the developer*). Sense 2 shades **stative**, describing a standing condition of a problem (*the same question vexes every committee*), and the participle *vexed* has drifted into adjective territory in *a vexed question*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the person or group troubled is obligatory: *it does not take much to vex him*, ✗ *small delays vex* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes** — *editors have been vexed by that inconsistency for years*, *mathematicians were vexed by the problem for a century*. The passive participle is in fact the commonest live form of the verb in American writing.

To annoy or trouble someone, especially over a stretch of time.

**1. Annoy, irritate.** Formal and slightly old-fashioned in American speech, where *annoy* or *bug* is the everyday word; *vexing* and *vexed* stay current in writing. (досаждать, раздражать)

1. Slow permits *vexed* the developer for two full years.
2. It does not take much to *vex* him before his first cup of coffee.
3. Small delays *vex* her far more than big ones do.

**2. Baffle, resist solution.** Of a question or problem that will not yield — the sense behind *a vexed question* and *a vexing issue*. The problem is the subject and the people working on it are the object. (ставить в тупик, мучить (о задаче))

4. The same question *vexes* every committee that looks at it.
5. That inconsistency *has vexed* editors since the style guide came out.
6. The problem *had vexed* mathematicians for a century before anyone cracked it.

**3. Afflict, harass (archaic).** The old strong sense — storms, illness, or enemies tormenting a person or a country. You meet it in the King James Bible and older literature, not in modern American prose. (мучить, изводить)

7. Storms *vexed* the coast all that winter.
8. A fever *had vexed* the camp for a month.

### wrap

**Pronunciation:** /ræp/ &middot; *RAP*
**Русский:** окутывать; завёртывать
**Forms:** *wrap* /ræp/ &middot; *wrapped* /ræpt/ &middot; *wrapped* /ræpt/ (archaic *wrapt* /ræpt/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base + -ed (archaic base + -t) &middot; **Group:** 8
**Third person:** *wraps* &middot; **-ing form:** *wrapping* (the p doubles)
**Contrast:** The w is silent, so *wrapped* sounds exactly like *rapt* "completely absorbed" and like *rapped* "knocked" — all three are /ræpt/ and no two are spelled alike: *wrapped in a blanket*, *rapt attention*, *rapped on the door*. The archaic spelling *wrapt* is what made that confusion possible; modern American English writes *wrapped* and nothing else.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, literal or figurative (*wrapped the leftovers*, *we wrapped up the audit*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the covering, winding, and finishing senses (*she wraps every gift*, *wrap the pipes*, *we wrapped up the audit*), intransitive in the film sense and in dressing warmly (*they wrap on Friday*, *wrap up — it's ten degrees out*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *every gift is wrapped in plain brown paper*, *the shoot had been wrapped two days early*. Sense 6 is passive-only in form: *he was wrapped up in the game*, *she has been wrapped up in that manuscript for months*. The idiom in sense 7 refuses it: ✗ *the numbers can't be wrapped my head around*.

To cover something by folding material around it — and figuratively, to finish, to enfold, or to be absorbed in something.

**1. Cover by folding material around.** Paper, foil, or cloth folded over an object until it is covered. The everyday sense. (заворачивать, упаковывать)

1. She *wraps* every gift in plain brown paper.
2. He *wrapped* the leftovers and put them in the fridge.
3. I *have wrapped* half the presents and hidden none of them.

**2. Wind around.** The material goes around the thing rather than over it — a scarf, a bandage, a cord, an arm. (обматывать, обвивать)

4. *Wrap* the pipes before the first hard freeze.
5. She *wrapped* a towel around her hair.
6. Vines *have wrapped* themselves around the whole trellis.

**3. Wrap up (finish).** To bring a meeting, a job, or a deal to a close. *Let's wrap up* is standard American office speech. (завершать, заканчивать)

7. Let's *wrap* this up before lunch.
8. We *wrapped* up the audit two days early.
9. The committee *has wrapped* up its work for the year.

**4. Finish filming.** Film and television: production ends. Hence the announcement *that's a wrap* and the noun *wrap party*. (закончить съёмки)

10. They *wrap* on Friday no matter where the schedule stands.
11. The crew *had wrapped* the shoot two days early.

**5. Wrap up (dress warmly).** Intransitive or reflexive: *wrap up warm*, *wrapped up in a parka*. Heard more in British speech but understood everywhere. (укутаться, одеться потеплее)

12. *Wrap* up — it's ten degrees out there.
13. The kids *were wrapped* up to the eyes and still cold.

**6. Be wrapped up in.** Passive only: so absorbed in something that nothing else registers. Also *wrapped up in each other*. (быть поглощённым (чем-л.))

14. He *was wrapped* up in the game and never heard the phone.
15. She *has been wrapped* up in that manuscript for months.

**7. Wrap your head around.** Informal fixed phrase for finally understanding something difficult, and usually negative — *I can't wrap my head around it*. (уложить в голове, осмыслить)

16. I still can't *wrap* my head around the numbers.
17. Nobody *has wrapped* their head around what the new rule actually requires.

**8. Wrap a car around something.** Colloquial American, and only in this frame: *wrap a car around* a pole or a tree, describing a violent head-on crash. (разбить машину (об столб))

18. He *wrapped* his truck around a utility pole on the county road.
19. Two kids *had wrapped* a car around that same tree the year before.
