Phrasal verbs: cast — cut#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs cast, catch, choose… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word — off, up, across, on to — and the pair almost never means what its parts mean separately: come across an old photo has nothing to do with crossing, and cut it out removes no material. The second thing to learn about each one is where the object can stand. Separable verbs let the object sit in the middle — cut the power off or cut off the power — and when that object is a pronoun it must sit there: cut it off, never ✗ cut off it. Inseparable ones never split: come across a photo, never ✗ come a photo across. Every entry below states which it is, because that one line is the difference between sounding native and sounding wrong.

cast about#

Pronunciation
/ˌkæst əˈbaʊt/ · cast-a-BOUT · the particle carries the stress, which is what fuses the two words into one verb
Русский
искать, лихорадочно подыскивать
Base verb
cast
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle), normally followed by for
Separable
no — nothing can stand between cast and about; what you are hunting for goes after for. ✗ cast an excuse about, ✗ cast it about for.
Transitive
no — intransitive; the thing sought is introduced by for
Register
formal — commoner in writing than in speech; in American speech cast around has largely replaced it
Verb type
action — dynamic; searching is something you do, and it takes the progressive freely: he was casting about for an excuse.
Passive
no — intransitive, and the prepositional passive fails too: ✗ an excuse was cast about for.

To search, in the mind or in a drawer, for something you need at once.

  1. He cast about for an excuse and found none.
  2. She cast about for the right word for the better part of a minute.
  3. The board has cast about for a replacement since March.
  4. I cast about in the junk drawer for a pen that still worked.
  5. Investors cast about for somewhere safer to park their money.

cast
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)

To throw something away; in the passive, to be stranded by a shipwreck.

1. Throw away, be rid of. Used of things and, more often, of feelings and chances. (отбрасывать, выбрасывать)

  1. He cast away the last of his savings on a bad idea.
  2. She cast her fear away and stepped out onto the ice.
  3. You don't get chances like that twice — don't cast it away.

2. Be shipwrecked. Nearly always passive, and the source of the noun castaway. (быть выброшенным на берег после кораблекрушения)

  1. The crew were cast away on a reef two hundred miles from land.
  2. He wrote as if he had been cast away rather than merely transferred to Ohio.

phrasal verb (verb + particle)

1. Shed, get rid of. Used of clothes, habits, rules, and people. (сбрасывать, отвергать)

2. Untie and leave (of a boat). No object; standard among American boaters. (отдавать швартовы, отчаливать)

  1. We cast off just after five and were out of the harbor by six.

3. Finish a knitted piece. The last row, where the stitches are closed. (закрывать петли)

  1. Knit two more rows, then cast off loosely.

To become popular, or to work out what is going on.

catch up#

Pronunciation
/ˌkætʃ ˈʌp/ · catch-UP · stress on up; the noun catch-up keeps it there in play catch-up
Русский
догнать; наверстать; поболтать, обменяться новостями
Base verb
catch
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
mostly no — in the everyday senses it takes no object at all (run to catch up), and the person or work involved goes after with or on. There is one older transitive use, "snatch up," which does separate: catch up your coat, catch it up. British speakers also split it in the "reach" sense — I'll catch you up — where Americans say I'll catch up with you.
Transitive
either — intransitive in the common senses, transitive in the dated "snatch up" sense and in the passive be caught up in
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense; closing a gap takes time and takes the progressive: I spent Sunday catching up.
Passive
restricted — nothing to passivize in the common intransitive senses (✗ was caught up by me for I'll catch up), but sense 4 exists only as a passive: two of the clerks were caught up in the investigation.

To close a gap — in distance, in work, or in news.

1. Reach someone ahead of you. (догнать, нагнать)

  1. Go on ahead; I'll catch up.
  2. She was two laps down and never caught up.

2. Get current on work or sleep. Usually with on. (наверстать)

  1. I spent Sunday catching up on email.

3. Exchange news. A whole American social ritual lives in this sense. (поболтать, узнать новости друг о друге)

  1. We should catch up sometime — it's been a year.

4. Be caught up in — absorbed or entangled. Passive only. (быть втянутым, увлечённым)

  1. He got caught up in the excitement and bid twice what he meant to.
  2. Two of the clerks were caught up in the investigation.

5. Snatch up quickly. Dated and literary; this is the one use that separates. (подхватить, схватить)

  1. She caught up her bag and ran for the gate.

Type
prepositional verb in the "find" sense, phrasal verb in the "seem" sense
Separable
no — come across an old photo, never ✗ come an old photo across, and a pronoun stays put too: come across it.
Transitive

To find something by chance, or to make a particular impression on other people.

1. Find by chance. The thing found is not being looked for; that is the whole point of the verb. (натолкнуться на, случайно найти)

  1. I came across an old photo of my grandfather in a shoebox.
  2. She came across the discrepancy while closing the books.
  3. If you come across it, hang on to it for me.

2. Come across (as) — give an impression. Followed by as plus an adjective or a noun phrase, or — with well and badly — by the adverb alone. (производить впечатление, казаться)

  1. He comes across as arrogant, but he's just shy.
  2. In the interview she came across very well.

3. Come across with — hand over what is owed. Informal, faintly underworld. (раскошелиться, выложить)

  1. They wouldn't ship until he came across with the deposit.
  2. Come across with the rest of it and we're done.
