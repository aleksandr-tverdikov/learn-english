# The i — a — u pattern

> Three different forms following one regular vowel shift: /ɪ/ in the base, /æ/ in the past, /ʌ/ in the participle — *sing, sang, sung*.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Ten verbs here climb the same vowel staircase: the base has /ɪ/, the past opens to /æ/, and the participle settles on /ʌ/ — *sing, sang, sung*; *drink, drank, drunk*; *swim, swam, swum*. Eight of the ten end in *-ing* or *-ink*, so once the shift is in your ear for one of them it works for the rest. In the Russian source this is group 2 (Все три формы разные: 1[i] 2[æ] 3[ʌ]). The step to guard is the middle one: the /æ/ form stands alone as a past tense, and the /ʌ/ form needs *have*, *has*, or *had* in front of it — though with *sink*, *shrink*, *spring*, and *stink*, American speakers also use the /ʌ/ form as a plain past, which is why those four need a closer look than the others.

---

### begin

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈɡɪn/ &middot; *bih-GIN*
**Русский:** начинать
**Forms:** *begin* /bɪˈɡɪn/ &middot; *began* /bɪˈɡæn/ &middot; *begun* /bɪˈɡʌn/
**Register:** everyday — one of the first verbs a learner meets and an American never stops using
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *begins* &middot; **-ing form:** *beginning* (the n doubles)
**Contrast:** The past *began* and the participle *begun* are the two forms learners swap. *Begun* never stands alone as a past tense — ✗ *I begun the report* — it needs *have*: *I have begun the report*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every live sense, of starting something (*we begin the meeting*) or of something starting (*the rain began*). Not stative, not linking; the frozen *to begin with* is a sentence adverb, not a working verb.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in sense 1 (*we begin the meeting*, also *begin to study* / *begin studying*), intransitive in sense 2 (*the rain began*, *flu season begins earlier*). The object is optional; *begin with / begin at* take a prepositional phrase, not an object.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive sense — *the new wing was begun in 1890*, *the report has been begun but not finished*; it sounds formal, and American speech usually reaches for *started*. No passive of the intransitive *the ceremony had begun*.

To start doing something, or to start happening — the neutral everyday word for the opening of an action, a period, or a process.

**1. Start doing something.** The core transitive sense, also used with *to* + verb or with the *-ing* form; the two are interchangeable in most contexts. Slightly more formal than *start*, which is the default in speech. (начинать, приниматься за)

1. We *begin* the meeting at nine every Monday.
2. She *began* studying Portuguese last spring.
3. He *has begun* writing his second novel.
4. He *begins* every class the same way.

**2. Start to happen, get under way.** Intransitive, with an event, season, or process as the subject. Remember that *begun* never stands alone as a past — ✗ *the show begun late*. (начинаться)

5. The rain *began* just as we left the house.
6. By the time we arrived, the ceremony *had begun*.
7. Flu season *begins* earlier every year.

**3. begin with — have as its first part.** Used of lists, words, books, journeys — anything with an order. The related *begin at* gives a starting point on a scale or a price. (начинаться с)

8. Every chapter *begins* with a quotation.
9. The tour *began* with a walk through the old town.
10. Tickets *begin* at forty dollars.

**4. to begin with (fixed phrase).** A frozen infinitive used as a sentence adverb, meaning either 'first of all' when listing reasons or 'at the outset' when saying how something originally was. Not a live use of the verb. (во-первых; для начала; изначально)

11. *To begin with*, the numbers in this report don't add up.
12. The engine was noisy *to begin with*, and it only got worse.

**5. not begin to — not come close to.** Emphatic and negative only. *Cannot begin to* means the speaker falls hopelessly short, not that they have failed to start. (даже близко не; и близко не)

13. I *cannot begin* to thank you for what you did.
14. That settlement doesn't *begin* to cover the damage.
15. Nothing *had begun* to prepare us for the noise.

### countersink

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaʊntərsɪŋk/ &middot; *KOWN-tur-sink*
**Русский:** тех. зенковать
**Forms:** *countersink* /ˈkaʊntərsɪŋk/ &middot; *countersank* /ˈkaʊntərsæŋk/ &middot; *countersunk* /ˈkaʊntərsʌŋk/
**Register:** rare — shop and machining vocabulary; most people know only the adjective *countersunk*
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *countersinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *countersinking* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** This is [sink](#sink) with a prefix, so it changes exactly the way *sink* does. The wrinkle is that *countersunk* is also the everyday adjective — *a countersunk screw* — so it is the form most people have seen, and the past *countersank* can look invented. It is not: *he countersank the holes*, *he has countersunk the holes*. Shop and machining vocabulary; you will rarely meet it elsewhere.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, a physical shop operation on a hole or a fastener. No stative or linking use; the participle *countersunk* doubles as an ordinary adjective (*a countersunk screw*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in both senses, and the object is obligatory — either the hole (*countersink the pilot holes*) or the fastener (*countersink the bolts*). ✗ *He countersank all morning* is incomplete without one.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the commonest form in shop writing — *every fastener on the panel has been countersunk*, *the holes were countersunk before assembly* (never ✗ *were countersank*). The passive blurs into the adjective: *the screws are countersunk*.

To widen the mouth of a drilled hole, or to seat a fastener in it, so that the head sits flush with or below the surface; shop and machining vocabulary.

**1. Bevel a hole for a screw head.** The core sense: cut a cone-shaped recess at the top of a pilot hole, usually with a countersink bit. Technical register — you will meet it in woodworking, metalwork, and assembly instructions, and almost nowhere else. (зенковать, раззенковывать (отверстие))

1. Always *countersink* the pilot holes before you drive the screws.
2. He *countersank* each hole so the hinge would sit flat against the door.
3. The shop *has countersunk* every fastener on the panel.

**2. Set a fastener flush or below the surface.** Said of the screw or bolt rather than of the hole. The participle *countersunk* doubles as the ordinary adjective — *a countersunk screw* — which is why the past *countersank* can look invented to people who have only seen the adjective. (утапливать (головку винта, болта))

4. *Countersink* the bolts so nothing catches on the deck.
5. By lunch we *had countersunk* all forty screws in the frame.
6. She *countersinks* the heads whenever the surface is going to be painted.

**See also:** [sink](#sink)

### drink

**Pronunciation:** /drɪŋk/ &middot; *DRINK*
**Русский:** пить
**Forms:** *drink* /drɪŋk/ &middot; *drank* /dræŋk/ &middot; *drunk* /drʌŋk/
**Register:** everyday — as ordinary as *eat*, from the kitchen table to the wedding toast
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *drinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *drinking* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** *Drank* is the past and *drunk* the participle — ✗ *I drunk the whole bottle* is one of the most common slips in English. *Drunk* is also the adjective meaning intoxicated (*he was drunk*), and before a noun that adjective becomes *drunken* (*a drunken argument*). Keep the participle where it belongs: after *have*, *has*, or *had*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, from the literal swallowing to the figurative *drink in the view*. No stative or linking use; the intoxicated *he was drunk* is the adjective, not the verb.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with the liquid as object (*she drank the whole bottle*, *he drank in every word*), intransitive in the alcohol sense (*he doesn't drink*, *they drank all through the reception*). The object is optional; *drink to* takes a prepositional phrase.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive sense — *the whole bottle was drunk before dinner*, *his words were drunk in by the crowd* (stiff but grammatical). Watch the clash with the adjective: *the wine was drunk* reads as the passive, *the guests were drunk* as intoxication. No passive of intransitive *he doesn't drink*.

To take liquid into the mouth and swallow it — and, with no object, to consume alcohol.

**1. Swallow liquid.** The neutral core sense, transitive or intransitive. *Drank* is the past and *drunk* the participle — ✗ *I drunk the whole bottle* is one of the most common slips in English. (пить)

1. I *drink* coffee only in the morning.
2. She *drank* the whole bottle of water in one go.
3. He *had drunk* two cups before the meeting even started.

**2. Consume alcohol.** With no object, *drink* means specifically to drink alcohol, often as a habit. The participle *drunk* is also the adjective for intoxicated — *he was drunk* — and before a noun that adjective becomes *drunken*: *a drunken argument*. (пить, употреблять спиртное)

4. He doesn't *drink*.
5. They *drank* steadily all through the reception.
6. She *has drunk* nothing stronger than seltzer in ten years.

**3. drink to — raise a toast.** Fixed with *to*; also *drink a toast to*. The standalone *I'll drink to that* means wholehearted agreement and needs no actual glass. (пить за (кого-л., что-л.))

7. Let's *drink* to the happy couple.
8. We *drank* to absent friends and then sat down.

**4. drink in — take in eagerly.** Figurative, of a view, an atmosphere, or someone's words. Slightly literary but common in descriptive prose and journalism. (упиваться, жадно впитывать)

9. She stood at the rail and *drank in* the view.
10. The crowd *drank in* every word of his speech.
11. He *has drunk in* the applause all season.

**5. drink up — finish off a drink.** Usually an imperative urging someone to hurry and finish. The related *drink something down* means to swallow it quickly, often unwillingly. (допивать (до дна))

12. *Drink up* — the cab is here.
13. They *drank up* and headed for the door.

### ring

**Pronunciation:** /rɪŋ/ &middot; *RING*
**Русский:** звенеть; звучать; звонить
**Forms:** *ring* /rɪŋ/ &middot; *rang* /ræŋ/ &middot; *rung* /rʌŋ/
**Register:** everyday — bells, phones, and alarms ring all day in ordinary American speech
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *rings* &middot; **-ing form:** *ringing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Two different verbs share this spelling. The bell verb is irregular — *ring, rang, rung* — but the verb meaning "encircle" or "put a ring on" is perfectly regular: *police ringed the building*, *the biologist ringed the bird's leg*. Within the bell verb, watch the usual swap: ✗ *I rung the doorbell*. The plain past is *rang*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the bell, phone, and resounding senses; also **linking (copular)** in the fixed *ring true / false / hollow*, where an adjective complement follows instead of an object (*his apology didn't ring true*). The separate encircling verb (*ring, ringed, ringed*) is dynamic too.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive when the bell or phone is the subject (*my phone has rung four times*, *the hall rang with laughter*), transitive when a person rings something (*she rang the doorbell*, *the cashier rang up our groceries*, British *he rang her twice*). The linking *ring true* takes a complement, not an object.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the bell was rung at noon*, *our groceries were rung up in under a minute*. **No** passive of the intransitive *the phone rang* or of the linking use: ✗ *true was rung by his apology*.

To make, or to cause, a clear bell-like sound — and by extension to resound, to telephone, or to strike the ear as true or false.

**1. Sound a bell, phone, or alarm.** Works both ways: the bell *rings*, and a person *rings* the bell. The plain past is *rang* — ✗ *I rung the doorbell*. (звонить; звенеть)

1. The church bells *ring* at noon every Sunday.
2. She *rang* the doorbell twice and waited on the porch.
3. My phone *has rung* four times this morning.

**2. Resound, be filled with sound.** Of a place full of noise (usually *ring with*), of ears after something loud, or of a sound that carries (*ring out*). (звенеть; оглашаться; раздаваться)

4. The hall *rang* with laughter.
5. My ears *have rung* ever since the concert.
6. A single shot *rang out* across the valley.

**3. ring a bell — sound familiar.** Idiomatic, and always vague — you half remember but cannot place it. The subject is normally a name, a title, or a fact. (напоминать что-то, казаться знакомым)

7. That name *rings* a bell, but I can't place it.
8. The address *rang* a bell the moment she said it.

**4. ring true / false / hollow.** Of a statement, excuse, or apology: strike the listener as genuine or not. The complement is a fixed short list — *true*, *false*, *hollow*. (звучать правдиво / фальшиво / неубедительно)

9. His apology didn't *ring* true.
10. Every promise in that speech *rang* hollow.
11. Her account *has rung* true with everyone who has heard it.

**5. ring up — total a sale.** American retail usage: enter a purchase on the register. Extended to totals in general — a studio *rings up* box-office millions. (пробивать (на кассе); набирать сумму)

12. The cashier *rang up* our groceries in under a minute.
13. The film *has rung up* three hundred million worldwide.

**6. ring someone — call by phone.** British and Australian, not American: an American says *call*. The same varieties use *ring up* and *ring back* the same way. (звонить кому-л. по телефону)

14. *Ring* me when you land, love. (British)
15. He *rang* her twice from the station. (British)

**7. Encircle, put a band on — regular verb.** A separate verb that only shares the spelling, and it is completely regular: *ring, ringed, ringed*. Never ✗ *rang* in this sense. (окружать; кольцевать)

16. Police *ringed* the building before dawn.
17. Researchers *have ringed* every chick in the colony.


**Phrasal verbs:** [ring back](19-phrasal-m-r.md#ring-back) &middot; [ring in](19-phrasal-m-r.md#ring-in) &middot; [ring off](19-phrasal-m-r.md#ring-off) &middot; [ring out](19-phrasal-m-r.md#ring-out) &middot; [ring up](19-phrasal-m-r.md#ring-up)

### shrink

**Pronunciation:** /ʃrɪŋk/ &middot; *SHRINK*
**Русский:** сокращаться; сжиматься; съёживаться
**Forms:** *shrink* /ʃrɪŋk/ &middot; *shrank* /ʃræŋk/ &middot; *shrunk* /ʃrʌŋk/
**Register:** everyday — sweaters, budgets, and economies all shrink; heard constantly
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *shrinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *shrinking* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** American English accepts two pasts. *Shrank* is the one edited prose prefers — *the sweater shrank in the dryer* — while *shrunk* is common in speech and in titles like *Honey, I Shrunk the Kids*. The participle is always *shrunk*: *it has shrunk*. *Shrunken* is an adjective only — *a shrunken head* — never ✗ *it has shrunken*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and an ergative pair: the same verb names the change of state (*the sweater shrank*) and the causing of it (*they shrank the logo*). No stative or linking use — *shrunken* is an adjective only, never ✗ *it has shrunken*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive when the thing gets smaller or a person recoils (*the lake has shrunk*, *the economy shrank*, *the dog shrank back*, *he never shrinks from a hard conversation*), transitive when someone reduces it (*the company shrinks its inventory*, *they shrank the logo*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive sense — *the department has been shrunk by half*, *the image was shrunk to fit the label*. No passive of the intransitive *the lake has shrunk*, and none of *shrink from*: ✗ *the conversation was shrunk from*.

To get smaller — in size, in quantity, or in nerve — or to make something smaller.

**1. Get smaller, contract.** Especially of fabric in heat or water. American English accepts *shrank* and *shrunk* as the past, with *shrank* preferred in edited writing; the participle is always *shrunk*, and *shrunken* is an adjective only — ✗ *it has shrunken*. (садиться, сжиматься, уменьшаться)

1. Wool sweaters *shrink* if you wash them in hot water.
2. My favorite T-shirt *shrank* two sizes in the dryer.
3. The lake *has shrunk* every summer for a decade.

**2. Make something smaller.** Transitive: deliberately reduce a size, a budget, a staff, or an image on a screen. (сокращать, уменьшать)

4. The company *shrinks* its inventory every January.
5. They *shrank* the logo so it would fit on the label.
6. Two rounds of layoffs *have shrunk* the department by half.

**3. Decline in amount or value.** The standard word in economic and statistical writing for a quantity going down over time. (сокращаться, падать (об объёме, доходах))

7. The economy *shrank* for two straight quarters.
8. Their lead *had shrunk* to three points by halftime.

**4. shrink from — avoid out of reluctance.** Figurative, of duties, conflict, or hard truths, and most often negative — *never shrinks from*. (уклоняться от, страшиться, избегать)

9. He never *shrinks* from a hard conversation.
10. She *has* never *shrunk* from telling people exactly what she thinks.

**5. Draw back, cower.** Physical recoil from fear or pain, normally with *back*, *away*, or *into*. (съёживаться, отпрянуть, вжаться)

11. The dog *shrank* back when he raised his hand.
12. The child *shrinks* into the corner whenever visitors arrive.

**See also:** [sink](#sink), [drink](#drink)

### sing

**Pronunciation:** /sɪŋ/ &middot; *SING*
**Русский:** петь
**Forms:** *sing* /sɪŋ/ &middot; *sang* /sæŋ/ &middot; *sung* /sʌŋ/
**Register:** everyday — the plain word for what a voice, a bird, or a kettle does
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *sings* &middot; **-ing form:** *singing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** *Sang* is the past, *sung* the participle. ✗ *She sung at the wedding* is wrong for a plain past — that is *she sang*. *Sung* belongs after *have*, *has*, or *had*, and in the passive: *the anthem was sung by a local choir*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, whether a voice, a bird, a kettle, or an informer is the subject. No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both**, and **ditransitive** in one pattern — transitive with the song as object (*she sang the national anthem*), ditransitive with a hearer added (*sing me a lullaby* / *sing a lullaby to me*), intransitive in the bird, informer, and *sing along* senses (*cardinals sing outside the window*, *one of the crew sang*). The object is optional.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is common — *the anthem was sung by a local choir*, *the lullaby was sung so quietly I almost missed it*. No passive of the intransitive senses: ✗ *it was sung by the kettle*.

To produce musical sounds with the voice — and figuratively to make any clear ringing sound, or to talk when you were supposed to keep quiet.

**1. Produce music with the voice.** The core sense, transitive or not, covering everything from humming in the shower to performing on stage. *Sang* is the past; *sung* belongs after *have*, *has*, *had*, and in the passive. (петь)

1. We *sing* the same two songs at every family birthday.
2. She *sang* the national anthem before the game.
3. That choir *has sung* in this hall for thirty years.
4. The lullaby was *sung* so quietly I almost missed it.

**2. (Of birds or things) make a musical sound.** Of birds, kettles, wind, and telephone wires — any clear sustained tone. Intransitive. (петь (о птицах); свистеть, звенеть)

5. Cardinals *sing* outside the window all morning.
6. The kettle *sang* on the back burner.

**3. sing along — join in.** With *along* or *along with*, of joining a song already in progress. The related *sing out* means to call something loudly. (подпевать)

7. The whole theater *sang along* with the chorus.
8. By the third verse everyone *had sung along* at least once.

**4. Inform on someone (slang).** Crime-story slang: talk to the police about your accomplices. Intransitive, often *sing to the cops*. (сдать сообщников, «запеть»)

9. They were sure he would *sing* once the deal was on the table.
10. One of the crew *sang*, and the whole case came together.

**5. sing someone's praises.** Fixed phrase only: praise someone warmly and publicly, usually to a third party. (расхваливать, петь дифирамбы)

11. Her old boss *sings* her praises to anyone who asks.
12. Every review *has sung* the restaurant's praises.


**Phrasal verbs:** [sing along](20-phrasal-s.md#sing-along) &middot; [sing out](20-phrasal-s.md#sing-out)

**See also:** [ring](#ring)

### sink

**Pronunciation:** /sɪŋk/ &middot; *SINK*
**Русский:** погружаться; тонуть; опускаться
**Forms:** *sink* /sɪŋk/ &middot; *sank* /sæŋk/ &middot; *sunk* /sʌŋk/
**Register:** everyday — boats, hearts, and free throws; used without a second thought
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *sinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *sinking* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** *Sank* is the standard past — *the boat sank* — but *sunk* is also acceptable in American English and turns up often in speech (*the boat sunk*); edited prose keeps *sank*. The participle is always *sunk*: *the boat has sunk*. *Sunken* is an adjective only: *a sunken living room*, *sunken ships*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and an ergative pair: it names the going down (*the ferry sank*) and the sending down (*a torpedo sank the destroyer*). No stative or linking use — *sunken* is an adjective only, as in *a sunken living room*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the downward senses (*stones sink*, *the sun sinks behind the ridge*, *my heart sank*, *the news finally sank in*), transitive in the causative and idiomatic ones (*sank the destroyer*, *sink the posts four feet*, *sank half a million into that restaurant*, *sank the free throw*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the destroyer was sunk by a single torpedo*, *two new wells have been sunk since the drought*, *the putt was sunk with two seconds left*. No passive of the intransitive *the ferry sank* or *it took a week to sink in*.

To go down — beneath water, to a lower level, or into a worse state — and, transitively, to drive or send something downward.

**1. Go under water, go down.** The core sense. *Sank* is the standard past — *sunk* is heard in American speech but edited prose keeps *sank*. The participle is always *sunk*; *sunken* is an adjective only, as in *a sunken living room*. (тонуть, погружаться, идти ко дну)

1. Stones *sink*; driftwood floats.
2. The ferry *sank* in under twenty minutes.
3. Divers found a freighter that *had sunk* in 1904.

**2. Send a ship to the bottom.** Transitive, with a torpedo, a storm, or a crew scuttling its own vessel as the agent. (потопить, топить)

4. A single torpedo *sank* the destroyer.
5. Storms *have sunk* more boats on this coast than any war.

**3. Drop to a lower level or position.** Of the sun, of ground and buildings settling, or of a body lowering itself — usually with *behind*, *below*, or *into*. (опускаться, оседать, садиться (о солнце))

6. The sun *sinks* behind the ridge around seven.
7. The old porch *has sunk* three inches on the east side.
8. She *sank* into the armchair without a word.

**4. Fall in value, amount, or spirits.** Figurative decline — prices, ratings, hopes. *My heart sank* is the fixed English way of saying a hope collapsed in an instant. (падать, снижаться; упасть (о сердце, настроении))

9. Property values *have sunk* since the plant closed.
10. My heart *had sunk* before he finished the sentence.

**5. sink into — pass into a state.** Of sleep, silence, despair, debt, or decay: a gradual slide into a deeper or worse condition. (погружаться, впадать (в сон, отчаяние, долги))

11. He *sank* into a depression that winter.
12. The town *has sunk* into a slow decline since the mill closed.

**6. Drive something down into a surface.** Transitive: posts into ground, teeth into food, a well into rock. This is the sense the derived verb *countersink* is built on. (вбивать, вонзать, погружать)

13. Crews *sink* the posts four feet into the clay.
14. The dog *sank* its teeth into the rope toy.
15. They *have sunk* two new wells since the drought.

**7. sink in — be fully understood.** Of news or a fact registering after a delay. Intransitive and always with *in*. (доходить (до сознания), укладываться в голове)

16. It took a week for the news to *sink in*.
17. The verdict *had* finally *sunk in* by the time she reached the car.

**8. sink money into — invest, often wastefully.** Informal, and usually implying the money is gone or the return is doubtful. Related: *sunk cost*, money already spent and unrecoverable. (вбухать, вложить (деньги) — часто впустую)

18. They *sank* half a million into that restaurant.
19. He *has sunk* every spare dollar into the boat.

**9. sink a putt or a shot.** Sports usage — golf, pool, basketball: put the ball exactly where it is meant to go. (закатить, забить (мяч в лунку, в корзину))

20. She *sinks* twenty-foot putts like they're nothing.
21. He *sank* the free throw with two seconds left.

### spring

**Pronunciation:** /sprɪŋ/ &middot; *SPRING*
**Русский:** вскочить; возникнуть
**Forms:** *spring* /sprɪŋ/ &middot; *sprang* /spræŋ/ &middot; *sprung* /sprʌŋ/
**Register:** everyday — *spring up*, *spring a leak*, and *sprang to his feet* are all ordinary speech
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *springs* &middot; **-ing form:** *springing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Edited American prose prefers *sprang* for the past — *she sprang to her feet* — but *sprung* is widely used in speech and is not an error. The participle is *sprung*: *the trap has sprung*. Note the fixed phrase *spring a leak*, whose past is *sprang a leak*, and the adjective *sprung* in *the mattress is sprung*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, of sudden elastic movement (*he sprang out of his chair*), of a mechanism releasing (*the trap sprang*), and of things appearing or originating (*coffee shops have sprung up*, *the project sprang from a conversation*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the leap, snap, *spring up*, and *spring from* senses, transitive in *spring a leak*, *spring someone* (get them released), and *spring something on someone* (*they sprang a surprise inspection on the department*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural in the surprise and release senses: *a surprise inspection was sprung on the whole department*, *she was sprung from the clinic a day early*. No passive of the intransitive *he sprang out of his chair*, and the fixed *spring a leak* resists it: ✗ *a leak was sprung by the old boat*.

To move suddenly and elastically — to leap, to snap, to burst into being — and to produce something on someone without warning.

**1. Jump, leap suddenly.** A quick movement from a standing or crouching start. Edited American prose prefers *sprang*, but *sprung* is widely used in speech and is not an error. (прыгать, вскакивать, бросаться)

1. Cats *spring* onto the counter the second you turn around.
2. He *sprang* out of his chair when the phone rang.
3. The goalie *has sprung* left on every shot tonight.

**2. Snap shut or fly open by elastic force.** Of doors, traps, latches, and springs releasing stored tension. The adjective *sprung* describes a mattress or a suspension that has lost its bounce. (распрямляться, захлопываться, срабатывать (о пружине))

4. Old hinges *spring* back harder than new ones.
5. The trap *sprang* the moment he touched the bait.
6. The screen door *had sprung* shut behind her.

**3. spring up — appear suddenly.** Of businesses, buildings, rumors, and weeds — things that show up fast and in numbers. Nearly always with *up*. (возникать, вырастать (как грибы))

7. New coffee shops *have sprung up* all over the neighborhood.
8. Tent cities *sprang up* along the highway within a week.

**4. spring from — originate in.** Of ideas, movements, or feelings traced back to a source. The same sense covers water issuing from the ground, which is where the noun *spring* comes from. (происходить из, проистекать из)

9. The whole project *sprang* from a conversation at lunch.
10. Her caution *springs* from years of being burned.
11. Two creeks *have sprung* from that hillside for as long as anyone remembers.

**5. spring something on someone.** Present news, a question, or a demand with no warning — the thing sprung is normally unwelcome. (огорошить, преподнести неожиданно)

12. She *springs* the same question on me every year.
13. They *sprang* a surprise inspection on the whole department.

**6. spring a leak.** Fixed phrase: start leaking, of a boat, roof, or pipe. Extended to organizations that begin leaking information. (дать течь)

14. The old boat *sprang* a leak two miles out.
15. The roof *has sprung* a leak over the kitchen again.

**7. spring someone — get them released.** Informal: secure someone's release from jail, a hospital, or anywhere confining. (вызволить, вытащить (из тюрьмы, больницы))

16. His brother *sprang* him on bail the same night.
17. We *have sprung* her from the clinic a day early.


**Phrasal verbs:** [spring for](20-phrasal-s.md#spring-for) &middot; [spring on](20-phrasal-s.md#spring-on) &middot; [spring up](20-phrasal-s.md#spring-up)

**See also:** [begin](#begin), [ring](#ring)

### stink

**Pronunciation:** /stɪŋk/ &middot; *STINK*
**Русский:** вонять, смердеть
**Forms:** *stink* /stɪŋk/ &middot; *stank* /stæŋk/ &middot; *stunk* /stʌŋk/
**Register:** everyday — blunt and casual, but *that stinks* is constant American speech
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *stinks* &middot; **-ing form:** *stinking* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Both *stank* and *stunk* serve as the past in American English — *the fridge stank* and *the fridge stunk* are both heard, with *stank* the more traditional choice in writing. The participle is only *stunk*: *it has stunk all week*. The *-ing* form doubles as a casual intensifying adjective: *this stinking heat*.
**Verb type:** action in form but **stative in behavior** — it reports an ongoing property (smelling foul, being no good), not an event, so the simple present is the normal tense (*those old sneakers stink*, *our defense stinks this season*) and the progressive is rare in American English: ✗ *the fridge is stinking*.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every core sense — *the whole kitchen stank*, *the movie stank*, and in *stink of* the *of*-phrase is a complement, not an object (*the deal stinks of a payoff*). Only the phrasal *stink up* takes an object: *somebody stank up the break room*.
**Passive:** **no** — the verb is intransitive, so there is nothing to promote: ✗ *the kitchen was stunk*, ✗ *the movie was stunk by the cast*. Only *stink up* passivizes at all, and even that is marginal and informal: ?*the break room was stunk up again*, more naturally *got stunk up*.

To smell very bad — and, informally, to be very bad indeed.

**1. Smell foul.** Blunt but not vulgar, and much stronger than *smell bad*. Both *stank* and *stunk* work as the past in American English, with *stank* the more traditional written choice; the participle is only *stunk*. (вонять, смердеть)

1. Those old sneakers *stink*.
2. The whole kitchen *stank* of burnt garlic.
3. That basement *has stunk* since the flood.
4. The trash *had stunk* for two days before anyone took it out.

**2. Be terrible, be no good (informal).** Casual American usage about teams, movies, performances, and situations. *That stinks* is the standard sympathetic reply to someone's bad news. (быть отвратительным, никуда не годиться)

5. Our defense *stinks* this season.
6. He said the movie *stank*, and he was right.
7. Their last three albums *have stunk*, and they know it.

**3. stink of — reek of something suspect.** Figurative: give off a strong impression of corruption, money, or desperation. Always with *of*. (попахивать, отдавать (чем-то нечистым))

8. The whole deal *stinks* of a payoff.
9. The contract *stank* of favoritism from the first page.

**4. stink up — fill with a bad smell; ruin.** Literally to fill a room with stench; in sports talk, to play so badly that you spoil the game — *stink up the joint*. (провонять; испортить, загубить)

10. Somebody *stank up* the break room with fish again.
11. He *has stunk up* the joint for three games straight.

**See also:** [drink](#drink), [shrink](#shrink)

### swim

**Pronunciation:** /swɪm/ &middot; *SWIM*
**Русский:** плавать
**Forms:** *swim* /swɪm/ &middot; *swam* /swæm/ &middot; *swum* /swʌm/
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary word for moving through water
**Pattern:** i — a — u &middot; **Group:** 2
**Third person:** *swims* &middot; **-ing form:** *swimming* (the m doubles)
**Contrast:** *Swam* is the past and *swum* the participle. ✗ *I have swam a mile* is the standard error — the participle is *swum*: *I have swum a mile*. *Swum* is rare enough on its own that speakers reach for *swam* after *have*, but only *swum* is correct there.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, of moving through water (*I swim laps three mornings a week*) and, figuratively, of being awash (*the plate swam in gravy*) or feeling dizzy (*my head swims*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — usually intransitive (*she swam across the lake*, *the room swam for a second*), transitive when the distance or the water crossed is the object (*swim the Channel*, *swim two miles*, *nobody had swum that stretch of river*). The object is optional and only appears in that crossing sense.
**Passive:** **restricted** — possible only in the transitive crossing sense, and mostly in sports writing: *that stretch of river hadn't been swum in years*, *the Channel has been swum by hundreds of people*. No passive of the intransitive uses: ✗ *the lake was swum across by her*, ✗ *the room was swum*.

To move through water under your own power — and figuratively to be awash in something, or to feel the world spinning.

**1. Move through water.** The core sense, of people and animals alike. *Swam* is the past and *swum* the participle — ✗ *I have swam a mile* is the standard error. (плавать, плыть)

1. I *swim* laps three mornings a week.
2. She *swam* across the lake and back before breakfast.
3. He *has swum* competitively since he was six.

**2. Cross or cover by swimming.** Transitive, with the distance or the body of water as the object — *swim the Channel*, *swim two miles*. (переплывать, проплывать (дистанцию))

4. She wants to *swim* the Channel before she turns fifty.
5. Nobody *had swum* that stretch of river in years.

**3. Be awash, float in liquid.** Of food or objects surrounded by far too much liquid, normally *swimming in*. Extended to being overwhelmed: *swimming in paperwork*. (плавать в чём-л., быть залитым)

6. The fries came *swimming* in grease.
7. The whole plate *swam* in gravy.

**4. (Of the head or the room) spin.** Of dizziness — the head *swims*, or the room *swims* around you. Common after standing up fast, after a blow, or after too much information. (кружиться (о голове); плыть перед глазами)

8. My head *swims* whenever I stand up too fast.
9. The room *swam* for a second and then steadied.
10. Her head *had swum* all afternoon before she finally ate something.

**5. sink or swim / swim against the tide.** Two fixed phrases: manage entirely on your own with no help, and hold a position against prevailing opinion. (либо выплыть, либо утонуть; идти против течения)

11. New hires here *sink or swim*.
12. He *has swum* against the tide his whole career.

**See also:** [sink](#sink)
