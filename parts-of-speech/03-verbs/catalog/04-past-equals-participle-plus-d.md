# Past = participle, vowel change plus -d

> Like the previous group, with a -d added to the changed stem.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Every verb here builds one form that serves as both the past and the participle: the stem vowel shifts, and a **-d** lands on the end of it. *Tell* becomes *told*, *hear* becomes *heard*, *flee* becomes *fled* — and that single form does double duty, in *I told her yesterday* and in *I have told her twice* alike (как №3, плюс окончание "d"). Because the past and the participle are identical, none of these verbs can produce the *began/begun* mistake; the work is remembering that the -d is there at all and that the vowel underneath it is no longer the one you started with. Twelve of the seventeen entries below are *tell*, *sell*, and *hear* wearing a prefix, so three verbs get you most of the list.

---

### can

**Pronunciation:** /kæn/ &middot; *KAN* &middot; weak form /kən/ in *I can go*
**Русский:** мочь; уметь
**Forms:** *can* /kæn/ &middot; *could* /kʊd/ &middot; *could* /kʊd/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** an — ould (a modal, not an ordinary verb) &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *can* — no -s, ✗ *he cans* &middot; **-ing form:** none; modals have no *-ing* and no infinitive
**Contrast:** *Could* is a past tense, not a real participle: English has no ✗ *I have could*. Where a perfect is needed, *be able to* takes over — *I have been able to drive since I was seventeen*. *Could* also serves as a present-tense politeness or a hypothetical (*Could you hold this?*, *We could leave now*), so a *could* in front of you is not automatically about the past. The l is silent, as in *would* and *should*. A separate and fully regular *can* means "put food in cans": *canned*, *canning*.
**Verb type:** **modal auxiliary** — never a main verb: it takes a bare infinitive (*can carry*), has no *-s*, no *-ing*, and no participle (✗ *he cans*, ✗ *I have could*, ✗ *to can*), so *be able to* fills the perfect and infinitive slots. A separate, fully regular action verb *can* means "put food in cans."
**Transitivity:** **neither transitive nor intransitive** in the ordinary sense — a modal takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object: *I can carry both bags*. The only nominal object it ever shows is the fixed *he can't help it*.
**Passive:** **no** — modals have no passive of their own; voice is carried by the verb they support (*the bags can be carried*), never by *can* itself.

The modal of ability, permission, and possibility: *can* in the present, *could* for the past and for polite or hypothetical use — and no participle at all, so the perfect is *have been able to*.

**1. Be able to (ability).** The core sense: the subject has the strength, the power, or the opportunity to do something. *Could* covers past ability and also softens a present claim. (мочь, быть в состоянии)

1. I *can* carry both bags if you get the door.
2. She *could* swim before she turned four.
3. We *could* not find a parking spot anywhere downtown.

**2. Know how to (learned skill).** Where Russian uses *уметь*: a skill acquired by learning rather than a one-time possibility. English does not mark the difference — *can* does both jobs. (уметь)

4. I *can* read Spanish well enough, but I *can't* speak it.
5. He *can* play three instruments.
6. By the end of the summer she *could* ride without training wheels.

**3. Be allowed to (permission).** Permission granted by a rule or by a person. *May* is the formal equivalent, but American speech uses *can* almost everywhere. (можно, иметь право)

7. You *can* park on this side after six.
8. Back then you *could* still buy a ticket at the gate.
9. Students *cannot* register until their fees are paid.

**4. Requests and offers — *Can you…?*.** *Can you* asks someone to do something; *Can I* offers or asks permission. *Could* here is politeness, not past time — the most useful thing to know in the whole entry. (не могли бы вы; можно мне)

10. *Can* you hold the door for a second?
11. *Could* you say that again a little slower?
12. *Can* I get you anything from the kitchen?

**5. Possibility — what sometimes happens.** A general or occasional possibility, not a prediction about one occasion; for a specific guess about the future English uses *may* or *might*. (бывает, может случиться)

13. Winters here *can* get brutal.
14. That intersection *can* be dangerous in the rain.
15. The old road *could* wash out after a heavy rain.

**6. *can't* — impossibility, confident deduction.** Negative *can't* is the strong "that is impossible" counterpart of *must*: not a lack of ability but a refusal to believe. In past time it is *can't have* or *couldn't have*. (не может быть, никак не может)

16. That *can't* be right — check the total again.
17. He *couldn't* have known; nobody told him.
18. She *can't* be more than twenty.

**7. *could* — hypothetical, suggestion, missed chance.** A present or future hypothetical, a soft suggestion, or — with *have* — something that was possible and did not happen. The time reference is not past, despite the form. (мог бы; можно было бы)

19. We *could* leave now if you want to beat the traffic.
20. You *could* have called instead of driving all the way over.
21. *Could* we move the meeting to Thursday?

**8. *can see*, *can hear* — perception.** With verbs of perception and mental state, *can* carries the load a progressive would carry elsewhere: ✗ *I am hearing you* comes out as *I can hear you*. (вижу, слышу (сейчас))

22. I *can* see the ocean from the top floor.
23. We *could* hear them arguing through the wall.
24. *Can* you smell that?

**9. *can't help* — be unable to stop yourself.** A fixed frame, always negative, followed by an *-ing* form or by *it*: ✗ *can't help to laugh*. (не могу удержаться, ничего не могу поделать)

25. I *can't help* laughing every time he tells that story.
26. She *couldn't help* noticing the missing signature.
27. He *can't help* it — he was raised that way.

**10. No participle — use *be able to*.** *Could* is a past tense, not a real participle: English has no ✗ *I have could* and no ✗ *to can*. Wherever a perfect, an infinitive, or a future is needed, *be able to* takes over. (смочь (в перфекте и инфинитиве))

28. Since the surgery he *has been able to* walk without a cane — never ✗ *has could*.
29. By March we *had been able to* clear most of the backlog.
30. She *could* not get through on the phone, and she *has not been able to* since.

### clothe

**Pronunciation:** /kloʊð/ &middot; *KLOHTH* &middot; the *th* of *the*, not of *thin*
**Русский:** одевать
**Forms:** *clothe* /kloʊð/ &middot; *clad* /klæd/ &middot; *clad* /klæd/
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing (*clothe and feed*), uncommon in speech, where an American says *dress* or *put clothes on*
**Pattern:** othe — ad &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *clothes* /kloʊðz/ &middot; **-ing form:** *clothing* (spelled like the noun *clothing*)
**Contrast:** In ordinary American English the past and participle are the regular *clothed* — *the shelter clothed and fed them*. *Clad* is the older form, and it survives mostly as a participial adjective, almost always with *in* or inside a compound: *scantily clad*, *ivy-clad*, *steel-clad*. Both are standard; *clad* is literary or technical, *clothed* is what goes in a report. Keep the verb *clothe* /kloʊð/ apart from the noun *cloth* /klɔθ/, whose *th* is voiceless and whose plural is *cloths*.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic in every sense, from supplying clothing (*the shelter clothes and feeds them*) to covering a surface (*snow clad the hills*). The participle *clad* now does most of its work as an adjective: *scantily clad*, *ivy-clad*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory: *clothe the children*, ✗ *the shelter clothed* on its own. The facing sense adds *in*: *they clad the new tower in blue glass*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the *be clad in* sense is normally passive — *the reactor core is clad in steel*, *every wall of the lobby had been clad in walnut*.

Put clothing on someone or supply them with it — and, as *clad*, cover a surface as if with clothing.

**1. Dress someone, supply clothing.** Transitive, and usually paired with *feed*. In this everyday sense the regular *clothed* is the normal American past and participle; *clad* would sound biblical. (одевать, обеспечивать одеждой)

1. The shelter *clothes* and feeds about forty families every winter.
2. They had almost nothing to *clothe* the children in.
3. By noon the volunteers *had clothed* and fed two hundred people.

**2. *be clad in* — faced, sheathed.** Technical and architectural, nearly always passive and followed by *in*: a surface covered with a protective or decorative material. (быть облицованным, покрытым (чем-л.))

4. The reactor core *is clad* in steel.
5. Every wall of the lobby *had been clad* in walnut.
6. They *clad* the new tower in blue glass.

**3. Cover as if with clothing.** Literary, of snow, fog, ivy, or light lying over a landscape. *Clad* is the usual form here. (окутывать, покрывать)

7. Snow *clad* the hills overnight.
8. Fog *clothes* the valley most mornings.
9. By dawn the fields *had been clad* in frost.

**4. *clad* as an adjective.** The participle does most of its modern work as an adjective, either with a modifier in front (*scantily clad*, *poorly clad*) or inside a compound (*ivy-clad*, *steel-clad*). (одетый (во что-л.))

10. Two scantily *clad* dancers came out for the finale.
11. The hikers arrived *clad* in bright rain gear.

**See also:** [shoe](#shoe), [seethe](#seethe)

### flee

**Pronunciation:** /fli/ &middot; *FLEE*
**Русский:** бежать; спасаться
**Forms:** *flee* /fli/ &middot; *fled* /flɛd/ &middot; *fled* /flɛd/
**Register:** neutral — standard in news and narrative (*fled the scene*); conversation usually says *run* or *get out*
**Pattern:** ee — ed &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *flees* &middot; **-ing form:** *fleeing* (the e stays — ✗ *fleing*)
**Contrast:** *Fled* and *flew* belong to different verbs. *Flee/fled/fled* is escaping; *fly/flew/flown* is air travel: *the family fled the country*, but *the family flew to Denver*. *Flee* takes its object bare — *fled the city* — where learners often write *fled from the city*; both occur, but the bare object is the one in edited American prose.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic throughout, including the literary use with abstract subjects: *sleep fled the moment the phone rang*, *all the color had fled her face*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with a bare object in edited American prose (*fled the city*, *fled the scene*, *fled the country*), intransitive or with *from* when the danger is left unnamed (*families flee whenever a hurricane is forecast*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but avoided: ✗ *the scene was fled by the driver* reads badly, and writers keep the active. The intransitive use has no passive at all.

Run from danger — leave a place or a person quickly in order to escape.

**1. Escape from danger.** The core sense. The object comes bare in edited American prose — *fled the city* — though *flee from* also occurs. Do not confuse *fled* with *flew*, which belongs to *fly*. (бежать, спасаться бегством)

1. Families *flee* the coast whenever a hurricane is forecast.
2. Thousands *fled* the city in a single week.
3. She *has fled* two wars and hopes never to do it again.

**2. Flee justice, abscond.** Legal and journalistic: leaving to avoid arrest, prosecution, or a court order. Common in *flee the scene* and *flee the country*. (скрываться, бежать (от правосудия))

4. The driver *had fled* the scene before officers arrived.
5. He *fled* the country a week ahead of the indictment.
6. Two of the suspects *have fled* the state.

**3. Avoid, keep away from.** Figurative and somewhat formal: shunning a subject, a duty, or a kind of company rather than a physical danger. (избегать, бежать от)

7. He *flees* any conversation that turns to money.
8. Young doctors *are fleeing* rural practice.

**4. Vanish, slip away (of abstract things).** Literary. Sleep, color, hope, and daylight *flee* rather than simply going away. (исчезать, улетучиваться)

9. Sleep *fled* the moment the phone rang.
10. All the color *had fled* her face.

**See also:** [lead](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#lead), [feed](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#feed)

### foretell

**Pronunciation:** /fɔrˈtɛl/ &middot; *for-TEL*
**Русский:** предсказывать
**Forms:** *foretell* /fɔrˈtɛl/ &middot; *foretold* /fɔrˈtoʊld/ &middot; *foretold* /fɔrˈtoʊld/
**Register:** rare — recognized but seldom produced; prophecy and literature, while an American *predicts* an election and *forecasts* the weather
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *foretells* &middot; **-ing form:** *foretelling* (nothing doubles — *tell* already ends in a double l)
**Contrast:** It inflects exactly like [tell](#tell); only the meaning is special. *Foretell* belongs to prophecy and literature — in everyday American writing you *predict* an election and *forecast* the weather, and you save *foretold* for oracles, omens, and anything that reads as fate.
**Verb type:** **action** with a human subject prophesying (*two economists foretold the collapse*); closer to **stative** with an inanimate subject that merely points to an outcome — *nothing in the data foretells a crash*, ✗ *the data is foretelling a crash*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — object obligatory, either a noun or a *that*-clause: *foretold his own retirement*, *foretold that the deal would fail*. ✗ *He foretold* alone is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the collapse was foretold and nobody listened*; *the ending had been foretold in the opening chapter*.

Say in advance that something will happen — the vocabulary of prophecy rather than of forecasting.

**1. Prophesy, predict.** Of a person or a text announcing the future. Formal and literary; for ordinary predictions American English uses *predict* for events and *forecast* for weather and numbers. (предсказывать, пророчить)

1. The old almanac claimed to *foretell* the winter from the thickness of the corn husks.
2. Two economists *foretold* the collapse and were ignored.
3. He *foretold* his own retirement a year before he announced it.

**2. Be a sign of what is coming.** With an inanimate subject — data, an omen, an opening chapter — meaning that it points to a later outcome. No intention is involved. (предвещать, предзнаменовать)

4. Nothing in the data *foretells* a crash.
5. The opening chapter *had foretold* the ending, and nobody noticed.
6. Everything that letter *has foretold* has come true.

**See also:** [tell](#tell), [retell](#retell)

### hear

**Pronunciation:** /hɪr/ &middot; *HEER*
**Русский:** слышать, услышать
**Forms:** *hear* /hɪr/ &middot; *heard* /hɝd/ &middot; *heard* /hɝd/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ear /ɪr/ — eard /ɝd/ &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *hears* &middot; **-ing form:** *hearing* (also the noun — *a hearing*)
**Contrast:** The spelling adds one letter, but the vowel changes completely: *hear* rhymes with *near*, *heard* rhymes with *bird* — /hɝd/, never ✗ /hɪrd/. Spoken aloud, *heard* and *herd* are identical. And *hear* is not *listen*: hearing happens to you, listening is something you do (*I heard the alarm* / *I listened to the message*), which is why *hear* resists the progressive — say *I can hear you*, not ✗ *I am hearing you*.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the core perception sense, which rejects the progressive: ✗ *I am hearing you* — say *I can hear you*. **Action** in the deliberate institutional senses *hear a case*, *hear the testimony*, *hear someone out*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object may be a noun, a *that*-clause, or a small clause (*nobody heard the alarm go off*). It is intransitive only with a preposition attached: *hear from the landlord*, *hear of that band*, *wouldn't hear of it*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the alarm was heard three blocks away*, *the committee heard testimony* → *testimony was heard for six hours*, *the senator was heard to say it*. Even the prepositional sense passivizes in the fixed *he has not been heard from since April*.

Take in a sound — and by extension get news, get word from someone, or listen formally to a case.

**1. Perceive a sound.** Involuntary perception, not the deliberate act of *listen*: hearing happens to you. It resists the progressive — say *I can hear you*, not ✗ *I am hearing you*. (слышать)

1. I *hear* the train from my bedroom every night.
2. Do you *hear* that clicking noise?
3. Nobody *heard* the alarm go off.

**2. Learn, get news of.** Followed by a *that*-clause or by *about*. The simple present often reports something quite recent: *I hear you're moving*. (слышать, узнавать (новость))

4. She *heard* the news from a neighbor.
5. I *hear* you're moving to Denver.
6. We *had heard* about the layoffs before the memo went out.

**3. *hear from* — get word from someone.** Contact from a person, in any medium. Extremely common in the negative perfect: *haven't heard from him*. (получать известия от кого-л.)

7. We *have heard* nothing from the landlord since April.
8. Did you ever *hear from* your sister?
9. He *heard from* the school on Tuesday.

**4. *hear of* — know that something exists.** Almost always negative or interrogative: *never heard of it*. Distinct from *hear about*, which is about news rather than bare existence. (слышать о ком-л./чём-л.)

10. I have never *heard of* that band.
11. Nobody *had heard of* him before the trial.
12. Have you *heard of* a town called Winslow?

**5. Hear a case, take testimony.** Legal and institutional: a judge, a board, or a committee formally receives argument or evidence. (заслушивать, рассматривать (дело))

13. The judge *hears* motions on Thursday mornings.
14. The committee *heard* testimony for six hours.
15. The board *has heard* three appeals this month.

**6. *hear someone out* — listen to the end.** Separable, and usually imperative or negative; it implies the listener was ready to interrupt. (выслушать до конца)

16. *Hear* me out before you say no.
17. They never *heard* her out.
18. Once he *had heard* them out, he changed his position.

**7. *won't hear of it* — refuse to allow.** A fixed negative frame with *will* or *would*, rejecting a suggestion outright — often a generous one. (и слышать не хотеть)

19. I offered to pay and she *wouldn't hear of it*.
20. My father *would not hear of* my dropping out.

**8. *I hear you* — I take your point.** A conversational American formula acknowledging what someone said, usually right before disagreeing or naming a limit. Informal. (понимаю тебя; слышу тебя)

21. I *hear* you, but the budget won't stretch that far.
22. She said she *heard* me, and then did exactly what she wanted.


**Phrasal verbs:** [hear about](18-phrasal-h-l.md#hear-about) &middot; [hear about, hear from, hear of](22-verb-preposition.md#hear-about-hear-from-hear-of) &middot; [hear from](18-phrasal-h-l.md#hear-from) &middot; [hear of](18-phrasal-h-l.md#hear-of) &middot; [hear out](18-phrasal-h-l.md#hear-out)

**See also:** [overhear](#overhear), [mishear](#mishear), [rehear](#rehear)

### mishear

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɪsˈhɪr/ &middot; *mis-HEER*
**Русский:** ослышаться
**Forms:** *mishear* /ˌmɪsˈhɪr/ &middot; *misheard* /ˌmɪsˈhɝd/ &middot; *misheard* /ˌmɪsˈhɝd/
**Register:** neutral — a prefixed derivative, but a live one: *sorry, I must have misheard you* is ordinary speech
**Pattern:** ear — eard &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *mishears* &middot; **-ing form:** *mishearing* (also a noun: *a common mishearing of the lyric*)
**Contrast:** Inflects like [hear](#hear). It means you heard something and got it wrong — not that you failed to hear it; for that, American English says *didn't hear* or *couldn't hear*. The verb is most at home in the perfect after *must*: *I must have misheard*.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic and momentary, a single mishap rather than the ongoing state of *hear*: *I misheard the gate number*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, often with an *as*-complement (*she had misheard "fifteen" as "fifty"*). The object drops when it is recoverable — *I must have misheard* is complete.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural in the *as* frame (*the name is regularly misheard as Cohen*), but the verb lives in the active perfect *must have misheard*, and ✗ *I was misheard by him* is barely used.

Hear something and get it wrong — take one word for another.

**1. Hear something incorrectly.** You did hear it; you simply got it wrong. Failing to hear at all is *didn't hear* or *couldn't hear*, never *mishear*. Often built with *as*. (ослышаться, неправильно расслышать)

1. In a crowded room it is easy to *mishear* a name.
2. I *misheard* the gate number and nearly missed the flight.
3. She *had misheard* "fifteen" as "fifty."
4. He regularly *mishears* song lyrics and sings them wrong for years.

**2. *must have misheard* — take the blame for a mix-up.** The verb's most common home is this polite perfect formula, where the speaker takes responsibility for a misunderstanding rather than accusing the other person of misspeaking. (должно быть, я ослышался)

5. I must *have misheard* — did you say Tuesday or Thursday?
6. If I *have misheard* you, please say it again.

**See also:** [hear](#hear), [overhear](#overhear)

### outsell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊtˈsɛl/ &middot; *owt-SEL*
**Русский:** продавать больше или дороже
**Forms:** *outsell* /ˌaʊtˈsɛl/ &middot; *outsold* /ˌaʊtˈsoʊld/ &middot; *outsold* /ˌaʊtˈsoʊld/
**Register:** rare — a prefixed derivative confined to business and trade writing; in conversation an American says *sold more than*
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *outsells* &middot; **-ing form:** *outselling*
**Contrast:** Three prefixed forms of [sell](#sell) sit close together and mean different things: *outsell* is about volume (you sold more than the other guy), [undersell](#undersell) is about price (you charged less), and [oversell](#oversell) is about promising more than you can deliver. The object of *outsell* may be the rival or the rival's product: *outsold Ford*, *outsold every other pickup*.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic, reporting a result over a period: *trucks outsell sedans in most of the country*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — object obligatory, and it names the rival or the rival's product, never the goods you moved: *outsold Ford*, *outsold every other pickup*, ✗ *outsold ten thousand trucks*.
**Passive:** **yes**, though the active carries most of the traffic: *sedans are outsold by trucks nearly everywhere*, *the hardcover was outsold ten to one*.

Beat someone or something in sales — move more units than the competition.

**1. Sell more than a competitor.** Of a company, a store, or a salesperson. The object is the rival, not the goods: *outsold Ford*, *outsold the whole team*. (продавать больше, чем (конкурент))

1. Their small team *outsold* the entire regional office last quarter.
2. No dealership in the state can *outsell* them on used trucks.
3. By December the franchise *had outsold* every other location.

**2. Of a product: sell in greater numbers.** Here both subject and object are goods, and the comparison is units moved. For price it is *undersell*; for hype it is *oversell*. (продаваться лучше, чем)

4. Trucks *outsell* sedans in most of the country.
5. The paperback will probably *outsell* the hardcover ten to one.
6. That one album *has outsold* everything else the label ever released.
7. Nothing on the menu *outsells* the breakfast burrito.

**See also:** [sell](#sell), [undersell](#undersell), [oversell](#oversell)

### overhear

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈhɪr/ &middot; *oh-ver-HEER*
**Русский:** подслушивать; нечаянно услышать
**Forms:** *overhear* /ˌoʊvɚˈhɪr/ &middot; *overheard* /ˌoʊvɚˈhɝd/ &middot; *overheard* /ˌoʊvɚˈhɝd/
**Register:** everyday — prefixed, but fully current: *I overheard them talking* is ordinary conversation
**Pattern:** ear — eard &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *overhears* &middot; **-ing form:** *overhearing*
**Contrast:** Inflects like [hear](#hear). In American usage *overhear* is accidental — you were there and the words reached you. Deliberate listening is *eavesdrop* (regular: *eavesdropped*), so the Russian gloss splits across two English verbs: *I overheard them arguing* is innocent, *I was eavesdropping* is not.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic but involuntary; deliberate listening is the separate regular verb *eavesdrop*. Commonly takes an *-ing* clause: *overheard two nurses talking*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — object obligatory: *in an open office you overhear everything*, *they had overheard the whole argument*. ✗ *I overheard* alone leaves the sentence hanging.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is a journalistic staple: *the senator was overheard telling an aide the deal was dead*, *he has been overheard complaining more than once*.

Hear what was not meant for you — by accident, not by design.

**1. Accidentally hear.** In American usage the accident is built into the word: you were there and the words reached you. Deliberate listening is *eavesdrop*, which is regular — *eavesdropped*. (нечаянно услышать, случайно подслушать)

1. In an open office you *overhear* everything.
2. I *overheard* two nurses talking about the schedule.
3. They *had overheard* the whole argument through the wall.
4. She *has overheard* enough to guess what the meeting was about.

**2. *be overheard* saying something.** The passive is a journalistic staple: someone is caught saying in private what they would not have said publicly. (быть услышанным, попасться на словах)

5. The senator *was overheard* telling an aide the deal was dead.
6. He *has been overheard* complaining about the new policy more than once.

### oversell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈsɛl/ &middot; *oh-ver-SEL*
**Русский:** распродавать
**Forms:** *oversell* /ˌoʊvɚˈsɛl/ &middot; *oversold* /ˌoʊvɚˈsoʊld/ &middot; *oversold* /ˌoʊvɚˈsoʊld/
**Register:** neutral — airline and marketing vocabulary, but *the flight was oversold* and *don't oversell it* come out without effort
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *oversells* &middot; **-ing form:** *overselling*
**Contrast:** Inflects like [sell](#sell). Two senses in American English: selling more of a thing than actually exists (airlines *oversell* flights) and praising something past what it can deliver (*don't oversell the feature*). Selling your whole stock is a different phrase — *sell out*, *sold out* — so a concert that *sold out* is good news and a flight that was *oversold* is not.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic in both the capacity sense (*airlines oversell popular routes*) and the hype sense (*don't oversell the feature*). The participle *oversold* is also a finance adjective: *the index looks oversold*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — *the venue oversold the show by two hundred seats*, *the company has oversold this product for years*; the object is normally required.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the usual form when the booking lands on you: *our flight had been oversold*, *traders had oversold the sector* → *the sector had been oversold by spring*.

Sell more of something than exists, or claim more for it than it can deliver.

**1. Sell more than you have.** Airlines, hotels, and venues deliberately book past capacity. Usually passive when it lands on you. Not the same as *sell out*, which just means the stock is gone. (продавать больше, чем есть в наличии)

1. Airlines routinely *oversell* popular routes.
2. The venue *oversold* the show by two hundred seats.
3. Our flight *had been oversold*, and four of us volunteered to wait.

**2. Overhype, promise too much.** Praising a product, an idea, or a person past what they can deliver. Often a warning to the speaker: *don't oversell it*. (перехваливать, преувеличивать достоинства)

4. Try not to *oversell* the software in the demo.
5. The company *has oversold* this product for years.
6. Every ad *oversells* something.

**3. *oversold* — a market that has fallen too far.** Finance only, and only in the participle: prices have dropped further than the fundamentals justify, so traders expect a bounce. The opposite is *overbought*. (перепроданный (о рынке, бумаге))

7. Traders *had oversold* the sector by early spring.
8. The index looks *oversold* after five straight down days.

Sell on what you bought instead of keeping it.

7. I had to *resell* the board on a plan they had already approved.
8. By Friday she *had resold* the whole team on the deadline.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌriˈtɛl/ &middot; *ree-TEL*
**Русский:** пересказывать
**Forms:** *retell* /ˌriˈtɛl/ &middot; *retold* /ˌriˈtoʊld/ &middot; *retold* /ˌriˈtoʊld/

Tell a story again — usually in a new shape.

**1. Tell a story again, in a new form.** Adaptation: myths, folktales, and films get retold for a new audience. The noun *a retelling* is as common as the verb itself. (пересказывать, излагать заново)

2. Let me *retell* it the way my grandmother did.
3. The story *has been retold* on stage, on film, and in a graphic novel.

**2. Recount something over and over.** Repeating your own account of an event. Note that *retell* takes the story as its object, never the listener: ✗ *retell them the address* — say *tell them again*. (рассказывать снова и снова)

4. He *retold* the joke badly and nobody laughed.
5. She *had retold* the accident so many times that the details had smoothed out.

### seethe

**Pronunciation:** /sið/ &middot; *SEETH* &middot; voiced *th*, as in *breathe*
**Русский:** кипеть; кипятить
**Forms:** *seethe* /sið/ &middot; *sod* /sɑd/ &middot; *sod* /sɑd/
**Register:** neutral to formal — the anger sense is at home in writing and understood by everyone; the strong forms *sod* and *sodden* are archaic
**Pattern:** eethe — od &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *seethes* &middot; **-ing form:** *seething*
**Contrast:** This is the one entry in the group whose old forms are no longer live. In modern American English *seethe* is a regular verb — *seethed*, *have seethed* — and *sod* survives only as a noun meaning a strip of turf. The old participle left a fossil behind as well: *sodden*, now purely an adjective meaning soaked through. Write *the crowd seethed*, never ✗ *the crowd sod*. The meaning has drifted too: literal boiling is now *boil*, and *seethe* usually describes anger that stays silent.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic, a continuing process, and comfortable in the progressive: *the sidewalk was seething with tourists*. Modern American forms are regular (*seethed*, *have seethed*); ✗ *the crowd sod*.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every live sense — *he seethes*, *the station seethed with commuters* — and what it churns with follows *with*. Only the dead archaic "boil something" sense was transitive: *Jacob sod pottage*.
**Passive:** **no** — an intransitive verb has no passive: ✗ *the meeting was seethed through*. The archaic transitive use left behind only the adjective *sodden*.

Boil or churn — and, in today's English, be furious without showing it.

**1. Be quietly furious.** The dominant modern sense: anger held in rather than let out. Here the verb is regular — *seethed*, *have seethed* — and ✗ *sod* is impossible. (кипеть от злости, негодовать)

1. Whenever the subject comes up, he *seethes*.
2. She *seethed* through the entire meeting and never said a word.
3. The newsroom *had seethed* for a week before anyone quit.

**2. *seethe with* — churn, be crowded with.** Of a place full of moving people, or of an atmosphere thick with rumor or resentment. Regular forms only. (кишеть, бурлить (чем-л.))

4. The station *seethed* with commuters.
5. The department *has seethed* with rumors since the merger.
6. By nine the sidewalk *seethes* with tourists.

**3. Boil, churn (of liquid).** The original literal sense, now literary — ordinary cooking uses *boil*. Modern usage is regular. (кипеть, бурлить (о жидкости))

7. You can watch the water *seethe* where the river hits the rocks.
8. The surf *seethed* over the rocks all night.
9. The pot *had seethed* down to nothing while she was on the phone.

**4. Archaic: boil something (*sod*).** Dead in modern English, and listed only because it is where this group's forms *sod / sod* come from. It survives as the noun *sod* (a strip of turf) and the adjective *sodden*. (варить, кипятить (устар.))

10. In the King James Bible, "Jacob *sod* pottage" — that is, he boiled it.
11. The old perfect ran on the same form: *by nightfall they had sod the meat*.
12. Three days of rain left the field *sodden*, which is all that remains of the old participle.

### sell

**Pronunciation:** /sɛl/ &middot; *SEL*
**Русский:** торговать, продавать
**Forms:** *sell* /sɛl/ &middot; *sold* /soʊld/ &middot; *sold* /soʊld/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *sells* &middot; **-ing form:** *selling*
**Contrast:** There is no ✗ *selled*, and *sold* covers both slots: *we sold it in June*, *we have sold three*. *Sold* is pronounced exactly like *soled*, as in resoled shoes. The noun is *sale*, not *sell*: *the sale of the house*, ✗ *the sell of the house*. And *sell out* / *sold out* means the stock is gone, which is not what [oversell](#oversell) means.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic throughout, including the figurative *sell someone on an idea* and *sell yourself* in an interview.
**Transitivity:** **both** — ditransitive or transitive with *to* (*sold a neighbor the truck* / *sold the truck to a neighbor*), and intransitive with the merchandise as subject, usually with an adverb or a figure (*winter coats sell fast*, *the album sold two million copies*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the house has been sold twice since we moved out*, *I am not sold on the new design*. The goods-as-subject use has no matching passive: *winter coats sell fast* is not ✗ *winter coats are sold fast*.

Hand something over in exchange for money — and, figuratively, get an idea or a person accepted.

**1. Exchange something for money.** The core transitive sense, with the buyer marked by *to*. There is no ✗ *selled*: *sold* fills both the past and the participle, and it sounds exactly like *soled*. (продавать)

1. We *sold* the truck to a neighbor.
2. The house *has sold* twice since we moved out.
3. They want to *sell* before the school year starts.

**2. Deal in, carry as stock.** Of a business: what it offers generally, not one transaction. The subject is the shop or the person whose trade it is. (торговать чем-л., держать в продаже)

4. They *sell* coffee beans by the pound.
5. He *sells* insurance in three states.
6. The corner store *has sold* newspapers since 1948.

**3. Of goods: be bought, move.** Intransitive, with the merchandise as subject. Usually with an adverb (*sell well*, *sell fast*) or a figure. (продаваться, идти (о товаре))

7. Nothing *is selling* this month.
8. The album *sold* two million copies in a year.
9. Winter coats *sell* fast once the weather turns.

**4. *sell out* — run out of stock.** The stock is gone, and for a show that is good news. Do not confuse it with *oversell*, which is booking past capacity. (распродать; быть распроданным)

10. By Friday the bakery *had sold* out of every loaf.
11. Tickets *sold* out in eleven minutes.
12. The tour *has sold* out three nights running.

**5. *sell out* — betray your principles or your side.** Informal and disapproving. *Sell someone out* is betraying a particular person for your own gain. (продаться, предать)

13. Fans said the band *sold* out the moment the ad aired.
14. He *sold* his partners out to avoid charges.
15. She felt they *had sold* out everything the campaign stood for.

**6. *sell someone on* — persuade.** Getting agreement rather than money. What you are persuading them of follows *on*. (убедить кого-л. в чём-л.)

16. I couldn't *sell* the committee on the idea.
17. She *had sold* everyone on the move within a week.
18. Good luck *selling* him on a six a.m. flight.

**7. *be sold on* — be convinced.** The passive of the previous sense, and mostly used in the negative: *I'm not sold on it yet*. (быть убеждённым в чём-л.)

19. I am not *sold on* the new design.
20. After the demo the whole team *was sold on* it.

**8. *sell off* — dispose of assets.** Business usage: getting rid of a division, a holding, or excess inventory, usually cheaply or under pressure. The noun is *a selloff*. (распродавать (активы))

21. The airline *sold* off its regional routes.
22. They *have sold* off most of the land already.
23. By 2019 the family *had sold* off everything but the mill.

**9. *sell yourself* — present yourself well.** In interviews and pitches. The matching failure is *sell yourself short* — undervaluing what you can do. (подать себя, показать себя с лучшей стороны)

24. You have to *sell* yourself in the first two minutes.
25. He *sold* himself short in the interview and knew it.
26. She *has sold* herself well at every stage of her career.

### tell

**Pronunciation:** /tɛl/ &middot; *TEL*
**Русский:** рассказывать
**Forms:** *tell* /tɛl/ &middot; *told* /toʊld/ &middot; *told* /toʊld/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *tells* &middot; **-ing form:** *telling* (also an adjective: *a telling detail*)
**Contrast:** No ✗ *telled*, and no past/participle swap to worry about, since *told* is both. The real trap is *tell* against *say*: *tell* needs a listener and takes one bare (*tell me*, *told her the truth*), while *say* refuses a bare listener (*said to me*). ✗ *He told that he was late*, ✗ *He said me he was late*. *Tell* is also the verb in a short fixed list: *tell the truth*, *tell a lie*, *tell time*, *tell the difference*, *tell them apart*.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic in the reporting, narrating, and instructing senses; closer to **stative** in *can tell* "perceive, work out," which leans on a modal and rejects the progressive: ✗ *I am telling the twins apart*.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive** at its core, with the listener bare and obligatory — *told me the flight had been canceled*, ✗ *told to me*, ✗ *He told that he was late*. Also plain transitive in the fixed list (*tell the truth*, *tell time*) and intransitive with *on* (*the years have told on that engine*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *we were told to stay in the car*, *I have never been told off like that in my life*. The listener is the natural passive subject: *she was told the news*, while *the news was told to her* is stiff.

Give someone information — say something to a listener, narrate it, instruct them, or make it out.

**1. Give someone information.** *Tell* needs a listener and takes one bare (*tell me*), while *say* refuses a bare listener: ✗ *He told that he was late*, ✗ *He said me he was late*. (говорить, сообщать кому-л.)

1. She *told* me the flight had been canceled.
2. Don't *tell* him about the party.
3. We *have told* the landlord three times.

**2. Narrate, relate.** Of a story, a joke, or an account, with or without a listener named. (рассказывать)

4. He *tells* the story better than anyone.
5. I *tell* my students the same thing every fall.
6. By then he *had told* the story to everyone in the building.

**3. *tell someone to* — instruct, order.** With an infinitive this is a plain order or instruction; the negative is *tell someone not to*, with *not* before the infinitive. (велеть, приказывать)

7. The officer *told* us to stay in the car.
8. I *have told* her twice not to leave the gate open.
9. *Tell* him to call me when he lands.

**4. Make out, distinguish.** Almost always with *can*, *could*, or *be able to*: perceiving a difference or working something out from evidence. Hence *tell the difference*, *tell them apart*, *tell by*. (определять, различать)

10. I can't *tell* the twins apart.
11. You could *tell* from her face that the news was bad.
12. There is no *telling* how long the repair will take.

**5. *tell the truth*, *tell a lie*, *tell time*.** A short fixed list where only *tell* works: ✗ *say the truth*, ✗ *read the time*. *To tell you the truth* also opens a sentence as a discourse marker. (говорить правду; лгать; определять время по часам)

13. He *told* the truth and got fired for it.
14. She is six and can already *tell* time.
15. To *tell* you the truth, I *had* never *told* anyone that story before.

**6. *tell on* — show the strain.** Intransitive, of stress, age, or long hours gradually having a visible effect on a person or a machine. (сказываться на ком-л.)

16. Twelve-hour shifts began to *tell* on him.
17. The years *have told* on that engine.

**7. *tell on someone* — inform, snitch.** Children's usage first, but it carries into adult talk about reporting someone to an authority. (ябедничать, доносить на кого-л.)

18. If you break it, I am *telling* on you.
19. His brother *told* on him.
20. Someone *had told* on them before the shift ended.

**8. *tell someone off* — scold.** Informal and separable: a sharp reprimand delivered face to face. (отчитать, отругать)

21. She *told* him off in front of the whole team.
22. I *have* never *been told* off like that in my life.

**9. *I told you so*, *you never can tell*.** Two fixed formulas: the first claims vindication after the fact, the second admits that nothing here can be predicted. (я же говорил; никогда не знаешь)

23. He couldn't resist saying *I told you so*.
24. You never can *tell* with that crowd.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndɚˈsɛl/ &middot; *un-der-SEL*
**Русский:** продавать дешевле других
**Forms:** *undersell* /ˌʌndɚˈsɛl/ &middot; *undersold* /ˌʌndɚˈsoʊld/ &middot; *undersold* /ˌʌndɚˈsoʊld/
**Register:** rare — a prefixed derivative kept alive by trade writing; ordinary speech says *cheaper than*
**Pattern:** ell — old &middot; **Group:** 4
**Third person:** *undersells* &middot; **-ing form:** *underselling*
**Contrast:** Inflects like [sell](#sell). Two senses, both of them "less": charging less than a rival (*they undersell every store in town*) and describing something more modestly than it deserves (*the brochure undersells the view*). Compare [outsell](#outsell), which is more units, and [oversell](#oversell), which is more promise than product.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic, comparing price (*the chain undersold every local shop*) or the fit between a description and its subject (*the brochure badly undersells the view*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — object obligatory, naming the rival or the thing described, not the price: *undersell them on lumber*, *undersells the view*, reflexive in *don't undersell yourself*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *every local shop was undersold by the chain*. The "understate" sense passivizes less comfortably and is usually left in the active: *reviewers have undersold how strange the film is*.

Charge less than the competition — or describe something as less than it is.

**1. Charge less than a competitor.** The comparison is price, not volume. Compare *outsell*, which is more units, and *oversell*, which is more promise than product. (продавать дешевле конкурентов)

1. Nobody in the county can *undersell* them on lumber.
2. The chain *undersold* every local shop and then raised its prices.
3. Warehouse clubs *undersell* supermarkets on nearly everything.

**2. Understate, describe too modestly.** Of a description that fails to do justice to the thing — a brochure, a title, a summary, a review. (преуменьшать, недооценивать (достоинства))

4. The brochure badly *undersells* the view.
5. Reviewers *have undersold* how strange the film is.

**3. *undersell yourself* — undervalue what you offer.** Reflexive: asking too little for your work, or presenting your own experience as smaller than it is. (недооценивать себя, продешевить)

6. He *had undersold* his own experience in the interview and knew it.
7. Don't *undersell* yourself — that work took months.
8. Their bid *has undersold* the team's expertise by a wide margin.
