Phrasal verbs: make — run#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs make, mean, meet… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is an ordinary verb plus a small word that together mean something you could not have worked out from either half: put off has nothing to do with putting, and you run into an old friend without touching her (фразовые глаголы). Two things decide whether you sound like a native speaker. The first is stress — with a true particle you lean on the little word, put it OFF, not PUT it off, which is what welds the pair into a single word; with a preposition the stress stays back on the verb, RUN into her. The second is separability: a particle lets the object sit on either side (put off the meeting, put the meeting off) and a pronoun object must come in the middle (put it off, never ✗ put off it), while a preposition never lets go of its object (run into her, never ✗ run her into). Every entry below tells you which kind you are holding.

Mean is the one base here that builds nothing: mean well and what do you mean by that are the plain verb doing plain work, so no mean entry appears below.

make for#

Pronunciation
/ˈmeɪk fɔr/ · MAKE-for · a preposition, so the stress stays back on the verb and for is reduced to /fɚ/
Русский
направляться к; способствовать, приводить к
Base verb
make
Type
prepositional verb
Separable
no — for never lets go of its object. ✗ made the door for, and with a pronoun the object still follows the whole phrase: made for it, never ✗ made it for.
Transitive
yes, there is always something after for
Register
everyday
Verb type
both — action in sense 1, dynamic physical movement toward a goal (she made for the exit). Sense 2 is stative, a situation standing in a relation to its result, and it resists the progressive: ✗ rain is making for a sloppy game.
Passive
no — the prepositional passive fails in both senses: ✗ the exit was made for, ✗ a long day is made for by two connecting flights.

To head straight toward something, or to be the thing that produces a certain result.

1. Head toward, move in the direction of. Physical movement with a destination in mind, and usually in a hurry — you make for an exit, a door, higher ground. (направляться к, устремляться к)

  1. As soon as the credits rolled she made for the exit.
  2. The dog made straight for the water.
  3. We made for higher ground as soon as the river came up.

2. Produce a result, contribute to. The subject is a situation rather than a person, and what follows is the outcome: makes for a long day, makes for good television. (способствовать, приводить к, обеспечивать)

  1. Two connecting flights make for a very long day.
  2. Rain in the fourth quarter made for a sloppy game.
  3. Cheap parts make for expensive repairs later.

make of#

Pronunciation
/ˈmeɪk əv/ · MAKE-of · stress on the verb; of is unstressed and said /əv/
Русский
понимать, истолковывать
Base verb
make
Type
prepositional verb, in the frame make something of something
Separable
no — of stays glued to its object, but the frame always puts a word in front of it: make nothing of it, what do you make of her? ✗ make of it nothing.
Transitive
yes, and doubly so — it needs both an object and an of phrase
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic but mental, the act of forming or voicing an opinion. No stative or linking use; the frame needs both a what / much / nothing object and an of phrase.
Passive
restricted — natural only in sense 2, where the quantity is promoted: a great deal was made of one careless sentence. Sense 1 has no passive at all: ✗ what is made of the new manager?

To form an opinion about something, or to treat it as significant.

1. Interpret, form an opinion. Nearly always a question or a negative — what do you make of…, I don't know what to make of… You cannot use it to report an opinion you already hold. (понимать, думать о, истолковывать)

  1. What do you make of the new manager?
  2. I didn't know what to make of her answer.
  3. Nobody could make much of the handwriting on the envelope.
  4. The jury will make what it likes of his silence.

2. Make something of it — treat as an issue. Confrontational in the first shape (want to make something of it?) and simply "give weight to" in make much of. (раздувать, придавать значение; «имеешь что-то против?»)

  1. "You're late." "You want to make something of it?"
  2. The papers made a great deal of one careless sentence.
  3. He never made anything of the fact that he had paid for it himself.

make out#

Pronunciation
/ˌmeɪk ˈaʊt/ · make-OUT · the particle takes the stress — make it OUT, not MAKE it out
Русский
разобрать, различить; справляться; выписывать (чек); целоваться
Base verb
make
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes — make out the address or make the address out, and with a pronoun the object must split the pair: make it out, never ✗ make out it. Senses 2 and 5 take no object at all, so nothing separates there.
Transitive
either, depending on the sense
Register
everyday; the kissing sense is informal
Verb type
action — dynamic in senses 2–5 (faring, writing out a check, claiming, kissing). Sense 1 is a perception verb close to stative: it lives with can / could and avoids the progressive, ✗ I am making out a figure.
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the signature could barely be made out, the check was made out to the county clerk, he was made out to be some kind of expert. Intransitive senses 2 and 5 have none: ✗ was made out at the DMV.

To manage to see, hear, or understand something; and, in other senses, to fare, to write out, to claim, or to kiss.

1. Discern, manage to see or hear. Almost always with can/could, and very often negative — the thing is at the edge of what your eyes or ears can handle. (разобрать, различить, расслышать)

  1. I could just make out a figure at the end of the dock.
  2. The signature was so faded that I couldn't make it out.
  3. Read the sign and tell me whether you can make the street name out.

2. Manage, fare, get on. Intransitive and thoroughly American, usually in a question about how something went. (справляться, преуспевать)

  1. How did you make out at the DMV?
  2. Their first store made out better than anyone expected.

3. Write out a check or a form. The money sense; the payee follows to. (выписывать (чек, документ))

  1. Make the check out to the county clerk.
  2. She made the receipt out for the full amount.

4. Claim, represent as. With that or with out to be, and always with a hint that the claim is false. (изображать, выставлять (кем-л.))

  1. He makes himself out to be some kind of expert.
  2. The article made out that the whole delay was the mayor's fault.

5. Kiss and grope. Informal American, intransitive, often make out with someone. Not used in careful writing. (целоваться, обжиматься)

  1. They were making out in the back row of the theater.
  2. She admitted she had made out with him at the party.

make up#

Pronunciation
/ˌmeɪk ˈʌp/ · make-UP · the particle takes the stress — make it UP; the noun makeup is stressed on the front, MAKE-up
Русский
выдумывать; составлять; мириться; навёрстывать
Base verb
make
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes in most senses — make up a story or make the story up, and a pronoun must separate them: make it up, never ✗ make up it. Two exceptions worth memorizing: the "constitute" sense never separates (women make up half the class, ✗ make half the class up), and the "reconcile" sense has no object at all.
Transitive
either, depending on the sense
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in senses 1 and 3–6 (inventing, reconciling, catching up, applying cosmetics, preparing). Sense 2, 'constitute', is stative and refuses the progressive: ✗ volunteers are making up half the staff.
Passive
restricted — fine in the dynamic senses: the story was made up on the drive over, the prescription was made up in ten minutes, she was made up for the stage lights. Sense 2 passivizes only in the fixed be made up of — the district is made up of six counties, never ✗ made up by six counties — and sense 3 is intransitive: ✗ she was made up with.

To invent something, to form a whole, to settle a quarrel, or to make good on what was missed.

1. Invent, fabricate. Stories, excuses, names, rules. The implication is that none of it is true. (выдумывать, сочинять, придумывать)

  1. He made up a story about a flat tire.
  2. She made the whole thing up on the drive over.
  3. Don't make it up — just say you forgot.

2. Constitute, form. Statistical and structural: the parts are the subject and the whole follows. This sense never separates. (составлять, образовывать)

  1. Volunteers make up almost half of the staff.
  2. Six counties make up the district.

3. Reconcile after a quarrel. Intransitive, often make up with someone. (мириться, помириться)

  1. They fought all week and made up on Sunday.
  2. She has never made up with her sister.

4. Make good on what was missed. Hours, work, a test, lost ground. (навёрстывать, отрабатывать, восполнять)

  1. I'll make up the hours on Saturday.
  2. He made the missed quiz up after school.
  3. The runner made it up on the last lap.

5. Apply cosmetics. Usually reflexive or passive — made up, make yourself up. (краситься, гримировать)

  1. She was made up for the stage lights, not for daylight.

6. Prepare, put together. Beds, prescriptions, lists, packages. (готовить, составлять, приготовлять)

  1. The pharmacy made up the prescription in ten minutes.
  2. Could you make the guest bed up before they get here?

  1. The proposal met with stiff resistance from the neighbors.
  2. His first novel met with almost no success.
  3. Her plan finally met with the board's approval.

Transitive
yes, always takes an object

To hand over money for something — and, idiomatically, to suffer the consequences of something.

1. Buy, settle the cost of. The plain money sense, which is transparent enough on its own but sets up everything below. (платить за, оплачивать)

  1. Her parents paid for the whole wedding.
  2. The grant pays for two more technicians.

  1. You'll pay for that remark.
  2. He paid for the extra beers with a whole ruined Sunday.
  3. The team paid for a slow first quarter.
  4. She has paid for that decision every day since.

3. Pay for itself. A fixed idiom: the thing saves enough money to cover what it cost. (окупаться, окупить себя)

  1. The new furnace paid for itself in three winters.

To clear a debt completely, to bribe somebody, or — with no object — to turn out to have been worth it.

1. Clear a debt in full. The difference from pay down is completeness: you pay down a balance but pay off a loan. (выплатить полностью, погасить)

  1. They paid off the house in eleven years.

2. Bribe. Informal, and always shady — the object is the person being bought. (подкупить, дать взятку)

  1. Somebody had paid off the inspector.
  2. You can't pay a jury off in a town this size.

3. Produce a result, prove worth it. Intransitive, with the effort or the gamble as the subject. (окупиться, дать результат, оправдать себя)

  1. All those early mornings finally paid off.
  2. The gamble on a second location has paid off.

4. Dismiss with severance. British, and dated even there; American English says lay off or buy out. (рассчитать, уволить с выходным пособием)

  1. Half the yard staff were paid off when the contract ended.
