Phrasal verbs: say — swing#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs say, see, seek… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word, and the pair means something neither half says alone: see through a liar has nothing to do with eyesight, stand for something nothing to do with standing, spring for dinner nothing to do with jumping. Learn each one whole, the way you learned dog; taking it apart only leads you to the wrong meaning. The second thing to learn with it is separability — whether the object may sit between the verb and the particle. Sit out the next dance can also be sit this one out, but sit through the lecture can never be ✗ sit the lecture through, and once the object is a pronoun a separable verb must split: shake it off, never ✗ shake off it. Every entry below answers that question outright, because that one line is the difference between sounding native and sounding translated. A note on the range: say, slide, spend, sting, and strive build no true phrasal verbs in American use and so contribute nothing here, while set and stand between them account for nearly a fifth of the list.

see about#

Pronunciation
/ˈsiː əˌbaʊt/ · SEE-a-bout · the verb keeps the stress here and the preposition is reduced
Русский
заняться (чем-л.), разобраться с; подумать насчёт
Base verb
see
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — the object follows the whole phrase: see about the tickets, see about it. ✗ see the tickets about.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object — a noun, an -ing form, or it
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic; attending to something is a task you perform, and it takes the progressive freely: she's seeing about a table for us. Sense 2 (we'll see about that) is a fixed formula, not a free verb.
Passive
no — the prepositional passive is not idiomatic here: ✗ a new passport was seen about, ✗ the leak has been seen about. Use see to when you need a passive.

Look into something and deal with it — and, said flatly, refuse it.

1. Attend to something, look into it. The everyday sense: take charge of a job that still needs doing. Close to see to, but see about leans toward finding out what is involved, while see to is getting it finished. (заняться, разобраться с)

  1. I need to see about a new passport before we travel.
  2. She went inside to see about getting us a table.
  3. Could you see about the leak in the basement?

2. we'll see about that — a flat refusal. With the stress on that, it announces that the speaker intends to stop whatever was just said. (ну это мы ещё посмотрим)

  1. He says he's dropping out? We'll see about that.
  2. You think you can park in my spot? We'll see about it.

Carry something to the end, carry someone through a hard stretch, or refuse to be fooled.

1. Carry something to the end. The object can go on either side, but see it through is the fixed shape most Americans reach for. (довести до конца)

  1. Whatever happens, I intend to see this project through.
  2. She saw the reforms through over four long years.
  3. It's a lousy job, but I'll see it through.

  1. Her sense of humor saw her through the divorce.
  2. Two crates of instant noodles saw us through the semester.

  1. His mother saw through the excuse in about a second.
  2. Voters see right through that kind of promise.

Take care of something so that it actually gets done.

  1. See to it that the doors are locked by ten.

Have your whole stock bought up — or betray people, or your own principles, for money.

1. Be bought up completely. Of tickets, stock, or a show; usually with no object, and very often in the passive-looking sold out. (быть распроданным)

  1. The first two shows sold out in an hour.
  2. Get there early; that place sells out by noon.

2. Betray someone for your own gain. Separable, and the pronoun splits it: sold us out. (предать, сдать)

  1. He sold out his own crew for a lighter sentence.
  2. She'd sell her own brother out to close a deal.
  3. They sold us out the minute the money showed up.

3. Abandon your principles for money. Disapproving, no object, and used constantly about bands, artists, and startups. (продаться)

  1. Fans said the band sold out when the song turned up in a car ad.
  2. You can call it selling out; I call it paying the mortgage.

/ˌswɛr ˈɔf/ · swear-OFF · the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one
Русский
зарекаться, завязать с (чем-л.)
Base verb
swear
Type
phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)
Separable
no in practice — what you renounce follows the particle, pronouns included: swear off sugar, I've sworn off it. ✗ swear it off.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object — a noun or an -ing form
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic and punctual: the moment of renouncing, not the abstaining that follows. He swore off cigarettes in January; the perfect carries the result: she's sworn off dating coworkers.
Passive
no. The thing renounced will not become subject: ✗ cigarettes were sworn off by him, ✗ sugar is sworn off every February. Keep it active, or say he gave up cigarettes.

Give something up and promise yourself you are finished with it.

  1. He swore off cigarettes in January.
  2. She's sworn off dating coworkers.
  3. I swore off it after the first hangover.
  4. They swore off cable and never went back.
  5. Every February somebody in that office swears off sugar.

Pronunciation
/ˈswɛr tuː/ · SWEAR-to · the verb keeps the stress here and the preposition is reduced
Русский
поклясться в, ручаться за
Base verb
swear
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — the object follows the preposition: swear to the time, swear to it. ✗ swear it to.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic, an act of vouching; most often under a modal in the hedge I couldn't swear to it, where it names an ability rather than an event.
Passive
restricted. Alive only in legal boilerplate — subscribed and sworn to before me this day, the affidavit was sworn to in open court. Outside that, no: ✗ the exact time couldn't be sworn to.

State that something is true and take responsibility for it; commonest in the hedge I couldn't swear to it.

  1. I couldn't swear to the exact time.
  2. She swore to it in court.
  3. He'd swear to anything for the right money.
  4. I think it was a Tuesday, but don't make me swear to it.
  5. Nobody would swear to having seen him there.

Base verb
sweep
Type
phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)
Separable
yes — sweep away the old rules or sweep the old rules away; with a pronoun it must split: swept it away.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in all three senses — the physical carrying off (the flood swept away the bridge), the wholesale abolishing (the new law swept away sixty years of rules), and the overwhelming by feeling.

Carry something off and destroy it, abolish it wholesale, or — in the passive — overwhelm someone with feeling.

1. Carry off and destroy. Water, wind, and landslides do this literally. (смыть, снести)

  1. The flood swept away the bridge.
  2. A wave swept it away in seconds.

2. Abolish completely. Of rules, systems, and traditions: nothing is left standing. (смести, покончить с)

  1. The new law swept away sixty years of rules.
  2. They swept the old system away in a single season.

3. be swept away — be overwhelmed by feeling. Passive only. (быть захваченным, потерять голову)

  1. She was swept away by the whole thing and agreed on the spot.

Русский

Base verb
sweep
Type
phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)
Separable
yes when there is an object — sweep up the glass or sweep the glass up, and a pronoun splits it: sweep them up. It also works with no object: I'll sweep up.
Transitive
either
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic: cleaning (he swept the shop up after closing), gathering fast (she swept the papers up and left), or being caught up in something bigger.
Passive
yes in the transitive senses, and the third is passive-only: the glass was swept up before anybody stepped in it, they got swept up in the excitement and bought two. No passive from the objectless use: ✗ it was swept up by me for I'll sweep up.

Clean up by sweeping, gather things together fast, or — in the passive — get caught up in something bigger.

1. Clean by sweeping. With or without an object; sweep up on its own means do the sweeping. (подмести, замести)

  1. Sweep up the glass before somebody steps in it.
  2. He swept the shop up after closing.
  3. You go ahead — I'll sweep up.

2. Gather things quickly. One armful, one motion. (собрать, сгрести)

  1. The papers were still spread across the table; she swept them up and left without a word.

3. be swept up in — be caught up in something. Passive, of excitement, a movement, a scandal. (быть втянутым, захваченным)

  1. They got swept up in the excitement and bought two.
