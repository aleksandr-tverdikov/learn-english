Phrasal verbs: deal — freeze#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs deal, dig, do… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle that together mean something the two parts do not: dig up a rumor has nothing to do with soil, and a deal that falls through never falls anywhere (значение целого не выводится из частей). The second thing to learn about each one is separability — whether the object can sit between the verb and the particle (dig the file out, dig it out) or has to follow the whole phrase (fall for it, never ✗ fall it for). Word order and stress go together here: a true particle takes the stress and can move, while a preposition does neither, which is why dig it OUT is a phrasal verb and DIG into it is not. The entries below are alphabetical by the whole phrase, not by the base verb.

deal in#

Pronunciation
/ˈdil ɪn/ · DEAL-in · in the trade sense in is a preposition and deal keeps the stress; in the card sense it is a true particle and takes the stress — deal me IN
Русский
торговать (чем-л.); включить в игру, взять в долю
Base verb
deal
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition) in the trade sense; phrasal verb (verb + particle) in the card sense
Separable
it depends on the sense. Trading: no — the gallery deals in prints, ✗ deals prints in, ✗ deals it in. Cards: yes, and the object always separates — deal me in, deal the new guy in, never ✗ deal in me.
Transitive
yes — a commodity in the trade sense, a person in the card sense
Register
everyday — plain business talk, and deal me in is plain speech
Verb type
action — dynamic in both senses: the habitual trade sense (the gallery deals in prints) and the one-time card sense (deal me in). No linking or stative use.
Passive
restricted — the card sense passivizes: I was dealt in on the second hand. The trade sense barely does: ✗ nineteenth-century prints are dealt in by the gallery.

To buy and sell a particular kind of goods, or to include someone in a card game or a scheme.

1. Trade in, buy and sell. Of a business and the goods it handles, and figuratively of what a person or a paper traffics in. In here is a preposition, so nothing can come between the two words. (торговать чем-либо)

  1. The gallery deals in nineteenth-century prints.
  2. His family dealt in grain for a century.
  3. That website deals in rumor more than news.

2. Include someone in the game. From the card table, and extended to any scheme with money in it. Here in is a particle and the person always sits in front of it. (включить в игру, взять в долю)

  1. Deal me in — I've got twenty bucks.
  2. She dealt the new guy in on the second hand.
  3. If there's money in it, deal us in.

To hand something out portion by portion, especially cards, or to hand out punishment.

deal
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — the object always follows with. ✗ deal the complaint with, ✗ deal it with. Only the passive moves the object out front: the complaint was dealt with.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in the handling and business senses (I'm dealing with vendor complaints), but stative in the be-about sense: ✗ chapter four is dealing with the war years.

To handle a problem or a person, to do business with someone, or — of a text — to be about a subject.

1. Handle, cope with. The core sense. The object is a problem, a complaint, or a difficult person. (справляться с, разбираться с)

  1. I deal with vendor complaints all morning.
  2. He dealt with the same landlord for six years.
  3. I can't deal with her today.

2. Do business with. Of the companies and people you transact with regularly. (иметь дело с, сотрудничать)

  1. We only deal with suppliers who deliver on time.
  2. The claim has already been dealt with by their office.

3. Be about (of a text). Of books, chapters, reports, and laws: what the text covers. (быть посвящённым, рассматривать)

  1. Chapter four deals with the war years.
  2. The report dealt with only one of the three sites.

dig
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
usually there is nothing to separate, because the verb takes no object. The one object it does take is heels, in the idiom, and there both orders work — dig in your heels / dig your heels in.
Transitive
usually no; the one object it takes is heels, in the idiom
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic in all three senses: starting to eat, entrenching, and the dig in one's heels idiom. Progressive is normal — management has been digging in for weeks.
Passive

To start eating, to entrench yourself, or to refuse to move from a position.

1. Start eating. Nearly always the bare imperative, said over a table of food. (приниматься за еду, налетать)

  1. Everybody grab a plate and dig in.
  2. The kids dug in before I sat down.

2. Entrench, take a defensive position. Military first, then figurative: a side that will not move. (окапываться; занять жёсткую позицию)

  1. The battalion dug in along the ridge and waited.
  2. Management has dug in and won't reopen the contract.

3. Dig in one's heels. A fixed idiom for refusing to budge. The object is always heels, and it can sit on either side of the particle. (упереться, стоять на своём)

  1. She dug her heels in and refused to sign.
  2. Once he digs in his heels, no argument moves him.
