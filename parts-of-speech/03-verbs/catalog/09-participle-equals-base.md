# Participle = base, past changes vowel

> The participle returns to the base form; only the past differs, by a regular vowel change.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

This is the tidiest group in the catalog (3-я равна 1-й, во 2-й "а" меняется на "э"). The first and third forms are spelled and said exactly alike — *come* and *come*, *run* and *run* — and only the past steps aside with one changed vowel: *came*, *ran*. Everything else in the group is one of those two verbs wearing a prefix, so *become*, *overcome*, *outrun*, *overrun* and *rerun* change in the same place and nowhere else. Because the participle looks like the base, the entire difficulty is remembering that the past does not: ✗ *I have came*, ✗ *she has ran*.

### become

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkʌm/ &middot; *bih-KUM*
**Русский:** становиться, делаться, превращаться
**Forms:** *become* /bɪˈkʌm/ &middot; *became* /bɪˈkeɪm/ &middot; *become* /bɪˈkʌm/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** o — a — o (ʌ — eɪ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *becomes* &middot; **-ing form:** *becoming* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** *Become* changes exactly like [come](#come), its base verb. The trap is the past form after *have*: ✗ *He has became a citizen*. The participle is the bare base — *He has become a citizen*.
**Verb type:** **linking (copular)** in the main sense — it joins the subject to a noun or adjective rather than acting on anything (*she became a citizen*, *the neighborhood became quieter*), and it is dynamic, so the progressive is fine: *he is becoming impatient*. The formal *suit / befit* senses (*that green becomes you*, *such language hardly becomes a judge*) are **stative** and reject the progressive: ✗ *that green is becoming you*.
**Transitivity:** **Linking** in senses 1–2 — what follows is a subject complement, not an object, and *become of* is a fixed intransitive pattern used almost only with *what/whatever* (*whatever became of the family next door?*). Senses 3–4 are **transitive** with a person as object, and that object is obligatory: ✗ *the short haircut became*.
**Passive:** **no.** A subject complement can never move to subject position: ✗ *a citizen was become by her*. The transitive *suit/befit* senses are stative and refuse it too: ✗ *you are become by that deep green*, ✗ *a judge is hardly become by such language*.

To grow or turn into something, to come to be a certain way; in formal use, also to suit or be fitting for someone.

**1. Turn into, come to be.** The core sense, used with a noun or an adjective. It works as a linking verb, so what follows describes the subject rather than receiving an action. (становиться, делаться, превращаться)

1. Kids *become* teenagers faster than their parents expect.
2. She *became* a US citizen last spring.
3. The neighborhood *has become* much quieter since the highway opened.
4. By 1995 the store *had become* the busiest one in town.

**2. Become of — happen to.** A fixed pattern, almost always in a question with *what* or *whatever*, asking about somebody's or something's fate. It never takes an ordinary subject. (статься с, случиться с)

5. Whatever *became* of the family that lived next door?
6. No one knows what *has become* of the original blueprints.
7. What will *become* of the shop once she retires?

**3. Suit, look good on.** Formal and slightly old-fashioned; you will meet it in writing far more than in speech. The everyday American equivalent is *look good on*. (быть к лицу, идти (об одежде, цвете))

8. That deep green *becomes* you.
9. The short haircut *became* her more than the long one ever did.
10. No hat she owned *had* ever *become* her so well.

**4. Befit, be proper for.** Formal, and most often met in the participle *becoming* or its negative in *conduct unbecoming an officer*. It says an action suits somebody's position, not their looks. (подобать, приличествовать)

11. He answered with a patience that *became* a man of his rank.
12. Such language hardly *becomes* a judge.
13. Her silence *had become* her better than any excuse would have.

### come

**Pronunciation:** /kʌm/ &middot; *KUM*
**Русский:** приходить
**Forms:** *come* /kʌm/ &middot; *came* /keɪm/ &middot; *come* /kʌm/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** o — a — o (ʌ — eɪ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *comes* &middot; **-ing form:** *coming* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** *Came* and *come* get swapped in both directions: ✗ *They have came already* and ✗ *He come home late last night*. Use *came* on its own for the past, and *come* only after *have/has/had* or *be*. Note also that the o here is said /ʌ/, not /oʊ/ — *come* rhymes with *some*, not with *home*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic verb of motion and arrival (*my parents come over on Sundays*, *spring has come late*). Also **linking** in a short fixed list only — *come true, come loose, come undone, come clean, come free* — which is not productive: ✗ *she came tired*.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every documented sense. Anything that looks like an object arrives through a preposition: *come from Ohio*, *come to forty-two dollars*, *come across her old letters*, *come up with a name*, *come down with the flu*. ✗ *she came the station* is impossible.
**Passive:** **no** — an intransitive verb has no passive: ✗ *the package was come two days early*. The prepositional idioms resist the prepositional passive as well: ✗ *her old letters were come across*, ✗ *the flu was come down with*.

To move toward the speaker or arrive; by extension, to reach a place, a time, a state, or a total, and the base of a large family of everyday phrasal verbs.

**1. Arrive, move toward the speaker.** The core sense. *Come* points toward the speaker or toward the place being talked about, where *go* points away from it. (приходить, приезжать, подходить)

1. My parents *come* over for dinner most Sundays.
2. The package *came* two days early.
3. She *has* not *come* back from lunch yet.
4. We *had come* a long way for a game that got rained out.

**2. Happen, arrive (of a time or event).** Used of seasons, dates, and events that arrive on their own schedule. Common in the set phrase *when the time comes*. (наступать, приходить (о времени, событии))

5. Spring *has come* late this year.
6. The rains *came* three weeks after the planting.
7. Payday *comes* on the fifteenth.

**3. Come from — originate, be from.** Used both for a person's hometown or background and for the source of a thing. With people it stays in the simple present even about the distant past. (быть родом из, происходить из)

8. Both of her grandparents *come* from Ohio.
9. The money *came* from a small family trust.
10. Most of our produce *has come* from the same two farms for years.

**4. Come + adjective — reach a state.** A linking use limited to a short fixed list: *come true*, *come loose*, *come undone*, *come clean*, *come free*. It is not productive — you cannot say a person *comes tired*. (становиться, оказываться (в каком-либо состоянии))

11. The screws on the handle *came* loose after one winter.
12. Everything she predicted *has come* true.
13. Wishes like that rarely *come* true.

**5. Come in — be available in (sizes, colors).** Used of products and options, with the thing sold as the subject and *in* introducing the range. (выпускаться, поставляться (в размерах, цветах))

14. The jacket *comes* in four colors.
15. That model *came* in a diesel version until 2014.
16. The tables *have* always *come* in two heights.

**6. Come to — add up to, total.** Used for bills, totals, and sums. In the negative it often carries the idea of amounting to nothing — *the whole plan came to nothing*. (составлять, обходиться в (сумму))

17. The bill *came* to forty-two dollars with tip.
18. Repairs *have come* to more than the car is worth.
19. Their savings *come* to about six months of rent.

**7. Come to — regain consciousness.** An idiom with no object, used of someone waking from a faint or from anesthesia. Here *to* is stressed and said in full, not reduced. (приходить в себя, очнуться)

20. He *came* to on the sidewalk with a crowd around him.
21. She *had* not *come* to by the time the ambulance arrived.
22. Most patients *come* to within a few minutes.

**8. Come across — find by chance.** Always accidental: you cannot *come across* something you were deliberately searching for. (натыкаться, случайно находить)

23. I *came* across her old letters while cleaning the attic.
24. We *have come* across this bug twice this month.
25. You sometimes *come* across real bargains at that store.

**9. Come across as — give an impression.** About how a person is perceived, usually unintentionally and often wrongly. It takes an adjective or a noun phrase after *as*. (производить впечатление, казаться)

26. He *comes* across as arrogant, but he is just shy.
27. Her email *came* across as far colder than she meant it.
28. The witness *has come* across as credible so far.

**10. Come up with — think of, produce.** Used of ideas, plans, names, and excuses, and also of money found on short notice. (придумать, найти (решение, идею, деньги))

29. We need to *come* up with a better name.
30. She *came* up with the whole plan in one afternoon.
31. They *had come* up with the deposit by Friday.

**11. Come out — be released, become known.** Used of books, films, and records being published, and of facts becoming public. With no object it also means to say publicly that one is gay. (выходить (в свет), выясняться, обнаруживаться)

32. The album *came* out in March.
33. The truth about the contract *has come* out.
34. New models *come* out every fall.

**12. Come down with — fall ill.** Only for minor catchable illnesses — a cold, the flu, a stomach bug. You do not *come down with* a broken leg. (заболеть, слечь (с чем-либо))

35. Half the office *came* down with the flu.
36. He *has come* down with something and is staying home.
37. Kids *come* down with everything their first year of school.

### outrun

**Pronunciation:** /aʊtˈrʌn/ &middot; *owt-RUN*
**Русский:** перегонять; опережать
**Forms:** *outrun* /aʊtˈrʌn/ &middot; *outran* /aʊtˈræn/ &middot; *outrun* /aʊtˈrʌn/
**Register:** neutral — understood by everyone but produced far less often than plain *run*; mostly sports talk and the *you can't outrun it* idiom
**Pattern:** u — a — u (ʌ — æ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *outruns* &middot; **-ing form:** *outrunning* (the n doubles)
**Contrast:** *Outrun* copies [run](#run) and adds nothing: past *outran*, participle *outrun*. ✗ *Demand has outran supply* — it is *has outrun*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses, the literal speed one (*a deer can outrun a coyote*) and the figurative outpacing one (*costs have outrun the budget*). No linking or stative use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** only, and the object is obligatory: *she outran every other sprinter*, *demand tends to outrun supply*. ✗ *the deer outran* is incomplete — compare plain *run*, which is happy with no object.
**Passive:** **yes**, though the active is far more common: *the coyote was easily outrun*, *supply has been outrun by demand for three quarters*.

To run faster than someone or something, and by extension to outpace or get ahead of it.

**1. Run faster than, escape by running.** The literal sense, about speed on foot or on wheels. Common with animals, runners, and vehicles. (обогнать, убежать от)

1. A healthy deer can easily *outrun* a coyote.
2. She *outran* every other sprinter at the district meet.
3. Nobody *has outrun* a storm like that on foot.

**2. Outpace, exceed, get ahead of.** The figurative sense: demand outruns supply, costs outrun a budget, a rumor outruns the announcement. Also in *outrun its usefulness*, meaning to last past the point of being useful. (опережать, превышать)

4. Costs *have outrun* the budget three quarters in a row.
5. The rumor *outran* the official announcement by a full day.
6. Demand tends to *outrun* supply in the first month.

**See also:** [run](#run), [overrun](#overrun), [rerun](#rerun)

### overcome

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈkʌm/ &middot; *oh-vur-KUM*
**Русский:** побороть, победить, преодолеть
**Forms:** *overcome* /ˌoʊvɚˈkʌm/ &middot; *overcame* /ˌoʊvɚˈkeɪm/ &middot; *overcome* /ˌoʊvɚˈkʌm/
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in talk about difficulties and emotions: *overcame her fear*, *overcome by smoke*
**Pattern:** o — a — o (ʌ — eɪ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *overcomes* &middot; **-ing form:** *overcoming* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** Built on [come](#come) and changing in the same spot, so ✗ *has overcame* is the mistake to watch. *Overcome* is also common in the passive — *overcome with grief*, *overcome by smoke* — and that is the participle, never the past.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: getting past a difficulty (*she overcame a serious injury*) or defeating an opponent (*the visitors overcame a twelve-point deficit*). After *be*, the participle in *overcome with grief* shades into an adjective describing a state.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in every sense, with an obligatory object — a problem, a feeling, or an opponent: ✗ *recruits overcome* on its own. The object is the difficulty itself, not the person who has it: *overcome their fear of heights*, not ✗ *overcome the recruit of his fear*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and very common — *a twelve-point deficit was overcome in the fourth quarter*. Sense 2 is passive-only in practice: *two firefighters were overcome by smoke*, *he was overcome with relief*. The form after *be* is always the participle: ✗ *he was overcame*.

To get the better of a difficulty or an opponent; in the passive, to be completely overwhelmed by a feeling or a physical force.

**1. Get past, conquer (a difficulty).** The main sense, used of fears, obstacles, addictions, and setbacks. The object is the problem itself, not a person. (преодолеть, побороть, справиться с)

1. Good training helps recruits *overcome* their fear of heights.
2. She *overcame* a serious injury to make the team.
3. The company *has overcome* worse setbacks than this one.

**2. Be overcome by/with — be overwhelmed.** Passive or near-passive only, with emotions (*grief*, *emotion*, *relief*) or with smoke and fumes. The form after *be* is the participle *overcome*, never *overcame*. (быть охваченным, сражённым (чувством, дымом))

4. Two firefighters were *overcome* by smoke before the roof gave way.
5. Grief *had overcome* her before she finished the first sentence.
6. He was *overcome* with relief when the call finally came.

**3. Defeat (an opponent).** Formal or literary where ordinary speech says *beat*. It is standard in sports writing about a comeback — *overcame a twelve-point deficit*. (одолеть, победить)

7. The visitors *overcame* a twelve-point deficit in the fourth quarter.
8. No challenger *has overcome* him in three years.
9. A smaller army can *overcome* a larger one on the right ground.

**See also:** [come](#come), [become](#become)

### overrun

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈrʌn/ &middot; *oh-vur-RUN*
**Русский:** переливаться через край, наводнять
**Forms:** *overrun* /ˌoʊvɚˈrʌn/ &middot; *overran* /ˌoʊvɚˈræn/ &middot; *overrun* /ˌoʊvɚˈrʌn/
**Register:** neutral — usually met in the passive *overrun with* or the noun *cost overrun*; the plain active verb is uncommon in speech
**Pattern:** u — a — u (ʌ — æ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *overruns* &middot; **-ing form:** *overrunning* (the n doubles)
**Contrast:** Follows [run](#run), so ✗ *weeds have overran the yard* is wrong — the participle is *overrun*. Stress moves with the part of speech: the verb is *oh-vur-RUN* /ˌoʊvɚˈrʌn/, while the noun in *a cost overrun* is *OH-vur-run* /ˈoʊvɚˌrʌn/.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout: spreading over something (*weeds overrun that lot every summer*), going past a limit (*the meeting overran its slot*), or rolling past a stopping point (*the jet overran the runway*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with an object in most uses (*ants have overrun the pantry*, *the project has overrun its schedule*), but intransitive when the limit is left unsaid: *long agendas always overrun*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the infest sense is most often met in the passive: *by August the garden had been completely overrun*, *the place is overrun with tourists*. No passive of the intransitive use: ✗ *the slot was overrun by the meeting* in the sense of *the meeting overran*.

To spread over something and take it over, or to go past a set limit of time, money, or distance.

**1. Infest, swarm, take over.** Used of weeds, insects, tourists, and invading troops. Very often passive — *the place is overrun with tourists* — where *with* introduces whatever has taken over. (наводнять, заполонять, кишеть)

1. Weeds *overrun* that empty lot every summer.
2. Ants *have overrun* the pantry again.
3. Deer *overran* the orchard as soon as the fruit started dropping.
4. By August the vegetable garden *had* been completely *overrun*.

**2. Exceed a limit (time, budget).** Used of meetings, projects, and costs going past what was planned. The related noun in *a cost overrun* is stressed on the first syllable. (превышать (срок, бюджет), выходить за рамки)

5. The meeting *overran* its slot by twenty minutes.
6. The project *has overrun* its schedule twice.
7. Long agendas always *overrun*.

**3. Run past a stopping point.** Technical, mostly aviation and rail: an aircraft going off the end of a runway, or a train passing a signal it should have stopped at. (проскочить, выкатиться за пределы)

8. The jet *overran* the runway in the rain.
9. Two freight trains *have overrun* that signal this year.
10. A wet runway makes it easy to *overrun* the stopping point.

**See also:** [run](#run), [outrun](#outrun), [rerun](#rerun)

### rerun

**Pronunciation:** /ˌriˈrʌn/ &middot; *ree-RUN*
**Русский:** показывать повторно
**Forms:** *rerun* /ˌriˈrʌn/ &middot; *reran* /ˌriˈræn/ &middot; *rerun* /ˌriˈrʌn/
**Register:** neutral — everyday as a noun (*TV reruns*); the verb belongs mostly to work and lab talk, *rerun the numbers*
**Pattern:** u — a — u (ʌ — æ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *reruns* &middot; **-ing form:** *rerunning* (the n doubles)
**Contrast:** Follows [run](#run): ✗ *They have reran the test* should be *have rerun*. Stress also splits by part of speech — the verb is *ree-RUN* /ˌriˈrʌn/, but the noun in *a rerun of the finale* is *REE-run* /ˈriˌrʌn/, and in everyday American speech the noun is the form you hear most.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: broadcasting something a second time (*the station reruns the pilot every holiday weekend*) or carrying a process out again (*we reran the numbers*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** only, with an obligatory object — an episode, a test, a program, a vote: ✗ *the lab has rerun* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the ordinary way to say it in official and lab contexts: *the court ordered the election to be rerun in three counties*, *that episode has been rerun a dozen times*.

To broadcast something a second time, or to carry out a process again from the start.

**1. Broadcast again (TV, radio).** The most common American use, though the noun *a rerun* is heard even more than the verb. The noun is stressed on the first syllable, the verb on the second. (показывать повторно, пускать в повтор)

1. The station *reruns* the pilot every holiday weekend.
2. Cable channels *rerun* old sitcoms all summer.
3. They *have rerun* that episode a dozen times.

**2. Carry out again (a test, a program, a vote).** Used of experiments, computer jobs, calculations, and elections that have to be held over. Frequently passive — *the election was rerun*. (повторно проводить, прогнать заново)

4. We *reran* the numbers and got the same answer.
5. The lab *has rerun* the sample twice.
6. The court ordered the election to be *rerun* in three counties.
7. Let's *rerun* the test with a clean database.

**See also:** [run](#run), [outrun](#outrun), [overrun](#overrun)

### run

**Pronunciation:** /rʌn/ &middot; *RUN*
**Русский:** бежать; течь
**Forms:** *run* /rʌn/ &middot; *ran* /ræn/ &middot; *run* /rʌn/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** u — a — u (ʌ — æ — ʌ) &middot; **Group:** 9
**Third person:** *runs* &middot; **-ing form:** *running* (the n doubles)
**Contrast:** ✗ *I have ran three miles* is one of the most common slips in American speech; after *have/has/had* the form is *run* — *I have run three miles*. The mistake also goes the other way: plain past needs *ran*, so ✗ *He run to the store yesterday*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; also **linking** in the fixed *run low / run late / run high / run deep / run dry* (*supplies run low*, *the well has run dry*), which reports a level rather than any movement.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the movement, flow, route, and extent senses (*she runs two miles*, *the faucet ran all night*, *the bus runs every fifteen minutes*), transitive in the management and operation senses (*run a bakery*, *run the sample*, *run the numbers*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the store is run by her sister*, *the sample was run twice*. No passive of the intransitive ones: ✗ *two miles were run by her* in the ordinary sense, ✗ *low was run by supplies*.

To move fast on foot; also to flow, to operate or manage something, to extend in a direction, and the base of a wide family of everyday idioms.

**1. Move fast on foot.** The core sense, for people and animals, and the ordinary word for the sport — *run a marathon*, *run track*. (бежать, бегать)

1. I *run* two miles before work most days.
2. He *ran* the whole way to the station and still missed the train.
3. She *has run* three marathons this year.

**2. Manage, operate (a business, an event).** Used of businesses, departments, meetings, and programs. Completely neutral in register and extremely common in American English. (управлять, руководить, вести (дело))

4. She *has run* that bakery for twelve years.
5. He *runs* the warehouse on the night shift.
6. They *ran* the whole festival on volunteers.

**3. Flow, leak; bleed (of dye).** Used of water, tears, and anything liquid, and of a color bleeding in the wash. A nose that will not stop is also said to *run*. (течь, литься; линять (о краске))

7. The faucet *had run* all night before anyone noticed.
8. Tears *ran* down his face during the whole speech.
9. Wash it cold or the red *runs*.

**4. Operate, execute (a machine, a program, a test).** Intransitive for a machine that is working, transitive for a program, a test, or a report that somebody starts. The transitive use is the ordinary word in computing and in labs. (работать (о механизме); запускать, прогонять (программу, тест))

10. The generator *runs* on propane.
11. The lab *ran* the sample twice.
12. We *have run* the numbers and the deal still works.

**5. Go on a route or schedule.** Used of buses, trains, ferries, and shuttles. The subject is the service itself, not the driver. (ходить, курсировать (о транспорте))

13. The bus *runs* every fifteen minutes until midnight.
14. That ferry *ran* only in summer back then.
15. Trains *have run* on this line since 1904.

**6. Extend, stretch, go along.** Used of roads, fences, wires, cracks, and borders. Nothing actually moves; the subject simply occupies a line. (тянуться, проходить, идти (о линии, границе))

16. A stone wall *runs* the length of the property.
17. The crack *ran* from the ceiling to the baseboard.
18. Cables *have run* under this street since the sixties.

**7. Continue for a period (a show, a lease).** Used of plays, exhibits, series, leases, and offers, usually with *for* plus a length of time or *through* plus an end date. (идти, длиться (о спектакле, договоре))

19. The show *ran* for six seasons.
20. The exhibit *runs* through Labor Day.
21. Our lease *has run* month to month since January.

**8. Run for — be a candidate.** Standard American political usage; British English says *stand for* instead. The office follows *for*. (баллотироваться, выдвигать свою кандидатуру)

22. He plans to *run* for mayor next spring.
23. She *ran* for city council twice and lost both times.
24. No incumbent *has run* unopposed in this district in years.

**9. Run + adjective — be or become (low, late, high).** A linking use with a fixed short list: *run low*, *run late*, *run high*, *run deep*, *run dry*. It reports a level or a tendency rather than any movement. (быть на исходе, идти с опозданием, накаляться)

25. Supplies *run* low by the end of the month.
26. Tempers *ran* high at the meeting.
27. The well *has run* dry twice this decade.

**10. Run into — meet by chance; collide with.** With a person it is an unplanned meeting; with an object or a problem it is hitting it. With a number it means reaching that amount — *costs ran into the millions*. (случайно встретить; врезаться, наткнуться на)

28. I *ran* into my old roommate at the airport.
29. The truck *ran* into a guardrail.
30. We *have run* into the same problem every quarter.

**11. Run out of — use up, be exhausted.** Two shapes: the supply *runs out*, or a person *runs out of* it. Also used with time, patience, and excuses. (заканчиваться, иссякать; исчерпать запас)

31. We *ran* out of milk before breakfast.
32. Batteries *run* out fast in the cold.
33. He *had run* out of excuses by the third week.

**12. Run over — hit with a vehicle; go past a limit.** One phrasal with two everyday uses: striking something with a car, and a meeting going past its allotted time. A third, *run over* a document, means to review it quickly. (переехать (машиной); затянуться, выйти за рамки времени)

34. A car *ran* over the mailbox during the storm.
35. The meeting *has run* over by twenty minutes.
36. Let's *run* over the agenda one more time.
