Past has "o", participle = past + -n#

The past takes an /oʊ/ vowel and the participle is that past form plus -n.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

These verbs bend the same way twice. The past swaps the base vowel for an /oʊ/ or /ɔr/ sound, and then the participle is simply that past form with -n or -en glued on: speak → spoke → spoken, freeze → froze → frozen, wear → wore → worn (во 2-й звук "о", 3-я = 2-я + "n"). Once you know the past form, the participle costs you one letter, which is why this is the friendliest of the irregular families. Two members — hide → hid → hidden and bite → bit → bitten — keep the "+ -n" half of the rule but shorten the vowel instead of rounding it, and two more — delve and cleave — preserve the pattern only in archaic forms that modern Americans have replaced with regular ones.

Awake follows wake exactly, and carries the same traps. It is also an adjective — I was awake at four is a state, not a past tense — and it has a regular cousin awaken/awakened/awakened, which is what most American writers reach for. The past cannot serve as the participle: ✗ had awoke, ✓ had awoken.
Verb type
action — dynamic, and literary beside everyday wake up; keep the verb apart from the adjective awake (I was awake at four is a state, not a past tense)
Transitivity

Passive

To stop sleeping, or to rouse someone from sleep — a more literary word than wake up — and, figuratively, to stir something that was dormant.

1. Stop sleeping. Intransitive and a little literary; everyday American speech says wake up. Keep it apart from the adjective awake — I was awake at four is a state, not a past tense. (просыпаться, пробуждаться)

  1. I awake before the alarm most mornings.
  2. She awoke to the sound of rain on the roof.
  3. The whole house had awoken by six.

2. Wake someone up. Transitive, and rarer than the intransitive sense; most American writers reach for wake or the regular awaken here. (будить, разбудить)

  1. Try not to awake the children when you come in.
  2. The sirens awoke half the neighborhood.
  3. The dog has awoken me at dawn every day this week.

3. Stir up a feeling or memory. Figurative and transitive, common in writing: a sight, a smell, or a story brings something dormant back to life. (пробуждать (чувства, воспоминания))

  1. Something in the story awoke an old memory.
  2. Small kindnesses awake loyalty in people.
  3. The photographs have awoken a homesickness she thought was gone.

4. awake to — become aware of. Followed by a noun: to finally see something you had been ignoring. The adjective version, be awake to, is at least as common. (осознать, понять)

  1. The country slowly awoke to the scale of the problem.
  2. We awake to our mistakes only after the fact.
  3. Investors have awoken to the risk.

bear#

Pronunciation
/bɛr/ · BAIR
Русский
родить, нести; переносить; выносить
Forms
bear /bɛr/ · bore /bɔr/ · born /bɔrn/
Register
everyday — can't bear it and was born are in every American's mouth
Pattern
ear /ɛr/ — ore /ɔr/ — orn
Group
13
Third person
bears · -ing form: bearing
Contrast
English keeps two participles here and they are not interchangeable. Born belongs to birth, and only in the passive: She was born in Ohio. Every other sense — carrying, enduring, producing — takes borne: He has borne the cost alone, The plan has borne fruit. The base is also a homophone of the adjective bare.
Verb type
action — dynamic in the carrying, enduring, and producing senses; closer to stative in bear a name / bear a resemblance / bear interest, where the progressive is odd: ✗ the building is bearing her name
Transitivity
both — transitive almost everywhere (bear the cost, bear three children, bear fruit), intransitive in the direction sense (bear left where the road forks)
Passive
restricted — natural for costs and burdens (the legal fees have been borne by the company), and be born is passive-only (she was born in Ohio; there is no active ✗ she borned). But the name and resemblance senses resist it: ✗ her grandfather's name is borne by the building, and ✗ left was borne at the fork.

To carry or support something, to endure it, or to bring it forth — plus the passive be born, the one sense that takes born instead of borne.

1. Endure, put up with. Very often negative or with can't: I can't bear it. The participle is borne, never born. (терпеть, выносить)

  1. I can't bear the sound of a dentist's drill.
  2. She bore the delay without a word of complaint.
  3. He has borne worse news than this.

2. be born (passive only). Passive only, and the only sense spelled born. There is no active ✗ she borned; birth in the active takes bear/bore/borne. (родиться)

  1. Their twins were born in a snowstorm.
  2. He was born the year the mill closed.
  3. All three of her children had been born by the time she turned thirty.

3. Give birth to, bring forth. The active birth sense, formal or literary; ordinary speech says have a baby. Here the participle is borne: She has borne three children. (рожать, родить)

  1. Mares usually bear one foal at a time.
  2. She bore three children in five years.
  3. The queen had borne no heir.

4. Carry the weight of, support. The structural sense — of beams, walls, ice, or a person carrying a burden or a cost. (нести (нагрузку), выдерживать вес)

  1. These beams bear the weight of the whole roof.
  2. The ice bore his weight for two steps and then cracked.
  3. The company has borne the legal fees since March.

5. Carry, bring (formal). Formal or literary for physically carrying something from one place to another — bearing gifts, bearing a message. (нести, приносить)

  1. The servers bear trays over their heads.
  2. The messengers bore the news from the capital.
  3. Pilgrims have borne these relics along the same road for centuries.

6. Produce, yield. Of trees, investments, and efforts. Bear fruit works literally and figuratively; bear interest is the banking use. (приносить (плоды, доход, результат))

  1. The old apple tree bears less fruit every year.
  2. The account bore four percent interest.
  3. Two years of talks have borne fruit at last.

7. Have or show (a name, mark, resemblance). Of documents, buildings, and faces: to carry a name, a signature, a scar, or a likeness. (носить (имя, знак), иметь)

  1. The building bears her grandfather's name.
  2. The check bore no signature.
  3. The town has borne that name since 1802.

8. bear in mind. A fixed phrase, most often imperative, meaning to keep a fact in view while deciding something. (иметь в виду, учитывать)

  1. Bear in mind that the office closes at four.
  2. We bore in mind that she was new.
  3. The committee has borne the deadline in mind throughout.

9. bear with (someone). A request for patience, usually imperative: bear with me. (потерпеть, отнестись с терпением)

  1. Bear with me — this will take a minute.
  2. She bore with his questions for a full hour.
  3. Readers have borne with this digression long enough.

10. Turn, veer in a direction. Of a road, a vehicle, or a walker; also nautical. Bear left, bear right, bear north. (брать (влево, вправо), держать курс)

  1. Bear left where the road forks.
  2. The trail bore east along the ridge.
  3. We had borne too far south and lost the river.

11. bear out — confirm. Of evidence, data, or events proving a claim or a person right. (подтверждать)

  1. The data bear out her theory.
  2. The audit bore out every complaint.
  3. Events have borne him out.
Phrasal verbs
bear down · bear down on · bear on · bear out · bear up · bear with
See also
forbear, wear, tear, swear

A formal, literary verb meaning to be evidence of something; its past form bespoke leads a second life as a British adjective for custom-made goods.

1. Be a sign of, give evidence of. The only living verb sense in American English, and formal even then. The subject is almost always a thing — a voice, a room, a detail — not a person. (свидетельствовать о, говорить о)

  1. Cracked paint and a sagging porch bespeak neglect.
  2. The empty shelves bespoke a store about to close.
  3. The silence had bespoken trouble long before anyone admitted it.
  4. Her steady voice bespeaks years of practice.

2. Order or arrange in advance. Archaic: to speak for something ahead of time — a suit, a room, a dinner. This is the sense that produced the tailoring adjective. (заказывать заранее, договариваться о)

  1. He bespoke a suit from a tailor on Savile Row.
  2. In older novels a traveler would bespeak dinner at the inn.
  3. The rooms had been bespoken months before the wedding.

3. bespoke — custom-made (adjective). British, and not a verb use at all, though it is where most readers meet the form. Americans say custom-made, made-to-order, or custom. (сделанный на заказ, индивидуальный)

  1. He wears a bespoke suit to every hearing.
  2. The firm sells bespoke software to hospitals.

/baɪt/ · BITE
Русский
кусать
Forms
bite /baɪt/ · bit /bɪt/ · bitten /ˈbɪtən/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
i-e /aɪ/ — i /ɪ/ — i + -ten
Group
13
Third person
bites · -ing form: biting (the silent e drops)
Contrast
The past is bit and the participle is bitten: ✗ I have bit my tongue, ✓ I have bitten my tongue. Because bit is also an everyday noun (a bit of sugar) and a past tense that sounds complete on its own, the short form slips in after have more than almost any other verb in this group.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, from teeth (the terrier bit the mail carrier) to tires gripping snow to cold and tariffs taking hold
Transitivity
both — transitive with an object (bite your tongue, bite back a laugh), intransitive with the object left out (dogs bite, nobody bit, the wind bites)
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — she was bitten by a spider in the garage. No passive of the intransitive trout bite best after sunrise.

To cut or grip something with the teeth — and, by extension, to sting, to grip a surface, or to take hold painfully.

1. Cut or grip with the teeth. The core sense, transitive or intransitive. Remember the split: past bit, participle bitten — ✗ I have bit my tongue. (кусать, укусить)

  1. Dogs bite when they are frightened.
  2. The neighbor's terrier bit the mail carrier last spring.
  3. I have bitten my nails since I was nine.

2. Sting (of insects and snakes). In American usage mosquitoes, spiders, ants, and snakes bite; bees and wasps sting. (кусать, жалить)

  1. Mosquitoes bite worst at dusk.
  2. The mosquito bit me twice before I felt it.
  3. She was bitten by a spider in the garage.

3. Grip a surface, take hold. Of tires, blades, screws, and anchors — anything that digs in rather than slipping. (врезаться, впиваться, цепляться)

  1. Winter tires bite into packed snow.
  2. The blade bit deep into the log.
  3. The anchor had bitten into the sand by then.

4. Take effect painfully. Of cold, wind, budget cuts, sanctions, or prices — the moment something stops being abstract and starts to hurt. (сказываться, бить (по кому-л.), пробирать)

  1. The wind bites on that side of the building.
  2. By then the cold had bitten through my gloves.
  3. The new tariffs bit hardest in the smallest towns.

5. Take the bait, respond to an offer. Of fish, and figuratively of buyers or of anyone falling for a trick. Often negative: nobody bit. (клюнуть, попасться на удочку)

  1. Trout bite best just after sunrise.
  2. We advertised for a week and nobody bit.
  3. Two buyers have bitten since we dropped the price.

6. bite the bullet. A fixed phrase: to accept something painful you have been putting off. (стиснуть зубы, решиться на неприятное)

  1. You'll have to bite the bullet and tell her.
  2. We finally bit the bullet and replaced the roof.
  3. He has bitten the bullet on far worse repairs.

7. bite your tongue, bite back. To stop yourself from saying something. Bite back takes an object: bite back a reply. (прикусить язык, сдержаться)

  1. I bit my tongue and said nothing.
  2. She had to bite back a laugh.
  3. He has bitten his tongue through three straight meetings.
Phrasal verbs
bite back · bite into · bite off
See also

Pronunciation
/breɪk/ · BRAKE
Русский
ломать; разбивать
Forms
break /breɪk/ · broke /broʊk/ · broken /ˈbroʊkən/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
ea /eɪ/ — o /oʊ/ — o + -n
Group
13
Third person
breaks · -ing form: breaking
Contrast
✗ I have broke it — after have, it is always broken. Bare broke doubles as an adjective meaning "out of money" (I'm broke until Friday), which is part of why the wrong form sounds passable to some ears. The base is a homophone of brake.
Verb type
action — dynamic, and ergative: the same event runs transitive or intransitive (he broke the vase / the vase broke). Bare broke also lives on as an adjective, "out of money"
Transitivity
both — transitive when someone breaks a thing, a rule, or a record (she broke the state record), intransitive of things that break, fail, or begin (my phone broke, day breaks around five, war broke out)
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the window had been broken before we arrived, the story was broken on Sunday. No passive of the intransitive day breaks or the talks broke down.

To separate something into pieces or stop it working — and, figuratively, to violate a rule, interrupt a state, or surpass a limit.

1. Break into pieces, smash. The literal core, transitive or intransitive. After have it is always broken: ✗ I have broke it. (ломать, разбивать; ломаться)

  1. Glass jars break if you stack them.
  2. He broke the vase reaching for the light.
  3. Someone had broken the window before we arrived.

2. Stop working, fail. Of machines and devices. For cars and larger machinery Americans usually add down. (ломаться, выходить из строя)

  1. Cheap chargers break within a month.
  2. My phone broke the day the warranty ended.
  3. The washing machine has broken twice this year.

3. Fracture a bone. Used of the person and of the bone alike: he broke his wrist, his wrist broke. (сломать (кость))

  1. Old bones break easily.
  2. He broke his wrist on the ice last winter.
  3. She has broken the same finger twice.

4. Violate a rule, law, or promise. Figurative but completely ordinary — break the law, break a contract, break your word. (нарушать (закон, обещание, правило))

  1. Don't break your promise to her.
  2. The company broke the terms of the lease.
  3. He has broken every rule in the handbook.

5. Interrupt, put an end to a continuous state. Of silence, a habit, a tie, a heat wave, a fall, a journey — anything that has been running unbroken. (прерывать, нарушать (тишину, привычку, ничью))

  1. Nobody wanted to break the silence.
  2. A branch broke his fall.
  3. The rain has broken the heat wave at last.

6. Beat a record or a limit. Of records, thresholds, and numbers: break a record, break four minutes, break a hundred degrees. (побить (рекорд), преодолеть (порог))

  1. Records break at every Olympics.
  2. She broke the state record in the 400.
  3. Three runners have broken four minutes on this track.

7. break the news, break a story. To be the one who tells something for the first time — gently, in the case of bad news; first, in the case of journalism. (сообщить (новость), первым опубликовать)

  1. She breaks the news gently whenever she can.
  2. The paper broke the story on Sunday.
  3. Someone had broken the news to him already.

8. Of dawn, weather, or a voice. A cluster of intransitive uses: day breaks, a storm breaks, and a voice breaks either with emotion or in adolescence. (наступать (о рассвете); разразиться; ломаться (о голосе))

  1. Day breaks around five in June.
  2. His voice broke when he thanked her.
  3. The storm had broken before we reached the car.

9. break down. Three common uses: a vehicle or negotiation fails, a person loses composure, or something is separated into parts for analysis. (сломаться; сорваться, разрыдаться; разбить на части)

  1. Break the costs down by department.
  2. Our car broke down on the interstate.
  3. The talks have broken down completely.

10. break up. To end a relationship, to disperse a gathering, or to come apart into pieces. Of a phone call it means the signal is cutting out. (расстаться; разойтись; распадаться)

  1. Ice on the river breaks up in April.
  2. They broke up after four years.
  3. The meeting had broken up by noon.

11. break in, break into. Break into a building or a car is burglary; break in also means to soften new shoes or train a newcomer, and break into a market or a laugh starts something abruptly. (вломиться, проникнуть; разносить (обувь))

  1. It takes a week to break in new boots.
  2. Someone broke into the garage last night.
  3. Thieves have broken into three cars on this block.

12. break out. Of fires, wars, arguments, and epidemics starting suddenly; also of escaping from confinement, and of skin — break out in a rash. (вспыхнуть (о войне, пожаре); сбежать)

  1. Fires break out in that canyon every August.
  2. War broke out that September.
  3. Two inmates had broken out through the laundry.
Phrasal verbs
break away · break down · break even · break in · break into · break off · break out · break out of · break through · break up · break with

speak, steal, wake

Two o's in the present, one in the past — and completely different vowels: choose /tʃuz/ but chose /tʃoʊz/. Since the spellings differ by a single letter, the common error is a misspelling rather than a tense mistake: ✗ I chose to walk to work every day when you mean choose. There is no ✗ choosed.

To pick one thing out of several, or to decide on a course of action.

1. Pick one out of several. The core sense. Watch the spelling more than the tense: present choose /tʃuz/, past chose /tʃoʊz/, and there is no ✗ choosed. (выбирать, выбрать)

  1. You choose the restaurant this time.
  2. They chose the cheaper apartment.
  3. We have chosen a name for the dog.

2. choose to — decide to do something. With an infinitive, meaning to do something deliberately rather than by default. Often used to underline that it was a free decision. (предпочесть, решить)

  1. People choose to live here for the quiet.
  2. She chose to walk rather than wait for the bus.
  3. He has chosen not to comment.

3. Select someone for a role. Of committees, voters, and teams. Very often passive: she was chosen from a field of forty. (избирать, отбирать, назначать)

  1. Each team chooses a captain in September.
  2. Voters chose him by a wide margin.
  3. The committee had chosen her before the interviews ended.

4. choose between, choose from. Between takes two options, among or from takes more. There is nothing to choose between them means they are equally good. (выбирать между; выбирать из)

  1. You have to choose between the two offers.
  2. She chose from a list of six.
  3. I have chosen from this menu so often I know it by heart.

5. chosen — selected, elect (adjective). As an adjective the participle carries a religious or elevated tone: the chosen people, the chosen one. (избранный)

  1. He acts like the chosen one.
  2. They saw themselves as a chosen people.
Phrasal verbs
choose between · choose up

Two unrelated verbs share this spelling with nearly opposite meanings — to split something apart, and to cling to it. Both are regular in modern American English; clove and cloven survive only in fixed phrases and older writing.

1. Split apart, chop in two. The living form is cleaved; clove and cloven are archaic and will read as deliberate old-fashioned style. (раскалывать, разрубать)

  1. Frost can cleave a boulder in half.
  2. One blow clove the log in two.
  3. Lightning had cloven the old oak from crown to root.
  4. Today a writer would simply say the axe cleaved the log.

2. Cut through water or air. Literary, of ships, blades, and aircraft moving cleanly through something. (рассекать)

  1. The jet cleaves the air with almost no noise.
  2. The bow of the boat clove the water cleanly.
  3. The prow had cloven a channel through the slush.

3. cloven — split in two (adjective). Survives almost entirely in cloven hoof and cloven foot, and so carries a faint biblical or devilish echo. (раздвоенный, расщепленный)

  1. Goats and sheep have cloven hooves.
  2. The devil is always drawn with a cloven foot.

4. cleave to — cling, stay faithful to. A separate verb that happens to be spelled the same; always regular (cleaved to), and formal or biblical in tone. (держаться (за), хранить верность)

  1. She cleaves to the plan no matter what anyone says.
  2. He cleaved to the old customs all his life.
  3. They have cleaved to one another through worse than this.

weave, delve, interweave

To dig — today almost always figuratively, into records, a subject, or the bottom of a bag. The strong forms dolve and dolven are archaic; modern usage is regular.

1. delve into — research deeply. By far the commonest use, and fully regular: delve, delved, delved. Almost always with into. (углубляться в, копаться в (материале))
