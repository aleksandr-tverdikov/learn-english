# Past = base + -d, participle = base + -n

> The past adds -d and the participle adds -n, both to the unchanged base.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

These verbs leave the base form untouched and simply hang a different ending on each of the other two: *show — showed — shown*, *sow — sowed — sown*, *strew — strewed — strewn* (2-я = 1-я + "d", 3-я = 1-я + "n"). The past is perfectly regular, which is what makes the group feel easy; the only irregular thing anywhere in it is that **-n** on the participle. In American English that -n form is still the working participle for a handful of these verbs — *shown*, *strewn*, *sewn*, *swollen* — but for most of the rest it has retreated into adjectives (*molten* metal, a *rotten* board, *clean-shaven*, a *graven* image) and left the plain *-ed* participle in charge. So read the **Contrast** line in each entry before you trust the -n form in your own writing: this is the group where the spreadsheet's pattern and current American usage disagree most often.

---

## The verbs, A–Z

### disprove

**Pronunciation:** /dɪsˈpruv/ &middot; *dis-PROOV*
**Русский:** опровергать; доказывать ложность (чего-л.)
**Forms:** *disprove* /dɪsˈpruv/ &middot; *disproved* /dɪsˈpruvd/ &middot; *disproven* /dɪsˈpruvən/ &middot; American prose more often uses *disproved*
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in argument, news, and science writing; nothing marked about it
**Pattern:** base — base + -d — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *disproves* &middot; **-ing form:** *disproving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Prefix plus [prove](#prove), and it behaves exactly like its base verb. Both participles are correct, but edited American writing uses *has disproved* noticeably more than *has disproven*, while the -n form is most at home in front of a noun: *a disproven theory*. The past tense is always *disproved*: ✗ *he disproven it*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic verb of demonstration, comfortable in the progressive (*they're disproving it point by point*). No stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is a claim, theory, rumor, or alibi, and it is obligatory: *disprove her central claim*; ✗ *the lab disproved* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes** — *nobody has disproved her central claim yet*, *by 1960 the idea had been disproven several times over*.

To show by evidence or argument that a claim, theory, or story is false.

**1. Show something to be false.** The core sense, and nearly the only one. The object is a claim, theory, rumor, or alibi — you disprove a statement, not a person. The -n participle *disproven* is standard in front of a noun (*a disproven theory*), but the perfect tense in edited American prose is usually *has disproved*. (опровергать, доказывать ложность)

1. One clean experiment can *disprove* a decade of theory.
2. The lab *disproved* the original finding in under a month.
3. Nobody *has disproved* her central claim yet.
4. By 1960 the idea *had been disproven* several times over.
5. A *disproven* hypothesis still teaches you something.

**2. disprove a negative — the argument phrase.** A fixed idiom about the burden of proof: *you can't disprove a negative* means the person making the claim has to support it, not the skeptic. Common in debate, law, and science writing. (доказать отсутствие (чего-л.), опровергнуть отрицательное утверждение)

6. You can't *disprove* a negative, so the burden stays with the accuser.
7. He *disproves* every rumor about the company by simply showing up.

**See also:** [prove](#prove), [reprove](#reprove)

### engrave

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈɡreɪv/ &middot; *in-GRAYV*
**Русский:** гравировать
**Forms:** *engrave* /ɪnˈɡreɪv/ &middot; *engraved* /ɪnˈɡreɪvd/ &middot; *engraven* /ɪnˈɡreɪvən/ &middot; the American participle is *engraved*
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary American word for what a jeweler or a trophy shop does
**Pattern:** base — base + -d — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *engraves* &middot; **-ing form:** *engraving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** In modern American English *engrave* is simply a regular verb: *engraved* is both the past and the participle. *Engraven* is a nineteenth-century form you will meet only in older books and as an echo of *graven image* — never write ✗ *has engraven*. Same story as [grave](#grave), the older verb this one was built from.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses: cutting a surface, making a printing plate, and fixing something in the memory.
**Transitivity:** **both** — normally transitive (*engraved our initials inside the ring*), but the object drops when the technique is the point: *he engraves by hand*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the memory sense is nearly always passive — *that afternoon is engraved on my memory*; also *US currency is still engraved rather than photographed onto the plate*.

To cut letters or a design into a hard surface — and, figuratively, to fix something permanently in the mind.

**1. Cut letters or a design into a surface.** The everyday sense, used of metal, glass, stone, and jewelry. In modern American English the verb is simply regular: *engraved* serves as both past and participle, and ✗ *has engraven* is a nineteenth-century form nobody writes now. (гравировать, вырезать (надпись))

1. They *engrave* the winner's name on the cup every June.
2. The jeweler *engraved* our initials inside the ring.
3. She *has engraved* glass for thirty years.
4. He *engraves* by hand, without a machine.

**2. Make a printing plate; produce an engraving.** The printmaking and currency sense: cutting an image into a plate or block so it can be inked and printed. Mostly technical or historical now. (гравировать (печатную форму), делать гравюру)

5. Winslow Homer *engraved* wood blocks for the illustrated weeklies.
6. US currency is still *engraved* rather than photographed onto the plate.
7. The bank note *had been engraved* by hand in 1890.

**3. Fix permanently in the memory.** Figurative, usually passive and followed by *on* or *in*: *engraved on my memory*. The nineteenth-century form *engraven* survives only as an echo of *graven image* in older hymns and poetry. (запечатлеть, врезать (в память))

8. That afternoon is *engraved* on my memory.
9. Thirty years of practice *have engraved* the movement into her hands.
10. The rule *had been engraved* in every trainee's head by week two.
11. Nineteenth-century hymns speak of a name *engraven* on the heart.

**See also:** [grave](#grave), [shape](#shape)

### gnaw

**Pronunciation:** /nɔ/ &middot; *NAW*
**Русский:** грызть, глодать
**Forms:** *gnaw* /nɔ/ &middot; *gnawed* /nɔd/ &middot; *gnawn* /nɔn/ &middot; the American participle is *gnawed*
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — common of animals literally and of worry figuratively
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *gnaws* &middot; **-ing form:** *gnawing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** The g is silent in every form — /nɔ/, not ✗ /ɡnɔ/. *Gnawn* is rare enough that most Americans have never seen it in print; write *has gnawed*. Compare [saw](#saw) and [whipsaw](#whipsaw), which share the same /ɔ/ vowel and the same optional, mostly British -n participle.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and iterative (repeated small bites); the figurative *gnaw at* is just as dynamic, not stative.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive when a hole results (*something gnawed a hole in the bag*), intransitive with *on* or *at* when none does (*puppies gnaw on anything*, *doubt gnawed at him*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — fine with a real direct object: *the insulation has been gnawed through again*. The *gnaw at* pattern resists it: ✗ *he was gnawed at by doubt*.

To bite at something over and over with small, wearing bites — literally, or in the sense of a worry that keeps working away at you.

**1. Bite at something repeatedly.** The literal sense, typical of rodents, dogs, and teething children. Often with *on* or *at* when no hole is made, and with a direct object when one is. The g is silent in every form, /nɔ/. (грызть, глодать)

1. Puppies *gnaw* on anything within reach.
2. Something *gnawed* a hole in the bottom of the bag.
3. Mice *have gnawed* through the insulation again.
4. The dog *gnaws* the same bone every evening.

**2. gnaw at — nag, torment.** Figurative, with a worry, doubt, guilt, or unanswered question as the subject. Almost always with *at*, and the feeling is persistent rather than sharp. (грызть, мучить, не давать покоя (о сомнении, тревоге))

5. Doubt *had gnawed* at him all week.
6. The question *gnaws* at me every time I drive past that exit.
7. Guilt *gnawed* at her for years afterward.

**3. Wear away gradually.** Of water, wind, rust, or inflation eating slowly into something. Written rather than spoken register; *gnawn* is rare enough in American print that you should write *has gnawed*. (подтачивать, разъедать, размывать)

8. The river *gnaws* a little more of the bank away each spring.
9. Rust *has gnawed* the bottom of both doors.
10. In older writing you will find a rope described as *gnawn* clear through.

**See also:** [saw](#saw), [whipsaw](#whipsaw)

### grave

**Pronunciation:** /ɡreɪv/ &middot; *GRAYV*
**Русский:** гравировать
**Forms:** *grave* /ɡreɪv/ &middot; *graved* /ɡreɪvd/ &middot; *graven* /ˈɡreɪvən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** archaic — the verb itself died out; only *graven image* keeps the participle alive
**Pattern:** base — base + -d — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *graves* &middot; **-ing form:** *graving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Not the noun *grave* ("burial place") or the adjective *grave* ("serious") — this is an old verb meaning "carve," now almost completely replaced by [engrave](#engrave). Only the participle is still in circulation, and only as an adjective: the *graven image* of the Ten Commandments, or lines *deeply graven* in a face. For everyday use, write *engrave* or *carve*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic verb of carving, but archaic; only the participle *graven* is still in circulation, and it works as an adjective (*a graven image*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is the lettering or the surface worked: *the mason graved the family name across the lintel*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is where the verb survives: *the motto was graven over the doorway*, *those letters had been graved into the stone centuries earlier*.

An old verb meaning to carve or cut into a hard surface, now alive almost only in its participle *graven*.

**1. Carve or cut into stone or metal.** Archaic. Not the noun *grave* ("burial place") or the adjective *grave* ("serious") — this is the old verb that [engrave](#engrave) replaced. For anything you write yourself, use *engrave* or *carve*. (гравировать, высекать, вырезать)

1. Older writers use *grave* where we would now say *engrave*.
2. The mason *graved* the family name across the lintel.
3. Those letters *had been graved* into the stone centuries earlier.
4. The printmaker *graves* the plate first and pulls a proof afterward.

**2. graven — carved; cut deep.** The participle is the only part of the verb still in circulation, and it works as an adjective: the *graven image* of the Ten Commandments, or lines *graven* in a face by hard years. Slightly biblical or literary in tone. (изваянный, высеченный; врезавшийся (в память, в лицо))

5. The commandment against *graven* images is the second one.
6. Two hard years *have graven* deep lines in his face.
7. The motto was *graven* over the doorway in Latin.

**See also:** [engrave](#engrave), [shave](#shave)

### hew

**Pronunciation:** /hju/ &middot; *HYOO*
**Русский:** рубить; тесать
**Forms:** *hew* /hju/ &middot; *hewed* /hjud/ &middot; *hewn* /hjun/ &middot; American also *hewed* /hjud/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a specialized woodworking verb, alive mainly in *hew to* and *rough-hewn*
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *hews* &middot; **-ing form:** *hewing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Both participles are alive in American English, but they divide the work. *Hewn* almost always stands in front of a noun — *hewn beams*, *rough-hewn stone* — while *hewed* carries the perfect tense: *the board has hewed to the same policy for years*. That second, figurative sense, *hew to* meaning "stick closely to," is far commoner in American writing than the woodcutting one. Note the /j/ glide, /hju/, which [strew](#strew) does not have.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, the literal chopping and the figurative *hew to* alike. *Rough-hewn* is a fixed adjective, not a verb form in use.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the cutting senses (*hewed the beam square*, *hewed a path through the brush*), intransitive with *to* in the commoner figurative sense (*the committee has hewed closely to the original plan*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — yes for the literal sense: *the cabin walls were hewn from a single stand of pine*, *crews have hewn a road out of solid rock*. No passive of *hew to*: ✗ *the plan has been hewed to*.

To cut or chop something hard with heavy blows — and, in the commonest American use, to stick closely to a line, a plan, or a principle.

**1. Chop or cut with heavy blows.** The literal sense, used of timber and stone worked with an ax, adze, or chisel. Here the participle is *hewn*, especially before a noun: *hewn beams*, *rough-hewn stone*. (рубить, тесать)

1. They still *hew* the timbers by hand at the restoration site.
2. He *hewed* the beam square with a broadax.
3. Masons *have hewn* stone on this hillside since the 1800s.
4. The cabin walls were *hewn* from a single stand of pine.

**2. hew to — stick closely to.** Figurative and by far the commoner sense in American writing — of a policy, a script, a party line, or a schedule. This sense takes *hewed* in the perfect, not *hewn*. (твёрдо придерживаться, следовать (чему-л.))

5. The committee *has hewed* closely to the original plan.
6. The adaptation *hewed* to the novel almost line for line.
7. She *hews* to her schedule no matter who complains.

**3. hew out — make by cutting away.** Carving something usable out of raw material or wilderness — a path, a road, a farm, a career. Carries a flavor of hard, physical effort even when the object is abstract. (прорубать, вырубать; создавать с трудом)

8. He *hewed* a path through the brush with a machete.
9. Crews *have hewn* a road out of solid rock.
10. Settlers *hew* a farm out of the woods one acre at a time.
11. Each block *had been hewn* to roughly the same size.

**4. rough-hewn — unpolished.** A fixed compound adjective, used of surfaces left rough by the ax and, by extension, of a plain, unrefined manner or style. Only the participle appears in this use. (грубо отёсанный; неотёсанный, простоватый)

12. The furniture is deliberately *rough-hewn*.
13. His manner was *rough-hewn* but never rude.

**See also:** [mow](#mow), [strew](#strew), [saw](#saw)

### melt

**Pronunciation:** /mɛlt/ &middot; *MELT*
**Русский:** плавить
**Forms:** *melt* /mɛlt/ &middot; *melted* /ˈmɛltɪd/ &middot; *molten* /ˈmoʊltən/ &middot; the working American participle is *melted* /ˈmɛltɪd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — o-vowel + -en &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *melts* &middot; **-ing form:** *melting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** *Melt* is a regular verb today: *the snow has melted*, never ✗ *the snow has molten*. *Molten* survives only as an adjective, only in front of a noun, and only for things that melt at furnace heat — *molten lava*, *molten glass*, *molten steel*. Nobody says ✗ *molten ice cream*. Compare [swell](#swell), whose old -en participle is still fully in use, and [rot](#rot), which stranded its -en form the same way this one did.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and ergative: the same event stated intransitively (*the ice melted*) or transitively (*melt the butter*). Never stative, never linking; *molten* is an adjective only.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive with the thing that changes as subject (*all the snow has melted off the north slope*), transitive with a causer (*melt the butter first*, *they melted down the old trophies*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural for the transitive senses: *the trophies were melted down for the silver*. The intransitive senses have no passive at all: ✗ *the snow was melted away*, ✗ *the crowd was melted into*.

To go from solid to liquid under heat, or to make something do that; by extension, to soften, fade out, or disappear.

**1. Turn from solid to liquid with heat.** The core sense, both transitive and intransitive. *Melt* is a regular verb today — *the snow has melted*, never ✗ *has molten*. *Molten* survives only as an adjective, only before a noun, and only for furnace-hot material. (плавить(ся), таять, растапливать)

1. *Melt* the butter first, then whisk in the flour.
2. The ice *melted* before we got the cooler open.
3. All the snow *has melted* off the north slope.
4. By noon the road tar *had melted* into black ribbons.
5. The furnace holds forty tons of *molten* iron.

**2. Dissolve in the mouth or in liquid.** Used of food that goes soft without any obvious heat, and of sugar or salt disappearing into a hot liquid. The compliment *melts in your mouth* is fixed. (таять (во рту), растворяться)

6. These cookies *melt* in your mouth.
7. The sugar *had melted* into the hot cream before she stirred it.
8. The cheese *melted* down into the bread.

**3. Soften emotionally.** Of a person, a face, or a heart giving way to affection or pity. Intransitive, informal and warm in tone; the transitive use (*that melted him*) is common too. (растрогаться, смягчиться, растаять)

9. Her whole face *melts* when the dog comes in.
10. He *melted* the second she started crying.
11. That video *has melted* every parent who has seen it.

**4. melt away — disappear gradually.** Of a crowd, a lead, a fortune, or a fear that shrinks to nothing rather than ending at once. (растаять, рассеяться, испариться)

12. The crowd *melted away* as soon as the police arrived.
13. His lead in the polls *has melted away* since June.
14. Whatever nerve she had *melted away* at the microphone.

**5. melt down — reduce to liquid; lose control.** Three uses of one phrasal verb: melting metal down for its material, a reactor core melting down, and — informally — a person falling apart emotionally. (переплавлять; расплавиться (об активной зоне); сорваться, устроить истерику)

15. They *melted down* the old trophies for the silver.
16. Reactor cores *melt down* when the coolant fails.
17. The toddler *had melted down* twice before we reached the gate.

**6. melt into — blend imperceptibly.** Of someone slipping out of sight into a crowd or a background, or of one color, sound, or landscape shading into another with no clear edge. (слиться, раствориться (в толпе, в пейзаже))

18. He *melted into* the crowd before anyone got a photograph.
19. The hills *melt into* haze by mid-afternoon.
20. The suspect *had melted into* the neighborhood by the time the units arrived.

**See also:** [swell](#swell), [rot](#rot), [wax](#wax)

### mow

**Pronunciation:** /moʊ/ &middot; *MOH*
**Русский:** косить, жать (жатва)
**Forms:** *mow* /moʊ/ &middot; *mowed* /moʊd/ &middot; *mown* /moʊn/ &middot; American usually *mowed* /moʊd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — every American with a lawn says it weekly
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *mows* &middot; **-ing form:** *mowing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Americans say *I have mowed the lawn*; *have mown* reads as British. *Mown* does survive here as an adjective — *newly mown grass*, *a mown field* — and the fixed phrasal verb is always *mowed down*, never ✗ *mown down*, for people cut down by gunfire or a car. Rhymes with [sow](#sow) and [sew](#sew), and splits its participles the same way.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; no stative or linking use anywhere in the entry.
**Transitivity:** **both** — the object is usual but optional: *mow the yard*, *mow the back forty for hay*, yet *I mow every other Saturday* is complete on its own.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the crew has mowed the ball field twice this week* → *the ball field has been mowed twice this week*; *machine guns had mowed down the first two waves* → *the first two waves had been mowed down*.

To cut down grass or standing grain — and, in *mow down*, to cut down people or things in numbers.

**1. Cut grass short.** The everyday American sense: a lawn, a field, a ball field, a ditch bank. Americans write *have mowed*; *have mown* reads as British, though *mown* survives here as an adjective — *freshly mown grass*. (косить, стричь (газон, траву))

1. I *mow* the yard every other Saturday all summer.
2. He *mowed* around the flower beds without being asked.
3. The crew *has mowed* the ball field twice this week.
4. Nothing smells better than freshly *mown* hay.

**2. Cut a hay or grain crop.** The older farming sense, where the point is the harvest rather than a tidy lawn. The field, not the crop, can be the object: *mow the back forty*. (жать, косить (сено, хлеб))

5. They *mow* the back forty for hay in July.
6. Crews *mowed* the alfalfa twice last season.
7. By the time we arrived, someone *had mowed* the whole hillside.

**3. mow down — cut down in numbers.** Of gunfire, a vehicle, or a disease killing or knocking down many at once, and in sports of a pitcher or team dispatching opponents easily. The idiom is always *mowed down*, never ✗ *mown down*. (скосить, смести (очередью, машиной); разгромить)

8. The truck jumped the curb and *mowed down* a row of mailboxes.
9. Machine guns *had mowed down* the first two waves.
10. Their closer *mows down* the bottom of the order every night.

**See also:** [sow](#sow), [sew](#sew), [hew](#hew)

### prove

**Pronunciation:** /pruv/ &middot; *PROOV*
**Русский:** доказывать; оказаться
**Forms:** *prove* /pruv/ &middot; *proved* /pruvd/ &middot; *proven* /ˈpruvən/ &middot; the ordinary American participle is *proved* /pruvd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** base — base + -d — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *proves* &middot; **-ing form:** *proving* (drop the e)
**Contrast:** Both participles are standard, and they divide the labor. In a perfect tense, edited American prose prefers *proved*: *she has proved her point*. *Proven* is the form that goes in front of a noun — *a proven method*, *proven reserves* — and it is frozen into the legal verdict *not proven*. The past tense is always *proved*: ✗ *he proven it in court*. See also [disprove](#disprove) and [reprove](#reprove), which inherit the pattern.
**Verb type:** **action** in the demonstrating senses (*prove a theorem*, *prove his doubters wrong*), but **linking (copular)** in sense 2, *turn out to be*: *the drive proved longer than the map suggested*, *the drug had proved safe*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in the demonstrating senses, object obligatory (*the prosecution never proved he was in the building*), often with a following complement (*proved his doubters wrong*). The linking sense takes a subject complement and no object: *that shortcut always proves to be slower*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — yes in the demonstrating senses: *no one has proved the conjecture*, *the estate had been proved in probate court*. **No** passive of the linking sense: ✗ *it was proved longer*, ✗ *safe was proved by the drug*.

To establish that something is true, or to turn out to be a certain way.

**1. Show something to be true.** The core sense: evidence or argument that settles a question. In the perfect tense American prose prefers *proved*; *proven* is the form that goes in front of a noun — *a proven method*, *proven reserves*. The past is always *proved*: ✗ *he proven it in court*. (доказывать, подтверждать)

1. Numbers by themselves don't *prove* much.
2. The prosecution never *proved* he was in the building.
3. She *has proved* every one of her critics wrong.
4. They only hire people with a *proven* track record.

**2. Turn out to be.** A linking verb, followed by an adjective, a noun, or *to be* — the subject is not being demonstrated to anyone, it simply turns out a certain way. This sense has no passive: ✗ *it was proved longer*. (оказаться (каким-л.))

5. The drive *proved* longer than the map suggested.
6. The drug *had proved* safe in two earlier trials.
7. That shortcut always *proves* to be slower.
8. Time *proves* most confident predictions wrong.

**3. prove oneself — show what one can do.** Reflexive, about earning a reputation rather than establishing a fact. Also *prove someone wrong*, which is the commonest fixed pattern built on this verb. (проявить себя, показать, на что способен)

9. Rookies have to *prove* themselves all over again in the playoffs.
10. She *has proved* herself in three different departments.
11. He *proved* his doubters wrong in a single season.

**4. Prove a theorem.** The mathematical and logical sense: deriving a result from axioms or premises. Here *proven* is common in both slots — *has proven*, *a proven result* — more so than in ordinary prose. (доказывать (теорему, утверждение))

12. You can *prove* it in two lines with induction.
13. Wiles *proved* Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1990s.
14. No one *has proved* the conjecture in either direction.

**5. Test, put to the test.** The original sense, now archaic on its own but preserved in *proving ground*, in the saying *the exception that proves the rule*, and in the legal *prove a will*. (испытывать, проверять на прочность)

15. "The exception that *proves* the rule" uses the old sense, "tests."
16. The desert *proved* every part of the vehicle.

**Transitivity:** **ditransitive and more** — two objects (*show me the photo* / *show the photo to me*), plain transitive (*the audit showed no missing funds*), and intransitive in the *be visible* sense (*the stain doesn't show under the rug*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and either object can become the subject: *both witnesses were shown the same lineup*, *her work has been shown in three cities*, *he hates being shown up in front of his kids*. No passive of the intransitive *the stain doesn't show*.

To let someone see something — and from there to demonstrate, guide, teach, reveal, be visible, or turn up.

**1. Let someone see something.** The core sense, with two objects: *show me the photo*, *show the photo to me*. The past is *showed*, the participle is *shown*: ✗ *he has showed me the file* wants *has shown*, and ✗ *he shown me the file* wants *showed*. (показывать, предъявлять)

1. Let me *show* you where the fuse box is.
2. He *showed* his ID at the door and walked straight in.
3. She *has shown* me that same photograph four times.
4. Both witnesses were *shown* the same lineup.

**2. Demonstrate, be evidence of.** With data, research, a record, or a result as the subject. The standard verb of scientific and news writing: *the study shows that…* (показывать, свидетельствовать, доказывать)

5. The data *shows* a steady drop since March.
6. The audit *showed* no missing funds.
7. Two later studies *have shown* the opposite.

**3. Guide, lead someone somewhere.** Always with a direction word: *show someone in, out, around, to their seat, up the stairs*. (проводить, отвести, показать дорогу)

8. An usher will *show* you to your seat.
9. She *showed* us around the plant for an hour.
10. They *had shown* him out before he finished the sentence.

**4. Teach by demonstrating.** Usually *show someone how to* do something — teaching by doing it in front of them rather than explaining. (научить, показать (как что-л. делать))

11. *Show* me how you tie that knot.
12. He *showed* the whole crew the shortcut in five minutes.
13. Nobody *had shown* him how the machine worked.

**5. Reveal a feeling or quality.** Of a person letting an emotion appear, or of a body, a machine, or a patient showing signs of something. Often negative: *never shows any nerves*. (проявлять, выказывать, обнаруживать)

14. He never *shows* frustration on the mound.
15. The patient *showed* no signs of infection.
16. She *has shown* nothing but patience with him.

**6. Be visible.** Intransitive, with no object — a stain, a repair, a seam, or a feeling that can be seen. Also of pregnancy: *she's starting to show*. (быть заметным, виднеться, проступать)

17. The stain doesn't *show* under the rug.
18. His nerves *showed* the whole first period.
19. The patch has faded so much it barely *shows* now.

**7. Exhibit or screen.** Of a gallery hanging work, a theater running a film, or an artist having work exhibited. Frequently passive: *her work has been shown in three cities*. (выставлять; демонстрировать, показывать (фильм))

20. The museum is *showing* forty of her drawings.
21. They *showed* the film twice a night all summer.
22. Her work *has been shown* in three cities.

**8. show up — arrive, appear.** Informal but extremely common; also of a thing becoming detectable — *the crack showed up on the X-ray*. (появиться, объявиться, прийти)

23. Half the team never *showed up*.
24. He *has shown up* late to every rehearsal.
25. Nobody *shows up* to those meetings anymore.

**9. show off — display to impress.** Intransitive of a person behaving boastfully, transitive of the thing being displayed. Mildly disapproving; the noun is *a show-off*. (выпендриваться, хвастаться, красоваться)

26. He *shows off* every time there's a camera.
27. She *showed off* the new car for a solid ten minutes.
28. They *have shown off* that kitchen to everyone who visits.

**10. show someone up — outdo, embarrass.** The object goes between the verb and *up*. Distinct from *show up* meaning "arrive" — here someone is made to look bad by comparison. (утереть нос, выставить в невыгодном свете)

29. The intern *showed up* three senior analysts at the whiteboard.
30. He hates being *shown up* in front of his kids.

### sow

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ &middot; *SOH*
**Русский:** (по)сеять
**Forms:** *sow* /soʊ/ &middot; *sowed* /soʊd/ &middot; *sown* /soʊn/ &middot; American also *sowed* /soʊd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in farming and in the figurative *sow doubt*, *as you sow, so shall you reap*
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *sows* &middot; **-ing form:** *sowing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Two different words share this spelling. The verb here rhymes with *go* — /soʊ/ — and means "plant seed"; the noun *sow*, a female pig, rhymes with *now*, /saʊ/. Both participles are standard American, but *sown* is the usual one, and it is nearly automatic in the figurative sense: *doubt sown early*. Sounds identical to [sew](#sew).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the farming sense and in the figurative *sow doubt* alike; no stative or linking use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, with either the seed or the ground as object: *sowed rye across the whole back field*, *sowed the north forty in clover*. The object drops when the method is the point: *she sows by hand*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the usual shape of the figurative sense: *doubt had been sown long before the vote*; also *the terraces had been sown with barley for centuries*.

To scatter seed on ground so it will grow — and, figuratively, to plant an idea or a feeling that will grow later.

**1. Plant seed.** The core farming sense, with the seed or crop as the object. Rhymes with *go*, /soʊ/ — the noun *sow* meaning a female pig is a different word, /saʊ/. Both participles are standard, but *sown* is the usual American choice. (сеять, высевать)

1. We *sow* the cover crop right after the last harvest.
2. He *sowed* rye across the whole back field.
3. The wind *has sown* thistle along every fence line.
4. She *sows* the small plots by hand.

**2. Plant a field with a crop.** The same action seen from the other end: the ground is the object, and the crop follows with *in* or *with*. (засевать (поле, участок))

5. They *sowed* the north forty in clover.
6. A field *sown* in late October comes up thin.
7. The terraces *had been sown* with barley for centuries.

**3. Spread an idea or feeling.** Figurative, and almost always something unwelcome — doubt, discord, confusion, panic. *Sown* is nearly automatic here. The proverb *as you sow, so shall you reap* belongs to this sense. (сеять, посеять (сомнение, раздор, панику))

8. Doubt *had been sown* long before the vote.
9. Those rumors *sow* confusion on purpose.
10. The memo *sowed* panic in half the building.

**4. sow one's wild oats.** A fixed idiom about reckless behavior in youth, followed by settling down. Traditionally said of young men, and dated in tone. (перебеситься в молодости, нагуляться)

11. He *sowed* his wild oats and then settled down fast.
12. Everybody's entitled to *sow* a few wild oats.

### strew

**Pronunciation:** /stru/ &middot; *STROO*
**Русский:** усеять; устлать; разбрасывать; разбрызгивать
**Forms:** *strew* /stru/ &middot; *strewed* /strud/ &middot; *strewn* /strun/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing, especially *strewn*, less common in speech
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *strews* &middot; **-ing form:** *strewing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Unlike most of this group, *strew* keeps its -n participle firmly in American English: *the floor was strewn with paper*, not ✗ *strewed with paper*. *Strewed* is acceptable but uncommon. Watch the vowel — /stru/, like *true*, with no /j/ glide, unlike [hew](#hew) /hju/.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic verb of scattering. Unlike most of this group it keeps a live -n participle: *strewn*, not ✗ *strewed with paper*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — either the scattered things (*they strew sawdust on the floor*) or the covered surface (*every draft he strews with footnotes*) can be the object.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is the commonest shape of the verb: *the shoulder of the road was strewn with glass*, *his career is strewn with abandoned projects*.

To scatter things loosely over a surface so that they lie spread around.

**1. Scatter things over a surface.** The active sense: sawdust on a floor, petals on an aisle, gravel on a walk. Unlike most of this group, *strew* keeps its -n participle firmly in American English; *strewed* is acceptable but uncommon. (разбрасывать, посыпать, усыпать)

1. They *strew* sawdust on the floor before every show.
2. The storm *strewed* branches across both lanes of the highway.
3. Someone *has strewn* gravel all over the walkway.
4. She *strews* wildflower seed along the ditch every spring.

**2. be strewn with — lie covered in.** The passive is the commonest shape of this verb. The surface becomes the subject and the scattered things follow with *with*: *the floor was strewn with paper*. (быть усыпанным, усеянным (чем-л.))

5. The room *had been strewn* with unopened mail for weeks.
6. Papers lay *strewn* across every surface in the office.
7. The shoulder of the road was *strewn* with glass.

**3. Figurative — dotted with, full of.** Of a text, a career, or a record marked all over with something — errors, references, abandoned projects. Written register. (изобиловать, быть усеянным (ошибками, отсылками))

8. His career is *strewn* with abandoned projects.
9. Every draft he *strews* with footnotes nobody can follow.
10. The report *had been strewn* with numbers no one could source.

### swell

**Pronunciation:** /swɛl/ &middot; *SWEL*
**Русский:** надувать, раздувать; набухать; опухать
**Forms:** *swell* /swɛl/ &middot; *swelled* /swɛld/ &middot; *swollen* /ˈswoʊlən/ &middot; American also *swelled* /swɛld/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary of injuries, crowds, and rivers
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — o-vowel + -en &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *swells* &middot; **-ing form:** *swelling* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Both participles are standard, and Americans sort them by sense. Physical swelling takes *swollen*: *my ankle has swollen*, *a swollen river*. Growth in number, volume, or pride usually takes *swelled*: *the crowd has swelled to ten thousand*, *a swelled head*. The past tense is always *swelled*: ✗ *my ankle swollen overnight* needs *swelled*. This is the group's healthiest -en participle; compare [melt](#melt), where the same old ending died off entirely.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic process verb, fine in the progressive (*the crowd is swelling*). Not stative, despite the resultant adjective *swollen*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — overwhelmingly intransitive (*his ankle swelled within minutes*, *the chorus swelled until it filled the room*), transitive only with an external causer: *two good reviews have swelled his head*, *runoff swelled the channel*.
**Passive:** **restricted**, effectively no — the transitive use passivizes badly and *was swollen* reads as an adjective rather than an action: ✗ *his head was swelled by two good reviews*. The intransitive senses have no passive at all.

To get bigger — by puffing up, expanding, rising in number, or growing louder.

**1. Puff up (of the body).** Injury, infection, or an allergic reaction. This is the sense that takes *swollen*: *my ankle has swollen*, *a swollen lip*. The past is always *swelled* — ✗ *my ankle swollen overnight*. (опухать, отекать, распухать)

1. Bee stings *swell* more than they hurt.
2. His ankle *swelled* within minutes.
3. Her knee *has swollen* to twice its normal size.
4. He walked in with a *swollen* lip and would not say why.

**2. Grow in number or size.** Of a crowd, a budget, membership, or a population. Growth in quantity normally takes *swelled* rather than *swollen*. (расти, увеличиваться (в числе, в объёме))

5. The crowd *swelled* after the second band came on.
6. Membership *swells* every January and drops off by March.
7. Enrollment *has swelled* by a third since 2019.

**3. Rise, run high (of water).** Of a river or the sea rising above its normal level. The related noun *a swell* is the long rolling wave that follows a storm. (подниматься, вздуваться (о реке, о море))

8. The creek *had swollen* over the low bridge by dawn.
9. Every river in the county *swells* after a week like that.
10. The channel *swelled* with runoff for three days.

**4. Grow louder (of sound).** Of music, applause, or noise building gradually. Common in writing about performance; the musical noun is *a swell*. (нарастать, усиливаться (о звуке))

11. The strings *swell* under the last line of dialogue.
12. The chorus *swelled* until it filled the room.
13. The noise *had swelled* into something you could feel.
14. Applause *has swelled* every night this week.

**5. Fill with emotion.** Of a chest or a heart filling with pride, love, or anger. The fixed *a swelled head* means conceit, and takes *swelled*, not *swollen*. (переполняться (гордостью, чувством))

15. His chest *swelled* when they read her name.
16. Pride *had swelled* in him all evening.
17. Two good reviews *have swelled* his head considerably.

**6. Expand from moisture.** Of wood, seeds, beans, or paper taking up water and growing. Everyday practical usage. (разбухать, набухать)

18. Wooden doors *swell* in humid weather.
19. The beans *swelled* to twice their size overnight.
20. The frame *had swollen* shut after a week of rain.

### wax

**Pronunciation:** /wæks/ &middot; *WAKS*
**Русский:** прибывать (о Луне); развиваться; становиться
**Forms:** *wax* /wæks/ &middot; *waxed* /wækst/ &middot; *waxen* /ˈwæksən/ &middot; the American participle is *waxed* /wækst/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — waxing the car, waxing a floor, a waxing moon; only *waxen* is literary
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -en &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *waxes* (-es after x) &middot; **-ing form:** *waxing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** The verb is regular today: *the moon has waxed*, never ✗ *has waxen*. *Waxen* survives only as an adjective meaning "pale and stiff, like wax" — *a waxen face*. Its opposite is *wane*, and the pair is fixed: *waxed and waned*. This is the same regular verb as the everyday *wax* meaning "polish" or "remove hair." Compare [melt](#melt): another -en form stranded as an adjective.
**Verb type:** **action** in the growing sense (*the moon waxed all week*) and in the polishing and depilation senses; **linking (copular)** in the fixed *wax poetic / lyrical / eloquent / nostalgic*, meaning *become* — and it will not extend past those collocations: ✗ *he waxed angry*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive in the moon and influence senses (*his influence has waxed and waned*) and in the linking use; transitive in the everyday senses: *I wax the car twice a year*, *she waxes her own eyebrows*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — yes for the *apply wax* and *remove hair* senses: *the floors were waxed before the open house*, *both her arms were waxed in twenty minutes*. **No** passive of the growing sense (it is intransitive) or of the linking one: ✗ *nostalgic was waxed by the critics*.

To grow larger or stronger — said of the moon, and of moods, fashions, and reputations — and, in the everyday sense, to apply wax to something.

**1. Grow bigger (of the moon).** The lit face of the moon growing from new toward full. Its opposite is *wane*, and the pair is fixed: *waxed and waned*. The verb is regular — *the moon has waxed*, never ✗ *has waxen*. (прибывать (о Луне))

1. The moon *waxed* all week toward Friday's full.
2. A *waxing* crescent is easiest to see just after sunset.
3. The moon *has waxed* and waned twice since we started counting.

**2. Increase, grow stronger.** Of influence, enthusiasm, or interest. Formal and slightly literary; usually paired with *wane*, and rarely used alone in speech. (расти, усиливаться, крепнуть)

4. His influence *has waxed* and waned twice in ten years.
5. Interest in the case *waxes* every time a new document surfaces.
6. Enthusiasm *had waxed* considerably by the second rehearsal.

**3. wax poetic, wax nostalgic — become.** A linking use surviving in a handful of fixed collocations — *wax poetic, lyrical, eloquent, nostalgic, philosophical* — and usually with a light touch of mockery. You cannot extend it freely: ✗ *he waxed angry*. (становиться (лирическим, ностальгическим), пускаться в (рассуждения))

7. Critics still *wax* nostalgic about the first two albums.
8. He *waxed* poetic about a sandwich for ten minutes.
9. She *has waxed* eloquent on this subject before.

**4. Polish or coat with wax.** The ordinary modern sense, unrelated in feel to the growing one but the same regular verb: cars, floors, skis, surfboards. (натирать воском, вощить)

10. I *wax* the car twice a year.
11. She *waxed* the floors before the open house.
12. They *have waxed* these boards every fall since 1970.

**5. Remove hair with wax.** Salon usage, transitive with the body part or the person as the object. The related noun is *a wax*. (делать восковую депиляцию)

13. She *waxes* her own eyebrows.
14. The salon *waxed* both her arms in twenty minutes.
15. I *have* never *waxed* anything in my life.
16. She lay very still, her face *waxen* in the lamplight.

**See also:** [melt](#melt), [shave](#shave)

**Русский:** пилить лучковой пилой
**Forms:** *whipsaw* /ˈwɪpsɔ/ &middot; *whipsawed* /ˈwɪpsɔd/ &middot; *whipsawn* /ˈwɪpsɔn/ &middot; the American participle is *whipsawed* /ˈwɪpsɔd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a business-page verb built on *saw*; the literal woodworking sense is dead
**Pattern:** base — base + -ed — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11
**Third person:** *whipsaws* &middot; **-ing form:** *whipsawing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Built on [saw](#saw) and following it in everything, including the American preference for *whipsawed* over the British-flavored *whipsawn*. **Verb type:** action — dynamic; the living American sense is figurative (hit from two directions at once), the literal woodworking one nearly obsolete.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is the person, team, or market caught from both sides: *rising rates and falling rents whipsawed small landlords last year*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is the normal form: *the team had been whipsawed by injuries on both lines*, *traders have been whipsawed by three straight reversals*. American English uses *whipsawed*, not ✗ *whipsawn*.

To hurt someone from two directions at once — and, literally, to cut with a long two-handled saw.

**1. Hit from two directions at once.** The living American sense, a staple of business and sports writing, and usually passive: costs rising while revenue falls, injuries on both lines. Built on [saw](#saw) and following it in everything, including the American preference for *whipsawed* over British-flavored *whipsawn*. (бить с двух сторон, зажимать в тиски (обстоятельств))

1. Rising rates and falling rents *whipsawed* small landlords last year.
2. The team *had been whipsawed* by injuries on both lines.
3. A thin market *whipsaws* anyone who trades on momentum.

**2. Be caught by market reversals.** The specific trading sense: a price that reverses just after you act, so you lose on both the entry and the exit. Nearly always passive — *whipsawed by*. (выбить стоп-лоссы резкими разворотами (о рынке))

4. Traders *have been whipsawed* by three straight reversals.
5. The index *whipsawed* between gains and losses all afternoon.

**3. Cut with a two-handled saw.** The literal woodworking sense, worked by two people pulling in turn. Nearly obsolete outside historical and milling contexts. (пилить продольной (лучковой) пилой)

6. Two workers *whipsaw* a log from opposite ends of the blade.
7. They *had whipsawed* every timber in the barn by hand.
8. Old mill records describe the planks as *whipsawn* rather than milled.

### writhe

**Русский:** корчиться, скручиваться
**Forms:** *writhe* /raɪð/ &middot; *writhed* /raɪðd/ &middot; *writhen* /ˈraɪðən/ &middot; the American participle is *writhed* /raɪðd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing and description, less common in casual speech
**Pattern:** base — base + -d — base + -n &middot; **Group:** 11

To twist the body, usually from pain or acute embarrassment.

**1. Twist in pain.** The core sense. The w is silent and the *th* is the voiced /ð/ of *bathe*, not the /θ/ of *both*. American English uses *has writhed*; ✗ *has writhen* is archaic. (корчиться, извиваться (от боли))

1. Burn patients *writhe* even under sedation.
2. He *writhed* on the field for a full minute before the trainer reached him.
3. The patient *had writhed* all night before the medication took hold.

**2. Squirm from embarrassment.** Figurative, of acute social discomfort. Often *writhe with embarrassment* or *writhe through* something. (ёжиться, корчиться (от стыда, неловкости))

4. He *writhes* visibly whenever anyone brings up the old commercial.
5. She *has writhed* through worse interviews than that one.
6. I *writhed* in my seat for the whole toast.

**3. Twist, coil (of a shape).** Of snakes, smoke, vines, or roots moving or growing in twisting lines. The archaic *writhen* meaning "twisted" survives only in poetry; modern prose reaches for *twisted* or *gnarled*. (извиваться, скручиваться, виться)

7. Snakes *writhe* when you pin them behind the head.
8. Smoke *writhed* up from the pile all afternoon.
9. Old poems describe *writhen* roots gripping the bare rock.
