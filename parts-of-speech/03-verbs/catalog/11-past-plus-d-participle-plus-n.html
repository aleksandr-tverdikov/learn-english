Past = base + -d, participle = base + -n#

The past adds -d and the participle adds -n, both to the unchanged base.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

These verbs leave the base form untouched and simply hang a different ending on each of the other two: show — showed — shown, sow — sowed — sown, strew — strewed — strewn (2-я = 1-я + "d", 3-я = 1-я + "n"). The past is perfectly regular, which is what makes the group feel easy; the only irregular thing anywhere in it is that -n on the participle. In American English that -n form is still the working participle for a handful of these verbs — shown, strewn, sewn, swollen — but for most of the rest it has retreated into adjectives (molten metal, a rotten board, clean-shaven, a graven image) and left the plain -ed participle in charge. So read the Contrast line in each entry before you trust the -n form in your own writing: this is the group where the spreadsheet's pattern and current American usage disagree most often.

опровергать; доказывать ложность (чего-л.)
Forms
disprove /dɪsˈpruv/ · disproved /dɪsˈpruvd/ · disproven /dɪsˈpruvən/ · American prose more often uses disproved
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — ordinary in argument, news, and science writing; nothing marked about it
Pattern
base — base + -d — base + -n
Group
11
Third person
disproves · -ing form: disproving (drop the e)
Contrast
Prefix plus prove, and it behaves exactly like its base verb. Both participles are correct, but edited American writing uses has disproved noticeably more than has disproven, while the -n form is most at home in front of a noun: a disproven theory. The past tense is always disproved: ✗ he disproven it.
Verb type
action — dynamic verb of demonstration, comfortable in the progressive (they're disproving it point by point). No stative or linking use.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is a claim, theory, rumor, or alibi, and it is obligatory: disprove her central claim; ✗ the lab disproved is incomplete.
Passive
yes — nobody has disproved her central claim yet, by 1960 the idea had been disproven several times over.

To show by evidence or argument that a claim, theory, or story is false.

1. Show something to be false. The core sense, and nearly the only one. The object is a claim, theory, rumor, or alibi — you disprove a statement, not a person. The -n participle disproven is standard in front of a noun (a disproven theory), but the perfect tense in edited American prose is usually has disproved. (опровергать, доказывать ложность)

  1. One clean experiment can disprove a decade of theory.
  2. The lab disproved the original finding in under a month.
  3. Nobody has disproved her central claim yet.
  4. By 1960 the idea had been disproven several times over.
  5. A disproven hypothesis still teaches you something.

2. disprove a negative — the argument phrase. A fixed idiom about the burden of proof: you can't disprove a negative means the person making the claim has to support it, not the skeptic. Common in debate, law, and science writing. (доказать отсутствие (чего-л.), опровергнуть отрицательное утверждение)

  1. You can't disprove a negative, so the burden stays with the accuser.
  2. He disproves every rumor about the company by simply showing up.

гравировать
Forms
engrave /ɪnˈɡreɪv/ · engraved /ɪnˈɡreɪvd/ · engraven /ɪnˈɡreɪvən/ · the American participle is engraved
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — the ordinary American word for what a jeweler or a trophy shop does
Pattern
base — base + -d — base + -n
Group
11
Third person
engraves · -ing form: engraving (drop the e)
Contrast
In modern American English engrave is simply a regular verb: engraved is both the past and the participle. Engraven is a nineteenth-century form you will meet only in older books and as an echo of graven image — never write ✗ has engraven. Same story as grave, the older verb this one was built from.
Verb type
action — dynamic in all three senses: cutting a surface, making a printing plate, and fixing something in the memory.
Transitivity
both — normally transitive (engraved our initials inside the ring), but the object drops when the technique is the point: he engraves by hand.
Passive
yes, and the memory sense is nearly always passive — that afternoon is engraved on my memory; also US currency is still engraved rather than photographed onto the plate.

To cut letters or a design into a hard surface — and, figuratively, to fix something permanently in the mind.

1. Cut letters or a design into a surface. The everyday sense, used of metal, glass, stone, and jewelry. In modern American English the verb is simply regular: engraved serves as both past and participle, and ✗ has engraven is a nineteenth-century form nobody writes now. (гравировать, вырезать (надпись))

  1. They engrave the winner's name on the cup every June.
  2. The jeweler engraved our initials inside the ring.
  3. She has engraved glass for thirty years.
  4. He engraves by hand, without a machine.

2. Make a printing plate; produce an engraving. The printmaking and currency sense: cutting an image into a plate or block so it can be inked and printed. Mostly technical or historical now. (гравировать (печатную форму), делать гравюру)

  1. Winslow Homer engraved wood blocks for the illustrated weeklies.
  2. US currency is still engraved rather than photographed onto the plate.
  3. The bank note had been engraved by hand in 1890.

3. Fix permanently in the memory. Figurative, usually passive and followed by on or in: engraved on my memory. The nineteenth-century form engraven survives only as an echo of graven image in older hymns and poetry. (запечатлеть, врезать (в память))

  1. That afternoon is engraved on my memory.
  2. Thirty years of practice have engraved the movement into her hands.
  3. The rule had been engraved in every trainee's head by week two.
  4. Nineteenth-century hymns speak of a name engraven on the heart.
See also
grave, shape

Pronunciation
/nɔ/ · NAW

грызть, глодать
Forms
gnaw /nɔ/ · gnawed /nɔd/ · gnawn /nɔn/ · the American participle is gnawed
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — common of animals literally and of worry figuratively
Pattern
base — base + -ed — base + -n
Group
11
Third person
gnaws · -ing form: gnawing (no spelling change)
Contrast
The g is silent in every form — /nɔ/, not ✗ /ɡnɔ/. Gnawn is rare enough that most Americans have never seen it in print; write has gnawed. Compare saw and whipsaw, which share the same /ɔ/ vowel and the same optional, mostly British -n participle.
Verb type
action — dynamic and iterative (repeated small bites); the figurative gnaw at is just as dynamic, not stative.
Transitivity
both — transitive when a hole results (something gnawed a hole in the bag), intransitive with on or at when none does (puppies gnaw on anything, doubt gnawed at him).
Passive
restricted — fine with a real direct object: the insulation has been gnawed through again. The gnaw at pattern resists it: ✗ he was gnawed at by doubt.

To bite at something over and over with small, wearing bites — literally, or in the sense of a worry that keeps working away at you.

1. Bite at something repeatedly. The literal sense, typical of rodents, dogs, and teething children. Often with on or at when no hole is made, and with a direct object when one is. The g is silent in every form, /nɔ/. (грызть, глодать)

  1. Puppies gnaw on anything within reach.
  2. Something gnawed a hole in the bottom of the bag.
  3. Mice have gnawed through the insulation again.
  4. The dog gnaws the same bone every evening.

2. gnaw at — nag, torment. Figurative, with a worry, doubt, guilt, or unanswered question as the subject. Almost always with at, and the feeling is persistent rather than sharp. (грызть, мучить, не давать покоя (о сомнении, тревоге))

  1. Doubt had gnawed at him all week.
  2. The question gnaws at me every time I drive past that exit.
  3. Guilt gnawed at her for years afterward.

3. Wear away gradually. Of water, wind, rust, or inflation eating slowly into something. Written rather than spoken register; gnawn is rare enough in American print that you should write has gnawed. (подтачивать, разъедать, размывать)

  1. The river gnaws a little more of the bank away each spring.
  2. Rust has gnawed the bottom of both doors.
  3. In older writing you will find a rope described as gnawn clear through.
See also
saw, whipsaw

An old verb meaning to carve or cut into a hard surface, now alive almost only in its participle graven.

1. Carve or cut into stone or metal. Archaic. Not the noun grave ("burial place") or the adjective grave ("serious") — this is the old verb that engrave replaced. For anything you write yourself, use engrave or carve. (гравировать, высекать, вырезать)

  1. Older writers use grave where we would now say engrave.
  2. The mason graved the family name across the lintel.
  3. Those letters had been graved into the stone centuries earlier.
  4. The printmaker graves the plate first and pulls a proof afterward.

2. graven — carved; cut deep. The participle is the only part of the verb still in circulation, and it works as an adjective: the graven image of the Ten Commandments, or lines graven in a face by hard years. Slightly biblical or literary in tone. (изваянный, высеченный; врезавшийся (в память, в лицо))

  1. The commandment against graven images is the second one.
  2. Two hard years have graven deep lines in his face.
  3. The motto was graven over the doorway in Latin.

engrave, shave

Pronunciation
/hju/ · HYOO
Русский
рубить; тесать
Forms
hew /hju/ · hewed /hjud/ · hewn /hjun/ · American also hewed /hjud/
Verb class
irregular
Register
rare — a specialized woodworking verb, alive mainly in hew to and rough-hewn
Pattern
base — base + -ed — base + -n
Group
11
Third person
hews · -ing form: hewing (no spelling change)
Contrast
Both participles are alive in American English, but they divide the work. Hewn almost always stands in front of a noun — hewn beams, rough-hewn stone — while hewed carries the perfect tense: the board has hewed to the same policy for years. That second, figurative sense, hew to meaning "stick closely to," is far commoner in American writing than the woodcutting one. Note the /j/ glide, /hju/, which strew does not have.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, the literal chopping and the figurative hew to alike. Rough-hewn is a fixed adjective, not a verb form in use.
Transitivity
both — transitive in the cutting senses (hewed the beam square, hewed a path through the brush), intransitive with to in the commoner figurative sense (the committee has hewed closely to the original plan).
Passive
restricted — yes for the literal sense: the cabin walls were hewn from a single stand of pine, crews have hewn a road out of solid rock. No passive of hew to: ✗ the plan has been hewed to.

To cut or chop something hard with heavy blows — and, in the commonest American use, to stick closely to a line, a plan, or a principle.

1. Chop or cut with heavy blows. The literal sense, used of timber and stone worked with an ax, adze, or chisel. Here the participle is hewn, especially before a noun: hewn beams, rough-hewn stone. (рубить, тесать)

  1. They still hew the timbers by hand at the restoration site.
  2. He hewed the beam square with a broadax.
  3. Masons have hewn stone on this hillside since the 1800s.
  4. The cabin walls were hewn from a single stand of pine.

2. hew to — stick closely to. Figurative and by far the commoner sense in American writing — of a policy, a script, a party line, or a schedule. This sense takes hewed in the perfect, not hewn. (твёрдо придерживаться, следовать (чему-л.))

  1. The committee has hewed closely to the original plan.
  2. The adaptation hewed to the novel almost line for line.
  3. She hews to her schedule no matter who complains.

3. hew out — make by cutting away. Carving something usable out of raw material or wilderness — a path, a road, a farm, a career. Carries a flavor of hard, physical effort even when the object is abstract. (прорубать, вырубать; создавать с трудом)

  1. He hewed a path through the brush with a machete.
  2. Crews have hewn a road out of solid rock.
  3. Settlers hew a farm out of the woods one acre at a time.
  4. Each block had been hewn to roughly the same size.

4. rough-hewn — unpolished. A fixed compound adjective, used of surfaces left rough by the ax and, by extension, of a plain, unrefined manner or style. Only the participle appears in this use. (грубо отёсанный; неотёсанный, простоватый)

  1. The furniture is deliberately rough-hewn.
  2. His manner was rough-hewn but never rude.
See also
mow, strew, saw

melt#

Pronunciation
/mɛlt/ · MELT
Русский
плавить
Forms
melt /mɛlt/ · melted /ˈmɛltɪd/ · molten /ˈmoʊltən/ · the working American participle is melted /ˈmɛltɪd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base — base + -ed — o-vowel + -en
Group
11
Third person
melts · -ing form: melting (no spelling change)
Contrast
Melt is a regular verb today: the snow has melted, never ✗ the snow has molten. Molten survives only as an adjective, only in front of a noun, and only for things that melt at furnace heat — molten lava, molten glass, molten steel. Nobody says ✗ molten ice cream. Compare swell, whose old -en participle is still fully in use, and rot, which stranded its -en form the same way this one did.
Verb type
action — dynamic and ergative: the same event stated intransitively (the ice melted) or transitively (melt the butter). Never stative, never linking; molten is an adjective only.
Transitivity
both — intransitive with the thing that changes as subject (all the snow has melted off the north slope), transitive with a causer (melt the butter first, they melted down the old trophies).
Passive
restricted — natural for the transitive senses: the trophies were melted down for the silver. The intransitive senses have no passive at all: ✗ the snow was melted away, ✗ the crowd was melted into.

To go from solid to liquid under heat, or to make something do that; by extension, to soften, fade out, or disappear.

1. Turn from solid to liquid with heat. The core sense, both transitive and intransitive. Melt is a regular verb today — the snow has melted, never ✗ has molten. Molten survives only as an adjective, only before a noun, and only for furnace-hot material. (плавить(ся), таять, растапливать)

  1. Melt the butter first, then whisk in the flour.
  2. The ice melted before we got the cooler open.
  3. All the snow has melted off the north slope.
  4. By noon the road tar had melted into black ribbons.
  5. The furnace holds forty tons of molten iron.

2. Dissolve in the mouth or in liquid. Used of food that goes soft without any obvious heat, and of sugar or salt disappearing into a hot liquid. The compliment melts in your mouth is fixed. (таять (во рту), растворяться)

  1. These cookies melt in your mouth.
  2. The sugar had melted into the hot cream before she stirred it.
  3. The cheese melted down into the bread.

3. Soften emotionally. Of a person, a face, or a heart giving way to affection or pity. Intransitive, informal and warm in tone; the transitive use (that melted him) is common too. (растрогаться, смягчиться, растаять)

  1. Her whole face melts when the dog comes in.
  2. He melted the second she started crying.
  3. That video has melted every parent who has seen it.

4. melt away — disappear gradually. Of a crowd, a lead, a fortune, or a fear that shrinks to nothing rather than ending at once. (растаять, рассеяться, испариться)

  1. The crowd melted away as soon as the police arrived.
  2. His lead in the polls has melted away since June.
  3. Whatever nerve she had melted away at the microphone.

5. melt down — reduce to liquid; lose control. Three uses of one phrasal verb: melting metal down for its material, a reactor core melting down, and — informally — a person falling apart emotionally. (переплавлять; расплавиться (об активной зоне); сорваться, устроить истерику)

  1. They melted down the old trophies for the silver.
  2. Reactor cores melt down when the coolant fails.
  3. The toddler had melted down twice before we reached the gate.

6. melt into — blend imperceptibly. Of someone slipping out of sight into a crowd or a background, or of one color, sound, or landscape shading into another with no clear edge. (слиться, раствориться (в толпе, в пейзаже))

  1. He melted into the crowd before anyone got a photograph.
  2. The hills melt into haze by mid-afternoon.
  3. The suspect had melted into the neighborhood by the time the units arrived.
See also
swell, rot, wax

mows · -ing form: mowing (no spelling change)
Contrast
Americans say I have mowed the lawn; have mown reads as British. Mown does survive here as an adjective — newly mown grass, a mown field — and the fixed phrasal verb is always mowed down, never ✗ mown down, for people cut down by gunfire or a car. Transitivity

Passive

To cut down grass or standing grain — and, in mow down, to cut down people or things in numbers.

1. Cut grass short. The everyday American sense: a lawn, a field, a ball field, a ditch bank. Americans write have mowed; have mown reads as British, though mown survives here as an adjective — freshly mown grass. (косить, стричь (газон, траву))

  1. I mow the yard every other Saturday all summer.
  2. He mowed around the flower beds without being asked.
  3. The crew has mowed the ball field twice this week.
  4. Nothing smells better than freshly mown hay.

2. Cut a hay or grain crop. The older farming sense, where the point is the harvest rather than a tidy lawn. The field, not the crop, can be the object: mow the back forty. (жать, косить (сено, хлеб))

  1. They mow the back forty for hay in July.
  2. Crews mowed the alfalfa twice last season.
  3. By the time we arrived, someone had mowed the whole hillside.

3. mow down — cut down in numbers. Of gunfire, a vehicle, or a disease killing or knocking down many at once, and in sports of a pitcher or team dispatching opponents easily. The idiom is always mowed down, never ✗ mown down. (скосить, смести (очередью, машиной); разгромить)

  1. The truck jumped the curb and mowed down a row of mailboxes.
  2. Machine guns had mowed down the first two waves.
  3. Their closer mows down the bottom of the order every night.

Pronunciation
/pruv/ · PROOV
Русский
доказывать; оказаться
Forms
prove /pruv/ · proved /pruvd/ · proven /ˈpruvən/ · the ordinary American participle is proved /pruvd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
base — base + -d — base + -n
Group
11
Third person
proves · -ing form: proving (drop the e)
Contrast
Both participles are standard, and they divide the labor. In a perfect tense, edited American prose prefers proved: she has proved her point. Proven is the form that goes in front of a noun — a proven method, proven reserves — and it is frozen into the legal verdict not proven. The past tense is always proved: ✗ he proven it in court. See also disprove and reprove, which inherit the pattern.
Verb type
action in the demonstrating senses (prove a theorem, prove his doubters wrong), but linking (copular) in sense 2, turn out to be: the drive proved longer than the map suggested, the drug had proved safe.
Transitivity
transitive in the demonstrating senses, object obligatory (the prosecution never proved he was in the building), often with a following complement (proved his doubters wrong). The linking sense takes a subject complement and no object: that shortcut always proves to be slower.
Passive
restricted — yes in the demonstrating senses: no one has proved the conjecture, the estate had been proved in probate court. No passive of the linking sense: ✗ it was proved longer, ✗ safe was proved by the drug.

To establish that something is true, or to turn out to be a certain way.

1. Show something to be true. The core sense: evidence or argument that settles a question. In the perfect tense American prose prefers proved; proven is the form that goes in front of a noun — a proven method, proven reserves. The past is always proved: ✗ he proven it in court. (доказывать, подтверждать)

  1. Numbers by themselves don't prove much.
  2. The prosecution never proved he was in the building.
  3. She has proved every one of her critics wrong.
  4. They only hire people with a proven track record.

2. Turn out to be. A linking verb, followed by an adjective, a noun, or to be — the subject is not being demonstrated to anyone, it simply turns out a certain way. This sense has no passive: ✗ it was proved longer. (оказаться (каким-л.))

  1. The drive proved longer than the map suggested.
  2. The drug had proved safe in two earlier trials.
  3. That shortcut always proves to be slower.
  4. Time proves most confident predictions wrong.

3. prove oneself — show what one can do. Reflexive, about earning a reputation rather than establishing a fact. Also prove someone wrong, which is the commonest fixed pattern built on this verb. (проявить себя, показать, на что способен)

  1. Rookies have to prove themselves all over again in the playoffs.
  2. She has proved herself in three different departments.
  3. He proved his doubters wrong in a single season.

4. Prove a theorem. The mathematical and logical sense: deriving a result from axioms or premises. Here proven is common in both slots — has proven, a proven result — more so than in ordinary prose. (доказывать (теорему, утверждение))

  1. You can prove it in two lines with induction.
  2. Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1990s.
  3. No one has proved the conjecture in either direction.

5. Test, put to the test. The original sense, now archaic on its own but preserved in proving ground, in the saying the exception that proves the rule, and in the legal prove a will. (испытывать, проверять на прочность)

  1. "The exception that proves the rule" uses the old sense, "tests."
  2. The desert proved every part of the vehicle.
  3. The estate had been proved in probate court that spring.

6. Rise, ferment (of dough). Baking usage, chiefly British; American recipes normally say let it rise or, as a noun-derived verb, proof the dough. (подходить, подниматься (о тесте))

  1. Let the dough prove in a warm spot for an hour.
  2. The loaves had proved overnight in the cooler.

disprove, reprove

reproves · -ing form: reproving (drop the e)
Contrast
The -n participle listed for this group, reproven, is essentially unused. Unlike prove, reprove has no attributive slot to keep it alive — there is no ✗ a reproven student — so write has reproved. The verb itself is formal and slightly old-fashioned; in speech Americans say scolded, called out, or chewed out.
Verb type
action — dynamic verb of speech, formal in register; the participial adjective reproving (a reproving look) belongs to it.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is a person or a practice, and it is obligatory: reproved both attorneys, reproved the practice in a letter; ✗ the judge reproved alone is incomplete.
Passive
yes — he had been reproved in front of the whole department. Use reproved in the passive, never ✗ had been reproven.

To tell someone, formally and usually gently, that they have done something wrong.

1. Rebuke someone mildly. Formal and slightly old-fashioned; in speech Americans say scolded, called out, or chewed out. The participle is reproved — the -n form reproven is essentially unused, so never write ✗ has reproven. (упрекать, делать выговор, бранить)

  1. A good editor can reprove you without discouraging you.
  2. The judge reproved both attorneys for talking over each other.
  3. She has reproved him for the same habit more than once.
  4. He had been reproved in front of the whole department.

2. Condemn a practice or behavior. Here the object is conduct rather than a person, and the tone is public and moral. The participial adjective reproving — a reproving look, a reproving tone — belongs to this sense too. (порицать, осуждать (поступок, обычай))

  1. The bishop reproved the practice in a letter to every parish.
  2. Her silence reproves more effectively than any lecture.
  3. The editorial has reproved the whole industry for looking away.
  4. He gave me a reproving look and went back to his notes.
