# Final -d becomes -t

> The past and participle are identical and differ from the base only in the final consonant: -d becomes -t.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Every verb here builds its past and its past participle the same way, and they come out identical to each other: take the base form, knock the final **-d** off, and put a **-t** in its place. *Send* becomes *sent*, *build* becomes *built*, *bend* becomes *bent* — one form does the work of both the simple past (*I sent it yesterday*) and the participle (*I have sent it*). Most of the group ends in **-nd → -nt**; *build* and *gild* end in **-ld → -lt**, and *gird* in **-rd → -rt**. In the original Russian spreadsheet this is (смена окончания "d" на "t"). Four of these verbs — *blend*, *gild*, *gird*, and especially *wend* — have quietly gone regular in modern American English, and each entry says so where it matters. *Rend* has not gone regular; it has simply gone literary, and its past is still *rent*.

---

### bend

**Pronunciation:** /bɛnd/ &middot; *BEND*  
**Русский:** гнуть; наклоняться
**Forms:** *bend* /bɛnd/ &middot; *bent* /bɛnt/ &middot; *bent* /bɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *bends* &middot; **-ing form:** *bending* (no spelling change)  
**Contrast:** *Bent* does double duty as an everyday adjective: *a bent nail*, *bent out of shape*, *bent on winning*. Same spelling, same sound, different job in the sentence. There is no ✗ *bended* — the only place that survives is the fixed phrase *on bended knee*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, whether you curve the wire, lean over, or stretch a rule. No linking use; *be bent on winning* is the participle as a predicate adjective, not a copular *bend*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive when you do the curving (*bend the wire*, *bend the rules*, *bend the department to her will*), intransitive when you or the road do the bending (*she bends down*, *the road bends*, *he would not bend to pressure*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the coat hanger was bent into a hook*, *the curfew was bent for the seniors*. No passive of the intransitive uses: ✗ *down was bent by her*, ✗ *the road is bent past the mill* (in that reading).

To make something curve or lean the body over — and, figuratively, to relax a rule, to yield, or to force someone else to yield.

**1. Curve something, make it crooked.** The core physical sense: force something straight into a curve or an angle. The participle *bent* doubles as an everyday adjective — *a bent nail*. (гнуть, сгибать, изгибать)

1. *Bend* the wire into a circle before you solder it.
2. He *bent* the coat hanger into a hook and fished the keys out.
3. The ice storm *has bent* every sapling on the ridge.

**2. Bend down, bend over (lean the body).** Intransitive, about your own body, usually with *down*, *over*, or *forward*. (наклоняться, нагибаться)

4. She *bends* down to tie her shoes before every run.
5. I *bent* over to pick up the coin and my back locked up.
6. *Bend* your knees when you lift the box.

**3. Follow a curved course.** Intransitive, of a road, a river, a coastline, or light. Nothing is doing the bending — the thing simply runs in a curve. (изгибаться, поворачивать, делать изгиб)

7. The road *bends* sharply just past the old mill.
8. The river *bent* west and disappeared into the trees.
9. Water *has bent* light this way since long before anyone measured it.

**4. Bend the rules.** Stretch a rule without quite breaking it. A fixed collocation — *bend the rules*, *bend the truth*, *bend a deadline*. (обходить правила, делать поблажку)

10. I *have bent* the rules for you once already.
11. The coach *bent* the curfew for the seniors.
12. Good editors *bend* the style guide when the sentence needs it.

**5. Bend to something — yield, give in.** Figurative and intransitive, normally *bend to* pressure, a demand, or someone's will. (уступать, поддаваться, подчиняться)

13. He would not *bend* to pressure from the board.
14. In the end the city *bent* to the neighborhood's demands.
15. The union *has bent* on wages but not on hours.

**6. Bend someone or something to your will.** The transitive mirror of the sense above: you are the one doing the forcing. Slightly literary in tone. (подчинять своей воле, гнуть под себя)

16. She *bent* the whole department to her will inside a month.
17. You cannot *bend* a language to a rule it never had.

**7. Bend over backwards.** Idiom: go to extreme trouble to help or accommodate someone. Always in this exact shape. (из кожи вон лезть, делать всё возможное)

18. They *bend* over backwards for regular customers.
19. The landlord *has bent* over backwards to keep us.

**8. Be bent on something — determined.** The participle used as a predicate adjective with *on*. It never means physically curved here, and there is no active verb behind it. (твёрдо намеренный, одержимый (какой-л. целью))

20. He is *bent* on selling the farm no matter what we say.
21. They were *bent* on winning, whatever it cost them.

**See also:** [unbend](#unbend), [blend](#blend), [rend](#rend)

### blend

**Pronunciation:** /blɛnd/ &middot; *BLEND*  
**Русский:** смешивать
**Forms:** *blend* /blɛnd/ &middot; *blended* /ˈblɛndɪd/ (older *blent* /blɛnt/) &middot; *blended* /ˈblɛndɪd/ (older *blent* /blɛnt/)  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary kitchen and conversational English; *blend in* is said constantly  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt historically; regular -ed in modern American English &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *blends* &middot; **-ing form:** *blending*  
**Contrast:** The spreadsheet lists *blent* because *blend* once belonged to this group, but in American English today it is a plain regular verb: *blended*, *have blended*. *Blent* now turns up only in poetry and hymn texts. Write *blended*. Watch the spelling too — *blend* is not *bend* with an extra letter; the two verbs are unrelated.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, from mixing batter to a newcomer disappearing into a crowd.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive when you do the mixing (*blend the butter and the sugar*, *the distillery blended eleven barrels*), intransitive when things merge on their own or someone *blends in* (*their accents have blended*, *he blended in with the crowd*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the paint was blended on a scrap of cardboard*, *eleven barrels were blended before bottling*. No passive of *blend in* or the merging sense: ✗ *the crowd was blended in with by him*.

To mix things into a single smooth or harmonious whole, or to merge so completely that the parts stop being distinguishable.

**1. Mix into a smooth whole.** The everyday cooking and mixing sense. In modern American English the past and participle are *blended*; the old *blent* survives only in poetry and hymn texts. (смешивать, размешивать, взбивать)

1. *Blend* the butter and the sugar until the mixture is smooth.
2. She *blended* three paint colors on a scrap of cardboard.
3. I *have blended* this soup twice and it is still lumpy.

**2. Merge, become indistinguishable.** Intransitive and usually figurative: two things run together until you cannot tell where one ends. (сливаться, смешиваться воедино)

4. Their two accents *have blended* into something nobody can place.
5. The suburbs *blend* into one another for thirty miles along that highway.
6. By the second month the new hires *had blended* into the team completely.

**3. Combine varieties (coffee, whiskey, tea).** A trade sense: mix different lots or origins to get a consistent product. This is where the noun *a blend* comes from. (купажировать, составлять смесь)

7. This roaster *blends* beans from three countries.
8. The distillery *blended* eleven barrels before bottling.

**4. Blend in — go unnoticed, match the surroundings.** Phrasal, about people trying not to stand out or about a thing matching what is around it. (сливаться с окружением, не выделяться)

9. New siding helps the addition *blend* in with the old house.
10. He dressed down so he would *blend* in with the crowd.
11. The gray paint *blended* in so well we walked past the door twice.

**See also:** [bend](#bend), [rend](#rend), [send](#send)

### build

**Pronunciation:** /bɪld/ &middot; *BILD*  
**Русский:** строить
**Forms:** *build* /bɪld/ &middot; *built* /bɪlt/ &middot; *built* /bɪlt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking  
**Pattern:** -ld — -lt — -lt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *builds* &middot; **-ing form:** *building* (also the noun *a building*)  
**Contrast:** ✗ *builded* is not English. And because *built* covers both slots, *We built it* and *We have built it* use the same word — the difference lives entirely in *have*. Note the vowel: *build* rhymes with *filled*, not with *wild*; the *u* is silent.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, whether the object is a house, a career, or a case. Never linking and never stative; it always names work or growth over time.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the constructing and developing senses (*they build forty houses a year*, *she builds her arguments one fact at a time*), intransitive when something grows on its own (*pressure builds*, *the tension built through the third act*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and very common — *the whole case was built on a single witness*, *the cabinets are built into the wall*. Note the physique sense is passive-only, with no active behind it: *he is built like a linebacker* but ✗ *he builds like a linebacker*.

To make something by putting parts together — a house, a business, a case, a reputation — or, intransitively, to grow steadily stronger.

**1. Construct, put up.** The literal sense: assemble physical parts into a structure or an object. (строить, возводить, сооружать)

1. They *build* about forty houses a year.
2. My grandfather *built* this table by hand.
3. The district *has built* three new schools since 2020.

**2. Develop something over time.** Figurative but extremely common: a career, a business, a case, a reputation, a relationship. The object is abstract and the work is gradual. (создавать, выстраивать, наращивать)

4. She *builds* her arguments one fact at a time.
5. He *has built* a law practice out of nothing.
6. Two decades of steady work *built* that reputation.

**3. Grow, intensify.** Intransitive, with no object at all: pressure, tension, momentum, or a storm increases on its own. (нарастать, усиливаться, накапливаться)

7. Pressure *builds* along that fault for decades at a time.
8. The tension *built* through the entire third act.
9. By noon the heat *had built* to something dangerous.

**4. Build on / build upon — use as a foundation.** Take something already there and extend it. Very common in the passive: *built on*. (основываться на, развивать (начатое))

10. The sequel *builds* on everything the first film set up.
11. The whole case was *built* on a single witness.
12. Later research *has built* on her 1994 paper.

**5. Build in / build into — include from the start.** Make something a permanent, non-removable part of a design, a schedule, or an agreement. (встраивать, закладывать (в конструкцию, в договор))

13. *Build* a little slack into the schedule.
14. Congress *had built* a sunset clause into the law.
15. The cabinets are *built* into the wall and do not come with the house.

**6. Build up — accumulate, strengthen, or hype.** Three related phrasal uses: a substance collects, a person gains strength, or the press talks someone up in advance. (накапливать(ся), укреплять; расхваливать заранее)

16. Salt *builds* up on the sidewalk all winter.
17. She *built* up her savings for two years before quitting.
18. The press *had built* him up as unbeatable, which made the loss worse.

**7. Be built — of a person's physique.** Passive only, describing body type. There is no active verb behind it — you cannot say ✗ *He builds like a linebacker*. (быть сложенным (о телосложении))

19. He is *built* like a linebacker.
20. She was *built* for distance, not for sprints.


**Phrasal verbs:** [build in](14-phrasal-b.md#build-in) &middot; [build into](14-phrasal-b.md#build-into) &middot; [build on](14-phrasal-b.md#build-on) &middot; [build up](14-phrasal-b.md#build-up)

**See also:** [rebuild](#rebuild), [overbuild](#overbuild), [gild](#gild)

### gild

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɪld/ &middot; *GILD*  
**Русский:** позолачивать
**Forms:** *gild* /ɡɪld/ &middot; *gilded* /ˈɡɪldɪd/ (also *gilt* /ɡɪlt/) &middot; *gilded* /ˈɡɪldɪd/ (also *gilt* /ɡɪlt/)  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a narrow craft verb; most Americans meet it only in *gild the lily* and *the Gilded Age*  
**Pattern:** -ld — -lt — -lt historically; gilded is the ordinary American past and participle &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *gilds* &middot; **-ing form:** *gilding* (also a noun: *the gilding flaked off*)  
**Contrast:** Both forms are listed in American dictionaries, but *gilded* is what edited American prose uses for the verb — *they gilded the dome*, *the age was gilded*. *Gilt* has narrowed to an adjective in front of a noun (*a gilt frame*, *gilt-edged bonds*) and to a noun for the gold layer itself. Separately, do not confuse *gild* with *guild* — same pronunciation /ɡɪld/, but a guild is a trade association.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: applying gold leaf, or putting a flattering finish on something ordinary. The adjective *gilded* (*the Gilded Age*) is the participle, not a separate class.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in both senses, and the object is obligatory — *gild the dome*, *gild the lily*. ✗ *Craftsmen gild every fifty years* needs its object.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the capitol dome is gilded about every fifty years*, *a very ordinary hotel was gilded by the brochure*. The perfect passive is ordinary too: *the lettering has been gilded*.

To cover a surface with a thin layer of gold, or figuratively to put an attractive finish on something ordinary.

**1. Cover with gold leaf.** The literal craft sense — domes, frames, lettering, book edges. *Gilded* is the ordinary American past and participle; *gilt* has narrowed to an adjective before a noun. (золотить, покрывать позолотой)

1. Craftsmen *gild* the capitol dome about every fifty years.
2. The restorers *gilded* the picture frame last spring.
3. Someone *has gilded* the lettering above the door.

**2. Make something look better than it is.** Figurative and mildly disapproving, most often in the idiom *gild the lily* — decorate something already fine. The adjective *gilded* also labels a shiny surface over something less pleasant, as in *the Gilded Age*. (приукрашивать, наводить внешний лоск)

4. A third adjective just *gilds* the lily.
5. The brochure *gilded* a very ordinary roadside hotel.
6. Memoirists *have gilded* that decade past recognition.

**See also:** [build](#build), [gird](#gird)

### gird

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɝd/ &middot; *GURD*  
**Русский:** подпоясывать, опоясывать
**Forms:** *gird* /ɡɝd/ &middot; *girded* /ˈɡɝdɪd/ (also *girt* /ɡɝt/) &middot; *girded* /ˈɡɝdɪd/ (also *girt* /ɡɝt/)  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — elevated and mostly journalistic; alive in *gird for* and the biblical *gird your loins*  
**Pattern:** -rd — -rt — -rt historically; girded is the usual American form &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *girds* &middot; **-ing form:** *girding*  
**Contrast:** *Girded* is what you will read in American English, almost always in *gird your loins* or *gird for* something. *Girt* has retreated into fixed phrases and nautical description (*an island girt by reef*). Keep the r in the American vowel — /ɡɝd/, not the British /ɡɜːd/ — and note that the *g* is hard, as in *go*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, elevated in register: bracing yourself for something hard, or belting and encircling in the literal sense.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive or reflexive in the living sense (*retailers gird for the holidays*, *the mayor girded himself for a fight*), transitive in *gird your loins* and the literal encircling (*stone walls had girded the town*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural only in the literal encircling sense, and mostly as the short participle: *the island is girt by coral*, *the town was girded by stone walls*. No passive of *gird for*: ✗ *the holidays are girded for*.

To fasten a belt around something, and by extension to brace yourself for something hard.

**1. Gird for something — brace, prepare.** The only sense in ordinary modern American use, almost always *gird for* or *gird oneself for*. Slightly elevated in register — you meet it in news writing more than in speech. (готовиться, собираться с силами (перед трудным))

1. Retailers *gird* for the holidays as early as September.
2. The mayor *girded* for a long budget fight.
3. Hospitals *have girded* for flu season since August.

**2. Gird your loins.** A fixed biblical phrase, now used half in earnest and half as a joke before an unpleasant task. (препоясать чресла; собраться с духом)

4. *Gird* your loins — the auditors arrive Monday.
5. He *girded* his loins and knocked on the director's door.

**3. Belt, encircle, surround.** The literal sense of putting on a belt, and its literary or nautical extension to something ringed by water or walls. This is where the short form *girt* still lives. (опоясывать, окружать)

6. He *girds* his waist with a wide leather belt.
7. Stone walls *had girded* the town since the twelfth century.
8. The old atlas describes the island as *girt* by coral.

**See also:** [gild](#gild), [build](#build)

### lend

**Pronunciation:** /lɛnd/ &middot; *LEND*  
**Русский:** давать взаймы
**Forms:** *lend* /lɛnd/ &middot; *lent* /lɛnt/ &middot; *lent* /lɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *lends* &middot; **-ing form:** *lending* (also a noun: *bank lending*)  
**Contrast:** *Lend* and *borrow* point in opposite directions: you *lend* something **to** someone, and you *borrow* it **from** them. ✗ *Can you borrow me a pen?* is the classic error — say *Can you lend me a pen?* The past *lent* also sounds exactly like *Lent*, the church season, and it is one letter from *bent* and *sent*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all senses, even the figurative *lend weight* and *lend an ear*, where nothing physically changes hands.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive** in the core sense — two objects with no preposition (*lend me twenty dollars*), or one object plus *to* (*she lent the book to a friend*). Also plainly **transitive** in *lend weight*, and **intransitive** in the banking sense: *banks lend at whatever rate the market will bear*.
**Passive:** **yes**, with either object as subject — *the painting had been lent to Chicago*, *he was lent a car for the weekend*. The reflexive *lend itself to* has no passive: ✗ *the novel is lent itself to a two-hour film*.

To let someone use something of yours for a while, expecting it back — and, figuratively, to give a quality to something.

**1. Loan something, expecting it back.** You *lend* something **to** someone and they *borrow* it **from** you — the two verbs point in opposite directions. Takes two objects with no preposition: *lend me a pen*. (давать взаймы, одалживать (кому-л.))

1. *Lend* me twenty dollars until Friday.
2. I *lent* him my car for the weekend and got it back full of sand.
3. She *has lent* that book to at least six people.
4. The museum *had lent* the painting to Chicago before the fire.

**2. Provide money at interest.** The commercial sense, with a bank or a lender as the subject. Often intransitive: *banks stopped lending*. (кредитовать, выдавать ссуды)

5. Banks *lend* at whatever rate the market will bear.
6. Credit unions *lent* less that year than at any point since 2009.

**3. Give or add a quality.** Figurative, with an abstract object: *lend weight*, *lend credence*, *lend an air of*, *lend charm*. Nothing is returned here. (придавать (оттенок, вес, достоверность))

7. Those curtains *lend* the room a little warmth.
8. Her presence *lent* the event a seriousness it had lacked.
9. Two new studies *have lent* weight to the theory.

**4. Lend itself to — be suitable for.** Reflexive and fixed. The subject is a thing, and the sense is that its nature suits some use. (годиться для, хорошо поддаваться)

10. The novel does not *lend* itself to a two-hour film.
11. The basement *lent* itself perfectly to a workshop.

**5. Lend a hand, lend an ear.** Two set phrases where nothing is literally loaned: *lend a hand* is to help, *lend an ear* is to listen. (помочь, подсобить; выслушать)

12. Come *lend* a hand with the couch.
13. He *lent* a sympathetic ear and said nothing else all evening.


**Phrasal verbs:** [lend itself to](18-phrasal-h-l.md#lend-itself-to) &middot; [lend out](18-phrasal-h-l.md#lend-out)

**See also:** [send](#send), [spend](#spend), [bend](#bend)

### misspend

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɪsˈspɛnd/ &middot; *miss-SPEND*  
**Русский:** транжирить
**Forms:** *misspend* /ˌmɪsˈspɛnd/ &middot; *misspent* /ˌmɪsˈspɛnt/ &middot; *misspent* /ˌmɪsˈspɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — prefixed derivative of *spend*, met almost only as the participle in *a misspent youth*  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *misspends* &middot; **-ing form:** *misspending*  
**Contrast:** Prefix *mis-* on [spend](#spend), so the forms follow *spend* exactly and need no separate explanation. Two things to watch: the double **s** — ✗ *mispend* — and the fact that you will meet the participle far more often than the base form, nearly always in the set phrase *a misspent youth*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, the *mis-* judgment version of *spend*: money or years put to the wrong use.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, object obligatory — *misspend a windfall*, *misspent the entire grant*, *has misspent most of his twenties*. ✗ *The council misspent* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the entire grant was misspent on consultants*, *money meant for scholarships has been misspent*. In practice you meet the bare participle as an adjective far more often: *a misspent youth*.

To spend money or time on the wrong things.

**1. Spend money wrongly or wastefully.** Usually about public or entrusted money, and usually the verdict of an auditor or a critic rather than the spender. (тратить не по назначению, транжирить)

1. It is easy to *misspend* a windfall.
2. The council *misspent* the entire grant on consultants.
3. The trustees *have misspent* money that was meant for scholarships.

**2. Misspent youth (waste time or years).** You will meet the participle far more often than the base form, nearly always in the fixed phrase *a misspent youth* — often said with some affection. (растратить впустую (годы, молодость))

4. He *has misspent* most of his twenties.
5. By graduation she felt she *had misspent* four perfectly good years.
6. He credits his *misspent* youth with his skill at pool.

**See also:** [spend](#spend), [overspend](#overspend), [outspend](#outspend)

### outspend

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊtˈspɛnd/ &middot; *owt-SPEND*  
**Русский:** перерасходовать
**Forms:** *outspend* /ˌaʊtˈspɛnd/ &middot; *outspent* /ˌaʊtˈspɛnt/ &middot; *outspent* /ˌaʊtˈspɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in campaign and business writing, seldom said out loud  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *outspends* &middot; **-ing form:** *outspending*  
**Contrast:** Prefix *out-* on [spend](#spend); the forms match. The trap is the meaning, not the shape. To *outspend* someone is to spend **more than a rival does**, and it always takes that rival as its object: *she outspent her opponent*. Spending past your own budget is [overspend](#overspend) — ✗ *We outspent our budget* should be *We overspent our budget*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and comparative: it measures your spending against a rival's.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, and the object is obligatory and must be the rival — *her opponent outspent her by two million*. ✗ *We outspent our budget* is the wrong verb; that is *overspend*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is at least as common as the active in political and business reporting — *the startup was outspent from the first quarter on*, *being badly outspent did not hurt her*.

To spend more money than a rival does.

**1. Spend more than a competitor.** Always takes the rival as its object — *she outspent her opponent*. Spending past your own budget is *overspend*, not this verb. (тратить больше (чем соперник), обходить по расходам)

1. Big campaigns routinely *outspend* small ones ten to one.
2. Her opponent *outspent* her by two million dollars.
3. The league *has outspent* every other sport on television advertising.

**2. Be outspent (passive).** The passive is at least as common as the active, especially in political and business reporting. (уступать по расходам, быть перетраченным соперником)

4. Being badly *outspent* did not seem to hurt her at all.
5. The startup was *outspent* from the first quarter on and never caught up.

**See also:** [spend](#spend), [overspend](#overspend), [misspend](#misspend)

### overbuild

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪld/ &middot; *oh-ver-BILD*  
**Русский:** (чрезмерно) застраивать (район и т. п.)
**Forms:** *overbuild* /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪld/ &middot; *overbuilt* /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪlt/ &middot; *overbuilt* /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪlt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a real-estate and engineering term rather than general vocabulary  
**Pattern:** -ld — -lt — -lt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *overbuilds* &middot; **-ing form:** *overbuilding* (also a noun: *decades of overbuilding*)  
**Contrast:** Prefix *over-* on [build](#build), with the same forms — nothing new to learn. In practice *overbuilt* works most often as an adjective describing a place with more construction than it can fill: *an overbuilt waterfront*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: putting up more construction than a place needs, or engineering something far past the required strength.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with the place or market as object (*the city overbuilt its downtown*, *Miami has overbuilt its condo market*), intransitive with no object at all (*developers overbuild whenever credit gets cheap*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive sense — *the downtown was badly overbuilt in the 1980s*, *the frame had been overbuilt on purpose*. In *the bridge is famously overbuilt* the participle has gone adjectival rather than passive.

To put up more construction than a place needs — or, in engineering, to make something far stronger than it has to be.

**1. Build more than a market can absorb.** The real-estate sense: too many units for the tenants or buyers available. The participle works as an adjective for the place — *an overbuilt waterfront*. (чрезмерно застраивать, перенасыщать застройкой)

1. Developers *overbuild* whenever credit gets cheap.
2. The city badly *overbuilt* its downtown in the 1980s.
3. Miami *has overbuilt* its condo market more than once.

**2. Overbuilt — stronger than necessary.** An engineering use, almost always the participle as an adjective, and frequently a compliment rather than a complaint. (с избыточным запасом прочности, сделанный с перезапасом)

4. The bridge is famously *overbuilt* — it could carry twice the design load.
5. Engineers *had overbuilt* the frame on purpose, expecting heavier trucks.

### overspend

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnd/ &middot; *oh-ver-SPEND*  
**Русский:** перерасходовать; сорить деньгами
**Forms:** *overspend* /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnd/ &middot; *overspent* /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnt/ &middot; *overspent* /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in budget and finance writing, less common in speech  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *overspends* &middot; **-ing form:** *overspending* (also a noun: *government overspending*)  
**Contrast:** Prefix *over-* on [spend](#spend), same forms. Keep it apart from [outspend](#outspend): *overspend* measures you against your own budget, *outspend* measures you against somebody else. The participle *overspent* also works as an adjective for a person or a fund that has run past its limit: *an overspent account*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, measured against your own budget rather than a rival's (that is *outspend*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — the object is optional: *we overspent by four hundred dollars* and *households overspend every December* stand alone, while *the department has overspent its travel budget* takes one.
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical with a budget or line item as subject (*every line item had been overspent by the third quarter*), but American writing far prefers the participle as an adjective: *an overspent budget*, *an overspent account*.

To spend more money than you have or planned to spend.

**1. Spend past your own budget.** Measured against your own limit, which is what separates it from *outspend* — that one measures you against somebody else. Works with or without an object. (перерасходовать, тратить сверх бюджета)

1. Households *overspend* every December and regret it in January.
2. We *overspent* by four hundred dollars on the kitchen.
3. The department *has overspent* its travel budget again.

**2. Overspent — run past its limit.** The participle as an adjective for a fund, an account, or a line item that has nothing left in it. (перерасходованный, исчерпанный (о бюджете, фонде))

4. An *overspent* budget is easier to explain than an unfinished project.
5. By the third quarter the agency *had overspent* every line item.

**See also:** [spend](#spend), [outspend](#outspend), [misspend](#misspend)

### rebuild

**Pronunciation:** /ˌriˈbɪld/ &middot; *ree-BILD*  
**Русский:** перестроить
**Forms:** *rebuild* /ˌriˈbɪld/ &middot; *rebuilt* /ˌriˈbɪlt/ &middot; *rebuilt* /ˌriˈbɪlt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary for houses, engines, teams, and trust alike  
**Pattern:** -ld — -lt — -lt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *rebuilds* &middot; **-ing form:** *rebuilding* (also a noun: *the rebuilding effort*)  
**Contrast:** Prefix *re-* on [build](#build), with identical forms; ✗ *rebuilded* is as wrong as ✗ *builded*. One stress note: as a verb the stress falls on the second syllable, /ˌriˈbɪld/, but the noun pulls it forward — *the engine needs a total rebuild* /ˈribɪld/.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, always with the implication that something was broken or dismantled first — a town, an engine, a roster, a life.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in most senses (*they rebuilt the bridge*, *she rebuilt her life*), intransitive in the American sports sense with no object at all: *ownership chose to rebuild*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the engine was rebuilt twice before the car sold*, *the town had been rebuilt almost entirely*. No passive of the sports sense: ✗ *it was rebuilt by ownership* does not mean the roster overhaul.

To build something again after damage or dismantling — a town, an engine, a team, a life.

**1. Build again after damage.** The literal sense, typically after a fire, a flood, a storm, or a war. (отстраивать заново, восстанавливать)

1. Volunteers *rebuild* a few of these houses every summer.
2. They *rebuilt* the bridge in eleven months.
3. By the time we visited, the town *had rebuilt* almost everything the fire took.

**2. Overhaul a machine.** Take a mechanism apart and reassemble it with worn parts replaced. Also a noun: *the engine needs a rebuild*. (перебирать, капитально ремонтировать (двигатель))

4. He *rebuilds* carburetors in his garage on weekends.
5. The engine was *rebuilt* twice before the car finally sold.

**3. Rebuild a team or organization.** Near-technical in American sports: trade away veterans and start over with young players. Often used with no object at all — *the club decided to rebuild*. (перестраивать (команду), обновлять состав)

6. The team *has rebuilt* its roster around two rookies.
7. Ownership chose to *rebuild* rather than chase a wild card.

**4. Rebuild trust, confidence, a life.** Figurative, with an abstract object. The implication is always that something was broken first. (восстанавливать (доверие, репутацию, жизнь))

8. It took years to *rebuild* the trust he threw away in an afternoon.
9. She *rebuilt* her life in a town where nobody knew her.

**See also:** [build](#build), [overbuild](#overbuild)

### rend

**Pronunciation:** /rɛnd/ &middot; *REND*  
**Русский:** раздирать
**Forms:** *rend* /rɛnd/ &middot; *rent* /rɛnt/ &middot; *rent* /rɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — literary throughout; ordinary American speech says *tear*  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *rends* &middot; **-ing form:** *rending* (as in *heart-rending*)  
**Contrast:** The past *rent* is spelled and pronounced exactly like the everyday word *rent* — the money you pay for an apartment, and the regular verb *rent / rented / rented*. Only context separates them: *He rented an apartment downtown* (paid to use it) versus *Grief rent the family in two* (tore it apart). *Rend* itself is literary; in ordinary American speech people say *tear*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and violent, literary throughout; ordinary American speech uses *tear*. Today you meet the *-ing* form as a compound adjective (*heart-rending*) more often than the verb.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in every documented sense, object obligatory — *rend the sails*, *rent the party in two*, *a scream rent the silence*. ✗ *The scandal has rent* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical and occasionally used (*the alliance was rent beyond repair*, *the family was rent in two by grief*), but it stays literary; in ordinary prose you would say *torn*.

To tear something violently apart, literally or figuratively. Literary throughout — ordinary American speech uses *tear*.

**1. Tear violently apart.** The literal sense, and already elevated in register. Note that the past *rent* is spelled and said exactly like the ordinary word *rent* you pay for an apartment. (раздирать, разрывать на части)

1. High winds *rend* the sails of any boat left rigged.
2. The dogs *rent* the feed sack open and scattered it across the barn.
3. Frost *has rent* that ledge into loose slabs.

**2. Split a group, divide bitterly.** Figurative, about parties, families, churches, alliances. This is the sense you are most likely to meet in print. (раскалывать, разрывать (партию, семью))

4. The scandal *has rent* the party in two.
5. By then the treaty *had rent* the alliance beyond repair.
6. Grief *rends* that household every year on the anniversary.

**3. Rend the air or the silence.** Of a sudden loud sound. A fixed literary collocation — a scream, a siren, or a shot *rends* the air. (разрывать (тишину), оглашать)

7. A single scream *rent* the silence of the church.
8. Sirens *rend* the air every night on that block.

**4. Heart-rending.** The *-ing* form in a compound adjective is far more common today than the verb itself, and it is the form most learners will actually meet. (душераздирающий)

9. The choir sang a *heart-rending* setting of the psalm.
10. Her testimony was *heart-rending* and the room went quiet.

### send

**Pronunciation:** /sɛnd/ &middot; *SEND*  
**Русский:** посылать, присылать, отправлять, отсылать
**Forms:** *send* /sɛnd/ &middot; *sent* /sɛnt/ &middot; *sent* /sɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *sends* &middot; **-ing form:** *sending*  
**Contrast:** *Sent* is a perfect homophone of *cent* and *scent* — three spellings, one sound /sɛnt/, and only spelling tells them apart on the page. There is no ✗ *sended*. Like *lend*, *send* takes two objects with no preposition: *send me the file* or *send the file to me*, never ✗ *send to me the file*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all senses, from mailing a package to a gust that *sends papers flying*.
**Transitivity:** **ditransitive** in the core sense — *send me the file* or *send the file to me* (never ✗ *send to me the file*) — and **transitive** elsewhere, with the object obligatory: ✗ *I sent yesterday*. The *send + object + -ing/adjective* pattern adds a complement: *sent papers flying*.
**Passive:** **yes**, with either object as subject — *four reminders have been sent*, *their daughter was sent to school in Vermont*, *the National Guard was sent in*. Even the figurative sense passivizes: *the stock was sent down forty percent*.

To make something or someone go from one place to another — a letter, a package, a person, a signal — or to knock something suddenly into a new state.

**1. Dispatch mail, a message, or goods.** The everyday sense. Takes two objects with no preposition: *send me the file* or *send the file to me*, never ✗ *send to me the file*. (посылать, отправлять, отсылать)

1. I *send* my mother a postcard from every trip.
2. She *sent* the contract by overnight mail.
3. They *have sent* four reminders already this week.

**2. Make a person go somewhere.** The object is a person, and you have the authority to move them — a child to bed, an employee to a site, a patient to a specialist. (отправлять, посылать (кого-л. куда-л.))

4. The company *sends* two engineers to every site visit.
5. They *sent* their daughter to school in Vermont.
6. He *has sent* the kids to bed early all week.

**3. Knock into a sudden state or motion.** Pattern: *send* + object + *-ing* or an adjective phrase. Nothing travels anywhere in particular — the point is the abrupt change. (заставить резко (полететь, взлететь); вызвать (реакцию))

7. The gust *sent* papers flying across the parking lot.
8. One bad quarter *has sent* the stock down forty percent.
9. News like that *sends* the whole neighborhood to the windows.

**4. Send for — summon.** Have someone or something brought to you. The object of *for* is the thing summoned, not the messenger. (посылать за (кем-л.), вызывать)

10. *Send* for the doctor.
11. They *sent* for a tow truck and waited two hours in the rain.

**5. Send out — distribute or emit.** Either mass distribution to many recipients, or a source giving off light, heat, or a signal. (рассылать; испускать (сигнал, свет, запах))

12. The office *sends* out invoices on the first of the month.
13. The beacon *sent* out a pulse every nine seconds.
14. We *have sent* out three hundred invitations.

**6. Send off — mail away; see someone off.** Two uses: put something in the mail, or give someone a farewell. In British soccer commentary *sent off* also means ejected from the game — not an American use. (отсылать, отправлять; провожать)

15. We *sent* off the application on Monday.
16. The whole town *had sent* him off with a parade.

**7. Send in — submit; deploy.** Submit a form or entry to an organization, or order forces into a place. (подавать, присылать (заявку); вводить (войска))

17. *Send* in the form before the deadline.
18. The governor *sent* in the National Guard.

**8. Send back — return.** Return something you were given because it is wrong, broken, or unwanted. (возвращать, отсылать обратно)

19. She *sent* back the soup twice.
20. I *have sent* back everything that didn't fit.

**9. Transmit a signal.** Technical: a transmitter, satellite, sensor, or probe as the subject. The object is data or a signal rather than an object. (передавать (сигнал, данные))

21. The probe still *sends* data every eleven hours.
22. The tower *had sent* nothing for three days before anyone noticed.

### spend

**Pronunciation:** /spɛnd/ &middot; *SPEND*  
**Русский:** тратить; проводить (время)
**Forms:** *spend* /spɛnd/ &middot; *spent* /spɛnt/ &middot; *spent* /spɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *spends* &middot; **-ing form:** *spending* (also a noun: *consumer spending*)  
**Contrast:** One English verb covers both money and time — *spend fifty dollars*, *spend the weekend* — where Russian splits the job between *тратить* and *проводить*. There is no ✗ *spended*. The participle *spent* is also an adjective meaning used up or exhausted: *a spent match*, *spent fuel*, *I'm completely spent*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic for both money and time, and figuratively for using up force or goodwill. The participle *spent* has become an adjective (*a spent match*, *I'm completely spent*), but that is not a linking use of the verb.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is normally obligatory (*spend a hundred dollars*, *spend the afternoon*, *spend the night*), though the money object can be left out in economic talk: *Americans are spending less*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the entire budget has been spent*, *most of its force had been spent before landfall*. The time sense passivizes naturally too: *the whole afternoon was spent at the DMV*.

To pay out money, or to pass a stretch of time — one English verb where Russian splits the job between *тратить* and *проводить*.

**1. Pay out money.** The core sense, usually with *on* for the thing bought: *spend forty dollars on lunch*. (тратить, расходовать (деньги))

1. We *spend* about a hundred dollars a week on groceries.
2. She *spent* nine hundred dollars on a bike she rides twice a year.
3. The department *has spent* its entire budget already.

**2. Pass time.** Same verb, no money involved: *spend the afternoon*, *spend a year*, *spend time doing something*. The complement is usually a bare *-ing* clause. (проводить (время))

4. I *spent* the whole afternoon at the DMV.
5. She *spends* every summer in Maine.
6. He *has spent* years learning Japanese.

**3. Use up energy or resources.** Figurative: effort, force, ammunition, patience, political capital. The implication is that the supply is finite. (расходовать, тратить (силы, запас))

7. Do not *spend* your best material in the first ten minutes.
8. The storm *had spent* most of its force before landfall.
9. He *has spent* whatever goodwill he had left.

**4. Spend the night.** A fixed everyday phrase for staying somewhere overnight, and the source of the noun *a sleepover*. (ночевать, оставаться на ночь)

10. *Spend* the night here if the roads are bad.
11. We *spent* the night in the car outside Amarillo.

**5. Spent — used up.** The participle as an adjective for something with nothing left in it: *a spent match*, *spent fuel*, *spent shells*. (израсходованный, отработанный)

12. The plant stores *spent* fuel rods on site.
13. A *spent* match went into the ashtray.

**6. Spent — exhausted (of a person).** Predicate adjective, about people. *I'm completely spent* is ordinary spoken American English. (обессиленный, выдохшийся)

14. By mile twenty I was completely *spent*.
15. The team looked *spent* in the fourth quarter.

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈbɛnd/ &middot; *un-BEND*  
**Русский:** выпрямлять, разгибать
**Forms:** *unbend* /ʌnˈbɛnd/ &middot; *unbent* /ʌnˈbɛnt/ &middot; *unbent* /ʌnˈbɛnt/  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — prefixed derivative; in speech Americans say *straighten out* or *loosen up*  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *unbends* &middot; **-ing form:** *unbending* — also an adjective (see Contrast)  
**Contrast:** Prefix *un-* on [bend](#bend), same forms throughout. The catch is the *-ing* form, which pulls the other way as an adjective: the verb *unbend* means to straighten out or to relax, but *unbending* describes someone who will not bend at all — *an unbending judge*. Read the word by its job in the sentence, not by its shape.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: straightening something bent, or a stiff person loosening up. Watch the *-ing* form, which has gone adjectival in the opposite direction — *an unbending judge* will not yield at all.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive on physical objects (*unbend the paper clip*, *he unbent the hook*), intransitive both figuratively (*she rarely unbends in front of strangers*) and physically (*he unbent from the garden bed*).

To straighten something that was bent, or to loosen up and become less formal.

**1. Straighten something bent.** The literal transitive sense — wire, a hook, a paper clip, a fender. (разгибать, выпрямлять)

1. Slowly *unbend* the paper clip until it is straight.
2. He *unbent* the hook, looked at it, and threw it away.
3. The wire, once *unbent*, would not hold its shape again.

**2. Relax, become less formal.** Figurative and intransitive, about a stiff or reserved person warming up. Watch the *-ing* form, which pulls the opposite way: *an unbending judge* is one who will not yield at all. (смягчаться, оттаивать, держаться проще)

4. The old man *has unbent* a good deal since he retired.
5. She rarely *unbends* in front of strangers.
6. By the end of the evening our guests *had unbent* enough to laugh at themselves.

**3. Straighten up from a bent position.** Intransitive and physical: you were bent over and now you are not. Somewhat literary; in speech people say *straighten up*. (разгибаться, распрямляться)

7. He *unbent* slowly from the garden bed and pressed a hand to his back.
8. She *unbends*, wipes her forehead, and goes back to the row.

### wend

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnd/ &middot; *WEND*  
**Русский:** идти, держать путь (домой), направляться
**Forms:** *wend* /wɛnd/ &middot; *wended* /ˈwɛndɪd/ (historical *went* /wɛnt/) &middot; *wended* /ˈwɛndɪd/ (historical *went* /wɛnt/)  
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** archaic — no longer current except in the fossil phrase *wend one's way*  
**Pattern:** -nd — -nt — -nt historically (*went*); regular -ed in modern English &middot; **Group:** 6  
**Third person:** *wends* &middot; **-ing form:** *wending*  
**Contrast:** This is the one entry in the group you should read twice. The spreadsheet gives the past as *went*, and that is historically correct — *went* really was the past of *wend*. But English handed *went* over to *go* centuries ago, and it now belongs there and nowhere else: *I went home*, and never ✗ *I have went*. The modern past and participle of *wend* are **wended**: *we wended our way home*. The verb itself survives almost only in that phrase, *wend one's way*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and archaic, alive almost only in the fossil phrase *wend one's way*; also of a road or stream following a winding course.
**Transitivity:** **both** — technically transitive in *wend one's way*, but the object is fixed and reflexive (*we wended our way through the market*), never free; **intransitive** of a route: *the trail wends along the ridge*.
**Passive:** **no** — the only object is the fixed *one's way*, which cannot become a subject: ✗ *their way was wended through the market*. The winding-course sense is intransitive and has no passive at all.

To make your way somewhere, slowly and by a winding route. Survives almost entirely in the phrase *wend one's way*.

**1. Wend one's way — travel, head somewhere.** The living use of the verb, and it nearly always carries *one's way* with it. The modern past and participle are *wended*; *went* was historically the past of *wend* but belongs to *go* now and nowhere else. (держать путь, направляться, брести)

1. Every evening the cattle *wend* their way down to the river.
2. We *wended* our way through the crowded market.
3. By midnight the crowd *had wended* off toward the parking lots.

**2. Follow a winding course.** Of a road, trail, or stream running in curves — and figuratively of something inching through a slow process, as a bill does through a legislature. (виться, извиваться; медленно продвигаться)

4. The trail *wends* along the ridge for six miles.
5. The creek *wended* through that valley long before the road did.
6. The bill *has wended* its way through three committees.
