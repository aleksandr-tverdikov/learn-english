# Participle = base + -n

> The participle is the base plus -n. The past form varies.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

What holds this group together is the third form, not the second. *Go* and *beat* share nothing in the past — *went* against *beat* — yet both build the participle by hanging -n or -en on the base: *gone*, *beaten*. The Russian original names the group exactly that way (3-я = 1-я + "n" (2-я меняется по-разному)), and the parenthesis is the whole point: the past is unpredictable, the participle is not. *Be* is the extreme case, with a past borrowed from two other verbs — *was*, *were* — and a participle, *been*, that still obeys the rule.

### be

**Pronunciation:** /bi/ &middot; *BEE*
**Русский:** быть
**Forms:** *be* /bi/ &middot; *was* /wʌz/, *were* /wɝ/ &middot; *been* /bɪn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — the most-used verb in the language; no American gets through a sentence without it
**Pattern:** suppletive: be — was/were — been &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *is* (also *am*, *are*) &middot; **-ing form:** *being*
**Contrast:** *Be* has two past forms — *was* after *I / he / she / it*, *were* after *you / we / they* — so ✗ *We was late*. The participle needs a helping verb: ✗ *I been there twice*, but *I have been there twice*. Keep *been* apart from *being*: *He has been rude* reports something that happened, *He is being rude* describes him right now. In American speech *been* usually rhymes with *bin*, not with *bean*.
**Verb type:** **linking (copular)** in the core senses — identity, location, existence, occurrence, price (*she is a pediatrician*, *the total was a hundred dollars*) — and **auxiliary** in senses 6–7, building every progressive (*she is working*) and every passive (*the road was repaved*). Stative except the behavior progressive *he is being rude*.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** — *be* never takes an object, only a subject complement, and that complement is obligatory: *the keys are in the drawer*, but ✗ *She is.* outside the existential *There is.*
**Passive:** **no** — *be* builds everyone else's passive and has none of its own: ✗ *A pediatrician is been by her.* Copulas and auxiliaries do not passivize.

The most basic verb in English: it links a subject to what it is, where it is, or what it is like, states that something exists, and does the grammatical work of building continuous tenses and the passive.

**1. Link a subject to a description.** The core copula use: the subject equals, or is described by, what follows. In American speech the present forms almost always contract — I'm, he's, they're — and Russian normally drops the verb entirely in the present. (быть, являться)

1. She *is* a pediatrician at the children's hospital.
2. He *was* the tallest kid in our class.
3. It costs nothing to *be* polite.
4. They *have been* good friends since college.

**2. Be somewhere, be present.** Location or attendance rather than identity. This is the sense behind *Is anybody home?* and *Where were you?* (находиться, быть (где-л.))

5. The spare keys *are* in the drawer by the sink.
6. I *was* at the dentist all morning.
7. Nobody *had been* in that room for years.

**3. Exist; there is / there are.** The existential pattern uses a dummy *there*, and the verb agrees with the noun that follows it: *there is a problem*, *there are problems*. (существовать; есть, имеется)

8. There *is* a crack in the foundation.
9. There *were* four of us in the car.
10. There *have been* three outages this month.

**4. Happen, take place.** Used of events with a time or a place attached, where it stands in for *take place*. (быть, состояться, происходить)

11. The wedding *is* in June.
12. The fire *was* two years ago this week.
13. Practice *has been* on Tuesdays all season.

**5. Cost, come to, equal.** Prices at the register and arithmetic on the page. A cashier says *that'll be*; math says *six times seven is forty-two*. (стоить; равняться)

14. That will *be* fourteen fifty.
15. The total *was* just under a hundred dollars.
16. Six times seven *is* forty-two.

**6. Auxiliary: continuous tenses.** *Be* + -ing builds every progressive tense; the auxiliary carries the tense so the main verb does not have to. (вспомогательный глагол (Continuous))

17. She *is* working late tonight.
18. We *were* driving home when the hail started.
19. He *has been* studying since noon.

**7. Auxiliary: the passive.** *Be* + past participle turns the object of an action into the subject of the sentence. Any tense of *be* can do it. (вспомогательный глагол (страдательный залог))

20. The road *was* repaved last summer.
21. Ballots *are* counted by hand in this county.
22. The house *has been* sold twice since we moved out.

**8. *have been to* — visited and come back.** *Been* fills in for *gone* when the trip is over: *She has been to Denver* means she went and returned, while *She has gone to Denver* means she is still away. (побывать (где-л.))

23. Have you ever *been* to New Orleans?
24. I *have been* to that museum twice.
25. By the time she was ten she *had been* to nine states.

**9. *be to* + verb — scheduled or required.** Formal, common in news writing and in instructions: an arrangement, an order, or something due to happen. In the past it usually hints that the plan fell through. (должен, предстоит)

26. The president *is* to speak at noon.
27. We *were* to meet at six, but she never showed.
28. No one *is* to leave before the inspector gets here.

**10. *be being* — acting that way for now.** The progressive of the copula, limited to behavior and temporary states: *He is being rude* describes this moment, while *He has been rude* reports what happened. (вести себя (в данный момент))

29. You *are being* ridiculous.
30. The kids *were being* unusually quiet.
31. I know I *am being* difficult about the date.

### beat

**Pronunciation:** /bit/ &middot; *BEET*
**Русский:** бить
**Forms:** *beat* /bit/ &middot; *beat* /bit/ &middot; *beaten* /ˈbitən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking, in sports talk, the kitchen, and plain speech
**Pattern:** beat — beat — beat + -en &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *beats* &middot; **-ing form:** *beating*
**Contrast:** The past is spelled and said exactly like the base — ✗ *beated* — so only the participle marks the group: *beaten*. Edited American English keeps that -en: *We have beaten them twice*, not *We have beat them twice*, though the shorter form is common in casual sports talk. *Beat* also survives as an adjective meaning worn out: *I'm beat*.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic in every documented sense: defeat, strike repeatedly, throb, whisk, outrun a deadline. The *I'm beat* of sense 9 is an adjective, not a verb form.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the defeat, hitting, cooking, and get-there-first senses (*we beat Chicago 4-1*, *beat the cream*, *she beat me to the spot*), intransitive of rain, surf, and hearts (*rain beat against the windows*, *my pulse beat faster*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the prisoners had been beaten before anyone reached them*, *he looked like he had been beaten up*. No passive of the intransitive *the surf beats on those rocks*.

Hit something over and over, defeat an opponent, or move in a steady rhythm.

**1. Defeat, win against.** Games, elections, and rivals of any kind. Edited American English keeps the -en after *have* — *we have beaten them* — though casual sports talk often says *have beat*. (побеждать, обыгрывать)

1. A team that sloppy can still *beat* us.
2. We *beat* Chicago 4-1 last October.
3. Our rowing club *has beaten* every school in the state this season.

**2. Hit repeatedly.** Repeated blows with a fist, a stick, or a drumstick. A single blow is *hit* or *strike*; *beat* implies more than one. (бить, колотить)

4. He *beat* the rug with a broom handle until the dust stopped coming.
5. She *beats* the drum on the downbeat.
6. The prisoners *had been beaten* before anyone reached them.

**3. Strike against something over and over.** Intransitive, of rain, waves, wind, or sun, usually with *against*, *on*, or *down on*. (хлестать, стучать, биться (обо что-л.))

7. Rain *beat* against the windows all night.
8. The surf *beats* on those rocks year-round.
9. The sun *had beaten* down on the roof all afternoon.

**4. Throb, pulse.** Of the heart or the pulse, and by extension of wings — *the hawk beat its wings twice*. (биться (о сердце), пульсировать)

10. I could feel my heart *beat* against my ribs.
11. His pulse *beat* faster than the monitor liked.
12. Her heart *has beaten* on that pacemaker for nine years.

**5. Mix or whisk (cooking).** Kitchen sense: stir hard and fast with a fork, a whisk, or a mixer. (взбивать (яйца, тесто, сливки))

13. She *beats* two eggs into the batter first.
14. *Beat* the cream until it holds a peak.
15. I *beat* the frosting by hand and regretted it.

**6. Be better than (informal).** Informal, usually in the frame *it beats* or *you can't beat*, followed by a noun or an -ing form. (лучше, чем)

16. A cold sandwich *beats* nothing at all.
17. It *beat* sitting in traffic for two hours.
18. You can't *beat* their prices on lumber.

**7. Get there first, beat a deadline.** Arrive or act before someone or something else: *beat the traffic*, *beat the rain*, *beat someone to it*. (опередить, успеть раньше)

19. We left at four to *beat* the traffic.
20. She *beat* me to the last parking spot.
21. They *had beaten* the storm home by twenty minutes.

**8. *beat up* — attack and injure.** Separable — *beat him up*, not *beat up him*. Reflexively, *beat yourself up* means to keep blaming yourself for something. (избить)

22. Two guys *beat* him *up* outside the bar.
23. Don't *beat* yourself *up* over one bad quarter.
24. He looked like he *had been beaten up*.

**9. *be beat* — worn out.** Informal and adjectival, and the one place where the short form is standard: *I'm beat* for tiredness, never *I'm beaten*. (измотан, без сил)

25. I am *beat* — I'm going to bed.
26. We *were* all *beat* after the drive from Denver.
27. By Sunday night the whole crew *was beat*.


**Phrasal verbs:** [beat back](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-back) &middot; [beat down](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-down) &middot; [beat off](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-off) &middot; [beat out](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-out) &middot; [beat up](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-up) &middot; [beat up on](14-phrasal-b.md#beat-up-on)

### browbeat

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbraʊbit/ &middot; *BROW-beet*
**Русский:** запугивать; отпугивать; нагонять страх
**Forms:** *browbeat* /ˈbraʊbit/ &middot; *browbeat* /ˈbraʊbit/ &middot; *browbeaten* /ˈbraʊˌbitən/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a prefixed derivative of *beat* that Americans recognize but seldom produce; everyday speech says *bully* or *pressure*
**Pattern:** brow- + beat — beat — beaten &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *browbeats* &middot; **-ing form:** *browbeating*
**Contrast:** It follows [beat](#beat) form for form, so the past looks like the base — ✗ *browbeated* — and the participle takes -en. Note the stress: *browbeat* keeps it on the first part, unlike the *go* compounds in this group, which stress the second.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic, a verb of verbal pressure. The participle *browbeaten* doubles as an adjective: *he had the browbeaten look of a man who never wins an argument*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** only, with an obligatory human object, typically in the frame *browbeat someone into* doing something: *the attorney browbeat the witness*; ✗ *The attorney browbeat until the judge stepped in.*
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is common — *two suppliers had been browbeaten into cutting their prices*; *everyone left the meeting feeling browbeaten*.

Bully someone into doing something by badgering, hectoring, or intimidating them.

**1. Bully into doing something.** Pressure applied with words rather than force, usually in the frame *browbeat someone into* doing something. It follows *beat* form for form, so the past looks like the base. (запугивать, принуждать (давлением))

1. Don't let them *browbeat* you into signing anything today.
2. She *browbeats* her little brother into doing her chores.
3. The attorney *browbeat* the witness until the judge stepped in.
4. Management *has browbeaten* the staff about the new schedule for weeks.

**2. *browbeaten* — cowed, worn down.** The participle used as an adjective or in the passive, describing someone left meek by constant pressure. (запуганный, забитый)

5. Everyone left the meeting feeling *browbeaten*.
6. He had the *browbeaten* look of a man who never wins an argument.
7. Two suppliers *had been browbeaten* into cutting their prices.

**See also:** [beat](#beat)

### carve

**Pronunciation:** /kɑrv/ &middot; *KARV*
**Русский:** вырезать, резать (по дереву, кости)
**Forms:** *carve* /kɑrv/ &middot; *carved* /kɑrvd/ &middot; *carved* /kɑrvd/ — archaic participle *carven* /ˈkɑrvən/; the past *corve* that the source list records is not an English word
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — carving a pumpkin or a turkey is ordinary American talk; only the leftover participle *carven* is literary
**Pattern:** regular -ed, with a leftover -n participle &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *carves* &middot; **-ing form:** *carving* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** In modern American English *carve* is an ordinary regular verb: *carve — carved — carved*. It belongs to this group only because of *carven*, an old participle that now survives just as a literary adjective — *a carven door*, *carven stone* — and never after *have*: ✗ *I have carven the turkey*. The past *corve* that appears in some Russian tables is not an English word at all; use *carved*. Compare [grave](11-past-plus-d-participle-plus-n.md#grave) and [shave](11-past-plus-d-participle-plus-n.md#shave), which kept *graven* and *shaven* the same way.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic throughout: shaping wood or stone, slicing meat, incising letters, and the figurative *carve out* / *carve up*. *Carven* is no longer a verb form, only a literary adjective — ✗ *I have carven the turkey*.
**Transitivity:** **both**, but overwhelmingly transitive (*carve a pumpkin*, *carve her initials into the table*, *carve out two hours*); the object drops only in the absolute use *Who carves at your house?*
**Passive:** **yes** — *the turkey had been carved by the time we sat down*, *the old dairy farm was carved up into forty lots*.

Cut a shape out of a hard material, slice cooked meat, or cut a piece out of something larger.

**1. Cut a shape from wood or stone.** The core sense: shape a hard material by cutting away at it. In modern American English the verb is fully regular — *carve, carved, carved*. (вырезать, высекать (из дерева, камня, кости))

1. Every October he *carves* a pumpkin for the porch.
2. My grandfather taught me how to *carve* birds out of pine.
3. A neighbor *has carved* a bench out of the fallen oak.

**2. Slice cooked meat.** The table sense, most often heard about a turkey or a roast at a holiday dinner. (разрезать, нарезать (мясо))

4. Who *carves* the roast at your house?
5. He *carved* the ham into slices too thick for sandwiches.
6. By the time we sat down, my uncle *had carved* the turkey.

**3. Cut letters or marks into a surface.** Incising rather than shaping: initials in a tree, a date in a beam, a name in stone. (вырезать (надпись), высекать)

7. She *carved* her initials into the picnic table.
8. Please don't *carve* anything into that bench.
9. Somebody *had carved* a date into the beam above the door.

**4. *carve out* — make a place by effort.** Figurative: take something out of a larger whole through effort — a niche, a career, an hour in a full day. (выкроить, создать (нишу, время, репутацию))

10. She *carved out* a niche selling used farm equipment online.
11. We *carved out* two hours on Friday for the review.
12. He *has carved out* a reputation as the guy who answers the phone.

**5. *carve up* — divide into pieces.** Usually disapproving, of land, markets, or an estate split among competing claimants. (делить, дробить (на части))

13. Developers *carved up* the old dairy farm into forty lots.
14. Three chains *have carved up* the whole grocery market here.

**6. *carven* — literary adjective.** An old participle that survives only as a literary adjective in front of a noun — *a carven door*, *carven stone* — and never after *have*. (резной)

15. The museum keeps a *carven* panel from an old Norwegian church.
16. Rows of *carven* saints line the choir stalls.

**See also:** [shear](#shear), [cut](01-all-three-same.md#cut), [grave](11-past-plus-d-participle-plus-n.md#grave)

### forego

**Pronunciation:** /fɔrˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *for-GOH*
**Русский:** предшествовать
**Forms:** *forego* /fɔrˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *forewent* /fɔrˈwɛnt/ &middot; *foregone* /fɔrˈɡɔn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — the "go before" verb is dead in ordinary use; Americans meet it only as *the foregoing* and *a foregone conclusion*
**Pattern:** fore- + go — went — gone &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *foregoes* (-es after the o) &middot; **-ing form:** *foregoing*
**Contrast:** *Forego* and [forgo](#forgo) sound identical but do opposite work: *forego* means "go before," *forgo* means "do without." The finite forms of *forego* are formal and rare today; what you actually meet is *foregoing* ("the foregoing paragraph") and *foregone* in the fixed phrase *a foregone conclusion*. Dictionaries do record *forego* as a variant spelling of *forgo*, but careful American editing keeps the two words apart.
**Verb type:** **action** in the literal 'come or go before' sense, though the subject is an event rather than an agent (*months of quiet rehearsal forewent opening night*). What survives in live American English is adjectival: *the foregoing section*, *a foregone conclusion*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is what the subject precedes: *a hymn would forego the sermon*. No intransitive use; the finite forms are formal and rare either way.
**Passive:** **restricted** — formally possible but effectively unattested (*the sermon was foregone by a hymn* reads as an error for *forgone*); the participle lives on only as the adjective in *a foregone conclusion*.

Come or go before something else in time or order — a formal verb now met mostly as *the foregoing* and *a foregone conclusion*.

**1. Come or go before.** The literal sense — *fore-* plus *go*. Formal and rare in its finite forms today; you will meet it mostly in older, legal, or academic prose. (предшествовать)

1. In the older service, a hymn would *forego* the sermon.
2. Months of quiet rehearsal *forewent* opening night.
3. The events that *have foregone* the treaty are covered in chapter two.
4. Two smaller quakes *had foregone* the big one by about a week.

**2. *the foregoing* — what was said above.** Legal and academic writing, used as an adjective or as a noun: *the foregoing paragraph*, *nothing in the foregoing*. (вышеизложенное, вышесказанное)

5. The *foregoing* section explains where the numbers come from.
6. Nothing in the *foregoing* limits your right to cancel.

**3. *a foregone conclusion* — settled in advance.** A fixed phrase, and the most common place the participle still turns up. Keep the e: *foregone conclusion*, not *forgone*. (предрешённый исход)

7. His reelection was a *foregone* conclusion by the third quarter.
8. The verdict felt like a *foregone* conclusion before the trial even started.

**4. Spelling variant of *forgo*.** Dictionaries do record *forego* for *forgo* (do without), and it appears in print, but careful American editing keeps the two words apart. (= forgo: отказываться, воздерживаться (от чего-л.))

9. The board voted to *forego* raises this year — an editor would make that *forgo*.
10. You will see *forego* the fee on plenty of websites, though *forgo* is the standard spelling.

**See also:** [forgo](#forgo), [go](#go), [foresee](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#foresee)

### forgo

**Pronunciation:** /fɔrˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *for-GOH*
**Русский:** отказываться или воздерживаться (от чего-л.)
**Forms:** *forgo* /fɔrˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *forwent* /fɔrˈwɛnt/ &middot; *forgone* /fɔrˈɡɔn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing, especially legal and financial prose; speech says *give up* or *do without*, and the past *forwent* is rare anywhere
**Pattern:** for- + go — went — gone &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *forgoes* (-es after the o) &middot; **-ing form:** *forgoing*
**Contrast:** Same sound as [forego](#forego), different job: *forgo* is the one that means "do without" — *forgo dessert*, *forgo a salary*. The past *forwent* is correct but rare; in ordinary American writing *gave up* or *did without* usually takes its place. And *forgone* is not the *foregone* of *a foregone conclusion*, which keeps the e.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic, a deliberate choice to do without. *Forgone* also serves as an adjective in the economics sense: *forgone revenue*, *forgone wages*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, object obligatory — *I'll forgo dessert*, *the lender agreed to forgo interest for six months*; ✗ *The lender agreed to forgo.*
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but stiff and rare (*the bonus was forgone so the team could split it*); American writing keeps it active or reaches for the adjective, *ninety million in forgone revenue*.

Choose to do without something you could have had, or give up a claim to it.

**1. Do without, go without.** The everyday sense: give up something available to you, usually by choice. The past *forwent* is correct but rare — most Americans say *went without* or *gave up*. (отказаться, обойтись без (чего-л.))

1. I'll *forgo* dessert tonight.
2. Plenty of owners *forgo* a salary in the first year.
3. She *forwent* the bonus so her team could split it.
4. He *has forgone* two vacations to finish the house.

**2. Waive a right or a payment.** Formal, legal, and financial: a party forgoes interest, fees, or a claim it could otherwise enforce. (отказаться от (права, выплаты))

5. The lender agreed to *forgo* interest for six months.
6. The heirs *forwent* any claim on the house.
7. They *had forgone* health coverage for years before the new plan.

**3. *forgone* income — money not earned.** Economics and accounting: *forgone revenue*, *forgone wages* — the cost of a choice measured by what it gave up. (недополученный доход, упущенная выгода)

8. The tax break cost the state about ninety million in *forgone* revenue.
9. Their estimate includes *forgone* wages for the months she was out of work.

**See also:** [forego](#forego), [go](#go), [forsake](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#forsake)

### go

**Pronunciation:** /ɡoʊ/ &middot; *GOH*
**Русский:** идти
**Forms:** *go* /ɡoʊ/ &middot; *went* /wɛnt/ &middot; *gone* /ɡɔn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** go — went — gone &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *goes* (-es after the o) &middot; **-ing form:** *going*
**Contrast:** *Went* is the past and stands on its own; *gone* is the participle and needs *have*, *has*, or *had*. ✗ *I have went to the bank* and ✗ *He gone home already* are the two classic errors — say *I went to the bank* and *He has gone home already*. There is also a *gone / been* split: *She has gone to Denver* means she is still away, while *She has been to Denver* means she went and came back.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic in the movement, departure, process, and give-out senses (*they went to the coast*, *the transmission went*). Also **linking (copular)** with a bare adjective in *go bad*, *go sour*, *go blind*, *go bankrupt* — almost always a change for the worse.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense the entry documents; destinations, paths, and complements arrive through prepositions or particles (*go to the store*, *go through the file*, *go with those pants*). Even *six goes into forty-eight* is intransitive.
**Passive:** **no** — with no object there is nothing to promote: ✗ *The coast was gone to by them*, ✗ *Home had been gone by everyone.* Only the prepositional phrasal *go through* allows a marginal passive in casual speech — *every bag had been gone through*.

Move or travel from one place to another, leave, or — in dozens of extended uses — become, turn out, run, fit, fail, or stop.

**1. Move or travel to a place.** The core sense, away from the speaker. Note the *gone / been* split: *She has gone to Denver* means she is still away, while *She has been to Denver* means she went and came back. (идти, ехать, ходить)

1. We *go* to my sister's every Thanksgiving.
2. They *went* to the coast for three days.
3. My roommate *has gone* to the store.

**2. Leave, depart.** Intransitive, with no destination named. *I have to go* means either leaving or, politely, using the bathroom. (уходить, уезжать)

4. It's late — I should *go*.
5. She *went* without saying goodbye.
6. Everyone *had gone* home by nine o'clock.

**3. Become (usually a worse state).** A linking use with an adjective: *go bad*, *go blind*, *go bankrupt*, *go gray*, *go crazy*. Nearly always a change for the worse — improvements take *get* or *turn*. (становиться, делаться)

7. The milk *went* sour in one hot afternoon.
8. If the power fails, the alarms *go* dead.
9. Three of the mills *have gone* bankrupt since spring.

**4. Turn out, proceed.** How an event or a process works out — the sense behind *How did it go?* and *the meeting went well*. (проходить, идти (об успехе дела))

10. How did the interview *go*?
11. The first day *went* better than I expected.
12. Rehearsals *have gone* smoothly so far.

**5. Lead, extend, run.** Of roads, fences, wires, or ranges: the subject does not move, it stretches from one place to another. (вести, идти, тянуться)

13. This road *goes* all the way to the lake.
14. The old fence *went* right through where the barn is now.
15. The trail *has gone* around that ridge ever since they rerouted it.

**6. Belong, have its place.** Where a thing is kept or whether it fits: *Where do the plates go?*, *It won't go through the door*. (быть на месте, класться, помещаться)

16. The big pot *goes* in the cabinet under the sink.
17. Those files *went* in the gray box.
18. That couch will never *go* through this doorway.

**7. Give out, run out, be used up.** Of things that fail or disappear — a transmission, someone's hearing, the last of the coffee. The participle is common as a bare adjective: *the money is gone*. (кончаться; отказывать, ломаться)

19. The transmission *went* at a hundred and ten thousand miles.
20. His hearing is *going* in one ear.
21. Half the paycheck *had gone* to rent before he ever saw it.
22. The cookies are all *gone*.

**8. *be going to* — future or intention.** The ordinary spoken future, for plans already made and for predictions with visible evidence. In speech it reduces to *gonna*. (собираться (что-л. сделать))

23. I am *going* to call her tonight.
24. It is *going* to rain before we finish.
25. We *were going* to drive, but the flights got cheap.

**9. *go* + -ing — do an activity.** A fixed pattern for sports and errands: *go fishing, go running, go shopping*. Watch the preposition — you *go swimming in the lake*, not to the lake. (ходить (плавать, за покупками и т. п.))

26. He *goes* running before work.
27. We *went* fishing on Saturday.
28. I have never *gone* skiing.

**10. Match, suit, fit with.** Of colors, flavors, or furniture, usually as *go with*. The same verb covers arithmetic: *three goes into twelve four times*. (подходить, сочетаться)

29. That shirt does not *go* with those pants.
30. The blue rug *went* better with the couch than the green one.
31. Six *goes* into forty-eight eight times.

**11. *go on* — continue; happen.** Two everyday uses: keep doing something, and — with *what* — be taking place. On its own, *Go on!* means keep talking. (продолжаться; происходить)

32. The argument *went on* for an hour.
33. What is *going on* out there?
34. The strike *has gone on* longer than either side planned.

**12. *go off* — explode, sound; spoil.** Of bombs, guns, and alarms; also of food turning bad, though Americans usually say *go bad* for that. *Go off on someone* means lose your temper at them. (сработать, взорваться; испортиться)

35. The smoke alarm *goes off* every time I use the broiler.
36. The flash *went off* right in my eyes.
37. Two alarms *had gone off* before anybody called it in.

**13. *go out* — be extinguished; go out socially.** A fire or a light goes out; people go out for the evening, and *go out with* someone means date them. (погаснуть; выходить (в свет), встречаться)

38. The lights *went out* across three counties.
39. We hardly ever *go out* on weeknights.
40. Her parents *had gone out* for the evening.

**14. *go through* — endure; search.** Live through something hard, or look through things one by one. *Go through with* means carry out a plan you were hesitant about. (пережить; просмотреть, перебрать)

41. She *went through* a rough divorce last year.
42. *Go through* the file and pull anything from 2019.
43. Customs *had gone through* every bag on the flight.

### shear

**Pronunciation:** /ʃɪr/ &middot; *SHEER*
**Русский:** стричь
**Forms:** *shear* /ʃɪr/ &middot; *sheared* /ʃɪrd/ (the source list records the archaic *shore* /ʃɔr/) &middot; *shorn* /ʃɔrn/ (also *sheared* /ʃɪrd/)
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — specialized: sheep and wool on one side, engineering on the other; ordinary Americans say *cut* or *clip*
**Pattern:** shear — sheared — shorn &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *shears* &middot; **-ing form:** *shearing*
**Contrast:** Both participles are current in American English, and they split by sense: *shorn* for wool, hair, and anything figuratively stripped away — *shorn sheep*, *shorn of authority* — and *sheared* for machinery and for metal that snaps under load: *the bolt sheared off*. The past is *sheared*; the *shore* listed in older tables is archaic and today reads as the noun *shore*. Do not confuse the verb with its homophone, the adjective *sheer* /ʃɪr/.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic. The participle splits by sense: *shorn* for wool, hair, and figurative stripping (*shorn of authority*), *sheared* for metal snapping under load (*the bolt sheared off*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive of animals and hair (*they shear the flock every spring*, *a barber sheared his curls off*), intransitive in the mechanical sense (*a rivet can shear long before it pulls out*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses, and often the preferred form — *every animal on the place has been shorn*, *the recruits were shorn the first morning*. *Shorn of* is passive-only: *by 1919 the emperor had been shorn of every real power*. No passive of the intransitive *the bolt sheared off*.

Cut the wool or hair off an animal, or cut and snap cleanly through something.

**1. Shear a sheep, cut off wool.** The primary sense, and the one that takes *shorn*: *a shorn sheep*, *the flock has been shorn*. The past is *sheared*. (стричь (овец))

1. They *shear* the flock every spring.
2. The crew *sheared* four hundred ewes on Saturday.
3. Every animal on the place *has been shorn*.
4. By noon the ranch *had shorn* half the herd.

**2. Crop hair close.** Of people, and usually severe or forced — recruits, prisoners, patients. Most often passive with *shorn*. (остричь, обрить)

5. The recruits *were shorn* the first morning.
6. A barber *sheared* his curls off in about a minute.
7. She *has* been *shorn* nearly to the scalp for the treatments.

**3. Snap or cut cleanly through (mechanics).** Engineering sense: metal failing under a sideways load. Here the participle is *sheared*, never *shorn* — *the bolt sheared off*. (срезать(ся), ломаться (о болте, металле))

8. A rivet can *shear* long before it pulls out.
9. The bolt *sheared* off under the load.
10. Ice *has sheared* those pilings clean off twice.

**4. *shorn of* — stripped of.** Figurative and formal, passive only: left without power, rights, or assets. (лишённый (чего-л.))

11. The company came out of the lawsuit *shorn* of its patents.
12. By 1919 the emperor *had been shorn* of every real power.

### undergo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndɚˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *un-der-GOH*
**Русский:** испытывать, переносить, подвергаться
**Forms:** *undergo* /ˌʌndɚˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *underwent* /ˌʌndɚˈwɛnt/ &middot; *undergone* /ˌʌndɚˈɡɔn/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral to formal — ordinary in writing, especially medical and news prose; speech says *have* or *go through*
**Pattern:** under- + go — went — gone &middot; **Group:** 10
**Third person:** *undergoes* (-es after the o) &middot; **-ing form:** *undergoing*
**Contrast:** It inherits every form from [go](#go), so ✗ *undergoed* and ✗ *has underwent*. Unlike *go*, it is almost always transitive and takes an event or process as its object — you *undergo* surgery, testing, a change — and it never describes physical travel.
**Verb type:** **action** — dynamic but non-agentive: the subject is the one something is done to (*the design had undergone six revisions*, *she underwent knee surgery*). No linking or stative use, and it never means physical travel the way *go* does.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, object obligatory, and the object is always a process, treatment, or change: *undergo two weeks of training*, *undergo an ordeal*; ✗ *All new hires undergo.*
**Passive:** **no** — the subject is already the affected party, so promotion has nowhere to go: ✗ *Knee surgery was undergone by her*, ✗ *Six revisions had been undergone by the design.* Keep it active, or say *she had knee surgery*.

Experience something done to you — a process, a treatment, a change — or endure something difficult.

**1. Go through a process or treatment.** Almost always transitive, with an event or procedure as the object: surgery, training, testing, review. It never describes physical travel, unlike *go*. (проходить, подвергаться (процедуре, обучению))

1. All new hires *undergo* two weeks of training.
2. She *underwent* knee surgery in March.
3. By the time it shipped, the design *had undergone* six revisions.

**2. Experience a change.** Of places, materials, and institutions changing over time; the subject is affected rather than acting. (претерпевать (изменения))

4. Steel *undergoes* stress you can't see.
5. The language *underwent* a major shift in that century.
6. The neighborhood *has undergone* a lot of change since 2010.

**3. Endure something difficult.** Formal, of hardship borne rather than chosen: *undergo hardship*, *undergo an ordeal*. (переносить, испытывать (трудности))

7. No family should have to *undergo* that twice.
8. Prisoners *underwent* months of interrogation.
9. The crew *had undergone* worse on the previous crossing.

**See also:** [go](#go), [undertake](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#undertake)
