Past = participle, vowel change plus -d#

Like the previous group, with a -d added to the changed stem.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

Every verb here builds one form that serves as both the past and the participle: the stem vowel shifts, and a -d lands on the end of it. Tell becomes told, hear becomes heard, flee becomes fled — and that single form does double duty, in I told her yesterday and in I have told her twice alike (как №3, плюс окончание "d"). Because the past and the participle are identical, none of these verbs can produce the began/begun mistake; the work is remembering that the -d is there at all and that the vowel underneath it is no longer the one you started with. Twelve of the seventeen entries below are tell, sell, and hear wearing a prefix, so three verbs get you most of the list.

can#

Pronunciation
/kæn/ · KAN · weak form /kən/ in I can go
Русский
мочь; уметь
Forms
can /kæn/ · could /kʊd/ · could /kʊd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
an — ould (a modal, not an ordinary verb)
Group
4
Third person
can — no -s, ✗ he cans · -ing form: none; modals have no -ing and no infinitive
Contrast
Could is a past tense, not a real participle: English has no ✗ I have could. Where a perfect is needed, be able to takes over — I have been able to drive since I was seventeen. Could also serves as a present-tense politeness or a hypothetical (Could you hold this?, We could leave now), so a could in front of you is not automatically about the past. The l is silent, as in would and should. A separate and fully regular can means "put food in cans": canned, canning.
Verb type
modal auxiliary — never a main verb: it takes a bare infinitive (can carry), has no -s, no -ing, and no participle (✗ he cans, ✗ I have could, ✗ to can), so be able to fills the perfect and infinitive slots. A separate, fully regular action verb can means "put food in cans."
Transitivity
neither transitive nor intransitive in the ordinary sense — a modal takes a bare-infinitive complement, not an object: I can carry both bags. The only nominal object it ever shows is the fixed he can't help it.
Passive
no — modals have no passive of their own; voice is carried by the verb they support (the bags can be carried), never by can itself.

The modal of ability, permission, and possibility: can in the present, could for the past and for polite or hypothetical use — and no participle at all, so the perfect is have been able to.

1. Be able to (ability). The core sense: the subject has the strength, the power, or the opportunity to do something. Could covers past ability and also softens a present claim. (мочь, быть в состоянии)

  1. I can carry both bags if you get the door.
  2. She could swim before she turned four.
  3. We could not find a parking spot anywhere downtown.

2. Know how to (learned skill). Where Russian uses уметь: a skill acquired by learning rather than a one-time possibility. English does not mark the difference — can does both jobs. (уметь)

  1. I can read Spanish well enough, but I can't speak it.
  2. He can play three instruments.
  3. By the end of the summer she could ride without training wheels.

3. Be allowed to (permission). Permission granted by a rule or by a person. May is the formal equivalent, but American speech uses can almost everywhere. (можно, иметь право)

  1. You can park on this side after six.
  2. Back then you could still buy a ticket at the gate.
  3. Students cannot register until their fees are paid.

4. Requests and offers — Can you…?. Can you asks someone to do something; Can I offers or asks permission. Could here is politeness, not past time — the most useful thing to know in the whole entry. (не могли бы вы; можно мне)

  1. Can you hold the door for a second?
  2. Could you say that again a little slower?
  3. Can I get you anything from the kitchen?

5. Possibility — what sometimes happens. A general or occasional possibility, not a prediction about one occasion; for a specific guess about the future English uses may or might. (бывает, может случиться)

  1. Winters here can get brutal.
  2. That intersection can be dangerous in the rain.
  3. The old road could wash out after a heavy rain.

6. can't — impossibility, confident deduction. Negative can't is the strong "that is impossible" counterpart of must: not a lack of ability but a refusal to believe. In past time it is can't have or couldn't have. (не может быть, никак не может)

  1. That can't be right — check the total again.
  2. He couldn't have known; nobody told him.
  3. She can't be more than twenty.

7. could — hypothetical, suggestion, missed chance. A present or future hypothetical, a soft suggestion, or — with have — something that was possible and did not happen. The time reference is not past, despite the form. (мог бы; можно было бы)

  1. We could leave now if you want to beat the traffic.
  2. You could have called instead of driving all the way over.
  3. Could we move the meeting to Thursday?

8. can see, can hear — perception. With verbs of perception and mental state, can carries the load a progressive would carry elsewhere: ✗ I am hearing you comes out as I can hear you. (вижу, слышу (сейчас))

  1. I can see the ocean from the top floor.
  2. We could hear them arguing through the wall.
  3. Can you smell that?

9. can't help — be unable to stop yourself. A fixed frame, always negative, followed by an -ing form or by it: ✗ can't help to laugh. (не могу удержаться, ничего не могу поделать)

  1. I can't help laughing every time he tells that story.
  2. She couldn't help noticing the missing signature.
  3. He can't help it — he was raised that way.

10. No participle — use be able to. Could is a past tense, not a real participle: English has no ✗ I have could and no ✗ to can. Wherever a perfect, an infinitive, or a future is needed, be able to takes over. (смочь (в перфекте и инфинитиве))

  1. Since the surgery he has been able to walk without a cane — never ✗ has could.
  2. By March we had been able to clear most of the backlog.
  3. She could not get through on the phone, and she has not been able to since.

clothe#

Pronunciation
/kloʊð/ · KLOHTH · the th of the, not of thin
Русский
одевать
Forms
clothe /kloʊð/ · clad /klæd/ · clad /klæd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
neutral to formal — ordinary in writing (clothe and feed), uncommon in speech, where an American says dress or put clothes on
Pattern
othe — ad
Group
4
Third person
clothes /kloʊðz/ · -ing form: clothing (spelled like the noun clothing)
Contrast
In ordinary American English the past and participle are the regular clothed — the shelter clothed and fed them. Clad is the older form, and it survives mostly as a participial adjective, almost always with in or inside a compound: scantily clad, ivy-clad, steel-clad. Both are standard; clad is literary or technical, clothed is what goes in a report. Keep the verb clothe /kloʊð/ apart from the noun cloth /klɔθ/, whose th is voiceless and whose plural is cloths.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense, from supplying clothing (the shelter clothes and feeds them) to covering a surface (snow clad the hills). The participle clad now does most of its work as an adjective: scantily clad, ivy-clad.
Transitivity
transitive — the object is obligatory: clothe the children, ✗ the shelter clothed on its own. The facing sense adds in: they clad the new tower in blue glass.
Passive
yes, and the be clad in sense is normally passive — the reactor core is clad in steel, every wall of the lobby had been clad in walnut.

Put clothing on someone or supply them with it — and, as clad, cover a surface as if with clothing.

1. Dress someone, supply clothing. Transitive, and usually paired with feed. In this everyday sense the regular clothed is the normal American past and participle; clad would sound biblical. (одевать, обеспечивать одеждой)

  1. The shelter clothes and feeds about forty families every winter.
  2. They had almost nothing to clothe the children in.
  3. By noon the volunteers had clothed and fed two hundred people.

2. be clad in — faced, sheathed. Technical and architectural, nearly always passive and followed by in: a surface covered with a protective or decorative material. (быть облицованным, покрытым (чем-л.))

  1. The reactor core is clad in steel.
  2. Every wall of the lobby had been clad in walnut.
  3. They clad the new tower in blue glass.

3. Cover as if with clothing. Literary, of snow, fog, ivy, or light lying over a landscape. Clad is the usual form here. (окутывать, покрывать)

  1. Snow clad the hills overnight.
  2. Fog clothes the valley most mornings.
  3. By dawn the fields had been clad in frost.

4. clad as an adjective. The participle does most of its modern work as an adjective, either with a modifier in front (scantily clad, poorly clad) or inside a compound (ivy-clad, steel-clad). (одетый (во что-л.))

  1. Two scantily clad dancers came out for the finale.
  2. The hikers arrived clad in bright rain gear.
See also
shoe, seethe

flee#

Pronunciation
/fli/ · FLEE
Русский
бежать; спасаться
Forms
flee /fli/ · fled /flɛd/ · fled /flɛd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
neutral — standard in news and narrative (fled the scene); conversation usually says run or get out
Pattern
ee — ed
Group
4
Third person
flees · -ing form: fleeing (the e stays — ✗ fleing)
Contrast
Fled and flew belong to different verbs. Flee/fled/fled is escaping; fly/flew/flown is air travel: the family fled the country, but the family flew to Denver. Flee takes its object bare — fled the city — where learners often write fled from the city; both occur, but the bare object is the one in edited American prose.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, including the literary use with abstract subjects: sleep fled the moment the phone rang, all the color had fled her face.
Transitivity
both — transitive with a bare object in edited American prose (fled the city, fled the scene, fled the country), intransitive or with from when the danger is left unnamed (families flee whenever a hurricane is forecast).
Passive
restricted — grammatical but avoided: ✗ the scene was fled by the driver reads badly, and writers keep the active. The intransitive use has no passive at all.

Run from danger — leave a place or a person quickly in order to escape.

1. Escape from danger. The core sense. The object comes bare in edited American prose — fled the city — though flee from also occurs. Do not confuse fled with flew, which belongs to fly. (бежать, спасаться бегством)

  1. Families flee the coast whenever a hurricane is forecast.
  2. Thousands fled the city in a single week.
  3. She has fled two wars and hopes never to do it again.

2. Flee justice, abscond. Legal and journalistic: leaving to avoid arrest, prosecution, or a court order. Common in flee the scene and flee the country. (скрываться, бежать (от правосудия))

  1. The driver had fled the scene before officers arrived.
  2. He fled the country a week ahead of the indictment.
  3. Two of the suspects have fled the state.

3. Avoid, keep away from. Figurative and somewhat formal: shunning a subject, a duty, or a kind of company rather than a physical danger. (избегать, бежать от)

  1. He flees any conversation that turns to money.
  2. Young doctors are fleeing rural practice.

4. Vanish, slip away (of abstract things). Literary. Sleep, color, hope, and daylight flee rather than simply going away. (исчезать, улетучиваться)

  1. Sleep fled the moment the phone rang.
  2. All the color had fled her face.
See also
lead, feed

foretell#

Pronunciation
/fɔrˈtɛl/ · for-TEL
Русский
предсказывать
Forms
foretell /fɔrˈtɛl/ · foretold /fɔrˈtoʊld/ · foretold /fɔrˈtoʊld/
Verb class
irregular
Register
rare — recognized but seldom produced; prophecy and literature, while an American predicts an election and forecasts the weather
Pattern
ell — old
Group
4
Third person
foretells · -ing form: foretelling (nothing doubles — tell already ends in a double l)
Contrast
It inflects exactly like tell; only the meaning is special. Foretell belongs to prophecy and literature — in everyday American writing you predict an election and forecast the weather, and you save foretold for oracles, omens, and anything that reads as fate.
Verb type
action with a human subject prophesying (two economists foretold the collapse); closer to stative with an inanimate subject that merely points to an outcome — nothing in the data foretells a crash, ✗ the data is foretelling a crash.
Transitivity
transitive — object obligatory, either a noun or a that-clause: foretold his own retirement, foretold that the deal would fail. ✗ He foretold alone is incomplete.
Passive
yes — the collapse was foretold and nobody listened; the ending had been foretold in the opening chapter.

Say in advance that something will happen — the vocabulary of prophecy rather than of forecasting.

1. Prophesy, predict. Of a person or a text announcing the future. Formal and literary; for ordinary predictions American English uses predict for events and forecast for weather and numbers. (предсказывать, пророчить)

  1. The old almanac claimed to foretell the winter from the thickness of the corn husks.
  2. Two economists foretold the collapse and were ignored.
  3. He foretold his own retirement a year before he announced it.

2. Be a sign of what is coming. With an inanimate subject — data, an omen, an opening chapter — meaning that it points to a later outcome. No intention is involved. (предвещать, предзнаменовать)

  1. Nothing in the data foretells a crash.
  2. The opening chapter had foretold the ending, and nobody noticed.
  3. Everything that letter has foretold has come true.
See also
tell, retell

hear#

Pronunciation
/hɪr/ · HEER
Русский
слышать, услышать
Forms
hear /hɪr/ · heard /hɝd/ · heard /hɝd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
ear /ɪr/ — eard /ɝd/
Group
4
Third person
hears · -ing form: hearing (also the noun — a hearing)
Contrast
The spelling adds one letter, but the vowel changes completely: hear rhymes with near, heard rhymes with bird — /hɝd/, never ✗ /hɪrd/. Spoken aloud, heard and herd are identical. And hear is not listen: hearing happens to you, listening is something you do (I heard the alarm / I listened to the message), which is why hear resists the progressive — say I can hear you, not ✗ I am hearing you.
Verb type
stative in the core perception sense, which rejects the progressive: ✗ I am hearing you — say I can hear you. Action in the deliberate institutional senses hear a case, hear the testimony, hear someone out.
Transitivity
transitive — the object may be a noun, a that-clause, or a small clause (nobody heard the alarm go off). It is intransitive only with a preposition attached: hear from the landlord, hear of that band, wouldn't hear of it.
Passive
yes — the alarm was heard three blocks away, the committee heard testimony → testimony was heard for six hours, the senator was heard to say it. Even the prepositional sense passivizes in the fixed he has not been heard from since April.

Take in a sound — and by extension get news, get word from someone, or listen formally to a case.

1. Perceive a sound. Involuntary perception, not the deliberate act of listen: hearing happens to you. It resists the progressive — say I can hear you, not ✗ I am hearing you. (слышать)

  1. I hear the train from my bedroom every night.
  2. Do you hear that clicking noise?
  3. Nobody heard the alarm go off.

2. Learn, get news of. Followed by a that-clause or by about. The simple present often reports something quite recent: I hear you're moving. (слышать, узнавать (новость))

  1. She heard the news from a neighbor.
  2. I hear you're moving to Denver.
  3. We had heard about the layoffs before the memo went out.

3. hear from — get word from someone. Contact from a person, in any medium. Extremely common in the negative perfect: haven't heard from him. (получать известия от кого-л.)

  1. We have heard nothing from the landlord since April.
  2. Did you ever hear from your sister?
  3. He heard from the school on Tuesday.

4. hear of — know that something exists. Almost always negative or interrogative: never heard of it. Distinct from hear about, which is about news rather than bare existence. (слышать о ком-л./чём-л.)

  1. I have never heard of that band.
  2. Nobody had heard of him before the trial.
  3. Have you heard of a town called Winslow?

5. Hear a case, take testimony. Legal and institutional: a judge, a board, or a committee formally receives argument or evidence. (заслушивать, рассматривать (дело))

  1. The judge hears motions on Thursday mornings.
  2. The committee heard testimony for six hours.
  3. The board has heard three appeals this month.

6. hear someone out — listen to the end. Separable, and usually imperative or negative; it implies the listener was ready to interrupt. (выслушать до конца)

  1. Hear me out before you say no.
  2. They never heard her out.
  3. Once he had heard them out, he changed his position.

7. won't hear of it — refuse to allow. A fixed negative frame with will or would, rejecting a suggestion outright — often a generous one. (и слышать не хотеть)

  1. I offered to pay and she wouldn't hear of it.
  2. My father would not hear of my dropping out.

8. I hear you — I take your point. A conversational American formula acknowledging what someone said, usually right before disagreeing or naming a limit. Informal. (понимаю тебя; слышу тебя)

  1. I hear you, but the budget won't stretch that far.
  2. She said she heard me, and then did exactly what she wanted.
Phrasal verbs
hear about · hear about, hear from, hear of · hear from · hear of · hear out
See also
overhear, mishear, rehear

mishear#

Pronunciation
/ˌmɪsˈhɪr/ · mis-HEER
Русский
ослышаться
Forms
mishear /ˌmɪsˈhɪr/ · misheard /ˌmɪsˈhɝd/ · misheard /ˌmɪsˈhɝd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
neutral — a prefixed derivative, but a live one: sorry, I must have misheard you is ordinary speech
Pattern
ear — eard
Group
4
Third person
mishears · -ing form: mishearing (also a noun: a common mishearing of the lyric)
Contrast
Inflects like hear. It means you heard something and got it wrong — not that you failed to hear it; for that, American English says didn't hear or couldn't hear. The verb is most at home in the perfect after must: I must have misheard.
Verb type
action — dynamic and momentary, a single mishap rather than the ongoing state of hear: I misheard the gate number.
Transitivity
transitive, often with an as-complement (she had misheard "fifteen" as "fifty"). The object drops when it is recoverable — I must have misheard is complete.
Passive
restricted — natural in the as frame (the name is regularly misheard as Cohen), but the verb lives in the active perfect must have misheard, and ✗ I was misheard by him is barely used.

Hear something and get it wrong — take one word for another.

1. Hear something incorrectly. You did hear it; you simply got it wrong. Failing to hear at all is didn't hear or couldn't hear, never mishear. Often built with as. (ослышаться, неправильно расслышать)

  1. In a crowded room it is easy to mishear a name.
  2. I misheard the gate number and nearly missed the flight.
  3. She had misheard "fifteen" as "fifty."
  4. He regularly mishears song lyrics and sings them wrong for years.

2. must have misheard — take the blame for a mix-up. The verb's most common home is this polite perfect formula, where the speaker takes responsibility for a misunderstanding rather than accusing the other person of misspeaking. (должно быть, я ослышался)

  1. I must have misheard — did you say Tuesday or Thursday?
  2. If I have misheard you, please say it again.

hear, overhear

Pronunciation
/ˌaʊtˈsɛl/ · owt-SEL
Русский
продавать больше или дороже
Forms
outsell /ˌaʊtˈsɛl/ · outsold /ˌaʊtˈsoʊld/ · outsold /ˌaʊtˈsoʊld/
Verb class
irregular
Register
rare — a prefixed derivative confined to business and trade writing; in conversation an American says sold more than
Pattern

Group
4
Third person
outsells · -ing form: outselling
Contrast
Three prefixed forms of sell sit close together and mean different things: outsell is about volume (you sold more than the other guy), undersell is about price (you charged less), and oversell is about promising more than you can deliver. The object of outsell may be the rival or the rival's product: outsold Ford, outsold every other pickup.
Verb type
action — dynamic, reporting a result over a period: trucks outsell sedans in most of the country.
Transitivity
transitive — object obligatory, and it names the rival or the rival's product, never the goods you moved: outsold Ford, outsold every other pickup, ✗ outsold ten thousand trucks.
Passive
yes, though the active carries most of the traffic: sedans are outsold by trucks nearly everywhere, the hardcover was outsold ten to one.

Beat someone or something in sales — move more units than the competition.

1. Sell more than a competitor. Of a company, a store, or a salesperson. The object is the rival, not the goods: outsold Ford, outsold the whole team. (продавать больше, чем (конкурент))

  1. Their small team outsold the entire regional office last quarter.
  2. No dealership in the state can outsell them on used trucks.
  3. By December the franchise had outsold every other location.

2. Of a product: sell in greater numbers. Here both subject and object are goods, and the comparison is units moved. For price it is undersell; for hype it is oversell. (продаваться лучше, чем)

  1. Trucks outsell sedans in most of the country.
  2. The paperback will probably outsell the hardcover ten to one.
  3. That one album has outsold everything else the label ever released.
  4. Nothing on the menu outsells the breakfast burrito.

sell, undersell, oversell

Forms
overhear /ˌoʊvɚˈhɪr/ · overheard /ˌoʊvɚˈhɝd/ · overheard /ˌoʊvɚˈhɝd/
Verb class
irregular
Register
everyday — prefixed, but fully current: I overheard them talking is ordinary conversation
Pattern
ear — eard
Group
4
Third person
overhears · -ing form: overhearing
Contrast
Inflects like hear. In American usage overhear is accidental — you were there and the words reached you. Deliberate listening is eavesdrop (regular: eavesdropped), so the Russian gloss splits across two English verbs: I overheard them arguing is innocent, I was eavesdropping is not.
Verb type
action — dynamic but involuntary; deliberate listening is the separate regular verb eavesdrop. Commonly takes an -ing clause: overheard two nurses talking.
Transitivity

Passive
yes, and the passive is a journalistic staple: the senator was overheard telling an aide the deal was dead, he has been overheard complaining more than once.

Hear what was not meant for you — by accident, not by design.

1. Accidentally hear. In American usage the accident is built into the word: you were there and the words reached you. Deliberate listening is eavesdrop, which is regular — eavesdropped. (нечаянно услышать, случайно подслушать)

  1. In an open office you overhear everything.
  2. I overheard two nurses talking about the schedule.
  3. They had overheard the whole argument through the wall.
  4. She has overheard enough to guess what the meeting was about.

2. be overheard saying something. The passive is a journalistic staple: someone is caught saying in private what they would not have said publicly. (быть услышанным, попасться на словах)

  1. The senator was overheard telling an aide the deal was dead.
  2. He has been overheard complaining about the new policy more than once.

oversell#

Pronunciation
/ˌoʊvɚˈsɛl/ · oh-ver-SEL
Русский
распродавать
Forms
oversell /ˌoʊvɚˈsɛl/ · oversold /ˌoʊvɚˈsoʊld/ · oversold /ˌoʊvɚˈsoʊld/
Verb class
irregular
Register
neutral — airline and marketing vocabulary, but the flight was oversold and don't oversell it come out without effort
Pattern
ell — old
Group
4
Third person
oversells · -ing form: overselling
Contrast
Inflects like sell. Two senses in American English: selling more of a thing than actually exists (airlines oversell flights) and praising something past what it can deliver (don't oversell the feature). Selling your whole stock is a different phrase — sell out, sold out — so a concert that sold out is good news and a flight that was oversold is not.
Verb type
action — dynamic in both the capacity sense (airlines oversell popular routes) and the hype sense (don't oversell the feature). The participle oversold is also a finance adjective: the index looks oversold.
Transitivity
transitive — the venue oversold the show by two hundred seats, the company has oversold this product for years; the object is normally required.
Passive
yes, and it is the usual form when the booking lands on you: our flight had been oversold, traders had oversold the sector → the sector had been oversold by spring.

Sell more of something than exists, or claim more for it than it can deliver.

1. Sell more than you have. Airlines, hotels, and venues deliberately book past capacity. Usually passive when it lands on you. Not the same as sell out, which just means the stock is gone. (продавать больше, чем есть в наличии)

  1. Airlines routinely oversell popular routes.
  2. The venue oversold the show by two hundred seats.
  3. Our flight had been oversold, and four of us volunteered to wait.

2. Overhype, promise too much. Praising a product, an idea, or a person past what they can deliver. Often a warning to the speaker: don't oversell it. (перехваливать, преувеличивать достоинства)

  1. Try not to oversell the software in the demo.
  2. The company has oversold this product for years.
  3. Every ad oversells something.

3. oversold — a market that has fallen too far. Finance only, and only in the participle: prices have dropped further than the fundamentals justify, so traders expect a bounce. The opposite is overbought. (перепроданный (о рынке, бумаге))

  1. Traders had oversold the sector by early spring.
  2. The index looks oversold after five straight down days.
See also
sell, outsell, undersell

Forms

Register
rare — specialized to American appellate procedure; outside a courtroom nobody produces it
Pattern

Group
4
Third person
rehears · -ing form: rehearing — normally a noun in American legal English: the court granted a rehearing
Contrast
Inflects like hear, but the word is a courtroom term: a court rehears a case. For music or a recording, American English says listen to it again or play it back, never ✗ rehear it. It has nothing to do with rehearse, which is regular (rehearsed) and belongs to the theater.
