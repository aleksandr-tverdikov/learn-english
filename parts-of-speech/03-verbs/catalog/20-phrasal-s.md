# Phrasal verbs: say — swing

> Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs say, see, seek… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

[← Irregular verbs](README.md) &middot; [The grammar of verbs](../README.md)

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word, and the pair means something neither half says alone: *see through a liar* has nothing to do with eyesight, *stand for something* nothing to do with standing, *spring for dinner* nothing to do with jumping. Learn each one whole, the way you learned *dog*; taking it apart only leads you to the wrong meaning. The second thing to learn with it is **separability** — whether the object may sit between the verb and the particle. *Sit out the next dance* can also be *sit this one out*, but *sit through the lecture* can never be ✗ *sit the lecture through*, and once the object is a pronoun a separable verb **must** split: *shake it off*, never ✗ *shake off it*. Every entry below answers that question outright, because that one line is the difference between sounding native and sounding translated. A note on the range: *say*, *slide*, *spend*, *sting*, and *strive* build no true phrasal verbs in American use and so contribute nothing here, while *set* and *stand* between them account for nearly a fifth of the list.

### see about

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsiː əˌbaʊt/ &middot; *SEE-a-bout* &middot; the verb keeps the stress here and the preposition is reduced  
**Русский:** заняться (чем-л.), разобраться с; подумать насчёт  
**Base verb:** [see](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#see) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)  
**Separable:** no — the object follows the whole phrase: *see about the tickets*, *see about it*. ✗ *see the tickets about*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object — a noun, an *-ing* form, or *it*  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; attending to something is a task you perform, and it takes the progressive freely: *she's seeing about a table for us*. Sense 2 (*we'll see about that*) is a fixed formula, not a free verb.
**Passive:** **no** — the prepositional passive is not idiomatic here: ✗ *a new passport was seen about*, ✗ *the leak has been seen about*. Use [see to](#see-to) when you need a passive.

Look into something and deal with it — and, said flatly, refuse it.

**1. Attend to something, look into it.** The everyday sense: take charge of a job that still needs doing. Close to [see to](#see-to), but *see about* leans toward finding out what is involved, while *see to* is getting it finished. (заняться, разобраться с)

1. I need to *see about* a new passport before we travel.
2. She went inside to *see about* getting us a table.
3. Could you *see about* the leak in the basement?

**2. we'll see about that — a flat refusal.** With the stress on *that*, it announces that the speaker intends to stop whatever was just said. (ну это мы ещё посмотрим)

4. He says he's dropping out? We'll *see about* that.
5. You think you can park in my spot? We'll *see about* it.

**See also:** [see to](#see-to), [see through](#see-through), [set about](#set-about)

### see off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsiː ˈɔf/ &middot; *see-OFF* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** проводить (на вокзал, в аэропорт); отбить, дать отпор  
**Base verb:** [see](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#see) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *see off the visitors* or *see the visitors off*, and with a pronoun it must split: *see them off*, never ✗ *see off them*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses: going to the gate to wave, and driving a rival away.
**Passive:** **yes** in both senses — *the recruits were seen off by the whole town*; *the challengers were seen off in straight sets*.

Go with someone to the airport, station, or door to say goodbye — or drive an opponent away.

**1. Say goodbye at the point of departure.** You travel part of the way with the person purely in order to wave. (провожать)

1. We got up at four to *see* her *off* at the airport.
2. The whole town came to *see off* the recruits.
3. Don't bother *seeing* me *off* — I'll grab a cab.

**2. Drive away, fend off.** Of a rival, an intruder, or a challenge; very common in sports writing. (отбить, дать отпор)

4. The dog *saw off* two coyotes before dawn.
5. They *saw* the challengers *off* in straight sets.
6. He *saw* them *off* with nothing but a flashlight and a lot of yelling.

**See also:** [see out](#see-out), [send off](#send-off), [show out](#show-out)

### see out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsiː ˈaʊt/ &middot; *see-OUT* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** доработать до конца, продержаться до конца; проводить до двери  
**Base verb:** [see](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#see) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *see out the year* or *see the year out*; with a pronoun it must split: *see it out*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; sense 1 is durative (you keep going until the clock runs out), sense 2 a single act of escorting.
**Passive:** **restricted** — natural in sense 2, *the visitors were seen out through the side entrance*; strained in sense 1, ✗ *her contract was seen out*.

Stay with something until it ends, or walk a guest to the door.

**1. Last until the end of a period.** Of a contract, a term, a season, a winter — you stay in place until the clock runs out. (продержаться до конца, доработать)

1. She plans to *see out* her contract and then move to Denver.
2. He *saw* the winter *out* in a cabin with no phone.
3. It's a bad season, but we'll *see* it *out*.

**2. Escort someone to the door.** A shade politer than [show out](#show-out); the reflexive *see myself out* is the standard way to leave without being walked. (проводить до двери)

4. The assistant *saw* the visitors *out* through the side entrance.
5. Please don't get up — I'll *see* myself *out*.

**See also:** [see off](#see-off), [see through](#see-through), [show out](#show-out), [sit out](#sit-out)

### see through

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsiː ˈθruː/ &middot; *see-THROUGH* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** довести до конца; поддержать, помочь продержаться; видеть насквозь  
**Base verb:** [see](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#see) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** depends on the sense, and this is the entry to get right. *Finish it* and *sustain someone* separate, and with a pronoun they **must**: *see it through*, *her humor saw her through*. *Not be fooled by* never separates: *I can see through him* — ✗ *I can see him through*, which would mean something else entirely.  
**Transitive:** yes in every sense  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in senses 1 and 2 (finishing a job, carrying someone along); sense 3 is a **stative** verb of perception and resists the progressive: ✗ *I'm seeing through him* — you say *I can see through him*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — good in sense 1, *the reforms were seen through over four years*, and with a thing in sense 3, *the excuse was seen through in about a second*. Sense 2 has none: ✗ *she was seen through the divorce by her humor*.

Carry something to the end, carry someone through a hard stretch, or refuse to be fooled.

**1. Carry something to the end.** The object can go on either side, but *see it through* is the fixed shape most Americans reach for. (довести до конца)

1. Whatever happens, I intend to *see* this project *through*.
2. She *saw* the reforms *through* over four long years.
3. It's a lousy job, but I'll *see* it *through*.

**2. Sustain someone through difficulty.** The subject is whatever keeps the person going, and the person always sits between the verb and the particle. (поддержать, помочь продержаться)

4. Her sense of humor *saw* her *through* the divorce.
5. Two crates of instant noodles *saw* us *through* the semester.

**3. Not be fooled by someone.** Inseparable, and always about a person, a lie, or a performance. (видеть насквозь, раскусить)

6. His mother *saw through* the excuse in about a second.
7. Voters *see right through* that kind of promise.

**See also:** [see out](#see-out), [see off](#see-off), [stick with](#stick-with), [stand by](#stand-by)

### see to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsiː tuː/ &middot; *SEE-to* &middot; the verb keeps the stress here and the preposition is reduced  
**Русский:** позаботиться о, заняться; проследить, чтобы  
**Base verb:** [see](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#see) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)  
**Separable:** no — the object follows the preposition: *see to the details*, *see to it*. ✗ *see the details to*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; handling something to completion, and comfortable in the progressive: *who's seeing to the food?*
**Passive:** **yes** — one of the prepositional verbs that passivizes cleanly: *his hand was seen to before anyone asked a question*, *has the paperwork been seen to?* The frame *see to it that* has no passive.

Take care of something so that it actually gets done.

**1. Take care of something.** Where [see about](#see-about) is looking into it, *see to* is handling it. (позаботиться о, заняться)

1. I'll *see to* the paperwork if you handle the movers.
2. The nurse *saw to* his hand before anyone asked a question.
3. Who's *seeing to* the food?

**2. see to it that — make sure.** A fixed frame with a clause after it. In American English the *it* is not optional: ✗ *see that to*. (проследить, чтобы)

4. *See to it* that the doors are locked by ten.
5. He *saw to it* that everyone else got paid first.

**See also:** [see about](#see-about), [set about](#set-about), [stand by](#stand-by)

### seek out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsiːk ˈaʊt/ &middot; *seek-OUT* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** разыскать, отыскать, специально найти  
**Base verb:** [seek](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#seek) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *seek out new clients* or *seek new clients out*; with a pronoun it must split: *seek them out*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** formal — a written, deliberate word; in speech Americans usually say *go find* or *track down*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the deliberate effort is the whole meaning, and it takes the progressive: *she's seeking out the hardest route*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and common in writing — *the families were sought out before the names were released*.

Deliberately look for someone or something until you get to them — the effort is the whole point.

1. Reporters *sought out* the families before the names were released.
2. She *seeks out* the hardest route on every climb.
3. If you're new here, *seek* the old-timers *out* — they know where everything is.
4. He has always *sought out* work that scares him a little.
5. Good editors don't wait for talent; they *seek* it *out*.

**See also:** [see about](#see-about), [shake down](#shake-down), [stand out](#stand-out)

### sell off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛl ˈɔf/ &middot; *sell-OFF* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** распродать, сбыть (часто по дешёвке)  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *sell off the assets* or *sell the assets off*; with a pronoun it must split: *sell them off*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a disposal carried out over a stretch of time: *the chain is selling off its stores*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *everything that wasn't profitable was sold off after the merger*.

Get rid of property or stock by selling it, usually fast and often cheap.

1. The chain is *selling off* its stores in the Midwest.
2. They *sold* the furniture *off* piece by piece to make rent.
3. After the merger the new owners *sold off* everything that wasn't profitable.
4. He kept the guitars and *sold* the rest *off*.
5. The fund had to *sell* them *off* at a loss.

**See also:** [sell out](#sell-out), [sell up](#sell-up), [sell out of](#sell-out-of)

### sell on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛl ɑn/ &middot; *SELL-on* &middot; the verb keeps the stress and *on* is reduced to /ən/ — *SOLD me on it*  
**Русский:** убедить (кого-л. в чём-л.), склонить к  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)  
**Separable:** the person you are persuading always sits between the verb and *on*, and what you are persuading them of follows it: *sell the board on the plan*, *sell me on it*. ✗ *sell on me the plan*.  
**Transitive:** yes — two objects: *sell someone on something*  
**Register:** everyday — American, and everywhere in business talk
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; persuasion is something you do to someone, and it takes the progressive: *he's still selling us on the neighborhood*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the participle is the commonest shape of all — *I was never really sold on the idea*.

Persuade someone to accept an idea, a plan, or a person.

1. It took a month to *sell* the board *on* the new schedule.
2. You don't have to *sell* me *on* her — I've seen her work.
3. He *sold* us *on* the neighborhood by walking us through it at night.
4. I was never really *sold on* the idea.
5. *Sell* them *on* the price first; the features can wait.

**See also:** [sell short](#sell-short), [sell out](#sell-out), [speak up for](#speak-up-for)

### sell out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛl ˈaʊt/ &middot; *sell-OUT* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** быть распроданным; предать; продаться  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes in the betrayal sense — *sell out your friends* or *sell your friends out*, and with a pronoun it must split: *sell them out*. In the 'nothing left' sense there is no object to place at all: *the show sold out*.  
**Transitive:** either — no object when tickets or stock run out, an object when you betray someone  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses; in sense 1 the participle has drifted toward an adjective of state, *the show is sold out* describing the condition rather than the event.
**Passive:** **restricted** — sense 2 passivizes freely: *we were sold out by our own crew*. Senses 1 and 3 take no object and so have no passive; *the show sold out* is active despite the look of *sold out*.

Have your whole stock bought up — or betray people, or your own principles, for money.

**1. Be bought up completely.** Of tickets, stock, or a show; usually with no object, and very often in the passive-looking *sold out*. (быть распроданным)

1. The first two shows *sold out* in an hour.
2. Get there early; that place *sells out* by noon.

**2. Betray someone for your own gain.** Separable, and the pronoun splits it: *sold us out*. (предать, сдать)

3. He *sold out* his own crew for a lighter sentence.
4. She'd *sell* her own brother *out* to close a deal.
5. They *sold* us *out* the minute the money showed up.

**3. Abandon your principles for money.** Disapproving, no object, and used constantly about bands, artists, and startups. (продаться)

6. Fans said the band *sold out* when the song turned up in a car ad.
7. You can call it *selling out*; I call it paying the mortgage.

**See also:** [sell out of](#sell-out-of), [sell off](#sell-off), [sell short](#sell-short)

### sell out of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛl ˈaʊt əv/ &middot; *sell-OUT-of* &middot; the middle particle takes the stress; the last word is reduced  
**Русский:** распродать все запасы (чего-л.), остаться без  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb  
**Separable:** no — three-part verbs never split: *we sold out of tickets*, *we sold out of them*. ✗ *sold tickets out of*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a change of state reached through selling, usually reported as a completed fact: *they sold out of the blue ones by ten*.
**Passive:** **no** — the noun after *of* cannot be promoted: ✗ *croissants are sold out of by nine*. Say *the croissants sold out* instead.

Have none of something left because it has all been sold.

1. The bakery *sells out of* croissants by nine every Saturday.
2. We *sold out of* the blue ones in the first hour.
3. They had two hundred shirts and *sold out of* them before noon.
4. Order now — the store *sold out of* the first printing in a day.
5. Gas stations along the coast *sold out of* bottled water before the storm hit.

**See also:** [sell out](#sell-out), [sell off](#sell-off)

### sell short

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛl ˈʃɔrt/ &middot; *sell-SHORT* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** недооценивать; играть на понижение (бирж.)  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes, and in the everyday sense it *must* split — whoever is being undervalued sits in the middle: *don't sell yourself short*, *don't sell it short*. ✗ *don't sell short yourself*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses, though sense 1 usually reports a standing judgment: *people sell that town short until they visit*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *she was sold short by everyone who interviewed her*; in finance, *the airline was sold short a week before the news broke*.

Treat someone or something as worth less than they are — or, in finance, bet that a price will fall.

**1. Undervalue someone or something.** Almost always negative, in the shape *don't sell X short*. (недооценивать)

1. Don't *sell* yourself *short* — you were the best candidate in the room.
2. People *sell* that little town *short* until they visit.
3. I *sold* him *short*, and he proved me wrong inside a week.

**2. Bet that a stock will fall.** Finance: borrow the shares, sell them now, buy them back cheaper later. (играть на понижение, шортить)

4. Two funds *sold* the airline *short* a week before the news broke.
5. He made his name *selling* banks *short* in 2008.

**See also:** [sell out](#sell-out), [sell on](#sell-on), [stick up for](#stick-up-for)

### sell up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛl ˈʌp/ &middot; *sell-UP* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** продать всё (дом, дело) и уехать  
**Base verb:** [sell](04-past-equals-participle-plus-d.md#sell) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** nothing to separate — it takes no object: *they sold up and left*.  
**Transitive:** no — intransitive  
**Register:** British — an American in the same situation says *sold the house* or *sold the business*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a single decisive act, and intransitive: *after the second bad year he sold up*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, so there is nothing to promote: ✗ *the business was sold up*.

Sell your home or your business, usually in order to leave or retire.

1. They *sold up* and moved to Portugal.
2. After the second bad year he *sold up*.
3. Her parents *sold up* in Leeds and never looked back.
4. If the rent goes up again, we'll *sell up*.
5. Half the shops on the high street have *sold up* since the factory closed.

**See also:** [sell off](#sell-off), [sell out](#sell-out)

### send away

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛnd əˈweɪ/ &middot; *send-a-WAY* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** прогнать, отослать; отправить (учиться, лечиться)  
**Base verb:** [send](06-d-becomes-t.md#send) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *send away the applicants* or *send the applicants away*; with a pronoun it must split: *send them away*, never ✗ *send away them*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses; sense 2 is durative in effect (the person stays away for a stretch) but the verb itself names the act of dispatching.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the reporters at the gate were sent away*; *he was sent away to boarding school at eleven*.

Make someone leave, or dispatch them somewhere far off — school, camp, treatment.

**1. Make someone leave.** Often curt: the person wanted something and did not get it. (прогнать, отослать)

1. She *sent* the kids *away* so the adults could talk.
2. He knocked twice and they *sent* him *away*.
3. They *sent away* the reporters who had gathered at the gate.

**2. Dispatch someone to a distant place.** Of boarding school, basic training, a rehab program — the person is put somewhere else for a stretch of time. (отправить, отослать)

4. Their parents *sent* them *away* to boarding school at eleven.
5. They *sent* her *away* to her aunt's for the summer.
6. The company *sent* him *away* for six weeks of training.

**See also:** [send away for](#send-away-for), [send back](#send-back), [send off](#send-off), [send out](#send-out)

### send away for

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛnd əˈweɪ fər/ &middot; *send-a-WAY-for* &middot; the middle particle takes the stress; the last word is reduced  
**Русский:** выписать по почте, заказать (с доставкой)  
**Base verb:** [send](06-d-becomes-t.md#send) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb  
**Separable:** no — three-part verbs never split: *send away for a catalog*, *send away for it*. ✗ *send a catalog away for*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object  
**Register:** everyday — slightly dated now that everything is ordered online
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; ordering by mail and then waiting, so it sits happily in the past: *I sent away for a catalog and forgot about it*.
**Passive:** **no** — the three-part string blocks the prepositional passive: ✗ *a catalog was sent away for*, ✗ *the transcripts were sent away for in September*.

Order something by mail and wait for it to come.

1. I *sent away for* a catalog and forgot about it.
2. As a kid he *sent away for* every free sample he saw.
3. You had to *send away for* the decoder ring and wait six weeks.
4. She *sent away for* the transcripts in September.
5. We *sent away for* it in March and it turned up in June.

Return something to where it came from, usually because it is wrong.

### send down

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛnd ˈdaʊn/ &middot; *send-DOWN* &middot; the particle takes the stress, and that is what welds the two words into one  
**Русский:** отправить в низшую лигу; отчислить из университета; упрятать за решётку  
**Base verb:** [send](06-d-becomes-t.md#send) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + adverb particle)  
**Separable:** yes — *send him down*, *send down two pitchers*; with a pronoun it must split: *sent him down*.  
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object, and it is very often passive: *he was sent down*  
**Register:** everyday in the baseball sense (American); British in the university and prison senses
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses — a demotion, an expulsion, a sentence.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is the usual shape — *he was sent down to Triple-A in June*, *he was sent down from Oxford in his second year*, *he got sent down for fraud*.

Move a player down to a lower league — or, in British use, expel a student or jail a criminal.

**1. Demote a player to the minors.** Standard American baseball vocabulary. (отправить в низшую лигу)

1. The Mets *sent* him *down* to Triple-A in June.
2. They *sent* two pitchers *down* after the road trip.
