# Phrasal verbs: deal — freeze

> Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs deal, dig, do… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

[← Irregular verbs](README.md) &middot; [The grammar of verbs](../README.md)

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle that together mean something the two parts do not: *dig up* a rumor has nothing to do with soil, and a deal that *falls through* never falls anywhere (значение целого не выводится из частей). The second thing to learn about each one is **separability** — whether the object can sit between the verb and the particle (*dig the file out*, *dig it out*) or has to follow the whole phrase (*fall for it*, never ✗ *fall it for*). Word order and stress go together here: a true particle takes the stress and can move, while a preposition does neither, which is why *dig it OUT* is a phrasal verb and *DIG into it* is not. The entries below are alphabetical by the whole phrase, not by the base verb.

### deal in

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdil ɪn/ &middot; *DEAL-in* &middot; in the trade sense *in* is a preposition and *deal* keeps the stress; in the card sense it is a true particle and takes the stress — *deal me IN*
**Русский:** торговать (чем-л.); включить в игру, взять в долю
**Base verb:** [deal](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#deal) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition) in the trade sense; phrasal verb (verb + particle) in the card sense
**Separable:** it depends on the sense. Trading: no — *the gallery deals in prints*, ✗ *deals prints in*, ✗ *deals it in*. Cards: yes, and the object always separates — *deal me in*, *deal the new guy in*, never ✗ *deal in me*.
**Transitive:** yes — a commodity in the trade sense, a person in the card sense
**Register:** everyday — plain business talk, and *deal me in* is plain speech
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses: the habitual trade sense (*the gallery deals in prints*) and the one-time card sense (*deal me in*). No linking or stative use.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the card sense passivizes: *I was dealt in on the second hand*. The trade sense barely does: ✗ *nineteenth-century prints are dealt in by the gallery*.

To buy and sell a particular kind of goods, or to include someone in a card game or a scheme.

**1. Trade in, buy and sell.** Of a business and the goods it handles, and figuratively of what a person or a paper traffics in. *In* here is a preposition, so nothing can come between the two words. (торговать чем-либо)

1. The gallery *deals in* nineteenth-century prints.
2. His family *dealt in* grain for a century.
3. That website *deals in* rumor more than news.

**2. Include someone in the game.** From the card table, and extended to any scheme with money in it. Here *in* is a particle and the person always sits in front of it. (включить в игру, взять в долю)

4. *Deal* me *in* — I've got twenty bucks.
5. She *dealt* the new guy *in* on the second hand.
6. If there's money in it, *deal* us *in*.

**See also:** [deal out](#deal-out), [deal with](#deal-with), [do out of](#do-out-of)

### deal out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdil ˈaʊt/ &middot; *deel-OWT* &middot; the particle takes the stress, which is what makes the two words sound like one verb
**Русский:** раздавать, распределять; вершить (наказание)
**Base verb:** [deal](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#deal) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *deal out the cards* / *deal the cards out*, and with a pronoun it must separate: *deal them out*, never ✗ *deal out them*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday — the card sense is plain speech; the punishment sense sounds slightly formal
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout: handing out cards or portions, and administering a penalty. Progressive is normal — *he's dealing out the cards*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the estate was dealt out among four cousins*; *the same sentence is dealt out for a first offense*.

To hand something out portion by portion, especially cards, or to hand out punishment.

**1. Distribute in portions.** Cards first of all, and by extension shares, supplies, or anything divided among people. (раздавать, распределять)

1. He *dealt out* five cards to each player.
2. *Deal* them *out* and let's play.
3. The estate was *dealt out* among four cousins.

**2. Administer punishment or justice.** The object is a sentence, a penalty, or criticism. Slightly formal, and common in court reporting. (вершить, назначать наказание)

4. Judges here *deal out* the same sentence for a first offense.
5. The coach *deals* criticism *out* freely and takes none.
6. Whatever the league *deals out*, they'll appeal it.

**See also:** [deal in](#deal-in), [deal with](#deal-with), [drive home](#drive-home)

### deal with

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdil wɪθ/ &middot; *DEEL-with* &middot; *with* is a preposition, so *deal* keeps the stress; it never becomes ✗ *deel-WITH*
**Русский:** иметь дело с, разбираться с; быть посвящённым (теме)
**Base verb:** [deal](05-past-equals-participle-plus-t.md#deal) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — the object always follows *with*. ✗ *deal the complaint with*, ✗ *deal it with*. Only the passive moves the object out front: *the complaint was dealt with*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the handling and business senses (*I'm dealing with vendor complaints*), but **stative** in the be-about sense: ✗ *chapter four is dealing with the war years*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — full prepositional passive in the handling and business senses: *the complaint has already been dealt with*. The be-about sense resists it: ✗ *the war years are dealt with by chapter four*.

To handle a problem or a person, to do business with someone, or — of a text — to be about a subject.

**1. Handle, cope with.** The core sense. The object is a problem, a complaint, or a difficult person. (справляться с, разбираться с)

1. I *deal with* vendor complaints all morning.
2. He *dealt with* the same landlord for six years.
3. I can't *deal with* her today.

**2. Do business with.** Of the companies and people you transact with regularly. (иметь дело с, сотрудничать)

4. We only *deal with* suppliers who deliver on time.
5. The claim *has* already *been dealt with* by their office.

**3. Be about (of a text).** Of books, chapters, reports, and laws: what the text covers. (быть посвящённым, рассматривать)

6. Chapter four *deals with* the war years.
7. The report *dealt with* only one of the three sites.

**See also:** [deal in](#deal-in), [do away with](#do-away-with), [put up with](19-phrasal-m-r.md#put-up-with)

### dig in

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪɡ ˈɪn/ &middot; *dig-IN* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *dig IN*, not *DIG in*
**Русский:** приниматься за еду; окапываться; упереться, стоять на своём
**Base verb:** [dig](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#dig) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** usually there is nothing to separate, because the verb takes no object. The one object it does take is *heels*, in the idiom, and there both orders work — *dig in your heels* / *dig your heels in*.
**Transitive:** usually no; the one object it takes is *heels*, in the idiom
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses: starting to eat, entrenching, and the *dig in one's heels* idiom. Progressive is normal — *management has been digging in for weeks*.
**Passive:** **no** — the eating and entrenching senses take no object at all, and the one object it does take resists the passive: ✗ *her heels were dug in*.

To start eating, to entrench yourself, or to refuse to move from a position.

**1. Start eating.** Nearly always the bare imperative, said over a table of food. (приниматься за еду, налетать)

1. Everybody grab a plate and *dig in*.
2. The kids *dug in* before I sat down.

**2. Entrench, take a defensive position.** Military first, then figurative: a side that will not move. (окапываться; занять жёсткую позицию)

3. The battalion *dug in* along the ridge and waited.
4. Management *has dug in* and won't reopen the contract.

**3. Dig in one's heels.** A fixed idiom for refusing to budge. The object is always *heels*, and it can sit on either side of the particle. (упереться, стоять на своём)

5. She *dug* her heels *in* and refused to sign.
6. Once he *digs in* his heels, no argument moves him.

**See also:** [dig into](#dig-into), [dig out](#dig-out), [eat up](#eat-up)

### dig into

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪɡ ˌɪntu/ &middot; *DIG-in-too* &middot; *into* is a preposition, so *dig* keeps the stress; compare the true particle in *dig UP*
**Русский:** копаться в, вникать в; налечь на (еду); залезть в (сбережения)
**Base verb:** [dig](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#dig) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — *dig into the records*, ✗ *dig the records into*, ✗ *dig it into*. Pronouns follow the preposition: *dig into it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: investigating, tucking into food, drawing on savings. Progressive is common — *she has been digging into the family's history*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the prepositional passive is marginal even in the investigate sense: ?*his campaign filings were dug into for weeks*. None at all in the food and savings senses: ✗ *savings were dug into*.

To investigate something thoroughly, to start eating something with appetite, or to draw on a reserve.

**1. Investigate.** The reporter's and the engineer's word for looking past the surface of a record, a dataset, or a story. (копаться в, вникать в)

1. Reporters *dug into* his campaign filings.
2. I *dug into* the logs and found the bug in an hour.
3. She *has been digging into* the family's history for years.

**2. Start eating with appetite.** Of a plate of food in front of you. (налечь на, приниматься за)

4. He *dug into* a plate of ribs without a word.

**3. Draw on a reserve.** Of savings, a fund, or a margin you were trying not to touch. (залезть в, потратить часть)

5. We had to *dig into* savings to cover the repair.

**See also:** [dig up](#dig-up), [dig out](#dig-out), [eat into](#eat-into)

### dig out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪɡ ˈaʊt/ &middot; *dig-OWT* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *dig it OUT*
**Русский:** откопать, разыскать; выкопать, откапывать из-под снега
**Base verb:** [dig](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#dig) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *dig out the old photos* / *dig the old photos out*, and with a pronoun it must separate: *dig them out*, never ✗ *dig out them*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses: retrieving something stored away, and freeing something buried.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the warranty was dug out and emailed over*; *two cars were dug out of the drift*.

To find something that was stored away and forgotten, or to free something buried.

**1. Retrieve something put away.** Of papers, clothes, files — anything you have to go looking for. (откопать, разыскать)

1. I'll *dig out* the warranty and email it to you.
2. She *dug* her old resume *out* of a drawer.
3. Can you *dig* them *out* before the meeting?

**2. Free from snow or rubble.** Literal, and the standard word after a blizzard. (откапывать, расчищать)

4. Crews *dug* two cars *out* of the drift.
5. It took the town a week to *dig* itself *out*.

**See also:** [dig up](#dig-up), [dig into](#dig-into), [find out](#find-out)

### dig up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪɡ ˈʌp/ &middot; *dig-UP* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *dig it UP*
**Русский:** раскопать, разузнать (сведения); выкопать
**Base verb:** [dig](03-past-equals-participle-vowel.md#dig) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *dig up dirt on him* / *dig the dirt up*, and with a pronoun it must separate: *dig it up*, never ✗ *dig up it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: unearthing facts, producing something with effort, or turning over ground.
**Passive:** **yes** — *an old lawsuit was dug up by opposition researchers*; *the whole yard was dug up looking for the leak*.

To uncover information someone would rather keep buried, or to remove something from the ground.

**1. Unearth facts.** Of the damaging kind, usually. This is the verb behind *opposition research*. (раскопать, разузнать)

1. Opposition researchers *dug up* an old lawsuit.
2. The paper *dug* the whole story *up* two weeks before the vote.
3. If there's dirt, they'll *dig* it *up*.

**2. Produce something with effort.** Of a witness, a document, a spare part — something found only by looking hard. (раздобыть, откопать)

4. The defense *dug up* a witness nobody had talked to.

**3. Remove from the ground.** The literal sense, of yards, roads, and plants. (выкопать, перекопать)

5. We *dug up* the yard looking for the leak.

**See also:** [dig out](#dig-out), [dig into](#dig-into), [find out](#find-out)

### do away with

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu əˈweɪ wɪθ/ &middot; *doo-uh-WAY-with* &middot; the stress falls on *away*, not on *do* and not on *with*
**Русский:** покончить с, отменить; прикончить
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb
**Separable:** no — ordinary three-part verbs never split. *do away with the fee*, ✗ *do the fee away with*, ✗ *do it away with*. Pronouns go at the end: *do away with it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both the abolish sense and the dated kill sense. Progressive is fine — *they're doing away with paper permits*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the entry shows it — *the rule has been done away with twice and brought back twice*. The particle chain stays intact after the verb.

To abolish something, or — older and jokier — to kill someone.

**1. Abolish, get rid of.** Of rules, fees, offices, and practices. Slightly more formal than *get rid of*, and the standard verb in news writing. (отменить, упразднить)

1. The city *did away with* paper permits last year.
2. They want to *do away with* the whole department.
3. Nobody's going to *do away with* it before the election.
4. The rule *has been done away with* twice and brought back twice.

**2. Kill.** Dated and euphemistic, and now mostly a joke or a detective-story line. (прикончить, устранить)

5. In the last chapter somebody *does away with* the butler.

**See also:** [do without](#do-without), [do in](#do-in), [forget about](#forget-about)

### do for

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdu fɔr/ &middot; *DOO-for* &middot; *for* is a preposition, so *do* keeps the stress
**Русский:** доконать, погубить
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — *that hill did for me*, ✗ *did me for*. The object always follows the preposition.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** British — the active verb is British and dated; American English keeps only the participle, *done for*, meaning finished or doomed
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the British active sense (*that hill did for me*); the American survival, *be done for*, is a fixed participle used adjectivally, i.e. stative.
**Passive:** **restricted** — no true passive of the active verb (✗ *the pipes were done for by two winters*). Only the adjectival participle lives on: *if the bank calls the loan, we're done for*.

To exhaust, ruin, or finish someone or something off.

**1. Wear out or destroy.** British and old-fashioned in the active. (доконать, погубить)

1. That last hill just about *did for* me.
2. Two winters in that apartment *did for* the pipes.
3. Another season like this one would *do for* the team.

**2. Be done for.** The fixed participle, and the only form Americans use: finished, doomed, with no hope left. (пропал, конец)

4. If the bank calls the loan, we're *done for*.
5. He knew from her face that he was *done for*.

**See also:** [do in](#do-in), [do over](#do-over), [wear out](21-phrasal-t-w.md#wear-out)

### do in

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu ˈɪn/ &middot; *doo-IN* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *it did me IN*
**Русский:** вымотать, доконать; прикончить
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes, and with a pronoun it must separate: *that hike did me in*, never ✗ *did in me*. With a full noun both orders work: *the flu did in half the office* / *did half the office in*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** informal
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in both senses, exhausting someone and killing someone. The participle *done in* also serves as an adjective: *I'm done in*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the witness was done in before the trial*; *half the crew was done in by the heat*.

To exhaust someone completely, or to kill someone.

**1. Wear out, exhaust.** Most often with a pronoun object, or in the participle *done in*. (вымотать, доконать)

1. Three flights of stairs *did* me *in*.
2. The heat *did in* half the crew by noon.
3. I'm *done in* — I'm going to bed.

**2. Kill.** Crime-novel informal, never used in a report or a news story. (прикончить, убрать)

4. Somebody *did* the witness *in* before the trial.
5. He swore the cold weather would *do* him *in* eventually.

**See also:** [do for](#do-for), [do up](#do-up), [wear out](21-phrasal-t-w.md#wear-out)

### do out of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu ˈaʊt əv/ &middot; *doo-OWT-uv* &middot; the stress falls on *out*, the particle, not on *do*
**Русский:** обманом лишить, надуть на
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** three-part phrasal verb
**Separable:** yes, and it must be — this is the one three-part verb on the page that splits. The person cheated always sits between *do* and *out of*, and the thing lost follows: *they did her out of her commission*, *they did me out of it*. ✗ *do out of her the commission*, ✗ *do out of her her commission*.
**Transitive:** yes, two objects — the person cheated and the thing lost
**Register:** informal
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: a single act of cheating someone. Progressive is fine — *they're doing her out of her share*.
**Passive:** **yes**, with the person cheated as subject — *she was done out of her share of the house*. The thing lost cannot front: ✗ *her commission was done her out of*.

To cheat someone out of something they were owed.

1. The contractor *did* them *out of* a month's pay.
2. She says her brother *did* her *out of* her share of the house.
3. That parking ticket *did* me *out of* a night at the movies.
4. They'd *done* the old man *out of* his savings years before.
5. Don't let a technicality *do* you *out of* the refund.

**See also:** [do in](#do-in), [do over](#do-over), [find out](#find-out)

### do over

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *doo-OH-ver* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *do it OVER*
**Русский:** переделать заново; отделать (комнату) заново
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *do over the whole kitchen* / *do the whole kitchen over*, and with a pronoun it must separate: *do it over*, never ✗ *do over it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday — *do it over* is the standard American way to say redo; British *do over*, meaning burgle or beat up, is a separate informal verb
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in all three senses: redoing a task, redecorating a room, and British burgling or beating up.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the essay had to be done over*; *the kitchen was done over in the spring*; *their flat got done over while they were away*.

To do something again from the start, or to redecorate a room.

**1. Redo from the start.** The everyday American sense, and the source of the schoolyard noun *a do-over*. (переделать заново)

1. The whole page was wrong, so I *did* it *over*.
2. She made him *do* the essay *over*.
3. If I could *do* this year *over*, I would.

**2. Redecorate.** Of a room or a house given a new look. (отделать заново, переделать интерьер)

4. They *did over* the kitchen in the spring.

**3. Burgle or beat up.** British and informal, and a false friend for Americans. (обчистить; отделать, избить)

5. Their flat *got done over* while they were away.

**See also:** [do up](#do-up), [do in](#do-in), [fit up](#fit-up)

### do up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu ˈʌp/ &middot; *doo-UP* &middot; the particle takes the stress — *do it UP*
**Русский:** застегнуть, завязать; упаковать; отремонтировать
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** phrasal verb (verb + particle)
**Separable:** yes — *do up your coat* / *do your coat up*, and with a pronoun it must separate: *do it up*, never ✗ *do up it*.
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday — the fastening sense is commoner in British English; Americans more often say *button up* or *zip up*
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: fastening, wrapping, renovating. The participle *done up* doubles as an adjective — *she showed up done up like it was a wedding*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the package was done up in brown paper*; *the house was done up over two summers*.

To fasten something, to wrap it, or to fix a place up.

**1. Fasten.** Of buttons, zippers, laces, and ties. The opposite is *undo*. (застегнуть, завязать)

1. *Do up* your coat, it's freezing.
2. He couldn't *do* his tie *up* straight.
3. The laces are loose — *do* them *up*.

**2. Wrap or package.** Of parcels and presents. (упаковать, завернуть)

4. She *did* the package *up* in brown paper.

**3. Renovate, or dress up.** Of a run-down house, and of a person turned out for an occasion — *all done up*. (отремонтировать; принарядиться)

5. They bought a wreck and *did* it *up* over two summers.
6. She showed up *done up* like it was a wedding.

**See also:** [do over](#do-over), [fit out](#fit-out), [draw up](#draw-up)

### do with

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdu wɪθ/ &middot; *DOO-with* &middot; *with* is a preposition, so *do* keeps the stress
**Русский:** пригодиться, не помешало бы; иметь отношение к
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — *I could do with a break*, ✗ *do a break with*, ✗ *do it with*. Pronouns follow the preposition: *what did you do with it?*
**Transitive:** yes, always takes an object
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** **stative** in both fixed frames — *could do with* is a want or need and *have to do with* is a relation: ✗ *I am doing with a coffee*, ✗ *the delay is having nothing to do with the weather*. Only *what to do with* is dynamic.
**Passive:** **no** — both frames are fixed and take no passive: ✗ *a cup of coffee could be done with*, ✗ *the weather is had nothing to do with*.

To need or want something, in *could do with*, or to be connected with something, in *have to do with*.

**1. Could do with.** A mild way of saying you need something. Common in both American and British speech, though the British use it more. (пригодиться, не помешало бы)

1. I could *do with* a cup of coffee.
2. This room could *do with* a coat of paint.

**2. Have to do with.** Be connected to, be about. Almost always in this fixed frame. (иметь отношение к)

3. The delay *has* nothing *to do with* the weather.
4. What does any of this *have to do with* me?

**3. What to do with.** How to handle a thing, a problem, or yourself. (как поступить с)

5. She didn't know what to *do with* herself all afternoon.

**See also:** [do without](#do-without), [deal with](#deal-with), [feel like](#feel-like)

### do without

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu wɪˈðaʊt/ &middot; *doo-with-OWT* &middot; the stress lands on the second syllable of *without*; *do* is unstressed
**Русский:** обходиться без
**Base verb:** [do](12-participle-plus-n-vowel-change.md#do) &middot; **Type:** prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
**Separable:** no — *do without a car*, ✗ *do a car without*, ✗ *do it without*. It can also stand with no object at all: *we'll just have to do without*.
**Transitive:** either — with an object, or with none at all
**Register:** everyday
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, an ongoing managing rather than a state: *we're doing without a car this year* is fine. Works with an object or with none at all.
**Passive:** **no** — ✗ *a car was done without for two years*. The object stays after the preposition, and the objectless use has nothing to passivize.

To manage without something you would normally have.

1. We *did without* a car for two years.
