Final -d becomes -t#

The past and participle are identical and differ from the base only in the final consonant: -d becomes -t.

← The grammar of verbs · All groups

Every verb here builds its past and its past participle the same way, and they come out identical to each other: take the base form, knock the final -d off, and put a -t in its place. Send becomes sent, build becomes built, bend becomes bent — one form does the work of both the simple past (I sent it yesterday) and the participle (I have sent it). Most of the group ends in -nd → -nt; build and gild end in -ld → -lt, and gird in -rd → -rt. In the original Russian spreadsheet this is (смена окончания "d" на "t"). Four of these verbs — blend, gild, gird, and especially wend — have quietly gone regular in modern American English, and each entry says so where it matters. Rend has not gone regular; it has simply gone literary, and its past is still rent.

bend#

Pronunciation
/bɛnd/ · BEND
Русский
гнуть; наклоняться
Forms
bend /bɛnd/ · bent /bɛnt/ · bent /bɛnt/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt
Group
6
Third person
bends · -ing form: bending (no spelling change)
Contrast
Bent does double duty as an everyday adjective: a bent nail, bent out of shape, bent on winning. Same spelling, same sound, different job in the sentence. There is no ✗ bended — the only place that survives is the fixed phrase on bended knee.
Verb type
action — dynamic throughout, whether you curve the wire, lean over, or stretch a rule. No linking use; be bent on winning is the participle as a predicate adjective, not a copular bend.
Transitivity
both — transitive when you do the curving (bend the wire, bend the rules, bend the department to her will), intransitive when you or the road do the bending (she bends down, the road bends, he would not bend to pressure).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the coat hanger was bent into a hook, the curfew was bent for the seniors. No passive of the intransitive uses: ✗ down was bent by her, ✗ the road is bent past the mill (in that reading).

To make something curve or lean the body over — and, figuratively, to relax a rule, to yield, or to force someone else to yield.

1. Curve something, make it crooked. The core physical sense: force something straight into a curve or an angle. The participle bent doubles as an everyday adjective — a bent nail. (гнуть, сгибать, изгибать)

  1. Bend the wire into a circle before you solder it.
  2. He bent the coat hanger into a hook and fished the keys out.
  3. The ice storm has bent every sapling on the ridge.

2. Bend down, bend over (lean the body). Intransitive, about your own body, usually with down, over, or forward. (наклоняться, нагибаться)

  1. She bends down to tie her shoes before every run.
  2. I bent over to pick up the coin and my back locked up.
  3. Bend your knees when you lift the box.

3. Follow a curved course. Intransitive, of a road, a river, a coastline, or light. Nothing is doing the bending — the thing simply runs in a curve. (изгибаться, поворачивать, делать изгиб)

  1. The road bends sharply just past the old mill.
  2. The river bent west and disappeared into the trees.
  3. Water has bent light this way since long before anyone measured it.

4. Bend the rules. Stretch a rule without quite breaking it. A fixed collocation — bend the rules, bend the truth, bend a deadline. (обходить правила, делать поблажку)

  1. I have bent the rules for you once already.
  2. The coach bent the curfew for the seniors.
  3. Good editors bend the style guide when the sentence needs it.

5. Bend to something — yield, give in. Figurative and intransitive, normally bend to pressure, a demand, or someone's will. (уступать, поддаваться, подчиняться)

  1. He would not bend to pressure from the board.
  2. In the end the city bent to the neighborhood's demands.
  3. The union has bent on wages but not on hours.

6. Bend someone or something to your will. The transitive mirror of the sense above: you are the one doing the forcing. Slightly literary in tone. (подчинять своей воле, гнуть под себя)

  1. She bent the whole department to her will inside a month.
  2. You cannot bend a language to a rule it never had.

7. Bend over backwards. Idiom: go to extreme trouble to help or accommodate someone. Always in this exact shape. (из кожи вон лезть, делать всё возможное)

  1. They bend over backwards for regular customers.
  2. The landlord has bent over backwards to keep us.

8. Be bent on something — determined. The participle used as a predicate adjective with on. It never means physically curved here, and there is no active verb behind it. (твёрдо намеренный, одержимый (какой-л. целью))

  1. He is bent on selling the farm no matter what we say.
  2. They were bent on winning, whatever it cost them.
Phrasal verbs
bend over backward
See also
unbend, blend, rend

blend#

Pronunciation
/blɛnd/ · BLEND
Русский
смешивать
Forms
blend /blɛnd/ · blended /ˈblɛndɪd/ (older blent /blɛnt/) · blended /ˈblɛndɪd/ (older blent /blɛnt/)
Register
everyday — ordinary kitchen and conversational English; blend in is said constantly
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt historically; regular -ed in modern American English
Group
6
Third person
blends · -ing form: blending
Contrast
The spreadsheet lists blent because blend once belonged to this group, but in American English today it is a plain regular verb: blended, have blended. Blent now turns up only in poetry and hymn texts. Write blended. Watch the spelling too — blend is not bend with an extra letter; the two verbs are unrelated.
Verb type
action — dynamic in every sense, from mixing batter to a newcomer disappearing into a crowd.
Transitivity
both — transitive when you do the mixing (blend the butter and the sugar, the distillery blended eleven barrels), intransitive when things merge on their own or someone blends in (their accents have blended, he blended in with the crowd).
Passive
yes in the transitive senses — the paint was blended on a scrap of cardboard, eleven barrels were blended before bottling. No passive of blend in or the merging sense: ✗ the crowd was blended in with by him.

To mix things into a single smooth or harmonious whole, or to merge so completely that the parts stop being distinguishable.

1. Mix into a smooth whole. The everyday cooking and mixing sense. In modern American English the past and participle are blended; the old blent survives only in poetry and hymn texts. (смешивать, размешивать, взбивать)

  1. Blend the butter and the sugar until the mixture is smooth.
  2. She blended three paint colors on a scrap of cardboard.
  3. I have blended this soup twice and it is still lumpy.

2. Merge, become indistinguishable. Intransitive and usually figurative: two things run together until you cannot tell where one ends. (сливаться, смешиваться воедино)

  1. Their two accents have blended into something nobody can place.
  2. The suburbs blend into one another for thirty miles along that highway.
  3. By the second month the new hires had blended into the team completely.

3. Combine varieties (coffee, whiskey, tea). A trade sense: mix different lots or origins to get a consistent product. This is where the noun a blend comes from. (купажировать, составлять смесь)

  1. This roaster blends beans from three countries.
  2. The distillery blended eleven barrels before bottling.

4. Blend in — go unnoticed, match the surroundings. Phrasal, about people trying not to stand out or about a thing matching what is around it. (сливаться с окружением, не выделяться)

  1. New siding helps the addition blend in with the old house.
  2. He dressed down so he would blend in with the crowd.
  3. The gray paint blended in so well we walked past the door twice.
See also
bend, rend, send

build#

Pronunciation
/bɪld/ · BILD
Русский
строить
Forms
build /bɪld/ · built /bɪlt/ · built /bɪlt/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
-ld — -lt — -lt
Group
6
Third person
builds · -ing form: building (also the noun a building)
Contrast
✗ builded is not English. And because built covers both slots, We built it and We have built it use the same word — the difference lives entirely in have. Note the vowel: build rhymes with filled, not with wild; the u is silent.
Verb type
action — dynamic, whether the object is a house, a career, or a case. Never linking and never stative; it always names work or growth over time.
Transitivity
both — transitive in the constructing and developing senses (they build forty houses a year, she builds her arguments one fact at a time), intransitive when something grows on its own (pressure builds, the tension built through the third act).
Passive
yes, and very common — the whole case was built on a single witness, the cabinets are built into the wall. Note the physique sense is passive-only, with no active behind it: he is built like a linebacker but ✗ he builds like a linebacker.

To make something by putting parts together — a house, a business, a case, a reputation — or, intransitively, to grow steadily stronger.

1. Construct, put up. The literal sense: assemble physical parts into a structure or an object. (строить, возводить, сооружать)

  1. They build about forty houses a year.
  2. My grandfather built this table by hand.
  3. The district has built three new schools since 2020.

2. Develop something over time. Figurative but extremely common: a career, a business, a case, a reputation, a relationship. The object is abstract and the work is gradual. (создавать, выстраивать, наращивать)

  1. She builds her arguments one fact at a time.
  2. He has built a law practice out of nothing.
  3. Two decades of steady work built that reputation.

3. Grow, intensify. Intransitive, with no object at all: pressure, tension, momentum, or a storm increases on its own. (нарастать, усиливаться, накапливаться)

  1. Pressure builds along that fault for decades at a time.
  2. The tension built through the entire third act.
  3. By noon the heat had built to something dangerous.

4. Build on / build upon — use as a foundation. Take something already there and extend it. Very common in the passive: built on. (основываться на, развивать (начатое))

  1. The sequel builds on everything the first film set up.
  2. The whole case was built on a single witness.
  3. Later research has built on her 1994 paper.

5. Build in / build into — include from the start. Make something a permanent, non-removable part of a design, a schedule, or an agreement. (встраивать, закладывать (в конструкцию, в договор))

  1. Build a little slack into the schedule.
  2. Congress had built a sunset clause into the law.
  3. The cabinets are built into the wall and do not come with the house.

6. Build up — accumulate, strengthen, or hype. Three related phrasal uses: a substance collects, a person gains strength, or the press talks someone up in advance. (накапливать(ся), укреплять; расхваливать заранее)

  1. Salt builds up on the sidewalk all winter.
  2. She built up her savings for two years before quitting.
  3. The press had built him up as unbeatable, which made the loss worse.

7. Be built — of a person's physique. Passive only, describing body type. There is no active verb behind it — you cannot say ✗ He builds like a linebacker. (быть сложенным (о телосложении))

  1. He is built like a linebacker.
  2. She was built for distance, not for sprints.
Phrasal verbs
build in · build into · build on · build up
See also
rebuild, overbuild, gild

gild#

Pronunciation
/ɡɪld/ · GILD
Русский
позолачивать
Forms
gild /ɡɪld/ · gilded /ˈɡɪldɪd/ (also gilt /ɡɪlt/) · gilded /ˈɡɪldɪd/ (also gilt /ɡɪlt/)
Register
rare — a narrow craft verb; most Americans meet it only in gild the lily and the Gilded Age
Pattern
-ld — -lt — -lt historically; gilded is the ordinary American past and participle
Group
6
Third person
gilds · -ing form: gilding (also a noun: the gilding flaked off)
Contrast
Both forms are listed in American dictionaries, but gilded is what edited American prose uses for the verb — they gilded the dome, the age was gilded. Gilt has narrowed to an adjective in front of a noun (a gilt frame, gilt-edged bonds) and to a noun for the gold layer itself. Separately, do not confuse gild with guild — same pronunciation /ɡɪld/, but a guild is a trade association.
Verb type
action — dynamic: applying gold leaf, or putting a flattering finish on something ordinary. The adjective gilded (the Gilded Age) is the participle, not a separate class.
Transitivity
transitive in both senses, and the object is obligatory — gild the dome, gild the lily. ✗ Craftsmen gild every fifty years needs its object.
Passive
yes — the capitol dome is gilded about every fifty years, a very ordinary hotel was gilded by the brochure. The perfect passive is ordinary too: the lettering has been gilded.

To cover a surface with a thin layer of gold, or figuratively to put an attractive finish on something ordinary.

1. Cover with gold leaf. The literal craft sense — domes, frames, lettering, book edges. Gilded is the ordinary American past and participle; gilt has narrowed to an adjective before a noun. (золотить, покрывать позолотой)

  1. Craftsmen gild the capitol dome about every fifty years.
  2. The restorers gilded the picture frame last spring.
  3. Someone has gilded the lettering above the door.

2. Make something look better than it is. Figurative and mildly disapproving, most often in the idiom gild the lily — decorate something already fine. The adjective gilded also labels a shiny surface over something less pleasant, as in the Gilded Age. (приукрашивать, наводить внешний лоск)

  1. A third adjective just gilds the lily.
  2. The brochure gilded a very ordinary roadside hotel.
  3. Memoirists have gilded that decade past recognition.
See also
build, gird

gird#

Pronunciation
/ɡɝd/ · GURD
Русский
подпоясывать, опоясывать
Forms
gird /ɡɝd/ · girded /ˈɡɝdɪd/ (also girt /ɡɝt/) · girded /ˈɡɝdɪd/ (also girt /ɡɝt/)
Register
rare — elevated and mostly journalistic; alive in gird for and the biblical gird your loins
Pattern
-rd — -rt — -rt historically; girded is the usual American form
Group
6
Third person
girds · -ing form: girding
Contrast
Girded is what you will read in American English, almost always in gird your loins or gird for something. Girt has retreated into fixed phrases and nautical description (an island girt by reef). Keep the r in the American vowel — /ɡɝd/, not the British /ɡɜːd/ — and note that the g is hard, as in go.
Verb type
action — dynamic, elevated in register: bracing yourself for something hard, or belting and encircling in the literal sense.
Transitivity
both — intransitive or reflexive in the living sense (retailers gird for the holidays, the mayor girded himself for a fight), transitive in gird your loins and the literal encircling (stone walls had girded the town).
Passive
restricted — natural only in the literal encircling sense, and mostly as the short participle: the island is girt by coral, the town was girded by stone walls. No passive of gird for: ✗ the holidays are girded for.

To fasten a belt around something, and by extension to brace yourself for something hard.

1. Gird for something — brace, prepare. The only sense in ordinary modern American use, almost always gird for or gird oneself for. Slightly elevated in register — you meet it in news writing more than in speech. (готовиться, собираться с силами (перед трудным))

  1. Retailers gird for the holidays as early as September.
  2. The mayor girded for a long budget fight.
  3. Hospitals have girded for flu season since August.

2. Gird your loins. A fixed biblical phrase, now used half in earnest and half as a joke before an unpleasant task. (препоясать чресла; собраться с духом)

  1. Gird your loins — the auditors arrive Monday.
  2. He girded his loins and knocked on the director's door.

3. Belt, encircle, surround. The literal sense of putting on a belt, and its literary or nautical extension to something ringed by water or walls. This is where the short form girt still lives. (опоясывать, окружать)

  1. He girds his waist with a wide leather belt.
  2. Stone walls had girded the town since the twelfth century.
  3. The old atlas describes the island as girt by coral.
See also
gild, build

lend#

Pronunciation
/lɛnd/ · LEND
Русский
давать взаймы
Forms
lend /lɛnd/ · lent /lɛnt/ · lent /lɛnt/
Register
everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt
Group
6
Third person
lends · -ing form: lending (also a noun: bank lending)
Contrast
Lend and borrow point in opposite directions: you lend something to someone, and you borrow it from them. ✗ Can you borrow me a pen? is the classic error — say Can you lend me a pen? The past lent also sounds exactly like Lent, the church season, and it is one letter from bent and sent.
Verb type
action — dynamic in all senses, even the figurative lend weight and lend an ear, where nothing physically changes hands.
Transitivity
ditransitive in the core sense — two objects with no preposition (lend me twenty dollars), or one object plus to (she lent the book to a friend). Also plainly transitive in lend weight, and intransitive in the banking sense: banks lend at whatever rate the market will bear.
Passive
yes, with either object as subject — the painting had been lent to Chicago, he was lent a car for the weekend. The reflexive lend itself to has no passive: ✗ the novel is lent itself to a two-hour film.

To let someone use something of yours for a while, expecting it back — and, figuratively, to give a quality to something.

1. Loan something, expecting it back. You lend something to someone and they borrow it from you — the two verbs point in opposite directions. Takes two objects with no preposition: lend me a pen. (давать взаймы, одалживать (кому-л.))

  1. Lend me twenty dollars until Friday.
  2. I lent him my car for the weekend and got it back full of sand.
  3. She has lent that book to at least six people.
  4. The museum had lent the painting to Chicago before the fire.

2. Provide money at interest. The commercial sense, with a bank or a lender as the subject. Often intransitive: banks stopped lending. (кредитовать, выдавать ссуды)

  1. Banks lend at whatever rate the market will bear.
  2. Credit unions lent less that year than at any point since 2009.

3. Give or add a quality. Figurative, with an abstract object: lend weight, lend credence, lend an air of, lend charm. Nothing is returned here. (придавать (оттенок, вес, достоверность))

  1. Those curtains lend the room a little warmth.
  2. Her presence lent the event a seriousness it had lacked.
  3. Two new studies have lent weight to the theory.

4. Lend itself to — be suitable for. Reflexive and fixed. The subject is a thing, and the sense is that its nature suits some use. (годиться для, хорошо поддаваться)

  1. The novel does not lend itself to a two-hour film.
  2. The basement lent itself perfectly to a workshop.

5. Lend a hand, lend an ear. Two set phrases where nothing is literally loaned: lend a hand is to help, lend an ear is to listen. (помочь, подсобить; выслушать)

  1. Come lend a hand with the couch.
  2. He lent a sympathetic ear and said nothing else all evening.
Phrasal verbs
lend itself to · lend out
See also
send, spend, bend

misspend#

Pronunciation
/ˌmɪsˈspɛnd/ · miss-SPEND
Русский
транжирить
Forms
misspend /ˌmɪsˈspɛnd/ · misspent /ˌmɪsˈspɛnt/ · misspent /ˌmɪsˈspɛnt/
Register
rare — prefixed derivative of spend, met almost only as the participle in a misspent youth
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt
Group
6
Third person
misspends · -ing form: misspending
Contrast
Prefix mis- on spend, so the forms follow spend exactly and need no separate explanation. Two things to watch: the double s — ✗ mispend — and the fact that you will meet the participle far more often than the base form, nearly always in the set phrase a misspent youth.
Verb type
action — dynamic, the mis- judgment version of spend: money or years put to the wrong use.
Transitivity
transitive, object obligatory — misspend a windfall, misspent the entire grant, has misspent most of his twenties. ✗ The council misspent is incomplete.
Passive
yes — the entire grant was misspent on consultants, money meant for scholarships has been misspent. In practice you meet the bare participle as an adjective far more often: a misspent youth.

To spend money or time on the wrong things.

1. Spend money wrongly or wastefully. Usually about public or entrusted money, and usually the verdict of an auditor or a critic rather than the spender. (тратить не по назначению, транжирить)

  1. It is easy to misspend a windfall.
  2. The council misspent the entire grant on consultants.
  3. The trustees have misspent money that was meant for scholarships.

2. Misspent youth (waste time or years). You will meet the participle far more often than the base form, nearly always in the fixed phrase a misspent youth — often said with some affection. (растратить впустую (годы, молодость))

  1. He has misspent most of his twenties.
  2. By graduation she felt she had misspent four perfectly good years.
  3. He credits his misspent youth with his skill at pool.

spend, overspend, outspend

outspend#

Pronunciation
/ˌaʊtˈspɛnd/ · owt-SPEND
Русский
перерасходовать
Forms
outspend /ˌaʊtˈspɛnd/ · outspent /ˌaʊtˈspɛnt/ · outspent /ˌaʊtˈspɛnt/
Register
neutral to formal — ordinary in campaign and business writing, seldom said out loud
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt
Group
6
Third person
outspends · -ing form: outspending
Contrast
Prefix out- on spend; the forms match. The trap is the meaning, not the shape. To outspend someone is to spend more than a rival does, and it always takes that rival as its object: she outspent her opponent. Spending past your own budget is overspend — ✗ We outspent our budget should be We overspent our budget.
Verb type
action — dynamic and comparative: it measures your spending against a rival's.
Transitivity
transitive, and the object is obligatory and must be the rival — her opponent outspent her by two million. ✗ We outspent our budget is the wrong verb; that is overspend.
Passive
yes, and the passive is at least as common as the active in political and business reporting — the startup was outspent from the first quarter on, being badly outspent did not hurt her.

To spend more money than a rival does.

1. Spend more than a competitor. Always takes the rival as its object — she outspent her opponent. Spending past your own budget is overspend, not this verb. (тратить больше (чем соперник), обходить по расходам)

  1. Big campaigns routinely outspend small ones ten to one.
  2. Her opponent outspent her by two million dollars.
  3. The league has outspent every other sport on television advertising.

2. Be outspent (passive). The passive is at least as common as the active, especially in political and business reporting. (уступать по расходам, быть перетраченным соперником)

  1. Being badly outspent did not seem to hurt her at all.
  2. The startup was outspent from the first quarter on and never caught up.
See also
spend, overspend, misspend

overbuild#

Pronunciation
/ˌoʊvɚˈbɪld/ · oh-ver-BILD
Русский
(чрезмерно) застраивать (район и т. п.)
Forms
overbuild /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪld/ · overbuilt /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪlt/ · overbuilt /ˌoʊvɚˈbɪlt/
Register
rare — a real-estate and engineering term rather than general vocabulary
Pattern
-ld — -lt — -lt
Group
6
Third person
overbuilds · -ing form: overbuilding (also a noun: decades of overbuilding)
Contrast
Prefix over- on build, with the same forms — nothing new to learn. In practice overbuilt works most often as an adjective describing a place with more construction than it can fill: an overbuilt waterfront.
Verb type
action — dynamic: putting up more construction than a place needs, or engineering something far past the required strength.
Transitivity
both — transitive with the place or market as object (the city overbuilt its downtown, Miami has overbuilt its condo market), intransitive with no object at all (developers overbuild whenever credit gets cheap).
Passive
yes in the transitive sense — the downtown was badly overbuilt in the 1980s, the frame had been overbuilt on purpose. In the bridge is famously overbuilt the participle has gone adjectival rather than passive.

To put up more construction than a place needs — or, in engineering, to make something far stronger than it has to be.

1. Build more than a market can absorb. The real-estate sense: too many units for the tenants or buyers available. The participle works as an adjective for the place — an overbuilt waterfront. (чрезмерно застраивать, перенасыщать застройкой)

  1. Developers overbuild whenever credit gets cheap.
  2. The city badly overbuilt its downtown in the 1980s.
  3. Miami has overbuilt its condo market more than once.

2. Overbuilt — stronger than necessary. An engineering use, almost always the participle as an adjective, and frequently a compliment rather than a complaint. (с избыточным запасом прочности, сделанный с перезапасом)

  1. The bridge is famously overbuilt — it could carry twice the design load.
  2. Engineers had overbuilt the frame on purpose, expecting heavier trucks.

build, rebuild, overspend

overspend#

Pronunciation
/ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnd/ · oh-ver-SPEND
Русский
перерасходовать; сорить деньгами
Forms
overspend /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnd/ · overspent /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnt/ · overspent /ˌoʊvɚˈspɛnt/
Register
neutral to formal — ordinary in budget and finance writing, less common in speech
Pattern
-nd — -nt — -nt
Group
6
Third person
overspends · -ing form: overspending (also a noun: government overspending)
Contrast
Prefix over- on spend, same forms. Keep it apart from outspend: overspend measures you against your own budget, outspend measures you against somebody else. The participle overspent also works as an adjective for a person or a fund that has run past its limit: an overspent account.
Verb type
action — dynamic, measured against your own budget rather than a rival's (that is outspend).
Transitivity
both — the object is optional: we overspent by four hundred dollars and households overspend every December stand alone, while the department has overspent its travel budget takes one.
Passive
restricted — grammatical with a budget or line item as subject (every line item had been overspent by the third quarter), but American writing far prefers the participle as an adjective: an overspent budget, an overspent account.

To spend more money than you have or planned to spend.

1. Spend past your own budget. Measured against your own limit, which is what separates it from outspend — that one measures you against somebody else. Works with or without an object. (перерасходовать, тратить сверх бюджета)

  1. Households overspend every December and regret it in January.
  2. We overspent by four hundred dollars on the kitchen.
