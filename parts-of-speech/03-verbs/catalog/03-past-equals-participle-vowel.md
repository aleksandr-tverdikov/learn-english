# Past = participle, one vowel changed

> The past and participle are identical, differing from the base by a single vowel change.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

These verbs ask you to learn two forms instead of three: the past and the participle are the same word, and that word differs from the base by a single vowel. *Win* becomes *won* for both, *find* becomes *found* for both, *stand* becomes *stood* for both — and the consonants sit still while the vowel does all the work. Because the past and the participle never diverge, the trap here is not choosing between them, the way *began* and *begun* force you to choose; it is remembering that the vowel changes at all, since ✗ *I finded it* and ✗ *he winned* are the mistakes this group actually produces. In the original Russian spreadsheet this is the group described as (2-я и 3-я одинаковы, от 1-й отличаются сменой одного звука).

### abide

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbaɪd/ &middot; *uh-BIDE*
**Русский:** вытерпеть; обитать, оставаться верным
**Forms:** *abide* /əˈbaɪd/ &middot; *abode* /əˈboʊd/ &middot; *abode* /əˈboʊd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — legal and literary; *abide by* belongs to rules and rulings, and the dwelling sense is older still
**Pattern:** aɪ — oʊ — oʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *abides* &middot; **-ing form:** *abiding* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** In everyday American English the past is the regular *abided*, and it is the only choice in *abide by*: *We abided by the ruling*. *Abode* belongs to older and literary writing, and it survives most healthily as a noun — *a humble abode*.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the living *cannot abide* sense — an attitude, not an act, so the progressive is odd: ✗ *I am abiding the noise*. Action — dynamic only in the archaic dwelling sense (*her family abode in that valley*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the tolerate sense, where the object is obligatory (*I cannot abide people who talk*; ✗ *I cannot abide*); intransitive in the dwelling sense (*a strange quiet abides in that house*) and in the prepositional *abide by the ruling*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the stative tolerate sense resists it: ✗ *the noise could not be abided by her*. The prepositional passive works in legal writing: *every term of the settlement was abided by*. The dwelling sense is intransitive and has none.

To put up with something; in older usage, to stay or dwell somewhere.

**1. Tolerate, put up with.** Almost always negative — *cannot abide*, *could not abide*. This is the only sense alive in everyday American English, and it takes the regular *abided*. (терпеть, выносить)

1. I cannot *abide* people who talk during a movie.
2. She could not *abide* the noise from the construction site.
3. He has never *abided* criticism well.

**2. Abide by — comply with.** Fixed phrasal use, and always regular: *abided by*, never the strong form. (соблюдать, следовать (правилам))

4. We *abided by* the ruling even though we disagreed.
5. Everyone here *abides by* the same rules.
6. The company *has abided by* every term of the settlement.

**3. Dwell, remain.** Archaic and literary. This is the sense that takes *abode*, which survives far more healthily as a noun — *a humble abode*. (обитать, пребывать)

7. Her family *abode* in that valley for four generations.
8. The shepherds *have abode* in these hills since before the road was built.
9. A strange quiet *abides* in that house.

### alight

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlaɪt/ &middot; *uh-LITE*
**Русский:** спускаться, приземляться, сходить (из автобуса)
**Forms:** *alight* /əˈlaɪt/ &middot; *alit* /əˈlɪt/ &middot; *alit* /əˈlɪt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — recognized but seldom produced; Americans say *land* or *get off*
**Pattern:** aɪ — ɪ — ɪ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *alights* &middot; **-ing form:** *alighting*
**Contrast:** Both *alit* and the regular *alighted* are standard; Americans write *alighted* a little more often. Keep the verb apart from the adjective *alight*, meaning on fire — *the barn was alight* — which has no past tense at all.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a verb of motion and coming to rest (*a cardinal alit on the railing*). Keep it apart from the adjective *alight*, meaning on fire, which is not a verb form at all.
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in every sense — what is landed on or stepped out of comes after a preposition (*alight on the feeder*, *alights at the Franklin Street stop*, *alighted on a compromise*), never as a bare object: ✗ *she alit the railing*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, so there is no object to promote: ✗ *the railing was alit*. *The barn was alight* is the adjective, not a passive.

To come down and settle on something, or to step down out of a vehicle.

**1. Land, settle on something.** The core sense, used of birds, insects, and anything that comes lightly to rest. Both *alit* and the regular *alighted* are standard. (садиться, опускаться (о птице, насекомом))

1. Sparrows *alight* on the feeder the moment I go back inside.
2. A cardinal *alit* on the railing and stared at us.
3. The same dragonfly *has alit* on that reed all afternoon.

**2. Step down from a vehicle.** Formal, and commonest in transit announcements and British signage; ordinary American speech says *get off*. (сходить, высаживаться (из транспорта))

4. She *alights* at the Franklin Street stop every morning.
5. Two passengers *had alit* before the conductor noticed the open door.

**3. Alight on — happen upon.** Literary, used of a gaze or a thought coming to rest on something. Usually regular here: *alighted on*. (натолкнуться (на мысль, решение))

6. Her eye *alighted on* the one sentence that mattered.
7. We *have alit on* a compromise that might actually work.

### baby-sit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbeɪbiˌsɪt/ &middot; *BAY-bee-sit*
**Русский:** работать няней, присматривать за детьми
**Forms:** *baby-sit* /ˈbeɪbiˌsɪt/ &middot; *baby-sat* /ˈbeɪbiˌsæt/ &middot; *baby-sat* /ˈbeɪbiˌsæt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary American; only the hyphen is dated
**Pattern:** ɪ — æ — æ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *baby-sits* &middot; **-ing form:** *baby-sitting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** It follows [sit](#sit) exactly. Modern American style writes the word solid — *babysit, babysat, babysitting* — and the hyphen now looks dated.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a work verb, and equally at home in the informal sense of watching a process that should not need watching (*baby-sit the build*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with the child or the process as object (*she baby-sat my nephews*, *he baby-sat the server all night*), intransitive when the object is left out (*I baby-sit for the Novaks most Friday nights*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive uses — *the twins were baby-sat by a neighbor*, *that migration got baby-sat all night*. No passive of the bare intransitive *she baby-sits on Fridays*.

To look after someone else's children while the parents are out.

**1. Look after children.** The everyday sense. Modern American style writes the word solid — *babysit, babysat* — and the hyphen now looks dated. (сидеть с детьми, работать няней)

1. I *baby-sit* for the Novaks most Friday nights.
2. She *baby-sat* my nephews all through high school.
3. We *have baby-sat* for them twice this month already.
4. My brother *baby-sits* whenever he needs gas money.

**2. Keep an idle watch over something.** Informal and slightly resentful: to sit with a process or a person that ought not need supervising. (нянчиться с чем-л., присматривать (перен.))

5. Somebody has to *baby-sit* the build until it finishes.
6. He *baby-sat* the server all night waiting for the migration to clear.
7. I *have baby-sat* that spreadsheet for three days.

### backlight

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbækˌlaɪt/ &middot; *BAK-lite*
**Русский:** подсвечивать
**Forms:** *backlight* /ˈbækˌlaɪt/ &middot; *backlit* /ˈbækˌlɪt/ &middot; *backlit* /ˈbækˌlɪt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — a photography and display term; the participle *backlit* does most of the work
**Pattern:** aɪ — ɪ — ɪ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *backlights* &middot; **-ing form:** *backlighting*
**Contrast:** It follows [light](#light), including the option of a regular *backlighted*. *Backlit* is far commoner, especially in front of a noun: *a backlit screen*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a causative lighting verb. The participle *backlit* has gone on to work as a plain adjective: *a backlit screen*, *a backlit keyboard*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** only, and the object is obligatory — *the photographer backlit her subject*; ✗ *the photographer backlit* is incomplete.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is the commoner form — *every display in the lobby is backlit around the clock*, *the keyboard had been backlit in blue*.

To light something from behind.

**1. Light from behind.** Photography and stage lighting. Like *light*, it also allows a regular *backlighted*, but *backlit* is far commoner. (подсвечивать сзади)

1. We *backlight* the product shots to lift them off the wall.
2. The photographer *backlit* her subject with a single strobe.
3. They *have backlit* every sign in the terminal.
4. He *backlights* the stage with two amber panels.

**2. Backlit — lit from behind.** Almost always passive or in front of a noun: *a backlit screen*, *a backlit keyboard*. (с подсветкой (об экране, клавиатуре))

5. The keyboard *had* been *backlit* in blue since the last update.
6. Every display in the lobby *is backlit* around the clock.

### backslide

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbækˌslaɪd/ &middot; *BAK-slide*
**Русский:** отпадать; отступаться от веры
**Forms:** *backslide* /ˈbækˌslaɪd/ &middot; *backslid* /ˈbækˌslɪd/ &middot; *backslid* /ˈbækˌslɪd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — ordinary in writing about diets, faith, and democracy; less common in speech
**Pattern:** aɪ — ɪ — ɪ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *backslides* &middot; **-ing form:** *backsliding* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** It follows [slide](#slide). You will also meet the participle *backslidden* in religious writing, but *backslid* is the ordinary American form.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a process verb of gradual relapse, used of dieters, believers, and institutions alike (*democratic backsliding*).
**Transitivity:** **intransitive** in all three senses — what is lost ground on takes a preposition (*backslid on reading scores*, *backslid on every reform*), never a direct object: ✗ *she backslid her diet*.
**Passive:** **no** — intransitive, so nothing can be promoted to subject: ✗ *the reform was backslid*. Only the noun *backsliding* carries the sense in passive-shaped writing.

To slip back into a bad habit, an abandoned belief, or an earlier and worse condition.

**1. Relapse into an old habit.** The everyday sense, used of diets, sobriety, and any effort at self-improvement. (срываться, скатываться назад)

1. Dieters *backslide* around the holidays.
2. He *backslid* the week after he quit smoking.
3. She *has backslid* twice since January.

**2. Fall away from faith.** Religious register, and the original sense. In that writing you will also meet the participle *backslidden*. (отступаться от веры)

4. The preacher warned that a believer can *backslide* without ever noticing it.
5. Two of the congregation *had backslid* by the following spring.

**3. Regress, lose ground.** Of institutions, countries, or measured results. Common in journalism: *democratic backsliding*. (регрессировать, откатываться назад)

6. The department *backslides* every time the budget tightens.
7. Several districts *have backslid* on reading scores.
8. By March the government *had backslid* on every reform it promised.

**See also:** [slide](#slide)

### beget

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈɡɛt/ &middot; *bih-GET*
**Русский:** порождать
**Forms:** *beget* /bɪˈɡɛt/ &middot; *begot* /bɪˈɡɑt/ &middot; *begot* /bɪˈɡɑt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — biblical and aphoristic; ordinary speech says *father* or *lead to*
**Pattern:** ɛ — ɑ — ɑ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *begets* &middot; **-ing form:** *begetting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** It follows [get](#get). The archaic participle *begotten* is the form most Americans actually recognize, from scripture — *his only begotten son* — while *begot* is the plainer modern participle. The verb itself is formal or biblical; ordinary speech says *father* or *lead to*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a causative verb, formal and aphoristic (*violence begets violence*) or biblical (*Abraham begot Isaac*).
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, with an obligatory object in both senses — *cheap money can beget speculation*; ✗ *violence begets* alone says nothing.
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but stilted in the causal sense (*a decade of lawsuits was begot by that decision*); the passive that Americans actually meet is the frozen archaic participle *his only begotten son*.

To father a child; more broadly, to cause something to come into being.

**1. Give rise to, cause.** The living use, and it lives mostly in aphorisms and headlines. Formal; ordinary speech says *leads to*. (порождать, вызывать)

1. Violence *begets* violence.
2. Cheap money can *beget* speculation.
3. That one decision *has begot* a decade of lawsuits.
4. The scandal *had begot* three investigations before anyone resigned.

**2. Father a child.** Biblical and formal, and only ever of the father. The archaic participle *begotten* is the form most Americans recognize — *his only begotten son*. (порождать, зачинать (о мужчине))

5. Abraham *begot* Isaac.
6. He *had begotten* seven children by the time he was forty.

**See also:** [get](#get)

### behold

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈhoʊld/ &middot; *bih-HOHLD*
**Русский:** смотреть, созерцать, зреть
**Forms:** *behold* /bɪˈhoʊld/ &middot; *beheld* /bɪˈhɛld/ &middot; *beheld* /bɪˈhɛld/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** archaic — literary and ceremonial; alive in speech only in *lo and behold*
**Pattern:** oʊ — ɛ — ɛ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *beholds* &middot; **-ing form:** *beholding*
**Contrast:** It follows [hold](#hold). The verb is literary or ceremonial; in ordinary speech it survives mostly in the fixed phrase *lo and behold*.
**Verb type:** **stative-leaning perception verb**, patterning with *see* rather than *watch*: the progressive is unnatural — ✗ *the crowd is beholding the eclipse*. Literary throughout, and imperative-only in *Behold the size of that canyon*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, with an obligatory object (*the crowd beheld the eclipse*, *few travelers have beheld that valley*); the frozen *lo and behold* takes no object at all.
**Passive:** **restricted** — possible only in literary register: *a sight beheld by few* passes, while a full *the eclipse was beheld by the crowd* reads as costume English.

To look at something, especially something striking.

**1. Look upon, gaze at.** Literary or ceremonial; nobody says it in ordinary conversation. Do not confuse it with the adjective *beholden*, meaning indebted, which is a different word. (созерцать, взирать)

1. The crowd *beheld* the eclipse in complete silence.
2. Few travelers *have beheld* that valley under snow.
3. She *beholds* the city each morning from the same window.

**2. Behold! — presenting something.** Imperative only, and today usually playful or ironic when a thing is revealed. (вот!, смотрите!)

4. *Behold* the size of that canyon.
5. He opened the box and — *behold* — the missing part was inside.

**3. Lo and behold.** A frozen phrase introducing a surprise. It is the only place most Americans ever use the verb. (и вот, представьте себе)

6. We drove all that way and, lo and *behold*, the museum was closed.
7. I looked again and, lo and *behold*, the keys were sitting on the counter.
8. Nobody who *had beheld* the wreck could describe it calmly.

### betide

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtaɪd/ &middot; *bih-TIDE*
**Русский:** постигать; случаться
**Forms:** *betide* /bɪˈtaɪd/ &middot; *betid* /bɪˈtɪd/ &middot; *betid* /bɪˈtɪd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** archaic — no longer current; one frozen phrase, *woe betide*, keeps it in the language
**Pattern:** aɪ — ɪ — ɪ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *betides* &middot; **-ing form:** *betiding* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** Archaic. In living American English the verb appears in one frozen phrase, *woe betide* — a subjunctive, so it takes no ending: *Woe betide anyone who is late*. The past *betid* is museum English.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, but an event verb rather than a deed: something befalls a person. Archaic outside the subjunctive *woe betide*, which takes no ending (*woe betide anyone who is late*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with the person affected as object (*woe betide the student who forgets*, *great sorrow betid the household*), intransitive when the event simply occurs (*whatever may betide, we will meet again*).
**Passive:** **no** — the verb is archaic and its object is an experiencer, not a patient: ✗ *the household was betid by great sorrow* is not English in any register.

To happen to someone, especially to happen badly.

**1. Woe betide — a warning.** The one frozen phrase that keeps this verb alive. It is a subjunctive, so it normally takes no ending, though *woe betides* also turns up. (горе тому, кто...)

1. Woe *betide* the student who forgets that deadline.
2. Woe *betide* anyone who touches my coffee.
3. Woe *betides* the traveler who ignores that sign.

**2. Happen, befall.** Archaic outside of the phrase above; the past *betid* is museum English. (постигать, случаться (с кем-л.))

4. Whatever may *betide*, we will meet again in spring.
5. Great sorrow *betid* the household that winter.
6. Stranger things *had betid* the village before the bridge was built.

**See also:** [bide](#bide), [abide](#abide), [chide](#chide)

### bide

**Pronunciation:** /baɪd/ &middot; *BIDE*
**Русский:** выжидать (благоприятного момента)
**Forms:** *bide* /baɪd/ &middot; *bode* /boʊd/ &middot; *bode* /boʊd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** rare — alive only in *bide your time*; the strong *bode* is older still
**Pattern:** aɪ — oʊ — oʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *bides* &middot; **-ing form:** *biding* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** The one living use is *bide your time*, and there Americans say *bided*: *He bided his time*. *Bode* now works mostly as a separate regular verb meaning to be a sign of something — *this bodes well*, *it boded ill* — which has its own -d past and is not this verb at all.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, but alive only inside a fixed frame. *Bide one's time* is the one living use; do not confuse it with the separate regular verb *bode*, to be a sign of something (*this bodes well*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the living idiom, where the object is fixed and possessive (*he bided his time*), intransitive in the archaic dwelling sense (*he had bode in that harbor town all winter*).
**Passive:** **no** — the idiom's object cannot be promoted: ✗ *his time was bided*. The dwelling sense is intransitive and has no passive either.

To wait, especially to wait for the right moment.

**1. Bide one's time.** The one living use, and there Americans say *bided*. Do not confuse it with the separate regular verb *bode*, to be a sign of something — *this bodes well*. (выжидать удобного момента)

1. *Bide* your time and the price will come down.
2. She *bides* her time better than anyone I know.
3. He *bided* his time until the market turned.
4. They *have bided* their time long enough.

**2. Stay, dwell, remain.** Archaic and dialectal, and the sense that carries the strong forms *bode*, *bode*. (оставаться, пребывать)

5. The old sailor *bode* his time ashore for a year.
6. He *had bode* in that harbor town all winter, waiting for a berth.

### bind

**Pronunciation:** /baɪnd/ &middot; *BYND*
**Русский:** связать, завязать
**Forms:** *bind* /baɪnd/ &middot; *bound* /baʊnd/ &middot; *bound* /baʊnd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary in speech and writing, and *bound to* is pure conversation
**Pattern:** aɪ — aʊ — aʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *binds* &middot; **-ing form:** *binding*
**Contrast:** *Bound* is three words at once: this past tense; a regular verb meaning to leap — *the dog bounded up the steps*; and an adjective meaning headed for — *a train bound for Chicago*. Only the first is a form of *bind*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout. The participle *bound* has two frozen adjectival lives that are no longer verbal: *bound to happen* (certain to) and *bound for Chicago* (headed for).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the tying, obliging, uniting, bookbinding, and dressing senses (*she bound the stack of letters*, *the contract binds both parties*), intransitive in the setting and jamming senses (*an egg helps the mixture bind*, *the drawer binds whenever the wood swells*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the letters were bound with kitchen twine*, *both parties are bound by the contract for five years*. No passive of the intransitive *the drawer binds*, and *it was bound to happen* is an adjective, not a passive.

To tie things together, to place someone under an obligation, or to make separate things hold fast to each other.

**1. Tie, fasten together.** The literal core sense: joining things with rope, twine, wire, or tape. (связывать, завязывать)

1. We *bind* the reports in black covers.
2. She *bound* the stack of letters with kitchen twine.
3. Someone *has bound* all the cables with zip ties.

**2. Place under an obligation.** Legal and contractual, and the reason *binding* is an adjective in its own right. (обязывать, связывать обязательством)

4. The contract *binds* both parties for five years.
5. He *has bound* himself to a two-year lease.
6. The treaty *had bound* them long before the war started.

**3. Be bound to; be bound for.** Two frozen adjectival uses of the participle: *bound to* means certain to, and *bound for* means headed for. Neither behaves like an ordinary passive. (непременно, обязательно; направляющийся (куда-л.))

7. Something like this *was bound to* happen eventually.
8. We were on a train *bound for* Chicago.

**4. Hold together, unite.** Figurative, of what holds a group or a story together. (сплачивать, объединять)

9. Shared work *binds* a crew faster than anything else.
10. Grief *had bound* the two families together for good.

**5. Bind a book.** The bookbinding sense; the trade is called *binding*. (переплетать)

11. That shop still *binds* dissertations by hand.
12. They *bound* my grandfather's letters into a single volume.

**6. Bind a wound; bind up.** Somewhat formal or biblical; everyday speech says *bandage*. (перевязывать (рану))

13. Medics *bind* the wound before they move anyone.
14. She *bound up* his arm with a torn shirt.

**7. Hold fast; stick, jam.** Intransitive, used of a mixture that comes together and of a mechanism that seizes. (схватываться; заедать, заклинивать)

15. An egg helps the mixture *bind*.
16. The drawer *binds* whenever the wood swells.
17. The brake *has bound* against the rotor since the last repair.

**8. Bind to (chemistry, computing).** Technical, but a learner meets it constantly in science writing and in software documentation. (связываться (с рецептором); привязывать (к порту, ключу))

18. The antibody *binds* to a protein on the cell surface.
19. The molecule *has bound* to the receptor site.
20. We *bound* the shortcut to a different key.

### bleed

**Pronunciation:** /blid/ &middot; *BLEED*
**Русский:** кровоточить
**Forms:** *bleed* /blid/ &middot; *bled* /blɛd/ &middot; *bled* /blɛd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary American, literal and figurative alike
**Pattern:** i — ɛ — ɛ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *bleeds* &middot; **-ing form:** *bleeding*
**Contrast:** The vowel shortens and the spelling loses a letter with it: *bleed* has two e's, *bled* has one, and ✗ *bleeded* is never right. The same shortening runs through [feed](#feed), [breed](#breed) and [lead](#lead). The transitive sense keeps the strong form too — *he bled the brakes*, not ✗ *he bleeded the brakes*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a process verb literally (*his nose bled*) and figuratively (*the company bleeds cash*).

### fight

**Pronunciation:** /faɪt/ &middot; *FITE*
**Русский:** сражаться
**Forms:** *fight* /faɪt/ &middot; *fought* /fɔt/ &middot; *fought* /fɔt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** aɪ — ɔ — ɔ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *fights* &middot; **-ing form:** *fighting*
**Contrast:** The past is spelled *fought*, one of the -ought family along with *bought* and *brought*. ✗ *fighted* and ✗ *faught* are never right.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a verb of struggle, literal (*he fought in the Pacific*), interpersonal (*they fight about money*), and civic (*the union has fought three contract battles*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive when no opponent is named or one follows a preposition (*two guys fight outside that bar*, *we fought the whole drive home*), transitive with an opponent, an obstacle, or a claim as object (*crews fought the fire*, *he fought the ticket and won*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the fire was fought for nine days*, *the rezoning had been fought for two years*. No passive of the quarreling sense: ✗ *money is fought about by them*.

To take part in a struggle, physical or otherwise.

**1. Fight physically; wage war.** The literal sense, from a schoolyard scuffle to a war. The past is spelled *fought*, one of the *-ought* family with *bought* and *brought*. (драться, сражаться, воевать)

1. He *fought* in the Pacific.
2. Two guys *fight* outside that bar every weekend.
3. The champion *has fought* twice this year.

**2. Quarrel, argue.** No blows involved; usually *fight about* or *fight over* something. (ссориться, ругаться)

4. They *fight* about money every January.
5. We *fought* the whole drive home.

**3. Struggle against something.** The object is a problem rather than a person: a fire, an illness, the traffic. (бороться (с болезнью, пожаром, стихией))

6. Crews *fought* the fire for nine days.
7. She *has fought* the same infection three times.

**4. Campaign for or against.** Political and civic, of long public efforts. (отстаивать, бороться за/против)

8. We *had fought* the rezoning for two years before the vote.
9. The union *has fought* three contract battles this decade.

**5. Contest legally.** American usage lets you *fight a ticket*, *fight a claim*, or *fight a will*. (оспаривать (в суде), опротестовывать)

10. She *fights* every claim the insurer denies.
11. He *fought* the ticket and won.

**6. Fight back.** To resist after being attacked; also *fight back tears*, meaning to hold them in. (давать отпор, сопротивляться)

12. She *fought back* and the whole thing stopped.
13. Consumers *have fought back* with a boycott.

**7. Fight off.** To repel something coming at you — attackers, a cold, sleep. (отбиваться, побороть (болезнь, нападение))

14. He *fought off* a cold all week.
15. The company *has fought off* two takeover attempts.

**8. Fight one's way.** Movement achieved by effort, literal or figurative. (пробиваться, прокладывать себе путь)

16. We *fought* our way through the crowd.
17. They *had fought* their way into the semifinals.

### find

**Pronunciation:** /faɪnd/ &middot; *FYND*
**Русский:** находить
**Forms:** *find* /faɪnd/ &middot; *found* /faʊnd/ &middot; *found* /faʊnd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** aɪ — aʊ — aʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *finds* &middot; **-ing form:** *finding*
**Contrast:** *Found* is also a separate regular verb meaning to establish: *They founded the college in 1851*. So *the college was founded in 1851* (set up) says something quite different from *the college was found in 1851* (located).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the discovery senses (*she found my keys in the freezer*); **stative** in the judging sense, which reports an opinion rather than an act — *I find the whole argument unconvincing*, and the progressive is odd there: ✗ *I am finding the argument unconvincing*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** throughout, with an obligatory object (✗ *she found* is incomplete); senses 4 and 5 add an object complement (*I find the argument unconvincing*, *the jury found him guilty on two counts*), and *find someone the time* also allows two objects.
**Passive:** **yes** — *no evidence of tampering has been found*, *he was found guilty on two counts*, *the effect was found to disappear after a year*. Even the stative judging sense passivizes: *the argument was found unconvincing*.

To come upon something, to discover it after looking, or to form a judgment about it.

**1. Discover after searching.** The core sense: you were looking, and there it was. (находить, отыскивать)

1. She *found* my keys in the freezer.
2. We *had found* the leak before the plumber arrived.
3. Investigators *have found* no evidence of tampering.

**2. Come upon by chance.** No searching implied — the thing simply turned up. (натыкаться, обнаруживать случайно)

4. He *finds* good bookstores wherever he goes.
5. She *found* a twenty in an old coat.

**3. Learn, establish as a fact.** Research and reporting, usually *find that*. (выяснять, устанавливать)

6. Researchers *found* that the effect disappears after a year.
7. We *have found* the opposite to be true.

**4. Consider, judge to be.** Takes an object plus a description: *find it odd*, *find him difficult*, *find the class hard*. (находить, считать (каким-л.))

8. I *find* the whole argument unconvincing.
9. She *found* the class harder than she expected.

**5. Return a verdict (law).** Legal: a jury *finds* someone guilty, and a court *finds for* one side. (признавать (виновным), выносить решение)

10. The jury *found* him guilty on two counts.
11. The court *has found* for the plaintiff.

**6. Find oneself in a situation.** Reflexive, and it says the situation was not planned. (оказаться, очутиться)

12. We *found* ourselves in the wrong terminal.
13. He *has found* himself out of work twice this year.

**7. Manage to get (time, money, words).** The object is a resource that had to be produced from somewhere. (выкраивать, изыскивать)

14. I cannot *find* the time to read anything.
15. They *found* the money somewhere.

**8. Find out.** Phrasal, of learning a fact — often one somebody was hiding. (узнавать, выяснять)

16. *Find out* what it costs before you sign anything.
17. She *had found out* about the transfer from a coworker.

**9. Reach, arrive at (of a thing).** The subject is inanimate and gets where it naturally goes: *water finds its level*, *the shot found the corner*. (достигать, попадать (в цель))

18. Water *finds* its level.
19. The shot *found* the top corner.

### get

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɛt/ &middot; *GET*
**Русский:** получать; становиться; добираться
**Forms:** *get* /ɡɛt/ &middot; *got* /ɡɑt/ &middot; *got* /ɡɑt/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the commonest verbs in the language
**Pattern:** ɛ — ɑ — ɑ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *gets* &middot; **-ing form:** *getting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** The one place where American and British English part company hardest. The normal American participle is *gotten* — *I have gotten three offers*, *the weather has gotten worse*. *Got* survives as a participle in two fixed patterns that mean something else: *have got* for possession (*I've got a car*) and *have got to* for obligation (*You've got to see this*). British English uses *got* everywhere and finds *gotten* strange. The forms listed above follow the source list, which is British; in American writing, reach for *gotten*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the obtaining, fetching, and arriving senses; **linking (copular)** with an adjective, where it means *become* (*it got dark*, *the weather has gotten worse*); **auxiliary-like** in the informal passive *get hired, get married, get hurt*; and **stative** in *have got* (possession) and *have got to* (obligation), which are present-tense idioms, not perfects.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the obtaining, understanding, and catching senses (*she got a raise*, *nobody got the joke*), **ditransitive** in *get me a chair*, and intransitive or linking with an adjective, particle, or preposition (*it got dark*, *we had gotten halfway to Reno*, *they get by on one income*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — *get* itself barely passivizes: ✗ *a raise was gotten by her*, and the possessive idiom refuses outright, ✗ *a spare key is had got by me*. What *get* actually does is build other verbs' passives: *they got married in a courthouse*, *two people got hurt in the pileup*.

To obtain or receive something, to become something, or to arrive somewhere — the hardest-working verb in English.

**1. Obtain, receive.** The core sense. American English uses *gotten* for the participle here: *I have gotten three offers*, not *I have got three offers*. (получать, доставать)

1. I *get* the mail around noon.
2. She *got* a raise in March.
3. He *has gotten* three offers this month.

**2. Become something.** With an adjective: *get tired*, *get dark*, *get better*. The commonest way English says that a state is changing. (становиться, делаться)

4. This *gets* complicated fast.
5. It *got* dark before we found the car.
6. The weather *has gotten* worse since Tuesday.

**3. Arrive, reach a place.** Always with a preposition or *home*: *get to work*, *get there*, *get home*. (добираться, доезжать, попадать)

7. What time do you *get* to work?
8. We *had gotten* halfway to Reno before the tire blew.

**4. Fetch, go and bring.** Transitive, often with two objects: *get me a chair*. (принести, сходить за)

9. *Get* me a chair, would you?
10. He *got* his sister from the airport.

**5. Understand, get it.** Informal but universal in speech: *I get it*, *nobody got the joke*. (понимать, доходить (разг.))

11. I *get* it now.
12. Nobody *got* the joke.
13. She *has* never *gotten* my sense of humor.

**6. Catch an illness.** The everyday alternative to *contract*. (подхватить, заболеть)

14. Everyone in the office *got* the flu.
15. She *has gotten* strep twice this winter.

**7. Cause, persuade, arrange.** Two patterns: *get someone to do something* (persuade) and *get something done* (arrange for it). (заставить, уговорить; сделать (чужими руками))

16. I could not *get* him to sign anything.
17. We *got* the roof fixed in October.
18. She *has gotten* the whole team to show up early.

**8. Have got — possess.** Present-tense possession, not a perfect at all: *I've got a car* simply means I have one. This is the one place American English keeps *got* rather than *gotten*. (иметь (разг.))

19. I *have got* a spare key in the car.
20. She *has got* two brothers in Denver.

**9. Have got to — must.** Obligation, spoken far more than written, and often reduced to *gotta*. Again *got*, never *gotten*. (должен, нужно (разг.))

21. You *have got to* see this.
22. He *has got to* stop doing that.

**10. Get + past participle (passive).** An informal passive that stresses the event rather than the state: *get hired*, *get married*, *get hurt*. (оказаться, стать (страдательный оборот))

23. They *got* married in a courthouse.
24. Two people *got* hurt in the pileup.
25. Nobody *has gotten* fired over it yet.

**11. Get up.** To rise from bed or from a seat. (вставать, подниматься)

26. I *get up* at five.
27. He *had gotten up* before the alarm.

**12. Get over.** To recover from an illness, a shock, or a person. (оправиться, пережить, забыть)

28. She never quite *got over* it.
29. He *has gotten over* the worst of the jet lag.

**13. Get by.** To manage on barely enough. (перебиваться, справляться, обходиться)

30. They *get by* on one income.
31. We *got by* with a rental for a month.

**14. Get along with.** To have an easy relationship with someone; also *get along* on its own for managing. (ладить, уживаться)

32. The two of them *get along* fine.
33. He *has* never *gotten along* with his landlord.

### grind

**Pronunciation:** /ɡraɪnd/ &middot; *GRYND*
**Русский:** молоть
**Forms:** *grind* /ɡraɪnd/ &middot; *ground* /ɡraʊnd/ &middot; *ground* /ɡraʊnd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary American, from grinding coffee to the daily grind
**Pattern:** aɪ — aʊ — aʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *grinds* &middot; **-ing form:** *grinding*
**Contrast:** The past *ground* is spelled and said exactly like the noun *ground*, the earth, and like a separate regular verb *ground, grounded* meaning to keep someone home or a plane out of the air: *the flight was grounded*, never ✗ *the flight was ground*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; a process verb of crushing and abrasion, and the source of the noun in *the daily grind*. Keep it apart from the separate regular verb *ground, grounded* (*the flight was grounded*, never ✗ *the flight was ground*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the crushing, sharpening, and wearing-down senses (*I grind the beans right before brewing*, *the commute grinds people down*), intransitive in the grating and halting senses (*the gears ground every time he shifted*, *traffic had ground to a stop*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the blade had been ground down to almost nothing*, *coffee has been ground into the carpet*. No passive of *traffic ground to a halt*, and none of the homophonous *grounded*.

To crush something into powder, or to rub two hard surfaces against each other.

**1. Crush into powder.** Coffee, grain, pepper, meat. The past *ground* is spelled exactly like the noun *ground*, the earth. (молоть, перемалывать)

1. I *grind* the beans right before brewing.
2. She *ground* pepper over the entire plate.
3. Someone *has ground* coffee into the carpet.
4. The mill *grinds* corn for the whole county.

**2. Rub or scrape harshly.** Of teeth and of gears — anything that grates under pressure. (скрежетать, тереть со скрипом)

5. He *grinds* his teeth in his sleep.
6. The gears *ground* every time he shifted.

**3. Sharpen or shape on a wheel.** Trade use: knives, lenses, valves. Do not confuse the past with the separate regular verb *ground, grounded*, meaning to keep a plane or a teenager at home. (точить, шлифовать)

7. That shop *grinds* lenses to order.
8. They *had ground* the blade down to almost nothing.

**4. Grind to a halt.** A fixed phrase for machinery or an effort stopping slowly and heavily. (постепенно остановиться, застопориться)

9. Traffic *had ground* to a stop by seven.
10. The whole project *ground* to a halt in April.

**5. Grind down — wear someone out.** Figurative, of pressure applied long enough to break resistance. (изматывать, изнурять)

11. The commute *grinds* people down.
12. Six months of this *has ground* him down.

**6. Grind away, grind out.** The source of *the daily grind*. Of dull, effortful, repetitive work. (корпеть; выдавать через силу)

7. The state *hanged* him in 1892.

9. The deadline *hangs* over everything this week.
10. The lawsuit *had hung* over the family for years.

11. They *hang out* at the diner after practice.

**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking

To be on your feet, to be positioned or situated somewhere, or to remain in force.

**1. Be on your feet; rise.** The core intransitive sense. Watch the vowel: *stood* has the short /ʊ/ of *good*, not the long /u/ of *food*. (стоять; вставать)

1. We *stand* for the anthem.
2. She *stood* in line for two hours.
3. He *has stood* at that counter every morning for years.

**2. Be situated.** Of buildings, monuments, and trees — where something is, with no suggestion of movement. (стоять, находиться (о здании))

4. The barn *stands* where the original house was.
5. A church *stood* on that corner until 1961.
6. The statue *has stood* in the square since the war.

**3. Place something upright.** Transitive, usually with a particle or a phrase: *stand it up*, *stand them against the wall*. (поставить (вертикально))

7. *Stand* the ladder against the gutter.
8. He *stood* the bike against the fence.
9. Someone *has stood* all the chairs on the tables.

**4. Remain in force, still be true.** Of records, offers, rulings, and invitations: *the offer stands*. (оставаться в силе, держаться)

10. My offer *stands* until Friday.
11. The old record *stood* for forty years.
12. That ruling *has stood* since 1974.

**5. Tolerate, bear.** Almost always negative or in a question: *I can't stand it*, *how do you stand him?* (терпеть, выносить)

13. I can't *stand* the smell of that soap.
14. She *stood* it for about a week and then quit.
15. He *has* never *stood* being told what to do.

**6. stand trial; stand accused.** Formal and legal, with no article: *stand trial*, *stand accused of fraud*. (предстать перед судом; быть обвиняемым)

16. He will *stand* trial in the spring.
17. She *stood* trial for six weeks.
18. Three executives *have stood* accused of the same scheme.

**7. stand to gain, stand to lose.** Fixed with an infinitive: to be in a position where something is likely to happen to you. (иметь шансы выиграть; рисковать потерять)

19. You *stand* to lose the deposit.
20. They *stood* to make a fortune if the zoning changed.
21. Investors *have stood* to gain from every one of these delays.

**8. stand for.** Two uses: what an abbreviation means — *what does the D stand for?* — and, in the negative, what you refuse to put up with: *I won't stand for it*. (означать, символизировать; мириться с)

22. What does the second D *stand for*?
23. The flag *stood for* something different then.
24. The union *has* never *stood for* that kind of scheduling.

**9. stand out.** To be conspicuous, for good or bad. (выделяться, бросаться в глаза)

25. Bright colors *stand out* in that room.
26. One name *stood out* on the list.
27. Her application *has stood out* from the start.

**10. stand by.** Two uses: to stay loyal to a person or a statement — *I stand by what I said* — and to wait ready to act. (поддерживать; быть наготове)

28. Crews *stand by* whenever there's a storm warning.
29. He *stood by* her through the whole investigation.
30. The company *has stood by* its numbers.

**11. stand up.** Three uses: to rise; to hold up under scrutiny — *the evidence won't stand up in court*; and, informally, to fail to meet someone as arranged. (встать; выдержать проверку; не прийти на встречу)

31. Everybody *stand up*.
32. He *stood* her *up* twice.
33. That theory *has* never *stood up* to a serious test.

**12. stand up for, stand up to.** *Stand up for* someone means to defend them; *stand up to* someone means to face them down. (заступаться за; давать отпор)

34. Somebody has to *stand up for* the new hires.
35. She *stood up to* her manager in front of everyone.
36. He *has stood up for* that kid since day one.

### strike

**Pronunciation:** /straɪk/ &middot; *STRIKE*
**Русский:** ударить; бить; бастовать
**Forms:** *strike* /straɪk/ &middot; *struck* /strʌk/ &middot; *struck* /strʌk/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking
**Pattern:** aɪ — ʌ — ʌ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *strikes* &middot; **-ing form:** *striking* (the silent e drops)
**Contrast:** The ordinary participle is *struck*: *lightning has struck twice*. The older *stricken* survives in fixed expressions — *stricken with grief*, *panic-stricken* — and in the legal sense of deleting: *the remark was stricken from the record*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout: hitting, walking out, a clock sounding the hour, striking a deal. The *strike someone as* frame is the one near-stative use: *that name strikes me as familiar*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in most senses (*the car struck a guardrail*, *they struck a bargain*, *the judge struck the paragraph*), intransitive in the labor sense and in *disaster struck*, *lightning can strike twice*
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *a deal was struck over lunch*, *the remark was stricken from the record* (the older participle survives here), *two of the four claims have been struck*. No passive of the intransitive *the teachers struck*, and ✗ *I am struck as by that name* for the impression frame.

To hit something, to stop work in protest, or to produce a sudden and forceful effect.

**1. Hit forcefully.** Formal for everyday hitting — *he hit me* is ordinary speech, *he struck me* is a report or a legal statement — but standard of objects and vehicles. (ударить, бить)

1. Waves *strike* that seawall all winter.
2. The car *struck* a guardrail.
3. A branch *has struck* the roof twice this year.

**2. Stop work in protest.** Intransitive, and the source of the noun *a strike* and of *striker*. (бастовать, объявить забастовку)

4. Union members *strike* only as a last resort.
5. The teachers *struck* for eight days.
6. Machinists *have struck* this plant three times since 1990.

**3. strike someone as.** Fixed with *as*: to give a particular impression. *That strikes me as odd* is the everyday shape. (казаться, производить впечатление)

7. That name *strikes* me as familiar.
8. It *struck* us as a strange thing to say.
9. The whole plan *has* always *struck* me as backwards.

**4. Hit suddenly, of lightning or disaster.** Of lightning, illness, earthquakes, and bad luck, often with no object at all: *disaster struck*. (поразить, обрушиться, ударить)

10. Lightning can *strike* the same place twice.
11. The flu *struck* three days before the show.
12. The storm *had struck* before anyone got the boats in.

**5. Sound the hour, of a clock.** Of clocks and bells marking time. (бить (о часах), пробить)

13. That bell *strikes* every quarter hour.
14. The clock *struck* two.
15. Midnight *has struck* and nobody has left.

**6. Strike a match.** Of matches, flint, and sparks: the scraping motion that makes fire. (зажечь, чиркнуть (спичкой))

16. *Strike* a match so we can see.
17. He *struck* a match on the sole of his boot.
18. Somebody *has struck* every match in the box.

**7. Reach an agreement or a balance.** Fixed objects: *strike a deal*, *strike a bargain*, *strike a balance*, *strike the right tone*. (заключить (сделку); найти (баланс, тон))

19. We can *strike* a deal today.
20. They *struck* a bargain over lunch.
21. The report *has struck* a careful balance.

**8. Discover by digging.** *Strike oil*, *strike gold* — and figuratively, to hit on something unexpectedly valuable. (напасть на, обнаружить (нефть, золото))

22. Wildcatters still *strike* oil out there occasionally.
23. They *struck* gold in the second creek bed.
24. That studio *has struck* gold twice in a row.

**9. Delete from the record.** Legal, and the place where the older participle *stricken* survives: *the remark was stricken from the record*. *Stricken* also lives on in *panic-stricken* and *stricken with grief*. (вычеркнуть, изъять из протокола)

25. Move to *strike* that answer.
26. The judge *struck* the entire paragraph.
27. The court *has struck* two of the four claims.

**10. strike out.** Three uses: in baseball, to retire a batter on three strikes or to be retired that way; informally, to fail — *I struck out with that job*; and to cross a word out. (выбить на страйках (бейсбол); провалиться; вычеркнуть)

28. He can *strike out* the side on twelve pitches.
29. She *struck out* looking.
30. He *has struck out* on every date this app has sent him.

**11. strike up.** Of conversations and friendships begun on the spot, and of a band beginning to play. (завязать (разговор, дружбу); заиграть)

**Third person:** *winds* &middot; **-ing form:** *winding*
**Contrast:** Two traps in one small word. The verb *wind* is /waɪnd/ and rhymes with *find*; the noun *wind*, moving air, is /wɪnd/ and rhymes with *pinned*. And the past *wound* /waʊnd/ is spelled exactly like the noun *wound* /wund/, an injury: *he wound the clock* and *he cleaned the wound* look like relatives and sound nothing alike.
**Verb type:** action in the coiling and clock senses, but **stative** in *the road winds through six miles of switchbacks*, which describes a shape rather than a motion
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive when you coil or tighten something (*she wound the yarn into a ball*, *he wound the watch*), intransitive when a road or a river takes a twisting course and in *wind up at a diner*, *the party wound down*
**Passive:** **restricted** — yes in the transitive senses: *that clock has not been wound since Grandpa died*, *tape has been wound around that handle three times*, *the company's affairs have been wound up*. No passive of the stative *the river has wound through this valley*.

To turn something around a center, or to follow a twisting course.

**1. Follow a twisting course.** Of roads, rivers, and paths. Remember the verb is /waɪnd/, rhyming with *find*; the noun *wind*, moving air, is /wɪnd/. (виться, извиваться, петлять)

1. The road *winds* through six miles of switchbacks.
2. The trail *wound* along the ridge for another hour.
3. The river *has wound* through this valley for ten thousand years.

**2. Coil something around.** Transitive: yarn into a ball, tape around a handle, a cable around a post. (наматывать, обматывать)

4. *Wind* the cord around the post twice.
5. She *wound* the yarn into a ball.
6. He *has wound* tape around that handle three times.

**3. Wind a clock or a watch.** To tighten a spring by turning a key or a crown; also of winding up a toy. Note that the past *wound* /waʊnd/ is spelled exactly like the unrelated noun *wound* /wund/, an injury. (заводить (часы, механизм))

7. You have to *wind* the old clock every Sunday.
8. He *wound* the watch and set it by the radio.
9. Nobody *has wound* that clock since Grandpa died.

**4. wind up.** Two uses: to end in a situation you did not plan — *we wound up at a diner* — and to bring something to a close: *wind up the meeting*, *wind up a company*. In British English *wind someone up* also means to tease them. (оказаться в итоге; закончить, свернуть)

10. You'll *wind up* paying twice.
11. We *wound up* at a diner on Route 9.
12. The receiver *has wound up* the company's affairs.

**5. wind down.** Of an operation shrinking gradually, or of a person easing off at the end of a day. (сворачивать, сходить на нет; расслабляться)

13. They *wind down* production every August.
14. The party *wound down* around one.
15. The program *has wound down* to two staff.

**6. Blow a horn.** Archaic and literary, found in hunting and heroic writing: *he wound his horn*. You will meet it in older texts, never in speech. (трубить (в рог))

16. The huntsman *winds* his horn at the edge of the wood.
17. Roland *wound* his horn at Roncesvalles.
18. The watchman *had wound* the great horn before the gates closed.

### withhold

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθˈhoʊld/ &middot; *with-HOHLD*
**Русский:** вычитать, удерживать
**Forms:** *withhold* /wɪθˈhoʊld/ &middot; *withheld* /wɪθˈhɛld/ &middot; *withheld* /wɪθˈhɛld/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** formal — legal, tax, and administrative writing
**Pattern:** oʊ — ɛ — ɛ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *withholds* &middot; **-ing form:** *withholding*
**Contrast:** It follows [hold](#hold). Spell it with both h's — *with* plus *hold* — because ✗ *withold* is a frequent slip.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: keeping back information or consent, deducting tax, holding back a reaction
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory: *withhold evidence*, *withhold taxes*, *withhold judgment*; ✗ *the agency withheld* is incomplete unless the object is recoverable from context
**Passive:** **yes** — *four pages of the report were withheld*, *taxes are withheld from every paycheck*, *comment was withheld for three days*

To keep back something you could give — money, information, or consent.

**1. Keep back information or consent.** The general sense. Spell it with both h's — *with* plus *hold* — because ✗ *withold* is a frequent slip. (утаивать, не давать, скрывать)

1. You cannot *withhold* evidence from the defense.
2. The agency *withheld* four pages of the report.
3. He *has withheld* his consent so far.

**2. Deduct from pay.** American tax vocabulary: employers *withhold* income tax from every paycheck. The noun is *withholding*. (удерживать (налог из зарплаты))

4. Employers *withhold* taxes from every paycheck.
5. They *withheld* too little all year and she owed in April.
6. The company *has withheld* the wrong amount for two quarters.

**3. Hold back a reaction.** Of judgment, comment, or emotion: *withhold judgment until you have the numbers*. (воздерживаться, сдерживать (суждение, чувства))

7. *Withhold* judgment until you've read the whole thing.
8. She *withheld* comment for three days.
9. He *had withheld* his anger for as long as he could.

**See also:** [hold](#hold), [uphold](#uphold), [behold](#behold), [withstand](#withstand)

### withstand

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθˈstænd/ &middot; *with-STAND*
**Русский:** противиться; устоять, выстоять; выдержать
**Forms:** *withstand* /wɪθˈstænd/ &middot; *withstood* /wɪθˈstʊd/ &middot; *withstood* /wɪθˈstʊd/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** neutral — ordinary in writing about stress and weather, less common in speech
**Pattern:** æ — ʊ — ʊ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *withstands* &middot; **-ing form:** *withstanding*
**Contrast:** It follows [stand](#stand).
**Verb type:** **stative** in its commonest use — *these panels withstand winds over a hundred miles an hour* states a property of the panels, not something they do, and the progressive is odd: ✗ *the panels are withstanding a property test*. It reads as an action only of a one-time event: *the seawall withstood three hurricanes*.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory: *withstand the pressure*, *withstand scrutiny*; ✗ *the bridge has withstood* is incomplete
**Passive:** **restricted** — grammatical but stiff and rarely written, because the verb describes the subject's capacity rather than an action done to the object: ✗ *three hurricanes were withstood by the seawall* and ✗ *that kind of scrutiny is withstood by few arguments* both read badly. Keep it active.

To hold out against force or pressure without giving way.

**1. Endure physical force.** Of materials and structures — panels, seawalls, roofs. It follows *stand*. (выдерживать, выносить (нагрузку))

1. These panels *withstand* winds over a hundred miles an hour.
2. The seawall *withstood* three hurricanes.
3. The bridge *has withstood* everything the river has thrown at it.

**2. Resist pressure or opposition.** Figurative: to hold up against scrutiny, temptation, or attack. (противостоять, устоять перед)

4. Few arguments *withstand* that kind of scrutiny.
5. The company *withstood* two hostile bids.
6. Her account *has withstood* every cross-examination.

**See also:** [stand](#stand), [understand](#understand), [withhold](#withhold)

### wring

**Pronunciation:** /rɪŋ/ &middot; *RING*
**Русский:** скручивать; сжимать
**Forms:** *wring* /rɪŋ/ &middot; *wrung* /rʌŋ/ &middot; *wrung* /rʌŋ/
**Verb class:** irregular
**Register:** everyday — ordinary American, mostly in *wring out* and *wring your hands*
**Pattern:** ɪ — ʌ — ʌ &middot; **Group:** 3
**Third person:** *wrings* &middot; **-ing form:** *wringing* (the w stays silent)
**Contrast:** The w is silent, so *wring* sounds exactly like *ring* and *wrung* exactly like *rung*. They are different verbs: *ring, rang, rung* is a bell; *wring, wrung, wrung* is a wet towel.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic: twisting out liquid, extracting something under pressure, wringing your hands, wringing a neck
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is obligatory in every sense: *wring the shirt out*, *wring a raise out of management*, *wring your hands*
**Passive:** **yes** — *every towel in the house has been wrung out*, *a raise was wrung out of management*, *every dollar had been wrung out of the budget*. The fixed *wring one's hands* stays active.

To twist something forcefully, especially to squeeze the liquid out of it.

**1. Twist to squeeze out liquid.** Often with *out*: *wring out a shirt*. The w is silent, so *wring* sounds exactly like *ring* and *wrung* like *rung* — but *ring, rang, rung* is a bell, a different verb entirely. (выжимать, отжимать)

1. *Wring* the shirt out before you hang it.
2. He *wrung* the mop over the bucket.
3. She *has wrung* out every towel in the house.

**2. Extract with difficulty.** Figurative, usually with *out of*: to get something from someone only by sustained pressure. (выбить, вырвать (согласие, деньги))

4. You will not *wring* a straight answer out of him.
5. The union *wrung* a raise out of management.
6. They *had wrung* every dollar out of the budget.

**3. wring one's hands.** Literally a gesture of distress; figuratively, to fret in public without doing anything. The noun is *hand-wringing*. (заламывать руки; сокрушаться)

7. Don't just *wring* your hands about it.
8. She *wrung* her hands the whole time the jury was out.
9. Commentators *have wrung* their hands over this for a decade.

**4. Twist violently, of a neck.** Of killing a bird by twisting its neck, and in the threat *I could wring his neck*, meaning I am furious with him. (свернуть (шею))

10. Farmers used to *wring* a chicken's neck without thinking twice.
11. He *wrung* the bird's neck and got on with dinner.
12. My grandmother *had wrung* a hundred chickens' necks before she was twenty.
