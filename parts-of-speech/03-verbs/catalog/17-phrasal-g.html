Phrasal verbs: get — grow#

Phrasal verbs built on the irregular verbs get, give, go… — where the meaning is in the particle, not the verb.

← Irregular verbs · The grammar of verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle that behaves as one word, and its meaning is almost never the sum of its parts: give up has nothing to do with giving, and get over the flu has nothing to do with being above anything. The second thing to learn about each one is separability — whether the object may sit between the verb and the particle (get the message across, get it across) or must follow the whole phrase (get over the flu, never ✗ get the flu over) — because a separable verb with a pronoun object has to split: give it up, never ✗ give up it. Stress is the audible clue to which kind you are hearing: a true particle takes the stress (give it UP, get it aCROSS), while a preposition leans on the verb and stays weak (GO after him, GET to me). The five bases below are among the most productive in English — get alone accounts for more than a third of this page, and grind for four entries, which is all it honestly has.

get across#

Pronunciation
/ˌɡɛt əˈkrɔs/ · get-uh-CROSS · across is an adverb particle here, so it takes the stress — get it aCROSS, never GET it across
Русский
донести (мысль), объяснить доходчиво
Base verb
get
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
yes — get your point across or get across your point, and with a pronoun the object must split: get it across, never ✗ get across it.
Transitive
either — usually takes an object (get the idea across), but the message itself can be the subject: the warning got across.
Register
everyday
Verb type
action — dynamic and causative: you cause an idea to land (she gets her ideas across in meetings), and the progressive is normal — I'm still not getting it across. No stative or linking use.
Passive
restricted. Causative get fights the passive even where there is an object: the point was finally gotten across is possible but stiff, and Americans say the point finally got across instead. The object-less the joke got across has no passive at all.

Make an idea land — to say it in a way the other person actually takes in.

  1. She never quite gets her ideas across in meetings.
  2. He got across the danger in three sentences.
  3. I don't think the joke got across.
  4. Try a diagram if the numbers won't get the point across.
  5. I said it twice and still couldn't get it across.
  6. The ad gets across one message and one only.

get after#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡɛt ˌæftər/ · GET-af-ter · after is a preposition, so the verb keeps the main stress — GET after him
Русский
подгонять, дёргать (чтобы сделал); взяться за дело
Base verb
get
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — the object follows the whole phrase: get after your brother. ✗ get your brother after, ✗ get him after.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
informal — very common in American speech, rare in writing
Verb type
action — dynamic in both senses, nagging a person (my mother got after me for weeks) and attacking a job (let's get after it); the progressive is at home in each — somebody's getting after the landlord about the heat.
Passive
no. ✗ the landlord was gotten after about the heat is not English, and get after it is a frozen idiom with nothing to promote: ✗ it was gotten after.

Keep at somebody until they do the thing; or, of a job, attack it with energy.

1. Nag somebody into acting. American and informal, usually with about or to do. You get after a person, not after a task. (подгонять, дёргать)

  1. You need to get after him about that permission slip.
  2. My mother got after me for weeks until I called the dentist.
  3. Somebody should get after the landlord about the heat.

2. get after it — throw yourself into the work. A fixed American expression, at home in gyms, locker rooms, and job sites. (поднажать, взяться за дело)

  1. Practice starts at six, so let's get after it.
  2. They came out after halftime and really got after it.

Base verb
get
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle)
Separable
not applicable — no object. The other person arrives through with: get along with my sister, never ✗ get my sister along.
Transitive

Have a friendly relationship with somebody; also, manage well enough with what you have.

  2. I never got along with my first boss.
  3. They have gotten along much better since the move.

2. Manage, cope. Usually with on or without, about money, health, or age. (справляться, обходиться)

  1. She gets along on very little money.
  2. How is your father getting along after the surgery?

3. Be on your way. Dated in America, and always in the progressive: I should be getting along. (отправляться, идти)

  1. It's late — I should be getting along.

Base verb
get
Type
phrasal verb (verb + particle); prepositional when an obstacle follows
Separable
no — when there is an object it follows the whole phrase: get around the rules, never ✗ get the rules around.
Transitive

Register
everyday — British English says get round

Move about from place to place; find a way past something in your way; or, of news, spread.

1. Move about, travel. About mobility rather than distance: how a person navigates a town or a house. (передвигаться)

  1. He gets around town on an old bicycle.
  2. Since the hip replacement she gets around with a cane.

2. Evade something standing in your way. Rules, laws, and paperwork are the usual objects. (обходить (закон, правило))

  1. There is no getting around the paperwork.
  2. Their lawyers found a way to get around the ban.

3. Spread, of news. The subject is word, it, or a rumor; no object at all. (распространяться, расходиться)

  1. Word got around fast.
  2. It got around that he was leaving.

get at#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡɛt ət/ · GET-at · at is a preposition and normally reduces to /ət/, with the stress on the verb — though it takes the stress when it ends a question: What are you getting AT?
Русский
добраться до; клонить к; придираться к
Base verb
get
Type
prepositional verb (verb + preposition)
Separable
no — get at the truth, never ✗ get the truth at.
Transitive
yes, always takes an object
Register
everyday — the criticizing sense is informal
Verb type
action — dynamic in the reach, criticize, and tamper senses (the raccoons got at the trash, his manager is always getting at him). The be driving at sense is a verb of meaning and lives almost entirely in the progressive: what are you getting at?
Passive
restricted. The tamper sense passivizes naturally — the witness had been gotten at before the trial. Elsewhere it is marginal or impossible: the shelf can't be gotten at without a ladder is stiff, and ✗ what was being gotten at? fails outright.

Reach something hard to reach, or drive at a point, or keep picking at a person.

1. Reach, gain access to. Physical or informational: a high shelf, a locked file, the facts. (добраться до, достать)

  1. I can't get at the shelf without a ladder.
  2. The raccoons got at the trash again.

2. Be driving at, imply. Almost always in a question or a negative, and usually in the progressive. (клонить к, иметь в виду)

  1. What exactly are you getting at?
  2. I couldn't tell what she was getting at.

3. Keep criticizing somebody. Informal, and always about a person. (придираться к, доставать)

  1. He feels like his manager is always getting at him.

4. Tamper with, reach corruptly. Of witnesses, juries, and officials — someone has bribed or threatened them. (подкупить, повлиять на)

  1. Somebody had gotten at the witness before the trial.

Hand something over for nothing; or let a secret slip.

  1. His face gave him away.
  2. Don't give away the ending.

3. Lose through carelessness. Sports and negotiation: hand the other side an advantage. (упустить, отдать (победу))

  1. They gave away a three-goal lead in eleven minutes.

4. Present the bride. The wedding sense, always with a person as the object. (вести к алтарю, отдавать (невесту))

  1. Her uncle gave her away.
