# All three forms identical

> The base, past, and past participle are spelled the same. The one trap is *read*, where the spelling never changes but the past forms are pronounced /rɛd/.

[← The grammar of verbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Fifty-eight verbs in this group do nothing at all when they change tense. *Put* is *put* is *put*, and *cut*, *shut*, and *cost* behave the same way, so *I cut it yesterday* and *I have cut it twice* both use the form you started with (Все три формы одинаковы). What carries the tense instead is everything around the verb — a time word, an auxiliary, or the -s of the present third person (*he cuts* against *he cut*). One verb breaks the calm: *read* keeps its spelling but shifts its vowel, so the past and participle are said /rɛd/, and *I read it* is two different sentences depending on how you say it.

### beset

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsɛt/ &middot; *bih-SET*
**Русский:** осаждать, нападать со всех сторон
**Forms:** *beset* /bɪˈsɛt/ &middot; *beset* /bɪˈsɛt/ &middot; *beset* /bɪˈsɛt/
**Register:** rare — met almost only in the passive *beset by delays*; few Americans produce it otherwise
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *besets* &middot; **-ing form:** *besetting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** Almost always passive — *beset by* or *beset with* — so the participle is the form you actually meet: *a project beset by delays*. There is no *besetted*; it takes every form from [set](#set).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the *beset by delays* participle is so common that it works almost adjectivally, naming a condition rather than an event.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** in all three senses — the object is whatever the trouble surrounds (*doubts beset her*, *wolves beset the camp*). The object is obligatory: ✗ *small delays beset*.
**Passive:** **yes**, and the passive is the form you actually meet — *the expedition has been beset by bad weather since Tuesday*, *a crown beset with rubies*.

To trouble or harass someone from every side at once; in older use, to surround or hem something in.

**1. Trouble from every side.** The living sense, and almost always passive — *beset by* delays, *beset with* problems. The trouble is the subject and the person or project it surrounds is the object. (осаждать, донимать, преследовать)

1. Small delays *beset* every large construction job.
2. Doubts *beset* her the whole night before the interview.
3. The expedition *has been beset* by bad weather since Tuesday.
4. By March the project *had been beset* with lawsuits.

**2. Surround, hem in.** The literal military sense — attackers closing in on all sides. Literary and largely archaic; modern American English says *surround* or *besiege*. (окружать, обступать)

5. Wolves *beset* the camp on three sides that winter.
6. The travelers *had been beset* by bandits before they reached the pass.

**3. Stud, set thickly with.** Very rare and poetic: a surface *beset with* jewels. You will meet it only in older texts and in translations that keep an old flavor. (усыпать, украшать (драгоценностями))

7. A crown *beset* with rubies lay on the cushion.
8. The binding *has been beset* with small pearls along the spine.

### bet

**Pronunciation:** /bɛt/ &middot; *BET*
**Русский:** держать пари, биться об заклад
**Forms:** *bet* /bɛt/ &middot; *bet* /bɛt/ &middot; *bet* /bɛt/
**Register:** everyday — one of the verbs an American uses without thinking, in money talk and in *I bet*
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *bets* &middot; **-ing form:** *betting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** American English keeps *bet* in the past — *he bet fifty dollars* — where British writing sometimes allows *betted*. Watch the other trap too: *I bet* meaning "I'm fairly sure" (*I bet she's late*) is present tense even though it is the same shape as the past.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the wagering sense; *I bet she's late* and *you bet* are fixed speech formulas, present tense despite looking like the past.
**Transitivity:** **both** — intransitive (*I never bet*), transitive (*he bet twenty dollars*), ditransitive (*I'll bet you ten dollars*), and it also takes a that-clause.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the money sense passivizes stiffly (*a fortune was bet on that race*), and the idioms have no passive at all: ✗ *that she's late is bet by me*.

To risk money on an outcome, or, in everyday speech, to say you are fairly sure of something.

**1. Wager money on an outcome.** The core sense: *bet* an amount *on* something, or *bet someone* that something will happen. American English keeps *bet* in the past and participle where British writing sometimes allows *betted*. (делать ставку, ставить деньги, биться об заклад)

1. I never *bet* more than I can afford to lose.
2. He *bet* twenty dollars on the Sunday game and lost it.
3. She *has bet* on that same horse three years running.

**2. I bet — I'm fairly sure.** Present tense even though it is the same shape as the past, and nearly always first person. Conversational, and often ironic — *I bet you did*. (держу пари, спорим, наверняка)

4. I *bet* she's stuck in traffic again.
5. I'll *bet* he never even read the memo.

**3. You bet — certainly.** A fixed spoken reply meaning “yes, definitely,” and across much of the U.S. also a casual “you're welcome.” Speech only. (конечно, ещё бы; не за что)

6. “Can you cover Friday?” “*You bet*.”
7. “Thanks for the ride.” “*You bet*.”

**4. Bet on — rely on.** Figurative: to treat an outcome as safe enough to plan around. Very often negative — *don't bet on it*, *I wouldn't bet on him*. (рассчитывать на, полагаться на)

8. Don't *bet* on the weather holding through Sunday.
9. The whole plan *had bet* on interest rates staying low.

### broadcast

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbrɔdˌkæst/ &middot; *BRAWD-kast*
**Русский:** передавать по радио, вещать
**Forms:** *broadcast* /ˈbrɔdˌkæst/ &middot; *broadcast* /ˈbrɔdˌkæst/ &middot; *broadcast* /ˈbrɔdˌkæst/
**Register:** everyday — ordinary for radio, TV, and streaming, and figuratively for spreading news
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *broadcasts* &middot; **-ing form:** *broadcasting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Edited American prose keeps *broadcast* in all three slots — *the game was broadcast live*. *Broadcasted* turns up in casual and technical writing, but most editors still change it. [Rebroadcast](#rebroadcast), [telecast](#telecast), and [simulcast](#simulcast) behave the same way.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, from transmitting a program to scattering seed across a pasture.
**Transitivity:** **both** — normally transitive (*NPR broadcast the hearings live*), intransitive when the station itself is the subject (*we broadcast from a studio downtown*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the everyday form — *that episode has been broadcast in forty countries*, *the mix had been broadcast over the bare ground before the rain*.

To send a program or signal out over radio, television, or the internet; more loosely, to tell something to everyone.

**1. Transmit a program.** The main sense, for radio, TV, and streaming alike. Edited American prose keeps *broadcast* in all three slots; *broadcasted* is common in speech but most editors still change it. (передавать в эфир, транслировать, вещать)

1. The station *broadcasts* the local news at six.
2. NPR *broadcast* the hearings live all day Thursday.
3. That episode *has been broadcast* in forty countries.

**2. Announce to everyone.** Figurative and usually disapproving — spreading something widely that should have stayed private. (разглашать, раззвонить, трубить о)

4. Please don't *broadcast* my phone number to the whole office.
5. He *broadcast* the news before the family had been told.

**3. Scatter seed by hand.** The original agricultural sense — throwing seed widely instead of sowing it in rows — and the source of the radio meaning. Still current among farmers and gardeners. (разбрасывать (семена), сеять вразброс)

6. We *broadcast* clover seed across the pasture in March.
7. The mix *had been broadcast* over the bare ground before the rain.

**See also:** [cast](#cast), [rebroadcast](#rebroadcast), [telecast](#telecast), [simulcast](#simulcast), [forecast](#forecast)

### burst

**Pronunciation:** /bɝst/ &middot; *BURST*
**Русский:** взрываться; разрываться
**Forms:** *burst* /bɝst/ &middot; *burst* /bɝst/ &middot; *burst* /bɝst/
**Register:** everyday — pipes, balloons, tears, and laughter all burst in ordinary speech
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *bursts* &middot; **-ing form:** *bursting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** There is no *bursted*. Because the past is identical to the present, the time word carries the tense: *pipes burst every winter* is a habit, *the pipe burst last night* is one event that is over.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and sudden throughout; *be bursting with* and *be bursting to* are progressive-only frames.
**Transitivity:** **both**, but overwhelmingly **intransitive** (*the pipe burst*, *she burst into the room*); transitive only in a narrow set — *burst a balloon*, *burst a blood vessel*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the intransitive senses have no passive at all (✗ *the room was burst into*); the rare transitive allows *a blood vessel was burst by the strain*, but *popped* or *broke* reads better.

To break open suddenly under pressure, or to move or act with sudden force.

**1. Break open under pressure.** Of pipes, balloons, tires, dams — anything that gives way all at once. There is no *bursted*, so the time word carries the tense. (лопаться, разрываться, прорываться)

1. Balloons *burst* if you fill them too far.
2. Water pipes *burst* all over the city during the freeze last January.
3. Two of the tires *have burst* since we bought the trailer.

**2. Burst into — move suddenly.** Sudden, forceful movement into or out of a place; the preposition carries the direction. (врываться, вылетать (откуда-л.))

4. She *burst* into the room without knocking.
5. Three kids *had burst* out of the building before the alarm stopped.

**3. Burst into tears or flames.** A fixed frame for something that starts all at once, with a noun after *into*. *Burst into tears*, *burst into flames*, and *burst into song* are the common ones. (разразиться (слезами), вспыхнуть, грянуть)

6. He *burst* into tears in the middle of the ceremony.
7. That model *has burst* into flames twice on the highway.

**4. Burst out laughing / crying.** The same idea with an -ing verb instead of a noun. *Burst out* also introduces sudden speech: *“Enough!” he burst out*. (расхохотаться, разрыдаться)

8. He *bursts* out laughing every time he hears that song.
9. She *burst* out crying before she got through the first sentence.

**5. Be bursting with — overflowing.** Progressive only, and usually figurative — bursting with pride, energy, or news. A closet can be *bursting with* clothes as well. (быть переполненным (чем-л.))

10. The closets are *bursting* with things nobody uses.
11. He was *bursting* with news about the promotion.

**6. Be bursting to do something.** Informal, progressive only, and always with an infinitive: barely able to hold back from doing something. (не терпеться (что-л. сделать))

12. She was *bursting* to tell someone the whole time.
13. I am *bursting* to ask him what really happened.

**See also:** [bust](#bust), [split](#split), [thrust](#thrust), [hurt](#hurt)

### bust

**Pronunciation:** /bʌst/ &middot; *BUST*
**Русский:** сломать, разбить, разрушить
**Forms:** *bust* /bʌst/ &middot; *bust* /bʌst/ or *busted* /ˈbʌstɪd/ &middot; *bust* /bʌst/ or *busted* /ˈbʌstɪd/
**Register:** everyday — informal, but wholly ordinary in American speech and crime reporting
**Pattern:** no change (or regular *busted*) &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *busts* &middot; **-ing form:** *busting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Informal in every sense, and the regular *busted* is the commoner American past and participle: *the police busted him*, *my phone is busted*. Invariant *bust* sounds more British. In writing that matters, use *broke*, *arrested*, or *raided* instead.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and informal throughout; the participle *busted* doubles as an adjective (*my phone is busted*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive in the break, raid, and demote senses (*they busted the lock*, *vice busted that club*), intransitive of a couple in *bust up* (*they busted up over money*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and the *get*-passive is the commonest form — *he got busted for driving without a license*, *two sergeants have been busted since the investigation opened*.

Informal: to break something, to raid or arrest, or to reduce someone in rank. Everyday American usage prefers the regular *busted*.

**1. Break, smash.** Informal for *break*. American English usually says *busted* in the past and participle; the invariant *bust* sounds British or old-fashioned. In writing that matters, use *broke*. (сломать, разбить, расколотить)

1. Don't *bust* the lock — I have a key somewhere.
2. He *bust* his ankle skateboarding last summer.
3. Two of the hinges *had bust* clean off before we noticed.
4. My brother *busted* his phone screen again.

**2. Raid or arrest (police).** Slang from law enforcement and crime reporting: police *bust* a club, or *bust* someone *for* something. Write *raided* or *arrested* in formal prose. (накрыть, арестовать, устроить облаву)

5. Vice *has bust* that club twice this year.
6. He got *busted* for driving without a license.

**3. Demote in rank.** Military and police usage, usually passive: *he was busted to private*. Always regular here. (разжаловать, понизить в звании)

7. The colonel *busted* him to private for the stunt.
8. Two sergeants *have been busted* since the investigation opened.

**4. Bust up — break apart, brawl.** *Bust up* a fight, a marriage, or the furniture; intransitively, a couple *busts up*. Informal throughout. (разгромить, разнести; поссориться, разойтись)

9. They *busted up* over money after eleven years.
10. Somebody *had busted up* the picnic tables during the night.

**5. Bust a gut, bust chops.** Set slangy phrases: *bust a gut* is to work or laugh very hard, and *bust someone's chops* is to nag or needle them. (надрываться, из кожи вон лезть; придираться, пилить)

11. He *busted* a gut getting the truck loaded before dark.
12. Don't *bust* my chops about the deadline — I know.

### cast

**Pronunciation:** /kæst/ &middot; *KAST*
**Русский:** кидать; бросать; проводить кастинг
**Forms:** *cast* /kæst/ &middot; *cast* /kæst/ &middot; *cast* /kæst/
**Register:** everyday — everyone casts a vote, a shadow, a line, or a show
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *casts* &middot; **-ing form:** *casting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** No -ed form: *the film was cast in three weeks*, never *casted*. *Casted* survives only as shop talk inside the entertainment industry. Every compound here — [broadcast](#broadcast), [forecast](#forecast), [recast](#recast), [miscast](#miscast), [typecast](#typecast), [downcast](#downcast) — copies it exactly.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in every sense, literal and figurative alike; the participle is adjectival in *cast iron*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — overwhelmingly transitive (*cast a ballot*, *cast an unknown actor as the lead*); intransitive with a particle (*we cast off at dawn*, *she cast about for a polite way to say no*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *the bell was cast in a foundry outside Cincinnati*, *the whole film had been cast before the script was finished*.

To throw or send something out, to choose actors for parts, or to shape molten metal in a mold.

**1. Choose actors for parts.** The commonest modern sense: *cast someone as* a character, or *cast* a film as a whole. Never *casted* in edited prose — *casted* survives only as shop talk inside the industry. (распределять роли, проводить кастинг, утверждать на роль)

1. They *have cast* an unknown actor as the lead.
2. The director *had cast* the whole film before the script was finished.
3. She *casts* mostly out of open auditions.

**2. Throw, cast a line.** The old literal sense, alive in fishing (*cast a line*) and in writing with a formal flavor. Ordinary speech says *throw*. (бросать, кидать, забрасывать)

4. She *cast* the line into the shallow water and waited.
5. Learn to *cast* into the wind and you can fish anywhere.

**3. Cast a vote or ballot.** The fixed phrase for voting; *cast a ballot* and *cast a vote* are equally standard, and the news writes little else. (подавать голос, голосовать, опускать бюллетень)

6. Voters *cast* their ballots on Tuesday.
7. More than a million people *have cast* early votes this cycle.

**4. Cast light, a shadow, doubt.** A frame for whatever a thing projects, literal or figurative: *cast a shadow*, *cast light on the problem*, *cast doubt on a claim*. (отбрасывать (тень), проливать (свет), бросать тень сомнения)

8. The lamp *casts* a long shadow across the hallway.
9. The audit *has cast* doubt on every figure in the report.

**5. Cast a glance or eye.** A slightly formal way of saying *look*. Common in writing, rare in speech. (бросить взгляд, окинуть взглядом, просмотреть)

10. He *cast* a quick glance at the door and kept talking.
11. Would you *cast* an eye over these numbers before I send them?

**6. Cast blame or suspicion.** To direct blame, suspicion, or aspersions at someone. Formal, and a staple of reporting on scandals. (возлагать (вину), бросать тень (подозрения))

12. Don't *cast* the blame on people who weren't in the room.
13. The memo *had cast* suspicion on the wrong department.

**7. Cast a spell.** Fixed in magic and fantasy — *cast a spell on someone*. The figurative use follows easily: a place can *cast a spell over* you. (наводить чары, колдовать, заколдовывать)

14. The witch *casts* a spell that turns the prince to stone.
15. That coastline *has cast* a spell over painters for a century.

**8. Cast metal in a mold.** The foundry sense, and the source of *cast iron* and a plaster *cast*. Usually passive, and the participle often works as an adjective. (отливать (металл), формовать)

16. The bell *was cast* in a foundry outside Cincinnati.
17. They *cast* the replacement part in bronze last month.

**9. Cast off / cast aside.** *Cast off* is to untie a boat and, in knitting, to finish the last row; *cast aside* is to discard something or someone. Both are on the formal side. (отдавать швартовы; отбрасывать, отвергать)

18. We *cast* off at dawn and were out of the harbor by six.
19. He *had cast* aside every draft before the good one came.

**10. Cast about / around for.** To search for something intangible — an idea, an excuse, a replacement. Literary rather than spoken. (искать, подыскивать, ломать голову в поисках)

20. She *cast* about for a polite way to say no.
21. The committee *has cast* around for a new chair since March.

### clear-cut

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklɪrˌkʌt/ &middot; *KLEER-kut*
**Русский:** сплошая вырубка леса
**Forms:** *clear-cut* /ˈklɪrˌkʌt/ &middot; *clear-cut* /ˈklɪrˌkʌt/ &middot; *clear-cut* /ˈklɪrˌkʌt/
**Register:** rare — a forestry term; what Americans use daily is the unrelated adjective *a clear-cut case*
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *clear-cuts* &middot; **-ing form:** *clear-cutting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** The verb is far rarer than the adjective *clear-cut* meaning "obvious" — *a clear-cut case of fraud*. As a verb it takes its forms straight from [cut](#cut).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic, and purely a forestry verb; the common adjective *a clear-cut case of fraud* is a separate word that never behaves like it.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is the tract of forest (*loggers clear-cut that ridge*); it drops only when the practice itself is meant (*the company clear-cuts about a thousand acres a year*).
**Passive:** **yes**, and it is the usual form in reporting — *half the watershed has been clear-cut since the mill reopened*.

To log an area of forest by taking every tree at once.

**1. Log every tree at once.** A forestry term, and the only verb sense there is. The far commoner adjective *clear-cut* — *a clear-cut case of fraud* — is a separate word and never behaves like this verb. (вырубать лес сплошь, вести сплошную рубку)

1. The company *clear-cuts* about a thousand acres a year.
2. Loggers *clear-cut* that ridge back in the 1970s.
3. Half the watershed *has been clear-cut* since the mill reopened.
4. The slope *had been clear-cut* long before the state bought it.
5. They plan to *clear-cut* the northern parcel next spring.

### cost

**Pronunciation:** /kɔst/ &middot; *KAWST*
**Русский:** стоить
**Forms:** *cost* /kɔst/ &middot; *cost* /kɔst/ &middot; *cost* /kɔst/
**Register:** everyday — one of the first hundred verbs anyone uses
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *costs* &middot; **-ing form:** *costing* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** Two verbs share this spelling. Meaning "have a price," it never changes: *the repair cost four hundred dollars*. Meaning "work out the price of something," it is regular: *the contractor costed the job at $12,000*. Only that second, accounting sense takes *costed*.
**Verb type:** **stative** in the price and loss senses — a property of the thing, not an act, and it rejects the progressive: ✗ *the ticket is costing ninety dollars*. Only the accounting sense (*costed the job*) is a true action verb.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** with an amount (*the repair cost four hundred dollars*), and **ditransitive** with the person first (*it cost him the promotion*, *that mistake has cost us two customers*).
**Passive:** **no** in the price and loss senses — ✗ *four hundred dollars was cost by the repair*, ✗ *the promotion was cost him*. Only the dynamic accounting sense passivizes: *the job was costed at twelve thousand dollars*.

To have a particular price, or to make someone lose something.

**1. Have a price.** The core sense, invariant in all three forms: *the repair cost four hundred dollars*. Never *costed* here. (стоить)

1. A ticket *costs* about ninety dollars now.
2. Good tools *cost* money, but they last.
3. The repair *cost* more than the car was worth.
4. Rent *has cost* us more every year since we moved in.

**2. Make someone lose something.** The thing lost is the object and the person is the indirect object: *it cost him the promotion*. Money is not involved. (стоить (чего-л.), лишить, обойтись в)

5. That one mistake *has cost* us two customers already.
6. His temper *cost* him the promotion last year.

**3. Take effort or sacrifice.** Of effort rather than price: *it cost her a great deal to say that*. Slightly formal, and the object is usually an amount of pain or trouble. (даваться ценой, стоить усилий)

7. It *cost* her more than she let on to walk away.
8. Speaking up in that meeting *had cost* him every ally he had.

**4. Cost a fortune.** Fixed informal phrases for something expensive. *That'll cost you* is a joking price attached to a favor. (стоить целое состояние, влететь в копеечку)

9. Parking downtown *costs* an arm and a leg on game days.
10. Getting the roof fixed *cost* us an arm and a leg.

**5. Work out a price (costed).** The one sense that is regular — *costed*, never invariant *cost*. Accounting and contracting writing only. (калькулировать, рассчитывать себестоимость)

11. The contractor *costed* the job at twelve thousand dollars.
12. We *have costed* out both options and the second is cheaper.

### crosscut

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkrɔsˌkʌt/ &middot; *KRAWS-kut*
**Русский:** пересекать
**Forms:** *crosscut* /ˈkrɔsˌkʌt/ &middot; *crosscut* /ˈkrɔsˌkʌt/ &middot; *crosscut* /ˈkrɔsˌkʌt/
**Register:** rare — woodworking and film-editing shop talk, mostly met in *crosscut saw*
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *crosscuts* &middot; **-ing form:** *crosscutting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** Mostly technical — a saw cut across the grain, or a film that alternates between two scenes. Forms follow [cut](#cut), and in the film sense it is interchangeable with [intercut](#intercut).
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; the -ing form has hardened into an adjective in policy writing (*crosscutting concerns*, *crosscutting cleavages*).
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive on a board, a scene, or a set of categories (*he crosscut the plank*, *these loyalties crosscut every party line*), intransitive of how a tool behaves and in *crosscut between two conversations*.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the chase had been crosscut with shots of the empty house*.

To cut across something — across the grain of a board, or between two scenes on film.

**1. Saw across the grain.** Woodworking. A crosscut goes across the grain; ripping goes along it, and the tool for the first is a *crosscut saw*. (пилить поперёк волокон, делать поперечный распил)

1. You *crosscut* the board first, then rip it to width.
2. He *crosscut* the plank a quarter inch too short.
3. This blade *crosscuts* cleanly but tears the grain when you rip.

**2. Alternate two scenes on film.** Editing vocabulary, interchangeable with *intercut*. Usually *crosscut between* two lines of action. (монтировать параллельно, чередовать сцены)

4. The editor *has crosscut* between the two conversations.
5. The director *had crosscut* the chase with shots of the empty house.

**3. Cut across, run counter to.** Academic and policy writing, usually about loyalties or categories that ignore the usual dividing lines. The -ing form does most of the work — *crosscutting concerns*, *crosscutting cleavages*. (пересекать, идти вразрез, перекрывать границы)

6. These loyalties *crosscut* every party line in the region.

### cut

**Pronunciation:** /kʌt/ &middot; *KUT*
**Русский:** резать
**Forms:** *cut* /kʌt/ &middot; *cut* /kʌt/ &middot; *cut* /kʌt/
**Register:** everyday — among the commonest verbs in the language, literal and figurative alike
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *cuts* &middot; **-ing form:** *cutting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** Never *cutted*. Every compound in this file — [undercut](#undercut), [intercut](#intercut), [uppercut](#uppercut), [crosscut](#crosscut), [clear-cut](#clear-cut) — copies it without change.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic throughout, literal and figurative; the participle serves as an adjective in *a cut lip*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — normally transitive (*she cut the rope*, *they cut the budget twice*), intransitive of an engine or a person moving in (*the motor cut out*, *a pickup cut in front of us*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *the chapter has been cut down to four pages*, *the whole valley had been cut off by morning*, *I got my hair cut*.

To divide or wound with something sharp, to reduce an amount, and, in a long list of fixed phrases, to stop, shorten, or remove.

**1. Divide with a blade.** The literal sense — knife, scissors, saw. Usually with a phrase saying how: *cut it into slices*, *cut it in half*. Never *cutted*. (резать, разрезать, нарезать)

1. Please *cut* the bread into thick slices.
2. She *cut* the rope with a pocketknife.
3. Someone *has cut* the cake already.

**2. Wound with something sharp.** Transitive and often reflexive, with a body part as object: *I cut my finger*. The participle doubles as an adjective — *a cut lip*. (порезать(ся), поранить)

4. I *cut* my finger on the lid this morning.
5. He *had cut* his hand on the broken glass before anyone noticed.

**3. Reduce an amount.** Of budgets, prices, staff, taxes, and emissions. One of the most common verbs in business and political news. (сокращать, урезать, снижать)

6. Airlines *cut* fares whenever a new route opens.
7. The state *cut* funding before anyone was told.
8. They *have cut* the budget twice since June.

**4. Cut hair.** *Cut someone's hair*, or, for what you had done to you, the passive — *I got my hair cut*. (стричь)

9. She *cuts* everyone's hair in the family.
10. He *cut* his own hair during the lockdown and it showed.

**5. Shorten, trim down.** Taking material out of something: *cut a scene*, *cut two pages*, *cut a trip short*. (сокращать (текст), урезать, обрывать)

11. We *cut* the trip short because of the storm.
12. The editor *has cut* the chapter down to four pages.

**6. Skip class or work.** American school and workplace slang: *cut class*, *cut school*, *cut a shift*. British English says *skive*. (прогуливать)

13. He *cut* class three times last week.
14. Nobody *has cut* a shift since the new schedule went up.

**7. Cut off — interrupt, disconnect, isolate.** Three related uses: interrupting a speaker, ending a supply, and leaving a place unreachable. *Cut someone off* in traffic is a fourth. (перебивать; отключать; отрезать (от мира))

15. She *cut* me off before I finished the sentence.
16. The storm *had cut* off the whole valley by morning.

**8. Cut out — stop, remove.** *Cut it out* means stop that; *cut out* a picture is to remove it; an engine that *cuts out* dies suddenly. (прекратить; вырезать; заглохнуть)

17. *Cut* it out — people are trying to sleep.
18. The motor *cut* out halfway across the lake.
19. They *have cut* two songs out of the set list.

**9. Cut down (on) — reduce; fell.** *Cut down on* something is to consume less of it; *cut down* a tree is to fell it. The object decides which you mean. (сокращать (потребление); срубить)

20. I *have cut* down on coffee since the checkup.
21. They *cut* down the old maple last fall.

**10. Cut in — interrupt, overtake.** *Cut in line*, *cut in* on a conversation, or *cut in* on a car. Separately, *cut someone in* on a deal is to give them a share. (вмешаться, влезть без очереди, подрезать)

22. A pickup *cut* in front of us on the ramp.
23. Somebody *has cut* in line ahead of forty people.

**11. Cut a deal or check.** Fixed American phrases where *cut* simply means make. *Cut a check* is standard business usage for issuing a payment. (заключить сделку; выписать чек; записать пластинку)

24. The two sides *cut* a deal an hour before the deadline.
25. Accounting *has cut* the check already.

6. Six people *fit* in that car if nobody has luggage.
7. The couch *had fit* through the door on the way in, somehow.

8. They *fitted* the whole floor with new smoke alarms.

10. The tailor *fitted* the jacket in twenty minutes.

4. A truck *hit* the guardrail and rolled.
5. Two deer *have been hit* on that stretch this month.

6. Gas prices *have hit* five dollars a gallon.
7. The thermometer *hit* a hundred and four on Sunday.

8. Storms *hit* that coast every fall.

**Third person:** *hurts* &middot; **-ing form:** *hurting* (no spelling change)
**Contrast:** No *hurted*. Since the past is spelled and said exactly like the present, the time word decides: *my knee hurts* is now, *my knee hurt all week* is over. The participle doubles as an adjective — *three people were hurt* — where it means "injured" rather than forming a true passive.
**Verb type:** **both** — action in *I hurt my back* and *the scandal hurt the company*, but the intransitive *my knee hurts* names a state (it still takes the progressive: *my knee is hurting*). *Be hurting* for money is progressive-only.

To cause pain or damage, or to feel pain.

**1. Feel pain.** Intransitive, with the body part as subject: *my knee hurts*. There is no *hurted*, so the time word decides whether it is now or over. (болеть (о части тела))

1. My shoulder *hurts* when it rains.
2. My knee *hurt* all week after the hike.
3. That tooth *has hurt* on and off since Christmas.

**2. Injure, cause physical pain.** Transitive and often reflexive: *I hurt my back*. The passive participle doubles as an adjective — *three people were hurt* means injured rather than forming a true passive. (ушибить, повредить, причинить боль)

4. I *hurt* my back moving the couch on Saturday.
5. Nobody *has been hurt* at that intersection this year.

**3. Wound someone's feelings.** With a person or *feelings* as object. One of the commonest uses of the verb in ordinary speech. (обижать, задевать, ранить)

6. It *hurt* to hear her say that.
7. He never meant to *hurt* anyone.

**4. Damage, harm something.** Of reputations, chances, sales, and teams — the object is a thing rather than a body. (вредить, наносить ущерб)

8. The scandal *had hurt* the company long before the lawsuit.
9. Skipping practice *hurts* the whole team.

**5. It wouldn't hurt to.** A fixed negative frame for giving advice gently, and its cousin *it never hurt anyone*. (не помешало бы, не мешает)

10. It wouldn't *hurt* to call ahead and check.
11. A second opinion never *hurt* anybody.

**6. Be hurting — be struggling.** Informal and progressive only: an industry, a town, or a person short of money is *hurting*. (бедствовать, туго приходиться)

12. Small farms are *hurting* after two dry summers.
13. He was *hurting* for cash until the check cleared.

### let

**Pronunciation:** /lɛt/ &middot; *LET*
**Русский:** позволять
**Forms:** *let* /lɛt/ &middot; *let* /lɛt/ &middot; *let* /lɛt/
**Register:** everyday — a core verb of permission and of a dozen phrasal verbs
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *lets* &middot; **-ing form:** *letting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** Never *letted*. Two things to keep apart: *let* (allow) and *leave* (go away from) — ✗ *Let me alone at the station* — and *let* the verb versus *let's*, a contraction of *let us*. British English also uses *let* for "rent out," which is where [sublet](#sublet) comes from.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and causative, taking a bare infinitive after its object (*let me go*, never ✗ *let me to go*); *let's* is a frozen first-person imperative formula.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — object plus a bare infinitive or a particle (*let the dog out*, *let forty people go*); the object is obligatory: ✗ *he lets*.
**Passive:** **restricted** — the causative has no ordinary passive (✗ *he was let stay out past ten*; say *was allowed to stay*), but the fixed phrasal and rental uses do passivize: *forty people were let go*, *the cottage has been let every summer since 1990*.

To allow something to happen, and, in a long list of fixed phrases, to release, admit, disappoint, or stop.

**1. Allow, permit.** The core sense, with a bare infinitive after the object: *let me go*, never ✗ *let me to go*. Never *letted*, and do not confuse it with *leave*. (позволять, разрешать, давать)

1. My parents never *let* me stay out past ten.
2. He *lets* the dog sleep on the couch.
3. They *have let* the lease run out.

**2. Let me, let's — propose.** *Let me* offers to do something; *let's*, a contraction of *let us*, proposes doing it together. The standard negative is *let's not*. (давай(те), позволь(те))

4. *Let* me know when the package arrives.
5. *Let's* take the back road.

**3. Let go — release; fire.** Literally releasing your grip, figuratively giving up on something, and — as *let someone go* — the polite office word for firing them. (отпускать, выпускать из рук; увольнять)

6. *Let* go of the rope on three.
7. The company *has let* forty people go since January.

**4. Let in, let out.** Of doors, animals, and people. *Let out* also means end, for classes and shows — *school lets out at three*. (впускать; выпускать)

8. Somebody *let* the dog out during the night.
9. She *had let* him in through the back before anyone saw.

**5. Let down — disappoint.** *Let someone down* is to fail them; literally, *let down* a hem or a rope is to lower it. The noun *letdown* comes from the first. (подводить, разочаровывать)

10. He *let* the whole crew down and knew it.
11. The team *has* never *let* its fans down at home.

**6. Let off — excuse; set off.** *Let someone off* is to spare them punishment, *let off steam* is to vent, and *let off* fireworks is to set them off. (отпустить, простить; выпустить (пар), запустить (фейерверк))

12. The judge *let* him off with a fine.
13. Somebody *had let* off a firecracker in the hallway.

**7. Let up — ease off, stop.** Intransitive, of rain, pressure, pain, or effort: *the rain never let up*. (утихать, ослабевать, прекращаться)

14. The rain *let* up around four.
15. The pressure on the department *has* not *let* up all year.

**8. Let on — reveal, admit.** Informal, and usually negative: *don't let on that you know*. (выдавать (тайну), подавать вид)

16. She never *let* on that she had already read it.
17. He *has* not *let* on how much the repair cost.

**9. Let alone — much less.** A fixed connector following a negative: *he can't walk, let alone run*. Separately, *let someone alone* means leave them in peace. (не говоря уже о)

18. I can't afford the rent, *let alone* a car payment.
19. *Let* him alone until he's finished.

**10. Let it slide or slip.** *Let it slide* is to overlook something, *let it slip* is to reveal it by accident, and *let it drop* is to stop discussing it. (спускать на тормозах; проговориться; замять)

20. I *let* it slide the first two times.
21. He *had let* slip the date before the announcement.

**11. Rent out (British).** British English for renting property out — *rooms to let*. American English says *rent*, though *sublet* is standard on both sides and comes from this sense. (сдавать внаём)

22. They *let* the upstairs flat to a student last autumn.
23. The cottage *has been let* every summer since 1990.

### put

**Pronunciation:** /pʊt/ &middot; *PUT*
**Русский:** класть
**Forms:** *put* /pʊt/ &middot; *put* /pʊt/ &middot; *put* /pʊt/
**Register:** everyday — one of the handful of verbs no English sentence gets far without
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *puts* &middot; **-ing form:** *putting* /ˈpʊtɪŋ/ (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** *Putted* is a real word, but it belongs to a different verb — golf's *putt* /pʌt/: *she putted from twelve feet*. The verb *put* never takes -ed: ✗ *I putted the keys on the table*. And the vowel is the one in *foot*, not the one in *cut*, so *putting* /ˈpʊtɪŋ/ and *putting* /ˈpʌtɪŋ/ are two different words.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; it needs a place or manner complement as much as it needs an object.
**Transitivity:** **transitive**, and the object alone is not enough: ✗ *put the milk* — *put the milk back in the refrigerator*. In the three-word *put up with*, the object follows the whole phrase: ✗ *put the noise up with*.
**Passive:** **yes** — *the proposal had been put to the board twice already*, *my chair has been put out in the hallway*.

To move something into a place or a position, and, by extension, to express, assign, or apply something.

**1. Place something somewhere.** The core sense, and it needs a place: *put the milk in the fridge*, not ✗ *put the milk*. Never *putted* — that belongs to golf's *putt*. (класть, ставить, помещать)

1. *Put* the milk back in the refrigerator.
2. I *put* the keys on the counter an hour ago.
3. Someone *has put* my chair out in the hallway.

**2. Express in words.** Almost always with an adverb or a manner phrase: *to put it bluntly*, *how would you put it?*, *well put*. (выражать, формулировать, сказать)

4. *Put* it in your own words and it will make more sense.
5. She *had put* the objection more politely than I would have.

**3. Assign a figure or blame.** The frame is *put X at* a number or *put X on* a person: *put the damage at two million*, *put the blame on the contractor*. (оценивать (в), относить, возлагать (вину))

6. Investigators *put* the damage at two million dollars.
7. Nobody *has put* a firm date on the opening yet.

**4. Put money or effort into.** *Put money into* a business, *put work into* a project, *put your heart into* something. (вкладывать (деньги, силы, труд))

8. They *put* forty thousand dollars into the renovation.
9. He *has put* years into that manuscript.

**5. Put a question to someone.** Formal: *put the motion to a vote*, *put the question to the committee*. Courtroom and debate talk adds *I put it to you that...* (поставить (вопрос), вынести (на рассмотрение))

10. The chair *put* the motion to a vote.
11. The proposal *had been put* to the board twice already.

**6. Put on — wear, stage.** Four unrelated uses of one phrase: *put on a coat*, *put on ten pounds*, *put on a play*, and *put on an act*. *You're putting me on* means you're kidding me. (надевать; набирать (вес); ставить (спектакль); притворяться)

12. *Put* on a jacket — it's colder than it looks.
13. The school *has put* on the same play three years running.

**7. Put off — postpone; repel.** *Put off* a meeting is to postpone it; something that *puts you off* disgusts or discourages you. The adjective *off-putting* comes from the second. (откладывать; отталкивать, отбивать охоту)

14. We *put* the trip off until spring.
15. The smell *had put* half the class off lunch.

**8. Put up with — tolerate.** A fixed three-word phrasal verb; the object always follows the whole thing — *put up with the noise*, never ✗ *put the noise up with*. (мириться с, терпеть)

16. I *have put* up with that noise for two years.
17. She *put* up with a lot before she quit.

**9. Put out — extinguish; issue; inconvenience.** *Put out* a fire, *put out* a statement, or *put someone out*, meaning trouble them. As an adjective, *put out* means annoyed. (тушить; выпускать, издавать; доставлять неудобство)

18. Crews *put* the fire out before it reached the barn.
19. The company *has put* out three statements in two days.

**10. Put down — record, suppress.** Four uses that share nothing but the words: *put your name down*, *put down a rebellion*, *put a dog down*, and *put someone down*, meaning insult them. (записывать; подавлять; усыплять (животное); принижать)

20. *Put* your name down on the list by the door.
21. They *had* to *put* the old dog down last spring.

**11. Put up — build; raise; accommodate.** *Put up* a building or a shelf, *put up* your hand, or *put someone up* for the night. *Put up a fight* means resist. (строить, возводить; поднимать; приютить)

22. They *put* up a new warehouse on the lot last year.
23. My aunt *has put* us up every summer since I was small.

**12. Put through — connect, endure.** *Put a call through* is to connect it; *put someone through* something is to make them go through it; *put someone through school* is to pay for it. (соединять (по телефону); заставить пройти через; оплачивать учёбу)

24. *Put* me through to the front desk, please.
25. She *has put* two kids through college on one salary.

**Pronunciation:** /kwɪt/ &middot; *KWIT*

**Forms:** *quit* /kwɪt/ &middot; *quit* /kwɪt/ &middot; *quit* /kwɪt/
**Register:** everyday — the ordinary American word for stopping or leaving a job
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *quits* &middot; **-ing form:** *quitting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** American English uses *quit* for all three forms — *she quit her job in June*. *Quitted* exists in older and British writing but sounds wrong to American ears. The frame is *quit doing something* (*quit smoking*), never *quit to do something*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; it takes an -ing complement, never an infinitive: ✗ *quit to smoke*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive with a job, an activity, or a program (*she quit her job*, *quit checking your phone*), intransitive when what is given up is understood (*three people have quit since the new manager arrived*).
**Passive:** **restricted** — barely used: ✗ *her job was quit the week after the merger*. Even the computing sense prefers *the app was closed* to *was quit*.

To stop doing something, to leave a job for good, or to close a program.

**1. Stop doing something.** Takes an -ing form, never an infinitive: *quit smoking*, not ✗*quit to smoke*. American English keeps *quit* in all three slots; *quitted* sounds British and dated. (бросать, переставать)

1. I *quit* eating sugar in January and lasted about six days.
2. You should *quit* checking your phone every two minutes.
3. She *has quit* trying to explain it to him.

**2. Leave a job, resign.** The commonest sense in daily American speech. *Quit on someone* adds the sense of abandoning them. (увольняться, уходить (с работы))

4. She *quit* her job the week after the merger.
5. Three people *have quit* since the new manager arrived.
6. He *had quit* the team long before the season ended.

**3. Quit it, quit that.** A blunt spoken imperative, used to children or close friends. Rude from a stranger. (прекрати, хватит)

7. *Quit* it — you're going to break something.
8. My mother told us to *quit* fighting in the back seat.

**4. Close a program.** Computing. On a Mac, *Quit* shuts the whole application down, while *close* only closes one window. (закрывать программу, выходить из приложения)

9. *Quit* the app and open it again.
10. I *quit* Photoshop last night and the file was still locked this morning.
11. The installer won't run until you *have quit* every open program.

**5. Stop working, give out.** Informal, of engines, appliances, and body parts. Often *quit on someone*. (отказывать, глохнуть, ломаться)

12. The mower *quit* halfway through the yard.
13. My knees *have quit* on me since I turned fifty.

### read

**Pronunciation:** /rid/ &middot; *REED*
**Русский:** читать
**Forms:** *read* /rid/ &middot; *read* /rɛd/ &middot; *read* /rɛd/
**Register:** everyday — its irregularity is a trap for the ear, but the verb itself is basic
**Pattern:** spelling unchanged; /i/ — /ɛ/ — /ɛ/ &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *reads* /ridz/ &middot; **-ing form:** *reading* /ˈridɪŋ/ (both keep the REED vowel)
**Contrast:** This is the one verb in the group where the spelling lies. All three forms are *read*, but the past and the participle are pronounced /rɛd/, exactly like the color *red*: *I read (REED) the news every morning* against *I read (RED) it yesterday*. Only context or the speaker's voice separates them, and every prefixed form — [reread](#reread), [misread](#misread), [proofread](#proofread), [sight-read](#sight-read) — does the same thing.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic in the core senses; senses 4 and 5 (*that line reads as sarcastic*, *the sign reads 'No entry after six'*) are **stative** — how a text stands, not something anyone does.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive (*I read two newspapers every morning*), ditransitive (*he read the kids a story*), and intransitive in *read as / read like* and in *she reads every night*.
**Passive:** **yes** in the ordinary senses — *the meter has been read already*, *the whole list had been read aloud*. **No** in senses 4 and 5: ✗ *sarcastic is read as by that line*, ✗ *'No entry' is read by the sign*.

To take in written words, and by extension to interpret, to state, or to display information.

**1. Take in written words.** The core sense. The spelling never changes, but the past and participle are said /rɛd/, exactly like the color *red*. (читать)

1. I *read* (REED) two newspapers every morning.
2. She *read* (RED) the whole novel on the flight to Chicago.
3. He *has read* that book three times.

**2. Read aloud to someone.** Frames: *read to someone*, *read someone something*, *read something back/out*. (читать вслух, зачитывать)

4. Would you *read* this paragraph back to me?
5. He *read* the kids a story every night that summer.
6. By the time we got there the clerk *had read* the whole list aloud.

**3. Interpret, make sense of.** Figurative: read a situation, a person's face, a map, a defense. *Read someone's mind* is fixed. (истолковывать, понимать, разгадывать)

7. A good pitcher *reads* the batter's stance.
8. She *read* the room and dropped the subject.
9. We *have read* the market wrong twice this year.

**4. Come across a certain way.** Intransitive, usually *read as* or *read like*: the impression a text or a person gives. No passive. (восприниматься, звучать (как))

10. That line *reads* as sarcastic in print.
11. The apology *read* as insincere to everyone who saw it.

**5. Say, state (of a text).** Of a sign, label, or document — the wording is such-and-such. Takes no passive. (гласить, быть написанным)

12. The sign *reads* 'No entry after six.'
13. The original draft *read* differently on that point.
14. The plaque *had read* 'Founded 1902' before someone repainted it.

**6. Show or take a reading.** Works in both directions: the instrument *reads* a value, and a person *reads* the instrument. (показывать (о приборе); снимать показания)

15. The gauge *reads* forty pounds.
16. The thermometer *read* ninety-eight at noon.
17. Someone from the utility *has read* the meter already.

**7. Retrieve data (computing).** Of drives, scanners, and programs. The opposite operation is *write*. (считывать (данные))

18. The drive *reads* discs but won't burn them.
19. The scanner *read* the barcode on the second try.
20. The program *has read* the whole file into memory.

**8. read up on.** Learn about a subject by reading, usually in preparation for something. (почитать о, изучить (тему))

21. I need to *read* up on the new tax rules.
22. She *read* up on the company the night before the interview.
23. He *has read* up on every treatment option there is.

**9. read into.** Find a meaning that may not be there. Usually negative: *don't read too much into it*. (домысливать, вкладывать смысл)

24. Don't *read* too much into a one-word answer.
25. The press *read* a threat into a fairly bland statement.

**10. Study a subject (British).** British only, and formal even there: *read history at Cambridge*. Americans say *majored in*. (изучать, учиться (на факультете))

26. She *read* law at Oxford in the eighties.
27. He *has read* classics for three years now.

3. He *has reread* the contract and still has questions.
4. They *had reread* the instructions twice and were still stuck.
5. It helps to *reread* a hard paragraph out loud.

**2. Reinterpret a text.** Academic and critical writing: read something again and come out with a different reading of it. (переосмыслять, по-новому истолковывать)

6. Later critics *reread* the novel as a book about class, not romance.

To return something to its starting state, or to set it again to a new value.

**1. Return to the starting state.** The everyday sense, mostly of devices and accounts. Follows *set*, so never *resetted*. (сбрасывать, возвращать в исходное состояние)

1. *Reset* the router and try again.
2. I *reset* my password twice this morning and still can't get in.

4. Somebody *had reset* the clock an hour ahead.
5. This switch *resets* the counter to zero.
6. We need to *reset* the thermostat for the weekend.

7. Both sides want to *reset* the relationship after the election.
8. The new coach *reset* expectations in his first week.

9. The surgeon *reset* the wrist under anesthesia.
10. The break *had been reset* badly the first time.

**Pronunciation:** /rɪd/ &middot; *RID*

**Forms:** *rid* /rɪd/ &middot; *rid* /rɪd/ &middot; *rid* /rɪd/

**Third person:** *rids* &middot; **-ing form:** *ridding* (the d doubles)
**Contrast:** This verb almost never stands alone. It lives in *rid X of Y* — *rid the house of mice* — and in the fixed phrases *get rid of* and *be rid of*, where *rid* is the participle: *I'm glad to be rid of it*. *Ridded* exists but sounds strange to most Americans.

To free someone or something of something unwanted — almost always in *rid X of Y*, *get rid of*, or *be rid of*.

**1. get rid of.** By far the commonest use of the word, and the one learners need first. *Rid* here is the participle after *get*. (избавиться от, выбросить)

1. I finally got *rid* of the old couch.
2. How do you get *rid* of fruit flies?
3. We got *rid* of almost everything in the garage last weekend.

4. Nothing will *rid* this basement of that smell.
5. The new manager *rid* the department of its worst habits.
6. He *had rid* himself of every reminder of the marriage.

7. I'm glad to be *rid* of that car.
8. She won't be *rid* of the paperwork until spring.

### set

**Pronunciation:** /sɛt/ &middot; *SET*
**Русский:** помещать; ставить, установить
**Forms:** *set* /sɛt/ &middot; *set* /sɛt/ &middot; *set* /sɛt/
**Register:** everyday — one of the most-used verbs in the language, with senses in every domain
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *sets* &middot; **-ing form:** *setting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** Keep *set* apart from *sit*. *Set* takes an object — you *set* something down, set/set/set — while *sit* does not: you *sit* down, sit/sat/sat. ✗ *Set down and rest* is dialect, not standard. Every compound in this file — [beset](#beset), [inset](#inset), [offset](#offset), [reset](#reset), [upset](#upset) — copies *set* exactly.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; it also has a genuine intransitive life in *the sun set* and *let the concrete set*, where nothing is acted on at all.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive by default, and the object is obligatory, unlike *sit*: *set the box on the table*, ✗ *set down and rest*. Intransitive of the sun going down and of concrete, glue, or gelatin hardening.
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the film is set in a small town in Maine*, *bail was set at ten thousand dollars*. No passive for the sun or the concrete: ✗ *the ridge was set behind by the sun*.

To place something deliberately, to fix a value or state, or (of the sun) to go down — one of the busiest verbs in English.

**1. Place something somewhere.** The core sense, and it always takes an object. *Sit* is the one without an object: you *sit* down, but you *set* something down. ✗*Set down and rest* is dialect. (класть, ставить, помещать)

1. *Set* the box on the table by the door.
2. She *set* the tray down without a word.
3. They *had set* the table before the guests arrived.

**2. Fix a time, price, or setting.** Covers deadlines and prices as well as alarms, timers, and thermostats — anything given a value. (назначать, устанавливать, выставлять)

4. The committee *has set* a deadline of March first.
5. The judge *set* bail at ten thousand dollars.
6. She *set* her alarm for five and still slept through it.
7. We need to *set* a price before Monday.

**3. Go down (of the sun).** The one common sense with no object at all. The opposite is *rise*. (садиться, заходить (о солнце, луне))

8. The sun *set* behind the ridge around eight.
9. The moon *sets* before midnight this week.
10. The sun *had set* by the time we reached the trailhead.

**4. Become firm or solid.** Intransitive, of concrete, glue, jelly, and plaster. (застывать, схватываться, твердеть)

11. Let the concrete *set* overnight.
12. The gelatin *set* in about four hours.
13. The glue *has set*, so don't try to move it now.

**5. Establish a standard or record.** Fixed collocations: *set a record*, *set a precedent*, *set an example*, *set the tone*. (устанавливать (рекорд), подавать (пример), задавать (тон))

14. She *set* a school record in the two hundred meter.
15. This decision *sets* a precedent nobody wants.
16. He *has set* the tone for the whole department.

**6. Place a story in a time or place.** Nearly always passive: *the novel is set in 1930s Chicago*. (помещать действие (в определённое время или место))

17. The film *is set* in a small town in Maine.
18. They *had set* the whole play in a single room.

**7. Start someone or something going.** Frames: *set someone to work*, *set someone doing something*, *set something in motion*. (заставлять, побуждать, пускать в ход)

19. The foreman *set* two crews to work on the roof.
20. That remark *set* me thinking.
21. The noise *has set* the dogs barking.

**8. set up.** Very common and broad: arrange, assemble, or establish something. It also means to frame someone: *I was set up*. (устраивать, налаживать, собирать; подставлять)

22. Can you *set* up the projector before nine?
23. We *set* up the tent in the dark.
24. They *have set* up a fund for her medical bills.

**9. set off.** Three uses that share a shape: depart, trigger an alarm or reaction, and detonate something. (отправляться; вызывать; приводить в действие)

25. They *set* off for the coast before dawn.
26. The smoke *set* off every alarm on the floor.
27. One comment *has set* off a week of arguing.

**10. set out.** Depart on a journey, state something in an orderly way, or lay items out. *Set out to do something* means to intend it. (отправляться в путь; излагать; выкладывать)

28. She *set* out to prove them all wrong.
29. The memo *sets* out three options.
30. He *had set* out the chairs an hour early.

**11. set back.** Delay progress, or — informally, always with a person and an amount — cost that much. (задерживать, отбрасывать назад; обойтись (в сумму))

31. The storm *set* the project back a month.
32. That repair *set* me back four hundred dollars.
33. Two recalls *have set* the company back years.

**12. set aside.** Reserve time or money, or — as a legal term — annul a verdict or ruling. (откладывать; отменять (решение))

34. *Set* aside an hour for questions.
35. We *set* aside money every month for the trip.
36. The appeals court *has set* aside the verdict.

**Forms:** *shed* /ʃɛd/ &middot; *shed* /ʃɛd/ &middot; *shed* /ʃɛd/
**Register:** everyday — dogs shed, companies shed jobs, and evidence sheds light

**Third person:** *sheds* &middot; **-ing form:** *shedding* (the d doubles)
**Contrast:** Don't mix it up with [shred](#shred), a different verb one letter away. No *shedded*: *the dog shed all over the couch last night*.

**Transitivity:** **transitive** — hair, leaves, weight, jobs, tears, blood, or water (*the company shed four hundred jobs last quarter*).

To let something fall away — leaves, hair, tears, weight, or a burden.

**1. Drop hair, skin, or leaves.** The literal sense, of animals and plants. No *shedded*. (линять, сбрасывать (шерсть, листву, кожу))

1. Our dog *sheds* year round.
2. The maples *shed* their leaves early that year.
3. That snake *has shed* twice this spring.

4. He *has shed* twenty pounds since January.
5. The company *shed* four hundred jobs last quarter.
6. She *had shed* the last of her doubts by the second interview.

**3. shed tears, shed blood.** Fixed and slightly formal. Often passive in the negative: *not a tear was shed*. (проливать (слёзы, кровь))

7. They *shed* a few tears at the airport and then laughed about it.
8. Nobody wants to *shed* blood over a parking space.
9. Plenty of blood *had been shed* over that border already.

**4. shed light on.** Fixed idiom: make something clearer, usually a question or a mystery. (проливать свет на)

10. The new emails *shed* light on who knew what.
11. Her testimony *has shed* light on a very murky deal.

**5. Repel water.** Of coatings, fabrics, and roofs: water runs off instead of soaking in. (не пропускать (воду), отталкивать)

12. This jacket *sheds* rain but not wind.
13. The tarp *shed* water all night without leaking.

### shut

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʌt/ &middot; *SHUT*
**Русский:** закрывать
**Forms:** *shut* /ʃʌt/ &middot; *shut* /ʃʌt/ &middot; *shut* /ʃʌt/
**Register:** everyday — a core verb, blunter than *close* but just as common
**Pattern:** no change &middot; **Group:** 1
**Third person:** *shuts* &middot; **-ing form:** *shutting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** *Shut* and *close* mean the same thing, but *shut* is blunter and will not sit in front of a noun: *a closed door*, not ✗ *a shut door*. And *shut up* means "be quiet," which is rude.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic; unlike *close* it will not stand in front of a noun as an adjective: *a closed door*, ✗ *a shut door*.
**Transitivity:** **both** — transitive (*shut the window*, *they shut down two plants*), intransitive of a business closing (*the diner shuts at nine on weeknights*).
**Passive:** **yes** in the transitive senses — *the interstate has been shut down twice this month*, *the dog had been shut up in the garage all afternoon*.

To move something into a closed position, and the base of several very common phrasal verbs.

**1. Close something.** Blunter than *close*, and it will not sit in front of a noun: *a closed door*, not ✗*a shut door*. (закрывать)

1. Please *shut* the window — it's freezing.
2. He *shut* the door harder than he meant to.
3. She *shut* her eyes and counted to ten.

**2. Close for business.** Of stores, plants, and offices, temporarily or for good. (закрывать(ся) (о заведении))

4. They *have shut* the plant for two weeks of maintenance.
5. The diner *shuts* at nine on weeknights.
6. The store *had shut* by the time we got downtown.

**3. shut down.** Close an operation permanently, halt an activity, or power a machine off. Also of a person: *he shut down* means he stopped responding. (закрывать, останавливать; выключать)

7. The company *shut* down two plants last year.
8. *Shut* down the laptop before you unplug it.
9. Protests *have shut* down the interstate twice this month.

**4. shut off.** Stop a flow — water, gas, power — or cut something off from access. (перекрывать, отключать)

10. The city *shut* off the water at eight.
11. *Shut* off the burner when it starts to steam.
12. Somebody *had shut* off the alarm during the night.

**5. shut up.** Rude as an order. The second, older sense is to lock someone or something away: *shut up in a room*. (замолчать, заткнуться; запирать)

13. He told them to *shut* up, which did not help.
14. She *shut* up the second her boss walked in.
15. The dog *had been shut* up in the garage all afternoon.

**6. shut out.** Keep something outside, exclude someone, or — in baseball and hockey — hold the other side scoreless. (не впускать, отгораживаться; сыграть всухую)

16. Heavy curtains *shut* out most of the street noise.
17. The rookie *shut* out the Yankees for seven innings.
18. New rules *have shut* small growers out of the market.

**Third person:** *upsets* &middot; **-ing form:** *upsetting* (the t doubles)
**Contrast:** The verb and the participle-adjective are stressed up-SET; the noun — *a major UP-set* — moves the stress forward. Forms follow [set](#set), so never *upsetted*.
**Verb type:** action — dynamic and causative (*it upsets her when people are late*); the participle is a true adjective in *he looked upset*, stressed up-SET.
**Transitivity:** **transitive** — the object is the person, the stomach, the thing knocked over, the favorite, or the plan (*a tenth seed had upset the champion*).
**Passive:** **yes** — *the incumbent was upset by an unknown candidate*, *decades of settled practice have been upset*. Note that *she was upset* is usually the adjective, not a passive.

To make someone unhappy, to knock something over, or to beat a favorite.

**1. Make someone unhappy.** The everyday sense. The participle doubles as an adjective — *he looked upset* — and is stressed up-SET, while the noun *an UP-set* moves the stress forward. (расстраивать, огорчать)

1. It *upsets* her when people are late.
2. The email *upset* him more than he let on.
3. Try not to *upset* the baby right before a nap.

**2. Disturb the stomach.** Fixed collocation: *upset someone's stomach*, and the adjective *an upset stomach*. (расстраивать (желудок))

4. Rich food *has upset* my stomach all week.
5. The medication *upset* his stomach for two days.

**3. Knock something over.** The oldest sense and still current, though *knock over* is commoner in speech. (опрокидывать)

6. He *upset* an entire tray of glasses.
7. The cat *has upset* the water bowl twice today.

**4. Beat a favorite.** Sports and politics. The noun is even commoner: *a major upset*. (неожиданно побеждать фаворита)

9. A tenth seed *had upset* the champion in the first round.
10. An unknown candidate *upset* the incumbent by four points.

**5. Disrupt a plan or balance.** Of schedules, ecosystems, and settled arrangements. (нарушать (планы, равновесие))

11. The strike *upset* our whole delivery schedule.
12. One late shipment can *upset* the balance for a month.
13. The ruling *has upset* decades of settled practice.
