# Verbs

> A verb is the word that says what the subject does, experiences, or is — the engine that turns a group of words into a sentence.

> **📖 Irregular or phrasal verbs?** This file covers the *grammar* of verbs. The **[verb catalog](catalog/README.md)** holds **366 irregular verbs** — grouped by how their three forms change, each with General American pronunciation for all three, a Russian translation, and its full sense inventory — plus **696 phrasal verbs** and **229 verb + preposition patterns** built on them, each saying whether it separates (*turn it off*) or not (*look after it*). There is also an **[audio browser](catalog/browse.html)**.

## 1. What it does

The verb is the only word class that every complete English sentence must have. It carries the action or state, and it also carries the *time* information (tense), the *view of the action* (aspect), the *speaker's attitude* (mood and modality), and the *direction of the action* (voice). Nouns name things; verbs make things happen.

A verb also determines how many other pieces the sentence needs. `Sleep` needs only a subject (*The baby slept*). `Buy` needs a subject and an object (*She bought a car*). `Give` can take two objects (*He gave me the keys*). This requirement is called the verb's **valency**, and getting it wrong is one of the fastest ways to produce a sentence that sounds foreign.

Finally, verbs agree with their subjects in the present tense, and they change form to build tenses. This means a single verb can appear in many shapes — *go, goes, going, went, gone* — all of them the same word.

Annotated examples:

> My sister **teaches** third grade in Denver.
> — `teaches` is a present-simple action verb, third person singular (hence `-es`), transitive (it takes the object *third grade*).

> The soup **tastes** salty, so I **should have added** less broth.
> — `tastes` is a linking verb joining *soup* to the adjective *salty*; `should have added` is a modal + perfect auxiliary + past participle, expressing regret about the past.

> The bridge **was being repaired** when we **drove** past.
> — `was being repaired` is past continuous passive (three words doing one job); `drove` is an irregular past simple.

> **Don't** **let** him **forget** to **call**.
> — `Don't` is an auxiliary in the negative imperative, `let` is a causative, `forget` is a bare infinitive after *let*, and `to call` is a full infinitive.

## 2. Types

Verbs are classified along several independent axes. A single verb is usually several things at once: *give* is a lexical verb, an action verb, and ditransitive, all at the same time. Read each `###` below as one dimension of description, not as a mutually exclusive box.

### 2.1 Action (dynamic) verbs

Verbs that describe something the subject *does* — a physical or mental activity with duration or a clear endpoint. They accept the continuous aspect and the imperative.

Common members: *run, write, build, eat, throw, study, decide, listen, buy, break, drive, cook, explain, jump, paint, send*.

- She **runs** four miles every morning.
- They **are building** a deck behind the house.
- **Throw** me the ball.

### 2.2 Stative verbs

Verbs that describe a state, a relation, a possession, an opinion, or a sense perception rather than an activity. They usually resist the continuous form.

Common members: *know, believe, understand, want, need, like, love, hate, prefer, own, belong, contain, consist, seem, resemble, cost, weigh, mean, recognize, doubt, deserve*.

- I **know** the answer. (not *I am knowing*)
- This box **contains** old photographs.
- The house **belongs** to my aunt.

Some verbs are stative in one meaning and dynamic in another: *I **think** you're right* (opinion, stative) vs. *I**'m thinking** about moving* (mental activity, dynamic). See section 8.

### 2.3 Linking (copular) verbs

Verbs that connect the subject to a **subject complement** — a noun or adjective that renames or describes the subject. No action is transferred; nothing "receives" anything.

Common members: *be, become, seem, appear, look, feel, sound, taste, smell, grow, turn, get, remain, stay, prove, go* (in *go bad*), *come* (in *come true*).

- The forecast **is** wrong again.
- Your plan **sounds** expensive.
- He **became** a citizen last spring.

Test: if you can replace the verb with a form of *be* and the sentence still roughly works, it is linking. *Your plan **is** expensive.* Compare *She **looked** at the map* (action — no complement, a prepositional phrase instead).

### 2.4 Auxiliary (helping) verbs

Verbs that carry grammatical information and combine with a main verb: *be, have, do*. They form tenses, the passive, questions, negatives, and emphasis.

- **be** — continuous aspect and the passive: *He **is** working. The check **was** mailed.*
- **have** — perfect aspect: *They **have** arrived. She **had** already left.*
- **do** — "dummy" auxiliary for questions, negatives, and emphasis in the simple tenses: ***Do** you smoke? I **didn't** see it. I **do** like it.*

All three also work as full lexical verbs: *I **am** tired. We **have** a dog. She **did** the dishes.*

### 2.5 Modal auxiliary verbs

A closed set of auxiliaries expressing possibility, necessity, permission, ability, and prediction. They never take `-s`, `-ing`, or `-ed`, they are always followed by a bare infinitive, and they never stack (`*must can`).

Core modals: *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must*.

- You **must** show your ID at the door. (obligation)
- It **might** rain tonight. (possibility)
- She **could** read at age four. (past ability)

### 2.6 Semi-modals (marginal / quasi-modals)

Multi-word expressions that behave like modals in meaning but inflect at least partly like ordinary verbs.

Members: *have to, have got to, need to, ought to, be able to, be going to, be supposed to, be allowed to, used to, had better, would rather, dare, need* (as a bare modal in negatives/questions).

- I **have to** renew my license. / She **has to** renew hers.
- You **ought to** apologize.
- We **used to** live in Tucson.
- You **had better** leave now.
- I **would rather** stay home.

### 2.7 Transitive verbs

Verbs that require a direct object to complete their meaning.

Members: *make, take, bring, buy, need, want, discuss, enjoy, mention, prefer, describe, contain, resemble, lack*.

- She **mentioned** your name. (not *She mentioned.*)
- They **enjoyed** the concert.
- We **discussed** the budget. (Not *discussed about* — see section 6.)

### 2.8 Intransitive verbs

Verbs that take no direct object. Anything after them is an adverbial or a prepositional phrase, not an object.

Members: *arrive, go, come, sleep, die, fall, happen, exist, rise, sit, lie, laugh, cry, appear, disappear, wait, listen, belong*.

- The package **arrived** yesterday.
- Nothing **happened**.
- He **laughed** at my haircut. (*at my haircut* is a prepositional phrase, not an object.)

Intransitive verbs cannot be made passive: *\*It was happened* is impossible.

### 2.9 Ditransitive verbs

Verbs that take two objects: an **indirect object** (the recipient) and a **direct object** (the thing).

Members: *give, send, tell, show, offer, lend, teach, buy, make, pass, hand, promise, owe, sell, bring, wish, ask, cost, charge*.

- She **gave** me the keys. / She **gave** the keys **to** me.
- He **bought** his daughter a bike. / He **bought** a bike **for** his daughter.
- They **asked** me a question.

Note which preposition each verb takes when the order flips: *to* for transfer verbs (*give, send, tell, lend, hand, offer, show, pass, promise, sell, teach*), *for* for benefit verbs (*buy, make, cook, find, get, save, order*). Some verbs allow no reordering at all: *It **cost** me twenty dollars* (not *\*cost twenty dollars to me*).

### 2.10 Ambitransitive verbs

Verbs that work both ways, often with a meaning shift.

Members: *eat, read, write, sing, drive, win, change, break, open, close, grow, boil, melt, move, stop, start, increase*.

- We **ate** at six. / We **ate** dinner at six.
- The door **opened**. / She **opened** the door.
- The ice **melted**. / The sun **melted** the ice.

The *break/melt/open* pattern is called the **ergative** (or causative-inchoative) alternation: the object of the transitive use becomes the subject of the intransitive use.

### 2.11 Complex-transitive verbs

Verbs that take a direct object plus an **object complement** describing or renaming that object.

Members: *make, call, name, elect, consider, find, keep, leave, paint, appoint, declare, believe, think*.

- They **elected** her treasurer.
- The noise **drove** me crazy.
- We **painted** the kitchen yellow.

### 2.12 Prepositional verbs

Verb + preposition combinations where the preposition is fixed and the object always follows it. The two parts cannot be separated.

Members: *look at, listen to, wait for, depend on, believe in, apologize for, insist on, refer to, approve of, consist of, rely on, care about, deal with*.

- I'm **waiting for** the bus. (not *\*waiting the bus for*)
- Everything **depends on** the weather.
- She **apologized for** the delay.

### 2.13 Phrasal verbs

Verb + adverb particle, where the combination has a meaning you cannot deduce from the parts. Covered in detail in section 5.6.

- Please **turn off** the lights.
- The meeting **was called off**.
- I **made up** an excuse on the spot.

### 2.14 Phrasal-prepositional verbs

Three-part combinations: verb + particle + preposition. Always inseparable at the preposition.

Members: *put up with, look forward to, get along with, run out of, come up with, catch up on, look down on, get away with, cut down on, keep up with, face up to, drop out of*.

- I can't **put up with** this noise.
- We **ran out of** coffee.
- She **came up with** a better plan.

### 2.15 Reflexive verbs

Verbs whose object is a reflexive pronoun referring back to the subject. English has far fewer obligatory reflexives than Spanish, French, or German.

Members that require a reflexive: *pride oneself on, avail oneself of, perjure oneself, absent oneself, busy oneself*. Optional reflexives: *hurt, enjoy, blame, help, teach, cut, introduce, behave, dress, wash, shave*.

- He **prides himself on** his punctuality.
- **Help yourself** to more pie.
- I **cut myself** shaving.

Note that *wash, shave, dress, hide, shower* normally drop the reflexive in English: *I got dressed*, not *\*I dressed myself* (unless you're contrasting with someone else dressing you).

### 2.16 Causative verbs

Verbs that express making, letting, helping, or getting someone to do something. Covered in full in section 5.7.

Members: *make, have, let, get, help, cause, force, allow, permit, enable, require*.

- The teacher **made** us rewrite the essay.
- I **had** my car repaired.
- She **got** her brother **to** drive her.

### 2.17 Delexical (light) verbs

Verbs with almost no meaning of their own that combine with a noun to express the action.

Members: *have, take, make, do, give, get, pay*.

- **have** a drink, a snack, a conversation, an argument, a baby, a good time
- **take** a walk, a break, a shower, a nap, a look, a picture, a chance
- **make** a decision, a mistake, an effort, a call, progress
- **do** homework, business, the dishes, research, a favor
- **give** a speech, a hug, advice, permission, a hand
- **pay** attention, a visit, a compliment

Sentences: *Let's **take a break**. She **made a mistake**. I need to **do some research**.*

Where British English pairs these nouns with *have*, American English usually chooses *take*: AmE *take a shower, take a look, take a nap, take a rest*; BrE *have a shower, have a look, have a nap, have a rest*.

### 2.18 Impersonal (weather / dummy-subject) verbs

Verbs used with the dummy subject *it* that refers to nothing.

Members: *rain, snow, hail, drizzle, pour, thunder, get* (dark/late), *seem, appear, happen, turn out, matter*.

- **It's raining** hard.
- **It got** dark early.
- **It seems** that we're too late.

### 2.19 Performative verbs

Verbs whose utterance in the simple present *performs* the act they name.

Members: *promise, apologize, declare, name, pronounce, resign, admit, deny, accept, refuse, warn, thank, congratulate, sentence, object*.

- I **promise** to be there.
- I **apologize** for the confusion.
- I hereby **declare** the meeting adjourned.

### 2.20 Finite vs. non-finite verbs

A **finite** verb shows tense and agrees with a subject; it can head an independent clause. A **non-finite** verb (infinitive, gerund, participle) shows no tense and no agreement.

- She **works** downtown. (finite)
- She wants **to work** downtown. (non-finite infinitive)
- **Working** downtown is exhausting. (non-finite gerund)
- **Exhausted** by the commute, she quit. (non-finite past participle)

### 2.21 Infinitives

The base form, with or without *to*.

- **Full (to-)infinitive:** *I want **to leave**. It's hard **to explain**.*
- **Bare infinitive:** after modals (*I can **swim***), after *let, make, have* causatives (*She let me **go***), after perception verbs (*I saw him **fall***), after *had better / would rather* (*You'd better **hurry***), and after *why (not)* (*Why **wait**?*).
- **Perfect infinitive:** *She seems **to have forgotten**.*
- **Continuous infinitive:** *He appears **to be lying**.*
- **Passive infinitive:** *This needs **to be signed**.*
- **Split infinitive:** *to boldly go* — grammatically fine in American English, though some editors avoid it.

### 2.22 Gerunds

The `-ing` form used as a noun. It can be a subject, an object, or the object of a preposition.

- **Swimming** is good exercise. (subject)
- I enjoy **cooking**. (object)
- She's good at **negotiating**. (after a preposition)
- He admitted **having lied** to us. (perfect gerund)
- I hate **being interrupted**. (passive gerund)

A gerund can take a possessive or objective subject of its own: *I don't mind **his leaving** early* (formal) / *I don't mind **him leaving** early* (everyday American English).

### 2.23 Participles

**Present participle (`-ing`)** — active, ongoing:
- The **crying** baby kept us awake.
- **Walking** home, I saw the accident.

**Past participle (`-ed` / third form)** — passive, completed:
- a **broken** window; a **written** agreement
- **Exhausted**, he sat down.
- The car was **stolen**.

**Perfect participle** — sequence:
- **Having eaten**, we went for a walk.

### 2.24 Lexical (main) verbs

The open class of ordinary verbs carrying real meaning — everything not an auxiliary or modal. New members enter English constantly: *google, text, email, unfriend, screenshot, ghost, onboard, livestream*.

- She **googled** the address.
- He **texted** me twice.

## 3. Forms and inflection

English verbs have at most **five** distinct forms (the verb *be* is the sole exception, with eight).

Every English verb is either **regular** or **irregular**. A regular verb builds both its past simple and its past participle with `-ed`, so V2 and V3 are identical and fully predictable from the base: *walk → walked → walked*. An irregular verb forms V2 and/or V3 some other way — a vowel change (*sing → sang → sung*), a consonant change (*build → built → built*), an `-en` participle (*take → took → taken*), or no change at all (*cut → cut → cut*). There are roughly 200 irregular verbs in current use, but they include the most frequent verbs in the language, so they cannot be avoided. Regular verbs are the open, productive class: every new verb English coins (*google, text, unfriend*) is regular.

| Form | Name | Example (*talk*) | Example (*write*) | Example (*be*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | base / infinitive | talk | write | be |
| V2 | past simple | talked | wrote | was / were |
| V3 | past participle | talked | written | been |
| V4 | 3rd person singular present | talks | writes | is |
| V5 | present participle / gerund | talking | writing | being |

### 3.1 Third person singular `-s`

| Rule | Examples |
|---|---|
| Add `-s` to most verbs | work → works; run → runs; play → plays |
| Add `-es` after *-s, -sh, -ch, -x, -z, -o* | pass → passes; wash → washes; watch → watches; fix → fixes; buzz → buzzes; go → goes; do → does |
| Consonant + `-y` → `-ies` | study → studies; carry → carries; try → tries |
| Vowel + `-y` → just `-s` | play → plays; buy → buys; enjoy → enjoys |
| Irregular | have → has; be → is; do → does /dʌz/; say → says /sɛz/ |

### 3.2 Regular past `-ed`

| Rule | Examples |
|---|---|
| Add `-ed` | walk → walked; open → opened |
| Verb ends in `-e` → add `-d` | hope → hoped; live → lived |
| Consonant + `-y` → `-ied` | carry → carried; apply → applied |
| One syllable, CVC → double the final consonant | stop → stopped; plan → planned; beg → begged |
| Two syllables, stress on the last → double | permit → permitted; refer → referred; occur → occurred |
| Two syllables, stress on the first → **no** doubling in American English | travel → traveled; cancel → canceled; label → labeled |

Pronunciation of `-ed`: /t/ after voiceless sounds (*walked, kissed, laughed*), /d/ after voiced sounds (*played, robbed, moved*), /ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ (*wanted, needed, decided*).

### 3.3 `-ing` spelling

| Rule | Examples |
|---|---|
| Add `-ing` | go → going; read → reading |
| Drop silent `-e` | make → making; write → writing; come → coming |
| Keep `-e` in *-ee, -ye, -oe* | see → seeing; agree → agreeing; dye → dyeing; tiptoe → tiptoeing |
| One syllable CVC → double | run → running; sit → sitting; swim → swimming |
| Stressed final syllable → double | begin → beginning; prefer → preferring; forget → forgetting |
| `-ie` → `-ying` | die → dying; lie → lying; tie → tying |
| Unstressed second syllable → no doubling (AmE) | travel → traveling; cancel → canceling |

### 3.5 The verb *be* in full

| Subject | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|
| I | am | was |
| you *(singular and plural)* | are | were |
| he / she / it | is | was |
| we / they | are | were |

The remaining forms are invariable: base **be**, present participle **being**, past participle **been**. That makes eight distinct forms in all — *be, am, is, are, was, were, being, been* — more than any other English verb.

Contracted and negative forms: *I'm, you're, he's, we're, they're*; *isn't, aren't, wasn't, weren't*. There is no standard contraction for *am not*; in tag questions Americans say *aren't I?* (*I'm next, **aren't I**?*). *Ain't* is nonstandard in all persons.

Subjunctive forms differ from the indicative: present subjunctive **be** for every subject (*It is vital that she **be** informed*), past subjunctive **were** for every subject (*If he **were** here…*). See section 3.8.

### 3.6 The 12 tense-aspect combinations

Three times (past, present, future) × four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). Verb: *write*, subject: *she*.

| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Past** | she wrote | she was writing | she had written | she had been writing |
| **Present** | she writes | she is writing | she has written | she has been writing |
| **Future** | she will write | she will be writing | she will have written | she will have been writing |

Uses, one example each:

| Tense | Core use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | habits, facts, schedules | *The store **opens** at nine.* |
| Present continuous | happening now, temporary, near-future arrangements | *I**'m staying** with my cousin this week.* |
| Present perfect | past action with present relevance; unfinished time | *I**'ve lost** my keys.* |
| Present perfect continuous | duration up to now | *She**'s been waiting** for an hour.* |
| Past simple | completed action at a finished past time | *We **moved** here in 2019.* |
| Past continuous | background action interrupted by another | *I **was cooking** when the power went out.* |
| Past perfect | the earlier of two past events | *The train **had left** before we got there.* |
| Past perfect continuous | duration before a past point | *He **had been driving** for six hours when he stopped.* |
| Future simple | prediction, spontaneous decision, promise | *I**'ll call** you tonight.* |
| Future continuous | in progress at a future time | *This time tomorrow I**'ll be flying** to Chicago.* |
| Future perfect | completed before a future deadline | *By June we**'ll have paid off** the loan.* |
| Future perfect continuous | duration up to a future point | *In August she**'ll have been teaching** for twenty years.* |

Note: English has no future *tense* in the morphological sense — no `-s`/`-ed`-style ending marks the future. The future is expressed with modals (*will, shall*), with *be going to*, with present continuous (*We're leaving Friday*), or with present simple for schedules (*The flight leaves at 6*).

### 3.7 Voice

| Voice | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Active | subject does the action | *The committee **approved** the plan.* |
| Passive | subject receives the action: *be* + past participle | *The plan **was approved** (by the committee).* |

Passive in all tenses (verb: *build*):

| Tense | Passive |
|---|---|
| Present simple | The houses **are built** quickly. |
| Present continuous | The house **is being built**. |
| Present perfect | The house **has been built**. |
| Past simple | The house **was built** in 1920. |
| Past continuous | The house **was being built** that year. |
| Past perfect | The house **had been built** by then. |
| Future simple | The house **will be built** next year. |
| Future perfect | The house **will have been built** by June. |
| Modal | The house **must be built** to code. |
| Infinitive | The house needs **to be built** now. |
| Gerund | She hates **being told** what to do. |

Perfect continuous passives (*\*has been being built*) are grammatically possible but avoided in practice.

Only transitive verbs passivize. Use the passive when the doer is unknown, obvious, unimportant, or deliberately hidden: *My bike **was stolen**. Taxes **are collected** in April.*

Get-passive (informal, often for bad outcomes): *He **got fired**. She **got promoted**.*

### 3.8 Mood

| Mood | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Indicative | states facts, asks questions | *She **lives** in Boise. **Does** she **like** it?* |
| Imperative | commands, requests, instructions (base form, subject *you* understood) | ***Sit** down. **Don't touch** that. **Let's go**.* |
| Subjunctive | hypothetical, wished-for, or demanded situations | *I wish I **were** taller.* |

**Present (mandative) subjunctive** — the bare base form in a *that*-clause after verbs and adjectives of demand, suggestion, and necessity. American English uses this far more consistently than British English.

Triggers: *demand, insist, require, request, suggest, recommend, propose, ask, urge, order, move, advise*; and *it is essential / important / necessary / vital / crucial / imperative that*.

- The judge ordered that he **pay** the fine. (not *pays*)
- I insist that she **be** present.
- It is essential that every student **submit** the form.
- We recommend that the report **not be** released. (negative: *not* + base, no *do*)

**Past (were-)subjunctive** — *were* for all persons in unreal conditions and wishes.

- If I **were** you, I'd take the job.
- She acts as if she **were** the boss.
- I wish it **were** Friday.
- Suppose he **were** to refuse.

**Formulaic subjunctive** — fixed expressions: *God bless you. Be that as it may. Come what may. Long live the queen. So be it. Heaven forbid.*

**Conditionals** (closely tied to mood):

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Zero | if + present, present | *If you **heat** ice, it **melts**.* |
| First | if + present, will + base | *If it **rains**, we**'ll cancel**.* |
| Second | if + past/were, would + base | *If I **had** more time, I**'d learn** guitar.* |
| Third | if + past perfect, would have + V3 | *If she **had studied**, she **would have passed**.* |
| Mixed | if + past perfect, would + base | *If I **had taken** that job, I**'d be** in Seattle now.* |

## 4. Position and word order

### 4.1 Basic clause order

American English is firmly **Subject–Verb–Object**. The verb follows the subject and precedes the object; you cannot separate a verb from its direct object with an adverb.

- *Maria* **fixed** *the sink*.
- ✗ *She speaks fluently English.* → ✓ *She speaks English fluently.*

### 4.2 Order inside the verb phrase

When multiple auxiliaries stack, they appear in a fixed order:

**modal → have (perfect) → be (continuous) → be (passive) → main verb**

| Example | Breakdown |
|---|---|
| She **has been working**. | have + be(cont) + V-ing |
| It **must have been damaged**. | modal + have + be(pass) + V3 |
| The road **is being resurfaced**. | be(cont) + be(pass) + V3 |
| He **could have been being followed**. | modal + have + be(cont) + be(pass) + V3 (rare but grammatical) |

Each auxiliary determines the form of the next word: modal → base; *have* → past participle; *be*(continuous) → `-ing`; *be*(passive) → past participle.

### 4.3 Questions

Invert the subject and the **first** auxiliary. If there is no auxiliary in the simple tenses, insert *do/does/did*.

- She **is** leaving. → **Is** she leaving?
- They **have** finished. → **Have** they finished?
- He **works** here. → **Does** he **work** here? (base form after *do*)
- You **saw** it. → **Did** you **see** it?

Subject questions take no *do*: *Who **called**?* not *\*Who did call?*

### 4.4 Negation

*Not* goes after the first auxiliary; without one, insert *do*.

- She **is not** (isn't) coming.
- They **have not** (haven't) decided.
- I **do not** (don't) agree.
- He **did not** (didn't) know.

### 4.5 Adverb placement relative to the verb

| Adverb type | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency (*always, often, never, rarely*) | before the main verb, after *be*, after the first auxiliary | *She **often works** late. He **is often** late. I **have never been** there.* |
| Manner (*quickly, carefully*) | usually after the object, or before the verb | *He drove **carefully**. / He **carefully** opened the box.* |
| Time (*yesterday, soon*) | usually end position | *We left **yesterday**.* |
| Degree (*really, almost*) | before the verb | *I **almost** forgot.* |

### 4.6 Phrasal verb and object order

- **Separable + noun object:** either order — *Turn **off** the light. / Turn the light **off**.*
- **Separable + pronoun object:** must split — *Turn it **off**.* (✗ *\*Turn off it*)
- **Inseparable:** never split — *I ran **into** an old friend.* (✗ *\*I ran an old friend into*)

### 4.7 Imperatives and inversion

Imperatives put the verb first: ***Close** the door.* Negative imperatives use *don't*: ***Don't close** it.* Emphatic: ***Do** sit down.*

Inversion also occurs after fronted negatives: *Never **have I seen** such a mess. Rarely **does he** complain. Not only **did she** win, she set a record.*

## 5. Rules and patterns

### 5.1 Subject–verb agreement

The verb agrees with the subject in person and number, but only in the present tense (and in *was/were* for *be*).

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Third person singular takes `-s` in the present simple | *He **works** here. They **work** here.* |
| Compound subject with *and* → plural | *Tom and Ana **are** here.* |
| Compound subject naming one thing → singular | *Macaroni and cheese **is** my favorite.* |
| Subjects joined by *or / nor / either…or / neither…nor* → verb agrees with the **nearer** subject | *Neither the manager nor the employees **were** informed. / Neither the employees nor the manager **was** informed.* |
| Intervening phrases don't change agreement | *The box of chocolates **is** on the table.* |
| *As well as, along with, together with, in addition to* don't create a plural | *The CEO, along with two aides, **was** present.* |
| Indefinite pronouns *everyone, somebody, each, either, neither, nobody* → singular | *Everybody **wants** a raise. Each of the boys **has** a locker.* |
| *None, some, all, most, half, any* → depends on the noun that follows | *Some of the pie **is** gone. Some of the cookies **are** gone.* |
| *A number of* → plural; *the number of* → singular | *A number of seats **are** still open. The number of seats **is** limited.* |
| *Each / every* before a compound subject → singular | *Every book and magazine **was** on sale.* |
| Gerund or infinitive subject → singular, whatever nouns it contains | *Grading forty essays **takes** all weekend. To forgive **is** divine.* |
| *That*-clause subject → singular | *That they arrived late **doesn't** surprise me.* |
| Quantity/measure as one unit → singular | *Ten miles **is** a long walk. Twenty dollars **is** too much.* |
| Nouns ending in `-s` but singular in meaning | *The news **is** good. Physics **was** hard. Mathematics **is** required.* |
| Plural-only nouns | *These scissors **are** dull. My pants **were** wet.* |
| Collective nouns → singular in American English | *The team **is** winning. The staff **is** meeting.* |
| *There is/are* agrees with the following subject | *There **is** a problem. There **are** problems.* |
| Relative clause verb agrees with the antecedent | *He is one of those people who **complain** constantly.* |
| Titles and names → singular | *"The Avengers" **is** on tonight.* |
| Subject after inversion still controls the verb | *On the table **were** three envelopes.* |

### 5.2 Verb + infinitive vs. verb + gerund

**Verb + to-infinitive:** *agree, appear, arrange, ask, attempt, choose, decide, demand, deserve, expect, fail, hope, learn, manage, offer, plan, prepare, pretend, promise, refuse, seem, tend, threaten, wait, want, wish*.
- *They **agreed to help**. He **refuses to listen**.*

**Verb + gerund:** *admit, appreciate, avoid, can't help, consider, delay, deny, discuss, dislike, enjoy, escape, finish, imagine, involve, keep, mention, mind, miss, postpone, practice, quit, recommend, resist, risk, suggest, tolerate*.
- *She **denied taking** it. I **don't mind waiting**.*

**Verb + object + to-infinitive:** *advise, allow, ask, cause, convince, encourage, expect, force, invite, order, permit, persuade, remind, teach, tell, urge, want, warn*.
- *They **persuaded me to stay**.*

**Either, no real change in meaning:** *begin, start, continue, like, love, hate, prefer, can't stand, bother*.
- *It **started to rain** / **started raining**.*

**Either, with a meaning change:**

| Verb | + gerund | + infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| remember | recall a past act — *I **remember locking** the door.* | not forget to do — ***Remember to lock** the door.* |
| forget | fail to recall — *I'll never **forget seeing** her face.* | fail to do — *I **forgot to mail** it.* |
| stop | cease the activity — *He **stopped smoking**.* | pause in order to — *He **stopped to smoke**.* |
| try | experiment — ***Try adding** salt.* | attempt something hard — *I **tried to open** it.* |
| regret | be sorry about the past — *I **regret telling** him.* | be sorry to announce — *We **regret to inform** you…* |
| go on | continue the same thing — *She **went on talking**.* | move to something new — *She **went on to describe** the plan.* |
| mean | involve — *This **means working** weekends.* | intend — *I **meant to call**.* |
| need | passive sense — *The car **needs washing**.* | active sense — *I **need to wash** the car.* |

### 5.3 Gerund after prepositions

Any verb after a preposition takes the `-ing` form — including the *to* in *look forward to, be used to, get used to, object to, be opposed to, commit to*.

- She's **interested in learning** Spanish.
- I'm **looking forward to seeing** you. (not *\*to see*)
- He's **used to working** nights.

### 5.4 Stative verbs and the continuous

Stative verbs normally do not take the continuous: *\*I am knowing, \*I am wanting, \*She is owning*. But usage is shifting in informal American English for a few verbs (*I'm loving this*), and several stative verbs have dynamic senses (section 8.1).

### 5.5 Modal patterns

| Function | Present / future | Past |
|---|---|---|
| Ability | *can, is able to* — *She **can** swim.* | *could, was able to* — *He **was able to** escape.* |
| Permission | *can, may, could* — ***May** I come in?* | *was allowed to* — *We **were allowed to** leave.* |
| Obligation | *must, have to* — *You **must** sign.* | *had to* — *I **had to** wait.* |
| No obligation | *don't have to, needn't* — *You **don't have to** come.* | *didn't have to* |
| Prohibition | *must not, can't* — *You **mustn't** park here.* | *couldn't, wasn't allowed to* |
| Certainty | *must* — *He **must** be tired.* | *must have* — *She **must have** left.* |
| Possibility | *may, might, could* — *It **could** be true.* | *may/might/could have* — *He **might have** forgotten.* |
| Impossibility | *can't* — *That **can't** be right.* | *can't have / couldn't have* — *She **couldn't have** known.* |
| Advice | *should, ought to* — *You **should** rest.* | *should have* — *You **should have** told me.* |
| Habit in the past | — | *used to, would* — *We **used to** camp. He **would** read for hours.* |

Modal rules: no `-s` (*\*she cans*), no *to* (*\*must to go*), no stacking (*\*will can* → *will be able to*), and negation attaches directly (*cannot, shouldn't, mustn't*).

### 5.6 Phrasal verbs: separable and inseparable

**Separable transitive** — the object may go before or after the particle; a pronoun object *must* go in the middle.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| turn off / on | stop / start a device | *Turn the radio **off**. / Turn **it** off.* |
| put off | postpone | *They put the wedding **off**.* |
| pick up | collect, learn | *I'll pick **you** up at six.* |
| take off | remove | *Take your shoes **off**.* |
| fill out | complete a form | *Fill **it** out in ink.* |
| bring up | mention; raise a child | *Don't bring **that** up.* |
| call off | cancel | *They called the game **off**.* |
| give up | quit | *He gave **it** up last year.* |
| throw away | discard | *Throw those **away**.* |
| look up | search for information | *Look the word **up**.* |
| figure out | solve | *I can't figure **it** out.* |
| turn down | reject; lower | *She turned the offer **down**.* |
| set up | arrange | *We set the meeting **up**.* |
| write down | record | *Write **it** down.* |
| hand in | submit | *Hand your papers **in**.* |
| point out | indicate | *He pointed the error **out**.* |

**Inseparable transitive** — object always follows the whole unit.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| run into | meet by chance | *I ran into **Dan** downtown.* |
| look after | take care of | *She looks after **her mother**.* |
| get over | recover from | *He got over **the flu**.* |
| come across | find by chance | *I came across **an old letter**.* |
| look into | investigate | *We'll look into **the complaint**.* |
| take after | resemble | *She takes after **her dad**.* |
| go over | review | *Let's go over **the numbers**.* |
| stand for | represent | *What does "NASA" stand for?* |
| count on | rely on | *You can count on **me**.* |
| run out of | exhaust a supply | *We ran out of **gas**.* |

**Intransitive** — no object at all: *break down, show up, take off* (planes), *grow up, get up, come back, sit down, give in, pass out, work out, catch on*.
- *The car **broke down**. He never **showed up**. The plane **took off** late.*

### 5.7 Causative structures

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **make** + object + bare infinitive | force | *They **made him apologize**.* |
| **have** + object + bare infinitive | arrange for someone to do it | *I **had the plumber fix** the leak.* |
| **have** + object + past participle | arrange for something to be done | *I **had my hair cut**.* |
| **get** + object + to-infinitive | persuade | *She **got her son to clean** his room.* |
| **get** + object + past participle | arrange / manage | *We **got the car washed**.* |
| **let** + object + bare infinitive | allow | *My parents **let me stay** out late.* |
| **help** + object + (to) infinitive | assist; *to* optional | *He **helped me (to) move**.* |
| **allow / permit** + object + to-infinitive | give permission | *They **allowed us to enter**.* |
| **force / cause** + object + to-infinitive | compel | *The storm **caused the flight to be** canceled.* |

Note: the bare infinitive after active *make* recovers the *to* in the passive: *They made him apologize* → *He **was made to apologize**.* The same happens with perception verbs: *They saw him leave* → *He **was seen to leave**.* *Let* has no passive of this kind; English switches to *be allowed to*: *He **was allowed to** go.*

### 5.8 Perception verbs

*See, hear, watch, notice, feel, observe* + object + bare infinitive (complete action) or `-ing` (action in progress).

- I **saw him leave**. (the whole event)
- I **saw him leaving**. (mid-action)
- We **heard someone shout**. / We **heard someone shouting**.

### 5.9 Reported speech backshift

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| present simple → past simple | *"I **work** here."* → *He said he **worked** there.* |
| present continuous → past continuous | *"I**'m leaving**."* → *She said she **was leaving**.* |
| past simple → past perfect | *"I **saw** it."* → *He said he **had seen** it.* |
| present perfect → past perfect | *"I**'ve finished**."* → *She said she **had finished**.* |
| will → would | *"I**'ll help**."* → *He said he **would help**.* |
| can → could; may → might; must → had to | *"I **must** go."* → *She said she **had to** go.* |

No backshift is needed for permanent truths or when the reporting verb is present: *He says he **works** here. She told me water **boils** at 212°F.*

### 5.10 Tense sequencing and time markers

| Marker | Tense |
|---|---|
| *every day, usually, always, never* | present simple |
| *now, right now, at the moment, currently* | present continuous |
| *yesterday, last week, in 2010, ago* | past simple |
| *already, yet, just, ever, never, so far, recently, since, for* | present perfect |
| *by the time, before, after, when* (earlier event) | past perfect |
| *by next year, by then* | future perfect |

*Since* + a point in time (*since Monday*); *for* + a duration (*for three days*).

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *She don't like coffee.* → ✓ *She **doesn't** like coffee.* — Third person singular needs *does* in the negative.
- ✗ *He didn't went to the party.* → ✓ *He didn't **go** to the party.* — After the auxiliary *did*, the main verb returns to the base form.
- ✗ *I am agree with you.* → ✓ *I **agree** with you.* — *Agree* is a full verb, not an adjective; it needs no *be*.
- ✗ *I am living here since 2015.* → ✓ *I**'ve been living** here since 2015.* — Unfinished time up to now requires the present perfect (continuous), not the present continuous.
- ✗ *Yesterday I have called my boss.* → ✓ *Yesterday I **called** my boss.* — A finished past time expression forces the past simple; the present perfect can't take *yesterday*.
- ✗ *I am knowing the answer.* → ✓ *I **know** the answer.* — Stative verbs do not take the continuous.
- ✗ *She must to leave early.* → ✓ *She **must leave** early.* — Modals take the bare infinitive, never *to*.
- ✗ *We discussed about the budget.* → ✓ *We **discussed** the budget.* — *Discuss* is transitive and takes a direct object with no preposition (same for *enter, marry, approach, lack, resemble, mention*).
- ✗ *I look forward to meet you.* → ✓ *I look forward **to meeting** you.* — Here *to* is a preposition, so it takes a gerund.
- ✗ *He suggested me to call.* → ✓ *He suggested **that I call**.* / *He suggested **calling**.* — *Suggest* does not take object + infinitive.
- ✗ *The window was broke.* → ✓ *The window was **broken**.* — The passive needs the past participle (V3), not the past simple (V2).
- ✗ *If I would have known, I would have told you.* → ✓ *If I **had** known, I would have told you.* — *Would* does not appear in the *if*-clause of a third conditional.
- ✗ *Everyone are ready.* → ✓ *Everyone **is** ready.* — Indefinite pronouns ending in *-one/-body/-thing* are singular.
- ✗ *The list of items are on the desk.* → ✓ *The list of items **is** on the desk.* — The verb agrees with *list*, not with the noun inside the prepositional phrase.
- ✗ *I have went there twice.* → ✓ *I have **gone** there twice.* — *Went* is the past simple; the perfect requires *gone*.
- ✗ *Turn off it.* → ✓ *Turn **it** off.* — A pronoun object must sit between the verb and the particle of a separable phrasal verb.
- ✗ *He laid on the couch all afternoon.* → ✓ *He **lay** on the couch all afternoon.* — *Lie/lay/lain* is intransitive; *lay/laid/laid* needs an object.
- ✗ *I wish I was rich.* → ✓ *I wish I **were** rich.* — Formal American English uses the *were*-subjunctive in wishes and unreal conditions.
- ✗ *He explained me the rules.* → ✓ *He explained **the rules to me**.* — *Explain, describe, say, suggest, mention, recommend, introduce* do not take an indirect object directly.
- ✗ *She avoided to answer.* → ✓ *She avoided **answering**.* — *Avoid* belongs to the gerund-only group.

## 7. American English notes

The verb system is nearly identical on both sides of the Atlantic; the differences are real but few.

**1. Past participle of *get*.** American English keeps *gotten* for the "obtain/become" sense; British English uses *got*.
- AmE: *She has **gotten** much better at chess.*
- *Have got* meaning "possess" or "must" keeps *got* in both: *I've **got** two brothers. I've **got** to go.*

**2. Collective nouns take singular verbs.** American English treats *team, family, government, staff, company, band, committee* as single units.
- AmE: *The team **is** playing well.* BrE also allows *The team **are** playing well.*

**3. The mandative subjunctive is standard and common in AmE.** British English often prefers *should* + base.
- AmE: *We insist that he **resign**.* BrE also: *We insist that he **should resign**.*

**4. Regular past forms are preferred.** AmE favors *burned, dreamed, learned, spelled, spilled, smelled, leaned, leaped, spoiled*; BrE commonly uses *burnt, dreamt, learnt, spelt, spilt, smelt, leant, leapt, spoilt*.

**5. `-l` doubling.** American English does not double a final `-l` after an unstressed vowel: *traveled, traveling, canceled, canceling, labeled, modeled, quarreled, signaled*. British: *travelled, cancelled*. But both double when the last syllable is stressed: *controlled, propelled, expelled*.

**6. `-ize` vs. `-ise`.** American English uses `-ize` throughout: *organize, realize, apologize, recognize, criticize, memorize, analyze* (note `-yze`, not `-yse`).

**7. Past simple where BrE uses the present perfect.** With *just, already, yet*, American speakers accept the past simple.
- AmE: *Did you eat yet? I already told him.* BrE: *Have you eaten yet? I've already told him.* Both are correct in AmE; the present perfect is never wrong.

**8. *Shall* is rare in AmE.** It survives mainly in legal writing and in polite offers: *Shall we begin?* Everyday American English uses *will* or *should*.

**9. *Needn't* is rare in AmE.** Americans say *doesn't need to* or *doesn't have to* rather than *needn't*.

**10. *Do* as a substitute verb.** BrE: *"Will you come?" "I might do."* AmE drops the *do*: *"I might."*

**11. Some verb-preposition pairs differ.** AmE: *fill out a form, wait in line* (*on line* in New York), *protest something, write someone*. BrE: *fill in a form, queue up, protest against something, write to someone*. Americans also use *meet with someone* for a scheduled discussion, alongside plain *meet someone*, where British English uses only *meet*.

**12. Past participle *proven* is common in AmE** as an adjective and increasingly as a participle: *a **proven** track record; it has been **proven**/**proved***. Both are acceptable.

## 8. Tricky cases

### 8.1 Stative verbs that also have dynamic uses

| Verb | Stative | Dynamic |
|---|---|---|
| think | *I **think** you're right.* (opinion) | *I**'m thinking** about it.* (mental activity) |
| have | *She **has** a car.* (possess) | *She**'s having** lunch.* (experience) |
| see | *I **see** what you mean.* (understand) | *I**'m seeing** the doctor at three.* (meet) |
| taste | *The soup **tastes** good.* (quality) | *He**'s tasting** the soup.* (deliberate act) |
| smell | *It **smells** burnt.* | *She**'s smelling** the flowers.* |
| feel | *This **feels** soft.* | *I**'m feeling** better.* (both fine) |
| be | *He **is** rude.* (permanent trait) | *He**'s being** rude.* (behaving that way now) |
| weigh | *It **weighs** ten pounds.* | *He**'s weighing** the package.* |
| appear | *It **appears** difficult.* (seem) | *She**'s appearing** on TV tonight.* (perform) |
| expect | *I **expect** it's true.* (suppose) | *We**'re expecting** guests.* (await) |

### 8.2 Words that look like verbs but aren't

- **Gerunds are nouns in function:** *Swimming* is my hobby. The subject is a noun phrase, so the verb is singular.
- **Participial adjectives:** *interesting, boring, confusing, excited, tired, broken*. In *The movie was **boring***, *boring* is an adjective, not a continuous verb. Contrast *He was **boring** the audience* (verb).
- **Nouns from verbs:** *a run, a walk, a talk, a drive, a look, a change, a bite*. In *Let's take a **walk***, *walk* is a noun.
- **Verbs from nouns (conversion):** *to google, to text, to bookmark, to table, to chair, to host, to friend*. The form gives no clue; only position does.

### 8.3 Word pairs learners confuse

| Pair | Distinction | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| lie / lay | *lie* (lay, lain) = recline, no object; *lay* (laid, laid) = put down, needs an object | *I **lie** down. I **laid** the book down. Yesterday I **lay** down.* |
| rise / raise | *rise* (rose, risen) intransitive; *raise* (raised) transitive | *The sun **rose**. They **raised** the price.* |
| sit / set | *sit* (sat) intransitive; *set* (set) transitive | *She **sat** down. She **set** the cup down.* |
| fall / fell | *fall* (fell, fallen) intransitive; *fell* (felled) = cut down a tree | *He **fell**. They **felled** the oak.* |
| hang / hang | *hung* for objects; *hanged* for executions | *I **hung** the picture. The outlaw was **hanged**.* |
| find / found | *find* (found) = discover; *found* (founded) = establish | *They **found** gold. The city was **founded** in 1850.* |
| wind / wound | *wind* (wound) = coil; *wound* (wounded) = injure | *He **wound** the clock. She was **wounded**.* |
| bring / take | *bring* = toward the speaker; *take* = away | ***Bring** me the file. **Take** this to Jenny.* |
| do / make | *do* = perform/activity; *make* = create/produce | ***do** the dishes; **make** dinner* |
| say / tell | *tell* takes a person object; *say* doesn't | *He **told me** the truth. He **said** the truth was ugly.* |

### 8.4 *Used to* vs. *be used to* vs. *get used to*

- *I **used to** smoke.* — a past habit that has ended (+ base form)
- *I**'m used to** the noise / **to working** nights.* — accustomed (+ noun or gerund)
- *You'll **get used to** it.* — become accustomed

### 8.5 *Would* vs. *used to*

Both express past habits, but *would* works only with repeated actions, not with states.
- ✓ *Every summer we **would drive** to the lake.*
- ✗ *\*I would have a motorcycle.* → ✓ *I **used to have** a motorcycle.*

### 8.6 Verbs that resist the passive

Stative and relational verbs generally don't passivize even though they take an object:

- *She has two brothers.* → *\*Two brothers are had by her.*
- *The coat fits him.* → *\*He is fitted by the coat.*
- *The book cost ten dollars.* → *\*Ten dollars was cost by the book.*
- *Anna resembles her mother.* → *\*Her mother is resembled by Anna.*

The same holds for *lack, suit, equal, weigh* (in the measurement sense), and *have* (in the possession sense). Intransitive verbs never passivize at all, since there is no object to promote: *\*The accident was happened.*

### 8.7 *There is / there are* with lists

Formal agreement is with the first item: *There **is** a fork, two knives, and a spoon on the table.* Many speakers use *there are*; both circulate, but the singular with a singular first item is the traditional rule.

### 8.8 Dangling participles

A participial phrase must modify the subject of the main clause.
- ✗ *Walking to work, the rain started.* (The rain wasn't walking.)
- ✓ *Walking to work, **I** got caught in the rain.*

### 8.9 *Do* as auxiliary and as main verb in one sentence

- *What **did** you **do** yesterday?* — the first *do* is the auxiliary, the second the lexical verb.
- *I **didn't do** it.*

### 8.10 *Were* vs. *was* in *if*-clauses

Use *was* when the condition might actually be true: *If he **was** at the meeting, he heard the news* (maybe he was). Use *were* for unreal situations: *If he **were** the manager, things would change* (he isn't).

### 8.11 Verbs with an obligatory preposition vs. none

| Takes no preposition | Requires a preposition |
|---|---|
| discuss, enter, marry, approach, lack, resemble, answer, contact, phone | listen **to**, wait **for**, depend **on**, look **at**, apologize **to/for**, agree **with**, arrive **at/in**, belong **to** |

- ✓ *She **entered the room**.* / ✓ *She **listened to** the radio.*

### 8.12 Subject–verb agreement with *one of those who*

*He is one of those people who **complain** constantly* — the relative pronoun *who* refers to *people* (plural). Compare *He is the only one of those people who **complains**.*

## 9. Quick reference

| Subcategory | What it is | Examples | Sample sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Action (dynamic) | a doable activity | run, build, write | *They **built** a shed.* |
| Stative | a state or relation | know, own, belong | *She **owns** two cars.* |
| Linking | joins subject to complement | be, seem, become, taste | *It **seems** fine.* |
| Auxiliary | grammar helper | be, have, do | *She **has** finished.* |
| Modal | possibility / necessity | can, must, should | *You **should** rest.* |
| Semi-modal | modal meaning, verb form | have to, ought to, used to | *I **have to** go.* |
| Transitive | needs an object | discuss, enjoy, want | *We **discussed** it.* |
| Intransitive | takes no object | arrive, sleep, happen | *It **happened** fast.* |
| Ditransitive | takes two objects | give, send, tell | *He **gave** me a ride.* |
| Ambitransitive | works both ways | eat, open, break | *The door **opened**.* |
| Complex-transitive | object + complement | elect, consider, make | *They **elected** her chair.* |
| Prepositional | fixed preposition | look at, rely on | *I **rely on** him.* |
| Phrasal | verb + particle | turn off, give up | *Turn **it** off.* |
| Phrasal-prepositional | verb + particle + prep | put up with, run out of | *We **ran out of** milk.* |
| Reflexive | object = subject | pride oneself, help oneself | ***Help yourself**.* |
| Causative | make someone do it | make, have, let, get | *I **had it fixed**.* |
| Delexical (light) | meaning is in the noun | take a walk, make a call | *Let's **take a break**.* |
| Impersonal | dummy *it* subject | rain, snow, seem | ***It's snowing**.* |
| Performative | saying = doing | promise, apologize | *I **apologize**.* |
| Lexical (main) | ordinary meaning-bearing verb | write, google, text | *She **googled** it.* |
| Finite | shows tense + agreement | works, wrote, is | *She **wrote** back.* |
| Non-finite | no tense, no agreement | to go, going, gone | *He hopes **to go**.* |
| Infinitive | base ± *to* | to go, go | *I want **to go**.* |
| Gerund | `-ing` as a noun | swimming, reading | ***Reading** helps.* |
| Participle | `-ing` / V3 as modifier | boiling, broken | *a **broken** chair* |
| Regular | `-ed` past and participle | walk, open, google | *We **walked** home.* |
| Irregular | unpredictable V2 / V3 | go, write, cut | *She **wrote** back.* |

**Tense grid at a glance** (*eat*, third person singular):

| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Past | ate | was eating | had eaten | had been eating |
| Present | eats | is eating | has eaten | has been eating |
| Future | will eat | will be eating | will have eaten | will have been eating |

## 10. Practice

**A. Fill in the blank with the correct verb form.**

1. By the time we arrived, the movie ______ (already, start).
2. She insisted that he ______ (be) on time for the interview.
3. I'm looking forward to ______ (hear) from you.
4. Neither the students nor the teacher ______ (know) the answer.
5. If I ______ (be) you, I'd take the earlier flight.

**B. Identify the type of the bolded verb** (action, stative, linking, auxiliary, modal, phrasal, causative, or ditransitive).

6. The soup **smells** wonderful.
7. My landlord **had** the roof replaced last month.
8. She **lent** me her charger.

**C. Correct the error.**

9. He didn't went to the meeting because he was sick.
10. We discussed about the new policy for two hours.

**D. Rewrite in the passive, keeping the same tense.**

11. Someone stole my bike last night.
12. They are repairing the elevator this week.

**E. Choose the correct option.**

13. After the heart attack, he stopped (to smoke / smoking) for good.
14. I can't figure (out it / it out).
15. The list of finalists (was / were) posted this morning.
16. I have (drank / drunk) three cups of coffee today.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **had already started** — Past perfect: the movie began *before* the other past event (*arrived*). *By the time* and *already* both point to the perfect, and *already* sits after the auxiliary *had*.
2. **be** — Mandative subjunctive after *insist that*: the bare base form, regardless of the subject.
3. **hearing** — In *look forward to*, the *to* is a preposition, so it takes a gerund.
4. **knows** — With *neither…nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject (*the teacher*, singular).
5. **were** — The *were*-subjunctive in an unreal second conditional; *If I were you* is fixed.
6. **Linking verb** — *Smells* joins the subject *soup* to the adjective *wonderful*; no action is performed. Test: *The soup **is** wonderful.*
7. **Causative** — *have* + object + past participle means "arrange for something to be done" (the landlord did not personally replace the roof).
8. **Ditransitive** — *lent* takes an indirect object (*me*) and a direct object (*her charger*). Alternative: *She lent her charger **to** me.*
9. **He didn't go to the meeting because he was sick.** — After the auxiliary *did*, the main verb reverts to the base form; the tense is already carried by *did*.
10. **We discussed the new policy for two hours.** — *Discuss* is directly transitive; no preposition comes between it and its object.
11. **My bike was stolen last night.** — Past simple passive: *was* + past participle. The doer is unknown, so no *by*-phrase is needed.
12. **The elevator is being repaired this week.** — Present continuous passive: *is being* + past participle.
13. **smoking** — *Stop* + gerund means "quit the activity." *Stopped to smoke* would mean he paused in order to have a cigarette.
14. **it out** — *Figure out* is separable, and a pronoun object must sit between the verb and the particle.
15. **was** — The subject is *list* (singular); *of finalists* is an intervening prepositional phrase and does not control the verb.
16. **drunk** — The perfect takes the past participle (V3). *Drank* is the past simple and cannot follow *have*.
