# Parts of Speech

The nine word classes of American English, one folder each. Every folder holds a grammar reference; seven of them also hold a dictionary of the words in that class.

Traditional grammar counts eight parts of speech and files *the*, *my*, *some*, and *three* under adjectives. Modern descriptive grammars split those out as **determiners**, because they do not describe a noun — they anchor it, and they follow their own ordering rules. That gives nine.

## What's in a folder

Every folder has a `README.md` covering the same ten sections: what the class does, its types, forms and inflection, position and word order, rules and patterns, common mistakes, American English notes, tricky cases, a quick reference, and practice exercises with answers.

Seven folders also have a `catalog/` subfolder — the **lexicon** rather than the grammar. Five of them are the **closed classes**, where the full list of words is finite and can actually be written down:

```
07-conjunctions/
  README.md          the grammar: punctuation, parallel structure, clause types
  catalog/
    README.md        A–Z index of all 816 headwords
    browse.html      searchable, click any word to hear it
    01-coordinating.md … 12-formal-archaic.md
    data/            the same entries as JSON
```

Adjectives and adverbs get no dictionary because they are **open classes** — new ones enter the language constantly, so no list could ever be complete. **Verbs** and **nouns** are open classes too, but the parts of them that *misbehave* are finite, and those are exactly what a learner has to memorize. So each gets a catalog of that finite part: for verbs, the irregular ones; for nouns, the irregular plurals, the uncountables, and the sets that force their own verb agreement. Neither catalog tries to list every verb or every noun, because neither could.

## Suggested reading order

The numbered order is the traditional one and is fine for browsing. To read straight through, this order builds each class on the one before it:

1. **[Nouns](01-nouns/README.md)** — the anchor class; almost every other class is defined by its relationship to nouns
2. **[Determiners](09-determiners/README.md)** — read straight after nouns, since most singular countable nouns require one
3. **[Pronouns](02-pronouns/README.md)** — they replace whole noun phrases, so you need to recognize one first
4. **[Verbs](03-verbs/README.md)** — the largest chapter; with noun phrases available, verb types become easy to see
5. **[Adjectives](04-adjectives/README.md)** — the modifiers inside the noun phrase
6. **[Adverbs](05-adverbs/README.md)** — best read right after adjectives, since the two are constantly confused
7. **[Prepositions](06-prepositions/README.md)** — they take a noun phrase as their object, so this needs 1–3 in place
8. **[Conjunctions](07-conjunctions/README.md)** — joins finished clauses, so it comes after you can build one
9. **[Interjections](08-interjections/README.md)** — grammatically independent of everything above; read last, or any time

## Class is a job, not a label

A word does not own a class. It is decided by what the word is doing in a particular sentence:

- **book** — *I finished the **book*** (noun) / *Please **book** a table* (verb) / *a **book** club* (attributive noun)
- **fast** — *a **fast** car* (adjective) / *He drives **fast*** (adverb) / *They **fast** until sundown* (verb)
- **that** — ***That** book is mine* (determiner) / ***That** is mine* (pronoun) / *the car **that** broke down* (relative pronoun) / *She said **that** she would come* (subordinating conjunction)

The practical test is substitution, not translation. If *the* or *my* fits the slot, it is a determiner slot. If *it* can replace the whole chunk, that chunk is a noun phrase. If *very* can go in front, you probably have a gradable adjective or adverb.
