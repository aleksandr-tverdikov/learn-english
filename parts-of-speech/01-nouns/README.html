Nouns#

A noun is a word that names a person, animal, place, thing, substance, quality, action, or idea.

1. What it does#

A noun's core job is naming. Whenever English needs to point at something — a physical object, a location, a feeling, an event, a category — a noun does the pointing. Because nouns name things, they are the words that most often serve as the participants in a sentence: who did it, what got done, and to whom.

Grammatically, a noun (or the larger noun phrase built around it) fills the slots that require a "thing": subject, direct object, indirect object, object of a preposition, subject complement, object complement, and appositive. Verbs describe what happens; nouns supply the cast.

My sister gave the mechanic her keys in the parking lot. — My sister = subject; the mechanic = indirect object; her keys = direct object; the parking lot = object of the preposition in.

Honesty is the best policy, according to my grandmother. — Honesty = subject (an abstract noun); the best policy = subject complement; my grandmother = object of the preposition according to.

Nouns rarely travel alone. They attract determiners (the, a, my, this, three), adjectives (old, red, expensive), and modifying phrases (of the city, in the drawer, that I bought). The whole cluster is a noun phrase, and it behaves as a single unit — you can replace the entire thing with one pronoun:

The heavy wooden box that we found in the attic was empty. → It was empty.

One more job worth naming: nouns can modify other nouns. In a coffee cup, coffee is still a noun, but it is doing an adjective-like job. English builds enormous amounts of vocabulary this way (fire escape, bus driver, data breach, kitchen table).

2. Types#

The categories below overlap on purpose. A single noun is simultaneously common or proper, concrete or abstract, countable or uncountable, and possibly collective and compound as well. Herd in two herds of cattle is common, concrete, countable, and collective all at once.

Common nouns#

A common noun names a general class of person, place, or thing rather than one specific individual. Common nouns are not capitalized unless they begin a sentence or appear in a title.

Representative members: teacher, city, river, dog, computer, freedom, month, company, book, mountain.

The city built a new bridge over the river. Every student in the class received a certificate. My uncle bought a truck from a dealership in town.

Proper nouns#

A proper noun names one specific entity — a particular person, place, organization, brand, title, day, month, holiday, language, or nationality. Proper nouns are capitalized and usually take no article, though some fixed names do (the United States, the Nile, the Beatles).

Representative members: Maria, Chicago, the Pacific Ocean, Harvard University, Google, Thanksgiving, Tuesday, March, Spanish, Toyota, the Grand Canyon.

Maria moved from Boston to Austin last April. Congress adjourned before Thanksgiving. He reads Spanish better than he speaks it.

Note that a proper noun can be pushed back into common use: He bought a Toyota (a car of that make), There are three Jennifers in my class.

Concrete nouns#

A concrete noun names something perceivable by the five senses — something you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell.

Representative members: chair, thunder, sand, perfume, bread, snow, hammer, dog, sidewalk, smoke.

The thunder rattled the windows. She spread butter on the warm bread. A thin layer of dust covered the shelves.

Abstract nouns#

An abstract noun names something with no physical form: a quality, state, emotion, concept, relationship, or field of study. Many are formed from adjectives or verbs with suffixes such as -ness, -ity, -tion, -ment, -ship, -hood, -ism, -ance/-ence, -th.

Representative members: freedom, honesty, jealousy, democracy, childhood, friendship, information, patience, growth, capitalism, importance, decision, warmth, luck.

Patience is the hardest virtue to teach. Her decision surprised everyone on the committee. They fought for independence for over a decade.

Countable nouns#

A countable noun (also count noun) names something that can be counted as separate units. It has both singular and plural forms, it can follow a/an, and it can take numbers and many, few, several.

Representative members: book, apple, idea, minute, dollar, problem, child, opportunity, suggestion, city.

I have three suggestions and one complaint. She waited for an hour and read two chapters. Few problems have easy solutions.

Uncountable nouns#

An uncountable noun (also mass noun or noncount noun) names something treated as an undivided mass or an abstraction. It has no plural form, takes no a/an, takes a singular verb, and pairs with much, little, some, a lot of. To quantify it, you use a partitive: a piece of advice, two liters of water, a bar of soap.

Collective nouns#

A collective noun names a group of individuals treated as a single unit: team, family, committee, jury, staff, crew, audience, government, class, band, herd, flock, crowd, faculty, couple, majority, public, company, board.

Also included are the animal-group terms: a herd of cattle, a flock of birds, a school of fish, a pack of wolves, a pride of lions, a swarm of bees, a pod of whales, a litter of puppies, a colony of ants, a gaggle of geese.

The committee meets on Thursdays. A flock of geese landed on the pond. The jury returned its verdict after six hours.

Agreement with collective nouns is the main difficulty; see sections 5 and 7.

Compound nouns#

A compound noun is a noun made of two or more words. Compounds are written three ways, and the spelling is a matter of convention rather than rule — check a dictionary when unsure.

FormExamples
Closed (one word)bedroom, toothpaste, notebook, sunflower, football, keyboard, haircut, software
Hyphenatedmother-in-law, editor-in-chief, passer-by, check-in, merry-go-round, runner-up, self-esteem
Open (two words)ice cream, high school, post office, swimming pool, real estate, coffee table, credit card

Compounds combine various word classes: noun + noun (bus stop), adjective + noun (blackboard), gerund + noun (swimming pool, washing machine), verb + noun (breakwater, scarecrow), noun + verb (haircut, sunrise), verb + particle (breakdown, takeoff), preposition + noun (underworld, overcoat).

Spelling drifts over time and varies by dictionary. Many compounds start out open, become hyphenated, and end up closed (web site → web-site → website), and American dictionaries close up more of them than British ones do — passer-by is usually written passerby (plural passersby) in American English.

She left her toothbrush at the swimming pool. My sister-in-law works at the post office. The breakdown happened on the highway.

Verbal nouns: gerunds#

A gerund is the -ing form of a verb used as a noun. It names an activity. Gerunds fill every noun slot: subject, object, complement, object of a preposition.

Swimming is good exercise. (subject) She enjoys cooking. (direct object) His hobby is collecting stamps. (subject complement) They left without saying goodbye. (object of a preposition)

A gerund keeps verb-like behavior — it can take its own object and adverbs (carefully reading the contract). A fully nominalized -ing form behaves like an ordinary noun instead, taking the and of (the reading of the contract, the killing of the bill), and some take plurals (meetings, buildings, feelings, savings).

Verbal nouns: infinitives as nouns#

The to-infinitive can also fill noun positions, though less freely than the gerund.

To forgive is difficult. (subject) She wants to leave early. (direct object) His only goal was to win. (subject complement)

Noun phrases#

A noun phrase is a noun plus everything that modifies it, functioning as a single unit. The structure is: (determiner) + (premodifiers) + HEAD NOUN + (postmodifiers).

The two rusty iron gates at the end of the driveway need paint. — determiner the, numeral two, adjective rusty, attributive (material) noun iron, head gates, prepositional phrase at the end of the driveway.

Everyone who signed up for the class received an email. A cup of strong black coffee is all I need.

Noun clauses (finite clauses that behave like noun phrases) also fill noun slots: I know that she left; What he said surprised me; Tell me where you put it.

Possessive (genitive) nouns#

The possessive or genitive is the noun form marked with 's or ' that shows possession, association, origin, measurement, or other relationships. It functions as a determiner within a noun phrase.

Maria's car is in the shop. That's the company's biggest client. Three weeks' vacation is not enough.

Full formation rules are in section 3; usage rules in section 5.

Attributive nouns (noun adjuncts)#

An attributive noun is a noun that modifies another noun. It stays a noun but occupies an adjective-like position. It is almost always singular even when the meaning is plural.

a car door, a toothbrush, a book club, a chicken sandwich The government shutdown lasted three weeks. She joined the city council and the school board.

Exceptions that keep the plural: a sports car, a savings account, a customs officer, an arms race, a clothes hanger, a systems analyst.

Plural-only nouns (pluralia tantum)#

These nouns exist only in the plural form and take plural verbs.

  • Tools and clothing with two parts: scissors, pliers, tongs, tweezers, glasses, binoculars, pants, trousers, jeans, shorts, pajamas, overalls, tights
  • Other: clothes, belongings, congratulations, thanks, outskirts, surroundings, premises, goods, riches, remains, savings, groceries, valuables, earnings, proceeds, whereabouts, odds, manners (= etiquette)

Your jeans are in the dryer. These scissors don't cut well. (To count them: a pair of scissors, two pairs of scissors.) Congratulations are in order.

Singular-only nouns with plural form#

These end in -s but take a singular verb: academic fields (mathematics, physics, economics, politics, linguistics, statistics, ethics), diseases (measles, mumps, rabies, diabetes), games (billiards, darts, checkers), and news.

Physics is my hardest class. The news was worse than we expected. Measles is preventable.

Careful: some of these have a second, plural-verb sense — The statistics are misleading (the numbers), versus Statistics is a required course (the field).

Invariable nouns (same singular and plural)#

sheep, deer, fish, moose, salmon, trout, shrimp, swine, aircraft, spacecraft, offspring, series, species, means, corps, headquarters, Chinese, Japanese, Swiss.

One deer crossed; then five more deer followed. Two new species were identified. This series is good; those other series are not.

Partitive and measure nouns#

These nouns give countable shape to uncountable ones. They are how you count what cannot otherwise be counted.

TypeExamples
Portiona piece of advice, a slice of bread, a bar of soap, a sheet of paper, a lump of sugar
Containera glass of water, a cup of coffee, a bottle of wine, a bag of flour, a carton of milk
Measurea pound of meat, a gallon of gas, a mile of road, a spoonful of sugar
Pair / seta pair of pants, a set of tools, a deck of cards, a suit of armor
Instancea bolt of lightning, a clap of thunder, a stroke of luck, a game of chess, an item of news

Let me give you a piece of advice. He drank two cups of coffee before noon. That was a stroke of luck.

Nouns of nationality and origin#

A subtype worth separating because their plurals are irregular: an American / two Americans, a German / two Germans, but a Frenchman / two Frenchmen, a Swiss / two Swiss, a Chinese / two Chinese. Collective use takes the: the French, the Dutch, the British, the Chinese.

Usage note: with the invariable -ese and -ss words, current American usage strongly prefers a noun of its own after the adjective — a Chinese student, two Japanese tourists, a Swiss banker — rather than the bare a Chinese, which now sounds curt or worse. Frenchman/Frenchwoman and Englishman/Englishwoman survive, but a French person and a British citizen are the safer neutral choices.

The Japanese have a long tradition of ceramics. Two Canadians and three Brazilians joined the team.

3. Forms and inflection#

English nouns inflect for only two things: number (singular/plural) and case (plain/genitive). English lost grammatical gender centuries ago; nouns have no gender agreement.

Words like data, media, criteria, bacteria, phenomena are technically plural. In careful American writing, The data are inconclusive and These criteria are strict are standard. In everyday and much business usage, data is treated as a mass noun taking a singular verb: The data is inconclusive. Media likewise: The media is biased is common; the media are is the conservative choice. Criteria and phenomena as singulars (this criteria, a phenomena) are still widely considered errors.

3.4 Plurals of compound nouns#

The rule: pluralize the head noun — the word that carries the main meaning.

TypeSingular → Plural
Head firstmother-in-law → mothers-in-law; passer-by → passers-by; attorney general → attorneys general; editor-in-chief → editors-in-chief; runner-up → runners-up; court-martial → courts-martial
Head last (most compounds)bedroom → bedrooms; toothbrush → toothbrushes; high school → high schools; swimming pool → swimming pools
No noun elementgrown-up → grown-ups; forget-me-not → forget-me-nots; merry-go-round → merry-go-rounds
Man/woman as first element (both change)manservant → menservants; woman doctor → women doctors; man-hour → man-hours (no change — man is not a modifier of a person here)

Note the double marking in menservants and women doctors, which is exceptional and limited to compounds in which both parts name the same person. Also note that attorney generals and court-martials are now common in American usage and appear in dictionaries, though attorneys general remains the formal preference.

3.5 Plurals of letters, numbers, and abbreviations#

The apostrophe survives in the lowercase-letter plurals because is and ts would be unreadable; some American editors extend it to A's and I's for the same reason, and both straight A's and straight As are in print.

Do not use an apostrophe for plural nouns generally. Tomato's for tomatoes is the classic error.

American style guides (the Chicago Manual of Style, the AP Stylebook with some exceptions) prefer 's after a singular noun ending in -s: the boss's desk, Charles's phone, Texas's economy. AP style makes two exceptions: proper names ending in s take the bare apostrophe (Charles' phone), and so do singular common nouns whose next word begins with s (the hostess' seat, but the hostess's invitation). Either convention is defensible; be consistent within a document.

Possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe: its, hers, his, ours, yours, theirs, whose. It's = it is; who's = who is.

3.7 The double genitive#

English allows of plus a possessive together when the meaning is "one of several."

a friend of mine; that cousin of Maria's; a painting of Picasso's (= one of the paintings he owns or painted)

Compare a painting of Picasso (a portrait depicting him) with a painting of Picasso's (one from his collection or output).

3.8 The group genitive and the elliptical genitive#

The 's does not attach to a word; it attaches to the end of the whole noun phrase. This is why the marker can land on a word that is not the possessor at all — the so-called group genitive.

the King of Spain's daughter (not "the King's of Spain daughter") the man next door's dog Ann and Bill's car the person I spoke to's name (possible in speech, but rewrite it: the name of the person I spoke to)

Long or clause-final group genitives get awkward fast; use of instead when the phrase runs past a few words.

The elliptical (independent) genitive drops the head noun when it is obvious from context or convention — most visibly in names of homes, businesses, and services.

I'm going to the dentist's. (= the dentist's office) We bought it at Macy's. / She stayed at her aunt's over the weekend. This car is Maria's, not Tom's.

3.9 Gender marking#

English does not inflect for grammatical gender. A few paired lexical items remain (actor/actress, waiter/waitress, host/hostess, prince/princess, widow/widower), but American usage increasingly prefers the unmarked form for all genders: actor, server, host, flight attendant, chair (rather than chairman), firefighter, police officer, mail carrier, spokesperson.

4.2 Order inside the noun phrase#

The canonical order is:

determiner → quantifier/number → opinion adjective → size → age → shape → color → origin → material → purpose/attributive noun → HEAD NOUN → postmodifiers

those two beautiful large old round brown Italian wooden dining tables in the corner

You will almost never use every slot at once — three modifiers before a noun is already a lot in natural English — but the relative order is fixed. A red big ball sounds wrong; a big red ball is right.

Only one central determiner is possible at a time. You cannot say the my book; you say that book of mine or my book. Predeterminers (all, both, half, twice, such) come before the determiner: all the students, both my brothers, half the cake, such a mess.

4.3 Postmodifiers, in order#

Modifiers that follow the head noun come in this rough sequence: prepositional phrase → non-finite clause → relative clause.

the book on the table written in 1902 that everyone recommends

Some adjectives normally follow the noun: with indefinite pronouns (something strange, anyone available), in fixed phrases (attorney general, court-martial, president-elect, notary public, time immemorial), and with measurement complements (a river three miles long, a man forty years old).

4.4 Possessive position#

The genitive noun functions as a determiner and takes the determiner slot at the front of the phrase:

my brother's old car — not the my brother's old car

Genitives can stack: my brother's wife's family.

4.5 Attributive noun position#

Attributive nouns sit closest to the head noun, after all adjectives:

a beautiful old brick farmhouse a cheap plastic water bottle the new city council

5. Rules and patterns#

5.1 Subject–verb agreement#

A singular noun takes a singular verb; a plural noun takes a plural verb.

The dog barks. / The dogs bark.

Agreement is with the head of the subject noun phrase, not with the nearest word:

The box of chocolates is on the table. (head = box) The causes of the failure were never identified. (head = causes)

Uncountable nouns take singular verbs: The information is helpful. The furniture was delivered.

Plural-only nouns take plural verbs: My pants are wet. The scissors are dull.

Subjects joined by and are plural (Bread and butter are on the list), unless they name one thing (Bread and butter is my favorite snack; Macaroni and cheese is on the menu).

With or / nor / either…or / neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject: Neither the manager nor the employees were informed.

Amounts and measurements treated as a single quantity take a singular verb: Twenty dollars is too much. Three miles is a long walk. Two hours seems like forever.

5.2 Collective noun agreement#

In American English, a collective noun normally takes a singular verb and a singular pronoun, because the group is conceived as a unit.

The team is winning. It has scored twice. The committee has made its decision. The staff was informed by email.

A plural verb is possible in American English when the emphasis genuinely falls on the members acting individually, but Americans typically rewrite instead: The team members are arguing rather than The team are arguing.

Some collectives are always plural in agreement: the police, the clergy, cattle, people, vermin. So are the the + adjective groups: the rich, the elderly, the unemployed, the French.

The police are investigating. (never "the police is") The unemployed are eligible for the new program.

Institution words like the military, the public, and the government go the other way in American English and normally take a singular verb: The military is stretched thin. The public is losing patience.

5.3 Determiner requirements#

A singular countable noun cannot stand alone. It requires a determiner: an article, a demonstrative, a possessive, or a quantifier.

✗ I bought car. → ✓ I bought a car. / I bought the car. / I bought my car.

Plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns can appear bare, with a generic or indefinite meaning:

Dogs are loyal. / We need water. / Honesty matters.

5.5 When to use 's versus of#

Use 's with people, animals, organizations, countries, and time expressions:

the doctor's advice; the dog's collar; the company's policy; Brazil's economy; yesterday's news; a day's work

Use of with inanimate objects and abstract relations:

the leg of the table (not the table's leg) the end of the movie; the cost of living; the top of the page

Both are possible in many cases, and 's with inanimates is spreading (the car's engine, the book's cover are both perfectly normal now). Use of when the possessor phrase is long: the opinion of the man who called yesterday, not the man who called yesterday's opinion.

Fixed 's expressions with time and measure: a week's vacation, two weeks' notice, an hour's drive, a stone's throw, in a year's time, at arm's length, for God's sake, money's worth.

5.6 The genitive as a determiner#

Because 's occupies the determiner slot, you cannot add another determiner in front of the head noun:

✗ the John's car → ✓ John's car ✗ a my friend → ✓ a friend of mine

5.7 Countability is a property of the word, not the thing#

Learners often reason from meaning. But advice is uncountable while suggestion is countable, and both name the same kind of thing. These must be learned individually. The most commonly mismanaged uncountables in English: advice, information, news, furniture, luggage, equipment, homework, work, research, evidence, knowledge, progress, traffic, weather, money, bread, software, feedback, staff.

✗ He gave me many advices. → ✓ He gave me a lot of advice. / He gave me several suggestions.

5.8 Proper noun capitalization patterns#

  • Capitalize personal names, place names, brands, organizations, days, months, holidays, languages, nationalities, religions, and titles of works.
  • Capitalize a title only when it precedes a name or replaces it as direct address: President Lincoln, but Lincoln was president; Thank you, Doctor.
  • Capitalize family terms used as names: I asked Mom. But not with a possessive: I asked my mom.
  • Do not capitalize seasons (spring, fall), directions (drive west), or academic subjects other than languages (I study biology and French). Capitalize regions: the South, the Pacific Northwest.

5.9 Common noun + preposition patterns#

Certain nouns are fixed to certain prepositions. These must be memorized.

reason for, cause of, solution to, answer to, key to, increase in, decrease in, effect on, influence on, interest in, attitude toward, need for, demand for, difficulty with/in, experience in/with, advantage of/over, relationship between/with, example of, lack of, respect for, ability to (+ verb)

There's no solution to this problem. Her attitude toward the project changed. The increase in prices had an effect on demand.

6. Common mistakes#

  • ✗ I need an information about the flight. → ✓ I need some information about the flight. — Information is uncountable, so it takes no a/an and no plural.
  • ✗ She gave me many advices. → ✓ She gave me a lot of advice. — Advice is uncountable; use much/a lot of and no -s.
  • ✗ My furnitures are new. → ✓ My furniture is new. — Furniture is uncountable and takes a singular verb.
  • ✗ He has three childs. → ✓ He has three children. — Child has the irregular plural children, not -s.
  • ✗ There are many womans in the office. → ✓ There are many women in the office. — Woman pluralizes by vowel change, not by -s.
  • ✗ I bought car yesterday. → ✓ I bought a car yesterday. — A singular countable noun always needs a determiner.
  • ✗ The childrens toys are everywhere. → ✓ The children's toys are everywhere. — Children is already plural, so the genitive is 's, and it must have an apostrophe.
  • ✗ The student's are late. → ✓ The students are late. — Plural nouns take no apostrophe; 's is only for possessives and contractions.
  • ✗ That is my sister house. → ✓ That is my sister's house. — Possession between nouns requires the genitive marker.
  • ✗ The police is looking for him. → ✓ The police are looking for him. — Police always takes a plural verb.
  • ✗ My trouser is dirty. → ✓ My pants are dirty. — Pants/trousers exist only in plural form and take a plural verb.
  • ✗ I have two homeworks tonight. → ✓ I have a lot of homework tonight. / I have two assignments tonight. — Homework is uncountable; switch to a countable synonym to count.
  • ✗ We discussed about the budget. → ✓ We discussed the budget. — Some verbs take a bare noun object; discuss takes no preposition.
  • ✗ Everyone have their own opinion about the news. → ✓ Everyone has their own opinion about the news. — Everyone is grammatically singular, and news takes a singular verb.
  • ✗ He is a good person, he has a good manner. → ✓ He has good manners. — In the sense "polite behavior," manners is plural-only.
  • ✗ The datas are incomplete. → ✓ The data are incomplete. / The data is incomplete. — Data is already the plural of datum; datas does not exist.
  • ✗ There were three deers in the yard. → ✓ There were three deer in the yard. — Deer is invariable: same form singular and plural.
  • ✗ She works in a shoes store. → ✓ She works in a shoe store. — Attributive nouns are singular; shoe store is the standard form.
  • ✗ This is the house of my parents. → ✓ This is my parents' house. — With people as possessors, English prefers the genitive to of.
  • ✗ The company are hiring. → ✓ The company is hiring. — American English treats collective nouns as singular.

7. American English notes#

The differences between American and British nouns are real but narrow. They cluster in the five places below.

7.1 Collective noun agreement (the biggest difference)#

American English treats collective nouns as singular. British English routinely allows the plural when the members are in focus.

The team is playing well.The team are playing well.
The government has announced its plan.The government have announced their plan.

This applies to companies, sports teams, bands, committees, and institutions. Both varieties treat the police as plural.

7.4 Possessive with singular nouns ending in -s#

American style guides more often recommend 's: the boss's office, Charles's book, Texas's law. British usage more often accepts the bare apostrophe. The AP Stylebook, widely followed in American journalism, is an exception and prefers Charles' book.

7.5 Institutional nouns and articles#

American English says in the hospital and at the university; British English says in hospital and at university when referring to the general institutional experience. Both say in school, in prison, in bed, at home.

✓ American: She spent a week in the hospital. ✓ British: She spent a week in hospital.

Beyond these, American and British nouns behave almost identically: the same plural rules, the same noun phrase structure, the same genitive marking. The remaining differences are scattered lexical facts rather than rules — American accommodations and sports (sports coverage) are countable or plural where British uses accommodation and sport (sport coverage), and American Legos pluralizes where British keeps Lego.

8. Tricky cases#

8.1 Words that shift class#

Most English nouns can be verbs, and most verbs can be nouns. Position tells you which.

I need a drink. (noun) / Let's drink some water. (verb) Take a long look. (noun) / Look at that. (verb) Send me a text. (noun) / I'll text you. (verb) That's a nice light. (noun) / Light the candle. (verb) / It's a light jacket. (adjective)

8.2 Gerund versus present participle#

Both end in -ing. The gerund is a noun; the participle is part of a verb or an adjective.

Swimming is fun. (gerund — subject) She is swimming. (participle — part of the progressive verb) A swimming pool. (gerund used attributively — a pool for swimming, not a pool that is swimming) I saw a running man. (participle — a man who is running) Running hurts my knees. (gerund — subject)

Test 1: if you can replace it with it or an ordinary noun, it is a gerund. Test 2 for the attributive cases: if the -ing word says what the noun is for, it is a gerund (a swimming pool, a washing machine, a walking stick); if it says what the noun is doing, it is a participle (a running man, boiling water, a sleeping child). Stress helps too — a gerund compound stresses the first word (SWIMMING pool), a participle phrase stresses the noun (running MAN).

8.3 Gerund versus deverbal noun#

✓ I object to his smoking in the house. (gerund — takes a possessive subject) ✓ I object to the smoking of cigars indoors. (deverbal noun — takes the* and of) ✗ the smoking cigars indoors* — mixing the two patterns.

Fully nominalized -ing forms take plurals and adjectives: long meetings, tall buildings, mixed feelings, painful memories, my savings.

8.4 Nouns that look plural but are singular#

News, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, statistics (the field), ethics (the field), linguistics, athletics, gymnastics, measles, mumps, diabetes, billiards, darts.

Economics is an unpopular major. Politics is a dirty business. (but: Her politics are conservative — the beliefs)

8.5 Nouns that look singular but are plural#

People, police, cattle, clergy, vermin, offspring (usually), media (formally).

Ten people were waiting. The cattle are in the north field.

8.7 Uncountables that fool learners#

These are singular in English but countable in many other languages, which is why they cause persistent trouble: advice, information, news, furniture, luggage, baggage, equipment, machinery, homework, work, research, evidence, knowledge, progress, traffic, weather, money, bread, toast, software, hardware, feedback, transportation, permission, damage, garbage, jewelry, poetry, scenery, vocabulary.

One to watch: accommodation is uncountable in British English, but American English uses the plural accommodations for lodging (The company paid for our accommodations.).

✓ a piece of advice / an item of news / a piece of furniture / a suitcase / a piece of equipment / an assignment

8.8 Number of versus amount of#

A number of + plural countable takes a plural verb; the number of takes a singular verb.

A number of students are absent. The number of students is decreasing. The amount of money is insufficient.

8.9 Attributive noun versus possessive versus adjective#

a children's hospital (possessive — a hospital for children) a child actor (attributive noun — an actor who is a child) a childish remark (adjective — resembling a child)

8.10 Nouns modified by whole clauses#

Fact, idea, belief, claim, news, rumor, possibility, chance, suggestion, reason take that-clauses as complements. These are complements, not relative clauses — the that cannot be omitted as freely. (Question and issue take whether-clauses instead: the question whether to appeal.)

the fact that he lied (complement) the lie that he told (relative clause — "that he told" describes the lie)

8.11 Zero article with abstract and generic nouns#

Life is short. (life in general) / The life of a soldier is hard. (a specific life) He went to prison. (as an inmate) / He went to the prison. (visited the building) She's in school. (a student) / She's in the school. (inside the building)

8.12 Proper nouns becoming common#

Brand names and personal names commonly get pluralized or lowercased: two Coca-Colas, a Xerox, all the Smiths, three Picassos, a Rembrandt. When a brand name becomes fully generic, it loses its capital: aspirin, escalator, zipper, thermos, google (as a verb).

8.13 Apposition and titles#

An appositive renames the noun beside it. Non-restrictive appositives take commas; restrictive ones do not.

My brother, an accountant, hates numbers. (I have one brother — non-restrictive) My brother Tom hates numbers. (I have several brothers — restrictive)

10. Practice#

A. Fill in the blank with the correct plural form.

  1. The scientist tested three different ________ (hypothesis) before publishing.
  2. Both of my ________ (sister-in-law) live in Denver.
  3. We saw several ________ (deer) near the highway at dusk.
  4. All the ________ (child) got new backpacks for the school year.
  5. The report listed five ________ (criterion) for evaluating the proposals.

B. Identify the type. For each bolded noun, name its category (proper, abstract, uncountable, collective, compound, gerund, or attributive).

  1. The jury deliberated for three days.
  2. Running every morning changed her mood completely.
  3. He keeps his tools in the toolbox in the garage.
  4. She admired his patience with the students.
  5. They renovated the kitchen cabinets last spring.

C. Correct the error.

  1. I need some informations about the tickets.
  2. My childrens school starts on Monday.
  3. The police is searching the neighborhood.
  4. She gave me many good advices about the interview.
  5. There are two womans waiting in the lobby.

D. Rewrite using a possessive (genitive) form.

  1. the toys that belong to the children → ________
  2. the office used by the boss → ________
  3. the essays written by the students → ________
  4. the apartment that Tom and Jerry share → ________
  5. a vacation lasting two weeks → ________

E. Countable or uncountable? Choose the correct option.

  1. She doesn't have (much / many) experience with contracts.
  2. There were (fewer / less) cars in the lot than usual.
  3. Could you buy (a / some) bread on your way home?
  4. The museum owns several (work / works) by Rivera.
  5. (The number / A number) of applicants has dropped this year.
Answers
  1. hypotheses — Greek -is → -es. Hypothesis is a Greek borrowing that keeps its original plural.
  2. sisters-in-law — In a hyphenated compound, the plural marker goes on the head noun (sister), not the last word.
  3. deer — Deer is an invariable noun: singular and plural are identical, like sheep and fish.
  4. children — Child takes the irregular -en plural, one of only a handful in English (child, ox, brother/brethren).
  5. criteria — Greek -on → -a. Criterion is singular; criteria is the plural. (Five criterions and a criteria are both errors.)
  6. Collective noun — Jury names a group of individuals treated as a single unit. In American English it takes a singular verb: the jury deliberates.
  7. Gerund — Running is the -ing form of a verb functioning as the subject of the sentence, so it is a noun here, not a participle.
  8. Compound noun — Toolbox is a closed compound made of tool + box, written as one word.
  9. Abstract (uncountable) noun — Patience names a quality with no physical form and has no plural; it takes no a/an.
  10. Attributive noun — Kitchen is a noun modifying another noun (cabinets). Note it stays singular, as attributive nouns almost always do.
  11. I need some information about the tickets. — Information is uncountable: no plural -s, and some rather than a number.
  12. My children's school starts on Monday. — Children is already an irregular plural, so the possessive adds 's; the apostrophe cannot be omitted.
  13. The police are searching the neighborhood. — Police is one of the few nouns that always takes a plural verb, in both American and British English.
  14. She gave me a lot of good advice about the interview. — Advice is uncountable, so no -s and no many; use a lot of or much. (Alternative: many good suggestions.)
  15. There are two women waiting in the lobby. — Woman pluralizes by vowel change to women, not by adding -s.
  16. the children's toys — Children is an irregular plural that does not end in -s, so the genitive is the full 's.
  17. the boss's office — A singular noun ending in -s takes the full 's in American style, including AP style here (see 3.6).
  18. the students' essays — A regular plural already ends in -s, so only the apostrophe is added.
  19. Tom and Jerry's apartment — Joint possession: the 's goes on the last name only. (Tom's and Jerry's apartments would mean two separate apartments.)
  20. two weeks' vacation — A time-measure genitive on a plural noun: the -s is already there, so the apostrophe follows it. Compare the singular a week's vacation.
  21. much — Experience is uncountable in the sense "accumulated practice." (Many experiences is fine in the countable sense "events one has lived through.")
  22. fewer — Cars is a countable plural, so fewer, not less.
  23. some — Bread is uncountable and takes no a/an. (A loaf of bread is the partitive alternative.)
  24. works — In the countable sense "individual creations," work takes a regular plural. The uncountable work ("labor") has none.
  25. The number — The number of is the head of the subject and is singular: The number of applicants has dropped. Contrast A number of applicants have dropped out.
