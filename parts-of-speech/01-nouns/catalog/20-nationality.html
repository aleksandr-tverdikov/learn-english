Nationality nouns#

Two Americans, two Japanese, two Spaniards — but never ✗ two Japaneses and never ✗ two Englishes. Four endings, four different grammars.

← The grammar of nouns · All groups

Nationality words are adjectives, nouns, or both, and which one depends entirely on the ending. Four patterns cover almost every country, and picking the wrong one produces some of the most recognizable learner errors in English.

Pattern 1 — -an, -ian, -i: fully countable nouns. An American, two Americans. A Russian, three Russians. An Israeli, two Israelis. These behave like any ordinary noun and give learners no trouble.

Pattern 2 — -ese, -ss: invariable. One Japanese, two Japanese — no -s ever. ✗ Two Chineses is wrong. In modern American English the noun use even in the singular sounds dated, and a Japanese person or a Chinese student is preferred. The collective the Japanese is fully normal.

Pattern 3 — -ish, -ch, -sh: adjective only, with a separate noun. English is not a noun for a person: ✗ two Englishes is wrong, and ✗ an English is wrong. You need an Englishman, a Briton, or an English person. Same for Spanish → a Spaniard, Danish → a Dane, Polish → a Pole, Turkish → a Turk, French → a Frenchman, Dutch → a Dutchman.

Pattern 4 — the collective the + adjective. The French are, the Dutch are, the Japanese are. Always plural, always with the, meaning the people as a whole.

One further note: nationality words are always capitalized in English, unlike in Russian, French, or Spanish. an American, not ✗ an american.
