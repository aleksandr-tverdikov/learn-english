1Plurals made by changing the vowelNo ending at all — the vowel inside the word changes: foot → feet, mouse → mice. Seven nouns, and that is the entire list.8
2The -en plurals and the odd ones left overChild → children, ox → oxen, and a handful of nouns whose plural is a different word entirely: person → people, die → dice, penny → pence.6
3Plurals that look exactly like the singularOne sheep, two sheep. No ending, no vowel change — only the surrounding words tell you which you mean.27
4When -f becomes -vesKnife → knives, leaf → leaves. The final /f/ voices to /v/ — but only for about two dozen words, and roof, chief, and belief are not among them.22
5Nouns ending in -oPotato → potatoes, but photo → photos. The ending decides, and so does where the word came from.25
6Latin -us → -iCactus → cacti, fungus → fungi. A real pattern with a short membership list — and virus, campus, and bonus are not in it.14
7Latin -a → -aeLarva → larvae, vertebra → vertebrae. A small group, and for several of them the plain English -s has already won.8
8Latin -um → -aDatum → data, medium → media. The plural often ends up feeling like a singular — which is why data is and media is are now normal American English.14
9Greek -is → -esAnalysis → analyses, crisis → crises. The spelling change is small; the sound change is not — the plural ends in /-siːz/, SEEZ.16
10Greek -on → -aCriterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena. A group of four, and two of them are among the most misused words in English.4
11-ex and -ix → -icesIndex → indices, matrix → matrices. The technical plural and the everyday plural often coexist — and mean slightly different things.7
12Plurals borrowed from other languagesGraffito → graffiti, cherub → cherubim, bureau → bureaux. Italian, Hebrew, and French plurals that English kept.11
13Uncountable nouns✗ an information, ✗ two advices, ✗ many furnitures. These nouns have no plural and take no a — you count them with a piece of, an item of, a bit of.33
14Nouns that are countable and uncountable — with different meaningsA hair is one strand; hair is what grows on your head. A glass is a container; glass is a material. Same word, two grammars, two meanings.17
15Plural-only nouns (pluralia tantum)Scissors, jeans, clothes. Always plural, always a plural verb, and no singular exists — to count them you need a pair of.19
16Nouns that end in -s but are singularThe news is good. Physics is hard. Measles is contagious. They look plural. They are not.16
17Collective nounsThe team is winning in America; the team are winning in Britain. One noun, one group, two grammars — and police is plural everywhere.16
18Plurals of compound nounsMothers-in-law, not ✗ mother-in-laws. Pluralize the noun that carries the meaning — which is usually not the last word.12
19Partitive nouns — how to count the uncountableA piece of advice, a slice of bread, a bar of soap, a pinch of salt. The uncountable noun cannot take a number, so a countable one carries it.18
20Nationality nounsTwo Americans, two Japanese, two Spaniards — but never ✗ two Japaneses and never ✗ two Englishes. Four endings, four different grammars.15
