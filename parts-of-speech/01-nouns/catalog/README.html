1Plurals made by changing the vowelNo ending at all — the vowel inside the word changes: foot → feet, mouse → mice. Seven nouns, and that is the entire list.8
2The -en plurals and the odd ones left overChild → children, ox → oxen, and a handful of nouns whose plural is a different word entirely: person → people, die → dice, penny → pence.6
3Plurals that look exactly like the singularOne sheep, two sheep. No ending, no vowel change — only the surrounding words tell you which you mean.47
4When -f becomes -vesKnife → knives, leaf → leaves. The final /f/ voices to /v/ — but only for about two dozen words, and roof, chief, and belief are not among them.37
5Nouns ending in -oPotato → potatoes, but photo → photos. The ending decides, and so does where the word came from.43
6Latin -us → -iCactus → cacti, fungus → fungi. A real pattern with a short membership list — and virus, campus, and bonus are not in it.33
7Latin -a → -aeLarva → larvae, vertebra → vertebrae. A small group, and for several of them the plain English -s has already won.22
8Latin -um → -aDatum → data, medium → media. The plural often ends up feeling like a singular — which is why data is and media is are now normal American English.30
9Greek -is → -esAnalysis → analyses, crisis → crises. The spelling change is small; the sound change is not — the plural ends in /-siːz/, SEEZ.29
10Greek -on → -aCriterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena. A group of four, and two of them are among the most misused words in English.4
11-ex and -ix → -icesIndex → indices, matrix → matrices. The technical plural and the everyday plural often coexist — and mean slightly different things.14
12Plurals borrowed from other languagesGraffito → graffiti, cherub → cherubim, bureau → bureaux. Italian, Hebrew, and French plurals that English kept.25
13Uncountable nouns✗ an information, ✗ two advices, ✗ many furnitures. These nouns have no plural and take no a — you count them with a piece of, an item of, a bit of.92
14Nouns that are countable and uncountable — with different meaningsA hair is one strand; hair is what grows on your head. A glass is a container; glass is a material. Same word, two grammars, two meanings.42
15Plural-only nouns (pluralia tantum)Scissors, jeans, clothes. Always plural, always a plural verb, and no singular exists — to count them you need a pair of.44
16Nouns that end in -s but are singularThe news is good. Physics is hard. Measles is contagious. They look plural. They are not.28
17Collective nounsThe team is winning in America; the team are winning in Britain. One noun, one group, two grammars — and police is plural everywhere.28
18Plurals of compound nounsMothers-in-law, not ✗ mother-in-laws. Pluralize the noun that carries the meaning — which is usually not the last word.24
19Partitive nouns — how to count the uncountableA piece of advice, a slice of bread, a bar of soap, a pinch of salt. The uncountable noun cannot take a number, so a countable one carries it.31
20Nationality nounsTwo Americans, two Japanese, two Spaniards — but never ✗ two Japaneses and never ✗ two Englishes. Four endings, four different grammars.30
21Collective nouns for animalsA pride of lions, a murder of crows, a parliament of owls. English has a specific collective noun for almost every animal, and using the wrong one is instantly noticeable.35
22Nouns that come in gender pairsActor/actress, waiter/waitress, widow/widower. Some pairs are alive, some are dying, and using a dying one marks you as out of date.20
23Proper nouns, brand names, and words that lost their capitalAspirin, escalator, and zipper were once trademarks. Kleenex and Xerox still are. The capital letter is the whole grammar here.22
