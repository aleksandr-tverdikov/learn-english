Uncountable nouns#

✗ an information, ✗ two advices, ✗ many furnitures. These nouns have no plural and take no a — you count them with a piece of, an item of, a bit of.

← The grammar of nouns · All groups

An uncountable noun names something English treats as a mass rather than as separate units. Three consequences follow, and every one of them produces a predictable learner error:

  1. No plural. ✗ informations, ✗ advices, ✗ furnitures, ✗ equipments.
  2. No a or an. ✗ an advice, ✗ a homework. Use some, or nothing at all.
  3. A singular verb. The news is good. This furniture is expensive.

To count them you need a partitive — a separate countable noun that packages the mass: a piece of advice, an item of clothing, a bit of luck, two slices of bread. Each entry below gives the partitive that native speakers actually use, because the wrong one is as noticeable as no partitive at all: a piece of furniture is normal, ✗ a slice of furniture is not.

Two warnings. First, countability is a property of a sense, not of a word — many nouns here have a countable sense too, and those are cross-referenced to dual-category nouns. Second, countability does not transfer between languages: information, advice, and furniture are all countable in Russian and uncountable in English, which is exactly why they are the errors you hear most.
