Collective nouns for animals#

A pride of lions, a murder of crows, a parliament of owls. English has a specific collective noun for almost every animal, and using the wrong one is instantly noticeable.

← The grammar of nouns · All groups

English has an unusually rich set of collective nouns for groups of animals, called terms of venery — venery being an old word for hunting. Most were compiled in fifteenth-century hunting manuals, and a good number were probably invented as wordplay even then. They survive because English speakers enjoy them.

Three tiers, and it is worth knowing which is which:

In genuine everyday use. A herd of cattle, a flock of birds, a school of fish, a pack of wolves, a swarm of bees, a pride of lions, a litter of puppies, a colony of ants. Getting these wrong sounds plainly incorrect.

Widely known but slightly literary. A gaggle of geese, a pod of whales, a troop of monkeys, a murder of crows, a covey of quail. Recognized by most native speakers; used mainly in writing and nature documentaries.

Ornamental and mostly for fun. A parliament of owls, an exaltation of larks, a shrewdness of apes, a business of ferrets. These are real and dictionary-attested, but a native speaker is as likely to say a group of owls. Deploy them for delight, not for correctness.

Grammatically these behave like any collective noun: American English takes a singular verb — the pride is hunting — and the verb follows the collective, not the animal.

A group of lions.

1. The group of lions. (прайд)

  2. The pride is led by two related males.

plague#

Pronunciation
/pleɪɡ/ · playg
Русский
нашествие (саранчи)
Countability
countable
Agreement
singular in American English — the plague is moving west
Counted with
a plague of locusts
Type
collective
Contrast
Used almost exclusively of locusts, with a strong biblical association. Also the disease, and a figurative sense: a plague of burglaries.

A group of locusts; also a widespread affliction.

1. The swarm of locusts. (нашествие саранчи)

  1. A plague of locusts destroyed the entire crop.
  2. The plague is moving west across the region.
  3. Both plagues were the worst in decades.

2. A widespread affliction. Figurative. (бедствие)

  1. The neighborhood suffered a plague of break-ins.
  2. It was the third plague of that century.

drove#

Pronunciation
/droʊv/ · drohv
Русский
гурт, стадо
Countability
countable
Agreement
singular in American English — the drove is on the move
Counted with
a drove of cattle, a drove of pigs
Type
collective
Contrast
Specifically animals being driven somewhere, as opposed to a herd simply standing. The plural droves means large numbers of people: they came in droves.

A group of animals being driven along.

1. The moving group. (гурт)

  1. A drove of cattle blocked the lane.
  2. The drove is being moved to summer pasture.
  3. Both droves took the old road.

2. Large numbers of people. Always plural. (толпы)

  1. Voters turned out in droves.
  2. They left the city in droves that summer.

pack#

/pæk/ · pak
Русский
стая (волков)
Countability
countable
Agreement
singular in American English — the pack is hunting
Counted with
a pack of wolves, a pack of dogs
Type
collective
Contrast
Used of wolves, dogs, and hounds. Also the container sense — see pack — and a group of cyclists in a race.

A group of wolves or dogs.

1. The group of canines. (стая волков)

  1. A pack of wolves brought down the elk.
  2. The pack is hunting at the edge of the range.
  3. Both packs defend separate territories.
  4. A pack is usually one family group.

2. A group of cyclists or runners. (пелотон, группа)

  1. He broke away from the pack on the last climb.
