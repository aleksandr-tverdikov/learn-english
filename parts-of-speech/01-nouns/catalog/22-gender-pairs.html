Nouns that come in gender pairs#

Actor/actress, waiter/waitress, widow/widower. Some pairs are alive, some are dying, and using a dying one marks you as out of date.

← The grammar of nouns · All groups

English does not have grammatical gender the way Russian, German, or Spanish do — an English noun is not masculine or feminine, and no article or adjective agrees with it. What English has instead is a set of lexical pairs: separate words for a male and a female person in the same role.

The interesting fact about this group is that it is shrinking, and fast. Three things are happening at once:

Pairs that survive name relationships and titles where sex is the point: husband/wife, son/daughter, king/queen, uncle/aunt, widow/widower. Nobody objects to these and nobody is replacing them.

Pairs that are collapsing name jobs. The -ess form is dropping out and the unmarked form is taking over for everyone: actor now covers all actors, server has largely replaced waiter/waitress, flight attendant has completely replaced steward/stewardess, and poetess and authoress are dead. A woman in these jobs is normally called by the plain form.

Compounds in -man that are being replaced by neutral coinages: chairman → chair or chairperson, spokesman → spokesperson, policeman → police officer, fireman → firefighter, salesman → salesperson, mailman → mail carrier. In American professional writing the neutral form is now the default, and the -man form reads as dated or as deliberately excluding.

The practical rule for a learner: when in doubt, use the unmarked form for anyone. Actor, server, chair, police officer are safe for any person. The -ess forms below are given so you can recognize them, with a note on whether they are still usable.

actor#

Pronunciation
/ˈæktər/ · AK-tur
Русский
актёр, актриса
Plural
actors — regular
Countability
countable
Agreement
plural verb — the actors were rehearsing
Type
concrete
Variants
actress /ˈæktrɪs/ is still used, especially in award categories
Contrast
Actor now covers performers of any sex, and most performers prefer it. Actress survives mainly because award shows keep separate categories. Neither is wrong, but actor is the safe default.

Someone who performs in plays or films.

1. A performer of any sex. (актёр, актриса)

  1. She is one of the finest actors of her generation.
  2. Both actors trained at the same school.
  3. The actors were still rehearsing at midnight.

2. The marked feminine form. (актриса)

  1. She won Best Actress that year.
  2. Many actresses now ask to be called actors.

Someone who serves food in a restaurant.

1. The neutral or male form. (официант)

  1. Ask the waiter for the check.

2. The marked feminine form. (официантка)

  1. Our waitress recommended the special.
  2. Two waitresses covered the whole patio.

Someone who looks after passengers; also a manager of property.

1. The aviation sense. Now replaced. (стюард)

  1. The flight attendants prepared the cabin. (current term)
  2. Airlines stopped saying stewardess decades ago.
  3. My grandmother was a stewardess in the 1960s.

2. A manager or caretaker. Still current. (управляющий, распорядитель)

  1. He is a careful steward of the endowment.
  2. Union stewards handle member complaints.

  1. The novel's heroine narrates the whole story.
  2. Both heroines end up disillusioned.

In American professional usage chair is now the default: she chairs the committee, the chair recognized the senator. Chairman is still correct but reads as traditional or male-specific. Note the plural takes the mutation plural of man: chairmen, not ✗ chairmans.

  1. She has been chair of the department since 2020.
  2. Both chairpersons signed the report.
  3. The chairwoman set a three-minute limit on questions.
