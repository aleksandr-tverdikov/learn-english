# The *-en* plurals and the odd ones left over

> *Child → children*, *ox → oxen*, and a handful of nouns whose plural is a different word entirely: *person → people*, *die → dice*, *penny → pence*.

[← The grammar of nouns](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Old English had a whole class of nouns that made their plural with *-an*. Modern English has **three survivors** — *children*, *oxen*, and the specialized *brethren* — and even those are not clean: *children* is a double plural, *-r* from the old plural plus *-en* added on top when the original ending stopped being recognizable.

Grouped with them here are the **suppletive** plurals, where the plural is not a modified form of the singular but a completely different word. *Person → people* is the one that matters daily; *die → dice* and *penny → pence* are narrower but catch learners because the "regular" forms also exist with different meanings.

---

### child

**Pronunciation:** /tʃaɪld/ &middot; *chylde*
**Русский:** ребёнок
**Plural:** *children* /ˈtʃɪldrən/ &middot; *CHIL-druhn*
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the children are asleep*
**Pattern:** *-ren* (a double plural) &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Contrast:** The vowel shortens in the plural: singular /aɪ/ as in *mild*, plural /ɪ/ as in *chill*. Saying ✗ /ˈtʃaɪldrən/ is the commonest learner error. ✗ *childs* and ✗ *childrens* are both wrong — the possessive is *children's*.

A young human, or anyone's son or daughter at any age.

**1. A young human being.** (ребёнок)

1. The *child* fell asleep in the car.
2. Six *children* were playing in the yard.
3. She has been teaching young *children* for twenty years.

**2. A son or daughter, regardless of age.** Here *child* says nothing about age — a sixty-year-old is still someone's child. (сын или дочь)

4. Both of their *children* live overseas now.
5. He is the only *child* of a famous architect.

**3. Someone shaped by a particular time or place.** Figurative, usually followed by *of*. (дитя, порождение)

6. She's a *child* of the nineties and it shows.
7. The whole design is a *child* of its era.

**See also:** [person](#person)

### ox

**Pronunciation:** /ɑːks/ &middot; *ahks*
**Русский:** вол, бык
**Plural:** *oxen* /ˈɑːksən/ &middot; *AHK-suhn*
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the oxen were yoked together*
**Pattern:** *-en* &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Contrast:** ✗ *oxes* is wrong. This is the last fully regular *-en* plural in the language. Note that *box → boxes* and *fox → foxes* are perfectly regular — only *ox* takes *-en*.

A domesticated bull used for pulling loads; more broadly, cattle of the genus *Bos*.

**1. The draft animal.** (вол)

1. A single *ox* can pull an astonishing weight.
2. Two *oxen* dragged the cart out of the mud.
3. The museum has a wagon with a full *oxen* team.

**2. In compounds and comparisons.** (в сравнениях)

4. He's as strong as an *ox*.
5. The *oxen* were yoked in pairs before dawn.

**See also:** [child](#child)

### brother

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbrʌðər/ &middot; *BRUH-thur*
**Русский:** брат
**Plural:** *brothers* /ˈbrʌðərz/ &middot; also *brethren* /ˈbrɛðrɪn/ in religious use
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *my brothers live nearby*
**Pattern:** regular *-s*, with an archaic *-en* plural surviving in one sense &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Variants:** *brothers* (family) &middot; *brethren* (a religious or fraternal body)
**Contrast:** The two plurals are **not** interchangeable. Family members are always *brothers*. *Brethren* refers to fellow members of a church, order, or society, and using it of your siblings sounds like a joke.

A male sibling; by extension, a fellow member of a group.

**1. A male sibling.** Always *brothers* in the plural. (брат)

1. My *brother* teaches high school chemistry.
2. She has three *brothers* and no sisters.
3. The two *brothers* opened a restaurant together.

**2. A fellow member of a community.** Of a church, union, or fraternity. This is the sense that keeps the old plural *brethren*. (собрат)

4. The *brethren* gathered for the evening service.
5. He addressed his union *brothers* and sisters.

**3. As a form of address, and as an exclamation.** Informal American speech. (брат, братан; ну и ну)

6. Thanks, *brother* — I owe you one.
7. Oh *brother*, here we go again.

**See also:** [child](#child)

### person

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɜːrsən/ &middot; *PUR-suhn*
**Русский:** человек
**Plural:** *people* /ˈpiːpəl/ &middot; also *persons* /ˈpɜːrsənz/ in legal and official use
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *people are waiting*
**Pattern:** suppletive — the plural is a different word &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Variants:** *people* (normal) &middot; *persons* (legal, official, signage)
**Contrast:** *People* is the everyday plural of *person*. *Persons* is not wrong, but it is confined to law, police reports, and signs — *maximum occupancy 40 persons*. Separately, *people* has its own singular sense meaning 'a nation', and that one pluralizes regularly: *the peoples of the Pacific*.

A human being. The word every learner needs and the plural nobody guesses.

**1. A human being.** (человек)

1. One *person* was waiting at the counter.
2. Twelve *people* signed up for the class.
3. She's the kindest *person* I know.

**2. persons — the official plural.** Legal documents, notices, and law enforcement. In ordinary speech it sounds stiff or bureaucratic. (лица)

4. The elevator holds eight *persons*.
5. Police are looking for two *persons* of interest.

**3. people — a nation or ethnic group.** A separate countable noun with a regular plural. (народ)

6. The Sámi are an indigenous *people* of northern Europe.
7. The exhibit covers the *peoples* of the Pacific Northwest.

**4. In grammar — first, second, third person.** (лицо)

8. Spanish marks *person* on every verb ending.

**See also:** [child](#child)

### die

**Pronunciation:** /daɪ/ &middot; *dy*
**Русский:** игральная кость; штамп
**Plural:** *dice* /daɪs/ (gaming) &middot; *dies* /daɪz/ (tools)
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the dice are loaded*
**Pattern:** suppletive in the gaming sense, regular in the tool sense &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Variants:** in casual American English *dice* is now often used for the singular too — *roll a dice*
**Contrast:** Two different plurals for two different senses. Gaming cubes: *dice*. Metal-shaping tools: *dies*. And be careful with the unrelated verb *die* 'to stop living', whose forms are *dies, died, dying*.

A small numbered cube used in games; also, in manufacturing, a shaped tool for cutting or stamping.

**1. A gaming cube.** Strictly the singular is *die*, but most American speakers say *a dice*, and *die* now sounds bookish outside board-game instructions. (игральная кость)

1. Roll the *dice* and move that many spaces.
2. He rolled a six on a single *die*.
3. Both *dice* came up threes.

**2. A shaping or cutting tool.** Regular plural. (штамп, матрица)

4. The shop cut new *dies* for the replacement parts.
5. A hardened steel *die* stamps the pattern.

**3. In fixed expressions.** (в устойчивых выражениях)

6. The *die* is cast — there's no going back now.
7. It was a roll of the *dice*, and it paid off.

**See also:** [penny](#penny)

### penny

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɛni/ &middot; *PEN-ee*
**Русский:** пенни, цент
**Plural:** *pennies* /ˈpɛniz/ (coins) &middot; *pence* /pɛns/ (a British sum of money)
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the pennies were in a jar*
**Pattern:** regular *-ies* for objects, suppletive *pence* for amounts &middot; **Group:** 2
**Type:** concrete
**Variants:** American English uses *pennies* almost exclusively; *pence* belongs to British money
**Contrast:** The split is objects versus amounts. Count the coins and you have *pennies*; state a price and British English uses *pence*. In the United States the coin is a *penny* but the unit is a *cent*, so *pence* is rare in American speech outside quoting a British price.

The one-cent coin in the United States, or the British unit worth one hundredth of a pound.

**1. The coin as an object.** (монета в один цент)

1. There were forty *pennies* at the bottom of the jar.
2. He paid the last dollar in *pennies*, which the cashier did not enjoy.
3. I found a *penny* on the sidewalk.

**2. An amount of British money.** (пенс)

4. The stamp cost sixty *pence*.

**3. In fixed expressions.** Very frequent in American speech. (в устойчивых выражениях)

5. A *penny* saved is a *penny* earned.
6. It didn't cost me a *penny*.
7. Then the *penny* dropped and I understood what she meant.
8. He pinches *pennies* but drives a new car.

**See also:** [die](#die)
