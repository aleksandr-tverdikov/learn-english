# Nationality nouns

> *Two Americans*, *two Japanese*, *two Spaniards* — but never ✗ *two Japaneses* and never ✗ *two Englishes*. Four endings, four different grammars.

[← The grammar of nouns](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Nationality words are adjectives, nouns, or both, and which one depends entirely on the ending. Four patterns cover almost every country, and picking the wrong one produces some of the most recognizable learner errors in English.

**Pattern 1 — *-an*, *-ian*, *-i*: fully countable nouns.** *An American, two Americans. A Russian, three Russians. An Israeli, two Israelis.* These behave like any ordinary noun and give learners no trouble.

**Pattern 2 — *-ese*, *-ss*: invariable.** *One Japanese, two Japanese* — no *-s* ever. ✗ *Two Chineses* is wrong. In modern American English the noun use even in the singular sounds dated, and *a Japanese person* or *a Chinese student* is preferred. The collective *the Japanese* is fully normal.

**Pattern 3 — *-ish*, *-ch*, *-sh*: adjective only, with a separate noun.** *English* is not a noun for a person: ✗ *two Englishes* is wrong, and ✗ *an English* is wrong. You need *an Englishman*, *a Briton*, or *an English person*. Same for *Spanish → a Spaniard*, *Danish → a Dane*, *Polish → a Pole*, *Turkish → a Turk*, *French → a Frenchman*, *Dutch → a Dutchman*.

**Pattern 4 — the collective *the* + adjective.** *The French are*, *the Dutch are*, *the Japanese are*. Always plural, always with *the*, meaning the people as a whole.

One further note: nationality words are always **capitalized** in English, unlike in Russian, French, or Spanish. *an American*, not ✗ *an american*.

### American

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmɛrɪkən/ &middot; *uh-MER-ih-kuhn*
**Русский:** американец, американка
**Plural:** *Americans* — regular
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *Americans are direct*
**Pattern:** *-an* → fully countable &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete
**Contrast:** Always capitalized. The collective is *Americans* rather than ✗ *the American*; the *the* + adjective pattern belongs to the *-ish* and *-ese* groups.

A person from the United States; also the adjective.

**1. A person from the United States.** (американец)

1. She married an *American*.
2. Two *Americans* were on the flight.
3. Most *Americans* have never held a passport.
4. He's an *American* living in Berlin.

**2. As an adjective.** (американский)

5. *American* English differs from British in more than spelling.

**See also:** [Russian](#russian) &middot; [Japanese](#japanese)

### Russian

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrʌʃən/ &middot; *RUSH-uhn*
**Русский:** русский, россиянин
**Plural:** *Russians* — regular
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *Russians take their tea seriously*
**Pattern:** *-an* → fully countable &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete
**Contrast:** Note that English uses one word where Russian distinguishes *русский* (ethnic) and *россиянин* (citizen). Context alone carries that difference in English.

A person from Russia; the language; also the adjective.

**1. A person from Russia.** (русский, россиянин)

1. Three *Russians* took the top places.
2. She married a *Russian*.
3. The *Russians* he met were all engineers.

**2. The language.** Uncountable. (русский язык)

4. He speaks fluent *Russian*.
5. *Russian* is written in the Cyrillic alphabet.

### Israeli

**Pronunciation:** /ɪzˈreɪli/ &middot; *iz-RAY-lee*
**Русский:** израильтянин
**Plural:** *Israelis* — regular
**Countability:** countable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *Israelis serve in the military*
**Pattern:** *-i* → fully countable &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete
**Contrast:** The *-i* nationality words take a plain *-s*: *Israelis*, *Iraqis*, *Pakistanis*, *Saudis*, *Yemenis*. Distinguish *Israeli* (a citizen of Israel) from *Israelite* (an ancient Hebrew) and *Jewish* (a religion and ethnicity) — three different words.

A person from Israel; also the adjective.

**1. A person from Israel.** (израильтянин)

1. Two *Israelis* were on the panel.
2. She's an *Israeli* who grew up in Haifa.
3. Most *Israelis* speak some English.
4. The *Israelis* and the Jordanians signed the accord.
5. He's an *Israeli* citizen by birth.

### Japanese

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/ &middot; *jap-uh-NEEZ*
**Русский:** японец, японка
**Plural:** *Japanese* — unchanged
**Countability:** countable but invariable
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the Japanese are famously polite*
**Pattern:** *-ese* → invariable &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete
**Variants:** *a Japanese person* is now preferred over the bare noun *a Japanese*
**Contrast:** ✗ *Japaneses* is always wrong. The singular noun — *a Japanese* — is grammatical but sounds dated and slightly impersonal in modern American English; say *a Japanese person*, *a Japanese woman*, *a Japanese colleague*. The collective *the Japanese* is perfectly normal.

A person from Japan; the language; also the adjective.

**1. A person from Japan.** Invariable. (японец)

1. Two *Japanese* were staying at the hotel.
2. She's a *Japanese* student at the university.
3. The *Japanese* are famously polite to visitors.

**2. The language.** Uncountable. (японский язык)

4. He reads *Japanese* but can't speak it.
5. *Japanese* uses three writing systems at once.

**See also:** [Chinese](#chinese) &middot; [Portuguese](#portuguese)

### English

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/ &middot; *ING-glish*
**Русский:** английский; англичанин
**Plural:** *the English* (collective) &middot; for individuals: *Englishmen*, *English people*, *Britons*
**Countability:** the adjective is not a countable noun for a person
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the English drink a lot of tea*
**Pattern:** *-ish* → adjective, with a separate noun &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete and abstract
**Contrast:** ✗ *An English* and ✗ *two Englishes* are both wrong for people. Say *an Englishman*, *an English person*, or *a Briton*. Note also that *English* and *British* are not synonyms: *English* means from England, *British* from the United Kingdom, which includes Scotland and Wales.

The language; the people of England; also the adjective.

**1. The language.** Uncountable. (английский язык)

1. *English* is spoken in more countries than any other language.
2. She teaches *English* to adults.

**2. The people of England.** Collective, with *the*. (англичане)

3. The *English* take their gardens seriously.
4. Two *English* people were ahead of us in line.
5. He's an *Englishman* married to a Scot.

**See also:** [French](#french) &middot; [Spanish](#spanish)

### Dutch

**Pronunciation:** /dʌtʃ/ &middot; *duch*
**Русский:** голландский; голландец
**Plural:** *the Dutch* (collective) &middot; for individuals: *Dutchmen*, *Dutch people*
**Countability:** the adjective is not a countable noun for a person
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the Dutch are the tallest people in Europe*
**Pattern:** *-ch* → adjective, with a separate noun &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete and abstract
**Contrast:** ✗ *A Dutch* and ✗ *two Dutches* are wrong. The country is the *Netherlands*; *Holland* is strictly only two of its provinces, though it is widely used for the whole. The people are *the Dutch*, never ✗ *the Netherlanders* in ordinary speech.

The language; the people of the Netherlands; also the adjective.

**1. The language.** Uncountable. (нидерландский язык)

1. *Dutch* is close to German in structure.
2. She learned *Dutch* in two years.

**2. The people of the Netherlands.** Collective, with *the*. (голландцы)

3. The *Dutch* are the tallest people in Europe.
4. Two *Dutch* students shared the apartment.

**3. In fixed expressions.** (в устойчивых выражениях)

5. Let's go *Dutch* and split the bill.

### Polish

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpoʊlɪʃ/ &middot; *POH-lish* — with a long *o*
**Русский:** польский; поляк
**Plural:** *the Polish* / *the Poles* (collective) &middot; for individuals: *a Pole*, *a Polish person*
**Countability:** the adjective is not a countable noun for a person
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the Poles have a long literary tradition*
**Pattern:** *-ish* → adjective, with a separate noun &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete and abstract
**Contrast:** The pronunciation is the trap: the nationality *Polish* is *POH-lish* with a long *o*, while the unrelated noun *polish* meaning shine is *PAH-lish* with a short one. The noun for a person is *a Pole*, and ✗ *a Polish* is wrong.

The language; the people of Poland; also the adjective.

**1. The language.** Uncountable. (польский язык)

1. *Polish* has seven grammatical cases.
2. She grew up speaking *Polish* at home.

**2. The people of Poland.** (поляки)

3. Two *Poles* were on the research team.
4. The *Polish* community here is large.
5. He's a *Pole* who has lived in Chicago for thirty years.

### Danish

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdeɪnɪʃ/ &middot; *DAY-nish*
**Русский:** датский; датчанин
**Plural:** *the Danes* (collective) &middot; for individuals: *a Dane*, *a Danish person*
**Countability:** the adjective is not a countable noun for a person
**Agreement:** plural verb — *the Danes cycle everywhere*
**Pattern:** *-ish* → adjective, with a separate noun &middot; **Group:** 20
**Type:** concrete and abstract
**Contrast:** ✗ *A Danish* meaning a person is wrong — though *a Danish* is perfectly correct for the pastry, which makes this one genuinely confusing. The person is *a Dane*. Compare *Swedish → a Swede*, *Finnish → a Finn*, *Turkish → a Turk*.

The language; the people of Denmark; also the adjective, and a pastry.

**1. The language.** Uncountable. (датский язык)

1. *Danish* is hard to pronounce even for other Scandinavians.

**2. The people of Denmark.** (датчане)

2. The *Danes* cycle everywhere, in any weather.
3. Two *Danes* finished ahead of him.
4. She's a *Dane* who studied in Boston.

**3. A pastry.** Countable, American English. (датская выпечка)

5. He had a coffee and a *Danish*.
