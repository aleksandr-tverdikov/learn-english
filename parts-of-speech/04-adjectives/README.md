# Adjectives

> An adjective is a word that describes or limits a noun or pronoun, telling us what kind, which one, how many, or whose.

## 1. What it does

An adjective's job is to add information to a noun. It narrows the noun down: *a car* becomes *a red car*, then *a fast red car*, then *a fast red Italian car*. Each adjective cuts the set of possible referents smaller. Grammarians call this **modification** — the adjective modifies the noun.

Adjectives do this work from two positions. They can sit directly in front of the noun (the **attributive** position), or they can sit after a linking verb and point back at the subject (the **predicative** position). Both sentences below say the same thing about the coffee:

> She poured the **hot** coffee into a mug. *(attributive — right before the noun)*
> The coffee was **hot**. *(predicative — after the linking verb* was*)*

A second job of adjectives is comparison. English adjectives change shape to rank things: *tall → taller → tallest*. No other word class in English does this except adverbs, which borrow the same endings.

> Denver is **high**, Aspen is **higher**, and Leadville is the **highest** town in Colorado.
> This is the **most reliable** truck I have ever owned.

Adjectives never mark number or gender in English. Unlike Spanish or French, an English adjective looks the same whether the noun is one or many, male or female:

> a **blue** shirt / three **blue** shirts / a **blue** dress *(never* blues shirts*)*

Note what an adjective is **not** doing: it does not name the thing (that's the noun's job), it does not tell you *how* an action happened (that's the adverb's job), and it does not by itself say *which* copy of the thing you mean — that's a determiner (*the*, *this*, *my*). Determiners are treated separately in this series because they behave very differently, even though older grammar books lumped them in with adjectives.

## 2. Types

Adjectives sort into a number of overlapping categories. Some categories describe **meaning** (descriptive, quantitative, proper), some describe **behavior** (gradable, non-gradable, attributive-only), and some describe **word formation** (participial, compound, denominal). A single adjective often belongs to several at once: *frightening* is descriptive, gradable, and participial all at the same time.

### Descriptive (qualitative) adjectives

These name a quality, property, or characteristic of the noun. This is the largest and most open class — new ones enter the language constantly. They answer *What kind?*

Common members: *big, small, hot, cold, beautiful, ugly, fast, slow, expensive, cheap, difficult, easy, loud, quiet, honest, rude, sweet, bitter, heavy, light, clean, dirty, strange, common.*

> The **narrow** road wound up a **steep** hill.
> He gave a **generous** tip to a **friendly** server.
> That was a **strange** thing to say.

### Quantitative adjectives

These tell how much or how many, without giving an exact number. (Many words in this area are more accurately called determiners; the ones below function adjectivally when they modify the noun's amount.)

Common members: *much, many, few, several, little, enough, ample, numerous, sufficient, scarce, abundant, plentiful.*

> We have **enough** chairs for **several** guests.
> There were **numerous** complaints about the **scarce** parking.
> He showed **little** interest in the offer.

### Numeral adjectives

Numbers used to modify nouns. **Cardinal** numerals count (*one, two, three*); **ordinal** numerals rank (*first, second, third, last, next*).

> She ran **three** miles on her **first** day back.
> The **twelfth** floor is the **last** one with a view.
> Take the **second** exit after the bridge.

### Demonstrative adjectives

*This, that, these, those* used before a noun to point at something. When they stand alone with no noun, they are demonstrative **pronouns** instead. See [Determiners](../09-determiners/README.md).

> **This** sandwich is better than **that** one.
> **Those** boxes go in the garage.
> **These** results surprised everyone.

### Possessive adjectives

*My, your, his, her, its, our, their* — they mark ownership and always precede the noun. Like demonstratives, most modern grammars classify these as determiners. Note the difference between these and the possessive **pronouns** (*mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs*), which stand alone with no noun after them: ***Her** apartment is bigger* vs. *That apartment is **hers***.

> **My** laptop crashed during **their** presentation.
> The dog buried **its** bone in **our** yard.
> **Her** apartment is bigger than **his** house.

### Interrogative adjectives

*Which, what, whose* placed before a noun to ask a question about it.

> **Which** train goes to Boston?
> **Whose** jacket is on the chair?
> **What** color did you paint the kitchen?

### Distributive adjectives

They single out members of a group one at a time: *each, every, either, neither.* They take a singular noun and a singular verb. (*Any* is often grouped with them, but it does not follow the singular rule: *any student*, *any students*, *any water*.)

> **Each** student receives a laptop.
> **Neither** answer was correct.
> **Every** window in the building was open.

### Proper adjectives

Adjectives derived from proper nouns. They stay capitalized.

Common members: *American, Mexican, Shakespearean, Victorian, Freudian, Christian, Buddhist, Parisian, Canadian, Marxist, Napoleonic, Newtonian.*

> She studies **Victorian** literature and **French** poetry.
> We ordered **Thai** food and opened a bottle of **Italian** wine.
> The professor gave a **Freudian** reading of the novel.

### Participial adjectives

Adjectives formed from verb participles. With verbs of emotion, the **-ing** form describes the source or cause of a feeling and the **-ed** form describes the person experiencing it. This is one of the highest-value distinctions in the whole class — see section 5 for the full rule.

The emotion pairs are the tricky ones, but plenty of participial adjectives have nothing to do with feelings. There the split is simply active vs. passive: **-ing** = doing the action (*running water*, *a leaking pipe*, *rising costs*), **-ed** = having the action done to it (*a broken window*, *stolen goods*, *fried rice*).

Common -ing members: *interesting, boring, exciting, surprising, confusing, tiring, terrifying, amazing, disappointing, relaxing, embarrassing, satisfying.*
Common -ed members: *interested, bored, excited, surprised, confused, tired, terrified, amazed, disappointed, relaxed, embarrassed, satisfied.*

> The lecture was **boring**, so the students looked **bored**.
> A **broken** window let in the **freezing** air.
> I found the instructions **confusing** and I am still **confused**.

### Compound adjectives

Two or more words acting as a single adjective, normally hyphenated before the noun. See section 3 for hyphenation rules.

Common patterns: *well-known, high-speed, five-year-old, part-time, state-of-the-art, world-famous, sugar-free, well-behaved, long-term, ice-cold, blue-green, English-speaking.*

> They took a **three-hour** flight on a **low-cost** airline.
> He is a **well-respected**, **hard-working** attorney.
> We bought a **stainless-steel** sink and a **built-in** oven.

### Gradable adjectives

Adjectives that describe a quality you can have more or less of. They accept *very, quite, rather, a little, extremely*, and they form comparatives and superlatives.

Common members: *hot, cold, tall, expensive, tired, happy, difficult, important, big, interesting.*

> The exam was **very difficult**, but the last one was **more difficult**.
> I'm **a little tired** and **fairly hungry**.
> It gets **extremely cold** in Minnesota.

### Non-gradable (absolute / classifying) adjectives

Adjectives naming an all-or-nothing quality or a category. They normally reject *very* and comparatives; they take *absolutely, completely, totally, utterly, entirely* instead.

Common members: *dead, unique, perfect, impossible, essential, freezing, boiling, furious, starving, terrified, wooden, nuclear, digital, annual, chemical, married, pregnant, correct.*

> The engine is **completely dead**. *(not* very dead*)*
> Her solution was **absolutely brilliant**.
> He works for a **nuclear** research lab.

### Attributive-only adjectives

Some adjectives appear only before the noun and never after a linking verb.

Common members: *main, chief, principal, mere, sheer, utter, former, latter, elder, only, outright, indoor, outdoor, lone, atomic (in* atomic energy*).*

> That's the **main** reason. *(never* The reason is main.*)*
> He is a **mere** child.
> The **former** president lives in Texas.

### Predicative-only adjectives

The mirror image: these come only after a linking verb. A large group begins with the prefix *a-*.

Common members: *afraid, alive, alone, asleep, awake, aware, ashamed, alike, ablaze, adrift, content, glad, unable, well (in health), ill (meaning "sick").*

Two cautions. *Ill* is predicative in the sense "sick" (*He is ill*, not *an ill man*), but a different, older sense of *ill* meaning "bad" is freely attributive: *ill health, ill will, ill effects.* And several adjectives that look predicative-only in one sense are attributive in another: *a **fond** farewell*, *an **upset** stomach*, *a **sorry** excuse.*

> The baby is **asleep**. *(not* the asleep baby*; say* the sleeping baby*)*
> The twins look **alike**.
> She was **afraid** of heights. *(not* an afraid girl*; say* a frightened girl*)*

### Postpositive adjectives

A small set that follows the noun. This happens in fixed legal or institutional phrases, after indefinite pronouns, and when the adjective carries its own complement.

Common members: *attorney general, court-martial, notary public, heir apparent, president-elect, secretary general, time immemorial, body politic, proof positive.* (American style hyphenates *president-elect* and *court-martial*; the others stay open.)

> Is there anything **interesting** on TV? *(after an indefinite pronoun)*
> The attorney **general** issued a statement.
> This is the only room **available**. *(also possible:* the only available room*)*
> She is a woman **eager to succeed**. *(the adjective carries a complement)*

### Denominal (relational / classifying) adjectives

Formed from nouns, they classify rather than describe. They are non-gradable and usually attributive-only.

Common members: *medical, financial, criminal, urban, dental, solar, coastal, presidential, municipal, industrial, atomic, verbal, mental.*

> She needs **medical** attention. *(not* very medical*)*
> The **criminal** case went to trial. *(compare the descriptive use:* a criminal act*, meaning "wicked")*
> They installed **solar** panels.

### Emphasizing and intensifying adjectives

They strengthen the noun rather than describe it. Attributive only.

Common members: *complete, total, utter, absolute, real, pure, outright, sheer, downright, very (as in* the very man*), certain.*

> That was a **complete** disaster.
> It's **sheer** nonsense.
> He is the **very** person I wanted to see.

### Nominalized adjectives (adjectives as nouns)

With *the*, some adjectives stand for a whole class of people or an abstract quality. They take a plural verb when referring to people.

Common members: *the poor, the rich, the young, the elderly, the homeless, the unemployed, the French, the British, the good, the unknown.*

> **The elderly** deserve better care. *(plural verb)*
> **The unknown** frightens people. *(abstract, singular verb)*
> **The Irish** have a long literary tradition.

## 3. Forms and inflection

English adjectives have exactly one kind of inflection: **degree**. They do not change for number, gender, or case.

### The three degrees

| Degree | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Positive (base) | plain quality | a **tall** building |
| Comparative | more than one other thing | a **taller** building |
| Superlative | most of three or more | the **tallest** building |

### Regular formation rules

| Adjective shape | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 syllable | add *-er* / *-est* | tall → taller → tallest; fast → faster → fastest |
| 1 syllable ending in silent *e* | add *-r* / *-st* | large → larger → largest; nice → nicer → nicest |
| 1 syllable, CVC ending | double the final consonant | big → bigger → biggest; hot → hotter → hottest; thin → thinner → thinnest |
| Ending in consonant + *-y* (1 or 2 syllables) | change *y* → *i*, add *-er* / *-est* | happy → happier → happiest; easy → easier → easiest; dry → drier → driest |
| 2 syllables ending in *-le, -er, -ow* | usually *-er* / *-est* | simple → simpler → simplest; clever → cleverer → cleverest; narrow → narrower → narrowest |
| Other 2-syllable | *more* / *most* | careful → more careful → most careful; modern → more modern → most modern |
| 3+ syllables | *more* / *most* | beautiful → more beautiful → most beautiful; expensive → more expensive → most expensive |
| Participial adjectives (any length) | *more* / *most* | tired → more tired → most tired; boring → more boring → most boring |

Notes on the CVC doubling rule: it applies only when a single vowel letter is followed by a single final consonant — *big → bigger*, but *loud → louder* (two vowel letters) and *fast → faster* (two final consonants). Final *w*, *x*, and *y* never double: *new → newer*, *slow → slower*.

Some two-syllable adjectives accept both forms. *Common, quiet, gentle, polite, handsome, pleasant, stupid, cruel* work either way:

> This street is **quieter** than mine. / This street is **more quiet** than mine.

### Irregular comparatives and superlatives

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| good | better | best | |
| bad | worse | worst | |
| far | farther / further | farthest / furthest | *farther* = physical distance; *further* = degree or extent |
| little | less | least | for uncountable amounts |
| little | smaller | smallest | when *little* means "small in size" |
| many / much | more | most | *many* + count, *much* + noncount |
| old | older / elder | oldest / eldest | *elder/eldest* mainly for family rank and attributive only (*my elder brother*, never *He is elder than me*); the fixed phrase *elder statesman* is the common exception |
| late | later / latter | latest / last | *later* = time; *latter* = second of two |
| near | nearer | nearest / next | *nearest* = distance; *next* = sequence |

> He drove **farther** than I did, but let's discuss it **further** tomorrow.
> My **elder** sister is the **eldest** of four children.
> The **latest** model is expensive; the **last** one was cheap.

### Comparison of equality and inferiority

| Pattern | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Equality | *as* + adjective + *as* | She is **as tall as** her brother. |
| Negative equality | *not as/so* + adj + *as* | This isn't **as hard as** I expected. |
| Inferiority | *less* + adj + *than* | The sequel was **less interesting than** the original. |
| Lowest degree | *the least* + adj | That's **the least** expensive option. |

### Degree modifiers with comparatives

Comparatives can themselves be modified by *much, far, a lot, a little, slightly, even, still, no*:

> The second draft was **much better** and only **slightly longer**.
> It's **far more complicated** than it looks.

### Compound adjective hyphenation

Hyphenation is a spelling rule, not a grammar rule, but it is tested constantly.

| Situation | Hyphen? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Compound before the noun | Yes | a **well-known** author; a **five-year** contract |
| Same compound after the noun | Usually no | The author is **well known**. |
| Adverb ending in *-ly* + adjective | No | a **highly regarded** surgeon; a **badly written** report |
| *well, ill, best, little, much* + participle, before noun | Yes | an **ill-advised** decision; a **best-selling** novel |
| Number + singular unit before noun | Yes | a **ten-mile** run; a **three-year-old** child |
| Number + unit after noun | No | The run was **ten miles** long. She is **three years old**. |
| Noun + past participle | Yes | **sun-dried** tomatoes; **hand-carved** furniture |
| Adjective + noun + *-ed* | Yes | a **blue-eyed** kid; a **short-tempered** boss |
| Color compounds before noun | Yes | a **blue-green** stripe; a **jet-black** coat |
| Comparative / superlative + participle, before noun | Yes | a **higher-priced** model; the **longest-running** show |
| Phrase used as one modifier | Yes | a **state-of-the-art** lab; an **out-of-date** map; a **hard-to-reach** shelf |
| Suspended compounds | Yes, with spacing | **two-** and **three-bedroom** apartments |
| Prefixes (usually) | No | **nonprofit**, **preexisting**, **antiwar**, **cooperative** |
| Prefix + proper noun or number | Yes | **anti-American**, **pre-Columbian**, **mid-1990s** |
| Prefix where a hyphen prevents a misreading | Yes | **re-cover** a sofa *(vs.* recover*)*; **re-creation** *(vs.* recreation*)*; **anti-inflammatory** |
| Fully lexicalized compounds | No | **airtight**, **nationwide**, **worldwide**, **lifelong** |

## 4. Position and word order

### The two main positions

**Attributive**: between the determiner and the noun.

> a **comfortable** chair / the **old** stone wall / my **favorite** restaurant

**Predicative**: after a linking verb, describing the subject.

> The chair is **comfortable**. / The wall looks **old**. / That restaurant seems **expensive**.

There is also an **object complement** position, after the direct object of certain verbs:

> The jury found him **guilty**.
> Paint the door **white**.
> Leave the window **open**.

### The royal order of adjectives

When several attributive adjectives stack up before one noun, English speakers order them in a fixed sequence. Violating it does not make a sentence ungrammatical exactly, but it sounds unmistakably wrong to a native ear.

| # | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Determiner | *a, the, my, three, some, this* |
| 2 | Opinion / quality | *lovely, ugly, delicious, boring, comfortable* |
| 3 | Size | *big, small, tiny, huge, long, short* |
| 4 | Physical quality / condition | *thin, rough, untidy, neat, clean* |
| 5 | Age | *old, young, new, ancient, ten-year-old* |
| 6 | Shape | *round, square, oval, triangular* |
| 7 | Color | *red, blue, dark green, pale* |
| 8 | Origin / nationality | *American, Italian, Eastern, lunar* |
| 9 | Material | *wooden, silk, plastic, gold, cotton* |
| 10 | Type / purpose | *electric, four-door, sleeping (bag), running (shoes)* |
| 11 | NOUN | |

A common mnemonic for the middle stretch: **O**pinion, **S**ize, **A**ge, **S**hape, **C**olor, **O**rigin, **M**aterial, **P**urpose — "OSASCOMP."

References disagree about age and shape: some list shape first. Both *a little old round table* and *a little round old table* occur, with the OSASCOMP order (age first) the more common. Everything else in the sequence is stable.

> a **lovely little old rectangular brown Italian leather** wallet
> two **beautiful large new white** cotton shirts
> my **ugly old plastic** lawn chair

In practice, native speakers rarely stack more than three. If you need four or more, split them: *a beautiful old wallet made of brown Italian leather.*

Two extra ordering facts:

1. **Opinion before fact.** Any evaluative adjective comes before any factual one: *a delicious hot meal*, not *a hot delicious meal*.
2. **The closer to the noun, the more essential.** *Purpose* and *material* sit closest because they practically form a compound with the noun: *a sleeping bag*, *a cotton shirt*.

### Commas between adjectives

Use a comma between **coordinate** adjectives — ones from the same category that could swap order or take *and* between them. Use no comma between **cumulative** adjectives, which build on each other in the royal order.

> a **long, boring** meeting *(coordinate — you could say "boring and long")*
> a **big red** barn *(cumulative — never "red and big")*
> a **cold, wet, miserable** day *(all opinion/condition)*
> three **old Spanish** coins *(cumulative — age then origin)*

Test: if *and* sounds natural between them and reversing them sounds fine, use a comma.

### Adjectives after the noun

Postpositive placement is required or preferred in these cases:

| Case | Example |
|---|---|
| After *something, anything, nothing, everyone, somebody*, etc. | Tell me something **useful**. |
| Fixed institutional phrases | attorney **general**, heir **apparent**, court-**martial** |
| Superlative + *available/possible/imaginable* | the best seats **available**; the worst outcome **imaginable** |
| Adjective with its own complement | a student **eager to learn**; a box **full of books** |
| Measurement phrases | a wall **six feet high**; a river **two miles wide** |
| *-able/-ible* adjectives after a superlative or *only* | the only tickets **obtainable** |
| Some fixed poetic/legal survivals | time **immemorial**, God **almighty**, the body **politic** |

### Adjectives with intensifiers and complements

The intensifier goes before the adjective; the complement goes after it.

> She was **extremely** proud **of her students**.
> The room is **too** small **for a conference**.
> He's **old enough** to vote. *(note:* enough *follows the adjective)*

### Position in comparative structures

> This is **the most interesting** book on the list.
> Never have I seen **so bright** a light. *(inverted, formal:* so/as/too/how *+ adj +* a *+ noun)*
> It was **too heavy** a box for one person.

## 5. Rules and patterns

### No agreement

English adjectives never agree with the noun. This is the single biggest relief for speakers of Romance and Slavic languages, and the single most common leftover error.

> five **tall** buildings *(not* talls buildings*)*
> a **beautiful** woman / a **beautiful** man *(no gender marking)*

### -ing versus -ed: the core rule

- **-ing** = the thing that causes the feeling (active, outward-facing).
- **-ed** = the person or animal that has the feeling (passive, inward-facing).

> The movie was **terrifying**. → I was **terrified**.
> His attitude is **annoying**. → I am **annoyed** by his attitude.
> The results were **surprising**. → We were **surprised**.

With this set of pairs, only a being that can have feelings takes the *-ed* form. A book cannot be *bored*, a project cannot be *interested*. But you can say *a bored student*, *a confused audience*, *a disappointed customer*. (This restriction is specific to the emotion pairs — inanimate things take *-ed* participial adjectives freely elsewhere: *a broken window*, *a paved road*.)

Watch the preposition that follows, because it is fixed by the adjective, not predictable from the rule. Most of these *-ed* adjectives take *by, at, with, about*, or *in*; a few take *of*:

> I'm **interested in** photography. / She was **shocked by** the news. / He's **worried about** money.
> He's **tired of** waiting. / She's **scared of** spiders. / I'm **ashamed of** my reaction. *(the* of *group)*

### Linking verbs take adjectives, not adverbs

After a linking verb, the complement describes the **subject**, so it must be an adjective:

Linking verbs: *be, become, seem, appear, look, sound, smell, taste, feel, get, grow, turn, remain, stay, prove, keep, go, come, fall, run.*

> The soup tastes **good**. *(not* tastes well *— unless you mean the soup has a good sense of taste)*
> She felt **bad** about the mistake. *(not* felt badly*)*
> The milk went **sour**.
> He grew **impatient**.

Many of these verbs are also action verbs, and then they take adverbs:

> The dog smelled **bad**. *(linking — it stank)*
> The dog smelled **badly**. *(action — its nose didn't work)*
> She looked **angry**. *(linking — her expression)*
> She looked **angrily** at him. *(action — the manner of looking)*

### Gradable versus non-gradable modification

| Type | Takes | Does not take |
|---|---|---|
| Gradable | very, quite, rather, fairly, extremely, a bit, slightly, too, so | absolutely, completely (usually) |
| Non-gradable (absolute) | absolutely, completely, totally, utterly, entirely, simply, virtually | very, rather, a bit |
| Non-gradable (classifying) | mainly, chiefly, entirely, purely | very, more, most |

> It was **absolutely freezing** outside. *(not* very freezing*)*
> The film was **very good**. *(not* absolutely good*)*
> The report is **purely financial**. *(not* very financial*)*

Some adjectives shift category with meaning. *Very unique* is widely criticized because *unique* means "one of a kind"; but in casual speech people use *pretty unique* to mean "unusual." In formal American writing, avoid grading *unique, perfect, complete, absolute, equal, fatal, final, impossible, universal.*

### Double comparison is forbidden

Use *-er/-est* **or** *more/most*, never both.

> **more expensive** *(not* more expensiver*)*
> **the best** *(not* the most best*)*

### Comparative for two, superlative for three or more

> Of the two candidates, she is **the better** speaker.
> Of the five candidates, she is **the best** speaker.

### *The* with superlatives

Superlatives normally take *the* or a possessive:

> **the tallest** mountain / **my best** idea / **the least** expensive route

Exception: when comparing the same thing in different states, *the* is often dropped:

> The lake is **deepest** at the north end.
> She is **happiest** when she's cooking.

### Parallel and repeated comparatives

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *the* + comparative, *the* + comparative | correlation | **The sooner** you leave, **the better** your chances. |
| comparative + *and* + comparative | continuous change | It got **colder and colder**. |
| *more and more* + adj | continuous change | The traffic became **more and more congested**. |

### Adjective + preposition patterns

These are collocations — they must be memorized, not derived.

| Preposition | Adjectives | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **of** | afraid, aware, capable, fond, full, guilty, jealous, proud, scared, short, sure, tired, ashamed, envious, typical, worthy | She's **proud of** her students. |
| **about** | angry (about a thing), anxious, concerned, curious, excited, happy, nervous, sad, serious, sorry, upset, worried, right, wrong, crazy | I'm **excited about** the trip. |
| **at** | good, bad, brilliant, hopeless, skilled, surprised, amazed, shocked, angry (at a person), terrible, adept | He's **good at** chess. |
| **with** | angry (with a person), bored, busy, careful, content, delighted, familiar, fed up, pleased, satisfied, wrong, obsessed, patient | Are you **satisfied with** the result? |
| **for** | eager, eligible, famous, grateful, late, necessary, ready, responsible, sorry (for a person), suitable, qualified, known | Boston is **famous for** its seafood. |
| **to** | accustomed, addicted, allergic, similar, kind, married, opposed, related, rude, used, close, loyal, essential, harmful, superior, inferior, senior, junior | She's **allergic to** peanuts. |
| **in** | interested, involved, experienced, disappointed (in a person), successful, rich, lacking, absorbed, deficient | Are you **interested in** the job? |
| **from** | absent, different, safe, far, free, exempt, derived, distinct | Life here is **different from** life in Denver. |
| **on** | based, dependent, keen, intent, hard (on someone) | The decision is **dependent on** funding. |
| **by** | surprised, shocked, amazed, impressed, bored, accompanied, plagued | We were **impressed by** her poise. |
| **between** | torn, divided | He's **torn between** two offers. |

Several adjectives change meaning with the preposition:

> He's **angry at** the referee. *(a person, AmE)* / He's **angry about** the call. *(a thing)*
> I'm **good at** math. *(skill)* / He's **good to** his mother. *(kind)* / Spinach is **good for** you. *(beneficial)*
> She's **sorry for** him. *(pity)* / She's **sorry about** the delay. *(apology)*
> He's **disappointed in** her. *(a person let him down)* / **disappointed with** the results. *(a thing)*

### Adjective + that-clause / infinitive

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| adj + *that*-clause | I'm **glad that** you came. |
| adj + infinitive | It's **hard to explain**. |
| *It is* + adj + *of* + person + inf | It was **kind of you to help**. |
| *It is* + adj + *for* + person + inf | It's **important for students to attend**. |
| adj + *enough* + inf | She's **old enough to drive**. |
| *too* + adj + inf | The box is **too heavy to lift**. |
| *It is* + adj + *that* + subjunctive | It is **essential that he be** present. |

The last row is the mandative subjunctive, triggered by adjectives of necessity and urgency — *essential, necessary, important, vital, imperative, crucial, advisable*. The verb in the *that*-clause stays in the bare form for every subject: *that he **be** present*, *that she **submit** the form*, *that they **not wait***. This is the standard American pattern; see section 7.

Note the *of* / *for* split: *of* + person judges the person's character (*kind, generous, rude, stupid, careless, nice*); *for* + person states what is necessary or possible (*important, essential, difficult, easy, possible, normal*).

### Adjective order with compound nouns

The adjective modifies the whole compound, so it goes first:

> a **broken** coffee cup *(not* a coffee broken cup*)*
> an **expensive** wedding dress

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *She has three beautifuls daughters.* → ✓ *She has three **beautiful** daughters.* — English adjectives never take a plural ending; they don't agree with the noun.
- ✗ *I was very boring during the lecture.* → ✓ *I was very **bored** during the lecture.* — *-ing* describes the cause; *-ed* describes the person feeling it. Saying you were *boring* means you bored other people.
- ✗ *This movie is more better than the book.* → ✓ *This movie is **better** than the book.* — Double comparison. Use *-er* or *more*, never both.
- ✗ *He is the most tallest player on the team.* → ✓ *He is **the tallest** player on the team.* — Same rule applied to superlatives.
- ✗ *The soup tastes deliciously.* → ✓ *The soup tastes **delicious**.* — After a linking verb use an adjective; the word describes the soup, not the manner of tasting.
- ✗ *I feel badly about what happened.* → ✓ *I feel **bad** about what happened.* — *Feel* is a linking verb here. *Feel badly* literally means your sense of touch is impaired.
- ✗ *She bought a red big Italian car.* → ✓ *She bought a **big red Italian** car.* — Royal order: size before color before origin.
- ✗ *It was a very unique experience.* → ✓ *It was a **unique** experience.* / *It was a **very unusual** experience.* — *Unique* is non-gradable; it can't be intensified with *very* in careful writing.
- ✗ *This exam is more easy than the last one.* → ✓ *This exam is **easier** than the last one.* — Two-syllable adjectives ending in *-y* take *-ier*, not *more*.
- ✗ *He is interested about photography.* → ✓ *He is **interested in** photography.* — Fixed collocation: *interested in*.
- ✗ *My car is different than yours.* → ✓ *My car is **different from** yours.* — *Different from* is the standard form; *different than* is accepted before a clause but *from* is safer.
- ✗ *Look at that asleep child.* → ✓ *Look at that **sleeping** child.* / *That child is **asleep**.* — *Asleep* is predicative-only; use *sleeping* before a noun.
- ✗ *That's the main.* → ✓ *That's the **main reason**.* — *Main* is attributive-only and cannot stand after a linking verb or alone.
- ✗ *He is well-known.* → ✓ *He is **well known**.* *(but: a **well-known** writer)* — Hyphenate a compound modifier before the noun, not after it.
- ✗ *A highly-regarded surgeon performed the surgery.* → ✓ *A **highly regarded** surgeon performed the surgery.* — Never hyphenate after an *-ly* adverb.
- ✗ *She is my more old sister.* → ✓ *She is my **older** sister.* / *my **elder** sister.* — Irregular comparative; *elder* is attributive-only and used mainly for rank within a family.
- ✗ *Of the two options, this one is the best.* → ✓ *Of the two options, this one is **the better**.* — Comparative for two, superlative for three or more.
- ✗ *I have a five-years-old son.* → ✓ *I have a **five-year-old** son.* — The unit stays singular inside a hyphenated modifier before a noun.
- ✗ *The room was very freezing.* → ✓ *The room was **absolutely freezing**.* / *The room was **very cold**.* — *Freezing* is an extreme, non-gradable adjective.
- ✗ *He is good in mathematics.* → ✓ *He is **good at** mathematics.* — *Good at* for skills; *good in* is not standard in this sense.

## 7. American English notes

Differences between American and British adjective usage are genuinely small — the grammar of the class is essentially identical. The real differences are in spelling, a handful of collocations, and one or two preferences.

### Spelling

| American | British | Note |
|---|---|---|
| color**ed**, favor**ite**, honor**able** | colour**ed**, favour**ite**, honour**able** | *-or* vs. *-our* |
| gray | grey | AmE prefers *gray* |
| skeptical | sceptical | |
| defense**less** | defence**less** | *-se* vs. *-ce* in the base noun; *defensive* and *offensive* keep the *s* in both varieties |
| practiced | practised | *a **practiced** liar* — BrE spells the verb and its participle with *s* |
| traveled, traveling, canceled | travelled, travelling, cancelled | AmE doesn't double final *l* in unstressed syllables — this affects participial adjectives: *a canceled flight* / *a cancelled flight* |
| judgment | judgement | affects *judgmental* |
| aging | ageing | |
| moldy | mouldy | *mold* vs. *mould* |
| pajama-clad | pyjama-clad | *pajamas* vs. *pyjamas* |

### Preferred forms and collocations

- **different from / than / to.** American English uses *different from* as standard and accepts *different than* especially before a clause (*It's different than I remembered*). British English uses *different from* or *different to*; *different than* is rare in BrE.
- **angry at / with.** AmE commonly says *angry at someone*; BrE prefers *angry with someone*. Both accept *angry about something*.
- **ill vs. sick.** In AmE, *sick* is the everyday adjective for unwell (*I'm sick*), and *ill* sounds formal or means seriously unwell. In BrE, *ill* is the everyday term and *sick* often means "nauseated / vomiting."
- ***good* as an adverb.** Both varieties consider *He plays good* nonstandard, but the informal AmE use of *good* for *well* (*I'm doing good*) is more common in American speech.
- ***real* as an intensifier.** *That was real nice* is a characteristically American colloquialism (nonstandard in writing); BrE would use *really*.
- **Written-out numbers as modifiers.** AmE style guides commonly hyphenate and write *a five-year-old boy*, *a 20-minute delay* — the same as BrE; the differences here are in style manuals, not grammar.
- **Collective nouns.** This affects predicative adjectives indirectly: AmE treats *the team, the government, the staff* as singular (*The team is ready*), BrE often as plural (*The team are ready*). The adjective itself never changes, but the verb does.
- ***burned / burnt*, *learned / learnt*, *spelled / spelt*, *dreamed / dreamt*.** As participial adjectives, AmE prefers the *-ed* forms: *burned toast*, *a learned skill*. BrE accepts *burnt toast*. AmE does keep *burnt* in a few fixed items (*burnt orange*, *burnt offering*, *burnt sienna*). Note that *learnèd* (two syllables, meaning "scholarly") keeps the *-ed* spelling in both varieties: *a learned professor*.
- **Adjectives of necessity + *that*-clause.** After *essential, necessary, important, vital, imperative, crucial*, AmE uses the bare subjunctive: *It is essential that he **be** present.* BrE more often inserts *should*: *It is essential that he **should be** present.* Both varieties accept the plain indicative in casual use (*It is essential that he **is** present*), but the subjunctive is the standard written American form.
- **Nationality adjectives.** AmE says *Hispanic, Latino/Latina* far more than BrE; BrE says *Asian* to mean South Asian, while AmE usually means East Asian.

### What is *not* different

The royal order of adjectives, the *-ed/-ing* distinction, comparative/superlative formation, attributive/predicative restrictions, and hyphenation of compound modifiers are the same in both varieties. Do not look for differences that aren't there.

## 8. Tricky cases

### Words that shift class

Many words function as more than one part of speech. Position and function decide.

| Word | As adjective | As another class |
|---|---|---|
| *fast* | a **fast** car | He drives **fast**. *(adverb)* |
| *hard* | **hard** work | She works **hard**. *(adverb)* — note *hardly* means "barely" |
| *late* | a **late** arrival | He arrived **late**. *(adverb)* — *lately* means "recently" |
| *well* | I feel **well**. *(adjective — health)* | She sings **well**. *(adverb)* |
| *light* | a **light** jacket | Turn on the **light**. *(noun)* |
| *iron* | an **iron** gate *(attributive noun)* | The **iron** is hot. *(noun)* |
| *running* | **running** shoes *(adjective/gerund modifier)* | He is **running**. *(verb)* |
| *that* | **that** book *(determiner)* | I know **that** he left. *(conjunction)* |

### Nouns used as modifiers are not adjectives

*A chicken sandwich*, *a bus stop*, *a computer screen* — *chicken*, *bus*, and *computer* are nouns doing an adjective's job (attributive nouns). The test: real adjectives can be graded and can appear predicatively.

> a **chicken** sandwich — but not *very chicken*, not *The sandwich is chicken.*
> a **delicious** sandwich — *very delicious*, *The sandwich is delicious.* ✓

### *-ly* words that are adjectives, not adverbs

*Friendly, lovely, lonely, silly, ugly, deadly, costly, elderly, cowardly, orderly, timely.* Most of these have no matching adverb, so you need a paraphrase.

> ✗ *She smiled friendly.* → ✓ *She smiled **in a friendly way**.*
> ✓ *That was a **timely** intervention.*
> Note *daily, weekly, monthly, early* work as both: *a **daily** paper* / *The paper comes **daily**.*
> *Likely* is an adjective everywhere (*the **likely** outcome*), and in American English it is also an ordinary adverb: *It will **likely** rain.* British usage prefers a hedge — *very likely*, *most likely* — in that adverbial slot.

### Adjectives that change meaning by position

| Adjective | Attributive (before the noun) | Predicative or postpositive (after it) |
|---|---|---|
| *present* | the **present** members *(current)* | the members **present** *(in attendance)* |
| *concerned* | a **concerned** parent *(worried)* | the parents **concerned** *(involved)* |
| *responsible* | a **responsible** adult *(reliable)* | the person **responsible** *(who did it)* |
| *involved* | an **involved** explanation *(complicated)* | the people **involved** *(participating)* |
| *proper* | **proper** procedure *(correct)* | the city **proper** *(strictly defined)* |
| *late* | the **late** Mr. Ford *(deceased)* | He was **late**. *(not on time)* |
| *old* | an **old** friend *(long-standing)* | My friend is **old**. *(aged)* |
| *poor* | my **poor** brother *(pitiable)* | My brother is **poor**. *(no money)* |
| *certain* | a **certain** person *(unspecified)* | I am **certain**. *(sure)* |
| *particular* | a **particular** case *(specific)* | He's very **particular**. *(fussy)* |

### The *-ed* adjectives that aren't past participles

Some *-ed* adjectives come from nouns, not verbs: *talented, gifted, skilled, bearded, freckled, wooded, spirited, moneyed, blue-eyed, left-handed.* There is no verb *to talent* and no verb *to money*; the *-ed* here means "having," not "having been ___ed."

> a **talented** musician / a **bearded** stranger / a **left-handed** pitcher

### Adjectives with irregular pronunciation of *-ed*

A few *-ed* adjectives are pronounced with the *-ed* as a separate syllable. Some of them contrast with an identically spelled verb form, which keeps the ordinary one-syllable pronunciation:

*aged* (AY-jid) *cheese* vs. *he aged* (AYJD); *blessed* (BLESS-id) *event* vs. *she blessed it* (BLEST); *learned* (LERN-id) *professor* vs. *he learned it* (LERND); *beloved* (bi-LUV-id).

Others are used mainly as adjectives and always take the extra syllable: *wicked, naked, rugged, crooked, ragged, jagged, dogged* (DAW-gid, as in *dogged determination*).

### *Very* + past participle

*Very* modifies adjectives, not verbs. When a past participle is still verbal (part of a passive), use *much, very much*, or *greatly*:

> ✓ I was **very tired**. *(tired = adjective)*
> ✓ The plan was **much criticized**. / **very much criticized**. *(criticized = still verbal)*
> ✗ *The plan was very criticized.*

### The *both … and* / *either … or* symmetry

Adjectives joined by correlative conjunctions must be parallel:

> ✓ The plan is **both ambitious and realistic**.
> ✗ *The plan is both ambitious and it is realistic.*

### *Such* vs. *so*

> **so** + adjective: The movie was **so long**.
> **such** (+ a) + adjective + noun: It was **such a long movie**.

### *than* + pronoun after a comparative

Both of these are in use, and they mean the same thing:

> She is taller **than I am**. / She is taller **than I**. *(formal —* than *as a conjunction, with the verb understood)*
> She is taller **than me**. *(normal spoken American English —* than *as a preposition)*

Keep the verb when dropping it would create real ambiguity: *He likes pizza more than I do* (I like it less) vs. *He likes pizza more than me* (he prefers pizza to my company). The same choice applies after *as … as*: *as tall as I am* / *as tall as me*.

### *fun* as an adjective

*Fun* began as a noun and is now also an adjective (*a fun evening*), but its comparative is unsettled. In careful writing use *more fun* and *the most fun*; *funner* and *funnest* are widely heard in casual American speech and still treated as nonstandard.

> The second day was **more fun** than the first.

### Adjective vs. determiner overlap

*Few / a few*, *little / a little*, *many*, *much*, *some*, *any*, *no*, *each*, *every* are classified as determiners in modern grammar, but older textbooks call them adjectives. Their behavior differs from true adjectives: they can't be graded (*very some* is impossible), they don't work predicatively the way true adjectives do (*The books are some* is impossible; *His friends are few* survives only as a literary turn), and they occupy the determiner slot at the very front of the noun phrase. See [Determiners](../09-determiners/README.md).

Note the meaning difference: *few* = "not many, almost none" (negative); *a few* = "some, a small number" (positive).

> He has **few** friends. *(lonely)*
> He has **a few** friends. *(a small but real group)*

### Absolute adjectives in real usage

Purists say *more perfect*, *very unique*, and *most complete* are illogical. Note that the U.S. Constitution opens with "a more perfect Union." Standard advice for learners: avoid grading absolutes in formal writing, but recognize that graded absolutes are common in speech and idiom.

### Adjective phrases as reduced relative clauses

An adjective with a complement after the noun is a shortened relative clause:

> The students **eager to graduate** filled the hall. *(= who were eager to graduate)*
> A man **angry at the world** is dangerous. *(= who is angry at the world)*

## 9. Quick reference

| Subcategory | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | names a quality | *big, honest, expensive, loud* |
| Quantitative | tells how much/many | *much, few, several, enough* |
| Numeral | counts or ranks | *three, first, twelfth, last* |
| Demonstrative | points | *this, that, these, those* |
| Possessive | shows ownership | *my, your, his, their* |
| Interrogative | asks | *which, what, whose* |
| Distributive | singles out members | *each, every, either, neither* |
| Proper | from a proper noun; capitalized | *American, Victorian, Freudian* |
| Participial (-ing) | source of a feeling; active | *boring, exciting, confusing, leaking* |
| Participial (-ed) | experiencer of a feeling; passive | *bored, excited, confused, broken* |
| Compound | multi-word, hyphenated before noun | *well-known, five-year-old, sugar-free* |
| Gradable | takes *very*, forms comparatives | *hot, tall, important* |
| Non-gradable | takes *absolutely*, no comparative | *dead, unique, nuclear, freezing* |
| Attributive-only | before the noun only | *main, mere, former, sheer* |
| Predicative-only | after a linking verb only | *asleep, afraid, alive, aware* |
| Postpositive | follows the noun | *attorney general*, *something useful* |
| Denominal / classifying | assigns a category | *medical, financial, solar, urban* |
| Emphasizing | intensifies the noun | *utter, complete, sheer, total* |
| Nominalized | adjective as a noun with *the* | *the poor, the elderly, the French* |

| Form rule | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 syllable | *-er / -est* | fast → fastest |
| 1 syllable, CVC | double consonant | hot → hottest |
| Consonant + *-y* | *y → i + er/est* | happy → happiest |
| 3+ syllables | *more / most* | expensive → most expensive |
| Irregulars | memorize | good/better/best; bad/worse/worst; far/farther/farthest; many/more/most |
| Equality | *as … as* | as tall as |
| Inferiority | *less … than* | less useful than |

| Position | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Attributive | det + ADJ + noun | a **new** phone |
| Predicative | subj + linking verb + ADJ | The phone is **new**. |
| Object complement | verb + obj + ADJ | Paint it **blue**. |
| Postpositive | noun/pronoun + ADJ | something **new** |

**Royal order:** Determiner → Opinion → Size → Physical quality → Age → Shape → Color → Origin → Material → Purpose → NOUN
*a lovely little old round brown Italian leather sewing box*

## 10. Practice

**A. Fill in the blank with the correct form.**

1. This puzzle is much ______ (difficult) than the last one.
2. She was ______ (embarrass) when the audience laughed.
3. Of the two routes, the coastal one is ______ (scenic).
4. That was the ______ (bad) storm in fifty years.
5. I'm not very ______ (interest) ______ politics. *(one word + one preposition)*

**B. Correct the error.**

6. He bought a Japanese small beautiful camera.
7. The instructions were very confused, so nobody knew what to do.
8. My grandmother is the most oldest person in the village.
9. A carefully-written report is a highly-valued document.
10. Of all my friends, Marco is the more generous.

**C. Choose the correct option.**

11. The soup smells (wonderful / wonderfully).
12. After the hike we were (very / absolutely) exhausted.
13. My cousin is married (to / with) a pediatrician.
14. I get a (ten-minutes / ten-minute) break at noon.
15. It is essential that every applicant (be / is) interviewed.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **more difficult** — *Difficult* has three syllables, so it takes *more*, not *-er*. *Much* is a normal intensifier for a comparative.
2. **embarrassed** — She is the one experiencing the feeling, so use the *-ed* participial adjective. *Embarrassing* would describe the situation, not the person.
3. **the more scenic** — Only two items are being compared, so use the comparative, not the superlative. *Scenic* is two syllables and doesn't end in *-y*, so *more* is correct.
4. **worst** — *Bad* is irregular: bad → worse → worst. Three or more items (fifty years of storms) calls for a superlative.
5. **interested in** — *-ed* form because the speaker feels it; the fixed collocation is *interested in*, never *interested about*.
6. **a beautiful small Japanese camera** — Royal order is opinion (*beautiful*) → size (*small*) → origin (*Japanese*).
7. **very confusing** — The instructions caused the confusion, so the *-ing* form is required. The people would be *confused*.
8. **the oldest** — Double superlative. Use *-est* or *most*, never both. *Old* is one syllable and takes *-est*.
9. **A carefully written report is a highly valued document.** — Never hyphenate a compound modifier when the first word is an *-ly* adverb.
10. **the most generous** — More than two people are being compared ("all my friends"), so the superlative is required.
11. **wonderful** — *Smell* is a linking verb here, so the complement is an adjective describing the soup. *Smells wonderfully* would claim the soup has a fine sense of smell.
12. **absolutely** — *Exhausted* is an extreme adjective; extremes take *absolutely, completely, utterly*, not the gradable intensifier *very*. (*Very tired* is fine, because *tired* is gradable.)
13. **to** — Fixed collocation: *married to someone*. *Married with* is only used in the different phrase *married with children*.
14. **ten-minute** — Inside a hyphenated modifier before a noun the unit stays singular. After the noun it goes back to plural and loses the hyphen: *The break is ten minutes long.*
15. **be** — Adjectives of necessity (*essential, necessary, vital, imperative*) trigger the bare subjunctive in a *that*-clause, whatever the subject; *is* would be the ordinary indicative, which is heard in casual speech but is not the standard written American form. British usage often writes *should be* instead.
