# Extreme and absolute adjectives

> *freezing*, *huge*, *delicious*, *unique*, *dead* — adjectives that already mean *very*, or that are simply true or not.

[← The grammar of adjectives](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Extreme adjectives already contain *very* — *freezing* means *very cold* — so they take *absolutely*, *completely* or *really*, never *very*: *absolutely freezing*, not *very freezing*. Absolute adjectives name a state that is either true or not (*dead*, *unique*, *pregnant*) and resist comparison, although real American usage bends that rule, and the entries say where.

---
### huge

**Pronunciation:** /hjuːdʒ/ &middot; *HYOOJ*
**Русский:** огромный
**Type:** size
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme, it already means *very big*: *absolutely huge*, *really huge*; *huger* and *hugest* exist but are rare — say *much bigger* or *the biggest*
**Contrast:** *Huge* already contains *very*, so *very huge* sounds like a learner: say *really huge*, *absolutely huge* or simply *very big*. *A huge fan* is someone who likes something a lot, not a large person.

Huge has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely large in size, amount or degree.** (огромный, громадный)

1. They live in a *huge* house outside Dallas.
2. The difference in price was *huge*.
3. Thanks — that's a *huge* help.

**2. Very popular, successful or important, informal.** (очень популярный; очень важный)

4. The band is still *huge* in Japan.
5. Podcasts got *huge* in just a few years.
6. Winning that contract is *huge* for a small company like ours.

### tiny

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtaɪni/ &middot; *TY-nee*
**Русский:** крошечный
**Type:** size
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *tinier*, *tiniest*
**Contrast:** *Tiny* is the extreme of *small*, so it goes more naturally with *really* or *absolutely* than with *very*, though *very tiny* is heard in speech. Americans often double it for effect: *a tiny little dog*, *teeny-tiny*. The *y* changes to *i* before *-er* and *-est*: *tinier*, *tiniest*.

Extremely small.

1. They rented a *tiny* apartment in Manhattan.
2. The print on the label was *tiny*.
3. She noticed even the *tiniest* mistake.

### enormous

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈnɔːrməs/ &middot; *ih-NOR-muhs*
**Русский:** огромный
**Type:** size
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely enormous*, not *very enormous*; *more enormous* is rare — say *even bigger*
**Contrast:** Extreme, so not *very enormous*: say *absolutely enormous* or *really enormous*, and use the adverb *enormously* to intensify other words (*enormously popular*). The noun *enormity* is a trap: careful writers keep it for great wickedness (*the enormity of the crime*) and object to *the enormity of the task* when only size is meant.

Extremely large in size, amount or degree.

1. An *enormous* truck blocked the whole street.
2. The pressure on the team was *enormous*.
3. She's made an *enormous* difference at this school.

### gigantic

**Pronunciation:** /dʒaɪˈɡæntɪk/ &middot; *jy-GAN-tik*
**Русский:** гигантский
**Type:** size
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely gigantic*, not *very gigantic*
**Contrast:** Extreme, so not *very gigantic*. *Giant* also works as an adjective, but only before a noun (*a giant step*, *giant waves*); after *be* use *gigantic* or *huge*: *The waves were gigantic*, not *The waves were giant*.

Extremely large; much bigger than usual.

1. A *gigantic* wave crashed over the pier.
2. The new mall is *gigantic*.
3. We ordered a *gigantic* pizza for the whole team.

### freezing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfriːzɪŋ/ &middot; *FREE-zing*
**Русский:** ледяной; очень холодный
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — sense 3 only before a noun (*freezing rain*, *freezing temperatures*)
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely freezing*, not *very freezing*
**Form:** -ing form of *freeze*
**Contrast:** Extreme, so *It's absolutely freezing*, not *very freezing*. *I'm freezing* means I feel very cold; *frozen* food or a *frozen* lake has actually turned to ice. In *freezing cold* the word works like an adverb meaning *extremely* (*freezing cold water*). In the United States the freezing point in a forecast is 32°F.

Freezing has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely cold, of the weather, a place, water or a part of the body.** (ледяной, морозный, очень холодный)

1. It's *freezing* out there — wear a hat.
2. We jumped into the *freezing* water of Lake Superior.
3. My hands are *freezing*.

**2. Feeling very cold, of a person.** (замёрзший (мне очень холодно))

4. Can you close the window? I'm *freezing*.
5. The kids came in from the snow *freezing* and hungry.

**3. At or below 32°F (0°C), the temperature at which water turns to ice.** (минусовой; ледяной (о дожде))

6. *Freezing* temperatures are expected in Atlanta tonight.
7. Drive carefully — there's *freezing* rain on the roads.

### boiling

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɔɪlɪŋ/ &middot; *BOY-ling*
**Русский:** очень горячий; кипящий
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — the heat sense usually after *be* (*It's boiling in here*); the sense of water or oil at boiling point only before a noun (*boiling water*)
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely boiling*, not *very boiling*
**Form:** -ing form of *boil*
**Contrast:** Extreme, so not *very boiling*. *Boiling water* is still bubbling (Russian *кипяток*); *boiled water* has been boiled and may have cooled (*кипячёная вода*), and a *boiled egg* is cooked. In *boiling hot* and *boiling mad* the word works like an adverb meaning *extremely*.

Boiling has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely hot, of the weather, a place or a person, informal.** (очень жаркий, раскалённый (мне жарко))

1. It's *boiling* in here — can we turn on the AC?
2. I was *boiling* in that wool sweater.
3. We waited two hours on a *boiling* sidewalk in Phoenix.

**2. Heated to the point where it bubbles and turns to steam.** (кипящий)

4. Drop the pasta into *boiling* water.
5. Pour the *boiling* water over the tea bags and wait three minutes.

### starving

**Pronunciation:** /ˈstɑːrvɪŋ/ &middot; *STAHR-ving*
**Русский:** умирающий с голоду; голодающий
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely starving*, not *very starving*
**Takes:** *for* + noun — wanting something badly (*starving for attention*)
**Form:** -ing form of *starve*
**Contrast:** *I'm starving* is everyday American for *I'm very hungry*, and nobody takes it literally. It is extreme, so not *very starving*: say *I'm starving* or *I'm so hungry*. For sense 3 the past participle is at least as common: *starved for attention*.

Starving has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Very hungry, informal.** (умирающий с голоду, очень голодный)

1. What's for dinner? I'm *starving*.
2. The kids were *starving* after soccer practice.

**2. Suffering or dying because there is not enough food.** (голодающий)

3. The charity sends food to *starving* families in the region.
4. Rescuers found the dogs cold and *starving*.

**3. Wanting something very much because you have not had enough of it.** (изголодавшийся (по чему-либо))

5. Small towns are *starving* for good jobs.
6. He was *starving* for someone to talk to.

### furious

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfjʊriəs/ &middot; *FYUUR-ee-uhs*
**Русский:** разъярённый; бешеный
**Type:** emotion
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** more/most — *more furious*, *most furious*; extreme, so *absolutely furious* rather than *very furious*
**Takes:** *at* or *with* a person; *about*, *over* or *at* a thing; *that*-clause
**Preposition patterns:** [furious with](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#furious-with)
**Contrast:** *Furious* already means *very angry*, so say *absolutely furious* or *really furious*, not *very furious*. The prepositions follow *angry*: *furious at* or *with* someone, *furious about* or *over* something. Sense 2 has nothing to do with anger — *a furious pace* is simply a very fast one.

Furious has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely angry.** (разъярённый, взбешённый)

1. My dad was *furious* when he saw the dent in the car.
2. She's *furious* with her brother for telling everyone.
3. Voters grew *more furious* about the tax increase every week.

**2. Very fast, energetic or violent.** (бешеный, неистовый)

4. The workers kept up a *furious* pace all week.
5. The storm hit the coast with *furious* force.
6. A *furious* debate broke out online.

### delicious

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈlɪʃəs/ &middot; *dih-LISH-uhs*
**Русский:** очень вкусный
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** more/most — *more delicious*, *most delicious*; extreme, so *absolutely delicious* rather than *very delicious*
**Contrast:** *Delicious* is much stronger than *tasty* or *good*. Because it already means *very tasty*, *really delicious* and *absolutely delicious* sound natural and *very delicious* sounds like a learner.

Delicious has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Having a very pleasant taste or smell.** (очень вкусный, аппетитный)

1. Thanks for dinner — the lasagna was *delicious*.
2. A *delicious* smell was coming from the kitchen.
3. That's the *most delicious* pie I've ever had.

**2. Very pleasant or enjoyable.** (восхитительный, упоительный)

4. There was a *delicious* irony in losing to his old team.
5. She had a *delicious* secret she couldn't wait to share.

### hilarious

**Pronunciation:** /hɪˈlɛriəs/ &middot; *hih-LAIR-ee-uhs*
**Русский:** уморительный
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** more/most — *more hilarious*, *most hilarious*; extreme, so *absolutely hilarious*, not *very hilarious*
**Contrast:** *Hilarious* means *extremely funny*, so *very hilarious* is wrong — say *really hilarious* or *absolutely hilarious*, or just *very funny*. It describes what makes people laugh, not how a person feels: *The movie was hilarious*, not *I felt hilarious*.

Extremely funny.

1. Her impression of our boss was *hilarious*.
2. We watched a *hilarious* video of a cat stealing a taco.
3. It was the *most hilarious* thing I'd seen all year.

### awful

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔːfəl/ &middot; *AW-fuhl*
**Русский:** ужасный
**Type:** evaluative; also emphasizing
**Position:** both — the 'very great' sense only before a noun (*an awful lot*)
**Comparison:** more/most — *more awful*, *most awful*; *really awful* or *absolutely awful* rather than *very awful*
**Takes:** *about* + noun or *-ing* — guilty or sorry (*feel awful about it*); *to* a person — unkind (*awful to her*); *that*-clause after *it* (*It's awful that he lost his job*)
**Form:** *awe* + *-ful*, with the *e* dropped
**Contrast:** *Awful* and *awesome* both come from *awe* but now mean opposite things: *awful* is very bad, *awesome* is great. Americans also use *awful* as an adverb in speech (*awful tired*, *awful nice*); that is informal — in writing use *awfully* or *really*. *An awful lot* just means a very large amount and is not negative. Spelling: *awful*, never *aweful*.

Awful has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Very bad or unpleasant.** (ужасный, отвратительный)

1. The traffic on I-95 was *awful* this morning.
2. He was *awful* to his little sister.
3. It was the *most awful* week of my life.

**2. Ill; not well.** (плохо (о самочувствии))

4. I feel *awful* — I think I'm getting the flu.
5. You look *awful*. Go home and rest.

**3. Guilty or sorry about something.** (ужасно стыдно, неловко)

6. I feel *awful* about forgetting your birthday.
7. She felt *awful* that she couldn't help.

**4. Very great in amount or degree, used for emphasis before a noun.** (ужасно много; страшный)

8. That's an *awful* lot of money for a used car.
9. He's gone to an *awful* lot of trouble for us.

### terrible

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtɛrəbəl/ &middot; *TER-uh-buhl*
**Русский:** ужасный
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** more/most — *more terrible*, *most terrible*; *really terrible* rather than *very terrible*
**Takes:** *at* + noun or *-ing* — bad at doing something (*terrible at math*); *about* — guilty or sorry (*feel terrible about it*); *to* a person — unkind
**Preposition patterns:** [terrible at](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#terrible-at)
**Contrast:** The adverb *terribly* usually just means *very* and is not negative (*terribly sorry*, *terribly important*). Don't confuse *terrible* with *terrific*, which in American English means *great* (*You did a terrific job*), even though it looks related to *terror*. For skill use *at*: *I'm terrible at math*, not *in math*.

Terrible has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Very bad, unpleasant or serious; causing great harm, pain or fear.** (ужасный, страшный)

1. There was a *terrible* accident on the highway last night.
2. The food at that place is *terrible*.
3. It was the *most terrible* storm the town had ever seen.

**2. Very bad at doing something; lacking skill.** (никудышный, бездарный)

4. I'm *terrible* at remembering names.
5. He's a *terrible* driver.

**3. Ill; not well.** (плохо (о самочувствии))

6. I feel *terrible* — I didn't sleep at all.
7. She looked *terrible* after the long flight.

**4. Guilty or sorry about something.** (ужасно стыдно, неловко)

8. I feel *terrible* about what I said to you.
9. He felt *terrible* that he'd missed her game.

### fantastic

**Pronunciation:** /fænˈtæstɪk/ &middot; *fan-TAS-tik*
**Русский:** потрясающий; фантастический
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme in the 'excellent' sense: *absolutely fantastic*, not *very fantastic*
**Contrast:** False friend: Russian *фантастика* is *science fiction* or *fantasy* — a sci-fi book is *a science fiction novel*, while *a fantastic novel* is just a wonderful one. In everyday American English *fantastic* almost always means *excellent*; sense 3 belongs to books, art and ideas. Extreme, so *absolutely fantastic*, not *very fantastic*.

Fantastic has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely good; wonderful.** (потрясающий, великолепный)

1. You look *fantastic*!
2. We had a *fantastic* time in New Orleans.
3. Your presentation was *fantastic*.

**2. Extremely large, of an amount.** (баснословный, огромный)

4. Some players earn *fantastic* sums of money.
5. The company grew at a *fantastic* rate.

**3. Strange and imaginative, or too strange to be believed.** (фантастический, причудливый, невероятный)

6. The book is full of *fantastic* creatures and talking animals.
7. He told some *fantastic* story about being chased by a bear.

### excellent

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛksələnt/ &middot; *EK-suh-luhnt*
**Русский:** отличный
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely excellent* or *truly excellent*, not *very excellent*; *most excellent* is a joke or a formal title
**Takes:** *at* + noun or *-ing* — very good at (*excellent at chess*); *for* — very well suited to (*excellent for beginners*)
**Contrast:** *Excellent* is already the top of *good*, so *very excellent* and *more excellent* are learner errors; to rank two excellent things, say one is *even better*. *Excellent!* on its own is a reply meaning *great*. Spelling: double *l* and *-ent*, not *-ant*.

Extremely good; of very high quality.

1. She speaks *excellent* Spanish.
2. The service at the hotel was *excellent*.
3. This park is *excellent* for little kids.

**Position:** both
**Comparison:** more/most — *more gorgeous*, *most gorgeous*; extreme, so *absolutely gorgeous* rather than *very gorgeous*
**Contrast:** *Gorgeous* is much stronger than *pretty* and is used for men as well as women (*a gorgeous guy*). Americans use it just as freely for weather, places and food, where it means *wonderful*. Extreme, so *absolutely gorgeous* or *so gorgeous*, not *very gorgeous*.

Gorgeous has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Very beautiful or attractive.** (очень красивый, великолепный)

1. The bride looked *gorgeous*.
2. They have a *gorgeous* view of the bay.
3. She's the *most gorgeous* baby I've ever seen.

**2. Very pleasant, of the weather, food or an experience.** (чудесный, дивный)

4. It's a *gorgeous* day — let's eat outside.
5. The weather in San Diego was *gorgeous* all week.

### ancient

**Pronunciation:** /ˈeɪnʃənt/ &middot; *AYN-shuhnt*
**Русский:** древний; античный
**Type:** age
**Position:** both — sense 1 only before a noun (*ancient Rome*)
**Comparison:** more/most — *more ancient*, *most ancient*
**Contrast:** False friend: Russian *античный* is English *ancient* or *classical* (*ancient Greece*, *classical art*), while English *antique* means old and valuable, like furniture (*антикварный*). *Ancient* is not the everyday word for an old person — *old* or *elderly* is; calling someone *ancient* is a joke or an insult.

Ancient has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Belonging to the very distant past, especially to the civilizations before the fall of Rome.** (древний, античный)

1. We studied *ancient* Greece and Rome in sixth grade.
2. The museum has a room full of *ancient* Egyptian jewelry.

**2. Having existed for a very long time.** (древний, старинный)

3. The town is built around an *ancient* oak tree.
4. These are some of the *most ancient* rocks on Earth.
5. The custom is *ancient*.

**3. Very old or old-fashioned, humorous.** (допотопный, древний (шутл.))

6. My laptop is *ancient* — it takes five minutes to start.
7. Don't ask him about TikTok; he's *ancient*.

### packed

**Pronunciation:** /pækt/ &middot; *PAKT*
**Русский:** переполненный; набитый
**Type:** participial
**Position:** both — sense 3 only after a verb (*I'm all packed*)
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *completely packed*, *absolutely packed* or *jam-packed*, not *very packed*
**Takes:** *with* + noun — full of (*packed with tourists*, *packed with vitamins*)
**Form:** past participle of *pack*
**Contrast:** *Packed* already means *very crowded*, so *very packed* sounds wrong; Americans say *packed*, *jam-packed* or *completely packed*. *Are you packed?* asks whether someone has finished packing a suitcase, not whether they are in a crowd. Don't confuse it with *packaged* (*packaged food*, sold in a package).

Packed has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely crowded.** (переполненный, битком набитый)

1. The subway was *packed* at 8 a.m.
2. The band played to a *packed* stadium.
3. The restaurant is always *packed* on Fridays.

**2. Containing a lot of something.** (полный, насыщенный (чем-либо))

4. The guidebook is *packed* with useful tips.
5. The movie is *packed* with action from start to finish.

**3. Having finished putting your things into bags for a trip.** (собравшийся, с собранными вещами)

6. Are you *packed* yet? The cab's coming at six.
7. I'm all *packed* and ready to go.

**4. Pressed down hard, of snow, soil or earth.** (утрамбованный, плотный)

8. Cars kept sliding on the *packed* snow.
9. The trail was *packed* dirt, hard as concrete.

### soaked

**Pronunciation:** /soʊkt/ &middot; *SOHKT*

**Type:** participial
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *completely soaked*, *soaked through*, not *very soaked*

**Form:** past participle of *soak*
**Contrast:** *Soaked* is the extreme of *wet*, so not *very soaked*: say *soaked*, *soaked through* or *soaking wet*. In *soaking wet* the *-ing* form works like an adverb meaning *completely*. Before a noun it often forms a hyphenated compound: *a blood-soaked shirt*, *a rain-soaked field*.

Soaked has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Completely wet.** (промокший насквозь, мокрый до нитки)

1. We got caught in the storm and came home *soaked*.
2. Take off those *soaked* socks.
3. His shirt was *soaked* through with sweat.

**2. Full of a quality or feeling, literary.** (пропитанный, проникнутый)

4. The film is *soaked* in nostalgia for the 1980s.
5. Boston is a city *soaked* in history.

### devastated

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɛvəsteɪtɪd/ &middot; *DEV-uh-stay-tid*
**Русский:** убитый горем; опустошённый
**Type:** emotion; also participial
**Position:** both

Devastated has 2 distinct senses.

2. The players were *devastated* to lose in the final seconds.

**2. Badly damaged or destroyed, of a place.** (опустошённый, разорённый)

4. Volunteers brought water to the *devastated* town.
5. After the hurricane, the whole coast looked *devastated*.

### ecstatic

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈstætɪk/ &middot; *ik-STAT-ik*
**Русский:** в восторге; экстатический
**Type:** emotion
**Position:** both
**Comparison:** not gradable — extreme: *absolutely ecstatic*, not *very ecstatic*
**Takes:** *about*, *over* or *at* + noun; *to*-infinitive; *that*-clause
**Contrast:** Russian *экстатический* suggests a trance or religious rapture; everyday American *ecstatic* just means *thrilled*: *I'm ecstatic about the new job*. Extreme, so *absolutely ecstatic* rather than *very ecstatic*.

Ecstatic has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Extremely happy and excited.** (в восторге, вне себя от радости)

1. My parents were *ecstatic* when I got into college.
