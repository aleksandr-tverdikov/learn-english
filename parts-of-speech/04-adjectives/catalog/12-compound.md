# Compound adjectives

> *well-known*, *part-time*, *old-fashioned*, *a five-year plan* — two words working as one.

[← The grammar of adjectives](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Compound adjectives join two or more words into a single modifier, and the hyphen is part of the grammar: *a well-known author*, but *the author is well known*. Number compounds take a singular noun — *a five-year plan*, never *a five-years plan* — which is the error a Russian speaker is most likely to make with this group.

---
### well-known

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛlˈnoʊn/ &middot; *wel-NOHN*
**Русский:** известный; общеизвестный
**Type:** compound — adverb + past participle
**Position:** both — always hyphenated before a noun (*a well-known writer*); after a verb often written open (*she is well known*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better-known*, *best-known*; after a verb *better known*, *best known*
**Takes:** *for* + noun or *-ing* (the reason people know it); *as* + noun (the role it is known in); *to* + person (who knows it); *that*-clause in *it is well known that*
**Form:** compound — *well* + past participle *known*
**Opposite:** *unknown*; *little-known*
**Contrast:** The comparative is *better-known*, *best-known*, not *weller-known*; *more well-known* is common in speech but is avoided in careful writing. The preposition changes the meaning: *well known for her novels* (the reason), *well known as a novelist* (the role), *well known to the police* (who knows her). A *well-known* fact is общеизвестный, not just известный, so it needs no *famous*: say *a well-known fact*, never *a famous fact*.

Well-known has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Known about by many people; famous.** (известный)

1. The restaurant is owned by a *well-known* chef from Chicago.
2. She is *well known* for her work on climate policy.
3. He is *better known* as a songwriter than as a singer.

**2. Of a fact or idea: known to most people, so that nobody needs to explain it.** (общеизвестный, всем известный)

4. It's a *well-known* fact that smoking causes cancer.
5. It is *well known* that the first year of a startup is the hardest.

### well-behaved

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛlbɪˈheɪvd/ &middot; *wel-bi-HAYVD*
**Русский:** послушный; хорошо себя ведущий
**Type:** compound — adverb + past participle
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*a well-behaved dog*); after a verb often written open (*the kids were well behaved*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better-behaved*, *best-behaved*; after a verb *better behaved*
**Form:** compound — *well* + past participle of *behave*
**Opposite:** *badly behaved*
**Contrast:** Russian *воспитанный* about an adult is usually *polite* or *well-mannered*; *well-behaved* is mostly said of children, pets and students, and describes how they act, not their upbringing. The comparative is *better-behaved*, not *more well-behaved* in careful writing.

Well-behaved has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of a child, student or animal: behaving politely and doing what they are told.** (послушный, хорошо себя ведущий)

1. Our neighbors have three very *well-behaved* kids.
2. The dog was surprisingly *well behaved* at the vet.
3. This year's class is the *best-behaved* group I've ever taught.

**2. Of a machine, program or process: working in a predictable way, without surprises.** (предсказуемый, работающий без сбоев)

4. A *well-behaved* app doesn't drain your battery in the background.
5. The function is *well behaved* for all positive values of x.

### well-off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛlˈɔːf/ &middot; *wel-AWF*
**Русский:** обеспеченный, состоятельный
**Type:** compound — adverb + adverb particle
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*a well-off family*); more often after a verb and written open (*they're well off*); the 'in a good situation' sense only after a verb
**Comparison:** irregular — *better-off*, *best-off*; after a verb *better off*, *best off*
**Takes:** in the 'in a good situation' sense, *better off* often takes *-ing* or *without* + noun: *You'd be better off taking the bus*; *She's better off without him*
**Form:** compound — *well* + *off*
**Opposite:** *badly off*; *poor*
**Contrast:** *Better off* is the everyday comparative and usually means 'in a better situation', not 'richer': *You'd be better off asking your boss* = тебе лучше спросить начальника. *Well-off* is politer than *rich* and a little less than *wealthy*. The British *well off for* (well supplied with: *well off for cash*) is rare in American English.

Well-off has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Having plenty of money; comfortably rich.** (обеспеченный, состоятельный)

1. She grew up in a *well-off* suburb outside Boston.
2. His parents are pretty *well off*, so he graduated without loans.
3. The tax cut mostly helps the *best-off* households.

**2. In a good or lucky situation.** (в хорошем положении; которому повезло)

4. You don't know how *well off* you are with a boss like that.
5. We'd be *better off* leaving before the traffic starts.
6. Honestly, she's *better off* without him.

### well-dressed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛlˈdrɛst/ &middot; *wel-DREST*
**Русский:** хорошо одетый, элегантный
**Type:** compound — adverb + past participle
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*a well-dressed man*); after a verb often written open (*he was well dressed*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better-dressed*, *best-dressed*; after a verb *better dressed*
**Form:** compound — *well* + past participle *dressed*
**Opposite:** *badly dressed*; *shabby*
**Contrast:** *Dressed* alone only means 'wearing clothes' (*Are you dressed yet?* = ты оделся?); *well-dressed* means wearing good, stylish clothes. Say *the best-dressed guest*, not *the most well-dressed guest* in careful writing.

Wearing attractive, stylish or expensive clothes.

1. A *well-dressed* woman in a gray suit walked into the lobby.
2. Everyone at the wedding was *well dressed* except the groom's brother.
3. The magazine publishes a list of the year's *best-dressed* celebrities.

### well-paid

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛlˈpeɪd/ &middot; *wel-PAYD*
**Русский:** высокооплачиваемый, хорошо оплачиваемый
**Type:** compound — adverb + past participle
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*a well-paid job*); after a verb often written open (*nurses aren't well paid*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better-paid*, *best-paid*; after a verb *better paid*
**Form:** compound — *well* + past participle *paid*
**Opposite:** *poorly paid*; *low-paid*
**Contrast:** *Well-paid* describes both the job and the person doing it. *Well-paying* describes only the job or the employer: *a well-paying job*, but not *a well-paying lawyer*. *Highly paid* means the same as *well-paid* and is common for people: *highly paid executives*.

Of a job or a person: earning or paying a lot of money.

1. He left teaching for a *well-paid* job in software.
2. Truck drivers are fairly *well paid* in North Dakota.
3. Surgeons are among the *best-paid* workers in the country.

### high-speed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaɪˈspiːd/ &middot; *hy-SPEED*
**Русский:** скоростной, высокоскоростной
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun; also classifying
**Position:** attributive only — after a verb use *fast*: *a high-speed train*, but *the train is fast*
**Comparison:** not gradable — technical writing has *higher-speed*, but in everyday English compare with *faster*
**Form:** compound — *high* + *speed*
**Contrast:** It goes before a noun: *a high-speed chase*, not *the chase was high-speed*, which sounds odd — say *the chase was fast*. *Speed* stays singular inside the compound. Russian *скоростной интернет* is *high-speed internet* or *broadband*, never *speed internet*.

High-speed has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Moving, working or sending data very fast.** (скоростной, высокоскоростной)

1. California has been trying to build *high-speed* rail for years.
2. The suspect led police on a *high-speed* chase down the interstate.
3. Every room in the hotel has free *high-speed* internet.

**2. Of a camera or photography: recording in a very short time, so that fast motion can be seen.** (скоростной (о съёмке, камере))

4. A *high-speed* camera caught the moment the balloon burst.
5. The scientists used *high-speed* video to study how the bird lands.

### part-time

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpɑːrtˈtaɪm/ &middot; *pahrt-TYM*
**Русский:** с неполной занятостью; на неполный рабочий день
**Type:** compound — noun + noun; also classifying
**Position:** both — *a part-time job*; *the job is part-time*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — *part* + *time*
**Opposite:** *full-time*
**Contrast:** In *She works part-time* the word is an adverb, not an adjective. Russian *полставки* is *part-time* or *half-time* in a job, but *halftime* on its own is the break in a sports game. The job is *part-time*, never *part-timed*; a person who works part-time is *a part-timer* (noun).

Part-time has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of a job, course or schedule: for only part of the usual working or study hours.** (с неполной занятостью, неполный (рабочий день))

1. I had a *part-time* job at a pizza place all through college.
2. The position is *part-time*, about twenty hours a week.

**2. Of a person: working or studying fewer than the usual hours, or doing something in addition to a main job.** (работающий неполный день; по совместительству)

3. The store hired three *part-time* workers for the holidays.
4. He's an accountant by day and a *part-time* musician on weekends.
5. She was a *part-time* student while her kids were small.

### full-time

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʊlˈtaɪm/ &middot; *fuul-TYM*
**Русский:** с полной занятостью; штатный
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun; also classifying
**Position:** both — *a full-time job*; *the job is full-time*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — *full* + *time*
**Opposite:** *part-time*
**Contrast:** In *She works full-time* the word is an adverb, not an adjective. *Full time* as a noun is British sports talk for the end of a match; Americans say *the end of regulation* or *the final whistle*. Russian *штатный сотрудник* is usually *a full-time employee* or *staff member*.

Full-time has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Of a job, course or schedule: for the whole of the normal working or study week.** (с полной занятостью, на полный рабочий день)

1. After the internship, they offered her a *full-time* position.
2. The program is *full-time*, so most students don't work.

**2. Of a person: working or studying the full number of hours, or doing something as their main activity.** (штатный, постоянный; занятый полный день)

3. The company has forty *full-time* employees.
4. She became a *full-time* mom after the twins were born.

**3. Taking up all your time and energy, in *a full-time job*.** (отнимающий всё время)

5. Taking care of my dad has become a *full-time* job.
6. Keeping that old house in shape is practically *full-time* work.

### long-term

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɔːŋˈtɜːrm/ &middot; *lawng-TURM*
**Русский:** долгосрочный; длительный
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun; also classifying
**Position:** both — mostly before a noun: *long-term goals*; after a verb: *the damage could be long-term*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *longer-term* is common: *a longer-term plan*
**Form:** compound — *long* + *term*
**Opposite:** *short-term*
**Contrast:** *In the long term* is a noun phrase; Americans more often say *in the long run*. *Long-term* is about time into the future or over a long period; *long-lasting* is about how long a thing keeps working (*a long-lasting battery*, not *a long-term battery*). *Term* stays singular: never *long-terms plans*.

Long-term has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Planned for, or having effects over, a long time into the future.** (долгосрочный)

1. Nobody really knows the *long-term* effects of the drug.
2. We need a *long-term* solution, not another quick fix.
3. The board wants a *longer-term* strategy for the company.

**2. Lasting or having existed for a long time.** (длительный, давний; хронический)

4. They've been in a *long-term* relationship for eight years.
5. Her grandmother lives in a *long-term* care facility.
6. The problem isn't temporary; it's *long-term*.

**3. In finance: lasting more than a year before it must be paid or sold.** (долгосрочный (о кредите, инвестиции))

7. *Long-term* capital gains are taxed at a lower rate.
8. The city took out a *long-term* loan to fix the bridge.

### short-term

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃɔːrtˈtɜːrm/ &middot; *short-TURM*
**Русский:** краткосрочный; кратковременный
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun; also classifying
**Position:** both — mostly before a noun: *a short-term contract*; after a verb: *the effect is only short-term*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *shorter-term* is used: *shorter-term goals*
**Form:** compound — *short* + *term*
**Opposite:** *long-term*
**Contrast:** *Short-term* describes something planned or meant to last a short time; *short-lived* describes something that ended sooner than expected (*a short-lived marriage*). Russian *кратковременная память* is *short-term memory*, never *short memory*.

Short-term has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Planned for, or having effects over, only the near future.** (краткосрочный)

1. Cutting prices is only a *short-term* fix.
2. The layoffs brought a *short-term* boost in profits but hurt morale.
3. Most investors care more about *shorter-term* returns.

**2. Lasting only a short time.** (кратковременный, временный)

4. The city is cracking down on *short-term* rentals.
5. Alcohol affects your *short-term* memory.
6. The side effects are usually mild and *short-term*.

**3. In finance: lasting a year or less before it must be paid or sold.** (краткосрочный (о кредите, инвестиции))

7. The company borrowed money with a *short-term* loan.
8. *Short-term* interest rates went up again this month.

### world-famous

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɜːrldˈfeɪməs/ &middot; *wurld-FAY-muhs*
**Русский:** всемирно известный
**Type:** compound — noun + adjective
**Position:** both — *a world-famous pianist*; *the bakery is world-famous*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Takes:** *for* + noun or *-ing* (what it is famous for)
**Form:** compound — *world* + *famous*
**Contrast:** Learners often say *worldwide famous* or *world famous by* — say *world-famous for*. It is also used jokingly about something only locally known: *Joe's world-famous chili*.

Known by people in many countries.

1. The Grand Canyon is a *world-famous* natural wonder.
2. The city is *world-famous* for its jazz clubs.
3. Stop by Rosa's for her *world-famous* pecan pie.

### sugar-free

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃʊɡərˈfriː/ &middot; *shuug-ur-FREE*
**Русский:** без сахара, не содержащий сахара
**Type:** compound — noun + *-free*; also classifying
**Position:** both — *sugar-free gum*; *the soda is sugar-free*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — noun + *-free* ('without')
**Contrast:** In compounds *-free* means 'without', not 'at no cost': *sugar-free* = без сахара, *tax-free* = без налога. *Sugar-free* food is usually sweetened with something else; *unsweetened* means nothing sweet was added, and *no sugar added* means the food may still contain natural sugar.

Containing no sugar, often sweetened with something artificial instead.

1. Could I get a *sugar-free* vanilla latte?
2. The label says the candy is *sugar-free*, but it still has calories.
3. Chewing *sugar-free* gum after meals is good for your teeth.

### state-of-the-art

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsteɪt əv ði ˈɑːrt/ &middot; *stayt uhv thee AHRT*
**Русский:** новейший, передовой, самый современный
**Type:** compound — phrase used as an adjective
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*a state-of-the-art lab*); after a verb often written open (*the equipment is state of the art*)
**Comparison:** not gradable — *the most state-of-the-art* is heard in advertising but avoided in careful writing
**Form:** compound — noun phrase *the state of the art* turned into a modifier
**Contrast:** The noun phrase *the state of the art* means the current level of development in a field: *the state of the art in cancer research*. The adjective means 'the newest and best'. Russian *последнее слово техники* is usually *state-of-the-art* or *cutting-edge*.

Using the newest and most advanced ideas, methods or technology.

1. The hospital just opened a *state-of-the-art* cancer center.
2. The gym's equipment is *state of the art*.
3. The team trains in a *state-of-the-art* facility outside Dallas.

### up-to-date

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌp tə ˈdeɪt/ &middot; *up tuh DAYT*
**Русский:** актуальный, свежий; современный; в курсе
**Type:** compound — phrase used as an adjective
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*up-to-date information*); after a verb usually written open (*the list is up to date*)
**Comparison:** more/most — *more up-to-date*, *most up-to-date*
**Takes:** *on* or *with* + noun: *up to date on the news* (informed about), *up to date with your payments* or *on your shots* (not behind)
**Form:** compound — prepositional phrase *up to date* used as a modifier
**Opposite:** *out-of-date*
**Contrast:** Russian *актуальный* is *up-to-date* or *current*, never *actual*: *actual* means 'real' (*the actual price* = реальная цена). Common verb patterns: *keep someone up to date*, *bring something up to date*, *stay up to date on*.

Up-to-date has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Containing the latest information.** (актуальный, свежий)

1. Make sure your résumé is *up to date* before you apply.
2. The app gives you *up-to-date* traffic reports.
3. This is the *most up-to-date* map we have.

**2. Of a person: knowing the latest news or developments.** (в курсе, осведомлённый)

4. I try to stay *up to date* on local politics.
5. Keep me *up to date* on how the surgery goes.

**3. Done or paid when due, with nothing left overdue.** (без задолженности; сделанный вовремя)

6. Is your dog *up to date* on its shots?
7. We're *up to date* with the rent now.

**4. Modern; using the newest methods, styles or equipment.** (современный)

8. The kitchen needs *more up-to-date* appliances.
9. The school uses fairly *up-to-date* teaching methods.

### out-of-date

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊt əv ˈdeɪt/ &middot; *owt uhv DAYT*
**Русский:** устаревший; просроченный
**Type:** compound — phrase used as an adjective
**Position:** both — hyphenated before a noun (*out-of-date software*); after a verb usually written open (*the software is out of date*)
**Comparison:** more/most — *more out-of-date*, *most out-of-date*
**Form:** compound — prepositional phrase *out of date* used as a modifier
**Opposite:** *up-to-date*
**Contrast:** For passports, credit cards, licenses and food, Americans usually say *expired*: *expired milk*, *an expired passport*; *out-of-date milk* sounds British. *Outdated* is a close synonym of the first sense. Russian *просроченный* is therefore most often *expired*.

Out-of-date has 2 distinct senses.

**1. No longer modern, useful or correct because things have changed.** (устаревший, неактуальный)

1. Our office still runs on *out-of-date* computers.
2. The phone number on the website is *out of date*.
3. This guidebook is even *more out of date* than the last one.

**2. Of a document or ticket: no longer valid because the date on it has passed.** (просроченный, недействительный)

4. You can't fly with an *out-of-date* ID.
5. His license was *out of date*, so he got a ticket.

### last-minute

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlæstˈmɪnɪt/ &middot; *last-MIN-it*
**Русский:** сделанный в последний момент; горящий (о туре)
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun
**Position:** attributive only — after a verb use *at the last minute*: *a last-minute change*, but *the plans changed at the last minute*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — *last* + *minute*
**Contrast:** After a verb, careful English uses the phrase *at the last minute*: *the plans changed at the last minute*; *it was all very last-minute* is casual speech. Russian *горящая путёвка* is *a last-minute deal*. *The last minute of the game* is an ordinary noun phrase meaning the final sixty seconds.

Done, made, bought or happening at the latest possible time.

1. The mall is full of *last-minute* Christmas shoppers.
2. There were a few *last-minute* changes to the schedule.
3. We found a *last-minute* deal on a cruise to Mexico.

### middle-aged

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɪdəlˈeɪdʒd/ &middot; *mid-uhl-AYJD*
**Русский:** средних лет; для людей средних лет
**Type:** compound — adjective + *-ed* from *age*; also age
**Position:** both — *a middle-aged man*; *my parents are middle-aged*
**Comparison:** not gradable — informal *more middle-aged* is possible in the 'unexciting' sense
**Form:** compound — *middle* + *aged* (adjective from the noun *age*)
**Contrast:** *Middle-aged* has nothing to do with *the Middle Ages*: Russian *средневековый* is *medieval*. Here *aged* is one syllable /eɪdʒd/; the separate adjective *aged* meaning 'very old' (*aged parents*) is two syllables /ˈeɪdʒɪd/. *Middle-aged* usually means about 45 to 65.

Middle-aged has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of a person: no longer young but not yet old, roughly between 45 and 65.** (средних лет)

1. A *middle-aged* couple bought the house next door.
2. Most of the people at the concert were *middle-aged*.
3. The drug is tested mainly on *middle-aged* adults.

**2. Typical of middle-aged people, especially in being dull or conservative.** (как у немолодых людей; скучный, консервативный)

4. Minivans have a *middle-aged* image he wanted to avoid.
5. Staying home on Friday night feels so *middle-aged*.

### left-handed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛftˈhændɪd/ &middot; *left-HAN-did*
**Русский:** леворукий; для левшей
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun + *-ed*
**Position:** both — *a left-handed pitcher*; *she is left-handed*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — *left* + *hand* + *-ed*
**Opposite:** *right-handed*
**Contrast:** Russian *левша* is a noun, but English usually uses the adjective: *She is left-handed*, not *She is a left-handed*; the nouns are *a left-hander* or informal *a lefty*. *Left-hand* without *-ed* means 'on the left': *the left-hand lane*. A *left-handed compliment* (US) is a remark that sounds nice but is really an insult.

Left-handed has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Of a person: using the left hand more easily than the right.** (леворукий, левша)

1. About one in ten people is *left-handed*.
2. The Dodgers need another *left-handed* pitcher.

**2. Made for, or done with, the left hand.** (для левой руки, для левшей; левой рукой)

3. Do you have any *left-handed* scissors?
4. He learned to play on a *left-handed* guitar.

**3. Of a compliment: seeming to praise but really meant as criticism.** (сомнительный, двусмысленный (о комплименте))

5. "You look great for your age" is a *left-handed* compliment.
6. Calling her essay "surprisingly good" was a pretty *left-handed* compliment.

**4. Of a screw thread: tightened by turning counterclockwise.** (с левой резьбой)

7. The left pedal has a *left-handed* thread, so you loosen it clockwise.
8. Some gas fittings use *left-handed* threads so they can't be mixed up.

### right-handed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌraɪtˈhændɪd/ &middot; *ryt-HAN-did*
**Русский:** праворукий; для правой руки
**Type:** compound — adjective + noun + *-ed*
**Position:** both — *a right-handed batter*; *most people are right-handed*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** compound — *right* + *hand* + *-ed*
**Opposite:** *left-handed*
**Contrast:** *Right-hand* without *-ed* means 'on the right' (*the right-hand side*), and *your right-hand man* is your most trusted helper, not someone who uses the right hand. Russian *правша* is a noun, but English says *He is right-handed*; the noun *a right-hander* is used mostly in sports.

Right-handed has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Of a person: using the right hand more easily than the left.** (праворукий, правша)

1. I'm *right-handed*, but I kick with my left foot.
2. The Cubs are facing a *right-handed* pitcher tonight.

**2. Made for, or done with, the right hand.** (для правой руки; правой рукой)

3. Most golf clubs in the store are *right-handed*.
4. Desks with a *right-handed* writing surface are hard for lefties.

**3. Of a screw thread: tightened by turning clockwise, as most are.** (с правой резьбой)
