# Nationalities and other proper adjectives

> *Russian*, *American*, *Catholic*, *Victorian* — the adjectives English always capitalizes.

[← The grammar of adjectives](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

In English, adjectives of nationality, religion and origin are always capitalized — *Russian*, *American*, *Catholic* — where Russian writes them in lowercase. Many of them also work as nouns for a person (*a Russian*) or a language (*Russian*), and a few have a separate noun altogether (*Polish*, but *a Pole*; *Dutch*, but *a Dutchman*). These entries cover the adjective and flag the nouns that differ.

---
### American

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmɛrɪkən/ &middot; *uh-MAIR-i-kuhn*
**Русский:** американский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *an American company*, *The company is American*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very American*, *more American* when it means 'typical of the US'
**Form:** -an from *America*
**Contrast:** In American usage *American* means 'of the United States'. For the continents English says *North American*, *South American* or *Latin American*, and Canadians and Mexicans do not call themselves *American*. *She's American* (adjective) and *She's an American* (noun) both work, and the adjective is more usual. Ancestry compounds take no hyphen in current American style: *Mexican American*, *Italian American*, *African American*. Always a capital *A*, unlike Russian *американский*.

American has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from the United States, its people or its culture.** (американский (о США))

1. My grandfather became an *American* citizen in 1962.
2. Is your car *American* or Japanese?
3. Tipping 20 percent is a very *American* habit.

**2. Of North, Central and South America as a whole.** (американский (об обеих Америках))

4. The Organization of *American* States includes both Canada and Brazil.
5. Corn, potatoes and tomatoes were first grown by the native peoples of the *American* continents.

### British

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbrɪtɪʃ/ &middot; *BRIT-ish*
**Русский:** британский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *a British passport*, *Her accent is British*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very British* when it means 'typical of Britain'
**Contrast:** *British* covers the whole United Kingdom: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. *English* is England only, so a Scot is British but not English. The person is not *a British* but *a British person*, *a Briton* (formal, in the news) or informally *a Brit*. *The British* (the people) takes a plural verb: *The British drink a lot of tea*. Americans often say *English* when they mean *British*, and Scots and Welsh people notice.

British has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from the United Kingdom, its people or its government.** (британский)

1. She has dual *British* and American citizenship.
2. The *British* government announced new visa rules on Monday.
3. Waiting patiently in line is supposed to be very *British*.

**2. Of the English used in the United Kingdom, as opposed to American English.** (британский (о варианте английского языка))

4. In *British* English, a truck is a lorry.
5. His spelling is *British* because he went to school in London.

### English

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/ &middot; *ING-glish*
**Русский:** английский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *an English village*, *The village is English*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very English* when it means 'typical of England'
**Contrast:** *English* means England only; for the whole United Kingdom the adjective is *British*, so it is *a British passport*, not *an English passport*. The person is *an Englishman*, *an Englishwoman* or *an English person*, never *an English*; *the English* (the people) takes a plural verb. *An English teacher* is usually someone who teaches English, and only sometimes a teacher from England. The capital stays even for a school subject: *English class*, not *english class*.

English has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of the English language.** (английский (о языке))

1. I have an *English* test tomorrow.
2. Some *English* spellings make no sense at all.
3. Our *English* teacher makes us read a novel a month.

**2. Of or from England, its people or its culture.** (английский (об Англии))

4. We rented a cottage in the *English* countryside.
5. Afternoon tea is an old *English* tradition.
6. The house has a very *English* garden, full of roses.

### French

**Pronunciation:** /frɛntʃ/ &middot; *FRENCH*
**Русский:** французский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *French wine*, *The wine is French*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very French* when it means 'typical of France'
**Contrast:** The person is *a Frenchman*, *a Frenchwoman* or *a French person*, never *a French*; *the French* (the people) takes a plural verb. *A French teacher* is usually someone who teaches French. Like every origin adjective, *French* comes late in a string of adjectives, after opinion, size, age and color: *a lovely old French farmhouse*, not *a French lovely old farmhouse*. *French fries*, *French toast* and *French doors* are fixed names, not claims about France.

French has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from France, its people or its culture.** (французский (о Франции))

1. We had a bottle of *French* wine with dinner.
2. Her husband is *French*, so the kids are bilingual.
3. The café felt very *French*, with tiny tables out on the sidewalk.

**2. Of the French language.** (французский (о языке))

4. My *French* class meets on Tuesdays and Thursdays.
5. *French* verbs have far more endings than English ones.

### German

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʒɜːrmən/ &middot; *JUR-muhn*
**Русский:** немецкий; германский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *a German car*, *The car is German*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very German* when it means 'typical of Germany'
**Contrast:** *German* is Germany and its language. For the language family, Russian *германский* is *Germanic*: English, Dutch and Swedish are *Germanic* languages, not *German* ones. The country is *Germany*, never *German*. *Dutch* means the Netherlands, not Germany, despite the German word *Deutsch*. The person is the noun *a German*.

German has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from Germany, its people or its culture.** (немецкий, германский (о Германии))

1. He drives a *German* car.
2. My great-grandparents were *German*.
3. The *German* economy is the largest in Europe.

**2. Of the German language.** (немецкий (о языке))

4. *German* nouns are always written with a capital letter.
5. The *German* word for 'car' is 'Auto.'

**Type:** origin

Italian has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from Italy, its people or its culture.** (итальянский (об Италии))

1. Let's get *Italian* food tonight.
2. The designer is *Italian*, but she lives in New York.
3. My grandmother's family is *Italian* — they came from Sicily in 1910.

**2. Of the Italian language.** (итальянский (о языке))

4. Musical terms like 'piano' and 'forte' are *Italian* words.
5. She's taking an *Italian* class before her trip to Rome.

### Spanish

**Pronunciation:** /ˈspænɪʃ/ &middot; *SPAN-ish*
**Русский:** испанский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *Spanish class*, *The painter was Spanish*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ish from *Spain*, with the vowel changed
**Contrast:** *Spanish* is Spain and the Spanish language, so a Mexican or a Colombian speaks Spanish but is not *Spanish*. For people from Spanish-speaking countries Americans say *Hispanic*, *Latino* or *Latina*, or name the country. The person from Spain is *a Spaniard* or *a Spanish person*, never *a Spanish*; *the Spanish* (the people) takes a plural verb. *Spanish-speaking* is hyphenated before a noun: *Spanish-speaking countries*.

Spanish has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of the Spanish language.** (испанский (о языке))

1. Our school has a *Spanish* teacher from Puerto Rico.
2. My *Spanish* homework took all evening.
3. *Spanish* spelling is much more regular than English spelling.

**2. Of or from Spain, its people or its culture.** (испанский (об Испании))

4. Paella is a *Spanish* rice dish.
5. Picasso was *Spanish*, but he spent most of his life in France.
6. *Spanish* missions were built across California in the 1700s.

### Russian

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrʌʃən/ &middot; *RUSH-uhn*
**Русский:** русский; российский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *Russian literature*, *My neighbor is Russian*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *very Russian* when it means 'typical of Russia'
**Contrast:** English has one word for both *русский* and *российский*: *Russian* covers the language and culture (*Russian literature*), ethnicity, and the state and its citizens (*the Russian government*, *a Russian passport*). To stress citizenship, say *a citizen of Russia*; to stress the language, *Russian-speaking*: *a Russian-speaking family from Kyiv*. The capital *R* is required: *russian* is a spelling error. The person is the noun *a Russian*.

Russian has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from Russia, its people, its culture or its government.** (русский, российский)

1. She grew up reading *Russian* novels.
2. The *Russian* team won three gold medals.
3. My neighbor is *Russian* — she moved here from Novosibirsk.

**2. Of the Russian language.** (русский (о языке))

4. *Russian* grammar has six cases.
5. The *Russian* alphabet takes about a week to learn.

### Ukrainian

**Position:** both — *the Ukrainian flag*, *Her parents are Ukrainian*

Ukrainian has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from Ukraine, its people or its culture.** (украинский (об Украине))

1. There's a *Ukrainian* church two blocks from our house.
2. Both of her parents are *Ukrainian*.
3. The *Ukrainian* flag is blue and yellow.

**2. Of the Ukrainian language.** (украинский (о языке))

4. The *Ukrainian* alphabet has several letters the Russian one lacks, such as ї.
5. He translates *Ukrainian* poetry into English.

### Chinese

**Pronunciation:** /tʃaɪˈniːz/ &middot; *chy-NEEZ*
**Русский:** китайский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *Chinese food*, *Her husband is Chinese*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ese from *China*
**Contrast:** Words in *-ese* add no *-s* for people: *two Chinese students*, and *the Chinese* (the people) with a plural verb. *A Chinese* for one person sounds rude to many Americans; say *a Chinese person* or *a Chinese woman*. The language usually means Mandarin, but Cantonese is *Chinese* too. Before a stressed noun the stress often moves forward: CHY-neez FOOD. For Americans of Chinese descent the term is *Chinese American*.

Chinese has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from China, its people or its culture.** (китайский (о Китае))

1. Let's order *Chinese* food tonight.
2. *Chinese* New Year falls in late January or February.
3. Her husband is *Chinese*, from Shanghai.

**2. Of the Chinese languages, especially Mandarin, or of their writing system.** (китайский (о языке, иероглифах))

4. You need about 3,000 *Chinese* characters to read a newspaper.
5. *Chinese* tones are the hardest part for English speakers.

### Canadian

**Русский:** канадский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *Canadian winters*, *Our neighbors are Canadian*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ian from *Canada*, with the stress moved
**Contrast:** The stress moves: KAN-uh-duh, but kuh-NAY-dee-uhn. A Canadian is *North American* but never *American*, which means the United States. *French Canadian* is a Canadian whose first language is French, while *Canadian French* is the language as spoken in Canada. *Canadian bacon* in the US is lean smoked pork loin, sliced like ham.

### Indian

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪndiən/ &middot; *IN-dee-uhn*
**Русский:** индийский; индейский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *Indian food*, *Her parents are Indian*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Contrast:** *Indian* has two unrelated meanings, *индийский* and *индейский*, and only context tells them apart. For the native peoples of the Americas, *Native American* is the usual neutral term. *American Indian* is still used in law, in names (*the National Museum of the American Indian*) and by many Native people themselves, but *Indian* alone in this sense can sound dated. For Americans whose families came from India, say *Indian American*. *Indian* is not *Hindu*: India also has large Muslim, Christian and Sikh communities. *Indian summer* (warm weather in the fall) is *бабье лето*.

Indian has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of or from India, its people or its culture.** (индийский)

1. We ordered *Indian* food — chicken tikka and naan.
2. Her parents are *Indian*, but she was born in New Jersey.
3. The *Indian* economy is growing fast.

**2. Of the native peoples of North or South America.** (индейский)

4. The museum has a large collection of *American Indian* art.
5. Many towns in Oklahoma were built on former *Indian* land.
6. The area is part of an *Indian* reservation.

### Scottish

**Русский:** шотландский
**Type:** origin
**Position:** both — *a Scottish accent*, *My great-grandfather was Scottish*

Scotland is part of Britain, so a Scottish person is *British* but not *English*. The person is *a Scot* or *a Scottish person*.

Of or from Scotland, its people or its culture.

2. My great-grandfather was *Scottish*.
3. We drove through the *Scottish* Highlands in the rain.

**Contrast:** In American English *a Victorian* (a noun) is usually a house built in that period: *They bought a Victorian on Elm Street*. The word names a British queen, but Americans use it for their own late 19th-century houses and manners too. The 'prudish' sense is critical, and it sounds a little old-fashioned itself.

Victorian has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of the time when Queen Victoria ruled Britain, 1837 to 1901, or in the ornate style of its houses and furniture.** (викторианский)

1. We're reading a *Victorian* novel by Charles Dickens.
2. They restored a *Victorian* house with a wraparound porch.
3. San Francisco is famous for its painted *Victorian* row houses.

**2. Strict and old-fashioned about morals, especially about sex.** (чопорный, ханжеский)

4. My grandparents had very *Victorian* ideas about dating.
5. The school's dress code is more *Victorian* than anyone expected.

### Shakespearean

**Pronunciation:** /ʃeɪkˈspɪriən/ &middot; *shayk-SPEER-ee-uhn*
**Русский:** шекспировский
**Type:** classifying
**Position:** both — mostly before a noun: *a Shakespearean actor*; after a verb in the 'grand and tragic' sense: *His downfall was almost Shakespearean*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ean from *Shakespeare*
**Contrast:** American spelling is *Shakespearean*; *Shakespearian* is a British variant. *A Shakespearean actor* is one who often plays Shakespeare, not a character from the plays. Russian *шекспировские страсти* is *a drama of Shakespearean proportions*.

Shakespearean has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Of William Shakespeare or his plays and poems.** (шекспировский)

1. She's a trained *Shakespearean* actor.
2. A *Shakespearean* sonnet has fourteen lines.
3. Students struggle with *Shakespearean* English at first.

**2. Like the events of Shakespeare's plays, especially tragic on a grand scale.** (шекспировский (о страстях, трагедии))

4. The family feud had become a tragedy of *Shakespearean* proportions.
5. The senator's downfall was almost *Shakespearean*.
