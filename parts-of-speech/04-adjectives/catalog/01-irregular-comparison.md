# Adjectives with irregular comparison

> *good*, *better*, *best* — and the adjectives whose comparative splits into two words with different jobs.

[← The grammar of adjectives](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Almost every gradable adjective compares with *-er/-est* (*taller*) or *more/most* (*more careful*). A handful do neither, and their forms have to be learned: *good*, *better*, *best*; *bad*, *worse*, *worst*. Several also split into two words with different jobs — *farther* for distance and *further* for degree, *older* for age and *elder* for family rank, *later* for time and *latter* for the second of two — so each form has its own entry.

---
### good

**Pronunciation:** /ɡʊd/ &middot; *GUUD*
**Русский:** хороший
**Type:** evaluative; also quality
**Position:** both — *a good book*, *The book is good*; in the senses 'large in amount' and 'complete and thorough' only before a noun (*a good hour*, *a good look*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better*, *best*
**Takes:** *at* + noun or *-ing* for skill (*good at chess*); *with* + people or things you handle (*good with kids*); *for* + the person or thing it helps (*good for your heart*); *to* + a person for kindness (*good to me*); *of* + person + *to*-infinitive (*good of you to come*); *about* after *feel* (*feel good about it*); *to*-infinitive (*good to know*); *that*-clause (*It's good that you called*)
**Preposition patterns:** [good at](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#good-at)
**Opposite:** *bad*
**Contrast:** *Good* is the adjective and *well* the adverb: *She's a good driver* / *She drives well* — *She drives good* is heard in casual speech but is marked wrong in careful English. After linking verbs use *good*: *It looks good*, *That smells good*. *I feel good* means happy or in good shape; *I feel well* means not sick. In American English *I'm good* means *I'm fine* and, when you are offered something, *No, thanks*. Skill is *good at* (not *good in*), kindness is *good to*, benefit is *good for*: *She's good at math*, *He was good to me*, *Fish is good for you*. The forms are *better* and *best*, never *gooder* or *more good*. Russian *добрый* about a person is usually *kind*, not *good*.

Good has 16 distinct senses.

**1. Of high quality; done or made well.** (хороший, качественный)

1. That was a really *good* movie.
2. The food here is *good*, but the service is slow.
3. Her second novel is even *better* than the first.

**2. Skillful; able to do something well.** (умелый, способный)

4. She's *good* at math.
5. He's a *good* driver — I always feel safe with him.
6. My dad is really *good* with his hands.

**3. Pleasant or enjoyable.** (приятный, хороший)

7. Did you have a *good* time at the party?
8. I've got some *good* news.
9. The weather should be *better* this weekend.

**4. Morally right; kind and honest.** (хороший, порядочный; добрый (к кому-то))

10. He's a *good* man who would never lie to you.
11. It was *good* of you to drive me home.
12. She's always been *good* to her neighbors.

**5. Well-behaved — of a child or an animal.** (послушный)

13. Be *good* for the babysitter, okay?
14. The kids were very *good* on the flight.
15. Who's a *good* dog?

**6. Beneficial; having a good effect on your health or well-being.** (полезный)

16. Walking every day is *good* for you.
17. Too much soda isn't *good* for your teeth.
18. Getting out of the city was *good* for her.

**7. Suitable or convenient.** (подходящий, удобный)

19. Is this a *good* time to talk?
20. Tuesday is *good* for me.
21. What's the *best* way to get to the airport?

**8. Acceptable; good enough for what is needed.** (годный, приемлемый; ладно)

22. "Let's meet at seven." "Sounds *good*."
23. The photo isn't perfect, but it's *good* enough for the website.
24. Your English is *good* enough for the job.

**9. Attractive or pleasing to look at.** (привлекательный; (хорошо) выглядящий)

25. You look *good* in that jacket.
26. The new kitchen looks really *good*.

**10. Valid; still able to be used.** (действительный)

27. This ticket is *good* for one ride.
28. The coupon is *good* until the end of the month.

**11. Still fresh; not spoiled.** (свежий, неиспорченный)

29. Is this milk still *good*?
30. The bread should be *good* for another day or two.

**12. Strong or sensible enough to be accepted.** (веский, уважительный)

31. Do you have a *good* reason for being late?
32. That's a *good* point.
33. She had a *good* excuse for missing the meeting.

**13. Fine; satisfied and not needing anything — informal American.** (в порядке; не нужно, спасибо)

34. "How are you?" "*Good*, thanks."
35. "Do you want more coffee?" "No, I'm *good*."
36. We're *good* — you don't owe me anything.

**14. Large in amount; at least as much as stated.** (изрядный; добрый (о количестве))

37. The hotel is a *good* ten miles from the beach.
38. We spent a *good* deal of money on repairs.
39. It took a *good* hour to get there.

**15. Complete and thorough.** (основательный; как следует)

40. Take a *good* look before you sign anything.
41. What I need is a *good* night's sleep.
42. She had a *good* cry and felt a lot better.

**16. Sure to pay back money owed — in *good for*.** (надёжный (о должнике))

43. I'll lend you fifty bucks — I know you're *good* for it.
44. Don't worry, his brother is *good* for the money.

### bad

**Pronunciation:** /bæd/ &middot; *BAD*
**Русский:** плохой
**Type:** evaluative; also quality
**Position:** both — *a bad idea*, *The idea is bad*
**Comparison:** irregular — *worse*, *worst*
**Takes:** *at* + noun or *-ing* for skill (*bad at math*); *with* + things you handle (*bad with names*); *for* + the person or thing harmed (*bad for your back*); *about* after *feel* for guilt (*feel bad about it*); *for* after *feel* for pity (*feel bad for him*); *to*-infinitive (*It's bad to skip breakfast*); *that*-clause (*It's bad that nobody told her*)
**Preposition patterns:** [bad at](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#bad-at)
**Opposite:** *good*
**Contrast:** *Bad* is the adjective and *badly* the adverb: *a bad singer* / *She sings badly*. After linking verbs use *bad*: *It smells bad*, *I feel bad*. *I feel bad about it* means guilty; *I feel bad for him* means sorry for him; *I feel badly* is common in American speech but disliked by careful writers. The forms are *worse* and *worst* — *more bad* is wrong, and *badder* is only slang. *Not bad* is praise: it means *pretty good*. Russian *мне плохо* is *I feel sick* or *I feel bad*, not *I'm bad*, which means *I behave badly*. *My bad* (my mistake) is a slang noun, not this adjective.

Bad has 15 distinct senses.

**1. Of low quality; not good.** (плохой, некачественный)

1. The service at that restaurant is really *bad*.
2. His handwriting is so *bad* I can't read it.
3. That was the *worst* movie I've ever seen.

**2. Unpleasant or unwelcome.** (плохой, неприятный)

4. I'm afraid I have some *bad* news.
5. It's been a *bad* day.
6. The weather got *worse* in the afternoon.

**3. Serious or severe.** (сильный, тяжёлый)

7. She has a *bad* cold.
8. There was a *bad* accident on the interstate.
9. Traffic is *worst* on Friday afternoons.

**4. Harmful.** (вредный)

10. Smoking is *bad* for you.
11. Is coffee *bad* for kids?
12. Sitting all day is *bad* for your back.

**5. Not suitable or convenient.** (неподходящий, неудобный)

13. Is this a *bad* time to talk?
14. Friday is *bad* for me — how about Monday?
15. He picked the *worst* possible moment to ask for a raise.

**6. Not skillful.** (неумелый, слабый)

16. I'm *bad* at math.
17. He's terrible with names, and I'm even *worse*.
18. She's a *bad* driver.

**7. Morally wrong; evil.** (дурной, нехороший, злой)

19. He's not a *bad* person — he just made a mistake.
20. Lying to her was a *bad* thing to do.

**8. Naughty — of a child or an animal.** (непослушный)

21. *Bad* dog! Get off the couch!
22. Were the kids *bad* for the sitter?

**9. Rude or offensive — of words and language.** (неприличный, грубый)

23. Mom, he said a *bad* word!
24. The movie is rated R for *bad* language.

**10. Spoiled or rotten.** (испорченный, тухлый)

25. The milk has gone *bad*.
26. Throw out any *bad* apples.

**11. Guilty or embarrassed about something you did — with *feel*.** (стыдно, неловко)

27. I feel *bad* about forgetting your birthday.
28. Don't feel *bad* — it wasn't your fault.

**12. Sorry about someone else's trouble — in *feel bad for*.** (жалко (кого-то))

29. I feel *bad* for her — she worked so hard for that job.
30. We all felt *bad* for the new guy.

**13. Unwell; or injured and painful.** (больной; плохо (о самочувствии))

31. I've had a *bad* back for years.
32. He has a *bad* knee, so he can't run.
33. She was feeling *worse* by evening, so we called the doctor.

**14. Unfortunate; a pity — in *too bad*.** (жаль)

34. It's too *bad* you can't come.
35. Too *bad* — the store's already closed.

**15. Not valid; not able to be paid or collected.** (недействительный, фальшивый; безнадёжный (о долге))

36. He paid for the car with a *bad* check.
37. The bank lost millions on *bad* loans.

### far

**Pronunciation:** /fɑːr/ &middot; *FAHR*
**Русский:** дальний; далёкий
**Type:** quality
**Position:** attributive only — *the far end*, *the far right*; in *It isn't far* and *How far is it?* the word is the adverb
**Comparison:** irregular — *farther*, *farthest* for distance; *further*, *furthest* for distance or degree
**Opposite:** *near*
**Contrast:** As an adjective *far* stands before a noun and usually means the more distant of two sides or ends: *the far side of the lake*. It is not the everyday word for *далёкий* — say *a distant country* or *a faraway place*; *a far country* sounds literary. *It's not far* and *How far is it?* are adverb uses, and plain *It's far* is informal — *It's a long way* is safer. The comparison splits: *farther* and *farthest* are for physical distance; *further* and *furthest* work for distance too and are the only choice for *additional* (*further questions*). *The far right* is a political label (*крайне правые*), not a direction.

Far has 5 distinct senses.

**1. Being the more distant of two sides, ends or edges.** (дальний, противоположный)

1. Our room was at the *far* end of the hall.
2. They live on the *far* side of town.
3. We took the *farthest* table from the kitchen because it was quieter.

**2. At the most distant part of a region.** (крайний)

4. She grew up in the *far* north of the state, near the Canadian border.
5. The storm hit the *far* western part of the state.

**3. Holding the most extreme political views — in *far left* and *far right*.** (крайний (левый, правый))

6. The party has moved toward the *far* right.
7. *Far*-left groups organized the protest.

**4. Distant in space or time — mostly literary, and in *the far future*.** (далёкий)

8. Robots may do that kind of work in the *far* future.
9. Pilgrims came from *far* lands to see the shrine.

**5. Very different from something — in *a far cry from*.** (совсем не то, что; далеко не)

10. The new apartment is a *far* cry from our old place.
11. The final product was a *far* cry from what they promised.

### little

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪtəl/ &middot; *LIT-uhl*
**Русский:** маленький; мало
**Type:** size; also quantity
**Position:** both — before a noun in every sense (*a little house*); after a verb mainly for age (*when I was little*), since *small* sounds more natural there for size (*The house is small*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *less*, *least* for amount; for size *smaller*, *smallest*, with *littler*, *littlest* in informal speech
**Opposite:** *big*; *much* for amount
**Contrast:** *Little* often carries feeling (*a cute little house*, *a nasty little man*); *small* is neutral and is the word to compare: *a smaller room*, rarely *a littler room*. For amount, *little* with an uncountable noun is negative, like *мало*: *We have little time*; *a little* is positive, like *немного*: *We have a little time*. Many grammars call this amount use a determiner, and its forms are *less* and *least*. With countable nouns use *few*: *few friends*, not *little friends*. In *a little tired* the word is an adverb, and in *I speak a little* a pronoun.

Little has 7 distinct senses.

**1. Small in size.** (маленький, небольшой)

1. They live in a *little* house by the lake.
2. Cut the potatoes into *little* pieces.
3. That's the *littlest* puppy I've ever seen.

**2. Young.** (маленький (о возрасте))

4. We have two *little* kids.
5. I lived in Ohio when I was *little*.

**3. Younger — of a brother or sister.** (младший)

6. This is my *little* brother, Sam.
7. Her *little* sister just started college.

**4. Short in time or distance.** (недолгий, короткий)

8. Can you wait a *little* while?
9. Let's take a *little* walk after dinner.

**5. Not important; minor.** (незначительный, мелкий)

10. It's just a *little* problem — don't worry about it.
11. She always notices the *little* things.

**6. Not much; hardly any — before uncountable nouns.** (мало)

12. There's *little* hope of finding them now.
13. He showed *little* interest in the job.
14. We had *less* time than we expected.

**7. Used to show affection or annoyance.** (маленький (ласкательно или пренебрежительно))

15. What a sweet *little* baby!
16. Get out of my room, you *little* brat!

### old

**Pronunciation:** /oʊld/ &middot; *OHLD*
**Русский:** старый
**Type:** age
**Position:** both — *an old house*, *The house is old*; in the senses 'former' and 'known for a long time' only before a noun (*my old boss*, *an old friend*)
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *older*, *oldest*; *elder*, *eldest* only for rank in a family
**Opposite:** *young*; *new*
**Contrast:** Age is *be* + number + *years old*, never *have*: *She is ten years old*, not *She has ten years*. Before a noun the phrase is hyphenated and singular: *a ten-year-old girl*. Position changes the meaning: *an old friend* is a friend you have known for years, not an elderly one, and *my old job* is my former job. *Older* and *oldest* are the normal forms, in families too (*my older brother*); *elder* and *eldest* go only before a noun and never take *than*. About people, *old* can sound blunt — *older* or *elderly* is politer.

Old has 8 distinct senses.

**1. Having lived for a long time; not young.** (старый, пожилой)

1. An *old* man was feeding the pigeons.
2. My grandparents are getting *old*.
3. She's the *oldest* person in our town.

**2. Of a particular age.** (в возрасте (… лет))

4. How *old* are you?
5. Our son is six years *old*.
6. My sister is two years *older* than me.

**3. Having existed or been used for a long time; not new.** (старый, поношенный)

7. I wear my *old* sneakers for yard work.
8. They bought an *old* farmhouse and fixed it up.
9. This is the *oldest* building in the city.

**4. Belonging to the distant past; traditional.** (старинный, древний)

10. It's an *old* family recipe.
11. The town has kept many of its *old* customs.

**5. Former; from an earlier time in your life.** (бывший, прежний)

12. I ran into my *old* boss at the mall.
13. Do you miss your *old* school?
14. In the *old* days, nobody locked their doors.

**6. Known for a long time.** (давний, старый)

15. Mike is an *old* friend from college.
16. The two teams are *old* rivals.

**7. Boring because it keeps happening — in *get old* and *the same old*.** (надоевший)

17. His jokes are getting *old*.
18. It's the same *old* story every Monday.

**8. Used informally to show affection or for emphasis.** (старый добрый; какой угодно)

19. Good *old* Dave — he always shows up.
20. Just grab any *old* shirt.
21. He drives a big *old* pickup truck.

### late

**Pronunciation:** /leɪt/ &middot; *LAYT*
**Русский:** опоздавший; поздний
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — *a late train*, *The train is late*; in the sense 'dead' only before a noun (*her late husband*)
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *later*, *latest*; *latter* and *last* are separate words
**Takes:** *for* + an event (*late for work*); *to* + an event or place, common in American speech (*late to class*); *with* + something owed or due (*late with the rent*)
**Preposition patterns:** [late for](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#late-for)
**Opposite:** *early*; *on time*
**Contrast:** Position changes the meaning: *her late husband* is dead, but *Her husband is late* means he has not arrived yet. *Lately* is not the adverb of this word — it means *recently*: *I've been busy lately*; *He arrived late* uses the adverb *late*. Russian *уже поздно* is *It's late*, with the adjective. *Later* is the comparative (*a later flight*) and *latest* the superlative, which also means *newest*; *latter* (the second of two) and *last* (final) have their own entries.

Late has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Arriving or happening after the expected or arranged time.** (опоздавший; запоздалый)

1. Sorry I'm *late* — the traffic was terrible.
2. The bus was twenty minutes *late*.
3. Don't be *late* for your interview.

**2. Happening after the usual time.** (поздний)

4. We had a *late* lunch.
5. Spring was *late* this year.
6. Is there a *later* flight to Denver?

**3. Near the end of a day or of a period of time.** (поздний; конец (периода))

7. It's getting *late* — let's go home.
8. The house was built in the *late* 1800s.
9. She's in her *late* thirties.

**4. Dead, especially recently.** (покойный)

10. The book is dedicated to her *late* husband.
11. The foundation was started by the *late* senator.

### near

**Pronunciation:** /nɪr/ &middot; *NEER*
**Русский:** близкий; ближний
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — after a verb for distance and time (*The station is near*); before a noun only in set uses (*the near future*, *a near miss*) and in the comparative and superlative (*the nearest store*)
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *nearer*, *nearest*
**Opposite:** *far*
**Contrast:** Do not put plain *near* before a noun to mean *close by*: say *a nearby store* or *a store nearby*, not *a near store* — though *the nearest store* is correct. *Near the park* is the preposition and *Come near* the adverb; neither is this adjective. American English says *near the beach*, rarely *near to the beach*. *A near miss* is an accident that almost happened. For family, *a close relative* is far more common in the US than *a near relative*.

Near has 5 distinct senses.

**1. Only a short distance away.** (близкий, недалёкий)

1. The hotel is quite *near* — we can walk.
2. Is the subway station very *near*?
3. Let's eat at the *nearest* diner.

**2. Soon in time.** (близкий, ближайший)

4. Prices will rise in the *near* future.
5. The holidays are *near*, and the stores are packed.

**3. Almost happening or almost complete.** (едва не случившийся; почти полный)

6. That was a *near* miss — the truck almost hit us.
7. The game ended in *near* silence.
8. The flood was a *near* disaster for the town.

**4. Being the closer of two sides.** (ближний)

9. Park on the *near* side of the street.
10. We swam from the *near* bank to the island.

**5. Closely related — formal.** (близкий (о родстве))

11. Only *near* relatives can inherit under the will.
12. She has no *near* family left.

### ill

**Pronunciation:** /ɪl/ &middot; *IL*
**Русский:** больной
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — in the sense 'sick' mostly after a verb (*She's ill*) or with an adverb before a noun (*seriously ill patients*); in the senses 'bad' and 'hostile' only before a noun (*ill effects*, *ill will*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *worse*, *worst*
**Takes:** *with* + an illness (*ill with the flu*)
**Opposite:** *well*; *healthy*
**Contrast:** In everyday American English *sick* is the usual word and *ill* is more formal: *I'm sick* / *She is seriously ill*. *An ill child* sounds stiff — say *a sick child* — but an adverb makes *ill* normal before a noun (*terminally ill patients*). In British English *sick* often means *vomiting*; in American English it means *ill*. Before a noun *ill* usually means *bad*, not *sick*: *ill effects*, *ill will*. The forms *worse* and *worst* belong to both *ill* and *bad*: *She got worse*. *Ill-* in compounds (*ill-advised*, *ill-treated*) is the adverb, and *the ills of society* is a noun.

Ill has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Sick; not in good health.** (больной)

1. Her father is seriously *ill*.
2. I felt *ill* after the long bus ride.
3. By Tuesday she was *worse*, so we took her to the ER.

**2. Bad or harmful.** (плохой, вредный)

4. The drug has no known *ill* effects.
5. He retired early because of *ill* health.

**3. Unfriendly or hostile.** (недобрый, враждебный)

6. I bear him no *ill* will.
7. There was a lot of *ill* feeling after the vote.

**4. Nervous and uncomfortable — in *ill at ease*.** (не по себе, неловко)

8. He looked *ill* at ease in a suit and tie.
9. Big parties make me feel *ill* at ease.

### well

**Pronunciation:** /wɛl/ &middot; *WEL*
**Русский:** здоровый
**Type:** quality
**Position:** predicative only — *I'm not well*; before a noun only in medical phrases like *a well-baby visit*
**Comparison:** irregular — *better*, *best*
**Opposite:** *ill*; *sick*
**Contrast:** The adjective *well* means *healthy*; the adverb *well* means *in a good way*: *I'm well* (not sick) / *She sings well*. *I feel well* is about health; *I feel good* is about health or mood, and in casual American English *I'm good* is the usual answer to *How are you?* The comparative is *better*: *I feel better*, not *more well*. Because it only follows a verb, *a well man* is wrong — say *a healthy man*. *Well* the noun (*колодец*) and the interjection (*Well, maybe*) are other words.

Well has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Healthy; not sick.** (здоровый)

1. I'm not feeling *well* today.
2. Is your mother *well*?
3. She was sick last week, but she's *better* now.

**2. In a good or satisfactory state.** (в порядке, хорошо)

4. All is *well* at home.
5. I hope all is *well* with you and your family.

**3. Lucky or a good thing, considering what happened — in *just as well*.** (к лучшему; и хорошо, что)

6. It's just as *well* we left early — the roads got icy.
7. She didn't get the job, which was just as *well*, since she hated the commute.

**4. Sensible or advisable — formal.** (разумно, желательно)

8. It would be *well* to get a second opinion.
9. It might be *well* to call ahead before you go.

### better

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɛtər/ &middot; *BET-ur*
**Русский:** лучший; лучше
**Type:** evaluative
**Position:** both — *a better job*, *This one is better*
**Comparison:** irregular — *better* is the comparative of *good* and *well*; the superlative is *best*
**Takes:** *than* + noun or clause; *at* + noun or *-ing* (*better at math*); *with* + people or things you handle; *for* + the person or thing it helps; *to*-infinitive (*It's better to wait*)
**Opposite:** *worse*
**Contrast:** *Better* is already comparative, so it never takes *more* or *very*: *much better*, *even better*, *a lot better*, not *more better* or *very better*. *I'm better* means *I have recovered*; *I'm good* only means *I'm fine*. *You'd better go* (*had better*) is a modal phrase, and *She sings better* is the adverb. *Better off* means *in a better situation, richer*: *We're better off without him*. Russian *лучший* is *better* when two things are compared (*a better car*) and *the best* for all others; careful English says *the better of the two*.

Better has 5 distinct senses.

**1. Of higher quality, more pleasant or more suitable than someone or something else.** (лучше, лучший)

1. This laptop is much *better* than my old one.
2. The weather is *better* today.
3. Would Friday be *better* for you?

**2. More skillful.** (лучше (умеющий), сильнее)

4. She's *better* at chess than her brother.
5. He's *better* with kids than I am.

### last

**Position:** both — mostly before a noun (*the last page*); after a verb in *be last* (*She was last in line*)

4. We went to Boston *last* weekend.

**3. Most recent.** (последний (недавний))

7. The *last* time I saw her, she was still in high school.
8. Prices have doubled over the *last* few years.

**4. Only remaining.** (последний, оставшийся)

10. Can I have the *last* cookie?
11. I spent my *last* ten dollars on gas.

**5. Least likely or least wanted.** (последний (о ком или чём меньше всего ожидаешь))

12. He's the *last* person I expected to see here.
