# Adjectives used as nouns with the

> *the rich*, *the young*, *the homeless* — adjectives that name a whole group of people.

[← The grammar of adjectives](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

With *the*, some adjectives stand for a whole group of people: *the rich*, *the young*, *the homeless*. The group is always plural — *the rich are*, never *the rich is* — and it cannot take a plural *-s* (*the riches* is a different word). For one person, English needs a noun: *a homeless man*, not *a homeless*.

---
### rich

**Pronunciation:** /rɪtʃ/ &middot; *RICH*
**Русский:** богатый
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for rich people as a group: *the rich are*; the ironic *That's rich* sense goes only after a verb
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *richer*, *richest*
**Takes:** *in* + noun (having a lot of something: *rich in iron*)
**Preposition patterns:** [rich in](../../06-prepositions/catalog/15-dependent-adjectives.md#rich-in)
**Opposite:** *poor*
**Contrast:** *The rich* means rich people as a group and always takes a plural verb: *The rich are getting richer*, not *The rich is*. It never takes a plural *-s*: *the riches* is a different word, a noun meaning great wealth. For one person say *a rich man*, *a rich woman* or *a rich person*, never *a rich*. Russian *богатый чем-то* is *rich in*, not *rich of*: *rich in oil*. About food, *rich* means full of butter, cream or sugar and can be praise; Russian *жирный* is *fatty* or *greasy*, which sound negative. *That's rich!* is ironic: the critic is guilty of the same thing. *Wealthy* is the more formal, more polite word, and only for money.

Rich has 8 distinct senses.

**1. Having a lot of money or property.** (богатый)

1. His uncle got *rich* selling real estate in Phoenix.
2. Tax cuts for the *rich* are a big issue this election.
3. They're one of the *richest* families in the country.

**2. rich in: containing or having a large amount of something useful or good.** (богатый (чем-то))

4. Spinach is *rich* in iron.
5. The region is *rich* in oil and natural gas.
6. Old newspapers are a *rich* source of local history.

**3. Of food: containing a lot of butter, cream, eggs or sugar, so that a little fills you up.** (сытный, калорийный)

7. The chocolate cake was too *rich* for me to finish.
8. She makes a *rich*, creamy sauce for the pasta.

**4. Of a color, sound or smell: deep, strong and pleasant.** (насыщенный, густой, глубокий)

9. The walls are painted a *rich* dark red.
10. He has a *rich*, warm voice that's perfect for radio.
11. The *rich* smell of coffee filled the kitchen.

**5. Full of interesting variety, detail or meaning.** (богатый, насыщенный)

12. New Orleans has a *rich* cultural history.
13. She's had a long and *rich* life.
14. The book gives a *richer* picture of the war than the movie does.

**6. Of soil or land: good for growing plants.** (плодородный)

15. The valley has *rich* soil for farming.
16. Adding compost makes the soil *richer*.

**7. Expensive and beautiful; luxurious.** (роскошный, дорогой)

17. The hotel lobby was full of *rich* fabrics and dark wood.
18. The dining room was decorated in *rich* velvet and gold.

**8. That's rich: used informally to say that a criticism is unfair or ridiculous because of who is making it.** (кто бы говорил; забавно слышать это от тебя)

19. You're calling me lazy? That's *rich*, coming from you.
20. Him complaining about people being late is pretty *rich*.

### young

**Pronunciation:** /jʌŋ/ &middot; *YUHNG*
**Русский:** молодой; маленький (о детях)
**Type:** age
**Position:** both — also with *the* for young people as a group: *the young*
**Comparison:** -er/-est — *younger*, *youngest*, both said with a hard g: /ˈjʌŋɡər/, /ˈjʌŋɡɪst/
**Opposite:** *old*
**Contrast:** *The young* means young people as a group and takes a plural verb: *The young are often the first to try new apps*. For one person say *a young man*, *a young woman* or *a young person*, never *a young*. *Its young* (*a bird feeding its young*) is a different use: a noun meaning an animal's babies. The comparative and superlative add a /ɡ/ the base word doesn't have: *young* /jʌŋ/ but *younger* /ˈjʌŋɡər/. Russian *младший брат* is *my younger brother* (or *little brother*), and *младший сын* among three or more is *the youngest son*; *junior* is for rank, not family. About children, *young* often means small: *young children* are *маленькие дети*, not teenagers.

Young has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Having lived or existed for only a short time; not old.** (молодой)

1. She was very *young* when she became mayor.
2. The coach wants a *younger* team next season.
3. My *younger* sister lives in Denver.

**2. Being a child; not yet grown up.** (маленький, юный)

4. They have two *young* kids, ages three and five.
5. When I was *young*, we spent every summer at the lake.
6. The show isn't suitable for *young* children.

**3. In an early stage; recently started or formed.** (молодой, недавно созданный)

7. It's still a *young* company, only three years old.
8. Come on, the night is still *young*!

**4. Looking, feeling or acting like a young person, or meant for young people.** (моложавый; молодёжный)

9. My grandmother is eighty but very *young* at heart.
10. You look *younger* with short hair.
11. That dress is a little *young* for her.

### homeless

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhoʊmləs/ &middot; *HOHM-luhs*
**Русский:** бездомный
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for homeless people as a group: *the homeless*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -less from *home*
**Contrast:** *The homeless* takes a plural verb: *The homeless need more than a bed for the night*. It never takes *-s*, and one person is *a homeless man*, *a homeless woman* or *a homeless person*, never *a homeless*. Many American writers and charities now prefer *homeless people* or *people experiencing homelessness*, because *the homeless* can sound as if people were only a category. The noun for the problem is *homelessness*: *Homelessness is rising*, not *Homeless is rising*. Don't confuse it with *homesick* (missing home) or *homely*, which in American English means plain or unattractive.

Having no home, and usually living on the street, in a car or in a shelter.

1. A *homeless* man was sleeping in the doorway.
2. The shelter serves hot meals to the *homeless* every night.
3. After the fire, forty families were *homeless*.

### unemployed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌnɪmˈplɔɪd/ &middot; *uhn-im-PLOYD*
**Русский:** безработный
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for people without jobs as a group: *the unemployed*
**Comparison:** not gradable — but *long-term unemployed* is common
**Form:** un- + *employed*
**Opposite:** *employed*
**Contrast:** *The unemployed* takes a plural verb (*The unemployed are*) and never takes *-s*; one person is *an unemployed worker* or *someone who is unemployed*, never *an unemployed*. The noun for the situation is *unemployment*: *Unemployment is at 4 percent*, not *Unemployed is*. Russian *он безработный* is an adjective after a verb in English: *He's unemployed*. In everyday speech Americans often say *out of work* or *between jobs*; *jobless* is common in news headlines.

Without a paid job, especially when you want one.

1. He's been *unemployed* since the plant closed.
2. The program helps the long-term *unemployed* find training.
3. *Unemployed* workers can apply for benefits online.

### injured

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪndʒərd/ &middot; *IN-jurd*
**Русский:** травмированный, пострадавший; уязвлённый
**Type:** participial
**Position:** both — also with *the* for injured people as a group: *the injured*; the 'offended' and legal senses go mostly before a noun: *injured pride*, *the injured party*
**Comparison:** not gradable — say *badly*, *seriously* or *slightly injured*
**Form:** -ed participle of *injure*
**Contrast:** *The injured* means the people who were hurt and takes a plural verb: *The injured were taken to three hospitals*. For one person say *an injured man* or *one of the injured*, never *an injured*. English splits Russian *раненый*, *травмированный*, *пострадавший* by cause: *injured* is hurt in an accident, a fall or a game (*an injured player*); *wounded* is hurt by a weapon (*wounded soldiers*); *hurt* is the everyday word, mostly after a verb (*Was anyone hurt?*). Things are *damaged*, not injured: *a damaged car*. *He was injured in a crash* is usually the passive of the verb *injure*.

Injured has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Physically hurt, for example in an accident, a fall or a game.** (травмированный, пострадавший)

1. Two *injured* passengers were taken to the hospital.
2. The *injured* were treated at the scene.
3. Our best player is *injured* and will miss the game.

**2. Of feelings: offended or upset because you have been treated unfairly.** (уязвлённый, обиженный)

4. Behind his jokes there was a lot of *injured* pride.
5. She spoke in an *injured* tone, as if we had betrayed her.

**3. In law: harmed or treated wrongly by someone else's actions.** (пострадавший, потерпевший)

6. The *injured* party can sue for damages.
7. The contract lets the *injured* party cancel the deal.

### blind

**Pronunciation:** /blaɪnd/ &middot; *BLYND*
**Русский:** слепой, незрячий
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for blind people as a group: *the blind*; the 'without judgment', 'without seeing', 'hard to see around' and 'no opening' senses go only before a noun: *blind faith*, *a blind curve*
**Comparison:** -er/-est or more/most — *blinder*, *blindest*, *more blind*; rare in the sight sense, where English says *partially*, *completely* or *legally blind*
**Takes:** *to* + noun (not noticing: *blind to the risks*); *in* + *one eye* (*blind in one eye*)
**Opposite:** *sighted*
**Contrast:** *The blind* means blind people as a group and takes a plural verb: *The blind are*, *a school for the blind*. One person is *a blind man*, *a blind woman* or *a blind person*, never *a blind*; many people prefer *blind people* or *people who are blind*, and *visually impaired* includes people with some sight. The noun *blind* is a window covering: *Pull down the blinds*. Losing your sight is *go blind*. Russian *вслепую* after a verb is usually *without looking* or *blindly* (*He signed the papers without reading them*), but set phrases keep the adjective: *a blind date*, *a blind taste test*. A street with no way out is *a dead end*; *a blind alley* is mostly figurative.

Blind has 6 distinct senses.

**1. Unable to see, from birth or because of illness or injury.** (слепой, незрячий)

1. Her grandfather went *blind* in his seventies.
2. He's been *blind* in one eye since the accident.
3. The museum offers tours for the *blind* with objects you can touch.

**2. blind to: not noticing, or not wanting to accept, something such as a fault or a danger.** (не замечающий, слепой (к))

4. Parents are often *blind* to their own kids' faults.
5. The company seemed *blind* to the risks.
6. No one was *more blind* to the problem than the boss himself.

**3. Not based on reason or careful thought; too strong to control.** (слепой, безрассудный)

7. The soldiers followed orders with *blind* obedience.
8. He stormed out in a *blind* rage.
9. It takes *blind* faith to invest in a company like that.

**4. Done without seeing or knowing something important in advance.** (проводимый вслепую; анонимный)

10. In a *blind* taste test, most people picked the cheaper cola.
11. My friends set me up on a *blind* date.
12. The judges do a *blind* review, so they never see the writers' names.

**5. Hard or impossible to see around or past, so you can't see what is coming.** (с плохим обзором, скрытый)

13. Slow down, there's a *blind* curve ahead.
14. The motorcycle was right in my *blind* spot.

**6. Of a wall, an alley or a passage: having no opening or no way through.** (глухой (о стене), тупиковый)

15. The back of the warehouse is a *blind* wall with no windows.
16. Every idea we tried led us up a *blind* alley.

### deaf

**Pronunciation:** /dɛf/ &middot; *DEF*
**Русский:** глухой, неслышащий
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for deaf people as a group: *the deaf*
**Comparison:** -er/-est or more/most — *deafer*, *deafest*, *more deaf*; in the hearing sense English usually says *partially*, *profoundly* or *completely deaf*
**Takes:** *to* + noun (refusing to listen: *deaf to our complaints*); *in* + *one ear* (*deaf in one ear*)
**Opposite:** *hearing*
**Contrast:** *Deaf* is /dɛf/, rhyming with *chef*, not /diːf/. *The deaf* takes a plural verb (*a school for the deaf*), and one person is *a deaf man* or *a deaf person*, never *a deaf*. With a capital, *Deaf* names the community and culture of people who use sign language; *deaf and dumb* and *deaf-mute* are offensive. For partial hearing loss Americans say *hard of hearing*. Russian *глухой* has meanings *deaf* doesn't: a remote place is *remote* (*a remote village*, not *a deaf village*), a muffled sound is *dull* or *muffled* (*a dull thud*), a wall with no windows is *blank*, and thick forest is *dense*.

Deaf has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Unable to hear, completely or partly.** (глухой, неслышащий)

1. She's *deaf* in her left ear.
2. Grandpa is getting *deafer* every year, so speak up.
3. The theater has captioned shows for the *deaf*.

**2. deaf to: unwilling to listen to or pay attention to something.** (глухой (к), не желающий слушать)

4. The city council was *deaf* to the neighbors' complaints.
5. Our warnings fell on *deaf* ears.
6. He turned a *deaf* ear to all our advice.

**3. Deaf: belonging to the community of deaf people who share sign language and a culture.** (относящийся к сообществу глухих)

7. *Deaf* culture has its own theater, poetry and humor.
8. She grew up in a *Deaf* family where everyone signed.

### disabled

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈseɪbəld/ &middot; *dih-SAY-buhld*
**Русский:** с инвалидностью; неисправный, отключённый
**Type:** quality; also participial
**Position:** both — also with *the* for disabled people as a group: *the disabled*; the 'for people with disabilities' sense goes only before a noun: *disabled parking*
**Comparison:** not gradable — say *severely*, *partially* or *permanently disabled*
**Form:** -ed participle of *disable*, from dis- + *able*
**Opposite:** *able-bodied*
**Contrast:** Russian *инвалид* is not English *invalid*: for a person that word is old-fashioned and offensive, and pronounced /ɪnˈvælɪd/ it means 'not valid' (*an invalid password*). Say *a disabled person* or *a person with a disability*; *handicapped* sounds dated in the US. *The disabled* takes a plural verb and never takes *-s*; one person is never *a disabled*, and many Americans prefer *disabled people* or *people with disabilities*. On US signs, parking spaces and restrooms are usually *accessible* or *handicapped*. A *disabled* car or phone setting is broken or switched off, which has nothing to do with people.

Disabled has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Having a physical or mental condition that limits how a person moves, senses things or does everyday activities.** (с инвалидностью)

1. The law protects *disabled* workers from discrimination.
2. Her son has been *disabled* since a car accident.
3. The new buses make it easier for the *disabled* to get around town.

**2. Meant for use by people with disabilities.** (для инвалидов)

4. You need a *disabled* parking placard to park there.
5. All the *disabled* parking spots near the entrance were taken.

**3. Of a machine, a vehicle or a feature: not working, or switched off.** (неисправный; отключённый)

6. A *disabled* truck is blocking the left lane.
7. Cookies are *disabled* in your browser.

### wealthy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛlθi/ &middot; *WEL-thee*
**Русский:** состоятельный, богатый
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for wealthy people as a group: *the wealthy*
**Comparison:** -er/-est or more/most — *wealthier*, *wealthiest*, *more wealthy*
**Form:** -y from *wealth*
**Opposite:** *poor*
**Contrast:** *The wealthy* takes a plural verb (*The wealthy are moving out of the city*) and never takes *-s*; one person is *a wealthy man* or *a wealthy woman*, never *a wealthy*. *Wealthy* is more formal and more polite than *rich*, and it covers only money and resources: food, colors and voices are *rich*, never *wealthy* (*a rich chocolate cake*). The comparative changes *y* to *i*: *wealthier*, *wealthiest*.

Wealthy has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Having a lot of money, property or valuable things.** (состоятельный, богатый)

1. She comes from a *wealthy* family in Connecticut.
2. Tax breaks for the *wealthy* are unpopular with voters.
3. It's one of the *wealthiest* suburbs in the country.

**2. wealthy in: having a large amount of a natural resource or other valuable thing.** (богатый (чем-то))

4. Wyoming is *wealthy* in coal and natural gas.
5. The country is *wealthy* in natural resources but has few good roads.

### disadvantaged

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪsədˈvæntɪdʒd/ &middot; *dis-uhd-VAN-tijd*
**Русский:** малообеспеченный, социально незащищённый; в невыгодном положении
**Type:** quality; also participial
**Position:** both — also with *the* for disadvantaged people as a group: *the disadvantaged*
**Comparison:** more/most — *more disadvantaged*, *most disadvantaged*
**Form:** dis- + *advantaged*, from the noun *advantage*
**Opposite:** *advantaged*; *privileged*
**Contrast:** *The disadvantaged* takes a plural verb and never takes *-s*; one person is *a disadvantaged student* or *someone from a disadvantaged background*, never *a disadvantaged*. It is a polite, official word for *poor*, used about groups, schools and neighborhoods more than about one person you know. For a single situation the everyday phrase is *at a disadvantage* (*Short players are at a disadvantage*), and *disadvantaged by* + noun is usually the passive of the verb *disadvantage*. Russian *неблагополучный* is often better as *troubled* (*a troubled family*) when the problem is not money.

Disadvantaged has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Lacking the money, education or opportunities that most people in a society have.** (малообеспеченный, социально незащищённый)

1. The program offers free tutoring to *disadvantaged* kids.
2. Schools in the *most disadvantaged* neighborhoods got extra funding.
3. The grants go first to the *disadvantaged*.

**2. In a worse position than other people in a particular situation.** (в невыгодном положении)

4. Small farmers feel *disadvantaged* next to the big agricultural companies.
5. Students without internet at home were *more disadvantaged* than anyone when classes moved online.

### accused

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkjuːzd/ &middot; *uh-KYOOZD*
**Русский:** обвиняемый
**Type:** participial
**Position:** attributive only — after a verb it is the passive of *accuse* (*She was accused of fraud*); also with *the* for the person or people on trial: *the accused*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ed participle of *accuse*
**Contrast:** Unlike *the rich* or *the young*, *the accused* often means one person, so the verb follows the meaning: *The accused was found guilty* (one defendant), *The accused were all released* (several). It never takes *-s*. Don't confuse it with *accusing*, which describes the one doing the blaming: *an accusing look* is *обвиняющий взгляд*, while *the accused man* is *обвиняемый*. *Accused of* + noun or *-ing* belongs to the verb: *He is accused of stealing*. In US news, *accused* before a noun stresses that nothing is proved yet: *the accused gunman*.

Charged with a crime or said to have done something wrong, but not yet proved guilty.

1. The *accused* man said nothing during the hearing.
2. The *accused* was led into the courtroom in handcuffs.
3. Police have released the name of the *accused* shooter.

### deceased

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈsiːst/ &middot; *dih-SEEST*
**Русский:** покойный, умерший
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for a dead person or dead people: *the deceased*
**Comparison:** not gradable
**Form:** -ed participle of the old verb *decease*
**Opposite:** *living*
**Contrast:** *Deceased* is formal and legal; in everyday speech say *dead*, or *late* before a name or a family word: *her late husband* (*её покойный муж*). Unlike *the rich* or *the young*, *the deceased* usually means one particular dead person and then takes a singular verb: *The deceased was a retired teacher*; for several people the verb is plural. It never takes *-s*. Don't confuse it with *diseased* /dɪˈziːzd/, 'sick, affected by a disease' (*diseased trees*): *deceased* has an /s/ sound, /dɪˈsiːst/.

No longer alive; dead (formal).

1. The form asks whether your parents are living or *deceased*.
2. The house was divided among the *deceased* man's children.
3. The family of the *deceased* has asked for privacy.

### needy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniːdi/ &middot; *NEE-dee*
**Русский:** нуждающийся, малоимущий; прилипчивый
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for poor people as a group: *the needy*
**Comparison:** -er/-est or more/most — *needier*, *neediest*, *more needy*
**Form:** -y from *need*
**Contrast:** *The needy* takes a plural verb and never takes *-s*: *The needy are*; one person is *a person in need* or part of *a needy family*, never *a needy*. In the money sense *needy* sounds a little old-fashioned, and *people in need* or *low-income families* are common today. In informal American English *needy* more often describes someone who wants too much attention, and it is a criticism: *He's so needy*. It doesn't mean *нужный*: something you need is *necessary* or *needed*.

Needy has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Poor; not having enough money, food or other basic things.** (нуждающийся, малоимущий)

1. The church collects coats for *needy* families every winter.
2. The scholarship goes to the *neediest* students first.
3. Volunteers serve Thanksgiving dinner to the *needy*.

**2. Wanting too much attention, love or reassurance from other people.** (прилипчивый, требующий постоянного внимания)

4. Her new boyfriend is really *needy*; he texts her every hour.
5. Our puppy got even *needier* after we moved.
6. I don't want to seem *needy*, but can you call me when you land?

### privileged

**Pronunciation:** /ˈprɪvəlɪdʒd/ &middot; *PRIV-uh-lijd*
**Русский:** привилегированный; польщённый
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for privileged people as a group: *the privileged*; the 'honored' sense goes only after a verb: *I'm privileged to*
**Comparison:** more/most — *more privileged*, *most privileged*
**Takes:** *to*-infinitive (honored: *privileged to know her*)
**Form:** -ed from the noun *privilege*
**Opposite:** *underprivileged*; *disadvantaged*
**Contrast:** *The privileged* takes a plural verb and never takes *-s*; one person is *a privileged kid* or *someone from a privileged background*, never *a privileged*. In American talk about race, class and money, *privileged* is often a criticism: *You're privileged and you don't see it*. But *privileged to* + verb is modest and polite: *I feel privileged to work here* means 'I'm honored', close to *для меня большая честь*. *Privileged* information is protected by law, not just secret. It is said in three syllables or two, /ˈprɪvəlɪdʒd/ or /ˈprɪvlɪdʒd/, and the ending is /ɪdʒd/, never /aɪdʒd/.

Privileged has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Having advantages, money or opportunities that most people do not have.** (привилегированный)

1. He grew up in a *privileged* family on the Upper East Side.
2. College was easier for the *privileged* than for the rest of us.
3. She knows she's *more privileged* than most of her students.

**2. Honored and lucky to have a particular experience.** (польщённый; (для меня) большая честь)

4. I'm *privileged* to be here tonight.
5. We were *privileged* to see the whales up close.

**3. Of information or communication: protected by law from being shared or used in court.** (конфиденциальный, защищённый законом)

6. What you tell your lawyer is *privileged*.
7. The memo contained *privileged* information.

**4. Having special rights or access that ordinary users or members do not have.** (с особыми правами (доступа))

8. Only *privileged* accounts can change these settings.
9. A few *privileged* guests got to go backstage.

### underprivileged

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndərˈprɪvəlɪdʒd/ &middot; *uhn-dur-PRIV-uh-lijd*
**Русский:** малоимущий, обездоленный
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also with *the* for poor people as a group: *the underprivileged*
**Comparison:** more/most — *more underprivileged*, *most underprivileged*
**Form:** under- + *privileged*
**Opposite:** *privileged*
**Contrast:** *The underprivileged* takes a plural verb and never takes *-s*; one person is *an underprivileged child*, never *an underprivileged*. Despite *privilege* inside it, it is not about legal rights: it means poor and without the chances others have, close to *disadvantaged*. Russian *бесправный* is *without rights* or *powerless*. It is a slightly old-fashioned, official word; American nonprofits now often say *low-income* instead.

Poor, and without the education, health care or opportunities that most people have.

1. The camp is free for kids from the city's *most underprivileged* neighborhoods.
2. She raises money for the *underprivileged* in her city.

### unknown

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌnˈnoʊn/ &middot; *uhn-NOHN*
**Русский:** неизвестный
**Type:** quality
**Position:** both — also after a noun with *to*: *a man unknown to the police*; *the unknown* is a singular abstract noun, not a group of people
**Comparison:** not gradable — strengthen with *completely*, *largely* or *relatively*; for 'less famous' say *less known* or *lesser-known*
**Takes:** *to* + person (not known by: *unknown to most voters*)
**Form:** un- + *known*
**Opposite:** *known*; *famous*
**Contrast:** Unlike *the rich* or *the young*, *the unknown* is not a group of people: it means everything that is not known and takes a singular verb. *Fear of the unknown* is *страх перед неизвестностью*. For people you don't know, add a noun: *an unknown caller*, *unknown attackers*. *Unknown to me* at the start of a sentence means 'without my knowing', like *без моего ведома*: *Unknown to her parents, she had quit school*. As a countable noun, *an unknown* is a person who isn't famous (*a total unknown*) or a fact nobody knows yet (*too many unknowns*); those are nouns.

Unknown has 3 distinct senses.

**1. Not known; not identified or not yet discovered.** (неизвестный)

1. The cause of the fire is still *unknown*.
2. He left the company for *unknown* reasons.
3. An *unknown* number of people were still inside.

**2. Not famous; not known to the public.** (неизвестный, безвестный)

4. The movie turned an *unknown* actress into a star.
5. The band was *unknown* outside Seattle until 1991.

**3. unknown to someone: not known by that person, or happening without their knowledge.** (без ведома; незнакомый (кому-то))

6. *Unknown* to his wife, he had borrowed money against the house.
7. The word was *unknown* to most of the class.
8. It was a disease *unknown* to doctors fifty years ago.

### wounded

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwuːndɪd/ &middot; *WOON-did*
**Русский:** раненый; уязвлённый
**Type:** participial
**Position:** both — also with *the* for wounded people as a group: *the wounded*
**Comparison:** not gradable — say *badly*, *seriously* or *mortally wounded*
**Form:** -ed participle of the verb *wound*, from the noun *wound* (an injury)
**Contrast:** *The wounded* takes a plural verb (*The wounded were flown to Germany*) and never takes *-s*; one person is *a wounded soldier* or *one of the wounded*, never *a wounded*. *Wounded* is for injuries from a weapon (a gun, a knife, a bomb), especially in war or crime; someone hurt in a car crash or a game is *injured*. *Wound* the injury and *wounded* have /uː/: /ˈwuːndɪd/. *Wound* with /aʊ/ is the past tense of *wind* (*She wound the clock*) and has nothing to do with this word.

Wounded has 2 distinct senses.

**1. Hurt by a weapon such as a gun, a knife or a bomb.** (раненый)

1. Medics carried the *wounded* soldiers to the helicopter.
2. The *wounded* were taken to a field hospital.
3. A *wounded* deer ran into the woods.

**2. Emotionally hurt or offended.** (уязвлённый, обиженный)

4. She gave him a *wounded* look and left the room.
5. After the breakup he felt *wounded* and embarrassed.
6. It was just *wounded* pride, nothing more.
