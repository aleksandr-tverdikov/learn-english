Adjectives that take a fixed preposition#

proud of, good at, interested in, similar to — the adjectives that bring their own preposition.

← The grammar of adjectives · All groups

The preposition after these adjectives is not chosen for its meaning — it is simply the one the adjective takes: afraid of, good at, interested in, married to. Russian uses different prepositions or a case ending, so the English one has to be learned with the word. Every entry names its preposition, says when a different one changes the meaning (angry at a person, angry about a thing), and links to the full pattern in the preposition dictionary.

keen#

Pronunciation
/kiːn/ · KEEN
Русский
острый; сильный; увлечённый
Type
quality; also emotion
Position
both — a keen eye, She wasn't keen on it
Comparison
-er/-est — keener, keenest
Takes
on + noun or -ing (liking: keen on jazz, mostly not keen on); to-infinitive (wanting to do: keen to help)
Preposition patterns
keen on
Contrast
Keen on + noun or -ing means liking something (I'm not keen on horror movies); keen to + verb means wanting to do it (She's keen to start). Never ✗ keen for doing or ✗ keen in. In American English the 'eager' sense sounds a little British or formal, except in the negative not too keen on; Americans more often say eager to, excited about or into (I'm really into jazz). The everyday American uses are the 'sharp' and 'strong' ones: a keen eye, a keen sense of smell, keen interest, keen competition. Peachy keen (great) is old-fashioned, joking slang.

Keen has 4 distinct senses.

1. Of the senses or the mind: sharp and quick to notice or understand. (острый, тонкий)

  1. Dogs have a much keener sense of smell than people.
  2. She has a keen eye for detail.
  3. He was a keen observer of small-town life.

2. Of a feeling or interest: strong and deep. (сильный, живой, глубокий)

  1. He took a keen interest in local politics.
  2. Competition for the job was keen.
  3. The company faces keen competition from overseas.

3. Wanting very much to do something, or liking something very much. (увлечённый, стремящийся; горящий желанием)

  1. I'm not too keen on the idea of camping in January.
  2. The new owners are keen to make changes.
  3. She was never very keen on sports.

4. Of a blade, having a very sharp edge; of wind, cold and biting — literary. (острый; пронизывающий (о ветре))

  1. The knife had a keen edge.
  2. A keen wind blew off the lake.

proud#

Pronunciation
/praʊd/ · PROWD
Русский
гордый; гордящийся
Type
emotion; also quality
Position
both — proud parents, We're proud of you
Comparison
-er/-est — prouder, proudest
Takes
of + noun or -ing (proud of you, proud of finishing); to-infinitive (proud to announce); that-clause (proud that she won)
Preposition patterns
proud of
Opposite
ashamed
Contrast
Russian гордиться + a noun in the instrumental case becomes proud of in English: I'm proud of my son, never ✗ proud with my son, ✗ proud for my son or ✗ proud by. Proud to + verb is about the honor of doing something now (proud to be here); proud of + -ing is about something done (proud of winning). Proud can be good (pleased about success, self-respect) or bad (arrogant): context decides, as with гордый. The noun is pride, and take pride in is the verb phrase (She takes pride in her work). Do someone proud (make them proud) is an adverb idiom.

Proud has 4 distinct senses.

1. Pleased and happy about something you, or someone close to you, did or has. (гордящийся, гордый (чем-то))

  1. Your mom and I are so proud of you.
  2. I've never been prouder of this team.
  3. We're proud to announce the opening of our second store.

2. Having self-respect and not wanting help or pity from others. (гордый, с чувством собственного достоинства)

  1. He was too proud to ask for money.
  2. They're a proud people with a long history.
  3. She's a proud woman who hates to complain.

3. Thinking that you are better or more important than other people. (высокомерный, надменный)

  1. He's too proud to admit he was wrong.
  2. She was a cold, proud woman who looked down on everyone.

4. Of a moment, day or thing: causing a feeling of pride. (торжественный; предмет гордости)

  1. Graduation was the proudest day of my father's life.
  2. It was a proud moment for the whole town.
  3. The new stadium is the school's proudest achievement.

jealous#

Pronunciation
/ˈdʒɛləs/ · JEL-uhs
Русский
завидующий; ревнивый
Type
emotion
Position
both — a jealous husband, He's jealous of her
Comparison
more/most — more jealous, most jealous
Takes
of + person or thing (jealous of her, jealous of his success)
Preposition patterns
jealous of
Form
noun jealousy
Contrast
Russian splits зависть and ревность; everyday American English uses jealous for both: I'm jealous of your vacation (завидую) and He gets jealous when she talks to other guys (ревнует). Envious covers only wanting what someone else has, and is more formal. Russian завидовать кому-то takes the dative, but English needs of: jealous of him, never ✗ jealous to him or ✗ jealous on him. I'm so jealous! is often friendly and light, like везёт же!. The verb for the envy sense is envy with no preposition (I envy you); there is no verb to jealous.

Jealous has 3 distinct senses.

1. Unhappy because someone has something you want. (завидующий, завистливый)

  1. Everyone at work is jealous of her new office.
  2. I'm so jealous — I've always wanted to go to Hawaii.
  3. He was jealous of his brother's success.

2. Afraid that someone you love is attracted to, or loves, someone else. (ревнивый, ревнующий)

  1. Her boyfriend gets jealous when she goes out without him.
  2. He was the most jealous husband I've ever met.
  3. The dog gets jealous whenever we hold the baby.

3. Protecting carefully something you have or value. (ревностно оберегающий)

  1. The family is jealous of its privacy.
  2. She kept a jealous watch over her little brother.

/ˈkeɪpəbəl/ · KAY-puh-buhl

Type
quality
Position
both — a capable manager, She's capable of more
Comparison
more/most — more capable, most capable
Takes
of + noun or -ing (capable of anything, capable of running a marathon), never a to-infinitive
Preposition patterns
capable of
Form
-able; noun capability
Opposite
incapable
Contrast
Capable of + -ing, never ✗ capable to do: She's capable of doing it, but She's able to do it. The two words take different patterns, and learners mix them. Russian способный ученик (talented, quick) is usually a bright student or a gifted student; a capable student means competent and reliable, not especially gifted. Способен на всё is capable of anything, often with a hint that the person might do something bad.

Capable has 3 distinct senses.

1. Having the ability, power or capacity to do something. (способный (сделать), в состоянии)

  1. The truck is capable of pulling a small boat.
  2. You're capable of much better work than this.
  3. The new stadium is capable of holding 60,000 fans.

2. Able to do things well; competent and skilled. (умелый, толковый, компетентный)

  1. Don't worry — the kids are in very capable hands.
  2. She's the most capable manager we've ever had.
  3. He seemed capable and calm in a crisis.

3. Willing or likely to do something bad or surprising. (способный (на что-то плохое))

  1. I didn't think he was capable of lying to me.
  2. When she's angry, she's capable of anything.

similar#

Pronunciation
/ˈsɪmələr/ · SIM-uh-lur
Русский
похожий, сходный
Type
quality
Position
both — a similar problem, The two houses are similar
Comparison
more/most — more similar, most similar
Takes
to + noun (similar to mine); in + the feature that is alike (similar in size)
Preposition patterns
similar to
Form
noun similarity
Opposite
different, dissimilar
Contrast
Similar to, never ✗ similar with, ✗ similar as or ✗ similar on. Russian похож на suggests on, but English uses to. For the feature, use in: similar in color, not ✗ similar by color. Similar means alike, not identical: for identical use the same as (My phone is the same as yours) or identical to. For people's faces, look like is more natural: She looks like her mother rather than She is similar to her mother. The comparative is more similar, never ✗ similarer.

Similar has 2 distinct senses.

1. Almost the same as someone or something, but not exactly. (похожий, сходный, аналогичный)

  1. Your car is very similar to mine.
  2. The two sisters are similar in size, so they share clothes.
  3. The new version is more similar to the original than I expected.

2. In geometry: having the same shape but not the same size. (подобный (о фигурах))

  1. The two triangles are similar because their angles are equal.
  2. Show that the triangles are similar.

different#

Pronunciation
/ˈdɪfərənt/ · DIF-ur-uhnt
Русский
другой; разный; отличающийся
Type
quality
Position
both — a different color, Her plan is different
Comparison
more/most — more different, most different
Takes
from + noun (standard); than + noun or clause (common American, best before a clause); to is British
Preposition patterns
different from
Form
noun difference; verb differ
Opposite
same, similar
Contrast
Different from is always correct in American English (Her account is different from his). Different than is common and natural before a clause (It was different than I expected); before a noun some editors still prefer from. Different to is British. Russian другой has three English matches: different (not the same: a different answer), another (one more or a replacement, singular: another cup), and other (the other side). Before a plural noun, different often means разные (different colors). The adverb is differently: She thinks differently, not She thinks different in careful English. Grade it with very, completely or a little; no different means exactly the same (Today was no different).

Different has 4 distinct senses.

1. Not the same as someone or something else. (отличающийся, непохожий, другой)

  1. My brother is completely different from me.
  2. The job turned out to be different than I expected.
  3. Life in the city couldn't be more different from life on the farm.

2. Before a plural noun: separate and of several kinds. (разный, различный)

  1. The shirt comes in five different colors.
  2. I've called three different times and nobody answers.
  3. People have different reasons for moving here.

3. Not the one already mentioned or usual; another. (другой, иной)

  1. Can I try a different size?
  2. She moved to a different school in the fall.
  3. Let's talk about something different.

4. Unusual or new, sometimes as a polite way to say you do not like something. (необычный, оригинальный)

  1. Well, the color is certainly different.
  2. I wanted something different for my birthday this year.

typical#

Pronunciation
/ˈtɪpɪkəl/ · TIP-ih-kuhl
Русский
типичный; обычный
Type
quality; also evaluative
Position
both — a typical day, That's typical of him
Comparison
more/most — more typical, most typical
Takes
of + person or group (typical of him); for + place, time or group, common in American English (typical for July); of + person + to-infinitive (It's typical of her to be late)
Preposition patterns
typical of
Form
-al from type
Opposite
atypical, unusual
Contrast
For behavior, It's typical of him to + verb is the standard pattern; typical for him is common in speech but less careful. For places and times, American English uses both: typical of and typical for this time of year. Never ✗ typical to him. Typical! on its own is a complaint: something annoying has happened again, as expected — как всегда!. Russian обычный matches typical only before a noun (on a typical day — в обычный день); after a verb use normal or usual (That's normal).

Typical has 3 distinct senses.

1. Having the usual qualities of a particular kind of person, thing or group. (типичный, характерный)

  1. It was a typical American breakfast: eggs, bacon and pancakes.
  2. This is typical of houses built in the 1950s.
  3. Heavy rain is typical for this time of year.

2. Normal and average; happening in the usual way. (обычный, средний)

  1. On a typical day, I get to the office at eight.
  2. The most typical customer is a woman in her thirties.
  3. A typical family here has two cars.

3. Behaving in the annoying way you expect of someone or something. (в духе (кого-то), как всегда)

  1. It's typical of him to leave the dishes for me.
  2. The bus is late again. Typical!
  3. That's so typical of this company.

related#

Pronunciation
/rɪˈleɪtɪd/ · rih-LAY-tid
Русский
связанный; родственный
Type
participial
Position
both — related issues, The two problems are related
Comparison
more/most — usually with closely: more closely related, most closely related
Takes
to + noun (connection or family: related to the case, related to me)
Preposition patterns
related to
Form
-ed from relate; also in compounds: work-related, stress-related
Opposite
unrelated
Contrast
Related to, never ✗ related with: Russian связан с pulls toward with, but English uses to. Are you two related? asks whether people are family; the noun for the person is a relative, not a related. Relative as an adjective means compared with something else (relative safety) and is a different word. In compounds, -related means caused by or connected with: a work-related injury, alcohol-related crashes.

Related has 3 distinct senses.

1. Connected with something, often by cause. (связанный, имеющий отношение (к))

  1. Police think the two robberies are related.
  2. She asked a few questions related to the budget.
  3. He takes a lot of sick days for stress-related health problems.

2. In the same family, by birth or marriage. (родственный, состоящий в родстве)

  1. Are you related to the Johnsons down the street?
  2. We have the same last name, but we're not related.
  3. She's related to me by marriage.

3. Of languages, animals or plants: coming from the same origin. (родственный (о языках, видах))

  1. Russian and Polish are closely related languages.
  2. Humans are more closely related to chimps than to gorillas.

used#

Pronunciation
/juːst; juːzd/ · YOOST; YOOZD
Русский
привыкший; подержанный
Type
participial
Position
both — used to after a verb (I'm used to it); used meaning secondhand before a noun (a used car)
Comparison
more/most — in the accustomed sense: more used to, so used to; not gradable in the secondhand sense
Takes
to + noun or -ing after be, get or become (used to the noise, used to working nights)
Preposition patterns
used to
Form
past participle of use
Contrast
Two different things are spelled used to. The adjective, after be or get, means accustomed and needs a noun or -ing: I'm used to getting up early, never ✗ I'm used to get up early. The habit-in-the-past form has no be and takes a bare verb: I used to get up early (раньше вставал рано, а теперь нет). Be used to is the state (привык); get used to is the process (привыкнуть): You'll get used to it. In used to both are said /juːst/, with the s of loose; a used car has /juːzd/, with a z. For a verb meaning 'employed', used is a passive verb, not this adjective: This room is used for storage.

Used has 3 distinct senses.

1. Familiar with something, so that it no longer seems strange or difficult. (привыкший (к))

  1. I'm not used to this heat yet.
  2. You'll get used to working nights after a week or two.
  3. After a year in Chicago, she's more used to the cold.

2. Owned by someone else before; not new. (подержанный, б/у)

  1. We bought a used car for our daughter.
  2. The store sells used books and records.

3. Already used once, so not clean or not able to be used again. (использованный)

  1. Put the used towels in the basket.
  2. Throw used needles into the red container.

participial
Position
both — accustomed to after a verb (She's accustomed to it); before a noun it means usual (his accustomed seat)
Comparison
more/most — more accustomed, most accustomed
Takes
to + noun or -ing after be, get, become or grow (accustomed to the noise, accustomed to working late)
Preposition patterns

Contrast
The to is a preposition, so a verb after it takes -ing: accustomed to driving, not ✗ accustomed to drive (that older pattern survives only in very formal writing). Accustomed to means the same as used to but is more formal; in conversation Americans say I'm used to it. Be accustomed is the state; get, become or grow accustomed is the change. Before a noun, accustomed means usual and is literary: in her accustomed place.

Accustomed has 2 distinct senses.

1. Familiar with something because you have experienced it often. (привыкший (к))

  1. She's accustomed to working long hours.
  2. My eyes slowly grew accustomed to the dark.
  3. The kids became more accustomed to the new school by spring.

2. Before a noun: usual or habitual — formal. (привычный, обычный)

  1. He sat in his accustomed seat by the window.
  2. She answered with her accustomed calm.

familiar#

Pronunciation
/fəˈmɪljər/ · fuh-MIL-yur
Русский
знакомый; осведомлённый; фамильярный
Type
quality
Position
both — a familiar face, The name sounds familiar
Comparison
more/most — more familiar, most familiar
Takes
with + thing (the person knows it: familiar with the software); to + person (it is known to them: familiar to most Americans)
Preposition patterns
familiar with
Form
noun familiarity
Opposite
unfamiliar, strange
Contrast
The preposition swaps the subject. I'm familiar with the song means I know it; The song is familiar to me means the same thing from the other side. Never ✗ I'm familiar to the song. Russian знаком с matches familiar with for things, but for people you have met, English prefers know or have met: I know him, not I'm familiar with him, which sounds like you only know about him. Фамильярный is only one sense (too informal), and the Russian noun знакомый (a person you know) is an acquaintance, not a familiar.

Familiar has 3 distinct senses.

1. Known to you, or easy to recognize because you have seen or heard it before. (знакомый)

  1. It was nice to see a familiar face at the party.
  2. Your name sounds familiar — have we met?
  3. The story will be familiar to anyone who grew up in a small town.

2. Knowing something well. (знакомый (с), хорошо знающий, осведомлённый)

  1. Are you familiar with this software?
  2. I'm not very familiar with the area.
  3. After a month, she was more familiar with the rules.

3. Friendly and informal, sometimes too informal with someone you do not know well. (фамильярный; непринуждённый)

  1. He was a little too familiar with the waitress.
  2. The two men were on familiar terms after years of working together.

anxious#

Pronunciation
/ˈæŋkʃəs/ · ANGK-shuhs
Русский
тревожный, встревоженный; стремящийся
Type
emotion
Position
both — an anxious mother, She was anxious about it
Comparison
more/most — more anxious, most anxious
Takes
about + noun or -ing (worry: anxious about the test); for + person (worry for them: anxious for his safety) or + thing (wanting it: anxious for news); to-infinitive (eager: anxious to leave); that-clause
Preposition patterns
anxious about
Form
noun anxiety
Contrast
The preposition changes the meaning. Anxious about is worry (anxious about money). Anxious to + verb and anxious for + thing usually mean eager, often with a little impatience (anxious to get home, anxious for news). Some American usage guides object to anxious for 'eager' and prefer eager, but it is very common. Anxious is stronger and more lasting than nervous: you are nervous before a speech, anxious about your health.

Anxious has 3 distinct senses.

1. Worried and nervous about something that might happen. (встревоженный, обеспокоенный)

  1. She's anxious about her job interview tomorrow.
  2. The longer we waited, the more anxious I got.
  3. Parents are anxious for the safety of their kids.

2. Of a time or a look: full of worry, or causing it. (тревожный)

  1. There were a few anxious moments before the plane landed.
  2. After an anxious wait, the doctor came out.

3. Wanting very much to do or have something, often impatiently. (стремящийся, жаждущий, с нетерпением ждущий)

  1. We're anxious to get home before dark.
  2. The company is anxious for a quick deal.
  3. He was anxious that nobody should find out.

sensitive#

Pronunciation
/ˈsɛnsətɪv/ · SEN-suh-tiv
Русский
чувствительный; чуткий; обидчивый; деликатный
Type
quality
Position
both — sensitive skin, She's sensitive about her age
Comparison
more/most — more sensitive, most sensitive
Takes
to + noun (reacting to, or aware of: sensitive to light, sensitive to her needs); about + noun (easily upset about: sensitive about his weight)
Preposition patterns
sensitive to
Form
noun sensitivity
Opposite
insensitive
Contrast
Sensitive to is reacting or being aware (sensitive to criticism, sensitive to the cold); sensitive about is getting upset about a personal matter (sensitive about her accent). Sensitive is a false friend of sensible, which means разумный: a sensible decision (разумное решение), a sensitive person (чуткий или обидчивый человек). Russian чувствительный is close, but чуткий (caring) is often thoughtful or understanding in speech.

Sensitive has 5 distinct senses.

1. Reacting quickly or strongly to something physical, such as light, cold or a substance. (чувствительный)

  1. Use a mild soap if you have sensitive skin.
  2. My eyes are very sensitive to light.
  3. Babies are more sensitive to heat than adults.

2. Understanding other people's feelings and needs. (чуткий, внимательный)

  1. He's a kind, sensitive guy.
  2. Doctors need to be sensitive to their patients' fears.

3. Easily hurt or upset by what people say or do. (обидчивый, ранимый)

  1. She's very sensitive about her weight, so don't mention it.
  2. Don't be so sensitive — I was only joking.
  3. Teenagers can be sensitive to criticism.

4. Needing to be handled carefully because it could upset people or is secret. (деликатный, щекотливый; секретный)

  1. Money is a sensitive subject in our family.
  2. The files contain sensitive information.

5. Of a machine or instrument: able to measure or detect very small changes. (чувствительный (о приборе))

  1. The microphone is sensitive enough to pick up a whisper.
  2. The test is the most sensitive one available.
