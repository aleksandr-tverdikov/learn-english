Adjectives that only go before a noun#

main, mere, former, utter — adjectives that stand before a noun and cannot follow a verb.

← The grammar of adjectives · All groups

The mirror image of the previous group: these go before a noun and cannot follow a verb. The main reason is English; the reason is main is not. Several of them are emphasizers that add force rather than a quality (a mere child, sheer luck, an utter disaster), and a few change meaning when they do appear after a verb, which each entry points out.

main#

Pronunciation
/meɪn/ · MAYN
Русский
главный, основной
Type
classifying — picks out the most important one of a group
Position
attributive only — never after a verb: the main problem is cost, not the problem is main
Comparison
not gradable — no mainer, no more main
Opposite
minor, secondary
Contrast
Russian главный can stand after a verb; main cannot. Что здесь главное? is What matters most here? and Кто здесь главный? is Who's in charge here?, never Who is main here? After a verb, use the main one or most important: Price is the main thing. The noun main is a large pipe or cable (a water main burst), and the adverb is mainly (mainly on weekends). The old sense 'full, all one's' survives only in the literary by main force.

Largest, most important or most central of its kind.

  1. The main reason I moved here was the job.
  2. The main entrance is on the other side of the building.
  3. Do you want chicken or fish for the main course?

chief#

Pronunciation
/tʃiːf/ · CHEEF
Русский
главный; старший (по должности)
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — never after a verb: the chief problem, not the problem is chief; the one exception is the fronted phrase chief among: Chief among our worries was money
Comparison
not gradable — no chiefer; chiefest is archaic
Opposite
minor, secondary
Contrast
The noun chief is the head of a group: the police chief, the fire chief. Do not confuse it with chef (шеф-повар). Russian шеф in the sense 'boss' is boss in everyday American English: My boss is on vacation; my chief sounds military or old-fashioned. After a verb the adjective does not work: say Cost is the chief concern, not The concern is chief. The adverb is chiefly (chiefly in the summer).

Chief has 2 distinct senses.

1. Most important; main. (главный, основной)

  1. The chief problem with the plan is that nobody can afford it.
  2. Safety is our chief concern on the job site.
  3. The chief complaint from customers is the long wait on the phone.

2. Highest in rank or authority; at the head of a team or organization. (главный, старший (по должности))

  1. She was promoted to chief engineer last spring.
  2. The chief executive officer announced the layoffs in an email.
  3. The chief financial officer will present the budget on Friday.

principal#

Pronunciation
/ˈprɪnsəpəl/ · PRIN-suh-puhl
Русский
главный, основной; ведущий
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — never after a verb: the principal cause, not the cause was principal
Comparison
not gradable
Opposite
minor, secondary
Contrast
Principal and principle sound exactly the same. Principal is the adjective 'main', and also a noun: the head of a school (директор школы) or the money you borrowed, not counting interest. Principle is only a noun, 'a basic rule or belief' (принцип): a matter of principle, the principles of physics. Principal is more formal than main; in conversation the main reason is more natural than the principal reason. The adverb is principally.

Principal has 2 distinct senses.

1. Most important; main. (главный, основной)

  1. Tourism is the principal source of income for the island.
  2. The principal cause of the fire was faulty wiring.
  3. Denver and Colorado Springs are the state's two principal cities.

2. First in rank among a group of performers or researchers; leading. (ведущий, первый (солист))

  1. She was a principal dancer with the New York City Ballet for ten years.
  2. He is the principal investigator on a federal research grant.
  3. The principal cellist played the solo beautifully.

mere#

Pronunciation
/mɪr/ · MEER
Русский
всего лишь, простой; один только
Type
emphasizing
Position
attributive only — right before the noun, after a, the or a possessive if there is one: a mere child, mere seconds; never the child was mere
Comparison
not gradable — there is no merer, but the superlative merest adds extra emphasis: the merest hint
Contrast
Mere stresses how small or unimportant something is. A singular noun or an amount takes a or the before it: a mere $5, the mere fact, not mere $5; a plural can stand alone: mere minutes later. After a verb use only or just: The difference is only $5, not The difference is mere. The adverb is merely (I was merely asking). The mere + noun says that this alone is enough: the mere mention of his name = одно только упоминание его имени.

Mere has 2 distinct senses.

1. Nothing more than; only, used to stress that something is small or unimportant. (всего лишь, простой)

  1. She was a mere child when her family moved to Texas.
  2. The tickets cost a mere $12, so the show sold out in hours.
  3. The fire spread through the building in mere minutes.

2. Even by itself, without anything more, used to say that something small has a big effect. (один только, одна лишь; само)

  1. The mere thought of flying makes him nervous.
  2. The merest hint of criticism upsets her for days.
  3. The mere fact that he called shows he's sorry.

sheer#

Pronunciation
/ʃɪr/ · SHEER
Русский
чистый, сущий; отвесный; прозрачный
Type
emphasizing; also quality
Position
both — attributive only in the main sense 'nothing but' (sheer luck); the senses 'very steep' and 'very thin' also follow a verb (the drop is sheer, the curtains are sheer)
Comparison
not gradable — in the main sense 'nothing but'; but the 'thin' sense grades: very sheer, sheerer, sheerest
Contrast
In its main sense sheer goes only before a noun and adds force: sheer luck = чистое везение, the sheer size = один только размер. The luck was sheer is not English. Do not confuse it with shear (same sound), a verb meaning 'to cut' (shear sheep); shears are large scissors. The verb sheer (sheer off, 'to swerve') and the noun sheers (thin curtains) are separate words.

Sheer has 3 distinct senses.

1. Nothing but; complete and total, used to stress how great something is. (чистый, сущий, явный)

  1. We found the place by sheer luck.
  2. The sheer size of the Grand Canyon is hard to take in.
  3. She finished the marathon through sheer willpower.

2. Extremely steep; almost vertical. (отвесный, крутой)

  1. The trail runs along a sheer cliff with no railing.
  2. On the north side, the drop to the river is sheer.

3. Very thin and light, so that you can see through it. (прозрачный, тонкий (о ткани))

  1. She wore a sheer black blouse over a camisole.
  2. The curtains are sheer, so the room gets plenty of light.
  3. For summer she wanted something sheerer than her winter tights.

utter#

Pronunciation
/ˈʌtər/ · UHT-ur
Русский
полный, совершенный, абсолютный
Type
emphasizing
Position
attributive only — never after a verb: an utter disaster, not the disaster was utter
Comparison
not gradable
Contrast
Utter goes mostly with bad or extreme nouns: utter nonsense, utter chaos, utter silence, an utter failure. For good things, a complete success or a total success is what people say. After a verb use complete or total: The chaos was complete. The verb utter is a different word, 'to say' (She didn't utter a word = не произнесла ни слова), and the adverb is utterly (utterly exhausted).

Complete and total, used mostly to stress something bad or extreme.

  1. The first day of the move was an utter disaster.
  2. That's utter nonsense, and you know it.
  3. When the power went out, the house fell into utter darkness.

former#

Pronunciation
/ˈfɔːrmər/ · FOR-mur
Русский
бывший; прежний
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — never after a verb: a former teacher, not the teacher is former
Comparison
not gradable
Form
an old comparative meaning 'earlier'; the adverb is formerly
Opposite
current, present; latter (of two)
Contrast
Russian бывший also works alone as a noun (мой бывший); English uses ex: my ex, my ex-husband, not my former. The former standing alone is a pronoun-like noun, paired with the latter: Of tea and coffee, I prefer the former (the first one named). To say something is no longer true, use used to or no longer: He used to be a teacher, not He is former. Do not mix up former and formal (официальный).

Former has 3 distinct senses.

1. Having been a particular thing in the past, but not now. (бывший)

  1. Two former presidents spoke at the funeral.
  2. I ran into my former boss at the grocery store.
  3. The museum is in a former train station.

2. Belonging to an earlier time. (прежний, былой)

  1. After the fire, the old theater was restored to its former glory.
  2. Since the illness he's been a shadow of his former self.
  3. In former times, the river was the only road into the valley.

3. First of two things just mentioned. (первый (из двух названных))

  1. Some students commute and some live on campus; the former group spends far less on rent.
  2. You can pay now or in installments, but the former option saves you about $200.

outright#

Pronunciation
/ˈaʊtraɪt/ · OWT-ryt
Русский
полный; откровенный; безоговорочный
Type
emphasizing
Position
attributive only — as an adjective it goes before the noun: an outright lie; after a verb outright is the adverb (she refused outright)
Comparison
not gradable
Form
compound — out + right
Contrast
The adjective is stressed on the first syllable, OWT-ryt: an outright ban. The adverb, which follows the verb, is stressed on the second, owt-RYT: refuse outright, buy a car outright (for the full price at once), killed outright (instantly), laugh outright (openly). So The ban is outright is wrong; say The ban is total or They banned it outright.

Outright has 4 distinct senses.

1. Complete and total, with no limits or exceptions. (полный, категорический)

  1. Several cities have passed an outright ban on fireworks.
  2. The committee's answer was an outright rejection of the plan.

2. Open and direct; not hidden or softened. (откровенный, неприкрытый)

  1. That wasn't a mistake — it was an outright lie.
  2. The meeting started politely and ended in outright hostility.

3. Clear and unquestioned, with no need to share or count again. (абсолютный, безоговорочный)

  1. No candidate won an outright majority, so there will be a runoff.
  2. She was the outright winner, ten points ahead of anyone else.

4. Paid or given in full at once, with no conditions or installments. (полный, единовременный (о покупке))

  1. An outright purchase costs more up front than a lease.
  2. Her grandparents gave her the house as an outright gift.

indoor#

Pronunciation
/ˈɪndɔːr/ · IN-dor
Русский
крытый; комнатный, домашний
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — after a verb use the adverb indoors: The party is indoors, not The party is indoor
Comparison
not gradable
Form
in + door; the adverb is indoors
Opposite
outdoor
Contrast
Indoor goes before a noun (an indoor pool); indoors goes after a verb (Let's eat indoors, The kids stayed indoors all day). In everyday speech inside is more common than indoors: Come inside, it's raining. Russian комнатная температура is room temperature, not indoor temperature, which means the temperature inside a building.

Indoor has 2 distinct senses.

1. Built, located or done inside a building, not in the open air. (крытый; в помещении)

  1. The hotel has an indoor pool and a gym.
  2. Our high school plays indoor soccer in the winter.
  3. Indoor smoking is banned in bars across the state.

2. Kept, used or suitable inside a home. (комнатный, домашний)

  1. Indoor cats usually live longer than cats that go outside.
  2. Most indoor plants need less water in winter.
  3. Kids, use your indoor voices, please.

outdoor#

Pronunciation
/ˈaʊtdɔːr/ · OWT-dor
Русский
уличный, на открытом воздухе
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — after a verb use the adverb outdoors: The concert is outdoors, not The concert is outdoor
Comparison
not gradable
Form
out + door; the adverb is outdoors
Opposite
indoor
Contrast
Outdoor goes before a noun (outdoor seating); outdoors goes after a verb (We ate outdoors) and is also a noun in the great outdoors (природа, дикая природа). For a person who loves hiking and camping, Americans use the informal adjective outdoorsy, which can follow a verb: She's very outdoorsy, not She's very outdoor.

Outdoor has 2 distinct senses.

1. Located, happening or used outside, not in a building. (уличный, на открытом воздухе)

  1. The restaurant added outdoor seating for the summer.
  2. They held the wedding at an outdoor venue by the lake.
  3. Put the outdoor furniture in the garage before the first frost.

2. Connected with or fond of activities in nature, such as hiking and camping. (для активного отдыха, туристический; любящий природу)

  1. He's never been the outdoor type.
  2. The store sells outdoor gear like tents and hiking boots.

lone#

Pronunciation
/loʊn/ · LOHN
Русский
одинокий; единственный
Type
quantity
Position
attributive only — after a verb use alone: a lone rider, but The rider was alone
Comparison
not gradable
Form
shortened from alone
Contrast
Three words, three jobs. Lone goes before a noun and says there is only one: a lone tree. Alone goes after a verb: She lives alone. Lonely is the sad feeling (одинокий, тоскующий) and works in both positions: a lonely old man, I felt lonely. So He was lone is wrong. British lone parent is single parent in American English; a lone wolf is a person who prefers to act alone.

Lone has 2 distinct senses.

1. Alone; with no other people or things nearby. (одинокий)

  1. A lone hiker was walking along the ridge.
  2. There was a lone tree in the middle of the field.
  3. The police believe a lone gunman fired the shots.

2. Being the only one. (единственный)

  1. She was the lone survivor of the crash.
  2. The senator cast the lone vote against the bill.
  3. Their lone goal came in the final minute.

upper#

Pronunciation
/ˈʌpər/ · UHP-ur
Русский
верхний; высший
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — after a verb use higher or on top: the upper shelf, but That shelf is higher
Comparison
not gradable — upper is itself an old comparative of up, so there is no more upper and no upper than; the related superlative is uppermost
Form
an old comparative of up
Opposite
lower
Contrast
Although upper looks like a comparative, it never takes than: The attic is higher than the second floor, not upper than. Upper names one of two or more levels: the upper floors, the upper lip. Idioms: have the upper hand (иметь преимущество), keep a stiff upper lip (не показывать чувств). The noun upper is the top part of a shoe, or slang for a stimulant drug.

Upper has 3 distinct senses.

1. Higher in position; above another part. (верхний)

  1. The upper floors of the building have the best views.
  2. He has a small scar on his upper lip.
  3. Most of this workout is for your upper body.

2. Higher in rank, level or amount. (высший; старший; верхний (предел))

  1. The report went to upper management for approval.
  2. Is there an upper age limit for the program?
  3. The Senate is often called the upper house of Congress.

3. Farther upstream, farther inland or farther north. (верхний (по течению); северный)

  1. Barges carry grain down from the upper Mississippi.
  2. They moved from Brooklyn to upper Manhattan.

inner#

Pronunciation
/ˈɪnər/ · IN-ur
Русский
внутренний
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — after a verb use inside: the inner door, but The door is inside
Comparison
not gradable — no more inner; the related superlative is innermost: my innermost thoughts
Form
an old comparative of in
Opposite
outer
Contrast
Inner city in American English is not the historic center (центр города): it means poorer, crowded neighborhoods near the center, as in inner-city schools. For the business center of a city, say downtown. After a verb inner does not work: use inside or rephrase. Inner feelings are private ones: inner peace = душевный покой.

Inner has 3 distinct senses.

1. Inside; closer to the center of something. (внутренний)

  1. The inner walls of the house are made of drywall.
  2. An infection of the inner ear can affect your balance.
  3. Runners in the inner lane have the shortest distance to cover.

2. Private and not shown to other people; of the mind or spirit. (внутренний, душевный)

  1. Meditation helped her find some inner peace.
  2. He rarely talks about his inner life.
  3. It took real inner strength to walk away.

3. Closest to the center of power and trusted with its secrets. (узкий, ближайший (круг))

  1. Only a few people in the governor's inner circle knew about the plan.
  2. She was never part of the company's inner circle.

outer#

Pronunciation
/ˈaʊtər/ · OW-tur
Русский
внешний, наружный
Type
classifying
Position
attributive only — after a verb use outside or on the outside: the outer wall, but The wall is on the outside
Comparison
not gradable — no more outer; the related superlative is outermost: the outermost planets
Form
an old comparative of out
Opposite
inner
Contrast
Outer always needs a noun after it; after a verb use outside or on the outside. Outer space is космос. On medicine labels English says external, not outer: For external use only (для наружного применения).

Outer has 3 distinct senses.

1. On the outside; farther from the center. (внешний, наружный)

  1. Peel off the tough outer leaves of the cabbage.
  2. The outer layer of the jacket is waterproof.
  3. Comets come from the outer edges of the solar system.

2. Farther from the center of a city or region. (отдалённый, окраинный)

  1. Rents are cheaper in the outer boroughs of New York.
  2. We live in one of the outer suburbs, an hour from downtown.

3. Seen from outside; showing on the surface rather than what someone really feels. (внешний (показной))

  1. Her outer calm hid how nervous she really was.
  2. Don't judge people by their outer appearance.

sole#

Pronunciation
/soʊl/ · SOHL
Русский
единственный; единоличный
Type
quantity
Position
attributive only — after a verb use the only one: the sole witness, but She was the only one who saw it
Comparison
not gradable
Contrast
Sole is more formal than only and needs the, a or a possessive before it: the sole reason, her sole goal. The noun sole is the bottom of a shoe or foot (подошва, ступня) and a flatfish (морской язык), and soul (душа) sounds exactly the same. The adverb is solely (solely responsible). Legal phrases: sole custody (опека одного родителя), sole proprietor (индивидуальный предприниматель).

Sole has 2 distinct senses.

1. Being the only one. (единственный)

  1. His sole purpose in coming was to see his daughter.
  2. The sole witness to the accident was a mail carrier.
  3. Money was not the sole reason she took the job.

2. Belonging to one person or group only; not shared. (единоличный, исключительный)

  1. After the divorce, she was granted sole custody of the kids.
  2. He is the sole owner of the business.
  3. The company has sole rights to sell the drug in the U.S.

downright#

Pronunciation
/ˈdaʊnraɪt/ · DOWN-ryt
Русский
явный, сущий, откровенный
Type
emphasizing
Position
attributive only — as an adjective only before a noun: a downright lie; before another adjective it is the adverb (downright rude)
Comparison
not gradable
Form
compound — down + right
Contrast
In American English downright is far more common as an adverb before an adjective: downright rude, downright dangerous, It was downright cold. As an adjective it goes only before a noun, usually a bad one: a downright lie, downright nonsense. The lie was downright is not English. The old sense 'blunt, frank' (a downright man) is now rare.

Complete and total, used to stress something bad.

  1. What he told the jury was a downright lie.
  2. Calling that car a bargain is downright nonsense.
  3. The way they treated the new hire was a downright disgrace.

entire#

Pronunciation
/ɪnˈtaɪər/ · in-TY-ur
Русский
весь, целый; полный
Type
quantity
Position
attributive only — after the, an, this or a possessive with a singular noun: the entire day; after a verb use whole or intact
Comparison
not gradable
Contrast
Russian весь is often all in English, but the patterns differ: all day or the entire day, not all the day or entire day. With a singular noun entire needs the, an, this or a possessive: the entire class, my entire life, an entire week. With a plural it means 'whole groups of' and can stand alone: Entire families lost their homes. After a verb use whole or intact: The vase was still whole; still entire is formal and rare. The adverb is entirely.

Entire has 2 distinct senses.

1. Whole; with no part left out. (весь, целый)

  1. We spent the entire day at the beach.
  2. I've lived in this town my entire life.
  3. Entire neighborhoods were flooded after the storm.

2. Complete in degree; total and without any doubt. (полный, безраздельный)

  1. The new baby has her entire attention these days.
  2. After the injury, he put his entire focus on getting well.

very#

Pronunciation
/ˈvɛri/ · VAIR-ee
Русский
тот самый; самый; само
Type
emphasizing
Position
attributive only — always after the, this, that or a possessive: the very moment, her very words
Comparison
not gradable — the old superlative veriest is archaic
Form
from Old French verai, 'true'
Contrast
The everyday very is the adverb 'очень' (very good). The adjective very stands right before a noun, always after the, this, that or a possessive, and means 'exactly this' or 'the extreme': the very day, the very top. Russian самый is the trap: самый конец is the very end, but самый красивый is the most beautiful, never the very beautiful. In the very best and the very first, very is the adverb again, strengthening best or first. The adjective can never follow a verb.

Very has 3 distinct senses.

1. Exactly this one and no other. (тот самый, именно этот)

  1. That's the very book I was looking for!
  2. At that very moment, the lights went out.
  3. They got married in the very church where they first met.

2. At the extreme point of something: right at the beginning, end, top or bottom. (самый (конец, верх))

  1. Read the note at the very bottom of the page.
  2. We stayed until the very end of the game.
  3. The problem was clear from the very beginning.

3. Just the thing itself, used to stress that it alone matters or has an effect. (один только; само)

  1. The very thought of public speaking makes me sweat.
  2. Budget cuts threaten the very existence of the program.
  3. The very idea of moving again made her tired.

live#

Pronunciation
/laɪv/ · LYV
Русский
живой; прямой (эфир); под напряжением; боевой
Type
classifying; also quality
Position
both — but in the sense 'not dead' only before a noun (a live lobster, but The lobster is still alive); and in real live ('real, in person'); live follows a verb in the senses 'not recorded', 'carrying current' and 'working' (The show is live, The wire is live, The site is live)
Comparison
not gradable
Opposite
recorded (of a performance); dead
Contrast
The adjective rhymes with five (laɪv); the verb live rhymes with give (lɪv). In the sense 'not dead', live goes only before a noun; after a verb use alive: They caught a live snake, but The snake was still alive, not still live. Living works in both positions (living things, Is he still living?). Live is also an adverb after verbs of broadcasting and performing: The game is shown live, She has never played live. Do not confuse it with lively (оживлённый, энергичный): a lively party is fun and busy; a live band plays in front of you.

Live has 8 distinct senses.

1. Happening in front of an audience, or broadcast while it happens, not recorded. (живой (о музыке); прямой (эфир))

  1. The bar has live music every Friday night.
  2. All the major networks carried the debate as a live broadcast.
  3. The show is live, so the hosts can't fix any mistakes.

2. Living; not dead. (живой)

  1. You can pick out a live lobster from the tank.
  2. The company says it does not test its products on live animals.
  3. He fishes with live bait.

3. Carrying electric current. (под напряжением)

  1. Don't touch that cable — it could still be live.
  2. The third rail is live, so stay off the subway tracks.

4. Able to explode or fire; not a blank or a dud. (боевой; неразорвавшийся)

  1. The soldiers trained with live ammunition for the first time.
  2. Construction workers dug up a live bomb from World War II.

5. Working and available for people to use, of a website, app or system. (запущенный, работающий)

  1. The new website goes live on Monday.
  2. The update is live for all users now.

6. Still burning or glowing. (горящий, тлеющий)

  1. Spread the live coals evenly under the grill.
  2. Never dump live ashes into a trash can.

7. Still being discussed and not yet settled; of current interest. (актуальный, нерешённый)

  1. Whether to build a new stadium is still a live issue in the city.
  2. A four-day workweek remains a live possibility at the company.

8. Real and in person, used in the informal phrase real live to show surprise or excitement. (настоящий, самый настоящий)

  1. The kids were thrilled to meet a real live astronaut.
  2. I'd never seen a real live movie star before.

Contrast
After a verb the word is drunk, never drunken: He was drunk, not He was drunken. Before a noun both occur, but they lean different ways: drunk is usual for people (a drunk driver; drunk driving is the everyday American term), while drunken is usual for actions and events (a drunken brawl, a drunken rant). A drunk is also a noun, a person who is often drunk (пьяница). The past participle of the verb is drunk: I have drunk, not I have drunken.

Drunken has 2 distinct senses.

1. Drunk, or often drunk, of a person. (пьяный; пьющий)

  1. A drunken fan ran onto the field in the ninth inning.
  2. The movie is about a drunken ex-cop trying to get sober.

2. Caused by or showing the effects of too much alcohol. (пьяный (о поведении), разгульный)

  1. Police broke up a drunken fight outside the bar.
  2. It was the most drunken party the fraternity had ever thrown.
  3. He left a drunken voicemail he regretted the next morning.

Contrast
Maiden as a noun (девушка, дева) is old-fashioned and poetic. The adjective lives on in fixed phrases: maiden voyage, maiden flight, maiden speech (the first of a kind) and maiden name (девичья фамилия). American forms often ask for last name at birth instead, and a man's earlier surname is never his maiden name. A maiden aunt (an older unmarried woman) sounds dated and can be unkind.

Maiden has 2 distinct senses.

1. First of its kind, used for a first trip, flight or speech. (первый)

  1. The Titanic sank on its maiden voyage in 1912.
  2. The new rocket completed its maiden flight without a problem.
  3. The senator gave her maiden speech on health care.

2. Relating to a woman before she married. (девичий)

  1. She kept her maiden name after the wedding.
  2. Your mother's maiden name is a common security question at banks.

Opposite
real, genuine
Contrast
The verb mock (насмехаться, передразнивать) is more common than the adjective: The other kids mocked his accent. The adjective always means 'not real'. A practice exam at school is a mock exam in British English but usually a practice test in American English; mock trial (a school competition that acts out a court case) and mock election are common in the US. A mock-up (noun) is a model of something that will be built.

Mock has 3 distinct senses.

1. Done as practice, as an imitation of a real event. (учебный, пробный)

  1. Our high school's mock trial team made it to the state finals.
  2. The career center runs mock interviews for seniors.
  3. The class held a mock election the week before the real one.

2. Pretended, not sincere, usually as a joke. (притворный, шутливый)

  1. She threw up her hands in mock horror.
  2. He raised an eyebrow in mock surprise when I showed up on time.

3. Made to look like something else. (имитирующий, под (что-то))

  1. He wore a black mock turtleneck under his blazer.
  2. Her grandmother's mock apple pie uses crackers instead of apples.
