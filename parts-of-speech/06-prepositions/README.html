Prepositions#

A preposition is a word (or fixed group of words) that links a noun, pronoun, or -ing form to the rest of the sentence and shows a relationship of time, place, direction, cause, manner, or a more abstract connection.

The lexicon — 1,297 entries across 13 categories, each with a complement line saying what may and may not follow it, numbered senses, a pattern, and the reduced pronunciation you actually hear — lives in the Preposition Dictionary. There is also an audio browser you can search and listen to.

1. What it does#

A preposition never works alone. It takes an object — almost always a noun phrase, a pronoun, or a gerund — and the two together form a prepositional phrase. That phrase then does a job in the sentence: it modifies a verb, a noun, an adjective, or a whole clause. The preposition itself is the piece that names the relationship; the object is the thing being related to.

Take a single sentence and change only the preposition, and the meaning of the whole event changes:

The cat slept on the box. (surface contact) The cat slept in the box. (enclosure) The cat slept under the box. (lower position) The cat slept beside the box. (adjacent)

The noun phrase the box is identical in all four. Only the preposition carries the spatial information. This is why prepositions are so hard to translate word-for-word: each language cuts up space, time, and cause differently.

Prepositional phrases attach to different hosts, and the host determines what the phrase means:

She waited at the corner. — attaches to the verb waited; tells us where. The house on the corner is for sale. — attaches to the noun house; tells us which house. I'm nervous about the test. — attaches to the adjective nervous; completes its meaning. In my opinion, the plan is too expensive. — attaches to the whole clause; frames the statement.

Many prepositions are also grammatically required rather than freely chosen. In depend on someone, afraid of spiders, or a reason for the delay, the preposition is fixed by the word in front of it. These are called dependent prepositions, and they have to be learned as part of the vocabulary item, not derived from a rule.

Annotated examples:

After the meeting, we walked to a diner on Third Street with two coworkers. — after = time; to = movement/goal; on = location (modifies diner); with = accompaniment.

The proposal was rejected by the committee because of a mistake in the budget. — by = agent (passive); because of = cause (a two-word complex preposition; the three-word equivalent is on account of); in = abstract containment.

2. Types#

Prepositions can be classified two ways: by meaning (what relationship they express) and by form (how many words they contain). Both classifications are standard, and both are given below. A single preposition usually belongs to several meaning groups — in is a preposition of time, place, and abstract relation all at once.

2.1 Prepositions of time#

Express when, how long, or in what temporal relation something happens.

Members
at, on, in, for, since, during, from, to, till, until, by, before, after, past, within, throughout, over, around, about, between, pending, prior to, subsequent to, up to, as of, ahead of, following.

The flight leaves at 6:15 on Thursday in October. We've lived here since 2019 — that's for almost seven years. Please submit the form by Friday, not on Friday.

2.2 Prepositions of place and position#

Express static location — where something is, without movement.

Members
at, in, on, above, below, over, under, underneath, beneath, beside, by, near, next to, between, among, amid, behind, in back of, in front of, opposite, across from, inside, outside, within, around, atop, alongside, against, upon, throughout.

The keys are on the counter, next to the mail. He stood between his sister and his cousin. There's a gas station across from the school and a diner behind it.

2.3 Prepositions of direction and movement#

Express a path, a source, or a destination. They typically follow verbs of motion.

Members
to, toward, into, onto, from, out of, off, off of (informal), up, down, across, through, along, around, past, over, under, by, via, away from, as far as, up to, down to.

She ran across the field and through the gate. The dog jumped off the couch and into my lap. We drove from Denver to Santa Fe via Taos.

Note the contrast between static in/on and dynamic into/onto: He was in the pool (position) vs. He jumped into the pool (movement).

2.4 Prepositions of agent#

Mark the doer of the action, chiefly in passive sentences and after certain nouns.

Members
by (the ordinary choice); marginally also at the hands of, through, courtesy of.

The novel was written by a former journalist. The bridge was designed by the same firm that built the stadium. The reforms were blocked by a single senator.

Do not confuse the agent by with the instrument with: The window was broken by a kid with a baseball.

2.5 Prepositions of instrument, means, and method#

Express what was used to accomplish something.

Members
with, by, through, via, using, by means of, by way of, on (for machines and media), in (for materials and languages).

He opened the crate with a crowbar. (physical tool) I paid by credit card and sent the receipt by email. (method — no article after "by") She reached me on my cell phone, then followed up through her assistant. The contract was written in Spanish in ink.

2.6 Prepositions of manner#

Express how something is done.

Members
with, without, in, by, like, as, after the manner of.

He answered with great confidence. They completed the job without any complaints. The children lined up in silence and left in a hurry.

2.7 Prepositions of cause, reason, and purpose#

Express why something happens, or what something is for.

Members
because of, due to, owing to, on account of, thanks to, for, from, out of, through, in view of, as a result of, for the sake of, in light of, for fear of.

The game was canceled because of lightning. She quit out of frustration, not for the money. Thanks to a last-minute donation, the clinic stayed open.

2.8 Prepositions of possession, belonging, and part-whole#

Express ownership, membership, or the relation of a part to a whole.

Members
of, with, to, belonging to, part of.

The roof of the barn needs repair. I met a man with a British accent. The key to the storage unit is missing.

2.9 Prepositions of source, origin, and material#

Express where something came from or what it is made of.

Members
from, of, out of, in, off.

This cheese is from Wisconsin. The table is made of oak. (material still recognizable) Paper is made from wood pulp. (material transformed) She carved a whistle out of a piece of pine.

2.10 Prepositions of measure, quantity, rate, and price#

Express amounts, ratios, and exchange values.

Members
by, at, for, per, to, within, over, under, up to, in excess of.

Eggs are sold by the dozen and gas by the gallon. The car gets thirty miles per gallon. I bought it for twenty dollars at a discount.

2.11 Prepositions of accompaniment and association#

Express who or what goes along with something.

Members
with, along with, together with, in addition to, as well as, accompanied by, plus.

She came with her brother along with two neighbors. The salary, together with the benefits, made the offer attractive.

Note: along with, together with, and as well as do not change the number of the verb — see §5.

2.12 Prepositions of support, opposition, and concession#

Express alignment for or against something, or a fact that fails to prevent something.

Members
for, against, with, contrary to, in favor of, in opposition to, versus, in spite of, despite, notwithstanding.

Most of the town voted for the bond measure; the mayor argued against it. Contrary to the forecast, it never rained. He went ahead despite the warnings. (never "despite of")

2.13 Prepositions of topic and reference#

Express what something is about.

Members
about, on, of, concerning, regarding, as to, as for, with regard to, with respect to, with reference to, in terms of, apropos of, re.

We spoke about the budget and argued over the timeline. She wrote a book on urban planning. ("on" suggests a more systematic treatment than "about") As for the deadline, nothing has changed.

2.14 Prepositions of exception, addition, and restriction#

Express what is left out or added in.

Members
but, except, except for, excepting, save, besides, apart from, aside from, other than, barring, excluding, including, in addition to, bar, short of.

Everyone except Marcus signed the letter. Nobody but you could have known that. Barring a miracle, we'll miss the deadline. The price is $40, including tax.

2.15 Prepositions of comparison and similarity#

Express resemblance, difference, or proportion.

Members
like, unlike, as, than, compared to, compared with, in comparison with, relative to, next to, versus.

She sings like her mother did. Unlike the earlier model, this one is waterproof. Compared to last year, sales are up.

2.16 Prepositions of role, capacity, and function#

Express the capacity in which someone or something acts. This is the specialized use of as.

Members
as, in the capacity of, by way of, for.

She works as a paralegal. He used a milk crate as a step stool. They hired me as a consultant, not as an employee.

2.17 Simple prepositions (one word, not derived)#

The core closed class. These are the highest-frequency prepositions in English, and in present-day English they are morphologically indivisible — whatever their distant history, no part of them is a live prefix or suffix.

Members
after, at, by, down, for, from, in, like, near, of, off, on, out, over, past, per, plus, since, than, through, till, to, under, up, via, with, as, but.

I heard of it from a friend at work. He walked past the bakery near the station.

2.18 Compound prepositions (one word, historically derived)#

Single words that were historically built from two elements — usually a particle or preposition plus a base — and are now written solid. They function exactly like simple prepositions.

Members
about, above, across, against, along, alongside, amid, among, around, atop, before, behind, below, beneath, beside, besides, between, beyond, despite, inside, into, notwithstanding, onto, outside, throughout, toward, underneath, until, upon, within, without.

The trail runs alongside the river and beneath the highway. Throughout the winter, the shop stayed open. He finished the report despite a power outage.

2.19 Complex (multi-word) prepositions#

Fixed sequences of two or three words that behave as one preposition. They cannot normally be broken up, and their internal articles are frozen.

Two-word
according to, ahead of, along with, apart from, as for, as of, aside from, away from, because of, close to, contrary to, devoid of, due to, except for, exclusive of, inside of, instead of, irrespective of, near to, next to, off of, other than, out of, outside of, owing to, prior to, pursuant to, rather than, regardless of, subsequent to, thanks to, together with, up to.
Three-word (and longer)
as far as, at the expense of, by means of, by virtue of, by way of, for the sake of, in accordance with, in addition to, in case of, in charge of, in comparison with, in exchange for, in favor of, in front of, in lieu of, in light of, in line with, in place of, in relation to, in return for, in search of, in spite of, in terms of, in the course of, in the event of, in the middle of, on account of, on behalf of, on top of, with reference to, with regard to, with respect to.

In spite of the rain, the parade went ahead of schedule. She signed on behalf of the entire department. In lieu of flowers, the family asked for donations.

2.20 Marginal and participial prepositions#

Words that began as verb participles or adjectives but now take an object like a preposition. They do not need a subject and do not agree with anything.

Members
barring, concerning, considering, counting, during, excepting, excluding, failing, following, given, granted, including, notwithstanding, pending, regarding, respecting, saving, touching, wanting.

The two-word participials — owing to, according to, pursuant to — belong here by origin but are listed with the complex prepositions in §2.19.

Considering her age, she recovered remarkably fast. Given the circumstances, the sentence was lenient. The decision is pending review by the board. Several people, including the director, objected.

2.21 Postpositions and deferred prepositions#

A small set of items follow their object instead of preceding it.

Members
ago, aside, apart, through (in "the whole night through"), notwithstanding, on (in "ten years on"), hence, over (in "the world over").

Three weeks ago we had no idea. (never "ago three weeks") Money aside, the plan is unworkable. The objections notwithstanding, the vote proceeded.

2.22 Dependent (bound) prepositions#

Prepositions selected by a specific verb, noun, or adjective rather than chosen for meaning. The choice is arbitrary and must be memorized. Full tables are in §5.

I depend on her. / I rely on her. / I count on her. (all three are fixed with "on") He's good at math but bad at spelling and interested in history. There's no need for panic and no excuse for rudeness.

3. Forms and inflection#

Prepositions do not inflect. They have no plural, no tense, no person, no gender, and no case. The one item that looks like an exception is near, which doubles as an adjective and so turns up as nearer and nearest (She moved nearer the fire); no other preposition has a comparative or superlative. In is in in every sentence it ever appears in. This makes prepositions morphologically the simplest word class in English and grammatically one of the hardest, because all of the difficulty is in which one to pick, not in what shape to give it.

Three things do change shape around prepositions, and learners must control all three.

3.1 The object of a preposition takes the objective case#

Pronouns after a preposition must be in the objective (accusative) form.

Subject formObjective form (used after a preposition)
Ime
youyou
hehim
sheher
itit
weus
theythem
whowhom (formal)

Between you and me, the plan is doomed. (not "between you and I") This package is for her and them. To whom should I address the letter? (formal; informally: "Who should I address it to?")

3.2 The object of a preposition can be a gerund, never a bare infinitive#

If a verb follows a preposition, it must be the -ing form.

PatternExample
preposition + gerundShe apologized for being late.
preposition + gerundI'm tired of waiting.
preposition + gerundHe left without saying goodbye.
✗ preposition + infinitive✗ He left without to say goodbye.

This trips up learners with the word to, which is both a preposition and an infinitive marker. When to is a preposition, a gerund follows:

to = infinitive marker (+ base verb)to = preposition (+ -ing)
I want to go.I look forward to going.
I need to swim.I'm used to swimming.
I decided to move.I object to moving.
I hope to work here.I'm committed to working here.
I plan to travel.I'm accustomed to traveling.

Test: replace the verb with a noun. If it fits (I look forward to the trip), to is a preposition and takes -ing.

3.3 Prepositions can be modified by intensifying adverbs#

Although they don't inflect, prepositions accept degree/emphasis modifiers before them — this is the closest thing they have to gradation.

ModifierExample

3.4 Related-form pairs to keep distinct#

Some prepositions have a look-alike that is written as two words or belongs to another class. The spelling difference carries a meaning difference.

3.5 A note on reduced spoken forms#

In casual American speech, of frequently reduces and fuses: kind of → "kinda," sort of → "sorta," out of → "outta," a lot of → "a lotta." Unstressed to behaves the same way, though there it is usually the infinitive marker rather than the preposition: want to → "wanna," going to → "gonna," have to → "hafta." These are pronunciation facts, not spellings. Never write them in formal English.

4. Position and word order#

4.1 Basic position: before the object#

The default and overwhelmingly common position is immediately before the object. Nothing normally comes between a preposition and its object except modifiers of the object itself.

in the old blue house — preposition + determiner + adjectives + noun for three very long hours

4.2 Where the whole prepositional phrase goes#

A prepositional phrase can occupy several slots:

FunctionPositionExample
Verb modifier (adverbial)after the verb (and its object)She parked behind the store.
Noun modifierimmediately after the nounThe man in the gray coat left.
Adjective complementafter the adjectiveI'm allergic to shellfish.
Verb complementdirectly after the verb, before other elementsWe looked at the map carefully.
Sentence adverbialfront or end, set off by a comma at frontIn my view, it's a mistake.
Subject complementafter beThe meeting is at noon.

Noun-modifying phrases must sit next to the noun they modify or the sentence changes meaning:

I saw the woman with the telescope. — ambiguous: she had it, or I used it. With the telescope, I saw the woman. — unambiguous: I used it.

4.3 Order of multiple prepositional phrases#

When several adverbial phrases follow a verb, American English strongly prefers manner – place – time:

He spoke calmly (manner) in the courtroom (place) on Tuesday (time). ✗ He spoke on Tuesday in the courtroom calmly.

Two other ordering pressures apply:

  1. Shorter before longer. She waited at the gate for what felt like an hour.
  2. Tighter before looser. A phrase required by the verb comes before an optional one: Put the box on the shelf after dinner.

Time phrases can also be fronted for emphasis or discourse flow, with a comma:

On Tuesday, he spoke calmly in the courtroom.

4.4 Direction before or after other complements#

With verbs that take both an object and a direction, the object comes first:

She threw the ball over the fence. (not "threw over the fence the ball") They moved the piano into the living room.

4.5 Preposition stranding#

A stranded preposition is one left at the end of a clause, separated from its object. This is normal, correct, and extremely common in American English. The alternative — moving the preposition to the front along with its object — is called pied-piping and is more formal.

ContextStranded (normal/informal)Pied-piped (formal)
Wh-questionWho did you talk to?To whom did you talk?
Relative clauseThe house I grew up in is gone.The house in which I grew up is gone.
PassiveThis bed has been slept in.(no pied-piped version)
Infinitive clauseI need a pen to write with.(no natural pied-piped version)
ExclamationWhat a mess he got us into!(no natural pied-piped version)
Adjective + infinitiveHe's easy to work with.(no pied-piped version)

Stranding is obligatory in several structures — pied-piping is simply impossible:

The person that I spoke to was helpful. — ✗ The person to that I spoke... The person I spoke to was helpful. (zero relative) — ✗ The person to I spoke... The proposal was laughed at. (prepositional passive) — ✗ At the proposal was laughed. That's the noise I can't put up with. (phrasal-prepositional verb) — ✗ the noise with which I can't put up

Pied-piping, on the other hand, is obligatory in partitive and genitive relative clauses, where the preposition cannot be left behind:

Three of the applicants, all of whom had degrees, were interviewed. — ✗ all whom had degrees of The building, the roof of which had collapsed, was condemned. — ✗ the roof which had collapsed of

The "rule" against ending a sentence with a preposition is not a rule of English. It was imported from Latin in the 17th century and has never described actual usage. In formal academic and legal writing, pied-piping is often preferred for tone; everywhere else, stranding is standard.

4.6 Fronting for contrast#

A prepositional phrase can be moved to the front for emphasis or contrast, sometimes triggering subject-verb inversion:

Under the porch slept three raccoons. (inversion after a fronted place phrase) On the table she left a note.

5. Rules and patterns#

5.1 A preposition requires an object#

A preposition is transitive by definition. If there is no object — stated or recoverable — the word is functioning as an adverb particle, not a preposition.

He climbed up the ladder. (preposition; object = the ladder) He stood up. (adverb; no object)

5.2 What can serve as the object#

He ran up the hill. — up = preposition (path). ✗ He ran the hill up. ✓ He ran up it. He ran up a huge bill. — up = particle. ✓ He ran a huge bill up. ✓ He ran it up.

Phrasal-prepositional verbs have both: verb + particle + preposition. The preposition's object never moves.

We've run out of milk. I can't put up with the noise. She looks forward to the weekend. He got away with it.

Separable vs. inseparable — the pronoun test. If a pronoun object can go between the verb and the second word, that word is a particle and the verb is separable.

5.11 Agreement is unaffected by prepositional phrases#

The verb agrees with the head noun of the subject, never with a noun inside a following prepositional phrase.

The box of chocolates is on the table. (not "are") A list of the winners was posted. The manager, along with two assistants, was fired. (the phrases "along with," "together with," "as well as," and "in addition to" do not create a plural subject)

Exception: with of-quantifiers, the verb agrees with the object of of.

Half of the pie is gone. / Half of the cookies are gone. A lot of money was spent. / A lot of people were waiting. None of the water is clean. / None of the students are ready. (both accepted in AE)

5.12 One preposition per object#

Do not double up prepositions with the same object.

✗ In which city do you live in? → ✓ Which city do you live in? or ✓ In which city do you live? ✗ Where are you at? → ✓ Where are you? ("where" already contains the locative meaning)

Legitimate stacked prepositions govern different objects and are fine: from behind the fence, until after dinner, out from under the table.

6. Common mistakes#

  • ✗ I'm going to home. → ✓ I'm going home. — Home, like downtown and abroad, is an adverb of place after motion verbs and takes no preposition.
  • ✗ We discussed about the merger. → ✓ We discussed the merger. — Discuss is transitive; about belongs with talk, not discuss.
  • ✗ Between you and I, he's lying. → ✓ Between you and me, he's lying. — The object of a preposition takes the objective case, even inside a compound.
  • ✗ She's married with a doctor. → ✓ She's married to a doctor. — Married to names the spouse; married with two kids means she has children.
  • ✗ Despite of the traffic, we made it. → ✓ Despite the traffic, we made it. — Despite never takes of. The of-form is in spite of.
  • ✗ I'm looking forward to meet you. → ✓ I'm looking forward to meeting you. — Here to is a preposition, so it requires a gerund.
  • ✗ He arrived to the station at noon. → ✓ He arrived at the station at noon. — Arrive takes at for points and in for cities/countries, never to.
  • ✗ I've lived here since three years. → ✓ I've lived here for three years. — For + a length of time; since + a starting point (since 2023).
  • ✗ Where do you live in? → ✓ Where do you live? — Where already contains the locative meaning; adding in doubles it.
  • ✗ She depends of her parents. → ✓ She depends on her parents. — Depend is a fixed on-verb; no meaning-based reasoning will get you there.
  • ✗ We'll meet in Monday. → ✓ We'll meet on Monday. — Days take on; months and years take in.
  • ✗ He explained me the rules. → ✓ He explained the rules to me. — Explain, describe, suggest, mention, and say require to before the recipient and cannot take a bare indirect object.
  • ✗ The book was written from a Frenchman. → ✓ The book was written by a Frenchman. — Agents in passives take by; from would mean source.
  • ✗ I'm interested about photography. → ✓ I'm interested in photography. — Interested in is fixed; interesting has no preposition at all in that slot.
  • ✗ Please fill this form. → ✓ Please fill out this form. — Fill out (AE) is the phrasal verb for completing a document; bare fill means to make full.
  • ✗ We were in the bus when it happened. → ✓ We were on the bus when it happened. — Public transportation you can stand up in takes on: bus, train, plane, ship.
  • ✗ He's good in math. → ✓ He's good at math. — Skill and ability take at.
  • ✗ She entered into the room. → ✓ She entered the room. — Enter is transitive in its literal sense; enter into is reserved for abstracts (enter into an agreement).
  • ✗ I congratulated her for the promotion. → ✓ I congratulated her on the promotion. — Congratulate on; contrast thank someone for.
  • ✗ The reason of the delay was weather. → ✓ The reason for the delay was weather. — Reason for (or the reason that…), never reason of.

7.4 American colloquialisms to recognize but avoid in formal writing#

  • off of — He fell off of the ladder. Standard AE speech; edit to off in formal prose.
  • outside of — Outside of Chicago, nobody cares. Very common in AE; outside alone is tighter.
  • on line vs. in line — New York City and immediate area say wait on line; the rest of the country says wait in line.
  • could care less — an idiomatic (illogical) AE variant of couldn't care less. Recognize it; don't write it.
  • based off of — increasingly common in speech; based on remains the standard form.

7.5 Where the varieties agree#

Stranding, complex prepositions, the in/on/at system for time and place, dependent prepositions with common verbs, and phrasal-verb structure are essentially identical in the two varieties. The differences above are a short list against a very large shared system — do not assume a difference exists just because a phrase sounds unusual.

8.3 Like vs. as#

Like is a preposition (+ noun phrase); as introduces a clause or names a role.

He swims like a fish. (comparison, + NP) Do as I say, not as I do. (+ clause) She works as an engineer. (role — she is one) She works like an engineer. (comparison — she may not be one)

In informal American speech like is widely used as a conjunction (It looks like it's going to rain). This is normal spoken usage, but formal writing prefers as if or as though.

8.5 Between vs. among#

The old rule ("between for two, among for more") is only half right. Use between whenever the relationship is individual and reciprocal, no matter how many items; use among for undifferentiated groups.

The treaty between the five nations took years. (each nation to each other — correct) He was among friends. (an undifferentiated group) Divide the money between the three of them. (each gets a share — standard AE)

8.6 Beside vs. besides#

Sit beside me. (next to) Besides the cost, there's the time. (in addition to / apart from)

8.7 In vs. within, by vs. until#

I'll finish in an hour. (at the end of the hour) I'll finish within an hour. (sometime before the hour is up) Finish it by five. (deadline — the action happens once, at or before five) Keep working until five. (continuous action ending at five)

8.8 Ago, before, and for in time reference#

He left three days ago. (counting back from now — requires past simple) He had left three days before. (counting back from a past point) ✗ He has left three days ago. — ago is incompatible with the present perfect.

8.9 Frozen complex prepositions vs. literal phrases#

The article inside a complex preposition is fixed, and adding or removing it changes it back into a literal noun phrase.

Complex prepositionLiteral phrase
in light of the new data (considering)in the light of the lamp (illumination)
in front of the storein the front of the store (the store's front section)
on top of everything elseon the top of the shelf
in case of firein the case of Smith v. Jones

8.10 Words that look like prepositions but are not#

  • Adverb particles in phrasal verbs: give up, pass out, take off. No object, no phrase.
  • Infinitive to: to swim, to leave. Followed by a base verb, not a noun.
  • Ago: a postposition/adverb — it follows its measure phrase instead of preceding a noun.
  • Subordinating conjunctions: because, although, if, unless, whereas. They take clauses.
  • Adjectival participles: the man standing there is a reduced relative, not a preposition, even though considering and including in the same shape are prepositions.

8.11 Dangling participial prepositions#

Considering, regarding, and given are true prepositions and need no subject. But related -ing forms that are still verbs do, and mixing them up produces a dangler.

✓ Considering the weather, the turnout was good. (preposition — fine) ✗ Walking to work, the rain started. → ✓ Walking to work, I got caught in the rain. (participle needs its subject)

8.12 Whom after prepositions#

Whom is required immediately after a preposition in formal writing. When the preposition is stranded, who is normal in all but the most formal registers.

To whom it may concern. (fixed formula) Who did you give it to? (standard informal AE) ✗ To who it may concern.

10. Practice#

A. Fill in the blank with the correct preposition.

  1. The concert starts ______ 8:00 ______ Saturday ______ March.
  2. She's been working here ______ 2021, which is ______ about five years now.
  3. I'm not very good ______ chess, but I'm interested ______ learning.
  4. Please fill ______ the form and hand it ______ to the receptionist.
  5. ______ the heavy traffic, we arrived ______ time for the ceremony.

B. Identify the type. For each bolded phrase, name the relationship it expresses (time, place, movement, agent, instrument, cause, topic, exception, comparison, or role).

  1. The report was reviewed by the auditor.
  2. He fixed the leak with a wrench.
  3. Everyone except the intern stayed late.
  4. She was hired as a translator.
  5. The plant died because of the frost.

C. Correct the error.

  1. I'm looking forward to hear from you.
  2. Despite of the rain, the game continued.
  3. We discussed about the new policy for an hour.
  4. He arrived to Boston on Monday morning.
  5. Between you and I, this contract is a disaster.

D. Particle or preposition? For each bolded word, decide whether it is a particle (fused to the verb) or a preposition (heading a phrase with the following noun). Apply the pronoun test from §5.10.

  1. She looked up the word in the dictionary.
  2. She walked up the stairs.
  3. They put off the decision until June.
  4. He jumped off the diving board.
  5. We ran into our neighbors at the airport.
Answers

A. Fill in the blank

  1. at 8:00 on Saturday in March — at for clock times, on for days, in for months. The precision ladder runs at → on → in.
  2. since 2021, for about five years — since marks a starting point; for marks a duration.
  3. good at chess, interested in learning — both are dependent prepositions fixed by the adjective; at for skills, in after interested. Note the gerund learning after the preposition in.
  4. fill out the form, hand it in — fill out is the American phrasal verb for completing a document; hand in is separable, so the pronoun it must come before the particle.
  5. Despite the heavy traffic, in time — despite takes no of; in time means "soon enough," while on time would mean "exactly at the scheduled moment."

B. Identify the type

  1. Agent — by marks the doer of the action in a passive sentence.
  2. Instrument — with names the tool used. Compare the agent by.
  3. Exception — except removes a member from an otherwise complete set.
  4. Role / capacity — as names the function someone serves in. Like a translator would mean she isn't one.
  5. Cause — because of is a two-word complex preposition taking a noun phrase; the conjunction because would require a clause.

C. Correct the error

  1. ✓ I'm looking forward to hearing from you. — In look forward to, the to is a preposition, so it takes a gerund, not an infinitive. Test: I look forward to the meeting works, so to is a preposition.
  2. ✓ Despite the rain, the game continued. — Despite never takes of. The alternative with of is in spite of.
  3. ✓ We discussed the new policy for an hour. — Discuss is a transitive verb and takes a direct object with no preposition. Talk about is the phrase that needs about.
  4. ✓ He arrived in Boston on Monday morning. — Arrive takes in for cities and countries, at for specific points (buildings, stations), and never to.
  5. ✓ Between you and me, this contract is a disaster. — The object of a preposition takes the objective case. Removing the compound makes it obvious: between me, never between I.

D. Particle or preposition?

  1. Particle — look up means "consult a reference," and the object can move around it: look the word up, look it up. The stress falls on up.
  2. Preposition — up the stairs is a path phrase. The object cannot move (✗ walked the stairs up), and the pronoun stays after the preposition: walked up them.
  3. Particle — put off means "postpone," and it is separable: put the decision off, put it off.
  4. Preposition — off the diving board names the path: jumped off it, never ✗ jumped the board off.
  5. Preposition — run into ("meet by chance") is a prepositional verb, so it is idiomatic but still inseparable: ran into them, never ✗ ran them into.
