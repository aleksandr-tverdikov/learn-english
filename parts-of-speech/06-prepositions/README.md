# Prepositions

> A preposition is a word (or fixed group of words) that links a noun, pronoun, or -ing form to the rest of the sentence and shows a relationship of time, place, direction, cause, manner, or a more abstract connection.

> **📖 Looking for a specific preposition?** This file covers the *grammar*. The *lexicon* — **[1,297 entries](catalog/README.md)** across 13 categories, each with a **complement** line saying what may and may not follow it, numbered senses, a pattern, and the reduced pronunciation you actually hear — lives in the **[Preposition Dictionary](catalog/README.md)**. There is also an **[audio browser](catalog/browse.html)** you can search and listen to.

## 1. What it does

A preposition never works alone. It takes an object — almost always a noun phrase, a pronoun, or a gerund — and the two together form a **prepositional phrase**. That phrase then does a job in the sentence: it modifies a verb, a noun, an adjective, or a whole clause. The preposition itself is the piece that names the relationship; the object is the thing being related to.

Take a single sentence and change only the preposition, and the meaning of the whole event changes:

> The cat slept **on** the box. *(surface contact)*
> The cat slept **in** the box. *(enclosure)*
> The cat slept **under** the box. *(lower position)*
> The cat slept **beside** the box. *(adjacent)*

The noun phrase *the box* is identical in all four. Only the preposition carries the spatial information. This is why prepositions are so hard to translate word-for-word: each language cuts up space, time, and cause differently.

Prepositional phrases attach to different hosts, and the host determines what the phrase means:

> She waited **at the corner**. — attaches to the verb *waited*; tells us where.
> The house **on the corner** is for sale. — attaches to the noun *house*; tells us which house.
> I'm nervous **about the test**. — attaches to the adjective *nervous*; completes its meaning.
> **In my opinion**, the plan is too expensive. — attaches to the whole clause; frames the statement.

Many prepositions are also grammatically required rather than freely chosen. In *depend **on** someone*, *afraid **of** spiders*, or *a reason **for** the delay*, the preposition is fixed by the word in front of it. These are called **dependent prepositions**, and they have to be learned as part of the vocabulary item, not derived from a rule.

Annotated examples:

> **After** the meeting, we walked **to** a diner **on** Third Street **with** two coworkers.
> — *after* = time; *to* = movement/goal; *on* = location (modifies *diner*); *with* = accompaniment.

> The proposal was rejected **by** the committee **because of** a mistake **in** the budget.
> — *by* = agent (passive); *because of* = cause (a two-word complex preposition; the three-word equivalent is *on account of*); *in* = abstract containment.

## 2. Types

Prepositions can be classified two ways: by **meaning** (what relationship they express) and by **form** (how many words they contain). Both classifications are standard, and both are given below. A single preposition usually belongs to several meaning groups — *in* is a preposition of time, place, and abstract relation all at once.

### 2.1 Prepositions of time

Express when, how long, or in what temporal relation something happens.

**Members:** at, on, in, for, since, during, from, to, till, until, by, before, after, past, within, throughout, over, around, about, between, pending, prior to, subsequent to, up to, as of, ahead of, following.

> The flight leaves **at** 6:15 **on** Thursday **in** October.
> We've lived here **since** 2019 — that's **for** almost seven years.
> Please submit the form **by** Friday, not **on** Friday.

### 2.2 Prepositions of place and position

Express static location — where something is, without movement.

**Members:** at, in, on, above, below, over, under, underneath, beneath, beside, by, near, next to, between, among, amid, behind, in back of, in front of, opposite, across from, inside, outside, within, around, atop, alongside, against, upon, throughout.

> The keys are **on** the counter, **next to** the mail.
> He stood **between** his sister and his cousin.
> There's a gas station **across from** the school and a diner **behind** it.

### 2.3 Prepositions of direction and movement

Express a path, a source, or a destination. They typically follow verbs of motion.

**Members:** to, toward, into, onto, from, out of, off, off of *(informal)*, up, down, across, through, along, around, past, over, under, by, via, away from, as far as, up to, down to.

> She ran **across** the field and **through** the gate.
> The dog jumped **off** the couch and **into** my lap.
> We drove **from** Denver **to** Santa Fe **via** Taos.

Note the contrast between static *in/on* and dynamic *into/onto*: *He was **in** the pool* (position) vs. *He jumped **into** the pool* (movement).

### 2.4 Prepositions of agent

Mark the doer of the action, chiefly in passive sentences and after certain nouns.

**Members:** by *(the ordinary choice)*; marginally also at the hands of, through, courtesy of.

> The novel was written **by** a former journalist.
> The bridge was designed **by** the same firm that built the stadium.
> The reforms were blocked **by** a single senator.

Do not confuse the agent *by* with the instrument *with*: *The window was broken **by** a kid **with** a baseball.*

### 2.5 Prepositions of instrument, means, and method

Express what was used to accomplish something.

**Members:** with, by, through, via, using, by means of, by way of, on *(for machines and media)*, in *(for materials and languages)*.

> He opened the crate **with** a crowbar. *(physical tool)*
> I paid **by** credit card and sent the receipt **by** email. *(method — no article after "by")*
> She reached me **on** my cell phone, then followed up **through** her assistant.
> The contract was written **in** Spanish **in** ink.

### 2.6 Prepositions of manner

Express how something is done.

**Members:** with, without, in, by, like, as, after the manner of.

> He answered **with** great confidence.
> They completed the job **without** any complaints.
> The children lined up **in** silence and left **in** a hurry.

### 2.7 Prepositions of cause, reason, and purpose

Express why something happens, or what something is for.

**Members:** because of, due to, owing to, on account of, thanks to, for, from, out of, through, in view of, as a result of, for the sake of, in light of, for fear of.

> The game was canceled **because of** lightning.
> She quit **out of** frustration, not **for** the money.
> **Thanks to** a last-minute donation, the clinic stayed open.

### 2.8 Prepositions of possession, belonging, and part-whole

Express ownership, membership, or the relation of a part to a whole.

**Members:** of, with, to, belonging to, part of.

> The roof **of** the barn needs repair.
> I met a man **with** a British accent.
> The key **to** the storage unit is missing.

### 2.9 Prepositions of source, origin, and material

Express where something came from or what it is made of.

**Members:** from, of, out of, in, off.

> This cheese is **from** Wisconsin.
> The table is made **of** oak. *(material still recognizable)*
> Paper is made **from** wood pulp. *(material transformed)*
> She carved a whistle **out of** a piece of pine.

### 2.10 Prepositions of measure, quantity, rate, and price

Express amounts, ratios, and exchange values.

**Members:** by, at, for, per, to, within, over, under, up to, in excess of.

> Eggs are sold **by** the dozen and gas **by** the gallon.
> The car gets thirty miles **per** gallon.
> I bought it **for** twenty dollars **at** a discount.

### 2.11 Prepositions of accompaniment and association

Express who or what goes along with something.

**Members:** with, along with, together with, in addition to, as well as, accompanied by, plus.

> She came **with** her brother **along with** two neighbors.
> The salary, **together with** the benefits, made the offer attractive.

Note: *along with*, *together with*, and *as well as* do **not** change the number of the verb — see §5.

### 2.12 Prepositions of support, opposition, and concession

Express alignment for or against something, or a fact that fails to prevent something.

**Members:** for, against, with, contrary to, in favor of, in opposition to, versus, in spite of, despite, notwithstanding.

> Most of the town voted **for** the bond measure; the mayor argued **against** it.
> **Contrary to** the forecast, it never rained.
> He went ahead **despite** the warnings. *(never "despite of")*

### 2.13 Prepositions of topic and reference

Express what something is about.

**Members:** about, on, of, concerning, regarding, as to, as for, with regard to, with respect to, with reference to, in terms of, apropos of, re.

> We spoke **about** the budget and argued **over** the timeline.
> She wrote a book **on** urban planning. *("on" suggests a more systematic treatment than "about")*
> **As for** the deadline, nothing has changed.

### 2.14 Prepositions of exception, addition, and restriction

Express what is left out or added in.

**Members:** but, except, except for, excepting, save, besides, apart from, aside from, other than, barring, excluding, including, in addition to, bar, short of.

> Everyone **except** Marcus signed the letter.
> Nobody **but** you could have known that.
> **Barring** a miracle, we'll miss the deadline.
> The price is $40, **including** tax.

### 2.15 Prepositions of comparison and similarity

Express resemblance, difference, or proportion.

**Members:** like, unlike, as, than, compared to, compared with, in comparison with, relative to, next to, versus.

> She sings **like** her mother did.
> **Unlike** the earlier model, this one is waterproof.
> **Compared to** last year, sales are up.

### 2.16 Prepositions of role, capacity, and function

Express the capacity in which someone or something acts. This is the specialized use of *as*.

**Members:** as, in the capacity of, by way of, for.

> She works **as** a paralegal.
> He used a milk crate **as** a step stool.
> They hired me **as** a consultant, not **as** an employee.

### 2.17 Simple prepositions (one word, not derived)

The core closed class. These are the highest-frequency prepositions in English, and in present-day English they are morphologically indivisible — whatever their distant history, no part of them is a live prefix or suffix.

**Members:** after, at, by, down, for, from, in, like, near, of, off, on, out, over, past, per, plus, since, than, through, till, to, under, up, via, with, as, but.

> I heard **of** it **from** a friend **at** work.
> He walked **past** the bakery **near** the station.

### 2.18 Compound prepositions (one word, historically derived)

Single words that were historically built from two elements — usually a particle or preposition plus a base — and are now written solid. They function exactly like simple prepositions.

**Members:** about, above, across, against, along, alongside, amid, among, around, atop, before, behind, below, beneath, beside, besides, between, beyond, despite, inside, into, notwithstanding, onto, outside, throughout, toward, underneath, until, upon, within, without.

> The trail runs **alongside** the river and **beneath** the highway.
> **Throughout** the winter, the shop stayed open.
> He finished the report **despite** a power outage.

### 2.19 Complex (multi-word) prepositions

Fixed sequences of two or three words that behave as one preposition. They cannot normally be broken up, and their internal articles are frozen.

**Two-word:** according to, ahead of, along with, apart from, as for, as of, aside from, away from, because of, close to, contrary to, devoid of, due to, except for, exclusive of, inside of, instead of, irrespective of, near to, next to, off of, other than, out of, outside of, owing to, prior to, pursuant to, rather than, regardless of, subsequent to, thanks to, together with, up to.

**Three-word (and longer):** as far as, at the expense of, by means of, by virtue of, by way of, for the sake of, in accordance with, in addition to, in case of, in charge of, in comparison with, in exchange for, in favor of, in front of, in lieu of, in light of, in line with, in place of, in relation to, in return for, in search of, in spite of, in terms of, in the course of, in the event of, in the middle of, on account of, on behalf of, on top of, with reference to, with regard to, with respect to.

> **In spite of** the rain, the parade went **ahead of** schedule.
> She signed **on behalf of** the entire department.
> **In lieu of** flowers, the family asked for donations.

### 2.20 Marginal and participial prepositions

Words that began as verb participles or adjectives but now take an object like a preposition. They do not need a subject and do not agree with anything.

**Members:** barring, concerning, considering, counting, during, excepting, excluding, failing, following, given, granted, including, notwithstanding, pending, regarding, respecting, saving, touching, wanting.

The two-word participials — *owing to*, *according to*, *pursuant to* — belong here by origin but are listed with the complex prepositions in §2.19.

> **Considering** her age, she recovered remarkably fast.
> **Given** the circumstances, the sentence was lenient.
> The decision is **pending** review by the board.
> Several people, **including** the director, objected.

### 2.21 Postpositions and deferred prepositions

A small set of items follow their object instead of preceding it.

**Members:** ago, aside, apart, through *(in "the whole night through")*, notwithstanding, on *(in "ten years on")*, hence, over *(in "the world over")*.

> Three weeks **ago** we had no idea. *(never "ago three weeks")*
> Money **aside**, the plan is unworkable.
> The objections **notwithstanding**, the vote proceeded.

### 2.22 Dependent (bound) prepositions

Prepositions selected by a specific verb, noun, or adjective rather than chosen for meaning. The choice is arbitrary and must be memorized. Full tables are in §5.

> I **depend on** her. / I **rely on** her. / I **count on** her. *(all three are fixed with "on")*
> He's **good at** math but **bad at** spelling and **interested in** history.
> There's no **need for** panic and no **excuse for** rudeness.

## 3. Forms and inflection

**Prepositions do not inflect.** They have no plural, no tense, no person, no gender, and no case. The one item that looks like an exception is *near*, which doubles as an adjective and so turns up as *nearer* and *nearest* (*She moved nearer the fire*); no other preposition has a comparative or superlative. *In* is *in* in every sentence it ever appears in. This makes prepositions morphologically the simplest word class in English and grammatically one of the hardest, because all of the difficulty is in *which one to pick*, not in *what shape to give it*.

Three things do change shape around prepositions, and learners must control all three.

### 3.1 The object of a preposition takes the objective case

Pronouns after a preposition must be in the objective (accusative) form.

| Subject form | Objective form (used after a preposition) |
|---|---|
| I | me |
| you | you |
| he | him |
| she | her |
| it | it |
| we | us |
| they | them |
| who | whom *(formal)* |

> Between you and **me**, the plan is doomed. *(not "between you and I")*
> This package is for **her** and **them**.
> To **whom** should I address the letter? *(formal; informally: "Who should I address it to?")*

### 3.2 The object of a preposition can be a gerund, never a bare infinitive

If a verb follows a preposition, it must be the **-ing** form.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| preposition + gerund | She apologized for **being** late. |
| preposition + gerund | I'm tired of **waiting**. |
| preposition + gerund | He left without **saying** goodbye. |
| ✗ preposition + infinitive | ✗ *He left without to say goodbye.* |

This trips up learners with the word *to*, which is both a preposition and an infinitive marker. When *to* is a preposition, a gerund follows:

| *to* = infinitive marker (+ base verb) | *to* = preposition (+ -ing) |
|---|---|
| I want **to go**. | I look forward **to going**. |
| I need **to swim**. | I'm used **to swimming**. |
| I decided **to move**. | I object **to moving**. |
| I hope **to work** here. | I'm committed **to working** here. |
| I plan **to travel**. | I'm accustomed **to traveling**. |

Test: replace the verb with a noun. If *it* fits (*I look forward to **the trip***), *to* is a preposition and takes *-ing*.

### 3.3 Prepositions can be modified by intensifying adverbs

Although they don't inflect, prepositions accept degree/emphasis modifiers before them — this is the closest thing they have to gradation.

| Modifier | Example |
|---|---|
| right | The mailbox is **right** in front of the door. |
| straight | She walked **straight** through the lobby. |
| just | We arrived **just** after midnight. |
| well | The bill came in **well** under budget. |
| all | Books were scattered **all** over the floor. |
| directly | The office is **directly** above ours. |
| far | He lives **far** from downtown. |

### 3.4 Related-form pairs to keep distinct

Some prepositions have a look-alike that is written as two words or belongs to another class. The spelling difference carries a meaning difference.

| One word (preposition) | Two words (other structure) |
|---|---|
| She walked **into** the room. | She turned her homework **in to** the teacher. *(particle + preposition)* |
| The cat climbed **onto** the roof. | Hold **on to** the railing. *(particle + preposition)* |
| We drove **around** the block. | Turn the car **around**. *(adverb particle)* |
| I've been **inside** the building. | Please step **inside**. *(adverb)* |
| Look **up** the chimney. *(preposition)* | Look **up** the word. *(particle)* |
| The decision rests **upon** the vote. | He climbed **up on** the counter. *(particle + preposition)* |

### 3.5 A note on reduced spoken forms

In casual American speech, *of* frequently reduces and fuses: *kind of* → "kinda," *sort of* → "sorta," *out of* → "outta," *a lot of* → "a lotta." Unstressed *to* behaves the same way, though there it is usually the infinitive marker rather than the preposition: *want to* → "wanna," *going to* → "gonna," *have to* → "hafta." These are pronunciation facts, not spellings. Never write them in formal English.

## 4. Position and word order

### 4.1 Basic position: before the object

The default and overwhelmingly common position is immediately before the object. Nothing normally comes between a preposition and its object except modifiers of the object itself.

> **in** the old blue house — preposition + determiner + adjectives + noun
> **for** three very long hours

### 4.2 Where the whole prepositional phrase goes

A prepositional phrase can occupy several slots:

| Function | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Verb modifier (adverbial) | after the verb (and its object) | She parked **behind the store**. |
| Noun modifier | immediately after the noun | The man **in the gray coat** left. |
| Adjective complement | after the adjective | I'm allergic **to shellfish**. |
| Verb complement | directly after the verb, before other elements | We looked **at the map** carefully. |
| Sentence adverbial | front or end, set off by a comma at front | **In my view**, it's a mistake. |
| Subject complement | after *be* | The meeting is **at noon**. |

Noun-modifying phrases must sit next to the noun they modify or the sentence changes meaning:

> I saw the woman **with the telescope**. — ambiguous: she had it, or I used it.
> **With the telescope**, I saw the woman. — unambiguous: I used it.

### 4.3 Order of multiple prepositional phrases

When several adverbial phrases follow a verb, American English strongly prefers **manner – place – time**:

> He spoke **calmly** *(manner)* **in the courtroom** *(place)* **on Tuesday** *(time)*.
> ✗ *He spoke on Tuesday in the courtroom calmly.*

Two other ordering pressures apply:

1. **Shorter before longer.** *She waited **at the gate** **for what felt like an hour**.*
2. **Tighter before looser.** A phrase required by the verb comes before an optional one: *Put the box **on the shelf** **after dinner**.*

Time phrases can also be fronted for emphasis or discourse flow, with a comma:

> **On Tuesday**, he spoke calmly in the courtroom.

### 4.4 Direction before or after other complements

With verbs that take both an object and a direction, the object comes first:

> She threw the ball **over the fence**. *(not "threw over the fence the ball")*
> They moved the piano **into the living room**.

### 4.5 Preposition stranding

A stranded preposition is one left at the end of a clause, separated from its object. This is normal, correct, and extremely common in American English. The alternative — moving the preposition to the front along with its object — is called **pied-piping** and is more formal.

| Context | Stranded (normal/informal) | Pied-piped (formal) |
|---|---|---|
| *Wh*-question | Who did you talk **to**? | **To** whom did you talk? |
| Relative clause | The house I grew up **in** is gone. | The house **in** which I grew up is gone. |
| Passive | This bed has been slept **in**. | *(no pied-piped version)* |
| Infinitive clause | I need a pen to write **with**. | *(no natural pied-piped version)* |
| Exclamation | What a mess he got us **into**! | *(no natural pied-piped version)* |
| Adjective + infinitive | He's easy to work **with**. | *(no pied-piped version)* |

Stranding is **obligatory** in several structures — pied-piping is simply impossible:

> The person **that** I spoke **to** was helpful. — ✗ *The person to that I spoke...*
> The person I spoke **to** was helpful. *(zero relative)* — ✗ *The person to I spoke...*
> The proposal was laughed **at**. *(prepositional passive)* — ✗ *At the proposal was laughed.*
> That's the noise I can't **put up with**. *(phrasal-prepositional verb)* — ✗ *the noise with which I can't put up*

Pied-piping, on the other hand, is **obligatory** in partitive and genitive relative clauses, where the preposition cannot be left behind:

> Three of the applicants, **all of whom** had degrees, were interviewed. — ✗ *all whom had degrees of*
> The building, **the roof of which** had collapsed, was condemned. — ✗ *the roof which had collapsed of*

**The "rule" against ending a sentence with a preposition is not a rule of English.** It was imported from Latin in the 17th century and has never described actual usage. In formal academic and legal writing, pied-piping is often preferred for tone; everywhere else, stranding is standard.

### 4.6 Fronting for contrast

A prepositional phrase can be moved to the front for emphasis or contrast, sometimes triggering subject-verb inversion:

> **Under the porch** slept three raccoons. *(inversion after a fronted place phrase)*
> **On the table** she left a note.

## 5. Rules and patterns

### 5.1 A preposition requires an object

A preposition is transitive by definition. If there is no object — stated or recoverable — the word is functioning as an adverb particle, not a preposition.

> He climbed **up the ladder**. *(preposition; object = the ladder)*
> He stood **up**. *(adverb; no object)*

### 5.2 What can serve as the object

| Object type | Example |
|---|---|
| Noun phrase | on **the counter** |
| Pronoun (objective) | with **them** |
| Gerund | after **eating** |
| *Wh*-clause | It depends on **what she says**. |
| Noun clause with *the fact that* | despite **the fact that he apologized** |
| Another prepositional phrase | from **behind the curtain**; until **after lunch** |
| Adverb of time/place | since **yesterday**; until **now**; from **here** |
| Adjective (fixed idiom only) | in **short**; in **general**; for **free**; at **best** |

Note that a *that*-clause cannot be the direct object of a preposition. You must insert *the fact*:

> ✗ *I'm surprised at that he left.* → ✓ *I'm surprised **that** he left.* or ✓ *I'm surprised **at the fact that** he left.*

### 5.3 The in / on / at contrast — TIME

| Preposition | Use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | clock times, precise points, mealtimes, festivals treated as points, *night*, *noon*, *midnight* | at 7:30, at dawn, at lunch, at Thanksgiving, at night, at that moment |
| **on** | days, dates, days + parts of day, specific occasions | on Monday, on July 4th, on my birthday, on Friday morning, on the weekend |
| **in** | months, seasons, years, decades, centuries, long periods, parts of the day | in June, in the summer, in 1998, in the 1980s, in the Middle Ages, in the morning |

**Boundary cases to memorize:**

| Expression | Preposition | Note |
|---|---|---|
| in the morning / afternoon / evening | **in** | general parts of day |
| at night | **at** | irregular — *not* "in the night" for the general sense |
| on Monday morning | **on** | *on* wins when a day is specified |
| at Christmas *(the season)* / on Christmas Day | at / on | period vs. single day |
| in the past / in the future | **in** | |
| at the moment / at present | **at** | |
| ∅ next week, last year, this morning, every day, tomorrow, yesterday | **no preposition** | ✗ *in next week* |

**Duration and boundaries:**

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| for | length of a period | We waited **for** two hours. |
| since | starting point of a period continuing now | We've waited **since** noon. |
| during | within the span of a named period | She called **during** the meeting. |
| in | after the end of a period *(future)*; within *(completion)* | I'll be back **in** ten minutes. He finished **in** an hour. |
| by | not later than *(deadline)* | Submit it **by** Friday. |
| until / till | continuing up to a point | The store is open **until** nine. |
| from … to / through | span with both ends | We're open Monday **through** Friday. |
| within | before the end of | Reply **within** 24 hours. |

### 5.4 The in / on / at contrast — PLACE

| Preposition | Core idea | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | a point, an address, an institution seen as a function | at the corner, at 400 Elm Street, at the door, at work, at school, at the airport, at a concert |
| **on** | a surface, a line, a floor of a building, a medium | on the table, on the wall, on Main Street, on the coast, on the third floor, on TV, on the bus |
| **in** | an enclosed or bounded space, a large area | in the box, in the kitchen, in Chicago, in Texas, in the car, in the water, in a book |

**Contrasting pairs:**

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| at the store / in the store | doing an errand / physically inside |
| at the corner / on the corner / in the corner | meeting point / a building's location / inside angle of a room |
| in the picture / on the picture | depicted in it / stuck on its surface |
| at school / in school | at the location / enrolled as a student |
| in bed / on the bed | under the covers, sleeping / on the surface |
| on the train, bus, plane, ship | public transport — always *on* |
| in the car, taxi, boat *(small)* | small private vehicle — always *in* |
| in the hospital / at the hospital | a patient / visiting or working |

**Address specificity ladder:** the smaller and more precise the location, the more likely *at*.

> She lives **in** the United States → **in** Ohio → **in** Columbus → **on** Bryden Road → **at** 1215 Bryden Road.

### 5.5 Zero preposition

Some place and time words reject prepositions entirely.

| Word type | Examples |
|---|---|
| *home*, *downtown*, *uptown*, *abroad*, *overseas*, *upstairs*, *downstairs*, *inside*, *outside* | Go **home**. She moved **downtown**. He studied **abroad**. — ✗ *go to home* |
| *next / last / this / every* + time noun | See you **next Friday**. — ✗ *on next Friday* |
| *yesterday*, *today*, *tomorrow*, *tonight* | It happened **yesterday**. |
| Days of the week, in casual American English | I'll call you **Monday**. *(AE often drops the "on")* |

### 5.6 Verbs that take a direct object and no preposition

These verbs are transitive in English even though their equivalents in many languages require a preposition.

| Correct | Common error |
|---|---|
| discuss the plan | ✗ discuss about the plan |
| enter the building | ✗ enter into the building *(literal sense)* |
| marry someone | ✗ marry with someone |
| approach the desk | ✗ approach to the desk |
| answer the question | ✗ answer to the question |
| lack experience | ✗ lack of experience *(as a verb)* |
| resemble her mother | ✗ resemble to her mother |
| contact / call / phone / email me | ✗ contact with me |
| attend the meeting | ✗ attend to the meeting *(different meaning)* |
| join the club | ✗ join to the club |
| reach the summit | ✗ reach to the summit |

### 5.10 Prepositions vs. particles in phrasal verbs

Two-word verbs come in two structurally different kinds, and they behave differently. A **particle** is an adverb fused to the verb's meaning; a **preposition** heads a phrase and takes an object.

| Property | Particle (*phrasal verb*) | Preposition (*prepositional verb*) |
|---|---|---|
| Example | turn **off** the light | look **into** the problem |
| Object can move before it | ✓ turn the light **off** | ✗ *look the problem into* |
| Pronoun object goes | before it: turn it **off** | after it: look **into** it |
| Stress | stressed: *turn it OFF* | unstressed: *LOOK into it* |
| Adverb can precede it | ✗ *turn right off the light* | ✓ ran **right up** the hill |
| Can be fronted with *wh-* | ✗ *Off which did you turn?* | ✓ **Into which** problem did they look? |
| Passive | ✓ The light was turned **off**. | ✓ The problem was looked **into**. *(prepositional passive)* |

**The minimal pair to remember:**

> He ran **up** the hill. — *up* = preposition (path). ✗ *He ran the hill up.* ✓ *He ran up it.*
> He ran **up** a huge bill. — *up* = particle. ✓ *He ran a huge bill up.* ✓ *He ran it up.*

**Phrasal-prepositional verbs** have both: verb + particle + preposition. The preposition's object never moves.

> We've **run out of** milk.
> I can't **put up with** the noise.
> She **looks forward to** the weekend.
> He **got away with** it.

**Separable vs. inseparable — the pronoun test.** If a pronoun object can go between the verb and the second word, that word is a particle and the verb is separable.

| Separable (particle) | Inseparable (preposition) |
|---|---|
| pick **up** → pick her up | look **after** → look after her ✗ *look her after* |
| fill **out** → fill it out | get **over** → get over it |
| put **on** → put them on | run **into** → run into him |
| throw **away** → throw it away | deal **with** → deal with it |
| call **off** → call it off | care **for** → care for them |

### 5.11 Agreement is unaffected by prepositional phrases

The verb agrees with the head noun of the subject, never with a noun inside a following prepositional phrase.

> The **box** of chocolates **is** on the table. *(not "are")*
> A **list** of the winners **was** posted.
> The **manager**, along with two assistants, **was** fired. *(the phrases "along with," "together with," "as well as," and "in addition to" do not create a plural subject)*

Exception: with *of*-quantifiers, the verb agrees with the object of *of*.

> **Half of** the pie **is** gone. / **Half of** the cookies **are** gone.
> **A lot of** money **was** spent. / **A lot of** people **were** waiting.
> **None of** the water **is** clean. / **None of** the students **are** ready. *(both accepted in AE)*

### 5.12 One preposition per object

Do not double up prepositions with the same object.

> ✗ *In which city do you live in?* → ✓ *Which city do you live **in**?* or ✓ ***In** which city do you live?*
> ✗ *Where are you at?* → ✓ *Where are you?* *("where" already contains the locative meaning)*

Legitimate stacked prepositions govern different objects and are fine: *from **behind** the fence*, *until **after** dinner*, *out **from under** the table*.

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *I'm going to home.* → ✓ *I'm going home.* — *Home*, like *downtown* and *abroad*, is an adverb of place after motion verbs and takes no preposition.
- ✗ *We discussed about the merger.* → ✓ *We discussed the merger.* — *Discuss* is transitive; *about* belongs with *talk*, not *discuss*.
- ✗ *Between you and I, he's lying.* → ✓ *Between you and **me**, he's lying.* — The object of a preposition takes the objective case, even inside a compound.
- ✗ *She's married with a doctor.* → ✓ *She's married **to** a doctor.* — *Married to* names the spouse; *married with two kids* means she has children.
- ✗ *Despite of the traffic, we made it.* → ✓ ***Despite** the traffic, we made it.* — *Despite* never takes *of*. The *of*-form is *in spite of*.
- ✗ *I'm looking forward to meet you.* → ✓ *I'm looking forward **to meeting** you.* — Here *to* is a preposition, so it requires a gerund.
- ✗ *He arrived to the station at noon.* → ✓ *He arrived **at** the station at noon.* — *Arrive* takes *at* for points and *in* for cities/countries, never *to*.
- ✗ *I've lived here since three years.* → ✓ *I've lived here **for** three years.* — *For* + a length of time; *since* + a starting point (*since 2023*).
- ✗ *Where do you live in?* → ✓ *Where do you live?* — *Where* already contains the locative meaning; adding *in* doubles it.
- ✗ *She depends of her parents.* → ✓ *She depends **on** her parents.* — *Depend* is a fixed *on*-verb; no meaning-based reasoning will get you there.
- ✗ *We'll meet in Monday.* → ✓ *We'll meet **on** Monday.* — Days take *on*; months and years take *in*.
- ✗ *He explained me the rules.* → ✓ *He explained the rules **to** me.* — *Explain*, *describe*, *suggest*, *mention*, and *say* require *to* before the recipient and cannot take a bare indirect object.
- ✗ *The book was written from a Frenchman.* → ✓ *The book was written **by** a Frenchman.* — Agents in passives take *by*; *from* would mean source.
- ✗ *I'm interested about photography.* → ✓ *I'm interested **in** photography.* — *Interested in* is fixed; *interesting* has no preposition at all in that slot.
- ✗ *Please fill this form.* → ✓ *Please fill **out** this form.* — *Fill out* (AE) is the phrasal verb for completing a document; bare *fill* means to make full.
- ✗ *We were in the bus when it happened.* → ✓ *We were **on** the bus when it happened.* — Public transportation you can stand up in takes *on*: bus, train, plane, ship.
- ✗ *He's good in math.* → ✓ *He's good **at** math.* — Skill and ability take *at*.
- ✗ *She entered into the room.* → ✓ *She **entered** the room.* — *Enter* is transitive in its literal sense; *enter into* is reserved for abstracts (*enter into an agreement*).
- ✗ *I congratulated her for the promotion.* → ✓ *I congratulated her **on** the promotion.* — *Congratulate on*; contrast *thank someone for*.
- ✗ *The reason of the delay was weather.* → ✓ *The reason **for** the delay was weather.* — *Reason for* (or *the reason that…*), never *reason of*.

## 7. American English notes

Prepositions are one of the areas where American and British English genuinely diverge, and the differences are systematic enough to list. Everything below is a documented difference rather than an invented one; where the split is a matter of frequency rather than of grammar, it is labeled as such.

### 7.1 Different preposition choices

| Context | American English | British English |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday and Sunday | **on** the weekend | **at** the weekend |
| Team membership | She's **on** the team. | She's **in** the team. |
| Being a patient | He's **in the hospital**. | He's **in hospital**. *(no article)* |
| Being enrolled | My son is **in school**. | My son is **at school**. |
| Address | I live **on** Maple Street. | I live **in** Maple Street. |
| Date range | Monday **through** Friday | Monday **to** Friday |
| Correspondence | Write **me** when you land. | Write **to** me when you land. |
| Comparison | different **from** / different **than** | different **from** / different **to** |
| Location behind | **in back of** the house | **behind** the house *("in back of" is not used)* |
| Serving a group | The menu caters **to** vegetarians. | The menu caters **for** vegetarians. |
| Naming | The park is named **for** a senator. *(distinctively AE; "named after" is equally standard here)* | The park is named **after** a senator. *("named for" is not used)* |
| Protest | They protested **the ruling**. *(transitive)* | They protested **against** the ruling. |
| Meeting | I'll meet **with** the client. | I'll meet the client. |
| Exclusion | **aside from** the cost *(frequency preference; "apart from" is also common in AE)* | **apart from** the cost |
| Documents | fill **out** a form | fill **in** a form |
| Being on a street | The kids play **on** the street. | The kids play **in** the street. |

### 7.2 Telling time

American English uses *after* and *of* alongside the shared *past* and *to*:

| Time | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| 10:15 | a quarter **after** ten / a quarter **past** ten | a quarter **past** ten |
| 9:45 | a quarter **of** ten / a quarter **to** ten / a quarter **till** ten *(regional)* | a quarter **to** ten |

### 7.3 Spelling and form preferences

| American | British | Note |
|---|---|---|
| toward | towards | Both exist in both varieties; AE strongly prefers the short form. |
| among | amongst | *Amongst* is rare and slightly archaic in AE. |
| amid | amidst | Same pattern. |
| while *(conj.)* | whilst | *Whilst* is essentially nonexistent in AE. |
| forward | forwards | AE prefers *forward* as both adverb and adjective. |

### 7.4 American colloquialisms to recognize but avoid in formal writing

- **off of** — *He fell off of the ladder.* Standard AE speech; edit to *off* in formal prose.
- **outside of** — *Outside of Chicago, nobody cares.* Very common in AE; *outside* alone is tighter.
- **on line** vs. **in line** — New York City and immediate area say *wait on line*; the rest of the country says *wait in line*.
- **could care less** — an idiomatic (illogical) AE variant of *couldn't care less*. Recognize it; don't write it.
- **based off of** — increasingly common in speech; *based on* remains the standard form.

### 7.5 Where the varieties agree

Stranding, complex prepositions, the *in/on/at* system for time and place, dependent prepositions with common verbs, and phrasal-verb structure are essentially identical in the two varieties. The differences above are a short list against a very large shared system — do not assume a difference exists just because a phrase sounds unusual.

## 8. Tricky cases

### 8.1 The same word as preposition, adverb, and conjunction

Many high-frequency words change class depending on what follows them. **A preposition has a noun-phrase object; a conjunction has a clause; an adverb has nothing.**

| Word | Preposition (+ NP) | Conjunction (+ clause) | Adverb (nothing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| before | **before** the war | **before** he arrived | I'd seen it **before**. |
| after | **after** lunch | **after** she left | He came in soon **after**. |
| since | **since** Monday | **since** you asked | I haven't seen her **since**. |
| until | **until** noon | **until** it stops | — |
| as | **as** a teacher | **as** I was leaving | — |
| than | taller **than** me | taller **than** I am | — |
| but | everyone **but** me | I tried, **but** I failed | — |
| in / out / up / down | **in** the box | — | Come **in**. |

### 8.2 *Because* vs. *because of*, and friends

*Because* is a conjunction and takes a clause. *Because of* is a preposition and takes a noun phrase. The same split applies to several pairs.

| + clause (conjunction) | + noun phrase (preposition) |
|---|---|
| **because** it rained | **because of** the rain |
| **although** he was tired | **despite** his fatigue |
| **while** we waited | **during** the wait |
| **if** it rains | **in case of** rain |
| **when** the show ended | **at** the end of the show |

### 8.3 *Like* vs. *as*

*Like* is a preposition (+ noun phrase); *as* introduces a clause or names a role.

> He swims **like** a fish. *(comparison, + NP)*
> Do **as** I say, not **as** I do. *(+ clause)*
> She works **as** an engineer. *(role — she is one)*
> She works **like** an engineer. *(comparison — she may not be one)*

In informal American speech *like* is widely used as a conjunction (*It looks like it's going to rain*). This is normal spoken usage, but formal writing prefers *as if* or *as though*.

### 8.4 Minimal pairs where the preposition changes the meaning

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| **in time** / **on time** | soon enough / exactly at the scheduled moment |
| **at the end** / **in the end** | at the final point of something / ultimately, finally |
| **in the way** / **on the way** | blocking / en route |
| **agree with** a person / **agree to** a proposal / **agree on** a plan | share an opinion / consent / reach a joint decision |
| **think of** / **think about** | recall or have an opinion / consider at length |
| **look at** / **look for** / **look after** / **look into** | direct your eyes / search / take care of / investigate |
| **wait for** / **wait on** | expect someone / serve someone (AE also = wait for, informal) |
| **made of** / **made from** / **made with** | material visible / material transformed / ingredient among others |
| **die of** / **die from** | disease / injury or external cause *(a traditional split; in practice both are widely used for either)* |
| **shout at** / **shout to** | in anger / to be heard at a distance |
| **talk to** / **talk with** | one-directional / mutual (AE prefers *with* for conversations) |
| **compare to** / **compare with** | liken, point out a resemblance / set side by side to weigh similarities and differences |
| **anxious about** / **anxious for** | worried / eager |
| **result from** / **result in** | be caused by / cause |
| **consist of** / **consist in** | be composed of / have its essence in |

### 8.5 *Between* vs. *among*

The old rule ("*between* for two, *among* for more") is only half right. Use **between** whenever the relationship is individual and reciprocal, no matter how many items; use **among** for undifferentiated groups.

> The treaty **between** the five nations took years. *(each nation to each other — correct)*
> He was **among** friends. *(an undifferentiated group)*
> Divide the money **between** the three of them. *(each gets a share — standard AE)*

### 8.6 *Beside* vs. *besides*

> Sit **beside** me. *(next to)*
> **Besides** the cost, there's the time. *(in addition to / apart from)*

### 8.7 *In* vs. *within*, *by* vs. *until*

> I'll finish **in** an hour. *(at the end of the hour)*
> I'll finish **within** an hour. *(sometime before the hour is up)*
> Finish it **by** five. *(deadline — the action happens once, at or before five)*
> Keep working **until** five. *(continuous action ending at five)*

### 8.8 *Ago*, *before*, and *for* in time reference

> He left three days **ago**. *(counting back from now — requires past simple)*
> He had left three days **before**. *(counting back from a past point)*
> ✗ *He has left three days ago.* — *ago* is incompatible with the present perfect.

### 8.9 Frozen complex prepositions vs. literal phrases

The article inside a complex preposition is fixed, and adding or removing it changes it back into a literal noun phrase.

| Complex preposition | Literal phrase |
|---|---|
| **in light of** the new data *(considering)* | **in the light of** the lamp *(illumination)* |
| **in front of** the store | **in the front of** the store *(the store's front section)* |
| **on top of** everything else | **on the top of** the shelf |
| **in case of** fire | **in the case of** Smith v. Jones |

### 8.10 Words that look like prepositions but are not

- **Adverb particles** in phrasal verbs: *give up*, *pass out*, *take off*. No object, no phrase.
- **Infinitive *to***: *to swim*, *to leave*. Followed by a base verb, not a noun.
- ***Ago***: a postposition/adverb — it follows its measure phrase instead of preceding a noun.
- **Subordinating conjunctions**: *because*, *although*, *if*, *unless*, *whereas*. They take clauses.
- **Adjectival participles**: *the man **standing** there* is a reduced relative, not a preposition, even though *considering* and *including* in the same shape are prepositions.

### 8.11 Dangling participial prepositions

*Considering*, *regarding*, and *given* are true prepositions and need no subject. But related -ing forms that are still verbs do, and mixing them up produces a dangler.

> ✓ **Considering** the weather, the turnout was good. *(preposition — fine)*
> ✗ *Walking to work, the rain started.* → ✓ *Walking to work, I got caught in the rain.* *(participle needs its subject)*

### 8.12 *Whom* after prepositions

*Whom* is required immediately after a preposition in formal writing. When the preposition is stranded, *who* is normal in all but the most formal registers.

> **To whom** it may concern. *(fixed formula)*
> Who did you give it **to**? *(standard informal AE)*
> ✗ *To who it may concern.*

15. ✓ *Between you and **me**, this contract is a disaster.* — The object of a preposition takes the objective case. Removing the compound makes it obvious: *between me*, never *between I*.

**D. Particle or preposition?**

16. **Particle** — *look up* means "consult a reference," and the object can move around it: *look the word up*, *look it up*. The stress falls on *up*.
17. **Preposition** — *up the stairs* is a path phrase. The object cannot move (✗ *walked the stairs up*), and the pronoun stays after the preposition: *walked up them*.
18. **Particle** — *put off* means "postpone," and it is separable: *put the decision off*, *put it off*.
19. **Preposition** — *off the diving board* names the path: *jumped off it*, never ✗ *jumped the board off*.
20. **Preposition** — *run into* ("meet by chance") is a prepositional verb, so it is idiomatic but still inseparable: *ran into them*, never ✗ *ran them into*.

</details>
