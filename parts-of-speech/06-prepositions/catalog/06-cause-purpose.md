# Cause, reason, purpose, and result

> The prepositions that answer *why* — what made it happen, what somebody was aiming at, and what came of it.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Everything in this file links two things by a *why* relation, and the relation runs in one of three directions: backward to a **cause** (*because of the fog*), forward to a **purpose** (*with a view to selling*), or outward to a **result** (*as a result of the crash*). Almost all of these words are complex prepositions — two, three, or four words welded into a single unit — because English ran out of simple ones for this job centuries ago and started building.

Two traps run through the whole group. The first is grammatical and very nearly absolute: **no preposition in this file can take a *that*-clause.** Every one of them has a clause-taking twin built with *that* or *the fact that*, and that twin is a conjunction — *because of the rain* but *because it rained*, *on the grounds of fraud* but *on the grounds that he lied*. Exactly one word in the file takes a finite clause at all, and it takes only a **wh-clause**: *a dispute over whether the vote was binding*, *confusion over how the money was spent*. Even *over* refuses *that*. The second trap belongs to the purpose phrases, which split into two lists that never mix: *in order to*, *so as to*, *in an effort to*, and *with intent to* take a bare infinitive, while *with a view to*, *for the purpose of*, *with an eye to*, *with the aim of*, and *in the hope of* take a gerund. ***With a view to reduce costs* is the error this file exists to prevent.**

Six of the nine core prepositions do heavy causal work — *for*, *from*, *of*, *with*, *by*, and *to* — but their entries live in [the core relational prepositions](02-core-relational.md) and [the core: in, on, at](01-core-in-on-at.md). What they do in this field is gathered in a table at the end.

**A note on the pronunciations below.** Nearly every phrase in this file ends in *of* or *to* or begins with *for*, *in*, or *with*, and in connected speech those function words reduce hard. Each entry gives the citation form first and the weak form after it, but the reductions are the same everywhere: *of* /əv/ → /ə/ (*because of* → /bɪˈkəzə/, *in light of* → /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/), *to* /tu/ → /tə/ (*due to*, *owing to*, *with a view to*), *for* /fɔr/ → /fɚ/ or /fər/ (*for the sake of* → /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/), *at* /æt/ → /ət/, *from* /frʌm/ → /frəm/ (*arising from*, *resulting from*), and *with* /wɪθ, wɪð/ → /wəθ, wəð/ (*what with*, *with an eye to*). The strong forms come back only under stress or at the end of a phrase — *What did you do it out **of**?* keeps /ʌv/.


## Cause and reason

The words that point backward to what produced the event. They differ almost entirely in register and in what kind of cause they will accept: *because of* takes anything, *out of* takes only a motive inside the actor, *through* takes only an abstract failing, *on the grounds of* takes only a stated justification, *on the pretext of* takes a stated justification the writer is calling a lie, *attributable to* takes a measured share of an effect and assigns it, and *thanks to* comes with an attitude attached. Entries are alphabetical.

### attributable to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tu/ &middot; weak /əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tə/ &middot; *uh-TRIB-yuh-tuh-buhl too*
**Type:** complex (adjective plus *to*) &middot; **Register:** formal — audits, reports, epidemiology, and the courts
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*attributable to having used last year's figures*). **Never a finite clause** — *attributable to the fact that* is the clause form, and it is clumsy enough that *because* is nearly always better.
**Pattern:** [noun] *is attributable to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *attributable to* [noun phrase] &middot; *directly / largely / wholly / partly / in part attributable to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** This is the phrase the whole *due to* rule is built on: the traditional test for *due to* is whether *attributable to* can be swapped in, and where it can, *due to* is safe under every analysis — see [the *due to* rule](#the-due-to-vs-because-of-rule) at the end of this file. Like adjectival *due to*, it follows *be* or postmodifies a noun and **does not front**: *Attributable to fog, the flight was canceled* is not English, where *Due to fog, …* at least gets argued about. Against *because of*, it is cooler and more quantitative, and it is the word of choice wherever a **share** of an effect is being assigned rather than a whole one.

Assigns an effect, or a measured portion of one, to a cause. The object is the cause and the noun in front of the phrase is the effect, which puts it on the same side of the direction trap as *as a result of*.

1. Most of the decline is *attributable to* a single bad quarter.
2. Roughly a third of the increase is *attributable to* population growth alone.
3. Deaths *attributable to* secondhand smoke have fallen by half since 1990.
4. The cost overrun is *attributable to* delays nobody could have foreseen.
5. Losses *attributable to* the outage were covered under the policy.
6. The discrepancy is *attributable to* having used last year's figures.
7. Only a small part of the warming is *attributable to* natural variation.

**See also:** [due to](#due-to), [because of](#because-of), [resulting from](#resulting-from), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [consequent on](#consequent-on)

### because of

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkɔz əv/ or /bɪˈkʌz əv/ &middot; weak /bɪˈkəz əv/ &middot; casual /ˈkʌzə/ (*'cause of*) &middot; *bih-KAWZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — the default causal preposition of American English
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*because of being late*), or a fused relative, which is still a noun phrase (*because of who he knows*, *because of what she said*). **Never a finite clause and never a *that*-clause.** The clause form is the conjunction *because* standing alone — *because it rained*, never *because of it rained* and never *because of that it rained*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *because of* [noun phrase] &middot; *Because of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *because of* [verb-ing] &middot; *partly / largely / mainly / only / precisely because of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Because* + clause against *because of* + noun phrase is the cleanest preposition/conjunction pair in the language: *We stayed home because it rained* / *We stayed home because of the rain*. Against *due to*, *because of* is adverbial by nature and no editor has ever objected to it, which makes it the safe choice whenever you are unsure — see [the *due to* rule](#the-due-to-vs-because-of-rule) at the end of this file. Against *owing to* and *on account of*, it is the plain, unmarked word. The internet construction *because science*, *because reasons* puts a bare noun after *because* alone; it is a deliberate joke and is not standard English.

Names the cause of whatever the main clause reports. It modifies the verb or the entire clause, and that adverbial job is exactly what the traditional usage rule reserved for it and denied to *due to*. It is neutral about whether the cause is welcome, which is what separates it from *thanks to* (grateful or sarcastic), *courtesy of* (wry), and *owing to* (formal).

1. The flight was canceled *because of* fog.
2. *Because of* the strike, deliveries are running two days late.
3. She missed the deadline *because of* a family emergency.
4. He got the job *because of* who he knows, not what he knows.
5. *Because of* being out of the country all spring, he never received the notice.
6. Attendance is down, mostly *because of* the parking.
7. We're only here *because of* you.

**See also:** [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [attributable to](#attributable-to), [because of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#because-of), [because (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/04-cause.md#because)

### by reason of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈrizən əv/ &middot; weak /bə ˈrizən ə/ &middot; *by-REE-zuhn uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal and formal — outside statutes and pleadings it sounds stilted
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically an abstract one (*by reason of insanity*, *by reason of age*). A gerund is possible (*by reason of having served twenty years*) but uncommon. **Never a finite clause** — the clausal form is the archaic *by reason that*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by reason of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *by reason of* [noun phrase] &middot; *not guilty by reason of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Because of* means the same thing and is shorter. *By reason of* survives because legal drafting is conservative and because a handful of fixed phrases have hardened around it — *not guilty by reason of insanity*, *disqualified by reason of age*. Do not confuse it with [*by virtue of*](#by-virtue-of), which names a qualification that **enables** something rather than a cause that produces it: you hold the seat *by virtue of* your office and you lose it *by reason of* your conviction.

The formal, chiefly legal way of naming a cause or a ground. In modern American writing it appears almost entirely in statutes, insurance policies, and court filings, plus the set phrases those documents have pushed out into general use.

1. He was found not guilty *by reason of* insanity.
2. No applicant may be refused *by reason of* race, religion, or national origin.
3. The lease terminates *by reason of* the tenant's default.
4. Benefits were denied *by reason of* his failure to file on time.
5. She was excused from jury service *by reason of* ill health.
6. Any loss *by reason of* fire is covered under Section 4.

**See also:** [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [because of](#because-of), [on the grounds of](#on-the-grounds-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [in consequence of](#in-consequence-of)

### by virtue of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu əv/ &middot; weak /bə ˈvɜrtʃu ə/ &middot; *by-VUR-choo uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*by virtue of being the oldest*). **Never a finite clause** — for a clause you need *by virtue of the fact that*, which is heavy enough that *because* is almost always the better answer.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by virtue of* [noun phrase] &middot; *By virtue of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *by virtue of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *By virtue of* names a **qualification** — a status, office, property, or fact that entitles or enables — where *because of* names a plain cause. *He got in by virtue of his last name* says the name functioned as a credential; *he got in because of his last name* says only that it was the reason. Against *by dint of*, which names effort rather than standing: *by dint of hard work*, but *by virtue of seniority*. *Virtue* here carries its old sense of "power, efficacy," not moral goodness.

Names the status, office, property, or established fact that entitles or enables the main event — the cause understood as a qualification rather than as a force.

1. *By virtue of* her office, the vice president presides over the Senate.
2. He inherited the title *by virtue of* being the eldest son.
3. The clause is enforceable *by virtue of* Section 12 of the agreement.
4. *By virtue of* its size alone, the company sets the price for the whole region.
5. She had access to the files *by virtue of* her security clearance.
6. Sound travels faster through steel *by virtue of* its density.

**See also:** [by reason of](#by-reason-of), [on the strength of](#on-the-strength-of), [because of](#because-of), [by dint of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-dint-of), [by virtue of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#by-virtue-of)

### courtesy of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɜrtəsi əv/ &middot; weak /ˈkɜrtəsiə/ &middot; *KUR-tuh-see uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, and heavily journalistic — captions, credit lines, sportswriting
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a person, organization, or thing. No gerund, and **never a finite clause**. In photo credits it also appears bare, with the *of* dropped: *photo courtesy the Newberry Library*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *courtesy of* [noun phrase] &middot; *Courtesy of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; (credit line) *Photograph courtesy of* [name] &middot; *by courtesy of* [noun phrase] (older, more formal)
**Contrast:** *Thanks to* with the credit spelled out. Both can be sincere or sarcastic, but *courtesy of* leans harder on agency — the thing named actually **supplied** the result — and it is far more common at the end of a sentence, tacked on as an afterthought, than at the front.

**1. Supplied by; with the permission or generosity of.** The literal credit line of captions and acknowledgments.
**2. Caused by, with a wry tip of the hat.** The news and sports use, where the cause is as often unwelcome as welcome.

1. Photograph *courtesy of* the National Archives.
2. Everyone got a ride home, *courtesy of* her uncle's van.
3. The map is reproduced *courtesy of* the state historical society.
4. The Rangers won it in overtime, *courtesy of* a deflection off a defenseman's skate.
5. Half of downtown was dry by noon, *courtesy of* a burst main on Sixth Street.
6. He spent the afternoon in the emergency room, *courtesy of* a ladder he should have thrown out years ago.
7. Video *courtesy of* KTLA; used with permission.
8. The lights came back on a little after four, *courtesy of* a crew that had been out since dawn.

**See also:** [thanks to](#thanks-to), [because of](#because-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [courtesy of (conjunctions catalog)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/04-cause.md#courtesy-of)

### due to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdu tu/ &middot; weak /ˈdu tə/ &middot; *DOO too* &middot; (British /ˈdjuː/, *DYOO*)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a live usage dispute over sense 2
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*due to being understaffed*). **Never a finite clause** — for a clause you need *due to the fact that*, which spends nine syllables on what *because* does in two. Note that *be due to* + a bare infinitive (*the train is due to arrive at six*) is a completely different construction: the adjective *due* plus a real infinitive, meaning "scheduled."
**Pattern:** [noun] *is due to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *due to* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *due to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Due to* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** See [the *due to* vs. *because of* rule](#the-due-to-vs-because-of-rule) at the end of this file — this is the single most-taught prescriptive rule about causal prepositions and the one most widely ignored. In short: traditionalists allow *due to* only where *attributable to* would fit and where it attaches to a noun, and prescribe *because of* or *owing to* everywhere else. *Because of* is never wrong; *due to* sometimes is, depending entirely on your reader.

**1. Attributable to; caused by — adjectival, after *be* or modifying a noun.** *The delay was due to fog.*
**2. Because of — adverbial, modifying the whole clause. Standard in American English, still contested.** *Due to fog, the flight was canceled.*

1. The delay was *due to* fog over the bay.
2. Most of the increase is *due to* a change in how the data is collected.
3. Absences *due to* illness must be reported by nine.
4. Errors *due to* rounding may make the columns disagree.
5. *Due to* the holiday, there will be no pickup on Monday.
6. The clinic cut its hours *due to* being understaffed all summer.
7. Two flights were canceled *due to* mechanical problems.
8. The shortfall was *due to* a single contract nobody had budgeted for.
9. Service was suspended *due to* flooding on the lower platform.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [attributable to](#attributable-to), [due to (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#due-to), [due to the fact that (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/04-cause.md#due-to-the-fact-that)

### for lack of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈlæk əv/ &middot; weak /fɚ ˈlæk ə/, also /fər/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-LAK uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a bare one with no article — *for lack of evidence*, *for lack of funding*, *for lack of a better word*. A gerund is possible but rare. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for lack of* [noun phrase] &middot; *For lack of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *for lack of a better* [noun] &middot; *for lack of anything better*
**Contrast:** *For lack of* and [*for want of*](#for-want-of) are one preposition in two vocabularies — *lack* is the everyday word, *want* the literary one — and *for lack of* is the one to use in ordinary American prose. Both name a **missing** cause: the reason something failed is that something else was absent. Watch the direction against *because of*: *because of the evidence* means the evidence caused it, *for lack of evidence* means the absence did.

Names an absence as the cause: the thing failed because something necessary was not there.

1. The case was dismissed *for lack of* evidence.
2. The project died *for lack of* funding.
3. He agreed, *for lack of* a better option.
4. Call it a hunch, *for lack of* a better word.
5. Three houses burned *for lack of* a working hydrant.
6. She stayed in the job *for lack of* anywhere else to go.

**See also:** [for want of](#for-want-of), [because of](#because-of), [in the absence of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-absence-of), [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent), [but for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#but-for)

### for want of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈwɑnt əv/ &middot; also /ˈwɔnt/ &middot; weak /fɚ ˈwɑnt ə/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-WAHNT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary — proverbial, and noticeably formal in American English
**Complement:** noun phrase, usually bare (*for want of a nail*). A gerund is possible but unusual. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for want of* [noun phrase] &middot; *For want of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *for want of a better* [noun] &middot; *for want of anything better to do*
**Contrast:** Identical in meaning to [*for lack of*](#for-lack-of); the whole difference is register. *Want* appears here in its old sense of "absence, shortage," which survives in modern American English almost nowhere else (*wanting*, *in want of*, *for want of*). Because that sense is opaque to most speakers, choosing *for want of* reads as a deliberate stylistic gesture rather than a neutral choice.

The literary twin of *for lack of*: it names an absence as the cause of a failure, using *want* in its old sense of "shortage."

1. *For want of* a nail the shoe was lost.
2. The bill failed *for want of* a second.
3. He called it a memoir, *for want of* a better term.
4. A dozen small farms went under *for want of* rain.
5. She read the manual twice *for want of* anything better to do.
6. The motion was denied *for want of* jurisdiction.

**See also:** [for lack of](#for-lack-of), [because of](#because-of), [in the absence of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-absence-of), [short of](08-accompaniment-exception.md#short-of)

### in light of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪt əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈlaɪt ə/, run together as /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/ &middot; *in-LYT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Variants:** *in the light of*, with the article, is the dominant British form. **American English strongly prefers *in light of***, and the article-less form is now spreading in British writing too.
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in light of having missed two payments*). **Never a finite clause** — use *in light of the fact that* if you must have one.
**Pattern:** *In light of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in light of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in light of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *In light of* names the **information** that leads someone to a decision, not the physical force that produces an event. You cancel a launch *in light of* the forecast, but the rocket does not explode *in light of* a faulty valve — that is *because of*. Against [*in view of*](#in-view-of), the two are near-identical; *in light of* is commoner in American English and suggests something newly learned, while *in view of* suggests a standing circumstance.

Names the new information or evidence in the light of which a decision is taken or reconsidered.

1. *In light of* the new evidence, the district attorney reopened the case.
2. The board postponed the vote *in light of* the auditor's report.
3. *In light of* everything that's happened, a delay seems reasonable.
4. The policy was rewritten *in light of* last year's lawsuit.
5. *In light of* his record, the judge suspended the sentence.
6. We reconsidered *in light of* having lost two of our biggest accounts.

**See also:** [in view of](#in-view-of), [on the grounds of](#on-the-grounds-of), [on the strength of](#on-the-strength-of), [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given), [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering), [in light of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#in-light-of)

### in view of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈvju əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈvju ə/ &middot; *in-VYOO uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a finite clause** — *in view of the fact that* for a clause.
**Pattern:** *In view of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in view of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in view of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** The literal spatial phrase *in view of* — "within sight of," as in *a cabin in view of the lake* — is a different animal and is not a preposition of cause; only the abstract use belongs here, and context always separates them. Against [*in light of*](#in-light-of): both name grounds for a decision, but *in view of* points at a circumstance already in place (*in view of his age*) where *in light of* points at something newly learned (*in light of the report*). Both are more formal than *given* and much more formal than *since*.

Names the standing circumstance that a decision takes account of — the formal way of saying *given*.

1. *In view of* his age, the court imposed probation instead.
2. The fee was waived *in view of* the delay on our end.
3. *In view of* the weather, the ceremony has been moved indoors.
4. The trial was halted early *in view of* the risks involved.
5. No further action was taken *in view of* the company's cooperation.
6. *In view of* having already paid twice, she refused the third invoice.

**See also:** [in light of](#in-light-of), [on the grounds of](#on-the-grounds-of), [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given), [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering), [in view of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#in-view-of)

### on account of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn əˈkaʊnt əv/ &middot; weak /ən əˈkaʊnt ə/ &middot; casual /ənəˈkaʊntə/ (*on accounta*) &middot; *on-uh-KOWNT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral in writing, colloquial in speech — and **the clause use is nonstandard**
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*on account of being sick*). In standard written English, **never a finite clause** — but *on account of he was sick*, with a full clause, is a real and widespread spoken form in Southern, Midland, and African American English, and the folk-doubled *on account of because* belongs to the same register. Recognize both; do not write either in edited prose.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on account of* [noun phrase] &middot; *On account of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *on account of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** A plain synonym of *because of* — one register down in speech, one register up in older writing. Do not confuse the causal preposition with the accounting phrase *on account* ("as partial payment") or with *on someone's account* ("for someone's sake"): *don't stay on my account* means "not for my sake," not "not because of me."

Names a cause in the plainest available words, with a spoken flavor that *because of* does not have.

1. School was closed *on account of* the ice.
2. *On account of* the recall, we're two weeks behind.
3. He couldn't travel *on account of* his knee.
4. She got the extension *on account of* being in the hospital.
5. The store closed early *on account of* the power outage.
6. *On account of* the drought, the town banned outdoor watering.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [on account of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#on-account-of), [on account of the fact that (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/04-cause.md#on-account-of-the-fact-that)

### on the grounds of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈɡraʊndz əv/ &middot; weak /ən ðə ˈɡraʊndz ə/ &middot; *on-thuh-GROWNDZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal and legal
**Variants:** *on grounds of*, without the article, is standard in journalism and legal drafting. The singular *on the ground of* is older and now mainly British.
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically an abstract noun naming a category of justification (*race*, *cruelty*, *national security*, *self-incrimination*). A gerund is possible but stiff. **Never a finite clause:** the clause form is *on the grounds that*, which is a conjunction, not this preposition.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the grounds of* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *on grounds of* [noun phrase] &middot; *on the grounds that* [clause] (the conjunction)
**Contrast:** **On the grounds of + noun phrase, on the grounds that + clause** is the pair to memorize; splicing them (*on the grounds of he lied*) is the standard error. Against *because of*, this phrase names a **justification somebody offered**, not a cause operating in the world: a permit is denied *on the grounds of* zoning, but a roof does not collapse *on the grounds of* snow. Against *on the basis of*, which points at evidence rather than at a reason for a decision. Against [*on the pretext of*](#on-the-pretext-of), which is the same phrase with the writer's judgment added: *on the grounds of* leaves open whether the stated reason is the true one, *on the pretext of* says it is not.

Names the stated justification for a decision, a refusal, a claim, or a legal action.

1. The claim was denied *on the grounds of* a pre-existing condition.
2. Discrimination *on the grounds of* age is prohibited.
3. He refused to testify *on the grounds of* self-incrimination.
4. The documents were withheld *on grounds of* national security.
5. She filed *on the grounds of* desertion.
6. The appeal was rejected *on the grounds of* untimeliness.

### on the pretext of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈpritɛkst əv/ &middot; weak /ən ðə ˈpritɛkst ə/ &middot; *on-thuh-PREE-tekst uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — journalism, history, and narrative
**Variants:** *under the pretext of* is at least as common in American English and is a shade more accusatory. *On the pretense of* is a near-synonym; *under the guise of* stresses the disguise rather than the excuse, and takes a noun more readily than a gerund.
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*on the pretext of checking the meter*). **Never a finite clause** — the clause form is *on the pretext that* + clause, which is a conjunction: *on the pretext that the meter needed checking*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the pretext of* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *under the pretext of* [noun phrase] &middot; *on the pretext that* [clause] (the conjunction)
**Contrast:** The dishonest twin of [*on the grounds of*](#on-the-grounds-of). Both name a **stated** reason rather than an operating cause, but *on the grounds of* is neutral about whether the stated reason is the real one, while *on the pretext of* asserts that it is not: a permit denied *on the grounds of* zoning may well be about zoning, but a search conducted *on the pretext of* a broken taillight is not about the taillight. Because the phrase commits the writer to that judgment, you cannot use it of your own motives except as a joke. Against [*in the name of*](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-name-of), which names a value invoked as cover and leaves the sincerity open.

Names the false reason somebody gave — the excuse offered to cover the real motive.

1. He got into the building *on the pretext of* delivering a package.
2. The troops crossed the border *under the pretext of* a training exercise.
3. She left the table *on the pretext of* a call she had to take.
4. Editors killed the piece *on the pretext of* space, and everybody knew better.
5. *Under the pretext of* reviewing the budget, the committee reopened the whole contract.
6. He came by twice a week *on the pretext of* returning tools he had never borrowed.
7. The stop was made *on the pretext of* a broken taillight.

### on the strength of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈstrɛŋkθ əv/ &middot; also /ˈstrɛŋθ/ &middot; weak /ən ðə ˈstrɛŋkθ ə/ &middot; *on-thuh-STRENGKTH uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*on the strength of having worked with them before*). **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the strength of* [noun phrase] &middot; *On the strength of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *on the strength of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Names the **evidence or asset somebody relied on** when they acted — a recommendation, a demo, one good quarter. *Because of* would state the cause flatly; *on the strength of* says the actor weighed something and found it convincing, and it carries a faint hint that the weighing may yet prove to have been optimistic. Against [*by virtue of*](#by-virtue-of), which names a standing status rather than a demonstration, and against *on the basis of*, which is colder and more procedural.

Names the demonstration, showing, or asset that persuaded someone to act.

1. She was hired *on the strength of* one interview.
2. The bank lent them the money *on the strength of* a signed letter of intent.
3. *On the strength of* two good quarters, the board approved the expansion.
4. He got the part *on the strength of* a two-minute audition tape.
5. The town issued bonds *on the strength of* projected tourism revenue.
6. They took the deal *on the strength of* having worked with the buyer before.

### out of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊt əv/ &middot; weak /ˈaʊtə/ &middot; *OWT uhv*, casual *OW-tuh*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in the **motive** sense (1), a **bare abstract noun with no determiner** — *out of curiosity*, *out of spite*, *out of respect*, *out of habit*. An article usually breaks it: *out of a sense of duty* works only because the head noun is *sense*, while *out of the curiosity* is impossible. A gerund is marginal in this sense (*out of not wanting to embarrass her* occurs and grates). In the **source** sense (2) the complement is an ordinary noun phrase with its determiners intact — *out of that meeting*, *out of one complaint letter* — and a fused relative works there as well, which is still a noun phrase and not a clause: *something good came out of what happened*. In the **exhaustion** sense (3) it is again a bare noun, this time naming a resource: *out of options*, *out of time*, *out of patience*. **Never a *that*-clause in any sense, and no other finite clause either.** **Bare *out* is a different word:** it is an adverb or a verb particle and takes no object at all (*she walked out*, *cross it out*, *the truth came out*), where *out of* is the two-word preposition and always requires one — *the truth came out* against *the truth came out of a deposition nobody read*. (The spatial *out of* — *out of the house* — is treated with the prepositions of [movement](04-movement-direction.md#out-of), and the full six-sense account of the word, proportions and *made out of* included, is at [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of).)
**Pattern:** [clause] *out of* [bare abstract noun] &middot; *Out of* [bare abstract noun], [clause] &middot; *out of a sense of* [noun] &middot; *out of respect / fear / spite / habit / necessity / curiosity / desperation* &middot; [*come / grow / arise / develop*] *out of* [noun phrase] &middot; *Out of* [resource noun], [clause]
**Contrast:** In sense 1 *out of* names the **motive inside the actor** — the feeling or disposition the action came out of — where *because of* names a cause outside. *She lied out of fear* is about her state; *she lied because of the threat* is about the threat. The determinerless noun is the surest sign you are in this sense rather than the spatial one. Against *from*, which overlaps in *acted from a sense of duty* but sounds more literary; see [from](02-core-relational.md#from). Sense 2 is the ancestor of the legal [*arising out of*](#arising-from), and there the direction reverses: the object is the origin and the subject is what grew from it. Sense 3 is the mirror of [*for lack of*](#for-lack-of) — both make an absence the reason — but *for lack of* names what was never there and *out of* names what ran out.

**1. The motive inside the actor.** The feeling, disposition, or state of mind an action springs from, named by a bare abstract noun. *I asked out of curiosity.*
**2. The source something developed from.** With verbs of emergence — *come*, *grow*, *arise*, *develop* — naming the episode a later thing grew out of. *The whole program grew out of one complaint letter.*
**3. The resource run down to nothing, offered as a reason.** Almost always fronted and absolute, naming the shortage that forced the outcome. *Out of options, they settled.*

1. I asked *out of* curiosity, not because I doubted her.
2. He kept the letters *out of* sentiment.
3. They took the dog in *out of* pity.
4. She resigned *out of* loyalty to her staff, not *out of* anger.
5. Most of them show up *out of* habit.
6. He agreed *out of* sheer exhaustion.
7. The company recalled the batch *out of* an abundance of caution.
8. The whole program grew *out of* one complaint letter.
9. Nothing useful came *out of* the second meeting.
10. Two good ideas came *out of* an afternoon nobody had wanted to sit through.
11. *Out of* options and nearly *out of* time, they settled on the courthouse steps.
12. *Out of* patience with a third round of revisions, she pulled the piece.

**See also:** [through](#through), [for fear of](#for-fear-of), [because of](#because-of), [arising from](#arising-from), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [out of (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#out-of), [out of (complex prepositions)](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of)

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *OH-ver* &middot; no weak form; the clipped *o'er* /ɔr/ is verse only
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*sued over failing to disclose the defect*), or a **wh-clause** — and the wh-clause is the one complement that separates *over* from every other word in this file: *a dispute over whether the vote was binding*, *confusion over how the money was spent*, *a fight over who pays*. **Never a *that*-clause:** *an argument over that he lied* is not English. *Over* is also an adverb (*the meeting is over*, *pull over*, *come over*), an adjective (*it's over between them*), and a verb particle (*think it over*, *hand it over*); **the preposition always has an object and the adverb never does** — *they argued over the fee* against *the argument is over*, *he handed over the keys* (particle plus object of the verb) against *he leaned over the rail* (preposition plus its own object).
**Pattern:** [noun or verb of conflict, loss, or strong feeling] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; *resign / quit / sue / be arrested / cry / agonize / apologize / lose sleep over* [noun phrase] &middot; *fuss / gush / fawn / rave / obsess over* [noun phrase] &middot; *disagreement / confusion / a fight over* [wh-clause] &middot; stranded: *what exactly are they fighting* **over**?
**Contrast:** This entry covers only the sense "the thing that provoked it." *Over*'s spatial and path senses live with the prepositions of [movement](04-movement-direction.md#over), its span sense with those of [time](05-time.md#over), its quantity sense with those of [measure](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#over), and its neutral topic use is worked out under [topic and reference](10-topic-reference-role.md#over). Against *about*: *about* is neutral, *over* has friction — you *talk about* money and *fight over* it. Against *because of*: *because of* attaches to any verb at all, but *over* needs a verb or noun that already implies conflict, loss, or strong feeling, which is why *he resigned over the memo* works and *he arrived over the memo* does not.

**1. The point of contention — what two parties are in conflict about.** *A ten-year fight over water rights.*
**2. The trigger of a strong reaction — a resignation, a lawsuit, an arrest, a firing.** *He resigned over the handling of the complaint.*
**3. The object of grief, worry, or regret — what somebody frets or mourns about.** *There's no use crying over spilled milk.*
**4. The object of a fuss — what somebody makes too much of, admiring as readily as complaining.** *They fussed over the baby all afternoon.*

1. The strike began as an argument *over* scheduling software.
2. Two counties have been in court *over* the same forty acres since 2003.
3. Congress deadlocked *over* the disaster funding bill.
4. There is still disagreement *over* whether the vote was binding.
5. Nobody could explain the confusion *over* how the money had been spent.
6. The deputy director resigned *over* the handling of the complaint.
7. The city was sued *over* the condition of the pumping station.
8. Two officers were suspended *over* failing to report the incident.
9. He's still beating himself up *over* one bad quarter.
10. She lost a week's sleep *over* a two-line email.
11. There's no use crying *over* spilled milk.
12. The whole family fussed *over* the baby for an hour.
13. Critics raved *over* a record the band itself had written off.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [out of](#out-of), [over (topic and conflict)](10-topic-reference-role.md#over), [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about), [over (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#over)

### owing to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊɪŋ tu/ &middot; weak /ˈoʊɪŋ tə/ &middot; *OH-ing too*
**Type:** marginal/participial (frozen out of the verb *owe*) &middot; **Register:** formal, and somewhat old-fashioned in American English
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*owing to being short-staffed*). **Never a finite clause** — *owing to the fact that* for a clause. Keep it separate from the ordinary predicative adjective *owing* (*there is still money owing*), where no preposition is involved at all.
**Pattern:** [clause] *owing to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Owing to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *owing to* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Traditional usage guides recommended *owing to* in precisely the position where they forbade adverbial *due to*, so *Owing to fog, the flight was canceled* was the sanctioned version of *Due to fog, the flight was canceled*. That is essentially why the phrase still exists: it is the escape hatch from a rule most Americans no longer apply. In present-day American English it reads as bookish or British, and *because of* does the same work in the same number of syllables.

Names a cause in a register one notch above *because of*. Frozen out of the verb *owe*, it is the phrase the old *due to* rule left standing.

1. *Owing to* a shortage of parts, deliveries have slipped by a month.
2. The concert was canceled *owing to* illness.
3. Attendance was down *owing to* the weather.
4. *Owing to* circumstances beyond our control, the office will be closed Friday.
5. The bridge was closed *owing to* structural concerns.
6. *Owing to* having lost their star witness, the prosecution offered a plea.

### thanks to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθæŋks tu/ &middot; weak /ˈθæŋks tə/ &middot; *THANKS too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — common in speech and in journalism
**Complement:** noun phrase, including an objective-case pronoun (*thanks to you*, *thanks to them*). A gerund is possible but uncommon. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *thanks to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Thanks to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *no thanks to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The only causal preposition with an attitude built into it. Literally it credits a benefactor; in practice it is at least as often sarcastic, and the sarcasm rides entirely on whether the result is good or bad — *thanks to her, we finished early* against *thanks to her, we missed the flight*. The negative *no thanks to* reverses it once more and names someone who actively did not help. *Because of* is the neutral word when you want none of this.

**1. Crediting the cause of something good.** *Thanks to a tailwind, we landed early.*
**2. Blaming the cause of something bad — ironic, and just as frequent.** *Thanks to the detour, we sat in traffic for an hour.*
**3. *No thanks to*: naming someone whose contribution was zero or negative.** *We made it, no thanks to you.*

8. *Thanks to* a clerical error, she got two paychecks in March and had to give one back.

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; *THROO* &middot; no true weak form, but in rapid speech the vowel shortens toward /θrʊ/ and, before a consonant, as far as /θrə/ (*through carelessness* /θrə ˈkɛrləsnəs/). The spelling *thru* is signage and shorthand, not prose.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral, a shade formal in this sense
**Complement:** in the causal sense, a noun phrase headed by an **abstract noun naming a fault, quality, or process** — *through carelessness*, *through sheer luck*, *through neglect* — very often with no determiner. A gerund is marginal. **Never a finite clause.** *Through* is also an adverb (*let me through*, *the bolt went through*), an adjective (*we're through*, *are you through with that?*), and a time preposition (*Monday through Friday*); **the preposition always has an object and the adverb never does** — *it failed through carelessness* (preposition plus object) against *pull the wire through* and *he read it through* (adverb, no object of its own). Note too that the movement sense strands freely (*the tunnel we drove* ***through***) while this causal sense does not: ***What did the file get deleted through?*** is not idiomatic.
**Pattern:** [clause] *through* [abstract noun] &middot; *Through* [abstract noun], [clause] &middot; *through no fault of* [possessive] *own* &middot; *through sheer* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Through* names the **failing or the quality by which** something came about, and it is at its most natural when blame is being assigned or denied — which is why the commonest instance of it in American English is the fixed exculpatory phrase *through no fault of his own*. Against *because of*, it is slightly more formal and far fussier about its object: *through carelessness* is idiomatic, *through the rain* is not. Against *by*, which names an agent or a method (*by mistake*, *by the treasurer*), where *through* names a quality that let the thing happen. Keep this entry apart from the **means and intermediary** sense — *I heard it through a friend*, *file it through the regional office*, *sold through a broker* — which takes an ordinary noun phrase naming a person, organization, or channel and is worked out at [through (means and agency)](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#through). The path, span, and completion senses live with [movement](04-movement-direction.md#through) and [time](05-time.md#through).

**1. The failing that let it happen — blame assigned or, just as often, denied.** *The file was deleted through carelessness.*
**2. The favorable quality, effort, or accident that let it happen.** *Through sheer luck, nobody was standing under it.*

1. The file was deleted *through* carelessness, not malice.
2. They lost the account *through* their own inattention.
3. He was laid off *through* no fault of his own.
4. The error crept in *through* a failure to check the second column.
5. The seat was lost *through* a clerical error nobody caught until Thursday.
6. Two of the three charges fell away *through* the state's own delay.
7. *Through* sheer luck, nobody was standing under it.
8. She learned the trade *through* years of watching her father.
9. *Through* stubbornness more than talent, he finished the degree in three years.

### what with

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt wɪθ/ &middot; also /ˈwɑt/, older /ˈhwʌt/ &middot; weak /wət wəθ/, also /wət wəð/ &middot; *WUT with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal and conversational — rare in edited prose
**Complement:** a noun phrase, very often a coordinated list, and very often rounded off with *and all*. It also takes a **small clause** — a noun phrase plus a participle, as in *what with the kids being sick* — which no other causal preposition does. **Never a finite clause:** *what with he was sick* is not English.
**Pattern:** *What with* [noun phrase] *and* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *What with* [noun phrase] *and all*, [clause] &middot; *What with* [noun phrase] [verb-ing], [clause] &middot; [clause], *what with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *What with* differs from *because of* in two ways that matter. Introduces a pile of contributing circumstances, usually by way of excuse for something that did not get done.

2. *What with* one thing and another, the whole afternoon was gone.
3. *What with* gas and tolls, driving isn't any cheaper.
4. *What with* the kids being sick all week, nothing got done.
5. *What with* the storm and all, hardly anybody showed up.
6. She's exhausted, *what with* two jobs and a night class.

## Result and consequence

Eight phrases built on the words *result*, *consequence*, *cause*, *arise*, and *secondary*. They carry a trap that catches learners and native speakers alike: **the object of every one of them is the cause, not the result.** In *four people were hospitalized as a result of the crash*, the crash is the cause and the hospitalizations are the result — the phrase names what the result came out of. The adverbs formed from the same words (*as a result*, *consequently*, *in consequence*) point the other way and take no object at all. Entries are alphabetical.

### arising from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraɪzɪŋ frʌm/ &middot; weak /əˈraɪzɪŋ frəm/ &middot; *uh-RY-zing frum*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** legal — contracts, insurance policies, pleadings
**Variants:** *arising out of* is the standard form in American contract drafting and the one you will meet in arbitration clauses; *arising from* is slightly plainer. *Arising under* is narrower and means "deriving from the terms of the instrument itself."
**Complement:** noun phrase. No gerund in practice, and **never a finite clause**. Unlike almost everything else in this file it **cannot be fronted**: it postmodifies a noun, so there is no *Arising from the accident, he sued*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *arising from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *arising out of or relating to* [noun phrase] &middot; *any claim arising out of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** A participial phrase that has hardened into a fixed causal preposition inside legal boilerplate, where its very breadth is the point — *arising out of or relating to this Agreement* is drafted to sweep in everything a court might otherwise exclude. Against *resulting from*, which is narrower and implies direct causation, and *caused by*, narrower still. Drafters stack all three because courts read them as different widths of the same idea.

Names the event or instrument that a claim, cost, obligation, or dispute grew out of. Its object is the cause, and the noun it modifies is the result.

1. Any dispute *arising from* this contract shall be settled by arbitration.
2. The policy excludes losses *arising from* flood.
3. She filed a claim for injuries *arising from* the collision.
4. Costs *arising from* the delay will be borne by the contractor.
5. All obligations *arising from* the merger survive termination.
6. The report covers complaints *arising out of* the November outage.

**See also:** [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [resulting from](#resulting-from), [consequent on](#consequent-on), [in consequence of](#in-consequence-of), [based on](12-marginal-participial.md#based-on), [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to)

### as a consequence of

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə ˈkɑnsəkwɛns əv/ &middot; weak /əz ə ˈkɑnsəkwəns ə/ &middot; *az-uh-KON-suh-kwens uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — academic and technical writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*as a consequence of having missed the deadline*). **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *as a consequence of* [noun phrase] &middot; *As a consequence of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *as a direct consequence of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The heavier twin of [*as a result of*](#as-a-result-of). *Consequence* carries a faint suggestion that the outcome was foreseeable or that it matters morally — *as a consequence of* the policy implies the policy had a logic that led here, where *as a result of* merely reports the sequence. Both are longer than *because of* and neither adds anything a careful *because of* could not carry. **Remember the direction: what follows is the cause.**

Names the cause an outcome followed from, with a suggestion that the outcome was foreseeable or that it matters. What follows the phrase is the cause, not the consequence.

1. Sea ice has thinned *as a consequence of* two unusually warm winters.
2. *As a consequence of* the merger, three plants were closed.
3. Enrollment fell sharply *as a consequence of* the tuition increase.
4. Several species disappeared *as a consequence of* the dam.
5. *As a consequence of* having missed the filing deadline, the firm lost its appeal.
6. The rule was suspended *as a direct consequence of* the court's ruling.

**See also:** [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [in consequence of](#in-consequence-of), [consequent on](#consequent-on), [because of](#because-of)

### as a result of

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə rɪˈzʌlt əv/ &middot; weak /əz ə rɪˈzʌltə/ &middot; *az-uh-rih-ZULT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the standard phrase of reports, news writing, and official statements
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*as a result of being denied a permit*). **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *as a result of* [noun phrase] &middot; *As a result of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *as a direct result of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **What follows *as a result of* is the cause.** The phrase is built out of the word *result*, and that misleads people constantly. Compare the adverb *as a result*, with no *of*, which points the opposite way and needs no object at all: *The crash closed the highway; as a result, four people were late.* Against *because of*, this is three syllables longer and slightly more formal, and it is the better choice when the causal chain is indirect or has several links.

Names the cause that an outcome followed from. The object of the phrase is the cause; the main clause reports the result.

1. Four people were hospitalized *as a result of* the crash.
2. *As a result of* the recall, production has been halted at two plants.
3. Property values rose *as a result of* the new rail line.
4. She lost her license *as a result of* the second conviction.
5. *As a result of* being denied a permit, the festival moved across the river.
6. Nothing changed *as a direct result of* the report.

### caused by

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɔzd baɪ/ &middot; weak /ˈkɔzd bə/ &middot; *KAWZD by*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral — the plainest causal participle in the language, at home everywhere from warranties to weather reports
**Complement:** noun phrase naming either an event or, unusually for this file, an **agent**: *damage caused by the contractor* as readily as *damage caused by the storm*. A gerund is possible but stiff (*caused by having left the valve open*). **Never a finite clause** — there is no *caused by that* and no *caused by the fact that* worth writing.
**Pattern:** [noun] *caused by* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *was caused by* [noun phrase] &middot; *damage / delay / injury / illness caused by* [noun phrase] &middot; *arising out of, resulting from, or caused by* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The **narrowest** of the result phrases, and the test the whole *due to* dispute is built around: where *caused by* fits, adjectival *due to* is safe under every analysis — see [the *due to* rule](#the-due-to-vs-because-of-rule) at the end of this file. Against [*resulting from*](#resulting-from), which names a process rather than an agent, and [*arising from*](#arising-from), which claims only connection; drafters stack all three because courts read them as three widths of one idea. Against *because of*, the difference is grammatical rather than semantic: *because of* is adverbial and fronts freely, while *caused by* follows *be* or postmodifies a noun and **does not front** — ***Caused by fog, the flight was canceled*** is not English.

Attaches a result-noun, or a subject after *be*, to the thing that directly produced it. Like everything in this section its object is the cause.

1. Most of the damage was *caused by* water, not by the fire itself.
2. Delays *caused by* the strike are excluded from the schedule.
3. The outage was *caused by* a squirrel in a substation.
4. Illness *caused by* contaminated well water accounted for half the cases.
5. Any injury *caused by* misuse of the ladder voids the warranty.
6. The crack was *caused by* settling, and the engineer put that in writing.
7. Losses *arising out of, resulting from, or caused by* flood are not covered.

### consequent on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑnsəkwɛnt ɑn/ &middot; weak /ˈkɑnsəkwənt ən/ &middot; *KON-suh-kwent on*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal and academic; in American English it is rare and slightly stiff
**Variants:** *consequent upon* is the older, heavier form and is commoner in British writing. *Consequent to* also occurs and is disliked by careful writers. American English prefers *consequent on* when it uses the phrase at all.
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [noun] *consequent on* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *consequent on* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *consequent upon* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Unlike everything else in this section, *consequent on* is built on an **adjective**, so it most naturally postmodifies a noun (*the layoffs consequent on the merger*) rather than modifying a clause. Do not confuse it with the adverb *consequently*, which introduces the result rather than the cause, or with the noun *consequence*. **What follows is the cause.**

An adjective-based phrase that attaches a result-noun to its cause: *the layoffs consequent on the merger*.

1. The layoffs *consequent on* the merger were announced in March.
2. Erosion *consequent on* the removal of the dune line has accelerated.
3. Changes *consequent upon* the new statute take effect in July.
4. The board addressed the liabilities *consequent on* the recall.
5. Delays *consequent on* the strike pushed the opening to fall.
6. The illness *consequent upon* prolonged exposure is well documented.

**See also:** [in consequence of](#in-consequence-of), [as a consequence of](#as-a-consequence-of), [arising from](#arising-from), [consequent upon (conjunctions catalog)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/04-cause.md#consequent-upon)

### in consequence of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑnsəkwəns əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈkɑnsəkwəns ə/ &middot; *in-KON-suh-kwuhns uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and dated — nineteenth-century in flavor
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in consequence of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In consequence of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** The most old-fashioned member of the *consequence* family and the least used in modern American English; *as a result of* has replaced it nearly everywhere outside deeds, wills, and older statutes. The bare adverb *in consequence* ("consequently") is a separate item that takes no object. **As everywhere in this section, the object is the cause.**

The dated, legal way of naming the cause an outcome followed from. Its object is the cause, not the consequence.

1. *In consequence of* the fire, the lease was declared void.
2. The estate passed to his nephew *in consequence of* the earlier bequest.
3. *In consequence of* these findings, the license was revoked.
4. Several officers were dismissed *in consequence of* the inquiry.
5. *In consequence of* her refusal to appear, the matter was continued.
6. The town lost its charter *in consequence of* the annexation.

### resulting from

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈzʌltɪŋ frʌm/ &middot; weak /rɪˈzʌltɪŋ frəm/ &middot; *rih-ZUL-ting frum*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — technical, legal, insurance, and medical writing
**Variants:** *stemming from* is the everyday journalistic equivalent and is the one to use in ordinary prose; *flowing from* is a drafting alternate; *caused by* is plainer and narrower still. American contracts habitually stack them — *arising out of or resulting from*.
**Complement:** noun phrase. A gerund is possible but rare (*resulting from having ignored the warning*), and there is **never a finite clause.** Like [*arising from*](#arising-from) it **cannot be fronted**: it postmodifies a noun, so there is no *Resulting from the crash, four people were hurt*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *resulting from* [noun phrase] &middot; *any damage / loss / injury resulting from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *stemming from* [noun phrase] &middot; *arising out of or resulting from* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Narrower than [*arising from*](#arising-from): *resulting from* claims **direct** causation where *arising from* claims only connection, which is exactly why drafters use both instead of choosing between them. **The object is the cause and the noun in front is the result** — the same direction trap as *as a result of*, and the reason *resulting from* is worth its own entry. Against *caused by*, which is narrower again and will name an agent (*damage caused by the contractor*) as readily as an event.

Attaches a result-noun to the event that directly produced it. It is a participle that has hardened into a preposition, and like every phrase in this section its object is the cause.

1. Any damage *resulting from* misuse is not covered by the warranty.
2. Injuries *resulting from* the collapse were treated at the scene.
3. Savings *resulting from* the merger will be reinvested in the plant.
4. The company denied liability for delays *resulting from* the strike.
5. Charges *stemming from* the November raid were dropped last week.
6. Complications *resulting from* the second surgery kept her in the hospital another week.
7. Costs *arising out of or resulting from* a breach are borne by the breaching party.

### secondary to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛkənˌdɛri tu/ &middot; weak /ˈsɛkənˌdɛri tə/ &middot; *SEK-uhn-derr-ee too*
**Type:** complex (adjective plus *to*) &middot; **Register:** technical — medicine, epidemiology, and engineering reports; outside them it reads as jargon
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a condition, event, or process (*secondary to diabetes*, *secondary to blunt trauma*). A gerund is rare and clinical (*secondary to having aspirated*). **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [noun] *secondary to* [noun phrase] &middot; [condition] *is secondary to* [condition] &middot; *renal failure secondary to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** In this sense *secondary to* means "**caused by**," not "less important than," and the two senses share a spelling: *renal failure secondary to diabetes* names a cause, while *cost is secondary to safety* ranks two things. Only the causal sense belongs here, and context always separates them. Against [*attributable to*](#attributable-to), which assigns a measured share of an effect; *secondary to* asserts a chain instead — a primary condition and the one that followed from it. Against [*caused by*](#caused-by), which is the plain-English version and the one to use with any reader outside the field. Like *attributable to* it follows *be* or postmodifies a noun and **does not front**: ***Secondary to diabetes, she was admitted*** is not English.

Names the primary condition or event that a later one developed out of — the clinical way of saying *caused by*.

1. She was admitted with renal failure *secondary to* long-standing diabetes.
2. The bleeding was *secondary to* a tear in the lining, not to the medication.
3. Hearing loss *secondary to* repeated infections is common in this group.
4. Deaths *secondary to* the storm went on for two weeks after landfall.
5. The fracture was *secondary to* a fall, and the fall was *secondary to* low blood pressure.
6. Most of the corrosion is *secondary to* a drainage failure on the upstream side.

## Purpose and intention

The words that point forward to an aim. Their meanings barely differ; what differs is what they take, and that difference is absolute. *In order to*, *so as to*, *in an effort to*, and *with intent to* take a **bare infinitive** and cannot take a noun at all, which strictly disqualifies the first two as prepositions — they are infinitival subordinators sitting in a preposition's slot, and they are listed here because that is where learners look for them. Everything else in this section takes a **gerund or a noun phrase** and refuses the infinitive: *with a view to*, *with an eye to*, *with the aim of*, *for the purpose of*, *for the sake of*, *for fear of*, *in the hope of*, *in the interest of*, and — noun only, no gerund — *for the benefit of*, *in furtherance of*, *in pursuance of*, and *in pursuit of*. **The tell is the *of*.** Every purpose phrase that ends in *of* takes a gerund; the ones that end in bare *to* take an infinitive — with the single exception of *with a view to*, whose *to* is a preposition, which is exactly why that one has to be memorized rather than worked out. The full table is [at the end of the file](#what-each-purpose-phrase-takes). Entries are alphabetical.

### for fear of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈfɪr əv/ &middot; weak /fɚ ˈfɪr ə/, also /fər/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-FEER uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*for fear of waking her*). **Never a finite clause** — the clause form is *for fear that* + clause, which is a conjunction: *for fear that she would wake*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *for fear of* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *for fear of* [noun phrase] &middot; *for fear that* [clause] (the conjunction)
**Contrast:** **Negative purpose.** The action is taken to *avoid* what follows, not to bring it about, which reverses the direction of every other phrase in this section: *he stayed home for the sake of his health* aims at health, *he stayed home for fear of infection* aims away from infection. *For fear of* + gerund and *for fear that* + clause are freely interchangeable and mean the same thing; splicing them (*for fear of she would wake*) is the error to watch. *Lest* is the literary conjunction with the same meaning.

Negative purpose: names the outcome the action is designed to avoid.

1. She whispered *for fear of* waking the baby.
2. Nobody spoke up, *for fear of* losing their jobs.
3. He wouldn't sign it *for fear of* a lawsuit.
4. The witnesses stayed anonymous *for fear of* retaliation.
5. They kept the diagnosis quiet *for fear of* worrying their daughter.
6. I didn't ask, *for fear of* the answer.

### for the benefit of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ðə ˈbɛnəfɪt əv/ &middot; weak /fɚ ðə ˈbɛnəfɪt ə/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-thuh-BEN-uh-fit uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a person, group, or institution. A gerund is not idiomatic here. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for the benefit of* [noun phrase] &middot; *For the benefit of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *held in trust for the benefit of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Names the **beneficiary** — who the action is meant to help — rather than the goal it aims at, which is what [*for the purpose of*](#for-the-purpose-of) does. Against [*for the sake of*](#for-the-sake-of), which also names a beneficiary but is warmer and, crucially, works with abstractions: *for the sake of clarity* is idiomatic where *for the benefit of clarity* is not. In law it is the fixed phrase for a trust beneficiary, and there it is not optional.

Names the person, group, or institution an action is meant to serve.

1. The fund is held in trust *for the benefit of* his grandchildren.
2. *For the benefit of* those just joining us, here's where we left off.
3. The recital was staged *for the benefit of* the music program.
4. She repeated the question *for the benefit of* the people in the back.
5. The easement exists *for the benefit of* the adjoining parcel.
6. He explained it twice, mostly *for the benefit of* the new hires.

**See also:** [for the sake of](#for-the-sake-of), [in the interest of](#in-the-interest-of), [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### for the purpose of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ðə ˈpɜrpəs əv/ &middot; weak /fɚ ðə ˈpɜrpəs ə/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-thuh-PUR-puhs uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and bureaucratic
**Variants:** *for purposes of* and the drafting plural *for the purposes of this section* are both standard; the plural is the normal form when the phrase fixes the scope of a document rather than stating the aim of an act.
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund only — never a bare infinitive.** *For the purpose of determining eligibility* is correct; *for the purpose of to determine eligibility* and *for the purpose of determine eligibility* are both errors. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for the purpose of* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *for the purpose of* [noun phrase] &middot; *For the purposes of* [this section / this Agreement], [clause]
**Contrast:** **The great redundancy of official prose.** Nine times in ten, *for the purpose of examining* reduces to *to examine* with nothing lost. Keep the phrase where the plural is doing real work — *for the purposes of this chapter, "vehicle" means…* — because there it limits a definition rather than stating an aim. Against [*in order to*](#in-order-to), which takes the very infinitive this phrase forbids: the two are identical in meaning and opposite in grammar. Against [*in furtherance of*](#in-furtherance-of), which names the larger scheme an act advances rather than the aim of the act itself.

**1. The aim of an act.** *The samples were collected for the purpose of establishing a baseline.*
**2. The scope within which a definition or a rule applies** — the drafting plural, and the use worth keeping. *For the purposes of this section, "resident" means…*

1. The samples were collected *for the purpose of* establishing a baseline.
2. He entered the building *for the purpose of* committing a felony.
3. The account was opened *for the purpose of* paying contractors.
4. Cameras were installed *for the purpose of* deterring theft, not surveillance.
5. She kept a separate notebook *for the purpose of* tracking expenses.
6. *For the purposes of* this section, "resident" means a person domiciled in the state.
7. *For purposes of* the rebate, a duplex counts as one dwelling.
8. Nothing in this paragraph limits the term *for the purposes of* Article IV.

### for the sake of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ðə ˈseɪk əv/ &middot; weak /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/ &middot; stressed *for* /fɔr/ &middot; *fer-thuh-SAYK uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*for the sake of getting it over with*). **Never a finite clause.** It also has a **possessive frame** with no *of* at all — *for her sake*, *for the children's sake*, *for heaven's sake*, *for old times' sake*, *for its own sake* — and note the bare apostrophe in *for goodness' sake* and *for old times' sake*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *for the sake of* [noun phrase] &middot; *For the sake of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *for the sake of* [verb-ing] &middot; *for* [possessive] *sake* &middot; *for the sake of argument* &middot; *for its own sake*
**Contrast:** Two jobs in one phrase. With a **person** it means "for their benefit" and overlaps [*for the benefit of*](#for-the-benefit-of), though it is warmer and more personal. With an **abstraction** — *clarity*, *argument*, *simplicity*, *appearances* — it means "in order to achieve or preserve," and there nothing else substitutes: *for the sake of clarity* is fixed where *for the benefit of clarity* is not English. *For the sake of argument* means "granting it provisionally," and *for its own sake* means "as an end in itself."

**1. For someone's benefit.** *They stayed together for the sake of the kids.*
**2. In order to get or preserve something abstract.** *For the sake of clarity, let's number them.*
**3. Fixed phrases: *for the sake of argument*, *for its own sake*, *for heaven's sake*.**

1. They stayed together *for the sake of* the children.
2. Do it *for the sake of* your mother, if not for mine.
3. *For the sake of* clarity, let's number the options.
4. He argued the other side *for the sake of* argument.
5. She studies Latin *for the sake of* the language itself, not for any use it has.
6. *For heaven's sake*, close the door.
7. They cut the last chapter *for the sake of* getting the book out on time.
8. She kept the old spelling *for old times' sake* and for nothing else.
9. *For the sake of* appearances, both of them came to the ribbon-cutting.

### in an effort to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ən ˈɛfərt tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn ən ˈɛfɚt tə/ &middot; *in-uhn-EF-ert too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — the workhorse purpose phrase of American news writing
**Variants:** *in an attempt to* is identical in grammar and a shade more tentative, since an *attempt* may fail where an *effort* is merely made. *In a bid to* is the headline version and is commoner in British journalism than American. *In hopes of* is **not** a variant of this phrase — it takes a gerund; see [in the hope of](#in-the-hope-of).
**Complement:** **a bare infinitive verb, and nothing else** — like *in order to* and *so as to*, and unlike every *of*-phrase in this section. *In an effort to cut costs*, never ***in an effort to cutting costs*** and never ***in an effort of cutting costs***. **Never a finite clause.** The noun *effort* is still live inside the phrase, which is why it accepts adjectives no true preposition would: *in a last-ditch effort to*, *in a renewed effort to*, *in a coordinated effort to*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in an effort to* [verb] &middot; *In an effort to* [verb], [clause] &middot; *in a* [adjective] *effort to* [verb] &middot; *in an attempt to* [verb]
**Contrast:** Belongs with [*in order to*](#in-order-to) and [*so as to*](#so-as-to) on the **infinitive** side of the purpose split, not with *with a view to* and *with the aim of* on the gerund side — ***in an effort to reducing costs*** is the error, and it is common precisely because so many neighboring phrases end in *of*. Against *in order to*, it adds exertion and a hint of doubt: *in an effort to* reports that somebody tried, and often implies the trying may not have worked. Reporters reach for it constantly because it states a motive without endorsing it.

States the purpose of an action with an infinitive, adding the sense that somebody exerted themselves and may not have succeeded. Like *in order to*, it can never take a noun or a gerund.

1. The city cut bus fares *in an effort to* boost ridership.
2. *In an effort to* keep the plant open, the union agreed to a wage freeze.
3. She took a second job *in an effort to* stay ahead of the medical bills.
4. The agency rewrote the form *in an effort to* reduce filing errors.
5. *In a last-ditch effort to* save the season, the club fired its coach.
6. Regulators tightened the rule *in an attempt to* close the loophole.
7. He apologized twice *in an effort to* put the whole thing behind them.

### in furtherance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfɜrðərəns əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈfɜrðərəns ə/ &middot; *in-FUR-thuh-ruhns uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal and institutional — indictments, bylaws, trust instruments, and mission statements
**Complement:** noun phrase — a scheme, plan, conspiracy, policy, purpose, or interest. A gerund occurs (*in furtherance of settling the claim*) but is unusual and reads as drafting slack. **Never a bare infinitive** (***in furtherance of to settle***) and **never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in furtherance of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In furtherance of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy* &middot; *in furtherance of its charitable purposes*
**Contrast:** Names the larger aim an act **advances**, where [*in pursuance of*](#in-pursuance-of) names an order the act **carries out** and [*for the purpose of*](#for-the-purpose-of) names the aim of the act itself. The difference does real work in criminal law: an overt act *in furtherance of* a conspiracy need not accomplish anything at all — it only has to move the scheme along, which is why mailing a letter can be one. Against [*in pursuit of*](#in-pursuit-of), which keeps the metaphor of a chase alive and can carry disapproval; *in furtherance of* is flat and institutional and passes no judgment.

Names the larger plan, purpose, or interest that an act is meant to advance.

1. The indictment alleges three overt acts *in furtherance of* the conspiracy.
2. The trustees may sell property *in furtherance of* the foundation's purposes.
3. *In furtherance of* the settlement, both parties agreed to a stay.
4. The grant was made *in furtherance of* the county's housing plan.
5. Nothing in this section authorizes a payment *in furtherance of* a private interest.
6. She was fired over an email sent *in furtherance of* the very policy she was accused of ignoring.

### in order to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈɔrdər tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈɔrdər tə/ &middot; *in-OR-der too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, a shade more formal than bare *to*
**Complement:** **a bare infinitive verb, and nothing else.** *In order to leave* — never *in order to departure*, never *in order to leaving*. It cannot take a noun phrase at all, which is what disqualifies it as a preposition in the strict sense; it is an infinitival subordinator occupying a preposition's slot. The negative is *in order not to* (*in order not to wake her*). For a finite clause the form is *in order that* + clause, usually with *may*, *might*, or *can*, and it is formal. The frame *in order for* [noun phrase] *to* [verb] supplies a different subject: *in order for this to work*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in order to* [verb] &middot; *In order to* [verb], [clause] &middot; *in order not to* [verb] &middot; *in order for* [noun phrase] *to* [verb]
**Contrast:** **The purpose phrases split cleanly by what they take.** *In order to*, [*so as to*](#so-as-to), and [*in an effort to*](#in-an-effort-to) take an infinitive; [*for the purpose of*](#for-the-purpose-of), [*with a view to*](#with-a-view-to), [*with an eye to*](#with-an-eye-to), [*with the aim of*](#with-the-aim-of), and [*in the hope of*](#in-the-hope-of) take a gerund or a noun. Crossing the lists is the most reliable purpose error there is. Against bare *to*, which means exactly the same thing: *in order* is deletable in nearly every sentence, and its real work is removing ambiguity where a plain *to* could be read as something else — *He stopped in order to talk* is unmistakable, *He stopped to talk* is not always.

States the purpose of an action with an infinitive. Strictly it is an infinitival subordinator rather than a preposition, since it can never take a noun; it is listed here because it competes directly with the gerund-taking purpose phrases and because that competition is where the errors come from.

1. *In order to* qualify, you have to have lived here a year.
2. She left early *in order to* beat the traffic.
3. He took a second job *in order to* pay off the loan.
4. *In order not to* embarrass anyone, we'll skip the names.
5. *In order for* the warranty to apply, you have to register the unit.
6. The fund was created *in order to* keep the theater open.

### in pursuance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn pərˈsuəns əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn pərˈsuəns ə/ &middot; *in-per-SOO-uhns uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal and official; rare anywhere else
**Complement:** noun phrase — typically a document, power, duty, or policy. Gerunds are possible but unusual. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in pursuance of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In pursuance of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Means "in carrying out" — the action gives effect to something already agreed or ordered. It is easily confused with [*pursuant to*](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to), which means "in accordance with" and points at authority rather than execution: you act *pursuant to* a statute because it permits you, and *in pursuance of* a policy because you are carrying it out. Distinguish both from *in pursuit of*, which names a thing chased (*in pursuit of a suspect*, *in pursuit of happiness*). American drafting prefers *pursuant to* and keeps *in pursuance of* mostly for older instruments. Against [*in furtherance of*](#in-furtherance-of), which advances a scheme rather than executing an order.

Names the order, policy, power, or agreement that an action is carrying out.

1. The warrant was issued *in pursuance of* the court's order.
2. *In pursuance of* this policy, all contracts are now reviewed centrally.
3. The trustees acted *in pursuance of* the powers conferred by Article VI.
4. Notices given *in pursuance of* this section must be in writing.
5. *In pursuance of* the treaty, both governments reduced their tariffs.
6. He sold the property *in pursuance of* the settlement agreement.

### in pursuit of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn pərˈsut əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn pɚˈsut ə/ &middot; *in-per-SOOT uhv* &middot; (British /pəˈsjuːt/, *per-SYOOT*)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal, and a shade elevated
**Complement:** noun phrase — a goal, a quarry, or an abstraction (*happiness*, *profit*, *a settlement*, *a suspect*). A gerund is unusual and usually awkward: *in pursuit of expanding the business* is clumsy where *with a view to expanding the business* is not. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in pursuit of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In pursuit of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *in hot pursuit of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in dogged / relentless / single-minded pursuit of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Names the **thing chased**, where [*in pursuance of*](#in-pursuance-of) names the order being carried out. The two look almost identical and are unrelated: you act *in pursuance of* the court's order because you are executing it, and you drive *in pursuit of* a stolen pickup because you are chasing it. Against [*with the aim of*](#with-the-aim-of), which takes a gerund and states a target coolly; *in pursuit of* keeps the metaphor of the chase alive, which lets it carry disapproval — *in pursuit of profit at any cost* judges, *with the aim of increasing profit* does not.

Names the goal or the quarry an action is chasing, keeping the metaphor of the chase alive.

1. He left a tenured job *in pursuit of* a startup idea.
2. Two cruisers went by *in pursuit of* a stolen pickup.
3. *In pursuit of* a Nobel, the lab spent a decade on a single protein.
4. The company cut corners *in pursuit of* a quarterly number.
5. Whole families moved west *in pursuit of* cheap land.
6. She reads three papers a day *in pursuit of* an edge nobody else has.
7. The deputies were *in hot pursuit of* a car that had run the checkpoint.

### in the hope of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp ə/ &middot; *in-thuh-HOHP uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *in the hopes of*, plural, is common in American speech and journalism and is now fully standard here; the singular is older and a shade more formal. *In hopes of*, with no article, is also thoroughly American. British writing keeps closer to the singular.
**Complement:** **a gerund or a noun phrase — never a bare infinitive.** *In the hope of finding work* is correct; ***in the hope of to find work*** and ***in the hope to find work*** are both errors. **Never a finite clause** — the clause form is *in the hope that* + clause, which is a conjunction: *in the hope that she would call*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the hope of* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *in the hopes of* [verb-ing] &middot; *In the hope of* [verb-ing], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in the hope of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in the hope that* [clause] (the conjunction)
**Contrast:** The purpose phrase that concedes the purpose may not be reached. [*With the aim of*](#with-the-aim-of) states a target and expects to hit it; *in the hope of* states one and admits the odds. It sits on the **gerund** side of the purpose split with *with a view to*, *with the aim of*, and *for the purpose of*, and it attracts the identical infinitive error. Against [*for fear of*](#for-fear-of), its exact mirror: one aims at a wanted outcome, the other away from an unwanted one — *she wrote in the hope of an answer* against *she didn't write for fear of an answer*.

States a purpose while conceding it may not be reached. Its *of* is a preposition, so a verb after it must be a gerund.

1. They moved to Phoenix *in the hope of* finding steady work.
2. He left the porch light on *in the hopes of* seeing her car pull in.
3. *In the hope of* settling before trial, both sides agreed to mediation.
4. She sent the manuscript out again *in the hope of* one honest reader.
5. The town rebuilt the pier *in hopes of* drawing summer traffic back.
6. He kept every receipt *in the hope of* a refund that never came.
7. *In the hopes of* heading off a strike, management reopened the contract.

### in the interest of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈɪntrəst əv/ &middot; also /ˈɪntərəst/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈɪntrəst ə/ &middot; *in-thee-IN-truhst uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the institutional word
**Variants:** *in the interests of*, plural, is fully standard and is the dominant form in British English. American English uses both, with the singular slightly ahead in edited prose; *in the interest of time* is fixed in the singular.
**Complement:** noun phrase — most often an abstract noun naming a value (*time*, *fairness*, *safety*, *transparency*, *full disclosure*) — or a party whose interests are being served, or a **gerund**, which is fully idiomatic here: *in the interest of avoiding delay*, *in the interest of getting this done today*. **Never a bare infinitive** (***in the interest of to avoid delay***) and **never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** *In the interest of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in the interest of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in the interests of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Overlaps [*for the sake of*](#for-the-sake-of) in the abstract sense — *in the interest of clarity* and *for the sake of clarity* are interchangeable — and differs only in tone: *in the interest of* belongs to meetings, memos, and disclosures, where *for the sake of* is personal. With a **party** rather than a value it means "to that party's advantage" and shades toward [*for the benefit of*](#for-the-benefit-of).

**1. The value an action is meant to serve.** *In the interest of time, let's take this offline.*
**2. The party an action is meant to advantage** — nearly always the plural *interests*. *The court sealed the file in the interests of the minor children.*

1. *In the interest of* time, let's take the rest of this offline.
2. The records were released *in the interest of* transparency.
3. *In the interest of* full disclosure, I own stock in the company.
4. *In the interest of* safety, the trail is closed until spring.
5. He recused himself *in the interest of* avoiding any appearance of bias.
6. The court sealed the file *in the interests of* the minor children.
7. The guardian may settle the claim *in the interests of* the estate.
8. Regulators blocked the deal *in the interests of* consumers, or said they had.

### so as to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ æz tu/ &middot; weak /soʊ əz tə/ &middot; *SOH az too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — commoner in writing than in speech
**Complement:** **a bare infinitive verb, and nothing else** — like *in order to* and unlike everything else in this section. *So as to avoid delay*, never *so as to avoidance*, never *so as to avoiding*. The negative *so as not to* is extremely common and is in fact the phrase's most frequent use. The understood subject of the infinitive must match the subject of the main clause; if it does not, the sentence dangles — *So as to save time, the meeting was shortened* leaves nobody saving time.
**Pattern:** [clause] *so as to* [verb] &middot; [clause] *so as not to* [verb]
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with [*in order to*](#in-order-to) in meaning, with two differences that matter. First, *so as to* is more formal and much rarer in speech. Second, it resists fronting: *In order to save time, we skipped lunch* is natural, *So as to save time, we skipped lunch* is stilted. It is at its most idiomatic in the negative, where *so as not to wake her* beats *in order not to wake her* every time. Keep it separate from the result correlative *so … as to* (*so cold as to be dangerous*), which is a different construction with a word wedged in the middle.

States purpose with an infinitive, in a register a step above *in order to*, and is at its most idiomatic in the negative *so as not to*. Like *in order to* it can never take a noun.

1. He spoke quietly *so as not to* wake the baby.
2. Fold the corner *so as to* leave a half-inch margin.
3. They staggered the shifts *so as to* keep the line running.
4. She phrased it carefully *so as not to* commit to anything.
5. The panels are angled *so as to* catch the afternoon sun.
6. Pack the mugs in newspaper *so as not to* chip them.

### with a view to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ə ˈvju tu/ &middot; weak /wəð ə ˈvju tə/ &middot; *with-uh-VYOO too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — business, diplomatic, and legal writing
**Complement:** **a gerund or a noun phrase — never a bare infinitive.** *With a view to reducing costs* is correct; ***with a view to reduce costs* is an error**, and a very common one, because the *to* looks like an infinitive marker and is not. It is a preposition, and prepositions take *-ing*. The test is substitution: if *with a view to reduction* works, the slot belongs to a preposition and the verb must be *-ing*. **Never a finite clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *with a view to* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *with a view to* [noun phrase] &middot; *With a view to* [verb-ing], [clause]
**Contrast:** The classic *to*-plus-gerund trap, in the same family as *look forward to*, *be committed to*, *object to*, and *be used to*. Against [*in order to*](#in-order-to), which takes the infinitive this phrase forbids — identical meaning, opposite grammar. Against [*with an eye to*](#with-an-eye-to) and [*with the aim of*](#with-the-aim-of), which take the same gerund and sound less formal. *With a view of* is a different phrase entirely and means "with a sight of": *a room with a view of the harbor*. Note that this is the one purpose phrase the *of*-test does not sort: it ends in *to* and still takes a gerund, which is why it has to be learned as an exception — compare [*with intent to*](#with-intent-to) **defraud**, where the *to* really is an infinitive marker.

States a purpose or eventual intention behind an action, in formal register. Its *to* is a preposition, so the verb after it must be a gerund.

1. The two sides met *with a view to* resolving the dispute before trial.
2. The land was bought *with a view to* eventual development.
3. Records are retained *with a view to* possible litigation.
4. She took the assistant's job *with a view to* learning the business.
5. *With a view to* cutting overhead, the firm subleased two floors.
6. The treaty was drafted *with a view to* the accession of new members.

**Variants:** *with the intent to* takes the same infinitive and is the fuller statutory form. **Do not treat *with the intention of* as a variant:** it takes a gerund — *with the intention of defrauding*, never ***with the intention of to defraud*** and never ***with the intention to defraud*** in careful prose; see [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of).

## The *due to* vs. *because of* rule

**The rule as it is taught.** *Due to* is an adjective phrase. Like any adjective, it should modify a noun — either directly (*absences due to illness*) or through a linking verb (*the delay was due to fog*). *Because of* is an adverb phrase and modifies verbs and whole clauses (*we were delayed because of fog*). The test: if you can swap in [*caused by*](#caused-by) or [*attributable to*](#attributable-to) and the sentence still stands, *due to* is safe. If you cannot, the traditional rule wants *because of*, *owing to*, or *on account of*.

| Sentence | Traditional verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The delay was *due to* fog. | correct | follows *be* and modifies the noun *delay* |
| Absences *due to* illness must be reported. | correct | modifies the noun *absences* |
| The flight was canceled *due to* fog. | disputed | modifies the verb *canceled* |
| *Due to* fog, the flight was canceled. | most disputed | modifies the whole clause, and is fronted |
| The flight was canceled *because of* fog. | correct | *because of* is adverbial by nature |

**What actually happens.** The adverbial *due to* has been standard in American English for the better part of a century. It appears in national newspapers, in federal regulations, in airline announcements, and in the prose of writers nobody would call careless, and the major descriptive usage guides accept it without qualification. The prohibition survives in three places: older style manuals, a handful of standardized tests, and copy editors trained on both.

**What to do about it.** *Because of* is correct under every analysis, in every position, for every reader — it costs nothing to prefer it, and that is the only practical argument for the rule. Reserve *due to* for the positions the rule allows, after *be* or attached to a noun, where it is not merely permitted but often better: *increases due to inflation* is tighter than *increases that were caused by inflation*. And note that fronting draws the most fire — *Due to fog, …* at the head of a sentence is the version an editor is likeliest to change.

## What each purpose phrase takes

The single most common purpose error in English is putting an infinitive where a gerund belongs, or the reverse. The two lists do not mix.

| Takes a bare infinitive | Takes a gerund or a noun |
|---|---|
| *in order to* **do** | *for the purpose of* **doing** |
| *so as to* **do** | *with a view to* **doing** |
| *in an effort to* **do** | *with an eye to* **doing** |
| *in an attempt to* **do** | *with the aim of* **doing** |
| *with intent to* **do** | *with the intention of* **doing** |
| *to* **do** (the plain infinitive of purpose) | *for the sake of* **doing** |
| | *for fear of* **doing** |
| | *in the hope of* **doing** |
| | *in the interest of* **doing** |

Four more belong on the right-hand side but take a **noun only**, never a gerund: *for the benefit of*, *in furtherance of*, *in pursuance of*, and *in pursuit of*. Nothing at all belongs in both columns.

**The *of* is the tell.** Every phrase in the right-hand column ends in *of* or is built on one, and every phrase in the left-hand column ends in bare *to* with no *of* in sight. *With intent to* **defraud** and *with the intention of* **defrauding** are the pair that makes the rule visible: same meaning, opposite grammar, and the only thing that changed is the *of*.

***With a view to reduce costs* is the error to watch for**, because the *to* in *with a view to* is a preposition and not an infinitive marker. The test is substitution: if a noun fits the slot — *with a view to reduction* — then the verb has to be *-ing*. The same test sorts the whole *to*-plus-gerund family outside this file: *look forward to* **seeing** you (never *look forward to see*), *object to* **paying** (never *object to pay*), *be accustomed to* **working** nights (never *accustomed to work*), *be used to* **getting up** early. In every one of them *to* is a preposition, and the giveaway is that a noun fits: *look forward to the weekend*, *object to the fee*, *accustomed to the noise*.

| **by** | means and agency, plus the *by accident / by mistake / by design* frame | [02-core-relational.md](02-core-relational.md#by) |
| **to** | the plain infinitive of purpose: *she stopped to think* | [02-core-relational.md](02-core-relational.md#to) |
| **at** | the trigger of an emotion: *angry at the delay*, *delighted at the news* | [01-core-in-on-at.md](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) |
| **on** | the *on purpose / on a whim / on principle* frame | [01-core-in-on-at.md](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) |
| **in** | the *in response / in retaliation / in anger* frame | [01-core-in-on-at.md](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) |

**Died of, died from, died with.** *Died of* names the disease itself (*died of cancer*); *died from* names an injury or a process (*died from blood loss*, *died from exposure*); *died with* names something present but not responsible (*died with the disease, not of it*) and has become a term of art in medicine. All three are standard American English, the *of*/*from* line is soft, and *of* is the older and slightly more formal choice.

## The one rule that covers the whole file

**No preposition in this file can take a *that*-clause.** Every one of them has a clause-taking counterpart formed with *that* or *the fact that*, and the counterpart is a conjunction, not a preposition.

There is exactly one crack in the wall, and it is worth knowing precisely: **[*over*](#over) takes a *wh*-clause** — *a dispute over whether the vote was binding*, *confusion over how the money was spent*, *a fight over who pays* — and nothing else in this file does. Even *over* refuses *that*: ***an argument over that he lied*** is not English. [*What with*](#what-with) takes a **small clause** (*what with the kids being sick*), which is not finite either. Those two aside, the ban is total.

| Preposition + noun phrase | Conjunction + clause |
|---|---|
| *because of* the rain | *because* it rained |
| *due to* the rain | *due to the fact that* it rained |
| *on account of* the rain | *on account of the fact that* it rained |
| *owing to* the delay | *owing to the fact that* it was delayed |
| *in light of* the report | *in light of the fact that* the report says so |
| *in view of* his age | *in view of the fact that* he is eighty |
| *on the grounds of* fraud | *on the grounds that* he committed fraud |
| *on the pretext of* a taillight | *on the pretext that* the taillight was out |
| *by virtue of* her office | *by virtue of the fact that* she holds the office |
| *for fear of* waking her | *for fear that* she would wake |
| *in the hope of* settling | *in the hope that* it would settle |
| *for the purpose of* settling it | *in order that* it might be settled |
| *with intent to* defraud | *with the intent that* the buyer be deceived |
| *by reason of* insanity | *by reason that* he was insane (archaic) |
| *attributable to* the fog | *attributable to the fact that* it was foggy |
| *as a result of* the delay | *as a result of the fact that* it was delayed |
| *over* the fee | *over* **whether** the fee applied (wh-clause only) |
| *caused by* the storm | — no clause form at all; recast with *because* |
| *secondary to* diabetes | — no clause form at all; recast with *because* |
| *in furtherance of* the plan | — no clause form; recast with *so that* or *in order that* |

Every *fact that* form is grammatical and every one is heavy. When you find yourself reaching for one, the plain conjunction is almost always available and always shorter: *because it rained* beats *due to the fact that it rained* by six syllables.
