Place and position#

The prepositions that answer where? — the ones that fix a thing in space relative to another thing without moving it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file locates something. It takes a landmark — a table, a river, a crowd, a city line — and places a second thing with respect to it: higher, lower, beside, inside, behind, or spread across. Three things unite the group and cause almost all the trouble. First, the static/dynamic split: nearly every word here has a twin sense of motion that lives in movement and direction, and the same form does both jobs — the boat is under the bridge is position, the boat went under the bridge is a path. The two places English marks that split with different forms are the two learners most often get wrong: in / into and on / onto. She was in the car is position and she got into the car is motion; the cat is on the roof is position and the cat jumped onto the roof is motion. Everywhere else in this file, one form covers both and only the verb tells you which you have. Second, the pairs that look interchangeable and are not: above/over, below/under, among/between, beside/besides, amid/among, in/within, and near/nearby each hinge on one precise distinction, given in the Contrast line of every entry.

Third, and most important grammatically: not one of these prepositions takes a that-clause. You cannot write behind he was standing, within the deadline passed, or beyond that he admitted it. What they take is a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (beside me, between you and me). A smaller set also takes a gerund — not above lying, against parking here, upon signing, alongside working nights, an argument over paying, questions around hiring, beyond saving, behind closing the plant, past caring, outside of being tired — and each of those says so in its own Complement line. When one of these words takes a verb, that verb is an -ing form and never an infinitive: ✗ against to park here, ✗ upon to sign, ✗ beyond to save. A still smaller set takes a wh-clause, which is a finite clause: beyond what he told us, nowhere near what we needed, the difference between what he said and what he did, an argument over who pays. Those four words — beyond, near, between, and over — are the only ones in this file with a finite complement, and the rule for every other entry here is the flat one: noun phrase in, clause out. A word that looks like a preposition but is followed by a plain subject and verb has become a conjunction (before he arrived) or an adverb (I've been here before), and the object test settles which: the preposition always has an object; the adverb and the particle do not.

Owned by other files. Nine prepositions are cross-referenced here rather than repeated: in, on, and at live in the core three, and of, to, for, from, by, and with live in core relational prepositions. Their weak forms — of /əv/ → /ə/, to /tə/, for /fɚ/, at /ət/, from /frəm/, with /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/ — belong to those entries and are not repeated here. The multi-word place phrases — next to, on top of, in front of, close to, inside of, outside of, and out of — are in complex and multi-word prepositions. Where a word here also has a motion sense or a time sense, the entry says so and links to it.

Vertical position: above, below, and on top#

The words that stack one thing over another. The rule that governs all of them is that height alone is not alignment: above and below claim only that one thing is higher or lower, while over and under line the two up and usually let the upper one cover the lower. Register sorts the rest — beneath is literary, underneath is emphatic and physical, atop and upon are formal.

above#

Pronunciation: /əˈbʌv/ · uh-BUV; the first syllable is always the reduced /ə/, and there is no further weak form Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund in the fixed not above doing X idiom (she's not above lying). Never a finite clause — write above the line that says "total", not above it says "total". Also an adverb (the sky above), an adjective (the above figures), and a noun (see the above); the preposition alone takes an object. Pattern: [be-verb] above [noun phrase] · [noun] above [noun phrase] · not above [verb-ing] · above all Contrast: The above/over line is height versus alignment. Above asserts only that one thing is higher; over adds vertical alignment and often covering or crossing. A lamp can be above or over the table, but a blanket can only be over it, and a village can be above the valley without being over anything. For measurements and scales, above is the word — above sea level, above average, above freezing — while over takes quantities and counts (over fifty people). Against on: on requires contact, above forbids it.

Higher than something, with air in between. That single geometric fact generates every other sense: higher on a map, higher on a measuring scale, higher in a hierarchy, and — by a short moral leap — too high in character to stoop to something. The one thing above never means is touching.

1. Higher than, without contact. A framed map hung above the couch. 2. Higher on a scale of measurement. The town sits 6,000 feet above sea level. 3. Greater than a stated number or level. Anything above ninety is an A. 4. Higher in rank, authority, or importance. There is no rank above general. 5. Too honorable or proud to do something. He is not above asking for help. 6. Earlier on the page, in written reference. The rates listed above have changed.

  1. A hawk circled above the parking lot. (1)
  2. She keeps the good glasses above the microwave. (1)
  3. Denver is a mile above sea level. (2)
  4. The reservoir is twelve feet above normal. (2)
  5. Temperatures stayed above forty all week. (3)
  6. Anything above two hundred dollars needs a second signature. (3)
  7. A lieutenant is one grade above a sergeant. (4)
  8. She put the team above her own record. (4)
  9. He is not above taking credit for someone else's work. (5)
  10. No one in that office is above suspicion. (5)
  11. The exceptions noted above apply only to renewals. (6)
  12. Everything above this line is a draft. (6)

atop#

Pronunciation: /əˈtɑp/ · uh-TAHP; also /əˈtɔp/ · uh-TAWP in dialects that keep the cot/caught distinction; the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form Type: compound (historically a- + top) · Register: formal or literary; common in journalism, rare in speech Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun only. No gerund, no clause. Atop of is nonstandard — the of belongs to on top of, not to atop. Pattern: [be-verb] atop [noun phrase] · [noun] atop [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] atop [noun phrase] Contrast: Atop is on top of compressed into one word, and it is the more formal of the two — no American says atop out loud at the kitchen table. Unlike above, it requires contact; unlike plain on, it insists the surface is a high or prominent one. On the table is neutral; atop the table sounds like a museum label.

Resting on the highest surface of something, in contact with it. It is a written word — headlines, captions, nature writing, and real-estate copy love it — and it survives mainly because it is shorter than on top of and grander than on. It also has a small figurative life in the language of rankings and standings.

1. On the highest surface of a physical thing. A weathervane atop the barn. 2. At the top of a ranking or standing (journalistic). The Dodgers finished atop the division.

  1. A single crow sat atop the utility pole. (1)
  2. The chapel atop the hill is visible from the highway. (1)
  3. Snow lay six inches deep atop the woodpile. (1)
  4. She placed the last shingle atop the stack. (1)
  5. The company ended the quarter atop the industry. (2)
  6. He has stayed atop the rankings for three seasons. (2)

See also: on top of, above, over, upon, on

below#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈloʊ/ · bih-LOH; weak /bəˈloʊ/ · buh-LOH Type: compound (historically be- + low) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No gerund, no clause. Also an adverb (the deck below, see below) and, in nautical use, an adverb meaning belowdecks; the preposition takes an object. Pattern: [be-verb] below [noun phrase] · [noun] below [noun phrase] · below [number or level] Contrast: The below/under line mirrors above/over. Below claims only lower position; under adds vertical alignment and often covering or containment. You sleep under a blanket, never below one, and you camp below the tree line, not under it. Measurements and scales take below — below freezing, below zero, below average — while under takes quantities and ages (under thirty, under twenty dollars). Against beneath, which is the same idea in a literary register.

Lower than something, with no claim about contact or alignment. Like its opposite above, it works on maps and on scales as readily as in the air: downstream is below, downhill is below, and any number smaller than the reference point is below it. It is the everyday word for lower position when nothing is doing any covering.

1. Lower than, without contact. The apartment below ours flooded. 2. Farther down a slope, a river, or a coast. The dam is two miles below the falls. 3. Less than a stated number, level, or standard. Sales came in below projections. 4. Lower in rank or grade. Everyone below the rank of captain reports here. 5. Later on the page, in written reference. The footnotes below explain the method.

  1. There is a crawlspace below the kitchen floor. (1)
  2. The clouds sat below the summit all morning. (1)
  3. The town lies just below the reservoir. (2)
  4. Fishing is better below the bridge than above it. (2)
  5. It stayed below freezing for nine days straight. (3)
  6. Her cholesterol is finally below two hundred. (3)
  7. No one below director level saw the memo. (4)
  8. He was two years below me in school. (4)
  9. See the table below for the current rates. (5)
  10. Everything below the dotted line is optional. (5)

beneath#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈniθ/ · bih-NEETH; weak /bəˈniθ/ · buh-NEETH; the th is voiceless /θ/, not the /ð/ of breathe Type: compound (historically be- + neath) · Register: formal or literary Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No finite clause. A gerund appears only in the frozen disdain idiom (beneath answering, beneath discussing) and is rare. Also an adverb (the valley beneath). Pattern: [be-verb] beneath [noun phrase] · [verb] beneath [noun phrase] · beneath [abstract noun] (beneath contempt) Variants: 'neath /niθ/ — a poetic clipping that belongs to verse, hymn, and song lyric ('neath the willow); never write it in prose Contrast: Beneath is the literary member of the under / underneath / below family and is almost never the natural spoken choice: Americans say the dog is under the table, and beneath the table sounds like a novel. What beneath owns outright is the moral sense — beneath contempt, beneath him, beneath her dignity — where under and below cannot go. It also handles the metaphor of a hidden interior better than either (beneath the surface).

Directly under something, usually with the weight or nearness of the upper thing felt. It is under in a raised register, plus two senses of its own: the surface that conceals something, and the standard that something falls short of. Reach for it when you want the sentence to sound written rather than spoken.

1. Directly under, often with weight or pressure. The porch steps creaked beneath him. 2. Under the surface of, hidden by an appearance. Beneath the cheerful tone was real panic. 3. Unworthy of, too low for someone's dignity. The remark was beneath her.

  1. The ice groaned beneath their boots. (1)
  2. Roots had buckled the sidewalk beneath the maple. (1)
  3. Beneath the fresh paint, the wood was rotten. (2)
  4. Beneath all the legal language, the offer is simple. (2)
  5. That kind of gossip is beneath contempt. (3)
  6. He seemed to think manual labor was beneath him. (3)

over#

Pronunciation: /ˈoʊvər/ · OH-ver; the second syllable is the r-colored /ɚ/ in all American speech and keeps it even unstressed, so over has no weak form of the kind of and to have — what happens in fast speech is that the whole word loses its beat, not that its vowels change. The r-less /ˈoʊvə/ is British, not an American reduction. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund (an argument over paying the bill, no control over spending) and a wh-clause (an argument over who pays, a fight over what to charge) in the dispute and authority senses. Never a that-clause — ✗ an argument over that he should pay; to hang a finite clause on it you need a noun to carry it (an argument over the claim that he should pay). Also an adverb (come over), an adjective (it's over), and a verb particle (think it over, hand it over), none of which takes an object; the preposition always has one. Pattern: [be-verb] over [noun phrase] · [noun] over [noun phrase] · over [number] · [choose/prefer] [X] over [Y] · stranded: the beam it hangs over Contrast: Against above: over implies vertical alignment and permits covering, above asserts only greater height. A tarp goes over the woodpile, never above it. Against on: on is contact and support, over is coverage from the top — the cloth on the table lies flat, the cloth over the table drapes down the sides. Against under, its exact opposite in every sense here. On sense 9, against beyond: over the ridge names a barrier you would cross to get there, beyond the ridge names only what lies farther out and crosses nothing. Note also that over is the most widely distributed word in this catalog: the path sense (jumped over the fence) is in movement and direction, the span-of-time sense (over the summer) is in time, the about sense (a fight over money) is in topic and reference, the bare quantity sense (over fifty) reappears in agent, instrument, and manner, and the preference sense in comparison and opposition. Senses 4 and 6 below are the positional face of those last two and are kept here because they fall straight out of the vertical geometry.

Positioned higher than something and, characteristically, lined up with it — which is why over slides so easily into the idea of covering. From physical coverage it reaches authority (one person positioned over another), quantity (a number higher than another), preference (one option placed above another), the channel a message travels along, and the far side of any barrier you would have to go over to reach. The positional senses are collected here; the moving, temporal, and topical senses live elsewhere in this catalog.

1. Higher than and vertically aligned with; spanning. A footbridge over the creek. 2. Covering, resting on top of and concealing. A sheet over the furniture. 3. Across the whole surface or extent of. She spilled coffee over the keyboard. 4. More than, with a number or amount (see also agent, instrument, and manner). Over two hundred people showed up. 5. In a position of authority above. She has three shift leads over her. 6. Chosen or ranked ahead of an alternative (see also comparison and opposition). They took the older candidate over the younger one. 7. By way of a channel or a medium. We settled the whole thing over the phone. 8. Past and no longer troubled by. She is finally over the flu. 9. On the far side of a barrier you would have to cross. Their place is over the ridge.

  1. The old sign still hangs over the entrance. (1)
  2. Power lines run over the whole subdivision. (1)
  3. He pulled the blanket over his head. (2)
  4. There was a tarp over the boat all winter. (2)
  5. A rash had spread over both arms by Tuesday. (3)
  6. Papers were scattered over every surface in the room. (3)
  7. The repair came to a little over nine hundred dollars. (4)
  8. Over half the class failed the first quiz. (4)
  9. No one over her signed off on the change. (5)
  10. He was given authority over both warehouses. (5)
  11. I'd take a long drive over a short flight any day. (6)
  12. The committee chose the local firm over two national ones. (6)
  13. They worked out the terms over the phone in ten minutes. (7)
  14. The alert went out over the emergency channel at four. (7)
  15. She is finally over the worst of the flu. (8)
  16. He is not over the layoff and may not be for a while. (8)
  17. Her cousins live over the border in Nogales. (9)
  18. The next town over the ridge has one gas station. (9)

under#

Pronunciation: /ˈʌndər/ · UN-der; the first vowel never reduces, so there is no weak form. What casual American speech does instead is drop the /d/ after the nasal, giving /ˈʌnɚ/ · UN-ner (under the bed → unner the bed). The r-less /ˈʌndə/ is British, not an American reduction. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No finite clause, no gerund — write under the impression that he had paid (the that-clause belongs to impression, not to under), never under he had paid. Also an adverb (the boat went under) and a prefix (underpaid); the preposition takes an object. Pattern: [be-verb] under [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] under [noun phrase] · under [number or age] · under [authority] · under [process noun] (under review) Contrast: Against below: under aligns and often covers, below only ranks lower. You are under a bridge when you pass through its shadow and below it when you are downstream. Against underneath: underneath is the emphatic, physical form and has none of under's abstract senses — you cannot say underneath new management or underneath thirty. Against beneath: the same relation in a literary register. And note that scales take below (below freezing) while counts and ages take under (under thirty). Under has two other homes: the path sense (rolled under the fence) is in movement and direction, and the bare quantity sense (under fifty) reappears in agent, instrument, and manner.

Directly lower than something and usually covered by it. That covering is what lets under become the workhorse abstraction of the group: things covered by an authority, by a rule, by a process, or by a heading are all under it. Of the four low-position words, this is by a wide margin the most useful and the most polysemous.

1. Directly below and typically covered by. The cat is under the bed. 2. Worn or placed beneath another layer. He had a T-shirt under his sweater. 3. Less than a number, an age, or an amount. Everything on this rack is under twenty dollars. 4. Subject to the authority, rule, or command of. The park is under federal jurisdiction. 5. Undergoing a stated process or condition. The bridge has been under construction for two years. 6. Classified within a heading or a name. The reservation is under Alvarez.

  1. Your keys are under the newspaper. (1)
  2. We waited out the storm under an overpass. (1)
  3. She wore thermals under her jeans. (2)
  4. Put a towel under the pot before you set it down. (2)
  5. Anyone under eighteen needs a signed waiver. (3)
  6. We finished the job under budget for once. (3)
  7. The clinic operates under state supervision. (4)
  8. He served under three different chiefs. (4)
  9. The proposal is still under review. (5)
  10. Two of the three claims are under investigation. (5)
  11. You'll find it filed under "permits." (6)
  12. She published under a pen name for a decade. (6)

underneath#

Pronunciation: /ˌʌndərˈniθ/ · un-der-NEETH; also stressed on the first syllable, /ˈʌndərˌniθ/ UN-der-neeth, when it precedes a noun; the th is voiceless /θ/. No vowel-reducing weak form — the stressed /i/ has nothing to reduce to — but casual speech drops the /d/ after the nasal exactly as it does in under, giving /ˌʌnɚˈniθ/ · un-ner-NEETH Type: compound (under + neath) · Register: neutral, slightly emphatic Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb (look underneath) and a noun (the underneath of the car); the preposition takes an object. Pattern: [be-verb] underneath [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] underneath [noun phrase] · stranded: the mat it was underneath Contrast: Underneath is the emphatic, strictly physical form of under: it insists that the upper thing covers and usually hides the lower one. That is the whole difference, and it is why underneath refuses every abstract sense under has — underneath new management, underneath thirty, and underneath investigation are all wrong. Against beneath, which says the same thing in a literary register, and below, which requires no covering at all.

Directly under and covered by, with the covering emphasized. When someone says look underneath rather than look under, they mean the thing is hidden. The only figurative extension it allows is the one that keeps the covering metaphor intact: an outward appearance with something concealed behind it.

1. Directly under and concealed or covered by. The spare key is underneath the mat. 2. Behind an outward appearance. Underneath all that swagger he is anxious.

  1. There is a whole box of photographs underneath the stairs. (1)
  2. She found the receipt underneath a stack of mail. (1)
  3. Something was rusting underneath the truck. (1)
  4. Underneath the gruffness he is a soft touch. (2)
  5. Underneath the confident delivery, the argument was thin. (2)

upon#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑn/ · uh-PAHN; also /əˈpɔn/ · uh-PAWN; weak /əˈpən/ · uh-puhn, and in fast speech the whole word flattens toward /əˈpɑn/ with no stress of its own Type: compound (up + on) · Register: formal, literary, or legal; also fixed idioms at every register Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund in the "immediately after" sense (upon arriving, upon signing the lease). Never a finite clause — write upon learning that he had left, never upon he had left. Pattern: [be-verb] upon [noun phrase] · [verb] upon [noun phrase] · upon [verb-ing] · [noun] upon [same noun] (row upon row) Contrast: Anywhere upon appears, on is also grammatical. Upon adds no meaning at all — only formality — so choose it for register, for rhythm, or because a fixed phrase demands it (once upon a time, row upon row, upon further review). Overusing it is the clearest sign of writing that is trying to sound official. Against atop, which insists the surface is high; upon makes no such claim. The motion sense (came upon a clearing) is handled in movement and direction.

A formal equivalent of on, identical in meaning and higher in register. It earns its keep in three places: legal and ceremonial prose, a set of fixed phrases that will not take on, and the "immediately after" construction with a gerund, where it is genuinely more idiomatic than on.

1. On a surface, in formal or literary style. A wreath upon the door. 2. Immediately after (with a noun or gerund). Upon arrival, please check in at the desk. 3. In fixed phrases and repeated-noun intensifiers. Mile upon mile of empty road.

  1. A heavy silence settled upon the room. (1)
  2. He laid the wreath upon the marker. (1)
  3. Upon signing, you receive the keys. (2)
  4. Upon further review, the call was reversed. (2)
  5. Wave upon wave of new applications came in. (3)
  6. Once upon a time there was a mill here. (3)

Beside, near, and facing#

Adjacency, at four degrees of precision: contact under pressure (against), parallel alignment (alongside), plain side-by-side position (beside), and unspecified proximity (near) — plus the three words for standing across a gap and looking at what is on the other side, facing, overlooking, and opposite. Two entries here are warnings rather than recommendations — nearby is not really a preposition in American English, and opposite has mostly lost the spatial sense to across from, which is the gap facing and overlooking have moved into.

against#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡɛnst/ · uh-GENST; also /əˈɡeɪnst/ · uh-GAYNST (minority American, standard British); unstressed it reduces to /əˈɡɛn(t)s/ · uh-GENS, with the /t/ squeezed out between the /n/ and the /s/, and in fast speech the first syllable can vanish entirely, giving 'genst Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (a rule against parking here, advised against going). Never a finite clause — write against the claim that he lied, not against he lied. Pattern: [be-verb / lean / prop / rest] against [noun phrase] · [noun] against [noun phrase] (backdrop) · against [verb-ing] · stranded: the wall it was leaning against Contrast: Against is the contact-under-pressure preposition, and that pressure is what separates it from its neighbors. On is contact with support from below; against is contact with force from the side. Next to and beside are adjacency with no contact at all: the ladder beside the wall is standing free, the ladder against the wall is leaning on it. For the backdrop sense, nothing else will do — dark against the sky cannot become dark on the sky. The forcible-motion sense (threw it against the door) is in movement and direction, and sense 3, opposition proper, is developed at length in comparison and opposition.

Touching something and pressing on it. Everything in this entry is the resting state that force leaves behind: a thing leaning, a thing braced, a thing outlined by whatever sits behind it. From physical pressure the word generalizes to opposition of every kind, which is why the same syllable covers a ladder on a wall and a vote in a legislature.

1. Touching under pressure; leaning or braced. The bike is against the fence. 2. Seen with something behind it as a backdrop. Bare branches against a gray sky. 3. In opposition to a rule, a person, or a side. That's against the law. 4. Providing protection or coverage from. Insured against flood damage.

  1. Two shovels leaned against the garage door. (1)
  2. He stood with his back against the counter. (1)
  3. The pressed flowers show up beautifully against the dark paper. (2)
  4. Her red coat was the only color against all that snow. (2)
  5. Parking here is against the building rules. (3)
  6. Three of the five directors voted against the merger. (3)
  7. The policy protects against theft but not against flooding. (4)
  8. Everyone in the unit is vaccinated against hepatitis. (4)

alongside#

Pronunciation: /əˌlɔŋˈsaɪd/ · uh-lawng-SYD; also /əˌlɑŋˈsaɪd/ · uh-lahng-SYD in cot/caught-merged speech; stress shifts to the first syllable before a noun in fast speech Type: compound (along + side) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund in the "at the same time as" sense (studied alongside working nights). No finite clause of any kind, and never a that-clause. Also an adverb (a tug pulled alongside), which takes no object at all; the preposition always has one. Pattern: [be-verb / pull / run] alongside [noun phrase] · [verb] alongside [noun phrase] · alongside [verb-ing] Contrast: Alongside adds parallel orientation to the plain adjacency of beside and next to: it wants two long things lying the same way, which is why it started life on the water and still sounds right with boats, trains, roads, and rows. A chair beside the desk is fine; a chair alongside the desk implies the chair is lined up with it. Alongside of is redundant — drop the of. And it is always one word.

Beside and parallel to, close enough to be lined up with. Its nautical origin still governs the physical sense — you pull alongside a dock the way a hull comes to rest against a pier. The figurative senses keep the parallelism intact: people who work alongside each other are doing the same kind of thing at the same time, as equals rather than as one under the other, and once the equality drops away what is left is bare simultaneity — a document released alongside another one.

1. Beside and parallel to, physically. The bike path runs alongside the highway. 2. Working or existing in parallel with, as an equal. She trained alongside professional dancers. 3. Together with; at the same time as. The report came out alongside a formal apology.

  1. The tugboat eased alongside the freighter. (1)
  2. There is a drainage ditch alongside the whole east fence. (1)
  3. A photographer jogged alongside the lead runner. (1)
  4. He learned the trade alongside his father. (2)
  5. The new system will run alongside the old one until June. (2)
  6. She finished the degree alongside working two jobs. (3)
  7. The findings were published alongside a lengthy rebuttal. (3)
  8. The tax cut passed alongside a two-year spending freeze. (3)

beside#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈsaɪd/ · bih-SYD; weak /bəˈsaɪd/ · buh-SYD Type: compound (historically be- + side) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun — beside me, never beside I. No clause, no gerund. Unlike besides, it is never an adverb and never a conjunct: you cannot write Beside, it's late. Pattern: [be-verb / sit / stand / walk] beside [noun phrase] · [noun] beside [noun phrase] · beside the point · beside [reflexive] (beside himself) Contrast: Beside and besides are different words, and confusing them is the single most common error in this entire category. Beside is position — at the side of. Besides is addition or exception — in addition to, apart from. The test is substitution: if next to fits, the word is beside (sit beside me); if in addition to or apart from fits, the word is besides (Besides me, only two people came). Beside also differs from alongside, which demands parallel alignment, and from against, which demands contact.

At the side of something, close by. It is the plainest adjacency word in English and carries no implication of contact, alignment, or facing. Two idioms have drifted away from the literal sense and are worth learning whole: beside the point, meaning irrelevant, and beside oneself, meaning overcome by an emotion.

Front, back, and beyond#

The axis running away from the observer. American English has largely handed the front position to in front of and kept before for courtrooms and ceremony; at the back, behind is doing all the work, with the regional in back of alongside it. Farther out, past is reckoned from a landmark and beyond from a limit.

Extent, spread, and orientation#

Position described by how something lies across a space rather than by a single point. Across cuts a line, along follows one, around encircles, astride straddles, and throughout fills. Also an adverb (the fire went out), an adjective (the truth is out), a noun (a called third strike is an out), a verb (the story outed him), and a very productive particle (hand it out, figure it out) — none of which takes an object, and the preposition always does: he leaned out is the adverb, he leaned out the window is the preposition. Pattern: [verb] out [opening] (look out the window) · [live / drive] out [route or direction] · out back / out west / out east Variants: out of — the general-purpose form; see out of Contrast: This is one of the sharpest American/British splits in the whole class. Americans say he leaned out the window; the British say out of the window, and out the window strikes them as substandard. In American English the bare form is not only acceptable but preferred with openings. Where the two agree: with containers, sources, and materials you need out of on both sides of the Atlantic — out of the box, out of money, made out of oak. Against outside, which is a static position rather than a passage through an opening.

Through an opening to the outside, or along a route leading away from a center. Bare prepositional out is a narrow word with a strong American accent: it lives on windows, doors, and highways, and it hands everything else to out of. Its enormous life as an adverb, adjective, noun, and particle is separate and is not the preposition.

1. Through an opening to the outside. She was staring out the window. 2. Along a road or in a direction leading away from town. They live out Route 9, past the quarry.

  4. Their place is a few miles out the old highway. (2)
  5. The dogs are out back if you want to see them. (2)
  6. Her people are all out west somewhere. (2)

throughout#

Pronunciation: /θruˈaʊt/ · throo-OWT; the initial cluster is /θr/, not /tr/; stress falls on the second syllable, so in connected speech the first one reduces to weak /θrəˈaʊt/ · thruh-OWT Type: compound (through + out) · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb with no object at all (the house has hardwood throughout), which is a common and fully standard use. Pattern: throughout [bounded space] · throughout [organization or region] · [noun phrase] ... throughout (adverb, no object) Contrast: Against through: through traces a path in one side and out the other, throughout saturates every part with no path at all. Water ran through the house is a leak; there is water damage throughout the house is an insurance claim. Against all over, the spoken equivalent — toys all over the house and toys throughout the house mean the same thing at different registers. Against across, which distributes over a surface or a set rather than filling a volume. The temporal sense is developed in time.

In every part of a space or a body. The word is built to be exhaustive — it claims completeness in a way in and across do not — which is why it belongs to inspection reports, specifications, and any prose that needs to say everywhere inside this thing, with no exceptions.

1. In every part of a space or a structure. There is Wi-Fi throughout the building. 2. In every part of an organization, a region, or a body. The change was announced throughout the district.

  1. The original tile is intact throughout the first floor. (1)
  2. Smoke detectors are required throughout the residence. (1)
  3. Recycling bins are placed throughout the campus. (1)
  4. Morale improved throughout the company after the announcement. (2)
  5. The species is found throughout the Southwest. (2)

Pronunciation: /ʌp/ · UP. No weak form — the vowel is already the lax /ʌ/ and has nothing further to reduce to, so unstressed up simply loses its beat (up the street /ʌp ðə ˈstrit/) without changing quality. The same is true of its opposite, down. In up to, only the second word weakens: UP-tuh, never uh-TOO Type: simple · Register: neutral; the "farther along" sense is characteristically American and informal Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Up is also an adverb (stand up), a verb particle (look it up, ran up a bill), an adjective (the system is up), a noun (ups and downs), and a verb (upped the price). The particle/preposition test is movement: you can say ran a bill up but not ran the hill up, because in ran up the hill the hill is the object of the preposition. Pattern: [be-verb] up [linear object] · [verb] up [linear object] · up the street / road / coast · up north Contrast: The mirror of down, and it inherits the same American quirk: up the block usually implies no elevation at all, only distance in a direction the speaker treats as away. Against above and atop, which are static height with no path; up implies a line you could travel. The one thing to be careful about is the word class — up is far more often a particle than a preposition, and the movement test above settles it every time. The motion sense is in movement and direction.

At or toward a higher point on something, or simply farther along it in the direction the speaker treats as away. As with down, American usage has drained most of the elevation out of the everyday sense, leaving a vague directional marker that pairs with down and varies by region — New Yorkers go up to the Bronx, New Englanders up to Maine, and neither is about altitude. What is left of the vertical idea survives cleanly in rankings and in two idioms that keep an object and therefore keep the preposition: up your alley, up the creek.

1. At a higher point on a slope or a vertical surface. The cabin is up the hill from the road. 2. Farther along; away from here (no elevation implied). He lives up the block from the school. 3. Toward the north, or inland along a river or coast. They have a place up north. 4. Higher in a ranking, a scale, or a sequence. She moved up the depth chart fast. 5. In the fixed idioms up someone's alley and up the creek. A job like that is right up her alley.
