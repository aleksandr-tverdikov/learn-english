Place and position#

The prepositions that answer where? — the ones that fix a thing in space relative to another thing without moving it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file locates something. It takes a landmark — a table, a river, a crowd, a city line — and places a second thing with respect to it: higher, lower, beside, inside, behind, or spread across. Three things unite the group and cause almost all the trouble. First, the static/dynamic split: nearly every word here has a twin sense of motion that lives in movement and direction, and the same form does both jobs — the boat is under the bridge is position, the boat went under the bridge is a path. The two places English marks that split with different forms are the two learners most often get wrong: in / into and on / onto. She was in the car is position and she got into the car is motion; the cat is on the roof is position and the cat jumped onto the roof is motion. Everywhere else in this file, one form covers both and only the verb tells you which you have. Second, the pairs that look interchangeable and are not: above/over, below/under, among/between, beside/besides, amid/among, in/within, and near/nearby each hinge on one precise distinction, given in the Contrast line of every entry.

Third, and most important grammatically: not one of these prepositions takes a that-clause. You cannot write behind he was standing, within the deadline passed, or beyond that he admitted it. What they take is a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (beside me, between you and me). A smaller set also takes a gerund — not above lying, against parking here, upon signing, alongside working nights, an argument over paying, questions around hiring, beyond saving, behind closing the plant, past caring, outside of being tired, on the edge of quitting — and each of those says so in its own Complement line. When one of these words takes a verb, that verb is an -ing form and never an infinitive: ✗ against to park here, ✗ upon to sign, ✗ beyond to save. A still smaller set takes a wh-clause, which is a finite clause: beyond what he told us, nowhere near what we needed, the difference between what he said and what he did, an argument over who pays, a diner across from where the depot used to be, keep abreast of what the committee decides. Those six — beyond, near, between, over, across from, and abreast of — are the only ones in this file with a finite complement, and the rule for every other entry here is the flat one: noun phrase in, clause out. A word that looks like a preposition but is followed by a plain subject and verb has become a conjunction (before he arrived) or an adverb (I've been here before), and the object test settles which: the preposition always has an object; the adverb and the particle do not.

Owned by other files. Nine prepositions are cross-referenced here rather than repeated: in, on, and at live in the core three, and of, to, for, from, by, and with live in core relational prepositions. Their weak forms — of /əv/ → /ə/, to /tə/, for /fɚ/, at /ət/, from /frəm/, with /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/ — belong to those entries and are not repeated here. The multi-word place phrases — next to, on top of, in front of, close to, inside of, outside of, and out of — are in complex and multi-word prepositions. A second group of multi-word phrases is developed here instead, because each one names a part of the landmark — its top, bottom, foot, head, edge, corner, center, or side — rather than a relation to the landmark as a whole: at the top of, at the bottom of, at the foot of, at the head of, at the edge of, at the corner of, in the center of, to the left of, and to the right of, together with the facing phrases across from and kitty-corner from. Where a word here also has a motion sense or a time sense, the entry says so and links to it.

Vertical position: above, below, and on top#

The words that stack one thing over another. The rule that governs all of them is that height alone is not alignment: above and below claim only that one thing is higher or lower, while over and under line the two up and usually let the upper one cover the lower. Register sorts the rest — beneath is literary, underneath is emphatic and physical, atop and upon are formal. Beside the simple words sit the landmark phrases that name an end of the object rather than a relation to it — at the top of, at the bottom of, at the foot of, and at the head of — plus the regional over top of and the biblical under the shadow of.

Contrast
The above/over line is height versus alignment. Above asserts only that one thing is higher; over adds vertical alignment and often covering or crossing. A lamp can be above or over the table, but a blanket can only be over it, and a village can be above the valley without being over anything. For measurements and scales, above is the word — above sea level, above average, above freezing — while over takes quantities and counts (over fifty people). Against on: on requires contact, above forbids it.

Higher than something, with air in between. That single geometric fact generates every other sense: higher on a map, higher on a measuring scale, higher in a hierarchy, and — by a short moral leap — too high in character to stoop to something. The one thing above never means is touching.

1. Higher than, without contact. A framed map hung above the couch. 2. Higher on a scale of measurement. The town sits 6,000 feet above sea level. 3. Greater than a stated number or level. Anything above ninety is an A. 4. Higher in rank, authority, or importance. There is no rank above general. 5. Too honorable or proud to do something. He is not above asking for help. 6. Earlier on the page, in written reference. The rates listed above have changed.

Against below and under, which are simple prepositions naming a relation between two things; at the bottom of names a part of one thing and puts you at it — below the stairs is underneath them, at the bottom of the stairs is standing on the floor they end at. Against beneath, which is literary and cannot name a part at all.

The low end of something, treated as a place you can be at. The phrase does three jobs that the simple prepositions cannot: it picks out a specific part of an object rather than a relation to the whole of it, it works on flat things laid out in reading order (pages, columns, lists, charts) as readily as on vertical ones, and by an old metaphor it names the thing lying underneath a situation as its cause.

Being the underlying cause of a situation. Money is usually at the bottom of these arguments.

Against at the top of, which head can usually replace and which cannot always replace head. Two things belong to head alone. The first is the source of running water: the head of a river, a valley, a bay, or a hollow is where it begins, and the top of the river means nothing. The second is authority: at the head of the department means running it, while at the top of the department means only occupying its highest rung. The upper, leading, or source end of something the language gives a body to. From the physical end it reaches two places the plain vertical words cannot follow it to — the place a river or a valley starts, and the position of the person in charge. What holds the senses together is precedence: whatever is at the head comes first, whether in space, in order, or in rank.

1. At the upper end of a table, a bed, or a flight of stairs. He always sat at the head of the table. 2. At the source or upper end of a valley, bay, or river. The town grew up at the head of the bay. 3. In the first position of a line, a list, or a column of text. Her name was at the head of the waiting list. 4. Leading a procession, a column, or a march. The pipers marched at the head of the parade. 5. In charge of an organization, a family, or a delegation. She has been at the head of the agency since March.

Enclosure, inclusion, and the space in the middle#

Being surrounded by something, or being one of the things doing the surrounding. The distinctions that matter are countability (amid takes a mass, among takes individuals), individuation (between keeps its objects separate and named, among pools them), and boundary (inside and outside mark a physical enclosure, within marks a limit). Three phrases join them: in among for being down inside a mass of things, in the center of for the geometric middle, and home to for a place named from the side of whatever lives in it.

Extent, spread, and orientation#

Position described by how something lies across a space rather than by a single point. Across cuts a line, along follows one, around encircles, astride straddles, and throughout fills. The four short words in this section — down, off, out, up — are the ones most often mistaken for adverbs, and the test is always the same: the preposition has an object it cannot be separated from. The orientation phrases to the left of, to the right of, at the corner of, and at the edge of belong here as well, and so does over to, the one item in this file that most grammars would rather analyze as an adverb followed by a preposition.
