Time#

The prepositions that put an event on the clock and the calendar — when it happened, how long it lasted, and what it came before, after, or inside of.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file answers one of three questions: when (a point — before noon, around six), how long (a span — during the war, throughout the winter), or by when (a boundary — until Friday, within an hour). The trap that runs through the whole group is that the words do not cross between those three jobs. During names a period and cannot measure one, so during three hours is an error for for three hours; since names a starting point and cannot measure a stretch, so since three years is an error for for three years; ago measures a stretch and cannot name a point, so Monday ago is not English at all.

A second trap is grammatical rather than semantic. After, before, since, till, until, and archaic ere are prepositions when a noun phrase follows and conjunctions when a whole clause follows — after the show against after the show ended. Several more of them — after, before, since, past, through, over, around, about, behind, beyond, throughout — are adverbs or verb particles when nothing follows at all. The preposition takes an object; the adverb does not. We ate soon after is the adverb, we ate after the show is the preposition, and the difference is the noun phrase.

A third trap is the -ing form, and it is the one that produces the single commonest preposition error learners make. After, before, since, upon, prior to, subsequent to, ahead of, in advance of, at the time of, in the course of, and in the middle of all take a gerund — after eating, upon signing, prior to filing, in the middle of moving. Through and into take one after a measure phrase (halfway through cooking dinner, ten minutes into filming), and between takes two inside its paired frame (between finishing school and starting the job). During and until never take one at all: during eating and until leaving are errors, and the repairs are while eating or in the middle of eating, and until he left. That is why the Complement line is the first thing to read in every entry below.

The nine core prepositions carry a heavy share of the time load, but they have their own homes: in, on, and at live in the core: in, on, at, and of, to, for, from, by, and with in the core relational prepositions. The contrasts that involve them — by vs. until, for vs. since vs. ago, during vs. for, in vs. within, and the American quarter of three — are worked out in the entries below and gathered again at the end of this file.

Sequence: what comes before and what comes after#

The words that order two events on a line. Every one of them has a plain register and a heavy one: before and after are the neutral pair, prior to, subsequent to, and in advance of the bureaucratic ones, following and on the eve of the journalistic ones, upon the contractual one, post the finance-desk one, and ere the archaic one. Four of these are also conjunctions, so check what follows: a noun phrase means preposition, a subject-and-verb means conjunction.

after#

/ˈæftər/ · AF-ter · no true weak form — the vowel keeps its quality even when unstressed, which makes after one of the few prepositions a learner can always hear
Русский
после — позже точки или события; амер. о минутах после часа: ten after six
Type
simple
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (after eating) — never a finite clause. After the show ended is the conjunction, a different word class doing a different job. It does take the relative pronoun in after which (the vote, after which he resigned). After is also an adverb (we ate soon after) and an attributive adjective (the after party); the preposition always has an object.
Pattern
after [noun phrase] · after [verb-ing] · [measure phrase] after [noun phrase] · just, right, soon, shortly, or long after [noun phrase] · [clause], after which [clause]
Contrast
After is the plain word; following is its newsroom dress, subsequent to its legal one, post its finance-desk one, and none of the three is clearer. Against since: after names one later point, while since opens a stretch that runs up to now — after 2019 is any time later, since 2019 is the whole span. On the clock, after is the standard American word for minutes past the hour (ten after six) where British English says only ten past six.

Places the main event later on the line than whatever follows after. It is the most general sequencing word in English and carries no meaning of its own beyond order — no cause, no consequence, no judgment about the size of the gap.

1. Later than a point in time. The pharmacy closes after nine. 2. Later than an event. After the surgery, she needed six weeks off. 3. Repeated succession, in the frame [noun] after [same noun]. day after day 4. Clock time: minutes past the hour (American). a quarter after three 5. In pursuit of, or in search of. The dog took off after a squirrel. 6. In imitation of, or in honor of. She was named after her grandmother.

Senses 5 and 6 are not temporal at all, but they belong to the same word and appear constantly. Sense 6 is the American idiom; British English also says named for, which Americans use too but hear as slightly formal.

  1. The pharmacy closes after nine.
  2. Call me any time after Tuesday.
  3. After the surgery, she needed six weeks off.
  4. He apologized only after being asked twice.
  5. Day after day, the same gray sky.
  6. One delay after another pushed the launch to March.
  7. It was a quarter after three when the power came back.
  8. The shuttle leaves at ten after the hour, every hour.
  9. The dog took off after a squirrel and didn't come back for an hour.
  10. Reporters have been after him for a comment all week.
  11. She was named after her grandmother.
  12. It's a courtyard after the Spanish style.

antecedent to#

Pronunciation
/ˌæntəˈsidənt tu/ · an-tuh-SEE-duhnt TOO · to reduces to /tə/ and the whole phrase flattens under the sentence stress — an-tuh-SEE-duhnt tuh
Русский
до, прежде — юр./книжн.: предшествующий по времени, причине или очерёдности прав
Type
complex (the adjective antecedent + to)
Register
formal — legal, philosophical, and older academic prose; dated everywhere else
Complement
a noun phrase (antecedent to the sale) or, far more rarely, a gerund (antecedent to filing) — never a finite clause. There is no antecedent to he filed; for a clause, use before. The gerund is grammatical on the model of prior to filing, but it is thin in real American usage and a noun phrase is the safe choice. The bare adjective (the antecedent agreement) and the grammarian's noun (the antecedent of the pronoun) are separate jobs for the same word; the preposition always has an object after to.
Pattern
antecedent to [noun phrase] · [noun] antecedent to [noun phrase] · logically antecedent to [noun phrase] · Antecedent to [noun phrase], [clause]
Contrast
Three phrases in this file mean before in a suit — prior to, previous to, and antecedent to — and this is the heaviest of the three and the only one with an extra idea in it. Antecedent to leans toward priority in a chain, not merely earlier on a clock, which is why a cause is antecedent to its effect and a recorded lien is antecedent to a later judgment. Its mirror is subsequent to. Do not confuse the preposition with the grammar term antecedent (the noun a pronoun points back to), and do not reach for it where before will do: in modern American prose it reads as period costume.

Earlier than — with the suggestion that the earlier thing also comes first in rank or in reasoning. An American meets it in property and evidence law, in philosophy, and in nineteenth-century treatises. In a memo or an email it is dead weight, and before is the word.

1. Earlier in time than a stated event, in formal or legal prose. The easement was recorded antecedent to the sale. 2. Earlier in a chain of cause or reasoning, and not merely on the clock. Every effect has a cause antecedent to it. 3. Ranking ahead of a later claim, in the law of liens and priorities. A mortgage antecedent to the judgment takes first position.

Sense 2 is the only one that earns five syllables, because before cannot carry the idea of logical priority — the duty is before the contract says something else. Sense 1 is before in a frock coat, and every American editor cuts it.

  1. The easement was recorded antecedent to the sale and survived it.
  2. Antecedent to the hearing, both sides exchanged exhibit lists.
  3. Every effect must have a cause antecedent to it.
  4. The duty of care is antecedent to any contract between them.
  5. A lien antecedent to the judgment takes priority over it.
  6. Antecedent to 1868, the state imposed no such requirement.
  7. Nothing in the record shows an agreement antecedent to the closing.

before#

Pronunciation
/bɪˈfɔr/ · bih-FOR · weak /bəˈfɔr/ · buh-FOR
Русский
до, перед — раньше точки или события; также «перед» инстанцией (testify before)
Type
simple
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (before leaving) — never a finite clause. Before you leave is the conjunction. Before is also an adverb with no object at all (I've seen this movie before), which is the surest test of the difference.
Pattern
before [noun phrase] · before [verb-ing] · [measure phrase] before [noun phrase] · just, shortly, or long before [noun phrase] · before long
Contrast
Before vs. by: before Friday excludes Friday, by Friday includes it — a deadline written with before is a day tighter than most writers intend. Before vs. ago: ago counts backward from now and takes the simple past (three days ago), before counts backward from a point already in the past and goes with the past perfect (he had left three days before). Before vs. prior to: identical in meaning, and before is a syllable shorter. Before vs. until: finish it before Friday is a deadline, wait until Friday is a duration.

Places the main event earlier on the line than whatever follows before — the exact mirror of after.

1. Earlier than a point in time. We should be there before noon. 2. Earlier than an event. Sign the lease before the walk-through. 3. Earlier in an ordering or a queue, where the sequence is not literally temporal. B comes before C. 4. In the presence of a person or body with authority to judge. She testified before the committee. 5. Facing, or still ahead of, someone. The whole summer lay before them.

Sense 4 is formal and mostly spatial — you appear before a judge, a court, a board, or a congregation — but it inherits the ordering logic of senses 1 through 3: the person judged comes first in line. Sense 5 is literary and always has a stretch of time or an opportunity as its subject.

  1. The mail usually comes before noon.
  2. Get here before eight or you'll miss the opening.
  3. Read the whole contract before signing anything.
  4. Before the storm, nobody had bothered to trim the trees.
  5. She quit two weeks before the merger was announced.
  6. In the alphabet, B comes before C.
  7. Your name comes before mine on the list.
  8. Before long, the whole office knew.
  9. He appeared before the zoning board three times.
  10. The bill goes before the full Senate on Thursday.
  11. At nineteen, she had the whole thing before her.

ere#

Pronunciation
/ɛr/ · AIR · no weak form — the word is short, stressed, and identical in sound to air
Русский
устар./поэт. прежде, до — книжная замена before
Type
simple
Register
archaic and literary
Complement
noun phrase — and, in older texts, a finite clause, where it is a conjunction instead (ere the sun set). A gerund is vanishingly rare and reads as pastiche; the period pattern is a bare noun phrase. No modern American writes any of it except for deliberate period effect.
Pattern
ere [noun phrase] · ere long · ere [the] [time noun]
Contrast
A dead-plain synonym of before, with nothing added but age. Do not confuse it with the homophone e'er (a contraction of ever), with err /ɜr/ ("to make a mistake"), or with air, which it is pronounced exactly like.

Earlier than. You will meet it in hymns, in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in nineteenth-century verse, and in modern writing that is reaching for one of those. It survives in living American speech only in the fixed phrase ere long, and even that is bookish.

1. Earlier than a point in time. They were on the road ere dawn. 2. Earlier than an event. Not a word was spoken ere the reading of the will. 3. In the fixed adverbial ere long, before much time has passed. Ere long, the whole village had heard.

Sense 3 is the only one a modern American is likely to write on purpose, and even it is a costume. Senses 1 and 2 are read, not written.

  1. They were on the road ere dawn.
  2. He swore he would return ere nightfall.
  3. Ere long, the whole village had heard.
  4. Not a word was spoken ere the reading of the will.
  5. Ere the year's end, three of them were dead.
  6. Ere the last bell, the church was already full.
  7. Ere the frost came, the last of the hay was in.

following#

Pronunciation
/ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ · FAH-loh-ing · no weak form — the stress stays on the first syllable and nothing reduces, which is part of why the word feels heavy next to after
Русский
после, вслед за — нейтр.-книжн., газетный оборот вместо after
Type
marginal/participial
Register
neutral to formal — heavily journalistic
Complement
a noun phrase, characteristically one naming an event (following the announcement). A gerund is grammatical but clumsy, and a finite clause is impossible — there is no following the vote was counted. Note that the same string is also a present participle (the dog following me) and an adjective (the following week).
Pattern
Following [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] following [noun phrase] · immediately following [noun phrase]
Contrast
After means the same thing and is never ambiguous; following can be misread as the verb, which is why Following the mayor, the reporters filed out stalls a reader for half a second. Against subsequent to, following at least sounds like English. Reserve it for cases where the noun really is an event and after has already been used twice in the paragraph.

After, in time, with a faint suggestion that the second event grew out of the first.

1. Later in time than a named event. Following the vote, the chairman resigned. 2. In the wake of, shading into cause. Three stores closed following the flood.

That second sense is why press releases like it: following lets a writer imply a connection without claiming one. In careful prose that is a reason to distrust the word rather than a reason to use it.

  1. Following the vote, the chairman resigned.
  2. A press conference is scheduled following the hearing.
  3. Three stores on Main closed following the flood.
  4. Following two years of losses, the company sold its warehouse.
  5. The reception begins immediately following the ceremony.
  6. Following his release, he moved to Tucson and said nothing more about it.
  7. Following the recall, the company replaced its entire safety team.

in advance of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ədˈvæns əv/ · in ad-VANSS uhv · of reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — in ad-VANSS uh
Русский
заранее до, в преддверии — офиц.: до события, часто с подготовкой к нему
Type
complex
Register
formal — business, official, and journalistic
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (in advance of filing) — never a finite clause. There is no in advance of he arrived; for a clause, use before. The bare adverbial in advance (pay in advance, a week in advance) takes no object at all and is the commoner form by far.
Pattern
in advance of [noun phrase] · in advance of [verb-ing] · In advance of [event], [clause] · [quantity of time] in advance (adverb, no object)
Contrast
In advance of is not a free synonym of before. It carries the idea of preparation — you do something early so as to be ready for what is coming. She trained in advance of the race is idiomatic because the training served the race; she was born in advance of the war is not, because birth prepares for nothing. Plain before has no such restriction, which is why it fits everywhere this phrase does and many places it does not. Against ahead of: the two overlap almost completely in the run-up sense, and ahead of is shorter. Against prior to: prior to is pure sequence with no preparation in it.

Earlier than a coming event, and usually in preparation for it. The phrase is four syllables where before is two, so it earns its place only when the preparation is the point.

1. Early, in preparation for a coming event. Crews sandbagged the levee in advance of the storm. 2. In the run-up to a scheduled event, without the preparation sense. Both sides filed briefs in advance of the hearing.

Sense 1 is the one that justifies the phrase. Sense 2 is the newsroom use, where it competes directly with ahead of and loses on length.

  1. Crews sandbagged the levee in advance of the storm.
  2. Please submit your questions in advance of the meeting.
  3. She trained for six months in advance of the race.
  4. In advance of filing, the firm circulated a draft to the client.
  5. Both sides filed briefs in advance of Tuesday's hearing.
  6. The hotel needs a deposit two weeks in advance of your stay.
  7. In advance of the audit, the department rebuilt its whole ledger.

in the aftermath of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ði ˈæftərˌmæθ əv/ · in thee AF-ter-math uhv · the is /ði/ before the vowel of aftermath, and of reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — in thee AF-ter-math uh
Русский
после, в период после — о последствиях катастрофы или потрясения
Type
complex
Register
neutral to formal — journalistic and historical
Complement
a noun phrase naming a disaster, a shock, or an upheaval — the flood, the shooting, the crash, the war. No gerund: in the aftermath of resigning is possible but stiff, and in the aftermath of his resignation is the idiomatic repair. No finite clause. The article is fixed — in aftermath of is not English — and the noun aftermath also stands alone (the aftermath was worse than the storm), where it is not a preposition at all.
Pattern
In the aftermath of [event], [clause] · [clause] in the aftermath of [event] · in the immediate aftermath of [event]
Contrast
Against in the wake of, its close twin: both mean "in the period following, and shaped by," and in the wake of is the wider of the two — a policy can follow in the wake of a report, where the aftermath of a report is odd unless the report did damage. The restriction worth memorizing is the event: an aftermath is the bad stretch after something bad. In the aftermath of the wedding is a joke; in the aftermath of the fire is not. Against after and following, which are neutral about consequence, and against on the heels of, which stresses how fast the second thing came rather than how much wreckage the first left.

In the stretch of time that follows a destructive event and is still shaped by it. The word is a farming term — math is an old word for a mowing, and the aftermath was the second growth that came up after the first cut — and that is exactly the image: what comes up in ground the event has already gone over.

1. In the period following a disaster or a shock. In the aftermath of the flood, half the block moved out. 2. In the period following a political or institutional upheaval, with the consequences as the point. In the aftermath of the scandal, three directors resigned. 3. In the first hours or days after a disaster, in the narrowed frame with "immediate." In the immediate aftermath of the crash, nobody knew how many were aboard.

Sense 3 is the one that pins the phrase down. Without immediate the stretch floats: the aftermath of a storm is a week, the aftermath of a war can be a decade, and the phrase itself never says which.

  1. In the aftermath of the flood, half the block never came back.
  2. In the aftermath of the shooting, the school stayed closed a week.
  3. Insurance rates doubled in the aftermath of the fire.
  4. In the immediate aftermath of the crash, nobody knew how many were aboard.
  5. In the aftermath of the scandal, three board members resigned within a month.
  6. In the aftermath of the war, the city rebuilt the whole downtown in brick.
  7. The rule was written in the aftermath of a collapse nobody had planned for.

a noun phrase naming an event, and essentially nothing else (on the eve of the election, on the eve of war). Literally it means the night or the day before, and in on the eve of Christmas it still does; figuratively it has loosened to mean "in the last stretch before," which can be a week or a year. Against ahead of and in advance of, both of which are neutral, on the eve of is the dramatic option — it exists to make the coming event sound momentous, so it wants a war, an election, a launch, or a verdict, not a dentist appointment.

Immediately before a significant event, with the event's importance built into the phrase. The noun eve is a worn-down even, the old word for evening, which survives independently in Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve.

1. On the day or night immediately before, literally. They arrived on the eve of the wedding. 2. In the last stretch before a momentous event, figuratively. On the eve of the election, the polls tightened.

Sense 2 has no fixed length and takes all the weight. If the event is not genuinely momentous, the phrase reads as inflation and before is the honest word.

  1. They arrived on the eve of the wedding.
  2. On the eve of the election, the polls finally tightened.
  3. The company announced layoffs on the eve of the holidays.
  4. On the eve of war, the embassy burned its files.
  5. He resigned on the eve of the vote he was certain to lose.
  6. On the eve of the launch, a sensor failed and cost them the window.
  7. On the eve of the trial, the state offered a plea.

It is before plus a purpose: whatever comes first is done in order to get ready for what follows, which is the same restriction in advance of carries and the one prior to does not. Before, and as a preparation for. Nineteenth-century business and legal prose used the phrase freely; modern American writing keeps it in military orders, procedural manuals, and formal narration, where one step is being logged as a step toward the next.

1. Before a stated action, as a step toward it, with a gerund. He cleared his desk preparatory to leaving. 2. Before a stated event, in preparation for it, with a noun phrase. Troops moved south preparatory to the landing.

Sense 1 is where the phrase does work that nothing shorter does, because in preparation for cannot take a verb. In sense 2, with a plain noun after it, in preparation for is the more natural American choice and ahead of is shorter than either.

  1. He cleared his desk preparatory to leaving for good.
  2. The crew rigged the lines preparatory to casting off.
  3. Two divisions were moved south preparatory to the landing.
  4. She sold the house preparatory to moving to Phoenix.
  5. Preparatory to filing, counsel circulated a draft of the complaint.
  6. The company took a charge preparatory to the spin-off.
  7. He cleared his throat preparatory to saying something he had clearly rehearsed.

An exact synonym of prior to, which is itself an exact synonym of before. Usage guides have been striking it out since Fowler. Note that the adverb previously is alive and unremarkable (he had previously worked in Boston); it is only the preposition that is dated.

Earlier than. It carries nothing that before does not, and in American English it survives mainly in historical writing and in the fixed backward-pointing previous to that.

Duration: spans, stretches, and containment#

These say how long, or inside what stretch. Approximation: roughly when#

Seven ways to hedge a time. About and around are interchangeable in American English and cover everything; circa is restricted to dates; near is a static position close to a point; toward means "as it approaches, and not yet arrived"; nigh survives only in nigh on; and long about is the regional one, Southern and Midland, that says around in a particular voice. The legal hedges are on or about and in or about, and the colloquial one for durations and ages is going on (going on three years, going on eighty).

for / since / ago: for three years measures a stretch, since 2022 names a starting point, three years ago names a point by counting backward from now. ago vs. before: ago counts back from the moment of speaking and takes the simple past; before counts back from a point already in the past and takes the past perfect — he had left three days before. This is why ago cannot survive the tense shift of reported speech: she said she had quit three years earlier, not three years ago. ago vs. hence is the forward-backward pair.

Locates a point in the past by measuring backward from the present moment. It is the commonest preposition in modern English that comes after its object, a leftover from the Old English past participle āgān, "gone by" — literally three days gone by.

1. Counting back from now to a past point. We moved here six years ago. 2. In vague measures, a general past. That was a long time ago.

  1. We moved here six years ago.
  2. The package shipped two days ago.
  3. She called about ten minutes ago.
  4. That was a long time ago, and nobody remembers it the same way.
  5. Long ago, this was all orchard.
  6. How long ago did you two meet?
  7. Not long ago you couldn't get a coffee on this block.

come#

Pronunciation
/kʌm/ · KUM · weak /kəm/ · kuhm — in this prepositional use it is almost always unstressed, so the weak form is the ordinary one
Русский
разг. когда наступит, к — come Monday «когда настанет понедельник»
Type
marginal (a fossilized subjunctive of the verb come)
Register
informal — folksy and regional in flavor, but understood everywhere
Complement
a noun phrase naming a future time point — a day, a month, a season, a holiday, or an age (come Monday, come spring, come her next birthday). Never a past time (come last Monday is not English), never a gerund, never a clause.
Pattern
Come [future time], [clause] · [clause] come [future time] · [subject] will be [age] come [time]
Contrast
It means when [X] comes or by the time [X] arrives, and it is warmer and more colloquial than either. Unlike by, it sets no deadline — come Monday announces what will be true then, not what must be done before then. Its one hard restriction is direction: come can only look forward.

When the stated future time arrives. The construction is a leftover subjunctive — come Monday is [when] Monday come[s] — which is why it takes no -s and resists any tense marking at all. It gives a sentence a settled, almost prophetic tone, and it is at home in country lyrics, farm talk, and political prediction.

1. When a stated future day, month, or season arrives. Come Monday, everything changes. 2. By the time a stated future point arrives — with an age or a length of service. She'll be ninety come August.

Sense 2 is where the construction is most alive in ordinary American speech. It is the standard way to state an age or a tenure that has not been reached yet, and nothing shorter does the job: she'll be ninety in August loses the sense of a milestone waiting on the calendar.

  1. Come Monday, everything changes.
  2. She'll be ninety come August.
  3. Come spring, the whole hillside turns yellow.
  4. Come November, none of this will matter.
  5. He'll have been at the paper thirty years come the fall.
  6. Come Tuesday, the rates go back up.
  7. It'll be forty years come December since the mill closed.

Pronunciation
/ɪˈfɛktɪv/ · ih-FEK-tiv · weak /əˈfɛktɪv/
Русский

Type
marginal (an adjective pressed into prepositional service)
Register
formal — business, legal, official notices
Complement
a date, or a time adverb standing in for one (effective January 1, effective immediately, effective at midnight, effective next Monday). No gerund, no clause. It introduces only the moment at which a rule, an appointment, or a change takes force; it is not a general-purpose time preposition.
Pattern
Effective [date], [clause] · [clause], effective [date] · effective immediately · effective with [the next billing cycle]
Contrast
Effective looks forward only: it names the moment something starts to have force. As of does that and can also look backward to report a state — as of Friday, we had 400 orders, which effective Friday could not say. Beginning and starting do the same job in plainer registers, and British as from is the equivalent Britishism.

Taking force from the stated moment. The word is an adjective everywhere else in the language (an effective argument), and it earns a place in this dictionary because in official writing it takes a bare date as its object and sits exactly where a preposition sits — usually fronted, with a comma.

1. Taking force from a stated date. Effective January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars. 2. Taking force from a moment named by an adverb or a cycle rather than a date. Effective immediately, all travel requires written approval.

Sense 2 is why the word survives at all: effective immediately has no one-word substitute, because now is a description and this is an instrument. Note that the object may be a bare date with no preposition of its own (effective Monday), which is the clearest sign the word has stopped behaving like an adjective.

  1. Effective January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars.
  2. Effective immediately, all travel requires written approval.
  3. She resigned from the board, effective at the end of the month.
  4. The new rates are effective Monday.
  5. Effective with the next billing cycle, paper statements cost two dollars.
  6. The appointment is effective the first of the month.
  7. Effective at midnight, the contract is void.

hence#

Pronunciation
/hɛns/ · HENSS · no weak form — it keeps its stress even trailing a phrase, as in two years hence
Русский
книжн. спустя, через — ставится после срока, отсчёт вперёд: two years hence
Type
postpositive
Register
formal, literary, legal
Complement
it follows its object, and the object must be a measured length of time (three days hence, a century hence). Never a point in time, never a gerund, never a clause, and nothing at all may follow it.
Pattern
[quantity of time] hence · [clause], [quantity of time] hence
Contrast
The forward-facing twin of ago: three days ago counts back from now, three days hence counts forward. Ordinary American English says three days from now and leaves hence to contracts, sermons, and speeches. Do not confuse it with the far commoner conjunctive adverb hence meaning "therefore" (the road was closed; hence the detour), which takes no time phrase and is a different word entirely, or with the archaic adverb of place (get thee hence).

Counting forward from the present moment to a future point. It is one of the handful of English prepositions that follow their object — ago, hence, and notwithstanding are the everyday examples — and where ago is neutral, hence is a deliberate reach for gravity — which is why it survives mostly in legal drafting and in sentences about the judgment of history.

1. Counting forward from now by a measured stretch. The lease expires two years hence. 2. In vague or grand measures, a general future. What we build now will be judged a century hence.

The split is the same one ago makes in the other direction: sense 1 is a real interval a reader could put on a calendar, sense 2 is rhetorical distance. Sense 2 is the commoner one in speeches, and it is the reason the word sounds like a peroration.

  1. The lease expires two years hence.
  2. A decade hence, none of these arguments will matter.
  3. The court will reconvene thirty days hence.
  4. What we build now will be judged a century hence.
  5. Six months hence, the pilot program comes up for review.
  6. The vote is set for two weeks hence.
  7. A generation hence, nobody will remember which side won.

Pronunciation
/əv ə/ · uhv uh · both words are unstressed and of is often bare /ə/, so of an evening comes out uh-vuhn EEV-ning and of a Sunday as uh-vuh SUN-day — the phrase is never stressed and is easy to miss entirely
Русский
устар./диал. по (вечерам, субботам) — о привычном действии: of an evening «по вечерам»
Type
marginal (a fossilized adverbial genitive)
Register
archaic in general American; alive as a regional and folksy usage — Southern, Appalachian, and rural Northern; also British and Irish dialect
Complement
a singular noun naming a recurring time, with a or an, and nothing else — of an evening, of a morning, of a Sunday, of a winter night. No plural (of evenings is a separate and older adverbial), no gerund, no finite clause, and no definite article: of the evening is an ordinary possessive and means something else entirely (the cool of the evening). The construction is frozen and will not extend — of a meeting, of a birthday are not English.
Pattern
[habitual clause] of an evening · [habitual clause] of a [day of the week] · Of a [Sunday afternoon, winter night], [clause]
Variants
of a morning, of an evening, of a Sunday (the fixed members of the set), of a night (regional), of an afternoon (rarer)
Contrast
It does not name one occasion; it names a habit, and that is the whole of it. He read of an evening means he read in the evenings, as a rule, which is why the construction pulls would, used to, or a plain habitual past after it. Compare in the evening, which can be either one evening or a habit, and on Sundays, which is the plain modern way to say this. Against on a Sunday, which names one unspecified Sunday: he'd fish of a Sunday is a standing custom, he went fishing on a Sunday is one trip. Do not read the of as partitive — it is the last trace of an Old English genitive of time, the same one that survives in nowadays (now-a-days) and in he works nights.

In the course of a typical one of these — habitually, on evenings, mornings, or Sundays taken as a class. The construction is an Old English adverbial genitive gone fossil, and in American English it survives in speech that is regional, elderly, or reaching for one of the two.

1. Habitually in the evenings or in the mornings. He'd sit out on the porch of an evening. 2. Habitually on a named day. They'd drive into town of a Saturday. 3. In an extended noun phrase naming a kind of occasion. Of a winter night you could hear the trains from the house.

Every one of these has a plain modern equivalent — in the evenings, on Saturdays, on winter nights — and the plain form is what to write. The test that the construction is doing its job is the verb: it wants would, used to, or a habitual past. A single event breaks it, which is why he called of an evening cannot mean he called once.

  1. He'd sit out on the porch of an evening and not say much.
  2. Of a Saturday they'd drive into town for supplies.
  3. She still bakes of a Sunday, though nobody comes for it anymore.
  4. Of a morning the fog sits in the low fields until nine.
  5. We used to walk down to the landing of an evening.
  6. Of a winter night you could hear the trains all the way from the house.
  7. He'd take the truck out of a Sunday afternoon and just drive.

Telling time on the clock#

The half hour splits American English from British English twice over, and it splits American English from itself once.

Minutes after the hour. Americans say ten after six or ten past six; both are standard, and after is the more distinctly American of the two. British English uses only past. Half past six and quarter past six are alive on both sides of the Atlantic; the British colloquial half six (meaning 6:30) has no American equivalent and will be misheard as 5:30 or as a mistake.

Minutes before the hour. Here American English has three options where British English has one.

FormExampleRange
toten to sixgeneral American, and the only British form
ofa quarter of threeAmerican, strongest in the Northeast and among older speakers
tilla quarter till threeAmerican, Midland and Southern

All three are standard American. Of is the one that reliably confuses non-Americans, because nothing else in the language uses of for time — a quarter of three means 2:45, not 45 minutes of some three-hour thing. If you are writing for a mixed audience, the digits are safer than any of them.

All three also reduce to nothing much in speech, which is the other half of the difficulty. To is /tə/ (ten tuh six), of is /əv/ and usually bare /ə/ (a quarter uh three), and till is /təl/ (a quarter tuhl three). A listener who is not expecting the reduced of hears an unstressed syllable and no word at all, which is why this is the American clock form that most often has to be repeated.

And the endpoint problem. Open until Friday does not tell a reader whether Friday counts. American English fixed this with through — Monday through Friday includes Friday — and legal drafting fixed it with no later than and up to and including. British English has no equivalent of the through solution, which is why British schedules say Monday to Friday inclusive.
