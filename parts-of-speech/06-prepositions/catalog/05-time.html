Time#

The prepositions that put an event on the clock and the calendar — when it happened, how long it lasted, and what it came before, after, or inside of.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file answers one of three questions: when (a point — before noon, around six), how long (a span — during the war, throughout the winter), or by when (a boundary — until Friday, within an hour). The trap that runs through the whole group is that the words do not cross between those three jobs. During names a period and cannot measure one, so during three hours is an error for for three hours; since names a starting point and cannot measure a stretch, so since three years is an error for for three years; ago measures a stretch and cannot name a point, so Monday ago is not English at all.

A second trap is grammatical rather than semantic. After, before, since, till, until, and archaic ere are prepositions when a noun phrase follows and conjunctions when a whole clause follows — after the show against after the show ended. Several more of them — after, before, since, past, through, over, around, about, behind, beyond, throughout — are adverbs or verb particles when nothing follows at all. The preposition takes an object; the adverb does not. We ate soon after is the adverb, we ate after the show is the preposition, and the difference is the noun phrase.

A third trap is the -ing form, and it is the one that produces the single commonest preposition error learners make. After, before, since, upon, prior to, subsequent to, ahead of, in advance of, at the time of, in the course of, and in the middle of all take a gerund — after eating, upon signing, prior to filing, in the middle of moving. Through and into take one after a measure phrase (halfway through cooking dinner, ten minutes into filming), and between takes two inside its paired frame (between finishing school and starting the job). During and until never take one at all: during eating and until leaving are errors, and the repairs are while eating or in the middle of eating, and until he left. That is why the Complement line is the first thing to read in every entry below.

The nine core prepositions carry a heavy share of the time load, but they have their own homes: in, on, and at live in the core: in, on, at, and of, to, for, from, by, and with in the core relational prepositions. The contrasts that involve them — by vs. until, for vs. since vs. ago, during vs. for, in vs. within, and the American quarter of three — are worked out in the entries below and gathered again at the end of this file.

Sequence: what comes before and what comes after#

The words that order two events on a line. Every one of them has a plain register and a heavy one: before and after are the neutral pair, prior to, subsequent to, and in advance of the bureaucratic ones, following and on the eve of the journalistic ones, upon the contractual one, post the finance-desk one, and ere the archaic one. Four of these are also conjunctions, so check what follows: a noun phrase means preposition, a subject-and-verb means conjunction.

after#

Pronunciation: /ˈæftər/ · AF-ter · no true weak form — the vowel keeps its quality even when unstressed, which makes after one of the few prepositions a learner can always hear Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (after eating) — never a finite clause. After the show ended is the conjunction, a different word class doing a different job. It does take the relative pronoun in after which (the vote, after which he resigned). After is also an adverb (we ate soon after) and an attributive adjective (the after party); the preposition always has an object. Pattern: after [noun phrase] · after [verb-ing] · [measure phrase] after [noun phrase] · just, right, soon, shortly, or long after [noun phrase] · [clause], after which [clause] Contrast: After is the plain word; following is its newsroom dress, subsequent to its legal one, post its finance-desk one, and none of the three is clearer. Against since: after names one later point, while since opens a stretch that runs up to now — after 2019 is any time later, since 2019 is the whole span. On the clock, after is the standard American word for minutes past the hour (ten after six) where British English says only ten past six.

Places the main event later on the line than whatever follows after. It is the most general sequencing word in English and carries no meaning of its own beyond order — no cause, no consequence, no judgment about the size of the gap.

1. Later than a point in time. The pharmacy closes after nine. 2. Later than an event. After the surgery, she needed six weeks off. 3. Repeated succession, in the frame [noun] after [same noun]. day after day 4. Clock time: minutes past the hour (American). a quarter after three 5. In pursuit of, or in search of. The dog took off after a squirrel. 6. In imitation of, or in honor of. She was named after her grandmother.

Senses 5 and 6 are not temporal at all, but they belong to the same word and appear constantly. Sense 6 is the American idiom; British English also says named for, which Americans use too but hear as slightly formal.

  1. The pharmacy closes after nine.
  2. Call me any time after Tuesday.
  3. After the surgery, she needed six weeks off.
  4. He apologized only after being asked twice.
  5. Day after day, the same gray sky.
  6. One delay after another pushed the launch to March.
  7. It was a quarter after three when the power came back.
  8. The shuttle leaves at ten after the hour, every hour.
  9. The dog took off after a squirrel and didn't come back for an hour.
  10. Reporters have been after him for a comment all week.
  11. She was named after her grandmother.
  12. It's a courtyard after the Spanish style.

before#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈfɔr/ · bih-FOR · weak /bəˈfɔr/ · buh-FOR Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (before leaving) — never a finite clause. Before you leave is the conjunction. Before is also an adverb with no object at all (I've seen this movie before), which is the surest test of the difference. Pattern: before [noun phrase] · before [verb-ing] · [measure phrase] before [noun phrase] · just, shortly, or long before [noun phrase] · before long Contrast: Before vs. by: before Friday excludes Friday, by Friday includes it — a deadline written with before is a day tighter than most writers intend. Before vs. ago: ago counts backward from now and takes the simple past (three days ago), before counts backward from a point already in the past and goes with the past perfect (he had left three days before). Before vs. prior to: identical in meaning, and before is a syllable shorter. Before vs. until: finish it before Friday is a deadline, wait until Friday is a duration.

Places the main event earlier on the line than whatever follows before — the exact mirror of after.

1. Earlier than a point in time. We should be there before noon. 2. Earlier than an event. Sign the lease before the walk-through. 3. Earlier in an ordering or a queue, where the sequence is not literally temporal. B comes before C. 4. In the presence of a person or body with authority to judge. She testified before the committee. 5. Facing, or still ahead of, someone. The whole summer lay before them.

Sense 4 is formal and mostly spatial — you appear before a judge, a court, a board, or a congregation — but it inherits the ordering logic of senses 1 through 3: the person judged comes first in line. Sense 5 is literary and always has a stretch of time or an opportunity as its subject.

  1. The mail usually comes before noon.
  2. Get here before eight or you'll miss the opening.
  3. Read the whole contract before signing anything.
  4. Before the storm, nobody had bothered to trim the trees.
  5. She quit two weeks before the merger was announced.
  6. In the alphabet, B comes before C.
  7. Your name comes before mine on the list.
  8. Before long, the whole office knew.
  9. He appeared before the zoning board three times.
  10. The bill goes before the full Senate on Thursday.
  11. At nineteen, she had the whole thing before her.

See also: after, prior to, ahead of, ere, until, by, before (conjunction)

ere#

Pronunciation: /ɛr/ · AIR · no weak form — the word is short, stressed, and identical in sound to air Type: simple · Register: archaic and literary Complement: noun phrase — and, in older texts, a finite clause, where it is a conjunction instead (ere the sun set). A gerund is vanishingly rare and reads as pastiche; the period pattern is a bare noun phrase. No modern American writes any of it except for deliberate period effect. Pattern: ere [noun phrase] · ere long · ere [the] [time noun] Contrast: A dead-plain synonym of before, with nothing added but age. Do not confuse it with the homophone e'er (a contraction of ever), with err /ɜr/ ("to make a mistake"), or with air, which it is pronounced exactly like.

Earlier than. You will meet it in hymns, in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in nineteenth-century verse, and in modern writing that is reaching for one of those. It survives in living American speech only in the fixed phrase ere long, and even that is bookish.

1. Earlier than a point in time. They were on the road ere dawn. 2. Earlier than an event. Not a word was spoken ere the reading of the will. 3. In the fixed adverbial ere long, before much time has passed. Ere long, the whole village had heard.

Sense 3 is the only one a modern American is likely to write on purpose, and even it is a costume. Senses 1 and 2 are read, not written.

  1. They were on the road ere dawn.
  2. He swore he would return ere nightfall.
  3. Ere long, the whole village had heard.
  4. Not a word was spoken ere the reading of the will.
  5. Ere the year's end, three of them were dead.
  6. Ere the last bell, the church was already full.
  7. Ere the frost came, the last of the hay was in.

See also: before, prior to, nigh, hence, ere (conjunction)

following#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ · FAH-loh-ing · no weak form — the stress stays on the first syllable and nothing reduces, which is part of why the word feels heavy next to after Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal — heavily journalistic Complement: a noun phrase, characteristically one naming an event (following the announcement). A gerund is grammatical but clumsy, and a finite clause is impossible — there is no following the vote was counted. Note that the same string is also a present participle (the dog following me) and an adjective (the following week). Pattern: Following [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] following [noun phrase] · immediately following [noun phrase] Contrast: After means the same thing and is never ambiguous; following can be misread as the verb, which is why Following the mayor, the reporters filed out stalls a reader for half a second. Against subsequent to, following at least sounds like English. Reserve it for cases where the noun really is an event and after has already been used twice in the paragraph.

After, in time, with a faint suggestion that the second event grew out of the first.

1. Later in time than a named event. Following the vote, the chairman resigned. 2. In the wake of, shading into cause. Three stores closed following the flood.

That second sense is why press releases like it: following lets a writer imply a connection without claiming one. In careful prose that is a reason to distrust the word rather than a reason to use it.

  1. Following the vote, the chairman resigned.
  2. A press conference is scheduled following the hearing.
  3. Three stores on Main closed following the flood.
  4. Following two years of losses, the company sold its warehouse.
  5. The reception begins immediately following the ceremony.
  6. Following his release, he moved to Tucson and said nothing more about it.
  7. Following the recall, the company replaced its entire safety team.

See also: after, prior to, subsequent to, post, pending, given

in advance of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ədˈvæns əv/ · in ad-VANSS uhv · of reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — in ad-VANSS uh Type: complex · Register: formal — business, official, and journalistic Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in advance of filing) — never a finite clause. There is no in advance of he arrived; for a clause, use before. The bare adverbial in advance (pay in advance, a week in advance) takes no object at all and is the commoner form by far. Pattern: in advance of [noun phrase] · in advance of [verb-ing] · In advance of [event], [clause] · [quantity of time] in advance (adverb, no object) Contrast: In advance of is not a free synonym of before. It carries the idea of preparation — you do something early so as to be ready for what is coming. She trained in advance of the race is idiomatic because the training served the race; she was born in advance of the war is not, because birth prepares for nothing. Plain before has no such restriction, which is why it fits everywhere this phrase does and many places it does not. Against ahead of: the two overlap almost completely in the run-up sense, and ahead of is shorter. Against prior to: prior to is pure sequence with no preparation in it.

Earlier than a coming event, and usually in preparation for it. The phrase is four syllables where before is two, so it earns its place only when the preparation is the point.

1. Early, in preparation for a coming event. Crews sandbagged the levee in advance of the storm. 2. In the run-up to a scheduled event, without the preparation sense. Both sides filed briefs in advance of the hearing.

Sense 1 is the one that justifies the phrase. Sense 2 is the newsroom use, where it competes directly with ahead of and loses on length.

  1. Crews sandbagged the levee in advance of the storm.
  2. Please submit your questions in advance of the meeting.
  3. She trained for six months in advance of the race.
  4. In advance of filing, the firm circulated a draft to the client.
  5. Both sides filed briefs in advance of Tuesday's hearing.
  6. The hotel needs a deposit two weeks in advance of your stay.
  7. In advance of the audit, the department rebuilt its whole ledger.

See also: ahead of, before, prior to, on the eve of, by

on the eve of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ði ˈiv əv/ · ahn thee EEV uhv · of reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/; the is /ði/ before the vowel of eve Type: complex · Register: formal to journalistic — heavily used in news writing and history Complement: a noun phrase naming an event, and essentially nothing else (on the eve of the election, on the eve of war). No gerund — on the eve of signing is possible but stiff, and on the eve of the signing is the idiomatic repair. No finite clause. The article is fixed: on eve of is headline clipping, not prose. Pattern: on the eve of [event noun] · On the eve of [event], [clause] · [clause] on the eve of [event] Contrast: Literally it means the night or the day before, and in on the eve of Christmas it still does; figuratively it has loosened to mean "in the last stretch before," which can be a week or a year. That drift is why editors distrust it. Against ahead of and in advance of, both of which are neutral, on the eve of is the dramatic option — it exists to make the coming event sound momentous, so it wants a war, an election, a launch, or a verdict, not a dentist appointment.

Immediately before a significant event, with the event's importance built into the phrase. The noun eve is a worn-down even, the old word for evening, which survives independently in Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve.

1. On the day or night immediately before, literally. They arrived on the eve of the wedding. 2. In the last stretch before a momentous event, figuratively. On the eve of the election, the polls tightened.

Sense 2 has no fixed length and takes all the weight. If the event is not genuinely momentous, the phrase reads as inflation and before is the honest word.

  1. They arrived on the eve of the wedding.
  2. On the eve of the election, the polls finally tightened.
  3. The company announced layoffs on the eve of the holidays.
  4. On the eve of war, the embassy burned its files.
  5. He resigned on the eve of the vote he was certain to lose.
  6. On the eve of the launch, a sensor failed and cost them the window.
  7. On the eve of the trial, the state offered a plea.

See also: ahead of, in advance of, before, prior to, upon

post#

Pronunciation: /poʊst/ · POHST · no weak form — the diphthong survives even unstressed, which is one reason the word still sounds like a prefix that got loose Type: simple (a preposition clipped out of the Latin prefix post-) · Register: informal-to-neutral in business, finance, tech, and journalism; still rejected by conservative editors Complement: a noun phrase, nearly always one naming an event or an era (post the merger, post pandemic). No gerund, no clause, and no article is required. It is far commoner as the hyphenated prefix (postwar, post-9/11, post-op), and that is the safe form in edited prose. Pattern: post [event noun] · post [era noun] · post-[noun] as an attributive modifier Contrast: It is after, in one syllable, borrowed out of a prefix. Against following and subsequent to — which are at least whole English words — post still reads as in-group jargon: post the acquisition is finance-speak for after the acquisition. The mirror form pre (pre the merger) exists but is rarer and sounds more British. When in doubt, hyphenate it onto a noun and it stops being controversial.

After a named event or era. The usage grew out of postwar and post-Watergate, where the prefix was doing real work, and then broke free of the hyphen sometime in the late twentieth century. American editors split on it: it is unremarkable in an earnings call and conspicuous in a magazine.

1. After a specific event. Margins recovered post the merger. 2. After an era, usually with no article. Post pandemic, nobody wants five days in the office.

  1. Margins recovered post the merger.
  2. Post pandemic, nobody wants five days a week in the office.
  3. Head count is down twelve percent post the reorganization.
  4. We'll revisit the forecast post earnings.
  5. Post the recall, the stock never fully came back.
  6. Everything in that neighborhood went up post-war.
  7. Post the split, the two halves were worth more than the whole had been.

See also: after, following, subsequent to, prior to

prior to#

Pronunciation: /ˈpraɪər tu/ · PRY-er TOO · to reduces to /tə/ — PRY-er tuh Type: complex · Register: formal, legal, bureaucratic Complement: noun phrase or gerund (prior to signing) — never a finite clause. For a clause you need before, since there is no prior to he arrived. Pattern: prior to [noun phrase] · prior to [verb-ing] · [clause] prior to [noun phrase] · Prior to [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: It means before, exactly and only, in three syllables instead of two, and every American usage guide tells you to cut it. Keep it in contracts, protocols, and medical charts, where it is house style and where prior pairs with subsequent. Do not stretch it to mean in advance of in the sense of preparation — she trained in advance of the race is not she trained prior to the race.

Earlier than. The phrase is built on the adjective prior ("earlier, previous"), which is why it attracts writers who want a sentence to sound procedural. In ordinary prose it adds length, not precision: prior to the meeting and before the meeting are the same statement.

1. Earlier than a stated event. All materials must be submitted prior to the deadline. 2. Earlier than a date or a year, as a background marker. Prior to 2018, the building had no sprinkler system. 3. Earlier than a stated action, with a gerund. Prior to signing, review the indemnification clause.

Sense 3 is the one that carries real weight in drafting, because it attaches a requirement to an act rather than to a clock: prior to signing binds whoever signs, whenever that happens.

  1. All materials must be submitted prior to the deadline.
  2. Prior to 2018, the building had no sprinkler system.
  3. Patients should fast for eight hours prior to the procedure.
  4. Prior to signing, review the indemnification clause.
  5. He held two other posts prior to his appointment as chief.
  6. Any defect reported prior to delivery is the seller's problem.
  7. Prior to dispensing the medication, the pharmacist must verify the allergy list.

since#

Pronunciation: /sɪns/ · SINSS · weak /səns/ in fast speech Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: a noun phrase naming a point in time (since Monday, since 2019, since the accident), a time adverb (since then, since yesterday), or a gerund (since leaving school). Never a length of time — since three years is the commonest single error in this file, and the word for a length is for. With a finite clause it is a conjunction (since I moved here), and with no object at all an adverb (I haven't seen her since). Pattern: [perfect verb] ... since [point in time] · since [verb-ing] · ever since [point in time] · Since [point in time], [perfect clause] · since when (fixed) Contrast: for / since / ago is the triangle: for takes a length (for three years), since takes a starting point (since 2022), and ago takes a length and counts backward from now (three years ago). Since also demands the perfect in the main clause — She has lived here since 2019, never she lives here since 2019. The causal since ("because") is the conjunction, not this word, and since meaning "afterward" (he has since retired) is an adverb.

Opens a stretch of time that starts at a named point and runs up to the present moment. The "runs to now" part is built into the word, and it is why the main verb must be a perfect: the stretch is still open.

1. From a past point up to now. We've been waiting since eight. 2. From a past point up to some other past point, with the past perfect. By March he had not written since the funeral.

  1. We've been waiting since eight o'clock.
  2. She's lived in Tucson since 2019.
  3. Nothing has been the same since the fire.
  4. He hasn't touched a piano since leaving school.
  5. Ever since the surgery, he's walked with a cane.
  6. By March, she had not heard from him since the funeral.
  7. The building had stood empty since the previous tenant's bankruptcy.

See also: ago, as of, until, for, from, since (conjunction)

subsequent to#

Pronunciation: /ˈsʌbsɪkwənt tu/ · SUB-si-kwuhnt TOO · to reduces to /tə/ — SUB-si-kwuhnt tuh Type: complex · Register: formal, legal Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause. There is no subsequent to he resigned; use after with a clause. Pattern: subsequent to [noun phrase] · subsequent to [verb-ing] · [clause] subsequent to [noun phrase] Contrast: The mirror of prior to, and heavier still: five syllables for a word of two. It exists mainly so that legal drafting can pair prior to with something symmetrical. Outside contracts and audit reports, write after.

After. The adjective subsequent means "coming later," and the phrase inherits that and nothing more. It carries no implication of consequence, despite the family resemblance to consequent: subsequent to the audit says only that something happened later, not that the audit caused it.

1. After a stated event. Subsequent to the audit, three controls were rewritten. 2. After a date or a cutoff, in claims, warranties, and financial statements. Any claim arising subsequent to the closing date is the buyer's. 3. After a stated action, with a gerund. Subsequent to filing, the plaintiff amended the complaint twice.

Sense 2 is the one with a technical life of its own: accountants call anything that happens after the balance-sheet date a subsequent event, and the phrase is the standard label for it. Senses 1 and 3 are ordinary after in a suit.

  1. Subsequent to the audit, three controls were rewritten.
  2. Any claim arising subsequent to the closing date is the buyer's.
  3. Subsequent to filing, the plaintiff amended the complaint twice.
  4. The defect was discovered subsequent to delivery.
  5. Subsequent to his resignation, the department was reorganized.
  6. Two findings were disclosed subsequent to the balance-sheet date.
  7. Subsequent to receiving notice, the tenant has ten days to cure.

See also: after, following, prior to, post, subsequent to (complex)

upon#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑn/ · uh-PAHN · also /əˈpɔn/ · uh-PAWN · the first syllable is already reduced to /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form Type: compound (up + on, fused) · Register: formal — contracts, notices, and fixed phrases Complement: a noun phrase naming an event (upon arrival, upon receipt) or a gerund (upon signing, upon hearing the verdict) — never a finite clause. The gerund is the construction that keeps the word alive in modern American writing. Pattern: upon [event noun] · upon [verb-ing] · Upon [noun phrase], [clause] · on or upon [receipt, arrival, completion, delivery] Contrast: In the time sense upon means "at the moment of, and immediately after," compressing a whole clause: upon arrival is as soon as he arrived in two words. Plain on does the same job and is shorter (on arrival, on receipt); the two are interchangeable here and upon is the drafting choice. Against after, which allows any interval: after signing could be a week later, upon signing is that instant. The contact and movement senses live in upon.

At the moment of the stated event, and therefore immediately after it. This is the one job in modern American English where upon is not merely a dressed-up on — it is the standard way contracts, invoices, and official notices attach an obligation to a triggering event.

1. At the moment a stated event occurs. Payment is due upon receipt. 2. With a gerund: as soon as someone does something. Upon signing, you have three days to cancel. 3. The frozen storytelling opener. Once upon a time...

  1. Payment is due upon receipt.
  2. Upon arrival, please check in at the front desk.
  3. The estate passes to her children upon her death.
  4. Upon signing, you have three business days to cancel.
  5. Upon hearing the verdict, he sat back down without a word.
  6. The balance becomes payable upon completion of the work.
  7. Once upon a time, this was all farmland.
  8. Once upon a time there were three sawmills on this river.

Duration: spans, stretches, and containment#

These say how long, or inside what stretch. The rule that governs the section is that a period and a length are different things grammatically: during, over, throughout, and through take a named period (the war, the summer, Tuesday), while a measured length (three hours) needs for — or, when the point is a deadline rather than a duration, within. The three complex phrases in this section — at the time of, in the course of, and in the middle of — are the ones that will take a gerund where during cannot, and they are the honest repair for during eating.

at the time of#

Pronunciation: /ət ðə ˈtaɪm əv/ · uht thuh TYME uhv · at reduces to /ət/ and of to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — uht thuh TYME uh Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal — legal, insurance, journalistic, and academic Complement: a noun phrase naming an event or a period (at the time of the accident), or a gerund (at the time of writing, at the time of filing) — never a finite clause. There is no at the time of he arrived; for a clause, use when. The frame at the time that [clause] is not an exception, since the clause there sits inside the noun phrase the time. The article is fixed in prose: bare at time of survives only as form-field clipping (at time of purchase, at time of service), which is why you meet it on receipts and nowhere else. Pattern: at the time of [event noun] · at the time of [verb-ing] · At the time of [event], [clause] · at time of [purchase, service, sale] (fixed transactional clipping) Contrast: Against during, which spreads an event across a whole period: an accident has no inside, so during the accident is odd where at the time of the accident pins a single moment. Against when, which is shorter but needs a subject and a tense — at the time of writing is the phrase's one indispensable use, because when I write this says something else. Against as of: as of this writing dates the claim from outside and is the commoner American business form; at the time of writing puts the writer inside the moment.

Pins one event to the moment another event happened. It is the phrase that legal, insurance, and academic writing reach for when exact simultaneity is the thing at issue — a policy in force, an age, an address, a state of knowledge.

1. At the moment a stated event occurred. She was twenty-six at the time of the crash. 2. With a gerund, at the moment of doing something. At the time of writing, the bill had not been signed. 3. In the transactional frame, at the moment a transaction takes place. Payment is collected at the time of booking.

Sense 3 is the one that drops its article on forms and signs. At time of purchase is standard on a receipt and wrong in a sentence; write at the time of purchase in prose.

  1. She was twenty-six at the time of the crash.
  2. At the time of writing, the bill still had not been signed.
  3. At the time of the merger, the company had nine hundred employees.
  4. The policy had lapsed at the time of the fire.
  5. Payment is collected at the time of booking, not at check-in.
  6. He was living in Denver at the time of his father's death.
  7. At the time of filing, neither party had counsel.

See also: during, in the middle of, in the course of, as of, upon, at, on

between#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈtwin/ · bih-TWEEN · weak /bəˈtwin/ Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: a noun phrase, or the paired frame between [X] and [Y] — the second half must be joined by and, never by or and never by to. Between 2 to 4 is always an error; write between 2 and 4 or from 2 to 4. Gerunds are fine in the paired frame — between finishing school and starting the job — and so is a bare plural or mass noun with no and at all (between meetings). No finite clause: between I got home and dinner is an error, and the repair is a noun phrase on each side. Pronouns take the objective case: between you and me, never between you and I. Between is also an adverb in in between (there's a week in between), where it has no object. Spreads an event across a stretch of time that the speaker is looking at whole, from the outside. Every sense below is that same image applied to a different kind of stretch: a season, a measured run of years, a meal, a quantity, or an illness.

  3. Toward dawn the rain finally stopped.
  4. Toward the end of the semester, everybody stops reading.
  5. Sales pick up toward the middle of December.
  6. He grew quieter toward the end of his life.
  7. Toward the close of the fiscal year, every department starts spending.

Postpositive and marginal time words#

Four oddities. Ago and hence are the two English prepositions that must follow their object rather than precede it, and they point in opposite directions. Come is a fossilized verb form doing a preposition's work, and effective is an adjective that business writing drafted into the same job.

ago#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡoʊ/ · uh-GOH · no separate weak form — the first syllable is already a schwa and never takes stress Type: postpositive · Register: neutral Complement: it follows its object, and the object must be a measured length of time — three days ago, a moment ago, a long time ago. Never a point in time (Monday ago is not English), never a gerund, never a clause, and never in front of its object (ago three days is not English). It also requires the simple past in the clause it modifies: He left three days ago, never he has left three days ago. Pattern: [quantity of time] ago · [quantity of time] ago, [past-tense clause] · long ago · how long ago · not long ago Variants: agone (archaic; found in older verse and in some rural speech) Contrast: for / since / ago: for three years measures a stretch, since 2022 names a starting point, three years ago names a point by counting backward from now. ago vs. before: ago counts back from the moment of speaking and takes the simple past; before counts back from a point already in the past and takes the past perfect — he had left three days before. This is why ago cannot survive the tense shift of reported speech: she said she had quit three years earlier, not three years ago. ago vs. hence is the forward-backward pair.

Locates a point in the past by measuring backward from the present moment. It is the commonest preposition in modern English that comes after its object, a leftover from the Old English past participle āgān, "gone by" — literally three days gone by.

1. Counting back from now to a past point. We moved here six years ago. 2. In vague measures, a general past. That was a long time ago.

  1. We moved here six years ago.
  2. The package shipped two days ago.
  3. She called about ten minutes ago.
  4. That was a long time ago, and nobody remembers it the same way.
  5. Long ago, this was all orchard.
  6. How long ago did you two meet?
  7. Not long ago you couldn't get a coffee on this block.

See also: since, hence, before, past, for, from, ago (participial)

come#

Pronunciation: /kʌm/ · KUM · weak /kəm/ · kuhm — in this prepositional use it is almost always unstressed, so the weak form is the ordinary one Type: marginal (a fossilized subjunctive of the verb come) · Register: informal — folksy and regional in flavor, but understood everywhere Complement: a noun phrase naming a future time point — a day, a month, a season, a holiday, or an age (come Monday, come spring, come her next birthday). Never a past time (come last Monday is not English), never a gerund, never a clause. Pattern: Come [future time], [clause] · [clause] come [future time] · [subject] will be [age] come [time] Contrast: It means when [X] comes or by the time [X] arrives, and it is warmer and more colloquial than either. Unlike by, it sets no deadline — come Monday announces what will be true then, not what must be done before then. Its one hard restriction is direction: come can only look forward.

When the stated future time arrives. The construction is a leftover subjunctive — come Monday is [when] Monday come[s] — which is why it takes no -s and resists any tense marking at all. It gives a sentence a settled, almost prophetic tone, and it is at home in country lyrics, farm talk, and political prediction.

1. When a stated future day, month, or season arrives. Come Monday, everything changes. 2. By the time a stated future point arrives — with an age or a length of service. She'll be ninety come August.

Sense 2 is where the construction is most alive in ordinary American speech. It is the standard way to state an age or a tenure that has not been reached yet, and nothing shorter does the job: she'll be ninety in August loses the sense of a milestone waiting on the calendar.

  1. Come Monday, everything changes.
  2. She'll be ninety come August.
  3. Come spring, the whole hillside turns yellow.
  4. Come November, none of this will matter.
  5. He'll have been at the paper thirty years come the fall.
  6. Come Tuesday, the rates go back up.
  7. It'll be forty years come December since the mill closed.

See also: as of, until, by, on, starting

effective#

Pronunciation: /ɪˈfɛktɪv/ · ih-FEK-tiv · weak /əˈfɛktɪv/ Type: marginal (an adjective pressed into prepositional service) · Register: formal — business, legal, official notices Complement: a date, or a time adverb standing in for one (effective January 1, effective immediately, effective at midnight, effective next Monday). No gerund, no clause. It introduces only the moment at which a rule, an appointment, or a change takes force; it is not a general-purpose time preposition. Pattern: Effective [date], [clause] · [clause], effective [date] · effective immediately · effective with [the next billing cycle] Contrast: Effective looks forward only: it names the moment something starts to have force. As of does that and can also look backward to report a state — as of Friday, we had 400 orders, which effective Friday could not say. Beginning and starting do the same job in plainer registers, and British as from is the equivalent Britishism.

Taking force from the stated moment. The word is an adjective everywhere else in the language (an effective argument), and it earns a place in this dictionary because in official writing it takes a bare date as its object and sits exactly where a preposition sits — usually fronted, with a comma.

1. Taking force from a stated date. Effective January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars. 2. Taking force from a moment named by an adverb or a cycle rather than a date. Effective immediately, all travel requires written approval.

Sense 2 is why the word survives at all: effective immediately has no one-word substitute, because now is a description and this is an instrument. Note that the object may be a bare date with no preposition of its own (effective Monday), which is the clearest sign the word has stopped behaving like an adjective.

  1. Effective January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars.
  2. Effective immediately, all travel requires written approval.
  3. She resigned from the board, effective at the end of the month.
  4. The new rates are effective Monday.
  5. Effective with the next billing cycle, paper statements cost two dollars.
  6. The appointment is effective the first of the month.
  7. Effective at midnight, the contract is void.

See also: as of, upon, pending, from, on, beginning

hence#

Pronunciation: /hɛns/ · HENSS · no weak form — it keeps its stress even trailing a phrase, as in two years hence Type: postpositive · Register: formal, literary, legal Complement: it follows its object, and the object must be a measured length of time (three days hence, a century hence). Never a point in time, never a gerund, never a clause, and nothing at all may follow it. Pattern: [quantity of time] hence · [clause], [quantity of time] hence Contrast: The forward-facing twin of ago: three days ago counts back from now, three days hence counts forward. Ordinary American English says three days from now and leaves hence to contracts, sermons, and speeches. Do not confuse it with the far commoner conjunctive adverb hence meaning "therefore" (the road was closed; hence the detour), which takes no time phrase and is a different word entirely, or with the archaic adverb of place (get thee hence).

Counting forward from the present moment to a future point. It is one of the handful of English prepositions that follow their object — ago, hence, and notwithstanding are the everyday examples — and where ago is neutral, hence is a deliberate reach for gravity — which is why it survives mostly in legal drafting and in sentences about the judgment of history.

1. Counting forward from now by a measured stretch. The lease expires two years hence. 2. In vague or grand measures, a general future. What we build now will be judged a century hence.

The split is the same one ago makes in the other direction: sense 1 is a real interval a reader could put on a calendar, sense 2 is rhetorical distance. Sense 2 is the commoner one in speeches, and it is the reason the word sounds like a peroration.

  1. The lease expires two years hence.
  2. A decade hence, none of these arguments will matter.
  3. The court will reconvene thirty days hence.
  4. What we build now will be judged a century hence.
  5. Six months hence, the pilot program comes up for review.
  6. The vote is set for two weeks hence.
  7. A generation hence, nobody will remember which side won.

See also: ago, ere, until, from, hence (participial)

Telling time on the clock#

The half hour splits American English from British English twice over, and it splits American English from itself once.

Minutes after the hour. Americans say ten after six or ten past six; both are standard, and after is the more distinctly American of the two. British English uses only past. Half past six and quarter past six are alive on both sides of the Atlantic; the British colloquial half six (meaning 6:30) has no American equivalent and will be misheard as 5:30 or as a mistake.

Minutes before the hour. Here American English has three options where British English has one.

FormExampleRange
toten to sixgeneral American, and the only British form
ofa quarter of threeAmerican, strongest in the Northeast and among older speakers
tilla quarter till threeAmerican, Midland and Southern

All three are standard American. Of is the one that reliably confuses non-Americans, because nothing else in the language uses of for time — a quarter of three means 2:45, not 45 minutes of some three-hour thing. If you are writing for a mixed audience, the digits are safer than any of them.

All three also reduce to nothing much in speech, which is the other half of the difficulty. To is /tə/ (ten tuh six), of is /əv/ and usually bare /ə/ (a quarter uh three), and till is /təl/ (a quarter tuhl three). A listener who is not expecting the reduced of hears an unstressed syllable and no word at all, which is why this is the American clock form that most often has to be repeated.

And the endpoint problem. Open until Friday does not tell a reader whether Friday counts. American English fixed this with through — Monday through Friday includes Friday — and legal drafting fixed it with no later than and up to and including. British English has no equivalent of the through solution, which is why British schedules say Monday to Friday inclusive.
