# Agent, instrument, means, manner, and measure

> The prepositions that answer *by whom*, *with what*, *how*, and *how much* — the words that attach a doer, a tool, a method, a style, or a number to an event.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Four questions run through this group and they shade into one another so gradually that no two dictionaries draw the lines in the same place: who did it (**agent**), what they did it with (**instrument**), how the result was brought about (**means** and **manner**), and how much of it there was (**measure**). English marks all four with the same small handful of workhorses — *by*, *with*, *through*, *in*, *at*, *per* — and then supplies a long tail of complex phrases (*by means of*, *at the rate of*, *in the neighborhood of*) that say the same thing at greater length and higher register. The rule that runs through the whole group is that **the short form is almost always the better one**: *with a crowbar* beats *by means of a crowbar*, *more than 200* beats *in excess of 200*, and *for $500* beats *in the amount of $500* everywhere except a contract. The trap that runs through it is the ear — most of these phrases end in *of*, which reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ in speech, which is why *could of* and *would of* get written down, and why every entry below gives the weak form.

## Where the core nine live

**All nine of the most common prepositions do work in this semantic field** — seven of them do the bulk of it — and every one is written up in full in file 01 or file 02. **Nothing below repeats those entries.** Look for the relevant senses there:

- **[by](02-core-relational.md#by)** — the agent of a passive (*written by Baldwin*), the method (*by train*, *by check*, *by hand*), the distributive rate (*by the pound*, *by the hour*, *by the dozen*), the margin of difference (*won by six*), and the dimensions of a rectangle (*a three by five card*).
- **[with](02-core-relational.md#with)** — the instrument (*cut it with a knife*) and manner adverbials (*with care*, *with difficulty*, *with a straight face*).
- **[in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in)** — the medium and the manner (*in pencil*, *in ink*, *in Spanish*, *in a whisper*, *in cash*, *in a hurry*), magnitude (*losses in the millions*), and the proportion frame (*one in five households*, *a one-in-a-million chance*).
- **[on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on)** — the means of travel that has no vehicle around you (*on foot*, *on horseback*) and the powered medium (*on the phone*, *on TV*).
- **[at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at)** — rate, speed, and price (*at speed*, *at 60 miles an hour*, *at $5 a pound*, *at 6 percent*).
- **[to](02-core-relational.md#to)** — the precision of a rounded figure (*to the nearest dollar*, *accurate to three decimal places*) and ratios (*a three-to-one margin*, *ten to one against*).
- **[for](02-core-relational.md#for)** — price in a single transaction (*sold it for $500*), and the exchange rate of one thing against another (*a dollar for a dollar*).
- **[from](02-core-relational.md#from)** — the raw material a thing was made out of (*wine from grapes*, *a bench from an old barn door*) and the source a piece of information came from (*I got it from her*).
- **[of](02-core-relational.md#of)** — the substance a thing is made of (*a table of oak*), and the measure phrase that carries almost every quantity in written English (*a crowd of two hundred*, *a distance of ten miles*, *an increase of nine percent*).

Everything below is a word this file owns outright, or a word whose measure-and-manner sense lives here while its spatial or temporal sense lives elsewhere.

## Agent — who or what did it

Plain *by* is the default agent marker in English and covers nearly every case. The four phrases here are the specialists: one for agents of harm, one for the party who ordered the thing done, one for agents who supplied something, and one for pinning behavior on an institution.

### at the behest of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə bɪˈhɛst ʌv/ &middot; *at thuh bih-HEST uhv*; weak /ət ðə bəˈhɛst əv/, and in running speech the *of* goes down to bare /ə/ — /ət ðə bəˈhɛst ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; standard in journalism, history, and legal writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, and like the other agent phrases the complement must be **animate or institutional** — a person, an office, an agency. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand. With a pronoun, the possessive construction is commoner: *at his behest* rather than *at the behest of him*.
**Pattern:** [passive clause] *at the behest of* [instigator] &middot; *At the behest of* [instigator], [clause] &middot; [clause] *at* [possessive] *behest*
**Variants:** *at the request of* is the neutral everyday form and *at the urging of* and *at the insistence of* grade the pressure upward; *at the instigation of* is the most accusatory and the rarest. *At the direction of* is the civilian softening of military *by direction of*, both of which are sketched at [by order of](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#by-order-of).
**Contrast:** The one phrase in this section that names **who ordered it rather than who did it**. Plain [by](02-core-relational.md#by) marks the hand; *at the behest of* marks the voice behind the hand, which is why *the memo was written by a staffer at the behest of the governor* can put two different agents in one sentence and no other construction can. Against [on the part of](#on-the-part-of): that phrase hangs an abstract noun on its doer, this one hangs a completed act on its instigator. *Behest* is a fossil noun exactly like *dint* in [by dint of](#by-dint-of) — it survives only inside this phrase, so it takes no plural and no modifier: ✗ *a strong behest*, ✗ *at several behests*.

Names the party whose request or order set the action going. It leaves the doer in place and adds a second, superior agent above them, which is why it is the standard phrase for reporting influence, pressure, and chains of command.

1. The investigation was reopened *at the behest of* two members of the committee.
2. *At the behest of* the state, the district rewrote its attendance policy over the summer.
3. The clause was inserted *at the behest of* a single lender and nobody else read it closely.
4. She took the job *at the behest of* a mentor she could not say no to.
5. The audit was commissioned *at his behest* and delivered to him alone.
6. The park was renamed *at the urging of* a neighborhood association that had been asking for nine years.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [on the part of](#on-the-part-of), [at the hands of](#at-the-hands-of), [courtesy of](#courtesy-of), [by order of](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#by-order-of)

### at the hands of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə ˈhændz ʌv/ &middot; *at thuh HANDZ uhv*; weak /ət ðə ˈhændz əv/, and in running speech the *of* goes all the way down to bare /ə/ — /ət ðə ˈhændz ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; heavy in journalism and history writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, and the complement must be **animate or institutional** — a person, a group, a government, a team. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [noun of suffering] *at the hands of* [agent] &middot; [passive verb of suffering] *at the hands of* [agent]
**Contrast:** A specialized [by](02-core-relational.md#by). It marks the agent only of something bad — defeat, injury, humiliation, death — and it casts that agent as a perpetrator. *He was killed by his brother* is neutral reporting; *He died at the hands of his brother* is an accusation. Because the complement must be animate, it cannot cover instruments or natural forces: ✗ *killed at the hands of a falling tree*, ✗ *flattened at the hands of the storm*.

Attributes harm to a responsible party. The phrase is doing rhetorical work that plain *by* does not do: it foregrounds the agent, implies that the suffering was inflicted rather than merely caused, and invites the reader to assign blame. Sportswriting borrowed it for defeats, where the same framing turns a loss into something done *to* a team.

**1. Suffering, injury, or death inflicted by someone.**

1. Thousands died *at the hands of* the regime in the first eighteen months alone.
2. She described years of mistreatment *at the hands of* a supervisor everyone had complained about.
3. He lost the house *at the hands of* a servicer that had misplaced two years of payments.

**2. Defeat in a contest.**

4. The team suffered its worst loss in a decade *at the hands of* a program it had beaten nine straight times.
5. The bill died *at the hands of* a committee that never scheduled a hearing on it.
6. Her first campaign ended *at the hands of* an incumbent nobody thought was vulnerable.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [courtesy of](#courtesy-of), [on the part of](#on-the-part-of), [through](#through)

### courtesy of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɚtəsi ʌv/ &middot; *KUR-tuh-see uhv*; weak /ˈkɚtəsi əv/, casually /ˈkɚtəsi ə/ — which is one reason the bare credit-line form *courtesy the Newberry Library* got written down in the first place
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in captions, credits, and journalism
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a person, institution, or source. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It frequently appears with no verb at all, as a freestanding credit line.
**Pattern:** [clause] *courtesy of* [source] &middot; [noun phrase], *courtesy of* [source] &middot; *Courtesy of* [source] (caption)
**Variants:** *by courtesy of* — older and more formal, still standard in British programs and exhibition labels. **Bare *courtesy* with no *of* is normal American credit-line style**: *Photo courtesy Getty Images*.
**Contrast:** Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by): *by* credits authorship, *courtesy of* credits permission or supply — *a photograph by Ansel Adams* versus *a photograph courtesy of the Adams estate*. Against [thanks to](11-complex-multiword.md#thanks-to): *thanks to* names a cause of an outcome; *courtesy of* names a giver. In the ironic use the two converge exactly, and both go sour on purpose: *a two-hour delay courtesy of the FAA*. This entry owns the **credit-and-supply** sense; the purely causal use (*half of downtown was dry by noon, courtesy of a burst main*) is written up with the prepositions of cause at [courtesy of](06-cause-purpose.md#courtesy-of).

Credits the party that supplied, permitted, or provided the thing. Its native habitat is the small print under a photograph, but it has spread into ordinary prose in two directions: as a neutral attribution of a source, and — far more commonly in speech — as a sarcastic attribution of blame, where the "courtesy" is anything but.

**1. Supplied or permitted by; with the permission of.**

1. All images *courtesy of* the National Archives unless otherwise noted.
2. Data *courtesy of* the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
3. Dinner was *courtesy of* the hotel, which had lost our reservation twice.

**2. Caused by — usually ironic, occasionally admiring.**

4. We spent the night in Denver, *courtesy of* a mechanical problem in Phoenix.
5. The winning run came *courtesy of* a throwing error by the shortstop.
6. He has a scar over his left eye *courtesy of* a hockey stick and a bad decision.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [thanks to](11-complex-multiword.md#thanks-to), [at the hands of](#at-the-hands-of), [through](#through), [courtesy of (cause)](06-cause-purpose.md#courtesy-of)

### on the part of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈpɑrt ʌv/ &middot; *on thuh PART uhv*; weak /ən ðə ˈpɑrt əv/, casually /ən ðə ˈpɑrt ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, academic, and bureaucratic prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, always **animate or institutional**. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. With a pronoun, most writers switch to the possessive construction instead: *on his part*, *for my part*.
**Pattern:** [abstract noun of behavior] *on the part of* [agent] &middot; *On the part of* [agent], [clause]
**Contrast:** It hangs an agent on an abstract noun — a failure, a decision, a reluctance — where [by](02-core-relational.md#by) would be ambiguous: *a failure by the board* can be read as a failure the board caused, while *a failure on the part of the board* can only mean the board failed. That precision is why the phrase survives in legal writing, and nine times out of ten in ordinary prose the possessive is shorter and just as clear: *the board's failure*. Do not confuse it with [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of), which means *representing*, not *committed by*.

Assigns the doer of an abstract action to the noun that names it. Whenever a nominalization has swallowed a verb — *negligence*, *reluctance*, *misstatement*, *effort* — the subject of that buried verb has to be reattached somehow, and this is the formal way to do it.

1. The report found no wrongdoing *on the part of* the responding officers.
2. There was real reluctance *on the part of* the faculty to move the deadline.
3. Any misstatement *on the part of* the applicant voids the policy.
4. It took months of quiet work *on the part of* two staffers to get the language drafted.
5. An error *on the part of* the pilot does not by itself establish negligence.
6. The delay reflected a genuine failure of nerve *on the part of* the leadership.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of), [at the hands of](#at-the-hands-of)

## Instrument and means — what it was done with

Instrumental *with* and method-marking *by* handle almost all of this in speech. The words below are what English reaches for when the instrument is a process rather than an object, when the instrument is the raw material the thing was made out of, when there is an intermediary in the chain, or when the register demands more syllables.

### by dint of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈdɪnt ʌv/ &middot; *by DINT uhv*; weak /baɪ ˈdɪnt əv/, casually /baɪ ˈdɪnt ə/ &middot; *by* itself has no weak form and keeps its full /aɪ/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, literary; faintly bookish
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically abstract (*effort*, *persistence*, *sheer luck*, *brute force*) — or a gerund (*by dint of working nights*). Never a finite clause, never a to-infinitive.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by dint of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *By dint of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Narrower than [by means of](#by-means-of). *By means of* takes an instrument or a procedure; *by dint of* takes exertion, stubbornness, or a personal quality, and it says the result was forced out of an unwilling world. ✗ *by dint of a crowbar* is wrong where *by dint of brute force* is right. *Dint* is a fossil word meaning *a blow, a stroke*, and it survives in modern English only inside this phrase — which is why nobody can misuse the noun and everybody occasionally misspells it *by dent of*.

Attributes an achievement to effort or to a quality rather than to a tool. It always implies that the thing was hard and that the doer supplied whatever was missing out of their own resources.

1. *By dint of* sheer persistence she got the sealed records released.
2. He made the roster *by dint of* being the only left-handed catcher in three counties.
3. The town got through the winter *by dint of* two generators and a great deal of goodwill.
4. She made partner *by dint of* long hours and an unnerving memory for names.
5. *By dint of* repetition alone, the slogan started to sound like common sense.
6. They kept the paper alive *by dint of* not paying themselves for two years.

**See also:** [by means of](#by-means-of), [through](#through), [by virtue of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-virtue-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### by means of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈminz ʌv/ &middot; *by MEENZ uhv*; weak /baɪ ˈminz əv/, casually /baɪ ˈminz ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; technical, scientific, and legal writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*by means of dissolving the residue in acetone*). Never a finite clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by means of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *By means of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** The long form of instrumental [with](02-core-relational.md#with) and of method-marking [by](02-core-relational.md#by). Ordinary English says *with a centrifuge*; a patent application says *by means of a centrifuge*, because patent drafters want the instrument marked unmistakably and *with* has too many other jobs. Against [by dint of](#by-dint-of): means versus effort. Against [via](#via): *via* is compact and can also mark a route, which *by means of* cannot. Against [using](#using): *using* is the modern technical-prose default and is shorter.

Names the instrument, mechanism, or procedure that produced the result. Its whole value is unambiguity: in a sentence where *with* could be read as *accompanied by*, *by means of* cannot be.

1. The two fractions were separated *by means of* a benchtop centrifuge.
2. Access to the vault is controlled *by means of* a keycard and a four-digit PIN.
3. The signal reaches the valley *by means of* a repeater on the ridge.
4. He supported the family *by means of* a night job and two lodgers.
5. Payment may be made *by means of* certified check or wire transfer.
6. The sample was purified *by means of* passing it twice through a silica column.

**See also:** [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [using](#using), [via](#via), [by dint of](#by-dint-of), [by way of](#by-way-of), [by means of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-means-of)

### by way of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈweɪ ʌv/ &middot; *by WAY uhv*; weak /baɪ ˈweɪ əv/, casually /baɪ ˈweɪ ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase; a gerund (*by way of explaining himself*); or — in the manner sense only — a **bare article-less abstract noun**: *by way of apology*, *by way of introduction*, *by way of illustration*. Never a finite clause of any kind, and never a to-infinitive: ✗ *by way of to explain*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by way of* [place] (route) &middot; [clause] *by way of* [bare abstract noun] (purpose) &middot; [clause] *by way of* [noun phrase] (means)
**Contrast:** Three jobs share one phrase and the complement tells you which is running. A place name means **route** and equals [via](#via). A bare abstract noun means **as a form of** and equals *as* or *as a kind of*. Anything else means **means** and equals [by means of](#by-means-of), which is the weakest of the three uses and usually cuttable — *by way of illustration* is just *for example*. Compare the reference-and-topic use at [by way of](10-topic-reference-role.md#by-way-of).

**1. By the route of; passing through.**

1. We drove home *by way of* Flagstaff to get around the storm.
2. Mail from the islands still comes *by way of* Miami.
3. The flight goes to Managua *by way of* Houston and adds four hours to the trip.

**2. As a form of; for the purpose of.** Takes a bare noun with no article, or a gerund.

4. *By way of* apology he sent a bottle of wine and a note that explained nothing.
5. She mentioned the lawsuit only *by way of* introduction and then moved on.
6. He put up a slide of the 1954 map *by way of* showing how little had changed.

**3. Through the mechanism of; by means of.**

7. The money reaches the districts *by way of* a county trust.
8. He learned Portuguese *by way of* two years in São Paulo and a patient landlady.
9. Complaints get to the board *by way of* an ombudsman nobody has ever met.

**See also:** [via](#via), [through](#through), [by means of](#by-means-of), [out of](#out-of), [by way of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-way-of), [by way of](10-topic-reference-role.md#by-way-of)

### out of

**Pronunciation:** /aʊt ʌv/ &middot; *OWT uhv*; weak /ˈaʊt əv/ and, at any ordinary speed, the two words run together as /ˈaʊtə/ &middot; *OW-tuh* — which is what the eye-spelling *outta* is transcribing, and the reason learners write ✗ *out a curiosity*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; one of the highest-frequency prepositions in the language
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a **fused relative** (*built out of what was left in the barn*). A **gerund** occurs only in the motive sense treated elsewhere (*out of not wanting to embarrass her*), and even there it grates. **Never a finite clause and never a *that*-clause** — ✗ *out of that he was tired*. **It strands as a unit**, both words together: *What is it made out of?*, *the fund the raise came* **out of** — but the *of* can never be dropped and never left behind: ✗ *what is it made out?*
**Pattern:** [verb of making] *out of* [material] &middot; [number] *out of* [number] &middot; [cost or payment] *out of* [fund or source]
**Contrast:** This entry owns the **material**, **proportion**, and **funding-source** senses. The spatial sense (*out of the building*) is at [out of](04-movement-direction.md#out-of), the motive sense (*out of spite*, *out of curiosity*) at [out of](06-cause-purpose.md#out-of), and the general all-senses entry at [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of). **Against [of](02-core-relational.md#of) for materials:** *made of oak* names the substance and leaves the substance recognizable; *made out of oak* insists that raw stuff was transformed — which is why *a table made out of a door* is idiomatic and ✗ *a table made of a door* is not. Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from): *from* is the marker when the material is no longer visible in the product (*wine from grapes*, *paper from rags*), *out of* when it still is. **Against [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) for proportions:** *one in five households* is a rate over a population, *nine out of ten dentists* is a count taken from a stated set — which is why a test score is always *92 out of 100* and never ✗ *92 in 100*. ***Out* alone is an adverb and a verb particle** (*She went out*, *Cross it out*, *The fire is out*) and takes **no object at all**, which is the test; the preposition is the two-word form.

**1. The material a thing was made from.** The stuff is still recognizable in the product.

1. He built the whole shed *out of* salvaged barn siding.
2. The bowl is turned *out of* a single piece of walnut.
3. She made supper *out of* whatever was left in the pantry.

**2. Out of a stated set — the proportion sense.** Both numbers are given, and the second is the whole.

4. Nine *out of* ten of the households surveyed had never heard of the program.
5. She scored ninety-two *out of* a hundred and was furious about the eight.
6. Three *out of* the four bridges on that stretch are rated structurally deficient.

**3. The fund or source a cost was paid from.**

7. The overtime came *out of* the general fund, not *out of* the grant.
8. Two of the positions are paid *out of* federal money that runs out in June.
9. He covered the difference *out of* his own pocket, which is the line the auditors underlined.

**See also:** [of](02-core-relational.md#of), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [by means of](#by-means-of), [via](#via), [out of (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#out-of), [out of (cause)](06-cause-purpose.md#out-of), [out of (complex)](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of)

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; *THROO* — no true weak form; in rapid speech the vowel shortens toward /θrʊ/ and, before a consonant, as far as /θrə/ (*through the door* /θrə ðə ˈdɔr/). The spelling *thru* is signage and shorthand, not standard prose.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or **gerund** (*through working weekends for two years*). Never a finite clause. **It strands freely**: *the agency I found it* **through**, *Who did you hear it* **through**?
**Pattern:** [clause] *through* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *Through* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; stranded: *the program I got the job* **through**
**Contrast:** This entry covers the **means and intermediary** senses; the spatial sense (*through the tunnel*) is at [through](04-movement-direction.md#through), the inclusive-date sense (*Monday through Friday*) is at [through](05-time.md#through), and the fault-and-failing sense sketched in sense 3 below is worked out in full at [through](06-cause-purpose.md#through). Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by): *by* marks the direct agent or the medium, *through* marks the channel that carried it — *I heard it by phone* is the medium, *I heard it through a friend* is the intermediary. Against [via](#via): near-synonyms for the intermediary sense, but *through* takes gerunds and strands where *via* does neither. **Also an adverb and a particle** — *Let me through*, *We got through*, *I'm through with him* — and there it has no object at all, which is the test.

**1. By the means or method of.** The process that produced the result.

1. He built the business *through* twenty years of eighteen-hour days.
2. Most of our hiring happens *through* referrals rather than postings.
3. She got her start *through* working weekends at a station nobody listened to.

**2. Through the agency of; via an intermediary.**

4. She got the loan *through* a credit union rather than a bank.
5. You can only reach him *through* his agent, and his agent does not return calls.
6. The house sold *through* a broker who never once set foot in it.

**3. Because of — normally with a fault, an accident, or a lack.**

7. The mix-up happened *through* a clerical error, not *through* malice.
8. *Through* no fault of her own she ended up in the wrong terminal in the wrong city.
9. Two boxes of personnel records were lost *through* sheer inattention.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [via](#via), [by means of](#by-means-of), [by way of](#by-way-of), [through (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#through), [through (time)](05-time.md#through), [through (cause)](06-cause-purpose.md#through)

### using

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjuzɪŋ/ &middot; *YOO-zing*; casually /ˈjuzɪn/ &middot; no weak form — it keeps its own stress, which is one sign it is still half a verb
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default in technical writing and instructions
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. Never a finite clause, and — unlike a true preposition — **it does not take a gerund**: ✗ *using boiling the water*. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [clause] *using* [noun phrase] &middot; *Using* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** A present participle that has drifted most of the way into prepositionhood. It competes with instrumental [with](02-core-relational.md#with) and formal [by means of](#by-means-of), and technical prose prefers it because it names a tool without any of *with*'s other meanings: *Open the file with Sarah* is ambiguous, *Open the file using Sarah's account* is not. **Its participial origin still shows**: when the main clause has no plausible user, you get a dangling modifier — ✗ *Using a spreadsheet, the numbers were checked* says the numbers used the spreadsheet. Compare [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given) and [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering), which have gone further and no longer need a subject to attach to.

Names the tool, material, or resource an agent employed. It is the plainest instrumental marker English has after *with*, and unlike *with* it says nothing about accompaniment, possession, or manner.

1. Tighten the bracket *using* a ten-millimeter socket.
2. The map was drawn *using* satellite imagery from 2019.
3. She paid *using* a card she had reported stolen the week before.
4. *Using* nothing but a paperclip, he got the battery cover off.
5. We track attendance *using* a shared spreadsheet nobody has permission to edit.
6. The team modeled the outbreak *using* three separate sets of assumptions.

**See also:** [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [by means of](#by-means-of), [with the aid of](#with-the-aid-of), [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given), [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering)

### via

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvaɪə/ &middot; *VY-uh*; also /ˈviə/ &middot; *VEE-uh* — the second is commoner in British English and in Latin tags. No weak form: it carries a stress of its own, which is part of why it feels crisper than *by way of*.
**Type:** simple (Latin *via*, the ablative of *via* "road") &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; very common in technical and business prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. **Never a gerund and never a finite clause** — ✗ *via sending an email*, ✗ *via he told me*. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [clause] *via* [noun phrase] &middot; [destination] *via* [waypoint] &middot; [noun phrase] *via* [medium]
**Contrast:** The travel-route sense is at [via](04-movement-direction.md#via); this entry is the **medium and intermediary** sense. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by) for media: both work, but *by* wants a bare article-less noun (*by email*, *by phone*, ✗ *by the app*) while *via* takes either (*via email*, *via the app*). Against [through](#through): identical in meaning for intermediaries, but *through* takes gerunds and strands and *via* does neither — **the single commonest error with this word is putting an *-ing* form after it.** Against [by means of](#by-means-of): *via* is the compact modern equivalent and has almost displaced it outside legal drafting.

**1. By way of a medium or channel of transmission.**

1. Send the signed copy *via* email; the original can follow by mail.
2. Tickets are available only *via* the mobile app, which is the whole complaint.
3. Updates go out *via* text message and a recorded call.

**2. Through an intermediary — a person, an agency, a system.**

4. The story reached us *via* a stringer in Ankara.
5. Payments clear *via* the ACH network, usually in two business days.
6. He got the interview *via* a former roommate who owed him a favor.

**See also:** [through](#through), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [by means of](#by-means-of), [by way of](#by-way-of), [via](04-movement-direction.md#via)

### with the aid of

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ði ˈeɪd ʌv/ &middot; *with thee AYD uhv*; weak /wəθ ði ˈeɪd əv/, casually /wəθ ði ˈeɪd ə/ — *with* reduces to /wəθ/ and the *of* to bare /ə/, so the whole phrase can come out as three unstressed syllables around *aid*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase — an instrument or a person. A gerund is possible but rare and clumsy. Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *with the aid of* [noun phrase] &middot; *With the aid of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *with the help of* — the everyday form and the commoner of the two in American English, and the only comfortable choice when the complement is a person. *With the assistance of* — the most formal, standard in official and academic prose.
**Contrast:** Against plain instrumental [with](02-core-relational.md#with): *He read the inscription with a magnifying glass* and *with the aid of a magnifying glass* mean the same thing, but the long form implies a difficulty that had to be overcome. Against [by means of](#by-means-of): *by means of* is neutral about difficulty and treats the instrument as sufficient; *with the aid of* treats it as assistance to an agent who was already working at it. Against [using](#using): *using* is neutral and modern, *with the aid of* is formal and slightly effortful.

Marks an instrument or a helper that made an already-difficult thing possible. The agent stays firmly in the sentence's subject position; the aid is subordinate, which is exactly the difference from *by*.

1. The worn inscription was finally read *with the aid of* raking light.
2. She walks now *with the aid of* a cane and a great deal of stubbornness.
3. *With the help of* two neighbors, they got the tree off the driveway before dark.
4. The bill passed *with the assistance of* four members of the other party.
5. He finished the manuscript *with the aid of* a small grant and a very patient editor.
6. Divers recovered the recorder *with the aid of* a remotely operated vehicle.

**See also:** [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [using](#using), [by means of](#by-means-of), [thanks to](11-complex-multiword.md#thanks-to), [courtesy of](#courtesy-of)

## Manner — how it was done

Manner is where *like* and *as* fight their famous fight, and where American English keeps a small graded set of imitation markers running from the light and journalistic (*à la*) to the heavy and art-historical (*after the manner of*). Note that plain [with](02-core-relational.md#with) supplies most everyday manner adverbials (*with care*, *with difficulty*), and [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) supplies the rest (*in a whisper*, *in Spanish*, *in pencil*, *in haste*). *As* is not written up here at all: its role sense (*works as a lawyer*) is at [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as) and its resemblance-and-comparison sense at [as](09-comparison-opposition.md#as). The entries below are the **manner** half of a pair — *like*, *unlike*, and *in the manner of* are also treated as prepositions of [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md), which is where the parallelism and case rules are worked out at length.

### à la

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑ ˈlɑ/ &middot; *ah-LAH*; commonly reduced to /ə ˈlɑ/
**Type:** complex (French *à la manière de*, clipped) &middot; **Register:** neutral to journalistic; knowing in tone
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a **proper name** — a person, a work, a brand, a place, an era. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [clause] *à la* [proper name] &middot; [noun phrase] *à la* [proper name]
**Variants:** written *a la* without the accent in a great deal of American copy; *à la* is the careful spelling and the one dictionaries head. The French gender agreement does not survive: it is *à la* before every complement, masculine or not.
**Contrast:** A compact *in the style of*. Against [like](#like): *like* compares anything to anything and asserts nothing about intent, while *à la* compares a **way of doing** to a known model and always carries a whiff of imitation, homage, or parody. Against [after the manner of](#after-the-manner-of): the same meaning at the opposite register — *à la* is light and current, *after the manner of* is heavy and belongs in a museum label. Do not confuse the preposition with the frozen culinary nouns *à la carte*, *à la mode*, *à la king*, which take no complement at all.

Says that something is done in the recognizable style of a named model. The name is doing the definitional work, so it has to be a name the reader already carries around — this is a preposition that fails completely on an unfamiliar complement.

1. He gave a rambling hour-long press conference *à la* his predecessor.
2. The episode ends on a freeze-frame *à la* every sitcom made between 1979 and 1988.
3. She writes long comma-spliced paragraphs *à la* Kerouac and edits none of them.
4. They want a walkable downtown *à la* Portland, on a third of Portland's budget.
5. The team plays a switch-everything defense *à la* the 2015 Warriors.
6. He does the whole deadpan-narrator thing *à la* Wes Anderson.

**See also:** [like](#like), [after the manner of](#after-the-manner-of), [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as), [in the manner of](09-comparison-opposition.md#in-the-manner-of)

### according to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tu/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding too*; weak /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tə/
**Type:** complex (participial in origin, from the verb *accord*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a ***wh*-nominal** — either a **fused relative** (*according to what the manual says*, *according to whoever answered the phone*) or an **embedded interrogative** (*according to how much water you use*, *according to which shift you are on*). **Never a *that*-clause, never any other finite clause, and never a gerund** — ✗ *according to he said*, ✗ *according to that the manual says*, ✗ *according to reading the file*. The *to* here is a preposition and not the infinitive marker, so a bare infinitive is impossible too: ✗ *according to conserve water*. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** *According to* [source], [clause] &middot; [clause], *according to* [source] &middot; [clause] *according to* [standard or plan]
**Contrast:** Two distinct jobs, and this file owns the second and third. As an **evidential** (*according to the mayor*) it reports someone else's claim and quietly declines to endorse it — which is why ✗ *according to me* is odd and why attaching it to an obvious fact (*according to the calendar, today is Tuesday*) reads as sarcasm. As a **manner** preposition (*according to plan*, *according to the rules*) it means *in conformity with* and equals [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with) and [in keeping with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-keeping-with). The general entry is at [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to); [as per](#as-per) is the business-memo version of the conformity sense.

**1. On the authority of; as reported by.** The source is somebody other than the writer.

1. *According to* the medical examiner, the death was accidental.
2. Turnout was up nine points, *according to* preliminary county figures.
3. *According to* whoever answered the phone at the clerk's office, the filing window closes at noon.

**2. In conformity with; in the manner prescribed by.**

4. Everything went *according to* plan until about the second hour.
5. Assemble the shelves *according to* the diagram on page four, not the one on the box.
6. Members are seated *according to* seniority, which takes longer than the vote does.

**3. In proportion to; varying with.**

7. Rates change *according to* how much water you use.
8. Class size is set *according to* the age of the students.
9. The bonus is prorated *according to* what each office billed in the fourth quarter.

**See also:** [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to), [as per](#as-per), [per](#per), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), [in keeping with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-keeping-with)

### after the manner of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftɚ ðə ˈmænɚ ʌv/ &middot; *AF-ter thuh MAN-er uhv*; weak /ˈæftɚ ðə ˈmænɚ əv/, casually /ˈæftɚ ðə ˈmænɚ ə/ — five syllables of which only two are stressed, which is most of why the phrase feels heavy
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, literary; standard in art criticism and museum cataloguing
**Complement:** noun phrase, typically a proper name, a named school, or a period. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *after the manner of* [model] &middot; [noun phrase] *after the manner of* [model]
**Variants:** *in the manner of* — commoner in American English and the form to prefer in ordinary prose; it has its own entry at [in the manner of](09-comparison-opposition.md#in-the-manner-of). *In the style of* is the plainest member of the family and the one to use when no art-world echo is wanted. **Bare *after* in museum and auction cataloguing** (*After Cuyp*) is a term of art, not a casual clipping: it means a later copy by another hand.
**Contrast:** The formal counterpart of [à la](#à-la) and of manner [like](#like). Against *like*: *like* asserts resemblance, *after the manner of* asserts deliberate imitation of a method. **In cataloguing the terms are graded and legally meaningful**: *by* is autograph, *attributed to* is probably autograph, *studio of* means the workshop produced it, *in the manner of* means a follower whose hand is unknown, and *after* means somebody else copied it later. Using them loosely in a sale description is a misrepresentation, not a style choice.

Says that the thing was made or done in conscious imitation of a named model's method. Where *à la* is a quick comparison, this is a claim about how the work was actually made.

1. He wrote a sequence of unrhymed sonnets *after the manner of* Berryman.
2. The chapel was decorated *in the manner of* the Venetian churches he had spent two years studying.
3. She argued the case *after the manner of* a prosecutor rather than a defense attorney.
4. The label read simply "Landscape with Cattle, *after* Cuyp."
5. The house rambles *in the manner of* a New England farmhouse that grew by accretion.
6. The score is orchestrated *after the manner of* the late Romantics, down to the doubled horns.

**See also:** [à la](#à-la), [like](#like), [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as), [in the guise of](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-the-guise-of), [in the manner of](09-comparison-opposition.md#in-the-manner-of)

### as per

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈpɚ/ &middot; *az PUR*; weak /əz pɚ/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** business and bureaucratic; disliked by every usage authority that mentions it
**Complement:** noun phrase only — almost always a document, an instruction, or a prior communication (*as per your email*, *as per the contract*). Never a gerund, never a *wh*-nominal, never a finite clause: ✗ *as per you asked* → *as per your request*, or better still *as you asked*.
**Pattern:** *As per* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *as per* [noun phrase] &middot; fixed: *as per usual*
**Contrast:** A redundant compound: [per](#per) already means *according to*, so the *as* adds nothing, and commentators have been calling the phrase illiterate since the 1800s. It survives because it sounds official. In plain prose write *per*, *according to*, *following*, or nothing at all — *as per our conversation* is *as we discussed*. Note that *as per usual* is a separate jokey idiom that is **meant** to sound wrong, and it is the one use nobody objects to. Do not confuse it with distributive [per](#per) (*sixty miles per hour*), which is a different job and perfectly respectable. Its life as a document-naming phrase is treated with the prepositions of reference at [as per](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-per).

States that something conforms to an instruction, a document, or an established practice. It is pure register: it signals that the writer is operating inside a bureaucracy and is covering themselves.

1. The deposit was refunded *as per* the terms of the lease.
2. *As per* your request, I have attached last quarter's figures.
3. Invoices are due within thirty days *as per* company policy.
4. The units were installed *as per* the manufacturer's instructions.
5. He turned up forty minutes late, *as per* usual.
6. Access was revoked *as per* the offboarding checklist.

**See also:** [per](#per), [according to](#according-to), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to), [as per (reference)](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-per)

### like

**Pronunciation:** /laɪk/ &middot; *LYK* — a full lexical word with no reduced form, though it is routinely unstressed
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral as a preposition; the conjunction use (*like I said*) is informal, and the approximative use is informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, **objective-case pronoun** (*like me*, *like her and me* — ✗ *like I*, ✗ *like she*), a gerund (*It's like flying*), or a **fused relative** (*like what happened in 2019*, *like whoever ran it last year*). **As a preposition it cannot take a finite clause, and never a that-clause.** *Like I said* and *like he does* are the **conjunction** *like*, which is standard in American speech and casual writing and is still edited out of formal prose in favor of *as*. It strands only in the fixed interrogative *What is she like?*
**Pattern:** [verb] *like* [noun phrase] &middot; [be] *like* [noun phrase] &middot; *like* [verb-ing] &middot; *Like* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; fixed: *What is X* **like**?
**Contrast:** **Against *as*, the classic split.** *Like* takes a noun and asserts resemblance; *as* takes a clause, or a role actually held — *She works like a lawyer* means she resembles one, *She works as a lawyer* means she is one; see [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as). The three-way ladder is worth memorizing: *Do it like me* (imitate my example, everywhere), *Do it as I do* (formal writing), *Do it like I do* (standard American speech, flagged in formal writing). For introducing examples, *such as* is the conservative choice in edited prose because *like* can be read as *resembling* — *poets like Frost* may or may not include Frost — though the distinction is not observed in general American usage, where *like* introduces examples freely. **Also a verb (*I like it*), a noun (*and the like*), an adjective (*of like mind*), an adverb, a conjunction (*like I said*), a suffix (*childlike*), a quotative (*he was like, no way*), and a discourse particle** — the preposition is the one with a noun-phrase object and no clause after it. The comparison entry, with the case and parallelism rules in full, is at [like](09-comparison-opposition.md#like).

**1. Resembling; in the manner of.** The core sense.

1. He drives *like* a man who has never been ticketed in his life.
2. The fabric feels *like* raw silk and costs about a tenth as much.
3. Standing there in the doorway, she sounded exactly *like* her mother.
4. He was shaking *like* a leaf and pretending he was cold.

**2. Characteristic of; typical of.** Almost always with *be*, and very often negated.

5. It is just *like* him to volunteer for a committee and then miss every meeting.
6. That is not *like* her at all — she is never late.
7. It would be just *like* the county to repave the street the week after the parade.

**3. Introducing an example.**

8. Bring something small: a paperback, a deck of cards, something *like* that.
9. Cities *like* Tucson and El Paso have been planning for this for twenty years.
10. We want something *like* what they built in Boise, only smaller.

**4. Approximately — informal, spoken.**

11. The whole repair came to *like* four hundred dollars.
12. We waited *like* twenty minutes and then just left.

**5. In the fixed question *What is X like?*** Asking for a description.

13. What is the new place *like*?
14. I have not met him. What is he *like*?

**6. Apparently, seemingly — after *look, sound, feel, seem, smell, taste*.**

15. It looks *like* rain before noon.
16. That sounds *like* a plan nobody has thought through.

**See also:** [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as), [unlike](#unlike), [à la](#à-la), [after the manner of](#after-the-manner-of), [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [like (comparison)](09-comparison-opposition.md#like), [such as](11-complex-multiword.md#such-as)

### unlike

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈlaɪk/ &middot; *un-LYK* — stressed on the second syllable, and no weak form
**Type:** compound (*un-* + *like*, no longer analyzed as two pieces) — **also an adjective** (*two unlike quantities*, *unlike poles attract*), where it takes no object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*unlike me*), a gerund (*Unlike renting, buying builds equity*), or a **fused relative** (*unlike what I said last week*). **Never a finite clause** — ✗ *unlike I said*; the repairs are *unlike what I said* or a contrastive conjunction such as *whereas*. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** *Unlike* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *unlike* [noun phrase] &middot; [be] *unlike* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The negation of prepositional [like](#like), with two readings that split by position. Sentence-initial it sets up a **contrast** between two things filling the same role — **and the two must be genuinely comparable or the sentence dangles**: ✗ *Unlike most cities, Houston's zoning is minimal* compares cities to zoning; write *Unlike most cities, Houston has almost no zoning*. Predicatively (*That's unlike him*) it means *uncharacteristic*, matching sense 2 of *like*. It is not the verb *dislike*, and it is not quite *not like*: *He is not like his father* denies resemblance in general, while *Unlike his father, he…* asserts a contrast on one named point. The litotes *not unlike* is a hedge meaning *somewhat similar to*. For the adjectival rival, American English writes *different from*, and *different than* before a clause; British *different to* does not belong in American copy — see [different from](09-comparison-opposition.md#different-from). The full comparison entry is at [unlike](09-comparison-opposition.md#unlike).

**1. In contrast to.**

1. *Unlike* her sister, she never learned to swim.
2. *Unlike* most of the coast, this stretch has no public access at all.
3. *Unlike* renting, a mortgage builds equity, at least in theory.

**2. Not characteristic of; not resembling.**

4. It is *unlike* him to leave without saying anything.
5. Her handwriting is completely *unlike* his, which is how the forgery was caught.
6. The new engine is not *unlike* the one it replaced, only quieter.

**See also:** [like](#like), [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as), [other than](08-accompaniment-exception.md#other-than), [in contrast to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-contrast-to), [unlike (comparison)](09-comparison-opposition.md#unlike), [different from](09-comparison-opposition.md#different-from)

## Rate, ratio, and distribution

The distributive constructions — *twice a day*, *sixty miles an hour*, *five dollars per pound*, *by the dozen* — are where English keeps three interchangeable markers in circulation at three different registers. The *by the* pattern belongs to [by](02-core-relational.md#by): *sold by the pound*, *paid by the hour*, *bought by the dozen*, *rented by the week*, always with *the* and always with a bare singular unit.

### a

**Pronunciation:** /eɪ/ &middot; *AY* (stressed, citation form); weak /ə/ &middot; *uh* — **the weak form is the normal one here**
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the everyday spoken form
**Complement:** a **bare singular count noun of time, quantity, or measure**: *a day*, *a week*, *a head*, *a pound*, *a gallon*, *a person*, *a mile*. Never a plural (✗ *twice a days*), never a gerund, never a clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [frequency] *a* [unit] &middot; [price] *a* [unit] &middot; [quantity] *a* [unit]
**Variants:** *an* before a vowel sound (*an hour*, *an ounce*, *an acre*). [per](#per) is the formal and technical equivalent; *the* replaces it inside the *by* construction (*by the hour*).
**Contrast:** **This is not the indefinite article, even though it is spelled and pronounced identically.** It descends from Old English *on* worn down to nothing — the same erosion that produced *nowadays*, *asleep*, *ashore* — and it means *per*. The test is substitution: *twice a day* is *twice per day*, never *twice one day*. Against [per](#per): identical in meaning, opposite in register — *sixty miles an hour* on the road, *60 miles per hour* on the spec sheet. Do not mix the two: ✗ *per an hour*. British English uses this form somewhat more freely in prices (*two pounds a kilo*); American English is comfortable with either.

Distributes a quantity over a unit. It is the most frequent rate marker in spoken American English by a wide margin, and it is invisible to most speakers, who take it for the article.

1. She takes the medication twice *a* day with food.
2. Ground chuck was four dollars *a* pound that week.
3. He drives about eighty miles *an* hour on the interstate, which explains the three tickets.
4. Tickets were fifteen dollars *a* head at the door and twelve in advance.
5. The plant turns out two thousand units *a* week on one shift.
6. We were getting maybe three calls *an* hour by the end of the night.

**See also:** [per](#per), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [at the rate of](#at-the-rate-of)

### at the rate of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə ˈreɪt ʌv/ &middot; *at thuh RAYT uhv*; weak /ət ðə ˈreɪt əv/, casually /ət ðə ˈreɪt ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; standard in finance, contracts, and technical reporting
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a quantity — a number, a sum, a percentage, or a whole ratio phrase. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *at the rate of* [quantity] *per* [unit] &middot; [clause] *at a rate of* [percentage]
**Variants:** *at a rate of* — used when the rate is being introduced rather than pointed back to. Both are standard American English and the choice is the ordinary *a*/*the* choice.
**Contrast:** The explicit form of rate-marking [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), which is what plain prose uses: *at 6 percent* rather than *at a rate of 6 percent*. Against [per](#per): *per* names the unit, *at the rate of* frames the whole ratio, and the two normally appear in the same phrase. **Do not confuse it with the idiom *at this rate* / *at that rate***, which takes no *of* and means *if things keep going the way they are going*.

Introduces an explicit ratio of quantity to unit. It is the register-marked version of what *at*, *a*, and *per* do more briefly, and it earns its length only when the ratio itself is the point of the sentence.

1. The loan accrues interest *at the rate of* six percent a year, compounded monthly.
2. Water was coming into the hold *at a rate of* about forty gallons a minute.
3. Overtime is paid *at the rate of* one and a half times the base wage.
4. The glacier has been retreating *at a rate of* roughly thirty feet a year since 1998.
5. Bonds were redeemed *at the rate of* a hundred dollars on the thousand.
6. The state reimburses mileage *at the rate of* sixty-seven cents a mile.

**See also:** [per](#per), [a](#a), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### per

**Pronunciation:** /pɝ/ &middot; *PUR* (stressed, citation form); **weak /pɚ/** &middot; *pur* — **the weak form is the normal one**, and the vowel is r-colored either way, so the unstressed form rhymes with the second syllable of *water*: *sixty miles per hour* is /ˈsɪksti ˈmaɪlz pɚ ˈaʊɚ/
**Type:** simple (Latin *per* "through") &middot; **Register:** formal, technical, and commercial in the rate sense; the *according to* sense is business jargon
**Complement:** in the rate sense, a **bare singular noun with no determiner**: *per hour*, *per person*, *per capita*, *per unit* — ✗ *per an hour*, ✗ *per the hour*. In the *according to* sense it does take a determiner (*per our agreement*, *per the memo*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It can stand postpositively with the unit understood: *forty dollars per*.
**Pattern:** [quantity] *per* [bare unit] &middot; [quantity] *per* (unit understood) &middot; *Per* [document], [clause]
**Contrast:** Against [a](#a): identical in the rate sense, different only in register. Against [for](02-core-relational.md#for): *four dollars per pound* is a rate, *four dollars for a pound* is one transaction. Against [as per](#as-per): *as per* is this word with a useless *as* bolted on, and usage guides that tolerate *per your email* still object to *as per your email*. Watch the Latin fossils, which keep the article-less rule: *per annum*, *per capita*, *per diem*, *per se*, *per stirpes*, and *per cent* — **written as one word, *percent*, in American English and as two in British**.

**1. For each — the rate sense.**

1. The room is two hundred dollars *per* night, plus tax and a resort fee nobody explains.
2. Output is measured in barrels *per* day.
3. The state spends about twelve thousand dollars *per* student.
4. Income *per* capita has been essentially flat for a decade.
5. Delivery is nine dollars a box, or seven *per* if you order a pallet.

**2. According to; in accordance with.** Business register.

6. *Per* our agreement, the deposit is nonrefundable after Friday.
7. The forms were filed *per* the court's instructions.
8. *Per* the revised handbook, mileage is reimbursed only with a receipt.

**3. Through; by means of.** Surviving mainly in commercial and legal formulas.

9. The goods were shipped *per* the SS *Marquette* out of New Orleans.
10. Notice may be given *per* certified mail or *per* hand delivery.
11. Payment was remitted *per* the escrow agent named in Schedule B.

**See also:** [a](#a), [at the rate of](#at-the-rate-of), [as per](#as-per), [according to](#according-to), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### times

**Pronunciation:** /taɪmz/ &middot; *TYMZ* — a full lexical word with **no weak form**; it keeps its own stress even between two stressed numerals (*seven TIMES eight*)
**Type:** simple (a plural noun reanalyzed as a preposition) — **also the ordinary plural noun** (*three times a week*, *at times*, *the good times*), which takes no complement &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase — a number, a quantity, a measure noun, or a fused relative (*three times what they paid*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [number] *times* [number] &middot; [number] *times* [the] [noun phrase] &middot; [number] *times* [comparative adjective] &middot; [number] *times* *as* [adjective] *as* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** As a preposition it is the spoken form of the multiplication sign, and **the phrase it heads is singular**: *Six times four is twenty-four*, not ✗ *are*. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by): dimensions take *by* (*a three by five card*, *eight by ten glossies*), multiplication takes *times*. Against the *-fold* suffix: *threefold* attaches to the number, *three times* is a phrase. **The classic ambiguity: *three times more than* and *three times as much as* are used interchangeably in American English, but strictly the first means 4× and the second 3×** — in careful writing use *as much as*, or give both numbers. Note that *times* in *three times a week* and *at times* is the ordinary plural noun, not this preposition.

**1. Multiplied by — arithmetic.**

1. Seven *times* eight is fifty-six.
2. Multiply the width *times* the height to get the square footage.
3. Twelve *times* twelve is a gross, which is the only place most people still meet the word.

**2. Expressing a multiple or a ratio.**

4. The house is worth three *times* what they paid for it in 2011.
5. Rents downtown are nearly four *times* the county average.
6. She earns ten *times* as much as she did at her first job.
7. The new chip is roughly twenty *times* faster at the same power draw.

**See also:** [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [minus](#minus), [plus](#plus), [over](#over), [to](02-core-relational.md#to)

## Measure, quantity, and price

Every entry in this section is a way of saying *more than*, *less than*, *about*, or *for* — and in every case the plain word is shorter and usually better. What the long forms buy is precision under adversarial reading (*in excess of the deductible*), a documented sum (*in the amount of $500*), or a deliberate editorial tone (*to the tune of three hundred million*). Choose accordingly. The hedges sort themselves by how much slack they claim: *about* and *around* say **close to the number**, [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to) says the same thing from below, [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of) says **the right range**, [on the order of](#on-the-order-of) says **the right power of ten**, and [upward of](#upward-of) says something else again and should usually be rewritten. Two more say something about the *size* of the number rather than its accuracy: [a matter of](#a-matter-of) insists it is small, and [the better part of](#the-better-part-of) insists it is most of a larger one.

### a matter of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmætɚ ʌv/ &middot; *uh MAT-er uhv*; weak /ə ˈmædɚ əv/ — the *t* is a flap, so it sounds like *madder* — casually /ə ˈmædɚ ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a **small** quantity, characteristically a stretch of time (*a matter of minutes*, *a matter of a few dollars*, *a matter of inches*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause in this sense. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in a matter of* [small quantity] &middot; [be] *a matter of* [small quantity] &middot; *only a matter of* [quantity]
**Variants:** *in a matter of* is the commonest full form and is the one that carries the time sense (*in a matter of hours*). *It is only a matter of time* is a frozen idiom meaning *it will certainly happen eventually*, and there the quantity is deliberately unstated.
**Contrast:** **The measure sense always means *only that much*, never *that much or more*** — *it took a matter of minutes* says the job was quick, and ✗ *it took a matter of nine hours* is odd because nine hours is not small. That is the whole rule, and it is why the phrase cannot carry a figure the reader would think large. Do not confuse it with the topic noun in *a matter of principle*, *a matter of public record*, *a matter of opinion*, which names a category rather than a quantity and belongs with the prepositions of reference at [in the matter of](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-the-matter-of). Against [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of): that phrase hedges a figure it does not know, this one belittles a figure it does.

Puts a small number on something in order to say how little of it there was. The figure is real, but the smallness is the point, which is why the phrase resists any quantity a reader would consider substantial.

1. The fire went through the attic in *a matter of* minutes.
2. The whole difference between the two bids is *a matter of* a few hundred dollars.
3. He missed the cut by *a matter of* inches.
4. In *a matter of* three weeks the neighborhood had collected the entire amount.
5. Getting the permit turned out to be *a matter of* two phone calls and a fax nobody could send.
6. Whether the levee holds is now *a matter of* hours, not days.

**See also:** [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [shy of](#shy-of), [within](#within), [the better part of](#the-better-part-of), [in the matter of](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-the-matter-of)

### about

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *uh-BOWT* — the first syllable is a bare schwa, and the whole word clips to /baʊt/ in casual speech: *'bout a hundred people*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default hedge in both speech and writing
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity — a number, a sum, a measure, an age, a proportion. Never a gerund and never a finite clause in this sense. It does not strand. **Also an adverb of degree** (*about right*, *about finished*, *just about done*), where it has no object at all, and that is the test. *About to leave* is a near-future construction, not a prepositional phrase; *a book about running* is the topic word, treated at [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about).
**Pattern:** *about* [number] &middot; *about* [sum] &middot; *about* [measure] &middot; *about as* [adjective] *as* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** [around](#around) is the interchangeable twin, a shade more casual. *Approximately* is the lab-report register, *roughly* the neutral written alternative, *some* the terse journalistic one (*some 200 people*). British *round about two hundred* is not American.
**Contrast:** This entry covers the **quantity** hedge; the clock-and-calendar hedge (*about seven*, *about three weeks*) is at [about](05-time.md#about) and the topic sense at [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about). *About* claims to be **close** to the number, which is what separates it from the rest of the family: [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of) claims only the right range, [on the order of](#on-the-order-of) only the right power of ten. Two habits to avoid: do not hedge a figure that is already exact (*about 217 people* undercuts itself), and do not stack it on a range, which is already doing the hedging — write *forty to fifty people*, not *about forty to fifty people*.

Hedges a quantity by a small margin. It is the most frequent approximator in American English and the one that carries the least attitude: it makes no joke, claims no precision it does not have, and is at home anywhere from a text message to an annual report.

1. *About* two hundred people showed up for a room booked for eighty.
2. The repair came to *about* nine hundred dollars.
3. The tank holds *about* twelve gallons, which gets you to Tucumcari and no farther.
4. Attendance was *about* half what it was in 2019.
5. He is *about* six two, give or take an inch of hair.
6. The two proposals cost *about* the same and differ in every other respect.
7. *About* one in five households in the county has no broadband at all.

**See also:** [around](#around), [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [on the order of](#on-the-order-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [about (time)](05-time.md#about), [about (topic)](10-topic-reference-role.md#about)

### around

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *uh-ROWND* — clipped to /raʊnd/ in casual speech: *'round fifty bucks*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; a shade more casual than *about*
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity, a sum, or a measure. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand in this sense. **Also an adverb and a verb particle** (*stick around*, *turn it around*, *the other way around*), where it has no object — that is the test.
**Pattern:** *around* [number] &middot; *around* [sum] &middot; *somewhere around* [quantity]
**Variants:** ***round* is the British form and is not American English.** An American writer uses *around* for the preposition and reserves *round* for the noun, verb, and adjective (*a round of drinks*, *round the numbers off*, *a round figure*); the contraction *'round* belongs to songs and speech, not to edited prose. *Around* is also the American choice in *year-round*, *around the clock*, and *the other way around*.
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with [about](#about) for quantities, with a mild division of labor: *about* is commoner with measured amounts and in formal writing, *around* commoner in speech and with round figures. Both claim to be close to the number. Against [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of): the long phrase widens the tolerance and adds a folksy tone. The spatial and movement senses (*around the corner*, *around the lake*) are at [around](04-movement-direction.md#around); the clock-and-calendar sense is at [around](05-time.md#around).

Hedges a quantity, informally. It is the spoken default — in conversation Americans reach for it about as often as for *about*, and the choice between the two is rhythm rather than meaning.

1. The truck goes for *around* forty thousand new.
2. They are expecting *around* fifty people, which in that family means sixty-five.
3. The building is *around* a hundred years old and has the wiring to prove it.
4. Enrollment has held steady at *around* twelve hundred since the mill closed.
5. We ended up somewhere *around* eight hundred dollars over budget.
6. He weighed *around* two-forty when they signed him and *around* two-ninety when they cut him.

**See also:** [about](#about), [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [around (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#around), [around (time)](05-time.md#around)

### at the expense of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ði ɪkˈspɛns ʌv/ &middot; *at thee ik-SPENSS uhv*; weak /ət ði ɪkˈspɛns əv/, casually /ət ði ɪkˈspɛns ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*at the expense of getting any sleep*). Never a finite clause. In the payment sense a possessive replaces the *of*: *at the taxpayers' expense*, *at his own expense*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *at the expense of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *at* [possessive] *expense*
**Variants:** *at the cost of* — interchangeable in the sacrifice sense, and *at a cost of* is the only one of the family that takes a plain dollar figure (*at a cost of $4 million*).
**Contrast:** Three senses share a form. **Sacrifice**: *speed at the expense of accuracy* names what was given up. **Payment**: *at the taxpayers' expense* names who paid, and there the possessive is normal. **Humiliation**: *a joke at his expense*. Against [to the tune of](#to-the-tune-of): both carry editorial weight, but *to the tune of* is about the size of a sum and this is about what the sum cost somebody.

**1. With the sacrifice of; by giving up.**

1. The redesign gained speed *at the expense of* battery life.
2. He finished the degree *at the expense of* nearly every weekend for three years.
3. She built the department up *at the expense of* two smaller programs that got folded in.
4. The plant runs cleaner now, though *at the cost of* about eleven percent of its output.

**2. Paid for by.** The possessive is the normal shape here.

5. The trip was made entirely *at the taxpayers' expense*, which is how it became a story.
6. Two of the flights were booked *at the expense of* the campaign committee.
7. The repairs were finally made *at the landlord's expense* after the second inspection.

**3. At the humiliation of.**

8. They kept telling the same story every Thanksgiving, always *at the expense of* the same cousin.
9. The whole toast was ten minutes of jokes *at the expense of* the groom's driving.
10. He got the laugh, but *at the expense of* the one person in the room who could not answer back.

**See also:** [in the amount of](#in-the-amount-of), [to the tune of](#to-the-tune-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [in exchange for](11-complex-multiword.md#in-exchange-for)

### in excess of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs ʌv/ &middot; *in ik-SESS uhv*; weak /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs əv/, casually /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, financial, and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase, essentially always a quantity or a named threshold. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [quantity] *in excess of* [threshold] &middot; [clause] *in excess of* [quantity]
**Contrast:** A five-word way of saying *more than*, and in ordinary prose it should be cut: *in excess of 200 people* is *more than 200 people*. It earns its place in contracts and specifications, where [over](#over) can be misread spatially and *more than* can sound like an estimate: *amounts in excess of the deductible* is unambiguous and *amounts over the deductible* is not quite. Against [upward of](#upward-of): *upward of* is an estimate near the number, *in excess of* is a hard floor. Against [north of](#north-of): same meaning, opposite register.

Marks a quantity as exceeding a stated threshold, with the threshold treated as a boundary rather than an approximation. Everything above the line is included and nothing below it is.

1. The settlement was reported to be *in excess of* four million dollars.
2. Speeds *in excess of* ninety miles an hour were recorded on that stretch of road.
3. Any amount *in excess of* the policy limit is the insured's own responsibility.
4. The building has stood for a period *in excess of* two centuries.
5. Loads *in excess of* four hundred pounds will void the warranty.
6. Applicants with *in excess of* ten years' service are exempt from the review.

**See also:** [over](#over), [north of](#north-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [under](#under), [to the extent of](#to-the-extent-of)

### in the amount of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði əˈmaʊnt ʌv/ &middot; *in thee uh-MOWNT uhv*; weak /ɪn ði əˈmaʊnt əv/, casually /ɪn ði əˈmaʊnt ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal and commercial; distinctly American bureaucratic English
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a sum of money — figures or words. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [check / judgment / invoice / refund / bond] *in the amount of* [sum]
**Variants:** *in the sum of* — older, and commoner in British legal drafting. *In the total amount of* for aggregates. *For* alone is the plain-English replacement and the one federal plain-language guidance recommends.
**Contrast:** Pure legalese for *for* or *of*: *a check in the amount of $500* is *a check for $500* and nothing else. It survives because in a document that will be read adversarially it keeps the sum visibly separate from the instrument, and because forms have always been written this way. Against [to the tune of](#to-the-tune-of): same territory, opposite register and opposite attitude — the legal phrase is scrupulously neutral, the journalistic one is not.

Attaches an exact sum to a financial instrument or an obligation. It states the figure and takes no position on whether the figure is large.

1. Enclosed is a check *in the amount of* twelve hundred dollars.
2. The court entered judgment *in the amount of* $84,317 plus costs.
3. We received an invoice *in the amount of* $2,415.60, which does not match the estimate.
4. A refund *in the amount of* the deposit will be issued within ten business days.
5. Bond was set *in the amount of* fifty thousand dollars.
6. The district issued notes *in the total amount of* nine million dollars.

**See also:** [to the tune of](#to-the-tune-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [to the extent of](#to-the-extent-of), [at the expense of](#at-the-expense-of)

### in the neighborhood of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈneɪbɚˌhʊd ʌv/ &middot; *in thuh NAY-ber-hood uhv*; weak /ɪn ðə ˈneɪbɚhʊd əv/, casually /ɪn ðə ˈneɪbɚhʊd ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; distinctly American
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity or a sum, or a **fused relative** standing in for one (*in the neighborhood of what the appraiser said*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the neighborhood of* [quantity] &middot; *somewhere in the neighborhood of* [quantity]
**Variants:** *in the ballpark of* — more informal, also American, from the same family as *a ballpark figure*. *In the region of* and *of the order of* are the British equivalents and read as foreign in American copy. *Somewhere in the neighborhood of* is the usual full form in speech.
**Contrast:** A hedge with a wider tolerance than [about](#about) and a friendlier tone than [on the order of](#on-the-order-of). The three make different claims about precision: *about $200* claims to be close, *in the neighborhood of $200* claims only the right range, *on the order of $200* claims only the right power of ten. Because it is folksy, it is out of place in a document where the number matters.

Gives an approximate quantity while explicitly declining to be precise. The metaphor is spatial and does real work: a neighborhood is a region, not a point, and the phrase concedes that the speaker has not measured.

1. Repairs will run *in the neighborhood of* three thousand dollars, maybe a little more.
2. They are expecting somewhere *in the neighborhood of* forty thousand people.
3. The engine puts out *in the neighborhood of* four hundred horsepower.
4. He has been doing this for something *in the neighborhood of* twenty years.
5. The house sold for something *in the ballpark of* what the appraiser had said.
6. Attendance was *in the ballpark of* what we saw last spring.

**See also:** [about](#about), [around](#around), [on the order of](#on-the-order-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [shy of](#shy-of)

### minus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmaɪnəs/ &middot; *MY-nuhs* — **no weak form**; the second syllable is already a schwa and the first keeps its stress
**Type:** simple (Latin, the neuter of *minor*) — **also a noun** (*a minus*, *the minuses outweigh the pluses*) and **an adjective** (*a minus sign*, *a B-minus*), neither of which takes an object &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic; informal in the *lacking* sense
**Complement:** noun phrase or numeral. Never a gerund, never a clause. It does not strand. **In the temperature and negative-number use it is not really a preposition at all** but part of the numeral, and there it takes no preceding quantity: *minus forty*, *a minus sign*.
**Pattern:** [number] *minus* [number] &middot; [noun phrase] *minus* [noun phrase] &middot; *minus* [number] (temperature)
**Contrast:** Against [plus](#plus): exact opposites in arithmetic and mirror images in the informal sense — *the team minus its starting center* against *the team plus two draft picks*. Against *less*: in commercial writing *less* is the standard subtractor for deductions (*gross pay less withholding*) and *minus* is the arithmetic one; the exception-marking senses of both are at [less](08-accompaniment-exception.md#less) and [minus](08-accompaniment-exception.md#minus). **American temperature convention:** *minus ten* in writing, *ten below* in speech, and *negative ten* in scientific and increasingly in general American use.

**1. Arithmetic subtraction; reduced by.**

1. Nine *minus* four is five.
2. Net proceeds are the sale price *minus* commissions and closing costs.
3. Enter the gross figure *minus* the two adjustments on line nine.

**2. Below zero — temperatures and negative quantities.**

4. It was *minus* twelve in Fargo the morning they left.
5. The freezer holds steady at *minus* eighteen Celsius.
6. The wind chill hit *minus* thirty and the district held classes anyway.

**3. Without; lacking.** Informal, and usually a little comic.

7. He came back from the trip *minus* a suitcase and one shoe.
8. They played the whole second half *minus* their two best defenders.
9. The band finished the tour *minus* a drummer and most of their gear.

**See also:** [plus](#plus), [times](#times), [over](#over), [minus](08-accompaniment-exception.md#minus), [less](08-accompaniment-exception.md#less), [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without)

### north of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɔrθ ʌv/ &middot; *NORTH uhv*; weak /ˈnɔrθ əv/, casually /ˈnɔrθ ə/
**Type:** complex — **bare *north* is a noun, an adjective, and an adverb** (*due north*, *the north side*, *drive north*), none of which takes an object; only *north of* is the preposition &middot; **Register:** informal; journalistic, especially in business and sports writing; American
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity — a sum, a number, an age, a speed — or a **fused relative** standing in for one (*south of what the campaign was promising*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *north of* [quantity] &middot; [noun phrase] *north of* [quantity]
**Variants:** *south of* — the mirror image, meaning *less than*, slightly less common and with the same swagger.
**Contrast:** A live metaphor from the map, where north is up the page: *north of $2 million* is *more than $2 million*. Keep it out of formal writing, where [in excess of](#in-excess-of) or plain *more than* belongs, and keep it well away from actual geography, where *north of Denver* means exactly what it says and a reader will take it literally. Against [upward of](#upward-of): *upward of* is an estimate, *north of* asserts a floor with attitude.

Puts a quantity above a stated number, informally and with a note of relish. The number named is almost always a round one, and the point is usually that it is impressively or scandalously large.

1. The contract is reportedly worth *north of* forty million dollars.
2. He was still throwing *north of* ninety-five in the eighth inning.
3. Redoing the kitchen alone will run *north of* thirty thousand.
4. She is *north of* sixty and still runs half marathons.
5. Turnout came in *south of* what the campaign had been promising all month.
6. The stock closed *south of* nine dollars for the first time since 2019.

**See also:** [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [over](#over), [under](#under), [upward of](#upward-of), [shy of](#shy-of)

### on the order of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˈɔrdɚ ʌv/ &middot; *on thee OR-der uhv*; weak /ən ði ˈɔrdɚ əv/, casually /ən ði ˈɔrdɚ ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; scientific and technical
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity, usually a round number or a power of ten; in the second sense, a noun phrase naming a kind of thing. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the order of* [quantity] &middot; [noun phrase] *on the order of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *of the order of* is the standard British form and *in the order of* is also British and criticized there. **American English uses *on the order of*.** None of them is *in order to*, and none is *in the order of* meaning *in the sequence of*.
**Contrast:** In physics and engineering it has a technical meaning — *within a factor of ten of* — so *on the order of a thousand* legitimately covers a few hundred to a few thousand. In general prose it has weakened to *approximately*, and a scientific reader will still hear the stronger claim. Against [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of): the neighborhood form is folksy and implies a narrower range. The second sense, *something on the order of a memoir*, is a manner use rather than a measure one and means *of the kind of*.

**1. Approximately; to within a factor of ten.**

1. The device draws *on the order of* two watts in standby.
2. Losses were *on the order of* a hundred million dollars.
3. Sample sizes were *on the order of* ten thousand per site.
4. Recovery is expected to take *on the order of* six months.

**2. Of the same kind as; along the lines of.**

5. What he has written is *on the order of* a very long apology.
6. She wants something *on the order of* a sabbatical, only paid.
7. The building is *on the order of* a warehouse with better windows.

**See also:** [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [about](#about), [around](#around)

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvɚ/ &middot; *OH-ver* — no weak form; the second syllable is a syllabic /ɚ/, and the flapped *over a* runs together as /ˈoʊvɚɹə/
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a numeral; in the medium sense, a channel noun that normally keeps its article (*over the phone*, *over the air*, *over a secure line*). **Never a gerund in any of the senses below and never a finite clause** — the gerund-taking sense (*sued over failing to disclose the defect*) is the cause preposition at [over](06-cause-purpose.md#over). It strands (*the number we came in* **over**, *what did you hear it* **over**?), and **it is also an adverb and a particle** (*Come over*, *It's over*, *turn it over*), where it has no object — that is the test.
**Pattern:** [verb] *over* [quantity] &middot; [be] *over* [budget / limit] &middot; [number] *over* [number] (a fraction) &middot; [verb of communication] *over* [the channel]
**Contrast:** This entry covers the **excess-of-quantity** senses and the **transmission-medium** sense. The others are spread across the catalog: the spatial senses at [over](04-movement-direction.md#over), the span-of-time and simultaneity senses at [over](05-time.md#over), the trigger-of-conflict sense at [over](06-cause-purpose.md#over), the preference sense (*chose Denver over Phoenix*) at [over](09-comparison-opposition.md#over), and the topic sense (*a fight over money*) at [over](10-topic-reference-role.md#over). Against *more than*: **the old rule that *over* cannot mean *more than* with numbers was dropped by the AP Stylebook in 2014 and was never grounded in real usage** — both are correct and always were. Against *above*: with plain counts *over* is the default (*over sixty people*), while *above* is preferred when the number sits on a named scale (*above 30,000 feet*, *above average*, *above freezing*). Against [under](#under): exact opposites here. **The medium sense against the time sense**: *over dinner* names a stretch of time you can measure, but *over the phone* names a channel and no stretch of anything — which is why *we settled it over the phone in four minutes* is fine and ✗ *we settled it over dinner in four minutes* is not. Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): *on the phone* is the state of being connected, *over the phone* is the route the words took, so *she's on the phone* but *he told me over the phone*.

**1. More than a stated quantity.**

1. *Over* two hundred people showed up for a meeting the room was booked for forty.
2. Anything *over* fifty pounds ships freight.
3. The bridge has carried *over* a million vehicles since it reopened.

**2. Exceeding a budget, a limit, or a standard.**

4. The project came in three weeks late and *over* budget.
5. We are eight hundred dollars *over* for the month and it is the fourteenth.
6. He was doing eighty in a fifty-five — twenty-five *over*.

**3. Divided by — the spoken form of a fraction bar.**

7. Three *over* four is the same as seventy-five percent.
8. The odds were listed as one *over* twelve hundred.

**4. Through a channel of transmission — the medium sense.** The complement is the wire, the air, or the service that carried it.

9. He gave notice *over* the phone, which his father never forgave.
10. The warning went out *over* the emergency band twenty minutes before the sirens.
11. Nothing sensitive goes *over* email, and nothing at all goes *over* the hotel's wifi.

**See also:** [under](#under), [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [north of](#north-of), [upward of](#upward-of), [via](#via), [through](#through), [over (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#over), [over (time)](05-time.md#over), [over (cause)](06-cause-purpose.md#over), [over (topic)](10-topic-reference-role.md#over)

### plus

**Pronunciation:** /plʌs/ &middot; *PLUHS* — **no weak form**; it is stressed even in *four plus four*, which is what keeps it audible in dictation
**Type:** simple (Latin, "more") — **also a noun** (*a real plus*, *the pluses outweigh the minuses*), **an adjective** (*a plus sign*, *a B-plus*), and **a coordinating conjunction** (*Plus, it's cheaper*), none of which takes a prepositional object &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic and pricing; informal as a sentence connector
**Complement:** noun phrase or numeral. Never a gerund in the arithmetic sense. **The informal *Plus, it's cheaper* takes a whole clause, but that is a coordinating conjunction, not this preposition.** It does not strand. Postpositively it attaches to a number with no complement at all: *fifty-plus*, *a B-plus*, *thirty years plus*.
**Pattern:** [number] *plus* [number] &middot; [noun phrase] *plus* [noun phrase] &middot; [number]-*plus* (postpositive) &middot; *Plus*, [clause] (informal conjunction)
**Contrast:** Against [minus](#minus): the arithmetic opposites. Against *and*: **a *plus* phrase does not make a singular subject plural** — *The base rate plus the surcharge is eighty dollars*, singular, is the careful form, exactly as with *along with* and *as well as*. The additive and accompaniment senses are at [plus](08-accompaniment-exception.md#plus). Against *positive*: for temperatures, American English says *ten above* or simply *ten*, never ✗ *plus ten*, which is a European convention.

**1. Arithmetic addition; increased by.**

1. Four *plus* four is eight.
2. Enter the base salary *plus* any bonus in the second column.

**2. With the addition of — the price-and-package sense.**

3. Rent is eleven hundred a month *plus* utilities.
4. The fare is thirty dollars *plus* tip.

**3. Postpositive: and more; at least that much.**

5. The venue holds five hundred *plus*.
6. She has thirty-*plus* years of classroom experience.

**See also:** [minus](#minus), [times](#times), [plus](08-accompaniment-exception.md#plus), [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [along with](11-complex-multiword.md#along-with)

### shy of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʃaɪ ʌv/ &middot; *SHY uhv*; weak /ˈʃaɪ əv/, casually /ˈʃaɪ ə/
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed *of*) — **bare *shy* is an ordinary adjective** (*a shy child*, *don't be shy*) and takes no object &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; American
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity, an age, or a point in time, or a **fused relative** standing in for one (*just shy of what the board had authorized*). Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It is nearly always preceded by a measure of the shortfall or by *just*.
**Pattern:** [size of shortfall] *shy of* [target] &middot; *just shy of* [quantity]
**Contrast:** Means *short of*, *not quite up to*. Unlike [short of](08-accompaniment-exception.md#short-of), it has none of the exception senses: ✗ *shy of a miracle, we are finished*. *Just shy of* is by far the commonest form and hedges downward — *just shy of a million* is a little under a million. Note the collocational quirk: the phrase wants a measured gap in front of it, so *shy of forty* alone is odd where *two months shy of forty* is idiomatic. Against [under](#under) and *less than*, which state a range; *shy of* states a near miss.

Places a quantity just below a target, with the implication that the target was the thing being aimed at. The gap is small by definition, and naming it is most of the point.

1. He died two weeks *shy of* his ninetieth birthday.
2. The bill came in just *shy of* four hundred dollars.
3. She finished a tenth of a second *shy of* the school record.
4. We ended up three votes *shy of* a majority.
5. The tank was a gallon *shy of* full when we got back.
6. The bid came in just *shy of* what the board had authorized.

**See also:** [short of](08-accompaniment-exception.md#short-of), [under](#under), [within](#within), [to within](#to-within), [north of](#north-of), [a matter of](#a-matter-of)

### the better part of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈbɛtɚ ˈpɑrt ʌv/ &middot; *thuh BET-er PART uhv*; weak /ðə ˈbɛdɚ pɑrt əv/ — the *t* of *better* flaps — casually /ðə ˈbɛdɚ pɑrt ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; faintly literary
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a **divisible** quantity — a stretch of time, a sum, a distance, a countable population. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand.
**Pattern:** [verb of spending or lasting] *the better part of* [quantity] &middot; *for the better part of* [stretch of time]
**Variants:** *the best part of* is the British form and turns up in American speech as well; *the greater part of* is the formal one; *most of* is the plain replacement and is shorter every single time.
**Contrast:** **It means *most of*, not *the superior half of***. The *better* is a fossil comparative meaning *larger*, the same one buried in *the better half of the field*, so *the better part of an hour* is about fifty minutes and says nothing about their quality. Two rules keep it honest: the complement has to be divisible (✗ *the better part of a decision*), and the phrase claims **more than half and less than all**, so it cannot be stacked on a hedge — ✗ *about the better part of a year*. Against [about](#about): *about a year* may be over or under, *the better part of a year* is always under, which makes it a downward hedge from a round number. Do not confuse it with the proverb *discretion is the better part of valor*, where *part* means *component* and the whole thing is a different idiom.

Names most of a quantity while conceding it was not all of it. Whoever writes *the better part of a million* is telling you the figure did not quite get there.

1. They spent *the better part of* a year waiting on the environmental review.
2. He drove *the better part of* four hundred miles without stopping once.
3. *The better part of* the crop was in the ground before the rain came.
4. The hearing took up *the better part of* two days and settled nothing.
5. She has lived on that street for *the best part of* thirty years.
6. Redoing the second floor ate *the better part of* the bequest.

**See also:** [about](#about), [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [shy of](#shy-of), [a matter of](#a-matter-of), [under](#under)

### to the extent of

**Pronunciation:** /tu ði ɪkˈstɛnt ʌv/ &middot; *tuh thee ik-STENT uhv*; weak /tə ði ɪkˈstɛnt əv/, casually /tə ði ɪkˈstɛnt ə/ — both the *to* and the *of* reduce, and only *-tent-* carries stress
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and insurance drafting
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*to the extent of resigning*). **Never a finite clause** — with a clause the phrase changes shape to *to the extent that*, which is a subordinating conjunction and a different item.
**Pattern:** [clause] *to the extent of* [quantity / noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; compare the conjunction: *to the extent that* [clause]
**Contrast:** Two readings sorted by the complement. With a number it is a **measure and a ceiling**: *liable to the extent of $50,000* means that is the maximum. With an abstract noun or a gerund it means *as far as, going so far as to*: *he backed her to the extent of resigning*. **Do not blur it into *to the extent that***, which introduces a clause and functions as a conditional (*to the extent that funds are available* = *insofar as*). Against [in the amount of](#in-the-amount-of): that phrase fixes an exact sum, this one fixes an upper bound.

**1. Up to a stated limit; to the maximum of.**

1. Each partner is liable *to the extent of* his capital contribution and no further.
2. The policy covers water damage *to the extent of* fifty thousand dollars.
3. The town rebuilt the levee *to the extent of* its available funds.

**2. Going as far as; to the point of.**

4. He backed her *to the extent of* putting his own name on the loan.
5. She disagreed, *to the extent of* filing a written dissent.
6. The company cooperated *to the extent of* turning over four boxes and then stopped.

**See also:** [in the amount of](#in-the-amount-of), [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [up to](11-complex-multiword.md#up-to), [within](#within)

### to the tune of

**Pronunciation:** /tu ðə ˈtun ʌv/ &middot; *tuh thuh TOON uhv*; weak /tə ðə ˈtun əv/, casually /tə ðə ˈtun ə/
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; journalistic
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a sum of money, characteristically a large one. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *to the tune of* [sum]
**Contrast:** **Not a neutral way of stating an amount.** It always carries an editorial nudge — that the sum is outrageous, wasteful, or improbably large. If you want the figure to land neutrally, use *of* or *for*; if you are drafting a document, use [in the amount of](#in-the-amount-of). It resists small numbers, which is a reliable test: ✗ *fined to the tune of forty dollars* is comic rather than damning. The literal musical sense (*sing it to the tune of "Yankee Doodle"*) is ordinary [to](02-core-relational.md#to) plus a noun, not this idiom.

Attaches a large sum to an action while signaling disapproval or astonishment. The metaphor is that the payer is dancing to somebody else's music.

1. Taxpayers subsidized the stadium *to the tune of* three hundred million dollars.
2. The firm was fined *to the tune of* eleven million for the disclosure failures.
3. He ran up credit card debt *to the tune of* sixty thousand dollars in under two years.
4. The district overspent its transportation budget *to the tune of* about four million.
5. The settlement compensated the plaintiffs *to the tune of* twelve thousand dollars apiece.
6. The state has backstopped the fund *to the tune of* a billion and a half since 2018.

**See also:** [in the amount of](#in-the-amount-of), [at the expense of](#at-the-expense-of), [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### to within

**Pronunciation:** /tu wɪˈðɪn/ &middot; *tuh with-IN*; weak /tə wɪˈðɪn/
**Type:** complex (a preposition stacked on a preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; technical
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a tolerance — a distance, a margin, a percentage. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. A trailing *of* + target is usually obligatory.
**Pattern:** [verb of measuring or approaching] *to within* [tolerance] *of* [target]
**Contrast:** One of the small set of stacked prepositions English allows (*from behind the door*, *except in an emergency*, *until after lunch*). It differs from plain [within](#within) by adding a movement or a measurement that closes on the target: *accurate within a millimeter* states a standing property, *machined to within a millimeter* states a result that was achieved. Against [shy of](#shy-of): *shy of* names a shortfall from a goal, *to within* names a tolerance around a target.

Marks how close a process or measurement came to a target. The *to* supplies the movement and the *within* supplies the margin, which is why neither works alone in these sentences.

1. The part is machined *to within* half a thousandth of an inch.
2. The final polls had it *to within* two points of the actual result.
3. They climbed *to within* a hundred yards of the summit before turning back.
4. Our estimate turned out to be good *to within* about five percent.
5. The clock keeps time *to within* a second a month.
6. The probe mapped the ridge *to within* a few centimeters of vertical accuracy.

**See also:** [within](#within), [shy of](#shy-of), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [within](05-time.md#within)

### under

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndɚ/ &middot; *UHN-der* — **no weak form**; before a vowel the *r* links and the *d* flaps, so *under an hour* comes out /ˈʌndɚɹən ˈaʊɚ/
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a numeral. Never a gerund in this sense, never a finite clause. It strands marginally (*the figure we came in* **under**) and **is also an adverb** (*The whole block went under*), where it has no object.
**Pattern:** [verb] *under* [quantity] &middot; [be / come in] *under* [budget / limit] &middot; *under* [age]
**Contrast:** This entry covers the **below-a-quantity** senses; the spatial sense is at [under](04-movement-direction.md#under). Against *less than*: interchangeable with plain numbers (*under fifty people*, *less than fifty people*), though *fewer than* is the fussier choice for things you can count. Against *below*: *under* for plain quantities and thresholds, *below* for named scales and reference lines (*below freezing*, *below sea level*, *below average*, *below cost*). Against [over](#over): exact opposites. Note the separate *subject to* sense — *under warranty*, *under oath*, *under contract*, *under anesthesia* — which is not a measure at all.

**1. Less than a stated quantity.**

1. Anything *under* twenty dollars they simply write off.
2. The whole crossing took *under* three hours.

**2. Beneath a budget, a limit, or a standard — the good kind of shortfall.**

3. The renovation finished two months early and *under* budget.
4. We came in *under* the county's noise limit at every measuring point.

**3. Younger than.**

5. Nobody *under* eighteen is admitted without a parent.
6. The program serves children *under* five and their caregivers.

**See also:** [over](#over), [within](#within), [shy of](#shy-of), [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [under](04-movement-direction.md#under)

### upward of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌpwɚd ʌv/ &middot; *UHP-werd uhv*; weak /ˈʌpwɚd əv/, casually /ˈʌpwɚd ə/ — which is why the *-s* of *upwards* is so easy to lose in speech and so audible on the page
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a quantity. Never a gerund, never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *upward of* [quantity] &middot; [noun phrase] *upward of* [quantity]
**Variants:** *upwards of* — the dominant British form and common in American writing too. **American style guides prefer *upward of***, in line with the general American preference for *toward*, *forward*, *backward*, and *afterward* without the *-s*.
**Contrast:** **Two competing readings, and this is the word's real problem.** Historically and in careful usage it means *more than* (*upward of a thousand* = over a thousand); in a great deal of current American writing it means *approaching, as much as* (*upward of a thousand* = nearly a thousand). A reader cannot tell which you intend, so when the difference matters write *more than* or *nearly*. Against [in excess of](#in-excess-of), which is unambiguously a floor, and [north of](#north-of), which is the informal version of the same claim.

Places a quantity at or approaching a stated number. It is the most ambiguous of the measure prepositions, and the only one in this section whose two readings point in opposite directions — which is why it should be replaced whenever the exact reading matters and left alone when it does not.

1. The repair will take *upward of* six hours, so plan on the whole day.
2. *Upward of* twelve thousand people applied for four hundred jobs.
3. The equipment costs *upward of* a quarter of a million dollars.
4. They lost *upward of* half their inventory in the flood.
5. She has *upwards of* thirty years in the same building.
6. The hearing ran *upward of* five hours with no break.

**See also:** [in excess of](#in-excess-of), [north of](#north-of), [over](#over), [in the neighborhood of](#in-the-neighborhood-of), [on the order of](#on-the-order-of)

### within

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɪn/ &middot; *with-IN*; also /wɪˈθɪn/ for some American speakers. **No weak form** — the stress is on the second syllable, and the *with*- inside it does **not** reduce to /wəθ/ the way free-standing [with](02-core-relational.md#with) does
**Type:** compound (Old English *wiðinnan*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. **Never a gerund and never a finite clause** — ✗ *within finishing the job*, ✗ *within we agreed*. It does not strand. It is also an adverb in legal and literary English (*see within*, *the terms set out within*), where it has no object.
**Pattern:** [be / fall] *within* [tolerance or limit] &middot; [verb] *within* [margin] *of* [target] &middot; *within* [budget / reason / reach / one's rights]
**Contrast:** This entry covers the **tolerance, limit, and distance** senses; the deadline sense (*within two weeks*) is at [within](05-time.md#within). Against [to within](#to-within): *within* states a standing range, *to within* states a result measured up to a margin. Against *in*: *in tolerance* is shop floor talk and *within tolerance* is the written form; *inside* is spatial only. **Against [under](#under), a real distinction:** *under budget* means you spent less than the budget, *within budget* means you did not exceed it — and *within* includes spending every last dollar.

**1. Inside a stated tolerance or margin.**

1. Every reading was *within* tolerance except the one at station six.
2. The measurements came back *within* half a percent of the specification.

**2. Not exceeding a budget, a rule, or a right.**

3. The department finished the year *within* budget for the first time since 2016.
4. Asking to see the file is entirely *within* your rights.

**3. Inside a stated distance of something.**

5. There are three hospitals *within* twenty miles of the plant.
6. He got *within* a stroke of the lead on Saturday and then fell apart on Sunday.

**See also:** [to within](#to-within), [under](#under), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [within](05-time.md#within), [inside of](11-complex-multiword.md#inside-of)
