# Place and position

> The prepositions that answer *where?* — the ones that fix a thing in space relative to another thing without moving it.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file locates something. It takes a landmark — a table, a river, a crowd, a city line — and places a second thing with respect to it: higher, lower, beside, inside, behind, or spread across. Three things unite the group and cause almost all the trouble. **First, the static/dynamic split**: nearly every word here has a twin sense of motion that lives in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md), and the same form does both jobs — *the boat is **under** the bridge* is position, *the boat went **under** the bridge* is a path. The two places English marks that split with *different* forms are the two learners most often get wrong: **in / into** and **on / onto**. *She was **in** the car* is position and *she got **into** the car* is motion; *the cat is **on** the roof* is position and *the cat jumped **onto** the roof* is motion. Everywhere else in this file, one form covers both and only the verb tells you which you have. **Second, the pairs that look interchangeable and are not**: *above*/*over*, *below*/*under*, *among*/*between*, *beside*/*besides*, *amid*/*among*, *in*/*within*, and *near*/*nearby* each hinge on one precise distinction, given in the **Contrast** line of every entry.

**Third, and most important grammatically: not one of these prepositions takes a *that*-clause.** You cannot write *behind he was standing*, *within the deadline passed*, or *beyond that he admitted it*. What they take is a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (*beside **me***, *between you and **me***). A smaller set also takes a **gerund** — *not above lying*, *against parking here*, *upon signing*, *alongside working nights*, *an argument over paying*, *questions around hiring*, *beyond saving*, *behind closing the plant*, *past caring*, *outside of being tired*, *on the edge of quitting* — and each of those says so in its own **Complement** line. When one of these words takes a verb, that verb is an *-ing* form and never an infinitive: ✗ *against to park here*, ✗ *upon to sign*, ✗ *beyond to save*. A still smaller set takes a **wh-clause**, which is a finite clause: *beyond **what he told us***, *nowhere near **what we needed***, *the difference between **what he said** and **what he did***, *an argument over **who pays***, *a diner across from **where the depot used to be***, *keep abreast of **what the committee decides***. Those six — [beyond](#beyond), [near](#near), [between](#between), [over](#over), [across from](#across-from), and [abreast of](#abreast-of) — are the only ones in this file with a finite complement, and the rule for every other entry here is the flat one: noun phrase in, clause out. A word that looks like a preposition but is followed by a plain subject and verb has become a conjunction (*before he arrived*) or an adverb (*I've been here before*), and the object test settles which: **the preposition always has an object; the adverb and the particle do not.**

**Owned by other files.** Nine prepositions are cross-referenced here rather than repeated: [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), and [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) live in [the core three](01-core-in-on-at.md), and [of](02-core-relational.md#of), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), and [with](02-core-relational.md#with) live in [core relational prepositions](02-core-relational.md). Their weak forms — *of* /əv/ &rarr; /ə/, *to* /tə/, *for* /fɚ/, *at* /ət/, *from* /frəm/, *with* /wɪθ/ &rarr; /wəθ/ — belong to those entries and are not repeated here. The multi-word place phrases — [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to), [inside of](11-complex-multiword.md#inside-of), [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of), and [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of) — are in [complex and multi-word prepositions](11-complex-multiword.md). A second group of multi-word phrases is developed here instead, because each one names a **part of the landmark** — its top, bottom, foot, head, edge, corner, center, or side — rather than a relation to the landmark as a whole: [at the top of](#at-the-top-of), [at the bottom of](#at-the-bottom-of), [at the foot of](#at-the-foot-of), [at the head of](#at-the-head-of), [at the edge of](#at-the-edge-of), [at the corner of](#at-the-corner-of), [in the center of](#in-the-center-of), [to the left of](#to-the-left-of), and [to the right of](#to-the-right-of), together with the facing phrases [across from](#across-from) and [kitty-corner from](#kitty-corner-from). Where a word here also has a motion sense or a time sense, the entry says so and links to it.

## Vertical position: above, below, and on top

The words that stack one thing over another. The rule that governs all of them is that **height alone is not alignment**: *above* and *below* claim only that one thing is higher or lower, while *over* and *under* line the two up and usually let the upper one cover the lower. Register sorts the rest — *beneath* is literary, *underneath* is emphatic and physical, *atop* and *upon* are formal. Beside the simple words sit the landmark phrases that name an end of the object rather than a relation to it — *at the top of*, *at the bottom of*, *at the foot of*, and *at the head of* — plus the regional *over top of* and the biblical *under the shadow of*.

### above

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbʌv/ &middot; *uh-BUV*; the first syllable is always the reduced /ə/, and there is no further weak form
**Русский:** выше, над — расположен выше без соприкосновения; выше по шкале, рангу или в тексте
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund in the fixed *not above doing X* idiom (*she's not above lying*). **Never a finite clause** — write *above the line that says "total"*, not *above it says "total"*. Also an adverb (*the sky above*), an adjective (*the above figures*), and a noun (*see the above*); the preposition alone takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *above* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *above* [noun phrase] &middot; *not above* [verb-ing] &middot; *above all*
**Contrast:** **The *above*/*over* line is height versus alignment.** *Above* asserts only that one thing is higher; *over* adds vertical alignment and often covering or crossing. A lamp can be *above* or *over* the table, but a blanket can only be *over* it, and a village can be *above* the valley without being over anything. For measurements and scales, *above* is the word — *above sea level*, *above average*, *above freezing* — while [over](#over) takes quantities and counts (*over fifty people*). Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): *on* requires contact, *above* forbids it.

Higher than something, with air in between. That single geometric fact generates every other sense: higher on a map, higher on a measuring scale, higher in a hierarchy, and — by a short moral leap — too high in character to stoop to something. The one thing *above* never means is touching.

**1. Higher than, without contact.** A framed map hung *above* the couch.
**2. Higher on a scale of measurement.** The town sits 6,000 feet *above* sea level.
**3. Greater than a stated number or level.** Anything *above* ninety is an A.
**4. Higher in rank, authority, or importance.** There is no rank *above* general.
**5. Too honorable or proud to do something.** He is not *above* asking for help.
**6. Earlier on the page, in written reference.** The rates listed *above* have changed.

1. A hawk circled *above* the parking lot. *(1)*
2. She keeps the good glasses *above* the microwave. *(1)*
3. Denver is a mile *above* sea level. *(2)*
4. The reservoir is twelve feet *above* normal. *(2)*
5. Temperatures stayed *above* forty all week. *(3)*
6. Anything *above* two hundred dollars needs a second signature. *(3)*
7. A lieutenant is one grade *above* a sergeant. *(4)*
8. She put the team *above* her own record. *(4)*
9. He is not *above* taking credit for someone else's work. *(5)*
10. No one in that office is *above* suspicion. *(5)*
11. The exceptions noted *above* apply only to renewals. *(6)*
12. Everything *above* this line is a draft. *(6)*

**See also:** [over](#over), [atop](#atop), [below](#below), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of)

### at the bottom of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈbɑtəm əv/ &middot; *at-thuh-BAH-tuhm-uv* &middot; **weak form** — only *bottom* keeps a beat: *at* reduces to /ət/, *the* to /ðə/, and *of* to /ə/ before a consonant, so *at the bottom of the stairs* comes out /ət ðə ˈbɑɾəm ə ðə ˈstɛrz/ &middot; *uht-thuh-BAH-duhm-uh-thuh-STAIRZ*, with the medial /t/ of *bottom* flapped to a /d/-like tap
**Русский:** внизу, на дне, у подножия — в самой нижней точке; в конце списка; в основе (причина)
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — *at the bottom of the page*, *at the bottom of it*. **No gerund and no *that*-clause**: ✗ *at the bottom of falling behind*, ✗ *at the bottom of that he lied*. To hang a finite clause on it you need a noun to carry the clause: *at the bottom of the claim that he lied*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *at the bottom of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at the bottom of* [noun phrase] &middot; [sign / add / start] *at the bottom of* [page or column] &middot; *be at the bottom of* [trouble] (cause)
**Variants:** *in the bottom of* — a different preposition, and the distinction is worth keeping: *at the bottom of the drawer* is the low end of it, *in the bottom of the drawer* is inside it, down at the bottom. The motion form is *to the bottom of* (*sank to the bottom of the lake*, *get to the bottom of this*).
**Contrast:** Against [below](#below) and [under](#under), which are simple prepositions naming a relation between two things; *at the bottom of* names **a part of one thing** and puts you at it — *below the stairs* is underneath them, *at the bottom of the stairs* is standing on the floor they end at. Against [at the foot of](#at-the-foot-of), the anatomical twin, which is fixed to a short list of landmarks: *at the bottom of the list* is right and ✗ *at the foot of the list* is odd, while both work for stairs and hills. Against [beneath](#beneath), which is literary and cannot name a part at all.

The low end of something, treated as a place you can be at. The phrase does three jobs that the simple prepositions cannot: it picks out a specific part of an object rather than a relation to the whole of it, it works on flat things laid out in reading order (pages, columns, lists, charts) as readily as on vertical ones, and by an old metaphor it names the thing lying underneath a situation as its cause.

**1. At the lowest point of a vertical or sloping thing.** The mail piles up *at the bottom of* the stairs.
**2. At the lowest point on a page, a column, or a list.** Sign *at the bottom of* the second page.
**3. Underneath a pile, a container, or a body of water.** Her keys were *at the bottom of* the bag.
**4. In the last position in a ranking or a standing.** They finished *at the bottom of* the division.
**5. Being the underlying cause of a situation.** Money is usually *at the bottom of* these arguments.

1. He waited *at the bottom of* the hill with the sled. *(1)*
2. There is a drain *at the bottom of* the window well. *(1)*
3. Add the subtotal *at the bottom of* the third column. *(2)*
4. The disclaimer runs *at the bottom of* every page. *(2)*
5. Divers found the truck *at the bottom of* the quarry. *(3)*
6. The good scissors are *at the bottom of* that drawer somewhere. *(3)*
7. Our team spent the whole season *at the bottom of* the standings. *(4)*
8. A billing error was *at the bottom of* the entire dispute. *(5)*
9. I suspect plain boredom is *at the bottom of* it. *(5)*

**See also:** [at the top of](#at-the-top-of), [at the foot of](#at-the-foot-of), [below](#below), [under](#under), [beneath](#beneath), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at)

### at the foot of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈfʊt əv/ &middot; *at-thuh-FUUT-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *at* /ət/, *the* /ðə/, *of* /ə/ before a consonant, with only *foot* stressed: *at the foot of the bed* is /ət ðə ˈfʊt ə ðə ˈbɛd/ &middot; *uht-thuh-FUUT-uh-thuh-BED*. The vowel of *foot* is the lax /ʊ/ of *book*, never the /u/ of *boot*
**Русский:** у подножия, в ногах — у основания горы, лестницы, памятника; в ногах кровати
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + body-part noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a faint literary tilt in the landscape senses
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund and no *that*-clause**: ✗ *at the foot of climbing the ridge*. And the object is restricted — it has to be a thing the body metaphor already covers (a bed, a hill, a cliff, a staircase, a page, a column, a cross), which is why ✗ *at the foot of the argument* fails where *at the bottom of the argument* succeeds.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *at the foot of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at the foot of* [noun phrase] &middot; *at the foot of the bed / the stairs / the hill / the page*
**Variants:** the motion form is *to the foot of* (*walked to the foot of the falls*); *at the base of* is the neutral technical paraphrase for cliffs, towers, and trees
**Contrast:** Against [at the bottom of](#at-the-bottom-of), which is the general-purpose phrase and fits anything with a low end. *Foot* is the **anatomical** member of the pair and is frozen to a short list of landmarks — beds, stairs, hills, mountains, cliffs, pages, columns, crosses. Both are right for stairs; only *bottom* is right for a list or a drawer. Against [at the head of](#at-the-head-of), its opposite end. Against [below](#below), which relates two things instead of naming a part of one.

The lower end of something that the language treats as having a body. The metaphor is old and it is closed: English decided long ago which things have feet, and a learner who extends the phrase to a new noun will usually be wrong. Within its list, though, it is the ordinary word — no American calls the place where a hillside meets flat ground anything but the foot of the hill.

**1. At the base of a hill, mountain, cliff, or tower.** They camped *at the foot of* the ridge.
**2. At the bottom of a flight of stairs.** A radiator sits *at the foot of* the staircase.
**3. At the lower end of a bed.** The cat sleeps *at the foot of* the bed.
**4. At the lower end of a page or a column of writing.** Her signature was *at the foot of* the last page.
**5. At the base of a cross, a monument, or a statue.** They left flowers *at the foot of* the memorial.

1. We left the car *at the foot of* the trail and hiked in. *(1)*
2. A stone marker stands *at the foot of* the bluff. *(1)*
3. She was waiting *at the foot of* the stairs with her coat on. *(2)*
4. The dog barked *at the foot of* the attic ladder for an hour. *(2)*
5. He folded the quilt *at the foot of* the mattress. *(3)*
6. There is a cedar chest *at the foot of* the bed. *(3)*
7. The footnotes run *at the foot of* each page rather than at the end. *(4)*
8. Add the totals *at the foot of* the column. *(4)*
9. Someone had propped a wreath *at the foot of* the statue. *(5)*

**See also:** [at the head of](#at-the-head-of), [at the bottom of](#at-the-bottom-of), [below](#below), [beneath](#beneath), [under](#under), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at)

### at the head of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈhɛd əv/ &middot; *at-thuh-HED-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *at* /ət/, *the* /ðə/, *of* /ə/, with only *head* stressed: *at the head of the table* is /ət ðə ˈhɛd ə ðə ˈteɪbəl/ &middot; *uht-thuh-HED-uh-thuh-TAY-bul*
**Русский:** во главе, в изголовье — у верхнего конца стола или кровати; первым в списке, в колонне
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + body-part noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund and no *that*-clause**: ✗ *at the head of running the office* — write *at the head of the office*. As with its opposite, the object is restricted to nouns the body metaphor covers: tables, beds, stairs, lines, columns, valleys, bays, rivers, processions, and organizations.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand / march] *at the head of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at the head of* [noun phrase] &middot; *at the head of the table / the line / the stairs / the department*
**Variants:** the motion form is *to the head of* (*moved to the head of the line*); *at the top of* is the neutral paraphrase for stairs and lists, and *in charge of* for the authority sense
**Contrast:** Against [at the top of](#at-the-top-of), which *head* can usually replace and which cannot always replace *head*. Two things belong to *head* alone. The first is **the source of running water**: the head of a river, a valley, a bay, or a hollow is where it begins, and *the top of the river* means nothing. The second is **authority**: *at the head of the department* means running it, while *at the top of the department* means only occupying its highest rung. Against [before](#before) and [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of): *at the head of the line* is a position **in** the line, *in front of the line* is outside it.

The upper, leading, or source end of something the language gives a body to. From the physical end it reaches two places the plain vertical words cannot follow it to — the place a river or a valley starts, and the position of the person in charge. What holds the senses together is precedence: whatever is at the head comes first, whether in space, in order, or in rank.

**1. At the upper end of a table, a bed, or a flight of stairs.** He always sat *at the head of* the table.
**2. At the source or upper end of a valley, bay, or river.** The town grew up *at the head of* the bay.
**3. In the first position of a line, a list, or a column of text.** Her name was *at the head of* the waiting list.
**4. Leading a procession, a column, or a march.** The pipers marched *at the head of* the parade.
**5. In charge of an organization, a family, or a delegation.** She has been *at the head of* the agency since March.

1. A guide was waiting *at the head of* the stairs. *(1)*
2. Put the extra pillows *at the head of* the bed. *(1)*
3. There is a trailhead *at the head of* the canyon. *(2)*
4. The old mill stood *at the head of* the creek. *(2)*
5. Put the date *at the head of* the memo, not the foot. *(3)*
6. He finished the morning *at the head of* the leaderboard. *(3)*
7. Two motorcycles rode *at the head of* the funeral procession. *(4)*
8. She spent nine years *at the head of* the school board. *(5)*
9. The senator *at the head of* the delegation refused to comment. *(5)*

**See also:** [at the foot of](#at-the-foot-of), [at the top of](#at-the-top-of), [above](#above), [before](#before), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [ahead of](11-complex-multiword.md#ahead-of)

### at the top of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈtɑp əv/ &middot; *at-thuh-TAHP-uv* &middot; also /ət ðə ˈtɔp əv/ in dialects that keep the cot/caught distinction; **weak form** — *at* /ət/, *the* /ðə/, *of* /ə/ before a consonant, with only *top* stressed: *at the top of the page* is /ət ðə ˈtɑp ə ðə ˈpeɪdʒ/ &middot; *uht-thuh-TAHP-uh-thuh-PAYJ*
**Русский:** наверху, на вершине — в высшей точке; в начале списка; на первом месте в рейтинге
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — *at the top of the hill*, *at the top of it*. **No gerund and no *that*-clause**: ✗ *at the top of finishing the roof*, ✗ *at the top of that it was late*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *at the top of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at the top of* [noun phrase] &middot; *at the top of* [page / list / hour / stairs] &middot; *at the top of one's lungs / voice*
**Variants:** *on top of* (see [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of)) is a different preposition, not a variant — see the contrast below. The motion form is *to the top of*; *in the top of* puts the thing inside (*in the top of the closet*).
**Contrast:** **The pair to keep apart is *at the top of* and [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of).** *At the top of the hill* is a location — you stand at its high point. *On top of the hill* is contact with the surface, and it is what a house or a water tower does. You climb *to the top of* a ladder but you sit *on top of* a wall. Against [above](#above), which is height without any part named, and [atop](#atop), which is contact on a high surface in a formal register. Against [at the head of](#at-the-head-of), which adds the ideas of source and authority that *top* does not carry. And note that the clock sense — *at the top of the hour* — is time, not place, and belongs to [time](05-time.md).

The high end of something, treated as a place. Like its opposite, the phrase names a part of an object rather than a relation between two objects, and it runs freely over things that are not vertical at all: a page has a top, so does a list, a chart, a batting order, and an hour. What it never does is claim contact — that job belongs to *on top of* and *atop*.

**1. At the highest point of a vertical or sloping thing.** There is a bench *at the top of* the hill.
**2. At the beginning of a page, a list, or a chart.** Put your name *at the top of* the form.
**3. In the leading position of a ranking or an organization.** The album sat *at the top of* the chart for a month.
**4. In the two volume idioms, at the top of one's lungs and at the top of one's voice.** She yelled *at the top of* her lungs.

1. She was standing *at the top of* the stairs with the phone in her hand. *(1)*
2. A ranger station sits *at the top of* the pass. *(1)*
3. The date goes *at the top of* the second page. *(2)*
4. Milk was *at the top of* the shopping list. *(2)*
5. Two women *at the top of* the firm signed the letter. *(3)*
6. She finished the year *at the top of* her class. *(3)*
7. The kids were singing *at the top of* their lungs the whole ride. *(4)*
8. He called her name *at the top of* his voice and got no answer. *(4)*

**See also:** [at the bottom of](#at-the-bottom-of), [at the head of](#at-the-head-of), [atop](#atop), [above](#above), [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), [over](#over)

### atop

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtɑp/ &middot; *uh-TAHP*; also /əˈtɔp/ &middot; *uh-TAWP* in dialects that keep the cot/caught distinction; the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form
**Русский:** на вершине, поверх — книжн., газетное; в разговорной речи почти не встречается
**Type:** compound (historically *a-* + *top*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; common in journalism, rare in speech
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. No gerund, no clause. ***Atop of* is nonstandard** — the *of* belongs to *on top of*, not to *atop*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *atop* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Atop* is [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of) compressed into one word, and it is the more formal of the two — no American says *atop* out loud at the kitchen table. Unlike [above](#above), it requires contact; unlike plain [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), it insists the surface is a high or prominent one. *On the table* is neutral; *atop the table* sounds like a museum label.

Resting on the highest surface of something, in contact with it. It is a written word — headlines, captions, nature writing, and real-estate copy love it — and it survives mainly because it is shorter than *on top of* and grander than *on*. It also has a small figurative life in the language of rankings and standings.

**1. On the highest surface of a physical thing.** A weathervane *atop* the barn.
**2. At the top of a ranking or standing (journalistic).** The Dodgers finished *atop* the division.

1. A single crow sat *atop* the utility pole. *(1)*
2. The chapel *atop* the hill is visible from the highway. *(1)*
3. Snow lay six inches deep *atop* the woodpile. *(1)*
4. She placed the last shingle *atop* the stack. *(1)*
5. The company ended the quarter *atop* the industry. *(2)*
6. He has stayed *atop* the rankings for three seasons. *(2)*

**See also:** [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), [above](#above), [over](#over), [upon](#upon), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on)

### below

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈloʊ/ &middot; *bih-LOH*; weak /bəˈloʊ/ &middot; *buh-LOH*
**Русский:** ниже, под — ниже без соприкосновения; меньше указанного уровня; ниже по тексту
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *low*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No gerund, no clause. Also an adverb (*the deck below*, *see below*) and, in nautical use, an adverb meaning belowdecks; the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *below* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *below* [noun phrase] &middot; *below* [number or level]
**Contrast:** **The *below*/*under* line mirrors *above*/*over*.** *Below* claims only lower position; [under](#under) adds vertical alignment and often covering or containment. You sleep *under* a blanket, never *below* one, and you camp *below* the tree line, not under it. Measurements and scales take *below* — *below freezing*, *below zero*, *below average* — while *under* takes quantities and ages (*under thirty*, *under twenty dollars*). Against [beneath](#beneath), which is the same idea in a literary register.

Lower than something, with no claim about contact or alignment. Like its opposite *above*, it works on maps and on scales as readily as in the air: downstream is *below*, downhill is *below*, and any number smaller than the reference point is *below* it. It is the everyday word for lower position when nothing is doing any covering.

**1. Lower than, without contact.** The apartment *below* ours flooded.
**2. Farther down a slope, a river, or a coast.** The dam is two miles *below* the falls.
**3. Less than a stated number, level, or standard.** Sales came in *below* projections.
**4. Lower in rank or grade.** Everyone *below* the rank of captain reports here.
**5. Later on the page, in written reference.** The footnotes *below* explain the method.

1. There is a crawlspace *below* the kitchen floor. *(1)*
2. The clouds sat *below* the summit all morning. *(1)*
3. The town lies just *below* the reservoir. *(2)*
4. Fishing is better *below* the bridge than above it. *(2)*
5. It stayed *below* freezing for nine days straight. *(3)*
6. Her cholesterol is finally *below* two hundred. *(3)*
7. No one *below* director level saw the memo. *(4)*
8. He was two years *below* me in school. *(4)*
9. See the table *below* for the current rates. *(5)*
10. Everything *below* the dotted line is optional. *(5)*

### beneath

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈniθ/ &middot; *bih-NEETH*; weak /bəˈniθ/ &middot; *buh-NEETH*; the *th* is voiceless /θ/, not the /ð/ of *breathe*
**Русский:** под, из-под — книжн. синоним under; также «ниже достоинства» (beneath her)
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *neath*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No finite clause.** A gerund appears only in the frozen disdain idiom (*beneath answering*, *beneath discussing*) and is rare. Also an adverb (*the valley beneath*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *beneath* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *beneath* [noun phrase] &middot; *beneath* [abstract noun] (*beneath contempt*)
**Variants:** 'neath /niθ/ — a poetic clipping that belongs to verse, hymn, and song lyric (*'neath the willow*); never write it in prose
**Contrast:** *Beneath* is the literary member of the *under / underneath / below* family and is almost never the natural spoken choice: Americans say *the dog is **under** the table*, and *beneath the table* sounds like a novel. What *beneath* owns outright is the moral sense — *beneath contempt*, *beneath him*, *beneath her dignity* — where [under](#under) and [below](#below) cannot go. It also handles the metaphor of a hidden interior better than either (*beneath the surface*).

Directly under something, usually with the weight or nearness of the upper thing felt. It is *under* in a raised register, plus two senses of its own: the surface that conceals something, and the standard that something falls short of. Reach for it when you want the sentence to sound written rather than spoken.

**1. Directly under, often with weight or pressure.** The porch steps creaked *beneath* him.
**2. Under the surface of, hidden by an appearance.** *Beneath* the cheerful tone was real panic.
**3. Unworthy of, too low for someone's dignity.** The remark was *beneath* her.

1. The ice groaned *beneath* their boots. *(1)*
2. Roots had buckled the sidewalk *beneath* the maple. *(1)*
3. *Beneath* the fresh paint, the wood was rotten. *(2)*
4. *Beneath* all the legal language, the offer is simple. *(2)*
5. That kind of gossip is *beneath* contempt. *(3)*
6. He seemed to think manual labor was *beneath* him. *(3)*

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *OH-ver*; the second syllable is the r-colored /ɚ/ in all American speech and keeps it even unstressed, so *over* has **no weak form** of the kind *of* and *to* have — what happens in fast speech is that the whole word loses its beat, not that its vowels change. **The r-less /ˈoʊvə/ is British, not an American reduction.**
**Русский:** над, поверх, через — прямо над, накрывая или пересекая; больше чем; по (телефону)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a **gerund** (*an argument over paying the bill*, *no control over spending*) and a **wh-clause** (*an argument over who pays*, *a fight over what to charge*) in the dispute and authority senses. **Never a *that*-clause** — ✗ *an argument over that he should pay*; to hang a finite clause on it you need a noun to carry it (*an argument over the claim that he should pay*). Also an adverb (*come over*), an adjective (*it's over*), and a verb particle (*think it over*, *hand it over*), none of which takes an object; the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; *over* [number] &middot; [choose/prefer] [X] *over* [Y] &middot; stranded: *the beam it hangs* **over**
**Contrast:** Against [above](#above): *over* implies vertical alignment and permits covering, *above* asserts only greater height. A tarp goes *over* the woodpile, never *above* it. Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): *on* is contact and support, *over* is coverage from the top — *the cloth **on** the table* lies flat, *the cloth **over** the table* drapes down the sides. Against [under](#under), its exact opposite in every sense here. On sense 9, against [beyond](#beyond): *over the ridge* names a barrier you would cross to get there, *beyond the ridge* names only what lies farther out and crosses nothing. Note also that ***over* is the most widely distributed word in this catalog**: the path sense (*jumped over the fence*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#over), the span-of-time sense (*over the summer*) is in [time](05-time.md#over), the *about* sense (*a fight over money*) is in [topic and reference](10-topic-reference-role.md#over), the bare quantity sense (*over fifty*) reappears in [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#over), and the preference sense in [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#over). Senses 4 and 6 below are the positional face of those last two and are kept here because they fall straight out of the vertical geometry.

Positioned higher than something and, characteristically, lined up with it — which is why *over* slides so easily into the idea of covering. From physical coverage it reaches authority (one person positioned over another), quantity (a number higher than another), preference (one option placed above another), the channel a message travels along, and the far side of any barrier you would have to go over to reach. The positional senses are collected here; the moving, temporal, and topical senses live elsewhere in this catalog.

**1. Higher than and vertically aligned with; spanning.** A footbridge *over* the creek.
**2. Covering, resting on top of and concealing.** A sheet *over* the furniture.
**3. Across the whole surface or extent of.** She spilled coffee *over* the keyboard.
**4. More than, with a number or amount (see also [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#over)).** *Over* two hundred people showed up.
**5. In a position of authority above.** She has three shift leads *over* her.
**6. Chosen or ranked ahead of an alternative (see also [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#over)).** They took the older candidate *over* the younger one.
**7. By way of a channel or a medium.** We settled the whole thing *over* the phone.
**8. Past and no longer troubled by.** She is finally *over* the flu.
**9. On the far side of a barrier you would have to cross.** Their place is *over* the ridge.

1. The old sign still hangs *over* the entrance. *(1)*
2. Power lines run *over* the whole subdivision. *(1)*
3. He pulled the blanket *over* his head. *(2)*
4. There was a tarp *over* the boat all winter. *(2)*
5. A rash had spread *over* both arms by Tuesday. *(3)*
6. Papers were scattered *over* every surface in the room. *(3)*
7. The repair came to a little *over* nine hundred dollars. *(4)*
8. *Over* half the class failed the first quiz. *(4)*
9. No one *over* her signed off on the change. *(5)*
10. He was given authority *over* both warehouses. *(5)*
11. I'd take a long drive *over* a short flight any day. *(6)*
12. The committee chose the local firm *over* two national ones. *(6)*
13. They worked out the terms *over* the phone in ten minutes. *(7)*
14. The alert went out *over* the emergency channel at four. *(7)*
15. She is finally *over* the worst of the flu. *(8)*
16. He is not *over* the layoff and may not be for a while. *(8)*
17. Her cousins live *over* the border in Nogales. *(9)*
18. The next town *over* the ridge has one gas station. *(9)*

### over top of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvɚ ˈtɑp əv/ &middot; *OH-ver-TAHP-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *of* reduces to /ə/ before a consonant, giving *OH-ver-TAHP-uh*, and in fast speech the whole phrase carries one main beat, on *top*. Written and said as one word it is *overtop of* /ˌoʊvɚˈtɑp əv/ &middot; *oh-ver-TAHP-uv*
**Русский:** поверх, сверху на — разг., региональное (Зап. Пенсильвания, Огайо, Средний Запад)
**Type:** complex (*over* + *top* + *of*) &middot; **Register:** **regional and informal American** — strongest in Western Pennsylvania, the Ohio Valley, the inland Midland, the Upper Midwest, and Canada. It is ordinary in speech across that belt and it is not an error there, but **edited American prose does not use it**: copy editors change it to *over* or *on top of*.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *over top of painting the wall*.
**Pattern:** [put / wear / lay / pour / build] [object] *over top of* [noun phrase] &middot; [be-verb] *over top of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *overtop of* (one word plus *of*), *overtop* (bare, no *of* — chiefly Canadian: *she wore a vest overtop her sweater*), and *over the top of*, which is the fully standard expansion and the safe choice in writing
**Contrast:** It is [over](#over) with the covering made emphatic, and it adds no meaning the shorter word lacks: *a tarp over the woodpile* and *a tarp over top of the woodpile* describe the same tarp, and the second one tells a linguist where the speaker grew up. Against [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), which requires contact and support from below; *over top of* covers, with or without touching — you can pour gravy *over top of* mashed potatoes and hang a sheet *over top of* a doorway. Against [atop](#atop), its opposite in register: *atop* is formal and written, *over top of* is spoken and regional.

Covering something from above, with the covering insisted on. The phrase is a regional doubling — *over* already means this, and *top* is added for emphasis the way *off of* adds *of* to *off*. Learners should recognize it, because they will hear it constantly in a wide band of the country, and should not write it, because outside that band it reads as an error rather than as a dialect.

**1. Covering, spread across the top of.** She pulled a blanket *over top of* the couch.
**2. Resting on the upper surface of.** The extra chairs are stacked *over top of* the freezer.
**3. Worn as an outer layer.** He wears a windbreaker *over top of* his uniform.

1. Put a lid *over top of* the pot or it will spatter. *(1)*
2. They poured a new slab *over top of* the old one. *(1)*
3. Somebody laid plywood *over top of* the opening. *(1)*
4. The paint cans are *over top of* the paint tray. *(2)*
5. She wore a wool vest *over top of* her flannel shirt. *(3)*
6. In edited prose: he wore a jacket *over top of* his sweater &rarr; write *over* his sweater, or *on top of* his sweater. *(3)*

### under

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndər/ &middot; *UN-der*; the first vowel never reduces, so there is **no weak form**. What casual American speech does instead is drop the /d/ after the nasal, giving /ˈʌnɚ/ &middot; *UN-ner* (*under the bed* &rarr; *unner the bed*). **The r-less /ˈʌndə/ is British, not an American reduction.**
**Русский:** под — прямо под и обычно накрытый; меньше (числа, возраста); под властью, в процессе
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No finite clause, no gerund** — write *under the impression that he had paid* (the *that*-clause belongs to *impression*, not to *under*), never *under he had paid*. Also an adverb (*the boat went under*) and a prefix (*underpaid*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; *under* [number or age] &middot; *under* [authority] &middot; *under* [process noun] (*under review*)
**Contrast:** Against [below](#below): *under* aligns and often covers, *below* only ranks lower. You are *under* a bridge when you pass through its shadow and *below* it when you are downstream. Against [underneath](#underneath): *underneath* is the emphatic, physical form and has none of *under*'s abstract senses — you cannot say *underneath new management* or *underneath thirty*. Against [beneath](#beneath): the same relation in a literary register. And note that scales take [below](#below) (*below freezing*) while counts and ages take *under* (*under thirty*). *Under* has two other homes: the path sense (*rolled under the fence*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#under), and the bare quantity sense (*under fifty*) reappears in [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#under).

Directly lower than something and usually covered by it. That covering is what lets *under* become the workhorse abstraction of the group: things covered by an authority, by a rule, by a process, or by a heading are all *under* it. Of the four low-position words, this is by a wide margin the most useful and the most polysemous.

**1. Directly below and typically covered by.** The cat is *under* the bed.
**2. Worn or placed beneath another layer.** He had a T-shirt *under* his sweater.
**3. Less than a number, an age, or an amount.** Everything on this rack is *under* twenty dollars.
**4. Subject to the authority, rule, or command of.** The park is *under* federal jurisdiction.
**5. Undergoing a stated process or condition.** The bridge has been *under* construction for two years.
**6. Classified within a heading or a name.** The reservation is *under* Alvarez.

1. Your keys are *under* the newspaper. *(1)*
2. We waited out the storm *under* an overpass. *(1)*
3. She wore thermals *under* her jeans. *(2)*
4. Put a towel *under* the pot before you set it down. *(2)*
5. Anyone *under* eighteen needs a signed waiver. *(3)*
6. We finished the job *under* budget for once. *(3)*
7. The clinic operates *under* state supervision. *(4)*
8. He served *under* three different chiefs. *(4)*
9. The proposal is still *under* review. *(5)*
10. Two of the three claims are *under* investigation. *(5)*
11. You'll find it filed *under* "permits." *(6)*
12. She published *under* a pen name for a decade. *(6)*

### under the shadow of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndɚ ðə ˈʃædoʊ əv/ &middot; *UN-der-thuh-SHAD-oh-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *the* is /ðə/ and *of* reduces to /ə/ before a consonant, so *under the shadow of the wall* is /ˈʌndɚ ðə ˈʃædoʊ ə ðə ˈwɔl/ &middot; *UN-der-thuh-SHAD-oh-uh-thuh-WAWL*. Two beats only, on *un-* and *shad-*
**Русский:** в тени, под сенью — книжн., библ.: рядом с подавляющим; под угрозой; под защитой
**Type:** complex (*under* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** **literary, elevated, and biblical.** The 1611 King James Version fixed it in the American ear — *under the shadow of the Almighty*, *under the shadow of thy wings* — and it has stayed in that register ever since. **In ordinary modern American English the live form is *in the shadow of*.**
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund and no clause**: ✗ *under the shadow of losing the farm* — write *under the shadow of a foreclosure*, or use *in danger of losing the farm*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / live / grow up / work] *under the shadow of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *under the shadow of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** **in the shadow of** — the ordinary modern American form, and the one to write; *beneath the shadow of* and the metrical clipping *'neath the shadow of* (see ['neath](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#neath)) belong to hymns and to nineteenth-century verse
**Contrast:** Against plain [under](#under), which is bare geometry; the shadow adds a caster — something large enough to throw one — and with it the whole figurative freight of the phrase. Against [beneath](#beneath), which is literary but has no metaphor of a looming presence built in. Against [overlooking](#overlooking), which puts the big thing above and gives it a view; *under the shadow of* stands beneath it and gives it a threat. And note the register split with *in the shadow of*: the two are interchangeable in meaning, but an American who writes *under* in a news story or a memo will sound as if he is quoting scripture.

Beneath the shade that something large casts — and, far more often, beneath the presence, the reputation, or the menace of it. The literal sense is now the rarer one. What keeps the phrase alive is the metaphor: to live *under the shadow of* a mountain, a mill, a famous parent, or a diagnosis is to have that thing standing over everything you do.

**1. In the shade cast by something.** They ate lunch *under the shadow of* the water tower.
**2. Immediately beside something large and dominating.** The village sits *under the shadow of* the volcano.
**3. Overshadowed by a greater reputation, event, or memory.** He wrote for thirty years *under the shadow of* his father.
**4. Under the continuing threat of something.** The town lived *under the shadow of* the mill closing.
**5. Under the protection of something powerful (the biblical sense).** *Under the shadow of* thy wings will I make my refuge.

1. The flock grazed *under the shadow of* the ridge until noon. *(1)*
2. Three benches sit *under the shadow of* the old oak. *(1)*
3. Whole neighborhoods grew up *under the shadow of* the refinery. *(2)*
4. The chapel stands *under the shadow of* the cathedral wall. *(2)*
5. Every quarterback the school produced played *under the shadow of* that one season. *(3)*
6. The negotiations went on *under the shadow of* a possible strike. *(4)*
7. She finished the treatment but still lives *under the shadow of* the diagnosis. *(4)*
8. In modern prose: rewrite *under the shadow of the bridge* as *in the shadow of the bridge* unless the elevated tone is wanted. *(2)*

**See also:** [under](#under), [beneath](#beneath), [below](#below), [overlooking](#overlooking), [underneath](#underneath), ['neath](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#neath)

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndərˈniθ/ &middot; *un-der-NEETH*; also stressed on the first syllable, /ˈʌndərˌniθ/ *UN-der-neeth*, when it precedes a noun; the *th* is voiceless /θ/. **No vowel-reducing weak form** — the stressed /i/ has nothing to reduce to — but casual speech drops the /d/ after the nasal exactly as it does in [under](#under), giving /ˌʌnɚˈniθ/ &middot; *un-ner-NEETH*
**Русский:** под, снизу под — прямо под и скрытый чем-то; за внешней видимостью
**Type:** compound (*under* + *neath*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly emphatic
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*look underneath*) and a noun (*the underneath of the car*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *underneath* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *underneath* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the mat it was* **underneath**
**Contrast:** *Underneath* is the emphatic, strictly physical form of [under](#under): it insists that the upper thing covers and usually hides the lower one. Directly under and covered by, with the covering emphasized. When someone says *look underneath* rather than *look under*, they mean the thing is hidden. The only figurative extension it allows is the one that keeps the covering metaphor intact: an outward appearance with something concealed behind it.

**1. Directly under and concealed or covered by.** The spare key is *underneath* the mat.
**2. Behind an outward appearance.** *Underneath* all that swagger he is anxious.

### upon

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑn/ &middot; *uh-PAHN*; also /əˈpɔn/ &middot; *uh-PAWN*; weak /əˈpən/ &middot; *uh-puhn*, and in fast speech the whole word flattens toward /əˈpɑn/ with no stress of its own
**Русский:** на — книжн. и юр. форма on; также «сразу по» (upon arrival) и усиление mile upon mile
**Type:** compound (*up* + *on*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or legal; also fixed idioms at every register
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the "immediately after" sense** (*upon arriving*, *upon signing the lease*). Never a finite clause — write *upon learning that he had left*, never *upon he had left*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; *upon* [verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *upon* [same noun] (*row upon row*)
**Contrast:** **Anywhere *upon* appears, [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is also grammatical.** *Upon* adds no meaning at all — only formality — so choose it for register, for rhythm, or because a fixed phrase demands it (*once upon a time*, *row upon row*, *upon further review*). The motion sense (*came upon a clearing*) is handled in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#upon).

A formal equivalent of *on*, identical in meaning and higher in register. It earns its keep in three places: legal and ceremonial prose, a set of fixed phrases that will not take *on*, and the "immediately after" construction with a gerund, where it is genuinely more idiomatic than *on*.

**1. On a surface, in formal or literary style.** A wreath *upon* the door.
**2. Immediately after (with a noun or gerund).** *Upon* arrival, please check in at the desk.
**3. In fixed phrases and repeated-noun intensifiers.** Mile *upon* mile of empty road.

3. *Upon* signing, you receive the keys. *(2)*

5. Wave *upon* wave of new applications came in. *(3)*

## Beside, near, and facing

Adjacency, at four degrees of precision: contact under pressure (*against*), parallel alignment (*alongside*), plain side-by-side position (*beside*), and unspecified proximity (*near*) — plus the three words for standing across a gap and looking at what is on the other side, *facing*, *overlooking*, and *opposite*. Two entries here are warnings rather than recommendations — *nearby* is not really a preposition in American English, and *opposite* has mostly lost the spatial sense to *across from*, which is the gap *facing* and *overlooking* have moved into. That American replacement has its own entry here now, along with the diagonal *kitty-corner from*, the technical *adjacent to*, the official *in the vicinity of*, and the level-with *abreast of*. Four more belong to old or regional text and to nothing else — *anear*, *anigh*, *forenenst*, and *hard by* — and two are warnings like *nearby*: bare *next* is archaic in American English, and *upside* survives in one informal frame.

### abreast of

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbrɛst əv/ &middot; *uh-BREST-uv* &middot; **weak form** — the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, and *of* drops to /ə/ before a consonant, so *abreast of the news* is /əˈbrɛst ə ðə ˈnuz/ &middot; *uh-BREST-uh-thuh-NOOZ*
**Русский:** вровень с, на уровне — бок о бок и наравне; keep abreast of — быть в курсе
**Type:** complex (*a-* + *breast* + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral. The spatial sense is nautical, military, and traffic-technical; the figurative "up to date" sense is neutral to formal and is by far the commoner of the two in American English.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **also a fused relative — a wh-clause standing in for a noun phrase — in the figurative sense**: *stay abreast of **what the committee decides***, *keep abreast of **what is filed***. **No *that*-clause** (✗ *abreast of that the rules changed*), and **the gerund is marginal**: write *keep abreast of changes in the rules*, not ✗ *keep abreast of changing the rules*, which says something else entirely.
**Pattern:** [draw / come / pull / run] *abreast of* [vessel or vehicle] &middot; [be-verb] *abreast of* [noun phrase] &middot; [keep / stay / remain] *abreast of* [developments]
**Variants:** *abreast with* is dated and now rare; bare *abreast* is an **adverb** (*three abreast*, *riding abreast*) and takes no object at all — that is the form you see on a bike-lane sign
**Contrast:** Against [alongside](#alongside), which asks for parallel orientation but not for the two things to be level; *abreast of* insists they are **side by side and even**, front edges lined up, so a car that has drawn *abreast of* another is exactly beside it and not a length back. Against [beside](#beside) and [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), plain adjacency with no claim about alignment. The figurative sense belongs with the words of information and is developed in [topic and reference](10-topic-reference-role.md); it is kept here because it is the same picture — you and the moving thing are level.

Level with something and beside it. The word is built on *breast* in its old sense of chest or front, so the picture is two fronts in line: two ships, two horses, two cars, two runners. From there English took the obvious step to information — to be *abreast of* the news is to be keeping pace with it rather than trailing behind — and that figurative use has almost swallowed the literal one in ordinary American writing.

**1. Side by side with and level with.** The patrol boat came *abreast of* the barge.
**2. Having drawn level with a point along a route.** We were *abreast of* the lighthouse by six.
**3. Keeping pace with information; up to date with.** She keeps *abreast of* every filing in the case.

1. A second squad car pulled *abreast of* the truck. *(1)*
2. Two riders came *abreast of* the leader on the last turn. *(1)*
3. The ferry passed *abreast of* the breakwater and turned north. *(2)*
4. Once you are *abreast of* the second buoy, cut the engine. *(2)*
5. Doctors are expected to stay *abreast of* the research. *(3)*
6. He reads two trade papers to keep *abreast of* what his competitors are doing. *(3)*
7. Adverb, no object, and therefore not this preposition: the horses came down the stretch three *abreast*. *(1)*

**See also:** [alongside](#alongside), [beside](#beside), [across from](#across-from), [alongside (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#alongside), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)

### across from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkrɔs frəm/ &middot; *uh-KRAWS-frum* &middot; also /əˈkrɑs frəm/ &middot; *uh-KRAHS-frum* in cot/caught-merged speech; **weak form** — *from* is almost never stressed and reduces to /frəm/ and, in fast speech, to a syllabic /fṛm/, so *across from the school* runs *uh-KRAWS-frm-thuh-SKOOL*. The first syllable of *across* is already /ə/ and reduces no further
**Русский:** напротив, через дорогу от — обычный американский способ сказать «напротив»
**Type:** complex (*across* + *from*) &middot; **Register:** neutral &mdash; **this is the ordinary American English way to say "opposite,"** and it belongs equally to speech and to edited prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **also a fused relative introduced by *where***: *across from **where the depot used to be***, *across from **where they hold the market***. **No gerund and no *that*-clause**: ✗ *across from parking the car*, ✗ *across from that the school was there*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *across from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *across from* [noun phrase] &middot; [sit / live / park / stand] *across from* [noun phrase] &middot; *directly / right / just / diagonally across from*
**Contrast:** ***Across from* is what Americans say where the British say [opposite](#opposite).** *The bank across from the courthouse* is ordinary American; *the bank opposite the courthouse* is correct but reads as British or as formal description. Against [facing](#facing), which adds orientation: two houses can be *across from* each other while one of them shows the other a blank wall, but a house *facing* the park is turned toward it. Against bare [across](#across), which spans or covers a surface — *a bridge across the river* is not *across from* anything. Against [kitty-corner from](#kitty-corner-from), which is diagonal rather than straight across. And note that the object attaches to *from*, not to *across*: it is *across from the school*, never ✗ *across the school from*.

On the other side of a street, a room, a table, or any narrow gap, and facing back this way. It is the plainest of the American facing words and the one that has quietly taken over the spatial work of *opposite*: an address, a seating arrangement, a restaurant's location, and a witness's position are all given with *across from*. The gap has to be narrow enough to look over — you are *across from* a neighbor, not *across from* a state.

**1. On the other side of a street or open space from.** The pharmacy is *across from* the post office.
**2. Seated or standing facing someone over a table or an aisle.** He sat *across from* me for the entire deposition.
**3. On the facing side of a printed or laid-out spread.** The photograph *across from* the title page.

1. They rented the house *across from* the elementary school. *(1)*
2. There is a food truck parked *across from* the courthouse most days. *(1)*
3. We ended up *across from* an old classmate at the rehearsal dinner. *(2)*
4. Sit *across from* the window so I can see you on the call. *(2)*
5. The chart runs *across from* the summary on page nine. *(3)*
6. The lot *across from where the cannery stood* is a park now. *(1)*
7. She lives directly *across from* the fire station and has stopped hearing the sirens. *(1)*

**See also:** [opposite](#opposite), [facing](#facing), [across](#across), [kitty-corner from](#kitty-corner-from), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)

### adjacent to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈdʒeɪsənt tə/ &middot; *uh-JAY-suhnt-tuh* &middot; **weak form** — *to* is /tə/ before a consonant and /tu/ before a vowel, and it is never stressed; the final /t/ of *adjacent* and the /t/ of *to* commonly merge into a single held /t/, giving *uh-JAY-suhn-tuh*
**Русский:** примыкающий к, смежный с — офиц.-техн.: граничащий вплотную, с общей границей
**Type:** complex (adjective + *to*) &middot; **Register:** **formal, technical, and bureaucratic** — zoning ordinances and planning documents, real-estate and insurance filings, engineering drawings, medical charts, and academic prose. **In speech Americans say [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)**, and an editor will usually make the same substitution in ordinary writing.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — *adjacent to the parcel*, *adjacent to it*. **No gerund and no clause**: ✗ *adjacent to renovating the wing* — write *adjacent to the wing being renovated*. (*Adjacent to parking* is fine, but only because *parking* is a noun there, not a gerund governed by *to*.)
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *adjacent to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *adjacent to* [noun phrase] &middot; *immediately / directly adjacent to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** bare *adjacent* is an **adjective** and takes no object (*the adjacent lot*, *adjacent rooms*); *adjoining* is the near-synonym that insists on a shared wall or boundary; the dependent-adjective use is catalogued in [dependent adjectives](15-dependent-adjectives.md)
**Contrast:** **The line between *adjacent to* and [near](#near) is contact.** Things *adjacent to* each other touch or share a boundary; things *near* each other merely are not far apart. A vacant lot *adjacent to* the school shares a fence line with it; a diner *near* the school is two blocks away. Against [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), which means the same thing in one register down, and against *contiguous with*, which is stricter still and demands an unbroken shared border. Against [beside](#beside), which is about position rather than about property lines, and is the wrong word for a survey.

Immediately next to, sharing a border or an edge. The word came into English out of Latin as a surveyor's and geometer's term and it has never lost that flavor: it is what you write when the exact boundary matters, which is why it dominates the language of land, buildings, and diagrams. A recent extension has given it a second life in journalism, where *adjacent to* an industry or a movement means bordering on it without belonging to it.

**1. Next to and sharing a boundary with.** The garage is *adjacent to* the alley.
**2. Immediately connected in a system, a sequence, or a diagram.** The two cells *adjacent to* the header are locked.
**3. Bordering on something without belonging to it (a recent journalistic use).** She works in a field *adjacent to* publishing.

1. The county rezoned the parcel *adjacent to* the wetlands. *(1)*
2. Guests in the room *adjacent to* the elevator complained about the noise. *(1)*
3. Their property is *adjacent to* federal land on two sides. *(1)*
4. Each node is linked only to the nodes *adjacent to* it. *(2)*
5. Store the reagents in the cabinet *adjacent to* the fume hood. *(2)*
6. He described the startup as *adjacent to* health care rather than part of it. *(3)*
7. In speech, the same sentence is ordinary as *the garage is next to the alley*. *(1)*

**See also:** [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [beside](#beside), [near](#near), [alongside](#alongside), [next door to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-door-to), [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to)

### against

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *uh-GENST*; also /əˈɡeɪnst/ &middot; *uh-GAYNST* (minority American, standard British); unstressed it reduces to /əˈɡɛn(t)s/ &middot; *uh-GENS*, with the /t/ squeezed out between the /n/ and the /s/, and in fast speech the first syllable can vanish entirely, giving *'genst*
**Русский:** к, об, о — прижавшись вплотную; на фоне (чего-то); против, вопреки; от (страховка)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*a rule against parking here*, *advised against going*). **Never a finite clause** — write *against the claim that he lied*, not *against he lied*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / lean / prop / rest] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *against* [noun phrase] (backdrop) &middot; *against* [verb-ing] &middot; stranded: *the wall it was leaning* **against**
**Contrast:** *Against* is the **contact-under-pressure** preposition, and that pressure is what separates it from its neighbors. [On](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is contact with support from below; *against* is contact with force from the side. [Next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to) and [beside](#beside) are adjacency with no contact at all: *the ladder **beside** the wall* is standing free, *the ladder **against** the wall* is leaning on it. For the backdrop sense, nothing else will do — *dark **against** the sky* cannot become *dark on the sky*. The forcible-motion sense (*threw it against the door*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#against), and sense 3, opposition proper, is developed at length in [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#against).

Touching something and pressing on it. Everything in this entry is the resting state that force leaves behind: a thing leaning, a thing braced, a thing outlined by whatever sits behind it. From physical pressure the word generalizes to opposition of every kind, which is why the same syllable covers a ladder on a wall and a vote in a legislature.

**1. Touching under pressure; leaning or braced.** The bike is *against* the fence.
**2. Seen with something behind it as a backdrop.** Bare branches *against* a gray sky.
**3. In opposition to a rule, a person, or a side.** That's *against* the law.
**4. Providing protection or coverage from.** Insured *against* flood damage.

1. Two shovels leaned *against* the garage door. *(1)*
2. He stood with his back *against* the counter. *(1)*
3. The pressed flowers show up beautifully *against* the dark paper. *(2)*
4. Her red coat was the only color *against* all that snow. *(2)*
5. Parking here is *against* the building rules. *(3)*
6. Three of the five directors voted *against* the merger. *(3)*
7. The policy protects *against* theft but not against flooding. *(4)*
8. Everyone in the unit is vaccinated *against* hepatitis. *(4)*

**See also:** [beside](#beside), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [alongside](#alongside), [against (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#against)

### alongside

**Pronunciation:** /əˌlɔŋˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-lawng-SYD*; also /əˌlɑŋˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-lahng-SYD* in cot/caught-merged speech; stress shifts to the first syllable before a noun in fast speech
**Русский:** рядом с, вдоль — параллельно бок о бок; наравне с кем-то; одновременно с
**Type:** compound (*along* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund** in the "at the same time as" sense (*studied alongside working nights*). **No finite clause of any kind, and never a *that*-clause.** Also an adverb (*a tug pulled alongside*), which takes no object at all; the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / pull / run] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; *alongside* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *Alongside* adds **parallel orientation** to the plain adjacency of [beside](#beside) and [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to): it wants two long things lying the same way, which is why it started life on the water and still sounds right with boats, trains, roads, and rows. *A chair beside the desk* is fine; *a chair alongside the desk* implies the chair is lined up with it. ***Alongside of*** is redundant — drop the *of*. And it is always one word.

Beside and parallel to, close enough to be lined up with. Its nautical origin still governs the physical sense — you pull *alongside* a dock the way a hull comes to rest against a pier. The figurative senses keep the parallelism intact: people who work *alongside* each other are doing the same kind of thing at the same time, as equals rather than as one under the other, and once the equality drops away what is left is bare simultaneity — a document released *alongside* another one.

**1. Beside and parallel to, physically.** The bike path runs *alongside* the highway.
**2. Working or existing in parallel with, as an equal.** She trained *alongside* professional dancers.
**3. Together with; at the same time as.** The report came out *alongside* a formal apology.

1. The tugboat eased *alongside* the freighter. *(1)*
2. There is a drainage ditch *alongside* the whole east fence. *(1)*
3. A photographer jogged *alongside* the lead runner. *(1)*
4. He learned the trade *alongside* his father. *(2)*
5. The new system will run *alongside* the old one until June. *(2)*
6. She finished the degree *alongside* working two jobs. *(3)*
7. The findings were published *alongside* a lengthy rebuttal. *(3)*
8. The tax cut passed *alongside* a two-year spending freeze. *(3)*

**See also:** [beside](#beside), [along](#along), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [near](#near), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### anear

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnɪr/ &middot; *uh-NEER* &middot; two syllables, stress on the second; the first is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is **no further weak form**. The vowel of the stressed syllable is the /ɪ/ + /r/ of *near*, not the /i/ of *knee*
**Русский:** близ, подле — устар., поэт.
**Type:** compound (the worn-down Old English *an* "on, in" + *near*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and poetic.** It belongs to Romantic and Victorian verse and to the dialect writing of the same century — Tennyson, Swinburne, Hardy, and the ballad collectors. An American meets it in a poetry anthology or a hymnal, never in prose written after about 1900. **Do not present it as a modern option.**
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** It is also an **adverb** (*a rider drew anear*), which takes no object — and the adverb is the commoner of the two even in the period that used the word.
**Pattern:** [verb of position or motion] *anear* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *anear* [noun phrase] &middot; *anear* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** Against [near](#near), the live word and the only one to write today. *Anear* belongs to a whole family of *a-* forms in which the prefix is that old *an* "on, in": [aboard](#aboard), [atop](#atop), [astride](#astride), [abaft](#abaft), [athwart](04-movement-direction.md#athwart), *anigh*, *anear*. Some of that family survived into ordinary English and some did not; *anear* and [anigh](#anigh) are on the losing side, and the difference between them is only that *anear* is built on *near* and *anigh* on *nigh*.

Near; close to. The word says nothing that *near* does not, and it existed for the reason most poetic forms exist — it gave a writer an extra unstressed syllable and an archaic color. Its interest to a modern reader is historical: it shows the *a-* prefix still alive and productive at a date when it had already frozen inside *aboard* and *atop*.

**1. Near, close to (archaic and poetic).** No one came *anear* the house that winter.
**2. Adverb, with no object: near at hand.** A rider drew *anear*.

1. Nineteenth-century verse: she would not walk *anear* the water after dark. *(1)*
2. Dialect fiction: there's a good spring *anear* the old orchard. *(1)*
3. The flock grazed *anear* the barrow all that summer. *(1)*
4. Ballad diction: he laid him down *anear* the churchyard wall. *(1)*
5. Modern rendering: *anear* the river becomes *near the river* &mdash; the archaic form has no place in current prose. *(1)*
6. Adverb, and therefore not this preposition: the storm came *anear* and passed over. *(2)*

### anigh

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnaɪ/ &middot; *uh-NY* &middot; two syllables, stress on the second; the *-gh* is silent, exactly as in *nigh* and *high*; the first syllable is the reduced /ə/ and there is **no further weak form**
**Русский:** близ, подле — устар., поэт.; ещё реже, чем anear
**Type:** compound (the old *an* "on, in" + *nigh*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and poetic, and rarer than [anear](#anear)** — chiefly nineteenth-century verse, ballad imitation, and dialect writing. It is dead in modern prose and reads as costume.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** Also an **adverb** (*he came anigh*), which takes no object.
**Pattern:** [verb of position or motion] *anigh* [noun phrase] &middot; *anigh* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** The base word *nigh* has outlived its own compound: *nigh* survives in *well-nigh*, *nigh on*, and *nigh unto*, while *anigh* did not survive at all. Against [anear](#anear), which is the same formation on *near* and is slightly the commoner of the two in verse. Against [near](#near), the modern word, and [hard by](#hard-by), the other archaic proximity phrase an American is likely to meet in period writing.

Near; close to. It is the same archaic *a-* + adjective pattern as *anear*, built on *nigh* instead of on *near*, and it had the same job: a spare syllable and an old-fashioned sound. It is listed here so that a reader who meets it in a ballad or a hymn can identify it as a preposition and give it an object, rather than mistaking it for an adverb.

**1. Near, close to (archaic and poetic).** He would not come *anigh* the fire.
**2. Adverb, with no object: near at hand.** Winter draws *anigh*.

1. Ballad diction: no living soul came *anigh* the tower. *(1)*
2. Nineteenth-century verse: the herd would not graze *anigh* the fence. *(1)*
3. Dialect fiction: don't you go *anigh* that dog. *(1)*
4. Hymn diction: keep me *anigh* the shepherd's path. *(1)*
5. Modern rendering: *anigh* the mill becomes *near the mill* &mdash; there is no current use of the older form. *(1)*
6. Adverb, and therefore not this preposition: the hour drew *anigh*. *(2)*

### beside

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsaɪd/ &middot; *bih-SYD*; weak /bəˈsaɪd/ &middot; *buh-SYD*
**Русский:** рядом с, возле — сбоку от; по сравнению с; не путать с besides «кроме того»
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — ***beside me***, never *beside I*. **No clause, no gerund.** Unlike *besides*, it is never an adverb and never a conjunct: you cannot write *Beside, it's late*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand / walk] *beside* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *beside* [noun phrase] &middot; *beside the point* &middot; *beside* [reflexive] (*beside himself*)
**Contrast:** ***Beside* and [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides) are different words, and confusing them is the single most common error in this entire category.** *Beside* is position — at the side of. *Besides* is addition or exception — in addition to, apart from. The test is substitution: if *next to* fits, the word is *beside* (*sit **beside** me*); if *in addition to* or *apart from* fits, the word is *besides* (***Besides** me, only two people came*). *Beside* also differs from [alongside](#alongside), which demands parallel alignment, and from [against](#against), which demands contact.

At the side of something, close by. It is the plainest adjacency word in English and carries no implication of contact, alignment, or facing. Two idioms have drifted away from the literal sense and are worth learning whole: *beside the point*, meaning irrelevant, and *beside oneself*, meaning overcome by an emotion.

**1. At the side of; next to.** He sat *beside* her the whole flight.
**2. Compared with (a slightly formal use).** Her troubles look small *beside* his.
**3. In the fixed idioms *beside the point* and *beside oneself*.** That objection is *beside* the point.

1. There is a bench *beside* the trailhead. *(1)*
2. Park *beside* the blue truck and walk up. *(1)*
3. The first draft looks amateurish *beside* the final version. *(2)*
4. A two-hour delay is nothing *beside* what they went through. *(2)*
5. Whether he meant it is *beside* the point. *(3)*
6. She was *beside* herself when the dog came back. *(3)*

**See also:** [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [alongside](#alongside), [near](#near), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### facing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfeɪsɪŋ/ &middot; *FAY-sing*; two syllables, stress on the first, and no weak form — like every participial preposition it keeps the shape of the verb it came from
**Русский:** лицом к, выходящий на — обращённый в сторону чего-либо (окна, фасад, места)
**Type:** participial (the *-ing* form of *face*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard word in real-estate, architectural, seating, and travel description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — *facing the park*, *facing us*. **No clause, no gerund.** One warning is worth the whole entry: ***the company is facing a recall* is the verb *face* in the progressive, not this preposition.** The test is substitution — if *opposite* or *across from* can replace it without touching the rest of the clause, it is the preposition (*a cabin facing the lake* &rarr; *a cabin across from the lake*); if only *confronting* fits, it is the verb.
**Pattern:** [noun] *facing* [noun phrase] (*the seats facing the stage*) &middot; [be-verb] *facing* [noun phrase] &middot; [sit / stand / park] *facing* [noun phrase] &middot; [page / plate] *facing* [page number]
**Contrast:** *Facing* is the participial member of the *opposite / across from* group, and it is the one that states orientation outright. [Opposite](#opposite) and *across from* only put two things on either side of a gap; *facing* says which way the thing is turned. A house can be *across from* the park and still show it a blank wall, but a house *facing* the park looks at it. Against [before](#before), the formal and largely ceremonial word for the same geometry. Against the near-twin [overlooking](#overlooking), which adds height and a downward view — *a room overlooking the harbor* is above it, *a room facing the harbor* need not be. And against [toward](04-movement-direction.md#toward), which is orientation with motion implied; *facing* is orientation at rest. (**Note the spelling there: *toward* is the American form and *towards* the British one**, and the same split runs through *amid*/*amidst*, *among*/*amongst*, and *around*/*round* in this file.)

Turned toward something and looking at it across an intervening space. The word has quietly taken over the work American English has been withdrawing from *opposite*, and it does that work with the orientation made explicit, which is why floor plans, seating charts, and listings reach for it so often. It stays close to its verb: whatever does the facing is understood to have a front.

**1. Turned toward; looking out at.** A cabin *facing* the lake.
**2. On the side of a structure that looks toward something.** The windows *facing* the alley are painted shut.
**3. Directly opposite in a printed or laid-out spread.** The illustration *facing* page 40.

1. They asked for a room *facing* the courtyard. *(1)*
2. He sat *facing* the door for the whole meal. *(1)*
3. Two lots *facing* the water sold in the first week. *(1)*
4. All the offices *facing* the parking lot get afternoon glare. *(2)*
5. The bleachers *facing* the visitors' bench were empty by the fifth. *(2)*
6. The map on the page *facing* the index is out of date. *(3)*
7. The photograph *facing* the title page was taken in 1911. *(3)*

### forenenst

**Pronunciation:** /fɔrˈnɛnst/ &middot; *for-NENST* &middot; also /fərˈnɪnst/ &middot; *fuhr-NINST* (spelled *forninst*) and /fɔrˈnɛnt/ &middot; *for-NENT* (spelled *fornent*). Stress falls on the second syllable in every variant and the first reduces to /fɚ/, which is the **weak form** you will hear in running dialect speech: *fuhr-NENST*
**Русский:** напротив; вплотную к — диал. (шотл., ольстерское, аппалачское) и устар.
**Type:** simple (historically *fore* + *anent*) &middot; **Register:** **regional and archaic.** It is Scots and Ulster Scots, northern English, and — in this country — the Scots-Irish inheritance of Appalachia, western Pennsylvania, and the rural Midland. An American meets it in dialect fiction, in folk-song and oral-history transcription, and in the speech of older rural speakers; **it does not appear in edited American prose, and a reader outside that belt will not know it.**
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** It is also used adverbially in some dialect writing, with no object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand / build] *forenenst* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *forenenst* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** fornent, forninst, fornenst, forenent — all the same word, and the spelling in any given text tells you which dialect the writer was transcribing. The related *anent* survived on its own into legal and jocular English, but it drifted to mean "concerning," not "opposite."
**Contrast:** The word is worth an entry because it covers **three relations that modern English splits between three different prepositions** — facing ([opposite](#opposite), [across from](#across-from)), alongside ([beside](#beside)), and in contact with ([against](#against)) — and a reader who assumes only the first will misread half its occurrences. Against *anent*, its own descendant, which kept the form and changed the meaning. Against [hard by](#hard-by), the other proximity word an American meets mainly in period text: *hard by* is literary and English, *forenenst* is spoken and Scots-Irish.

Directly opposite; right beside; up against. The dialects that kept it use it for any close confrontation of two things, and context decides which of the three readings is meant. It is included here as a reading aid rather than as a usage recommendation: the modern American writer has *across from*, *beside*, and *against*, and should use them.

**1. Directly opposite, facing.** The smokehouse stood *forenenst* the barn.
**2. Right beside, alongside.** He set his chair *forenenst* the stove.
**3. Up against, in contact with.** The ladder was leaning *forenenst* the wall.

1. Dialect fiction: the church sits *forenenst* the schoolhouse. *(1)*
2. Oral history: they built the new cabin *forenenst* the spring. *(1)*
3. Appalachian speech: come sit *forenenst* me where I can hear you. *(2)*
4. Scots usage: the two farms lie *forenenst* one another across the burn. *(1)*
5. Ulster usage: he had his back *forenenst* the door the whole time. *(3)*
6. Modern rendering: *forenenst the barn* becomes *across from the barn*, *beside the barn*, or *against the barn*, depending on which of the three is meant. *(1)*

### hard by

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɑrd ˈbaɪ/ &middot; *hard-BY* &middot; two full vowels, with the main stress on *by*; **neither word reduces**, and that is part of what makes the phrase sound deliberate — *by* is the one preposition here that keeps its full /aɪ/ even in a compound
**Русский:** совсем рядом с, подле — устар., книжн.
**Type:** complex (the old adverb *hard* "closely" + *by*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** Shakespeare and the King James Version put it in the language, nineteenth-century narrative kept it, and it now survives as a conscious literary flourish and in British topographical description. An American meets it in older fiction, in guidebook prose reaching for period color, and in historical-marker English.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** Bare *hard by* also occurs adverbially in older writing (*a mill stood hard by*), with no object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / stand / lie / live] *hard by* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *hard by* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **The *hard* here means "closely," not "with difficulty."** It is the same adverb as in *hard on his heels*, *hard aground*, *hard aport*, and *hard upon*, and reading it as *difficult* is the standard misparse. Against [near](#near) and [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to), the everyday words: *hard by* is closer than either — it means immediately beside, with nothing in between. Against [beside](#beside), which says the same thing in a modern register, and [nigh unto](13-formal-latin-archaic.md), the other archaic proximity phrase.

Immediately beside; very close to. The phrase is a fossil of a much older adverbial *hard*, and it survives only because writers occasionally want its weight. Where *near* leaves the distance vague, *hard by* closes it entirely: a cottage *hard by* the church is at the churchyard wall, not down the lane.

**1. Immediately beside, very close to (archaic and literary).** The inn stood *hard by* the ford.

1. Period narrative: their cottage was *hard by* the churchyard wall. *(1)*
2. A historical marker: the first mill was built *hard by* this crossing in 1774. *(1)*
3. Guidebook prose: the abbey ruins lie *hard by* the river path. *(1)*
4. Older fiction: he took lodgings *hard by* the university gates. *(1)*
5. Scriptural cadence: they camped *hard by* the well and waited. *(1)*
6. Modern rendering: *hard by the station* becomes *right next to the station* or *just beside the station*. *(1)*

### in the vicinity of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə vəˈsɪnəti əv/ &middot; *in-thuh-vuh-SIN-uh-tee-uv* &middot; **weak form** — everything but *sin* is unstressed: *in* /ɪn/, *the* /ðə/, the first syllable of *vicinity* /və/, and *of* /ə/ before a consonant. The medial /t/ is flapped, so the ordinary American pronunciation is *in-thuh-vuh-SIN-uh-dee-uh*
**Русский:** поблизости от, в районе — офиц.: язык сводок и отчётов; также «примерно» о сумме
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** **formal and official.** It is the standard phrase in police blotters and incident reports, aviation and marine advisories, environmental impact statements, insurance claims, and real-estate filings — the places where an American actually meets it. **In ordinary prose it is five words doing the work of *near*, and American editors cut it.**
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun in the spatial sense; **a quantity** in the approximation sense (*in the vicinity of forty thousand dollars*). **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *in the vicinity of closing the road*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / occur / be seen] *in the vicinity of* [place] &middot; *in the vicinity of* [amount] &middot; *in the immediate vicinity of* [place]
**Variants:** *in the neighborhood of* is the colloquial twin and is used mainly for the quantity sense (*in the neighborhood of two hundred dollars*); *in the area of* and *in proximity to* are the other bureaucratic equivalents
**Contrast:** Against [near](#near), which says the same thing in one syllable and is almost always the better choice. What the longer phrase buys is a deliberate vagueness that official writing sometimes wants: a report that places a suspect *in the vicinity of* the building has committed to less than one that places him *at* it. Against [around](#around) and *about* for the quantity sense, which are shorter and unmarked. Against [in the area of](11-complex-multiword.md), which is the same padding with a different noun.

Near; in the space surrounding something. The phrase exists because officialdom needs a way to be approximate on purpose, and both of its senses do that job — the spatial one refuses to fix a distance, and the numerical one refuses to fix an amount. Outside that setting it is padding, and the test is simple: if *near* fits, use *near*.

**1. Near; in the area surrounding.** The car was last seen *in the vicinity of* the fairgrounds.
**2. Approximately, of an amount.** Repairs will run *in the vicinity of* eight thousand dollars.

1. Residents *in the vicinity of* the plant were asked to stay indoors. *(1)*
2. Two aircraft were operating *in the vicinity of* the field at the time. *(1)*
3. Fishing is prohibited *in the immediate vicinity of* the dam. *(1)*
4. Witnesses placed him *in the vicinity of* the intersection around nine. *(1)*
5. The settlement is expected to be *in the vicinity of* two million. *(2)*
6. Attendance was *in the vicinity of* four hundred. *(2)*
7. In ordinary prose: *a diner in the vicinity of the courthouse* is better written *a diner near the courthouse*. *(1)*

### kitty-corner from

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɪti ˌkɔrnɚ frəm/ &middot; *KIT-ee-kor-ner-frum* &middot; the *catty-* form is /ˈkæti/ &middot; *KAT-ee*; **weak form** — *from* is never stressed and reduces to /frəm/ or to a syllabic /fṛm/, so the whole phrase runs to one main beat on *KIT*: *KIT-ee-korn-er-frm*
**Русский:** наискосок от — разг., амер.: по диагонали через перекрёсток
**Type:** complex (compound adverb + *from*) &middot; **Register:** **informal and regional American**, and fully standard within American speech; **unknown in British English**, where a reader will simply not recognize it. It belongs to speech, letters, and dialogue rather than to formal writing, where *diagonally across from* is the safe form.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.**
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / live / park] *kitty-corner from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *kitty-corner from* [noun phrase] &middot; *kitty-corner to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** **catty-corner from**, **catercorner from**, *cater-cornered from*, *kitty-cornered from*, *catty-cornered from* — and every one of them also takes *to* in place of *from*. The regional split is real: ***kitty-corner* is Northern and Western, *catty-corner* Southern and Midland.** The original is *catercorner*, from the dialect adverb *cater* "diagonally," which is French *quatre* — the four spots on a die — and neither cats nor kittens ever had anything to do with it. Bare *kitty-corner* is an **adverb** (*the store sits kitty-corner*) and takes no object.
**Contrast:** Against [across from](#across-from), which is straight across: *the deli across from the bank* faces it over one street, while *the deli kitty-corner from the bank* is across the intersection and one street over, on the diagonal. Against [opposite](#opposite), which cannot express the diagonal at all. Against *diagonally across from*, the plain-English equivalent — longer, unregional, and the right choice when the reader may not be American.

Diagonally opposite; across an intersection rather than straight across a street. It is one of the few genuinely American place prepositions, and its geometry is precise enough to be useful: given a four-way intersection, one corner is *across from* you, one is *kitty-corner from* you, and the fourth is simply next door. The spelling and the first syllable will tell a linguist which half of the country the speaker comes from.

**1. Diagonally opposite across an intersection.** The pharmacy is *kitty-corner from* the bank.
**2. Set at a diagonal to something rather than square with it.** He parked the trailer *kitty-corner from* the barn.

1. There is a taqueria *kitty-corner from* the high school. *(1)*
2. We waited *kitty-corner from* the courthouse until the parade passed. *(1)*
3. Southern usage: the feed store sits *catty-corner from* the depot. *(1)*
4. She grew up *kitty-corner from* the fire station and never got used to it. *(1)*
5. The old desk sat *kitty-corner from* the window to catch the light. *(2)*
6. Formal writing: *the annex is diagonally across from the main building*, not *kitty-corner from* it. *(1)*
7. Adverb, no object, and therefore not this preposition: the rug is laid *kitty-corner*. *(2)*

### near

**Pronunciation:** /nɪr/ &middot; *NEER*; one syllable; the vowel is the /ɪ/ + /r/ sequence, not the /i/ of *knee*. No weak form — *near* keeps its full vowel even unstressed.
**Русский:** около, близ — недалеко в пространстве или во времени; близко к состоянию или числу
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; also a **fused relative** — a wh-clause standing in for a noun phrase — in the degree use: *nowhere near **what we needed***, *not anywhere near **what it cost***. ✗ *Never a *that*-clause**, and a gerund is marginal at best: *came near winning* is idiomatic, but ✗ *near finishing the report* is not. ✗ *Alone among the simple prepositions, *near* is gradable and inflects for degree** — *nearer the door*, *nearest the exit* — a leftover from its life as an adjective. It is also an adjective (*a near miss*), an adverb (*don't come near*), and a verb (*the project neared completion*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *near* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *near* [noun phrase] &middot; *nearer / nearest* [noun phrase] &middot; *near to* [noun phrase] &middot; *nowhere near* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** near to (chiefly British and slightly formal in American English; bare *near* is the American default)
**Contrast:** *Near* and [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to) are interchangeable in most sentences; *near* is shorter and takes the comparative directly (*nearer the fire*, where you would need *closer to the fire*). Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by), which means immediately beside — *stand **by** the door* puts you at it, *stand **near** the door* leaves you a few feet away. Against [beside](#beside), which is adjacency with no distance at all. **And note that [nearby](#nearby) is not a preposition in careful American English** — *nearby the station* should be *near the station*.

Not far from, in space, in time, or on a scale of condition. It is the vaguest of the proximity words and that vagueness is the point: *near the airport* commits to nothing about how near. Its gradability is what sets it apart from every other preposition in this file, and it is the reason *near* survives in a class that otherwise refuses comparison.

**1. Not far from in space.** They live *near* the reservoir.
**2. Not far from in time.** *Near* the end of the movie she walks out.
**3. Close to a state, a condition, or a number.** The company came *near* bankruptcy twice.

1. There's a decent diner *near* the courthouse. *(1)*
2. Sit *nearer* the front if you want to hear. *(1)*
3. The fire started somewhere *near* the fuse box. *(1)*
4. It was *near* midnight before anyone left. *(2)*
5. *Near* the end of the season he tore a hamstring. *(2)*
6. She was *near* tears by the time she finished. *(3)*
7. The bid came *nowhere near* what we needed. *(3)*

### nearby

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɪrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *neer-BY*; stressed on the second syllable as an adverb (*a diner is nearby*), on the first as an attributive adjective, /ˈnɪrˌbaɪ/ *NEER-by* (*a nearby diner*)
**Русский:** поблизости, близлежащий — как предлог в амер. англ. нестандартно, пишите near
**Type:** compound (*near* + *by*) &middot; **Register:** neutral as adjective and adverb; **the prepositional use is nonstandard in American English**
**Complement:** **As a preposition it takes a noun phrase — but do not use it that way.** *Nearby the station* is attested and is regularly flagged as an error by American editors; write *near the station*. In its standard lives it takes no object at all: adjective before a noun (*a nearby town*), adverb after the verb (*they live nearby*).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] is *nearby* (adverb) &middot; a *nearby* [noun] (adjective) &middot; **avoid:** *nearby* [noun phrase] (preposition)
**Contrast:** **The rule is short: *near* takes an object, *nearby* does not.** *A store near the school* and *a nearby store* are both correct; *a store nearby the school* is the error. This is the cleanest illustration in the file of the test that runs through the whole class — a preposition has an object, an adverb stands alone. Compare [near](#near), which is a true preposition; the two-word *close by* behaves exactly like *nearby* and takes no object either.

Close at hand. It is listed here because learners reach for it as a preposition and American editors take it back out. Treat it as an adjective and an adverb, and hand the prepositional work to *near*. Its inclusion in a preposition dictionary is a warning label, not an endorsement.

**1. Adverb: at a short distance, with no object.** There's a hardware store *nearby*.
**2. Adjective before a noun: located close at hand.** They evacuated three *nearby* buildings.
**3. Preposition (nonstandard in American English): near.** *Nearby* the river &mdash; write *near the river* instead.

1. We ate at a taqueria *nearby*. *(1)*
2. Is there a gas station *nearby*? *(1)*
3. A *nearby* fire station handled the call. *(2)*
4. Two *nearby* counties reported the same outage. *(2)*
5. Nonstandard: they camped *nearby* the lake. Standard: they camped *near* the lake. *(3)*
6. Nonstandard: the office is *nearby* the courthouse. Standard: the office is *near* the courthouse. *(3)*

### next

**Pronunciation:** /nɛkst/ &middot; *NEKST* &middot; one syllable. **No weak form** — the vowel is already the short /ɛ/ — but the final cluster routinely loses its /t/ before another consonant, so *next the wall* comes out /nɛks ðə ˈwɔl/ &middot; *NEKS-thuh-WAWL*. The same /t/-loss is what gives *next to* its ordinary spoken shape, *NEKS-tuh*
**Русский:** рядом с, подле — как предлог устар. в амер. англ.; современная норма — next to
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** **archaic in American English.** The bare preposition is still current in British English and was ordinary in American writing through the nineteenth century; today it survives here in one fixed phrase and nowhere else. **Write [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to).**
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** In its three ordinary modern lives *next* takes **no object at all** — adjective (*next week*, *the next street*), adverb (*what happens next*), noun (*the next of them*) — and that is the test: the preposition is the only one of the four with something after it that it governs.
**Pattern:** [sit / stand / lie] *next* [noun phrase] (archaic, and to be avoided) &middot; *next the skin* (the one surviving fixed phrase) &middot; **standard American:** [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to) [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *next to* is the American form and the only one to write; *next door to* narrows it to buildings; *nearest* covers the superlative (*the seat nearest the door*)
**Contrast:** This entry is a warning label of exactly the kind [nearby](#nearby) carries, and the rule is the mirror image of that one. There, the word takes no object and learners give it one; here, the word can take an object and American English has stopped letting it. *He sat next the fire* is British or Victorian; Americans write *next to the fire*. Watch the word-class trap too: *next Tuesday* and *the next exit* are the **adjective**, which never takes *to*, so ✗ *next to Tuesday* means something quite different — beside a person named Tuesday. Against [beside](#beside), [alongside](#alongside), and [by](02-core-relational.md#by), all of which are live American prepositions for the same position.

Immediately beside, with nothing in between. The word is the superlative of the old *nigh* — *nigh, near, next* — and it once did prepositional work freely. American English has handed that work to *next to* and kept bare *next* for its adjective and adverb duties. The one exception is the clothing phrase *next the skin*, which survives in older writing and in outdoor and tailoring usage, and even there *next to the skin* is now more common.

**1. Immediately beside (archaic in American English; current in British English).** He sat *next* the fire all evening.
**2. In the fixed phrase next the skin: in direct contact with the body.** Wear a wool layer *next* the skin.
**3. Word-class note: with no object it is not this preposition at all.** Who is *next*?

1. British or nineteenth-century: she took the chair *next* the window. American: the chair *next to* the window. *(1)*
2. British or nineteenth-century: the church stands *next* the courthouse. American: *next to* the courthouse. *(1)*
3. Outdoor usage: silk worn *next* the skin dries fastest. *(2)*
4. Tailoring usage: the lining sits *next* the skin and takes the wear. *(2)*
5. Adjective, not this preposition, and no *to* is possible: we leave *next* Tuesday. *(3)*
6. Adverb, not this preposition, and no object at all: read the second paragraph *next*. *(3)*

### opposite

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑpəzɪt/ &middot; *AH-puh-zit*; also /ˈɑpəsɪt/ &middot; *AH-puh-sit*; the final syllable is reduced in every American pronunciation
**Русский:** напротив — звучит по-британски, в амер. англ. обычнее across from; также «в паре с» об актёре
**Type:** simple (functioning as preposition, adjective, adverb, and noun) &middot; **Register:** neutral, but the spatial preposition sounds British or formal in American English
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adjective (*the opposite side*), a noun (*the opposite of true*), and an adverb (*they sat opposite*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *opposite* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *opposite* [noun phrase] &middot; [act / play / star] *opposite* [person] &middot; *opposite to / from* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** opposite to, opposite from (both acceptable; bare *opposite* is the standard prepositional form)
**Contrast:** **For the spatial sense, American English says *across from*, or [facing](#facing) when the orientation matters.** *The bank across from the courthouse* is the ordinary American sentence; *the bank opposite the courthouse* is correct but reads as British or as formal description. What *is* fully American is the casting sense — *she played opposite Denzel Washington* — which no other preposition covers. Against [across](#across), which only requires spanning or being on the other side; *opposite* adds facing.

Facing something from the other side of a space between. The space matters: things *opposite* each other are separated and turned toward one another, which is why the word works for seats at a table, buildings on a street, and actors sharing a scene. Americans keep the second of those senses and quietly hand the first to *across from*.

**1. Facing, on the other side of a space (chiefly British in this use).** The pharmacy is *opposite* the library.
**2. As the counterpart of another performer.** He starred *opposite* Meryl Streep.

1. She took the chair *opposite* mine. *(1)*
2. The hotel stands *opposite* the train station. *(1)*
3. There is a small park *opposite* the school gates. *(1)*
4. She was cast *opposite* an actor she had never met. *(2)*
5. Playing *opposite* him was the best part of the job. *(2)*

### over against

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚ əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *oh-ver-uh-GENST* &middot; also *oh-ver-uh-GAYNST* for speakers who use /əˈɡeɪnst/; **weak form** — *over* loses its beat entirely and *against* reduces to /əˈɡɛn(t)s/, with the /t/ squeezed out between /n/ and /s/, so the phrase runs *oh-ver-uh-GENS*
**Русский:** напротив — устар., библ.; в книжн. речи также «в противопоставлении чему-либо»
**Type:** complex (*over* + *against*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and biblical in the spatial sense** — the 1611 King James Version is where an American has met it (*over against the treasury*, *over against the sepulchre*), and nothing since has kept that sense alive. **The contrastive sense is formal and scholarly** and is still written today, chiefly in theology, philosophy, literary criticism, and continental-influenced social theory.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *over against saying that*. In the scholarly sense the object is characteristically an abstract noun — *over against nature*, *over against the self*, *over against the state*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand] *over against* [place] (archaic) &middot; [define / set / place] [X] *over against* [Y] (scholarly) &middot; [noun] *over against* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** In the spatial sense it has simply been replaced: modern American English says [across from](#across-from), or [opposite](#opposite) in a formal register. In the contrastive sense it competes with [as against](09-comparison-opposition.md#as-against), *as opposed to*, and *in contrast with*, and it is the **most bookish of the four** — it carries a faint air of translated German philosophy, which is where much of its modern life comes from. Against [against](#against) alone, which is opposition proper; *over against* sets two things side by side for comparison rather than putting one in conflict with the other.

Directly opposite, facing across a space — and, by a natural extension that has outlived the original, set beside something for the sake of the contrast. The spatial sense is a museum piece. The contrastive one is alive but narrow: it belongs to writing that is defining a term by holding it up next to its opposite.

**1. Directly opposite; facing across a space (archaic and biblical).** They pitched their tents *over against* the city.
**2. In contrast with; set beside for comparison (formal and scholarly).** He defines freedom *over against* necessity.

1. King James cadence: he sat *over against* the treasury and watched. *(1)*
2. Older narrative: the two houses stood *over against* each other across the lane. *(1)*
3. Modern rendering: *over against the church* becomes *across from the church*. *(1)*
4. Theology: the creature is understood only *over against* the creator. *(2)*
5. Criticism: she reads the late poems *over against* the early ones. *(2)*
6. Social theory: the individual is constituted *over against* the group. *(2)*
7. Plainer alternative: *over against the earlier estimate* is usually better as *compared with the earlier estimate*. *(2)*

**See also:** [opposite](#opposite), [across from](#across-from), [against](#against), [facing](#facing), [as against](09-comparison-opposition.md#as-against), [as opposed to](11-complex-multiword.md#as-opposed-to)

### overlooking

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈlʊkɪŋ/ &middot; *oh-ver-LOOK-ing*; four syllables, main stress on *look*; the vowel of *look* is the lax /ʊ/ of *book*, not the /u/ of *Luke*; the second syllable keeps the r-colored /ɚ/ in all American speech. Like every participial preposition it holds the full shape of the verb it came from, so **there is no weak form** — what fast speech does is compress the two unstressed syllables, not change their quality
**Русский:** с видом на; возвышающийся над
**Type:** participial (the *-ing* form of *overlook*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard word in real-estate, hotel, and travel description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — *overlooking the bay*, *overlooking us*. **No clause of any kind, and no gerund**: the thing seen is always a noun phrase (*overlooking the harbor*), never a verb (✗ *overlooking swimming*). The same warning that governs [facing](#facing) governs this word: ***the auditor is overlooking a whole account* is the verb *overlook* in the progressive, not this preposition.** The test is substitution — if *with a view of* can replace it without touching the rest of the clause, it is the preposition (*a terrace overlooking the river* &rarr; *a terrace with a view of the river*); if only *failing to notice* fits, it is the verb.
**Pattern:** [noun] *overlooking* [noun phrase] (*a room overlooking the courtyard*) &middot; [be-verb] *overlooking* [noun phrase] &middot; [sit / stand / perch] *overlooking* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***Overlooking* is [facing](#facing) plus height.** Both say which way the thing is turned, but *overlooking* adds that it is above what it sees and looks down at it: *a room **facing** the harbor* may be at sea level, *a room **overlooking** the harbor* is not. Against [above](#above), which is height with no view and no orientation at all — a floor can be *above* the lobby without seeing anything. Against [over](#over), which is height with alignment and no viewer. Against [opposite](#opposite) and *across from*, which need a gap but neither elevation nor orientation. And keep the two lives of the word apart: as a preposition it gives you a view, as a verb it means you missed something — same form, opposite attitudes.

Above something and looking down at it. Its whole meaning is what *facing* and *above* deliver together, which is why American listings reach for it any time there is a view to sell. The object is always the thing seen and never the viewer, and whatever does the overlooking is understood to have both a front and a vantage point — which is how the word came to work for terrain and fortifications as easily as for windows.

**1. Situated above and giving a view of.** A deck *overlooking* the canyon.
**2. Standing high above and dominating, with no viewer implied.** A ridge *overlooking* the highway.

1. They booked a room *overlooking* the courtyard. *(1)*
2. Her office is on the ninth floor, *overlooking* the river. *(1)*
3. We ate on a terrace *overlooking* the vineyard. *(1)*
4. The old fort sits on a bluff *overlooking* the harbor entrance. *(2)*
5. A water tower *overlooking* the interchange is the only landmark for miles. *(2)*
6. Guns were dug in along the ridge *overlooking* the valley floor. *(2)*

**See also:** [facing](#facing), [above](#above), [over](#over), [opposite](#opposite), [atop](#atop)

### upside

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌpˈsaɪd/ &middot; *up-SYD* — in the prepositional idiom the stress goes to the **second** syllable and the phrase runs straight into what follows, so *upside the head* comes out /ʌpˈsaɪ də ˈhɛd/ &middot; *up-SY-duh-HED*. **This is the opposite of the noun**, which is stressed on the first syllable, /ˈʌpˌsaɪd/ &middot; *UP-syd* (*the upside of the deal*), and which is a different word entirely
**Русский:** по (голове) — разг., афроамер. и южн.: об ударе сбоку, почти всегда по голове
**Type:** simple (a compound of *up* + *side*, welded) &middot; **Register:** **informal and regional.** It belongs to African American English and to Southern American English, in one frame — *upside the head* — and from there it has spread through comedy, song, and quoted speech into general informal American usage. **It never appears in edited expository prose**, and outside its frame it is not used at all.
**Complement:** a noun phrase, and in practice almost always a possessed body part: *upside his head*, *upside my face*, *upside the ear*. **No gerund, no clause.** The narrowness of the complement is the entry's main fact — ✗ *upside the fence*, ✗ *upside the door* are not English, where *against the fence* and *against the door* are.
**Pattern:** [hit / smack / slap / knock / whack / pop] [object] *upside* [the head] &middot; [get hit / catch one] *upside the head*
**Contrast:** Against [against](#against), the general word for contact under pressure, which takes any object and any register; *upside* is one idiom and one target. Against [across](#across), which is the neutral verb-frame for a blow to the face (*hit him across the face*) and cannot be swapped in here. Against *on the side of*, the flat paraphrase — *hit him on the side of the head* — which is what the same sentence becomes in a police report. And keep the homographs apart: the noun *upside* (an advantage, a gain) and the adverb phrase *upside down* have nothing to do with this preposition.

Against the side of — of a blow, and nearly always a blow to the head. It is the narrowest preposition in this file: a single fixed frame, a single body part, and a single register. What earns it an entry is that learners meet it constantly in American dialogue, song lyrics, and comedy and have no way to parse it, because no dictionary sense of *up* or *side* predicts it. A figurative extension is now common — anything sudden and jarring can hit you *upside the head*.

**1. Against the side of, of a blow — nearly always to the head.** He got smacked *upside* the head with a rolled-up newspaper.
**2. Figurative: hitting someone suddenly and jarringly.** The bill hit us *upside* the head in January.

1. In a cartoon, the anvil catches him *upside* the head. *(1)*
2. Quoted speech: "Say that again and I'll smack you *upside* the head." *(1)*
3. Sports commentary: the ball caught the shortstop *upside* the ear. *(1)*
4. Comic narration: she popped him *upside* the head with a dish towel. *(1)*
5. Figurative: the diagnosis hit him *upside* the head and he sat down. *(2)*
6. Figurative: that last chapter comes *upside* your head out of nowhere. *(2)*
7. In edited prose the same event is written *struck him on the side of the head*. *(1)*

**See also:** [against](#against), [across](#across), [beside](#beside), [alongside](#alongside), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [over](#over)


## Enclosure, inclusion, and the space in the middle

Being surrounded by something, or being one of the things doing the surrounding. The distinctions that matter are countability (*amid* takes a mass, *among* takes individuals), individuation (*between* keeps its objects separate and named, *among* pools them), and boundary (*inside* and *outside* mark a physical enclosure, *within* marks a limit). Three phrases join them: *in among* for being down inside a mass of things, *in the center of* for the geometric middle, and *home to* for a place named from the side of whatever lives in it.

### aboard

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbɔrd/ &middot; *uh-BORD*; one stress, on the second syllable; the first syllable is always /ə/
**Русский:** на борту — на судне, в самолёте, поезде, автобусе; перен. в составе команды
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *board*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb (*all aboard*, *welcome aboard*), which is how the word most often appears.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *aboard* [vessel or vehicle] &middot; [verb of motion] *aboard* [vessel] &middot; *aboard* (adverb, no object)
**Variants:** on board (two words; identical in meaning, and the dominant form in the figurative business sense — *bring her on board*)
**Contrast:** *Aboard* is the vehicle-specific member of the *in/on* system: you are **in** a car, **on** a bus or a plane, and **aboard** a ship, a plane, a train, or a spacecraft — anything with an interior you can walk around in. It is more formal than either, which is why it belongs to announcements and news copy. Note the split with the two-word *on board*: for the figurative sense of joining a team, American English overwhelmingly writes *on board*, and *aboard* sounds nautical.

On or inside a vessel or a large vehicle. It covers both the static position and the act of getting there, which is why the same word does *everyone aboard survived* and *she came aboard in Seattle*. The figurative use — joining an organization — is a live metaphor that most writers now spell as two words.

**1. On or inside a ship, plane, train, or bus.** There were forty-two passengers *aboard* the ferry.
**2. As a member of a crew, team, or organization (figurative).** We are glad to have you *aboard* the project.

1. Everyone *aboard* the flight was accounted for. *(1)*
2. The equipment stayed *aboard* the research vessel all winter. *(1)*
3. She was the last passenger *aboard* the train. *(1)*
4. Welcome *aboard* &mdash; your badge is at the front desk. *(2)*
5. Two new engineers came *aboard* this month. *(2)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [within](#within), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [astride](#astride)

### amid

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmɪd/ &middot; *uh-MID*; *amidst* /əˈmɪdst/ &middot; *uh-MIDST*; both keep the schwa in the first syllable
**Русский:** среди, посреди — книжн.: окружённый сплошной массой, а не счётной группой; на фоне событий
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *mid*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary in the physical sense; standard journalism in the background-events sense
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically a **mass noun or a plural** (*amid the wreckage*, *amid rising costs*). **No clause, no gerund.** A *that*-clause after *amid speculation* belongs to the noun *speculation*, not to *amid*.
**Pattern:** *amid* [mass noun] &middot; *amid* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *amid* [event noun] (*amid the confusion*)
**Variants:** amidst — chiefly British and slightly more literary; **American English prefers *amid***, and the two are otherwise identical
**Contrast:** **The *amid*/[among](#among) split is countability.** *Amid* surrounds you with something undifferentiated — wreckage, smoke, chaos, rumors taken as a mass; *among* surrounds you with individuals you could point at. *Amid the crowd* treats the crowd as a texture; *among the crowd* invites you to pick out faces. *Amid* also does something *among* cannot: set a scene of ongoing events behind a piece of news (*amid falling revenues*), which is now its most frequent use in print. The emphatic long form is [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of) or *in the midst of*.

In the middle of something that is treated as a mass rather than counted. The physical sense is literary and slightly old-fashioned in speech, but the abstract one is everywhere in news writing, where *amid* is the standard way to hang a story on a backdrop of continuing conditions without claiming a causal link.

**1. Surrounded by, in the middle of a mass.** A cabin *amid* the pines.
**2. Against a background of continuing events or conditions.** The bill passed *amid* fierce opposition.

1. They found the ring *amid* the ashes. *(1)*
2. A single lit window *amid* all that darkness. *(1)*
3. Shares fell *amid* growing concern about the supply chain. *(2)*
4. The mayor resigned *amid* questions about the contract. *(2)*
5. Talks resumed *amid* signs that both sides were tiring. *(2)*

**See also:** [among](#among), [between](#between), [within](#within), [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of), [throughout](#throughout)

### among

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmʌŋ/ &middot; *uh-MUNG*; *amongst* /əˈmʌŋst/ &middot; *uh-MUNGST*; the first syllable is always /ə/ and never carries stress
**Русский:** среди, между — в окружении группы из трёх и более; в числе; распределяя между всеми
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *mong*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a plural, collective, or mass noun phrase** — *among the trees*, *among the staff*, *among us*. ***Among the tree* is ungrammatical**: the object must denote more than one thing. Objective pronouns only (*among them*, *among us*). No clause, no gerund.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *among* [plural noun phrase] &middot; [divide / share / distribute] [object] *among* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *among* [plural] (*common among teenagers*) &middot; *among other things*
**Variants:** amongst — chiefly British; it is not wrong in American English but it is markedly bookish, and edited American prose uses *among*
**Contrast:** **The schoolroom rule "*between* for two, *among* for more" is only half true.** [Between](#between) is correct for any number when the parties are individually in view and the relation is one-to-one — *a treaty between six nations* is perfectly standard. *Among* is the word when the group is an undifferentiated mass — *the money was divided among the heirs* treats them as a pool, *between the two heirs* names them. Against [amid](#amid): *among* counts its objects, *amid* does not. Against [within](#within): *within* bounds a limit, *among* surrounds with members.

Surrounded by or included in a group of more than one. Its four senses all follow from that: physically in the middle of a group, a member of a set, a share distributed to a group, and a trait holding across a group. The one hard requirement is plurality — whatever follows *among* must be countable as more than one or collective in meaning.

**1. In the middle of, surrounded by a group.** The cabin sits *among* the pines.
**2. Included in a set; one of.** She is *among* the three finalists.
**3. Distributed to the members of a group.** The estate was divided *among* the four children.
**4. Holding across the members of a group.** The habit is common *among* first-year students.

1. We found him *among* the crowd at the gate. *(1)*
2. A few red maples stood out *among* all the oaks. *(1)*
3. Her novel is *among* the best things published that year. *(2)*
4. *Among* other duties, he handles scheduling. *(2)*
5. Split the tips evenly *among* the servers. *(3)*
6. The land was parceled out *among* six families. *(3)*
7. Turnout was highest *among* voters over sixty. *(4)*
8. Sleep problems are widespread *among* shift workers. *(4)*

**See also:** [between](#between), [amid](#amid), [within](#within), [inside](#inside), [throughout](#throughout)

### between

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwin/ &middot; *bih-TWEEN*; weak /bəˈtwin/ &middot; *buh-TWEEN*; the clipped literary form *'tween* /twin/ survives only in poetry and in *'tween decks*
**Русский:** между — между двумя или несколькими названными по отдельности объектами (ср. among)
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *tween*, from *twain* "two") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a plural noun phrase (*between the buildings*), or ✗ *two or more noun phrases joined by *and*** — ***never by *or***. Objective pronouns only: ***between you and me***, never *between you and I*. **Gerunds are fine as coordinates** (*torn between working and commuting*), and so are **fused relatives**: *the difference between **what he said** and **what he did***. ✗ *Never a *that*-clause** — ✗ *between that he called and that he wrote*. Also an **adverb** with no object at all (*a fence with a gate in between*, *the years between*), and the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** *between* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *between* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *between* [pronoun] *and* [pronoun] &middot; *in between* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** betwixt (archaic — see its own entry)
**Contrast:** Three errors cluster here, and all three are worth memorizing. **First:** *between you and I* is wrong. A preposition takes the objective case, and coordination does not change that — *between you and me*, *between him and her*. ✗ *Second: the coordinator must be *and*.** *Choose between the red or the blue* should be *between the red **and** the blue*. ✗ *Third: ranges take *and*, not *to*.** *Between 1990 to 1995* is an error; write *between 1990 **and** 1995* or *from 1990 **to** 1995*. On the *between*/[among](#among) question, see that entry: the deciding factor is whether the parties are individually in view, not how many there are. The temporal sense is treated in [time](05-time.md#between).

In the space, the interval, or the relationship separating two or more distinct things that are individually in view. That last clause is what really governs the word — *between* keeps its objects separate and named, which is why it works for gaps, for treaties, for choices, and for joint efforts, and why it is fine with six parties as long as you can point at each one.

**1. In the physical space separating two or more things.** There is an alley *between* the two buildings.
**2. In the interval separating two times or points (see also [time](05-time.md#between)).** *Between* Tuesday and Friday the price doubles.
**3. Connecting or dividing two or more parties, reciprocally.** The agreement *between* the union and the city.
**4. Marking a choice among named alternatives.** You have to choose *between* the two offers.
**5. Pooling the resources or efforts of a set of people.** *Between* the three of us we raised nine hundred dollars.

1. A narrow gap *between* the fence and the shed lets the water through. *(1)*
2. She was sitting *between* her two brothers. *(1)*
3. Call me *between* nine and eleven. *(2)*
4. There were four collisions on that stretch *between* January and March. *(2)*
5. The treaty *between* the six nations took a decade to draft. *(3)*
6. Whatever is said *between* you and me stays here. *(3)*
7. He had to choose *between* the job in Denver and the one in Austin. *(4)*
8. There is not much to choose *between* the two proposals. *(4)*
9. *Between* them they own half the block. *(5)*
10. *Between* the deposit and the first month's rent, it came to three thousand. *(5)*

**See also:** [among](#among), [amid](#amid), [betwixt](#betwixt), [within](#within), [between (time)](05-time.md#between)

### betwixt

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwɪkst/ &middot; *bih-TWIKST*; weak /bəˈtwɪkst/ &middot; *buh-TWIKST*
**Русский:** меж, между — устар., книжн.; живо лишь в обороте betwixt and between «ни то ни сё»
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *twixt*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in modern American English only in the fixed phrase *betwixt and between*
**Complement:** the same as *between* — a plural noun phrase or two noun phrases joined by *and*, with objective pronouns. No clause, no gerund.
**Pattern:** *betwixt* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *betwixt* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *betwixt and between* (fixed idiom)
**Contrast:** *Betwixt* is [between](#between) in period costume. It means exactly the same thing and has no context in which it is the better choice for ordinary prose — using it in a business memo reads as a joke or an affectation. The one exception is the reduplicated idiom *betwixt and between*, meaning undecided or in an awkward middle state, which is still current and is not replaceable by *between and between*. Compare [ere](05-time.md#ere), the archaic *before*, which behaves the same way.

An archaic form of *between*, kept alive by poetry, by deliberate period flavor, and by one surviving idiom. Learners should be able to recognize it in older texts and should not produce it. Note that the idiom *betwixt and between* has drifted away from the preposition entirely and now works as an adjective phrase.

**1. Archaic or literary: between.** The valley lies *betwixt* two ranges.
**2. In the fixed phrase *betwixt and between*: undecided, in an awkward middle state.** He is *betwixt and between* about the move.

1. The old deed describes the parcel as lying *betwixt* the river and the road. *(1)*
2. There is little to choose *betwixt* the two, as an older writer would have put it. *(1)*
3. *Betwixt* dawn and full light the whole field was white with frost. *(1)*
4. She has quit one job and not started the next, so she is *betwixt and between*. *(2)*
5. The design is *betwixt and between* &mdash; too plain for a gallery, too fussy for a kitchen. *(2)*

**See also:** [between](#between), [among](#among), [amid](#amid), [ere](05-time.md#ere), [within](#within)

### home to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhoʊm tə/ &middot; *HOHM-tuh* &middot; **weak form** — *to* is never stressed here and reduces to /tə/ before a consonant and /tu/ before a vowel, so *home to a colony of bats* is /ˈhoʊm tu ə ˈkɑləni əv ˈbæts/ &middot; *HOHM-too-uh-KAH-luh-nee-uv-BATS*, and *home to three museums* is *HOHM-tuh-THREE-myoo-ZEE-umz*
**Русский:** быть домом для — где живут люди или животные, где размещается учреждение
**Type:** marginal (a predicative noun, *home*, welded to *to* and behaving as a unit) &middot; **Register:** neutral; it is the standard frame in journalism, tourism copy, natural history, and reference writing, and it is common in speech
**Complement:** noun phrase only — the residents or the contents. **No gerund and no clause**: ✗ *home to manufacturing cars* — write *home to a car plant*. The object may be a bare plural (*home to wolves*), a number phrase (*home to 40,000 people*), or a definite noun phrase (*home to the state's oldest courthouse*).
**Pattern:** [place] *is home to* [noun phrase] &middot; [place noun] *home to* [noun phrase] (reduced relative: *a valley home to three villages*) &middot; *once home to* / *still home to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **The direction is fixed, and reversing it is the standard learner error.** The **place** is the subject and the **inhabitants** are the object: *the marsh is home to herons*, never ✗ *the herons are home to the marsh*. The test is substitution — if *houses* or *is the home of* fits, the frame is right. Watch the word-class trap as well: in *he went home to his family*, *home* is a directional adverb and *to his family* a separate phrase, and *houses* cannot be substituted; that is not this preposition. Against [inside](#inside) and [within](#within), which put one thing in another with no idea of residence, and against *houses*, the plain verb that does the same job in one word.

Containing something as its residents or its permanent features. The frame is the workhorse of descriptive writing about places: a country is *home to* a population, a park is *home to* a species, a small city is *home to* a university and two museums. It is on the edge of the preposition class — *home* is still recognizably a noun — but it behaves as a fixed unit, cannot be modified from inside, and is worth learning whole.

**1. Containing as residents or inhabitants.** The island is *home to* about four hundred people.
**2. Containing as a permanent institution, business, or feature.** The old bank building is now *home to* a bookstore.
**3. Formerly containing, in the frame once home to.** The lot was *once home to* a cannery.

1. The refuge is *home to* the largest heron rookery in the state. *(1)*
2. Three counties *home to* fewer than ten thousand people share one hospital. *(1)*
3. The reef is *home to* several species found nowhere else. *(1)*
4. The campus is *home to* the country's oldest astronomy department. *(2)*
5. That block is *home to* two theaters and a dozen restaurants. *(2)*
6. The warehouse district was *once home to* the packing industry. *(3)*
7. Reversed and wrong: ✗ *the herons are home to the marsh*. Correct: *the marsh is home to the herons*. *(1)*

**See also:** [within](#within), [inside](#inside), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [throughout](#throughout), [among](#among)

### in among

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈmʌŋ/ &middot; *in-uh-MUNG* &middot; **weak form** — *in* is unstressed /ɪn/ and the whole phrase carries one beat, on the second syllable of *among*, so it runs as a single word: *in-uh-MUNG*. Before a vowel the /n/ links forward — *in among old letters* is *i-nuh-MUNG-old-LET-erz*
**Русский:** среди, вперемешку с — разг.: затерявшись внутри массы предметов
**Type:** complex (*in* + *among*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning conversational; edited prose often trims it to plain *among*, which is not quite the same thing
**Complement:** a **plural or collective** noun phrase, or an objective pronoun — *in among the rocks*, *in among them*. **No gerund, no clause.** A singular count noun is wrong: ✗ *in among the box*, ✗ *in among the crowd member*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / find / lose / grow] *in among* [plural noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in among* [plural noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in amongst* — chiefly British, and markedly bookish in American English (see [amongst](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#amongst)); *down in among* and *back in among* add a further direction
**Contrast:** **The *in* is not padding.** [Among](#among) distributes something across a group and says no more; *in among* puts it **down inside** the mass, mixed in and hard to pick out. *A receipt among the papers* is one of the papers; *a receipt in among the papers* is buried in them. Against [amid](#amid), which is formal and wants a mass noun or an abstract event; *in among* is spoken and wants countable plurals. Against [inside](#inside), which needs a container with a boundary — a pile of rocks has no inside, but you can still be *in among* them. Against [between](#between), which keeps its objects separate and named.

Down inside a mass of things and surrounded by them. It is *among* with the immersion made explicit, and the extra word does real work: it tells you the thing is not merely one of the group but tangled up in it, mixed through it, or lost in it. That is why it is the natural phrase for searching, for things growing in a jumble, and for whatever has ended up somewhere it does not belong.

**1. Mixed into and surrounded by a mass of things.** Wildflowers grow *in among* the rocks.
**2. Hidden or lost in a mass of things.** The lease is *in among* those files somewhere.
**3. Moving down into a crowd or a thicket.** He waded *in among* the reeds to free the line.

1. Bluebells come up *in among* the ferns every April. *(1)*
2. She keeps the good photographs *in among* the tax records, which is why nobody finds them. *(2)*
3. There were two counterfeit bills *in among* the deposit. *(2)*
4. The dog went straight *in among* the sheep and had to be called off. *(3)*
5. He was somewhere *in among* the crowd at the gate. *(1)*
6. Compare: a note *among* the papers is one of them; a note *in among* the papers is buried in them. *(1)*

**See also:** [among](#among), [amid](#amid), [between](#between), [inside](#inside), [within](#within), [throughout](#throughout)

### in the center of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈsɛntɚ əv/ &middot; *in-thuh-SEN-ter-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *in* /ɪn/, *the* /ðə/, and *of* /ə/ before a consonant, with the only beat on *cen-*: *in the center of the room* is /ɪn ðə ˈsɛnɚ ə ðə ˈrum/ &middot; *in-thuh-SEN-er-uh-thuh-ROOM*, since ordinary American speech drops the /t/ of *center* after the /n/
**Русский:** в центре, посередине — на равном расстоянии от краёв; в центральной части города
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral. **The American spelling is *center*; *centre* is British** and appears in American text only inside proper names (*Lincoln Centre* is a name, not a spelling choice).
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — *in the center of the table*, *in the center of it*. **No gerund and no clause**: ✗ *in the center of arguing about it*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand / place] *in the center of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in the center of* [noun phrase] &middot; *right / dead / smack in the center of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in the middle of* is the everyday word (see [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of)); *at the center of* is the figurative form; *in the heart of* is the warmer version for towns and districts
**Contrast:** **Two lines matter here.** The first is *in* against *at*: *in the center of the room* is a physical position, while *at the center of the investigation* is a role — the figurative sense takes *at*, and mixing them produces the odd ✗ *in the center of the scandal*. The second is *center* against *middle*: [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of) is vaguer and makes no claim about equal distance from the edges, so a table can be in the middle of a room without being in its center, and only *middle* works for stretches of time (*in the middle of the night*, not ✗ *in the center of the night*). Against [amid](#amid) and [among](#among), which say "surrounded by" and say nothing about the geometric middle.

At the point equally distant from the edges of something. It is the precise member of the middle family and it earns that precision honestly — a florist, a surveyor, and a page designer all mean the same thing by it. Its second sense, the busiest part of a town, is a mild extension of the same geometry and is the one American speech uses most.

**1. At the point equally distant from the edges of.** She set the cake *in the center of* the table.
**2. In the busiest or most built-up part of a place.** Their office is *in the center of* town.

1. There is a drain *in the center of* the garage floor. *(1)*
2. Put the fold line exactly *in the center of* the page. *(1)*
3. A fountain stands *in the center of* the plaza. *(1)*
4. He found a spot right *in the center of* the third row. *(1)*
5. Parking is impossible *in the center of* the city on a weekday. *(2)*
6. The hotel is *in the center of* the historic district. *(2)*
7. Figurative, and therefore *at* rather than *in*: she was *at the center of* the whole negotiation. *(2)*

**See also:** [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of), [amid](#amid), [among](#among), [within](#within), [inside](#inside), [throughout](#throughout)

### inside

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈsaɪd/ &middot; *in-SYD*; **stress distinguishes the parts of speech**: preposition and adverb /ɪnˈsaɪd/ *in-SYD*, noun and attributive adjective /ˈɪnsaɪd/ *IN-syd* (*an **in**side job*)
**Русский:** внутри, в — в пределах вместилища; внутри организации, в отличие от посторонних
**Type:** compound (*in* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*come inside*), a noun (*the inside of the lid*), and an adjective (*inside information*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *inside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb of motion] *inside* [noun phrase] &middot; *inside of* [period of time] &middot; stranded: *the box it was* **inside**
**Variants:** inside of — informal in the spatial sense (drop the *of* in edited prose); **idiomatic and standard in the time sense**, *inside of an hour*
**Contrast:** Against [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in): *in* is the neutral container word, *inside* insists on the enclosure and implies a contrasting outside. Both work for *in / inside the box*, but only *in* can go abstract — *in trouble*, *in love*, *in a hurry* have no *inside* version. Against [within](#within): *inside* is physical enclosure, *within* is limits and abstractions (*within the law*, not *inside the law*). And against [into](04-movement-direction.md#into): *inside* is where something is, *into* is where it is going. The parent pair splits the same way — ***in* is position, *into* is motion** (*she was **in** the office* / *she walked **into** the office*) — with a short list of fixed phrases where *in* survives with a motion verb (*put it in the drawer*, *throw it in the trash*).

Within the bounds of a container, a building, or a boundary, with the boundary itself in the picture. It is the emphatic counterpart of *in* and the exact opposite of *outside*, and it keeps that contrast in every sense — a physical interior, the interior of an organization, and, in one informal American use, the interior of a stretch of time.

**1. Within the bounds of a container or enclosed space.** The receipt is *inside* the envelope.
**2. Within an organization, as opposed to outside it.** The leak came from someone *inside* the department.
**3. In less than a stated period of time (informal, usually *inside of*).** They had it running again *inside of* an hour.

1. Everything you need is *inside* the blue folder. *(1)*
2. Wait *inside* the building until they call your name. *(1)*
3. Sources *inside* the agency describe a very different timeline. *(2)*
4. The decision was made *inside* the family, not by the board. *(2)*
5. The whole repair was done *inside of* twenty minutes. *(3)*
6. If we leave now we'll be there *inside of* two hours. *(3)*

**See also:** [outside](#outside), [within](#within), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [inside of](11-complex-multiword.md#inside-of), [aboard](#aboard)

### outside

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd/ &middot; *owt-SYD*; **stress distinguishes the parts of speech**: preposition and adverb /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd/ *owt-SYD*, noun and attributive adjective /ˈaʊtˌsaɪd/ *OWT-syd* (*the **out**side lane*)
**Русский:** снаружи, вне, за пределами — вне границ; вне компетенции; кроме (outside of)
**Type:** compound (*out* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the "apart from" sense only**, and there almost always with *of* — *outside of being tired, I'm fine*. **No clause of any kind**, and never a *that*-clause. Also an adverb (*wait outside*), a noun (*the outside of the house*), and an adjective (*an outside chance*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *outside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *outside* [noun phrase] &middot; *outside of* [noun phrase] &middot; *outside* [abstract limit] (*outside the law*)
**Variants:** outside of — extremely common in American speech; **edited prose drops the *of*** for the spatial and scope senses, but keeps it for the "apart from" sense, where *besides* or *apart from* is cleaner still
**Contrast:** The mirror image of [inside](#inside), and it inherits the same rules: physical and scope senses only, with the abstractions handed to other words. On ***outside* vs. *outside of***: American speech uses both freely, but editors delete the *of* in *he waited outside the courthouse*. The "apart from" sense (*outside of a few complaints, it went fine*) is the one place the *of* is normal — and it is also the place where [apart from](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from) or [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides) reads better.

Beyond the bounds of something — a building, a boundary, a jurisdiction, or a scope. The physical sense is the base; the scope sense (*outside my expertise*, *outside the law*) is where the word does most of its work in adult prose, because it draws a limit and puts something firmly past it.

**1. Beyond the physical bounds of.** He was waiting *outside* the courthouse.
**2. Beyond the scope, limits, or authority of.** That question is *outside* my expertise.
**3. Apart from, other than (usually *outside of*).** *Outside of* a cracked screen, the phone is fine.

1. There were reporters *outside* the building all afternoon. *(1)*
2. The house sits just *outside* the city limits. *(1)*
3. Anything over a hundred pounds is *outside* the elevator's rating. *(2)*
4. Enforcement of that rule is *outside* our authority. *(2)*
5. *Outside of* the opening chapter, the book holds up. *(3)*
6. *Outside of* Tuesday, I'm free all week. *(3)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [beyond](#beyond), [without](#without), [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of), [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides)

### within

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɪn/ &middot; *with-IN*; the *th* is **voiced** /ð/, as in *this*, not the /θ/ of *thin*; weak /wəˈðɪn/ &middot; *wuh-THIN*
**Русский:** внутри, в пределах — в границах области, диапазона или срока
**Type:** compound (*with* + *in*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund** — write *within the limits that the statute sets*, never *within the statute sets*. Also an adverb (*inquire within*), which is the form on old shop signs.
**Pattern:** *within* [bounded area] &middot; *within* [distance or measure] &middot; *within* [period of time] &middot; *within* [organization or person]
**Contrast:** **The heart of this entry is *in* versus *within*, and in the time sense the difference has legal force.** *In three days* means three days from now; *within three days* means at any point before three days have elapsed — a deadline, not a date. Spatially, [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) locates and *within* bounds: *in the city* says where you are, *within the city limits* says which side of a line you are on. Against [inside](#inside): *inside* is physical enclosure, *within* is limits, ranges, and abstractions. The temporal sense is developed further in [time](05-time.md#within).

Inside the limits of something. The limits are the point: *within* always implies a boundary you have not crossed, whether that boundary is a city line, a budget, a tolerance, a deadline, or the edge of an organization. It is the preposition of not-exceeding, and that is why contracts, specifications, and regulations lean on it so heavily.

**1. Inside the bounds of an area.** The house is *within* the historic district.
**2. Inside a range or a measured limit.** The part came in *within* a thousandth of an inch.
**3. Before a stated period has elapsed (see also [time](05-time.md#within)).** Respond *within* ten business days.
**4. Inside a person, a group, or an organization.** There is real disagreement *within* the committee.

1. Everything *within* the fence line belongs to the county. *(1)*
2. The site is *within* a mile of the river. *(1)*
3. The project finished *within* budget by about four percent. *(2)*
4. Keep the reading *within* tolerance or the batch is rejected. *(2)*
5. Payment is due *within* thirty days of invoice. *(3)*
6. She had three offers *within* a week of posting her resume. *(3)*
7. The split *within* the party is wider than it looks. *(4)*
8. He kept the anger *within* himself for years. *(4)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [among](#among), [throughout](#throughout), [within (time)](05-time.md#within)

### without

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/ &middot; *with-OWT*; the *th* is **voiced** /ð/; weak /wəˈðaʊt/ &middot; *wuh-THOWT*
**Русский:** вне, за пределами — устар., книжн. в этом пространственном значении: вне городских стен
**Type:** compound (*with* + *out*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic or literary in this spatial sense**; the modern "lacking" sense is fully neutral and lives in [accompaniment and exception](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without)
**Complement:** in the spatial sense, a noun phrase only — *without the walls*, *without the gates*. **No clause, no gerund** in this sense. Also an adverb in the fixed pairing *within and without*.
**Pattern:** *without* [walls / gates / city] (archaic spatial) &middot; *within and without* (fixed adverbial pair)
**Contrast:** This entry covers only the **obsolete sense "outside of,"** which is the exact opposite of [within](#within) and is the reason the two words are built from the same halves. In living American English that job belongs entirely to [outside](#outside); *without the city* now reads as a Bible verse or a history book, and a modern reader will hear the "lacking" sense instead and be confused. **The productive modern sense — *without money*, *without asking* — is treated in [accompaniment and exception](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without) and is not repeated here.**

Outside of, beyond the bounds of. This was once the ordinary opposite of *within*, and the two words still show their shared architecture. It survives in scripture, in older legal descriptions of land, in hymn and poetry, and in the frozen pair *within and without*. Recognize it; do not write it.

**1. Archaic: outside of, beyond the walls or bounds of.** The camp lay *without* the city gates.
**2. In the fixed adverbial pair *within and without*: inside and outside alike.** The house was repainted *within and without*.

1. The old charter refers to the parishes *without* the walls. *(1)*
2. They buried him *without* the churchyard, as the custom then required. *(1)*
3. A green hill far away, *without* a city wall. *(1)*
4. The ship was rebuilt *within and without*. *(2)*
5. She was calm *within and without*, whatever it cost her. *(2)*

**See also:** [outside](#outside), [within](#within), [beyond](#beyond), [without (lacking)](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without), [betwixt](#betwixt)


## Front, back, and beyond

The axis running away from the observer. American English has largely handed the front position to *in front of* and kept *before* for courtrooms and ceremony; at the back, *behind* is doing all the work, with the regional *in back of* alongside it. Farther out, *past* is reckoned from a landmark and *beyond* from a limit, and *this side of* is the near-side counterpart of both. The nautical pair *abaft* and *astern of* cuts the same axis aboard a vessel and in the water behind one.

### abaft

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbæft/ &middot; *uh-BAFT* &middot; also /əˈbɑft/ &middot; *uh-BAHFT* in older American and in British speech; two syllables, stress on the second, and the first is already the reduced /ə/, so there is **no further weak form**
**Русский:** позади, ближе к корме, чем — мор. термин; за пределами флота устар.
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *baft*, itself Old English *be* + *æftan* "behind") &middot; **Register:** **nautical technical, and archaic everywhere else.** It is live, ordinary, and unremarkable in seamanship, naval writing, sailing instruction, and marine survey; outside that world it is costume, and a reader will hear it as pirate diction rather than as English.
**Complement:** noun phrase — in practice a part of a vessel or a bearing: *abaft the beam*, *abaft the mainmast*, *abaft the bridge*. **No gerund, no clause.** It is also an **adverb** (*the wind hauled abaft*), which takes no object; that is the form most often heard on deck.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *abaft* [part of a vessel] &middot; [noun] *abaft* [part of a vessel] &middot; *abaft the beam* &middot; *well abaft* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **The pair to learn is *abaft* against [astern of](#astern-of), and the difference is whether you are on the ship.** *Abaft* is a position **on board**, farther toward the stern than the thing named — the galley can be *abaft* the mast. *Astern of* is a position **in the water behind** the whole vessel — the tender lies *astern of* the yacht. Its live opposite is *afore* (see [afore](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#afore)), "farther forward than," which survives in the same nautical corner and nowhere else. Against [behind](#behind), the ordinary word, which knows nothing about bows and sterns and is what a passenger would say.

Farther toward the stern than something else, aboard a vessel. The word divides a ship along its length and is precise about it: everything between the reference point and the stern is *abaft* that point. Its one non-shipboard use is the bearing phrase *abaft the beam*, which cuts the horizon in half at right angles to the keel and puts anything in the rear half *abaft the beam* — the phrase a lookout, a navigator, or a racing sailor actually uses.

**1. Farther toward the stern of a vessel than.** The engine room is *abaft* the bulkhead.
**2. In the bearing phrase abaft the beam: in the rear half of the horizon as seen from the ship.** The wind was two points *abaft* the beam.
**3. Adverb, with no object: toward the stern.** The wind hauled *abaft* during the night.

1. The crew quarters are *abaft* the mainmast. *(1)*
2. Everything *abaft* the funnel was damaged in the fire. *(1)*
3. Stow the spare anchor *abaft* the cockpit. *(1)*
4. A tanker showed up broad on the quarter, well *abaft* the beam. *(2)*
5. With the breeze *abaft* the beam she made eight knots easily. *(2)*
6. Adverb, no object, and therefore not this preposition: the sea was running *abaft*. *(3)*
7. Ashore the same idea is simply *behind*: *the storeroom behind the office*, not ✗ *abaft the office*. *(1)*

**See also:** [astern of](#astern-of), [behind](#behind), [aboard](#aboard), [afore](13-formal-latin-archaic.md#afore), [past](#past), [beyond](#beyond)

### astern of

**Pronunciation:** /əˈstɚn əv/ &middot; *uh-STURN-uv* &middot; **weak form** — the first syllable is the reduced /ə/ and *of* drops to /ə/ before a consonant, so *astern of the ferry* is /əˈstɚn ə ðə ˈfɛri/ &middot; *uh-STUR-nuh-thuh-FAIR-ee*. The stressed vowel is the r-colored /ɚ/ of *stern*
**Русский:** за кормой, позади — мор.: следом за другим судном
**Type:** complex (*a-* + *stern* + *of*) &middot; **Register:** **nautical technical.** Neutral and ordinary inside sailing, shipping, racing, and naval writing; outside that field it is archaic-sounding and a general reader will substitute *behind* without thinking.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — another vessel, a buoy, a mark, a landmark. **No gerund, no clause.** **Bare *astern* is an adverb** (*the wake astern*, *slow astern*, *look astern*) and takes no object at all; the adverb is far commoner than this preposition and is what is heard on a bridge.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / lie / fall / drop] *astern of* [vessel] &middot; [noun] *astern of* [vessel] &middot; *a mile astern of* [vessel]
**Variants:** *aft of* is the common American yachting alternative and is drifting toward *abaft*'s job as well; *behind* is the plain word for a non-nautical reader
**Contrast:** Against [abaft](#abaft), the pair to learn: *abaft* is on board, *astern of* is outside the hull in the water behind it. Against [behind](#behind), which covers both and cares about neither. Against [past](#past) and [beyond](#beyond), which put a thing on the far side of a landmark rather than in trail of a moving vessel. And note that the racing sense — one boat *astern of* another — is about order in a fleet, so it competes with *behind* in the standings sense of that word.

Behind a vessel, in the water it has passed through. The word is *stern* with the old *a-* prefix, and adding *of* is what turns the adverb into a preposition and gives it an object. Two uses account for nearly all of it: the physical one, where a tow, a tender, or a following ship lies *astern of* something, and the competitive one, where a boat that has lost ground is *astern of* the leader.

**1. Behind, in the water behind another vessel.** The barge rode a hundred yards *astern of* the tug.
**2. Trailing another vessel in a race, a line, or a convoy.** She finished two minutes *astern of* the leader.

1. The tender lay *astern of* the yacht all afternoon. *(1)*
2. Keep well *astern of* the dredge until it signals. *(1)*
3. A second freighter came up half a mile *astern of* the first. *(1)*
4. The fleet strung out for three miles *astern of* the committee boat. *(2)*
5. He rounded the mark *astern of* two boats he had beaten all week. *(2)*
6. Adverb, no object, and therefore not this preposition: the harbor fell *astern* by noon. *(1)*
7. For a general reader the sentence is simply *the barge was behind the tug*. *(1)*

**See also:** [abaft](#abaft), [behind](#behind), [past](#past), [beyond](#beyond), [aboard](#aboard), [alongside](#alongside)

### before

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *bih-FOR*; weak /bəˈfɔr/ &middot; *buh-FOR*, and in fast speech /bfɔr/ with the first vowel gone entirely
**Русский:** перед, пред — книжн., юр.: перед лицом суда; раньше в порядке следования
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *fore*) &middot; **Register:** **formal, legal, ceremonial, or literary in the spatial sense**; the temporal sense is neutral and lives in [time](05-time.md#before)
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **In the spatial sense there is no gerund** — *before leaving* is the temporal *before*, not this one. **Never a finite clause after the preposition**; *before he arrived* is the subordinating conjunction, a different word class with the same spelling.
**Pattern:** [stand / appear / kneel] *before* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *before* [noun phrase] (*the task before us*) &middot; [X] comes *before* [Y] (order)
**Contrast:** **In modern American English the ordinary spatial word is [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), not *before*.** *He parked in front of the house* is what people say; *before the house* sounds like a nineteenth-century novel. Spatial *before* survives in three places: courtrooms and hearings (*the case before the judge*), ceremony and scripture (*before God*), and the metaphor of a task or a choice facing someone (*the decision before us*). Watch the word-class trap too — *before* is a preposition with a noun phrase, a **conjunction** with a clause, and an **adverb** with nothing (*I've been here before*).

In front of, in the physical or figurative presence of. The spatial sense is the older one and has been largely displaced in speech by *in front of*, retreating into formal registers where its stiffness is an asset. Two live uses remain fully current: appearing *before* an authority, and a task or choice standing *before* a person.

**1. In front of, in the presence of (formal).** She stood *before* the judge.
**2. Earlier in a sequence or order.** *E* comes *before* *F*.
**3. Facing someone as a task, choice, or prospect.** The question *before* the committee is simple.

1. The witness appeared *before* the grand jury on Thursday. *(1)*
2. He knelt *before* the altar. *(1)*
3. In the alphabet, *M* comes *before* *N*. *(2)*
4. Safety comes *before* speed on this crew. *(2)*
5. The choice *before* us is not a comfortable one. *(3)*
6. A long winter lay *before* them. *(3)*

**See also:** [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [behind](#behind), [opposite](#opposite), [ahead of](11-complex-multiword.md#ahead-of), [before (time)](05-time.md#before)

### behind

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈhaɪnd/ &middot; *bih-HYND*; weak /bəˈhaɪnd/ &middot; *buh-HYND*; the /h/ can drop in very fast speech, giving /bəˈaɪnd/
**Русский:** за, позади — с задней стороны; отставая; стоящий за чем-то — как поддержка или причина
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *hind*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the cause sense** (*the thinking behind closing the plant*, *the money behind building the arena*). **No finite clause** — write *behind the decision to close*, never ✗ *behind they decided to close*, and never a *that*-clause. Also an adverb (*he stayed behind*) and a noun (informal); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *behind* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *behind* [noun phrase] &middot; *behind* [schedule / on rent] &middot; *behind* [person] (support) &middot; stranded: *the door he was hiding* **behind**
**Variants:** in back of, and bare back of — genuinely American alternatives for the spatial sense, common in speech and regionally strong. *In back of the house* is the wider of the two; bare *back of the barn* is Midland, Southern, and rural, and is the older form. Both are unknown in British English and both are avoided in formal writing, where *behind* is always available
**Contrast:** Against [after](05-time.md#after): *after* orders things in time or sequence, *behind* places them in space or measures a lag. *He arrived after me* is sequence; *he finished behind me* is a gap. Against [beyond](#beyond) and [past](#past), which put something on the far side of a thing rather than at its back. Note also the American variants **in back of** and bare **back of**, which are standard in speech and jarring to British ears, and the temporal sense (*behind schedule*), developed in [time](05-time.md#behind).

At the back of something, on the side away from the front or from the observer. From that position the word picks up four extensions that all preserve the geometry: falling back in a race or a schedule, standing back of someone in support, working back of a public event as its hidden cause, and putting an experience back of you in the past.

**1. At the back of, on the far side of.** The trash cans are *behind* the garage.
**2. Later or slower than; in arrears (see also [time](05-time.md#behind)).** We're two weeks *behind* schedule.
**3. Supporting, backing.** The whole department is *behind* her.
**4. Responsible for, as the hidden cause or agent.** No one knows who was *behind* the leak.
**5. In the past, left behind.** All that is *behind* him now.

1. There is a small yard *behind* the building. *(1)*
2. She hid the box *behind* a stack of paint cans. *(1)*
3. The Rangers finished nine games *behind* the leaders. *(2)*
4. He fell *behind* on rent after the layoff. *(2)*
5. The union is solidly *behind* the proposal. *(3)*
6. Get *behind* this or get out of the way. *(3)*
7. Investigators still do not know who was *behind* the transfer. *(4)*
8. There is a real grievance *behind* all the shouting. *(4)*
9. Those years are *behind* her and she does not discuss them. *(5)*
10. Once the audit is *behind* us we can hire again. *(5)*

**See also:** [before](#before), [beyond](#beyond), [past](#past), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [behind (time)](05-time.md#behind)

### beyond

**Pronunciation:** /biˈɑnd/ &middot; *bee-AHND*; also /bɪˈjɑnd/ &middot; *bih-YAHND* and /bɪˈjɔnd/ *bih-YAWND*; weak /bəˈjɑnd/ &middot; *buh-YAHND*
**Русский:** за, по ту сторону — дальше чего-либо; позже; вне пределов; сверх, больше чем
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *yond*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (*beyond saving*, *beyond repairing*), or a **wh-clause** (*beyond what she told us*). **Never a *that*-clause** — *beyond that she told us* is ungrammatical. Also an adverb (*the hills beyond*) and a noun (*the great beyond*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *beyond* [noun phrase] &middot; *beyond* [abstract limit] (*beyond repair*) &middot; *beyond* [verb-ing] &middot; *beyond* [wh-clause] &middot; *go beyond* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against [past](#past): *past* is close and dynamic — *just past the bank* is a landmark you have almost reached; *beyond* is static and farther out, and it dominates the abstract senses that *past* barely touches. Against [outside](#outside): both draw a limit, but *outside* draws a boundary around a category (*outside my expertise*), while *beyond* draws a threshold something has exceeded (*beyond my ability*). One grammatical point worth its own line: *beyond* is one of the prepositions that takes a **wh-clause**, which is why *beyond what he admitted* is fine while *beyond that he admitted* is not.

On the far side of something, and by extension past any limit at all. It is one of the most abstractly productive prepositions in English: once the literal sense of "farther than" is in place, everything that can have a limit — repair, belief, control, expectation, dispute — can be *beyond* it. The frozen phrases (*beyond repair*, *beyond belief*, *beyond doubt*) are worth learning as units.

**1. On the far side of; farther away than.** The hills *beyond* the river are all national forest.
**2. Later than a point in time.** The party went on well *beyond* midnight.
**3. Outside the limits, scope, or reach of.** The damage was *beyond* repair.
**4. More than; surpassing.** The response went *beyond* anything we expected.
**5. Apart from, other than (usually in a negative).** We know nothing *beyond* what the letter says.

1. There is nothing *beyond* the last cattle guard but sagebrush. *(1)*
2. The trail continues a half mile *beyond* the falls. *(1)*
3. Deliveries do not run *beyond* six o'clock. *(2)*
4. He stayed *beyond* his welcome by about two hours. *(2)*
5. The engine was *beyond* saving. *(3)*
6. That decision is *beyond* my authority. *(3)*
7. Her generosity went *beyond* anything the family asked for. *(4)*
8. The results exceeded the forecast *beyond* all reason. *(4)*
9. *Beyond* a short statement, the company has said nothing. *(5)*
10. I remember nothing *beyond* what I already told the officer. *(5)*

**See also:** [past](#past), [outside](#outside), [behind](#behind), [across](#across), [above](#above)

### past

**Pronunciation:** /pæst/ &middot; *PAST*; one syllable, full vowel; no weak form, though in connected speech the final /t/ drops before a consonant, giving /pæs/ &middot; *PASS* (*past the store*). Homophonous with *passed*, which — helped by that /t/-dropping — is the source of the commonest spelling error involving this word.
**Русский:** мимо, за — дальше указанного ориентира; за пределом возраста или стадии
**Type:** simple (from the old participle of *pass*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund only in a short frozen list** — *past caring*, *past saving*, *past praying for* — which is not a productive pattern (✗ *past finishing the report*). **No clause of any kind.** Also an adverb (*he walked past*), an adjective (*the past year*), and a noun (*in the past*) — **only the preposition has an object**, which is the test: *he walked **past*** is the adverb, *he walked **past the church*** is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *past* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *past* [landmark] &middot; *just past* [noun phrase] &middot; *past* [abstract limit] (*past caring*) &middot; *put it past* [someone]
**Contrast:** **The spelling trap first: *passed* is the verb, *past* is everything else.** *We passed the church* (verb) but *we drove past the church* (preposition) and *the mailbox is past the church* (preposition). If you can put *did* in front of it, you want *passed*. Against [beyond](#beyond): *past* is near and reckoned from a landmark you have just cleared, *beyond* is farther out and far more abstract. Note that *past* has three homes here: this positional entry, the motion sense in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#past), and the clock sense (*ten past four*) in [time](05-time.md#past).

On the far side of a landmark, reckoned from it. The positional sense is essentially how Americans give directions — everything is *past* the light, *past* the bank, *just past* the second stop sign — and it depends on a shared reference point in a way *beyond* does not. The same geometry then runs along scales instead of streets (*past forty*, *past its expiration date*), and a short frozen list (*past caring*, *past saving*, *wouldn't put it past her*) preserves the oldest sense of having gone beyond a limit for good.

**1. On the far side of a landmark; farther along than.** The clinic is two blocks *past* the light.
**2. Beyond a point on a scale, an age, or a stage of life.** That pitcher is *past* his prime.
**3. Beyond the point of, in the frozen gerund and idiom set.** He is *past* caring what they think.

1. The mailbox is just *past* the church on the right. *(1)*
2. Their driveway is a hundred yards *past* the cattle guard. *(1)*
3. Go *past* the second stop sign and it's on your left. *(1)*
4. That pitcher is *past* his prime and everyone knows it. *(2)*
5. The milk is well *past* its date. *(2)*
6. She was *past* fifty before she learned to swim. *(2)*
7. He is *past* caring what the committee thinks. *(3)*
8. The transmission was *past* saving by the time they looked at it. *(3)*
9. I wouldn't put it *past* her to call the mayor herself. *(3)*

**See also:** [beyond](#beyond), [behind](#behind), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [past (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#past), [past (time)](05-time.md#past)

### this side of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɪs ˌsaɪd əv/ &middot; *THISS-syd-uv* &middot; the initial *th* is the **voiced** /ð/ of *this*, not the /θ/ of *thick*; **weak form** — *of* reduces to /ə/ before a consonant, so *this side of the river* is /ˈðɪs saɪd ə ðə ˈrɪvɚ/ &middot; *THISS-syd-uh-thuh-RIV-er*, and in the idioms the whole phrase compresses to one beat on *side*
**Русский:** по эту сторону от — ближе границы; до (срока); почти, чуть не (this side of rude)
**Type:** complex (determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the spatial sense; **the superlative and "just short of" senses are idiomatic and informal**, and belong to speech and to writing with a voice
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun in senses 1 to 3. **No gerund and no clause.** Sense 4 is the exception worth knowing: there the complement is characteristically **an adjective or an adjective-like noun** — *just this side of legal*, *this side of rude*, *this side of a miracle* — which is the one place the phrase escapes the noun-phrase rule, and it is a frozen idiom rather than a productive pattern.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *this side of* [landmark] &middot; [noun] *this side of* [landmark] &middot; *the best* [noun] *this side of* [place] &middot; *just this side of* [adjective]
**Variants:** *on this side of* is the fuller and equally standard form for the spatial sense; *the other side of* and *the far side of* are the converses; *that side of* takes the speaker's viewpoint away
**Contrast:** Against [beyond](#beyond) and [past](#past), its two opposites: *this side of the river* is nearer than the river, *beyond the river* and *past the river* are farther. Against [before](#before), which orders things in a sequence rather than placing them on a map. For sense 4, against [short of](11-complex-multiword.md#short-of), the closest paraphrase — *just this side of rude* and *just short of rude* say the same thing, and *short of* is the one to use in writing that has no voice to spare. The time sense is developed in [time](05-time.md).

On the nearer side of a line, seen from where the speaker is. Everything else the phrase does grows out of that: a point in time can have a nearer side (*this side of Christmas*), a region can be treated as a boundary for the sake of a boast (*the best pie this side of the Mississippi*), and a quality can be approached without being reached (*just this side of legal*). The viewpoint is always the speaker's, which is why the phrase is useless in writing that has not established where the speaker stands.

**1. On the nearer side of a boundary, river, or line.** Everything *this side of* the ridge drains into the bay.
**2. Before a point in time.** We will not have an answer *this side of* the new year.
**3. In the boasting frame: anywhere nearer than a famous or distant place.** The best barbecue *this side of* Memphis.
**4. Falling just short of a quality or a limit.** His answer was *just this side of* rude.

1. The county line runs *this side of* the creek, not past it. *(1)*
2. There is not a hardware store *this side of* the interstate. *(1)*
3. Nothing is getting shipped *this side of* Monday. *(2)*
4. She will not be back *this side of* the holidays. *(2)*
5. It is the best diner *this side of* the Rockies, according to the sign. *(3)*
6. That contract is *just this side of* legal. *(4)*
7. The whole plan is *this side of* a miracle, and it worked. *(4)*

**See also:** [beyond](#beyond), [past](#past), [before](#before), [behind](#behind), [short of](11-complex-multiword.md#short-of), [outside](#outside)


## Extent, spread, and orientation

Position described by how something lies across a space rather than by a single point. *Across* cuts a line, *along* follows one, *around* encircles, *astride* straddles, and *throughout* fills. The four short words in this section — *down*, *off*, *out*, *up* — are the ones most often mistaken for adverbs, and the test is always the same: the preposition has an object it cannot be separated from. The orientation phrases *to the left of*, *to the right of*, *at the corner of*, and *at the edge of* belong here as well, and so does *over to*, the one item in this file that most grammars would rather analyze as an adverb followed by a preposition.

### across

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkrɔs/ &middot; *uh-KRAWS*; also /əˈkrɑs/ &middot; *uh-KRAHS* in cot/caught-merged speech; the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form; **the spelling and pronunciation *acrosst* are nonstandard**
**Русский:** через, поперёк; по ту сторону — с одной стороны на другую; по всей площади или группе
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *cross*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*he swam across*); the preposition takes an object. In the "facing" sense the object attaches to *from*: *across **from** the school*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *across* [noun phrase] (spanning) &middot; *across from* [noun phrase] (facing) &middot; *across* [area or group] (distribution) &middot; [be-verb] *across* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** acrost, acrosst — dialectal and nonstandard; never write them
**Contrast:** Against [through](04-movement-direction.md#through): *across* treats its object as a **two-dimensional surface** you cover the face of, *through* treats it as a three-dimensional medium you pass inside — *across the field*, *through the woods*. Against [along](#along): *across* cuts a line, *along* follows it. Contact is optional and the senses split on it: sense 1 usually has none (*a bridge **across** the canyon*), sense 4 always has it (*arms folded **across** his chest*). And note the Americanism that matters most here: ***across from*** is the everyday American way to say "facing," where British English uses [opposite](#opposite). The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#across).

Spanning something from one side to the other, or positioned on the far side of it. The static senses collected here are what is left when the motion drains out of the word: a bridge that has already crossed, a scar that already spans a cheek, a store that already sits on the other side of the street. The third sense — distribution over a whole area or population — is now heavy in business and news prose.

**1. Extending from one side to the other; spanning.** A steel bridge *across* the canyon.
**2. On the other side of; facing (usually *across from*).** The deli is *across from* the courthouse.
**3. Distributed over the whole of an area or group.** Enrollment fell *across* every district.
**4. Lying transversely on something, in contact with it.** A blanket *across* her knees.

1. There is a fallen pine *across* the driveway. *(1)*
2. A long scar ran *across* the back of his hand. *(1)*
3. They live *across* the street from us. *(2)*
4. Her office is *across* the hall. *(2)*
5. Costs rose *across* all six divisions. *(3)*
6. The policy applies *across* the entire system. *(3)*
7. He listened with his arms folded *across* his chest. *(4)*
8. She had a wool blanket *across* her knees for the whole drive. *(4)*

**See also:** [along](#along), [across from](#across-from), [opposite](#opposite), [throughout](#throughout), [astride](#astride), [across (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#across)

### along

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAWNG*; also /əˈlɑŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAHNG* in cot/caught-merged speech; the first syllable is always /ə/
**Русский:** вдоль, по — следуя длине чего-либо; где-то на протяжении; перен. на некоем отрезке пути
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *long*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — and the object must be **something with length**: a road, a river, a wall, a shelf, a route. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb and verb particle (*come along*, *bring it along*, *move along*), where it has no object at all.
**Pattern:** [noun] *along* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *along* [linear object] &middot; *somewhere along* [linear object] &middot; *all along* [linear object]
**Contrast:** **The object has to be long.** *Along the fence* works; *along the room* does not, because a room has no line to follow — and the figurative objects obey the same rule, since a *way*, a *line*, a *spectrum*, and a *continuum* are all lines. Against [across](#across), which cuts the line rather than following it — *walk **along** the beach* is parallel to the water, *walk **across** the beach* runs from the water to the dunes. Against [alongside](#alongside), which requires two separate parallel things: you can walk *along* a wall while touching it, but you walk *alongside* someone. Watch the particle too: in *bring it along*, *along* has no object and is not a preposition.

Following the length of something, or located at some point on that length. The senses are really one geometry seen at different scales — the whole line, a spot on it, or a line that exists only in the mind. What unites every use is that the object must be linear; the moment it is not, the sentence stops working.

**1. In a line following the length of something.** Cottonwoods grow *along* the creek.
**2. At an unspecified point on the length of something.** The leak is somewhere *along* this wall.
**3. At a point on a notional line, scale, or continuum (figurative).** Somewhere *along* the way we stopped asking.

1. There are streetlights *along* the whole east side of the park. *(1)*
2. Fenceposts run *along* the property line for a mile. *(1)*
3. She lined the books up *along* the top shelf. *(1)*
4. Somewhere *along* Route 6 we missed the turn. *(2)*
5. The pipe froze at a joint *along* the north wall. *(2)*
6. The trail loses elevation gradually all *along* the ridge. *(2)*
7. The new proposal is *along* the same lines as last year's. *(3)*
8. Somewhere *along* the way the project lost half its budget. *(3)*

**See also:** [alongside](#alongside), [across](#across), [around](#around), [down](#down), [along (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#along)

### around

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *uh-ROWND*; in casual speech the first syllable can drop entirely, giving /ˈraʊnd/ &middot; *ROWND* (often written *'round*); the full form is /əˈraʊnd/
**Русский:** вокруг, по — со всех сторон; повсюду в; за (углом); примерно (о числе)
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *round*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; also a bare number or clock time in the approximation sense (*around noon*, *around fifty*), and a **gerund** in the jargon sense 5 (*questions around hiring contractors*). **No clause, and never a *that*-clause.** Also an adverb (*look around*, *stick around*) and a particle; the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; *around* [number or time] &middot; *around here / there* &middot; *around the corner*
**Variants:** round — **chiefly British** for the physical senses; see its own entry
**Contrast:** **Use *around*, not *round*, in American English** for every physical sense: *around the corner*, *around the table*, *around the block*. For approximation, *around* and [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about) are both American and interchangeable, with *around* commoner in speech and *approximately* the formal choice. Sense 5 — *a conversation around funding* — is business jargon that has spread fast and that many editors still delete in favor of *about*. The motion sense (*drove around the lake*) belongs to [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#around), and the temporal approximation is treated in [time](05-time.md#around).

Encircling something, or scattered in its vicinity. From the circle come two useful extensions: getting to the far side by going around an obstruction, and — because a circle drawn around a number is a rough boundary rather than a point — approximation. It is one of the highest-frequency prepositions in American speech and one of the vaguest.

**1. Encircling; on all sides of.** There's a stone wall *around* the garden.
**2. Scattered in the vicinity of; here and there in.** There were toys *around* the whole living room.
**3. On the other side of, reached by going along a curve.** The bakery is *around* the corner.
**4. Approximately, with a number or a time (see also [time](05-time.md#around)).** *Around* fifty people came.
**5. Centered on a topic (business jargon; prefer *about*).** We need a conversation *around* staffing.

1. They put a fence *around* the pool last spring. *(1)*
2. Twelve chairs were set *around* the long table. *(1)*
3. His papers were scattered *around* the office. *(2)*
4. Is there a hardware store *around* here? *(2)*
5. The entrance is *around* the back of the building. *(3)*
6. Her place is just *around* the corner from mine. *(3)*
7. It cost *around* three hundred dollars. *(4)*
8. Let's say *around* seven, give or take. *(4)*
9. The whole meeting was structured *around* risk. *(5)*
10. There are real questions *around* the timeline. *(5)*

**See also:** [round](#round), [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about), [near](#near), [throughout](#throughout), [around (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#around)

### astride

**Pronunciation:** /əˈstraɪd/ &middot; *uh-STRYD*; the first syllable is always /ə/ and never stressed
**Русский:** верхом на; по обе стороны от — книжн.: ноги по обе стороны; по обе стороны линии
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *stride*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; common in journalism and military writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb (*she rode astride* — that is, not sidesaddle).
**Pattern:** [sit / ride / stand] *astride* [noun phrase] &middot; [place or thing] *astride* [line or route]
**Contrast:** *Astride* demands **two sides straddled**, which is exactly what [across](#across) does not require: a bridge is *across* a river because it spans it, but a city is *astride* a border because it has territory on both sides. [Over](#over) is the weaker word: it may cover a thing and even touch it (*a blanket **over** the rail*), but it never claims a leg or a side on each side of it, which is the entire content of *astride* (*sitting **astride** the rail*). And note the adverbial use, which carries a specific historical meaning in equestrian contexts — riding *astride* rather than sidesaddle.

With one leg, one side, or one part on each side of something. The literal sense is equestrian, and the figurative sense keeps the geometry exactly: a town *astride* a highway has buildings on both sides of it. Strategic and geopolitical writing likes the word because it implies control of what passes between.

**1. With a leg on each side of something.** She sat *astride* the fence rail.
**2. Extending on both sides of a line, route, or boundary.** The town sits *astride* the state line.

1. He rode *astride* a borrowed mare all afternoon. *(1)*
2. The child sat *astride* his father's shoulders. *(1)*
3. The village lies *astride* the old post road. *(2)*
4. The refinery sprawls *astride* the county line. *(2)*
5. A single garrison stood *astride* the only pass. *(2)*

**See also:** [across](#across), [over](#over), [atop](#atop), [alongside](#alongside), [athwart](04-movement-direction.md#athwart)

### at the corner of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈkɔrnɚ əv/ &middot; *at-thuh-KOR-ner-uv* &middot; **weak form** — everything but *cor-* is unstressed: *at* /ət/, *the* /ðə/, *of* /ə/ before a consonant. *At the corner of Fifth and Main* runs /ət ðə ˈkɔrnɚ ə ˈfɪfθ ən ˈmeɪn/ &middot; *uht-thuh-KOR-ner-uh-FIFTH-un-MAYN*, with *and* reduced all the way to /ən/
**Русский:** на углу — на пересечении улиц; в углу страницы, экрана или помещения
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral &mdash; **the standard American way to name a street intersection**, in speech, in directions, and in print
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically two street names joined by *and*, or a single noun naming a bounded surface. **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *at the corner of turning onto Main*.
**Pattern:** *at the corner of* [street] *and* [street] &middot; *at the corner of* [room / field / page / lot] &middot; [building] *on the corner of* [street] *and* [street]
**Variants:** **on the corner of** — used when the thing sits on the corner lot itself (*the drugstore on the corner of Ninth and Elm*); bare *at* plus the two street names (*at Ninth and Elm*) is the shortest and most idiomatic American form; *at the corner of* [street] *at* [street] is not English
**Contrast:** **Three prepositions front *the corner of* and they do three different jobs.** *At the corner of* names a point you can meet someone at or turn at. *On the corner of* puts a building on the corner lot. *In the corner of* puts a thing inside an angle — *in the corner of the room*, *in the corner of my eye*, *in the corner of the drawer* — and it is the only one of the three that requires an interior. A **street intersection therefore takes *at* or *on* and never *in***: ✗ *in the corner of Fifth and Main*. Against [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) generally, of which this is the sharpened form: *at Fifth and Main* is the point sense of *at*, and *at the corner of* just names the point out loud.

The place where two streets, two walls, or two edges meet. It is the American address-giving phrase, and it is worth an entry chiefly for the *at* / *on* / *in* split, which learners get wrong in both directions — putting a shop *in* an intersection or a stain *at* the corner of a rug. The rule underneath is the ordinary one for these three: *at* is a point, *on* is a surface, *in* is an interior.

**1. At a street intersection.** The bus stops *at the corner of* Twelfth and Vine.
**2. At the angle where two edges or walls of something meet.** There is a water stain *at the corner of* the ceiling.
**3. At the outer angle of a page, a card, or a screen.** The date is printed *at the corner of* the receipt.

1. Meet me *at the corner of* Fourth and Pine at six. *(1)*
2. The old hotel stood *at the corner of* Broadway and Ninth until 1978. *(1)*
3. There has been a light *at the corner of* Mill and Route 9 since the accident. *(1)*
4. A cot was set up *at the corner of* the gym nearest the door. *(2)*
5. The fence post *at the corner of* the lot has rotted through. *(2)*
6. Her initials are *at the corner of* every drawing. *(3)*
7. Compare the three: the deli sits *on the corner of* Ninth and Elm; meet me *at the corner of* Ninth and Elm; the mop is *in the corner of* the kitchen. *(1)*

**See also:** [at the edge of](#at-the-edge-of), [kitty-corner from](#kitty-corner-from), [across from](#across-from), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in)

### at the edge of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ði ˈɛdʒ əv/ &middot; *at-thee-EJ-uv* &middot; note the article — ***the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *edge***, not the /ðə/ it takes before a consonant. **Weak form** — *at* reduces to /ət/ and *of* keeps its /v/ before a following vowel but drops to /ə/ before a consonant, so *at the edge of the field* is /ət ði ˈɛdʒ ə ðə ˈfild/ &middot; *uht-thee-EJ-uh-thuh-FEELD*
**Русский:** на краю, у края — на границе участка, у обрыва; перен. на грани (on the edge of)
**Type:** complex (*at* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **A gerund is possible only in the *on the edge of* variant, and only in the figurative sense**: *on the edge of quitting*, *on the edge of losing the house*. ✗ *At the edge of quitting* is not English. **No clause of any kind.**
**Pattern:** [be-verb / stand / stop / build] *at the edge of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at the edge of* [noun phrase] &middot; *on the edge of* [state or event] &middot; *on the edge of* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** **on the edge of** — interchangeable with *at the edge of* for the literal senses (*a house at / on the edge of the woods*), and **the only one of the two that carries the figurative sense** of being close to a state: *on the edge of collapse*, *on the edge of tears*, *on the edge of a breakthrough*. The seat idiom is fixed with *on*: *on the edge of your seat*, never ✗ *at the edge of your seat*.
**Contrast:** Against [on the verge of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-the-verge-of), the figurative twin and the more formal of the two — *on the verge of bankruptcy* is what the filing says, *on the edge of bankruptcy* what the interview says. Against [outside](#outside) and [beyond](#beyond): something at the edge of an area is **still inside it**, which is exactly what makes the phrase useful — a cabin *at the edge of* the forest is in the forest, a cabin *outside* the forest is not. Against [near](#near), which places a thing without reference to any boundary, and against *at the end of*, which needs a length or a sequence rather than an area.

At the outer boundary of an area or the rim of a surface — and, in the *on* variant, at the boundary of a condition. The literal sense is doing quiet geographical work all through American description: towns sit at the edge of counties, houses at the edge of woods, ice at the edge of a pond. The figurative sense is one of the language's main ways of saying that something has not happened yet but is about to.

**1. At the outer boundary of an area.** They built the barn *at the edge of* the field.
**2. At the rim of a surface or a drop.** She stood *at the edge of* the platform and looked down.
**3. On the edge of: close to a state, an event, or an emotion.** The company was *on the edge of* collapse.
**4. In the fixed idiom on the edge of one's seat.** The last ten minutes had us *on the edge of* our seats.

1. A row of cottonwoods grows *at the edge of* the pasture. *(1)*
2. Their subdivision sits *at the edge of* the county, half a mile from the line. *(1)*
3. Do not leave the glass *at the edge of* the counter. *(2)*
4. Ice was still forming *at the edge of* the pond in April. *(2)*
5. He was *on the edge of* tears by the end of the sentence. *(3)*
6. The whole department is *on the edge of* quitting over the schedule. *(3)*
7. That third act kept everybody *on the edge of* their seats. *(4)*

### down

**Pronunciation:** /daʊn/ &middot; *DOWN*; one syllable, full diphthong; **no reduced form** — *down* destresses without changing its vowel, which is unusual for a preposition this frequent
**Русский:** вниз по; дальше по — ниже по склону; дальше по улице (амер. разг., без перепада высот)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "farther along" sense is characteristically American and informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** *Down* is also an adverb (*sit down*), a verb particle (*turn it down*), an adjective (*the server is down*), a noun (*third down*, *goose down*), and a verb (*downed the drink*) — **the preposition is the only one with an object**, and the test is that you cannot move it: *ran **down** the hill* has no *ran the hill down*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *down* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *down* [linear object] &middot; *down the street / road / hall* &middot; *down south / down the coast*
**Contrast:** Sense 2 is the one to learn: ✗ *in American English *down the street* usually has nothing to do with elevation** — it means "farther along, away from here," and *down* and [along](#along) are nearly interchangeable there. Against [below](#below) and [under](#under), which are pure vertical position with no path involved; *down* always implies a line you could travel. ✗ *The British "attributable to" sense — *that's down to the weather*, *it's down to you* — is not American**; Americans say *because of*, *due to*, or *up to you*. The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#down).

At or toward a lower point on something, or simply farther along it. American usage has largely bleached the elevation out of the second sense — you go *down* to the corner store on flat ground — which leaves *down* functioning as a vague marker of distance from the speaker, paired with *up* in a way that is regional as much as geographic. Once distance along a line is all the word carries, it takes the last short step and starts measuring distance in time: *down the road* is next year.

**1. At a lower point on a slope or a vertical surface.** The cabin is *down* the hill from the lodge.
**2. Farther along; away from here (no elevation implied).** The bar is three doors *down* the block.
**3. Toward the south, or along a coast or river (regional and idiomatic).** They moved *down* the coast to Monterey.
**4. Lower in a list, a ranking, or a sequence.** Her name is well *down* the ballot.
**5. Later in time, in the fixed *down the road* and *down the line*.** We can revisit that *down* the road.

1. The trailhead is a quarter mile *down* the ridge. *(1)*
2. Water was running *down* the outside of the foundation. *(1)*
3. The pharmacy is two blocks *down* the street. *(2)*
4. His office is *down* the hall on the left. *(2)*
5. Her family is *down* south for the winter. *(3)*
6. We drove *down* the coast as far as Big Sur. *(3)*
7. He is third *down* the depth chart and unlikely to play. *(4)*
8. The Dodgers slid two spots *down* the standings in a week. *(4)*
9. That shortcut will cost somebody money *down* the road. *(5)*
10. Two owners *down* the line, nobody remembered the handshake deal. *(5)*

### off

**Pronunciation:** /ɔf/ &middot; *AWF*; also /ɑf/ &middot; *AHF* in cot/caught-merged speech; no reduced form — the vowel stays full
**Русский:** с, от — снятый с поверхности; отходящий от (комната off the kitchen); в отдалении от берега
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; ***off of*** is standard American **speech** and is deleted in edited prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*the lid came off*), an adjective (*the milk is off*, *his timing is off*), a noun, a verb, and a very productive particle (*call it off*, *put it off*) — **none of which takes an object, and the preposition always does**: *he is off* is the adjective, *he is off his game* is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *off* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *off* [noun phrase] (branching) &middot; *off the coast of* [place] &middot; *off* [substance] (abstaining) &middot; *off* [standard] (*off his game*)
**Variants:** off of — ubiquitous in American speech, absent from edited prose; ***off from*** is nonstandard everywhere
**Contrast:** **Two usage points.** First, ***off of***: *get off the couch* is the written form and *get off of the couch* is what people say — the *of* is redundant and editors remove it, but it is not a marker of poor speech in America the way it is in Britain. Second, ***off from*** is simply wrong; there is no such preposition. Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from), which names a source without implying prior contact — *she took it **from** the shelf* versus *she took it **off** the shelf*, where *off* says it was resting there. Sense 2 (*a bedroom off the hallway*) has no synonym and is worth learning on its own.

No longer on, or at a remove from, something it was attached to or aligned with. The static senses gathered here all describe the state after separation: a lid not on its jar, a room opening off a corridor, an island at a distance from a coast, a person no longer using a substance, a performance no longer at its usual level.

**1. Not on; separated from a surface it was resting on.** The lid is *off* the jar.
**2. Opening from or branching out of.** There's a half bath *off* the kitchen.
**3. At a distance from, especially seaward of a coast.** An island *off* the coast of Maine.
**4. No longer using or consuming.** He's been *off* caffeine for a month.
**5. Below the usual standard.** The whole team was *off* its game.

1. Keep the dog *off* the furniture. *(1)*
2. One shutter had come *off* the front window. *(1)*
3. The pantry opens *off* the back hallway. *(2)*

5. The wreck lies four miles *off* Cape Hatteras. *(3)*
6. They anchored just *off* the point. *(3)*
7. She's been *off* dairy since January. *(4)*
8. He went *off* his medication without telling anyone. *(4)*
9. The pitcher was *off* his game from the first inning. *(5)*
10. Sales for the quarter came in *off* last year's pace. *(5)*

### out

**Pronunciation:** /aʊt/ &middot; *OWT*; one syllable, full diphthong; no reduced form
**Русский:** наружу через — смотреть out the window «в окно»; дальше по дороге прочь от города
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a restricted noun phrase.** As a bare preposition in American English, *out* takes essentially only **openings** (*out the window*, *out the door*, *out the chimney*) and a few direction and route nouns (*out Route 9*, *out back*, *out west*). For everything else, American English requires [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of). **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*the fire went out*), an adjective (*the truth is out*), a noun (*a called third strike is an out*), a verb (*the story outed him*), and a very productive particle (*hand it out*, *figure it out*) — **none of which takes an object, and the preposition always does**: *he leaned out* is the adverb, *he leaned out the window* is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [verb] *out* [opening] (*look out the window*) &middot; [live / drive] *out* [route or direction] &middot; *out back / out west / out east*
**Variants:** out of — the general-purpose form; see [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of)
**Contrast:** **This is one of the sharpest American/British splits in the whole class.** Americans say *he leaned **out** the window*; the British say *out **of** the window*, and *out the window* strikes them as substandard. In American English the bare form is not only acceptable but preferred with openings. Where the two agree: with containers, sources, and materials you need *out of* on both sides of the Atlantic — *out of the box*, *out of money*, *made out of oak*. Against [outside](#outside), which is a static position rather than a passage through an opening.

Through an opening to the outside, or along a route leading away from a center. Bare prepositional *out* is a narrow word with a strong American accent: it lives on windows, doors, and highways, and it hands everything else to *out of*. Its enormous life as an adverb, adjective, noun, and particle is separate and is not the preposition.

**1. Through an opening to the outside.** She was staring *out* the window.
**2. Along a road or in a direction leading away from town.** They live *out* Route 9, past the quarry.

1. Don't throw anything *out* the car window. *(1)*
2. He walked *out* the side door without a word. *(1)*
3. Smoke was pouring *out* the vent. *(1)*
4. Their place is a few miles *out* the old highway. *(2)*
5. The dogs are *out* back if you want to see them. *(2)*
6. Her people are all *out* west somewhere. *(2)*

### over to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvɚ tə/ &middot; *OH-ver-tuh* &middot; **weak form** — *to* is never stressed and reduces to /tə/ before a consonant and /tu/ before a vowel, so *over to the window* is /ˈoʊvɚ tə ðə ˈwɪndoʊ/ &middot; *OH-ver-tuh-thuh-WIN-doh*, and ✗ *oh-ver-TOO* is wrong in every context except a deliberate contrast. *Over* itself keeps its r-colored /ɚ/ and has no reduced form
**Русский:** в сторону; туда к — правее или левее от точки взгляда; подойти к; передать слово
**Type:** complex, and a contested one — see the complement note &middot; **Register:** neutral and characteristically spoken American
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause.** **The word-class warning is the point of this entry: most grammars analyze *over to* as the adverb *over* followed by the preposition *to*** — *he walked over | to the desk* — rather than as a single preposition. Two facts argue for treating it as a unit anyway. It resists interruption: *he walked over quickly to the desk* is a different reading, with *over* attached to the verb. And it survives with no verb of motion at all — *the shutoff valve is over to the left* — where nothing has moved and *over* cannot be a directional adverb. Note that it strands on *to* alone: *the desk he walked over to* is fine, and there is no ✗ *the desk he walked to over*.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *over to* [noun phrase] &middot; [be-verb] *over to* [the left / the right / one side] &middot; *over to* [person] (handing a turn) &middot; *come over to* [place]
**Contrast:** Against bare [to](02-core-relational.md#to): *over* adds the idea of crossing a space the speaker treats as small and familiar. *Come to my place* is neutral; *come over to my place* says you are nearby and coming across. Against *across to*, which insists on a definite thing crossed — a room, a street, a river. Against the parallel members of the same family, [back to](04-movement-direction.md#back-to), [down to](04-movement-direction.md#down-to), and [up to](04-movement-direction.md#up-to), each of which adds its own direction to the same *to*. **The motion sense proper belongs to [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#over)**; what is kept in this file is the static use, *the valve is over to the left*, which is pure position and is the reason the phrase earns a place among the place prepositions at all.

Off to one side, across a short space. The static sense is the one that matters here: something *over to* the left or the right is displaced in that direction from where you are looking, without any motion at all. The motion sense — going *over to* a place — is the commoner one in speech, and it carries the same idea of a short, familiar crossing.

**1. Static: displaced to one side of where the speaker is looking.** The main breaker is *over to* the right.
**2. Across a short space to (with a verb of motion).** He walked *over to* the counter and waited.
**3. Handing a turn, a signal, or a broadcast to someone.** And now *over to* our reporter at the courthouse.

1. The shutoff valve is *over to* the left of the meter. *(1)*
2. Slide the couch a little *over to* the window side. *(1)*
3. She came *over to* our table to say hello. *(2)*
4. Bring the kids *over to* the house on Sunday. *(2)*
5. He wandered *over to* the fence and looked at the horses. *(2)*
6. That's all from the studio &mdash; *over to* you, Marcus. *(3)*
7. Stranding: that's the desk he walked *over to*. *(2)*

### round

**Pronunciation:** /raʊnd/ &middot; *ROWND*; identical to the noun and adjective *round*; the apostrophe spelling *'round* marks it as a clipping of *around* and belongs to song lyrics and dialect writing
**Русский:** вокруг, за — брит. вариант around; в амер. англ. лишь в устойчивых оборотах (round the clock)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** **chiefly British** as a general preposition; in American English it survives only in fixed phrases
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — the same range as *around*. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb, adjective, noun, and verb, all of which are fully standard in American English; **only the general prepositional use is British**.
**Pattern:** *round* [noun phrase] (British) &middot; American fixed forms only: *round the clock*, *year-round*, *round-trip*, *round about* &middot; **British, not American:** *round the bend*, *all round*
**Variants:** around — **the American form**; use it everywhere except in the fixed phrases listed here
**Contrast:** **Americans write [around](#around).** *Round the corner*, *round the table*, and *round the block* are British; the American equivalents all take *around*. What American English keeps is a short list of frozen compounds and idioms where *round* is the only possible form: *round the clock*, *year-round*, *round-trip*, and the dated approximating *round about*. **Do not take *round the bend* (crazy) or *all-round* for American forms** — both are British, and the American equivalents are *around the bend* and *all-around*. Treat any other prepositional *round* in a text as a signal that the writer is British or is imitating speech.

A reduced form of *around* that became a separate word. In British English it is the ordinary preposition for encircling and vicinity; in American English it has been almost entirely displaced by *around* and remains only where it is locked into a compound or an idiom. Learners should recognize it and write *around*.

**1. British: around, in every physical sense.** *Round* the corner &mdash; American *around the corner*.
**2. American: in fixed phrases and compounds only.** The crew worked *round* the clock.

1. British usage: they sat *round* the kitchen table. American: *around* the kitchen table. *(1)*
2. British usage: the shop is just *round* the corner. American: *around* the corner. *(1)*
3. British usage: by the third week he was going *round* the bend. American: *around* the bend. *(1)*

### throughout

**Pronunciation:** /θruˈaʊt/ &middot; *throo-OWT*; the initial cluster is /θr/, not /tr/; stress falls on the second syllable, so in connected speech the first one reduces to weak /θrəˈaʊt/ &middot; *thruh-OWT*
**Русский:** по всему, повсюду в — в каждой части помещения, региона или организации
**Type:** compound (*through* + *out*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb with no object at all (*the house has hardwood throughout*), which is a common and fully standard use.
**Pattern:** *throughout* [bounded space] &middot; *throughout* [organization or region] &middot; [noun phrase] ... *throughout* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** Against [through](04-movement-direction.md#through): *through* traces a path in one side and out the other, *throughout* saturates every part with no path at all. *Water ran **through** the house* is a leak; *there is water damage **throughout** the house* is an insurance claim. Against *all over*, the spoken equivalent — *toys all over the house* and *toys throughout the house* mean the same thing at different registers. Against [across](#across), which distributes over a surface or a set rather than filling a volume. The temporal sense is developed in [time](05-time.md#throughout).

In every part of a space or a body. The word is built to be exhaustive — it claims completeness in a way *in* and *across* do not — which is why it belongs to inspection reports, specifications, and any prose that needs to say *everywhere inside this thing, with no exceptions*.

**1. In every part of a space or a structure.** There is Wi-Fi *throughout* the building.
**2. In every part of an organization, a region, or a body.** The change was announced *throughout* the district.

1. The original tile is intact *throughout* the first floor. *(1)*
2. Smoke detectors are required *throughout* the residence. *(1)*
3. Recycling bins are placed *throughout* the campus. *(1)*
4. Morale improved *throughout* the company after the announcement. *(2)*
5. The species is found *throughout* the Southwest. *(2)*

### to the left of

**Pronunciation:** /tə ðə ˈlɛft əv/ &middot; *tuh-thuh-LEFT-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *to* /tə/, *the* /ðə/, and *of* /ə/ before a consonant, with the only beat on *left*: *to the left of the door* is /tə ðə ˈlɛft ə ðə ˈdɔr/ &middot; *tuh-thuh-LEFT-uh-thuh-DOR*. Before a vowel *of* keeps its /v/ (*to the left of it*)
**Русский:** слева от — с левой стороны для наблюдателя; левее в ряду или в записи числа
**Type:** complex (*to* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral &mdash; the standard frame for describing a layout, a diagram, a photograph, a stage, or a screen
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *to the left of parking the truck*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *to the left of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *to the left of* [noun phrase] &middot; *just / immediately / directly / far to the left of* [noun phrase] &middot; *left of* [noun] (article dropped, technical and political)
**Variants:** ***left of*** with the article dropped is standard in technical and political writing — *left of center*, *left of the decimal point*, *three characters left of the cursor*; *on the left of* is chiefly British; *at the left of* is not idiomatic for position relative to another object, though *at the left* alone is fine for a region of a page
**Contrast:** **The question the phrase never answers by itself is *whose left*.** By default it is the observer's — *the lamp to the left of the couch* is left as you look at it. But an object with a front of its own imposes its own orientation: **stage left** is the performer's left and therefore the audience's right, and a **patient's left** is the doctor's right. When it matters, say *from the audience's point of view* or *the patient's left*, because the phrase alone will be read both ways. Against [beside](#beside) and [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), which give adjacency and no side at all. Against [before](#before) and [behind](#behind), the other axis. And note that *left of center* has been taken over by politics, so a purely spatial reading of it now needs help.

On the left-hand side of something. It is one of the two phrases English uses to lay a scene out in a row, and its whole difficulty is the point of view: unlike *above* and *below*, which are fixed by gravity, left and right flip depending on which way the observer and the object are facing. Beyond the physical sense it does duty on any scale that runs left to right — a line of text, a number line, a timeline, a political spectrum.

**1. On the left-hand side of, as seen by the observer.** The light switch is *to the left of* the door.
**2. Earlier in a row, a line of text, or a left-to-right scale.** Every digit *to the left of* the decimal point is a whole number.
**3. In the phrase left of center: on the political left, with the article dropped.** Her voting record is well *left of* center.

1. The extra chairs are stacked *to the left of* the piano. *(1)*
2. In the photograph, my grandmother is standing *to the left of* the porch rail. *(1)*
3. Park *to the left of* the blue truck and leave room for the gate. *(1)*
4. The exit is immediately *to the left of* the elevator bank. *(1)*
5. The footnote marker sits just *to the left of* the margin. *(2)*
6. Everything *to the left of* the cursor was deleted. *(2)*
7. The bill has support from members well *left of* center. *(3)*
8. Ambiguous without help: *the scar is to the left of his nose* — say *his left* or *your left*. *(1)*

**See also:** [to the right of](#to-the-right-of), [beside](#beside), [across from](#across-from), [before](#before), [behind](#behind), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)

### to the right of

**Pronunciation:** /tə ðə ˈraɪt əv/ &middot; *tuh-thuh-RYT-uv* &middot; **weak form** — *to* /tə/, *the* /ðə/, *of* /ə/ before a consonant, with the only beat on *right*: *to the right of the stove* is /tə ðə ˈraɪt ə ðə ˈstoʊv/ &middot; *tuh-thuh-RYT-uh-thuh-STOHV*. Before a vowel *of* keeps its /v/, and the /t/ of *right* commonly flaps into it, giving *RY-duv*
**Русский:** справа от — с правой стороны для наблюдателя; правее в ряду или в записи числа
**Type:** complex (*to* + determiner + noun + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral &mdash; the mirror of [to the left of](#to-the-left-of), and used in exactly the same places
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No gerund, no clause**: ✗ *to the right of loading the truck*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *to the right of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *to the right of* [noun phrase] &middot; *just / immediately / directly / far to the right of* [noun phrase] &middot; *right of* [noun] (article dropped, technical and political)
**Variants:** ***right of*** with the article dropped is standard in technical and political writing — *right of center*, *right of the decimal point*; *on the right of* is chiefly British. **Do not confuse the dropped-article form with the noun phrase *right of*** in *right of way*, *right of first refusal*, and *right of entry*, where *right* is the noun meaning an entitlement and the phrase is not this preposition at all.
**Contrast:** The same point-of-view problem as its mirror, with the same solutions: **stage right** is the performer's right and the audience's left, and a **patient's right** is the examiner's left. Against [to the left of](#to-the-left-of), which is its exact opposite in every sense, including the political one. Against [beside](#beside), which gives adjacency without a side. And note the trap the left-hand entry does not have: *right* is four different words in English — the direction, the entitlement, the adjective *correct*, and the intensifier (*right next to the door*) — and only the first is this preposition. In *right next to the door*, *right* is the intensifier and the preposition is *next to*.

On the right-hand side of something. Everything said of *to the left of* applies here unchanged: the default is the observer's viewpoint, an object with a front of its own overrides it, and the phrase extends naturally to any left-to-right scale. The one extra hazard is the word *right* itself, which carries three unrelated meanings that show up in the same sentences.

**1. On the right-hand side of, as seen by the observer.** The thermostat is *to the right of* the hall closet.
**2. Later in a row, a line of text, or a left-to-right scale.** The digits *to the right of* the decimal point are the cents.
**3. In the phrase right of center: on the political right, with the article dropped.** The new appointee is somewhat *right of* center.

1. The recycling bin goes *to the right of* the back steps. *(1)*
2. In the family photo, the twins are *to the right of* my father. *(1)*
3. The freight elevator is immediately *to the right of* the loading dock. *(1)*
4. Take the second door *to the right of* the mural. *(1)*
5. Round anything more than two places *to the right of* the decimal. *(2)*
6. The column *to the right of* the totals is for initials. *(2)*
7. He ran a campaign well *right of* center and won the primary. *(3)*
8. Not this preposition: the utility has a *right of* way across the back lot. *(1)*

### up

**Pronunciation:** /ʌp/ &middot; *UP*. **No weak form** — the vowel is already the lax /ʌ/ and has nothing further to reduce to, so unstressed *up* simply loses its beat (*up the street* /ʌp ðə ˈstrit/) without changing quality. The same is true of its opposite, [down](#down). In *up to*, only the second word weakens: *UP-tuh*, never *uh-TOO*
**Русский:** вверх по; дальше по — выше по склону; дальше по улице; севернее, вверх по реке
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "farther along" sense is characteristically American and informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** *Up* is also an adverb (*stand up*), a verb particle (*look it up*, *ran up a bill*), an adjective (*the system is up*), a noun (*ups and downs*), and a verb (*upped the price*). **The particle/preposition test is movement**: you can say *ran a bill up* but not *ran the hill up*, because in *ran up the hill* the hill is the object of the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *up* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *up* [linear object] &middot; *up the street / road / coast* &middot; *up north*
**Contrast:** The mirror of [down](#down), and it inherits the same American quirk: ***up the block* usually implies no elevation at all**, only distance in a direction the speaker treats as away. Against [above](#above) and [atop](#atop), which are static height with no path; *up* implies a line you could travel. The one thing to be careful about is the word class — *up* is far more often a particle than a preposition, and the movement test above settles it every time. The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#up).

At or toward a higher point on something, or simply farther along it in the direction the speaker treats as away. As with *down*, American usage has drained most of the elevation out of the everyday sense, leaving a vague directional marker that pairs with *down* and varies by region — New Yorkers go *up* to the Bronx, New Englanders *up* to Maine, and neither is about altitude. What is left of the vertical idea survives cleanly in rankings and in two idioms that keep an object and therefore keep the preposition: *up your alley*, *up the creek*.

**1. At a higher point on a slope or a vertical surface.** The cabin is *up* the hill from the road.
**2. Farther along; away from here (no elevation implied).** He lives *up* the block from the school.
**3. Toward the north, or inland along a river or coast.** They have a place *up* north.
**4. Higher in a ranking, a scale, or a sequence.** She moved *up* the depth chart fast.
**5. In the fixed idioms *up someone's alley* and *up the creek*.** A job like that is right *up* her alley.

1. Their mailbox is halfway *up* the driveway. *(1)*
2. There's a crack running *up* the chimney. *(1)*
3. The laundromat is two doors *up* the street. *(2)*
4. She works at a shop *up* the road from here. *(2)*
5. We drove *up* the coast to Mendocino. *(3)*
6. His family summers *up* north on the lake. *(3)*
7. He worked his way *up* the ladder from the loading dock. *(4)*
8. The song climbed *up* the chart for six straight weeks. *(4)*
9. A restoration that size is right *up* his alley. *(5)*
10. If the loan falls through we are *up* the creek. *(5)*
