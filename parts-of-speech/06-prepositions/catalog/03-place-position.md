# Place and position

> The prepositions that answer *where?* — the ones that fix a thing in space relative to another thing without moving it.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file locates something. It takes a landmark — a table, a river, a crowd, a city line — and places a second thing with respect to it: higher, lower, beside, inside, behind, or spread across. Three things unite the group and cause almost all the trouble. **First, the static/dynamic split**: nearly every word here has a twin sense of motion that lives in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md), and the same form does both jobs — *the boat is **under** the bridge* is position, *the boat went **under** the bridge* is a path. The two places English marks that split with *different* forms are the two learners most often get wrong: **in / into** and **on / onto**. *She was **in** the car* is position and *she got **into** the car* is motion; *the cat is **on** the roof* is position and *the cat jumped **onto** the roof* is motion. Everywhere else in this file, one form covers both and only the verb tells you which you have. **Second, the pairs that look interchangeable and are not**: *above*/*over*, *below*/*under*, *among*/*between*, *beside*/*besides*, *amid*/*among*, *in*/*within*, and *near*/*nearby* each hinge on one precise distinction, given in the **Contrast** line of every entry.

**Third, and most important grammatically: not one of these prepositions takes a *that*-clause.** You cannot write *behind he was standing*, *within the deadline passed*, or *beyond that he admitted it*. What they take is a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (*beside **me***, *between you and **me***). A smaller set also takes a **gerund** — *not above lying*, *against parking here*, *upon signing*, *alongside working nights*, *an argument over paying*, *questions around hiring*, *beyond saving*, *behind closing the plant*, *past caring*, *outside of being tired* — and each of those says so in its own **Complement** line. When one of these words takes a verb, that verb is an *-ing* form and never an infinitive: ✗ *against to park here*, ✗ *upon to sign*, ✗ *beyond to save*. A still smaller set takes a **wh-clause**, which is a finite clause: *beyond **what he told us***, *nowhere near **what we needed***, *the difference between **what he said** and **what he did***, *an argument over **who pays***. Those four words — [beyond](#beyond), [near](#near), [between](#between), and [over](#over) — are the only ones in this file with a finite complement, and the rule for every other entry here is the flat one: noun phrase in, clause out. A word that looks like a preposition but is followed by a plain subject and verb has become a conjunction (*before he arrived*) or an adverb (*I've been here before*), and the object test settles which: **the preposition always has an object; the adverb and the particle do not.**

**Owned by other files.** Nine prepositions are cross-referenced here rather than repeated: [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), and [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) live in [the core three](01-core-in-on-at.md), and [of](02-core-relational.md#of), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), and [with](02-core-relational.md#with) live in [core relational prepositions](02-core-relational.md). Their weak forms — *of* /əv/ &rarr; /ə/, *to* /tə/, *for* /fɚ/, *at* /ət/, *from* /frəm/, *with* /wɪθ/ &rarr; /wəθ/ — belong to those entries and are not repeated here. The multi-word place phrases — [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to), [inside of](11-complex-multiword.md#inside-of), [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of), and [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of) — are in [complex and multi-word prepositions](11-complex-multiword.md). Where a word here also has a motion sense or a time sense, the entry says so and links to it.

## Vertical position: above, below, and on top

The words that stack one thing over another. The rule that governs all of them is that **height alone is not alignment**: *above* and *below* claim only that one thing is higher or lower, while *over* and *under* line the two up and usually let the upper one cover the lower. Register sorts the rest — *beneath* is literary, *underneath* is emphatic and physical, *atop* and *upon* are formal.

### above

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbʌv/ &middot; *uh-BUV*; the first syllable is always the reduced /ə/, and there is no further weak form
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund in the fixed *not above doing X* idiom (*she's not above lying*). **Never a finite clause** — write *above the line that says "total"*, not *above it says "total"*. Also an adverb (*the sky above*), an adjective (*the above figures*), and a noun (*see the above*); the preposition alone takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *above* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *above* [noun phrase] &middot; *not above* [verb-ing] &middot; *above all*
**Contrast:** **The *above*/*over* line is height versus alignment.** *Above* asserts only that one thing is higher; *over* adds vertical alignment and often covering or crossing. A lamp can be *above* or *over* the table, but a blanket can only be *over* it, and a village can be *above* the valley without being over anything. For measurements and scales, *above* is the word — *above sea level*, *above average*, *above freezing* — while [over](#over) takes quantities and counts (*over fifty people*). Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): *on* requires contact, *above* forbids it.

Higher than something, with air in between. That single geometric fact generates every other sense: higher on a map, higher on a measuring scale, higher in a hierarchy, and — by a short moral leap — too high in character to stoop to something. The one thing *above* never means is touching.

**1. Higher than, without contact.** A framed map hung *above* the couch.
**2. Higher on a scale of measurement.** The town sits 6,000 feet *above* sea level.
**3. Greater than a stated number or level.** Anything *above* ninety is an A.
**4. Higher in rank, authority, or importance.** There is no rank *above* general.
**5. Too honorable or proud to do something.** He is not *above* asking for help.
**6. Earlier on the page, in written reference.** The rates listed *above* have changed.

1. A hawk circled *above* the parking lot. *(1)*
2. She keeps the good glasses *above* the microwave. *(1)*
3. Denver is a mile *above* sea level. *(2)*
4. The reservoir is twelve feet *above* normal. *(2)*
5. Temperatures stayed *above* forty all week. *(3)*
6. Anything *above* two hundred dollars needs a second signature. *(3)*
7. A lieutenant is one grade *above* a sergeant. *(4)*
8. She put the team *above* her own record. *(4)*
9. He is not *above* taking credit for someone else's work. *(5)*
10. No one in that office is *above* suspicion. *(5)*
11. The exceptions noted *above* apply only to renewals. *(6)*
12. Everything *above* this line is a draft. *(6)*

**See also:** [over](#over), [atop](#atop), [below](#below), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of)

### atop

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtɑp/ &middot; *uh-TAHP*; also /əˈtɔp/ &middot; *uh-TAWP* in dialects that keep the cot/caught distinction; the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form
**Type:** compound (historically *a-* + *top*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; common in journalism, rare in speech
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. No gerund, no clause. ***Atop of* is nonstandard** — the *of* belongs to *on top of*, not to *atop*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *atop* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Atop* is [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of) compressed into one word, and it is the more formal of the two — no American says *atop* out loud at the kitchen table. Unlike [above](#above), it requires contact; unlike plain [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), it insists the surface is a high or prominent one. *On the table* is neutral; *atop the table* sounds like a museum label.

Resting on the highest surface of something, in contact with it. It is a written word — headlines, captions, nature writing, and real-estate copy love it — and it survives mainly because it is shorter than *on top of* and grander than *on*. It also has a small figurative life in the language of rankings and standings.

**1. On the highest surface of a physical thing.** A weathervane *atop* the barn.
**2. At the top of a ranking or standing (journalistic).** The Dodgers finished *atop* the division.

1. A single crow sat *atop* the utility pole. *(1)*
2. The chapel *atop* the hill is visible from the highway. *(1)*
3. Snow lay six inches deep *atop* the woodpile. *(1)*
4. She placed the last shingle *atop* the stack. *(1)*
5. The company ended the quarter *atop* the industry. *(2)*
6. He has stayed *atop* the rankings for three seasons. *(2)*

**See also:** [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of), [above](#above), [over](#over), [upon](#upon), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on)

### below

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈloʊ/ &middot; *bih-LOH*; weak /bəˈloʊ/ &middot; *buh-LOH*
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *low*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No gerund, no clause. Also an adverb (*the deck below*, *see below*) and, in nautical use, an adverb meaning belowdecks; the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *below* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *below* [noun phrase] &middot; *below* [number or level]
**Contrast:** **The *below*/*under* line mirrors *above*/*over*.** *Below* claims only lower position; [under](#under) adds vertical alignment and often covering or containment. You sleep *under* a blanket, never *below* one, and you camp *below* the tree line, not under it. Measurements and scales take *below* — *below freezing*, *below zero*, *below average* — while *under* takes quantities and ages (*under thirty*, *under twenty dollars*). Against [beneath](#beneath), which is the same idea in a literary register.

Lower than something, with no claim about contact or alignment. Like its opposite *above*, it works on maps and on scales as readily as in the air: downstream is *below*, downhill is *below*, and any number smaller than the reference point is *below* it. It is the everyday word for lower position when nothing is doing any covering.

**1. Lower than, without contact.** The apartment *below* ours flooded.
**2. Farther down a slope, a river, or a coast.** The dam is two miles *below* the falls.
**3. Less than a stated number, level, or standard.** Sales came in *below* projections.
**4. Lower in rank or grade.** Everyone *below* the rank of captain reports here.
**5. Later on the page, in written reference.** The footnotes *below* explain the method.

1. There is a crawlspace *below* the kitchen floor. *(1)*
2. The clouds sat *below* the summit all morning. *(1)*
3. The town lies just *below* the reservoir. *(2)*
4. Fishing is better *below* the bridge than above it. *(2)*
5. It stayed *below* freezing for nine days straight. *(3)*
6. Her cholesterol is finally *below* two hundred. *(3)*
7. No one *below* director level saw the memo. *(4)*
8. He was two years *below* me in school. *(4)*
9. See the table *below* for the current rates. *(5)*
10. Everything *below* the dotted line is optional. *(5)*

**See also:** [under](#under), [beneath](#beneath), [underneath](#underneath), [above](#above), [down](#down)

### beneath

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈniθ/ &middot; *bih-NEETH*; weak /bəˈniθ/ &middot; *buh-NEETH*; the *th* is voiceless /θ/, not the /ð/ of *breathe*
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *neath*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No finite clause.** A gerund appears only in the frozen disdain idiom (*beneath answering*, *beneath discussing*) and is rare. Also an adverb (*the valley beneath*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *beneath* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *beneath* [noun phrase] &middot; *beneath* [abstract noun] (*beneath contempt*)
**Variants:** 'neath /niθ/ — a poetic clipping that belongs to verse, hymn, and song lyric (*'neath the willow*); never write it in prose
**Contrast:** *Beneath* is the literary member of the *under / underneath / below* family and is almost never the natural spoken choice: Americans say *the dog is **under** the table*, and *beneath the table* sounds like a novel. What *beneath* owns outright is the moral sense — *beneath contempt*, *beneath him*, *beneath her dignity* — where [under](#under) and [below](#below) cannot go. It also handles the metaphor of a hidden interior better than either (*beneath the surface*).

Directly under something, usually with the weight or nearness of the upper thing felt. It is *under* in a raised register, plus two senses of its own: the surface that conceals something, and the standard that something falls short of. Reach for it when you want the sentence to sound written rather than spoken.

**1. Directly under, often with weight or pressure.** The porch steps creaked *beneath* him.
**2. Under the surface of, hidden by an appearance.** *Beneath* the cheerful tone was real panic.
**3. Unworthy of, too low for someone's dignity.** The remark was *beneath* her.

1. The ice groaned *beneath* their boots. *(1)*
2. Roots had buckled the sidewalk *beneath* the maple. *(1)*
3. *Beneath* the fresh paint, the wood was rotten. *(2)*
4. *Beneath* all the legal language, the offer is simple. *(2)*
5. That kind of gossip is *beneath* contempt. *(3)*
6. He seemed to think manual labor was *beneath* him. *(3)*

**See also:** [under](#under), [underneath](#underneath), [below](#below), [above](#above), [behind](#behind)

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *OH-ver*; the second syllable is the r-colored /ɚ/ in all American speech and keeps it even unstressed, so *over* has **no weak form** of the kind *of* and *to* have — what happens in fast speech is that the whole word loses its beat, not that its vowels change. **The r-less /ˈoʊvə/ is British, not an American reduction.**
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a **gerund** (*an argument over paying the bill*, *no control over spending*) and a **wh-clause** (*an argument over who pays*, *a fight over what to charge*) in the dispute and authority senses. **Never a *that*-clause** — ✗ *an argument over that he should pay*; to hang a finite clause on it you need a noun to carry it (*an argument over the claim that he should pay*). Also an adverb (*come over*), an adjective (*it's over*), and a verb particle (*think it over*, *hand it over*), none of which takes an object; the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; *over* [number] &middot; [choose/prefer] [X] *over* [Y] &middot; stranded: *the beam it hangs* **over**
**Contrast:** Against [above](#above): *over* implies vertical alignment and permits covering, *above* asserts only greater height. A tarp goes *over* the woodpile, never *above* it. Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): *on* is contact and support, *over* is coverage from the top — *the cloth **on** the table* lies flat, *the cloth **over** the table* drapes down the sides. Against [under](#under), its exact opposite in every sense here. On sense 9, against [beyond](#beyond): *over the ridge* names a barrier you would cross to get there, *beyond the ridge* names only what lies farther out and crosses nothing. Note also that ***over* is the most widely distributed word in this catalog**: the path sense (*jumped over the fence*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#over), the span-of-time sense (*over the summer*) is in [time](05-time.md#over), the *about* sense (*a fight over money*) is in [topic and reference](10-topic-reference-role.md#over), the bare quantity sense (*over fifty*) reappears in [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#over), and the preference sense in [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#over). Senses 4 and 6 below are the positional face of those last two and are kept here because they fall straight out of the vertical geometry.

Positioned higher than something and, characteristically, lined up with it — which is why *over* slides so easily into the idea of covering. From physical coverage it reaches authority (one person positioned over another), quantity (a number higher than another), preference (one option placed above another), the channel a message travels along, and the far side of any barrier you would have to go over to reach. The positional senses are collected here; the moving, temporal, and topical senses live elsewhere in this catalog.

**1. Higher than and vertically aligned with; spanning.** A footbridge *over* the creek.
**2. Covering, resting on top of and concealing.** A sheet *over* the furniture.
**3. Across the whole surface or extent of.** She spilled coffee *over* the keyboard.
**4. More than, with a number or amount (see also [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#over)).** *Over* two hundred people showed up.
**5. In a position of authority above.** She has three shift leads *over* her.
**6. Chosen or ranked ahead of an alternative (see also [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#over)).** They took the older candidate *over* the younger one.
**7. By way of a channel or a medium.** We settled the whole thing *over* the phone.
**8. Past and no longer troubled by.** She is finally *over* the flu.
**9. On the far side of a barrier you would have to cross.** Their place is *over* the ridge.

1. The old sign still hangs *over* the entrance. *(1)*
2. Power lines run *over* the whole subdivision. *(1)*
3. He pulled the blanket *over* his head. *(2)*
4. There was a tarp *over* the boat all winter. *(2)*
5. A rash had spread *over* both arms by Tuesday. *(3)*
6. Papers were scattered *over* every surface in the room. *(3)*
7. The repair came to a little *over* nine hundred dollars. *(4)*
8. *Over* half the class failed the first quiz. *(4)*
9. No one *over* her signed off on the change. *(5)*
10. He was given authority *over* both warehouses. *(5)*
11. I'd take a long drive *over* a short flight any day. *(6)*
12. The committee chose the local firm *over* two national ones. *(6)*
13. They worked out the terms *over* the phone in ten minutes. *(7)*
14. The alert went out *over* the emergency channel at four. *(7)*
15. She is finally *over* the worst of the flu. *(8)*
16. He is not *over* the layoff and may not be for a while. *(8)*
17. Her cousins live *over* the border in Nogales. *(9)*
18. The next town *over* the ridge has one gas station. *(9)*

**See also:** [above](#above), [under](#under), [atop](#atop), [across](#across), [beyond](#beyond), [over (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#over), [over (time)](05-time.md#over), [over (topic)](10-topic-reference-role.md#over)

### under

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndər/ &middot; *UN-der*; the first vowel never reduces, so there is **no weak form**. What casual American speech does instead is drop the /d/ after the nasal, giving /ˈʌnɚ/ &middot; *UN-ner* (*under the bed* &rarr; *unner the bed*). **The r-less /ˈʌndə/ is British, not an American reduction.**
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No finite clause, no gerund** — write *under the impression that he had paid* (the *that*-clause belongs to *impression*, not to *under*), never *under he had paid*. Also an adverb (*the boat went under*) and a prefix (*underpaid*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; *under* [number or age] &middot; *under* [authority] &middot; *under* [process noun] (*under review*)
**Contrast:** Against [below](#below): *under* aligns and often covers, *below* only ranks lower. You are *under* a bridge when you pass through its shadow and *below* it when you are downstream. Against [underneath](#underneath): *underneath* is the emphatic, physical form and has none of *under*'s abstract senses — you cannot say *underneath new management* or *underneath thirty*. Against [beneath](#beneath): the same relation in a literary register. And note that scales take [below](#below) (*below freezing*) while counts and ages take *under* (*under thirty*). *Under* has two other homes: the path sense (*rolled under the fence*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#under), and the bare quantity sense (*under fifty*) reappears in [agent, instrument, and manner](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#under).

Directly lower than something and usually covered by it. That covering is what lets *under* become the workhorse abstraction of the group: things covered by an authority, by a rule, by a process, or by a heading are all *under* it. Of the four low-position words, this is by a wide margin the most useful and the most polysemous.

**1. Directly below and typically covered by.** The cat is *under* the bed.
**2. Worn or placed beneath another layer.** He had a T-shirt *under* his sweater.
**3. Less than a number, an age, or an amount.** Everything on this rack is *under* twenty dollars.
**4. Subject to the authority, rule, or command of.** The park is *under* federal jurisdiction.
**5. Undergoing a stated process or condition.** The bridge has been *under* construction for two years.
**6. Classified within a heading or a name.** The reservation is *under* Alvarez.

1. Your keys are *under* the newspaper. *(1)*
2. We waited out the storm *under* an overpass. *(1)*
3. She wore thermals *under* her jeans. *(2)*
4. Put a towel *under* the pot before you set it down. *(2)*
5. Anyone *under* eighteen needs a signed waiver. *(3)*
6. We finished the job *under* budget for once. *(3)*
7. The clinic operates *under* state supervision. *(4)*
8. He served *under* three different chiefs. *(4)*
9. The proposal is still *under* review. *(5)*
10. Two of the three claims are *under* investigation. *(5)*
11. You'll find it filed *under* "permits." *(6)*
12. She published *under* a pen name for a decade. *(6)*

**See also:** [underneath](#underneath), [beneath](#beneath), [below](#below), [over](#over), [above](#above), [under (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#under)

### underneath

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌndərˈniθ/ &middot; *un-der-NEETH*; also stressed on the first syllable, /ˈʌndərˌniθ/ *UN-der-neeth*, when it precedes a noun; the *th* is voiceless /θ/. **No vowel-reducing weak form** — the stressed /i/ has nothing to reduce to — but casual speech drops the /d/ after the nasal exactly as it does in [under](#under), giving /ˌʌnɚˈniθ/ &middot; *un-ner-NEETH*
**Type:** compound (*under* + *neath*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly emphatic
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*look underneath*) and a noun (*the underneath of the car*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *underneath* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *underneath* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the mat it was* **underneath**
**Contrast:** *Underneath* is the emphatic, strictly physical form of [under](#under): it insists that the upper thing covers and usually hides the lower one. That is the whole difference, and it is why *underneath* refuses every abstract sense *under* has — ***underneath new management***, ***underneath thirty***, and ***underneath investigation*** are all wrong. Against [beneath](#beneath), which says the same thing in a literary register, and [below](#below), which requires no covering at all.

Directly under and covered by, with the covering emphasized. When someone says *look underneath* rather than *look under*, they mean the thing is hidden. The only figurative extension it allows is the one that keeps the covering metaphor intact: an outward appearance with something concealed behind it.

**1. Directly under and concealed or covered by.** The spare key is *underneath* the mat.
**2. Behind an outward appearance.** *Underneath* all that swagger he is anxious.

1. There is a whole box of photographs *underneath* the stairs. *(1)*
2. She found the receipt *underneath* a stack of mail. *(1)*
3. Something was rusting *underneath* the truck. *(1)*
4. *Underneath* the gruffness he is a soft touch. *(2)*
5. *Underneath* the confident delivery, the argument was thin. *(2)*

**See also:** [under](#under), [beneath](#beneath), [below](#below), [inside](#inside), [atop](#atop)

### upon

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑn/ &middot; *uh-PAHN*; also /əˈpɔn/ &middot; *uh-PAWN*; weak /əˈpən/ &middot; *uh-puhn*, and in fast speech the whole word flattens toward /əˈpɑn/ with no stress of its own
**Type:** compound (*up* + *on*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or legal; also fixed idioms at every register
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the "immediately after" sense** (*upon arriving*, *upon signing the lease*). Never a finite clause — write *upon learning that he had left*, never *upon he had left*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; *upon* [verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *upon* [same noun] (*row upon row*)
**Contrast:** **Anywhere *upon* appears, [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is also grammatical.** *Upon* adds no meaning at all — only formality — so choose it for register, for rhythm, or because a fixed phrase demands it (*once upon a time*, *row upon row*, *upon further review*). Overusing it is the clearest sign of writing that is trying to sound official. Against [atop](#atop), which insists the surface is high; *upon* makes no such claim. The motion sense (*came upon a clearing*) is handled in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#upon).

A formal equivalent of *on*, identical in meaning and higher in register. It earns its keep in three places: legal and ceremonial prose, a set of fixed phrases that will not take *on*, and the "immediately after" construction with a gerund, where it is genuinely more idiomatic than *on*.

**1. On a surface, in formal or literary style.** A wreath *upon* the door.
**2. Immediately after (with a noun or gerund).** *Upon* arrival, please check in at the desk.
**3. In fixed phrases and repeated-noun intensifiers.** Mile *upon* mile of empty road.

1. A heavy silence settled *upon* the room. *(1)*
2. He laid the wreath *upon* the marker. *(1)*
3. *Upon* signing, you receive the keys. *(2)*
4. *Upon* further review, the call was reversed. *(2)*
5. Wave *upon* wave of new applications came in. *(3)*
6. Once *upon* a time there was a mill here. *(3)*

**See also:** [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [atop](#atop), [over](#over), [above](#above), [upon (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#upon)


## Beside, near, and facing

Adjacency, at four degrees of precision: contact under pressure (*against*), parallel alignment (*alongside*), plain side-by-side position (*beside*), and unspecified proximity (*near*) — plus the three words for standing across a gap and looking at what is on the other side, *facing*, *overlooking*, and *opposite*. Two entries here are warnings rather than recommendations — *nearby* is not really a preposition in American English, and *opposite* has mostly lost the spatial sense to *across from*, which is the gap *facing* and *overlooking* have moved into.

### against

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *uh-GENST*; also /əˈɡeɪnst/ &middot; *uh-GAYNST* (minority American, standard British); unstressed it reduces to /əˈɡɛn(t)s/ &middot; *uh-GENS*, with the /t/ squeezed out between the /n/ and the /s/, and in fast speech the first syllable can vanish entirely, giving *'genst*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*a rule against parking here*, *advised against going*). **Never a finite clause** — write *against the claim that he lied*, not *against he lied*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / lean / prop / rest] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *against* [noun phrase] (backdrop) &middot; *against* [verb-ing] &middot; stranded: *the wall it was leaning* **against**
**Contrast:** *Against* is the **contact-under-pressure** preposition, and that pressure is what separates it from its neighbors. [On](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is contact with support from below; *against* is contact with force from the side. [Next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to) and [beside](#beside) are adjacency with no contact at all: *the ladder **beside** the wall* is standing free, *the ladder **against** the wall* is leaning on it. For the backdrop sense, nothing else will do — *dark **against** the sky* cannot become *dark on the sky*. The forcible-motion sense (*threw it against the door*) is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#against), and sense 3, opposition proper, is developed at length in [comparison and opposition](09-comparison-opposition.md#against).

Touching something and pressing on it. Everything in this entry is the resting state that force leaves behind: a thing leaning, a thing braced, a thing outlined by whatever sits behind it. From physical pressure the word generalizes to opposition of every kind, which is why the same syllable covers a ladder on a wall and a vote in a legislature.

**1. Touching under pressure; leaning or braced.** The bike is *against* the fence.
**2. Seen with something behind it as a backdrop.** Bare branches *against* a gray sky.
**3. In opposition to a rule, a person, or a side.** That's *against* the law.
**4. Providing protection or coverage from.** Insured *against* flood damage.

1. Two shovels leaned *against* the garage door. *(1)*
2. He stood with his back *against* the counter. *(1)*
3. The pressed flowers show up beautifully *against* the dark paper. *(2)*
4. Her red coat was the only color *against* all that snow. *(2)*
5. Parking here is *against* the building rules. *(3)*
6. Three of the five directors voted *against* the merger. *(3)*
7. The policy protects *against* theft but not against flooding. *(4)*
8. Everyone in the unit is vaccinated *against* hepatitis. *(4)*

**See also:** [beside](#beside), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [alongside](#alongside), [against (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#against)

### alongside

**Pronunciation:** /əˌlɔŋˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-lawng-SYD*; also /əˌlɑŋˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-lahng-SYD* in cot/caught-merged speech; stress shifts to the first syllable before a noun in fast speech
**Type:** compound (*along* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund** in the "at the same time as" sense (*studied alongside working nights*). **No finite clause of any kind, and never a *that*-clause.** Also an adverb (*a tug pulled alongside*), which takes no object at all; the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / pull / run] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; *alongside* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *Alongside* adds **parallel orientation** to the plain adjacency of [beside](#beside) and [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to): it wants two long things lying the same way, which is why it started life on the water and still sounds right with boats, trains, roads, and rows. *A chair beside the desk* is fine; *a chair alongside the desk* implies the chair is lined up with it. ***Alongside of*** is redundant — drop the *of*. And it is always one word.

Beside and parallel to, close enough to be lined up with. Its nautical origin still governs the physical sense — you pull *alongside* a dock the way a hull comes to rest against a pier. The figurative senses keep the parallelism intact: people who work *alongside* each other are doing the same kind of thing at the same time, as equals rather than as one under the other, and once the equality drops away what is left is bare simultaneity — a document released *alongside* another one.

**1. Beside and parallel to, physically.** The bike path runs *alongside* the highway.
**2. Working or existing in parallel with, as an equal.** She trained *alongside* professional dancers.
**3. Together with; at the same time as.** The report came out *alongside* a formal apology.

1. The tugboat eased *alongside* the freighter. *(1)*
2. There is a drainage ditch *alongside* the whole east fence. *(1)*
3. A photographer jogged *alongside* the lead runner. *(1)*
4. He learned the trade *alongside* his father. *(2)*
5. The new system will run *alongside* the old one until June. *(2)*
6. She finished the degree *alongside* working two jobs. *(3)*
7. The findings were published *alongside* a lengthy rebuttal. *(3)*
8. The tax cut passed *alongside* a two-year spending freeze. *(3)*

**See also:** [beside](#beside), [along](#along), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [near](#near), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### beside

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsaɪd/ &middot; *bih-SYD*; weak /bəˈsaɪd/ &middot; *buh-SYD*
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — ***beside me***, never *beside I*. **No clause, no gerund.** Unlike *besides*, it is never an adverb and never a conjunct: you cannot write *Beside, it's late*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb / sit / stand / walk] *beside* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *beside* [noun phrase] &middot; *beside the point* &middot; *beside* [reflexive] (*beside himself*)
**Contrast:** ***Beside* and [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides) are different words, and confusing them is the single most common error in this entire category.** *Beside* is position — at the side of. *Besides* is addition or exception — in addition to, apart from. The test is substitution: if *next to* fits, the word is *beside* (*sit **beside** me*); if *in addition to* or *apart from* fits, the word is *besides* (***Besides** me, only two people came*). *Beside* also differs from [alongside](#alongside), which demands parallel alignment, and from [against](#against), which demands contact.

At the side of something, close by. It is the plainest adjacency word in English and carries no implication of contact, alignment, or facing. Two idioms have drifted away from the literal sense and are worth learning whole: *beside the point*, meaning irrelevant, and *beside oneself*, meaning overcome by an emotion.

**1. At the side of; next to.** He sat *beside* her the whole flight.
**2. Compared with (a slightly formal use).** Her troubles look small *beside* his.
**3. In the fixed idioms *beside the point* and *beside oneself*.** That objection is *beside* the point.

1. There is a bench *beside* the trailhead. *(1)*
2. Park *beside* the blue truck and walk up. *(1)*
3. The first draft looks amateurish *beside* the final version. *(2)*
4. A two-hour delay is nothing *beside* what they went through. *(2)*
5. Whether he meant it is *beside* the point. *(3)*
6. She was *beside* herself when the dog came back. *(3)*

**See also:** [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [alongside](#alongside), [near](#near), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### facing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfeɪsɪŋ/ &middot; *FAY-sing*; two syllables, stress on the first, and no weak form — like every participial preposition it keeps the shape of the verb it came from
**Type:** participial (the *-ing* form of *face*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard word in real-estate, architectural, seating, and travel description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — *facing the park*, *facing us*. **No clause, no gerund.** One warning is worth the whole entry: ***the company is facing a recall* is the verb *face* in the progressive, not this preposition.** The test is substitution — if *opposite* or *across from* can replace it without touching the rest of the clause, it is the preposition (*a cabin facing the lake* &rarr; *a cabin across from the lake*); if only *confronting* fits, it is the verb.
**Pattern:** [noun] *facing* [noun phrase] (*the seats facing the stage*) &middot; [be-verb] *facing* [noun phrase] &middot; [sit / stand / park] *facing* [noun phrase] &middot; [page / plate] *facing* [page number]
**Contrast:** *Facing* is the participial member of the *opposite / across from* group, and it is the one that states orientation outright. [Opposite](#opposite) and *across from* only put two things on either side of a gap; *facing* says which way the thing is turned. A house can be *across from* the park and still show it a blank wall, but a house *facing* the park looks at it. Against [before](#before), the formal and largely ceremonial word for the same geometry. Against the near-twin [overlooking](#overlooking), which adds height and a downward view — *a room overlooking the harbor* is above it, *a room facing the harbor* need not be. And against [toward](04-movement-direction.md#toward), which is orientation with motion implied; *facing* is orientation at rest. (**Note the spelling there: *toward* is the American form and *towards* the British one**, and the same split runs through *amid*/*amidst*, *among*/*amongst*, and *around*/*round* in this file.)

Turned toward something and looking at it across an intervening space. The word has quietly taken over the work American English has been withdrawing from *opposite*, and it does that work with the orientation made explicit, which is why floor plans, seating charts, and listings reach for it so often. It stays close to its verb: whatever does the facing is understood to have a front.

**1. Turned toward; looking out at.** A cabin *facing* the lake.
**2. On the side of a structure that looks toward something.** The windows *facing* the alley are painted shut.
**3. Directly opposite in a printed or laid-out spread.** The illustration *facing* page 40.

1. They asked for a room *facing* the courtyard. *(1)*
2. He sat *facing* the door for the whole meal. *(1)*
3. Two lots *facing* the water sold in the first week. *(1)*
4. All the offices *facing* the parking lot get afternoon glare. *(2)*
5. The bleachers *facing* the visitors' bench were empty by the fifth. *(2)*
6. The map on the page *facing* the index is out of date. *(3)*
7. The photograph *facing* the title page was taken in 1911. *(3)*

**See also:** [overlooking](#overlooking), [opposite](#opposite), [across](#across), [before](#before), [toward](04-movement-direction.md#toward), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of)

### near

**Pronunciation:** /nɪr/ &middot; *NEER*; one syllable; the vowel is the /ɪ/ + /r/ sequence, not the /i/ of *knee*. No weak form — *near* keeps its full vowel even unstressed.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; also a **fused relative** — a wh-clause standing in for a noun phrase — in the degree use: *nowhere near **what we needed***, *not anywhere near **what it cost***. **Never a *that*-clause**, and a gerund is marginal at best: *came near winning* is idiomatic, but ✗ *near finishing the report* is not. **Alone among the simple prepositions, *near* is gradable and inflects for degree** — *nearer the door*, *nearest the exit* — a leftover from its life as an adjective. It is also an adjective (*a near miss*), an adverb (*don't come near*), and a verb (*the project neared completion*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *near* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *near* [noun phrase] &middot; *nearer / nearest* [noun phrase] &middot; *near to* [noun phrase] &middot; *nowhere near* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** near to (chiefly British and slightly formal in American English; bare *near* is the American default)
**Contrast:** *Near* and [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to) are interchangeable in most sentences; *near* is shorter and takes the comparative directly (*nearer the fire*, where you would need *closer to the fire*). Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by), which means immediately beside — *stand **by** the door* puts you at it, *stand **near** the door* leaves you a few feet away. Against [beside](#beside), which is adjacency with no distance at all. **And note that [nearby](#nearby) is not a preposition in careful American English** — *nearby the station* should be *near the station*.

Not far from, in space, in time, or on a scale of condition. It is the vaguest of the proximity words and that vagueness is the point: *near the airport* commits to nothing about how near. Its gradability is what sets it apart from every other preposition in this file, and it is the reason *near* survives in a class that otherwise refuses comparison.

**1. Not far from in space.** They live *near* the reservoir.
**2. Not far from in time.** *Near* the end of the movie she walks out.
**3. Close to a state, a condition, or a number.** The company came *near* bankruptcy twice.

1. There's a decent diner *near* the courthouse. *(1)*
2. Sit *nearer* the front if you want to hear. *(1)*
3. The fire started somewhere *near* the fuse box. *(1)*
4. It was *near* midnight before anyone left. *(2)*
5. *Near* the end of the season he tore a hamstring. *(2)*
6. She was *near* tears by the time she finished. *(3)*
7. The bid came *nowhere near* what we needed. *(3)*

**See also:** [nearby](#nearby), [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [beside](#beside), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### nearby

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɪrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *neer-BY*; stressed on the second syllable as an adverb (*a diner is nearby*), on the first as an attributive adjective, /ˈnɪrˌbaɪ/ *NEER-by* (*a nearby diner*)
**Type:** compound (*near* + *by*) &middot; **Register:** neutral as adjective and adverb; **the prepositional use is nonstandard in American English**
**Complement:** **As a preposition it takes a noun phrase — but do not use it that way.** *Nearby the station* is attested and is regularly flagged as an error by American editors; write *near the station*. In its standard lives it takes no object at all: adjective before a noun (*a nearby town*), adverb after the verb (*they live nearby*).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] is *nearby* (adverb) &middot; a *nearby* [noun] (adjective) &middot; **avoid:** *nearby* [noun phrase] (preposition)
**Contrast:** **The rule is short: *near* takes an object, *nearby* does not.** *A store near the school* and *a nearby store* are both correct; *a store nearby the school* is the error. This is the cleanest illustration in the file of the test that runs through the whole class — a preposition has an object, an adverb stands alone. Compare [near](#near), which is a true preposition; the two-word *close by* behaves exactly like *nearby* and takes no object either.

Close at hand. It is listed here because learners reach for it as a preposition and American editors take it back out. Treat it as an adjective and an adverb, and hand the prepositional work to *near*. Its inclusion in a preposition dictionary is a warning label, not an endorsement.

**1. Adverb: at a short distance, with no object.** There's a hardware store *nearby*.
**2. Adjective before a noun: located close at hand.** They evacuated three *nearby* buildings.
**3. Preposition (nonstandard in American English): near.** *Nearby* the river &mdash; write *near the river* instead.

1. We ate at a taqueria *nearby*. *(1)*
2. Is there a gas station *nearby*? *(1)*
3. A *nearby* fire station handled the call. *(2)*
4. Two *nearby* counties reported the same outage. *(2)*
5. Nonstandard: they camped *nearby* the lake. Standard: they camped *near* the lake. *(3)*
6. Nonstandard: the office is *nearby* the courthouse. Standard: the office is *near* the courthouse. *(3)*

**See also:** [near](#near), [close to](11-complex-multiword.md#close-to), [beside](#beside), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), [around](#around)

### opposite

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑpəzɪt/ &middot; *AH-puh-zit*; also /ˈɑpəsɪt/ &middot; *AH-puh-sit*; the final syllable is reduced in every American pronunciation
**Type:** simple (functioning as preposition, adjective, adverb, and noun) &middot; **Register:** neutral, but the spatial preposition sounds British or formal in American English
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adjective (*the opposite side*), a noun (*the opposite of true*), and an adverb (*they sat opposite*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *opposite* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *opposite* [noun phrase] &middot; [act / play / star] *opposite* [person] &middot; *opposite to / from* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** opposite to, opposite from (both acceptable; bare *opposite* is the standard prepositional form)
**Contrast:** **For the spatial sense, American English says *across from*, or [facing](#facing) when the orientation matters.** *The bank across from the courthouse* is the ordinary American sentence; *the bank opposite the courthouse* is correct but reads as British or as formal description. What *is* fully American is the casting sense — *she played opposite Denzel Washington* — which no other preposition covers. Against [across](#across), which only requires spanning or being on the other side; *opposite* adds facing.

Facing something from the other side of a space between. The space matters: things *opposite* each other are separated and turned toward one another, which is why the word works for seats at a table, buildings on a street, and actors sharing a scene. Americans keep the second of those senses and quietly hand the first to *across from*.

**1. Facing, on the other side of a space (chiefly British in this use).** The pharmacy is *opposite* the library.
**2. As the counterpart of another performer.** He starred *opposite* Meryl Streep.

1. She took the chair *opposite* mine. *(1)*
2. The hotel stands *opposite* the train station. *(1)*
3. There is a small park *opposite* the school gates. *(1)*
4. She was cast *opposite* an actor she had never met. *(2)*
5. Playing *opposite* him was the best part of the job. *(2)*

**See also:** [across](#across), [facing](#facing), [overlooking](#overlooking), [before](#before), [near](#near), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of)

### overlooking

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚˈlʊkɪŋ/ &middot; *oh-ver-LOOK-ing*; four syllables, main stress on *look*; the vowel of *look* is the lax /ʊ/ of *book*, not the /u/ of *Luke*; the second syllable keeps the r-colored /ɚ/ in all American speech. Like every participial preposition it holds the full shape of the verb it came from, so **there is no weak form** — what fast speech does is compress the two unstressed syllables, not change their quality
**Type:** participial (the *-ing* form of *overlook*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard word in real-estate, hotel, and travel description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — *overlooking the bay*, *overlooking us*. **No clause of any kind, and no gerund**: the thing seen is always a noun phrase (*overlooking the harbor*), never a verb (✗ *overlooking swimming*). The same warning that governs [facing](#facing) governs this word: ***the auditor is overlooking a whole account* is the verb *overlook* in the progressive, not this preposition.** The test is substitution — if *with a view of* can replace it without touching the rest of the clause, it is the preposition (*a terrace overlooking the river* &rarr; *a terrace with a view of the river*); if only *failing to notice* fits, it is the verb.
**Pattern:** [noun] *overlooking* [noun phrase] (*a room overlooking the courtyard*) &middot; [be-verb] *overlooking* [noun phrase] &middot; [sit / stand / perch] *overlooking* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***Overlooking* is [facing](#facing) plus height.** Both say which way the thing is turned, but *overlooking* adds that it is above what it sees and looks down at it: *a room **facing** the harbor* may be at sea level, *a room **overlooking** the harbor* is not. Against [above](#above), which is height with no view and no orientation at all — a floor can be *above* the lobby without seeing anything. Against [over](#over), which is height with alignment and no viewer. Against [opposite](#opposite) and *across from*, which need a gap but neither elevation nor orientation. And keep the two lives of the word apart: as a preposition it gives you a view, as a verb it means you missed something — same form, opposite attitudes.

Above something and looking down at it. Its whole meaning is what *facing* and *above* deliver together, which is why American listings reach for it any time there is a view to sell. The object is always the thing seen and never the viewer, and whatever does the overlooking is understood to have both a front and a vantage point — which is how the word came to work for terrain and fortifications as easily as for windows.

**1. Situated above and giving a view of.** A deck *overlooking* the canyon.
**2. Standing high above and dominating, with no viewer implied.** A ridge *overlooking* the highway.

1. They booked a room *overlooking* the courtyard. *(1)*
2. Her office is on the ninth floor, *overlooking* the river. *(1)*
3. We ate on a terrace *overlooking* the vineyard. *(1)*
4. The old fort sits on a bluff *overlooking* the harbor entrance. *(2)*
5. A water tower *overlooking* the interchange is the only landmark for miles. *(2)*
6. Guns were dug in along the ridge *overlooking* the valley floor. *(2)*

**See also:** [facing](#facing), [above](#above), [over](#over), [opposite](#opposite), [atop](#atop)


## Enclosure, inclusion, and the space in the middle

Being surrounded by something, or being one of the things doing the surrounding. The distinctions that matter are countability (*amid* takes a mass, *among* takes individuals), individuation (*between* keeps its objects separate and named, *among* pools them), and boundary (*inside* and *outside* mark a physical enclosure, *within* marks a limit).

### aboard

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbɔrd/ &middot; *uh-BORD*; one stress, on the second syllable; the first syllable is always /ə/
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *board*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb (*all aboard*, *welcome aboard*), which is how the word most often appears.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *aboard* [vessel or vehicle] &middot; [verb of motion] *aboard* [vessel] &middot; *aboard* (adverb, no object)
**Variants:** on board (two words; identical in meaning, and the dominant form in the figurative business sense — *bring her on board*)
**Contrast:** *Aboard* is the vehicle-specific member of the *in/on* system: you are **in** a car, **on** a bus or a plane, and **aboard** a ship, a plane, a train, or a spacecraft — anything with an interior you can walk around in. It is more formal than either, which is why it belongs to announcements and news copy. Note the split with the two-word *on board*: for the figurative sense of joining a team, American English overwhelmingly writes *on board*, and *aboard* sounds nautical.

On or inside a vessel or a large vehicle. It covers both the static position and the act of getting there, which is why the same word does *everyone aboard survived* and *she came aboard in Seattle*. The figurative use — joining an organization — is a live metaphor that most writers now spell as two words.

**1. On or inside a ship, plane, train, or bus.** There were forty-two passengers *aboard* the ferry.
**2. As a member of a crew, team, or organization (figurative).** We are glad to have you *aboard* the project.

1. Everyone *aboard* the flight was accounted for. *(1)*
2. The equipment stayed *aboard* the research vessel all winter. *(1)*
3. She was the last passenger *aboard* the train. *(1)*
4. Welcome *aboard* &mdash; your badge is at the front desk. *(2)*
5. Two new engineers came *aboard* this month. *(2)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [within](#within), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [astride](#astride)

### amid

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmɪd/ &middot; *uh-MID*; *amidst* /əˈmɪdst/ &middot; *uh-MIDST*; both keep the schwa in the first syllable
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *mid*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary in the physical sense; standard journalism in the background-events sense
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically a **mass noun or a plural** (*amid the wreckage*, *amid rising costs*). **No clause, no gerund.** A *that*-clause after *amid speculation* belongs to the noun *speculation*, not to *amid*.
**Pattern:** *amid* [mass noun] &middot; *amid* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *amid* [event noun] (*amid the confusion*)
**Variants:** amidst — chiefly British and slightly more literary; **American English prefers *amid***, and the two are otherwise identical
**Contrast:** **The *amid*/[among](#among) split is countability.** *Amid* surrounds you with something undifferentiated — wreckage, smoke, chaos, rumors taken as a mass; *among* surrounds you with individuals you could point at. *Amid the crowd* treats the crowd as a texture; *among the crowd* invites you to pick out faces. *Amid* also does something *among* cannot: set a scene of ongoing events behind a piece of news (*amid falling revenues*), which is now its most frequent use in print. The emphatic long form is [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of) or *in the midst of*.

In the middle of something that is treated as a mass rather than counted. The physical sense is literary and slightly old-fashioned in speech, but the abstract one is everywhere in news writing, where *amid* is the standard way to hang a story on a backdrop of continuing conditions without claiming a causal link.

**1. Surrounded by, in the middle of a mass.** A cabin *amid* the pines.
**2. Against a background of continuing events or conditions.** The bill passed *amid* fierce opposition.

1. They found the ring *amid* the ashes. *(1)*
2. A single lit window *amid* all that darkness. *(1)*
3. Shares fell *amid* growing concern about the supply chain. *(2)*
4. The mayor resigned *amid* questions about the contract. *(2)*
5. Talks resumed *amid* signs that both sides were tiring. *(2)*

**See also:** [among](#among), [between](#between), [within](#within), [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of), [throughout](#throughout)

### among

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmʌŋ/ &middot; *uh-MUNG*; *amongst* /əˈmʌŋst/ &middot; *uh-MUNGST*; the first syllable is always /ə/ and never carries stress
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *mong*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a plural, collective, or mass noun phrase** — *among the trees*, *among the staff*, *among us*. ***Among the tree* is ungrammatical**: the object must denote more than one thing. Objective pronouns only (*among them*, *among us*). No clause, no gerund.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *among* [plural noun phrase] &middot; [divide / share / distribute] [object] *among* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *among* [plural] (*common among teenagers*) &middot; *among other things*
**Variants:** amongst — chiefly British; it is not wrong in American English but it is markedly bookish, and edited American prose uses *among*
**Contrast:** **The schoolroom rule "*between* for two, *among* for more" is only half true.** [Between](#between) is correct for any number when the parties are individually in view and the relation is one-to-one — *a treaty between six nations* is perfectly standard. *Among* is the word when the group is an undifferentiated mass — *the money was divided among the heirs* treats them as a pool, *between the two heirs* names them. Against [amid](#amid): *among* counts its objects, *amid* does not. Against [within](#within): *within* bounds a limit, *among* surrounds with members.

Surrounded by or included in a group of more than one. Its four senses all follow from that: physically in the middle of a group, a member of a set, a share distributed to a group, and a trait holding across a group. The one hard requirement is plurality — whatever follows *among* must be countable as more than one or collective in meaning.

**1. In the middle of, surrounded by a group.** The cabin sits *among* the pines.
**2. Included in a set; one of.** She is *among* the three finalists.
**3. Distributed to the members of a group.** The estate was divided *among* the four children.
**4. Holding across the members of a group.** The habit is common *among* first-year students.

1. We found him *among* the crowd at the gate. *(1)*
2. A few red maples stood out *among* all the oaks. *(1)*
3. Her novel is *among* the best things published that year. *(2)*
4. *Among* other duties, he handles scheduling. *(2)*
5. Split the tips evenly *among* the servers. *(3)*
6. The land was parceled out *among* six families. *(3)*
7. Turnout was highest *among* voters over sixty. *(4)*
8. Sleep problems are widespread *among* shift workers. *(4)*

**See also:** [between](#between), [amid](#amid), [within](#within), [inside](#inside), [throughout](#throughout)

### between

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwin/ &middot; *bih-TWEEN*; weak /bəˈtwin/ &middot; *buh-TWEEN*; the clipped literary form *'tween* /twin/ survives only in poetry and in *'tween decks*
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *tween*, from *twain* "two") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a plural noun phrase (*between the buildings*), or **two or more noun phrases joined by *and*** — ***never by *or***. Objective pronouns only: ***between you and me***, never *between you and I*. **Gerunds are fine as coordinates** (*torn between working and commuting*), and so are **fused relatives**: *the difference between **what he said** and **what he did***. **Never a *that*-clause** — ✗ *between that he called and that he wrote*. Also an **adverb** with no object at all (*a fence with a gate in between*, *the years between*), and the preposition always has one.
**Pattern:** *between* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *between* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *between* [pronoun] *and* [pronoun] &middot; *in between* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** betwixt (archaic — see its own entry)
**Contrast:** Three errors cluster here, and all three are worth memorizing. **First:** *between you and I* is wrong. A preposition takes the objective case, and coordination does not change that — *between you and me*, *between him and her*. **Second: the coordinator must be *and*.** *Choose between the red or the blue* should be *between the red **and** the blue*. **Third: ranges take *and*, not *to*.** *Between 1990 to 1995* is an error; write *between 1990 **and** 1995* or *from 1990 **to** 1995*. On the *between*/[among](#among) question, see that entry: the deciding factor is whether the parties are individually in view, not how many there are. The temporal sense is treated in [time](05-time.md#between).

In the space, the interval, or the relationship separating two or more distinct things that are individually in view. That last clause is what really governs the word — *between* keeps its objects separate and named, which is why it works for gaps, for treaties, for choices, and for joint efforts, and why it is fine with six parties as long as you can point at each one.

**1. In the physical space separating two or more things.** There is an alley *between* the two buildings.
**2. In the interval separating two times or points (see also [time](05-time.md#between)).** *Between* Tuesday and Friday the price doubles.
**3. Connecting or dividing two or more parties, reciprocally.** The agreement *between* the union and the city.
**4. Marking a choice among named alternatives.** You have to choose *between* the two offers.
**5. Pooling the resources or efforts of a set of people.** *Between* the three of us we raised nine hundred dollars.

1. A narrow gap *between* the fence and the shed lets the water through. *(1)*
2. She was sitting *between* her two brothers. *(1)*
3. Call me *between* nine and eleven. *(2)*
4. There were four collisions on that stretch *between* January and March. *(2)*
5. The treaty *between* the six nations took a decade to draft. *(3)*
6. Whatever is said *between* you and me stays here. *(3)*
7. He had to choose *between* the job in Denver and the one in Austin. *(4)*
8. There is not much to choose *between* the two proposals. *(4)*
9. *Between* them they own half the block. *(5)*
10. *Between* the deposit and the first month's rent, it came to three thousand. *(5)*

**See also:** [among](#among), [amid](#amid), [betwixt](#betwixt), [within](#within), [between (time)](05-time.md#between)

### betwixt

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwɪkst/ &middot; *bih-TWIKST*; weak /bəˈtwɪkst/ &middot; *buh-TWIKST*
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *twixt*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in modern American English only in the fixed phrase *betwixt and between*
**Complement:** the same as *between* — a plural noun phrase or two noun phrases joined by *and*, with objective pronouns. No clause, no gerund.
**Pattern:** *betwixt* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *betwixt* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *betwixt and between* (fixed idiom)
**Contrast:** *Betwixt* is [between](#between) in period costume. It means exactly the same thing and has no context in which it is the better choice for ordinary prose — using it in a business memo reads as a joke or an affectation. The one exception is the reduplicated idiom *betwixt and between*, meaning undecided or in an awkward middle state, which is still current and is not replaceable by *between and between*. Compare [ere](05-time.md#ere), the archaic *before*, which behaves the same way.

An archaic form of *between*, kept alive by poetry, by deliberate period flavor, and by one surviving idiom. Learners should be able to recognize it in older texts and should not produce it. Note that the idiom *betwixt and between* has drifted away from the preposition entirely and now works as an adjective phrase.

**1. Archaic or literary: between.** The valley lies *betwixt* two ranges.
**2. In the fixed phrase *betwixt and between*: undecided, in an awkward middle state.** He is *betwixt and between* about the move.

1. The old deed describes the parcel as lying *betwixt* the river and the road. *(1)*
2. There is little to choose *betwixt* the two, as an older writer would have put it. *(1)*
3. *Betwixt* dawn and full light the whole field was white with frost. *(1)*
4. She has quit one job and not started the next, so she is *betwixt and between*. *(2)*
5. The design is *betwixt and between* &mdash; too plain for a gallery, too fussy for a kitchen. *(2)*

**See also:** [between](#between), [among](#among), [amid](#amid), [ere](05-time.md#ere), [within](#within)

### inside

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈsaɪd/ &middot; *in-SYD*; **stress distinguishes the parts of speech**: preposition and adverb /ɪnˈsaɪd/ *in-SYD*, noun and attributive adjective /ˈɪnsaɪd/ *IN-syd* (*an **in**side job*)
**Type:** compound (*in* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*come inside*), a noun (*the inside of the lid*), and an adjective (*inside information*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *inside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb of motion] *inside* [noun phrase] &middot; *inside of* [period of time] &middot; stranded: *the box it was* **inside**
**Variants:** inside of — informal in the spatial sense (drop the *of* in edited prose); **idiomatic and standard in the time sense**, *inside of an hour*
**Contrast:** Against [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in): *in* is the neutral container word, *inside* insists on the enclosure and implies a contrasting outside. Both work for *in / inside the box*, but only *in* can go abstract — *in trouble*, *in love*, *in a hurry* have no *inside* version. Against [within](#within): *inside* is physical enclosure, *within* is limits and abstractions (*within the law*, not *inside the law*). And against [into](04-movement-direction.md#into): *inside* is where something is, *into* is where it is going. The parent pair splits the same way — ***in* is position, *into* is motion** (*she was **in** the office* / *she walked **into** the office*) — with a short list of fixed phrases where *in* survives with a motion verb (*put it in the drawer*, *throw it in the trash*).

Within the bounds of a container, a building, or a boundary, with the boundary itself in the picture. It is the emphatic counterpart of *in* and the exact opposite of *outside*, and it keeps that contrast in every sense — a physical interior, the interior of an organization, and, in one informal American use, the interior of a stretch of time.

**1. Within the bounds of a container or enclosed space.** The receipt is *inside* the envelope.
**2. Within an organization, as opposed to outside it.** The leak came from someone *inside* the department.
**3. In less than a stated period of time (informal, usually *inside of*).** They had it running again *inside of* an hour.

1. Everything you need is *inside* the blue folder. *(1)*
2. Wait *inside* the building until they call your name. *(1)*
3. Sources *inside* the agency describe a very different timeline. *(2)*
4. The decision was made *inside* the family, not by the board. *(2)*
5. The whole repair was done *inside of* twenty minutes. *(3)*
6. If we leave now we'll be there *inside of* two hours. *(3)*

**See also:** [outside](#outside), [within](#within), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [inside of](11-complex-multiword.md#inside-of), [aboard](#aboard)

### outside

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd/ &middot; *owt-SYD*; **stress distinguishes the parts of speech**: preposition and adverb /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd/ *owt-SYD*, noun and attributive adjective /ˈaʊtˌsaɪd/ *OWT-syd* (*the **out**side lane*)
**Type:** compound (*out* + *side*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the "apart from" sense only**, and there almost always with *of* — *outside of being tired, I'm fine*. **No clause of any kind**, and never a *that*-clause. Also an adverb (*wait outside*), a noun (*the outside of the house*), and an adjective (*an outside chance*); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *outside* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *outside* [noun phrase] &middot; *outside of* [noun phrase] &middot; *outside* [abstract limit] (*outside the law*)
**Variants:** outside of — extremely common in American speech; **edited prose drops the *of*** for the spatial and scope senses, but keeps it for the "apart from" sense, where *besides* or *apart from* is cleaner still
**Contrast:** The mirror image of [inside](#inside), and it inherits the same rules: physical and scope senses only, with the abstractions handed to other words. On ***outside* vs. *outside of***: American speech uses both freely, but editors delete the *of* in *he waited outside the courthouse*. The "apart from" sense (*outside of a few complaints, it went fine*) is the one place the *of* is normal — and it is also the place where [apart from](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from) or [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides) reads better.

Beyond the bounds of something — a building, a boundary, a jurisdiction, or a scope. The physical sense is the base; the scope sense (*outside my expertise*, *outside the law*) is where the word does most of its work in adult prose, because it draws a limit and puts something firmly past it.

**1. Beyond the physical bounds of.** He was waiting *outside* the courthouse.
**2. Beyond the scope, limits, or authority of.** That question is *outside* my expertise.
**3. Apart from, other than (usually *outside of*).** *Outside of* a cracked screen, the phone is fine.

1. There were reporters *outside* the building all afternoon. *(1)*
2. The house sits just *outside* the city limits. *(1)*
3. Anything over a hundred pounds is *outside* the elevator's rating. *(2)*
4. Enforcement of that rule is *outside* our authority. *(2)*
5. *Outside of* the opening chapter, the book holds up. *(3)*
6. *Outside of* Tuesday, I'm free all week. *(3)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [beyond](#beyond), [without](#without), [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of), [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides)

### within

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɪn/ &middot; *with-IN*; the *th* is **voiced** /ð/, as in *this*, not the /θ/ of *thin*; weak /wəˈðɪn/ &middot; *wuh-THIN*
**Type:** compound (*with* + *in*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund** — write *within the limits that the statute sets*, never *within the statute sets*. Also an adverb (*inquire within*), which is the form on old shop signs.
**Pattern:** *within* [bounded area] &middot; *within* [distance or measure] &middot; *within* [period of time] &middot; *within* [organization or person]
**Contrast:** **The heart of this entry is *in* versus *within*, and in the time sense the difference has legal force.** *In three days* means three days from now; *within three days* means at any point before three days have elapsed — a deadline, not a date. Spatially, [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) locates and *within* bounds: *in the city* says where you are, *within the city limits* says which side of a line you are on. Against [inside](#inside): *inside* is physical enclosure, *within* is limits, ranges, and abstractions. The temporal sense is developed further in [time](05-time.md#within).

Inside the limits of something. The limits are the point: *within* always implies a boundary you have not crossed, whether that boundary is a city line, a budget, a tolerance, a deadline, or the edge of an organization. It is the preposition of not-exceeding, and that is why contracts, specifications, and regulations lean on it so heavily.

**1. Inside the bounds of an area.** The house is *within* the historic district.
**2. Inside a range or a measured limit.** The part came in *within* a thousandth of an inch.
**3. Before a stated period has elapsed (see also [time](05-time.md#within)).** Respond *within* ten business days.
**4. Inside a person, a group, or an organization.** There is real disagreement *within* the committee.

1. Everything *within* the fence line belongs to the county. *(1)*
2. The site is *within* a mile of the river. *(1)*
3. The project finished *within* budget by about four percent. *(2)*
4. Keep the reading *within* tolerance or the batch is rejected. *(2)*
5. Payment is due *within* thirty days of invoice. *(3)*
6. She had three offers *within* a week of posting her resume. *(3)*
7. The split *within* the party is wider than it looks. *(4)*
8. He kept the anger *within* himself for years. *(4)*

**See also:** [inside](#inside), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), [among](#among), [throughout](#throughout), [within (time)](05-time.md#within)

### without

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/ &middot; *with-OWT*; the *th* is **voiced** /ð/; weak /wəˈðaʊt/ &middot; *wuh-THOWT*
**Type:** compound (*with* + *out*) &middot; **Register:** **archaic or literary in this spatial sense**; the modern "lacking" sense is fully neutral and lives in [accompaniment and exception](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without)
**Complement:** in the spatial sense, a noun phrase only — *without the walls*, *without the gates*. **No clause, no gerund** in this sense. Also an adverb in the fixed pairing *within and without*.
**Pattern:** *without* [walls / gates / city] (archaic spatial) &middot; *within and without* (fixed adverbial pair)
**Contrast:** This entry covers only the **obsolete sense "outside of,"** which is the exact opposite of [within](#within) and is the reason the two words are built from the same halves. In living American English that job belongs entirely to [outside](#outside); *without the city* now reads as a Bible verse or a history book, and a modern reader will hear the "lacking" sense instead and be confused. **The productive modern sense — *without money*, *without asking* — is treated in [accompaniment and exception](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without) and is not repeated here.**

Outside of, beyond the bounds of. This was once the ordinary opposite of *within*, and the two words still show their shared architecture. It survives in scripture, in older legal descriptions of land, in hymn and poetry, and in the frozen pair *within and without*. Recognize it; do not write it.

**1. Archaic: outside of, beyond the walls or bounds of.** The camp lay *without* the city gates.
**2. In the fixed adverbial pair *within and without*: inside and outside alike.** The house was repainted *within and without*.

1. The old charter refers to the parishes *without* the walls. *(1)*
2. They buried him *without* the churchyard, as the custom then required. *(1)*
3. A green hill far away, *without* a city wall. *(1)*
4. The ship was rebuilt *within and without*. *(2)*
5. She was calm *within and without*, whatever it cost her. *(2)*

## Front, back, and beyond

The axis running away from the observer. American English has largely handed the front position to *in front of* and kept *before* for courtrooms and ceremony; at the back, *behind* is doing all the work, with the regional *in back of* alongside it. Farther out, *past* is reckoned from a landmark and *beyond* from a limit.

### before

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *bih-FOR*; weak /bəˈfɔr/ &middot; *buh-FOR*, and in fast speech /bfɔr/ with the first vowel gone entirely
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *fore*) &middot; **Register:** **formal, legal, ceremonial, or literary in the spatial sense**; the temporal sense is neutral and lives in [time](05-time.md#before)
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **In the spatial sense there is no gerund** — *before leaving* is the temporal *before*, not this one. **Never a finite clause after the preposition**; *before he arrived* is the subordinating conjunction, a different word class with the same spelling.
**Pattern:** [stand / appear / kneel] *before* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *before* [noun phrase] (*the task before us*) &middot; [X] comes *before* [Y] (order)
**Contrast:** **In modern American English the ordinary spatial word is [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), not *before*.** *He parked in front of the house* is what people say; *before the house* sounds like a nineteenth-century novel. Spatial *before* survives in three places: courtrooms and hearings (*the case before the judge*), ceremony and scripture (*before God*), and the metaphor of a task or a choice facing someone (*the decision before us*). Watch the word-class trap too — *before* is a preposition with a noun phrase, a **conjunction** with a clause, and an **adverb** with nothing (*I've been here before*).

In front of, in the physical or figurative presence of. The spatial sense is the older one and has been largely displaced in speech by *in front of*, retreating into formal registers where its stiffness is an asset. Two live uses remain fully current: appearing *before* an authority, and a task or choice standing *before* a person.

**1. In front of, in the presence of (formal).** She stood *before* the judge.
**2. Earlier in a sequence or order.** *E* comes *before* *F*.
**3. Facing someone as a task, choice, or prospect.** The question *before* the committee is simple.

1. The witness appeared *before* the grand jury on Thursday. *(1)*
2. He knelt *before* the altar. *(1)*
3. In the alphabet, *M* comes *before* *N*. *(2)*
4. Safety comes *before* speed on this crew. *(2)*
5. The choice *before* us is not a comfortable one. *(3)*
6. A long winter lay *before* them. *(3)*

**See also:** [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [behind](#behind), [opposite](#opposite), [ahead of](11-complex-multiword.md#ahead-of), [before (time)](05-time.md#before)

### behind

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈhaɪnd/ &middot; *bih-HYND*; weak /bəˈhaɪnd/ &middot; *buh-HYND*; the /h/ can drop in very fast speech, giving /bəˈaɪnd/
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *hind*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund in the cause sense** (*the thinking behind closing the plant*, *the money behind building the arena*). **No finite clause** — write *behind the decision to close*, never ✗ *behind they decided to close*, and never a *that*-clause. Also an adverb (*he stayed behind*) and a noun (informal); the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *behind* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *behind* [noun phrase] &middot; *behind* [schedule / on rent] &middot; *behind* [person] (support) &middot; stranded: *the door he was hiding* **behind**
**Variants:** in back of — a genuinely American alternative for the spatial sense, common in speech and regionally strong; unknown in British English and avoided in formal writing
**Contrast:** Against [after](05-time.md#after): *after* orders things in time or sequence, *behind* places them in space or measures a lag. *He arrived after me* is sequence; *he finished behind me* is a gap. Against [beyond](#beyond) and [past](#past), which put something on the far side of a thing rather than at its back. Note also the American variant **in back of**, which is standard in speech and jarring to British ears, and the temporal sense (*behind schedule*), developed in [time](05-time.md#behind).

At the back of something, on the side away from the front or from the observer. From that position the word picks up four extensions that all preserve the geometry: falling back in a race or a schedule, standing back of someone in support, working back of a public event as its hidden cause, and putting an experience back of you in the past.

**1. At the back of, on the far side of.** The trash cans are *behind* the garage.
**2. Later or slower than; in arrears (see also [time](05-time.md#behind)).** We're two weeks *behind* schedule.
**3. Supporting, backing.** The whole department is *behind* her.
**4. Responsible for, as the hidden cause or agent.** No one knows who was *behind* the leak.
**5. In the past, left behind.** All that is *behind* him now.

1. There is a small yard *behind* the building. *(1)*
2. She hid the box *behind* a stack of paint cans. *(1)*
3. The Rangers finished nine games *behind* the leaders. *(2)*
4. He fell *behind* on rent after the layoff. *(2)*
5. The union is solidly *behind* the proposal. *(3)*
6. Get *behind* this or get out of the way. *(3)*
7. Investigators still do not know who was *behind* the transfer. *(4)*
8. There is a real grievance *behind* all the shouting. *(4)*
9. Those years are *behind* her and she does not discuss them. *(5)*
10. Once the audit is *behind* us we can hire again. *(5)*

**See also:** [before](#before), [beyond](#beyond), [past](#past), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [behind (time)](05-time.md#behind)

### beyond

**Pronunciation:** /biˈɑnd/ &middot; *bee-AHND*; also /bɪˈjɑnd/ &middot; *bih-YAHND* and /bɪˈjɔnd/ *bih-YAWND*; weak /bəˈjɑnd/ &middot; *buh-YAHND*
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *yond*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (*beyond saving*, *beyond repairing*), or a **wh-clause** (*beyond what she told us*). **Never a *that*-clause** — *beyond that she told us* is ungrammatical. Also an adverb (*the hills beyond*) and a noun (*the great beyond*).
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *beyond* [noun phrase] &middot; *beyond* [abstract limit] (*beyond repair*) &middot; *beyond* [verb-ing] &middot; *beyond* [wh-clause] &middot; *go beyond* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against [past](#past): *past* is close and dynamic — *just past the bank* is a landmark you have almost reached; *beyond* is static and farther out, and it dominates the abstract senses that *past* barely touches. Against [outside](#outside): both draw a limit, but *outside* draws a boundary around a category (*outside my expertise*), while *beyond* draws a threshold something has exceeded (*beyond my ability*). One grammatical point worth its own line: *beyond* is one of the prepositions that takes a **wh-clause**, which is why *beyond what he admitted* is fine while *beyond that he admitted* is not.

On the far side of something, and by extension past any limit at all. It is one of the most abstractly productive prepositions in English: once the literal sense of "farther than" is in place, everything that can have a limit — repair, belief, control, expectation, dispute — can be *beyond* it. The frozen phrases (*beyond repair*, *beyond belief*, *beyond doubt*) are worth learning as units.

**1. On the far side of; farther away than.** The hills *beyond* the river are all national forest.
**2. Later than a point in time.** The party went on well *beyond* midnight.
**3. Outside the limits, scope, or reach of.** The damage was *beyond* repair.
**4. More than; surpassing.** The response went *beyond* anything we expected.
**5. Apart from, other than (usually in a negative).** We know nothing *beyond* what the letter says.

1. There is nothing *beyond* the last cattle guard but sagebrush. *(1)*
2. The trail continues a half mile *beyond* the falls. *(1)*
3. Deliveries do not run *beyond* six o'clock. *(2)*
4. He stayed *beyond* his welcome by about two hours. *(2)*
5. The engine was *beyond* saving. *(3)*
6. That decision is *beyond* my authority. *(3)*
7. Her generosity went *beyond* anything the family asked for. *(4)*
8. The results exceeded the forecast *beyond* all reason. *(4)*
9. *Beyond* a short statement, the company has said nothing. *(5)*
10. I remember nothing *beyond* what I already told the officer. *(5)*

**See also:** [past](#past), [outside](#outside), [behind](#behind), [across](#across), [above](#above)

### past

**Pronunciation:** /pæst/ &middot; *PAST*; one syllable, full vowel; no weak form, though in connected speech the final /t/ drops before a consonant, giving /pæs/ &middot; *PASS* (*past the store*). Homophonous with *passed*, which — helped by that /t/-dropping — is the source of the commonest spelling error involving this word.
**Type:** simple (from the old participle of *pass*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; **a gerund only in a short frozen list** — *past caring*, *past saving*, *past praying for* — which is not a productive pattern (✗ *past finishing the report*). **No clause of any kind.** Also an adverb (*he walked past*), an adjective (*the past year*), and a noun (*in the past*) — **only the preposition has an object**, which is the test: *he walked **past*** is the adverb, *he walked **past the church*** is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *past* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *past* [landmark] &middot; *just past* [noun phrase] &middot; *past* [abstract limit] (*past caring*) &middot; *put it past* [someone]
**Contrast:** **The spelling trap first: *passed* is the verb, *past* is everything else.** *We passed the church* (verb) but *we drove past the church* (preposition) and *the mailbox is past the church* (preposition). If you can put *did* in front of it, you want *passed*. Against [beyond](#beyond): *past* is near and reckoned from a landmark you have just cleared, *beyond* is farther out and far more abstract. Note that *past* has three homes here: this positional entry, the motion sense in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#past), and the clock sense (*ten past four*) in [time](05-time.md#past).

On the far side of a landmark, reckoned from it. The positional sense is essentially how Americans give directions — everything is *past* the light, *past* the bank, *just past* the second stop sign — and it depends on a shared reference point in a way *beyond* does not. The same geometry then runs along scales instead of streets (*past forty*, *past its expiration date*), and a short frozen list (*past caring*, *past saving*, *wouldn't put it past her*) preserves the oldest sense of having gone beyond a limit for good.

**1. On the far side of a landmark; farther along than.** The clinic is two blocks *past* the light.
**2. Beyond a point on a scale, an age, or a stage of life.** That pitcher is *past* his prime.
**3. Beyond the point of, in the frozen gerund and idiom set.** He is *past* caring what they think.

1. The mailbox is just *past* the church on the right. *(1)*
2. Their driveway is a hundred yards *past* the cattle guard. *(1)*
3. Go *past* the second stop sign and it's on your left. *(1)*
4. That pitcher is *past* his prime and everyone knows it. *(2)*
5. The milk is well *past* its date. *(2)*
6. She was *past* fifty before she learned to swim. *(2)*
7. He is *past* caring what the committee thinks. *(3)*
8. The transmission was *past* saving by the time they looked at it. *(3)*
9. I wouldn't put it *past* her to call the mayor herself. *(3)*

**See also:** [beyond](#beyond), [behind](#behind), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [past (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#past), [past (time)](05-time.md#past)


## Extent, spread, and orientation

Position described by how something lies across a space rather than by a single point. *Across* cuts a line, *along* follows one, *around* encircles, *astride* straddles, and *throughout* fills. The four short words in this section — *down*, *off*, *out*, *up* — are the ones most often mistaken for adverbs, and the test is always the same: the preposition has an object it cannot be separated from.

### across

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkrɔs/ &middot; *uh-KRAWS*; also /əˈkrɑs/ &middot; *uh-KRAHS* in cot/caught-merged speech; the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form; **the spelling and pronunciation *acrosst* are nonstandard**
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *cross*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*he swam across*); the preposition takes an object. In the "facing" sense the object attaches to *from*: *across **from** the school*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *across* [noun phrase] (spanning) &middot; *across from* [noun phrase] (facing) &middot; *across* [area or group] (distribution) &middot; [be-verb] *across* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** acrost, acrosst — dialectal and nonstandard; never write them
**Contrast:** Against [through](04-movement-direction.md#through): *across* treats its object as a **two-dimensional surface** you cover the face of, *through* treats it as a three-dimensional medium you pass inside — *across the field*, *through the woods*. Against [along](#along): *across* cuts a line, *along* follows it. Contact is optional and the senses split on it: sense 1 usually has none (*a bridge **across** the canyon*), sense 4 always has it (*arms folded **across** his chest*). And note the Americanism that matters most here: ***across from*** is the everyday American way to say "facing," where British English uses [opposite](#opposite). The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#across).

Spanning something from one side to the other, or positioned on the far side of it. The static senses collected here are what is left when the motion drains out of the word: a bridge that has already crossed, a scar that already spans a cheek, a store that already sits on the other side of the street. The third sense — distribution over a whole area or population — is now heavy in business and news prose.

**1. Extending from one side to the other; spanning.** A steel bridge *across* the canyon.
**2. On the other side of; facing (usually *across from*).** The deli is *across from* the courthouse.
**3. Distributed over the whole of an area or group.** Enrollment fell *across* every district.
**4. Lying transversely on something, in contact with it.** A blanket *across* her knees.

1. There is a fallen pine *across* the driveway. *(1)*
2. A long scar ran *across* the back of his hand. *(1)*
3. They live *across* the street from us. *(2)*
4. Her office is *across* the hall. *(2)*
5. Costs rose *across* all six divisions. *(3)*
6. The policy applies *across* the entire system. *(3)*
7. He listened with his arms folded *across* his chest. *(4)*
8. She had a wool blanket *across* her knees for the whole drive. *(4)*

**See also:** [along](#along), [opposite](#opposite), [throughout](#throughout), [astride](#astride), [across (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#across)

### along

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAWNG*; also /əˈlɑŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAHNG* in cot/caught-merged speech; the first syllable is always /ə/
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *long*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — and the object must be **something with length**: a road, a river, a wall, a shelf, a route. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb and verb particle (*come along*, *bring it along*, *move along*), where it has no object at all.
**Pattern:** [noun] *along* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *along* [linear object] &middot; *somewhere along* [linear object] &middot; *all along* [linear object]
**Contrast:** **The object has to be long.** *Along the fence* works; *along the room* does not, because a room has no line to follow — and the figurative objects obey the same rule, since a *way*, a *line*, a *spectrum*, and a *continuum* are all lines. Against [across](#across), which cuts the line rather than following it — *walk **along** the beach* is parallel to the water, *walk **across** the beach* runs from the water to the dunes. Against [alongside](#alongside), which requires two separate parallel things: you can walk *along* a wall while touching it, but you walk *alongside* someone. Watch the particle too: in *bring it along*, *along* has no object and is not a preposition.

Following the length of something, or located at some point on that length. The senses are really one geometry seen at different scales — the whole line, a spot on it, or a line that exists only in the mind. What unites every use is that the object must be linear; the moment it is not, the sentence stops working.

**1. In a line following the length of something.** Cottonwoods grow *along* the creek.
**2. At an unspecified point on the length of something.** The leak is somewhere *along* this wall.
**3. At a point on a notional line, scale, or continuum (figurative).** Somewhere *along* the way we stopped asking.

1. There are streetlights *along* the whole east side of the park. *(1)*
2. Fenceposts run *along* the property line for a mile. *(1)*
3. She lined the books up *along* the top shelf. *(1)*
4. Somewhere *along* Route 6 we missed the turn. *(2)*
5. The pipe froze at a joint *along* the north wall. *(2)*
6. The trail loses elevation gradually all *along* the ridge. *(2)*
7. The new proposal is *along* the same lines as last year's. *(3)*
8. Somewhere *along* the way the project lost half its budget. *(3)*

**See also:** [alongside](#alongside), [across](#across), [around](#around), [down](#down), [along (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#along)

### around

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *uh-ROWND*; in casual speech the first syllable can drop entirely, giving /ˈraʊnd/ &middot; *ROWND* (often written *'round*); the full form is /əˈraʊnd/
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *round*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; also a bare number or clock time in the approximation sense (*around noon*, *around fifty*), and a **gerund** in the jargon sense 5 (*questions around hiring contractors*). **No clause, and never a *that*-clause.** Also an adverb (*look around*, *stick around*) and a particle; the preposition takes an object.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; *around* [number or time] &middot; *around here / there* &middot; *around the corner*
**Variants:** round — **chiefly British** for the physical senses; see its own entry
**Contrast:** **Use *around*, not *round*, in American English** for every physical sense: *around the corner*, *around the table*, *around the block*. For approximation, *around* and [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about) are both American and interchangeable, with *around* commoner in speech and *approximately* the formal choice. Sense 5 — *a conversation around funding* — is business jargon that has spread fast and that many editors still delete in favor of *about*. The motion sense (*drove around the lake*) belongs to [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#around), and the temporal approximation is treated in [time](05-time.md#around).

Encircling something, or scattered in its vicinity. From the circle come two useful extensions: getting to the far side by going around an obstruction, and — because a circle drawn around a number is a rough boundary rather than a point — approximation. It is one of the highest-frequency prepositions in American speech and one of the vaguest.

**1. Encircling; on all sides of.** There's a stone wall *around* the garden.
**2. Scattered in the vicinity of; here and there in.** There were toys *around* the whole living room.
**3. On the other side of, reached by going along a curve.** The bakery is *around* the corner.
**4. Approximately, with a number or a time (see also [time](05-time.md#around)).** *Around* fifty people came.
**5. Centered on a topic (business jargon; prefer *about*).** We need a conversation *around* staffing.

1. They put a fence *around* the pool last spring. *(1)*
2. Twelve chairs were set *around* the long table. *(1)*
3. His papers were scattered *around* the office. *(2)*
4. Is there a hardware store *around* here? *(2)*
5. The entrance is *around* the back of the building. *(3)*
6. Her place is just *around* the corner from mine. *(3)*
7. It cost *around* three hundred dollars. *(4)*
8. Let's say *around* seven, give or take. *(4)*
9. The whole meeting was structured *around* risk. *(5)*
10. There are real questions *around* the timeline. *(5)*

**See also:** [round](#round), [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about), [near](#near), [throughout](#throughout), [around (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#around)

### astride

**Pronunciation:** /əˈstraɪd/ &middot; *uh-STRYD*; the first syllable is always /ə/ and never stressed
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *stride*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; common in journalism and military writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb (*she rode astride* — that is, not sidesaddle).
**Pattern:** [sit / ride / stand] *astride* [noun phrase] &middot; [place or thing] *astride* [line or route]
**Contrast:** *Astride* demands **two sides straddled**, which is exactly what [across](#across) does not require: a bridge is *across* a river because it spans it, but a city is *astride* a border because it has territory on both sides. [Over](#over) is the weaker word: it may cover a thing and even touch it (*a blanket **over** the rail*), but it never claims a leg or a side on each side of it, which is the entire content of *astride* (*sitting **astride** the rail*). And note the adverbial use, which carries a specific historical meaning in equestrian contexts — riding *astride* rather than sidesaddle.

With one leg, one side, or one part on each side of something. The literal sense is equestrian, and the figurative sense keeps the geometry exactly: a town *astride* a highway has buildings on both sides of it. Strategic and geopolitical writing likes the word because it implies control of what passes between.

**1. With a leg on each side of something.** She sat *astride* the fence rail.
**2. Extending on both sides of a line, route, or boundary.** The town sits *astride* the state line.

1. He rode *astride* a borrowed mare all afternoon. *(1)*
2. The child sat *astride* his father's shoulders. *(1)*
3. The village lies *astride* the old post road. *(2)*
4. The refinery sprawls *astride* the county line. *(2)*
5. A single garrison stood *astride* the only pass. *(2)*

**See also:** [across](#across), [over](#over), [atop](#atop), [alongside](#alongside), [athwart](04-movement-direction.md#athwart)

### down

**Pronunciation:** /daʊn/ &middot; *DOWN*; one syllable, full diphthong; **no reduced form** — *down* destresses without changing its vowel, which is unusual for a preposition this frequent
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "farther along" sense is characteristically American and informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** *Down* is also an adverb (*sit down*), a verb particle (*turn it down*), an adjective (*the server is down*), a noun (*third down*, *goose down*), and a verb (*downed the drink*) — **the preposition is the only one with an object**, and the test is that you cannot move it: *ran **down** the hill* has no *ran the hill down*.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *down* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *down* [linear object] &middot; *down the street / road / hall* &middot; *down south / down the coast*
**Contrast:** Sense 2 is the one to learn: **in American English *down the street* usually has nothing to do with elevation** — it means "farther along, away from here," and *down* and [along](#along) are nearly interchangeable there. Against [below](#below) and [under](#under), which are pure vertical position with no path involved; *down* always implies a line you could travel. **The British "attributable to" sense — *that's down to the weather*, *it's down to you* — is not American**; Americans say *because of*, *due to*, or *up to you*. The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#down).

At or toward a lower point on something, or simply farther along it. American usage has largely bleached the elevation out of the second sense — you go *down* to the corner store on flat ground — which leaves *down* functioning as a vague marker of distance from the speaker, paired with *up* in a way that is regional as much as geographic. Once distance along a line is all the word carries, it takes the last short step and starts measuring distance in time: *down the road* is next year.

**1. At a lower point on a slope or a vertical surface.** The cabin is *down* the hill from the lodge.
**2. Farther along; away from here (no elevation implied).** The bar is three doors *down* the block.
**3. Toward the south, or along a coast or river (regional and idiomatic).** They moved *down* the coast to Monterey.
**4. Lower in a list, a ranking, or a sequence.** Her name is well *down* the ballot.
**5. Later in time, in the fixed *down the road* and *down the line*.** We can revisit that *down* the road.

1. The trailhead is a quarter mile *down* the ridge. *(1)*
2. Water was running *down* the outside of the foundation. *(1)*
3. The pharmacy is two blocks *down* the street. *(2)*
4. His office is *down* the hall on the left. *(2)*
5. Her family is *down* south for the winter. *(3)*
6. We drove *down* the coast as far as Big Sur. *(3)*
7. He is third *down* the depth chart and unlikely to play. *(4)*
8. The Dodgers slid two spots *down* the standings in a week. *(4)*
9. That shortcut will cost somebody money *down* the road. *(5)*
10. Two owners *down* the line, nobody remembered the handshake deal. *(5)*

**See also:** [up](#up), [below](#below), [along](#along), [under](#under), [down (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#down)

### off

**Pronunciation:** /ɔf/ &middot; *AWF*; also /ɑf/ &middot; *AHF* in cot/caught-merged speech; no reduced form — the vowel stays full
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; ***off of*** is standard American **speech** and is deleted in edited prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*the lid came off*), an adjective (*the milk is off*, *his timing is off*), a noun, a verb, and a very productive particle (*call it off*, *put it off*) — **none of which takes an object, and the preposition always does**: *he is off* is the adjective, *he is off his game* is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *off* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *off* [noun phrase] (branching) &middot; *off the coast of* [place] &middot; *off* [substance] (abstaining) &middot; *off* [standard] (*off his game*)
**Variants:** off of — ubiquitous in American speech, absent from edited prose; ***off from*** is nonstandard everywhere
**Contrast:** **Two usage points.** First, ***off of***: *get off the couch* is the written form and *get off of the couch* is what people say — the *of* is redundant and editors remove it, but it is not a marker of poor speech in America the way it is in Britain. Second, ***off from*** is simply wrong; there is no such preposition. Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from), which names a source without implying prior contact — *she took it **from** the shelf* versus *she took it **off** the shelf*, where *off* says it was resting there. Sense 2 (*a bedroom off the hallway*) has no synonym and is worth learning on its own.

No longer on, or at a remove from, something it was attached to or aligned with. The static senses gathered here all describe the state after separation: a lid not on its jar, a room opening off a corridor, an island at a distance from a coast, a person no longer using a substance, a performance no longer at its usual level.

**1. Not on; separated from a surface it was resting on.** The lid is *off* the jar.
**2. Opening from or branching out of.** There's a half bath *off* the kitchen.
**3. At a distance from, especially seaward of a coast.** An island *off* the coast of Maine.
**4. No longer using or consuming.** He's been *off* caffeine for a month.
**5. Below the usual standard.** The whole team was *off* its game.

1. Keep the dog *off* the furniture. *(1)*
2. One shutter had come *off* the front window. *(1)*
3. The pantry opens *off* the back hallway. *(2)*
4. Their driveway runs *off* County Road 12. *(2)*
5. The wreck lies four miles *off* Cape Hatteras. *(3)*
6. They anchored just *off* the point. *(3)*
7. She's been *off* dairy since January. *(4)*
8. He went *off* his medication without telling anyone. *(4)*
9. The pitcher was *off* his game from the first inning. *(5)*
10. Sales for the quarter came in *off* last year's pace. *(5)*

### out

**Pronunciation:** /aʊt/ &middot; *OWT*; one syllable, full diphthong; no reduced form
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a restricted noun phrase.** As a bare preposition in American English, *out* takes essentially only **openings** (*out the window*, *out the door*, *out the chimney*) and a few direction and route nouns (*out Route 9*, *out back*, *out west*). For everything else, American English requires [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of). **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb (*the fire went out*), an adjective (*the truth is out*), a noun (*a called third strike is an out*), a verb (*the story outed him*), and a very productive particle (*hand it out*, *figure it out*) — **none of which takes an object, and the preposition always does**: *he leaned out* is the adverb, *he leaned out the window* is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [verb] *out* [opening] (*look out the window*) &middot; [live / drive] *out* [route or direction] &middot; *out back / out west / out east*
**Variants:** out of — the general-purpose form; see [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of)
**Contrast:** **This is one of the sharpest American/British splits in the whole class.** Americans say *he leaned **out** the window*; the British say *out **of** the window*, and *out the window* strikes them as substandard. In American English the bare form is not only acceptable but preferred with openings. Where the two agree: with containers, sources, and materials you need *out of* on both sides of the Atlantic — *out of the box*, *out of money*, *made out of oak*. Against [outside](#outside), which is a static position rather than a passage through an opening.

Through an opening to the outside, or along a route leading away from a center. Bare prepositional *out* is a narrow word with a strong American accent: it lives on windows, doors, and highways, and it hands everything else to *out of*. Its enormous life as an adverb, adjective, noun, and particle is separate and is not the preposition.

**1. Through an opening to the outside.** She was staring *out* the window.
**2. Along a road or in a direction leading away from town.** They live *out* Route 9, past the quarry.

1. Don't throw anything *out* the car window. *(1)*
2. He walked *out* the side door without a word. *(1)*
3. Smoke was pouring *out* the vent. *(1)*
4. Their place is a few miles *out* the old highway. *(2)*
5. The dogs are *out* back if you want to see them. *(2)*
6. Her people are all *out* west somewhere. *(2)*

**See also:** [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of), [outside](#outside), [off](#off), [inside](#inside), [out (motion)](04-movement-direction.md#out)

### round

**Pronunciation:** /raʊnd/ &middot; *ROWND*; identical to the noun and adjective *round*; the apostrophe spelling *'round* marks it as a clipping of *around* and belongs to song lyrics and dialect writing
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** **chiefly British** as a general preposition; in American English it survives only in fixed phrases
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — the same range as *around*. No clause, no gerund. Also an adverb, adjective, noun, and verb, all of which are fully standard in American English; **only the general prepositional use is British**.
**Pattern:** *round* [noun phrase] (British) &middot; American fixed forms only: *round the clock*, *year-round*, *round-trip*, *round about* &middot; **British, not American:** *round the bend*, *all round*
**Variants:** around — **the American form**; use it everywhere except in the fixed phrases listed here
**Contrast:** **Americans write [around](#around).** *Round the corner*, *round the table*, and *round the block* are British; the American equivalents all take *around*. What American English keeps is a short list of frozen compounds and idioms where *round* is the only possible form: *round the clock*, *year-round*, *round-trip*, and the dated approximating *round about*. **Do not take *round the bend* (crazy) or *all-round* for American forms** — both are British, and the American equivalents are *around the bend* and *all-around*. Treat any other prepositional *round* in a text as a signal that the writer is British or is imitating speech.

A reduced form of *around* that became a separate word. In British English it is the ordinary preposition for encircling and vicinity; in American English it has been almost entirely displaced by *around* and remains only where it is locked into a compound or an idiom. Learners should recognize it and write *around*.

**1. British: around, in every physical sense.** *Round* the corner &mdash; American *around the corner*.
**2. American: in fixed phrases and compounds only.** The crew worked *round* the clock.

1. British usage: they sat *round* the kitchen table. American: *around* the kitchen table. *(1)*
2. British usage: the shop is just *round* the corner. American: *around* the corner. *(1)*
3. British usage: by the third week he was going *round* the bend. American: *around* the bend. *(1)*
4. Dispatchers staffed the line *round* the clock for six days. *(2)*
5. The resort is open year-*round*. *(2)*
6. We booked a *round*-trip out of Denver and drove from there. *(2)*

**See also:** [around](#around), [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about), [throughout](#throughout), [near](#near), [across](#across)

### throughout

**Pronunciation:** /θruˈaʊt/ &middot; *throo-OWT*; the initial cluster is /θr/, not /tr/; stress falls on the second syllable, so in connected speech the first one reduces to weak /θrəˈaʊt/ &middot; *thruh-OWT*
**Type:** compound (*through* + *out*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** Also an adverb with no object at all (*the house has hardwood throughout*), which is a common and fully standard use.
**Pattern:** *throughout* [bounded space] &middot; *throughout* [organization or region] &middot; [noun phrase] ... *throughout* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** Against [through](04-movement-direction.md#through): *through* traces a path in one side and out the other, *throughout* saturates every part with no path at all. *Water ran **through** the house* is a leak; *there is water damage **throughout** the house* is an insurance claim. Against *all over*, the spoken equivalent — *toys all over the house* and *toys throughout the house* mean the same thing at different registers. Against [across](#across), which distributes over a surface or a set rather than filling a volume. The temporal sense is developed in [time](05-time.md#throughout).

In every part of a space or a body. The word is built to be exhaustive — it claims completeness in a way *in* and *across* do not — which is why it belongs to inspection reports, specifications, and any prose that needs to say *everywhere inside this thing, with no exceptions*.

**1. In every part of a space or a structure.** There is Wi-Fi *throughout* the building.
**2. In every part of an organization, a region, or a body.** The change was announced *throughout* the district.

1. The original tile is intact *throughout* the first floor. *(1)*
2. Smoke detectors are required *throughout* the residence. *(1)*
3. Recycling bins are placed *throughout* the campus. *(1)*

### up

**Pronunciation:** /ʌp/ &middot; *UP*. **No weak form** — the vowel is already the lax /ʌ/ and has nothing further to reduce to, so unstressed *up* simply loses its beat (*up the street* /ʌp ðə ˈstrit/) without changing quality. The same is true of its opposite, [down](#down). In *up to*, only the second word weakens: *UP-tuh*, never *uh-TOO*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "farther along" sense is characteristically American and informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. **No clause, no gerund.** *Up* is also an adverb (*stand up*), a verb particle (*look it up*, *ran up a bill*), an adjective (*the system is up*), a noun (*ups and downs*), and a verb (*upped the price*). **The particle/preposition test is movement**: you can say *ran a bill up* but not *ran the hill up*, because in *ran up the hill* the hill is the object of the preposition.
**Pattern:** [be-verb] *up* [linear object] &middot; [verb] *up* [linear object] &middot; *up the street / road / coast* &middot; *up north*
**Contrast:** The mirror of [down](#down), and it inherits the same American quirk: ***up the block* usually implies no elevation at all**, only distance in a direction the speaker treats as away. Against [above](#above) and [atop](#atop), which are static height with no path; *up* implies a line you could travel. The one thing to be careful about is the word class — *up* is far more often a particle than a preposition, and the movement test above settles it every time. The motion sense is in [movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md#up).

At or toward a higher point on something, or simply farther along it in the direction the speaker treats as away. As with *down*, American usage has drained most of the elevation out of the everyday sense, leaving a vague directional marker that pairs with *down* and varies by region — New Yorkers go *up* to the Bronx, New Englanders *up* to Maine, and neither is about altitude. What is left of the vertical idea survives cleanly in rankings and in two idioms that keep an object and therefore keep the preposition: *up your alley*, *up the creek*.

**1. At a higher point on a slope or a vertical surface.** The cabin is *up* the hill from the road.
**2. Farther along; away from here (no elevation implied).** He lives *up* the block from the school.
**3. Toward the north, or inland along a river or coast.** They have a place *up* north.
**4. Higher in a ranking, a scale, or a sequence.** She moved *up* the depth chart fast.
**5. In the fixed idioms *up someone's alley* and *up the creek*.** A job like that is right *up* her alley.

1. Their mailbox is halfway *up* the driveway. *(1)*
2. There's a crack running *up* the chimney. *(1)*
3. The laundromat is two doors *up* the street. *(2)*
4. She works at a shop *up* the road from here. *(2)*
5. We drove *up* the coast to Mendocino. *(3)*
6. His family summers *up* north on the lake. *(3)*
7. He worked his way *up* the ladder from the loading dock. *(4)*
8. The song climbed *up* the chart for six straight weeks. *(4)*
9. A restoration that size is right *up* his alley. *(5)*
10. If the loan falls through we are *up* the creek. *(5)*
