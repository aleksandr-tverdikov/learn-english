Complex (multi-word) prepositions#

The fixed groups of two, three, and four words that behave as a single preposition — in front of, according to, because of, with regard to — and the rules that keep them frozen.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

A complex preposition is a phrase that has stopped being a phrase. In front of looks like preposition + noun + preposition, but you cannot say in a front of, in the fronts of, or in front upon: the internal parts have fused, and the whole thing takes a single object the way near or before does. That frozenness is the test that separates a real complex preposition from a literal string that merely looks like one — compare frozen in the face of criticism (meaning "despite") with literal in the face of the statue (meaning a face, on a statue).

Three traps run through the entire class, and they account for nearly every error learners make with it.

First, none of these takes a that-clause. A preposition governs a noun phrase, and a that-clause is not one: in the event of takes a noun phrase and in the event that takes a clause; in case of takes a noun phrase and in case takes a clause; on the basis of takes a noun phrase and on the basis that takes a clause; and despite the fact that and in spite of the fact that exist only because despite and in spite of cannot swallow a that-clause on their own. But do not overcorrect. A free relative — a nominal wh-clause like what he said, whether it rains, how the money is spent — is a noun-phrase equivalent, and a large share of these prepositions take one freely: in light of what we learned, contrary to what you may have heard, regardless of whether you attend, because of who he knows, in line with what the committee recommended. The rule to memorize is therefore narrower and sharper than "no clauses": no that-clause, but a wh-clause is a noun phrase and is usually fine. Each entry below states which of the two it accepts.

Second, the last word is fixed and unforgiving — it is with regard to, never with regards to; in accordance with, never in accordance to; in exchange for, never in exchange of; and as regards, which takes no to at all.

Third, where the phrase ends in to, that to is a preposition and not an infinitive marker, so what follows is a noun phrase or an -ing form, never a bare verb: with a view to reducing costs (not to reduce), prior to signing (not to sign), accustomed to working nights (not to work), subject to being audited, opposed to raising taxes. The same rule governs the prepositional verbs that sit just outside this class — look forward to hearing from you, object to being questioned, get used to living alone — and it is the single most common preposition error in learner English.

The in ___ of family#

The largest and most productive frame in the class: in + a bare noun (or the + noun) + of. The noun in the middle is what carries the meaning, and it is almost always article-less — in case of, in light of, in search of, in advance of, in violation of — which is itself the sign of a frozen unit, since English nouns do not normally appear bare in the singular. Where the article is present it is fixed too: in the event of keeps its the, in view of refuses one. Note the shared pronunciation fact: the closing of is unstressed in every member and reduces to /əv/ and then to a bare /ə/, so in front of it is in FRUN-tuh-vit and in light of that is in LY-tuh-that.

in advance of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ədˈvæns əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ədˈvæn sə/ · in ud-VANSS uhv Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral; scheduling, logistics, and news writing Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in advance of filing). Never a that-clause. A nominal wh-clause is barely possible in sense 1 but is entirely normal in sense 2 (well in advance of what anyone expected). Pattern: [clause] in advance of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · In advance of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: Before plus foresight — it says not merely that one thing preceded another but that the earlier thing was done in preparation for the later one, which is why it collocates with deadlines, storms, votes, and deliveries. Against bare before, it is longer and adds that implication of deliberateness; against prior to, which adds nothing at all, it at least earns the extra words. Against ahead of, it is the more formal of the two and lacks ahead of's competitive sense 3. Bare in advance is an adverbial and takes no object: Pay in advance, Let me know in advance — where the preposition always needs one.

1. Earlier than, and in preparation for. The county opened shelters in advance of the storm. 2. Further developed than; ahead of (a standard or era). The design was years in advance of anything else on the market.

  1. The county opened four shelters in advance of the storm.
  2. Ballots must be requested in advance of the October 20 deadline.
  3. In advance of the vote, both sides released their whip counts.
  4. She read the entire file in advance of meeting the family.
  5. Payment is required in advance of delivery, not on receipt.
  6. The design was years in advance of anything else on the market.
  7. His thinking on the subject was well in advance of the profession's.

in anticipation of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃən əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃə nə/ · in an-tiss-uh-PAY-shun uhv Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral; news writing, business, and scheduling Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in anticipation of moving abroad). Never a that-clause — the bare noun anticipation can take one (the anticipation that prices would fall), but the frozen preposition cannot, and anticipating that or plain expecting is how to write a clause. A free relative is fine (in anticipation of what the exhibit would draw). Pattern: [clause] in anticipation of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · In anticipation of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: Against in advance of, which treats the later event as settled fact, in anticipation of puts the reason inside the actor's head: the expected thing may never arrive, and the sentence is still true. That is the whole difference between shelters opened in advance of the storm (the storm came) and shelters opened in anticipation of a storm (they expected one). Against ahead of, it is the more explicitly motivated of the two. Bare in anticipation is an adverbial and takes no object: She waited in anticipation — where the preposition always needs one.

Names the expected event that explains a preparation. Because the motive is an expectation rather than a fact, the phrase survives intact even when the anticipated thing never happens.

  1. The city stockpiled salt in anticipation of a hard winter.
  2. In anticipation of the ruling, both firms had statements drafted.
  3. Shares rose in anticipation of strong holiday sales.
  4. She sold the second car in anticipation of moving abroad.
  5. In anticipation of heavy turnout, the county added six machines.
  6. The museum extended its hours in anticipation of what the exhibit would draw.

in case of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈkeɪs əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈkeɪs ə/ · in KAYSS uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral, with a strong pull toward signage and instructions Complement: noun phrase only, and characteristically a bare article-less noun (in case of fire). Never a that-clause and never a wh-clause — for a clause you need the conjunction in case (Take an umbrella in case it rains) or in the event that. Pattern: In case of [noun phrase], [imperative or clause]. · [clause] in case of [noun phrase]. Contrast: In case of + noun phrase names the emergency; in case + clause names a precaution. In case of fire, use the stairs tells you what to do when there is a fire; Bring a flashlight in case the power goes out tells you to prepare for one. Substituting one for the other is the single most common error with this phrase.

Names the situation that would trigger the instruction attached to it. It is the fixed formula of public safety notices and contract clauses, which is why it survives with a bare noun where ordinary English would demand an article.

  1. In case of fire, use the stairs.
  2. In case of emergency, break the glass.
  3. In case of a tie, the chair casts the deciding vote.
  4. The policy explains what happens in case of cancellation.
  5. In case of rain, the ceremony moves indoors.
  6. Passengers should remain seated in case of turbulence.

See also: in the event of, in the face of, barring, in

in charge of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈtʃɑrdʒ əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈtʃɑrdʒ ə/ · in CHARJ uhv — the of reduces to a bare schwa, so in charge of it comes out in CHARJ uh-vit Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund (in charge of hiring), or a nominal wh-clause (in charge of what goes into the report). Never a that-clause. Frequently stranded or used with no complement at all, in which case of drops: Who's in charge here? Pattern: [person] is in charge of [noun phrase] · the [person] in charge of [noun phrase] · [person] put someone in charge of [noun phrase] Contrast: In charge of and in the charge of reverse the roles. The nurse in charge of the ward runs the ward; the ward in the charge of the nurse is the thing being run. The single word the flips who has the authority, and this is a genuine trap in formal writing.

Assigns responsibility and authority over what follows. The noun charge here is the old sense "a duty entrusted to someone," now surviving almost nowhere else, which is what makes the phrase frozen: you cannot say in a charge of or in full charge of and keep the same meaning.

  1. She is in charge of the entire western region.
  2. Who was in charge of locking up last night?
  3. They put a twenty-six-year-old in charge of a hundred-million-dollar budget.
  4. The agent in charge of the investigation refused to comment.
  5. He is in charge of nothing and complains about everything.
  6. I want to speak to whoever is in charge of customer accounts.

See also: on behalf of, in the name of, on top of

in danger of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈdeɪndʒər əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈdeɪndʒə rə/ · in DAYN-jur uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or gerund, and the gerund is much the commoner (in danger of losing the house, in danger of collapse). Because of is a preposition, in danger of to lose the house is wrong, and so is in danger to fall. Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause. Pattern: be in danger of [noun phrase / verb-ing] · [noun] in danger of [verb-ing] · in real/serious/imminent danger of [verb-ing] Contrast: The bad outcome named after of is one the subject would suffer or commit, which is what keeps The bridge is in danger of collapsing right and The bridge is in danger of the flood wrong — the flood is the threat, not the outcome. For the threat itself the phrase is in danger from (in danger from rising water). Against at risk of, which takes identical complements, this is the more vivid and less statistical of the two; against on the verge of, which is neutral about whether the outcome is welcome, in danger of is always unwelcome. Bare in danger takes no object: She knew she was in danger.

States a bad outcome the subject may be about to suffer or cause. It is one of the few members of this family whose default complement is a gerund rather than a noun, and it takes a degree adverb inside it — in real danger of, in serious danger of — the way in need of does.

  1. Two of the schools are in danger of losing accreditation.
  2. The seawall is in danger of collapse within the decade.
  3. He was in danger of missing the filing deadline entirely.
  4. Nobody here is in any danger of being overpaid.
  5. The species is in danger of disappearing from the state altogether.
  6. She was in real danger of talking herself out of the job.

See also: on the verge of, close to, short of, subject to, in need of

in defiance of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn dɪˈfaɪəns əv/ · connected speech /ɪn dɪˈfaɪən sə/ · in dih-FYE-unss uhv Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral; news writing and legal reporting Complement: noun phrase — an order, ban, law, or authority — or a gerund (in defiance of being told twice), or a free relative (in defiance of what the court had ordered). Never a that-clause. Pattern: [clause] in defiance of [noun phrase]. · In defiance of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: The deliberate twin of in violation of. In violation of reports that a rule was broken and says nothing about intent; in defiance of asserts that the breach was open and meant as a challenge, which is why it attaches to orders, bans, sanctions, and warnings rather than to section numbers. Against in spite of and despite, which merely concede an obstacle, this one requires an authority that was deliberately refused. It always carries a judgment — admiration or condemnation, depending on who is telling the story.

Reports that the action was taken in open refusal of an authority. Because the phrase asserts intent, it is the wrong word wherever the actor may simply not have known the rule.

  1. The march went ahead in defiance of the mayor's order.
  2. Three ships sailed in defiance of the blockade.
  3. In defiance of the ban, the paper published the transcript in full.
  4. She kept the clinic open in defiance of two cease-and-desist letters.
  5. The council raised the levy in defiance of what the state auditor had recommended.
  6. He stayed on the ballot in defiance of his own party's leadership.

See also: in violation of, contrary to, in spite of, regardless of, in defiance of

in excess of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ɪkˈsɛ sə/ · in ik-SESS uhv — the noun here is /ɪkˈsɛs/, stressed on the second syllable, not the /ˈɛksɛs/ of excess baggage Type: complex · Register: formal; financial, legal, and technical Complement: a quantity expression only — a number, a sum, a measure, or a noun naming a limit (in excess of $2 million, in excess of the posted speed). Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause, never a gerund. Pattern: [quantity or clause] in excess of [quantity] · be in excess of [quantity] · amounts in excess of [figure] Contrast: A four-word more than, and outside drafting more than is better: damages in excess of $50,000 is damages over $50,000. It earns its length only where the threshold is legally operative, which is why it is the standard phrasing of jurisdictional minimums, coverage caps, and permit limits. Against over, it has the advantage of being unmistakable — over $50,000 can be misread as spatial or temporal in a crowded sentence, and in excess of cannot. Against short of, it is the exact opposite: one passes the line, the other stops before it.

States that a figure passes a stated threshold. It is a threshold word rather than a comparison word: the object has to be a limit, not simply another quantity being ranked against it.

  1. The plaintiff seeks damages in excess of $75,000.
  2. Repairs are expected to run in excess of two million dollars.
  3. Loads in excess of four tons require a permit.
  4. The fund has held cash in excess of its stated target for six quarters.
  5. Any hours in excess of forty are paid at time and a half.
  6. Rainfall was in excess of the seasonal average for the third year running.

See also: short of, up to, ahead of, in excess of, in the amount of

in favor of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈfeɪvər əv/ · casual /ɪn ˈfeɪvərə/ · in FAY-vur uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, gerund (in favor of raising the cap), or a nominal wh-clause (in favor of whatever gets the building open). Never a that-clause — for that you need in favor of the proposition that. The predicative pattern be in favor of is the commonest form. Pattern: be in favor of [noun phrase / verb-ing] · [reject/drop X] in favor of [noun phrase] · rule/decide in favor of [noun phrase] Variants: in favour of is the British spelling; American English uses favor without the u. Contrast: Against for, this is the deliberative register — I'm for it in conversation, I am in favor of the amendment in a meeting. Against instead of, sense 2 adds a note of preference and judgment: you drop one thing in favor of a better one, but you simply do one thing instead of another. Bare in favor is not this preposition — with no complement it is an adverbial phrase meaning "supporting the motion" (Three voted in favor, two against), and the possessive in his favor is a different construction again (The ruling went in his favor).

1. Supporting. Nine members voted in favor of the measure. 2. Choosing one thing over another. The city dropped the streetcar plan in favor of dedicated bus lanes. 3. To the legal or financial advantage of. The court ruled in favor of the tenants.

  1. Nine members voted in favor of the measure.
  2. Are you in favor of moving the meeting to Thursday?
  3. The city dropped the streetcar plan in favor of dedicated bus lanes.
  4. She turned down the corporate offer in favor of a research fellowship.
  5. The court ruled in favor of the tenants.
  6. Write the check in favor of the trust, not the individual.
  7. Public opinion has shifted sharply in favor of the proposal.

See also: instead of, in place of, in lieu of, as opposed to, for

in front of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈfrʌnt əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈfrʌn(t)ə/ · in FRUNT uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun (in front of me, never in front of I). No gerund — in front of leaving is impossible, because the object has to be a thing or a person that occupies space. Never a that-clause. A free relative is fine when it names such a thing (in front of what used to be the courthouse), since a free relative is a noun phrase; a wh-clause that names a proposition is not (in front of whether he agreed is nonsense). Pattern: [clause] in front of [noun phrase] · In front of [noun phrase], [clause]. · right/directly/just in front of [noun phrase] Contrast: In front of and in the front of are different prepositions. In front of the bus is outside it, ahead of it; in the front of the bus is inside it, in the forward section. Against before, in front of is the ordinary spatial word — before in a spatial sense is formal or literary (He knelt before the altar). Against opposite and across from, in front of does not require the two things to face each other. Bare in front is an adverb, not this preposition, and takes no object: the car in front and He sat in front are complete, where in front of demands one — the car in front of us.

1. Positioned ahead of something, facing it or blocking it. A truck was parked in front of the hydrant. 2. In the presence of an audience, especially an inhibiting one. Don't say that in front of the children.

  1. A truck was parked in front of the hydrant.
  2. Meet me in front of the library at six.
  3. She spilled the whole cup right in front of him.
  4. Don't say that in front of the children.
  5. He had to defend the budget in front of the entire board.
  6. There were forty people in front of us in line.

See also: ahead of, in the middle of, on top of, next to, close to

in lieu of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈlu əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈlu ə/ · in LOO uhv (British English often /ɪn ˈljuː əv/, in LYOO uhv) Type: complex · Register: formal; legal, financial, and funeral-notice English Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in lieu of paying cash); a nominal wh-clause is possible but formal (in lieu of what was promised). Never a that-clause. Pattern: [clause] in lieu of [noun phrase] · In lieu of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: Semantically identical to instead of and in place of, but three registers higher. In lieu of belongs in wills, leases, and obituaries; using it in casual speech reads as stiff or as a mistake for in view of, which it is often confused with and which means something completely different ("considering").

Substitutes one thing for another that was expected or owed. Lieu is a fossil — a French word for "place" that survives in English in this phrase and essentially nowhere else — so the expression admits no modification at all.

  1. In lieu of flowers, the family asks for donations to the food bank.
  2. He accepted stock in lieu of a year-end bonus.
  3. The company offered store credit in lieu of a refund.
  4. Employees may take compensatory time in lieu of overtime pay.
  5. The tenant paid two months' rent in lieu of notice.
  6. In lieu of a formal hearing, the parties agreed to arbitration.

See also: instead of, in place of, in favor of, in view of

in light of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈlaɪt əv/ · flapped /ɪn ˈlaɪɾ əv/ · casual /ɪn ˈlaɪɾ ə/ · in LYTE uhv Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (in light of what we learned yesterday). Never a that-clause — write in light of the fact that if you need one. Pattern: In light of [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] in light of [noun phrase]. Variants: in the light of is the dominant British form; American English overwhelmingly drops the article. Contrast: Near-identical to in view of and given, but in light of implies new information that reframes what came before — you reconsider something in light of fresh evidence. In view of is neutral about novelty. Do not confuse with in lieu of, which means "instead of."

Introduces the consideration that explains or justifies the decision in the main clause. The metaphor is illumination: the object shines on the situation and changes how it looks.

  1. In light of the new evidence, the charges were dropped.
  2. The board reconsidered the layoffs in light of record third-quarter earnings.
  3. In light of recent events, the reception has been postponed.
  4. Her comments look very different in light of what we learned yesterday.
  5. In light of his age, the judge suspended the sentence.
  6. The guidance was rewritten in light of the audit findings.

See also: in view of, in the face of, given, considering, because of

in need of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈnid əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈni də/ · in NEED uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, very often an abstract bare noun (in need of repair). Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause, and never an infinitive — in need of to rest is impossible, and even the gerund is awkward: in need of rest, not in need of resting. Pattern: be in need of [noun phrase] · [noun] in need of [noun phrase] · badly/urgently/sorely in need of [noun phrase] Contrast: In need of is the adjectival, describing form; plain need is the verb. The roof needs repair and the roof is in need of repair mean the same thing, but the phrase is more formal and, unlike the verb, can modify a noun directly: a roof in need of repair.

States a deficiency as a property of the subject. This is one of the few complex prepositions that accepts a degree adverb inside it — badly in need of, sorely in need of — because need here retains enough of its nominal life to be measured.

  1. The building is in need of serious structural work.
  2. He arrived tired, hungry, and in need of a shower.
  3. Families in need of assistance should call the county office.
  4. The argument is sound but badly in need of editing.
  5. She is in need of nothing so much as a week off.
  6. This is a neighborhood in need of investment, not surveillance.

See also: in search of, devoid of, short of

in place of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈpleɪs əv/ · casual /ɪn ˈpleɪsə/ · in PLAYSS uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (in place of what was there before). Never a that-clause. Pattern: [clause] in place of [noun phrase] · In place of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: Interchangeable with instead of in most contexts, but in place of keeps a stronger sense of physical or structural substitution — one thing occupying the slot the other vacated. Instead of is the everyday word and the only one of the three that comfortably takes a gerund in speech; in lieu of is the formal one. Do not confuse with the literal string in the place of, which means "in that location."

Marks a replacement filling a role. The substitute and the original are understood to be comparable — same job, different filler.

  1. Use olive oil in place of butter.
  2. The deputy chaired the meeting in place of the director.
  3. In place of the annual review, managers will hold quarterly check-ins.
  4. They planted native grasses in place of the lawn.
  5. A photograph hung in place of the mirror that had broken.
  6. He offered an apology in place of an explanation.

in receipt of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn rɪˈsit əv/ · connected speech /ɪn rɪˈsi də/ · in ruh-SEET uhv (the p is silent) Type: complex · Register: formal; commercial and bureaucratic correspondence Complement: noun phrase; never a gerund and never a that-clause. A free relative is possible but stiff and rare (in receipt of what the court had ordered). Pattern: be in receipt of [noun phrase] · upon being in receipt of [noun phrase] Contrast: A four-word way of saying have received, and almost always worse. We are in receipt of your letter says exactly what We received your letter says, at twice the length. Keep it only where a formal record is genuinely wanted — benefit determinations, acknowledgments of legal service.

Marks the subject as having taken delivery of something. It survives almost entirely in fixed openings of business letters and in social-services language (households in receipt of benefits).

  1. We are in receipt of your letter of March 3.
  2. Households in receipt of federal assistance are exempt from the fee.
  3. The clerk confirmed that the office was in receipt of the filing.
  4. Once in receipt of payment, we will release the shipment.
  5. Any employee in receipt of such a notice should contact HR immediately.

in respect of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn rɪˈspɛkt əv/ · connected speech /ɪn rɪˈspɛk tə/ · in ruh-SPEKT uhv Type: complex · Register: formal; British and legal. Flagged: this is not the ordinary American form. Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (in respect of what was paid). Never a that-clause. Pattern: [clause] in respect of [noun phrase] Variants: in respect to is a chiefly American variant, itself less common than with respect to, which is the form American writers actually use. British and Commonwealth legal drafting prefers in respect of. Contrast: In respect of means "concerning, with regard to" — it has nothing to do with admiration. American editors will usually change it to with respect to, regarding, or plain about or for. Note that with respect to is neutral and standard in American usage while in respect of reads as an importation.

Introduces the matter the statement is limited to. In British legal and accounting prose it is the default way to attach a topic to a claim; in American prose it is a marked, faintly foreign choice.

  1. No claim may be brought in respect of losses arising before the policy date.
  2. The company made provision in respect of deferred tax.
  3. Payments in respect of prior years must be listed separately.
  4. She was questioned in respect of three separate incidents.
  5. Nothing is owed in respect of the canceled order.

in search of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈsɜrtʃ əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈsɜrtʃ ə/ · in SURCH uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral, with a literary tilt Complement: noun phrase, and never a gerund, since what is sought must be a thing (in search of work, not in search of working). Never a that-clause. A free relative naming a thing is fine and is common (in search of whatever work they could find, in search of what was left of the wreck). Pattern: [clause] in search of [noun phrase] · In search of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: In search of states a purpose and implies motion or effort; looking for is the plain equivalent. Compare in need of, which states a lack without implying that anyone is doing anything about it.

Names what a journey or effort is aimed at obtaining. Almost always follows a verb of motion or of striving, and it is the standard headline formula for a quest.

  1. They moved north in search of work.
  2. He spent the afternoon in search of a parking space.
  3. Whales travel thousands of miles in search of food.
  4. In search of quiet, she rented a cabin for the winter.
  5. Reporters descended on the town in search of a story.
  6. The expedition set out in search of the wreck.
  7. They spent two seasons in search of whatever the first party had left behind.

See also: in need of, for the sake of, with a view to

in spite of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈspaɪt əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈspaɪ də/ · in SPYTE uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in spite of being warned). Never a that-clause — in spite of that he was warned is impossible, and for a clause you must write in spite of the fact that or switch to the conjunction although. A nominal wh-clause is marginal here and best avoided. Pattern: In spite of [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause]. · [clause] in spite of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. Contrast: Fully interchangeable with despite, which has exactly the same complement rule: despite takes a noun phrase (despite the forecast) or a gerund (despite being warned) and never a that-clause — despite that he was warned is wrong, and despite the fact that he was warned is the workaround. Despite is one word shorter and marginally more formal; the only real difference is length and rhythm. Two errors to avoid: the blend despite of, which is never correct in either direction, and in spite of that-clause. In spite of has no connection to the noun spite meaning malice — nobody is being spiteful.

The ___ to family#

An adjective or participle plus to. Every member shares one pronunciation fact worth learning before anything else: the to is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, and it reduces to /tə/, so prior to is PRY-ur tuh and according to is uh-KOR-ding tuh. That same fact produces the family's defining grammatical trap — because the to is prepositional, what follows must be a noun phrase or a gerund, never a bare verb. Prior to submitting the form is correct; prior to submit the form is not.

This is the single most productive error in learner English, and it reaches well past this file. The same prepositional to governs a whole set of adjectives and prepositional verbs that behave identically, and all of them demand an -ing form where the learner reaches for an infinitive:

CorrectWrong
I look forward to hearing from you.I look forward to hear from you.
They object to being questioned twice.They object to be questioned twice.
She is accustomed to working nights.She is accustomed to work nights.
He got used to living alone.He got used to live alone.
We are opposed to raising the fee.We are opposed to raise the fee.
The offer is subject to being approved.The offer is subject to be approved.
The plan is devoted to / committed to cutting waste.committed to cut waste

The test is mechanical: try substituting a plain noun. If I look forward to your letter works, the to is a preposition and the verb after it must be -ing. If a noun cannot go there — I want to your letter is nonsense — the to is an infinitive marker and a bare verb is correct.

Several members of the family are also disputed on usage grounds: due to has the oldest live controversy in English usage, and prior to and subsequent to are bureaucratic padding for before and after.

The as ___ family#

As leads a triple life — preposition (as a doctor), conjunction (as I said), and adverb of degree (as tall as) — and every phrase built on it inherits some of that instability. Never a that-clause — the clause-taking form is as a result of the fact that, which is worth avoiding, or simply because. Pattern: [clause] as a result of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · As a result of [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: The bare adverbial as a result is not this preposition. With no complement it is a conjunctive adverb joining two sentences: Sales fell. As a result, we cut hours. The preposition requires an object. Against because of, this is heavier and frames the object as a cause whose consequences are being traced; against due to, it is adverbial and therefore never disputed.

Names the event or condition whose consequence the main clause reports. It is the causal preposition of reports and investigations, where the point is usually to establish a chain rather than simply assign a reason.

  1. Two hundred jobs were lost as a result of the merger.
  2. As a result of the audit, three policies were rewritten.
  3. She missed a month of school as a result of the surgery.
  4. Premiums rose sharply as a result of last year's claims.
  5. As a result of being understaffed, the clinic cut its weekend hours.
  6. The bridge was closed as a result of damage found during a routine inspection.

as far as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈfɑr æz/ · weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ · az FAR az Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: Three different grammars. As a preposition of distance it takes a noun phrase naming a place or point (as far as Denver). As a topic marker it takes a noun phrase that must be closed by is concerned, goes, or a similar verb — as far as the budget is concerned. As a conjunction it takes a full finite clause (as far as I know). It never takes a gerund. Pattern: [verb of motion] as far as [place] · As far as [noun phrase] is concerned, [clause]. · as far as [clause] (conjunction) Contrast: The truncation trap. In speech Americans routinely drop the completing verb — As far as money, we're fine — and editors routinely restore it. The phrase that needs no verb is as for: As for money, we're fine is correct as it stands. The two are one syllable apart and are constantly swapped. The distance sense is treated with the movement prepositions and the full topic formula with the topic prepositions; both are linked below.

1. To the point of; up to (distance). We drove as far as Santa Fe and turned around. 2. To the extent or limit of (degree). The offer goes as far as free replacement, but no further. 3.   1. We drove as far as Santa Fe and then turned around.

  7. As far as I know, the meeting is still on.

as for#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈfɔr/ · weak /əz fər/ · az FOR Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (as for me, never as for I), gerund (as for finding a replacement), or a nominal wh-clause (as for what happens next). Never a that-clause. Pattern: As for [noun phrase], [clause]. — overwhelmingly sentence-initial; the postposed [clause] as for [noun phrase] is unidiomatic. Contrast: As for needs no completing verb, where as far as does: As for the budget, we're fine / As far as the budget is concerned, we're fine. Against as to, which introduces an uncertainty or a question, as for introduces a contrasting participant that has already been mentioned — it presupposes prior context and cannot open a conversation cold. Against regarding, it is conversational rather than bureaucratic.

Shifts the topic to one item already in play, usually to say something different about it from what was just said about the others. The characteristic rhythm is a list followed by an exception: everyone else did X; as for him, he did Y.

  1. As for me, I'd rather stay home.
  2. The others left at six. As for Dana, nobody knows.
  3. As for the money, we'll figure that out later.
  4. As for your second question, I don't have an answer yet.
  5. As for finding a replacement, the search starts Monday.
  6. He's furious. As for the rest of us, we're just tired.

as per#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈpɜr/ · weak /əz pər/ · az PUR Type: complex · Register: commercial and bureaucratic; widely disliked in edited prose Complement: noun phrase only. Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause, never a gerund. Pattern: [clause] as per [noun phrase]. · As per [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: A redundancy. Latin per already means "according to," so the as adds nothing: per your request and according to your request both say what as per your request says, in fewer words. The one form with a life of its own is the jocular fixed phrase as per usual, which nobody intends seriously.

Cites an instruction, agreement, or standard as the authority for what was done. It is boilerplate — the language of purchase orders and shipping notices — and it signals institutional register more than it carries meaning.

  1. The goods were shipped as per your instructions.
  2. Payment is due in thirty days as per the contract.
  3. As per the attached schedule, the site closes at four.
  4. Invoices will be processed as per company policy.
  5. He was late again, as per usual.
  6. The panels were installed as per the manufacturer's drawings.

as regards#

Pronunciation: /æz rɪˈɡɑrdz/ · weak /əz rɪˈɡɑrdz/ · az rih-GARDZ Type: complex · Register: formal, somewhat old-fashioned Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (as regards what was agreed). Never a that-clause. And note what cannot follow it at all: as regards to is an error — the phrase takes no to. Pattern: As regards [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] as regards [noun phrase]. Contrast: With regard to needs its to; as regards refuses one. The reason is grammatical history: in with regard to, regard is a noun that requires the preposition to, while in as regards the -s is the third-person verb ending of an old impersonal clause ("as [it] regards"), and a verb takes its object directly. Learners who know one form contaminate the other, producing as regards to and with regards to, both wrong.

Introduces the topic of the statement. It is the most formal of the topic markers and reads as slightly archaic in American prose, where regarding, on, or about almost always fits better.

  1. As regards the second contract, negotiations are ongoing.
  2. The report is silent as regards enforcement.
  3. As regards staffing, the department has made no decision.
  4. Nothing has changed as regards eligibility.
  5. As regards funding the expansion, the board remains divided.

Contrast: Two jobs with different fates. Fronted, it is a topic-shifter and a stiffer twin of as for. Mid-sentence after a noun of uncertainty — question, doubt, dispute, evidence, confusion — it introduces the content of that uncertainty, and there it is very often deletable: no question as to whether he signed is simply no question whether he signed. Against about, it is the formal register's choice and is usually padding outside legal drafting.

1. On the subject of (fronted topic-shifter). As to the second charge, the defense concedes nothing. 2. Introducing the content of a question, doubt, or dispute. There is real doubt as to whether the signature is genuine.

  5. She gave no hint as to what she would decide.
  6. The statute is silent as to remedies.
  7. Opinions differ sharply as to how the money should be spent.

as well as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈwɛl æz/ · weak /əz ˈwɛl əz/ · az WELL az Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (as well as what the grant covers). Never a that-clause. When the added item is a verb and the main clause already has a finite verb of its own, the gerund is obligatory: She runs the department as well as teaching two sections, not as well as teach. (The pattern He writes as well as directs, with two finite verbs sharing one subject, is as well as working as a coordinating conjunction, not as this preposition.) Pattern: [subject], as well as [noun phrase], [singular verb] · [clause] as well as [noun phrase / verb-ing]. Contrast: Two traps, both common. First, verb agreement: as well as does not build a compound subject, so The manager, as well as two clerks, was present — singular, where and would give were. The same holds for along with, together with, and in addition to. Second, it is not a synonym for and: it subordinates, presenting the material after it as the lesser or already-known item, so He plays guitar as well as piano foregrounds the guitar. Distinguish also the ordinary comparative as well as meaning "as skillfully as": She sings as well as her sister is a comparison of ability, not an addition.

Adds a second item while keeping it grammatically and rhetorically subordinate to the first. That subordination is the whole point, and it is why writers who mean plain coordination should use and.

  1. The fee covers materials as well as labor.
  2. The senator, as well as two aides, was subpoenaed.
  3. She runs the department as well as teaching two sections a semester.
  4. He speaks Korean as well as Japanese.
  5. As well as the main gallery, there's a sculpture garden out back.
  6. The storm knocked out power in three counties as well as half of the next state.
  7. She sings as well as her sister does, which is saying something.

Remaining fixed phrases#

The leftovers of the class, grouped only by the fact that they fit no frame. Four of them — along with and together with here, as well as and in addition to above — share the single most consequential grammatical fact in this file: they do not create compound subjects, so the verb still agrees with the original subject alone. The rest are individually idiosyncratic. But for demands a counterfactual main clause. Rather than is the only member of the entire class that accepts a bare infinitive. Such as introduces examples where like introduces resemblances. For fear of is the cleanest surviving example of the noun-phrase-versus-clause split, since it keeps a separate conjunction, for fear that, for the clause. And depending on takes wh-clauses as freely as regardless of does, which almost nothing else in this file will do.

but for#

Pronunciation: /bʌt ˈfɔr/ · weak /bət fər/ · but FOR Type: complex · Register: formal, literary, and legal Complement: noun phrase or gerund. Never a that-clause and never a wh-clause — the clause equivalent is the conjunction if it had not been for the fact that, or simply if ... had not.   1. But for the seatbelt, she would not have survived.
  2. But for a last-minute donation, the shelter would have closed.
  3. But for his testimony, the case would never have gone to trial.
  4. The building would be gone but for a single preservation lawsuit.
  5. But for the weather, we would have finished the roof on Tuesday.
  6. But for one dissenting vote, the measure would have passed unanimously.

for fear of#

Pronunciation: /fɔr ˈfɪr əv/ · weak /fər ˈfɪr ə/ · for FEER uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase or gerund (for fear of waking the baby). Never a that-clause — the clause-taking companion is the conjunction for fear that (for fear that the baby would wake), which is a different item, exactly as in case is to in case of. A free relative is fine, since it is a noun phrase (for fear of what the neighbors would say), and never an infinitive: for fear of to wake the baby is wrong. Pattern: [negative or cautious clause] for fear of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · [clause] for fear that [clause] — the conjunction, not this preposition. Variants: out of fear of is the looser, more psychological variant. Do not confuse it with in fear of, which means "afraid of" and names a state rather than a motive: living in fear of deportation is how someone feels, he said nothing for fear of deportation is why he acted. Contrast: It gives the feared outcome as the reason something was not done, so the main clause is characteristically negative, hedged, or evasive — said nothing, never reported it, kept the door locked. Against because of, the feared thing need never have happened at all, which is exactly the difference between a cause and a motive. Against to avoid, which takes the same gerund and names the same purpose, for fear of adds the emotion and concedes the timidity. Its structural neighbor is for lack of, which blames an absence where this one blames an anticipation.

Names the outcome whose prospect stopped the subject from acting. The phrase reports a motive rather than a cause, and the motive is always avoidance.

  1. She said nothing for fear of making it worse.
  2. Nobody reported the leak for fear of losing the contract.
  3. Witnesses would not come forward for fear of retaliation.
  4. They moved the horses inside for fear of the storm.
  5. He avoided the subject entirely for fear of another argument.
  6. The family kept the diagnosis quiet for fear of what the neighbors would say.

for lack of#

Pronunciation: /fɔr ˈlæk əv/ · weak /fər ˈlæ kə/ · for LAK uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, characteristically a bare abstract or mass noun (for lack of evidence, for lack of funding). Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause, never a gerund. Pattern: [clause] for lack of [noun phrase]. · for lack of anything better · for lack of a better [noun] Variants: For want of is the older and more literary twin — For want of a nail the shoe was lost — and is somewhat commoner in British English; for lack of is the ordinary American form. Both take the same complement, and neither puts an article on the noun unless the noun demands one. Contrast: It states a cause that consists in an absence, which is exactly what separates it from because of: dismissed because of the evidence and dismissed for lack of evidence say opposite things. Against in the absence of, which supplies a condition and looks forward (in the absence of an agreement, state law governs), for lack of supplies a reason and looks back at something that has already failed. The apologetic formula for lack of a better word is fixed and cannot be varied.

Gives the missing thing as the reason for the outcome, and the outcome is nearly always a failure, a refusal, or a collapse. Because the phrase blames an absence, it cannot take an object that was present.

  1. The case was dismissed for lack of evidence.
  2. The project died for lack of funding.
  3. For lack of a better word, the meeting was a disaster.
  4. Two positions went unfilled for lack of qualified applicants.
  5. The garden failed for lack of water, not for lack of attention.

Against with a view to, which takes the same gerund but implies the goal has not been reached, for the purpose of simply names the aim; against for the sake of, which names a beneficiary or a value, this names an objective.

States the aim an action was designed to serve. Its survival is entirely institutional: it marks the purpose as officially declared rather than merely intended.

  1. The land was acquired for the purpose of building a transfer station.
  2. The account exists for the purpose of holding client funds and nothing else.
  3. For the purposes of this section, "resident" means a person domiciled in the state.
  4. Records may be retained for the purpose of audit.
  5. He formed the company for the purpose of limiting his personal liability.

for the sake of#

Pronunciation: /fɔr ðə ˈseɪk əv/ · weak /fər ðə ˈseɪk əv/ · casual /fər ðə ˈseɪ kə/ · for thuh SAYK uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase or gerund (for the sake of keeping the peace). Never a that-clause; a free relative is possible but uncommon (for the sake of whoever comes after us). Pattern: [clause] for the sake of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · For the sake of [noun phrase], [clause]. · for [possessive] sake (fixed: for heaven's sake) Variants: for the sake of argument and the possessive pattern for argument's sake are both current; the possessive drops both the and of. Against on behalf of, which means "as the agent of," this means "for the benefit of," and confusing them makes a speaker claim authority they do not have. The possessive forms — for heaven's sake, for old times' sake, for goodness' sake — are a separate frozen construction and take no of.

Names the person served or the end pursued. The purpose reading is the commoner one in writing, where it introduces a stylistic or argumentative goal rather than a beneficiary.

1. For the benefit of (a person or group). They stayed together for the sake of the children. 2. In order to achieve or preserve (a purpose or quality). For the sake of clarity, let me restate the question.

  1. They stayed together for the sake of the children.
  2. She took the fall for the sake of the team.
  3. He gave up the appeal for the sake of his mother, who could not face another hearing.
  4. For the sake of clarity, let me restate the question.
  5. He conceded the point for the sake of argument.
  6. For the sake of keeping the peace, nobody mentioned the will.
  7. Don't argue for the sake of arguing.

Pronunciation: /seɪv ˈfɔr/ · weak /seɪv fər/ · sayv FOR Type: complex · Register: formal and literary Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal wh-clause (save for what little was left). In American prose it reads as a deliberate stylistic choice rather than a neutral one, and using it in ordinary business writing draws attention to itself. Against but for, which is counterfactual and demands a conditional main clause, save for simply subtracts from a real situation. Do not confuse the preposition with the verb save; the preposition never has a subject of its own.

Removes one item from an otherwise complete picture. Its natural home is descriptive narrative, where the exception is meant to stand out against an emptiness or a uniformity.

  1. The street was silent save for a dog barking two blocks over.

  3. Save for one abstention, the vote was unanimous.
  4. He remembered nothing of the evening save for the drive home.
  5. Every ship in the harbor was lost, save for the two in dry dock.
  6. Save for an hour on Sunday, she worked the entire week.

such as#

Pronunciation: /sʌtʃ ˈæz/ · weak /sətʃ əz/ · such AZ Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase (usually a list) or gerund. Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause. The complement must be a genuine member of the class named before it — fruits such as apples, not fruits such as sweetness. Pattern: [general noun] such as [example], [example] · [clause], such as [noun phrase] Contrast: Such as introduces examples; like introduces resemblances. The traditional rule reserves like for comparison and such as for exemplification: a writer like Hemingway means someone who resembles him, a writer such as Hemingway means him, offered as an instance. Speech ignores the distinction; edited prose still keeps it. Note the related split in the same pair: as a preposition like takes a noun phrase (He works like a machine), while as introduces a role (He works as a machine operator) and, as a conjunction, a full clause (Do as I say) — like I say is spoken English that edited prose still changes to as I say. Two mechanical points on such as: no comma before it when the examples restrict the class (fruits such as apples and pears), a comma when they merely illustrate; and do not end the list with etc., since such as already announces that the list is partial. Neither half stands alone: bare such is a determiner (such a mess) and bare as is a different preposition or a conjunction; only the pair is this item.

Offers one or more members of a class as illustrations of it. The class must be stated first, which is why such as cannot open a sentence the way like sometimes can.

  1. Root vegetables such as carrots and parsnips keep for months.
  2. Heavy industries such as steel and shipbuilding left the region entirely.
  3. She avoids stimulants such as coffee and black tea.
  4. The grant covers direct costs such as travel, equipment, and printing.
  5. Small tasks such as returning calls pile up fast.
  6. Cities such as Tucson and Phoenix face the same water problem.
