Complex (multi-word) prepositions#

The fixed groups of two, three, and four words that behave as a single preposition — in front of, according to, because of, with regard to — and the rules that keep them frozen.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

A complex preposition is a phrase that has stopped being a phrase. In front of looks like preposition + noun + preposition, but you cannot say in a front of, in the fronts of, or in front upon: the internal parts have fused, and the whole thing takes a single object the way near or before does. That frozenness is the test that separates a real complex preposition from a literal string that merely looks like one — compare frozen in the face of criticism (meaning "despite") with literal in the face of the statue (meaning a face, on a statue).

Three traps run through the entire class, and they account for nearly every error learners make with it.

First, none of these takes a that-clause. A preposition governs a noun phrase, and a that-clause is not one: in the event of takes a noun phrase and in the event that takes a clause; in case of takes a noun phrase and in case takes a clause; on the basis of takes a noun phrase and on the basis that takes a clause; and despite the fact that and in spite of the fact that exist only because despite and in spite of cannot swallow a that-clause on their own. But do not overcorrect. A free relative — a nominal wh-clause like what he said, whether it rains, how the money is spent — is a noun-phrase equivalent, and a large share of these prepositions take one freely: in light of what we learned, contrary to what you may have heard, regardless of whether you attend, because of who he knows, in line with what the committee recommended. The rule to memorize is therefore narrower and sharper than "no clauses": no that-clause, but a wh-clause is a noun phrase and is usually fine. Each entry below states which of the two it accepts.

Second, the last word is fixed and unforgiving — it is with regard to, never with regards to; in accordance with, never in accordance to; in exchange for, never in exchange of; and as regards, which takes no to at all.

Third, where the phrase ends in to, that to is a preposition and not an infinitive marker, so what follows is a noun phrase or an -ing form, never a bare verb: with a view to reducing costs (not to reduce), prior to signing (not to sign), accustomed to working nights (not to work), subject to being audited, opposed to raising taxes. The same rule governs the prepositional verbs that sit just outside this class — look forward to hearing from you, object to being questioned, get used to living alone — and it is the single most common preposition error in learner English.

The in ___ of family#

The largest and most productive frame in the class: in + a bare noun (or the + noun) + of. The noun in the middle is what carries the meaning, and it is almost always article-less — in case of, in light of, in search of, in advance of, in violation of — which is itself the sign of a frozen unit, since English nouns do not normally appear bare in the singular. Where the article is present it is fixed too: in the event of keeps its the, in view of refuses one. Note the shared pronunciation fact: the closing of is unstressed in every member and reduces to /əv/ and then to a bare /ə/, so in front of it is in FRUN-tuh-vit and in light of that is in LY-tuh-that.

Русский
до, в преддверии — заранее и в порядке подготовки; тж. опережая (эпоху, уровень)
Type
complex
Register
formal to neutral; scheduling, logistics, and news writing
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (in advance of filing). Never a that-clause. A nominal wh-clause is barely possible in sense 1 but is entirely normal in sense 2 (well in advance of what anyone expected).
Pattern
[clause] in advance of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · In advance of [noun phrase], [clause].
Contrast
Before plus foresight — it says not merely that one thing preceded another but that the earlier thing was done in preparation for the later one, which is why it collocates with deadlines, storms, votes, and deliveries. Against bare before, it is longer and adds that implication of deliberateness; against prior to, which adds nothing at all, it at least earns the extra words. Against ahead of, it is the more formal of the two and lacks ahead of's competitive sense 3. Bare in advance is an adverbial and takes no object: Pay in advance, Let me know in advance — where the preposition always needs one.

1. Earlier than, and in preparation for. The county opened shelters in advance of the storm. 2. Further developed than; ahead of (a standard or era). The design was years in advance of anything else on the market.

  1. The county opened four shelters in advance of the storm.
  2. Ballots must be requested in advance of the October 20 deadline.
  3. In advance of the vote, both sides released their whip counts.
  4. She read the entire file in advance of meeting the family.
  5. Payment is required in advance of delivery, not on receipt.
  6. The design was years in advance of anything else on the market.
  7. His thinking on the subject was well in advance of the profession's.

Pronunciation
/ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃən əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃə nə/ · in an-tiss-uh-PAY-shun uhv
Русский
в ожидании, в предвидении — готовясь к ожидаемому (которое может и не наступить)
Type
complex
Register
formal to neutral; news writing, business, and scheduling
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (in anticipation of moving abroad). Never a that-clause — the bare noun anticipation can take one (the anticipation that prices would fall), but the frozen preposition cannot, and anticipating that or plain expecting is how to write a clause. A free relative is fine (in anticipation of what the exhibit would draw).
Pattern
[clause] in anticipation of [noun phrase / verb-ing]. · In anticipation of [noun phrase], [clause].
Contrast
Against in advance of, which treats the later event as settled fact, in anticipation of puts the reason inside the actor's head: the expected thing may never arrive, and the sentence is still true. That is the whole difference between shelters opened in advance of the storm (the storm came) and shelters opened in anticipation of a storm (they expected one). Against ahead of, it is the more explicitly motivated of the two. Bare in anticipation is an adverbial and takes no object: She waited in anticipation — where the preposition always needs one.

Names the expected event that explains a preparation. Because the motive is an expectation rather than a fact, the phrase survives intact even when the anticipated thing never happens.

  1. The city stockpiled salt in anticipation of a hard winter.
  2. In anticipation of the ruling, both firms had statements drafted.
  3. Shares rose in anticipation of strong holiday sales.
  4. She sold the second car in anticipation of moving abroad.
  5. In anticipation of heavy turnout, the county added six machines.
  6. The museum extended its hours in anticipation of what the exhibit would draw.

Русский
в благоговении перед — восхищение с примесью робости, ощущение своей малости
Type
complex
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase or objective-case pronoun (in awe of him); a gerund is possible but uncommon (in awe of being asked at all). Never a that-clause. A free relative is idiomatic (in awe of what she had built).
Pattern
be/stand in awe of [noun phrase] · a little/completely in awe of [noun phrase] · hold [someone] in awe
Contrast
The emotion is admiration with fear folded into it — you are impressed by a performance but in awe of the performer, and only the second implies that you feel small. Against afraid of, which is dread with no admiration, and against impressed with, which is admiration with no smallness, in awe of is exactly the mixture of the two. Bare in awe is an adverbial and takes no object: She just stood there in awe. Note also that the frozen preposition is of; in awe at is not the fixed form.

Reports admiration with intimidation folded into it, directed at a person, a skill, or a scale of thing. The construction is predicative — it attaches to be, stand, sit, remain — and it admits a degree adverb inside it, a little in awe of, completely in awe of, the way in need of does.

  1. New associates are visibly in awe of her.
  2. He grew up in awe of his older brother and never quite got over it.
  3. Standing at the base of the dam, you are in awe of the sheer engineering.
  4. I'm a little in awe of anyone who can keep four kids fed and quiet.
  5. The junior staff were in awe of what she had built in eight years.
  6. Nobody who has heard him play live is anything but in awe of the man.

in behalf of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn bɪˈhæf əv/ · connected speech /ɪn bɪˈhæ fə/ · in bih-HAF uhv
Русский
в интересах, ради блага кого-то — амер., устар.; ср. on behalf of «от имени»
Type
complex
Register
formal and receding; American English only, and now chiefly in careful legal, charitable, and religious writing
Complement
noun phrase naming a person or group who benefits. Never a that-clause, never a gerund, and no wh-clause.
Pattern
[clause] in behalf of [noun phrase]. · In behalf of [noun phrase], [clause].
Variants
on behalf of is the form nearly all American writers now use for both meanings; British English has never used in behalf of at all.
Contrast
The traditional American split is in behalf of = "for the benefit of," on behalf of = "as the agent or representative of." She spoke on behalf of the union means the union sent her; She spoke in behalf of the strikers means she spoke for their good, whether or not they asked her to. The distinction is prescriptive, it is losing, and on behalf of has absorbed both senses in ordinary American prose — but it is still observed in careful drafting, so recognize it even if you do not write it.

Names the party whose interest the action serves. The entry exists almost entirely for its contrast with on behalf of: if you cannot say which of the two meanings you want, write for the benefit of and be done with it.

  1. The bishop appealed in behalf of the families left homeless by the fire.
  2. Funds were raised in behalf of the widows of the men killed in the mine.
  3. He testified in behalf of a former employee, though nobody had asked him to.
  4. In behalf of the children of this district, I urge you to vote no.
  5. The society was founded in behalf of seamen stranded in foreign ports.
  6. Careful drafters still write in behalf of a beneficiary and on behalf of an agent.

in breach of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˈbritʃ əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈbri tʃə/ · in BREECH uhv
Русский
в нарушение (договора) — юр.; о неисполнении принятого на себя обязательства
Type
complex
Register
formal; contract law, employment disputes, and the business press
Complement
noun phrase naming an obligation — a contract, a covenant, a duty, an undertaking (in breach of contract, in breach of her fiduciary duty). Never a that-clause and never a gerund: in breach of paying late is impossible, and the way to say it is by paying late. A free relative is marginal at best.
Pattern
be/find [someone] in breach of [noun phrase] · [clause] in breach of [noun phrase] · in breach of contract (article-less, fixed)
Contrast
Four near-neighbors divide the labor, and the division is worth memorizing. In breach of is for agreements — contracts, leases, covenants, settlements. In violation of is for rules — statutes, regulations, court orders, terms of service. In contravention of does the same work as in violation of two registers higher. In defiance of adds deliberate refusal, which none of the other three claims. Bare in breach is adverbial and appears with no object in British legal prose (the tenant is in breach); American usage prefers to name the thing breached.

States that an obligation created by agreement has not been performed. The frozen collocation in breach of contract keeps no article, and the whole expression resists ordinary modification: not in serious breach of but in material breach of, which is itself a term of art.

  1. The court found the developer in breach of the settlement agreement.
  2. Selling the mailing list was plainly in breach of the confidentiality clause.
  3. She resigned rather than act in breach of her fiduciary duty.
  4. The tenant is in breach of the lease for the third time this year.
  5. In breach of the covenant, the company opened a competing store two blocks away.
  6. Any party in breach of Section 9 must cure the default within thirty days.

in case of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˈkeɪs əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˈkeɪs ə/ · in KAYSS uhv
Русский
в случае (чего-то) — называет ситуацию, при которой действует данное указание
Type
complex
Register
neutral, with a strong pull toward signage and instructions
Complement
noun phrase only, and characteristically a bare article-less noun (in case of fire). Never a that-clause and never a wh-clause — for a clause you need the conjunction in case (Take an umbrella in case it rains) or in the event that.
Pattern
In case of [noun phrase], [imperative or clause]. · [clause] in case of [noun phrase].
Contrast
In case of + noun phrase names the emergency; in case + clause names a precaution. In case of fire, use the stairs tells you what to do when there is a fire; Bring a flashlight in case the power goes out tells you to prepare for one. Substituting one for the other is the single most common error with this phrase.

Names the situation that would trigger the instruction attached to it. It is the fixed formula of public safety notices and contract clauses, which is why it survives with a bare noun where ordinary English would demand an article.

  1. In case of fire, use the stairs.
  2. In case of emergency, break the glass.
  3. In case of a tie, the chair casts the deciding vote.
  4. The policy explains what happens in case of cancellation.
  5. In case of rain, the ceremony moves indoors.
  6. Passengers should remain seated in case of turbulence.

in contravention of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˌkɑntrəˈvɛnʃən əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˌkɑntrəˈvɛnʃə nə/ · in kahn-truh-VEN-shun uhv
Русский
в нарушение (нормы) — юр., книжн.; регистром выше, чем in violation of
Type
complex
Register
formal; legal, regulatory, and diplomatic. An American meets it in agency orders, sanctions notices, treaty language, and appellate opinions, and essentially nowhere else.
Complement
noun phrase naming a rule, statute, treaty, order, permit, or policy. Never a that-clause and never a gerund.
Pattern
[clause] in contravention of [noun phrase]. · In contravention of [noun phrase], [clause].
Contrast
A one-for-one substitute for in violation of, and outside legal and diplomatic drafting in violation of is the better word — American courts use both, but contravention is heavier and its verb contravene lives in the same narrow registers. Against in breach of, which is for agreements, in contravention of is for rules imposed from above. Against contrary to, which merely reports inconsistency, this one asserts that something forbidden was actually done.

Reports that conduct went against a rule or an instrument that forbade it. Notice that the noun is contravention — not controversion, not contradiction — and that the phrase always closes with of.

  1. The shipments were made in contravention of the sanctions regime.
  2. Two directors voted in contravention of the company's own bylaws.
  3. In contravention of the treaty, the fleet remained in the strait for six weeks.
  4. The agency found the discharge in contravention of the facility's permit.
  5. Any transfer in contravention of this section is void.
  6. He was disbarred for conduct in contravention of the state's rules of professional responsibility.

Русский
под угрозой, рискующий — о плохом исходе, который может вот-вот наступить
Type

Complement
noun phrase or gerund, and the gerund is much the commoner (in danger of losing the house, in danger of collapse). Because of is a preposition, in danger of to lose the house is wrong, and so is in danger to fall. Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause.
Pattern

Contrast
The bad outcome named after of is one the subject would suffer or commit, which is what keeps The bridge is in danger of collapsing right and The bridge is in danger of the flood wrong — the flood is the threat, not the outcome. Against at risk of, which takes identical complements, this is the more vivid and less statistical of the two; against on the verge of, which is neutral about whether the outcome is welcome, in danger of is always unwelcome. Bare in danger takes no object: She knew she was in danger.

States a bad outcome the subject may be about to suffer or cause. It is one of the few members of this family whose default complement is a gerund rather than a noun, and it takes a degree adverb inside it — in real danger of, in serious danger of — the way in need of does.

  1. Two of the schools are in danger of losing accreditation.
  2. The seawall is in danger of collapse within the decade.
  3. He was in danger of missing the filing deadline entirely.
  4. Nobody here is in any danger of being overpaid.
  5. The species is in danger of disappearing from the state altogether.
  6. She was in real danger of talking herself out of the job.

in default of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn dɪˈfɔlt əv/ · also with noun stress /ɪn ˈdifɔlt əv/ · connected speech /ɪn dɪˈfɔl də/ · in dih-FAWLT uhv
Русский
за неимением, при неисполнении — юр.; условие включения запасного правила
Type
complex
Register
formal and somewhat archaic; wills, trusts, promissory notes, and older literary prose
Complement
noun phrase, normally naming something that was owed and did not arrive — payment, issue (children), agreement, proof. A gerund is possible but rare (in default of paying within ten days). Never a that-clause and never a wh-clause.
Pattern
In default of [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] in default of [noun phrase].
Contrast
It means "if the named thing is not supplied," which makes it a condition, not an accusation — and that is exactly where it parts company with in breach of. In default of payment, the note accelerates says what happens if nobody pays; in breach of the note says somebody broke it. Against in the absence of and failing, it is the older and narrower word and clusters around money and inheritance. Do not confuse it with the verb pattern default on (default on a loan), and note the separate fossil in default of a better word, a stiff way of saying "for want of a better word."

Supplies the negative condition — the thing owed that never came — under which a fallback rule takes effect. It is a drafting word, and outside wills and notes failing or if there is no will nearly always read better.

  1. In default of payment, the entire balance becomes due at once.
  2. The estate passes to her nephews in default of issue.
  3. In default of agreement, the rent is set by an independent appraiser.
  4. In default of proof to the contrary, the presumption stands.
  5. He called it, in default of a better word, a reorganization.
  6. In default of any nomination by the board, the chair appoints.

in defiance of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn dɪˈfaɪəns əv/ · connected speech /ɪn dɪˈfaɪən sə/ · in dih-FYE-unss uhv
Русский
вопреки, наперекор — с открытым и намеренным неподчинением власти
Type
complex
Register
formal to neutral; news writing and legal reporting
Complement
noun phrase — an order, ban, law, or authority — or a gerund (in defiance of being told twice), or a free relative (in defiance of what the court had ordered). Never a that-clause.
Pattern
[clause] in defiance of [noun phrase]. · In defiance of [noun phrase], [clause].
Contrast
The deliberate twin of in violation of. In violation of reports that a rule was broken and says nothing about intent; in defiance of asserts that the breach was open and meant as a challenge, which is why it attaches to orders, bans, sanctions, and warnings rather than to section numbers. Against in spite of and despite, which merely concede an obstacle, this one requires an authority that was deliberately refused. It always carries a judgment — admiration or condemnation, depending on who is telling the story.

Reports that the action was taken in open refusal of an authority. Because the phrase asserts intent, it is the wrong word wherever the actor may simply not have known the rule.

  1. The march went ahead in defiance of the mayor's order.
  2. Three ships sailed in defiance of the blockade.
  3. In defiance of the ban, the paper published the transcript in full.
  4. She kept the clinic open in defiance of two cease-and-desist letters.
  5. The council raised the levy in defiance of what the state auditor had recommended.
  6. He stayed on the ballot in defiance of his own party's leadership.

in derogation of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˌdɛrəˈɡeɪʃən əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ˌdɛrəˈɡeɪʃə nə/ · in derr-uh-GAY-shun uhv
Русский
в умаление (права) — юр.; ущемляет правовой режим, не обязательно нарушая его
Type
complex
Register
legal and formal only; an American meets it in appellate opinions, statutory-construction briefs, and the first year of law school
Complement
noun phrase naming a right, a rule, a body of law, or an instrument (in derogation of the common law, in derogation of the shareholders' rights). Never a that-clause, never a gerund, never a wh-clause.
Pattern
[noun] in derogation of [noun phrase] · [clause] in derogation of [noun phrase]
Contrast
It means "detracting from, cutting into, partially displacing" — not "insulting," which is what the everyday adjective derogatory means, and that false friend is the whole reason the phrase gets misused. Against in violation of, which says a rule was broken, in derogation of says a rule was narrowed or displaced, usually quite lawfully. The canonical sentence every American law student learns is statutes in derogation of the common law are strictly construed.

States that something takes away from a right or a body of law without necessarily breaking it. Because the relation is encroachment rather than breach, the object is nearly always a legal regime rather than a person or an act.

  1. Statutes in derogation of the common law are strictly construed.
  2. The clause operates in derogation of the tenant's statutory right to notice.
  3. Counsel argued that the ordinance stood in derogation of the state constitution.
  4. Nothing in this agreement shall be read in derogation of the parties' existing remedies.
  5. The court refused to find an implied waiver in derogation of a vested right.
  6. The rule was attacked as in derogation of two centuries of maritime practice.

in excess of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ɪkˈsɛs əv/ · connected speech /ɪn ɪkˈsɛ sə/ · in ik-SESS uhv — the noun here is /ɪkˈsɛs/, stressed on the second syllable, not the /ˈɛksɛs/ of excess baggage
Русский
свыше, сверх — превышая названный порог (не просто «больше, чем»)
Type
complex
Register
formal; financial, legal, and technical
Complement
a quantity expression only — a number, a sum, a measure, or a noun naming a limit (in excess of $2 million, in excess of the posted speed). Never a that-clause, never a wh-clause, never a gerund.
Pattern
[quantity or clause] in excess of [quantity] · be in excess of [quantity] · amounts in excess of [figure]
Contrast
A four-word more than, and outside drafting more than is better: damages in excess of $50,000 is damages over $50,000. It earns its length only where the threshold is legally operative, which is why it is the standard phrasing of jurisdictional minimums, coverage caps, and permit limits. Against over, it has the advantage of being unmistakable — over $50,000 can be misread as spatial or temporal in a crowded sentence, and in excess of cannot. Against short of, it is the exact opposite: one passes the line, the other stops before it.

States that a figure passes a stated threshold. It is a threshold word rather than a comparison word: the object has to be a limit, not simply another quantity being ranked against it.

  1. The plaintiff seeks damages in excess of $75,000.
  2. Repairs are expected to run in excess of two million dollars.
  3. Loads in excess of four tons require a permit.
  4. The fund has held cash in excess of its stated target for six quarters.
  5. Any hours in excess of forty are paid at time and a half.
  6. Rainfall was in excess of the seasonal average for the third year running.

The ___ to family#

An adjective or participle plus to. Every member shares one pronunciation fact worth learning before anything else: the to is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, and it reduces to /tə/, so prior to is PRY-ur tuh and according to is uh-KOR-ding tuh. That same fact produces the family's defining grammatical trap — because the to is prepositional, what follows must be a noun phrase or a gerund, never a bare verb. Prior to submitting the form is correct; prior to submit the form is not.

This is the single most productive error in learner English, and it reaches well past this file. The same prepositional to governs a whole set of adjectives and prepositional verbs that behave identically, and all of them demand an -ing form where the learner reaches for an infinitive:

CorrectWrong
I look forward to hearing from you.I look forward to hear from you.
They object to being questioned twice.They object to be questioned twice.
She is accustomed to working nights.She is accustomed to work nights.
He got used to living alone.He got used to live alone.
We are opposed to raising the fee.We are opposed to raise the fee.
The offer is subject to being approved.The offer is subject to be approved.
The plan is devoted to / committed to cutting waste.committed to cut waste

The test is mechanical: try substituting a plain noun. If I look forward to your letter works, the to is a preposition and the verb after it must be -ing. If a noun cannot go there — I want to your letter is nonsense — the to is an infinitive marker and a bare verb is correct.

Several members of the family are also disputed on usage grounds: due to has the oldest live controversy in English usage, and prior to and subsequent to are bureaucratic padding for before and after.

Remaining fixed phrases#

The leftovers of the class, grouped only by the fact that they fit no frame. Four of them — along with and together with here, as well as and in addition to above — share the single most consequential grammatical fact in this file: they do not create compound subjects, so the verb still agrees with the original subject alone. The rest are individually idiosyncratic. But for demands a counterfactual main clause. Rather than is the only member of the entire class that accepts a bare infinitive. Such as introduces examples where like introduces resemblances. For fear of is the cleanest surviving example of the noun-phrase-versus-clause split, since it keeps a separate conjunction, for fear that, for the clause. And depending on takes wh-clauses as freely as regardless of does, which almost nothing else in this file will do.
