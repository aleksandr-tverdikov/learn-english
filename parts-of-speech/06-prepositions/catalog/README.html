The American English Preposition Dictionary#

Every preposition in American English — simple, compound, multi-word, participial, and bound — 584 entries across 13 categories.

Prepositions are a closed class, so this dictionary aims at genuine completeness. They are also the most polysemous class in the language: on alone has more than twenty distinct senses, so entries number their senses and give examples for each rather than collapsing them into a single gloss.

Each entry carries an IPA pronunciation with the reduced form (of /əv/ → /ə/, to /tə/ — what you actually hear), a type and register label, a complement line saying what can and cannot follow, a pattern, a contrast with its confusable neighbor, and at least five example sentences.

Open the audio browser to search all 584 entries and hear any of them spoken.

Where to start#

The first two files carry most of the difficulty. in, on, at are the highest-frequency and highest-error prepositions in English; of, to, for, from, by, with carry more grammatical load than lexical meaning and vanish into reductions in speech. Read those two before anything else.

How to read an entry#

### despite

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈspaɪt/ · *dih-SPYT*       ← IPA, respelling, and weak form
**Type:** simple · **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund —          ← what may and may NOT follow
               never a finite clause
**Pattern:** despite [noun phrase]               ← substitute your own words in
**Contrast:** Despite of is always an error.     ← the confusable neighbor

Complement is the line to read first. It is what prevents the two most common preposition errors in English: putting a finite clause after a preposition that cannot take one (despite he was late), and putting a bare infinitive where a gerund is required (look forward to go).
