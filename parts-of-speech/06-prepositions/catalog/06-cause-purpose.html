Cause, reason, purpose, and result#

The prepositions that answer why — what made it happen, what somebody was aiming at, and what came of it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Everything in this file links two things by a why relation, and the relation runs in one of three directions: backward to a cause (because of the fog), forward to a purpose (with a view to selling), or outward to a result (as a result of the crash). Almost all of these words are complex prepositions — two, three, or four words welded into a single unit — because English ran out of simple ones for this job centuries ago and started building.

Two traps run through the whole group. The first is grammatical and very nearly absolute: no preposition in this file can take a that-clause. Every one of them has a clause-taking twin built with that or the fact that, and that twin is a conjunction — because of the rain but because it rained, on the grounds of fraud but on the grounds that he lied. Exactly one word in the file takes a finite clause at all, and it takes only a wh-clause: a dispute over whether the vote was binding, confusion over how the money was spent. Even over refuses that. The second trap belongs to the purpose phrases, which split into two lists that never mix: in order to, so as to, in an effort to, and with intent to take a bare infinitive, while with a view to, for the purpose of, with an eye to, with the aim of, and in the hope of take a gerund. With a view to reduce costs is the error this file exists to prevent.

Six of the nine core prepositions do heavy causal work — for, from, of, with, by, and to — but their entries live in the core relational prepositions and the core: in, on, at. What they do in this field is gathered in a table at the end.

A note on the pronunciations below. Nearly every phrase in this file ends in of or to or begins with for, in, or with, and in connected speech those function words reduce hard. Each entry gives the citation form first and the weak form after it, but the reductions are the same everywhere: of /əv/ → /ə/ (because of → /bɪˈkəzə/, in light of → /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/), to /tu/ → /tə/ (due to, owing to, with a view to), for /fɔr/ → /fɚ/ or /fər/ (for the sake of → /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/), at /æt/ → /ət/, from /frʌm/ → /frəm/ (arising from, resulting from), and with /wɪθ, wɪð/ → /wəθ, wəð/ (what with, with an eye to). The strong forms come back only under stress or at the end of a phrase — What did you do it out of? keeps /ʌv/.

Cause and reason#

The words that point backward to what produced the event. They differ almost entirely in register and in what kind of cause they will accept: because of takes anything, out of takes only a motive inside the actor, through takes only an abstract failing, on the grounds of takes only a stated justification, on the pretext of takes a stated justification the writer is calling a lie, attributable to takes a measured share of an effect and assigns it, and thanks to comes with an attitude attached. Two of them are not standard written English at all — along of and 'count of are dialect, set down here so you can recognize them rather than write them — and two more, by operation of and in reliance on, live entirely inside the law. Entries are alphabetical.

along of#

Pronunciation
/əˈlɔŋ əv/ · weak /əˈlɔŋə/ · uh-LAWNG uhv · also /əˈlɑŋ əv/
Русский
из-за, по вине — устар. и диал.; во 2-м знач. вместе с, в компании с
Type
complex
Register
archaic and dialectal — English regional speech (West Country, Kentish, later Cockney) from the seventeenth century on, carried into older rural American speech and now met almost only in nineteenth-century fiction, in folk song, and in dialogue written to sound old
Variants
'long of and long of are the same phrase with the first syllable worn away, and they are the commoner spellings in American dialect writing. Alonger is a further-worn British form.
Complement
noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — along of you, along of the drink. No gerund (✗ along of missing the tide) and never a finite clause. It also refuses fronting: there is no Along of you, we missed the tide. The phrase sits after the verb, very often after all.
Pattern
[clause] all along of [noun phrase] · [clause] along of [noun phrase] · (sense 2) come / go / take [something] along of [pronoun]
Contrast
Two senses that look like one word and are not. The causal sense descends from Old English gelang ("dependent on, owing to") and has nothing to do with motion at all; the accompaniment sense is the ordinary along of along with. The two fell together in spelling centuries ago and have been confusing readers of dialect ever since. In the causal sense it is a country cousin of because of and on account of, and like them it belongs to speech. Do not read it as ordinary modern usage — a present-day American who writes all along of the weather is writing costume drama, and the only live reason to know the phrase is that Hardy, Kipling, and the ballads are full of it.

1. Because of; owing to — the causal sense. From Old English gelang, and usually with all. It's all along of you we missed the tide. 2. Along with; in the company of — the accompaniment sense. He's gone off along of the others.

  1. It's all along of you that we missed the tide.
  2. The crop failed, and that along of a summer nobody had seen the like of.
  3. She lost the cottage along of a debt her husband never mentioned.
  4. "All along of the drink," he said, "and nothing else."
  5. He's gone off up the lane along of the dog.
  6. Take the little one along of you and mind the gate.
  7. The boys went down to the river along of their uncle.

attributable to#

Pronunciation
/əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tu/ · weak /əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tə/ · uh-TRIB-yuh-tuh-buhl too
Русский
объясняемый чем-л., относимый на счёт чего-л. — книжн., о доле причины
Type
complex (adjective plus to)
Register
formal — audits, reports, epidemiology, and the courts
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (attributable to having used last year's figures). Never a finite clause — attributable to the fact that is the clause form, and it is clumsy enough that because is nearly always better.
Pattern
[noun] is attributable to [noun phrase] · [noun] attributable to [noun phrase] · directly / largely / wholly / partly / in part attributable to [noun phrase]
Contrast
This is the phrase the whole due to rule is built on: the traditional test for due to is whether attributable to can be swapped in, and where it can, due to is safe under every analysis — see the due to rule at the end of this file. Like adjectival due to, it follows be or postmodifies a noun and does not front: Attributable to fog, the flight was canceled is not English, where Due to fog, … at least gets argued about. Against because of, it is cooler and more quantitative, and it is the word of choice wherever a share of an effect is being assigned rather than a whole one.

Assigns an effect, or a measured portion of one, to a cause. The object is the cause and the noun in front of the phrase is the effect, which puts it on the same side of the direction trap as as a result of.

  1. Most of the decline is attributable to a single bad quarter.
  2. Roughly a third of the increase is attributable to population growth alone.
  3. Deaths attributable to secondhand smoke have fallen by half since 1990.
  4. The cost overrun is attributable to delays nobody could have foreseen.
  5. Losses attributable to the outage were covered under the policy.
  6. The discrepancy is attributable to having used last year's figures.
  7. Only a small part of the warming is attributable to natural variation.

because of#

Pronunciation
/bɪˈkɔz əv/ or /bɪˈkʌz əv/ · weak /bɪˈkəz əv/ · casual /ˈkʌzə/ ('cause of) · bih-KAWZ uhv
Русский
из-за, по причине — основной, нейтральный предлог причины
Type
complex
Register
neutral — the default causal preposition of American English
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (because of being late), or a fused relative, which is still a noun phrase (because of who he knows, because of what she said). Never a finite clause and never a that-clause. The clause form is the conjunction because standing alone — because it rained, never because of it rained and never because of that it rained.
Pattern
[clause] because of [noun phrase] · Because of [noun phrase], [clause] · because of [verb-ing] · partly / largely / mainly / only / precisely because of [noun phrase]
Contrast
Because + clause against because of + noun phrase is the cleanest preposition/conjunction pair in the language: We stayed home because it rained / We stayed home because of the rain. Against due to, because of is adverbial by nature and no editor has ever objected to it, which makes it the safe choice whenever you are unsure — see the due to rule at the end of this file. Against owing to and on account of, it is the plain, unmarked word. The internet construction because science, because reasons puts a bare noun after because alone; it is a deliberate joke and is not standard English.

Names the cause of whatever the main clause reports. It modifies the verb or the entire clause, and that adverbial job is exactly what the traditional usage rule reserved for it and denied to due to. It is neutral about whether the cause is welcome, which is what separates it from thanks to (grateful or sarcastic), courtesy of (wry), and owing to (formal).

  1. The flight was canceled because of fog.
  2. Because of the strike, deliveries are running two days late.
  3. She missed the deadline because of a family emergency.
  4. He got the job because of who he knows, not what he knows.
  5. Because of being out of the country all spring, he never received the notice.
  6. Attendance is down, mostly because of the parking.
  7. We're only here because of you.

by operation of#

Pronunciation
/baɪ ˌɑpəˈreɪʃən əv/ · weak /bə ˌɑpəˈreɪʃən ə/ · by-op-uh-RAY-shuhn uhv
Русский
в силу (закона) — юр.; о последствии, наступающем автоматически, без чьих-либо действий
Type
complex
Register
legal — statutes, deeds, title opinions, and the anti-assignment clause of almost every commercial contract; the one place a non-lawyer meets it is the fixed phrase by operation of law
Complement
noun phrase, and in practice a very short list of them — law, the statute, this section, the trust instrument, the merger doctrine. No gerund (✗ by operation of transferring the deed) and never a finite clause. The object names a rule, never a person: ✗ by operation of the trustee.
Pattern
by operation of law · [clause] by operation of [statute or instrument] · by operation of law or otherwise · whether by agreement or by operation of law
Contrast
Names a consequence that arrives automatically, with nobody acting to bring it about — which is the whole content of the phrase and the reason drafters cannot do without it. A joint tenant's share passes by operation of law the moment the co-tenant dies, and no one signs anything; that is why an anti-assignment clause says by operation of law or otherwise, to catch the transfers a merger or a death produces without an assignment. Against by virtue of, which names the standing or qualification that lets somebody act, and against pursuant to, which names authority somebody is exercising: both of those have a human actor behind them and by operation of deliberately has none.

Names the legal machinery itself as the cause: the result follows from a rule, automatically, without an act by any party.

  1. On her death the title passed to the survivor by operation of law.
  2. This lease may not be assigned, whether voluntarily or by operation of law.
  3. The debt was discharged by operation of law when the limitations period ran out.
  4. The easement was extinguished by operation of the merger doctrine, not by any release.
  5. A dissolved corporation loses its franchise by operation of the statute rather than by any act of the secretary of state.
  6. In most states an earlier will was revoked by operation of law on marriage until the probate code was rewritten.
  7. Nothing in this section prevents a transfer by operation of law to a surviving spouse.

by reason of#

Pronunciation
/baɪ ˈrizən əv/ · weak /bə ˈrizən ə/ · by-REE-zuhn uhv
Русский
по причине, вследствие — юр. и книжн.; вне канцелярии звучит напыщенно
Type
complex
Register
legal and formal — outside statutes and pleadings it sounds stilted
Complement
noun phrase, characteristically an abstract one (by reason of insanity, by reason of age). A gerund is possible (by reason of having served twenty years) but uncommon. Never a finite clause — the clausal form is the archaic by reason that.
Pattern
[clause] by reason of [noun phrase] · [noun] by reason of [noun phrase] · not guilty by reason of [noun phrase]
Contrast
Because of means the same thing and is shorter. By reason of survives because legal drafting is conservative and because a handful of fixed phrases have hardened around it — not guilty by reason of insanity, disqualified by reason of age. Do not confuse it with by virtue of, which names a qualification that enables something rather than a cause that produces it: you hold the seat by virtue of your office and you lose it by reason of your conviction.

The formal, chiefly legal way of naming a cause or a ground. In modern American writing it appears almost entirely in statutes, insurance policies, and court filings, plus the set phrases those documents have pushed out into general use.

  1. He was found not guilty by reason of insanity.
  2. No applicant may be refused by reason of race, religion, or national origin.
  3. The lease terminates by reason of the tenant's default.
  4. Benefits were denied by reason of his failure to file on time.
  5. She was excused from jury service by reason of ill health.
  6. Any loss by reason of fire is covered under Section 4.

by virtue of#

Pronunciation
/baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu əv/ · weak /bə ˈvɜrtʃu ə/ · by-VUR-choo uhv
Русский
в силу, на основании — книжн.; называет статус или качество, дающее право
Type
complex
Register
formal
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (by virtue of being the oldest). Never a finite clause — for a clause you need by virtue of the fact that, which is heavy enough that because is almost always the better answer.
Pattern
[clause] by virtue of [noun phrase] · By virtue of [noun phrase], [clause] · by virtue of [verb-ing]
Contrast
By virtue of names a qualification — a status, office, property, or fact that entitles or enables — where because of names a plain cause. He got in by virtue of his last name says the name functioned as a credential; he got in because of his last name says only that it was the reason. Against by dint of, which names effort rather than standing: by dint of hard work, but by virtue of seniority. Virtue here carries its old sense of "power, efficacy," not moral goodness.

Names the status, office, property, or established fact that entitles or enables the main event — the cause understood as a qualification rather than as a force.

  1. By virtue of her office, the vice president presides over the Senate.
  2. He inherited the title by virtue of being the eldest son.
  3. The clause is enforceable by virtue of Section 12 of the agreement.
  4. By virtue of its size alone, the company sets the price for the whole region.
  5. She had access to the files by virtue of her security clearance.
  6. Sound travels faster through steel by virtue of its density.

'count of#

Pronunciation
/ˈkaʊnt əv/ · weak /ˈkaʊntə/, written counta in dialogue · KOWNT uhv, casual KOWN-tuh
Русский
из-за, потому как — простореч. и диал., стяжённое on account of
Type
complex, aphetic — on account of with the front worn off
Register
nonstandard and regional — Southern, Appalachian, South Midland, and African American English speech. In print it is eye-dialect, and an American meets it in fiction, in song lyrics, and in oral-history transcripts, essentially never in edited prose.
Variants
on count of keeps the on; 'count of and count of drop it; count o' drops the f as well. All of them are on account of at different stages of wear, and the apostrophe is the writer's mark rather than anything the speaker does.
Complement
noun phrase or gerund, exactly as with on account of — and, in the varieties where it lives, a full finite clause as well: 'count of it was raining. That clause is ordinary inside the dialect and nonstandard in edited writing, which is the same split the on account of entry describes. Never a that-clause.
Pattern
[clause] 'count of [noun phrase] · [clause] 'count of [verb-ing] · (dialect) [clause] 'count of [clause] · on count of [noun phrase]
Contrast
Not a separate preposition but a worn-down on account of, exactly as 'cause is a worn-down because — and the parallel runs all the way down, since both are unremarkable in speech, both are marked in writing, and both live mostly in reported dialogue. Use it to place a speaker in fiction; do not use it in your own prose, and do not treat the clause pattern as an error in somebody else's mouth. Against because of, the neutral written word, and against on account of, which is itself already one step down in register.

The clipped, regional form of on account of. Recognize it, and write because of.

  1. "We never went, 'count of the creek was up."
  2. School let out early 'count of the ice.
  3. "It ain't 'count of the money," he said, "and you know it."
  4. He quit the mill 'count of his back.
  5. "On count of what happened last spring, nobody's asking her twice."
  6. She stayed home 'count of being the only one in the house who could drive.
  7. The verse is there 'count of the rhyme and nothing else.

due to#

Pronunciation
/ˈdu tu/ · weak /ˈdu tə/ · DOO too · (British /ˈdjuː/, DYOO)
Русский
вызванный чем-л., обусловленный чем-л.; наречно — из-за, по причине
Type
complex
Register
neutral, with a live usage dispute over sense 2
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (due to being understaffed). Never a finite clause — for a clause you need due to the fact that, which spends nine syllables on what because does in two. Note that be due to + a bare infinitive (the train is due to arrive at six) is a completely different construction: the adjective due plus a real infinitive, meaning "scheduled."
Pattern
[noun] is due to [noun phrase] · [noun] due to [noun phrase] · [clause] due to [noun phrase] · Due to [noun phrase], [clause]
Contrast
See the due to vs. because of rule at the end of this file — this is the single most-taught prescriptive rule about causal prepositions and the one most widely ignored. In short: traditionalists allow due to only where attributable to would fit and where it attaches to a noun, and prescribe because of or owing to everywhere else. Because of is never wrong; due to sometimes is, depending entirely on your reader.

1. Attributable to; caused by — adjectival, after be or modifying a noun. The delay was due to fog. 2. Because of — adverbial, modifying the whole clause. Standard in American English, still contested. Due to fog, the flight was canceled.

  1. The delay was due to fog over the bay.
  2. Most of the increase is due to a change in how the data is collected.
  3. Absences due to illness must be reported by nine.
  4. Errors due to rounding may make the columns disagree.
  5. Due to the holiday, there will be no pickup on Monday.
  6. The clinic cut its hours due to being understaffed all summer.
  7. Two flights were canceled due to mechanical problems.
  8. The shortfall was due to a single contract nobody had budgeted for.
  9. Service was suspended due to flooding on the lower platform.

Pronunciation

Русский

Type

Complement
noun phrase, characteristically a bare one with no article — for lack of evidence, for lack of funding, for lack of a better word. A gerund is possible but rare. Never a finite clause.
Pattern
[clause] for lack of [noun phrase] · For lack of [noun phrase], [clause] · for lack of a better [noun] · for lack of anything better

For lack of and for want of are one preposition in two vocabularies — lack is the everyday word, want the literary one — and for lack of is the one to use in ordinary American prose. Both name a missing cause: the reason something failed is that something else was absent. Watch the direction against because of: because of the evidence means the evidence caused it, for lack of evidence means the absence did.

Names an absence as the cause: the thing failed because something necessary was not there.

  1. The case was dismissed for lack of evidence.
  2. The project died for lack of funding.
  3. He agreed, for lack of a better option.
  4. Call it a hunch, for lack of a better word.
  5. Three houses burned for lack of a working hydrant.
  6. She stayed in the job for lack of anywhere else to go.

for want of#

Pronunciation
/fɔr ˈwɑnt əv/ · also /ˈwɔnt/ · weak /fɚ ˈwɑnt ə/ · stressed for /fɔr/ · fer-WAHNT uhv
Русский
за неимением, за недостатком — книжн., с оттенком пословицы
Type
complex
Register
neutral to literary — proverbial, and noticeably formal in American English
Complement
noun phrase, usually bare (for want of a nail). A gerund is possible but unusual. Never a finite clause.
Pattern
[clause] for want of [noun phrase] · For want of [noun phrase], [clause] · for want of a better [noun] · for want of anything better to do

Identical in meaning to for lack of; the whole difference is register. The literary twin of for lack of: it names an absence as the cause of a failure, using want in its old sense of "shortage."

in light of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˈlaɪt əv/ · weak /ɪn ˈlaɪt ə/, run together as /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/ · in-LYT uhv
Русский
в свете чего-л., учитывая — о новых сведениях, а не о физической причине
Type
complex
Register
neutral to formal
Variants
in the light of, with the article, is the dominant British form. American English strongly prefers in light of, and the article-less form is now spreading in British writing too.
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (in light of having missed two payments). Never a finite clause — use in light of the fact that if you must have one.
Pattern
In light of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] in light of [noun phrase] · in light of [verb-ing]
Contrast
In light of names the information that leads someone to a decision, not the physical force that produces an event. You cancel a launch in light of the forecast, but the rocket does not explode in light of a faulty valve — that is because of. Against in view of, the two are near-identical; in light of is commoner in American English and suggests something newly learned, while in view of suggests a standing circumstance.

Names the new information or evidence in the light of which a decision is taken or reconsidered.

  1. In light of the new evidence, the district attorney reopened the case.
  2. The board postponed the vote in light of the auditor's report.
  3. In light of everything that's happened, a delay seems reasonable.
  4. The policy was rewritten in light of last year's lawsuit.
  5. In light of his record, the judge suspended the sentence.
  6. We reconsidered in light of having lost two of our biggest accounts.

in reliance on#

Pronunciation
/ɪn rɪˈlaɪəns ɑn/ · weak /ɪn rɪˈlaɪəns ən/ · in-rih-LY-uhns on
Русский
полагаясь на, доверившись — юр.; о поступке, совершённом на основании заявления
Type
complex
Register
formal and legal — contracts, opinion letters, securities filings, and fraud and estoppel pleadings
Variants
in reliance upon is the heavier drafting form and is usual in older instruments. In reasonable reliance on and in justifiable reliance on are the pleading formulas, and the modifier there is doing legal work rather than decorating.
Complement
noun phrase — a statement, representation, promise, opinion, certificate, or document — or a gerund (in reliance on being told the title was clear). Never a finite clause. The clause hangs off the noun instead: in in reliance on the representation that the title was clear, the that belongs to representation, not to the preposition.
Pattern
[clause] in reliance on [noun phrase] · In reliance on [noun phrase], [clause] · in reasonable reliance on [noun phrase] · in reliance upon [noun phrase] · in reliance on [verb-ing]
Contrast

Result and consequence#

Nine phrases: eight of them built on the words result, consequence, cause, arise, and secondary, plus to the detriment of, which is the odd one out. The eight carry a trap that catches learners and native speakers alike: the object of every one of them is the cause, not the result. In four people were hospitalized as a result of the crash, the crash is the cause and the hospitalizations are the result — the phrase names what the result came out of. The adverbs formed from the same words (as a result, consequently, in consequence) point the other way and take no object at all. To the detriment of points somewhere else again: its object is neither the cause nor the result but whoever the result fell on. Entries are alphabetical.

Purpose and intention#

The words that point forward to an aim. Their meanings barely differ; what differs is what they take, and that difference is absolute. In order to, so as to, in an effort to, and with intent to take a bare infinitive and cannot take a noun at all, which strictly disqualifies the first two as prepositions — they are infinitival subordinators sitting in a preposition's slot, and they are listed here because that is where learners look for them. Everything else in this section takes a gerund or a noun phrase and refuses the infinitive: with a view to, with an eye to, with the aim of, for the purpose of, for the sake of, for fear of, in the hope of, in the interest of, in contemplation of, in preparation for, in recognition of, and on pain of, and — noun only, no gerund — for the benefit of, in aid of, in furtherance of, in honor of, in mitigation of, in obedience to, in pursuance of, in pursuit of, and in token of. The tell is the last word. Where the phrase ends in a preposition — of in nearly all of them, for in in preparation for, and the deceptive to of with a view to and in obedience to — any verb after it must be a gerund, and in several of them no verb fits at all. Where the phrase ends in the infinitive marker to, the verb is bare. With a view to is the trap, because its to is a preposition wearing the other one's clothes. /ɪn ˌkɑntəmˈpleɪʃən əv/ · weak /ɪn ˌkɑntəmˈpleɪʃən ə/ · in-kon-tuhm-PLAY-shuhn uhv
Русский
в предвидении, в расчёте на — юр.: дарение в предвидении смерти
Type
complex
Register
legal and formal — wills and estate-tax practice, bankruptcy, family law, and the litigation-privilege rules; an American meets it in the phrases a gift in contemplation of death and a will made in contemplation of marriage
Complement
noun phrase naming an anticipated event — death, marriage, divorce, bankruptcy, litigation, a sale — or a gerund (in contemplation of filing for bankruptcy). Never a bare infinitive (✗ in contemplation of to file) and never a finite clause. In contemplation that + clause does occur in older drafting and is rare enough that most drafters recast; in contemplation of the fact that is not idiomatic at all.
Pattern
[noun] made in contemplation of [noun phrase] · [clause] in contemplation of [verb-ing] · a gift in contemplation of death · in contemplation of marriage · prepared in contemplation of litigation
Contrast
The phrase does two jobs at once: it names an expected event and asserts that the act was done with that event in mind, which is why it carries legal consequences a plain time preposition would not. A transfer before bankruptcy is a fact about the calendar; a transfer in contemplation of bankruptcy is a claim about the transferor's mind, and it can be undone. Against in preparation for, which claims only that arrangements were being made and accuses nobody; against in anticipation of, the ordinary-English near-synonym, which carries none of the legal charge. Nothing to do with contemplation in the sense of quiet thought.

Names the event an act was done with an eye to — and, in law, asserts that the actor had that event in mind, which is what makes the act reviewable.

  1. A gift made in contemplation of death is pulled back into the taxable estate.
  2. The will was executed in contemplation of marriage and survived the wedding intact.
  3. Transfers made in contemplation of bankruptcy can be set aside by the trustee.
  4. The memo was prepared in contemplation of litigation and is therefore privileged.
  5. In contemplation of filing an appeal, counsel ordered the full trial transcript.
  6. They signed the lease in contemplation of a rezoning that never came.
  7. Nothing was done in contemplation of insolvency, and the trustee never seriously argued otherwise.

Against under penalty of, the American oath formula, which certifies that something is true rather than threatening someone who does not act.

The due to vs. because of rule#

The rule as it is taught. Due to is an adjective phrase. Like any adjective, it should modify a noun — either directly (absences due to illness) or through a linking verb (the delay was due to fog). Because of is an adverb phrase and modifies verbs and whole clauses (we were delayed because of fog). The test: if you can swap in caused by or attributable to and the sentence still stands, due to is safe. If you cannot, the traditional rule wants because of, owing to, or on account of.

SentenceTraditional verdictWhy
The delay was due to fog.correctfollows be and modifies the noun delay

The flight was canceled due to fog.disputedmodifies the verb canceled
Due to fog, the flight was canceled.most disputedmodifies the whole clause, and is fronted
The flight was canceled because of fog.correctbecause of is adverbial by nature

What actually happens. The adverbial due to has been standard in American English for the better part of a century. It appears in national newspapers, in federal regulations, in airline announcements, and in the prose of writers nobody would call careless, and the major descriptive usage guides accept it without qualification. The prohibition survives in three places: older style manuals, a handful of standardized tests, and copy editors trained on both.

What to do about it. Because of is correct under every analysis, in every position, for every reader — it costs nothing to prefer it, and that is the only practical argument for the rule. Reserve due to for the positions the rule allows, after be or attached to a noun, where it is not merely permitted but often better: increases due to inflation is tighter than increases that were caused by inflation. And note that fronting draws the most fire — Due to fog, … at the head of a sentence is the version an editor is likeliest to change.

The one rule that covers the whole file#

No preposition in this file can take a that-clause. Every one of them has a clause-taking counterpart formed with that or the fact that, and the counterpart is a conjunction, not a preposition.

There is exactly one crack in the wall, and it is worth knowing precisely: over takes a wh-clause — a dispute over whether the vote was binding, confusion over how the money was spent, a fight over who pays — and nothing else in this file does. Even over refuses that: an argument over that he lied is not English. What with takes a small clause (what with the kids being sick), which is not finite either. Those two aside, the ban is total in edited English.
