Cause, reason, purpose, and result#

The prepositions that answer why — what made it happen, what somebody was aiming at, and what came of it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Everything in this file links two things by a why relation, and the relation runs in one of three directions: backward to a cause (because of the fog), forward to a purpose (with a view to selling), or outward to a result (as a result of the crash). Almost all of these words are complex prepositions — two, three, or four words welded into a single unit — because English ran out of simple ones for this job centuries ago and started building.

Two traps run through the whole group. The first is grammatical and very nearly absolute: no preposition in this file can take a that-clause. Every one of them has a clause-taking twin built with that or the fact that, and that twin is a conjunction — because of the rain but because it rained, on the grounds of fraud but on the grounds that he lied. Exactly one word in the file takes a finite clause at all, and it takes only a wh-clause: a dispute over whether the vote was binding, confusion over how the money was spent. Even over refuses that. The second trap belongs to the purpose phrases, which split into two lists that never mix: in order to, so as to, in an effort to, and with intent to take a bare infinitive, while with a view to, for the purpose of, with an eye to, with the aim of, and in the hope of take a gerund. With a view to reduce costs is the error this file exists to prevent.

Six of the nine core prepositions do heavy causal work — for, from, of, with, by, and to — but their entries live in the core relational prepositions and the core: in, on, at. What they do in this field is gathered in a table at the end.

A note on the pronunciations below. Nearly every phrase in this file ends in of or to or begins with for, in, or with, and in connected speech those function words reduce hard. Each entry gives the citation form first and the weak form after it, but the reductions are the same everywhere: of /əv/ → /ə/ (because of → /bɪˈkəzə/, in light of → /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/), to /tu/ → /tə/ (due to, owing to, with a view to), for /fɔr/ → /fɚ/ or /fər/ (for the sake of → /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/), at /æt/ → /ət/, from /frʌm/ → /frəm/ (arising from, resulting from), and with /wɪθ, wɪð/ → /wəθ, wəð/ (what with, with an eye to). The strong forms come back only under stress or at the end of a phrase — What did you do it out of? keeps /ʌv/.

Cause and reason#

The words that point backward to what produced the event. They differ almost entirely in register and in what kind of cause they will accept: because of takes anything, out of takes only a motive inside the actor, through takes only an abstract failing, on the grounds of takes only a stated justification, on the pretext of takes a stated justification the writer is calling a lie, attributable to takes a measured share of an effect and assigns it, and thanks to comes with an attitude attached. Entries are alphabetical.

attributable to#

Pronunciation: /əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tu/ · weak /əˈtrɪbjətəbəl tə/ · uh-TRIB-yuh-tuh-buhl too Type: complex (adjective plus to) · Register: formal — audits, reports, epidemiology, and the courts Complement: noun phrase or gerund (attributable to having used last year's figures). Never a finite clause — attributable to the fact that is the clause form, and it is clumsy enough that because is nearly always better. Pattern: [noun] is attributable to [noun phrase] · [noun] attributable to [noun phrase] · directly / largely / wholly / partly / in part attributable to [noun phrase] Contrast: This is the phrase the whole due to rule is built on: the traditional test for due to is whether attributable to can be swapped in, and where it can, due to is safe under every analysis — see the due to rule at the end of this file. Like adjectival due to, it follows be or postmodifies a noun and does not front: Attributable to fog, the flight was canceled is not English, where Due to fog, … at least gets argued about. Against because of, it is cooler and more quantitative, and it is the word of choice wherever a share of an effect is being assigned rather than a whole one.

Assigns an effect, or a measured portion of one, to a cause. The object is the cause and the noun in front of the phrase is the effect, which puts it on the same side of the direction trap as as a result of.

  1. Most of the decline is attributable to a single bad quarter.
  2. Roughly a third of the increase is attributable to population growth alone.
  3. Deaths attributable to secondhand smoke have fallen by half since 1990.
  4. The cost overrun is attributable to delays nobody could have foreseen.
  5. Losses attributable to the outage were covered under the policy.
  6. The discrepancy is attributable to having used last year's figures.
  7. Only a small part of the warming is attributable to natural variation.

See also: due to, because of, resulting from, as a result of, owing to, consequent on

because of#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈkɔz əv/ or /bɪˈkʌz əv/ · weak /bɪˈkəz əv/ · casual /ˈkʌzə/ ('cause of) · bih-KAWZ uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral — the default causal preposition of American English Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (because of being late), or a fused relative, which is still a noun phrase (because of who he knows, because of what she said). Never a finite clause and never a that-clause. The clause form is the conjunction because standing alone — because it rained, never because of it rained and never because of that it rained. Pattern: [clause] because of [noun phrase] · Because of [noun phrase], [clause] · because of [verb-ing] · partly / largely / mainly / only / precisely because of [noun phrase] Contrast: Because + clause against because of + noun phrase is the cleanest preposition/conjunction pair in the language: We stayed home because it rained / We stayed home because of the rain. Against due to, because of is adverbial by nature and no editor has ever objected to it, which makes it the safe choice whenever you are unsure — see the due to rule at the end of this file. Against owing to and on account of, it is the plain, unmarked word. The internet construction because science, because reasons puts a bare noun after because alone; it is a deliberate joke and is not standard English.

Names the cause of whatever the main clause reports. It modifies the verb or the entire clause, and that adverbial job is exactly what the traditional usage rule reserved for it and denied to due to. It is neutral about whether the cause is welcome, which is what separates it from thanks to (grateful or sarcastic), courtesy of (wry), and owing to (formal).

  1. The flight was canceled because of fog.
  2. Because of the strike, deliveries are running two days late.
  3. She missed the deadline because of a family emergency.
  4. He got the job because of who he knows, not what he knows.
  5. Because of being out of the country all spring, he never received the notice.
  6. Attendance is down, mostly because of the parking.
  7. We're only here because of you.

See also: due to, owing to, on account of, as a result of, thanks to, attributable to, because of (complex prepositions), because (conjunction)

by reason of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈrizən əv/ · weak /bə ˈrizən ə/ · by-REE-zuhn uhv Type: complex · Register: legal and formal — outside statutes and pleadings it sounds stilted Complement: noun phrase, characteristically an abstract one (by reason of insanity, by reason of age). A gerund is possible (by reason of having served twenty years) but uncommon. Never a finite clause — the clausal form is the archaic by reason that. Pattern: [clause] by reason of [noun phrase] · [noun] by reason of [noun phrase] · not guilty by reason of [noun phrase] Contrast: Because of means the same thing and is shorter. By reason of survives because legal drafting is conservative and because a handful of fixed phrases have hardened around it — not guilty by reason of insanity, disqualified by reason of age. Do not confuse it with by virtue of, which names a qualification that enables something rather than a cause that produces it: you hold the seat by virtue of your office and you lose it by reason of your conviction.

The formal, chiefly legal way of naming a cause or a ground. In modern American writing it appears almost entirely in statutes, insurance policies, and court filings, plus the set phrases those documents have pushed out into general use.

  1. He was found not guilty by reason of insanity.
  2. No applicant may be refused by reason of race, religion, or national origin.
  3. The lease terminates by reason of the tenant's default.
  4. Benefits were denied by reason of his failure to file on time.
  5. She was excused from jury service by reason of ill health.
  6. Any loss by reason of fire is covered under Section 4.

See also: by virtue of, because of, on the grounds of, on account of, in consequence of

by virtue of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu əv/ · weak /bə ˈvɜrtʃu ə/ · by-VUR-choo uhv Type: complex · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase or gerund (by virtue of being the oldest). Never a finite clause — for a clause you need by virtue of the fact that, which is heavy enough that because is almost always the better answer. Pattern: [clause] by virtue of [noun phrase] · By virtue of [noun phrase], [clause] · by virtue of [verb-ing] Contrast: By virtue of names a qualification — a status, office, property, or fact that entitles or enables — where because of names a plain cause. He got in by virtue of his last name says the name functioned as a credential; he got in because of his last name says only that it was the reason. Against by dint of, which names effort rather than standing: by dint of hard work, but by virtue of seniority. Virtue here carries its old sense of "power, efficacy," not moral goodness.

Names the status, office, property, or established fact that entitles or enables the main event — the cause understood as a qualification rather than as a force.

  1. By virtue of her office, the vice president presides over the Senate.
  2. He inherited the title by virtue of being the eldest son.
  3. The clause is enforceable by virtue of Section 12 of the agreement.
  4. By virtue of its size alone, the company sets the price for the whole region.
  5. She had access to the files by virtue of her security clearance.
  6. Sound travels faster through steel by virtue of its density.

See also: by reason of, on the strength of, because of, by dint of, by virtue of (complex prepositions)

courtesy of#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɜrtəsi əv/ · weak /ˈkɜrtəsiə/ · KUR-tuh-see uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral, and heavily journalistic — captions, credit lines, sportswriting Complement: noun phrase naming a person, organization, or thing. No gerund, and never a finite clause. In photo credits it also appears bare, with the of dropped: photo courtesy the Newberry Library. Pattern: [clause], courtesy of [noun phrase] · Courtesy of [noun phrase], [clause] · (credit line) Photograph courtesy of [name] · by courtesy of [noun phrase] (older, more formal) Contrast: Thanks to with the credit spelled out. Both can be sincere or sarcastic, but courtesy of leans harder on agency — the thing named actually supplied the result — and it is far more common at the end of a sentence, tacked on as an afterthought, than at the front.

1. Supplied by; with the permission or generosity of. The literal credit line of captions and acknowledgments. 2. Caused by, with a wry tip of the hat. The news and sports use, where the cause is as often unwelcome as welcome.

  1. Photograph courtesy of the National Archives.
  2. Everyone got a ride home, courtesy of her uncle's van.
  3. The map is reproduced courtesy of the state historical society.
  4. The Rangers won it in overtime, courtesy of a deflection off a defenseman's skate.
  5. Half of downtown was dry by noon, courtesy of a burst main on Sixth Street.
  6. He spent the afternoon in the emergency room, courtesy of a ladder he should have thrown out years ago.
  7. Video courtesy of KTLA; used with permission.
  8. The lights came back on a little after four, courtesy of a crew that had been out since dawn.

See also: thanks to, because of, on account of, courtesy of (conjunctions catalog)

due to#

Pronunciation: /ˈdu tu/ · weak /ˈdu tə/ · DOO too · (British /ˈdjuː/, DYOO) Type: complex · Register: neutral, with a live usage dispute over sense 2 Complement: noun phrase or gerund (due to being understaffed). Never a finite clause — for a clause you need due to the fact that, which spends nine syllables on what because does in two. Note that be due to + a bare infinitive (the train is due to arrive at six) is a completely different construction: the adjective due plus a real infinitive, meaning "scheduled." Pattern: [noun] is due to [noun phrase] · [noun] due to [noun phrase] · [clause] due to [noun phrase] · Due to [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: See the due to vs. because of rule at the end of this file — this is the single most-taught prescriptive rule about causal prepositions and the one most widely ignored. In short: traditionalists allow due to only where attributable to would fit and where it attaches to a noun, and prescribe because of or owing to everywhere else. Because of is never wrong; due to sometimes is, depending entirely on your reader.

1. Attributable to; caused by — adjectival, after be or modifying a noun. The delay was due to fog. 2. Because of — adverbial, modifying the whole clause. Standard in American English, still contested. Due to fog, the flight was canceled.

  1. The delay was due to fog over the bay.
  2. Most of the increase is due to a change in how the data is collected.
  3. Absences due to illness must be reported by nine.
  4. Errors due to rounding may make the columns disagree.
  5. Due to the holiday, there will be no pickup on Monday.
  6. The clinic cut its hours due to being understaffed all summer.
  7. Two flights were canceled due to mechanical problems.
  8. The shortfall was due to a single contract nobody had budgeted for.
  9. Service was suspended due to flooding on the lower platform.

for lack of#

Pronunciation: /fɔr ˈlæk əv/ · weak /fɚ ˈlæk ə/, also /fər/ · stressed for /fɔr/ · fer-LAK uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, characteristically a bare one with no article — for lack of evidence, for lack of funding, for lack of a better word. A gerund is possible but rare. Never a finite clause. Pattern: [clause] for lack of [noun phrase] · For lack of [noun phrase], [clause] · for lack of a better [noun] · for lack of anything better Contrast: For lack of and for want of are one preposition in two vocabularies — lack is the everyday word, want the literary one — and for lack of is the one to use in ordinary American prose. Both name a missing cause: the reason something failed is that something else was absent. Watch the direction against because of: because of the evidence means the evidence caused it, for lack of evidence means the absence did.

Names an absence as the cause: the thing failed because something necessary was not there.

  1. The case was dismissed for lack of evidence.
  2. The project died for lack of funding.
  3. He agreed, for lack of a better option.
  4. Call it a hunch, for lack of a better word.
  5. Three houses burned for lack of a working hydrant.
  6. She stayed in the job for lack of anywhere else to go.

See also: for want of, because of, in the absence of, absent, but for

for want of#

Pronunciation: /fɔr ˈwɑnt əv/ · also /ˈwɔnt/ · weak /fɚ ˈwɑnt ə/ · stressed for /fɔr/ · fer-WAHNT uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to literary — proverbial, and noticeably formal in American English Complement: noun phrase, usually bare (for want of a nail). A gerund is possible but unusual. Never a finite clause. Pattern: [clause] for want of [noun phrase] · For want of [noun phrase], [clause] · for want of a better [noun] · for want of anything better to do Contrast: Identical in meaning to for lack of; the whole difference is register. Want appears here in its old sense of "absence, shortage," which survives in modern American English almost nowhere else (wanting, in want of, for want of). Because that sense is opaque to most speakers, choosing for want of reads as a deliberate stylistic gesture rather than a neutral choice.

The literary twin of for lack of: it names an absence as the cause of a failure, using want in its old sense of "shortage."

  1. For want of a nail the shoe was lost.
  2. The bill failed for want of a second.
  3. He called it a memoir, for want of a better term.
  4. A dozen small farms went under for want of rain.
  5. She read the manual twice for want of anything better to do.
  6. The motion was denied for want of jurisdiction.

in light of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈlaɪt əv/ · weak /ɪn ˈlaɪt ə/, run together as /ɪn ˈlaɪtə/ · in-LYT uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Variants: in the light of, with the article, is the dominant British form. American English strongly prefers in light of, and the article-less form is now spreading in British writing too. Complement: noun phrase or gerund (in light of having missed two payments). Never a finite clause — use in light of the fact that if you must have one. Pattern: In light of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] in light of [noun phrase] · in light of [verb-ing] Contrast: In light of names the information that leads someone to a decision, not the physical force that produces an event. You cancel a launch in light of the forecast, but the rocket does not explode in light of a faulty valve — that is because of. Against in view of, the two are near-identical; in light of is commoner in American English and suggests something newly learned, while in view of suggests a standing circumstance.

Names the new information or evidence in the light of which a decision is taken or reconsidered.

  1. In light of the new evidence, the district attorney reopened the case.
  2. The board postponed the vote in light of the auditor's report.
  3. In light of everything that's happened, a delay seems reasonable.
  4. The policy was rewritten in light of last year's lawsuit.
  5. In light of his record, the judge suspended the sentence.
  6. We reconsidered in light of having lost two of our biggest accounts.

in view of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈvju əv/ · weak /ɪn ˈvju ə/ · in-VYOO uhv Type: complex · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase or gerund. Never a finite clause — in view of the fact that for a clause. Pattern: In view of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] in view of [noun phrase] · in view of [verb-ing] Contrast: The literal spatial phrase in view of — "within sight of," as in a cabin in view of the lake — is a different animal and is not a preposition of cause; only the abstract use belongs here, and context always separates them. Against in light of: both name grounds for a decision, but in view of points at a circumstance already in place (in view of his age) where in light of points at something newly learned (in light of the report). Both are more formal than given and much more formal than since.

Names the standing circumstance that a decision takes account of — the formal way of saying given.

  1. In view of his age, the court imposed probation instead.
  2. The fee was waived in view of the delay on our end.
  3. In view of the weather, the ceremony has been moved indoors.
  4. The trial was halted early in view of the risks involved.
  5. No further action was taken in view of the company's cooperation.
  6. In view of having already paid twice, she refused the third invoice.

on account of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn əˈkaʊnt əv/ · weak /ən əˈkaʊnt ə/ · casual /ənəˈkaʊntə/ (on accounta) · on-uh-KOWNT uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral in writing, colloquial in speech — and the clause use is nonstandard Complement: noun phrase or gerund (on account of being sick). In standard written English, never a finite clause — but on account of he was sick, with a full clause, is a real and widespread spoken form in Southern, Midland, and African American English, and the folk-doubled on account of because belongs to the same register. Recognize both; do not write either in edited prose. Pattern: [clause] on account of [noun phrase] · On account of [noun phrase], [clause] · on account of [verb-ing] Contrast: A plain synonym of because of — one register down in speech, one register up in older writing. Do not confuse the causal preposition with the accounting phrase on account ("as partial payment") or with on someone's account ("for someone's sake"): don't stay on my account means "not for my sake," not "not because of me."

Names a cause in the plainest available words, with a spoken flavor that because of does not have.

  1. School was closed on account of the ice.
  2. On account of the recall, we're two weeks behind.
  3. He couldn't travel on account of his knee.
  4. She got the extension on account of being in the hospital.
  5. The store closed early on account of the power outage.
  6. On account of the drought, the town banned outdoor watering.

on the grounds of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ðə ˈɡraʊndz əv/ · weak /ən ðə ˈɡraʊndz ə/ · on-thuh-GROWNDZ uhv Type: complex · Register: formal and legal Variants: on grounds of, without the article, is standard in journalism and legal drafting. The singular on the ground of is older and now mainly British. Complement: noun phrase — characteristically an abstract noun naming a category of justification (race, cruelty, national security, self-incrimination). A gerund is possible but stiff. Never a finite clause: the clause form is on the grounds that, which is a conjunction, not this preposition. Pattern: [clause] on the grounds of [noun phrase] · [clause] on grounds of [noun phrase] · on the grounds that [clause] (the conjunction) Contrast: On the grounds of + noun phrase, on the grounds that + clause is the pair to memorize; splicing them (on the grounds of he lied) is the standard error. Against because of, this phrase names a justification somebody offered, not a cause operating in the world: a permit is denied on the grounds of zoning, but a roof does not collapse on the grounds of snow. Against on the basis of, which points at evidence rather than at a reason for a decision. Against on the pretext of, which is the same phrase with the writer's judgment added: on the grounds of leaves open whether the stated reason is the true one, on the pretext of says it is not.

Names the stated justification for a decision, a refusal, a claim, or a legal action.

  1. The claim was denied on the grounds of a pre-existing condition.
  2. Discrimination on the grounds of age is prohibited.
  3. He refused to testify on the grounds of self-incrimination.
  4. The documents were withheld on grounds of national security.
  5. She filed on the grounds of desertion.
  6. The appeal was rejected on the grounds of untimeliness.

See also: on the pretext of, by reason of, in view of, on the strength of, on the basis of, on the grounds that (conjunction)

on the pretext of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ðə ˈpritɛkst əv/ · weak /ən ðə ˈpritɛkst ə/ · on-thuh-PREE-tekst uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal — journalism, history, and narrative Variants: under the pretext of is at least as common in American English and is a shade more accusatory. On the pretense of is a near-synonym; under the guise of stresses the disguise rather than the excuse, and takes a noun more readily than a gerund. Complement: noun phrase or gerund (on the pretext of checking the meter). Never a finite clause — the clause form is on the pretext that + clause, which is a conjunction: on the pretext that the meter needed checking. Pattern: [clause] on the pretext of [verb-ing] · [clause] under the pretext of [noun phrase] · on the pretext that [clause] (the conjunction) Contrast: The dishonest twin of on the grounds of. Both name a stated reason rather than an operating cause, but on the grounds of is neutral about whether the stated reason is the real one, while on the pretext of asserts that it is not: a permit denied on the grounds of zoning may well be about zoning, but a search conducted on the pretext of a broken taillight is not about the taillight. Because the phrase commits the writer to that judgment, you cannot use it of your own motives except as a joke. Against in the name of, which names a value invoked as cover and leaves the sincerity open.

Names the false reason somebody gave — the excuse offered to cover the real motive.

  1. He got into the building on the pretext of delivering a package.
  2. The troops crossed the border under the pretext of a training exercise.
  3. She left the table on the pretext of a call she had to take.
  4. Editors killed the piece on the pretext of space, and everybody knew better.
  5. Under the pretext of reviewing the budget, the committee reopened the whole contract.
  6. He came by twice a week on the pretext of returning tools he had never borrowed.
  7. The stop was made on the pretext of a broken taillight.

on the strength of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ðə ˈstrɛŋkθ əv/ · also /ˈstrɛŋθ/ · weak /ən ðə ˈstrɛŋkθ ə/ · on-thuh-STRENGKTH uhv Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase or gerund (on the strength of having worked with them before). Never a finite clause. Pattern: [clause] on the strength of [noun phrase] · On the strength of [noun phrase], [clause] · on the strength of [verb-ing] Contrast: Names the evidence or asset somebody relied on when they acted — a recommendation, a demo, one good quarter. Against by virtue of, which names a standing status rather than a demonstration, and against on the basis of, which is colder and more procedural.

  1. She was hired on the strength of one interview.
  2. The bank lent them the money on the strength of a signed letter of intent.
  3. On the strength of two good quarters, the board approved the expansion.
  4. He got the part on the strength of a two-minute audition tape.
  5. The town issued bonds on the strength of projected tourism revenue.
  6. They took the deal on the strength of having worked with the buyer before.

out of#

Pronunciation: /ˈaʊt əv/ · weak /ˈaʊtə/ · OWT uhv, casual OW-tuh Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: in the motive sense (1), a bare abstract noun with no determiner — out of curiosity, out of spite, out of respect, out of habit. An article usually breaks it: out of a sense of duty works only because the head noun is sense, while out of the curiosity is impossible. A gerund is marginal in this sense (out of not wanting to embarrass her occurs and grates). In the source sense (2) the complement is an ordinary noun phrase with its determiners intact — out of that meeting, out of one complaint letter — and a fused relative works there as well, which is still a noun phrase and not a clause: something good came out of what happened. In the exhaustion sense (3) it is again a bare noun, this time naming a resource: out of options, out of time, out of patience. Never a that-clause in any sense, and no other finite clause either. Bare out is a different word: it is an adverb or a verb particle and takes no object at all (she walked out, cross it out, the truth came out), where out of is the two-word preposition and always requires one — the truth came out against the truth came out of a deposition nobody read. (The spatial out of — out of the house — is treated with the prepositions of movement, and the full six-sense account of the word, proportions and made out of included, is at out of.) Pattern: [clause] out of [bare abstract noun] · Out of [bare abstract noun], [clause] · out of a sense of [noun] · out of respect / fear / spite / habit / necessity / curiosity / desperation · [come / grow / arise / develop] out of [noun phrase] · Out of [resource noun], [clause] Contrast: In sense 1 out of names the motive inside the actor — the feeling or disposition the action came out of — where because of names a cause outside. She lied out of fear is about her state; she lied because of the threat is about the threat. The determinerless noun is the surest sign you are in this sense rather than the spatial one. Against from, which overlaps in acted from a sense of duty but sounds more literary; see from. Sense 2 is the ancestor of the legal arising out of, and there the direction reverses: the object is the origin and the subject is what grew from it. Sense 3 is the mirror of for lack of — both make an absence the reason — but for lack of names what was never there and out of names what ran out.

1. The motive inside the actor. The feeling, disposition, or state of mind an action springs from, named by a bare abstract noun. I asked out of curiosity. 2. The source something developed from. Result and consequence#

Eight phrases built on the words result, consequence, cause, arise, and secondary. They carry a trap that catches learners and native speakers alike: the object of every one of them is the cause, not the result. In four people were hospitalized as a result of the crash, the crash is the cause and the hospitalizations are the result — the phrase names what the result came out of. The adverbs formed from the same words (as a result, consequently, in consequence) point the other way and take no object at all. Entries are alphabetical.

arising from#

Pronunciation: /əˈraɪzɪŋ frʌm/ · weak /əˈraɪzɪŋ frəm/ · uh-RY-zing frum Type: marginal/participial · Register: legal — contracts, insurance policies, pleadings Variants: arising out of is the standard form in American contract drafting and the one you will meet in arbitration clauses; arising from is slightly plainer. Arising under is narrower and means "deriving from the terms of the instrument itself." Complement: noun phrase. No gerund in practice, and never a finite clause. Unlike almost everything else in this file it cannot be fronted: it postmodifies a noun, so there is no Arising from the accident, he sued. Pattern: [noun] arising from [noun phrase] · [noun] arising out of or relating to [noun phrase] · any claim arising out of [noun phrase] Contrast: A participial phrase that has hardened into a fixed causal preposition inside legal boilerplate, where its very breadth is the point — arising out of or relating to this Agreement is drafted to sweep in everything a court might otherwise exclude. Against resulting from, which is narrower and implies direct causation, and caused by, narrower still. Drafters stack all three because courts read them as different widths of the same idea.

Names the event or instrument that a claim, cost, obligation, or dispute grew out of. Its object is the cause, and the noun it modifies is the result.

  5. All obligations arising from the merger survive termination.
  6. The report covers complaints arising out of the November outage.

Purpose and intention#

The words that point forward to an aim. Their meanings barely differ; what differs is what they take, and that difference is absolute. In order to, so as to, in an effort to, and with intent to take a bare infinitive and cannot take a noun at all, which strictly disqualifies the first two as prepositions — they are infinitival subordinators sitting in a preposition's slot, and they are listed here because that is where learners look for them. Everything else in this section takes a gerund or a noun phrase and refuses the infinitive: with a view to, with an eye to, with the aim of, for the purpose of, for the sake of, for fear of, in the hope of, in the interest of, and — noun only, no gerund — for the benefit of, in furtherance of, in pursuance of, and in pursuit of. The tell is the of. Every purpose phrase that ends in of takes a gerund; the ones that end in bare to take an infinitive — with the single exception of with a view to, whose to is a preposition, which is exactly why that one has to be memorized rather than worked out. The full table is at the end of the file. Entries are alphabetical.

The due to vs. because of rule#

The rule as it is taught. Due to is an adjective phrase. Like any adjective, it should modify a noun — either directly (absences due to illness) or through a linking verb (the delay was due to fog). Because of is an adverb phrase and modifies verbs and whole clauses (we were delayed because of fog). The test: if you can swap in caused by or attributable to and the sentence still stands, due to is safe. If you cannot, the traditional rule wants because of, owing to, or on account of.

The delay was due to fog.correctfollows be and modifies the noun delay

The flight was canceled due to fog.disputedmodifies the verb canceled
Due to fog, the flight was canceled.most disputedmodifies the whole clause, and is fronted

What actually happens. The adverbial due to has been standard in American English for the better part of a century. It appears in national newspapers, in federal regulations, in airline announcements, and in the prose of writers nobody would call careless, and the major descriptive usage guides accept it without qualification. The prohibition survives in three places: older style manuals, a handful of standardized tests, and copy editors trained on both.

What to do about it. Because of is correct under every analysis, in every position, for every reader — it costs nothing to prefer it, and that is the only practical argument for the rule. Reserve due to for the positions the rule allows, after be or attached to a noun, where it is not merely permitted but often better: increases due to inflation is tighter than increases that were caused by inflation. And note that fronting draws the most fire — Due to fog, … at the head of a sentence is the version an editor is likeliest to change.

The single most common purpose error in English is putting an infinitive where a gerund belongs, or the reverse. The two lists do not mix.

Four more belong on the right-hand side but take a noun only, never a gerund: for the benefit of, in furtherance of, in pursuance of, and in pursuit of. Nothing at all belongs in both columns.

The of is the tell. Every phrase in the right-hand column ends in of or is built on one, and every phrase in the left-hand column ends in bare to with no of in sight. With intent to defraud and with the intention of defrauding are the pair that makes the rule visible: same meaning, opposite grammar, and the only thing that changed is the of.

With a view to reduce costs is the error to watch for, because the to in with a view to is a preposition and not an infinitive marker. The test is substitution: if a noun fits the slot — with a view to reduction — then the verb has to be -ing. The same test sorts the whole to-plus-gerund family outside this file: look forward to seeing you (never look forward to see), object to paying (never object to pay), be accustomed to working nights (never accustomed to work), be used to getting up early. In every one of them to is a preposition, and the giveaway is that a noun fits: look forward to the weekend, object to the fee, accustomed to the noise.

The one rule that covers the whole file#

No preposition in this file can take a that-clause. Every one of them has a clause-taking counterpart formed with that or the fact that, and the counterpart is a conjunction, not a preposition.

There is exactly one crack in the wall, and it is worth knowing precisely: over takes a wh-clause — a dispute over whether the vote was binding, confusion over how the money was spent, a fight over who pays — and nothing else in this file does. Even over refuses that: an argument over that he lied is not English. What with takes a small clause (what with the kids being sick), which is not finite either. Those two aside, the ban is total.
