Agent, instrument, means, manner, and measure#

The prepositions that answer by whom, with what, how, and how much — the words that attach a doer, a tool, a method, a style, or a number to an event.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Four questions run through this group and they shade into one another so gradually that no two dictionaries draw the lines in the same place: who did it (agent), what they did it with (instrument), how the result was brought about (means and manner), and how much of it there was (measure). English marks all four with the same small handful of workhorses — by, with, through, in, at, per — and then supplies a long tail of complex phrases (by means of, at the rate of, in the neighborhood of) that say the same thing at greater length and higher register. The rule that runs through the whole group is that the short form is almost always the better one: with a crowbar beats by means of a crowbar, more than 200 beats in excess of 200, and for $500 beats in the amount of $500 everywhere except a contract. The trap that runs through it is the ear — most of these phrases end in of, which reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ in speech, which is why could of and would of get written down, and why every entry below gives the weak form.

Where the core nine live#

All nine of the most common prepositions do work in this semantic field — seven of them do the bulk of it — and every one is written up in full in file 01 or file 02. Nothing below repeats those entries. Look for the relevant senses there:

  • by — the agent of a passive (written by Baldwin), the method (by train, by check, by hand), the distributive rate (by the pound, by the hour, by the dozen), the margin of difference (won by six), and the dimensions of a rectangle (a three by five card).
  • with — the instrument (cut it with a knife) and manner adverbials (with care, with difficulty, with a straight face).
  • in — the medium and the manner (in pencil, in ink, in Spanish, in a whisper, in cash, in a hurry), magnitude (losses in the millions), and the proportion frame (one in five households, a one-in-a-million chance).
  • on — the means of travel that has no vehicle around you (on foot, on horseback) and the powered medium (on the phone, on TV).
  • at — rate, speed, and price (at speed, at 60 miles an hour, at $5 a pound, at 6 percent).
  • to — the precision of a rounded figure (to the nearest dollar, accurate to three decimal places) and ratios (a three-to-one margin, ten to one against).
  • for — price in a single transaction (sold it for $500), and the exchange rate of one thing against another (a dollar for a dollar).
  • from — the raw material a thing was made out of (wine from grapes, a bench from an old barn door) and the source a piece of information came from (I got it from her).
  • of — the substance a thing is made of (a table of oak), and the measure phrase that carries almost every quantity in written English (a crowd of two hundred, a distance of ten miles, an increase of nine percent).

Everything below is a word this file owns outright, or a word whose measure-and-manner sense lives here while its spatial or temporal sense lives elsewhere.

Agent — who or what did it#

Plain by is the default agent marker in English and covers nearly every case. The four phrases here are the specialists: one for agents of harm, one for the party who ordered the thing done, one for agents who supplied something, and one for pinning behavior on an institution.

at the behest of#

Pronunciation: /æt ðə bɪˈhɛst ʌv/ · at thuh bih-HEST uhv; weak /ət ðə bəˈhɛst əv/, and in running speech the of goes down to bare /ə/ — /ət ðə bəˈhɛst ə/ Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral; standard in journalism, history, and legal writing Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, and like the other agent phrases the complement must be animate or institutional — a person, an office, an agency. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand. With a pronoun, the possessive construction is commoner: at his behest rather than at the behest of him. Pattern: [passive clause] at the behest of [instigator] · At the behest of [instigator], [clause] · [clause] at [possessive] behest Variants: at the request of is the neutral everyday form and at the urging of and at the insistence of grade the pressure upward; at the instigation of is the most accusatory and the rarest. At the direction of is the civilian softening of military by direction of, both of which are sketched at by order of. Contrast: The one phrase in this section that names who ordered it rather than who did it. Plain by marks the hand; at the behest of marks the voice behind the hand, which is why the memo was written by a staffer at the behest of the governor can put two different agents in one sentence and no other construction can. Against on the part of: that phrase hangs an abstract noun on its doer, this one hangs a completed act on its instigator. Behest is a fossil noun exactly like dint in by dint of — it survives only inside this phrase, so it takes no plural and no modifier: ✗ a strong behest, ✗ at several behests.

Names the party whose request or order set the action going. It leaves the doer in place and adds a second, superior agent above them, which is why it is the standard phrase for reporting influence, pressure, and chains of command.

  1. The investigation was reopened at the behest of two members of the committee.
  2. At the behest of the state, the district rewrote its attendance policy over the summer.
  3. The clause was inserted at the behest of a single lender and nobody else read it closely.
  4. She took the job at the behest of a mentor she could not say no to.
  5. The audit was commissioned at his behest and delivered to him alone.
  6. The park was renamed at the urging of a neighborhood association that had been asking for nine years.

See also: by, on the part of, at the hands of, courtesy of, by order of

at the hands of#

Pronunciation: /æt ðə ˈhændz ʌv/ · at thuh HANDZ uhv; weak /ət ðə ˈhændz əv/, and in running speech the of goes all the way down to bare /ə/ — /ət ðə ˈhændz ə/ Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal; heavy in journalism and history writing Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, and the complement must be animate or institutional — a person, a group, a government, a team. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand. Pattern: [noun of suffering] at the hands of [agent] · [passive verb of suffering] at the hands of [agent] Contrast: A specialized by. It marks the agent only of something bad — defeat, injury, humiliation, death — and it casts that agent as a perpetrator. He was killed by his brother is neutral reporting; He died at the hands of his brother is an accusation. Because the complement must be animate, it cannot cover instruments or natural forces: ✗ killed at the hands of a falling tree, ✗ flattened at the hands of the storm.

Attributes harm to a responsible party. The phrase is doing rhetorical work that plain by does not do: it foregrounds the agent, implies that the suffering was inflicted rather than merely caused, and invites the reader to assign blame. Sportswriting borrowed it for defeats, where the same framing turns a loss into something done to a team.

1. Suffering, injury, or death inflicted by someone.

  1. Thousands died at the hands of the regime in the first eighteen months alone.
  2. She described years of mistreatment at the hands of a supervisor everyone had complained about.
  3. He lost the house at the hands of a servicer that had misplaced two years of payments.

2. Defeat in a contest.

  1. The team suffered its worst loss in a decade at the hands of a program it had beaten nine straight times.
  2. The bill died at the hands of a committee that never scheduled a hearing on it.
  3. Her first campaign ended at the hands of an incumbent nobody thought was vulnerable.

See also: by, courtesy of, on the part of, through

courtesy of#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɚtəsi ʌv/ · KUR-tuh-see uhv; weak /ˈkɚtəsi əv/, casually /ˈkɚtəsi ə/ — which is one reason the bare credit-line form courtesy the Newberry Library got written down in the first place Type: complex · Register: neutral; standard in captions, credits, and journalism Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a person, institution, or source. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It frequently appears with no verb at all, as a freestanding credit line. Pattern: [clause] courtesy of [source] · [noun phrase], courtesy of [source] · Courtesy of [source] (caption) Variants: by courtesy of — older and more formal, still standard in British programs and exhibition labels. Bare courtesy with no of is normal American credit-line style: Photo courtesy Getty Images. Contrast: Against by: by credits authorship, courtesy of credits permission or supply — a photograph by Ansel Adams versus a photograph courtesy of the Adams estate. Against thanks to: thanks to names a cause of an outcome; courtesy of names a giver. In the ironic use the two converge exactly, and both go sour on purpose: a two-hour delay courtesy of the FAA. This entry owns the credit-and-supply sense; the purely causal use (half of downtown was dry by noon, courtesy of a burst main) is written up with the prepositions of cause at courtesy of.

Credits the party that supplied, permitted, or provided the thing. Its native habitat is the small print under a photograph, but it has spread into ordinary prose in two directions: as a neutral attribution of a source, and — far more commonly in speech — as a sarcastic attribution of blame, where the "courtesy" is anything but.

1. Supplied or permitted by; with the permission of.

  1. All images courtesy of the National Archives unless otherwise noted.
  2. Data courtesy of the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
  3. Dinner was courtesy of the hotel, which had lost our reservation twice.

2. Caused by — usually ironic, occasionally admiring.

  1. We spent the night in Denver, courtesy of a mechanical problem in Phoenix.
  2. The winning run came courtesy of a throwing error by the shortstop.
  3. He has a scar over his left eye courtesy of a hockey stick and a bad decision.

See also: by, thanks to, at the hands of, through, courtesy of (cause)

on the part of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ðə ˈpɑrt ʌv/ · on thuh PART uhv; weak /ən ðə ˈpɑrt əv/, casually /ən ðə ˈpɑrt ə/ Type: complex · Register: formal; legal, academic, and bureaucratic prose Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, always animate or institutional. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. With a pronoun, most writers switch to the possessive construction instead: on his part, for my part. Pattern: [abstract noun of behavior] on the part of [agent] · On the part of [agent], [clause] Contrast: It hangs an agent on an abstract noun — a failure, a decision, a reluctance — where by would be ambiguous: a failure by the board can be read as a failure the board caused, while a failure on the part of the board can only mean the board failed. That precision is why the phrase survives in legal writing, and nine times out of ten in ordinary prose the possessive is shorter and just as clear: the board's failure. Do not confuse it with on behalf of, which means representing, not committed by.

Assigns the doer of an abstract action to the noun that names it. Whenever a nominalization has swallowed a verb — negligence, reluctance, misstatement, effort — the subject of that buried verb has to be reattached somehow, and this is the formal way to do it.

  1. The report found no wrongdoing on the part of the responding officers.
  2. There was real reluctance on the part of the faculty to move the deadline.
  3. Any misstatement on the part of the applicant voids the policy.
  4. It took months of quiet work on the part of two staffers to get the language drafted.
  5. An error on the part of the pilot does not by itself establish negligence.
  6. The delay reflected a genuine failure of nerve on the part of the leadership.

See also: by, on behalf of, at the hands of

Instrument and means — what it was done with#

Instrumental with and method-marking by handle almost all of this in speech. The words below are what English reaches for when the instrument is a process rather than an object, when the instrument is the raw material the thing was made out of, when there is an intermediary in the chain, or when the register demands more syllables.

by dint of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈdɪnt ʌv/ · by DINT uhv; weak /baɪ ˈdɪnt əv/, casually /baɪ ˈdɪnt ə/ · by itself has no weak form and keeps its full /aɪ/ Type: complex · Register: formal, literary; faintly bookish Complement: noun phrase — characteristically abstract (effort, persistence, sheer luck, brute force) — or a gerund (by dint of working nights). Never a finite clause, never a to-infinitive. Pattern: [clause] by dint of [noun phrase / verb-ing] · By dint of [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: Narrower than by means of. By means of takes an instrument or a procedure; by dint of takes exertion, stubbornness, or a personal quality, and it says the result was forced out of an unwilling world. ✗ by dint of a crowbar is wrong where by dint of brute force is right. Dint is a fossil word meaning a blow, a stroke, and it survives in modern English only inside this phrase — which is why nobody can misuse the noun and everybody occasionally misspells it by dent of.

Attributes an achievement to effort or to a quality rather than to a tool. It always implies that the thing was hard and that the doer supplied whatever was missing out of their own resources.

  1. By dint of sheer persistence she got the sealed records released.
  2. He made the roster by dint of being the only left-handed catcher in three counties.
  3. The town got through the winter by dint of two generators and a great deal of goodwill.
  4. She made partner by dint of long hours and an unnerving memory for names.
  5. By dint of repetition alone, the slogan started to sound like common sense.
  6. They kept the paper alive by dint of not paying themselves for two years.

See also: by means of, through, by virtue of, by

by means of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈminz ʌv/ · by MEENZ uhv; weak /baɪ ˈminz əv/, casually /baɪ ˈminz ə/ Type: complex · Register: formal; technical, scientific, and legal writing Complement: noun phrase or gerund (by means of dissolving the residue in acetone). Never a finite clause. It does not strand. Pattern: [clause] by means of [noun phrase / verb-ing] · By means of [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: The long form of instrumental with and of method-marking by. Ordinary English says with a centrifuge; a patent application says by means of a centrifuge, because patent drafters want the instrument marked unmistakably and with has too many other jobs. Against by dint of: means versus effort. Against via: via is compact and can also mark a route, which by means of cannot. Against using: using is the modern technical-prose default and is shorter.

Names the instrument, mechanism, or procedure that produced the result. Its whole value is unambiguity: in a sentence where with could be read as accompanied by, by means of cannot be.

  1. The two fractions were separated by means of a benchtop centrifuge.
  2. Access to the vault is controlled by means of a keycard and a four-digit PIN.
  3. The signal reaches the valley by means of a repeater on the ridge.
  4. He supported the family by means of a night job and two lodgers.
  5. Payment may be made by means of certified check or wire transfer.
  6. The sample was purified by means of passing it twice through a silica column.

See also: with, by, using, via, by dint of, by way of, by means of

by way of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈweɪ ʌv/ · by WAY uhv; weak /baɪ ˈweɪ əv/, casually /baɪ ˈweɪ ə/ Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase; a gerund (by way of explaining himself); or — in the manner sense only — a bare article-less abstract noun: by way of apology, by way of introduction, by way of illustration. Never a finite clause of any kind, and never a to-infinitive: ✗ by way of to explain. Pattern: [clause] by way of [place] (route) · [clause] by way of [bare abstract noun] (purpose) · [clause] by way of [noun phrase] (means) Contrast: Three jobs share one phrase and the complement tells you which is running. A place name means route and equals via. A bare abstract noun means as a form of and equals as or as a kind of. Anything else means means and equals by means of, which is the weakest of the three uses and usually cuttable — by way of illustration is just for example. Compare the reference-and-topic use at by way of.

1. By the route of; passing through.

  1. We drove home by way of Flagstaff to get around the storm.
  2. Mail from the islands still comes by way of Miami.
  3. The flight goes to Managua by way of Houston and adds four hours to the trip.

2. As a form of; for the purpose of. Takes a bare noun with no article, or a gerund.

  1. By way of apology he sent a bottle of wine and a note that explained nothing.
  2. She mentioned the lawsuit only by way of introduction and then moved on.
  3. He put up a slide of the 1954 map by way of showing how little had changed.

3. Through the mechanism of; by means of.

  1. The money reaches the districts by way of a county trust.
  2. He learned Portuguese by way of two years in São Paulo and a patient landlady.
  3. Complaints get to the board by way of an ombudsman nobody has ever met.

See also: via, through, by means of, out of, by way of, by way of

out of#

Pronunciation: /aʊt ʌv/ · OWT uhv; weak /ˈaʊt əv/ and, at any ordinary speed, the two words run together as /ˈaʊtə/ · OW-tuh — which is what the eye-spelling outta is transcribing, and the reason learners write ✗ out a curiosity Type: complex · Register: neutral; one of the highest-frequency prepositions in the language Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a fused relative (built out of what was left in the barn). A gerund occurs only in the motive sense treated elsewhere (out of not wanting to embarrass her), and even there it grates. Never a finite clause and never a that-clause — ✗ out of that he was tired. It strands as a unit, both words together: What is it made out of?, the fund the raise came out of — but the of can never be dropped and never left behind: ✗ what is it made out? Pattern: [verb of making] out of [material] · [number] out of [number] · [cost or payment] out of [fund or source] Contrast: This entry owns the material, proportion, and funding-source senses. The spatial sense (out of the building) is at out of, the motive sense (out of spite, out of curiosity) at out of, and the general all-senses entry at out of. Against of for materials: made of oak names the substance and leaves the substance recognizable; made out of oak insists that raw stuff was transformed — which is why a table made out of a door is idiomatic and ✗ a table made of a door is not. Against from: from is the marker when the material is no longer visible in the product (wine from grapes, paper from rags), out of when it still is. Against in for proportions: one in five households is a rate over a population, nine out of ten dentists is a count taken from a stated set — which is why a test score is always 92 out of 100 and never ✗ 92 in 100. Out alone is an adverb and a verb particle (She went out, Cross it out, The fire is out) and takes no object at all, which is the test; the preposition is the two-word form.

1. The material a thing was made from. The stuff is still recognizable in the product.

  1. He built the whole shed out of salvaged barn siding.
  2. The bowl is turned out of a single piece of walnut.
  3. She made supper out of whatever was left in the pantry.

2. Out of a stated set — the proportion sense. Both numbers are given, and the second is the whole.

  1. Nine out of ten of the households surveyed had never heard of the program.
  2. She scored ninety-two out of a hundred and was furious about the eight.
  3. Three out of the four bridges on that stretch are rated structurally deficient.

3. The fund or source a cost was paid from.

  1. The overtime came out of the general fund, not out of the grant.
  2. Two of the positions are paid out of federal money that runs out in June.
  3. He covered the difference out of his own pocket, which is the line the auditors underlined.

See also: of, from, in, by means of, via, out of (movement), out of (cause), out of (complex)

through#

Pronunciation: /θru/ · THROO — no true weak form; in rapid speech the vowel shortens toward /θrʊ/ and, before a consonant, as far as /θrə/ (through the door /θrə ðə ˈdɔr/). The spelling thru is signage and shorthand, not standard prose. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (through working weekends for two years). Never a finite clause. It strands freely: the agency I found it through, Who did you hear it through? Pattern: [clause] through [noun phrase / verb-ing] · Through [noun phrase], [clause] · stranded: the program I got the job through Contrast: This entry covers the means and intermediary senses; the spatial sense (through the tunnel) is at through, the inclusive-date sense (Monday through Friday) is at through, and the fault-and-failing sense sketched in sense 3 below is worked out in full at through. Against by: by marks the direct agent or the medium, through marks the channel that carried it — I heard it by phone is the medium, I heard it through a friend is the intermediary. Against via: near-synonyms for the intermediary sense, but through takes gerunds and strands where via does neither. Also an adverb and a particle — Let me through, We got through, I'm through with him — and there it has no object at all, which is the test.

1. By the means or method of. The process that produced the result.

  1. He built the business through twenty years of eighteen-hour days.
  2. Most of our hiring happens through referrals rather than postings.
  3. She got her start through working weekends at a station nobody listened to.

2. Through the agency of; via an intermediary.

  1. She got the loan through a credit union rather than a bank.
  2. You can only reach him through his agent, and his agent does not return calls.
  3. The house sold through a broker who never once set foot in it.

3. Because of — normally with a fault, an accident, or a lack.

  1. The mix-up happened through a clerical error, not through malice.
  2. Through no fault of her own she ended up in the wrong terminal in the wrong city.
  3. Two boxes of personnel records were lost through sheer inattention.

See also: by, via, by means of, by way of, through (movement), through (time), through (cause)

using#

Pronunciation: /ˈjuzɪŋ/ · YOO-zing; casually /ˈjuzɪn/ · no weak form — it keeps its own stress, which is one sign it is still half a verb Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral; the default in technical writing and instructions Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. Never a finite clause, and — unlike a true preposition — it does not take a gerund: ✗ using boiling the water. It does not strand. Pattern: [clause] using [noun phrase] · Using [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: A present participle that has drifted most of the way into prepositionhood. It competes with instrumental with and formal by means of, and technical prose prefers it because it names a tool without any of with's other meanings: Open the file with Sarah is ambiguous, Open the file using Sarah's account is not. Its participial origin still shows: when the main clause has no plausible user, you get a dangling modifier — ✗ Using a spreadsheet, the numbers were checked says the numbers used the spreadsheet. Compare given and considering, which have gone further and no longer need a subject to attach to.

Names the tool, material, or resource an agent employed. It is the plainest instrumental marker English has after with, and unlike with it says nothing about accompaniment, possession, or manner.

  1. Tighten the bracket using a ten-millimeter socket.
  2. The map was drawn using satellite imagery from 2019.
  3. She paid using a card she had reported stolen the week before.
  4. Using nothing but a paperclip, he got the battery cover off.
  5. We track attendance using a shared spreadsheet nobody has permission to edit.
  6. The team modeled the outbreak using three separate sets of assumptions.

See also: with, by means of, with the aid of, given, considering

via#

Pronunciation: /ˈvaɪə/ · VY-uh; also /ˈviə/ · VEE-uh — the second is commoner in British English and in Latin tags. No weak form: it carries a stress of its own, which is part of why it feels crisper than by way of. Type: simple (Latin via, the ablative of via "road") · Register: neutral to formal; very common in technical and business prose Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. Never a gerund and never a finite clause — ✗ via sending an email, ✗ via he told me. It does not strand. Pattern: [clause] via [noun phrase] · [destination] via [waypoint] · [noun phrase] via [medium] Contrast: The travel-route sense is at via; this entry is the medium and intermediary sense. Against by for media: both work, but by wants a bare article-less noun (by email, by phone, ✗ by the app) while via takes either (via email, via the app). Against through: identical in meaning for intermediaries, but through takes gerunds and strands and via does neither — the single commonest error with this word is putting an -ing form after it. Against by means of: via is the compact modern equivalent and has almost displaced it outside legal drafting.

1. By way of a medium or channel of transmission.

  1. Send the signed copy via email; the original can follow by mail.
  2. Tickets are available only via the mobile app, which is the whole complaint.
  3. Updates go out via text message and a recorded call.

2. Through an intermediary — a person, an agency, a system.

  1. The story reached us via a stringer in Ankara.
  2. Payments clear via the ACH network, usually in two business days.
  3. He got the interview via a former roommate who owed him a favor.

See also: through, by, by means of, by way of, via

with the aid of#

Pronunciation: /wɪð ði ˈeɪd ʌv/ · with thee AYD uhv; weak /wəθ ði ˈeɪd əv/, casually /wəθ ði ˈeɪd ə/ — with reduces to /wəθ/ and the of to bare /ə/, so the whole phrase can come out as three unstressed syllables around aid Type: complex · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase — an instrument or a person. A gerund is possible but rare and clumsy. Never a finite clause. Pattern: [clause] with the aid of [noun phrase] · With the aid of [noun phrase], [clause] Variants: with the help of — the everyday form and the commoner of the two in American English, and the only comfortable choice when the complement is a person. With the assistance of — the most formal, standard in official and academic prose. Contrast: Against plain instrumental with: He read the inscription with a magnifying glass and with the aid of a magnifying glass mean the same thing, but the long form implies a difficulty that had to be overcome. Against by means of: by means of is neutral about difficulty and treats the instrument as sufficient; with the aid of treats it as assistance to an agent who was already working at it. Against using: using is neutral and modern, with the aid of is formal and slightly effortful.

Marks an instrument or a helper that made an already-difficult thing possible. The agent stays firmly in the sentence's subject position; the aid is subordinate, which is exactly the difference from by.

  1. The worn inscription was finally read with the aid of raking light.
  2. She walks now with the aid of a cane and a great deal of stubbornness.
  3. With the help of two neighbors, they got the tree off the driveway before dark.
  4. The bill passed with the assistance of four members of the other party.
  5. He finished the manuscript with the aid of a small grant and a very patient editor.
  6. Divers recovered the recorder with the aid of a remotely operated vehicle.

Manner — how it was done#

Manner is where like and as fight their famous fight, and where American English keeps a small graded set of imitation markers running from the light and journalistic (à la) to the heavy and art-historical (after the manner of). Note that plain with supplies most everyday manner adverbials (with care, with difficulty), and in supplies the rest (in a whisper, in Spanish, in pencil, in haste). As is not written up here at all: its role sense (works as a lawyer) is at as and its resemblance-and-comparison sense at as. The entries below are the manner half of a pair — like, unlike, and in the manner of are also treated as prepositions of comparison and opposition, which is where the parallelism and case rules are worked out at length.

à la#

Pronunciation: /ˌɑ ˈlɑ/ · ah-LAH; commonly reduced to /ə ˈlɑ/ Type: complex (French à la manière de, clipped) · Register: neutral to journalistic; knowing in tone Complement: noun phrase, characteristically a proper name — a person, a work, a brand, a place, an era. Never a gerund, never a finite clause. It does not strand. Pattern: [clause] à la [proper name] · [noun phrase] à la [proper name] Variants: written a la without the accent in a great deal of American copy; à la is the careful spelling and the one dictionaries head. The French gender agreement does not survive: it is à la before every complement, masculine or not. Contrast: A compact in the style of. Against like: like compares anything to anything and asserts nothing about intent, while à la compares a way of doing to a known model and always carries a whiff of imitation, homage, or parody. Against after the manner of: the same meaning at the opposite register — à la is light and current, after the manner of is heavy and belongs in a museum label. Do not confuse the preposition with the frozen culinary nouns à la carte, à la mode, à la king, which take no complement at all.

Rate, ratio, and distribution#

The distributive constructions — twice a day, sixty miles an hour, five dollars per pound, by the dozen — are where English keeps three interchangeable markers in circulation at three different registers. The by the pattern belongs to by: sold by the pound, paid by the hour, bought by the dozen, rented by the week, always with the and always with a bare singular unit.

a#

Pronunciation: /eɪ/ · AY (stressed, citation form); weak /ə/ · uh — the weak form is the normal one here Type: simple · Register: neutral; the everyday spoken form Complement: a bare singular count noun of time, quantity, or measure: a day, a week, a head, a pound, a gallon, a person, a mile. Never a plural (✗ twice a days), never a gerund, never a clause. It does not strand. Pattern: [frequency] a [unit] · [price] a [unit] · [quantity] a [unit] Variants: an before a vowel sound (an hour, an ounce, an acre). per is the formal and technical equivalent; the replaces it inside the by construction (by the hour). Contrast: This is not the indefinite article, even though it is spelled and pronounced identically. It descends from Old English on worn down to nothing — the same erosion that produced nowadays, asleep, ashore — and it means per. The test is substitution: twice a day is twice per day, never twice one day. Against per: identical in meaning, opposite in register — sixty miles an hour on the road, 60 miles per hour on the spec sheet. Do not mix the two: ✗ per an hour. British English uses this form somewhat more freely in prices (two pounds a kilo); American English is comfortable with either.

Distributes a quantity over a unit. It is the most frequent rate marker in spoken American English by a wide margin, and it is invisible to most speakers, who take it for the article.

  1. She takes the medication twice a day with food.
  2. Ground chuck was four dollars a pound that week.
  3. He drives about eighty miles an hour on the interstate, which explains the three tickets.
  4. Tickets were fifteen dollars a head at the door and twelve in advance.
  5. The plant turns out two thousand units a week on one shift.
  6. We were getting maybe three calls an hour by the end of the night.

Measure, quantity, and price#

Every entry in this section is a way of saying more than, less than, about, or for — and in every case the plain word is shorter and usually better. What the long forms buy is precision under adversarial reading (in excess of the deductible), a documented sum (in the amount of $500), or a deliberate editorial tone (to the tune of three hundred million). Choose accordingly. The hedges sort themselves by how much slack they claim: about and around say close to the number, close to says the same thing from below, in the neighborhood of says the right range, on the order of says the right power of ten, and upward of says something else again and should usually be rewritten. Two more say something about the size of the number rather than its accuracy: a matter of insists it is small, and the better part of insists it is most of a larger one.

a matter of#
