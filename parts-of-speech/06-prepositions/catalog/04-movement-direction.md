# Movement and direction

> The prepositions that trace a path — where motion starts, what route it takes, and where it ends up.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file answers *which way?* rather than *where?* They attach to verbs of motion (*run, drive, throw, climb, push*) and they name one of three things: a **source** (*out of, off, away from*), a **path** (*across, through, along, past, via*), or a **goal** (*into, onto, toward, up to*). The single rule that runs through the whole group is the **position/motion split**: *in*, *on*, and *at* mark where something already is, while *into*, *onto*, and *to* mark where something is going. *He was **in** the pool* and *He jumped **into** the pool* are not stylistic variants — they describe different events, and choosing the static form for a dynamic verb is the most common spatial error learners make.

The second thing to watch is that almost every word here moonlights as an **adverb or a verb particle**. *Up*, *down*, *over*, *through*, *past*, *around*, *off*, and *out* all have a life outside this file. The test is the object: a preposition always has a noun phrase after it (*ran **up** the hill*), while an adverb or particle stands alone (*stand **up***) or belongs to the verb rather than the noun (*turned the offer **down***). That test also settles the two nastiest spelling traps in American English — *into* vs. *in to* and *onto* vs. *on to* — because when *in* or *on* belongs to the verb, the words stay apart.

Finally, the complement rule, which is where the real errors live. **Not one of these prepositions takes a *that*-clause.** You cannot write *into he was late* or *past she arrived* — the same rule that blocks *despite he was late* and *due to he was late* over in [cause and purpose](06-cause-purpose.md). What they do take is noun phrases and objective-case pronouns (*past **me***, *toward **them***). A subset noted entry by entry also takes a **gerund** (*advised **against** going*, *a step **toward** solving it*, *talked me **out of** quitting*, ***upon** signing*), and a gerund is what these prepositions want wherever a verb is called for — never a bare infinitive. A few — [into](#into), [over](#over), and [up to](#up-to) in one extraposed frame — take a nominal ***wh*-clause** (*look **into** what happened*, *a fight **over** who pays*, *it's **up to** you how we split it*), which is a noun phrase in disguise rather than an exception to the ban. Pronoun case matters here and is frequently botched in coordination: *walked past Ellen and **me***, never *and I*.

**Owned by other files.** Seven movement-capable prepositions have their homes elsewhere and are cross-referenced rather than repeated: [to](02-core-relational.md#to) (the destination preposition proper — *toward* aims, *to* arrives), [from](02-core-relational.md#from) (the source preposition proper), [by](02-core-relational.md#by) (motion past something), [for](02-core-relational.md#for) (destination of a departure — *left for the airport*, *bound for Denver*), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) (a target, as in *threw a rock at the window*), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in), and [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on). The purely static vertical prepositions — *above*, *below*, *beneath*, *underneath* — belong to the location group; only [under](#under) and [atop](#atop) appear here, because those are the two members of that set that take motion freely (*swam **under** the bridge*, *scrambled **atop** the boulder*). Two more words are split between files rather than duplicated: [beyond](#beyond) is here in its spatial sense and in [the time file](05-time.md#beyond) for *beyond midnight*, and *after* in its pursuit sense (*ran **after** the bus*) is noted with the sequencing preposition [after](05-time.md#after). Multi-word phrases that are only incidentally directional — *by way of*, *next to*, *on top of* — live in [complex and multi-word prepositions](11-complex-multiword.md); the ones whose whole job is direction — [en route to](#en-route-to), [on the way to](#on-the-way-to), [in the direction of](#in-the-direction-of), [clear of](#clear-of), and [out from under](#out-from-under) — are written out here.

## Goal and destination

Where the motion is headed, and what it hits when it gets there.

### aboard

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbɔrd/ &middot; *uh-BORD*
**Русский:** на борт, на борту — только о судне, самолёте, поезде; и движение, и положение сразу
**Type:** compound (*a-* "on" + *board*, fused — the *board* is a ship's side) &middot; **Register:** neutral, but tied to transport; formal or ceremonial outside it
**Complement:** noun phrase only — a vehicle or vessel. No gerunds, no clauses, no ordinary places: *aboard the terminal* is wrong. It is also a bare adverb with no object at all (*All aboard!*, *Welcome aboard*), which is the usual test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *aboard* [vessel / vehicle] &middot; [verb] [object] *aboard* [vessel / vehicle] &middot; *aboard* [vessel / vehicle], [main clause]
**Contrast:** *Aboard* covers both the motion and the resulting position, so it does the work of [onto](#onto) and [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) at once — but only for craft. Everyday American English says *get **on** the plane*; *climb **aboard** the plane* is the heightened or official register (announcements, journalism, nautical writing). **The spelling trap: the figurative "in agreement" sense is two words** — *get everyone **on board** with the plan*, never *onboard* or *aboard* — while *onboard* as one word is an adjective for equipment (*onboard navigation*).

The vessel preposition. It began as sailors' vocabulary and spread to trains, buses, planes, and spacecraft, keeping its slightly formal flavor the whole way. Its figurative use — joining a crew, and so joining a company or a project — is now common in business English, but almost always as the adverb (*welcome aboard*) rather than with an object.

**1. Motion onto a vessel or vehicle.** The passengers filed *aboard* the ferry.
**2. Position on a vessel or vehicle.** There were nineteen people *aboard* the flight.
**3. Joining a crew, staff, or undertaking (figurative).** She came *aboard* the campaign in June.

1. We climbed *aboard* the trawler before sunrise. *(1)*
2. They loaded the horses *aboard* the transport one at a time. *(1)*
3. Everyone *aboard* the train felt the jolt. *(2)*
4. The instruments *aboard* the probe are still returning data. *(2)*
5. Two of the three crew members *aboard* the vessel were rescued. *(2)*
6. He came *aboard* the firm as a junior partner. *(3)*
7. She brought two of her old analysts *aboard* the project with her. *(3)*
8. Compare the two-word idiom: we need the union *on board* before we announce anything. *(agreement — not this preposition)*

**See also:** [onto](#onto), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [into](#into), [off](#off), [upon](#upon)

### against

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *uh-GENST*; also /əˈɡeɪnst/ *uh-GAYNST* (minority American, standard British); casual /əˈɡɛns/ &middot; *uh-GENS*, with the final *t* dropped before a consonant (*against the wall*)
**Русский:** об, о (с силой) — соприкосновение с нажимом; против (противодействие); на фоне чего-то
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*advised against going*) — never a finite clause. Write *against the idea that he was lying*, not *against he was lying*.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; *against* [verb-ing] &middot; stranded: *the wall he threw it* **against**
**Contrast:** *Against* is the contact-with-force preposition; [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) and [onto](#onto) are neutral about force. *Put the ladder **on** the wall* is odd; *lean it **against** the wall* is right, because *against* supplies the pressure. For a target you aim at but may miss, use [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) — *threw a rock **at** the window* (aim) vs. *threw a rock **against** the window* (it landed).

Motion that terminates in forcible contact, or motion that pushes back on an opposing force. The static "leaning" sense is really the resting state left behind by that motion, which is why the same word covers both. From there the meaning generalizes to opposition of every kind — political, legal, and physical — and finally to a backdrop, where one thing is seen pressed up against another.

**1. Motion ending in forcible contact.** The hail rattled *against* the skylight.
**2. Resting contact under pressure.** He propped the bike *against* the fence.
**3. Motion opposing a force.** We were rowing *against* the current.
**4. Opposition, hostility, competition.** She voted *against* the measure.
**5. Seen with something behind it.** Black branches *against* a white sky.
**6. Protection or insurance from.** Vaccinated *against* measles.

1. The rain beat *against* the windshield the whole way home. *(1)*
2. He kicked the ball *against* the garage door until his mother made him stop. *(1)*
3. She leaned her shoulder *against* the doorframe and waited. *(2)*
4. Two rakes stood *against* the shed. *(2)*
5. Walking *against* that wind was like walking uphill. *(3)*
6. The salmon fight their way *against* the current for miles. *(3)*
7. Every senator from the state voted *against* the bill. *(4)*
8. Her lawyer advised *against* saying anything publicly. *(4)*
9. The lighthouse showed white *against* the storm clouds. *(5)*
10. His accent stood out *against* everyone else's. *(5)*
11. The clinic vaccinates *against* four strains. *(6)*
12. We're insured *against* flood but not against fire. *(6)*

**See also:** [toward](#toward), [into](#into), [onto](#onto), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on)

### as far as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɑr æz/ &middot; *az-FAR-az*; weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *uhz-FAR-uhz*
**Русский:** до (самого) — крайняя точка движения, протяжённости или охвата
**Type:** complex (three words) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. **With a clause it stops being a preposition and becomes a conjunction** (*as far as I know*, *as far as anyone can tell*) — a different construction with a different meaning.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *as far as* [noun phrase] &middot; [measurement] *as far as* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *As far as* marks the far end of a distance actually covered; [up to](#up-to) marks a ceiling that may not be reached (*up to fifty people* means fifty at most). *We walked as far as the bridge* says the bridge is where we stopped. Note also the disputed truncation *as far as money, we're fine* — standard edited English wants *as far as money **is concerned*** or simply *as for money*.

Names the limit of a movement or an extent: the point where the walking, the driving, or the seeing stops. The literal spatial sense extends easily to abstract reach (*as far as the evidence goes*). Beware the clause version, which is a completely different animal: *as far as I know* hedges a statement and has nothing to do with distance.

**1. The farthest point of a motion.** We drove *as far as* Flagstaff and turned back.
**2. The limit of an extent or a view.** Wheat *as far as* the horizon.
**3. The limit of an abstract reach.** The rules apply *as far as* the county line, and no further.

1. She walked *as far as* the mailbox and had to sit down. *(1)*
2. The bus goes *as far as* Ninth Street, then loops back. *(1)*
3. Corn *as far as* the eye could see. *(2)*
4. The flood reached *as far as* the second step. *(2)*
5. His authority runs *as far as* the state line. *(3)*
6. The grant covers tuition, but only *as far as* the first two years. *(3)*

**See also:** [up to](#up-to), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [toward](#toward), [down to](#down-to), [back to](#back-to)

### atop

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtɑp/ &middot; *uh-TAHP*; also /əˈtɔp/ &middot; *uh-TAWP* in the regions where the cot–caught merger settles on the rounded vowel — the same variation you hear in *on*, *onto*, and *upon*. The vowel is the one in *top*, so speakers who keep the two apart use /ɑ/ here
**Русский:** на вершине, на верх — сверху чего-то; перен. на первом месте в списке или иерархии
**Type:** compound (*a-* "on" + *top*, fused — the same *a-* that built *aboard*, *ashore*, *astride*, *away*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; commonest in journalism, sports writing, and physical description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses of any kind. It is one of the most restricted prepositions in this file: it wants a concrete object that has a top. Unlike *aboard*, *around*, and *along*, it has **no bare adverb use** — *he stood atop* with nothing after it is not English.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *atop* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *atop* [noun phrase] (*the flag atop the dome*) &middot; *sit / perch / sit atop* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *on top of* — the ordinary spoken equivalent and the safer choice in plain prose; see [on top of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of). The older *a-top of* is obsolete.
**Contrast:** *Atop* is [onto](#onto) and [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) compressed into one word with a summit added: it asserts that the object has a high point and that the thing is at it. *A book on the table* is neutral; *a book atop the table* is faintly odd, because a table's top is not a summit. Reserve it for things with a real high point — a hill, a roof, a pole, a stack, a standings table. Against [upon](#upon): both are raised in register, but *upon* is about contact and *atop* is about height.

The summit preposition. It was built the same way as *aboard* and *ashore* — a worn-down *on* fused to a noun — and it does in one word what *on top of* does in three, which is exactly why headline writers and sportswriters reach for it. Its figurative life is almost entirely about rankings and hierarchies: a team sits *atop* the standings, an executive sits *atop* an organization.

**1. Position on the top of.** A weather vane *atop* the barn.
**2. Motion to the top of.** He scrambled *atop* the boulder.
**3. Highest in a ranking or a hierarchy (figurative).** They finished the season *atop* the division.
**4. Piled on; added to what is already there.** Another delay *atop* the two we already had.

1. A wind sock hung *atop* the pole at the end of the runway. *(1)*
2. Snow was still sitting *atop* the ridge in June. *(1)*
3. She climbed *atop* the stepladder to reach the smoke detector. *(2)*
4. The cat hauled itself *atop* the refrigerator and stayed there all afternoon. *(2)*
5. They finished the week *atop* the conference for the first time since 2011. *(3)*
6. He spent twelve years *atop* the agency before anyone questioned the budget. *(3)*
7. A second rate hike *atop* the one in March would break the deal. *(4)*
8. The fee sits *atop* everything else the school already charges. *(4)*

**See also:** [onto](#onto), [upon](#upon), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [over](#over), [aboard](#aboard), [up](#up)

### back to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbæk tu/ &middot; *BAK-too*; weak /ˈbæk tə/ &middot; *BAK-tuh*
**Русский:** обратно в/к — возвращение к месту, состоянию, занятию или в прошлое
**Type:** complex (adverb *back* + preposition *to*, fixed as a unit) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*back to working nights*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *back to* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *back to* [noun phrase] &middot; *back to* [verb-ing] &middot; *dates back to* [year]
**Contrast:** Plain [to](02-core-relational.md#to) is neutral about whether you have been there before; *back to* asserts you have. *Go to Denver* is a trip; *go back to Denver* is a return. Do not confuse the temporal sense (*dates back to 1890*) with *since*, which measures forward from a start point rather than reaching backward from now.

Return motion — to a place, a state, an activity, or a point in the past. The *back* carries all the "again" meaning; *to* supplies the destination. In its temporal sense it reaches from the present into the past, which is why it pairs with *date*, *go*, *trace*, and *reach*.

**1. Return to a place.** She flew *back to* Seattle on Sunday.
**2. Return to a state or condition.** Things went *back to* normal within a week.
**3. Return to an activity or topic.** Let's get *back to* the schedule.
**4. Reaching into the past.** The building dates *back to* 1904.

1. We drove *back to* the hotel in the dark. *(1)*
2. He moved *back to* Ohio after the divorce. *(1)*
3. The swelling went *back to* normal overnight. *(2)*
4. After the layoffs, nothing went *back to* the way it was. *(2)*
5. She went *back to* teaching after ten years in software. *(3)*
6. Getting *back to* your question — no, we haven't decided. *(3)*
7. The family's records go *back to* the 1830s. *(4)*
8. The feud traces *back to* an argument over a fence. *(4)*

**See also:** [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [down to](#down-to), [up to](#up-to), [away from](#away-from), [from](02-core-relational.md#from)

### down to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdaʊn tu/ &middot; *DOWN-too*; weak /ˈdaʊn tə/ &middot; *DOWN-tuh*
**Русский:** вниз до; вплоть до — движение к нижней точке; снижение до уровня; включая мелочи
**Type:** complex (two words) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*came down to choosing between two*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *down to* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *down to* [noun phrase] &middot; *everything down to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Directional *down to* mirrors [up to](#up-to) and, like it, has an "as far as and including" sense — but *down to the last detail* scans the list from big to small, while *up to fifty* counts from zero to a ceiling. **The British sense "attributable to" (*it's down to you*, *that's down to the weather*) is not American**; Americans say *up to you* for responsibility and *because of* or *due to* for cause.

Motion to a lower place, a farther-along point, or a smaller amount. The "lower" is often social or geographic rather than literal — Americans go *down to* the city, *down to* Florida, and *down to* the store without any change in elevation.

**1. Motion to a lower or farther point.** We walked *down to* the water.
**2. Reduction to a level.** Trim it *down to* four minutes.
**3. As far as and including the smallest item.** She remembers it *down to* the color of his tie.

1. He went *down to* the basement to reset the breaker. *(1)*
2. They drove *down to* Austin for the weekend. *(1)*
3. The doctor got her dosage *down to* five milligrams. *(2)*
4. We cut the guest list *down to* forty. *(2)*
5. The set is period-accurate *down to* the light switches. *(3)*
6. Everything was accounted for *down to* the last penny. *(3)*

**See also:** [down](#down), [up to](#up-to), [back to](#back-to), [as far as](#as-far-as), [to](02-core-relational.md#to)

### en route to

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑn ˈrut tu/ &middot; *ahn-ROOT-too*; also /ˌɛn ˈrut tu/ &middot; *en-ROOT-too*. Weak /ˌɑn ˈrut tə/ &middot; *ahn-ROOT-tuh*, with the same *to* → /tə/ reduction as [up to](#up-to) and [back to](#back-to). **The *route* here is /rut/ — *ROOT*, never the American /raʊt/ *ROWT***. ✗*en ROWT* is the pronunciation error to avoid
**Русский:** по пути в/к, следуя в — в дороге к пункту назначения (офиц., книжн.)
**Type:** complex (three words — the French borrowing *en route* plus English [to](02-core-relational.md#to)) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in travel, shipping, aviation, and news writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — a destination or an outcome. A **gerund** is idiomatic in the figurative "on the way to achieving" sense that sportswriting made common (*en route to winning her third title*), but never a finite clause: *en route to he arrived* is not English. Strip the *to* and bare *en route* is an **adverb** with no object at all (*the parts are en route*), which is the test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** [be / verb of motion] *en route to* [destination] &middot; [clause], *en route to* [destination] &middot; *en route to* [verb-ing] (figurative)
**Variants:** ✗*enroute* — the closed spelling is common in aviation and military writing and is still treated as an error in edited American prose. Keep the two words.
**Contrast:** *En route to* insists that the trip has started and has not finished, which is what separates it from plain [to](02-core-relational.md#to) and from [toward](#toward): *flying to Denver* reports the whole trip, *en route to Denver* reports only the part in progress. Against [via](#via): *via* names the waypoint and *en route to* names the destination not yet reached, so the two sit at opposite ends of the same journey and often appear in one sentence (*en route to Santa Fe via Amarillo*).

The in-transit preposition. English already owns *to*, *toward*, and *on the way to*, but none of them states as flatly that the motion is under way, which is why dispatchers, airlines, and delivery notifications run on this one. Sportswriting then borrowed it for a figurative sense — the points a player scores *en route to* a win are the ones scored along the way — and that use is now at least as common in American print as the literal one.

**1. In transit to a destination.** The tanker is *en route to* Galveston.
**2. Stopping or passing through, on the way somewhere else.** We spent a night in Amarillo *en route to* Santa Fe.
**3. On the way to an outcome or an achievement (figurative).** She broke two records *en route to* the title.

1. The ambulance was *en route to* the hospital when the call was canceled. *(1)*
2. Your package is *en route to* the sorting facility in Memphis. *(1)*
3. Two of the crates were damaged *en route to* the warehouse. *(1)*
4. The delegation stopped in Reykjavik *en route to* Brussels. *(2)*
5. We refueled twice *en route to* the coast. *(2)*
6. He threw for four touchdowns *en route to* a 38–10 win. *(3)*
7. She set a personal best *en route to* winning her third state title. *(3)*
8. The bill picked up nine amendments *en route to* the governor's desk. *(3)*

**See also:** [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [toward](#toward), [via](#via), [as far as](#as-far-as), [on to](#on-to), [by way of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-way-of)

### in the direction of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə dəˈrɛkʃən əv/ &middot; *in-thuh-duh-REK-shun-uv*; weak /ɪn ðə dəˈrɛkʃən ə/ &middot; *in-thuh-duh-REK-shun-uh* — the final *of* reduces to a bare schwa in every ordinary use, exactly as it does in [out of](#out-of) and [off of](#off-of). Both /dəˈrɛkʃən/ and /daɪˈrɛkʃən/ are standard American, and the unstressed /də-/ is the commoner one inside this phrase
**Русский:** в направлении, в сторону — по курсу к чему-то, без обещания дойти
**Type:** complex (four words: preposition + determiner + noun + preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral, but four syllables longer than the word it replaces; in political and official prose it is usually a hedge
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — the object belongs to the final *of*. It also takes a nominal ***wh*-clause** (*in the direction of where the shots came from*), which is a noun phrase in disguise; it **never takes a *that*-clause**. A gerund is grammatical but graceless (*a move in the direction of settling*) — write *toward settling*.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *in the direction of* [noun phrase] &middot; *point / gesture / nod / look / turn in the direction of* [noun phrase] &middot; *a step / a move in the direction of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** This is [toward](#toward) in four words, and *toward* is nearly always the better choice. The phrase earns its length in exactly two places: when the direction is being *indicated* rather than traveled (*he nodded in the direction of the door* — he did not move, so *toward* would mislead), and when a writer wants to name a bearing without claiming a destination (*a move in the direction of reform* promises less than *a move toward reform*). Against [as far as](#as-far-as): that names a limit actually reached; this names a bearing only. If *toward* fits, use *toward*.

The long form of *toward*, useful mainly for pointing rather than going. Because it names a bearing and commits to nothing else, it is also the standard hedge in official and political language — nobody who calls a policy a step *in the direction of* something has promised to arrive. Learners meet it constantly in reading and should recognize it on sight, but should reach for the one-word preposition when writing.

**1. Moving on a bearing toward something.** The smoke drifted *in the direction of* the highway.
**2. Pointing, looking, or gesturing toward, with no motion of the pointer.** She nodded *in the direction of* the back office.
**3. Partial or hedged progress toward a goal (figurative).** The vote is a step *in the direction of* full repeal.

1. The herd moved slowly *in the direction of* the river. *(1)*
2. He drove off *in the direction of* the interstate without saying where he was going. *(1)*
3. Rescuers headed *in the direction of* where the last signal came from. *(1)*
4. She tipped her chin *in the direction of* the man by the window. *(2)*
5. The trooper pointed *in the direction of* the exit ramp. *(2)*
6. Everyone in the diner turned *in the direction of* the noise. *(2)*
7. The settlement is a small step *in the direction of* real oversight. *(3)*
8. Nothing in the budget moves *in the direction of* the reforms they promised. *(3)*

**See also:** [toward](#toward), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [as far as](#as-far-as), [up to](#up-to), [away from](#away-from), [via](#via)

### in to

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪn ˈtu/ &middot; *in-TOO*; weak /ɪn tə/ &middot; *in-tuh*
**Русский:** не предлог: частица глагола in + to — «сдал in to», «зашёл in to поговорить»; не into
**Type:** complex (two words) — strictly the adverb/particle *in* plus the preposition or infinitive marker [to](02-core-relational.md#to), not a preposition in its own right &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** whatever the following *to* takes — a noun phrase or objective pronoun when *to* is a preposition (*turned it in to the clerk*), or a bare verb when *to* is the infinitive marker (*came in to talk*). Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb + *in*] *to* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb + *in*] *to* [bare verb] &middot; [verb] [object] *in to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The mirror image of [on to](#on-to), and the test is the same. **If "to a position inside" fits, write [into](#into) as one word.** If *in* belongs to the verb — *turn in*, *hand in*, *drop in*, *come in*, *stop in*, *check in*, *log in*, *give in*, *cave in*, *tune in*, *sit in*, *call in* — the words stay apart. *He turned himself **in to** the police* means he surrendered; *he turned himself **into** the police* means he became them.

Not a preposition, but the counterpart the *onto* / *on to* pair makes everyone expect — and the error it prevents is funnier and commoner than its sibling's. Two distinct things hide behind the same two words: *to* as a real preposition after a particle verb (*hand it in **to** the office*) and *to* as an infinitive marker of purpose (*stopped in **to** say hello*). Neither can ever be spelled *into*.

1. She turned the paperwork *in to* the registrar on Friday. *(particle verb: turn in)*
2. He dropped *in to* see how the remodel was going. *(particle verb: drop in + infinitive)*
3. Log *in to* the payroll site with your employee number. *(particle verb: log in)*
4. They finally gave *in to* the union's last demand. *(particle verb: give in)*
5. I'll come *in to* sign the forms tomorrow morning. *(particle verb: come in + infinitive)*
6. Turn your keys *in to* the front desk when you check out. *(particle verb: turn in)*
7. Compare: she walked *into* the registrar's office. *(true preposition — one word)*

### into

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪntu/ &middot; *IN-too*; weak /ˈɪntə/ &middot; *IN-tuh*; casual /ˈɪnə/ &middot; *IN-uh*
**Русский:** в (внутрь) — движение внутрь, в отличие от статического in; врезаться в; превратиться в
**Type:** compound (*in* + *to*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (*got into arguing about it*), or a **wh-clause** in the inquiry sense (*an investigation into what went wrong*, *look into how this happened*) — never a *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *into* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *into* [noun phrase] &middot; *into* [wh-clause] &middot; stranded: *the room she walked* **into**; *what did you get yourself* **into**?
**Contrast:** **[in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in) is position, *into* is motion** — *He was in the pool* vs. *He jumped into the pool*. This is the central contrast of the whole file. Separately, **write [in to](#in-to) as two words when *in* belongs to the verb**: *turned himself **in to** the police* (he surrendered), *came **in to** talk*, *dropped **in to** say hi*. *Turned himself **into** the police* would mean he became them.

The general preposition of entry — motion from outside to inside, with the boundary crossed. From there it fans out into some of the widest polysemy in English: collision (where the "inside" is contact), transformation (where the thing enters a new state), penetration into a time span, inquiry, division, and the informal enthusiasm sense.

**1. Motion to a position inside.** She walked *into* the kitchen.
**2. Motion ending in collision.** He backed *into* a mailbox.
**3. Change of state or form.** The caterpillar turned *into* a moth.
**4. Penetration into a period or a distance.** They worked *into* the night.
**5. Inquiry, investigation.** An inquiry *into* the crash.
**6. Enthusiasm for (informal).** She's really *into* bluegrass.
**7. Chance encounter (with *run*, *bump*).** I ran *into* Marcus at the airport.
**8. Division (arithmetic).** Three *into* twelve is four.

1. Pour the batter *into* the pan and level it off. *(1)*
2. The dog bolted *into* the woods and didn't come back for an hour. *(1)*
3. She backed *into* a pole in the parking garage. *(2)*
4. He walked straight *into* the screen door. *(2)*
5. Translate the whole thing *into* Spanish by Friday. *(3)*
6. The argument turned *into* a shouting match. *(3)*
7. We were three weeks *into* the semester before anyone noticed. *(4)*
8. They kept rehearsing *into* the early morning. *(4)*
9. The board opened an investigation *into* the missing funds. *(5)*
10. I'll look *into* what happened with your order. *(5)*
11. He's been *into* woodworking since he retired. *(6)*
12. She's not *into* horror movies at all. *(6)*
13. I ran *into* my old landlord at the hardware store. *(7)*
14. We bumped *into* each other outside the courthouse. *(7)*
15. Four *into* twenty goes five times. *(8)*
16. Divide the class *into* groups of three. *(8)*

### on the way to

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈweɪ tu/ &middot; *ahn-thuh-WAY-too*; weak /ən ðə ˈweɪ tə/ &middot; *uhn-thuh-WAY-tuh* — *on* and the final *to* both fall to schwa, so the whole phrase is one unstressed run around a single stressed *WAY*. Casual speech goes further, to /ənə ˈweɪ tə/ &middot; *uh-nuh-WAY-tuh*
**Русский:** по пути в/к — по дороге к цели, попутно; перен. на пути к результату
**Type:** complex (four words: preposition + determiner + noun + preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral — the plain-English everyday equivalent of [en route to](#en-route-to)
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or **gerund** in the figurative sense (*on the way to becoming a nurse*) — never a finite clause: ✗*on the way to he arrived*. **The *to* here is a preposition, not an infinitive marker**, so a verb after it has to be an *-ing* form: ✗*on the way to become a nurse* is the error to avoid, the same trap that catches *look forward to* and *object to*. Strip the *to* and bare *on the way* is an adverbial with no object at all (*the parts are on the way*), which is the test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** [be / verb of motion] *on the way to* [destination] &middot; [clause], *on the way to* [destination] &middot; *on the way to* [verb-ing] &middot; [verb] [object] *on the way to* [destination]
**Variants:** *on my / your / his / her / our / their way to* — the possessive version is at least as common as the definite article and means the same thing (*she called on her way to the airport*). *On the road to* is the same frame with a different noun and is now almost entirely figurative (*on the road to recovery*).
**Contrast:** *On the way to* and [en route to](#en-route-to) mean the same thing; *en route to* is the shorter and more official one, and *on the way to* is what Americans say out loud. Against plain [to](02-core-relational.md#to): *drove to the airport* reports the whole trip, while *on the way to the airport* frames it as a stretch in the middle of one. Against [toward](#toward): *toward* names a bearing and promises no destination, while *on the way to* names the destination and says only that you have not reached it yet.

The plain-English in-transit phrase, and one of the highest-frequency multiword prepositions in American speech. Its literal job is to locate something inside a trip that has started and not finished, which is why so much of its work is incidental (*grab coffee on the way to the airport*). Its figurative sense — partway to an outcome — is where the common advanced error lives, because that *to* is a preposition and takes a gerund.

**1. In transit to a destination.** We're *on the way to* the hospital now.
**2. Passed or done during a trip; lying along the route.** There's a good diner *on the way to* the lake.
**3. Partway to an outcome or a state (figurative).** He's *on the way to* a full recovery.

1. Call me when you're *on the way to* the airport. *(1)*
2. The truck broke down *on the way to* the job site. *(1)*
3. We can pick up the cake *on the way to* your mother's. *(2)*
4. There's a hardware store *on the way to* the trailhead. *(2)*
5. The team is well *on the way to* a losing season. *(3)*
6. She's *on the way to* becoming the youngest partner the firm has had. *(3 — gerund complement)*
7. Compare the objectless adverbial: don't worry, the parts are *on the way*. *(no object — not this preposition)*

### on to

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑn ˈtu/ &middot; *ahn-TOO*; weak /ˈɑn tə/ &middot; *AHN-tuh*
**Русский:** не предлог: частица глагола on + to — «держаться on to», «перейти on to»; не onto
**Type:** complex (two words) — strictly the adverb/particle *on* plus the preposition [to](02-core-relational.md#to), not a preposition in its own right &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** whatever *to* takes — noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*moved on to fixing the roof*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb + *on*] *to* [noun phrase] &middot; *hold on to* [noun phrase] &middot; *pass* [object] *on to* [noun phrase] &middot; *move on to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The whole entry exists for one test. **If you can substitute "to a position on top of," write [onto](#onto) as one word.** If *on* belongs to the verb — *hold on*, *move on*, *pass on*, *hang on*, *sign on*, *log on*, *catch on* — keep the two words apart. *He held **on to** the rail* (he gripped it) vs. *He climbed **onto** the rail* (he got up on it). *Log **on to** the server*, not *log onto the server*.

Not a preposition, but the single most useful non-entry in this file: American writers get *onto* and *on to* wrong constantly, and the error is always in the same direction — collapsing a particle verb into a false preposition. Read the sentence with *on* attached to the verb; if it still makes sense, the words stay apart. One genuine gray area: *be on to someone* ("have discovered what they're doing") is written both ways in edited American prose, with *onto* now slightly commoner.

1. Hold *on to* the handrail — those steps are slick. *(particle verb: hold on)*
2. Let's move *on to* the second item. *(particle verb: move on)*
3. She passed the message *on to* her supervisor. *(particle verb: pass on)*
4. Log *on to* the portal with your employee ID. *(particle verb: log on)*
5. Hang *on to* that receipt in case it doesn't fit. *(particle verb: hang on)*
6. He signed *on to* the project in March. *(particle verb: sign on)*
7. Compare: she stepped *onto* the platform. *(true preposition — one word)*

### onto

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑntu/ &middot; *AHN-too*; also /ˈɔntu/ *AWN-too*; weak /ˈɑntə/ &middot; *AHN-tuh*
**Русский:** на (поверхность) — движение на поверхность, в отличие от статического on
**Type:** compound (*on* + *to*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun. Gerunds are marginal — *onto winning* is not idiomatic. Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *onto* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *onto* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the ledge he climbed* **onto**
**Contrast:** **[on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is position, *onto* is motion** — *The cat was on the roof* vs. *The cat jumped onto the roof*. And see [on to](#on-to) for the two-word spelling: use *onto* only where "to a position on top of" fits.

The surface counterpart of [into](#into): motion that ends with the moving thing resting on a surface. The extension to data and media (*copy it onto a drive*) treats the destination as a surface, and the informal "aware of" sense treats a person's scheme as something you have climbed on top of.

**1. Motion to a position on a surface.** He climbed *onto* the roof.
**2. Transfer onto a surface or medium.** Copy the files *onto* a flash drive.
**3. Emergence onto an open space.** The hallway opens *onto* a courtyard.
**4. Aware of, having caught on to (informal).** The auditors are *onto* him.

1. She stepped *onto* the platform just as the doors closed. *(1)*
2. The cat leapt *onto* the counter the second we left. *(1)*
3. Load the boxes *onto* the truck first. *(2)*
4. He transferred the tape *onto* his laptop. *(2)*
5. Their balcony looks *onto* the parking lot. *(3)*
6. The kitchen door opens *onto* the alley. *(3)*
7. I think she's *onto* something with that theory. *(4)*
8. Don't bother lying — they've been *onto* you for weeks. *(4)*

### toward

**Pronunciation:** /tɔrd/ &middot; *TORD* (the normal American pronunciation — one syllable); also /təˈwɔrd/ &middot; *tuh-WORD*, /ˈtoʊərd/ &middot; *TOH-erd*. **The one-syllable /tɔrd/ is itself the reduced form** — it is what Americans say in ordinary connected speech, and the two-syllable /təˈwɔrd/ is the careful or emphatic pronunciation, not the other way around. The *-s* of *towards* does not change any of this
**Русский:** к, по направлению к — направление, обращённость, приближение во времени, отношение к кому-то
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*a step toward solving it*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *toward* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *toward* [noun phrase] (*his attitude toward the job*) &middot; *toward* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *towards* — identical in meaning. **American English strongly prefers *toward*; *towards* is the dominant British form.** Both are correct in the United States; pick one and be consistent within a document. The same *-s* alternation runs through *forward(s)*, *backward(s)*, *upward(s)*, *afterward(s)*.
**Contrast:** **[to](02-core-relational.md#to) arrives; *toward* only aims.** *She ran to the door* means she got there; *she ran toward the door* leaves it open. That is the whole difference and it is reliable. Against [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at): *threw it at me* is aggressive and targeted, *threw it toward me* is merely directional.

Direction without commitment to arrival — which makes it the preposition of aim, orientation, approach, and partial progress. Its abstract senses all preserve that logic: an attitude *toward* something faces it, money *toward* a purchase moves you closer without completing it, and *toward evening* approaches evening without being it.

**1. Motion in the direction of.** He walked *toward* the exit.
**2. Facing or orientation.** She turned *toward* the window.
**3. Approaching a point in time.** *Toward* the end of the month things get tight.
**4. Attitude or feeling directed at.** His hostility *toward* the new manager was obvious.
**5. Contributing to a goal.** Every dollar goes *toward* the new roof.

1. The herd drifted *toward* the far fence. *(1)*
2. We rowed *toward* the lights on shore. *(1)*
3. He tilted the lamp *toward* the page. *(2)*
4. All the seats face *toward* the stage. *(2)*
5. It usually clouds over *toward* evening. *(3)*
6. *Toward* the end of the book she loses the thread. *(3)*
7. Her policy *toward* latecomers is not forgiving. *(4)*
8. Public opinion has shifted *toward* the proposal. *(4)*
9. That's a real step *toward* fixing the problem. *(5)*
10. Half his paycheck went *toward* the loan. *(5)*

### unto

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌntu/ &middot; *UN-too*; weak /ˈʌntə/ &middot; *UN-tuh*
**Русский:** устар., библ. к, до — стилизованная замена to; в живой речи только в застывших оборотах
**Type:** compound (*un-* + *to*, on the model of *until*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and biblical; literary
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses.
**Pattern:** [verb] *unto* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *unto* [noun phrase] (*a law unto himself*)
**Contrast:** In every living use, *unto* is a stylistically marked substitute for [to](02-core-relational.md#to). Using it in ordinary modern prose reads as either scriptural pastiche or unintentional comedy — the sole exceptions are the three fixed phrases below.

The King James Bible preserved *unto* long after ordinary English dropped it, which is why it survives almost entirely in quotation and in a handful of frozen idioms. It is worth knowing because Americans encounter it constantly in hymns, oaths, older legal boilerplate, and the phrases *a law unto himself* and *do unto others*.

**1. To (motion or recipient) — archaic.** Come *unto* me.
**2. Until, up to the point of — archaic.** Faithful *unto* death.
**3. Fixed modern idioms.** He is a law *unto* himself.

### up to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌp tu/ &middot; *UP-too*; weak /ˈʌp tə/ &middot; *UP-tuh*

Motion as far as a point — and then, by an easy extension, a maximum, a standard, a capability, and an obligation. The "doing something (usually shady)" sense is one of the most idiomatic constructions in casual American English and has no literal spatial content left in it.

**7. Meeting a standard.** The wiring isn't *up to* code.

### upon

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑn/ &middot; *uh-PAHN*; also /əˈpɔn/ &middot; *uh-PAWN*. The first syllable is **already reduced** and there is no further weak form: it is /ə/, never the full /ʌp/ of [up](#up), and ✗*UP-on* is the commonest mispronunciation among learners who see the spelling before they hear the word
**Русский:** на — книжн. вариант on; также «сразу после (чего-то)» и «наткнуться на»
**Type:** compound (*up* + *on*, fused) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or fixed-phrase; in plain modern prose [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) is the default
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or **gerund** (*upon signing the contract*) — never a finite clause. The gerund pattern is the one place *upon* beats *on* in modern American writing.
**Pattern:** [verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; *Upon* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [main clause] &middot; [noun] *upon* [same noun] (*row upon row*)
**Contrast:** *Upon* and [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) mean the same thing; *upon* is longer, heavier, and more formal. Reaching for it to sound elegant is the classic overcorrection — *sit upon the couch* is simply worse than *sit on the couch*. Reserve it for the four places it earns its keep: after certain verbs (*depend upon*, *call upon*, *seize upon*), in the temporal *upon* + gerund, in the doubling pattern (*row upon row*), and in frozen phrases (*once upon a time*).

The elevated twin of *on*, and — in its motion sense — a near-synonym of [onto](#onto) with a sudden or forceful edge: things *fall upon*, *descend upon*, and *come upon* one another. **1. On a surface (formal).** He laid a hand *upon* her shoulder.
**2. Motion onto, often sudden.** The dogs fell *upon* the scraps.
**3. Immediately after (with a noun or gerund).** *Upon* arrival, report to the front desk.
**4. Chance encounter (with *come*, *happen*, *stumble*).** We came *upon* a cabin in the woods.
**5. Doubling for quantity.** Row *upon* row of empty seats.

8. They stumbled *upon* the wreck by accident. *(4)*
9. Mile *upon* mile of the same brown grass. *(5)*
10. Thousands *upon* thousands of people showed up. *(5)*

## Source and departure

Where the motion starts, and what it leaves behind. English builds these freely by stacking [from](02-core-relational.md#from) onto another preposition, and four of the entries below are stacks of exactly that kind: [from before](#from-before), [from off](#from-off), [from out](#from-out), and [out from under](#out-from-under). Two of them, together with the dialect form [outen](#outen), are here to be recognized in reading rather than used in writing; each entry says which, and says why.

### away from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈweɪ frʌm/ &middot; *uh-WAY-frum*; weak /əˈweɪ frəm/ &middot; *uh-WAY-fruhm*
**Русский:** от, прочь от — удаление от точки; на расстоянии от; перен. уход от чего-то, избегание
**Type:** complex (adverb *away* + preposition *from*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*moving away from using coal*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *away from* [noun phrase] &middot; [measurement] *away from* [noun phrase] (*two blocks away from the station*) &middot; *away from* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Plain [from](02-core-relational.md#from) names the origin of a trip (*flew from Denver*); *away from* stresses increasing distance and often avoidance (*flew away from the storm*). Against [out of](#out-of): *out of* requires an enclosure to exit, *away from* does not — you move *away from* a tree, never *out of* one.

The direct opposite of [toward](#toward): motion whose whole point is that the gap is widening. It also does static duty as a distance measurement, and it carries a strong secondary sense of deliberate avoidance, which is why warnings use it (*stay away from the edge*).

**1. Motion increasing distance.** The boat pulled *away from* the dock.
**2. Distance measured from a point (static).** We're twenty minutes *away from* the airport.
**3. Abstract shift or change of course.** The industry is moving *away from* coal.
**4. Avoidance.** Stay *away from* him.

1. He backed slowly *away from* the dog. *(1)*
2. The plane banked *away from* the ridge. *(1)*

### clear of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklɪr əv/ &middot; *KLEER-uv*; weak /ˈklɪr ə/ &middot; *KLEER-uh* — the *of* drops to a bare schwa in every ordinary use, exactly as it does in [out of](#out-of) and [off of](#off-of). The stress never leaves *clear*, and ✗*kleer-UV* is the mistake to avoid
**Русский:** в стороне от, подальше от — на безопасном расстоянии; свободный от долга или подозрения
**Type:** complex (the adjective *clear* + [of](02-core-relational.md#of), fixed as a unit) &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in aviation, maritime, traffic, and safety writing, and in the everyday warning *steer clear of*
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or **gerund** in the avoidance sense (*steer clear of asking him about it*) — never a finite clause: ✗*clear of he was involved*. **The *to*-infinitive is impossible here**, because the *of* is a preposition: ✗*steer clear of to ask him* and ✗*steer clear of ask him* are both wrong, and only *steer clear of asking him* is English. With no object at all, *clear* is an adjective or an adverb (*the runway is clear*, *she got clear*), which is the test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** *steer / stay / keep clear of* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb of motion] *clear of* [noun phrase] (*pull, swing, haul, get clear of*) &middot; *be clear of* [noun phrase] &middot; *clear of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Against [away from](#away-from): *away from* says only that the gap is widening, while *clear of* says a specific hazard has been escaped completely — *the plane banked away from the ridge* reports the motion, *the plane was clear of the ridge* reports that the ridge no longer mattered. Against [out of](#out-of): *out of* needs an enclosure to exit, *clear of* needs only something to be obstructed or endangered by. Against [past](#past): *past the barge* is merely beyond it; *clear of the barge* is far enough beyond it to be safe.

The safe-distance preposition. The adjective *clear* — "unobstructed, free" — hardened onto *of* and now works as a single unit, with one idea running through all of it: something dangerous, obstructing, or entangling has been left far enough behind to stop counting. That is why controllers, dispatchers, and safety notices are built on the phrase, and why its commonest everyday shape is a warning rather than a report.

**1. Moving to a safe distance from a hazard or an obstruction.** The tug pulled *clear of* the pilings.
**2. At a safe distance from; no longer touching or threatened by.** The wingtip was barely *clear of* the hangar door.
**3. Free of an entanglement, a debt, or a suspicion (figurative).** Two more payments and she'll be *clear of* the loan.
**4. Deliberately avoiding, with steer, stay, or keep.** Steer *clear of* that intersection at five o'clock.

1. The ferry swung *clear of* the barge with about a yard to spare. *(1)*
2. He dragged the mower *clear of* the burning shed. *(1)*
3. Wait until the truck is *clear of* the crosswalk before you turn. *(2)*
4. The scaffolding has to stay eight feet *clear of* the power line. *(2)*
5. By March the company was finally *clear of* the receivership. *(3)*
6. He never got *clear of* the rumor, even after the charges were dropped. *(3)*
7. Stay *clear of* the tracks until the gates go back up. *(4)*
8. My advice is to steer *clear of* asking him about the sale. *(4 — gerund complement)*

### from before

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm bɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *frum-bee-FOR*; weak /frəm bəˈfɔr/ &middot; *fruhm-buh-FOR* — both words reduce, so in running speech the phrase is two schwas leading into the stressed *-FOR*. *From* is never stressed here unless the source is being contrasted with some other source
**Русский:** книжн. из пространства перед чем-то; о времени — «ещё до», «с времён до чего-то»
**Type:** complex (a preposition stack: [from](02-core-relational.md#from) + *before* in its "in front of" sense) &middot; **Register:** the spatial sense is formal, literary, and rare; the temporal sense (*from before the war*) is neutral and completely ordinary in American speech
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun — the object belongs to *before*. **A gerund is marginal** (*from before joining the firm*) and reads badly; rewrite with a noun (*from before her time at the firm*). **With a finite clause the *before* stops being a preposition and becomes a conjunction** — *I've known him from before he moved here* is good English, but there *from* governs a whole subordinate clause, which is a different construction from *from before the war*.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *from before* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *from before* [person] &middot; [noun] *from before* [period] &middot; *rise / step / clear / snatch from before* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The spatial sense is built exactly like [out from under](#out-from-under) and like *from behind*: a position preposition with [from](02-core-relational.md#from) stacked on the front to mark that position as the source. Against plain [from](02-core-relational.md#from): *took it from him* makes the person the source, *took it from before him* makes the space in front of him the source. American writers use the spatial sense sparingly, because readers hear the temporal *before* first and have to back up. For the temporal preposition on its own see [before (time)](05-time.md#before); for the spatial one see [before (place)](03-place-position.md#before).

Two senses that share a spelling and almost nothing else. The spatial stack is old and formal — a waiter clears a plate *from before* a guest, a figure rises *from before* a fire — and it survives mostly in literary description and in the reporting of ceremony. The temporal one is everyday American English and means simply "dating from earlier than": *a photo from before the fire*, *records from before the merger*. Only context separates them, which is why the spatial use needs a physical verb around it to be readable at all.

**1. Away from a position in front of something.** He rose *from before* the hearth.
**2. Taking something out of the space in front of a person.** She lifted the untouched plate *from before* him.
**3. Dating from a time earlier than.** A photograph *from before* the fire.

1. The dog had to be coaxed *from before* the oven door. *(1)*
2. The guard stepped *from before* the gate and waved them through. *(1)*
3. A waiter cleared the cups *from before* the two of them without a word. *(2)*
4. He pulled the microphone *from before* her face. *(2)*
5. Everything in the album is *from before* the divorce. *(3)*
6. She still has letters *from before* the family left Poland. *(3)*
7. Compare the clause version, where *before* is a conjunction: I've known him *from before* he moved to Denver. *(not this preposition)*

### from off

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm ˈɔf/ &middot; *frum-AWF*; weak /frəm ˈɔf/ &middot; *fruhm-AWF* — *from* reduces to /frəm/ and the stress lands on *off*, which has no weak form of its own (see [off](#off)). Speakers with the cot–caught merger say /frəm ˈɑf/ &middot; *fruhm-AHF*
**Русский:** устар., библ. и диал. с (поверхности) — то же, что off, но с упором на отделение
**Type:** complex (a preposition stack: [from](02-core-relational.md#from) + [off](#off)) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and biblical** in writing, and **regional** in American speech — ordinary in Appalachian, Southern, and older rural New England varieties. It is not standard edited American English in any register
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — the object belongs to *off*. No gerunds and no clauses of any kind: ✗*from off he left it there*. Neither half can be stranded on its own; the stack moves as a unit.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *from off* [surface] &middot; [verb] [object] *from off* [surface] &middot; *take / lift / wipe / sweep / roll from off* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against plain [off](#off), which means precisely the same thing: *took the book off the shelf* and *took the book from off the shelf* differ in register and in nothing else, and the *from* buys a modern writer nothing at all. Against [off of](#off-of): both are two-word doublets of *off*, but *off of* is unremarkable modern speech while *from off* sounds either scriptural or rural. Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from) alone: *from the shelf* is neutral about contact, *from off the shelf* insists the thing had been resting on the surface.

A preposition stack that standard American English has retired. It was ordinary in Early Modern English, and the King James Bible preserved it — *I will destroy it from off the face of the earth*, *wipe away tears from off all faces* — which is why it still reads as scripture rather than as merely old. It also survives as living regional speech in parts of the South and Appalachia, where it carries no archaic flavor whatever. Recognize it in reading and in dialogue; in your own writing use *off*.

**1. Away from a surface it had been resting on.** He took the crown *from off* his head.
**2. Away from a surface, in regional American speech.** Get that cat *from off* the table.

1. The stone was rolled *from off* the mouth of the tomb. *(1 — biblical register)*
2. The promise is that every tear will be wiped *from off* their faces. *(1 — biblical register)*
3. Dust was blown *from off* the ledger before anyone would open it. *(1 — deliberately archaic)*
4. She lifted the kettle *from off* the stove and set it on a trivet. *(2)*
5. Get your boots *from off* the good chair. *(2)*
6. He bought the melons *from off* a truck parked by the highway. *(2)*
7. Edited American prose writes every one of these with plain *off*. *(register note)*

### from out

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm ˈaʊt/ &middot; *frum-OWT*; weak /frəm ˈaʊt/ &middot; *fruhm-OWT*. With the *of* of the variant attached, the last two words run together exactly as they do in [out of](#out-of): /frəm ˈaʊɾə/ &middot; *fruhm-OW-duh*, the casual *outta*
**Русский:** книжн., поэт. из (изнутри) — усиленное out of; разг. from out of «(взялся) из ниоткуда»
**Type:** complex (a preposition stack: [from](02-core-relational.md#from) + [out](#out); the variant adds [of](02-core-relational.md#of) on the end) &middot; **Register:** bare *from out* is **literary and archaic** — nineteenth-century verse and heightened prose. *From out of* is not literary at all: it is ordinary informal American speech. Edited prose deletes the *from* from both
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. No gerunds, no clauses: ✗*from out of he was hiding*. Note that bare *from out* takes a **wider** object range than bare [out](#out) does on its own — modern *out* as a preposition is restricted to openings (*out the window*), while the verse form goes anywhere (*from out the shadows*, *from out the depths*), and that mismatch is itself part of what marks it as archaic. *From out of* simply takes the full range of [out of](#out-of).
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *from out* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb of motion] *from out of* [noun phrase] &middot; *come / rise / step / emerge from out of* [noun phrase] &middot; *from out of nowhere*
**Variants:** *from out of* — the living American form and the one you actually hear (*it came from out of nowhere*, *pulled it from out of the drawer*). Bare *from out* is the literary one. Both are redundant against plain [out of](#out-of), and a copy editor will cut the *from*.
**Contrast:** Against [out of](#out-of), which says the same thing in two words: *she stepped out of the shadows* is the plain sentence, and *she stepped from out of the shadows* adds emphasis on the origin plus a little theater. Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from): *from* names an origin without requiring an enclosure, while the stack insists there was something to come out of. Note that the commonest American instance is a frozen phrase — *out of nowhere* with *from* in front of it — where nobody hears the redundancy at all.

Two related stacks with quite different lives. Bare *from out* belongs to poetry and nineteenth-century prose (*from out the shadows*, *from out the depths*), and reaching for it today is a deliberate effect that reads as pastiche if it misses. *From out of* is not archaic in the least; it is everyday spoken American English, where the doubled source marking adds emphasis. Both get cut in edited writing, and both are worth recognizing on sight.

**1. Out of, with the origin emphasized, in literary style.** A voice came *from out* the dark.
**2. Out of, with emphasis, in ordinary speech.** He pulled a receipt *from out of* his wallet.
**3. The frozen phrase, of something sudden and unexplained.** The truck came *from out of* nowhere.

1. A single light showed *from out* the fog. *(1)*
2. A cry came *from out* the depths of the canyon. *(1)*

4. He dragged the box *from out of* the crawl space. *(2)*
5. A dog appeared *from out of* the corn and sat down in the road. *(3)*
6. The offer came *from out of* nowhere three days before the deadline. *(3)*
7. Edited prose deletes the *from* in every one of these. *(register note)*

### off

**Pronunciation:** /ɔf/ &middot; *AWF*; also /ɑf/ *AHF* for speakers with the cot–caught merger. **No weak form** — *off* keeps its full vowel even when unstressed, which is why [off of](#off-of) reduces in its second word (*offa* /ˈɔfə/) and never in its first
**Русский:** с (поверхности), прочь с — отделение от поверхности; сойти с дороги; жить на какие-то средства
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "obtain from a person" sense is informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a gerund only in the "sustained by" sense (*lives off selling parts*). Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *off* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *off* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the shelf it fell* **off**
**Contrast:** *Off* is the surface counterpart of [out of](#out-of): you get *off* a bus, a plane, a horse, or a roof (you were on it), but *out of* a car or a taxi (you were in it). Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from): *bought it off a guy* is informal for *bought it from a guy* — the *from* version is what edited prose wants. See [off of](#off-of) for the two-word variant.

Separation from a surface, and by extension from a route, a habit, or a supply. It is also one of the busiest verb particles in English (*take off*, *call off*, *put off*, *knock it off*), and the object test is the only way to tell the two apart: *He took his hat off the hook* has an object for *off*; *The plane took off* does not.

**1. Motion away from and losing contact with a surface.** He jumped *off* the porch.
**2. Removal from a surface.** Take the poster *off* the wall.
**3. Leaving a route; branching from.** A gravel road *off* Route 9.
**4. Situated at a distance from a coast or a road (static).** An island *off* the coast of Maine.
**5. No longer using or consuming.** She's been *off* caffeine for a year.
**6. Sustained by; drawing income from.** They live *off* her pension.
**7. Obtaining from a person (informal — prefer *from*).** I got the tickets *off* my cousin.

### off of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔf əv/ &middot; *AWF-uv*; weak /ˈɔf ə/ &middot; *AWF-uh*
**Русский:** разг. с (поверхности) — то же, что off; of лишнее, в письменной речи его убирают

### out

**Pronunciation:** /aʊt/ &middot; *OWT*; the final *t* flaps to /ɾ/ before a vowel — *out an open window* /ˌaʊɾ ən/ — which is the same reduction that turns *out of* into *outta*
**Русский:** в, через (наружу) — как предлог только с проёмами: out the window «в окно», out the door «в дверь»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "along" sense is informal and regional
**Complement:** noun phrase only, and only a narrow set of them — openings and routes (*the window*, *the door*, *the driveway*, *the highway*). It cannot take a gerund or a clause, and it cannot take most ordinary nouns: *out the building* is wrong; you need [out of](#out-of).
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *out* [opening] &middot; [verb] [object] *out* [opening]
**Contrast:** **As a true preposition, bare *out* is almost entirely restricted to openings** — *out the window*, *out the door*, *out the back*. Everywhere else the preposition is [out of](#out-of). Note the transatlantic split: Americans say *threw it **out** the window*; British speakers prefer *out **of** the window*. Elsewhere *out* is an adverb (*go out*, *the lights are out*), an adjective, a noun, and a verb.

Chiefly an adverb and a particle; a preposition only in the opening construction, where it names the aperture the motion passes through. American English has extended it informally to routes (*drive out the highway*), which sounds regional to many ears and should be avoided in writing.

**1. Through and beyond an opening.** She looked *out* the window.
**2. Along and away, of a road (informal, regional).** Drive *out* the old highway about six miles.

### out from under

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊt frəm ˈʌndər/ &middot; *owt-frum-UN-der* — the stress lands on *under*, and *from* is always the weak /frəm/, never the full /frʌm/; casual /ˌaʊt frəm ˈʌnɚ/ &middot; *owt-frum-UN-ner*, with the *d* dropped after the nasal exactly as in [under](#under)
**Русский:** из-под — движение из-под чего-то; перен. избавиться от груза, долга, чужой опеки
**Type:** complex (three words stacked: the particle *out* + [from](02-core-relational.md#from) + [under](#under)) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the figurative "free of a burden" sense is the commoner one in American writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — the object belongs to *under*. No gerunds, no clauses of any kind: write *out from under the mortgage*, never *out from under he owed money*. Unlike [out](#out) and [under](#under) on their own, the stack has **no bare adverb use** — *he finally got out from under* with nothing after it is heard in speech but leaves the object to be understood, not deleted.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *out from under* [noun phrase] &middot; *get / dig / climb / crawl / haul out from under* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *out from under* [noun phrase] &middot; *be out from under* [burden]
**Contrast:** Each of the three words does a job, which is why nothing shorter replaces it: *under* names the starting position, *from* marks it as a source, and *out* says the exit was completed. ✗*Out of the porch* is wrong (you were never inside it), and *from under the porch* loses the sense of having gotten clear. Against [out of](#out-of): *out of* needs an enclosure, *out from under* needs only something on top. Against [off](#off): *off* leaves a surface you were on, *out from under* leaves a weight you were beneath.

One of the few genuinely productive preposition stacks left in English, and American English treats it as a single unit. The literal sense is the exit of something covered or pinned — a cat *out from under* a parked truck, a foot *out from under* a fallen beam. The figurative sense generalizes the weight to anything that presses down: a debt, a boss, a contract, a verdict.

**1. Motion out of a position beneath something.** The dog crawled *out from under* the porch.
**2. Pulling something out of a position beneath something else.** He slid the mat *out from under* the door.
**3. Freed from a weight, a debt, or an authority (figurative).** The company is finally *out from under* its loans.

1. The cat shot *out from under* the parked truck when the engine started. *(1)*
2. She wriggled *out from under* the collapsed shelving without a scratch. *(1)*
3. He backed the mower *out from under* the deck stairs. *(2)*
4. Somebody yanked the rug *out from under* the whole argument. *(2)*
5. Two more payments and they'll be *out from under* the second mortgage. *(3)*
6. The division never got *out from under* the last reorganization. *(3)*
7. It took a decade to climb *out from under* that verdict. *(3)*

### out of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊt əv/ &middot; *OWT-uv*; weak /ˈaʊɾ ə/ &middot; *OW-duh* (the casual spelling *outta*)
**Русский:** из (изнутри) — выход наружу; из материала; из чувства (из страха); «без» — запас кончился
**Type:** complex (two words) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*talked me out of quitting*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *out of* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *out of* [noun phrase] &middot; *out of* [verb-ing] &middot; [number] *out of* [number] &middot; stranded: *the mess we got* **out of**
**Contrast:** *Out of* is the exit counterpart of [into](#into), just as [off](#off) is the exit counterpart of [onto](#onto): you get *out of* a car (you were in it) but *off* a bus (you were on it). Against [from](02-core-relational.md#from): *from* names an origin without an enclosure (*a letter from Ohio*), *out of* requires something to come out of (*a letter out of the file*).

The general preposition of exit — and then, through the metaphor of a container, the preposition of source, material, motive, depletion, and proportion. The depletion sense (*out of milk*) is so common that it has almost broken free of the spatial one, but the logic still holds: the supply is a container and you have come to the end of it.

**1. Motion from inside to outside.** She climbed *out of* the car.
**2. Material or source.** The bowl is carved *out of* walnut.
**3. Motive or cause.** He apologized *out of* guilt, not conviction.
**4. No longer having; depleted.** We're *out of* eggs.
**5. Beyond a range or limit.** The kids were *out of* earshot.
**6. Proportion; a score.** Nine *out of* ten dentists.
**7. Based in; operating from (characteristically American).** She runs the whole business *out of* a garage.

1. He dragged the mower *out of* the shed. *(1)*
2. Water was seeping *out of* a crack in the foundation. *(1)*
3. The whole cabinet is built *out of* reclaimed oak. *(2)*
4. She quoted a line *out of* the middle of the report. *(2)*
5. I only went *out of* curiosity. *(3)*
6. They stayed together *out of* habit. *(3)*
7. We're *out of* printer paper again. *(4)*
8. He's been *out of* work since the plant closed. *(4)*
9. Keep the knife *out of* reach of the kids. *(5)*
10. The signal drops as soon as you're *out of* the valley. *(5)*
11. She got eighty-eight *out of* a hundred. *(6)*
12. Four *out of* five respondents said the same thing. *(6)*
13. The band is *out of* Muscle Shoals, not Nashville. *(7)*
14. He flies *out of* O'Hare almost every week. *(7)*

### outen

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊtən/ &middot; *OW-tun*; commonly /ˈaʊʔn̩/ &middot; *OW-tn*, with a glottal stop and a syllabic *n*, and between vowels the *t* flaps to *OW-dun*. **No weak form** — this is a spoken form that carries its own stress, and the *out* keeps its full diphthong exactly as it does in [out](#out)
**Русский:** диал., прост. (юг США) из — местное произношение out of, нестандартное
**Type:** simple in shape, but historically [out of](#out-of) worn down — the *of* reduced onto the end as a syllabic *-en*, the same erosion that gives casual *outta* &middot; **Register:** **dialectal and nonstandard.** Recorded chiefly for the South and South Midland — Appalachia, the Ozarks, and rural Pennsylvania. It reaches print almost only as eye-dialect in fiction, folklore collections, and song lyrics, and it is never standard edited English
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. No gerunds, no clauses. It has no adverb use and is not stranded: ✗*that's the barn he came outen* is not how the speakers who have the form use it.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *outen* [noun phrase] &middot; *come / get / take / drink outen* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *outen* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Outen* is [out of](#out-of) in a regional pronunciation that acquired its own spelling — *he came outen the barn* is *he came out of the barn*, with nothing added. Compare the parallel dialect respellings *offen* (= *off of*), *ahind* (= *behind*), and *ontil* (= *until*). **Do not confuse it with the Pennsylvania Dutch English verb** *outen*, "put out, turn off" (*outen the light*): that word takes a direct object and heads no prepositional phrase, and the two have nothing to do with each other in use.

A regional American form of *out of*, listed here so readers can identify it and writers know to leave it alone. Where it lives it is ordinary speech and carries no stigma; outside those areas — in edited prose, or in a classroom — it will be read as an error, and the honest advice is to recognize it and write *out of*. It is included for the same reason [athwart](#athwart) is: you will meet it in reading, not because anyone should reach for it.

**1. Out of; from inside something.** He came *outen* the smokehouse with a ham.
**2. Made from; drawn from a source.** The whistle was cut *outen* a willow branch.

1. The kids came running *outen* the barn when the truck pulled in. *(1)*
2. Get *outen* my kitchen with those boots on. *(1)*
3. She drank the water right *outen* the dipper. *(1)*
4. The handle was whittled *outen* a piece of hickory. *(2)*
5. Nothing good ever came *outen* that hollow, he told us. *(2)*
6. Standard American English writes every one of these with *out of*. *(register note)*

## Path, route, and traversal

The middle of the journey: the surface crossed, the tunnel entered, the line followed.

### across

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkrɔs/ &middot; *uh-KRAWSS*; also /əˈkrɑs/ &middot; *uh-KRAHSS*. **No separate weak form** — the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and the stress never leaves *-cross*, which is true of the whole *a-* family here (*aboard*, *atop*, *along*, *alongside*, *astride*, *athwart*, *around*). The regional form *acrost* /əˈkrɔst/ is nonstandard.
**Русский:** через, поперёк — с одной стороны на другую; на той стороне; по всей площади
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses. It is also a **bare adverb** with no object at all (*we paddled across*, *go across at Fifth*), which is the usual test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *across* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *across* [noun phrase] (*the diner across the street*) &middot; stranded: *the river we swam* **across**
**Contrast:** The three-way traversal contrast is the one to memorize. ***Across* is a two-dimensional surface, side to side** (*swam **across** the river*); **[through](#through) is a three-dimensional medium you are inside of** (*waded **through** the river*); **[over](#over) is above an obstacle, or across a raised surface** (*jumped **over** the puddle*, *drove **over** the bridge*). All three can describe the same river; they describe different trips.

Motion from one edge of a surface to the opposite edge. Once the crossing is complete, the same word names the resulting position (*the diner across the street*), and once you stop thinking of a single line, it names distribution over a whole area (*offices across the country*).

*Across* has the usual second life outside this word class: an **adverb** with no object (*she waved and ran **across***) and a **verb particle** in two very common phrasal verbs, *come across* (encounter, or make an impression) and *get across* (communicate). The object test settles which is which — *swam **across** the river* has an object and is the preposition, *swam **across** to the far bank* has none and is the adverb, and in *the point never got **across*** the word belongs to the verb.

**1. Motion from one side to the other.** They swam *across* the channel.
**2. On the opposite side of (static).** The pharmacy is *across* the street.
**3. Distributed over an area.** Stores *across* the Midwest.
**4. Crosswise; forming a cross.** A branch lay *across* the trail.

1. She ran *across* the field to catch up. *(1)*
2. We drove *across* three states in a day. *(1)*
3. He lives *across* the hall from me. *(2)*
4. There's a bakery *across* the square. *(2)*
5. The company has offices *across* the country. *(3)*
6. Reaction was mixed *across* the department. *(3)*
7. A fallen pine lay *across* the driveway. *(4)*
8. She drew a line *across* the page. *(4)*

**See also:** [through](#through), [over](#over), [past](#past), [along](#along), [athwart](#athwart), [to](02-core-relational.md#to)

### along

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAWNG*; also /əˈlɑŋ/ &middot; *uh-LAHNG*. **No separate weak form** — like [across](#across), the first syllable is already /ə/ and cannot reduce further
**Русский:** вдоль, по — следуя по длине чего-то; перен. по линии рассуждения или развития
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only. It requires a **linear** object — a road, a river, a wall, a coast, a fence. *Along the room* is wrong; *across the room* is right.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *along* [linear noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *along* [linear noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the road we walked* **along**
**Contrast:** [Across](#across) cuts a surface at right angles to its length; *along* follows the length. Against [down](#down) and [up](#up): all three can describe walking a street, but *along* is neutral about direction while *up* and *down* pick one. Do not confuse the preposition with the complex preposition [along with](08-accompaniment-exception.md#along-with), which means "in addition to" and has no spatial sense.

Motion that follows the length of something linear, or position strung out over that length. Because it needs a line to follow, it is the natural preposition for roads, rivers, shorelines, fences, and walls, and it is wrong with an area. It is also a heavily used adverb and particle (*come along*, *bring it along*, *play along*, *get along*), where it takes no object at all.

**1. Following the length of something.** We walked *along* the beach.
**2. Positioned in a line beside something.** Cottonwoods *along* the ditch.
**3. Following an abstract line of thought or development.** Something *along* those lines.

1. She jogged *along* the canal every morning. *(1)*
2. The train runs *along* the river for twenty miles. *(1)*
3. We drove *along* the coast road until the pavement gave out. *(1)*
4. There are streetlights *along* the whole stretch. *(2)*
5. Wildflowers grow *along* the fence line. *(2)*
6. Sandbags were stacked *along* the levee for half a mile. *(2)*
7. He wants something *along* the lines of what we did last year. *(3)*
8. The reforms proceeded *along* familiar lines. *(3)*

**See also:** [across](#across), [alongside](#alongside), [down](#down), [up](#up), [past](#past), [around](#around), [along with](08-accompaniment-exception.md#along-with)

### alongside

**Pronunciation:** /əˌlɔŋˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-lawng-SYDE*; also /əˌlɑŋˈsaɪd/ *uh-lahng-SYDE*
**Русский:** рядом с, борт о борт — вплотную и параллельно; перен. вместе с, наряду с
**Type:** compound (*along* + *side*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun, and a **gerund** in the "in addition to" sense (*alongside running the shop, she does the books*) — never a finite clause. Write *alongside the claim that he lied*, never *alongside he lied*. It is also a **bare adverb** with no object at all (*the tug came alongside*, *she stayed alongside the whole way*), which is the usual test for telling the two uses apart.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *alongside* [noun phrase] &middot; *work / serve / fight / run alongside* [person] &middot; *pull / come alongside* [vessel or vehicle] &middot; *alongside* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *alongside of* — the redundant two-word form, exactly parallel to [off of](#off-of): unremarkable in speech, deleted in edited prose.
**Contrast:** [Along](#along) follows the length of a line; *alongside* puts you next to that line, facing the same way. Against *beside* (a static location preposition): *beside* is pure position, while *alongside* keeps the motion and the shared heading of *along* — *she sat beside him* is still, *the cruiser pulled alongside us* is not. Against [with](02-core-relational.md#with) for collaboration: *worked with the union* claims joint action, *worked alongside the union* claims only proximity and parallel effort, which is precisely why negotiators and press releases reach for it.

The nautical word that came ashore. A ship comes *alongside* a pier — parallel to it, touching or nearly touching — and every later sense keeps that picture: two things side by side, pointed the same way, one of them usually having moved to get there. The collaboration sense is now the commonest one in American writing, and its deliberate weakness compared with *with* is the whole reason it gets chosen.

**1. Motion to a position beside and parallel.** The patrol boat pulled *alongside* the freighter.
**2. Position beside and parallel (static).** A bike path runs *alongside* the highway.
**3. Working or serving in parallel with (figurative).** She trained *alongside* three other residents.
**4. In addition to; at the same time as.** The new rule takes effect *alongside* the tax change.

1. A pickup pulled *alongside* us and honked. *(1)*
2. The tug came *alongside* the barge and made fast. *(1)*
3. There's a drainage ditch *alongside* the whole access road. *(2)*
4. They parked the trailer *alongside* the barn. *(2)*
5. He worked *alongside* his father for twenty years. *(3)*
6. Volunteers served *alongside* the county crews all week. *(3)*
7. The rebate runs *alongside* the existing credit, not instead of it. *(4)*
8. *Alongside* the layoffs, the company cut its travel budget in half. *(4)*
9. *Alongside* teaching two sections, he advises the debate team. *(4 — gerund complement)*

**See also:** [along](#along), [astride](#astride), [past](#past), [across](#across), [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)

### around

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *uh-ROWND*; casual /əˈraʊn/ &middot; *uh-ROWN*, with the final *d* dropped before a consonant
**Русский:** вокруг, по — круговое движение и окружение; бесцельно по; разг. «около, примерно»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund in the abstract "centered on" sense (*built around getting the timing right*). Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *around* [noun phrase] &middot; *around* [number / time] &middot; stranded: *the block we walked* **around**
**Variants:** *round* — the same word with the *a-* worn off. **In American English *round* as a general preposition is British-sounding** (*walked round the lake*); Americans keep it only in fixed expressions: *round the clock*, *all year round*, *year-round*, *come round*, *round about*. Write *around* unless you are inside one of those phrases.
**Contrast:** Against [past](#past): *drove around the accident* means you took a curving path avoiding it; *drove past the accident* means you went by in a straight line. Against [through](#through): *around the crowd* skirts it, *through the crowd* penetrates it. The approximation sense competes with *about* — both are fine in American English, and *around* is the commoner spoken choice.

One of the most polysemous prepositions in the language, unified by the idea of a curved or enclosing path. From a circular route it moves to enclosure, to skirting an obstacle, to aimless wandering inside an area, and finally — because a circle has no exact edge — to approximation.

It is just as busy outside this word class, as an **adverb** (*turn around*, *stick around*, *is she around?*) and as a **verb particle** (*mess around*, *shop around*, *pass it around*), and in both of those jobs it takes no object at all. The object test separates them cleanly: *we walked **around** the block* is the preposition, with *the block* as its object, while *we walked **around** for an hour* is the adverb, with nothing after it. The same split holds for the variant: *come round* is the adverb, *round the clock* is the preposition.

**1. Circular or curving motion.** We drove *around* the block twice.
**2. Surrounding position.** A stone wall *around* the garden.
**3. Motion to the other side of an obstacle.** The store is just *around* the corner.
**4. Aimless motion within an area.** We wandered *around* the museum for hours.
**5. Approximately (of time, number, amount).** He got in *around* midnight.
**6. Centered on; organized about (abstract).** The whole plan is built *around* one assumption.

1. The trail loops *around* the lake and comes back. *(1)*
2. She wrapped a scarf *around* her neck. *(1)*
3. There's a fence *around* the whole property. *(2)*
4. The kids gathered *around* the fire pit. *(2)*
5. Traffic was routed *around* the construction. *(3)*
6. The bathroom is *around* the corner on your left. *(3)*
7. He spent the afternoon poking *around* the hardware store. *(4)*
8. She showed us *around* the house. *(4)*
9. There were *around* fifty people at the service. *(5)*
10. Let's say *around* four o'clock. *(5)*
11. The curriculum is organized *around* four themes. *(6)*
12. Their whole schedule is built *around* the baby's naps. *(6)*

**See also:** [past](#past), [through](#through), [across](#across), [along](#along), [over](#over), [astride](#astride), [athwart](#athwart)

### aslant

**Pronunciation:** /əˈslænt/ &middot; *uh-SLANT*. **No separate weak form** — the first syllable is already the reduced /ə/ and the stress never leaves *-slant*, which holds for the whole *a-* family in this file (*aboard*, *atop*, *across*, *along*, *astride*, *athwart*)
**Русский:** книжн., поэт. наискось через — по диагонали поверх чего-то
**Type:** compound (*a-* "on" + *slant*, fused — the same *a-* that built *aboard*, *atop*, and *astride*) &middot; **Register:** literary and poetic, and archaic outside verse and heightened description; rare in ordinary American prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses of any kind: ✗*aslant the roof was leaking*. With no object it is an **adverb** or a predicate **adjective** (*the blind hangs aslant*, *her hat sat aslant*), and that objectless use is in fact its commoner modern life, so the object test matters more here than the word's frequency would suggest.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *aslant* [noun phrase] &middot; *lie / fall / lean / grow aslant* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *aslant* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *aslant of* turns up in older prose and adds nothing. The relatives *slantwise*, *slantways*, and *aslope* are adverbs only and take no object at all.
**Contrast:** *Aslant* and [athwart](#athwart) are the two crosswise words in this file and both are literary; the difference is the angle and the intent. *Athwart* crosses squarely and usually obstructs, while *aslant* crosses on a diagonal and usually does not. Against [across](#across): *across* is the ordinary modern word and says nothing about angle, which is why it is almost always the better choice. Against [along](#along): *along* follows a line, *aslant* cuts one obliquely. In plain American prose write *across* or *at an angle across*.

An old crosswise word, one of the *a-* family built by fusing a worn-down preposition onto a noun. Modern American English keeps it alive mainly as an adverb or a predicate adjective; the prepositional use covered here has retreated to poetry, nature writing, and deliberately heightened description. Most readers have met it in a single line of Shakespeare — the willow that grows *aslant* a brook in *Hamlet* — and that is roughly the register it still occupies.

**1. Across at a slant; diagonally over.** A shadow fell *aslant* the porch.
**2. Leaning or growing across, of something rooted or resting.** A birch leaned *aslant* the trail.
**3. Cutting obliquely across an argument or an account (figurative, rare).** The finding runs *aslant* the official version.

1. Late light lay *aslant* the kitchen floor. *(1)*
2. Rain drove *aslant* the windshield the whole way over the pass. *(1)*
3. A ladder stood *aslant* the barn wall, one foot in the mud. *(1)*
4. In *Hamlet* the willow grows *aslant* a brook, which is where most readers meet the word. *(2)*
5. A fallen cottonwood lay *aslant* the creek and we crossed on it. *(2)*
6. Her account runs *aslant* everything the report concluded. *(3)*
7. Compare the bare adverb: the gate hangs *aslant* and will not latch. *(no object — not this preposition)*

**See also:** [athwart](#athwart), [across](#across), [astride](#astride), [along](#along), [over](#over), [around](#around)

### astride

**Pronunciation:** /əˈstraɪd/ &middot; *uh-STRYDE*
**Русский:** верхом на — с ногой по обе стороны; по обе стороны границы или рубежа
**Type:** compound (*a-* "on" + *stride*, fused — the same *a-* as *aboard*, *atop*, *ashore*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary; fully everyday only with horses, bikes, fences, and shoulders
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses. It also has a bare adverb use in older prose (*he rode astride* rather than sidesaddle), but the preposition is what you meet in modern American writing, and it always has an object.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *astride* [noun phrase] &middot; *sit / stand / swing / climb astride* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *astride* [noun phrase] (*a town astride the border*)
**Contrast:** Against [athwart](#athwart), the other crosswise word in this file: *athwart* lies across something and usually obstructs it, while *astride* straddles it with a leg — or a half — on each side and obstructs nothing. Against [across](#across): *a bridge across the river* only spans it, but *a city astride the river* has real substance on both banks. Against [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on): you ride *on* a horse in ordinary speech and *astride* one when the writer wants the posture in the picture.

The straddling preposition — one leg on each side, and by extension one part on each side. It stays alive in ordinary American English for riding (*astride a horse*, *astride the fence*) and in a productive figurative use for anything that sits on both sides of a line: a company astride two industries, a town astride a state border.

**1. Mounted or seated with a leg on each side.** She swung *astride* the mare.
**2. Extending across, with part on each side.** The village sits *astride* the river.
**3. Occupying both sides of a boundary or a category (figurative).** A firm *astride* banking and insurance.

1. He climbed *astride* the fence rail and refused to come down. *(1)*
2. The boy sat *astride* his father's shoulders for the whole parade. *(1)*
3. The county road runs *astride* the ridge for two miles. *(2)*
4. Their property lies *astride* the creek, which is why the survey took so long. *(2)*
5. The city is *astride* the state line and has two of everything. *(3)*
6. The company sits *astride* logistics and retail and answers to regulators in both. *(3)*

**See also:** [athwart](#athwart), [across](#across), [alongside](#alongside), [over](#over), [atop](#atop), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on)

### athwart

**Pronunciation:** /əˈθwɔrt/ &middot; *uh-THWORT*
**Русский:** книжн., мор. поперёк — крест-накрест, часто как преграда; перен. вопреки, наперекор
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** literary, nautical, and archaic — rare in ordinary American prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses of any kind.
**Pattern:** [verb] *athwart* [noun phrase] &middot; *lie / stand / run athwart* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** In its spatial sense it is a heightened [across](#across), with an added suggestion of obstruction or of a crosswise angle. In its figurative sense it means "in opposition to," where ordinary prose uses [against](#against). Using it unironically outside nautical writing or deliberate rhetoric will read as affectation.

An old word for "crosswise," surviving chiefly in sailing (*athwart the deck*, *athwartships*) and in one famous rhetorical formula — *to stand athwart history, yelling Stop*. Included here for completeness and because readers meet it in older prose, not because anyone should reach for it.

**1. Across, especially at an angle or as an obstruction.** A spar lay *athwart* the deck.
**2. In opposition to (figurative).** The ruling runs *athwart* fifty years of precedent.

1. A fallen mast lay *athwart* the bow. *(1)*
2. Shadows fell *athwart* the courtyard. *(1)*
3. Storm clouds moved *athwart* their course. *(1)*
4. The decision cuts *athwart* everything the agency has argued. *(2)*
5. He set himself *athwart* the reform effort from the first day. *(2)*

**See also:** [across](#across), [against](#against), [over](#over), [past](#past), [beyond](#beyond)

### beyond

**Pronunciation:** /biˈjɑnd/ &middot; *bee-YAHND*; weak /bəˈjɑnd/ &middot; *buh-YAHND* — the first syllable reduces to a schwa in every unstressed use
**Русский:** за, дальше — по ту сторону; мимо и дальше; сверх возможностей, за пределами чего-то
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *yond*, the old word for "that place over there") &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun; a **gerund only in a closed set** of "past the point of" idioms (*beyond caring*, *beyond saving*, *beyond repairing*) — you cannot invent new ones. **Never a finite clause:** write *beyond the fact that he was there*, never *beyond he was there*. *Beyond* is also a bare **adverb** (*the fields beyond*) and a **noun** (*the great beyond*); as always, the object is the test.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *beyond* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *beyond* [noun phrase] &middot; *go / stretch / extend / reach beyond* [noun phrase] &middot; *beyond* [verb-ing] (closed set) &middot; *far / well beyond* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against [past](#past), its plainer twin: both put a line behind you, but *past* is the ordinary word for the motion (*drove past the mill*) and *beyond* is the literary word for what lies on the far side (*the country beyond the mill*). Against [over](#over): *over the hill* passes above it, *beyond the hill* is on its far side. Against *outside* for limits: *beyond my control* and *outside my control* both work, with *beyond* the more emphatic and the more final. **This entry covers the spatial and figurative senses only; for the temporal senses — *beyond midnight*, *ran beyond Friday* — see [beyond](05-time.md#beyond) in the time file.**

The far-side preposition. Where *past* reports the crossing, *beyond* describes the territory on the other side, which is why it leans literary and why so much of its use has gone figurative: a limit, a capacity, an authority, or an understanding is a boundary you can be on the wrong side of, and *beyond* is the word that puts you there.

**1. On the far side of; farther out than.** The road continues *beyond* the ridge.
**2. Motion past and onward.** The plow pushed *beyond* the last mailbox.
**3. Outside the limit of a capacity, a power, or an authority.** That's *beyond* my control.
**4. Past the point of, in a closed set of idioms.** The truck was *beyond* repair.
**5. More than; other than (usually under a negative).** We know nothing *beyond* what she told us.

1. There's nothing *beyond* the last cattle guard but sagebrush. *(1)*
2. On a clear day you can see three ranges *beyond* the valley. *(1)*
3. The trail runs *beyond* the wilderness boundary and keeps climbing. *(2)*
4. She walked *beyond* the last streetlight and then turned back. *(2)*
5. The cost is *beyond* anything we budgeted for. *(3)*
6. Whether the permit clears is *beyond* my authority. *(3)*
7. The frame was *beyond* saving after the second collision. *(4)*
8. By the third hour he was *beyond* caring who was right. *(4)*
9. She said nothing *beyond* her name and her attorney's number. *(5)*
10. There's no evidence *beyond* the one receipt. *(5)*

**See also:** [past](#past), [over](#over), [across](#across), [away from](#away-from), [out of](#out-of), [beyond](05-time.md#beyond)

### down

**Pronunciation:** /daʊn/ &middot; *DOWN*. **No weak form** — the diphthong keeps its full quality even unstressed, so *down* is always audible; the same is true of its opposite, [up](#up)
**Русский:** вниз по — сверху вниз вдоль чего-то; но также просто «по (улице)», без вертикали
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses. (The look-alike *down to* does take a gerund; see [down to](#down-to).)
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *down* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *down* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the hill we rolled* **down**
**Contrast:** Against [up](#up): with vertical objects the pair really is opposite (*up the ladder* / *down the ladder*), but with streets and hallways both simply mean "along," and the choice is idiomatic or relative to the speaker (*down the hall*, *up the street*). Against [off](#off): you fall *down* the stairs (traveling along them) but *off* the ladder (losing contact with it).

Motion from a higher point to a lower one along a path — and then, with the vertical sense bleached out, plain motion along a horizontal path. Americans go *down* the hall, *down* the street, and *down* to the store with no change in elevation. Its two figurative senses run in exact parallel with [up](#up): an order travels *down* the chain of command as readily as a complaint works its way *up* it, and a decision pushed *down the road* is postponed rather than lowered. It is equally at home as an adverb and particle (*sit down*, *the system is down*, *turn it down*), and there the object test applies: *ran **down** the hill* has an object, *the car broke **down*** does not.

**1. From higher to lower along something.** The ball rolled *down* the driveway.
**2. Along a path (no vertical sense).** Her office is *down* the hall.
**3. Following the course of a flow or a coast.** They floated *down* the river.
**4. Onward through a stretch of time.** The story has come *down* the generations.
**5. Lower on a scale, a list, or a chain of authority (figurative).** The order came *down* the chain of command.

2. Water was running *down* the inside of the wall. *(1)*
3. Walk *down* Third Street until you hit the light. *(2)*
4. The copier is *down* the corridor on the right. *(2)*
5. We paddled *down* the Green for three days. *(3)*
6. The fog moved *down* the coast overnight. *(3)*
7. The recipe has come *down* the family since her great-grandmother. *(4 — literary)*
8. *Down* the years the town has barely changed. *(4 — literary)*
9. We'll worry about the second phase *down* the road. *(4 — everyday)*
10. Three owners *down* the line, nobody could find the original deed. *(4 — everyday)*

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *OH-ver*. **No true weak form** — the stressed /oʊ/ keeps its full quality however fast you talk, which is why *over* is always audible where [of](02-core-relational.md#of) and [to](02-core-relational.md#to) vanish. What does happen is that the second syllable goes syllabic and swallows a following *the*: *over the hill* runs together as /ˈoʊvɚðə/ &middot; *OH-vruh-thuh*
**Русский:** над, через, по — поверх и на ту сторону; свыше (числа); в течение (периода); по (радио, сети)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (*argued over paying the bill*), or a **wh-clause** in the dispute sense (*a fight over who gets the house*) — never a *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; *over* [wh-clause] &middot; stranded: *the fence he jumped* **over**
**Contrast:** For traversal, see the three-way rule under [across](#across): *over* implies height — an obstacle cleared or a raised surface crossed. Against *above* (a static preposition, filed with the location group): *above* is pure vertical position with no contact and no motion, so *a blanket **above** her knees* is impossible, while *over* allows motion, covering, and contact. On quantity, *over thirty people* and *more than thirty people* are both standard American English — the old newsroom ban on quantitative *over* was dropped by the AP Stylebook in 2014.

Along with [into](#into), the widest-ranging preposition in this file. The core is a curved path passing above something; from there it spreads to covering, to superiority, to exceeding a number, to spanning a period, and to the topic of a dispute — a chain that makes sense once you see that all of them involve one thing positioned above or beyond another.

*Over* also has a heavy second life outside this word class. It is an **adverb** (*come over*, *move over*, *the plane flew over*), a **verb particle** (*hand over the keys*, *turn it over*, *think it over*, *start over*), and a predicate **adjective** meaning finished (*the meeting is over*, *it's over between them*). Two tests separate them. The object test: *he climbed **over** the fence* has an object, *the fence fell **over*** does not. The movement test for particles: *handed **over** the keys* can be split (*handed the keys over*), while a true preposition can never be pulled away from its object — ✗*climbed the fence over*.

**1. Motion above and across.** The ball sailed *over* the fence.
**2. Traversal of a raised or extended surface.** We drove *over* the pass.
**3. Position above, without contact.** A lamp hung *over* the table.
**4. Covering.** She pulled a sheet *over* the couch.
**5. More than (number, age, amount).** *Over* two hundred people applied.
**6. Spanning a period of time.** We talked it out *over* the weekend.
**7. The subject of a dispute or feeling.** They're fighting *over* money.
**8. Authority or control.** She has three crews *over* her.
**9. By means of a medium.** I'd rather not do this *over* the phone.
**10. Recovered from.** He's finally *over* the flu.

2. A hawk circled *over* the field. *(1)*

4. We walked *over* the footbridge single file. *(2)*

### past

**Pronunciation:** /pæst/ &middot; *PAST*; casual /pæs/ &middot; *PASS*, with the final *t* dropped before a consonant (*past the store*) — which is one reason the ✗*passed* misspelling below is so persistent: in speech the two words are often identical
**Русский:** мимо — мимо точки в движении или положении; о часах «столько-то минут после» (ten past three)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund in the "beyond the point of" sense (*past caring*, *past worrying about it*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *past* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *past* [noun phrase] &middot; [minutes] *past* [hour] &middot; *be past* [verb-ing] &middot; stranded: *the store we drove* **past**
**Contrast:** **The spelling trap: *past* is the preposition, *passed* is the past tense of the verb *pass*.** *We drove **past** the school* and *We **passed** the school* are both correct; ***We drove passed the school* is not.** If a form of *have* or *be* could precede it, or if it is the main verb, you want *passed*. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by): *drove by the house* and *drove past the house* are near-identical, with *past* slightly stronger on the idea of going beyond. Against [through](#through) and [around](#around): *past* skirts the thing entirely without entering or circling it.

Motion that goes beyond a point without stopping, and then position beyond that point. The clock sense (*quarter past four*) is the same beyond-ness applied to time, and the abstract sense — *past his prime*, *past caring* — applies it to a limit or a capacity.

*Past* is unusually busy outside this word class: an **adjective** (*past events*, *the past-due balance*), a **noun** (*in the past*, *she has a past*), and an **adverb** with no object at all (*a truck rumbled **past***). The object test matters more here than almost anywhere else in the file, because the adverb and the preposition sit in the same slot after the same verbs: *she walked **past** the diner* has an object and is the preposition; *she walked **past** without looking up* has none and is the adverb. The adverb is also the form stranded at the end of a relative clause (*the store we drove **past***), where the object has moved to the front rather than disappeared.

**1. Beyond a point, in motion.** She walked *past* me without a word.
**2. Beyond a point, in position.** The house is two blocks *past* the light.
**3. Later than the hour, on a clock.** It's ten *past* nine.
**4. Beyond a limit, an age, or a capacity.** The milk is *past* its date.

1. A cruiser went *past* the window with its lights off. *(1)*
2. He shoved *past* us to get to the door. *(1)*
3. The trailhead is a quarter mile *past* the bridge. *(2)*
4. Their place is just *past* the grain silo. *(2)*
5. The meeting started at half *past* two. *(3)*
6. It was already twenty *past* by the time she called. *(3)*
7. Honestly, I'm *past* caring what he thinks. *(4)*
8. The car is well *past* the point of being worth fixing. *(4)*

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; *THROO*; weak /θrə/ &middot; *thruh* in rapid speech before a consonant (*through the door*)
**Русский:** через, сквозь — насквозь; на протяжении всего периода; посредством; амер. «по … включительно»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral; the "finished" sense is informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (*got through cleaning the garage*) — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *through* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *through* [noun phrase] &middot; [day] *through* [day] &middot; stranded: *the tunnel we came* **through**
**Variants:** *thru* — a respelling, not a separate word. It is acceptable only in signage and trade names (*drive-thru*, *thruway*) and in informal notes; in any edited prose it is an error.
**Contrast:** See the three-way traversal rule under [across](#across): *through* means you were **inside** the thing. Also note a distinctively American use: **inclusive *through* in date ranges** — *Monday through Friday* includes Friday. British English does not use it this way and says *Monday to Friday* or *up to and including Friday*, which is why *May through September* can confuse an international reader. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by) for means: *found it through a friend* (the friend was the channel) vs. *written by a friend* (the friend was the agent).

Motion entering one side of a volume and exiting the other, or moving within a medium. Everything else follows from that: a period of time is a stretch you pass through, a process is something you come out the far side of, and an intermediary is what your action travels through. The inclusive date sense is one of the few places where American and British usage genuinely diverge in meaning rather than style.

Outside this word class, *through* is an **adverb** (*let me through*, *the bullet went straight through*), a **verb particle** (*the deal fell through*, *read it through*, *see it through*, *follow through*), and — in sense 6 — a predicate **adjective** (*we're through*, *are you through?*). The object test applies as usual: *we drove **through** the tunnel* has an object, *the call didn't go **through*** does not.

**1. In one side and out the other.** The bullet went *through* the door.
**2. Moving within a medium or a mass.** He pushed *through* the crowd.
**3. From beginning to end of a period or process.** She slept *through* the whole flight.
**4. By means of; via an intermediary.** I found the apartment *through* a coworker.
**5. Up to and including (dates — American).** The office is closed December 24 *through* January 2.
**6. Finished with (informal, predicative).** Are you *through* with the stapler?

1. We walked *through* the tunnel with a flashlight. *(1)*
2. Rain was coming *through* a gap in the roof. *(1)*
3. She waded *through* knee-deep water to reach the truck. *(2)*
4. The plow cut *through* two feet of drift. *(2)*
5. He sat *through* three hours of testimony. *(3)*
6. They got *through* the audit without a single finding. *(3)*
7. The grant came *through* the state, not the city. *(4)*
8. You learn that kind of thing *through* experience. *(4)*
9. The pool is open Memorial Day *through* Labor Day. *(5)*
10. Applications are accepted Monday *through* Thursday. *(5)*
11. I'm *through* with the whole argument. *(6)*
12. Let me know when you're *through* with the car. *(6)*

### throughout

**Pronunciation:** /θruˈaʊt/ &middot; *throo-OWT*; weak /θrəˈaʊt/ &middot; *thruh-OWT* — the stress sits on the second syllable, so the first reduces in connected speech
**Русский:** по всему, на протяжении всего — в каждой части пространства, периода или группы
**Type:** compound (*through* + *out*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses. It needs an object with **extent**: a space, a period, or a group. *Throughout the box* is odd; *throughout the building* is right.
**Pattern:** [verb] *throughout* [noun phrase of space or time] &middot; *Throughout* [noun phrase], [main clause]
**Contrast:** [Through](#through) traces a path from one end to the other; *throughout* saturates every part with no path implied. *The smoke went **through** the house* describes a route; *smoke spread **throughout** the house* describes total coverage. Against *during*: *during the war* locates an event inside a period, *throughout the war* insists it lasted the whole of it.

Complete penetration of a space, a period, or a population — "in every part of," never "along a route." Its two senses, spatial and temporal, are the same idea applied to extent in space and extent in time. It also works as an adverb with no object (*the house was rewired throughout*), which is the usual test for separating the two.

**1. In or to every part of a space.** The smell of paint spread *throughout* the house.
**2. For the whole of a period.** It rained *throughout* the night.
**3. Among every part of a group or a body of work.** The same motif recurs *throughout* her novels.

1. Speakers were installed *throughout* the building. *(1)*
2. The blight has turned up *throughout* the county. *(1)*
3. He stayed calm *throughout* the interview. *(2)*
4. *Throughout* the 1970s the plant ran three shifts. *(2)*
5. The error appears *throughout* the dataset. *(3)*
6. Support for the measure was strong *throughout* the district. *(3)*

### under

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndər/ &middot; *UN-der*; casual /ˈʌnɚ/ &middot; *UN-ner*, with the *d* dropped after the nasal in rapid speech (*under the bed*)
**Русский:** под — под чем-то; меньше (числа, возраста); под властью или по условиям; в процессе чего-то
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses. Write *under the assumption that he'd agree*, never *under he'd agree*.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *under* [noun phrase] &middot; *under* [number] &middot; stranded: *the bed it rolled* **under**
**Contrast:** *Under* is the everyday word and the only one of the four that comfortably takes motion (*swam under the bridge*). *Below* is about level on a scale or a vertical position, often at a distance (*below sea level*, *the apartment below*). *Beneath* is *under* with a formal or literary register, plus the figurative "unworthy of" sense (*beneath contempt*). *Underneath* stresses being covered or hidden and is often an adverb (*the note was underneath*). Against [over](#over): they are a true opposite pair across most of their senses.

Motion to or position in a place lower than something, usually with the upper thing covering or spanning it. The abstract senses all preserve a sense of being subordinate to something above: less than a number, subject to an authority, or in the middle of a process that is being applied to you.

*Under* is also a bare **adverb** with no object (*the swimmer went under*, *the business went under*) and a productive prefix (*underpaid*, *understaffed*, *underestimate*). It is only a marginal verb particle, though — unlike *up*, *down*, *over*, *off*, and *out*, it does not build many phrasal verbs — so the preposition is what you meet nearly every time, and the object test rarely has to work hard: *the ball rolled **under** the couch* has an object, *the ship rolled and went **under*** does not.

**1. Motion to a position beneath.** The ball rolled *under* the couch.
**2. Position beneath.** The dog sleeps *under* the desk.
**3. Covered by.** She was buried *under* three blankets.
**4. Less than (number, age, amount).** Everything in the store is *under* twenty dollars.
**5. Subject to an authority, a rule, or a condition.** The company is *under* new management.
**6. In the process of.** The bridge has been *under* repair since spring.
**7. Filed or classified as.** Look it up *under* "amortization."

### up

**Pronunciation:** /ʌp/ &middot; *UP*. **No weak form** — the vowel is already lax and does not reduce further, which is why in [up to](#up-to) only the second word weakens (*UP-tuh*, never *uh-TOO*)

**2. By means of a channel, medium, or intermediary.** Send the files *via* the shared drive.

## Directional forms that are not prepositions

Three groups of words look like they belong in this file and do not. The test is the same as everywhere else: **a preposition has an object; these do not.**

**The *-ward* forms.** *Homeward, upward, downward, forward, backward, onward, inward, outward, seaward, skyward, northward, heavenward* are **adverbs and adjectives**, not prepositions. You can write *the ship turned homeward*, but not *the ship turned homeward the harbor* — with a named destination you need a real preposition (*toward home*, *to the harbor*). On the *-s*: ✗ *American English prefers the bare *-ward* form for the adverb** (*moved forward*, *glanced upward*, *stepped backward*), while British English leans to *-wards*; both are acceptable in the United States, but be consistent. As an ✗ *adjective the *-s* is always wrong**: *an upward trend*, never *an upwards trend*. The one member of the family that did become a preposition is [toward](#toward), which took an object and never let go.

**The bare adverbs and particles.** *Up, down, over, through, past, around, off, out, back, along, alongside, aboard, aside, apart, away, forth* all appear with no object at all — *sit down*, *the deal fell through*, *a truck rumbled past*, *come along*, *step aside*. In that position they are adverbs, or particles belonging to the verb. The moment a noun phrase follows, check which it is: *He ran **up** the hill* (preposition — *up the hill* is a unit, and you can front it: *Up the hill he ran*) versus *He ran **up** a big tab* (particle — you can split it: *He ran a big tab up*). That movement test is the reliable one.

**The archaic direction adverbs.** *Hence, thence, whence* mean "from here, from there, from where," and *hither, thither, whither* mean "to here, to there, to where." They already contain the preposition, which is why *from whence* is a redundancy that has been criticized since the eighteenth century (though it appears in the King James Bible and in Shakespeare). None of them takes an object. In modern American English they survive only in *hence* meaning "therefore," in *henceforth*, and in the fixed phrase *hither and thither*.
