# The core relational six: of, to, for, from, by, with

> The six workhorses that hold English sentences together — the words that mark possession, direction, purpose, source, agency, and company, and that shrink to a single unstressed vowel the moment anyone speaks at normal speed.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These six carry more grammatical load than lexical meaning: *of* does the work of a genitive case ending, *to* marks the indirect object, *by* marks the agent of a passive, and none of them means much standing alone. What unites them is extreme polysemy — fifteen to twenty distinct senses apiece is normal here, and the sense is supplied by the words on either side rather than by the preposition itself. The trap that runs through the whole group is the ear: **all six reduce hard in ordinary speech — *of* to /əv/ and then to a bare /ə/, *to* to /tə/, *for* to /fɚ/, *from* to /frəm/, *with* to /wəθ/, and even *by* to /bə/ in a phrase like *by the way*** — which is exactly why *could've* so often comes out written as *could of*, and why every entry below gives the weak form next to the strong one. The second trap is *to*, which is really two words — the preposition, which takes a noun or an *-ing* form (*look forward to going*), and the infinitive marker, which takes a bare verb (*want to go*).

## The two that are mostly grammar: *of* and *to*

These two have been bleached the furthest. *Of* is functionally a genitive case marker and *to* a dative one, which means that in a great many sentences neither word contributes any meaning at all — it is simply the piece of grammar that lets two other words connect. They are also the two that reduce hardest in speech, down to a single unstressed vowel.

### of

**Pronunciation:** /ʌv/ &middot; *UHV*; also /ɑv/ &middot; *AHV*; weak /əv/, and in fast speech bare /ə/ before a consonant &middot; *uhv, uh*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **gerund** (*the risk of falling*, *capable of doing it*, *accused of stealing it*), a possessive plus gerund in careful writing (*the possibility of his leaving*), and a *wh-* or *whether-*clause after a small set of nouns (*the question of whether to go*, *an account of what happened*) — **never a bare that-clause and never a to-infinitive**: ✗ *the risk of that he falls*, ✗ *capable of to do it*. To hang a finite clause on it you need *of the fact that*. Unlike *from*, it will not take another preposition or an adverb as its object: ✗ *of under the porch*, ✗ *of here*. It strands in the dependent-preposition uses (*What are you afraid of?*, *the one thing I'm sure of*) but not in the plain genitive, where *the roof of the house* has no stranded equivalent.
**Pattern:** [noun] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; [quantifier] *of* [the/this/my + noun] &middot; [adjective] *of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [verb] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *it is* [adjective] *of* [person] *to* [verb] &middot; [distance] *of* [landmark] &middot; stranded: *the one thing I'm sure* **of**
**Variants:** *o'* — an archaic clipping, now frozen into a handful of fixed forms (*o'clock*, *jack-o'-lantern*, *man-o'-war*, *will-o'-the-wisp*). Never write it as a live alternative to *of*. The habitual *of a morning*, *of a Sunday* survives in Southern and Appalachian American speech, but it is regional and not general American.
**Contrast:** Against the *'s*-genitive: use *'s* for people and animals (*Maria's car*), *of* for objects, abstractions, and any long or complex phrase (*the cover of the book I lent you*). Both appear together in the **double genitive**, which is standard and not an error: *a friend of mine*, *a cousin of Dave's*. Against *made from*: *of* keeps the material visible (*a table of oak*), *from* implies it has been transformed (*wine from grapes*). Against *about* for topic: *of* is the older and more literary choice (*he never speaks of it*), *about* the everyday one. Against *off*: *of* ends in /v/ and *off* in /f/; *get off the bus* is the careful form and *off of* is informal. **And because the reduced *have* of *could've* is pronounced /əv/ — exactly like weak *of* — people write *could of*, *should of*, *would of*, *must of*. All four are always errors.**

*Of* is the emptiest word in English. It carries almost no meaning of its own; it is the piece of machinery that English uses in place of a case ending, gluing one noun to another and leaving the two nouns to work out between them what the relationship actually is. That is why it is the most frequent preposition in the language and the hardest to define — *a cup of coffee*, *the King of Spain*, *a man of honor*, and *the destruction of the city* use the same three letters for four unrelated jobs.

**1. Possession and belonging.** The *of*-genitive, the alternative to *'s*.

1. The roof *of* the house needs replacing before winter.
2. She's working through the collected poems *of* Elizabeth Bishop.

**2. Part and whole.** A piece taken out of a larger set or mass. With a quantifier, *of* appears only in front of a determiner: *most students* but *most of the students*.

3. I ate half *of* the pizza standing at the counter.
4. Three *of* us are flying out Thursday.

**3. Quantity and measure.** After a measure noun, a container word, or a collective.

5. He poured a cup *of* coffee and stood there drinking it.
6. We picked up two pounds *of* ground chuck and a bag *of* yellow onions.

**4. Material and composition.** What a thing is made out of, when it is still recognizably that material — compare *made from*, which implies a transformation.

7. It's a table *of* solid oak, not veneer over particleboard.
8. She wore a dress made *of* raw silk.

**5. Apposition — naming.** The two nouns pick out the same thing: X is Y.

9. The city *of* New Orleans runs its own school board.
10. He was born in the month *of* August, during a hurricane.

**6. The binominal frame — *a* [noun] *of a* [noun].** The second appositive pattern, and an evaluative one: the first noun is the speaker's judgment and the second is the thing being judged, so *a monster of a truck* is a truck and not a monster. Informal, very American, and the article is obligatory on both halves: *a hell of a day*, *a jewel of a house*, *that idiot of a landlord*.

11. It was a hell *of* a week and nobody wants to talk about it.
12. He drives a monster *of* a truck that barely fits in the driveway.

**7. Subject or object of an action noun.** After a noun built from a verb, *of* can mark either the doer or the thing done, and the two readings can collide: *the shooting of the hunters* goes both ways.

13. The arrival *of* the guests threw off the whole schedule.
14. The destruction *of* the city took three days.

**8. Characteristic and attribute.** *Of* plus an abstract noun does the work of an adjective: *of value* = valuable.

15. He's a man *of* few words and fewer opinions.
16. The second report was *of* no use to anyone.

**9. Fixed verb or adjective plus *of* — the dependent preposition.** A large set in which *of* means nothing at all and simply has to be learned with the word in front of it: *consist of*, *approve of*, *think of*, *dream of*, *remind X of*, *accuse X of*, *convince X of*, *inform X of*, *warn of*, *get rid of*, *take care of*, *be aware of*, *be capable of*, *be fond of*, *be worthy of*. **Every one of them takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form and never a to-infinitive**: ✗ *capable of to swim*, ✗ *accused of to steal it* — write *capable of swimming*, *accused of stealing it*.

17. The whole meal consisted *of* white rice and a boiled egg.
18. She reminds me *of* somebody I went to high school with.
19. I finally got rid *of* the couch my brother left here.

**10. Cause, after certain verbs and adjectives.** *Die of*, *tired of*, *ashamed of*, *afraid of*, *proud of*, *guilty of*.

20. Her grandfather died *of* pneumonia at eighty-nine.
21. I'm sick and tired *of* hearing about the parking situation.

**11. Topic — what something is about.** Slightly more formal or literary than *about*.

22. We got news *of* the accident a little after midnight.
23. He almost never speaks *of* his years in the service.

**12. Separation and deprivation.** *Cure of*, *rob of*, *deprive of*, *rid of*, *relieve of*, *strip of* — a survival of the older sense that *of* once shared with *from*.

24. The treatment finally cured her *of* the infection.
25. The storm deprived half the county *of* power for a week.

**13. Sensory impression — what a thing smells or tastes like, or faintly suggests.** After *smell*, *taste*, *reek*, *stink*, *smack*, and *savor*. The verb is intransitive here, and *of* introduces the quality rather than an object. (*Speak of* is not this sense — that is topic, sense 11.)

26. The whole cabin smelled *of* woodsmoke and wet wool.
27. His explanation smacked *of* something he'd rehearsed.

**14. Evaluative — a quality attributed to a person through an act.** The frame is *it is* + adjective + *of* + person + to-infinitive. **Compare the *for*-frame directly: *It was kind of her to call* judges *her*; *It was hard for her to call* says nothing about her character at all.** Adjectives that take *of* here are the ones that describe people: *kind*, *good*, *nice*, *rude*, *stupid*, *careless*, *brave*, *generous*.

28. It was good *of* you to drive all the way out here.
29. That was careless *of* me, and I'm sorry.
30. It's rude *of* him to leave without saying anything.

**15. Position relative to a reference point.** With the compass words and a few others, *of* marks what the position is measured from. American English extends it to numbers, where *north of* means *more than*.

31. The ranch is about forty miles north *of* Amarillo.
32. The repair came in somewhere north *of* two thousand dollars.

**16. Time — minutes before the hour.** A genuine American regionalism, strongest in the Northeast and the industrial Midwest; *a quarter of six* is 5:45. Elsewhere the choices are *till*, *to*, and *before*.

33. It's a quarter *of* six — we should get going.
34. The bus gets in at ten *of* nine.

**17. Dates and fixed expressions.** *The fifth of July*, *of course*, *all of a sudden*, *as of*, *of late*, *of necessity*, *of one's own accord*.

35. The lease starts on the first *of* September.
36. All *of* a sudden every light in the block went out.
37. As *of* Monday, the office moves to the third floor.

### to

**Pronunciation:** /tu/ &middot; *TOO*; weak /tə/ before a consonant, /tʊ/ or /tu/ before a vowel &middot; *tuh*. In fast speech it fuses with the verb in front of it and is written that way only in dialogue: *going to* → *gonna*, *got to* → *gotta*, *want to* → *wanna*, *have to* → *hafta*, *ought to* → *oughta*. Every one of those is the infinitive marker, never the preposition.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*to me*, *to him and me*, *to whom*), a free relative (*give it to whoever asks first*, *drive us to where the trail starts*), and **gerund** — *look forward to going*, *be used to living here*, *object to being asked*, *be accustomed to working late*. ✗ *look forward to see you*, ✗ *object to be asked*, ✗ *accustomed to work late* are the standard errors. **No that-clause**; a *whether-*clause appears only inside a couple of fixed frames (*it comes down to whether we can pay for it*, *as to whether*). **The *to* that stands in front of a bare verb (*want to go*) is not this word at all: it is the infinitive marker, a separate item that happens to be spelled the same.** The test is substitution — if a noun fits the slot (*look forward to the trip*), the *to* is a preposition and an *-ing* form is required after it; if a noun does not fit (✗ *want to the trip*), it is the infinitive marker and a bare verb is required.
**Pattern:** [verb] *to* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *to* [noun phrase] &middot; [adjective] *to* [noun phrase] &middot; *from* [X] *to* [Y] &middot; [fixed verb + *to*] *to* [verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *to* [noun] &middot; stranded: *the person I gave it* **to**; *Who did you talk* **to**?
**Contrast:** Against *toward*: *to* implies arrival, *toward* only orientation — *She walked to the door* means she got there, *She walked toward the door* does not. **Spell it *toward* in American English; *towards* with the *-s* is the British default and looks imported in American copy.** Against *at*: *to* delivers and *at* targets — *throw the ball to me* is a pass, *throw the ball at me* is an assault; *shout to* is so you can hear, *shout at* is anger. Against *for*: *a letter to my sister* means she receives it, *a letter for my sister* means it is meant for her and I may still be holding it. Against *until*: for a stopping time, *to* needs a starting point — *open from nine to five*, but *stay until five*. Against *of* for possession: what a thing opens, answers, or belongs with takes *to* (*the key to the shed*, *the answer to the question*), not ✗ *the key of the shed*.

*To* is the arrow. Every one of its senses is a version of pointing one thing at another — a traveler at a destination, a gift at a recipient, one quantity at another quantity. Along with *of* it is the most grammaticalized preposition in English: it marks the indirect object the way a dative ending does in German or Latin, and its lookalike, the infinitive marker, has stopped being a preposition altogether. Keeping those two apart is the single most valuable thing an advanced learner can do with this word, because the preposition demands *-ing* and the marker forbids it.

**1. Direction and destination.** The physical core: movement that reaches a goal.

1. We drove *to* Santa Fe and stayed through the weekend.
2. She pointed *to* a booth at the back of the diner.

**2. Recipient — the indirect object.** With *give*, *send*, *hand*, *sell*, *offer* you get a choice of patterns (*give her the keys* or *give the keys to her*); with *explain*, *describe*, *say*, *suggest*, *mention*, and *announce* the *to* is obligatory.

3. Hand these *to* the woman at the front desk.
4. He explained the rules *to* us twice and we still got it wrong.

**3. Contact and attachment.** One surface fastened against another.

5. Tape the note *to* the door where she'll actually see it.
6. The skiff was still tied *to* the dock at dawn.

**4. Experiencer — the person something happens to.** Not a recipient: nothing is handed over. *To* marks whoever the event lands on, or whoever is treated well or badly.

7. What happened *to* your hand?
8. It never once occurred *to* me that he was lying.
9. That family has been very good *to* us.

**5. Limit and endpoint.** Paired with *from*, or standing alone after a verb of extent.

10. The hardware store is open from nine *to* five.
11. He was soaked *to* the skin by the time he got inside.

**6. Result — the state something ends up in.** *To* marks the finishing condition of a change, usually an extreme one: *torn to shreds*, *burned to the ground*, *frozen to death*, *bored to tears*, *smashed to bits*, *sing a child to sleep*, *reduced to rubble*.

12. The barn burned *to* the ground before the trucks even got there.
13. He was bored *to* tears by the second speaker.

**7. Time before the hour.** The general American form; the Northeast also says *of*, and *till* works everywhere.

14. It's ten *to* six and the meeting starts on the hour.
15. I'll be at the gate around a quarter *to* eight.

**8. Comparison, ratio, and proportion.** After *superior*, *inferior*, *prior*, *similar*, *equal*, *prefer*, and in scores and odds.

16. This model is far superior *to* the one we had before.
17. The final vote was seven *to* two.

**9. Per — one quantity for each unit of another.** The measure-rate frame, where *to* means *for every*: *miles to the gallon*, *cups to a quart*, *four to a box*.

18. The truck gets about eighteen miles *to* the gallon on a good day.
19. There are four cups *to* a quart, which is all the math this recipe needs.

**10. Response and accordance.** Reacting to something, or matching a standard.

20. They danced *to* an old Sam Cooke record until the neighbors complained.
21. The cabinets were built *to* order, which is why they took nine weeks.

**11. Relationship and belonging.** Where English uses *to* rather than *of* to link a thing to what it opens, answers, or serves.

22. Do you still have the key *to* the shed?
23. There's no easy answer *to* that question.

**12. In someone's judgment.** Framing the whole clause from a point of view.

24. *To* me, the whole arrangement sounds like a scam.
25. *To* everyone's surprise, she took the job.

**13. Extent and degree.** How far along a scale something goes.

26. He was right, *to* a point.
27. The gym was packed *to* capacity by seven.

**14. Paired nouns — the reciprocal frame.** Two bare nouns with *to* between them and no article on either: *face to face*, *back to back*, *side to side*, *door to door*, *heart to heart*, *bumper to bumper*.

28. We finally talked it through face *to* face.
29. Traffic on the bridge was bumper *to* bumper for an hour.

**15. The prepositional *to* plus *-ing* — the trap.** After these fixed expressions *to* is the preposition and must take an *-ing* form: *look forward to*, *be used to*, *get used to*, *be accustomed to*, *object to*, *be opposed to*, *admit to*, *confess to*, *resort to*, *be committed to*, *be dedicated to*, *take to*, *in addition to*, *when it comes to*.

30. I'm looking forward *to* seeing you in March.
31. She still isn't used *to* driving on the freeway at night.
32. The board objected *to* being kept out of the discussion.

**16. Not the preposition: the infinitive marker.** Same two letters, different word class, opposite requirement — the marker takes a bare verb and refuses *-ing*. The minimal pairs are worth memorizing, because the same verb can take either one with a change of meaning.

33. We stopped *to* eat in Winnemucca, and then we didn't stop eating until nine.
34. She's used *to* getting up early now, though she used *to* sleep until noon.
35. I want *to* go, and I'm looking forward *to* going.

**17. As an adverb, with no object at all.** *Come to* meaning regain consciousness is current American English; *pull the door to* meaning shut it is dated here and more at home in British writing. Either way there is no object and none can be supplied, so this is not the preposition: compare *She came to the door* (preposition, object *the door*) with *She came to* (adverb, nothing after it).

36. She came *to* about ten minutes after the fall.
37. He pulled the door *to* behind him and turned the lock.

## The four that still carry meaning: *by*, *for*, *from*, *with*

These four keep a recognizable core — agency and adjacency for *by*, purpose and benefit for *for*, source for *from*, accompaniment for *with* — and then extend it in a dozen directions apiece. Each one also anchors a contrast that decides whether a sentence sounds native: *by* against *until*, *for* against *to*, *from* against *since*, and *with* against *by*.

### by

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ/ &middot; *BY*; weak /bə/ in rapid speech only, chiefly in *by the way* &middot; *buh*. A homophone of *buy* and *bye*, and unlike them it can reduce — a *by* you hear as /bə/ is always the preposition.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **gerund** (*by working late*, *by not saying anything*), and a free relative (*by whatever means are necessary*, *by what little light there was*). **Never a finite clause** — ✗ *by he worked late*; *by the time* takes one, but that is a complex preposition, not this word alone. It strands freely in passives and questions (*Who was it written by?*). Note the zero-article rule in the transport and means senses: *by car*, *by bus*, *by plane*, *by mail*, *by phone* take no article, but the moment you switch prepositions you get one back — *in the car*, *on the bus*.
**Pattern:** [passive verb] *by* [agent] &middot; *by* [verb-ing] &middot; [verb] *by* [noun phrase] &middot; *by the* [unit] &middot; [number] *by* [number] &middot; [noun] *by* [same noun] &middot; stranded: *the woman this was written* **by**
**Contrast:** **Against *until*, which is the contrast that matters most: *by* is a deadline and *until* is a duration.** *Finish it by Friday* means at any point up to Friday; *Wait until Friday* means keep waiting the whole time. Against *with*: in a passive, *by* names the doer and *with* the instrument — *the window was broken by a kid with a rock*. Against *beside* and *near*: *by* means immediately alongside, *beside* is its slightly more formal twin, and *near* claims only the vicinity — and *besides*, with the *-s*, is a different word entirely, meaning *in addition to* or *except for*. Against *past* for motion: *walk by the house* and *walk past the house* are interchangeable, but only *past* survives when the point is that you went beyond something (*half past six*, ✗ *half by six*). And *by* is also an **adverb** with no object at all (*He drove by without stopping*, *Come by around six*), which is the reliable way to tell the two uses apart.

*By* is the preposition of agency and adjacency — the doer standing behind a passive verb, the means standing behind a result, the thing you are standing right next to. The senses look scattered, but nearly all of them run back to one idea: closeness, whether in space (*a house by the lake*), in time (*by Friday* — up against it, not past it), or in causation (*done by hand*).

**1. Agent in the passive.** The doer of the action, promoted out of subject position.

1. *Silent Spring* was written *by* Rachel Carson.
2. The proposal was rejected *by* the full committee.

**2. Means and method.** With a bare noun for the channel, or with an *-ing* form for the how. This is the sense that takes a gerund, and it never takes an infinitive: ✗ *by to work two jobs*.

3. We sent the contract *by* email and followed up *by* phone.
4. She got through school *by* working two jobs and sleeping very little.
5. I only know him *by* sight, and I couldn't tell you his last name.

**3. Transport and conveyance.** How a person or a thing travels. The noun is bare — *by car*, *by bus*, *by train*, *by plane*, *by boat*, *by bike*, *by subway*, *by mail* — and it stays bare only as long as *by* is there: switch prepositions and the article comes back (*in the car*, *on the bus*). **The one hole in the pattern is walking, which is *on foot*, not ✗ *by foot*.**

6. We went out *by* train and came back *by* bus.
7. Everything heavy ships *by* truck, and the small stuff still goes *by* mail.

**4. Proximity — right alongside.** Closer than *near*, and less formal than *beside*.

8. There's a bench *by* the fountain if you want to sit for a minute.
9. He sat *by* the window the entire flight and never opened the shade.

**5. Deadline — at or before a point in time.** The single most useful sense for anyone doing business in English.

10. Get me the revised numbers *by* Thursday morning.
11. *By* the time we got there, everyone had already eaten.

**6. Route and passing.** The path taken, or motion past a fixed point.

12. We came *by* the back road to get around the construction.
13. He walked *by* my desk three times without saying a word.

**7. Measure — the size of a difference.** How much one thing exceeds or misses another.

14. She won the primary *by* four points.
15. We missed the connection *by* about ten minutes.

**8. Rate and unit.** Note the obligatory *the*: *by the hour*, never *by hour*.

16. The movers charge *by* the hour, not *by* the job.
17. Out here people still buy firewood *by* the cord.

**9. Dimensions and arithmetic.** Multiplication, division, and the two numbers of a rectangle.

18. The rug is nine *by* twelve, so it should cover most of the room.
19. Divide the total *by* the number of guests and round up.

**10. Successive units.** *One by one*, *day by day*, *little by little*, *step by step*, *side by side*.

20. They came in one *by* one and signed the register.
21. Little *by* little, the swelling went down.

**11. Standard or authority.** According to some measure, rule, or reckoning. *Swear by* something is the colloquial extension: to treat it as your authority.

22. *By* my watch it's already six-thirty.
23. *By* law, the landlord has to give you thirty days' notice.
24. She swears *by* that cast-iron skillet and won't cook in anything else.

**12. The respect in which something holds.** *By trade*, *by birth*, *by training*, *by nature*, *by profession*, *by choice*. The noun takes no article, and the phrase narrows a claim to one dimension of a person.

25. He's a machinist *by* trade and a fiddler on the weekends.
26. She's cautious *by* nature, which is why the whole thing surprised us.

**13. The point where you take hold.** Note the definite article on the body part: *by the arm*, not *by his arm*.

27. He grabbed me *by* the elbow and steered me toward the door.
28. She led the horse *by* the bridle out of the smoke.

**14. Alone or unaided — *by* plus a reflexive.** Two readings live here: without company, and without help.

29. She built the entire deck *by* herself in one summer.
30. I'd rather eat *by* myself than sit through another work dinner.

**15. Fixed adverbial frames.** *By* anchors a long list of set phrases where no other preposition will substitute: *by and large*, *by far*, *by no means*, *by all means*, *by the way*, *by heart*, *by mistake*, *by accident*, *by chance*, *by surprise*, *by rights*.

31. *By* and large, the new schedule has worked out.
32. I took the wrong exit *by* mistake and lost twenty minutes.
33. She knew the whole second act *by* heart.

**16. Not the preposition: *by* as an adverb and verb particle.** No object follows, and none can be supplied. *Stop by*, *drop by*, *come by*, *go by*, *stand by*, *get by* (*put by* for saving money is chiefly British and sounds dated in American English). Compare *He walked by my desk* (preposition, object *my desk*) with *He walked by* (adverb, nothing after it).

34. Drop *by* whenever you're in the neighborhood.
35. The years went *by* faster than either of us expected.
36. We're getting *by* on one income for now.

### for

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr/ &middot; *FOR*; weak /fɚ/, and before a consonant often down to a syllabic /fr̩/ &middot; *fer*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*for her and me*), **gerund** when the meaning is the function of a thing (*a machine for grinding coffee*, *a room for storing files*), a free relative (*for what it's worth*, *for all I know*), and the distinctive **for + noun phrase + to-infinitive** pattern (*It's too far for her to walk*), in which *for* introduces the subject of the infinitive clause rather than an object of its own. **Never a finite that-clause** as a preposition: ✗ *sorry for that I was late* — use *sorry that I was late* or *sorry for being late*. Note that *for* is **also a coordinating conjunction** meaning roughly *because* (*He said nothing, for there was nothing to say*), and in that job it does take a full clause — but that is a different word class, it is formal to literary, and it can never begin a sentence.
**Pattern:** [verb] *for* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *for* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [adjective] *for* [noun phrase] &middot; *for* [noun phrase] *to* [verb] &middot; stranded: *What did you do that* **for**?
**Contrast:** Against *to*, on purpose: use *to* plus an infinitive for why a **person acts** (*I went to the store to buy milk*), and *for* plus a noun or an *-ing* form for what a **thing is used for** (*a knife for cutting bread*). ✗ ***I went to the store for buying milk* is the most common error there is with this word.** Against *since* and *during* for time: *for* gives the length (*for three years*), *since* the starting point (*since 2019*), *during* the period it falls inside (*during the war*). Against *because of*: *for* gives a reason only inside fixed frames (*famous for*, *arrested for*, *sorry for*), never as a general cause connector. And in listening, *for* is the word that reduces to /fɚ/ — *four* never does, so a reduced form is always the preposition.

*For* is the preposition of intention. Its core is that something is aimed at a purpose, a person, or a price, and nearly every sense is a variation on who or what is meant to receive the benefit. Two things trip people up constantly. It competes with *to* for purpose and loses whenever an infinitive is involved. And it is one of the very few prepositions with a conjunction twin — a *for* that takes an entire clause and means *because*.

**1. Beneficiary — who gets it.** The person or thing meant to receive something.

1. I made a pot of chili *for* the neighbors.
2. Is there a package *for* me at the front desk?

**2. Purpose and function.** With a noun or an *-ing* form. For the purpose of an **action**, English switches to *to* plus an infinitive.

3. That drawer is *for* batteries and takeout menus.
4. It's a short blade, mostly *for* trimming wicks.

**3. Duration — how long.** The length of a stretch of time, not its starting point.

5. They lived in Tucson *for* eleven years before moving back.
6. The power was out *for* most of the afternoon.

**4. Destination, with verbs of departure.** *Leave for*, *head for*, *set out for*, *bound for*.

7. She left *for* Chicago on the early flight.
8. This is the six-fifteen *for* Providence, all stops.

**5. Exchange and price.** What is given up in return.

9. He sold the truck *for* nine thousand and immediately regretted it.
10. I traded my middle seat *for* one on the aisle.

**6. In favor of.** The opposite of *against*.

11. Two council members voted *for* the measure and five against it.
12. I'm *for* whatever gets us out of this parking lot fastest.

**7. On behalf of, or in place of.** Acting as someone's representative, employee, or substitute.

13. She's been playing *for* Portland since the trade deadline.
14. Can you sign *for* the delivery if I'm not back yet?

**8. After verbs of seeking, waiting, and asking.** The dependent-preposition set, where *for* points at the thing wanted: *wait for*, *look for*, *search for*, *ask for*, *apply for*, *pay for*, *hope for*, *long for*, *care for*, *call for*, *account for*, *stand for*, *allow for*. These take a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, and they strand freely (*What are you waiting for?*).

15. We waited *for* the tow truck the better part of two hours.
16. She applied *for* three jobs in one afternoon and heard back from none of them.
17. Who's paying *for* all this?

**9. Cause and grounds.** Only inside fixed frames: *known for*, *famous for*, *fined for*, *arrested for*, *sorry for*, *thank you for*.

18. The town is known *for* its hot springs and not much else.
19. He was fined *for* parking in the bus lane again.

**10. Taken as — identity assigned, rightly or wrongly.** *Mistake X for Y*, *take X for Y*, *pass for*, *know for*, *take for granted*. The second noun is what somebody believes the first one to be.

20. In that coat I mistook him *for* his brother.
21. The copy is good enough to pass *for* the original.

**11. Considering — measured against what you would expect.** A quiet concessive: the standard being applied is the one implied by the noun.

22. She's tall *for* a seventh grader.
23. It's mild *for* January, even by Portland standards.

**12. Distance and extent.** How far something runs.

24. We walked *for* miles without passing another car.
25. There's nothing but scrub and fence line *for* forty miles.

**13. The target of a feeling.** After nouns like *love*, *respect*, *sympathy*, *weakness*, *eye*, *taste*.

26. He has a real weakness *for* old westerns.
27. Her respect *for* the old man never wavered.

**14. Scheduled at.** Fixing an appointment or event to a time.

28. The hearing is set *for* the twelfth.
29. I have an appointment *for* nine-thirty.

**15. Matched pairs — one unit against its equal.** Both nouns bare, no articles: *word for word*, *dollar for dollar*, *inch for inch*, *point for point*, *blow for blow*, *tit for tat*.

30. She repeated the conversation back to me word *for* word.
31. Dollar *for* dollar, it's the best tool in the shop.

**16. Fixed adverbial frames.** *For* heads a long list of set phrases that behave as single adverbs: *for now*, *for good*, *for free*, *for sure*, *for once*, *for example*, *for instance*, *for the most part*, *for a change*, *for real*, *for keeps*, *for the time being*.

32. We're staying put *for* now and we'll see how the winter goes.
33. He quit *for* good this time, or so he says.
34. *For* once, the train was actually early.

**17. Concessive *for all* — despite.** A fixed frame, and the twin of *with all* under [with](#with). *For all his money* concedes the money and then contradicts it; *for all I know* concedes the limits of what you know.

35. *For* all his complaining, he's never actually filed a grievance.
36. *For* all I know, they closed the place years ago.

**18. *For* plus a noun phrase plus a to-infinitive.** Here *for* introduces the **subject** of the infinitive clause. It is not taking an object of its own, which is why the pronoun is objective (*for her to decide*) even though it is functioning as a subject. **Contrast the *of*-frame: *It was rude of her to say that* judges her character; *It was hard for her to say that* does not.**

37. That crate is too heavy *for* one person to carry.
38. *For* this to work, everybody has to show up on time.

**19. Not the preposition: *for* as a coordinating conjunction.** Here it means roughly *because*, it takes a full finite clause, and it joins two independent clauses — so a comma goes in front of it and it can never open a sentence. Literary in register; in ordinary American prose use *because*.

39. He said nothing, *for* there was nothing left to say.
40. We turned back early, *for* the light was already going.

### from

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm/ &middot; *FRUHM*; also /frɑm/ &middot; *FRAHM*; weak /frəm/ &middot; *frum*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **gerund** (*kept her from leaving*, *stopped him from calling*, *far from being finished*), a *wh-*clause or free relative (*from what I hear*, *from where I'm sitting*), and — unusually — **another prepositional phrase or an adverb**: *from under the porch*, *from behind the curtain*, *from across the street*, *from among the finalists*, *from here*, *from abroad*. Almost no other preposition takes a preposition as its object. **Never a that-clause**: ✗ *from that he told me* — say *from what he told me*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *from* [noun phrase] &middot; *from* [X] *to* [Y] &middot; [verb] [object] *from* [verb-ing] &middot; *from* [prepositional phrase / adverb] &middot; stranded: *Where are you* **from**?
**Contrast:** Against *since* for time: *since* requires a point in the past and normally a perfect tense (*She's lived here since 2019*), while *from* accepts any point, including a future one, and normally needs an endpoint or *on* (*from next Monday on*). Against *of* for material: *made of* when the material is still recognizable (*a table made of oak*), *made from* when it has been transformed (*wine made from grapes*). Against *out of*: both cover exiting a container, but *out of* is more specific about the enclosure (*he climbed out of the truck*). **On *different*: *different from* is the safe standard; *different than* is widely accepted in American English, especially before a clause (*different than I expected*); *different to* is British and should be avoided in American writing.**

*From* is the source preposition and the mirror image of *to*: where *to* points at a destination, *from* points back at an origin — a place, a moment, a person, a cause, or a thing you are being kept away from. It is also the most syntactically flexible member of this group, because it is the one preposition that comfortably takes another preposition as its object, which is how English gets *from under*, *from behind*, and *from across*.

**1. Source of motion.** Where something came out of, fell off, or arrived from.

1. The letter came *from* my landlord, which is never good news.
2. A book slid *from* the top shelf and landed flat on the floor.

**2. Starting point in space.** The near end of a measured distance or a stretch of text.

3. It's about four hours *from* Denver to Santa Fe if the pass is clear.
4. Read *from* the top of page twelve.

**3. Starting point in time.** Paired with *to*, *until*, or the adverb *on*.

5. The office is open *from* nine until five, closed for lunch.
6. *From* that night on, he never mentioned her name again.

**4. Origin and provenance.** Where a person, a product, or a thing belongs to.

7. Her whole family is *from* eastern Kentucky.
8. The cheese is *from* a small dairy just outside Madison.

**5. Source of supply — who you got it from.** *Buy from*, *rent from*, *borrow from*, *hear from*, *learn from*, *order from*, *quote from*, *steal from*. The seller or giver stands as the origin, not as an agent.

9. I bought the trailer *from* a guy outside Bakersfield.
10. We haven't heard *from* her since the storm.

**6. Material that has been transformed.** Compare *made of*, which keeps the material visible.

11. The bread is made *from* a starter she's kept alive for years.
12. Most of the furniture was built *from* salvaged barn wood.

**7. Separation, removal, and prevention.** *Keep from*, *stop from*, *prevent from*, *protect from*, *ban from*, *subtract from*, *discourage from*. Note that the prevention verbs take a **gerund**, never an infinitive: ✗ *keep him from to finish*.

13. Nothing was going to keep him *from* finishing the race.
14. Take twelve *from* thirty and you get eighteen.

**8. Refraining and holding back — always with a gerund.** *Refrain from*, *abstain from*, *desist from*, *shy away from*, *stop yourself from*. Like the prevention verbs in sense 7, **these take an *-ing* form and never an infinitive**: ✗ *refrain from to smoke*.

15. Please refrain *from* smoking anywhere on the platform.
16. She abstained *from* voting and wouldn't say why.

**9. Absence — where somebody or something is not.** *Absent from*, *missing from*, *away from*, *excused from*. Stative, where sense 7 is dynamic.

17. Two pages were missing *from* the middle of the report.
18. He's been away *from* the office all week.

**10. Cause.** *Die of* is traditional for a disease and *die from* for an injury or a complication, but both are current in American English.

19. He's still sore *from* helping move the piano.
20. Two of the crew were suffering *from* smoke inhalation.

**11. Result and derivation — where an outcome or a word comes from.** *Result from*, *stem from*, *arise from*, *follow from*, *derive from*, *come from*. The subject is the effect and the *from*-phrase is what produced it, which is the exact reverse of *cause*.

21. Most of the delay resulted *from* one missing signature.
22. The word derives *from* an old Dutch term for a flat-bottomed boat.

**12. Distinction — telling one thing apart from another.** *Different from*, *tell X from Y*, *distinguish from*, *apart from*.

23. This year's model is barely different *from* last year's.
24. At that distance I couldn't tell a hawk *from* a vulture.

**13. Basis, evidence, or vantage point.** *From what I hear*, *judging from*, *from experience*, *from memory*.

25. *From* what I hear, they're closing the whole plant in March.
26. *From* where I'm sitting, the deal looks pretty good.

**14. Selection out of a set.** Which pool the choice was drawn from. Often *from among* when the set is a group of people.

27. The winner was picked *from* a field of nineteen.
28. They chose two finalists *from* among the summer interns.

**15. A range between two limits.** The lower bound of a spread of prices, sizes, or counts.

29. Tickets run *from* thirty to two hundred dollars.
30. Anywhere *from* six to ten people usually show up.

**16. With another preposition or an adverb as its object.** The construction that sets *from* apart syntactically from every other preposition in this file.

31. The cat came out *from* under the porch absolutely filthy.
32. Someone had been watching us *from* across the street.

**17. Fixed frames.** *From scratch*, *from memory*, *from time to time*, *from now on*, *from head to toe*, *from cover to cover*, *far from it*.

33. She made the whole dinner *from* scratch on a weeknight.
34. *From* time to time he still sends a postcard.
35. He read the manual *from* cover to cover and still couldn't fix it.

**See also:** [to](#to), [of](#of), [by](#by), [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of), [apart from](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from), [away from](11-complex-multiword.md#away-from)

### with

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθ/ &middot; *WITH*; also voiced /wɪð/, chiefly before a voiced sound; weak /wəθ/, /wəð/ &middot; *wuhth*, and often bare /wɪ/ before a consonant in fast speech (*with them* → /wɪ ðəm/)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **gerund** (*What's wrong with asking?*, *I'll start with cleaning the gutters*), a free relative (*with what I know now*), and the **absolute construction** — *with* plus a noun phrase plus a predicate, where the predicate can be an adjective (*with the door open*), a participle (*with prices rising*), a prepositional phrase (*with the kids at camp*), an adverb (*with the lights off*), or a to-infinitive (*with three games left to play*). **No finite clause**: ✗ *with he was late* and ✗ *with that it rained* are both impossible, and the clausal equivalent is *since* or *because*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *with* [noun phrase] &middot; [adjective] *with* [noun phrase] &middot; *with* [noun phrase] [adjective / participle / prepositional phrase] &middot; stranded: *Who did you go* **with**?
**Contrast:** Against *by*: *by* names the agent and *with* the instrument — *the log was split by my uncle with a maul*. **Watch *fight with*, *argue with*, and *play with*, which are genuinely ambiguous between alongside and against**: *he fought with the Free French* makes him a comrade, *he fought with his brother* makes it a brawl; when it matters, write *alongside* or *against*. A *with*-phrase also does not coordinate — *The manager, with two of his assistants, **was** waiting* takes a singular verb, because *with* adds a modifier and not a second subject. **And the case rule: *with my wife and me*, never ✗ *with my wife and I*. A preposition takes the objective case, and the *I* version is a hypercorrection, not a politeness.**

*With* is the preposition of togetherness, and its senses radiate outward from that one idea: the person you came with, the tool in your hand, the beard on a stranger's face, the cold that makes you shake. It is also the workhorse of the English absolute construction — *with the door open*, *with winter coming* — which packs a whole second situation into a prepositional phrase without ever using a finite verb.

**1. Accompaniment.** The oldest and plainest sense: in the company of.

1. She showed up *with* two friends and a wet dog.
2. I'll have the steak *with* a side of collards.

**2. Instrument.** The tool used, as opposed to the person using it.

3. He pried the lid off *with* a flathead screwdriver.
4. Sign it *with* a pen, not a pencil.

**3. Manner.** *With* plus an abstract noun does an adverb's job: *with care* = carefully.

5. She handled the whole situation *with* remarkable patience.
6. He set the box down *with* exaggerated care.

**4. Attribute — having.** A permanent or defining feature, attached to a noun.

7. The guy *with* the red beard is the one who runs the place.
8. We're looking for a house *with* a fenced yard and a garage.

**5. Contents — what fills or covers.** *Filled with*, *covered with*, *loaded with*, *packed with*.

9. The truck bed was loaded *with* gravel and looked ready to give out.
10. By morning the front steps were covered *with* ice.

**6. Opposition and reciprocal action.** The ambiguous sense: whether *with* means alongside or against is decided by the verb and the world, not by the preposition.

11. He got into an argument *with* the parking attendant.
12. The two of them have been competing *with* each other since grade school.

**7. Association and dealing.** *Work with*, *agree with*, *deal with*, *in love with*, *a problem with*.

13. I've worked *with* her on three projects and never seen her rattled.
14. There's something wrong *with* the transmission.

**8. The person a feeling is aimed at.** After *angry with*, *annoyed with*, *furious with*, *pleased with*, *disappointed with*, *impressed with*, *bored with*, *fed up with*, *patient with*, *honest with*, *gentle with*. **The rule of thumb is that a person takes *with* and a thing takes *at* or *about*: *angry with her*, but *angry about the bill*.**

15. He was angry *with* me for a week and never said why.
16. I'm honestly pleased *with* how the kitchen turned out.
17. Be patient *with* her — it's only her second day.

**9. Comparison and correspondence.** *Compare with*, *contrast with*, *coincide with*, *be consistent with*, *have nothing in common with*, *on a par with*, *level with*. **Note that *compare to* and *compare with* diverge: *compare to* likens (*compared her to her mother*), *compare with* measures side by side (*compared this quarter with last*).**

18. His account doesn't square *with* what the neighbor told us.
19. The two of them have nothing in common *with* each other except the last name.

**10. Membership and employment.** Which outfit somebody belongs to. Idiomatic and very common in American professional speech, where *at* would also work but *with* sounds less like an address.

20. She's *with* a firm in Chicago now.
21. He was *with* the paper for almost thirty years.

**11. Cause of a physical or emotional state.** Mostly with verbs and adjectives of trembling, flushing, and sickening.

22. His hands were shaking *with* cold by the time we found the car.
23. She went red *with* embarrassment and left the room.

**12. Regarding — in the case of.** Setting up a topic and then saying something about it.

24. *With* him, you never know what you're going to get.
25. The trouble *with* the plan is what it costs.

**13. Concomitant variation.** As one thing changes, the other changes along with it.

26. The pain gets noticeably worse *with* movement.
27. *With* time, the whole thing stopped bothering her.

**14. On your person, or in your keeping.** Carrying it, or having left it in someone's hands.

28. Do you have your license *with* you?
29. I left a spare key *with* the neighbors.

**15. On the same side, or following along.** Agreement, allegiance, comprehension, or simply the same direction of travel.

30. I'm *with* her on this one.
31. Are you still *with* me, or should I back up a step?

**16. Separation — the counterintuitive set.** A short list of verbs where *with* means giving something **up**, not holding onto it: *part with*, *break with*, *dispense with*, *do away with*, *be through with*, *be finished with*. The preposition of togetherness marks the moment togetherness ends.

32. He couldn't bring himself to part *with* his father's tools.
33. Let's dispense *with* the introductions and get started.

**17. The absolute construction.** *With* plus a noun phrase plus a predicate, expressing an attendant circumstance. No finite verb appears, which is why *with* and not *since* is doing the work.

34. He fell asleep *with* the TV still on and the lamp burning.
35. *With* both kids in college, they finally sold the house.

**18. Concessive *with all*.** A fixed frame meaning *despite*, always with *all*, and the twin of *for all* under [for](#for).

36. *With* all his money, he still drives that same rusted-out truck.
37. *With* all due respect, that is not what the contract says.
