Accompaniment, addition, exception, and restriction#

The prepositions that add something to a set, subtract something from it, or report that a piece of it is missing.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file does arithmetic on a set. Some widen it — along with, as well as, including, plus — some narrow it — except, but, excluding, minus, save — and some declare a piece of it absent altogether — without, absent, sans. Two traps run through the whole group. The first is grammatical: in the uses catalogued here, not one of these words is a conjunction — they are prepositions taking an object — so not one of them changes the number of the subject: The coach, as well as three players, was ejected, never were. (Several of them, but and except and save among them, do double as conjunctions elsewhere, and each entry says where the line falls.) The second is semantic: apart from, aside from, besides, and outside of can mean either "in addition to" or "except for", and nothing but the polarity of the sentence around them decides which.

Accompaniment and addition#

The base preposition of accompaniment is with, which has its own entry with the other core relational prepositions. The words below are the additive specialists built on top of it: two complex prepositions that mean only accompaniment and never instrument (along with, together with), two that mean only addition (as well as, in addition to), the participial of inclusion (including) and its commercial twin (inclusive of), the arithmetic plus and its emphatic over and above, the causal coupled with, the outfitting complete with, the aggrieved on top of, the rhetorical pair not to mention and to say nothing of, which add an item by pretending to leave it out, and let alone, which reverses their polarity and adds an item only to place it out of reach.

The agreement rule applies to all of them. A phrase introduced by any of these words is a supplement, not a second subject. It sits inside commas, it can be lifted out without breaking the sentence, and the verb continues to agree with the original subject alone.

along with#

Pronunciation: /əˈlɔŋ wɪð/ (also /əˈlɑŋ/ with the cot–caught merger; with also /wɪθ/) · weak /əˈlɔŋ wəð/, /əˈlɔŋ wəθ/ · uh-LAWNG with Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause, never a bare or to-infinitive Pattern: [clause] along with [noun phrase] · [subject], along with [noun phrase], [singular verb] · Along with [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: Interchangeable with together with, which is the more written of the two. Neither can do the instrumental work of plain with — you cut bread with a knife, not along with one. And along with is a preposition, not and: it does not make a singular subject plural.

Joins a second party or a second item to what has already been named, either as company on the way or as one more thing in the pile. It is the everyday additive of American speech and writing, equally at home in a shipping manifest and in conversation.

1. In the company of. Two people or things moving or acting together.

  1. She rode out to the site along with two state inspectors.
  2. The dog came along with us on every trip that summer.

2. In addition to. One more item added to a list, a price, or a transaction.

  1. The sale includes the appliances along with all the patio furniture.
  2. Bring a photo ID along with a copy of the lease.

3. Appended to a subject. The verb still agrees with the original subject alone.

  1. The governor, along with three of her aides, was subpoenaed.
  2. Interest, along with late fees, is added to the balance every month.

as well as#

Pronunciation: full /æz ˈwɛl æz/ · weak /əz ˈwɛl əz/ — both instances of as reduce, and the weak form is what you actually hear · uhz-WEL-uhz Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund; a bare infinitive when the main verb is itself a bare or to-infinitive (She can act as well as sing) — never a that-clause Pattern: [clause] as well as [noun phrase] · [subject], as well as [noun phrase], [singular verb] · [verb-ing] … as well as [verb-ing] Contrast: As well as is not and. And builds a compound subject and takes a plural verb; as well as subordinates the second item, so the verb agrees with the first subject alone — The pitcher, as well as two relievers, was ejected. Keep it apart from the comparative adverbial as well as meaning "as skillfully as" (He plays as well as she does), which is a different construction with a clause after it.

Adds a second item while keeping the first one in the foreground. This asymmetry is the whole point of the word: A and B gives the two equal billing, A as well as B makes B the afterthought — which is exactly why the verb never notices it.

1. In addition to a noun.

  1. The clinic treats adults as well as children.
  2. She speaks Portuguese as well as Spanish.

2. Linking two verbs. The second verb goes to -ing, unless a modal or a to-infinitive already governs it.

  1. He runs the front desk as well as handling the schedule.
  2. The grant covers tuition as well as paying for books.
  3. She wanted to direct as well as act.

3. Appended to a subject. Singular subject, singular verb.

  1. The mayor, as well as two council members, was invited.
  2. Fuel, as well as labor, accounts for most of the increase.

complete with#

Pronunciation: /kəmˈplit wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) · weak /kəmˈplit wəð/, /kəmˈplit wəθ/ · kum-PLEET with Type: complex (adjective + with) · Register: neutral; a fixture of advertising, real-estate copy, and description Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — no gerund, no finite clause, no infinitive Pattern: [noun] complete with [noun phrase] · [clause], complete with [noun phrase] Contrast: Complete with is not including. Including names a member of a set that has already been counted; complete with presents the extra as equipment the whole thing arrives furnished with. It is also positionally restricted where including is not: it postmodifies a noun or trails a clause and cannot open a sentence — ✗ Complete with a canoe, the cabin rents by the week. Against together with, which merely adds, complete with implies the addition finishes the picture, and that implication is what makes it the standard vehicle for a dry joke.

Presents a thing along with everything that outfits it. It sells hard, and because it sells so hard, half its working life is spent being sarcastic.

1. Listing what comes with a thing.

  1. The cabin rents complete with linens, firewood, and a canoe.
  2. They delivered the grill complete with a cover and a full tank of propane.

2. In descriptions of costume, staging, and setting.

  1. He showed up in a three-piece suit, complete with pocket watch.
  2. The lobby has been restored complete with the original brass mailboxes.

3. Sarcastically, for an unwelcome extra.

  1. We got the whole presentation, complete with the slideshow.
  2. It was a classic family dinner, complete with an argument about the will.

coupled with#

Pronunciation: /ˈkʌpəld wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) · weak /ˈkʌpəld wəð/, /ˈkʌpəld wəθ/ · KUP-uld with Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal; the analyst's and the reporter's word Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause Pattern: [noun phrase], coupled with [noun phrase], [verb agreeing with the first noun] · Coupled with [noun phrase], [clause] · coupled with [verb-ing] Variants: combined with, paired with, allied to — the same construction under different metaphors Contrast: Where along with and together with merely add a second item, coupled with claims the two work on each other to produce the result the main clause names. It is the causal member of the additive family, and swapping it for and loses that claim. Its agreement behavior is the family rule: Drought, coupled with two failed harvests, was enough to empty the valley — singular, because coupled with is a preposition and not a conjunction.

Joins two factors and reports what they do together. Reporters and analysts reach for it whenever one cause alone would be too simple an explanation.

1. Naming two factors behind one result.

  1. Low inventory, coupled with cheap credit, pushed prices past any honest appraisal.
  2. Coupled with the new tariffs, the shipping delays wiped out the quarter.

2. Adding a condition to an obligation or an offer.

  1. The grant carries a five-year commitment coupled with an annual audit.
  2. A guilty plea coupled with full restitution usually keeps a first offender out of prison.

3. With a gerund.

  1. Coupled with having to retrain the whole staff, the software change cost them a season.
  2. The rent increase, coupled with losing two tenants, put the building underwater.

4. Appended to a subject. The verb agrees with the original subject alone.

  1. Fatigue, coupled with poor visibility, was listed as the probable cause.
  2. The deductible, coupled with the monthly premium, makes the policy a bad deal.

in addition to#

Pronunciation: /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tu/ · weak /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tə/ · in-uh-DISH-un too Type: complex · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — the to here is a preposition, so a bare infinitive is an error: in addition to working, never in addition to work full time. No finite clause. Pattern: In addition to [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] in addition to [noun phrase] · in addition to [verb-ing] Contrast: The to-trap it shares with look forward to, object to, and be used to: the to is a preposition and demands -ing. Against as well as, it is heavier and more likely to open a sentence; against besides, it is unambiguous — besides can also mean "except for", and in addition to never can.

Names something extra that stands alongside what the main clause says, with no suggestion that either item is the more important. Because it is transparently compositional, it survives in formal writing where plus and besides would sound casual.

1. Adding a noun.

  1. In addition to the fine, he lost his license for ninety days.
  2. The apartment comes with a parking space in addition to a storage locker.

2. Adding an activity — always -ing.

  1. In addition to working full time, she takes two night classes.
  2. In addition to running the shop, he does all the bookkeeping himself.

3. Appended to a subject. The verb agrees with the subject alone.

  1. The principal, in addition to four teachers, was placed on leave.
  2. In addition to the tuition, students pay a lab fee and a parking permit.

including#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈkludɪŋ/ · in-KLOO-ding Type: marginal/participial (fully grammaticalized — it needs no subject to control it) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a wh-clause (including how the money was spent, including whether the lease can be broken) — one of the very few prepositions in this file that takes one. Never a that-clause and never a tensed clause on its own: ✗ including it was raining. Pattern: [total set], including [member], … · [amount] including [component] · including but not limited to [list] Contrast: The mirror of excluding. Against inclusive of, which is a commercial and contractual word attaching to a noun, including attaches loosely to a whole clause and is unrestricted in register. Against postposed included (tax included), which follows its noun rather than preceding it. Note that including names a sample, not the whole list — which is why American drafters write including without limitation when they need to say so out loud.

Picks out one or more members of a set that has just been named, or folds a component into a total. It does not claim the list that follows is complete, and in careful writing it should not be used as if it were: if the set really is exhausted, write namely or consisting of.

1. Naming members of a set. A pronoun after it goes in the objective case — including me, never including I.

  1. Nine passengers, including the pilot, walked away from the crash.
  2. Several states, including Ohio and Nevada, have already certified.
  3. Everyone including me had to retake the certification.

2. Counting something into a total.

  1. The rate is $180 a night, including breakfast.
  2. That comes to $42 including tax and delivery.

3. The drafting formula. Used when the list must be read as open-ended.

  1. All benefits, including but not limited to health and dental coverage, continue through June.
  2. The tenant pays all utilities, including without limitation gas, electric, water, and trash.

4. Before a wh-clause. The complement is a question folded into a noun slot.

  1. The audit covers everything, including how the reserve fund was spent.
  2. Several questions remain, including whether the lease can be broken at all.

5. With a gerund.

  1. The job has its costs, including driving ninety minutes each way.
  2. She listed her objections, including having to sign a five-year lease.

inclusive of#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈklusɪv ʌv/ · weak /ɪnˈklusɪv əv/, reduced /ɪnˈklusɪvə/ before a consonant · in-KLOO-siv uv Type: complex · Register: formal; chiefly commercial, contractual, and hospitality writing Complement: noun phrase only — no gerund, no clause Pattern: [noun] inclusive of [noun phrase] · [price] is inclusive of [noun phrase] Contrast: The exact opposite of exclusive of, and the stiff twin of including. Including can float anywhere in a sentence; inclusive of almost always postmodifies a noun or follows the verb be. Do not confuse the preposition with the adjective inclusive in a date range (March 3 through March 9 inclusive), which takes no of — and which American English rarely needs, since through is already inclusive where British to is not.

Declares what a stated price, figure, or period already contains. It exists because contracts and invoices need a word that cannot be misread, and it buys that precision at the cost of sounding like a contract.

1. Of a price, a rate, or a fee. The commonest use by far.

  1. All quoted prices are inclusive of shipping and handling.
  2. Rent is $2,150 a month, inclusive of water, sewer, and trash.
  3. The flat fee is inclusive of all filing costs.

2. Of a figure, a headcount, or a package.

  1. Salaries are stated inclusive of the employer's retirement contribution.
  2. The package is inclusive of airfare, transfers, and two guided excursions.
  3. Headcount stands at 240, inclusive of contractors and seasonal staff.

3. Of a span of dates. Where American English usually says through instead.

  1. The blackout period runs March 3 to March 9, inclusive of both dates.
  2. Coverage is inclusive of the first and the last day of the term.

let alone#

Pronunciation: /lɛt əˈloʊn/ · reduced /lɛɾ əˈloʊn/ — the t flaps between vowels · let-uh-LOHN Type: marginal (a frozen imperative that has become a connective) · Register: neutral to informal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, adjective, adverb, prepositional phrase, gerund, or a bare infinitive matched to the verb before it (can't walk, let alone run) — never a finite clause, and never a to-infinitive when the verb before it is bare: ✗ can't walk, let alone to run. Requires: a negative, near-negative, or question earlier in the sentence — not, never, nobody, nothing, hardly, barely, too … to, can't. Without one it is ungrammatical: ✗ He can drive, let alone fly a plane. Pattern: [negative clause], let alone [phrase parallel to what preceded] · can't [bare verb], let alone [bare verb] · not [noun], let alone [noun] Variants: much less — fully interchangeable in American English; never mind (informal) and still less (formal) do the same work. Contrast: Let alone is the mirror image of not to mention and to say nothing of. All three add a second item at the end, but not to mention upgrades — what follows is the heavier item actually in play — while let alone downgrades: what follows is the more extreme thing that is even further out of reach. He can't drive, let alone fly a plane says flying is the harder claim; reverse the two and the sentence collapses. Note also that whatever follows must be parallel to what preceded it: a bare verb answers a bare verb, a noun answers a noun.

Adds a second item only to say that it lies even further beyond whatever was just denied. It is a scaling device rather than a true addition: the first item marks the floor and let alone points past it.

1. After a negative, before a noun phrase.

  1. There wasn't enough money for repairs, let alone a new roof.
  2. He doesn't have a driver's license, let alone a car.

2. Before a bare infinitive matched to the verb before it.

  1. She can't walk to the mailbox, let alone run a 10K.
  2. Most of them couldn't find the building, let alone get past the front desk.

3. Before an adjective, an adverb, or a prepositional phrase.

  1. The road isn't paved, let alone plowed.
  2. Nobody goes down there in daylight, let alone after dark.

4. In questions and after near-negatives.

  1. Who has time for lunch, let alone a two-hour meeting?
  2. The team barely made the playoffs, let alone a deep run.

See also: not to mention, to say nothing of, besides, in addition to, short of

not to mention#

Pronunciation: /ˌnɑt tə ˈmɛnʃən/ · reduced /ˌnɑʔ tə ˈmɛnʃən/ — the t of not glottalizes and to is always the weak /tə/, never /tu/ · naht-tuh-MEN-shun Type: marginal (a frozen infinitival that has become a preposition) · Register: neutral to informal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, adjective, or a wh-clause. A that-clause only through the fact that (not to mention the fact that nobody asked). Never a bare finite clause. Pattern: [clause], not to mention [noun phrase] · [adjective], not to mention [adjective] · [clause], not to mention [verb-ing] Variants: to say nothing of — a shade more formal and fully interchangeable. Distinguish let alone, which reverses the polarity: it needs a negative before it and downgrades what follows (He can't drive, let alone fly a plane), where not to mention upgrades. Contrast: Against in addition to and as well as, which are neutral, not to mention is rhetorical: it pretends to omit the item while naming it, so whatever follows is always the heavier or the more damning of the two. It is also positionally fixed — it comes at the end of what it adds to and never opens a sentence: ✗ Not to mention the cost, the plan is impractical; write The plan is impractical, not to mention ruinously expensive.

Adds one last item under the pretense of skipping it. The pretense is the whole device: what follows lands harder than what came before precisely because the speaker claimed not to be bringing it up.

1. Adding a heavier item at the end of a list.

  1. The repair took three weeks and two thousand dollars, not to mention a rental car.
  2. She runs the department and teaches two seminars, not to mention a research group of nine.

2. Adding an adjective. A construction almost no other preposition allows.

  1. The apartment is tiny, not to mention four flights up.
  2. The plan is expensive, not to mention flatly illegal.

3. With a gerund.

  1. He had to redo three years of taxes, not to mention explaining the whole thing to his brother.
  2. Moving in February means ice and a truck deposit, not to mention taking a week off work.

4. With the fact that, where a clause is unavoidable.

  1. The lease is a bad deal, not to mention the fact that the building is for sale.
  2. He missed three deadlines, not to mention the fact that nobody could reach him for a week.

on top of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ˈtɑp ʌv/ · weak /ɑn ˈtɑp əv/, reduced /ɑnˈtɑpə/ before a consonant — the of loses its /v/ exactly as it does in out of · on-TOP-uv Type: complex · Register: neutral; the additive sense is conversational and nearly always aggrieved Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (on top of paying rent); a nominal wh-clause is possible in the additive sense (on top of what the movers charged). Never a that-clause and never a to-infinitive: ✗ on top of pay rent. Bare on top is an adverb and takes no object — the berries go on top — and that is the test: the preposition must have something after it. Pattern: On top of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] on top of [noun phrase] · on top of [verb-ing] · on top of everything else Contrast: The literal sense — the keys are on top of the refrigerator — is a spatial preposition and is covered with the complex prepositions; what belongs in this file is the additive one. Against in addition to, which is neutral and formal, on top of is informal and carries a complaint built in: the second item is a burden piled on the first, which is why on top of the rent is idiomatic and on top of the bonus sounds odd. Against besides, it can never be read as "except for". A third idiom, on top of meaning "in full control of" (she's on top of every deadline), is a separate sense again.

Piles one more demand onto a load that was already heavy. It is the American complaint word of the additive family, and the only one that presupposes the addition is unwelcome.

1. In addition to — with the second item as a burden.

  1. On top of the rent, they pay their own utilities and trash.
  2. The clinic charges a facility fee on top of the doctor's bill.

2. With a gerund.

  1. On top of working two jobs, she's finishing a degree at night.
  2. On top of having to repaint, we found rot under the siding.

3. The fixed phrase on top of everything else.

  1. On top of everything else, the furnace quit on the coldest night of the year.
  2. She lost the contract and, on top of that, the client wants the deposit back.

4. Before a wh-clause.

  1. On top of what the movers charged, we paid two weeks of storage.
  2. On top of whatever the insurer covers, there's a $2,000 deductible.

See also: in addition to, as well as, besides, over and above, plus, not to mention

over and above#

Pronunciation: /ˌoʊvər ənd əˈbʌv/ · weak /ˌoʊvɚ ən əˈbʌv/ — the and is always reduced to /ən/ or /n̩/ · OH-vur-un-uh-BUV Type: complex (frozen) · Register: formal to neutral; standard in compensation, contracts, and budgets Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund is possible but uncommon (over and above having to repay the advance). Never a finite clause. Pattern: [amount] over and above [amount] · [clause] over and above [noun phrase] · Over and above [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: Over and above is in addition to with one extra claim built in: the addition sits on top of a baseline that has already been satisfied. A bonus over and above base salary means the salary was already paid in full; an hour over and above the minimum means the minimum was already met. Where in addition to is neutral about the first item, over and above certifies it. Do not read the phrase compositionally — it is frozen, and neither over nor above keeps its spatial meaning inside it.

Names what is added once an obligation has already been discharged. It is the standard phrase for a bonus, an overage, and a duty gone beyond.

1. Money added to an amount already owed.

  1. The bonus is paid over and above base salary.
  2. Any expenses over and above the retainer must be approved in writing.

2. Effort or service beyond a required minimum.

  1. She did that work over and above anything the job actually required.
  2. The district demands sixty hours of training over and above the state minimum.

3. Adding a further consideration to an argument.

  1. Over and above the cost, there's the question of whether anyone would use it.
  2. The building is unsafe, and over and above that, it isn't even zoned for offices.

plus#

Pronunciation: /plʌs/ · weak /pləs/ in rapid speech · PLUHS Type: simple · Register: neutral as a preposition; the sentence-initial Plus, is informal Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund is possible but rare (plus having to repaint). Never a finite clause. The word is also an adjective (a plus sign, an A plus), a noun (that's a real plus), and an informal sentence adverb (Plus, it's cheaper) — in those uses it has no object and is not a preposition. Pattern: [number] plus [number] · [amount] plus [noun phrase] · Plus, [clause] (adverb — informal) Contrast: Plus is not and. And makes a compound subject and takes a plural verb; plus is a preposition, so The base salary plus the bonus was more than he expected keeps the singular. In arithmetic, Two plus two is four is the standard form. Against minus, its arithmetic opposite; against in addition to, its formal equivalent — a job application should not begin a sentence with Plus.

Adds one quantity or item to another. It began life as the Latin word for "more" in arithmetic and spread outward from there into prices, packages, and finally into casual speech as a discourse connector.

1. Arithmetic addition.

  1. Six plus four is ten.
  2. The perimeter is the length plus the width, doubled.

2. With the addition of. A cost, a component, an extra.

  1. Rent is $1,400 a month plus utilities.
  2. At closing you'll owe the principal plus accrued interest.

3. And also. An extra item, slightly informal.

  1. He brought his cousin plus two friends nobody had met.
  2. She got the promotion plus a corner office.

4. As a sentence adverb — not a preposition. No object follows; informal.

  1. It's too far to drive. Plus, the parking is impossible.
  2. The seats are terrible, and plus they cost twice as much.

to say nothing of#

Pronunciation: /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋ ʌv/ · weak /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋ əv/, reduced /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋə/ before a consonant — the to is always the weak /tə/, never /tu/ · tuh-SAY-NUTH-ing-uv Type: complex (a frozen infinitival that has become a preposition) · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or adjective; a that-clause only through the fact that. Never a bare finite clause, and — because the of is a preposition — never a bare infinitive: to say nothing of paying for it, not ✗ to say nothing of pay for it. Pattern: [clause], to say nothing of [noun phrase] · [clause], to say nothing of [verb-ing] · [adjective], to say nothing of [adjective] Variants: not to mention — the spoken equivalent, and interchangeable in almost every context Contrast: The formal twin of not to mention; the difference is register, not sense. To say nothing of is what you meet in edited prose, not to mention is what you hear. Both are rhetorical rather than neutral — whatever follows is the heavier item, named under the pretense of being skipped — and both are positionally fixed at the end of what they add to: ✗ To say nothing of the cost, the plan is impractical. Against let alone, which reverses the polarity and downgrades what follows, to say nothing of always upgrades.

Names one more item while claiming to pass over it. Like not to mention, the pretense is the whole device, and register is the only thing that separates the two.

1. Adding a heavier item at the end of a list.

  1. The delay cost them the season, to say nothing of the money.
  2. Two of the exhibits were mislabeled, to say nothing of the one that never arrived.

2. With a gerund.

  1. He'd have to sell the house, to say nothing of explaining it to his sister.
  2. Rewriting the code took a month, to say nothing of testing it.

3. Adding an adjective or a further judgment.

  1. The proposal is expensive, to say nothing of legally doubtful.
  2. The trip was long and cold, to say nothing of pointless.

4. With the fact that, where a clause is unavoidable.

  1. The deal is bad on its face, to say nothing of the fact that nobody on the board has read it.
  2. She was unqualified, to say nothing of the fact that she had been fired twice.

together with#

Pronunciation: /təˈɡɛðər wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) · weak /təˈɡɛðɚ wəð/, /təˈɡɛðɚ wəθ/ · tuh-GETH-ur with Type: complex · Register: formal to neutral; standard in legal and conveyancing language Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause Pattern: [clause] together with [noun phrase] · [subject], together with [noun phrase], [singular verb] Contrast: The formal twin of along with, and the two are freely interchangeable in meaning. Neither is instrumental — that is plain with. Like every word in this section, it is a preposition and does not pluralize a singular subject.

Attaches a second item to the first in writing that wants to sound deliberate. It is the standard formula in deeds and contracts, where together with all appurtenances has been boilerplate for centuries, and it carries a trace of that formality into ordinary prose.

1. Accompanying an item in a transaction or a submission.

  1. The deed conveys the parcel together with all easements and rights of way.
  2. Please return the signed form together with a check for the filing fee.

2. Adding one factor to another.

  1. Heat, together with the humidity, makes the afternoons unbearable.
  2. Send the transcript together with three letters of recommendation.

3. Appended to a subject. Singular verb.

Exception, exclusion, and subtraction#

The largest family in the file, and the one with the most rules. It splits four ways: the everyday exception words (except, except for, apart from, aside from, besides, other than, outside of, but, and the ponderous with the exception of); the participials and hypotheticals (barring, excluding, excepting, failing, leaving aside, not counting, short of, and the counterfactual pair but for and if not for); the arithmetic and commercial subtractors (less, minus, and the two-way give or take); and a literary and archaic layer that American readers still meet in older books and in contracts (save, save for, saving, bar, bating).

One rule governs the first group and is worth stating before you reach the entries. Bare except and bare but need an all-inclusive word in front of them — all, every, everyone, everything, everywhere, nothing, no one, nowhere, any-, a superlative, or a wh- question word. Without one, the sentence needs except for: Except the noise, it's fine is an error, Except for the noise, it's fine is correct, and Everything except the noise is fine is correct because everything is doing the work.

In the ranking sense ("second only to") it does take an ordinary noun phrase or a gerund — next to her sister, next to sleeping. Never a finite clause in either sense. The spatial next to, which takes any noun phrase, is covered with the position prepositions. Pattern: next to nothing · next to no [noun] · next to [adjective] · Next to [noun phrase], [superlative claim] (ranking) Contrast: In the limiting sense it is a near-synonym of all but (all but impossible) and little short of, and it cannot be swapped for the spatial next to without changing the word class of what follows: next to the bank is a place, next to nothing is a quantity. British and American usage agree completely here.

Squeezes a quantity or a quality down to the edge of zero. The ranking sense — "second only to" — is the same geometry applied to a list rather than to a scale.

1. Almost nothing, hardly any.

  1. The car sold for next to nothing at the county auction.
  2. He knows next to nothing about wiring.
  3. There's next to no parking on that block after five.

2. Practically, all but — before an adjective.

  1. Getting a permit downtown is next to impossible.
  2. The insulation in this place is next to useless.

3. Second only to — ranking.

  1. Next to her sister, she's the fastest swimmer on the team.
  2. Next to sleeping, reading is his favorite way to kill an afternoon.

sans#

Pronunciation: /sænz/ · SANZ — anglicized; the French /sɑ̃/ only when the speaker is being deliberately French Type: simple · Register: literary, jocular, or design jargon Complement: a bare noun phrase, usually with no determiner — sans makeup, not sans the makeup. Never a finite clause, and a gerund only as a joke. Pattern: [clause] sans [bare noun] · sans [noun], sans [noun], sans [noun] (the rhetorical series) Contrast: A self-conscious without. Borrowed from French in the fourteenth century, dropped, and revived as a mannerism, it now signals wit or style rather than plain absence. Note that sans serif is a frozen typographic compound, not a live preposition. Where without is invisible, sans is always noticed — which is the only reason to use it.

Reports absence with an arched eyebrow. Americans meet it most often in Shakespeare's catalogue of old age and in the type specimen book, and use it themselves mainly for effect.

1. Plain absence, delivered as a wink. Always a bare noun, never sans the.

  1. He took the meeting sans tie, which for him counted as rebellion.
  2. A martini sans olive is just cold gin.
  3. The remake is the original sans charm.

2. The rhetorical series. Repeated for cumulative effect — the construction Shakespeare fixed in the language.

  1. She showed up to the gala sans jewelry, sans makeup, sans apology.
  2. Jaques ends his speech "sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything."

3. In headline, menu, and catalog copy. Where brevity rather than wit is the motive.

  1. It's a Western sans horses, which is either brave or a mistake.
  2. A weeknight lasagna sans the four-hour sauce. (the determiner here is a copywriter's liberty)

without#

Pronunciation: /wɪˈðaʊt/ (also /wɪˈθaʊt/, common in American speech) · weak /wəˈðaʊt/ · with-OWT Type: compound (historically with + out) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or object + gerund (without anyone noticing). It can also stand alone as an adverb with no object after do, go, and manage (we'll have to do without). It can never take a finite clause and never a to-infinitive: not without to pay, not without he agrees. Pattern: without [noun phrase] · without [verb-ing] · without [noun phrase] [verb-ing] · do / go / manage without (no object) Contrast: The negative counterpart of with, and the center the whole absence family orbits: sans is its literary pose, minus its informal one, absent its legal one, in the absence of its bureaucratic one, and free of the one that treats the missing thing as good riddance. Against but for, without covers both plain absence (coffee without sugar) and counterfactual absence (without you, I'd have quit), where but for can only do the second. The regional conjunction without meaning "unless" (I won't go without you come along) is nonstandard in written American English.

The most heavily used preposition in this file and the most versatile. Its range runs from a missing spoonful of sugar to a counterfactual that reorganizes the whole sentence, and its gerund construction — without saying goodbye — is the standard English way of saying "and did not".

1. Not having, lacking.

  1. She drinks her coffee without sugar.
  2. A house without a roof isn't worth much.

2. Not accompanied by.

  1. He walked out without his keys.
  2. They went to the game without me.

3. Followed by a gerund — "and not doing the thing named".

  1. He left without saying goodbye to anyone.
  2. You can't cancel without giving thirty days' notice.

4. Followed by an object and a gerund.

  1. The bill passed without a single senator reading it.
  2. We finished the job without anyone getting hurt.

5. Counterfactually — "if there were not" or "if there had not been".

  1. Without your help, we'd still be arguing about the budget.
  2. Without the levee, half the town would have flooded.

6. As an adverb, with no object. After do, go, and manage.

  1. There's no coffee left, so we'll have to do without.
  2. They went without for most of that winter.

7. Archaically, "outside". The original sense, now confined to older writing and hymn texts. This is the with + out the word was built from.

  1. The old road still runs without the city gates.
  2. "There is a green hill far away, without a city wall."
