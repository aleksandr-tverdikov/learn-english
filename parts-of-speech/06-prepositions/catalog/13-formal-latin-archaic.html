Formal, legal, Latin, and archaic prepositions#

The prepositions that live outside ordinary conversation — in contracts, statutes, hymns, and Shakespeare — and the ones borrowed whole from Latin and French.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

What unites this group is that register is the meaning. Almost none of these words says anything that a plain English preposition cannot say: maugre is despite, 'neath is beneath, under and pursuant to is under, and as per is per with a syllable wasted. What they carry instead is a signal about where the sentence comes from — a deed, a statute, a pleading, a psalm — and using one outside its native habitat is the error, not any question of grammar.

Three traps run through the whole file. The first is that a great many of these words take no object at all, because the object is already inside them: hereunder means "under this document," per annum means "for each year," withal is a with whose object went by three words ago, and in witness whereof and mutatis mutandis govern nothing whatever. Attaching a noun phrase to any of them (✗ hereunder this contract) is the commonest mistake made by writers imitating legal style. The second is that no word in this file takes a finite clause while it is working as a preposition: ✗ subject to the board approves it, ✗ without prejudice to she may appeal, ✗ as per we agreed. Two entries here do take a clause — sith and afore — but only when they stop being prepositions and become conjunctions, and their entries say so. And where a verb has to follow one of the to-final members, that to is a preposition, not the infinitive marker, so the verb must be an -ing form: subject to obtaining a permit and without prejudice to refiling, never ✗ subject to obtain a permit or ✗ without prejudice to refile — the same trap that catches look forward to seeing you, object to being questioned, and accustomed to working late. And the third thing to hold onto is honesty about the archaic words: betwixt, sith, maugre, and o'er are not elegant alternatives you may reach for. They are quotations. The only one an American uses without knowing it is o'er, and only because of the national anthem.

One more thing to listen for. Several of these entries are multiword phrases built out of ordinary function words, and in running speech those words reduce exactly as they do everywhere else: of is /əv/ and often bare /ə/ before a consonant, so by order of the court comes out /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə ðə ˈkɔrt/; to is /tə/, so subject to change is /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə ˈtʃeɪndʒ/; and is /ən/, so save and except is /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/; for is /fɚ/, at is /ət/, from is /frəm/, and with is /wɪθ/ or /wəθ/ in the same unstressed way. Only the Latin tags resist reduction. Per annum, inter alia, sine die, and pro hac vice keep their full vowels, because a speaker reaching for one of them is quoting, and quotations get pronounced carefully.

Legal and official#

The register of contracts, deeds, pleadings, and posted notices. Three things run through it: doublets that say the same thing twice (save and except, under and pursuant to, by and through), fossils that swallowed their own object (hereunder, thereunder, in witness whereof), and reservations that exist to keep a right alive (save and except, without prejudice to, subject to). Only one word here — subject to — is genuinely part of ordinary American English; the rest you will read far more often than you write.

as per#

Pronunciation
/æz ˈpɜr/ · weak /əz pər/ · az-PUR
Русский
согласно, в соответствии с — коммерч. канцеляризм, избыточный per; as per usual — «как всегда» (шутл.)
Type
complex (two-word)
Register
business and commercial; widely condemned by usage guides
Complement
noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ as per we agreed; write as per our agreement), never a gerund (✗ as per shipping it Tuesday; write as per the Tuesday shipping date). Before usual it takes no article: as per usual, and the phrase is frozen — you cannot extend the pattern (✗ as per normal, ✗ as per always).
Pattern
as per [noun phrase] · [clause], as per [noun phrase] · as per usual (fixed)
Contrast
As per adds nothing whatever to bare per, and every American usage guide from Fowler forward calls it commercialese. Prefer per, or better still plain according to or as you requested. The one form nobody objects to is the jocular fixed phrase as per usual, where the redundancy is the joke.
Variants
per is the plain replacement, and according to the plainer one; as per usual is the frozen jocular phrase; per usual is the American clipping of it

A hybrid of English as and Latin per that grew up in nineteenth-century shipping and invoicing correspondence and never got out of it. It is not ungrammatical — only redundant, and dated in a way that makes business prose sound like a telegram.

1. In accordance with; as specified in — the ordinary commercial use. Shipped as per your instructions of the 14th. 2. In the frozen jocular phrase as per usual, "as always." He was twenty minutes late, as per usual.

  1. Shipped as per your instructions of the 14th.
  2. The invoice was prepared as per the terms of the purchase order.
  3. He was twenty minutes late, as per usual.
  4. As per the attached schedule, delivery begins in March.
  5. The specimens were tested as per ASTM D638.
  6. As per our conversation, I have enclosed the revised bid.
  7. As per usual, the meeting ran forty minutes over and settled nothing.

by and among#

Pronunciation
/ˌbaɪ ən əˈmʌŋ/ · weak /baɪ ən əˈmʌŋ/ · by-and-uh-MUNG
Русский
между (тремя и более сторонами) — юр., зачин преамбулы многостороннего договора
Type
complex (legal doublet)
Register
legal — the preamble of a contract with three or more parties
Complement
two or more coordinated noun phrases naming the parties, and in practice three or more — never a finite clause (✗ by and among they signed yesterday), never a gerund (✗ by and among selling the plant). Each party is normally followed by its state of organization and a defined short name: a Delaware corporation ("Buyer").
Pattern
THIS AGREEMENT is made by and among [Party A], [Party B], and [Party C]. · entered into by and among [the parties]
Contrast
The choice between this and by and between is a headcount: American form books use between for two signatories and among for three or more. The schoolroom rule behind the convention is only half true — between is correct for any number when the relations are pairwise — but the drafting habit is real, and a reviewer will change between to among on a four-party signature block without asking. Both are doublets in which by alone would carry the load, and plain-language guides cut them to among. Distinguish by and through, which names an agent rather than the parties themselves.
Variants
among alone is the plain replacement; by and between is the two-party form; by, between and among and made and entered into by and among are the fuller form-book versions

Opens the preamble of a multiparty contract — a credit agreement, a shareholders' agreement, a joint venture — which is where nearly every American transactional lawyer meets it, in the first line of the document rather than in running prose.

  1. THIS SHAREHOLDERS AGREEMENT is made by and among Corbin Holdings, Inc., the Investors listed on Schedule A, and the Founders.
  2. The credit agreement was entered into by and among the Borrower, the Administrative Agent, and the Lenders party thereto.
  3. This Amendment is made by and among the three original signatories and the successor trustee.
  4. The escrow was established by and among buyer, seller, and the title company.
  5. Settlement was reached by and among the class representatives, the defendant, and its two insurers.
  6. When a fourth party joins the deal, the form book switches from by and between to by and among.
  7. The operating agreement runs by and among the Company and each of its Members.

by and between#

Pronunciation
/ˌbaɪ ən bɪˈtwin/ · weak /baɪ ən bəˈtwin/ · by-and-bih-TWEEN
Русский
между (двумя сторонами) — юр., зачин преамбулы двустороннего договора
Type
complex (legal doublet)
Register
legal — the preamble of a two-party contract
Complement
exactly two coordinated noun phrases joined by and — two parties, or two sides each made up of several persons. Never a finite clause (✗ by and between the parties agree as follows), never a gerund (✗ by and between leasing the premises).
Pattern
THIS LEASE is made by and between [Landlord] and [Tenant]. · an agreement by and between [Party A] and [Party B]
Contrast
Identical in force to plain between: the by adds nothing at all, and every plain-language drafting manual in America strikes it. Use by and among when three or more parties sign. Do not read by as naming the party who prepared the document — the phrase says nothing whatever about who drafted it, which matters because contra proferentem construes ambiguities against the drafter and the preamble will not tell you who that was. Distinguish by and through, the pleading formula that names counsel rather than the parties.
Variants
between alone is the plain replacement; made and entered into by and between is the fuller form; by and among is the multiparty version

The first line of the body of nearly every American lease, purchase agreement, employment contract, and settlement agreement — and the phrase most often held up as the specimen of legal doubling that could simply be deleted.

  1. THIS LEASE is made by and between Palmetto Properties, LLC, and Devon Ruiz.
  2. The agreement is by and between the City and the contractor named on the signature page.
  3. Made and entered into this 4th day of June, by and between the Seller and the Buyer.
  4. The collective bargaining agreement by and between the district and the teachers' union runs three years.
  5. Older deeds read "made by and between the party of the first part and the party of the second part."
  6. Drafting manuals tell you to delete by and and leave the between standing.

by and through#

Pronunciation
/ˌbaɪ ən ˈθru/ · weak /baɪ ən θru/ · by-and-THROO
Русский
в лице, через — юр. формула, называет представителя: адвоката, опекуна
Type
complex (legal doublet)
Register
legal — pleadings and court filings
Complement
a noun phrase naming a representative (counsel, guardian, trustee, agent) — never a clause, never a gerund. Always set off by commas inside the party designation.
Pattern
[party], by and through [attorney, guardian, or agent], [verb].
Contrast
A doublet in which by alone would carry the whole meaning; it survives because it sits in every pleading form book. It points the opposite way from on behalf of: by and through names the agent who does the acting, on behalf of names the principal the acting is done for. The related by his attorney is the older and shorter form still used in some state courts.
Variants
by and through her undersigned counsel is the fullest form; by alone is the plain-language replacement

The standard American pleading formula that introduces the lawyer, guardian, or fiduciary through whom a party appears in court. Open almost any civil complaint filed in the United States and it is in the first line of the body.

  1. Plaintiff, by and through her attorneys, Ramirez & Hale LLP, alleges as follows.
  2. The minor child appears by and through his guardian ad litem.
  3. The estate acts by and through its personal representative.
  4. Defendant, by and through undersigned counsel, moves to dismiss the amended complaint.
  5. The tribe sued by and through its tribal council.
  6. Notice was served by and through the registered agent of record.

by order of#

Pronunciation
/baɪ ˈɔrdər ʌv/ · weak /baɪ ˈɔrɾər əv/ · fully reduced /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə/ before a consonant · by-OR-der-uv
Русский
по приказу, по распоряжению — называет издавшую приказ инстанцию
Type
complex
Register
official and legal — signage, notices, court orders
Complement
a noun phrase naming an authority, normally a title rather than a bare personal name — never a clause, never a gerund (✗ by order of closing the road).
Pattern
By order of [authority]. (standing alone as a sign or notice) · [clause] by order of [authority].
Contrast
By order of names the issuing authority; pursuant to names the instrument. A parking sign reads By order of the Chief of Police; a brief reads pursuant to Municipal Code § 12-4. Its military cousin by direction of signs correspondence for a commander, and at the direction of is the softer civilian form that reports an instruction without invoking legal force.
Variants
by direction of (military correspondence); by authority of (older signage)

Attributes a command to the office that issued it, and does so in a form short enough to fit on a placard. Americans meet it far more often on signs and posted notices than in running prose.

  1. NO TRESPASSING — By order of the Chief of Police.
  2. The building was closed by order of the county health department.
  3. By order of the court, all filings in this matter are sealed.
  4. Flags will fly at half-staff through Sunday by order of the governor.
  5. The road remains restricted by order of the Forest Service.
  6. Occupancy is limited to 240 persons by order of the fire marshal.

ex parte#

Pronunciation
/ɛks ˈpɑrti/ · also /ɛks ˈpɑrteɪ/ · eks-PAR-tee
Русский
юр. в одностороннем порядке — по заявлению одной стороны, без участия другой
Type
complex (Latin)
Register
legal
Complement
as a preposition, only a party's name, and only in a case title (Ex parte Milligan). Everywhere else in American practice it is an adjective (an ex parte motion) or an adverb (the judge ruled ex parte) and takes no object at all. Never a clause, never a gerund.
Pattern
Ex parte [name of the applying party] (case title) · an ex parte [motion / order / communication] (attributive) · [verb] ex parte (adverbial)
Contrast
Latin ex parte is "from a party" — one side only. Its opposite is inter partes, both sides participating, which belongs to the frozen family under inter. Do not confuse it with ex post facto (retroactive, and constitutionally forbidden for criminal statutes) or with the commercial ex of ex works.
Variants
Ex p. in older reporters; ex parte is set in italics in most American style manuals but roman in the Bluebook when part of a case name

Marks a proceeding brought by one side without the other present, or a communication with a judge behind an opponent's back — the second being the thing lawyers are ethically forbidden to do. Americans meet it in the great habeas cases Ex parte Milligan and Ex parte Young, in temporary-restraining-order practice, and in every judicial code of conduct.

1. In a case title: on the application of the party named, with no opponent before the court. Ex parte Quirin remains the leading military-commission case. 2. Of a proceeding or an order: heard and decided on one side only. She applied ex parte for emergency custody. 3. Of a communication: made to the judge behind the other side's back — the ethically forbidden sense. Counsel is prohibited from any ex parte contact with the court.

  1. The court granted an ex parte temporary restraining order that afternoon.
  2. Counsel is prohibited from any ex parte communication with the presiding judge.
  3. Ex parte Quirin remains the leading military-commission case.
  4. She applied ex parte for emergency custody of the children.
  5. The order was vacated because it had been obtained ex parte.
  6. Patent examiners handle ex parte reexamination without the challenger present.
  7. Ex parte Milligan held that civilians could not be tried by military commission while the civil courts were open.
  8. The judge disclosed the ex parte phone call on the record the next morning.
See also
inter, ex, pro, in re

ex rel.#

Pronunciation
/ˌɛks ˈrɛl/ · the full Latin ex relatione is /ɛks ˌrɛləʃiˈoʊni/ · eks-REL · no weak form: quoted Latin keeps its full vowels
Русский
юр. по заявлению, от имени (лат. ex relatione) — иск подан от имени государства
Type
complex (Latin, always abbreviated)
Register
legal — case names and citation
Complement
the name of the relator — the private person or public officer on whose information the action is brought — standing between the nominal plaintiff and the v. Never a clause, never a gerund, and it does not govern an ordinary noun phrase in running prose (✗ the suit was brought ex rel. a whistleblower; write on the relation of a whistleblower).
Pattern
[sovereign or nominal plaintiff] ex rel. [relator] v. [defendant] · reported ex rel. [relator]
Contrast
Latin ex relatione, "on the relation of" — that is, on the information of someone who is not the nominal plaintiff. It is the caption of the American qui tam action: under the False Claims Act the United States is the plaintiff and the whistleblower is the relator, so the case reads United States ex rel. Schutte v. SuperValu. Keep it clear of its shelfmates: ex parte says only one side was heard, sub nom. (see sub) says the case was reported under a different name on the way up, and in re says there was no adversary at all. Americans read it aloud as "ex rel," never expanded.
Variants
ex relatione is the full Latin and is effectively never written out; on the relation of is the English; qui tam names the kind of action rather than the caption; older habeas petitions used the same form (United States ex rel. Jones v. Warden)

Names the person who set a lawsuit going in somebody else's name. An American meets it in the caption of a whistleblower case, in state mandamus practice where a citizen sues in the name of the state, and in habeas reports from before the 1960s.

  1. United States ex rel. Schutte v. SuperValu Inc. turned on what the defendants actually believed.
  2. The caption reads State ex rel. Vasquez v. Board of Elections.
  3. Habeas petitions were once styled United States ex rel. Jones v. Warden of Sing Sing.
  4. The relator's name stays in the caption after ex rel. even once the government intervenes.
  5. Ohio's mandamus docket is full of ex rel. captions, because the citizen sues in the name of the state.
  6. Lawyers say "ex rel" out loud; nobody says ex relatione.
  7. American style writes it ex rel. with a period and sets it roman inside a case name.

hereunder#

Pronunciation
/ˌhɪrˈʌndər/ · flapped /ˌhɪrˈʌnər/ · heer-UN-der
Русский
юр. по настоящему договору — «по этому документу»; дополнения не берёт
Type
compound (fused here + under)
Register
legal — contract drafting
Complement
none. This word takes no object. Its object is already inside it: here means "this document," so hereunder is a complete phrase meaning "under this agreement." Writing hereunder this contract is an error, and so is hereunder to Section 4.
Pattern
[noun phrase] hereunder · [verb phrase] hereunder · rights and obligations hereunder
Contrast
Hereunder points at the document you are reading; thereunder points at some other instrument just named. Mixing them up changes what a contract means. The whole family is a preposition welded to a pointing word — hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, hereinafter, hereunder, herewith, hereinabove — and every plain-language drafting manual in America tells you to replace each of them with under this Agreement, to this Agreement, and so on.
Variants
under this Agreement is the plain-language replacement; the here- family, every member of which takes no object of its own: hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, herewith, hereinafter, hereinbefore, hereinabove, heretofore

A fossil of the Old English habit of putting the object first and letting an adverb swallow it. It is the reason contract prose can refer to itself without ever naming itself, and the reason non-lawyers find contracts unreadable.

  1. The Seller's obligations hereunder survive termination of this Agreement.
  2. Any notice given hereunder must be in writing and sent by certified mail.
  3. Payments due hereunder bear interest at six percent from the due date.
  4. Nothing hereunder creates a partnership or joint venture between the parties.
  5. The Licensee's rights hereunder are personal and non-transferable.
  6. Each party shall bear its own costs incurred hereunder.

in consideration of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən ʌv/ · weak /ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən əv/ · in-kun-sid-uh-RAY-shun-uv
Русский
юр. в обмен на, за встречное предоставление; книжн. ввиду, принимая во внимание
Type
complex
Register
legal in the contract sense; formal general prose in the other
Complement
noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ in consideration of he paid the deposit). For a clause you must write in consideration of the fact that, and you should usually write because instead. A verb after of takes the -ing form and nothing else: in consideration of releasing all claims, never ✗ in consideration of to release all claims.
Pattern
In consideration of [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] in consideration of [noun phrase] · in consideration of [verb-ing]
Contrast
Two different words wear the same coat. In contract law consideration is the technical name for the bargained-for exchange, and this phrase introduces it — a promise without consideration is unenforceable, so the words are doing legal work, not decorating. In ordinary formal prose it means "in view of, out of regard for," close to in light of. Keep it clear of out of consideration for, which means "out of politeness toward" and is a different idiom entirely.
Variants
for and in consideration of (the fuller conveyancing doublet); in consideration whereof (older)

The phrase that opens the operative clause of nearly every American contract and deed, and the hinge on which the enforceability of the whole document turns.

1. Contract law: in exchange for; as the bargained-for price of a promise. In consideration of the sum of ten dollars, Grantor conveys the following tract. 2. General formal prose: in view of; taking account of. The sentence was reduced in consideration of his cooperation.

  1. In consideration of the sum of ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration, Grantor conveys the following described tract.
  2. The release was signed in consideration of a lump-sum severance payment.
  3. In consideration of the mutual covenants set forth herein, the parties agree as follows.
  4. She accepted the easement in consideration of a one-time payment of $4,000.
  5. The sentence was reduced in consideration of his cooperation with the government.
  6. In consideration of her thirty years of service, the board voted her a full pension.
  7. The policy was rewritten in consideration of the flood risk along the coast.
  8. In consideration of releasing all claims against the estate, she received a lump sum of $250,000.

in default of#

Pronunciation
/ɪn dɪˈfɔlt ʌv/ · weak /ɪn dəˈfɔlt əv/ · fully reduced /ɪn dəˈfɔlt ə/ before a consonant · in-dih-FAWLT-uv
Русский
юр. за неимением, при отсутствии — если названное лицо или действие отсутствует
Type
complex
Register
formal and legal — wills, trusts, contracts, and court rules
Complement
noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ in default of he pays the premium; write in default of payment). The of is a preposition, so a verb after it must be an -ing form: in default of paying the premium, never ✗ in default of to pay the premium. The object names the act or event that fails to happen, not the person who fails: in default of issue, in default of appointment, in default of agreement.
Pattern
In default of [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] in default of [noun phrase] · in default of [verb-ing]
Contrast
It means "if that does not happen," not "because somebody defaulted." The bare adverbial in default, with no of, is the other word — a defendant who never answers is in default — and default on takes the loan or obligation as its object. Its plain neighbors are in the absence of, which is neutral about whose fault the gap is, and failing, which is the one-word equivalent and much the shorter. Estate drafters use one collocation above all others: in default of issue, meaning "if he leaves no children."
Variants
failing is the one-word equivalent; in the absence of and for want of are the plainer phrases; in default whereof is the older self-pointing form; in default of appointment and in default of issue are the fixed drafting collocations

Sets out what happens when the first arrangement fails. It is the hinge of a will, of a power of appointment, and of every clause that has to name a fallback.

1. Wills and trusts: if the named person or class fails. In default of issue, the property passes to my brother's children. 2. Contracts: if the required act is not done in time. In default of payment within thirty days, the whole balance falls due. 3. Procedure: if nobody appears, or nobody agrees. In default of agreement, the matter goes to arbitration.

  1. In default of issue, the remainder passes to the testator's nieces and nephews.
  2. The trust names takers in default of appointment, which is what saves the gift if the power is never exercised.
  3. In default of payment within thirty days, the entire balance becomes immediately due.
  4. In default of an appearance by the defendant, the clerk may enter judgment.
  5. In default of agreement between the two appraisers, a third is named by the court.
  6. In default of a written contract, the parties are left with whatever the emails prove.
  7. The policy lapses in default of the annual premium.
  8. In default of nomination by the union, the arbitrator is drawn from the standing panel.

failing, in the absence of, subject to, save and except, without prejudice to

in witness whereof#

Pronunciation
/ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈʌv/ · older wh- /ʍɛrˈʌv/ · weak /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈəv/ · in-WIT-nis-wair-UV
Русский
юр. в удостоверение чего — застывшая формула перед подписями в договоре
Type
complex (frozen formula)
Register
legal — execution and testimonium clauses
Complement
none. Whereof already contains its object — it means "of which," pointing back at the document — so the phrase is complete as it stands. It is always followed by a comma and then a full clause, never by a noun phrase (✗ in witness whereof this deed).
Pattern
In witness whereof, [the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above].
Contrast
The where- family is the third leg of a three-part system: hereunder points at the document you are holding, thereunder at another one just named, and whereof at the thing last mentioned. Its relatives are whereby, wherein, whereas, whereupon, wherefore, whereto. The variant in witness thereof is usually a drafting slip rather than a genuine alternative — thereof points away from the document that is being signed, which is not what the signer means.
Variants
in testimony whereof (state seals and notarial certificates); witness my hand and seal (the shorter American form); the where- family, which likewise governs nothing: whereof, whereby, wherein, whereas, whereupon, wherefore, whereto

The testimonium clause — the fixed line that opens the signature block of an American contract, deed, affidavit, or gubernatorial proclamation. It is not a productive preposition at all: it is a fossil you will read a thousand times and never build a new sentence with.

  1. In witness whereof, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
  2. In witness whereof, I have hereunto set my hand and caused the Great Seal of the State of Ohio to be affixed.
  3. In witness whereof, the undersigned has caused this Deed to be executed by its duly authorized officer.
  4. The notary's certificate appeared immediately below the in witness whereof line.
  5. In witness whereof, the Corporation has caused these presents to be signed this 3rd day of April.
  6. Older forms open the clause in testimony whereof rather than in witness whereof.

save and except#

Pronunciation
/ˌseɪv ənd ɪkˈsɛpt/ · weak /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/ · sayv-and-ik-SEPT
Русский
юр. за исключением — дублет в актах о передаче недвижимости, = except
Type
complex (legal doublet)
Register
legal — deeds, conveyances, oil-and-gas instruments
Complement
noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. The object is the thing carved out of the grant, and it must be described with the same precision as the grant itself.
Pattern
[granting clause], save and except [noun phrase] · Save and except [noun phrase], [clause].
Contrast
A doublet in which both halves mean the same thing: save is the native-English member and except the Latin-derived one, paired by the medieval habit of doubling a word with its Romance twin (will and testament, free and clear, give, devise and bequeath). Ordinary writing wants except alone — and note the division of labor between except and except for: bare except subtracts from a word already in the sentence (everyone except Marcia signed), while except for stands outside the whole clause and qualifies it (Except for the roof, the house is sound). Note also that bare save has a second life as a conjunction taking a clause (save that he arrived late); the doublet does not — save and except is a preposition and nothing else. Finally, a save and except clause reserves an interest to the grantor, while an exception to a warranty merely limits what is being warranted — Texas courts have decided a great many cases on that difference.
Variants

Latin and borrowed#

Latin and French prepositions borrowed into English, most of them locked inside a fixed phrase and unable to govern an English noun. The test to apply is whether the word can take a noun phrase you choose yourself: per, versus, chez, ex, and cum can, so they are living English prepositions; ad, apud, coram, de, in, inter, intra, pro, quoad, sensu, sine, sub, and ultra cannot, and survive only as tags quoted whole, each welded to the one Latin noun it was borrowed with. Watch the Latin plurals and the fixed word order — mutatis mutandis, never the reverse — and remember that a Latin phrase in an American document is usually a term of art with a precise meaning, not decoration.

Archaic and literary#

Words that dropped out of English between Chaucer and the Civil War, plus the metrical clippings that poets kept alive for the sake of the syllable count. Most of them are simply an ordinary preposition one syllable shorter — 'gainst, 'neath, o'er, 'twixt, 'midst — and the apostrophe is the whole story. Two are genuine traps rather than curiosities: withal, which is a stranded with and not an intensifier, and without in its lost spatial sense, which means the exact opposite of what a modern reader assumes. The pairs amid/amidst and among/amongst are the only entries here that involve a live editorial choice, and in American English the shorter form wins both times. The same choice turns up outside this file with toward/towards and round/around: American English takes toward, amid, among, and around; British English takes towards, amidst, amongst, and (in some senses) round. An American who reaches for the -s form is imitating British prose, whether or not he knows it.
