Formal, legal, Latin, and archaic prepositions#

The prepositions that live outside ordinary conversation — in contracts, statutes, hymns, and Shakespeare — and the ones borrowed whole from Latin and French.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

What unites this group is that register is the meaning. Almost none of these words says anything that a plain English preposition cannot say: maugre is despite, 'neath is beneath, under and pursuant to is under, and as per is per with a syllable wasted. What they carry instead is a signal about where the sentence comes from — a deed, a statute, a pleading, a psalm — and using one outside its native habitat is the error, not any question of grammar.

Three traps run through the whole file. The first is that a great many of these words take no object at all, because the object is already inside them: hereunder means "under this document," per annum means "for each year," withal is a with whose object went by three words ago, and in witness whereof and mutatis mutandis govern nothing whatever. Attaching a noun phrase to any of them (✗ hereunder this contract) is the commonest mistake made by writers imitating legal style. The second is that no word in this file takes a finite clause while it is working as a preposition: ✗ subject to the board approves it, ✗ without prejudice to she may appeal, ✗ as per we agreed. Two entries here do take a clause — sith and afore — but only when they stop being prepositions and become conjunctions, and their entries say so. And where a verb has to follow one of the to-final members, that to is a preposition, not the infinitive marker, so the verb must be an -ing form: subject to obtaining a permit and without prejudice to refiling, never ✗ subject to obtain a permit or ✗ without prejudice to refile — the same trap that catches look forward to seeing you, object to being questioned, and accustomed to working late. And the third thing to hold onto is honesty about the archaic words: betwixt, sith, maugre, and o'er are not elegant alternatives you may reach for. They are quotations. The only one an American uses without knowing it is o'er, and only because of the national anthem.

One more thing to listen for. Several of these entries are multiword phrases built out of ordinary function words, and in running speech those words reduce exactly as they do everywhere else: of is /əv/ and often bare /ə/ before a consonant, so by order of the court comes out /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə ðə ˈkɔrt/; to is /tə/, so subject to change is /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə ˈtʃeɪndʒ/; and is /ən/, so save and except is /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/; for is /fɚ/, at is /ət/, from is /frəm/, and with is /wɪθ/ or /wəθ/ in the same unstressed way. Only the Latin tags resist reduction. Per annum, inter alia, sine die, and pro hac vice keep their full vowels, because a speaker reaching for one of them is quoting, and quotations get pronounced carefully.

Legal and official#

The register of contracts, deeds, pleadings, and posted notices. Three things run through it: doublets that say the same thing twice (save and except, under and pursuant to, by and through), fossils that swallowed their own object (hereunder, thereunder, in witness whereof), and reservations that exist to keep a right alive (save and except, without prejudice to, subject to). Only one word here — subject to — is genuinely part of ordinary American English; the rest you will read far more often than you write.

as per#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈpɜr/ · weak /əz pər/ · az-PUR Type: complex (two-word) · Register: business and commercial; widely condemned by usage guides Complement: noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ as per we agreed; write as per our agreement), never a gerund (✗ as per shipping it Tuesday; write as per the Tuesday shipping date). Before usual it takes no article: as per usual, and the phrase is frozen — you cannot extend the pattern (✗ as per normal, ✗ as per always). Pattern: as per [noun phrase] · [clause], as per [noun phrase] · as per usual (fixed) Contrast: As per adds nothing whatever to bare per, and every American usage guide from Fowler forward calls it commercialese. Prefer per, or better still plain according to or as you requested. The one form nobody objects to is the jocular fixed phrase as per usual, where the redundancy is the joke. Variants: per is the plain replacement, and according to the plainer one; as per usual is the frozen jocular phrase; per usual is the American clipping of it

A hybrid of English as and Latin per that grew up in nineteenth-century shipping and invoicing correspondence and never got out of it. It is not ungrammatical — only redundant, and dated in a way that makes business prose sound like a telegram.

1. In accordance with; as specified in — the ordinary commercial use. Shipped as per your instructions of the 14th. 2. In the frozen jocular phrase as per usual, "as always." He was twenty minutes late, as per usual.

  1. Shipped as per your instructions of the 14th.
  2. The invoice was prepared as per the terms of the purchase order.
  3. He was twenty minutes late, as per usual.
  4. As per the attached schedule, delivery begins in March.
  5. The specimens were tested as per ASTM D638.
  6. As per our conversation, I have enclosed the revised bid.
  7. As per usual, the meeting ran forty minutes over and settled nothing.

See also: per, according to, in accordance with, pursuant to

by and through#

Pronunciation: /ˌbaɪ ən ˈθru/ · weak /baɪ ən θru/ · by-and-THROO Type: complex (legal doublet) · Register: legal — pleadings and court filings Complement: a noun phrase naming a representative (counsel, guardian, trustee, agent) — never a clause, never a gerund. Always set off by commas inside the party designation. Pattern: [party], by and through [attorney, guardian, or agent], [verb]. Contrast: A doublet in which by alone would carry the whole meaning; it survives because it sits in every pleading form book. It points the opposite way from on behalf of: by and through names the agent who does the acting, on behalf of names the principal the acting is done for. The related by his attorney is the older and shorter form still used in some state courts. Variants: by and through her undersigned counsel is the fullest form; by alone is the plain-language replacement

The standard American pleading formula that introduces the lawyer, guardian, or fiduciary through whom a party appears in court. Open almost any civil complaint filed in the United States and it is in the first line of the body.

  1. Plaintiff, by and through her attorneys, Ramirez & Hale LLP, alleges as follows.
  2. The minor child appears by and through his guardian ad litem.
  3. The estate acts by and through its personal representative.
  4. Defendant, by and through undersigned counsel, moves to dismiss the amended complaint.
  5. The tribe sued by and through its tribal council.
  6. Notice was served by and through the registered agent of record.

See also: on behalf of, by, under and pursuant to, vice

by order of#

Pronunciation: /baɪ ˈɔrdər ʌv/ · weak /baɪ ˈɔrɾər əv/ · fully reduced /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə/ before a consonant · by-OR-der-uv Type: complex · Register: official and legal — signage, notices, court orders Complement: a noun phrase naming an authority, normally a title rather than a bare personal name — never a clause, never a gerund (✗ by order of closing the road). Pattern: By order of [authority]. (standing alone as a sign or notice) · [clause] by order of [authority]. Contrast: By order of names the issuing authority; pursuant to names the instrument. A parking sign reads By order of the Chief of Police; a brief reads pursuant to Municipal Code § 12-4. Its military cousin by direction of signs correspondence for a commander, and at the direction of is the softer civilian form that reports an instruction without invoking legal force. Variants: by direction of (military correspondence); by authority of (older signage)

Attributes a command to the office that issued it, and does so in a form short enough to fit on a placard. Americans meet it far more often on signs and posted notices than in running prose.

  1. NO TRESPASSING — By order of the Chief of Police.
  2. The building was closed by order of the county health department.
  3. By order of the court, all filings in this matter are sealed.
  4. Flags will fly at half-staff through Sunday by order of the governor.
  5. The road remains restricted by order of the Forest Service.
  6. Occupancy is limited to 240 persons by order of the fire marshal.

See also: pursuant to, under and pursuant to, by virtue of, by

ex parte#

Pronunciation: /ɛks ˈpɑrti/ · also /ɛks ˈpɑrteɪ/ · eks-PAR-tee Type: complex (Latin) · Register: legal Complement: as a preposition, only a party's name, and only in a case title (Ex parte Milligan). Everywhere else in American practice it is an adjective (an ex parte motion) or an adverb (the judge ruled ex parte) and takes no object at all. Never a clause, never a gerund. Pattern: Ex parte [name of the applying party] (case title) · an ex parte [motion / order / communication] (attributive) · [verb] ex parte (adverbial) Contrast: Latin ex parte is "from a party" — one side only. Its opposite is inter partes, both sides participating, which belongs to the frozen family under inter. Do not confuse it with ex post facto (retroactive, and constitutionally forbidden for criminal statutes) or with the commercial ex of ex works. Variants: Ex p. in older reporters; ex parte is set in italics in most American style manuals but roman in the Bluebook when part of a case name

Marks a proceeding brought by one side without the other present, or a communication with a judge behind an opponent's back — the second being the thing lawyers are ethically forbidden to do. Americans meet it in the great habeas cases Ex parte Milligan and Ex parte Young, in temporary-restraining-order practice, and in every judicial code of conduct.

1. In a case title: on the application of the party named, with no opponent before the court. Ex parte Quirin remains the leading military-commission case. 2. Of a proceeding or an order: heard and decided on one side only. She applied ex parte for emergency custody. 3. Of a communication: made to the judge behind the other side's back — the ethically forbidden sense. Counsel is prohibited from any ex parte contact with the court.

  1. The court granted an ex parte temporary restraining order that afternoon.
  2. Counsel is prohibited from any ex parte communication with the presiding judge.
  3. Ex parte Quirin remains the leading military-commission case.
  4. She applied ex parte for emergency custody of the children.
  5. The order was vacated because it had been obtained ex parte.
  6. Patent examiners handle ex parte reexamination without the challenger present.
  7. Ex parte Milligan held that civilians could not be tried by military commission while the civil courts were open.
  8. The judge disclosed the ex parte phone call on the record the next morning.

See also: inter, ex, pro, in re

hereunder#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪrˈʌndər/ · flapped /ˌhɪrˈʌnər/ · heer-UN-der Type: compound (fused here + under) · Register: legal — contract drafting Complement: none. This word takes no object. Its object is already inside it: here means "this document," so hereunder is a complete phrase meaning "under this agreement." Writing hereunder this contract is an error, and so is hereunder to Section 4. Pattern: [noun phrase] hereunder · [verb phrase] hereunder · rights and obligations hereunder Contrast: Hereunder points at the document you are reading; thereunder points at some other instrument just named. Mixing them up changes what a contract means. The whole family is a preposition welded to a pointing word — hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, hereinafter, hereunder, herewith, hereinabove — and every plain-language drafting manual in America tells you to replace each of them with under this Agreement, to this Agreement, and so on. Variants: under this Agreement is the plain-language replacement; the here- family: hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, herewith, hereinafter

A fossil of the Old English habit of putting the object first and letting an adverb swallow it. It is the reason contract prose can refer to itself without ever naming itself, and the reason non-lawyers find contracts unreadable.

  1. The Seller's obligations hereunder survive termination of this Agreement.
  2. Any notice given hereunder must be in writing and sent by certified mail.
  3. Payments due hereunder bear interest at six percent from the due date.
  4. Nothing hereunder creates a partnership or joint venture between the parties.
  5. The Licensee's rights hereunder are personal and non-transferable.
  6. Each party shall bear its own costs incurred hereunder.

See also: thereunder, in witness whereof, under and pursuant to, under

in consideration of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən ʌv/ · weak /ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən əv/ · in-kun-sid-uh-RAY-shun-uv Type: complex · Register: legal in the contract sense; formal general prose in the other Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ in consideration of he paid the deposit). For a clause you must write in consideration of the fact that, and you should usually write because instead. A verb after of takes the -ing form and nothing else: in consideration of releasing all claims, never ✗ in consideration of to release all claims. Pattern: In consideration of [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] in consideration of [noun phrase] · in consideration of [verb-ing] Contrast: Two different words wear the same coat. In contract law consideration is the technical name for the bargained-for exchange, and this phrase introduces it — a promise without consideration is unenforceable, so the words are doing legal work, not decorating. In ordinary formal prose it means "in view of, out of regard for," close to in light of. Keep it clear of out of consideration for, which means "out of politeness toward" and is a different idiom entirely. Variants: for and in consideration of (the fuller conveyancing doublet); in consideration whereof (older)

The phrase that opens the operative clause of nearly every American contract and deed, and the hinge on which the enforceability of the whole document turns.

1. Contract law: in exchange for; as the bargained-for price of a promise. In consideration of the sum of ten dollars, Grantor conveys the following tract. 2. General formal prose: in view of; taking account of. The sentence was reduced in consideration of his cooperation.

  1. In consideration of the sum of ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration, Grantor conveys the following described tract.
  2. The release was signed in consideration of a lump-sum severance payment.
  3. In consideration of the mutual covenants set forth herein, the parties agree as follows.
  4. She accepted the easement in consideration of a one-time payment of $4,000.
  5. The sentence was reduced in consideration of his cooperation with the government.
  6. In consideration of her thirty years of service, the board voted her a full pension.
  7. The policy was rewritten in consideration of the flood risk along the coast.
  8. In consideration of releasing all claims against the estate, she received a lump sum of $250,000.

See also: in exchange for, in light of, in view of, for, subject to

in witness whereof#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈʌv/ · older wh- /ʍɛrˈʌv/ · weak /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈəv/ · in-WIT-nis-wair-UV Type: complex (frozen formula) · Register: legal — execution and testimonium clauses Complement: none. Whereof already contains its object — it means "of which," pointing back at the document — so the phrase is complete as it stands. It is always followed by a comma and then a full clause, never by a noun phrase (✗ in witness whereof this deed). Pattern: In witness whereof, [the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above]. Contrast: The where- family is the third leg of a three-part system: hereunder points at the document you are holding, thereunder at another one just named, and whereof at the thing last mentioned. Its relatives are whereby, wherein, whereas, whereupon, wherefore, whereto. The variant in witness thereof is usually a drafting slip rather than a genuine alternative — thereof points away from the document that is being signed, which is not what the signer means. Variants: in testimony whereof (state seals and notarial certificates); witness my hand and seal (the shorter American form)

The testimonium clause — the fixed line that opens the signature block of an American contract, deed, affidavit, or gubernatorial proclamation. It is not a productive preposition at all: it is a fossil you will read a thousand times and never build a new sentence with.

  1. In witness whereof, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
  2. In witness whereof, I have hereunto set my hand and caused the Great Seal of the State of Ohio to be affixed.
  3. In witness whereof, the undersigned has caused this Deed to be executed by its duly authorized officer.
  4. The notary's certificate appeared immediately below the in witness whereof line.
  5. In witness whereof, the Corporation has caused these presents to be signed this 3rd day of April.
  6. Older forms open the clause in testimony whereof rather than in witness whereof.

See also: hereunder, thereunder, in consideration of, of

save and except#

Pronunciation: /ˌseɪv ənd ɪkˈsɛpt/ · weak /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/ · sayv-and-ik-SEPT Type: complex (legal doublet) · Register: legal — deeds, conveyances, oil-and-gas instruments Complement: noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. The object is the thing carved out of the grant, and it must be described with the same precision as the grant itself. Pattern: [granting clause], save and except [noun phrase] · Save and except [noun phrase], [clause]. Contrast: A doublet in which both halves mean the same thing: save is the native-English member and except the Latin-derived one, paired by the medieval habit of doubling a word with its Romance twin (will and testament, free and clear, give, devise and bequeath). Ordinary writing wants except alone — and note the division of labor between except and except for: bare except subtracts from a word already in the sentence (everyone except Marcia signed), while except for stands outside the whole clause and qualifies it (Except for the roof, the house is sound). Note also that bare save has a second life as a conjunction taking a clause (save that he arrived late); the doublet does not — save and except is a preposition and nothing else. Finally, a save and except clause reserves an interest to the grantor, while an exception to a warranty merely limits what is being warranted — Texas courts have decided a great many cases on that difference. Variants: saving and excepting (the participial form); less and except (equally common in mineral deeds); reserving unto Grantor (the reservation, as opposed to exception, wording)

The reservation formula of American deed drafting, and above all of Texas and Oklahoma oil-and-gas conveyances, where a grantor uses it to keep the minerals while selling the surface. Read one mineral deed and you will meet it in the first paragraph.

  1. Grantor conveys the surface estate, save and except all oil, gas, and other minerals in and under said land.
  2. The lease covers the entire section, save and except forty acres surrounding the homestead.
  3. All fixtures pass with the sale, save and except the walk-in cooler and the display cases.
  4. Save and except the easement described in Exhibit B, the tract is unencumbered.
  5. The assignment transfers every well on the lease, save and except the Jones No. 2.
  6. Grantor conveys all of Lot 7, save and except that portion previously dedicated to the county for road purposes.

See also: save, except, excepting, with the exception of, subject to

subject to#

Pronunciation: /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tu/ · weak /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə/ · SUB-jikt-too Type: complex · Register: formal and legal, but fully current in general American English Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ subject to the board approves it; write subject to the board's approval or subject to approval by the board). The to is a preposition here, not the infinitive marker, so a verb after it must be an -ing form: subject to obtaining a permit, never ✗ subject to obtain a permit — the same trap as look forward to seeing you, object to being questioned, and accustomed to working late. The gerund may carry its own subject in the possessive or objective case: subject to the applicant's posting a bond. Pronoun objects take the objective case: subject to them, never ✗ subject to they. Pattern: Subject to [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] subject to [noun phrase] · [noun] subject to [noun phrase] · subject to [verb-ing] Contrast: Stress separates the preposition from the verb it looks like: the preposition and adjective are SUB-ject /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt/, while the verb meaning "to expose someone to something" is sub-JECT /səbˈdʒɛkt/ (they subjected him to a search). Against its neighbors, subject to makes the main event conditional and revocable — the condition can still undo it later — where contingent on and dependent on merely make it conditional up front, and pursuant to makes it authorized. Subject to change and subject to availability are fixed phrases. Variants: contingent on and dependent on are the near equivalents for the conditional sense; subject to change, subject to availability, and subject to approval are fixed phrases; subject thereto is the legal-fossil form that points back at a document already named

The most productive word in this file and the only one an ordinary American uses without noticing. Three senses run through it, and they shade into one another: a condition, an authority, and an exposure.

1. Conditional on; not final until something else happens. The offer is subject to a satisfactory inspection. 2. Under the authority, jurisdiction, or governance of. Federal contractors are subject to the same disclosure rules. 3. Liable to undergo; prone to. The coast is subject to flooding every spring.

  1. The offer is subject to a satisfactory home inspection.
  2. Subject to shareholder approval, the merger closes in April.
  3. All fares and schedules are subject to change without notice.
  4. Federal contractors are subject to the same disclosure rules as agencies.
  5. The territory remained subject to the crown for another century.
  6. Any amount subject to withholding must be reported on Form 1099.
  7. The coast is subject to flooding every spring.
  8. He has been subject to migraines since childhood.
  9. The permit is granted subject to the applicant posting a bond.
  10. Closing is subject to obtaining a firm commitment from the lender by June 1.

See also: pursuant to, without prejudice to, in accordance with, to, save and except

thereunder#

Pronunciation: /ˌðɛrˈʌndər/ · flapped /ˌðɛrˈʌnər/ · thair-UN-der Type: compound (fused there + under) · Register: legal and regulatory Complement: none — the object is incorporated. There points at a statute, contract, or provision already named, so thereunder means "under that." Thereunder the statute is an error. Pattern: [noun phrase] thereunder · [verb phrase] thereunder · the Act and the regulations promulgated thereunder Contrast: Hereunder points at the document you are reading; thereunder points at some other instrument just mentioned. Drafters confuse the two constantly, and the mistake is substantive, not stylistic — it can redirect an entire obligation. The there- family runs thereof, thereto, therein, thereby, thereafter, thereunder, therewith, theretofore, thereupon. Variants: under that Agreement / under the Act are the plain-language replacements; the there- family: thereof, thereto, therein, thereby, therewith

The pointing twin of hereunder, and the workhorse of American regulatory citation, where the standard formula is the Act and the regulations promulgated thereunder.

  1. The Act and the regulations promulgated thereunder apply to all interstate carriers.
  2. The lease and the rights granted thereunder were assigned in 2019.
  3. Any claim arising thereunder is barred by the statute of limitations.
  4. The trust and the powers conferred thereunder terminate at her death.
  5. He cited the treaty and two decisions rendered thereunder.
  6. Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 promulgated thereunder prohibit securities fraud.

See also: hereunder, in witness whereof, under and pursuant to, under

under and pursuant to#

Pronunciation: /ˈʌndər ənd pərˈsuənt tu/ · weak /ˈʌndər ən pərˈsuənt tə/ · UN-der-and-per-SOO-unt-too Type: complex (legal doublet) · Register: legal Complement: a noun phrase naming an instrument or a source of authority — a statute, an agreement, an order, a section number. Never a clause, never a gerund. Pattern: [clause] under and pursuant to [statute / agreement / order] · Under and pursuant to [instrument], [clause]. Contrast: A pure doublet: pursuant to already means "under." It persists because form books carry it, and it is the first phrase struck by every plain-language style guide, including the federal courts' own. Distinguish by order of, which names the issuing authority rather than the instrument, and in accordance with, which claims conformity rather than derivation. Variants: under alone is the plain-language replacement; pursuant to and in accordance with (a longer doublet of the same kind)

Derives an action from a written authority, twice over. The only reason to know it is that you will read it, not write it.

  1. The sale is made under and pursuant to the terms of the settlement agreement.
  2. Bonds issued under and pursuant to the Indenture rank equally with one another.
  3. The trustee acts under and pursuant to Article VII of the trust instrument.
  4. Notice was given under and pursuant to Section 1692g of the Act.
  5. The property was sold under and pursuant to a decree of foreclosure entered March 4.
  6. Under and pursuant to the authority vested in me by the Constitution, I hereby proclaim...

without prejudice to#

Pronunciation: /wɪˌðaʊt ˈprɛdʒədɪs tu/ · also /wɪˌθaʊt/ · weak to /tə/ · with-OUT-PREJ-uh-dis-too Type: complex · Register: legal Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ without prejudice to she may appeal; write without prejudice to her right to appeal). The to is a preposition here too, so a verb after it takes -ing: without prejudice to refiling, never ✗ without prejudice to refile. Pattern: Without prejudice to [noun phrase], [clause]. · [clause] without prejudice to [noun phrase] · dismissed without prejudice (adverbial, no object) Contrast: The bare adverbial without prejudice, with no to, is far commoner and means something narrower: a case dismissed without prejudice can be refiled, one dismissed with prejudice cannot. British practice also uses without prejudice to mark settlement correspondence inadmissible; in an American court that label has no magic effect, and lawyers write for settlement purposes only under Rule 408. Compare the ordinary privative without, which merely subtracts. Variants: without prejudice (adverbial, no object — the commoner form); without waiving (the same reservation in plainer words)

Reserves a right instead of surrendering it. It is the drafter's way of saying that doing this now does not cost me that later, and it is why a party can pay under protest, or settle one claim while keeping another alive.

1. Reserving a substantive right that the present act might otherwise waive. Without prejudice to its right to appeal, the company paid the penalty. 2. In litigation: leaving a claim, a defense, or a position free to be raised again later. The case was dismissed without prejudice to refiling in state court.

  1. Without prejudice to its right to appeal, the company paid the assessed penalty.
  2. The case was dismissed without prejudice to refiling in state court.
  3. Without prejudice to the foregoing, Buyer may terminate on ten days' written notice.
  4. She accepted the interim payment without prejudice to her larger claim.
  5. The stipulation is without prejudice to any party's position on the merits.
  6. Counsel appeared without prejudice to the pending jurisdictional objection.
  7. The parties settled the fee dispute without prejudice to litigating the merits in the pending action.

See also: without, subject to, notwithstanding, irrespective of

Latin and borrowed#

Latin and French prepositions borrowed into English, most of them locked inside a fixed phrase and unable to govern an English noun. The test to apply is whether the word can take a noun phrase you choose yourself: per, versus, chez, ex, and cum can, so they are living English prepositions; ad, de, inter, intra, pro, sine, sub, and ultra cannot, and survive only as tags quoted whole, each welded to the one Latin noun it was borrowed with. Watch the Latin plurals and the fixed word order — mutatis mutandis, never the reverse — and remember that a Latin phrase in an American document is usually a term of art with a precise meaning, not decoration.

ad#

Pronunciation: /æd/ · weak /əd/ · AD Type: simple (Latin) · Register: legal, academic, and commercial; a bound element, never a free preposition Complement: only the Latin noun the phrase was borrowed with — ad hoc, ad litem, ad valorem, ad interim, ad damnum, ad idem, ad nauseam. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ ad the committee; write to the committee), and it never takes a clause or a gerund. Pattern: an ad hoc [committee / arrangement] · guardian ad litem · ad valorem [tax / duty] · [officer] ad interim · the ad damnum clause Contrast: Latin ad is "to, toward, for the purpose of," and it is the ad- of adjacent, adhere, and administer; English to and for took the whole job, so the free preposition never entered the language. Against pro, the other Latin "for": pro is "on behalf of" — an attorney serves pro bono, for the public good — while ad is "for the purpose of," so a guardian ad litem is appointed for the lawsuit and nothing else. Watch the American stress and vowels: ad hoc is /æd ˈhɑk/ here, not the classical /ɑd ˈhoʊk/. Variants: ad- as a productive English prefix (adjoin, adjunct, advert); ad hoc has become an ordinary English adjective and adverb and is no longer felt as Latin; ad valorem is the opposite of a specific duty

Latin "to, toward, for." Like inter and pro it never became a free English preposition and survives only welded to the noun it governs — but the phrases it is welded into are among the commonest Latin tags in American public life.

1. Ad hoc: for this one purpose; improvised, not systematic. The mayor named an ad hoc committee on flooding. 2. Ad litem: for the lawsuit — of a representative appointed for the case alone. The court appointed a guardian ad litem for the child. 3. Ad valorem: according to value — the tax and customs sense. Property taxes are levied ad valorem. 4. Ad interim: for the time being; in the interval. She served as chargé d'affaires ad interim. 5. Frozen phrases of extent: ad damnum, ad nauseam, ad infinitum. The ad damnum clause demanded ten million dollars.

  1. The mayor named an ad hoc committee to study the flooding.
  2. Scheduling is done ad hoc, which is why nobody knows when the truck comes.

Archaic and literary#

Words that dropped out of English between Chaucer and the Civil War, plus the metrical clippings that poets kept alive for the sake of the syllable count. Most of them are simply an ordinary preposition one syllable shorter — 'gainst, 'neath, o'er, 'twixt, 'midst — and the apostrophe is the whole story. Two are genuine traps rather than curiosities: withal, which is a stranded with and not an intensifier, and without in its lost spatial sense, which means the exact opposite of what a modern reader assumes. The pairs amid/amidst and among/amongst are the only entries here that involve a live editorial choice, and in American English the shorter form wins both times. The same choice turns up outside this file with toward/towards and round/around: American English takes toward, amid, among, and around; British English takes towards, amidst, amongst, and (in some senses) round. An American who reaches for the -s form is imitating British prose, whether or not he knows it.

Modern writers use it as though it were "with all," a vague intensifier meaning something like "altogether" — it is not. It is either a postposed with, its object already gone by, or an adverb meaning "besides, moreover" or "nevertheless." Compare therewith, wherewith, and herewith, which are the same preposition fused to a pointing word instead of stranded, and note the parallel with modern stranding: the tools he worked with is the living construction that replaced it. Variants: wherewithal is the same element, now a noun meaning "the means"; therewith, herewith, wherewith are the fused relatives

A survival of the Old English habit of letting a preposition follow its object. Its three uses shade into one another, and only the first is genuinely prepositional.

1. Postpositive preposition: with, its object already stated earlier in the clause. He had no weapon to defend himself withal. 2. Adverb: besides, moreover, in addition. The room was small, and cold withal. 3. Adverb: nevertheless, for all that. He is a hard man, and honest withal.

  1. He had no weapon to defend himself withal.
  2. She gave him money to buy bread withal.
  3. The tools he worked withal were his father's before him.
  4. The room was small, and cold withal.
  5. He is a hard man, and honest withal.
  6. She spoke plainly, and withal kindly.
  7. It was a long book, and a dull one withal.
  8. The terms were harsh, and withal the only ones on offer.

without (= outside)#

Pronunciation: /wɪˈðaʊt/ · also /wɪˈθaʊt/ · weak /wəˈðaʊt/ · with-OUT Type: compound · Register: archaic and literary; alive in one legal collocation Complement: a noun phrase naming a place or a boundary — never a clause, never a gerund. It also occurs as an adverb with no object at all, chiefly as a stage direction (a noise without). This is a different sense from the everyday privative without, which has its own entry. Pattern: without [place or boundary] · within and without [noun phrase] (the surviving legal pair) · [noise / knocking] without (adverbial stage direction) Contrast: Everything turns on this: the living without means "lacking" (without money), while this archaic one means "on the outside of" (without the walls) and is the exact opposite of within. English lost the spatial sense around 1800 and now says outside of — but the old sense is why within and without still pair off in statutes and insurance policies, and why the outside world was once the world without. The Scots preposition outwith keeps the spatial sense alive north of the border, and it is not a variant an American can borrow. Variants: the spelling is identical to the privative without — only context separates them; outwith is the living Scots equivalent, not standard American

Where you meet it: the King James Bible ("they led him without the camp"), the hymn "There is a green hill far away, without a city wall," Shakespeare's stage directions, and the statutory phrase within and without the State. Never assume a modern American reader will take it correctly — most will read it as "lacking" and be confused for a line or two.

1. Outside of; on the outer side of — the lost spatial sense, and the exact opposite of within. They led him forth without the gate. 2. In the statutory pair within and without, of a state or other jurisdiction. Business conducted within and without the state. 3. Adverb, with no object at all: outside — chiefly a stage direction.
