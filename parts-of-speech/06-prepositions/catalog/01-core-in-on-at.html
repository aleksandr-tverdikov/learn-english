The core three: in, on, at#

The three highest-frequency and highest-error prepositions in English — the container, the surface, and the point — and the whole system of time and place they divide between them.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

In, on, and at are the first prepositions a learner meets and the last ones a learner gets right. Underneath all three lies one geometric idea — a container gets in, a surface or a line gets on, a point gets at — and that idea, projected onto time, produces the ladder at 6:15 → on Tuesday → in March, and projected onto space produces at 1215 Bryden Road → on Bryden Road → in Columbus. The geometry will carry you a long way, but it will not carry you all the way: each of these three words has a dozen or more further senses (state, medium, membership, ratio, topic, rate, target) that were metaphorical once and are frozen idiom now, and a handful of cases — at night, on the weekend, in the hospital — where the pattern simply breaks and the phrase has to be memorized.

Two more facts run through all three entries. First, these are the most heavily reduced words in the language: in ordinary speech all three collapse to a schwa, so at, in, and on sound like "uht," "uhn," and "uhn," which is why learners hear a blur where a preposition should be. Second, in and on each have an adverb-particle twin (Come in, The lights are on) that looks identical and is not a preposition, while at has no adverb life at all — it always has an object, even a stranded one. The two tables in the middle of this file, TIME and PLACE, collect the full contrast and every standard exception, and a closing section gives full entries to the three compound twins — into, onto, and upon — which are built out of in and on and cannot be explained apart from them.

The three entries#

at#

Pronunciation: /æt/ · AT; weak /ət/ · uht The weak form is the normal one — at takes full stress only under contrast (I said AT the store, not IN it). Between vowels the /t/ flaps to /ɾ/ — an American quick d, not an r — which is what makes it so hard to hear: at all comes out "uh-DAWL," at eight "uh-DATE," at a time "uh-duh-TIME." Before a consonant the /t/ is unreleased or gone altogether, so at school is "uh-SKOOL" and at that point is "uh-THAT point." The one thing that never happens is loss of the whole word: at reduces to a schwa but does not delete, which is why Where are you at? still has an audible tail. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (at him, at us, at whom) · gerund (good at cooking, no good at explaining things) · wh-clause (shocked at what it cost, good at what she does) · a bare article-less noun in frozen idiom only (at work, at school, at home, at night, at war, at sea). Never a bare infinitive (good at to swim) and never a finite that-clause — surprised at that he left is impossible; you must write surprised that he left or surprised at the fact that he left. The gerund may carry its own subject (surprised at his leaving so early, annoyed at the neighbors parking there), and a wh-word is fine (at what point, at whatever price) while whether is not — it depends at whether is impossible, because that frame belongs to on. With the reaction adjectives the three patterns divide cleanly: shocked at the price takes a noun phrase, shocked that the price had doubled takes the bare that-clause with no preposition at all, and shocked by is the ordinary passive-agent alternative. Unlike in and on, at has no adverb use whatsoever: it always has an object, even when that object has moved away. Pattern: at [noun phrase] · [verb] at [noun phrase] · [adjective] at [noun phrase / verb-ing] · [noun] at [noun phrase] (a look at the numbers) · stranded: What are you looking at? · the motel we stayed at · pied-piped (formal): At which point did you notice? Contrast: The point member of the in / on / at system — see the TIME and PLACE tables for the full grid. Against in: arrive at a building or an event but arrive in a city or country; at school is where you are standing, in school is enrolled; at the store is the errand, in the store is the interior. Against on: at the corner is a meeting point, on the corner is a building's address, in the corner is the inside angle of a room. Against to: throw it at him means he is the target and may get hit, throw it to him is a pass; shout at is anger, shout to is distance. Against by in the spatial sense: at the door is the point you are stationed at and implies purpose (you are answering it, waiting at it), while by the door only says "beside," with no claim about what you are doing there. On the calendar at takes the smallest unit of the three: at 6:15 is a tick, on Tuesday a day, in March a container.

The preposition of the point. Whatever it governs is treated as dimensionless — a dot on a map, a tick on a clock, a value on a scale — and that single idea generates a spatial sense, a temporal sense, a target sense, and a rate sense. What it never does is treat its object as a space you can be inside; the moment a location has an interior worth mentioning, English switches to in.

1. A point in space. A location seen as a dot rather than an area — an intersection, a doorway, a desk, a street address. This sense also carries the at the [top / bottom / front / back / end / edge] of frame, which picks a single point out of a larger object: at the top of the page, at the bottom of the stairs, at the end of the street, at the back of the room.

  1. She's waiting at the bus stop on Fourth.
  2. Turn left at the second light.

2. A place named by what happens there. Institutions, workplaces, and events, where the activity matters and the building does not.

  1. He's at work until six.
  2. We met at a wedding in Tulsa.

3. Engaged in an activity. The article-less frame at work, at play, at prayer, at study, at lunch, at table, plus the bare at it — where the phrase names an occupation rather than a place. He's at work is ambiguous between this sense and sense 2; at work on a new draft can only be this one.

  1. The two of them were at it again by Thursday.
  2. She's been at work on the same chapter since April.

4. Where someone can be reached. Phone numbers, extensions, email addresses, handles. The @ sign is read aloud as at in an address, but the symbol was not invented for it: @ is the old commercial at of sense 12, and 12 crates @ $4 meant "at the rate of" for centuries before it meant "at this domain."

  1. You can reach me at 555-0143.
  2. Questions go to the help desk at support@example.com.

5. A clock time or an exact moment.

  1. The flight boards at 6:15.
  2. At midnight the fireworks started.

6. An age or a stage.

  1. She published her first novel at nineteen.
  2. At that point in the season, nobody expected a comeback.

7. A target. The thing an action, a look, or a weapon is aimed at. With verbs of throwing and hitting, at implies the target may be missed and the intent is often hostile.

  1. Stop staring at me.
  2. Some kid threw a rock at the window.

8. An attempt. The thing an effort is pointed at, in the noun frame a try / a shot / a stab / a crack / a go at and with the verbs guess at, work at, pick at, hint at, grasp at. Like the target sense, it leaves open whether the aim succeeded.

  1. Take a crack at the first two problems and skip the rest.
  2. Don't guess at the dosage — call the pharmacy.

9. The stimulus behind a reaction. The thing that caused a feeling, after surprised, shocked, amazed, delighted, annoyed, angry.

  1. I was shocked at how much the repair cost.
  2. She was annoyed at being left off the list.

10. Prompted by, or on a given signal. The request, order, or cue that sets something off: at your request, at his insistence, at the sound of the bell, at the first sign of trouble, at gunpoint, at your convenience.

  1. The hearing was rescheduled at the mayor's request.
  2. At the first sign of smoke, the whole floor emptied.

11. Skill or ability. The activity someone is good, bad, or hopeless at.

  1. He's hopeless at directions.
  2. She's gotten very good at saying no.

12. Rate, price, speed, or level. A reading on a scale — the commercial sense the @ sign stands for.

  1. Gas is at four dollars a gallon this week.
  2. Bake it at 350 for forty minutes.

13. Distance, range, or remove. How far off something is held, seen, or kept: at a distance, at close range, at arm's length, at a depth of, at thirty thousand feet.

  1. The photo was taken at close range.
  2. She keeps everybody at arm's length.

14. A state or condition. A closed set of article-less idioms: at war, at peace, at rest, at risk, at fault, at odds, at large, at ease, at a loss, at liberty, at issue.

  1. The two countries have been at war since spring.
  2. Half the neighborhood is at risk of flooding.

15. Fixed adverbial phrases of degree and manner. At least, at most, at best, at worst, at once, at last, at length, at random, at any rate, at all, plus the at its + superlative frame (the garden is at its best in June). These are frozen; at all appears only in negatives, questions, and conditionals.

  1. At least twenty people showed up.
  2. I didn't like the ending at all.

Stranding and position. At strands freely and naturally in American English: What are you looking at? / the motel we stayed at / That's the one she's mad at. Pied-piping is available but formal (At which point did you notice?), and it is flatly impossible after that or a zero relative — the motel that we stayed at, never the motel at that we stayed. Because at has no adverb use, it can never be left pointing at nothing: Where are you at? is common in casual speech but doubles the location that where already supplies, and edited prose drops it.

See also: in, on, to, by, in front of

in#

Pronunciation: /ɪn/ · IN; weak /ən/, frequently reduced to a syllabic /n̩/ · uhn Before /p, b, m/ it assimilates: in place is "im-place," in bed "im-bed." The vowel stays full only when in is the adverb (Come IN) or under contrast. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (in it, in them) · gerund (interested in going, succeeded in getting the permit) · wh-clause (a difference in what they charge, no say in who gets hired) · a bare noun or adjective in frozen idiom only (in short, in general, in full, in private, in public). Never a bare infinitive (interested in to go) and never a finite that-clause. The sequence in that + clause does exist (It's unusual in that it has no engine), but that is a fixed two-word subordinator meaning "insofar as" — a conjunction, not this preposition taking a clause. Pattern: in [noun phrase] · in [verb-ing] · [verb] in [noun phrase] · [noun] in [noun phrase] (a change in policy) · [adjective] in [noun phrase] (rich in iron) · stranded: the town I grew up in · This bed has been slept in · pied-piped (formal): the town in which I grew up Contrast: The container member of the in / on / at system — see the TIME and PLACE tables. Against at: in the store is inside the building, at the store is the errand; in school is enrolled, at school is present. Against on: enclosure versus surface — in the car but on the bus; in bed but on the bed. Against into: in is position and into is the crossing of the boundary — He was in the pool is not He jumped into the pool — though after ordinary verbs of motion American English uses plain in freely (jump in the pool, put it in the drawer); when the crossing itself is the news, or when the two-word reading would change the meaning, use into. Against within: with a future interval, in an hour means when the hour is up and within an hour means before it is; with a boundary, within the city limits is strictly inside a perimeter while in the city merely says where. Within is the precise, legalistic word, it never reduces to a schwa, and it has none of in's other senses — no within cash, no within Spanish, no within love. Against during: both locate an event inside a period, but during insists on the span while in merely names the container. Against inside: inside is the emphatic form that insists on the boundary and contrasts with an outside — inside the box, inside the city limits, inside an hour — while in is the neutral default that makes no such point; He's in the house is where he is, He's inside the house answers someone who thought he was in the yard. Against for in duration: after a negative or a superlative, American English reaches for in where British English keeps for — I haven't seen him in years, the best game in a decade — and both varieties still allow for. Against the adverb particle in: the preposition always has an object (Come in the house), the particle never does (Come in).

The preposition of the container. Its object is a bounded space you are inside — physically, geographically, temporally, or metaphorically — and every one of its many senses is a projection of that one image onto a different kind of boundary: a room, a country, a month, a language, a profession, a mood. It runs neck and neck with on for the most polysemous of the three, and it carries the most fixed collocations of any preposition in the language.

1. Enclosure. Inside something with walls, sides, or a lid.

  1. The scissors are in the top drawer.
  2. She waited in the car with the engine running.

2. An area or region. Cities, states, countries, and any space large enough to be inside rather than at.

  1. They lived in Cleveland for six years.
  2. There's still snow in the mountains.

3. Inside a text, an image, or a performance.

  1. It's explained in chapter four.
  2. That's her in the second row of the photo.

4. Months, seasons, years, and long stretches of time. Also the general parts of the day — in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening — though not at night.

  1. The lease is up in August.
  2. He started teaching in the nineties.

5. Time until something happens, and time taken to finish. With a future event, in an hour means after the hour has gone by; with a completed action it means the hour was enough.

  1. The bus leaves in twenty minutes.
  2. She read the whole thing in one sitting.

6. Elapsed time after a negative or a superlative. The distinctively American use: in takes over from for after not, never, hardly, and after a superlative — I haven't seen him in years, the coldest week in a decade. British English keeps for in the first frame and in is spreading there too; in American writing in is the unmarked choice.

  1. We haven't had a summer like this in twenty years.
  2. That's the first straight answer I've gotten out of him in months.

7. Membership and participation.

  1. Both her sons are in the Navy.
  2. He plays bass in a cover band.

8. A state, a condition, or a set of circumstances. In love, in trouble, in pain, in danger, in debt, in a hurry, in good shape, in ruins.

  1. They're in trouble with the county over the fence.
  2. The whole building was in ruins by the time we got there.

9. Ambient conditions. Weather, light, and temperature treated as a medium you stand inside: in the rain, in the snow, in the dark, in the sun, in the cold, in the heat, in the fog, in the wind. English says in the rain where it says under the sun only in the ornamental sense — the ordinary weather frame is always in.

  1. They stood in the rain for two hours waiting for the gates to open.
  2. Don't read in the dark.

10. Manner. How something is done: in silence, in a whisper, in haste, in detail, in this way.

  1. The kids filed out in silence.
  2. He said it in a way that ended the conversation.

11. Medium, material, or form. Languages, substances, currencies, and formats.

  1. The contract was drafted in Spanish.
  2. He pays in cash and keeps every receipt.

12. Clothing. What someone has on.

  1. The man in the gray coat never sat down.
  2. She showed up in jeans and a blazer.

13. Proportion and ratio.

  1. One in five households lost power.
  2. The odds are about one in a thousand.

14. A field or a line of work.

  1. She has a degree in microbiology.
  2. He's been in insurance his whole career.

15. The respect in which something is true. The dimension a statement applies to: six feet in length, rich in iron, an increase in rent, similar in tone, differ in appearance.

  1. The room is about twelve feet in width.
  2. There's been a sharp increase in applications.

16. Arrangement. The shape or grouping something takes.

  1. Line up in twos, please.
  2. Cut the sandwich in half.

17. In + -ing: in the very act of. Formal, and close in force to by or when.

  1. In signing the waiver, you give up the right to sue.
  2. In trying to fix the leak, he cracked the pipe.

18. The domain of a superlative or a ranking. The field a "best," "biggest," or "only" is measured across. English uses in here where many languages reach for of. With a countable group or a time span, of is available too (the best of the four, the best movie of the year), but with a place or an institution in is the only choice: the tallest building in Ohio, never the tallest building of Ohio.

  1. It's the oldest bar in Cincinnati.
  2. She was the only sophomore in the entire program to place.

19. Fixed adverbial and discourse phrases. A large closed set that behaves as single words: in fact, in general, in short, in particular, in addition, in other words, in any case, in effect, in the meantime, in the first place, in my opinion, in a sense, in return, in vain, in person, in charge. Most take no article and resist modification — in fact cannot become in an important fact.

  1. In fact, the second estimate was lower than the first.
  2. She delivered the paperwork in person rather than mailing it.

Stranding and position. In strands freely: the town I grew up in, What state was she born in?, This bed has been slept in. Pied-piping (the town in which I grew up) is available and formal. Keep the preposition apart from its adverb-particle twin, and use the object test to tell them apart: the preposition has an object, the adverb does not. Come in (no object — adverb) versus Come in the house (object — preposition); Fill it in versus Fill it in the box; Get in versus Get in the car; The tide is in, Breathe in, Hand it in, Drop in sometime — all adverbs, all objectless. American English also uses in as an informal adjective (the in crowd, plaid is in this year), as a noun (He has an in with the county), and inside the fixed sequence in that as a subordinating conjunction (It's unusual in that it has no engine). None of those is the preposition.

on#

Pronunciation: /ɑn/ · AHN — /ɔn/ · AWN for the many American speakers who keep the cot / caught distinction; weak /ən/ · uhn On resists reduction slightly more than in and at, because it so often carries contrastive stress (ON the table, not under it), but in ordinary running speech on the is simply /ənðə/. Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (on it, on them) · gerund (insist on paying, on arriving) · wh-clause (it depends on what she says, a guide on how to file). Never a bare infinitive (insist on to pay) and never a finite that-clause — depends on that he comes is impossible. Use whether (depends on whether he comes) or insert a noun: on the grounds that, on the assumption that, on the condition that. Pattern: on [noun phrase] · on [verb-ing] · [verb] on [noun phrase] · [noun] on [noun phrase] (a tax on imports, a book on ethics) · stranded: What are you working on? · the shelf it was on · pied-piped (formal): the assumption on which the whole plan rests Contrast: The surface member of the in / on / at system — see the TIME and PLACE tables. Against in: surface versus enclosure — on the bus, the train, the plane (you can stand up in it) but in the car, the cab, a canoe (you sit down inside it). Against at: on the corner is a building located at an intersection, at the corner is the meeting point itself. Against onto: position versus the act of crossing onto a surface — The cat was on the roof is not The cat jumped onto the roof — though American English uses plain on freely with motion verbs (get on the bus, put it on the shelf). Against the two-word on to: keep the words apart whenever on belongs to the verb — hold on to the railing, move on to the next item, log on to the portal — and write one word only where "to a position on top of" fits (climb onto the railing). Against upon: upon is the formal, ceremonial, and literary twin (once upon a time, upon arrival) and adds register but no meaning. Against about: a book on Roman history is systematic and expert; a book about Roman history is looser and more general. Against over with the weekend and holidays: on the weekend names the block as a dated unit (we're closing on the weekend), over the weekend spreads an event across it (it rained all over the weekend) — and only over works with a stretch you pass through (over Thanksgiving, over the summer). Against in for media, the split is idiomatic rather than logical: a text is a container (in the paper, in the report) but a broadcast or a network is a surface you travel over (on TV, on the radio), so the same story is in the Times and on Channel 4. Against the adverb particle on: the preposition has an object (Put your coat on the chair), the particle does not (Put your coat on).

The preposition of the surface and the line. Its object supports something, is traced along, or is the medium something travels over — and from that come contact, routes, floors, calendars, broadcasts, and the great American set of on the team, on the board, on strike, on vacation. It shares a stretch of territory with at on the calendar (on Tuesday / at noon) and with in almost everywhere in space, so most of the learner's difficulty with on is really a difficulty about which of the three to pick.

1. Contact with a surface. Resting on, stuck to, or hanging from the face of something.

  1. Your keys are on the counter.
  2. There's a smudge on the screen.

2. A line, an edge, a border, or a route. Streets, rivers, coasts, boundaries, and paths.

  1. Their place is on Lake Erie.
  2. We stopped for gas on the way back.

3. A floor, a page, a list, or a position in a layout.

  1. Radiology is on the third floor.
  2. The footnote is on page 212.

4. Aboard. Anything you can stand up in or sit astride — bus, train, plane, ship, subway, bike, horse — plus on foot.

  1. I left my umbrella on the train.
  2. She crossed the whole state on a bicycle.

5. A day or a date. Named days, calendar dates, days combined with a part of the day, and — in American English — on the weekend.

  1. The closing is on March 3rd.
  2. He works late on Thursdays.

6. At the moment of. Formal, with a noun or a gerund: on arrival, on hearing the news, on his return, on further review.

  1. On arrival, please check in at the front desk.
  2. On hearing the verdict, half the room stood up.

7. Topic. What a book, a talk, a report, or a comment is about.

  1. She wrote the standard textbook on soil chemistry.
  2. The senator refused to comment on the report.

8. Medium or device. The channel something travels over — broadcast, wire, or instrument.

  1. It was on the news all morning.
  2. I'll be on the phone for another minute.

9. Networks, platforms, and apps. The modern extension of sense 8, and now one of the highest-frequency uses of the word: on the internet, on the web, on email, on Instagram, on Zoom, on Slack, on my laptop, on the app. The platform is treated as a surface you appear on, which is why on Facebook beats in Facebook; a document, by contrast, is still a container (in the spreadsheet, in the thread).

  1. Half the class only knows her on Instagram.
  2. We'll do the walkthrough on Zoom at four.

10. Membership of a body you are picked or appointed to. Distinctively American with teams and committees.

  1. She's on the school board now.
  2. Both twins made it on the team.

11. Basis, dependence, or fuel. What something rests on, runs on, or lives on.

  1. The whole argument rests on one email.
  2. The generator runs on propane.

12. A state, a process, or an activity under way. On fire, on sale, on strike, on duty, on call, on leave, on hold, on the run, on the rise, on the mend.

  1. The nurses have been on strike for two weeks.
  2. Everything in the store is on sale through Sunday.

13. A trip, an errand, or an assignment. A journey or a posting treated as a road you are out on: on vacation, on a trip, on the road, on tour, on business, on assignment, on patrol, on a hike, on an errand, on a break.

  1. He's on the road three weeks out of every month.
  2. She's on assignment in Phoenix until the end of the quarter.

14. Medication and regimens.

  1. He's on antibiotics for another five days.
  2. She's been on a low-sodium diet since March.

15. Paying for it.

  1. Dinner's on me tonight.
  2. The first round is on the house.

16. Directed at, and often against. Taxes, attacks, restrictions, and betrayals.

  1. The council imposed a new tax on short-term rentals.
  2. Don't be too hard on yourself.

17. The person inconvenienced. Very common in American speech and almost invisible in grammars: an on phrase naming whoever suffers the event. The pronoun is not the target of the verb but the person left holding the bag — The car died on me does not mean the car died on top of me.

  1. My laptop quit on me halfway through the exam.
  2. She hung up on him twice.

18. Carried on one's person. With have, carry, and bring, the surface is the body itself.

  1. Do you have a pen on you?
  2. I never carry cash on me anymore.

19. Fixed adverbial and discourse phrases. A closed set, frozen and mostly article-less: on purpose, on average, on the whole, on the other hand, on the contrary, on balance, on time, on hand, on end, on the spot, on second thought, on the record, on the house. Like in fact and at least, these are learned whole.

  1. He did it on purpose, and everyone knew it.
  2. On the whole, the first draft held up better than we expected.

Stranding and position. On strands freely: the shelf it was on, What are you working on?, That's the option we settled on. Pied-piping is formal but common in writing (the assumption on which the plan rests). As with in, do not confuse the preposition with the adverb particle, and use the object test: the preposition has an object, the particle does not. Put your coat on (no object — particle) versus Put your coat on the chair (object — preposition); Drive on, The lights are on, later on, from then on, and so on are all objectless and therefore not prepositions. Note also the two-word on to versus the one-word onto: Hold on to the railing (particle + preposition) is not Climb onto the railing (single preposition). On is also a noun-modifying adjective in the on switch and an on year, and it is never a conjunction — on that he arrived is not English.

See also: at, in, onto, upon, of, to, about, on top of, on behalf of

TIME: at, on, in#

The principle: points get at, days and dates get on, containers of time get in. Zoom out from a clock time to a day to a month and the preposition climbs the ladder with you — at 6:15 → on Tuesday → in March → in 2027.

The exceptions you have to memorize#

CaseRule
at night — but in the morning, in the afternoon, in the eveningThe one real hole in the pattern. In the night exists, but it means one particular night: I woke up twice in the night.
on Monday morning, on the night of the 12thA named day outranks the part of the day. Once a specific day is present, on wins over in and at.
on the weekend (American) vs. at the weekend (British)The best-known transatlantic split. American English also says on weekends and over the weekend; it does not say at the weekend.
at Christmas / at Thanksgiving vs. on Christmas Day / on ThanksgivingThe holiday as a season is a point (at); the single dated day is a day (on).
in the summer of 2019 vs. on the summer solsticeA stretch takes in; a dated day takes on.
at the beginning / at the end vs. in the beginning / in the endAt the end of the movie locates a moment in the sequence; in the end means "finally, after all." The pairs line up: at first goes with at last (points in a sequence), in the beginning goes with in the end (a summing-up of the whole stretch).
on Fridays vs. on FridayThe plural is habitual (The library is closed on Mondays); the singular is one occasion (The library is closed on Monday). Every Friday takes no preposition at all.
at present, at the moment vs. in the past, in the futureThe current instant is a point; past and future are stretches.
in the future (American) vs. in future (British)Meaning "from now on," American English keeps the article — In the future, please call ahead. The article-less in future is British and reads as an error in American copy.
in years, in ages, in a decade after a negative or a superlativeI haven't seen him in years, the coldest week in a decade. This is the American default where British English says for years. Both varieties accept for; only American English makes in the unmarked choice.
in time vs. on timeIn time is soon enough, with room to spare; on time is exactly at the scheduled moment.
in ten minutes vs. within ten minutesIn means after the ten minutes have elapsed; within means before they are up. Casual American speech often lets in do both jobs, but formal writing keeps them apart.
No preposition at all: next Friday, last year, this morning, every day, all week, tomorrow, yesterday, tonightThis / that / next / last / every / all + a time noun blocks the preposition outright. Never in next week, never on last Friday.
Days with the on dropped: I'll call you Monday. We're closed Sundays.Standard in American speech and informal writing. Keep the on in formal prose.
at the age of ten vs. in his thirtiesA single age is a point; a decade of life is a container.
Fixed and unanalyzable: at once, at last, at the last minute, on the dot, on schedule, in a minute, in the nick of time, in advanceMemorize as whole phrases.

PLACE: at, on, in#

The principle: a point gets at, a surface or a line gets on, an enclosure or an area gets in. Zoom in on a map and the preposition walks back down the ladder: in the United States → in Ohio → in Columbus → on Bryden Road → at 1215 Bryden Road. The smaller and more precise the location, the likelier at.

One note on movement#

Strictly, in and on are static and into and onto mark the crossing of a boundary. American English does not enforce this after ordinary motion verbs — jump in the pool, get on the bus, put it in the drawer are all completely standard — but the compound forms remain the safer choice whenever the crossing itself is the point, and in a few cases the two-word and one-word versions mean genuinely different things: He walked into the wall (collision) is not He walked in the wall, and Hold on to the railing (verb particle + preposition) is not Climb onto the railing (one preposition).

The compound twins: into, onto, upon#

Three prepositions in this class are in and on with a second word welded on: into = in + to, onto = on + to, upon = up + on. The first two add motion — the object is not where you are but where you end up — and the third adds nothing but register. None of them has an adverb life: unlike bare in and on, all three always take an object, which is one quick way to tell Come in (adverb) from Come into the kitchen (preposition). Each is also treated in the movement family; see into, onto, and upon in Movement and direction for their place among toward, out of, and off.

into#

Pronunciation: /ˈɪn.tu/ · IN-too; weak /ˈɪn.tə/ · IN-tuh; casual /ˈɪn.ə/ · IN-uh Stress sits on the first syllable and only the to reduces; the /t/ flaps or drops between vowels, so into it comes out "IN-uh-wit" and get into a fight is "geh-DIN-uh-fight." Spoken into and spoken in to are near-identical in running speech, which is why the one-word / two-word choice has to be settled by the grammar and never by the ear. Type: compound (in + to, fused) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (into it, into them) · gerund (talked her into staying, got into arguing about money, ran into trouble finding parking) · wh-clause, chiefly in the inquiry sense (an investigation into what went wrong, I'll look into how that happened, no insight into why she left). Never a bare infinitive — talked her into to stay is impossible — and never a finite that-clause: an inquiry into that the money vanished has to become an inquiry into the fact that the money vanished or simply an inquiry into the missing money. Into has no adverb use at all; like at, it always has an object, even a stranded one. Pattern: [verb of motion] into [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] into [noun phrase] · into [verb-ing] · into [wh-clause] · stranded: the room she walked into, What have you gotten yourself into? · pied-piped (formal): the state into which the house had fallen Contrast: Against in: position versus crossing — He was in the pool is not He jumped into the pool. This is the single most useful distinction in the pair, though American English lets plain in do the motion job after everyday verbs (jump in the pool, throw it in the trash). Against the two-word in to: keep them apart whenever in belongs to the verb — turned himself in to the police (he surrendered) is not turned himself into the police (he became them); likewise came in to talk, dropped in to say hi, tuned in to the broadcast. Against onto: interior versus surface, exactly as with in and on — fell into the pool but fell onto the deck. Against to: to names a destination reached, into insists that a boundary was crossed — drove to the garage stops at it, drove into the garage ends up inside. The two-word sequence has its own entry at in to.

The general preposition of entry: motion from outside to inside, with the boundary crossed. Every other sense is that image applied to something that is not a room — a wall you hit, a state you turn into, a night you work deep into, a subject you look inside.

1. Motion to a position inside.

  1. She walked into the kitchen without knocking.
  2. Pour the batter into the pan and level it off.

2. Motion ending in collision or forced contact. The "inside" collapses to the surface itself.

  1. He backed into a mailbox on the first try.
  2. The truck slid into the guardrail.

3. Change of state, form, or language. After turn, change, translate, convert, break, grow, make.

  1. The argument turned into a shouting match.
  2. Translate the whole section into Spanish by Friday.

4. Entry into an action or an outburst. The start of an activity treated as a space you break into: break into a run, burst into tears, launch into a speech, dive into the paperwork, go into detail, get into an argument, fall into the habit. The action begins abruptly, which is why broke into a run is not began to run.

  1. He broke into a run before the light changed.
  2. She burst into tears in the middle of the toast.

5. Penetration into a period or a distance. How far in you get.

  1. They kept rehearsing into the early morning.
  2. We were three weeks into the semester before anyone noticed.

6. Inquiry or investigation. After look, inquiry, investigation, probe, research, delve.

  1. The board opened an investigation into the missing funds.
  2. I'll look into what happened with your order.

7. Persuasion or pressure. Talk someone into, push into, bully into, guilt into — always with a gerund, never an infinitive.

  1. They talked her into running for the seat.
  2. Nobody pushed him into selling the house.

8. Enthusiasm for (informal). With be, and only predicatively.

  1. He's been into woodworking since he retired.
  2. She's not into horror movies at all.

9. Chance encounter. With run and bump.

  1. I ran into my old landlord at the hardware store.
  2. We bumped into each other outside the courthouse.

10. Division (arithmetic).

  1. Four into twenty goes five times.
  2. Divide the class into groups of three.

Stranding and position. Into strands normally: the mess we got into, What did she change into? Pied-piping is formal and slightly stiff (the state into which the house had fallen). Because into has no particle twin, a stranded into is always the preposition — but watch the reverse error, splitting a real into into two words: He got into trouble, never He got in to trouble.

onto#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑn.tu/ · AHN-too; also /ˈɔn.tu/ · AWN-too for speakers who keep the cot / caught distinction; weak /ˈɑn.tə/ · AHN-tuh As with into, stress stays on the first syllable and only the to reduces, so onto and on to are distinguishable in careful speech and usually not in fast speech — which is why the spelling has to be decided by the grammar, not by the ear. Type: compound (on + to, fused) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (onto it, onto them) · wh-clause in the informal "aware of" sense only (they're onto what he's been doing, she's onto how the scheme works) — everywhere else a clause is impossible. Gerunds are marginal at best — onto winning is not idiomatic, and where a gerund is wanted writers use the two-word on to instead (moved on to fixing the roof). This is the one clear complement difference between onto and into, which takes gerunds readily. Never a bare infinitive and never a finite that-clause: onto that he was lying is impossible, and the repair is onto the fact that he was lying. Onto has no adverb use: it always has an object. Pattern: [verb of motion] onto [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] onto [noun phrase] · stranded: the ledge he climbed onto, the flash drive I copied it onto Contrast: Against on: position versus crossing — The cat was on the roof is not The cat jumped onto the roof. Against the two-word on to: this is the error American writers actually make. Test: if "to a position on top of" fits, write one word. If on belongs to the verb — hold on, move on, pass on, hang on, log on, sign on, catch on, go on — keep two: hold on to the rail (gripped it) versus climbed onto the rail (got up on it); log on to the server, not log onto the server. The one genuine gray area is be on to someone ("have figured out what they are up to"), written both ways in edited American prose with onto now slightly commoner. Against into: surface versus interior — loaded it onto the truck (on the bed) but loaded it into the truck (in the cab).

The surface counterpart of into: motion that ends with the moving thing resting on top of something. The extensions treat a drive, a bill, or a person's scheme as a surface you land on.

1. Motion to a position on a surface.

  1. She stepped onto the platform just as the doors closed.
  2. The cat leapt onto the counter the second we left.

2. Transfer onto a surface or a medium.

  1. Copy the files onto a flash drive before you wipe the laptop.
  2. Load the boxes onto the truck first.

3. Emergence or outlook onto an open space. With open, look, give, back.

  1. The kitchen door opens onto the alley.
  2. Their balcony looks onto the parking lot.

4. Adding to an existing base. Charges, additions, extensions.

  1. The hotel tacked a resort fee onto the bill.
  2. They bolted a sunroom onto the back of the house.

5. Shifting a burden or a feeling onto a person. The person becomes the surface the load lands on: offload it onto, dump it onto, palm it off onto, pin the blame onto, project his anxiety onto. This is the psychological cousin of sense 2, and it is one of the few places where onto clearly beats plain on.

  1. He projects every bad mood onto whoever's nearest.
  2. They dumped the whole reconciliation onto the new hire.

6. Aware of, having caught on to (informal).

  1. Don't bother lying — they've been onto you for weeks.
  2. I think she's onto something with that theory.

Stranding and position. Onto strands, but reluctantly (the shelf I put it onto — most speakers prefer the shelf I put it on), and pied-pipes almost never — the ledge onto which he climbed is stiff enough that writers rephrase. Note that where the surface is already established, plain on is usually the more natural American choice: put it on the shelf beats put it onto the shelf.

upon#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑn/ · uh-PAHN; also /əˈpɔn/ · uh-PAWN; the first syllable is always a schwa and never takes stress Type: compound (up + on, fused) · Register: formal, literary, or fixed-phrase — in plain modern American prose on is the default and upon is the marked choice Complement: noun phrase · pronoun in the objective case (upon it, upon them) · gerund (upon signing the contract, upon arriving at the site) — this is the one place upon genuinely beats on in modern writing · wh-clause, exactly as with on (it depends upon what the auditor finds, acted upon what he had been told, seized upon whichever number helped). Never a bare infinitive (upon to sign) and never a finite that-clause: upon that he arrived is impossible, and the clause version is when he arrived or once he had arrived. Upon has no adverb use; the objectless on in later on can never be later upon. Pattern: [verb] upon [noun phrase] · Upon [noun phrase / verb-ing], [main clause] · [noun] upon [the same noun] (row upon row) · stranded only marginally: the fate they had stumbled upon Contrast: Against on: the same meaning at a higher register. Upon is longer, heavier, and more formal, and reaching for it to sound elegant is the classic overcorrection — sit upon the couch is simply worse than sit on the couch. Reserve it for the four places it earns its keep: after certain verbs (depend upon, call upon, seize upon, act upon), in the temporal upon + gerund, in the doubling pattern (row upon row), and in frozen phrases (once upon a time). Against onto: in its motion sense upon is a near-synonym of onto with a sudden or forceful edge — armies descend upon a town, not onto it.

The elevated twin of on, useful in exactly the places where its extra weight buys something. Its most genuinely modern job is temporal: upon arrival and upon signing compress a whole clause into two words, which is why contracts, instructions, and signage are full of it.

1. On a surface (formal or literary).

  1. A heavy snow lay upon the fields.
  2. The inscription upon the stone had worn away.

2. Motion onto, often sudden or forceful.

  1. Winter descended upon the valley all at once.
  2. The dogs fell upon the scraps before anyone could stop them.

3. Immediately after, with a noun or a gerund. The contract-and-signage sense.

  1. Upon arrival, report to the front desk.
  2. Upon signing, you'll receive a copy of the agreement.

4. Discourse frames of reconsideration. A small set of sentence adverbials in which upon is the standard word and plain on sounds wrong: upon reflection, upon further review, upon closer inspection, upon consideration, upon due deliberation. (Sports broadcasting is the exception that keeps on further review.)

  1. Upon reflection, I'd rather not put my name on it.
  2. Upon closer inspection, the signature turned out to be stamped.

5. Chance encounter. With come, happen, stumble.

  1. I happened upon the letter while cleaning out the attic.
  2. They stumbled upon the wreck by accident.

6. Doubling for quantity. [Noun] upon [the same noun], repeated with no article — singular in mile upon mile, plural in thousands upon thousands.

  1. Mile upon mile of the same brown grass.
  2. Thousands upon thousands of people showed up.

7. Fixed verb and noun collocations. Depend upon, call upon, seize upon, act upon, insist upon, look upon, impose upon, prevail upon — every one of which also works with on, and most of which are commoner with it.

  1. The whole plan depends upon the county approving the variance.
  2. She was called upon to testify twice.

Stranding and position. Upon strands only reluctantly (the discovery they had stumbled upon); most writers either pied-pipe (the discovery upon which the case rests) or drop to on. Note the frozen sentence-initial Upon + gerund is the only place where upon is clearly better than on, and that once upon a time is unanalyzable.
