# Formal, legal, Latin, and archaic prepositions

> The prepositions that live outside ordinary conversation — in contracts, statutes, hymns, and Shakespeare — and the ones borrowed whole from Latin and French.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What unites this group is that **register is the meaning**. Almost none of these words says anything that a plain English preposition cannot say: *maugre* is *despite*, *'neath* is *beneath*, *under and pursuant to* is *under*, and *as per* is *per* with a syllable wasted. What they carry instead is a signal about where the sentence comes from — a deed, a statute, a pleading, a psalm — and using one outside its native habitat is the error, not any question of grammar.

Three traps run through the whole file. The first is that **a great many of these words take no object at all**, because the object is already inside them: *hereunder* means "under this document," *per annum* means "for each year," *withal* is a *with* whose object went by three words ago, and *in witness whereof* and *mutatis mutandis* govern nothing whatever. Attaching a noun phrase to any of them (✗ *hereunder this contract*) is the commonest mistake made by writers imitating legal style. The second is that **no word in this file takes a finite clause while it is working as a preposition**: ✗ *subject to the board approves it*, ✗ *without prejudice to she may appeal*, ✗ *as per we agreed*. Two entries here do take a clause — *sith* and *afore* — but only when they stop being prepositions and become conjunctions, and their entries say so. And where a verb has to follow one of the *to*-final members, that *to* is a **preposition, not the infinitive marker**, so the verb must be an *-ing* form: *subject to obtaining a permit* and *without prejudice to refiling*, never ✗ *subject to obtain a permit* or ✗ *without prejudice to refile* — the same trap that catches *look forward to seeing you*, *object to being questioned*, and *accustomed to working late*. And the third thing to hold onto is honesty about the archaic words: *betwixt*, *sith*, *maugre*, and *o'er* are not elegant alternatives you may reach for. They are quotations. The only one an American uses without knowing it is *o'er*, and only because of the national anthem.

One more thing to listen for. Several of these entries are multiword phrases built out of ordinary function words, and in running speech those words reduce exactly as they do everywhere else: *of* is /əv/ and often bare /ə/ before a consonant, so *by order of the court* comes out /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə ðə ˈkɔrt/; *to* is /tə/, so *subject to change* is /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə ˈtʃeɪndʒ/; *and* is /ən/, so *save and except* is /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/; *for* is /fɚ/, *at* is /ət/, *from* is /frəm/, and *with* is /wɪθ/ or /wəθ/ in the same unstressed way. Only the Latin tags resist reduction. *Per annum*, *inter alia*, *sine die*, and *pro hac vice* keep their full vowels, because a speaker reaching for one of them is quoting, and quotations get pronounced carefully.

## Legal and official

The register of contracts, deeds, pleadings, and posted notices. Three things run through it: doublets that say the same thing twice (*save and except*, *under and pursuant to*, *by and through*), fossils that swallowed their own object (*hereunder*, *thereunder*, *in witness whereof*), and reservations that exist to keep a right alive (*save and except*, *without prejudice to*, *subject to*). Only one word here — *subject to* — is genuinely part of ordinary American English; the rest you will read far more often than you write.

### as per

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈpɜr/ &middot; weak /əz pər/ &middot; *az-PUR*
**Type:** complex (two-word) &middot; **Register:** business and commercial; widely condemned by usage guides
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ *as per we agreed*; write *as per our agreement*), never a gerund (✗ *as per shipping it Tuesday*; write *as per the Tuesday shipping date*). Before *usual* it takes no article: *as per usual*, and the phrase is frozen — you cannot extend the pattern (✗ *as per normal*, ✗ *as per always*).
**Pattern:** *as per* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *as per* [noun phrase] &middot; *as per usual* (fixed)
**Contrast:** *As per* adds nothing whatever to bare [per](#per), and every American usage guide from Fowler forward calls it commercialese. Prefer *per*, or better still plain [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to) or *as you requested*. The one form nobody objects to is the jocular fixed phrase *as per usual*, where the redundancy is the joke.
**Variants:** *per* is the plain replacement, and *according to* the plainer one; *as per usual* is the frozen jocular phrase; *per usual* is the American clipping of it

A hybrid of English *as* and Latin *per* that grew up in nineteenth-century shipping and invoicing correspondence and never got out of it. It is not ungrammatical — only redundant, and dated in a way that makes business prose sound like a telegram.

**1. In accordance with; as specified in — the ordinary commercial use.** Shipped *as per* your instructions of the 14th.
**2. In the frozen jocular phrase *as per usual*, "as always."** He was twenty minutes late, *as per* usual.

1. Shipped *as per* your instructions of the 14th.
2. The invoice was prepared *as per* the terms of the purchase order.
3. He was twenty minutes late, *as per* usual.
4. *As per* the attached schedule, delivery begins in March.
5. The specimens were tested *as per* ASTM D638.
6. *As per* our conversation, I have enclosed the revised bid.
7. *As per* usual, the meeting ran forty minutes over and settled nothing.

**See also:** [per](#per), [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to)

### by and through

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbaɪ ən ˈθru/ &middot; weak /baɪ ən θru/ &middot; *by-and-THROO*
**Type:** complex (legal doublet) &middot; **Register:** legal — pleadings and court filings
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a representative (counsel, guardian, trustee, agent) — never a clause, never a gerund. Always set off by commas inside the party designation.
**Pattern:** [party], *by and through* [attorney, guardian, or agent], [verb].
**Contrast:** A doublet in which [by](02-core-relational.md#by) alone would carry the whole meaning; it survives because it sits in every pleading form book. It points the opposite way from [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of): *by and through* names the agent who does the acting, *on behalf of* names the principal the acting is done for. The related *by his attorney* is the older and shorter form still used in some state courts.
**Variants:** *by and through her undersigned counsel* is the fullest form; *by* alone is the plain-language replacement

The standard American pleading formula that introduces the lawyer, guardian, or fiduciary through whom a party appears in court. Open almost any civil complaint filed in the United States and it is in the first line of the body.

1. Plaintiff, *by and through* her attorneys, Ramirez & Hale LLP, alleges as follows.
2. The minor child appears *by and through* his guardian ad litem.
3. The estate acts *by and through* its personal representative.
4. Defendant, *by and through* undersigned counsel, moves to dismiss the amended complaint.
5. The tribe sued *by and through* its tribal council.
6. Notice was served *by and through* the registered agent of record.

**See also:** [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [under and pursuant to](#under-and-pursuant-to), [vice](#vice)

### by order of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈɔrdər ʌv/ &middot; weak /baɪ ˈɔrɾər əv/ &middot; fully reduced /baɪ ˈɔrɾɚ ə/ before a consonant &middot; *by-OR-der-uv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** official and legal — signage, notices, court orders
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming an authority, normally a title rather than a bare personal name — never a clause, never a gerund (✗ *by order of closing the road*).
**Pattern:** *By order of* [authority]. (standing alone as a sign or notice) &middot; [clause] *by order of* [authority].
**Contrast:** *By order of* names the issuing authority; [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to) names the instrument. A parking sign reads *By order of the Chief of Police*; a brief reads *pursuant to Municipal Code § 12-4*. Its military cousin *by direction of* signs correspondence for a commander, and *at the direction of* is the softer civilian form that reports an instruction without invoking legal force.
**Variants:** *by direction of* (military correspondence); *by authority of* (older signage)

Attributes a command to the office that issued it, and does so in a form short enough to fit on a placard. Americans meet it far more often on signs and posted notices than in running prose.

1. NO TRESPASSING — *By order of* the Chief of Police.
2. The building was closed *by order of* the county health department.
3. *By order of* the court, all filings in this matter are sealed.
4. Flags will fly at half-staff through Sunday *by order of* the governor.
5. The road remains restricted *by order of* the Forest Service.
6. Occupancy is limited to 240 persons *by order of* the fire marshal.

**See also:** [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to), [under and pursuant to](#under-and-pursuant-to), [by virtue of](11-complex-multiword.md#by-virtue-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### ex parte

**Pronunciation:** /ɛks ˈpɑrti/ &middot; also /ɛks ˈpɑrteɪ/ &middot; *eks-PAR-tee*
**Type:** complex (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal
**Complement:** as a preposition, only a party's name, and only in a case title (*Ex parte Milligan*). Everywhere else in American practice it is an adjective (*an ex parte motion*) or an adverb (*the judge ruled ex parte*) and takes no object at all. Never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *Ex parte* [name of the applying party] (case title) &middot; an *ex parte* [motion / order / communication] (attributive) &middot; [verb] *ex parte* (adverbial)
**Contrast:** Latin *ex parte* is "from a party" — one side only. Its opposite is *inter partes*, both sides participating, which belongs to the frozen family under [inter](#inter). Do not confuse it with *ex post facto* (retroactive, and constitutionally forbidden for criminal statutes) or with the commercial [ex](#ex) of *ex works*.
**Variants:** *Ex p.* in older reporters; *ex parte* is set in italics in most American style manuals but roman in the *Bluebook* when part of a case name

Marks a proceeding brought by one side without the other present, or a communication with a judge behind an opponent's back — the second being the thing lawyers are ethically forbidden to do. Americans meet it in the great habeas cases *Ex parte Milligan* and *Ex parte Young*, in temporary-restraining-order practice, and in every judicial code of conduct.

**1. In a case title: on the application of the party named, with no opponent before the court.** *Ex parte* Quirin remains the leading military-commission case.
**2. Of a proceeding or an order: heard and decided on one side only.** She applied *ex parte* for emergency custody.
**3. Of a communication: made to the judge behind the other side's back — the ethically forbidden sense.** Counsel is prohibited from any *ex parte* contact with the court.

1. The court granted an *ex parte* temporary restraining order that afternoon.
2. Counsel is prohibited from any *ex parte* communication with the presiding judge.
3. *Ex parte* Quirin remains the leading military-commission case.
4. She applied *ex parte* for emergency custody of the children.
5. The order was vacated because it had been obtained *ex parte*.
6. Patent examiners handle *ex parte* reexamination without the challenger present.
7. *Ex parte* Milligan held that civilians could not be tried by military commission while the civil courts were open.
8. The judge disclosed the *ex parte* phone call on the record the next morning.

**See also:** [inter](#inter), [ex](#ex), [pro](#pro), [in re](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-re)

### hereunder

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrˈʌndər/ &middot; flapped /ˌhɪrˈʌnər/ &middot; *heer-UN-der*
**Type:** compound (fused *here* + *under*) &middot; **Register:** legal — contract drafting
**Complement:** **none.** This word takes no object. Its object is already inside it: *here* means "this document," so *hereunder* is a complete phrase meaning "under this agreement." Writing *hereunder this contract* is an error, and so is *hereunder to Section 4*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *hereunder* &middot; [verb phrase] *hereunder* &middot; rights and obligations *hereunder*
**Contrast:** *Hereunder* points at the document you are reading; [thereunder](#thereunder) points at some other instrument just named. Mixing them up changes what a contract means. The whole family is a preposition welded to a pointing word — *hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, hereinafter, hereunder, herewith, hereinabove* — and every plain-language drafting manual in America tells you to replace each of them with *under this Agreement*, *to this Agreement*, and so on.
**Variants:** *under this Agreement* is the plain-language replacement; the *here-* family: *hereof, hereto, herein, hereby, herewith, hereinafter*

A fossil of the Old English habit of putting the object first and letting an adverb swallow it. It is the reason contract prose can refer to itself without ever naming itself, and the reason non-lawyers find contracts unreadable.

1. The Seller's obligations *hereunder* survive termination of this Agreement.
2. Any notice given *hereunder* must be in writing and sent by certified mail.
3. Payments due *hereunder* bear interest at six percent from the due date.
4. Nothing *hereunder* creates a partnership or joint venture between the parties.
5. The Licensee's rights *hereunder* are personal and non-transferable.
6. Each party shall bear its own costs incurred *hereunder*.

**See also:** [thereunder](#thereunder), [in witness whereof](#in-witness-whereof), [under and pursuant to](#under-and-pursuant-to), [under](04-movement-direction.md#under)

### in consideration of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən ʌv/ &middot; weak /ɪn kənˌsɪdəˈreɪʃən əv/ &middot; *in-kun-sid-uh-RAY-shun-uv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal in the contract sense; formal general prose in the other
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *in consideration of he paid the deposit*). For a clause you must write *in consideration of the fact that*, and you should usually write *because* instead. A verb after *of* takes the *-ing* form and nothing else: *in consideration of releasing all claims*, never ✗ *in consideration of to release all claims*.
**Pattern:** *In consideration of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in consideration of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in consideration of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Two different words wear the same coat. In contract law *consideration* is the technical name for the bargained-for exchange, and this phrase introduces it — a promise without consideration is unenforceable, so the words are doing legal work, not decorating. In ordinary formal prose it means "in view of, out of regard for," close to [in light of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-light-of). Keep it clear of *out of consideration for*, which means "out of politeness toward" and is a different idiom entirely.
**Variants:** *for and in consideration of* (the fuller conveyancing doublet); *in consideration whereof* (older)

The phrase that opens the operative clause of nearly every American contract and deed, and the hinge on which the enforceability of the whole document turns.

**1. Contract law: in exchange for; as the bargained-for price of a promise.** *In consideration of* the sum of ten dollars, Grantor conveys the following tract.
**2. General formal prose: in view of; taking account of.** The sentence was reduced *in consideration of* his cooperation.

1. *In consideration of* the sum of ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration, Grantor conveys the following described tract.
2. The release was signed *in consideration of* a lump-sum severance payment.
3. *In consideration of* the mutual covenants set forth herein, the parties agree as follows.
4. She accepted the easement *in consideration of* a one-time payment of $4,000.
5. The sentence was reduced *in consideration of* his cooperation with the government.
6. *In consideration of* her thirty years of service, the board voted her a full pension.
7. The policy was rewritten *in consideration of* the flood risk along the coast.
8. *In consideration of* releasing all claims against the estate, she received a lump sum of $250,000.

**See also:** [in exchange for](11-complex-multiword.md#in-exchange-for), [in light of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-light-of), [in view of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-view-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [subject to](#subject-to)

### in witness whereof

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈʌv/ &middot; older *wh-* /ʍɛrˈʌv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈəv/ &middot; *in-WIT-nis-wair-UV*
**Type:** complex (frozen formula) &middot; **Register:** legal — execution and testimonium clauses
**Complement:** **none.** *Whereof* already contains its object — it means "of which," pointing back at the document — so the phrase is complete as it stands. It is always followed by a comma and then a full clause, never by a noun phrase (✗ *in witness whereof this deed*).
**Pattern:** *In witness whereof*, [the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above].
**Contrast:** The *where-* family is the third leg of a three-part system: [hereunder](#hereunder) points at the document you are holding, [thereunder](#thereunder) at another one just named, and *whereof* at the thing last mentioned. Its relatives are *whereby, wherein, whereas, whereupon, wherefore, whereto*. The variant *in witness thereof* is usually a drafting slip rather than a genuine alternative — *thereof* points away from the document that is being signed, which is not what the signer means.
**Variants:** *in testimony whereof* (state seals and notarial certificates); *witness my hand and seal* (the shorter American form)

The testimonium clause — the fixed line that opens the signature block of an American contract, deed, affidavit, or gubernatorial proclamation. It is not a productive preposition at all: it is a fossil you will read a thousand times and never build a new sentence with.

1. *In witness whereof*, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
2. *In witness whereof*, I have hereunto set my hand and caused the Great Seal of the State of Ohio to be affixed.
3. *In witness whereof*, the undersigned has caused this Deed to be executed by its duly authorized officer.
4. The notary's certificate appeared immediately below the *in witness whereof* line.
5. *In witness whereof*, the Corporation has caused these presents to be signed this 3rd day of April.
6. Older forms open the clause *in testimony whereof* rather than *in witness whereof*.

**See also:** [hereunder](#hereunder), [thereunder](#thereunder), [in consideration of](#in-consideration-of), [of](02-core-relational.md#of)

### save and except

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪv ənd ɪkˈsɛpt/ &middot; weak /ˌseɪv ən ɪkˈsɛpt/ &middot; *sayv-and-ik-SEPT*
**Type:** complex (legal doublet) &middot; **Register:** legal — deeds, conveyances, oil-and-gas instruments
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. The object is the thing carved out of the grant, and it must be described with the same precision as the grant itself.
**Pattern:** [granting clause], *save and except* [noun phrase] &middot; *Save and except* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** A doublet in which both halves mean the same thing: [save](08-accompaniment-exception.md#save) is the native-English member and *except* the Latin-derived one, paired by the medieval habit of doubling a word with its Romance twin (*will and testament*, *free and clear*, *give, devise and bequeath*). Ordinary writing wants [except](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except) alone — and note the division of labor between *except* and [except for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except-for): bare *except* subtracts from a word already in the sentence (*everyone except Marcia signed*), while *except for* stands outside the whole clause and qualifies it (*Except for the roof, the house is sound*). Note also that bare *save* has a second life as a **conjunction** taking a clause (*save that he arrived late*); the doublet does not — *save and except* is a preposition and nothing else. Finally, a *save and except* clause reserves an interest to the grantor, while an *exception* to a warranty merely limits what is being warranted — Texas courts have decided a great many cases on that difference.
**Variants:** *saving and excepting* (the participial form); *less and except* (equally common in mineral deeds); *reserving unto Grantor* (the reservation, as opposed to exception, wording)

The reservation formula of American deed drafting, and above all of Texas and Oklahoma oil-and-gas conveyances, where a grantor uses it to keep the minerals while selling the surface. Read one mineral deed and you will meet it in the first paragraph.

1. Grantor conveys the surface estate, *save and except* all oil, gas, and other minerals in and under said land.
2. The lease covers the entire section, *save and except* forty acres surrounding the homestead.
3. All fixtures pass with the sale, *save and except* the walk-in cooler and the display cases.
4. *Save and except* the easement described in Exhibit B, the tract is unencumbered.
5. The assignment transfers every well on the lease, *save and except* the Jones No. 2.
6. Grantor conveys all of Lot 7, *save and except* that portion previously dedicated to the county for road purposes.

**See also:** [save](08-accompaniment-exception.md#save), [except](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except), [excepting](12-marginal-participial.md#excepting), [with the exception of](11-complex-multiword.md#with-the-exception-of), [subject to](#subject-to)

### subject to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tu/ &middot; weak /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə/ &middot; *SUB-jikt-too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal and legal, but fully current in general American English
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *subject to the board approves it*; write *subject to the board's approval* or *subject to approval by the board*). **The *to* is a preposition here, not the infinitive marker**, so a verb after it must be an *-ing* form: *subject to obtaining a permit*, never ✗ *subject to obtain a permit* — the same trap as *look forward to seeing you*, *object to being questioned*, and *accustomed to working late*. The gerund may carry its own subject in the possessive or objective case: *subject to the applicant's posting a bond*. Pronoun objects take the objective case: *subject to them*, never ✗ *subject to they*.
**Pattern:** *Subject to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *subject to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *subject to* [noun phrase] &middot; *subject to* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Stress separates the preposition from the verb it looks like: the preposition and adjective are *SUB-ject* /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt/, while the verb meaning "to expose someone to something" is *sub-JECT* /səbˈdʒɛkt/ (*they subjected him to a search*). Against its neighbors, *subject to* makes the main event conditional **and revocable** — the condition can still undo it later — where *contingent on* and *dependent on* merely make it conditional up front, and [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to) makes it authorized. *Subject to change* and *subject to availability* are fixed phrases.
**Variants:** *contingent on* and *dependent on* are the near equivalents for the conditional sense; *subject to change*, *subject to availability*, and *subject to approval* are fixed phrases; *subject thereto* is the legal-fossil form that points back at a document already named

The most productive word in this file and the only one an ordinary American uses without noticing. Three senses run through it, and they shade into one another: a condition, an authority, and an exposure.

**1. Conditional on; not final until something else happens.** The offer is *subject to* a satisfactory inspection.
**2. Under the authority, jurisdiction, or governance of.** Federal contractors are *subject to* the same disclosure rules.
**3. Liable to undergo; prone to.** The coast is *subject to* flooding every spring.

1. The offer is *subject to* a satisfactory home inspection.
2. *Subject to* shareholder approval, the merger closes in April.
3. All fares and schedules are *subject to* change without notice.
4. Federal contractors are *subject to* the same disclosure rules as agencies.
5. The territory remained *subject to* the crown for another century.
6. Any amount *subject to* withholding must be reported on Form 1099.
7. The coast is *subject to* flooding every spring.
8. He has been *subject to* migraines since childhood.
9. The permit is granted *subject to* the applicant posting a bond.
10. Closing is *subject to* obtaining a firm commitment from the lender by June 1.

**See also:** [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to), [without prejudice to](#without-prejudice-to), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [save and except](#save-and-except)

### thereunder

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrˈʌndər/ &middot; flapped /ˌðɛrˈʌnər/ &middot; *thair-UN-der*
**Type:** compound (fused *there* + *under*) &middot; **Register:** legal and regulatory
**Complement:** **none** — the object is incorporated. *There* points at a statute, contract, or provision already named, so *thereunder* means "under that." *Thereunder the statute* is an error.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *thereunder* &middot; [verb phrase] *thereunder* &middot; the Act and the regulations promulgated *thereunder*
**Contrast:** [Hereunder](#hereunder) points at the document you are reading; *thereunder* points at some other instrument just mentioned. Drafters confuse the two constantly, and the mistake is substantive, not stylistic — it can redirect an entire obligation. The *there-* family runs *thereof, thereto, therein, thereby, thereafter, thereunder, therewith, theretofore, thereupon*.
**Variants:** *under that Agreement* / *under the Act* are the plain-language replacements; the *there-* family: *thereof, thereto, therein, thereby, therewith*

The pointing twin of *hereunder*, and the workhorse of American regulatory citation, where the standard formula is *the Act and the regulations promulgated thereunder*.

1. The Act and the regulations promulgated *thereunder* apply to all interstate carriers.
2. The lease and the rights granted *thereunder* were assigned in 2019.
3. Any claim arising *thereunder* is barred by the statute of limitations.
4. The trust and the powers conferred *thereunder* terminate at her death.
5. He cited the treaty and two decisions rendered *thereunder*.
6. Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 promulgated *thereunder* prohibit securities fraud.

**See also:** [hereunder](#hereunder), [in witness whereof](#in-witness-whereof), [under and pursuant to](#under-and-pursuant-to), [under](04-movement-direction.md#under)

### under and pursuant to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌndər ənd pərˈsuənt tu/ &middot; weak /ˈʌndər ən pərˈsuənt tə/ &middot; *UN-der-and-per-SOO-unt-too*
**Type:** complex (legal doublet) &middot; **Register:** legal
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming an instrument or a source of authority — a statute, an agreement, an order, a section number. Never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** [clause] *under and pursuant to* [statute / agreement / order] &middot; *Under and pursuant to* [instrument], [clause].
**Contrast:** A pure doublet: [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to) already means "under." It persists because form books carry it, and it is the first phrase struck by every plain-language style guide, including the federal courts' own. Distinguish [by order of](#by-order-of), which names the issuing authority rather than the instrument, and [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), which claims conformity rather than derivation.
**Variants:** *under* alone is the plain-language replacement; *pursuant to and in accordance with* (a longer doublet of the same kind)

Derives an action from a written authority, twice over. The only reason to know it is that you will read it, not write it.

1. The sale is made *under and pursuant to* the terms of the settlement agreement.
2. Bonds issued *under and pursuant to* the Indenture rank equally with one another.
3. The trustee acts *under and pursuant to* Article VII of the trust instrument.
4. Notice was given *under and pursuant to* Section 1692g of the Act.
5. The property was sold *under and pursuant to* a decree of foreclosure entered March 4.
6. *Under and pursuant to* the authority vested in me by the Constitution, I hereby proclaim...

**See also:** [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to), [by order of](#by-order-of), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with), [thereunder](#thereunder), [hereunder](#hereunder)

### without prejudice to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˌðaʊt ˈprɛdʒədɪs tu/ &middot; also /wɪˌθaʊt/ &middot; weak *to* /tə/ &middot; *with-OUT-PREJ-uh-dis-too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *without prejudice to she may appeal*; write *without prejudice to her right to appeal*). The *to* is a preposition here too, so a verb after it takes *-ing*: *without prejudice to refiling*, never ✗ *without prejudice to refile*.
**Pattern:** *Without prejudice to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *without prejudice to* [noun phrase] &middot; dismissed *without prejudice* (adverbial, no object)
**Contrast:** The bare adverbial *without prejudice*, with no *to*, is far commoner and means something narrower: a case dismissed *without prejudice* can be refiled, one dismissed *with prejudice* cannot. British practice also uses *without prejudice* to mark settlement correspondence inadmissible; in an American court that label has no magic effect, and lawyers write *for settlement purposes only* under Rule 408. Compare the ordinary privative [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without), which merely subtracts.
**Variants:** *without prejudice* (adverbial, no object — the commoner form); *without waiving* (the same reservation in plainer words)

Reserves a right instead of surrendering it. It is the drafter's way of saying that doing this now does not cost me that later, and it is why a party can pay under protest, or settle one claim while keeping another alive.

**1. Reserving a substantive right that the present act might otherwise waive.** *Without prejudice to* its right to appeal, the company paid the penalty.
**2. In litigation: leaving a claim, a defense, or a position free to be raised again later.** The case was dismissed *without prejudice to* refiling in state court.

1. *Without prejudice to* its right to appeal, the company paid the assessed penalty.
2. The case was dismissed *without prejudice to* refiling in state court.
3. *Without prejudice to* the foregoing, Buyer may terminate on ten days' written notice.
4. She accepted the interim payment *without prejudice to* her larger claim.
5. The stipulation is *without prejudice to* any party's position on the merits.
6. Counsel appeared *without prejudice to* the pending jurisdictional objection.
7. The parties settled the fee dispute *without prejudice to* litigating the merits in the pending action.

**See also:** [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without), [subject to](#subject-to), [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), [irrespective of](11-complex-multiword.md#irrespective-of)

## Latin and borrowed

Latin and French prepositions borrowed into English, most of them locked inside a fixed phrase and unable to govern an English noun. The test to apply is whether the word can take a noun phrase you choose yourself: *per*, *versus*, *chez*, *ex*, and *cum* can, so they are living English prepositions; *ad*, *de*, *inter*, *intra*, *pro*, *sine*, *sub*, and *ultra* cannot, and survive only as tags quoted whole, each welded to the one Latin noun it was borrowed with. Watch the Latin plurals and the fixed word order — *mutatis mutandis*, never the reverse — and remember that a Latin phrase in an American document is usually a term of art with a precise meaning, not decoration.

### ad

**Pronunciation:** /æd/ &middot; weak /əd/ &middot; *AD*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal, academic, and commercial; a bound element, never a free preposition
**Complement:** only the Latin noun the phrase was borrowed with — *ad hoc*, *ad litem*, *ad valorem*, *ad interim*, *ad damnum*, *ad idem*, *ad nauseam*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *ad the committee*; write *to the committee*), and it never takes a clause or a gerund.
**Pattern:** an *ad hoc* [committee / arrangement] &middot; guardian *ad litem* &middot; *ad valorem* [tax / duty] &middot; [officer] *ad interim* &middot; the *ad damnum* clause
**Contrast:** Latin *ad* is "to, toward, for the purpose of," and it is the *ad-* of *adjacent*, *adhere*, and *administer*; English [to](02-core-relational.md#to) and [for](02-core-relational.md#for) took the whole job, so the free preposition never entered the language. Against [pro](#pro), the other Latin "for": *pro* is "on behalf of" — an attorney serves *pro bono*, for the public good — while *ad* is "for the purpose of," so a guardian *ad litem* is appointed for the lawsuit and nothing else. Watch the American stress and vowels: *ad hoc* is /æd ˈhɑk/ here, not the classical /ɑd ˈhoʊk/.
**Variants:** *ad-* as a productive English prefix (*adjoin, adjunct, advert*); *ad hoc* has become an ordinary English adjective and adverb and is no longer felt as Latin; *ad valorem* is the opposite of a *specific* duty

Latin "to, toward, for." Like *inter* and *pro* it never became a free English preposition and survives only welded to the noun it governs — but the phrases it is welded into are among the commonest Latin tags in American public life.

**1. *Ad hoc*: for this one purpose; improvised, not systematic.** The mayor named an *ad hoc* committee on flooding.
**2. *Ad litem*: for the lawsuit — of a representative appointed for the case alone.** The court appointed a guardian *ad litem* for the child.
**3. *Ad valorem*: according to value — the tax and customs sense.** Property taxes are levied *ad valorem*.
**4. *Ad interim*: for the time being; in the interval.** She served as chargé d'affaires *ad interim*.
**5. Frozen phrases of extent: *ad damnum*, *ad nauseam*, *ad infinitum*.** The *ad damnum* clause demanded ten million dollars.

1. The mayor named an *ad hoc* committee to study the flooding.
2. Scheduling is done *ad hoc*, which is why nobody knows when the truck comes.
3. The court appointed a guardian *ad litem* to represent the child's interests.
4. An administrator *ad litem* was named because the executor had a conflict.
5. County property taxes are levied *ad valorem* on the assessed value.
6. Congress replaced the specific duty with an *ad valorem* rate of six percent.
7. She served as chargé d'affaires *ad interim* for eight months.
8. The appointment was *ad interim* and lapsed when the Senate reconvened.
9. The complaint's *ad damnum* clause demanded ten million dollars.
10. Counsel repeated the point *ad nauseam* until the judge cut him off.

**See also:** [pro](#pro), [de](#de), [sub](#sub), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### chez

**Pronunciation:** /ʃeɪ/ &middot; *SHAY*
**Type:** simple (French borrowing) &middot; **Register:** literary or jocular; mildly affected in plain American prose
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a person, a family, a household, or an establishment — a bare personal name (*chez Marie*), a family name with the article (*chez the Bernsteins*), a possessive-marked noun phrase (*chez his grandparents*), or a French pronoun in the fixed forms *chez moi, chez nous, chez toi, chez lui, chez elle*. Never an English pronoun (✗ *chez me*; say *at my place*), never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *chez* [name] &middot; *chez* [moi / nous / lui / elle] &middot; *chez* [family or institution] (extended sense)
**Contrast:** English [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at) plus a possessive does exactly the same work with no accent and no wink: *at Marie's*. What *chez* adds is tone. Note the extended jocular sense "in the world of, over at," where the object is a household, a family, or an institution — *things are tense chez Wilson* — which has no one-word English equivalent.
**Variants:** *chez soi* ("at one's own home," reflexive); *chez-soi* as a noun in French, not borrowed into English

French for "at the home or establishment of," borrowed whole and never naturalized. Americans meet it on restaurant awnings (*Chez Panisse*), in society columns, and in the mock-anthropological register of magazine profiles.

**1. At the home or place of business of.** We're eating *chez* Marie tonight.
**2. In the world of; over at — of a household, a family, or an institution, and always a little arch.** Things have been tense *chez* Wilson since the election.

1. We're eating *chez* Marie tonight.
2. Dinner is at eight, *chez* nous.
3. Things have been tense *chez* Wilson since the election.
4. He spent the whole summer *chez* his grandparents outside Lyon.
5. The recipe came from a cook I met *chez* Panisse.
6. *Chez* the Bernsteins, nobody went to bed before midnight.
7. *Chez* the Roosevelts, politics was simply the family business.

**See also:** [à la](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#à-la), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### contra

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrə/ &middot; *KAHN-truh*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal, academic, and accounting; never conversational
**Complement:** in the citation use, a full case or source citation rather than an ordinary noun phrase. In the free use it is an adverb or a noun with no object at all (*two justices wrote contra*; *the pros and contras*). Never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *Contra* [citation]. (a signal introducing disagreeing authority) &middot; a *contra* [account / entry] (attributive) &middot; *pro and contra* (adverbial pair)
**Contrast:** Latin *contra* is the ancestor of English [against](04-movement-direction.md#against) and of *counter-*, and it lost to both. In American legal citation it is the strongest negative signal on a scale that runs *see* → *cf.* → *but see* → *contra*: *contra* means the cited authority flatly contradicts the proposition. In accounting, a *contra* account is one that offsets another, as accumulated depreciation offsets an asset. The Nicaraguan *Contras* took their name from Spanish *contrarrevolucionario* and have nothing to do with the preposition's ordinary use.
**Variants:** *contra-* as an English prefix (*contraindicate, contraflow, contradistinction*); *contra proferentem* (construed against the drafter)

A Latin "against" that survives in three narrow trades and nowhere else. Note that the free-standing form is not a preposition at all but an adverb, since the thing it opposes is already on the page.

**1. Citation signal, governing a full citation: the authority cited flatly contradicts the proposition just stated.** *Contra* Smith v. Jones, 410 F.3d 112.
**2. Accounting, attributive: offsetting; paired with another account and netted against it.** Accumulated depreciation is a *contra* asset account.
**3. Free-standing adverb or noun, with no object at all: on the other side; against.** Two judges of the panel wrote *contra*.

1. *Contra* Smith v. Jones, 410 F.3d 112, 118 (9th Cir. 2005).
2. Accumulated depreciation is a *contra* asset account.
3. The doctrine of *contra proferentem* construes an ambiguity against the party that drafted it.
4. Two judges of the panel wrote *contra*, and the majority did not answer them.
5. Every argument pro was met within the hour by one *contra*.
6. The brief cites four cases in support and, *contra*, none at all.
7. Allowance for doubtful accounts is a *contra* account netted against receivables.
8. *Contra* Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90, which the panel never cited.

**See also:** [against](04-movement-direction.md#against), [pro](#pro), [versus](#versus), [ex parte](#ex-parte)

### cum

**Pronunciation:** /kʊm/, /kʌm/ &middot; *KUUM (rhyming with room for most Americans); KUM also heard*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** formal or jocular in the compound-noun use; technical in finance; now largely avoided in American English
**Complement:** a single bare noun, normally hyphenated to the noun before it — never a determiner of its own (✗ *a bedroom cum a study*), never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** [noun]-*cum*-[noun] &middot; *cum* [dividend / rights / coupon] (securities)
**Contrast:** The construction fuses two roles in one object: a *kitchen-cum-dining-room* is a single room that is both at once, whereas *and* would list two separate rooms. In securities pricing *cum* is the exact opposite of [ex](#ex) — a share sold *cum dividend* carries the coming payment, one sold *ex dividend* does not. The word is a homograph of a vulgar English term, and that, plainly, is why the compound construction has all but vanished from American writing while surviving in British.
**Variants:** *cum laude, magna cum laude, summa cum laude* (frozen; not the same construction); *cum grano salis* ("with a grain of salt," now always Englished)

Latin "with, combined with." The living English use is the hyphenated compound noun naming one thing that does two jobs, and it is the only genuinely productive Latin preposition left in the language — productive, but not much used.

**1. Combining two roles in one thing.** They built a workshop-*cum*-guest-room over the garage.
**2. Securities: carrying the benefit that is about to be detached.** The stock traded *cum* dividend through Thursday.

1. They converted the garage into a workshop-*cum*-guest-room.
2. He served for years as chauffeur-*cum*-bodyguard.
3. She runs a bookshop-*cum*-café on Third Street.
4. The building is a chapel-*cum*-concert-hall with terrible acoustics.
5. The stock traded *cum* dividend through Thursday and fell on Friday.
6. Bonds purchased *cum* coupon settle at the higher price.

**See also:** [ex](#ex), [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [sine](#sine), [qua](10-topic-reference-role.md#qua)

### de

**Pronunciation:** /deɪ/ &middot; also /di/ &middot; *DAY*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal, academic, and journalistic; a bound element inside fixed phrases
**Complement:** only the Latin noun fixed to it — *de facto*, *de jure*, *de novo*, *de minimis*, *de bene esse*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *de the record*; write *from the record*), and it never takes a clause or a gerund. The *de* of French borrowings (*coup de grâce*, *de rigueur*, *de trop*) is a different word doing a different job and is not this entry.
**Pattern:** *de facto* [leader / segregation / standard] &middot; *de jure* [segregation / government] &middot; review *de novo* &middot; a *de minimis* [amount / violation]
**Contrast:** The pair to hold onto is *de facto* against *de jure* — what is actually the case against what the law says — and the whole American desegregation story turns on it, because *Brown* struck down *de jure* segregation and left *de facto* segregation standing. *De novo* is not "again" in the loose sense but "from the beginning, giving no weight to what came before," which is why *de novo* review is the least deferential standard an appellate court applies. *De minimis* is the front of *de minimis non curat lex*, "the law does not concern itself with trifles." Against [ex](#ex), the other Latin word for "from": *ex* is "out of" a source, *de* is "down from, concerning."
**Variants:** the English prefix *de-* (*deflate, derail*) comes through French and is a different element; *de rigueur*, *de trop*, *coup de grâce* are French, not Latin; *de facto* is now set roman in most American style guides

Latin "from, down from, concerning." It never became a free English preposition, but the four phrases it heads are common enough in American law and journalism that a reader meets one of them most weeks.

**1. *De facto*: in fact, whether or not by law.** English is the *de facto* language of air traffic control.
**2. *De jure*: by law; as a matter of legal right.** The statute created *de jure* segregation.
**3. *De novo*: from the beginning, with no deference to the decision below.** Questions of law are reviewed *de novo*.
**4. *De minimis*: too small for the law to take notice of.** The court called the shortfall *de minimis*.

1. English is the *de facto* language of international air traffic control.
2. After the coup he was the *de facto* head of state for three years.
3. The statute imposed *de jure* segregation, and the Court struck it down.
4. A *de jure* government may control no territory at all.
5. Questions of law are reviewed *de novo* on appeal.
6. The tax court heard the matter *de novo* and disregarded the auditor's findings.
7. The shortfall was *de minimis*, and the auditors passed over it.
8. A *de minimis* violation will not support a private right of action.
9. The bishop's deposition was taken *de bene esse* in case he did not live to testify.
10. Ownership passed *de facto* long before the deed was ever recorded.

**See also:** [ad](#ad), [ex](#ex), [sub](#sub), [ultra](#ultra), [from](02-core-relational.md#from)

### ex

**Pronunciation:** /ɛks/ &middot; *EKS*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** commercial, financial, legal, and technical
**Complement:** it depends on the sense. In the shipping and securities senses, a **bare noun with no determiner** — *ex works*, *ex dividend*, never ✗ *ex the works*. In the provenance sense the object is an ordinary noun phrase and takes its determiner normally — *ex the Sackler collection*, *ex a Distorted Humor mare*. In the frozen phrases (*ex officio*, *ex post facto*, *ex rel.*) the noun is Latin and fixed. Never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *ex* [named delivery point] (shipping) &middot; *ex* [dividend / rights / coupon] (securities) &middot; *ex* [source] (provenance and pedigree)
**Contrast:** Latin *ex* means "out of, from" and gave English *exit*, *export*, and the prefix in *ex-president*. It is the exact opposite of [cum](#cum) in securities pricing. Against plain [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of) and [from](02-core-relational.md#from), which took over the whole job: *ex* survives only where a trade has frozen it into a term of art. The noun *an ex*, meaning a former spouse, is the same word turned into a noun and is not a preposition.
**Variants:** *ex-* as a productive English prefix (*ex-wife, ex-officio, ex-directory*); *ex works, ex ship, ex quay, ex warehouse* are defined Incoterms

A Latin "from" that survives in four separate trades, each of which has hardened it into a term with a precise and non-obvious meaning.

**1. Shipping: goods taken over by the buyer at the named point, with costs from there on the buyer.** The price quoted is *ex* works, Rotterdam.
**2. Securities: without the benefit that has just been detached.** The shares go *ex* dividend on the fifteenth.
**3. Provenance and pedigree: out of a named source or dam.** A Ming bowl, *ex* the Sackler collection.
**4. Frozen legal and academic phrases.** She sits on the committee *ex officio*.

1. The price quoted is *ex* works, Rotterdam; the buyer arranges carriage.
2. Delivery is *ex* warehouse, and freight is for the account of the purchaser.
3. The shares go *ex* dividend on the fifteenth of the month.
4. It traded *ex* rights all week and the price gap confused retail buyers.
5. A Ming bowl, *ex* the Sackler collection, sold for four million.
6. The colt is by Tapit, *ex* a Distorted Humor mare.
7. The treasurer serves on every subcommittee *ex* officio.
8. The dean sits on the tenure committee *ex* officio and rarely votes.

**See also:** [cum](#cum), [de](#de), [ex parte](#ex-parte), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [out of](11-complex-multiword.md#out-of), [per](#per)

### inter

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪntər/ &middot; flapped /ˈɪnər/ &middot; *IN-ter*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal and academic; not a free preposition in English
**Complement:** only the Latin noun that the phrase fixes to it — *inter alia*, *inter se*, *inter vivos*, *inter partes*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *inter the parties*; write *between the parties*), and it never takes a clause.
**Pattern:** *inter alia* &middot; *inter se* &middot; an *inter vivos* [trust / gift / transfer] &middot; binding *inter partes*
**Contrast:** The homograph *inter* meaning "to bury" is a different word with different stress — the verb is *in-TUR* /ɪnˈtɜr/ (*they interred him on Tuesday*), the Latin preposition *IN-ter*. Against [intra](#intra): *inter-* crosses between separate things, *intra-* stays inside one, and the entire reach of federal power in America has turned on the difference between *interstate* and *intrastate* commerce. The living English descendants are the prefix *inter-* and the preposition *between*.
**Variants:** *inter-* as a productive English prefix (*interstate, international, interlock*); *inter alios* ("among other people," as against *inter alia*, "among other things")

Latin "between, among." English took the prefix and left the preposition, which now appears only inside a handful of legal tags — but those tags are common enough that any American who reads a court opinion meets *inter alia* within a page or two.

**1. *Inter alia*: among other things — by far the commonest, and always parenthetical.** The complaint alleges, *inter alia*, breach of fiduciary duty.
**2. *Inter se*: among or between themselves — of the parties' rights against one another rather than against outsiders.** The partners' rights *inter se* are governed by the agreement.
**3. *Inter vivos*: between living persons — of a gift or trust taking effect during life rather than at death.** She created an *inter vivos* trust.
**4. *Inter partes*: between the parties — binding on them and nobody else.** The ruling binds the parties *inter partes*.

1. The complaint alleges, *inter alia*, breach of fiduciary duty and unjust enrichment.
2. The partners' rights *inter se* are governed by the operating agreement, not by statute.
3. She created an *inter vivos* trust rather than leaving the property by will.
4. The ruling binds the parties *inter partes* and settles nothing for anyone else.
5. The statute lists, *inter alia*, fraud, duress, coercion, and undue influence.
6. Gifts made *inter vivos* within three years of death are pulled back into the estate.
7. Shareholders may restrict transfers *inter se* without binding anyone outside the agreement.
8. An *inter partes* review is adversarial, unlike *ex parte* reexamination.

**See also:** [intra](#intra), [ex parte](#ex-parte), [between](03-place-position.md#between), [amongst](#amongst)

### intra

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪntrə/ &middot; *IN-truh*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal and technical; a prefix in English far more often than a preposition
**Complement:** only the fixed Latin nouns *vires* and *muros*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *intra the company*; write *within the company*), and it never takes a clause or a gerund. Everywhere else in English it is the bound prefix *intra-*.
**Pattern:** *intra vires* &middot; *intra*-[noun] as a prefix (*intracompany, intrastate, intravenous, intramural*)
**Contrast:** The pair to memorize is *intra vires*, within the powers granted, against *ultra vires*, beyond them — and *ultra vires* is far the commoner of the two in American corporate law, because nobody writes an opinion about an action that was perfectly authorized. Against [inter](#inter): *interstate* commerce crosses state lines and *intrastate* commerce does not. The living English equivalent is [within](03-place-position.md#within).
**Variants:** *intra-* as a productive English prefix; *ultra vires* is the opposite term and much commoner

Latin "within, inside." It never became a free preposition in English at all; it entered as a prefix and stayed one, and the few prepositional survivals are Latin phrases quoted whole.

**1. *Intra vires*: within the powers granted — the corporate and administrative sense.** The board's action was *intra vires*.
**2. As the bound English prefix *intra-*: inside one body, state, or system.** The transaction was *intra*-group.

1. The board's action was *intra vires* and cannot be set aside on that ground.
2. Debate over whether the bylaw was *intra vires* consumed the whole afternoon.
3. The transaction was *intra*-group and was eliminated on consolidation.
4. Congress may not reach purely *intra*state activity on a commerce theory alone.
5. The drug is administered *intra*venously over four hours.
6. He played *intra*mural hockey for three years without ever leaving campus.
7. The old charter distinguished the citizens *intra muros* from the households outside the walls.

**See also:** [inter](#inter), [ultra](#ultra), [within](03-place-position.md#within), [without (= outside)](#without--outside)

### mutatis mutandis

**Pronunciation:** /mjuˈtɑtɪs mjuˈtɑndɪs/ &middot; also /mjuˈteɪtɪs mjuˈtændɪs/ &middot; *myoo-TAH-tis myoo-TAHN-dis*
**Type:** marginal (a Latin ablative absolute working as an adverbial) &middot; **Register:** legal and academic
**Complement:** **none.** It takes no object at all. It modifies a whole clause — usually one saying that a rule applies somewhere new — and it is never followed by a noun phrase or a clause of its own. Where a target has to be named, the preposition doing that work is [to](02-core-relational.md#to): *applies mutatis mutandis to the Subsidiary*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *mutatis mutandis*. &middot; [rule] applies *mutatis mutandis* to [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The nearest English is *with the necessary changes made*, or the workaday *as applicable*. Keep it clear of *ceteris paribus* ("other things being equal"), which holds variables still while you argue, and of *pari passu* ("on an equal footing"), which ranks claims against one another. It is a preposition only by courtesy, and it is in this file because the reader will meet it filed among prepositions elsewhere.
**Variants:** *mutatis mutandis* is the only form — *mutandis mutatis* is an error

Latin for "the things that must be changed having been changed." It transplants a rule into a new setting and licenses in advance the small substitutions the transplant needs — swap the party names, swap the dates, and the borrowed clause reads correctly.

1. Article 12 applies *mutatis mutandis* to each Subsidiary.
2. The procedure for appeals applies, *mutatis mutandis*, to petitions for rehearing.
3. The same argument holds *mutatis mutandis* for the second experiment.
4. Sections 3 through 7 are incorporated *mutatis mutandis* into this Schedule.
5. What was said of the Senate applies *mutatis mutandis* to the House.
6. The rules of the Superior Court govern, *mutatis mutandis*, proceedings before the magistrate.

**See also:** [pro](#pro), [inter](#inter), [subject to](#subject-to), [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with)

### per

**Pronunciation:** /pɜr/ &middot; weak /pər/ &middot; *PUR*
**Type:** simple (Latin, fully naturalized) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the distributive sense; business or formal in the "according to" sense
**Complement:** in the distributive sense, a **bare singular count noun with no article** — *per person*, *per mile*, *per year*, never *per a person*. In the "according to" sense, a full noun phrase with a determiner is normal — *per the contract*, *per your email*. Never a finite clause (✗ *per we discussed*; write *per our discussion*), never a gerund.
**Pattern:** [amount] *per* [unit] &middot; *Per* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *per* [noun phrase] &middot; [Latin noun]: *per capita, per diem, per se, per stirpes*
**Contrast:** The distributive *per* competes with the indefinite article — *sixty miles an hour*, *twice a week* — which is what Americans actually say; *per* is what the label, the invoice, and the speedometer print. In the "according to" sense it competes with [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to) and [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with) and is the shortest of the three, though some readers still hear it as businessese. [As per](#as-per) is this same word with a redundant *as* bolted on. The clipped *per usual* is an American joke made out of *as per usual*.
**Variants:** *a* / *an* is the everyday spoken equivalent in the distributive sense; *p.a.* abbreviates *per annum* on financial documents

The one Latin preposition that became ordinary English. Four senses, and they sit at very different heights of formality — the first is on every gas pump in the country, the fourth is in the probate code.

**1. For each — the distributive sense, and by far the commonest.** Tickets are forty dollars *per* person.
**2. According to; on the authority or instruction of.** *Per* your instructions, I canceled the order.
**3. By means of — an older commercial sense, now mostly confined to shipping and correspondence.** Shipped *per* certified mail on the third.
**4. Inside frozen Latin phrases, where it keeps its original force.** Federal spending *per* capita rose again.

1. Tickets are forty dollars *per* person, and children under six are free.
2. The car gets thirty-four miles *per* gallon on the highway.
3. Interest accrues at six percent *per* year until the note is paid.
4. *Per* your instructions, I canceled the standing order.
5. The report was filed *per* the court's scheduling order.
6. The samples were prepared *per* the manufacturer's protocol.
7. Shipped *per* certified mail on the third of the month.
8. Notice may be given *per* facsimile or *per* overnight courier.
9. He was twenty minutes late, *per* usual.
10. Federal spending *per* capita rose for the fourth straight year.
11. The estate passes *per* stirpes to the children of the deceased brother.
12. The witness fee is fifty dollars *per diem* plus mileage.

### per annum

**Pronunciation:** /pər ˈænəm/ &middot; rarely /pɜr ˈɑnəm/ &middot; *per-AN-um*
**Type:** complex (frozen Latin adverbial) &middot; **Register:** formal, financial, and legal
**Complement:** **none** — *annum* is already the object of *per*, so the phrase is complete. It attaches to a rate or an amount that comes before it and cannot govern anything of its own (✗ *per annum the loan*).
**Pattern:** [rate or amount] *per annum* &middot; [amount] *per annum*, [clause] &middot; interest at [rate] *per annum*
**Contrast:** Ordinary American English says *a year* or *per year*; *per annum* is what the promissory note says, and it is the wording courts look for when they compute post-judgment interest. Its siblings are *per diem* (a day — which in American usage is also a noun, the daily travel allowance), *per mensem* (a month, rare), and *per capita* (a head). Note carefully that *per annum* means "each year," not "in the first year": six percent *per annum* on a five-year note is six percent every one of those years.
**Variants:** *p.a.* is the standard abbreviation on financial documents; *per year* and *a year* are the plain equivalents; *per diem*, *per mensem*, *per capita* are the parallel forms

Fixes a rate to an annual period, in the one register where the difference between an annual rate and a total matters legally. It is a fossil of the same construction that gives *per capita* and *per diem*, and like them it takes no object of its own.

1. The note bears interest at 7.5 percent *per annum* from the date of default.
2. Rent escalates three percent *per annum* over the term of the lease.
3. The trust distributes forty thousand dollars *per annum* to each beneficiary.
4. Judgment shall bear interest at the statutory rate *per annum* until satisfied.
5. The endowment spends four percent *per annum* of a trailing three-year average.
6. Ground rent of one peppercorn *per annum* was reserved in the original grant.

**See also:** [per](#per), [as per](#as-per), [of](02-core-relational.md#of)

### pro

**Pronunciation:** /proʊ/ &middot; *PROH*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal and technical inside fixed phrases; the free word is an adverb or a noun, not a preposition
**Complement:** only the Latin noun that the phrase fixes to it — *pro bono*, *pro rata*, *pro tempore*, *pro forma*, *pro se*, *pro hac vice*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *pro the plaintiff*; write *for the plaintiff*), and it never takes a clause.
**Pattern:** *pro bono* &middot; *pro rata* &middot; *pro se* &middot; *pro tem* &middot; *the pros and cons* (nouns, not prepositions)
**Contrast:** Latin *pro* means "for, on behalf of, in proportion to" and is the *pro-* of *pronoun* and *proponent*; English [for](02-core-relational.md#for) took over its entire job, which is why the free preposition never survived. Against [contra](#contra), its Latin opposite, and note that the everyday pair is *pros and cons*, both of them nouns. Watch the American shortenings: *pro bono publico* is always cut to *pro bono*, *pro tempore* to *pro tem*, and *pro hac vice* is said *proh-hahk-VEE-chay* or *-VY-see* depending on where the speaker went to law school.
**Variants:** *pro-* as an English prefix (*pro-rate, pro-forma* as adjectives); *pro tem* is the usual clipping of *pro tempore*

A Latin "for" that never made it into free English but is preserved intact inside a set of phrases every American lawyer uses daily and every American voter has heard at least once. Six of them carry the whole word.

**1. *Pro bono* (publico): for the public good — of legal work done free.** She took the eviction case *pro bono*.
**2. *Pro rata*: in proportion; share for share.** Losses are allocated *pro rata*.
**3. *Pro tempore*, clipped *pro tem*: for the time being.** The president *pro tempore* signed the bill.
**4. *Pro forma*: as a matter of form; on assumed rather than historical figures.** The company issued *pro forma* statements.
**5. *Pro se*: on one's own behalf, without a lawyer.** He appeared *pro se*.
**6. *Pro hac vice*: for this occasion only.** Counsel was admitted *pro hac vice*.

1. She took the eviction case *pro bono*.
2. The firm asks fifty *pro bono* hours a year of every associate.
3. Losses are allocated *pro rata* among the members according to capital account.
4. Unused premium is refunded *pro rata* if the policy is canceled early.
5. He appeared *pro se* and was outmatched from the first motion.
6. Courts read *pro se* filings liberally, but the deadlines still apply.
7. The president *pro tempore* of the Senate signed the enrolled bill.
8. She chaired the meeting *pro tem* while the chair was in surgery.
9. The company issued *pro forma* financial statements reflecting the merger.
10. The vote was *pro forma*; the outcome had been settled a week earlier.
11. Out-of-state counsel was admitted *pro hac vice* for this matter only.
12. His *pro hac vice* application was denied because of the earlier sanction.

### sine

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsaɪni/ &middot; also /ˈsɪneɪ/ &middot; *SY-nee*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal and academic; no free use in English
**Complement:** only the Latin noun fixed to it — *sine die*, *sine qua non*, *sine prole*. It cannot take an English noun phrase (✗ *sine a date*; write *without a date*), and it never takes a clause.
**Pattern:** adjourn *sine die* &middot; a *sine qua non* of [noun phrase] &middot; [name] died *sine prole*
**Contrast:** Latin *sine* is "without," and its descendant through French is [sans](08-accompaniment-exception.md#sans); the plain modern word is [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without). Do not confuse it with the trigonometric *sine* /saɪn/, which comes from Latin *sinus* and is a different word, though the *sine* in *sinecure* (from *sine cura*, "without care") is genuinely this one.
**Variants:** *sine qua non* takes a plural *sine quibus non* in strict Latin, never used in English

Alive in exactly three phrases. *Sine die* ends a session with no return date fixed, which is what every American hears reported when Congress adjourns. *Sine qua non* names the condition without which the thing cannot be. *Sine prole* marks a line that ended childless, in a will or a genealogy.

**1. *Sine die*: with no day fixed for reconvening.** The legislature adjourned *sine die*.
**2. *Sine qua non*: the indispensable condition — the thing without which not.** Trust is the *sine qua non* of the relationship.
**3. *Sine prole*: without issue; childless — in wills and genealogies.** The last holder died *sine prole*.

1. The legislature adjourned *sine die* on the thirtieth of May.
2. Trust is the *sine qua non* of the attorney-client relationship.
3. The hearing was continued *sine die* and never rescheduled.
4. The baronetcy lapsed when the last holder died *sine prole*.
5. Causation is a *sine qua non* of tort liability, not the whole of it.
6. The convention rose *sine die* at four in the morning.
7. The pedigree records both sons as having died *sine prole*, and the estate passed to a cousin.

**See also:** [sans](08-accompaniment-exception.md#sans), [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without), [cum](#cum), [pro](#pro), [sub](#sub)

### sub

**Pronunciation:** /sʌb/ &middot; weak /səb/ &middot; *SUB*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal and academic; a bound element and, far more often, an English prefix
**Complement:** only the Latin noun fixed to it — *sub judice*, *sub silentio*, *sub nomine*, *sub rosa*, *sub modo*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *sub the statute*; write *under the statute*), and it never takes a clause or a gerund. Everywhere else in English it is the bound prefix *sub-*.
**Pattern:** a matter *sub judice* &middot; overruled *sub silentio* &middot; reported *sub nom.* [case name] &middot; kept *sub rosa*
**Contrast:** Latin *sub* is "under," and English [under](04-movement-direction.md#under) took the entire job — which is why *hereunder* and *thereunder* are built on the English word and not on this one. In American citation *sub nom.* is the workhorse, marking a case that changed names on the way up — as happens whenever a suit against an official in his official capacity is decided under his successor's name. *Sub silentio* is a term of criticism: it says a court abandoned a holding without admitting it. *Sub rosa* has escaped law altogether and now just means "in secret." One transatlantic warning: *sub judice* in Britain names a real reporting restriction that bars comment on a pending case, while in the United States the First Amendment leaves commentators free, so an American who says a matter is *sub judice* is declining to comment, not citing a rule.
**Variants:** *sub-* as a productive English prefix (*subcontract, sublease, subsidiary*); *sub nom.* is the standard citation abbreviation; *subpoena* is *sub poena*, "under penalty," fused into a single word

Latin "under." English kept the prefix and kept *under*, and left the bare preposition to four courtroom and citation tags — one of which, *sub rosa*, has walked out of the courthouse into ordinary speech.

**1. *Sub judice*: before the court and not yet decided.** Counsel would not discuss a matter *sub judice*.
**2. *Sub silentio*: silently; without saying so — of a holding abandoned unacknowledged.** The panel overruled its earlier decision *sub silentio*.
**3. *Sub nomine*, abbreviated *sub nom.*: under the name of, in citation.** Affirmed *sub nom.* Carrillo v. Bettendorf.
**4. *Sub rosa*: in secret; off the record.** The negotiations were conducted *sub rosa*.

1. Counsel declined to comment on a matter *sub judice*.
2. The motion is *sub judice*, and the record remains sealed until it is decided.
3. The panel overruled its earlier holding *sub silentio* and never said so.
4. Two footnotes abandon the doctrine *sub silentio*.
5. The district court's ruling was affirmed *sub nom.* Carrillo v. Bettendorf.
6. It is reported *sub nom.* In re Estate of Whitfield.
7. The negotiations were conducted *sub rosa* for eleven months.
8. He kept the arrangement *sub rosa* even from his own partners.
9. The bequest was accepted *sub modo*, subject to the conditions attached to it.

### ultra

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌltrə/ &middot; *UL-truh*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** legal; a prefix and a stand-alone adjective in English far more often than a preposition
**Complement:** only the fixed Latin noun *vires* — and, in older writing, *petita*. It cannot govern an English noun phrase (✗ *ultra its powers*; write *beyond its powers*), and it never takes a clause or a gerund. As an English word *ultra* is either a prefix (*ultraviolet*) or an adjective meaning "extreme" (*an ultra position*), and neither of those is a preposition at all.
**Pattern:** an act *ultra vires* &middot; the *ultra vires* [doctrine / defense] &middot; *ultra*-[adjective] as a prefix
**Contrast:** The pair is *ultra vires*, beyond the powers granted, against *intra vires*, within them — see [intra](#intra) — and in American corporate and administrative law *ultra vires* is far the commoner of the two, because nobody writes an opinion about an act that was perfectly authorized. Against [beyond](03-place-position.md#beyond), the living English word: *beyond* is spatial and open-ended, while *ultra vires* is a verdict. Note also that the modern corporate statutes have largely abolished the *ultra vires* defense between a company and an outsider, while it stays fully alive against a government agency.
**Variants:** *ultra-* as a productive English prefix (*ultramarine, ultrasound, ultraviolet*); stand-alone *ultra* meaning "extreme" is English, not Latin; *intra vires* is the opposite term

Latin "beyond, on the far side of." It reached English as a prefix and as a one-word adjective, and as a preposition it governs exactly one noun — but that one phrase is the standard American name for an act that exceeded the actor's authority.

**1. *Ultra vires*: beyond the powers granted — the corporate and administrative sense.** The board's guarantee was *ultra vires*.
**2. *Ultra petita*: beyond what was asked — of a court or arbitrator granting more relief than any party sought.** The award was vacated as *ultra petita*.

1. The board's guarantee was *ultra vires* and could not be enforced against the company.
2. The agency's rule was struck down as *ultra vires*.
3. Shareholders sued to enjoin the transaction as *ultra vires*.
4. Most state statutes have abolished the *ultra vires* defense in ordinary contract actions.
5. Whether the ordinance was *ultra vires* took three days of argument.
6. An act *ultra vires* is void, not merely voidable.
7. The trustees acted *ultra vires* in pledging the endowment as collateral.
8. The arbitrator's award was vacated as *ultra petita* because it granted relief no party had requested.
9. A tribunal that rewrites the contract for the parties has ruled *ultra petita*.

### versus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɜrsəs/ &middot; casual /ˈvɜrsəz/ &middot; *VUR-sus*
**Type:** simple (Latin, fully naturalized) &middot; **Register:** neutral; formal in legal citation
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *versus he stayed home*; write *versus his staying home*). Because it coordinates, the two sides should be the same kind of phrase: *speed versus accuracy*, *driving versus flying*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *versus* [noun phrase] &middot; [gerund] *versus* [gerund] &middot; [case name] *v.* [case name] &middot; [team] *vs.* [team]
**Contrast:** Three written forms, three habitats. Spelled-out *versus* belongs to running prose; *vs.* to sports, science, and everyday shorthand; *v.* to legal citation — and Americans read *v.* aloud as "versus," so *Brown v. Board* is spoken "Brown versus Board," where British lawyers say "and" in civil cases. Against [against](04-movement-direction.md#against), which needs no second term to compare; against *compared with*, which is neutral where *versus* frames the two terms as rivals.
**Variants:** *vs.* (general, sporting, scientific); *v.* (legal citation; American style keeps the period); *versus* spelled out in running prose

Latin "turned toward, against," from *vertere*. It is the most thoroughly naturalized Latin preposition in the language after *per*, and its three senses run from the courtroom out into ordinary speech.

**1. Legal citation: one party against another.** *Brown v. Board of Education* was decided in 1954.
**2. Contest: one side against another.** Michigan *vs.* Ohio State kicks off at noon.
**3. Comparison: one option weighed against another.** It is a classic case of speed *versus* accuracy.

1. *Brown v. Board of Education* was decided in 1954.
2. The docket lists *Hernandez versus* the City of Tucson for Thursday.
3. Michigan *vs.* Ohio State kicks off at noon.
4. The heavyweight bout is Ali *versus* Frazier all over again.
5. It is a classic case of speed *versus* accuracy.
6. We are still weighing renting *versus* buying.
7. The chart plots unit cost *versus* production volume.
8. Twelve percent this quarter *versus* eight percent last year.

### vice

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvaɪsi/ &middot; also /ˈvaɪs/ &middot; *VY-see*
**Type:** simple (Latin) &middot; **Register:** formal and official; rare, and now confined to military, diplomatic, and civil-service appointment notices
**Complement:** a proper name or a title naming the predecessor — never a clause, never a gerund. It is always parenthetical, set off by commas, and it never begins a sentence.
**Pattern:** [appointment], *vice* [name of the person replaced], [reason for the vacancy].
**Contrast:** Two different words are spelled alike. This one is Latin *vice*, "in the place of," the same element as in *vice president*, *vice chair*, *viceroy*, *vice admiral*, and *vice versa*; the moral *vice* /vaɪs/ — and the workshop tool, which Americans spell *vise* — are unrelated. It does not mean "on behalf of": that is [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of).
**Variants:** *vice-* fused as a prefix in *vice president, vice chair, viceroy* is pronounced VYSS; *vice versa* /ˌvaɪs ˈvɜrsə/ is the same word and keeps the older one-syllable pronunciation

## Archaic and literary

Words that dropped out of English between Chaucer and the Civil War, plus the metrical clippings that poets kept alive for the sake of the syllable count. Most of them are simply an ordinary preposition one syllable shorter — *'gainst*, *'neath*, *o'er*, *'twixt*, *'midst* — and the apostrophe is the whole story. Two are genuine traps rather than curiosities: *withal*, which is a stranded *with* and not an intensifier, and *without* in its lost spatial sense, which means the exact opposite of what a modern reader assumes. The pairs *amid/amidst* and *among/amongst* are the only entries here that involve a live editorial choice, and in American English the shorter form wins both times. The same choice turns up outside this file with *toward/towards* and *round/around*: **American English takes *toward*, *amid*, *among*, and *around*; British English takes *towards*, *amidst*, *amongst*, and (in some senses) *round*.** An American who reaches for the *-s* form is imitating British prose, whether or not he knows it.

### afore

**Pronunciation:** /əˈfɔr/ &middot; weak /əˈfɚ/ &middot; clipped /fɔr/, written *'fore* &middot; *uh-FOR*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** archaic; surviving in nautical usage, in Southern and Appalachian speech, and inside frozen legal compounds
**Complement:** noun phrase or a pronoun in the objective case (*afore me*). As a **conjunction** — a separate job — it takes a full finite clause (*afore he comes*), and that use is still heard in rural speech; the preposition itself never takes a clause.
**Pattern:** *afore* [noun phrase] &middot; *afore the mast* (nautical) &middot; the *afore*[said / mentioned] [noun] (fused, no longer a preposition)
**Contrast:** It is simply the old form of [before](05-time.md#before) — the same root with the prefix *a-* instead of *be-*, and nothing but three hundred years between them. At sea it means "toward the bow" and is the live opposite of *abaft*, which is the one place it is still ordinary technical English. In law it survives only welded into *aforesaid*, *aforementioned*, and *aforethought*, where it is no longer a preposition at all — and *malice aforethought* sits in the murder statute of every American state.
**Variants:** *'fore* is the casual respelling of the same word (*'fore the storm hit*); *aforesaid, aforementioned, aforethought* are the fused legal survivals

Where an American meets it: hymns and the King James Bible, Twain and Hardy rendering rural speech, a sailing manual, and the charging language of a homicide indictment.

**1. Earlier than; before — of time, the ordinary archaic and dialect use.** He was gone *afore* daybreak.
**2. Nautical: forward of; toward the bow — the one place it is still live technical English.** The galley lies *afore* the mast.

1. He was gone *afore* daybreak.
2. The galley lies *afore* the mast.
3. She had worked the whole field *afore* noon.
4. Get the hay in *afore* the rain.
5. *Afore* the war, this was all orchard.
6. Nothing grew on that bottom land *afore* the flood.
7. The crew berthed *afore* the bulkhead and the officers aft of it.

**See also:** [before](05-time.md#before), [ere](05-time.md#ere), [prior to](11-complex-multiword.md#prior-to), ['gainst](#gainst)

### amidst

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmɪdst/ &middot; *uh-MIDST*
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *midst*) &middot; **Register:** literary; not archaic, but the marked member of its pair
**Complement:** noun phrase — characteristically a mass noun, a plural, or an abstract noun. Never a finite clause (✗ *amidst he was speaking*; write *while he was speaking*), and never a bare gerund clause (✗ *amidst being questioned*) — though a deverbal noun with a determiner is perfectly normal, since it is a noun and not a verb form: *amidst the shouting*, *amidst the fighting*. It is a preposition only: unlike [o'er](#oer) it has no adverbial use and must have an object.
**Pattern:** *Amidst* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *amidst* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Two contrasts matter here. Against *amid*: they are one preposition with two spellings, and American English clearly prefers **amid**, with *amidst* running several times rarer and reading as deliberately literary; British usage is more evenly split, so *amidst* in an American paragraph often signals a writer imitating British prose. Against [amongst](#amongst) and *among*: *amid* takes what you cannot count — *amid the wreckage*, *amid speculation* — while *among* takes countable individuals you are one of, *among the guests*. The plain spoken equivalent of both is [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of).
**Variants:** *amid* — the ordinary American form, and the one to use; *amidst* — literary; *midst*, *'midst*, *mid* — poetic; British writing uses *amidst* far more freely than American

Places something inside a surrounding mass or a surrounding condition. The choice between *amid* and *amidst* carries no difference of meaning at all — only of register, which is exactly why the choice is worth making deliberately.

**1. In the middle of a physical mass of stuff.** *Amidst* the wreckage they found the flight recorder.
**2. In the middle of a surrounding condition, noise, or state of affairs — now the commoner of the two.** He resigned *amidst* rumors of a federal inquiry.

1. The bill passed *amidst* fierce objections from both parties.
2. She kept working *amidst* the noise of the renovation.
3. *Amidst* the wreckage they found the flight recorder intact.
4. He resigned *amidst* rumors of a federal inquiry.
5. A single oak stood *amidst* the stumps.
6. *Amidst* all the celebration, no one noticed her leave.

**See also:** [amongst](#amongst), [midst](#midst), [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of), [between](03-place-position.md#between)

### amongst

**Pronunciation:** /əˈmʌŋst/ &middot; *uh-MUNGST*
**Type:** compound (*a-* + *mong* + a parasitic *-st*) &middot; **Register:** literary; in American prose it reads as an affectation
**Complement:** a plural noun phrase, a collective noun, or a mass of individuals — never a singular count noun (✗ *amongst the tree*), never a clause, never a gerund. Pronoun objects take the objective case: *amongst us*, never ✗ *amongst we*. Like *among*, it is a preposition only — it has no adverbial life and cannot stand without an object, unlike [o'er](#oer) and [withal](#withal).
**Pattern:** *Amongst* [plural noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *amongst* [plural noun phrase] &middot; divided *amongst* [plural noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against *among*: identical in meaning, and American English prefers **among** by something like ten to one — *amongst* is the dominant form in British writing and is one of the surest tells that an American is imitating it. Against [between](03-place-position.md#between): the schoolroom rule that *between* takes two and *among* takes three or more is only half right, because *between* is correct for any number when the relation is one-to-one and reciprocal (*a treaty between the seven signatories*), while *among* views the group as an undifferentiated mass (*divided among the heirs*). Against [amidst](#amidst), which takes uncountable stuff rather than countable individuals.
**Variants:** *among* — the ordinary American form; *amongst* — literary and British; *'mongst* — poetic

Puts something inside a plurality of separate individuals, without singling any of them out. The *-st* is a late accretion of the same kind that produced *whilst* and *amidst*, and it never carried any extra meaning.

**1. In the middle of a group of separate individuals; surrounded by them.** There was a traitor *amongst* them.
**2. Shared out or distributed to the members of a group.** The prize was divided *amongst* the four finalists.

1. There was a traitor *amongst* them.
2. The prize was divided *amongst* the four finalists.
3. *Amongst* the papers she found a letter dated 1912.
4. He was happiest *amongst* strangers.
5. Rumors spread *amongst* the crew within the hour.
6. *Amongst* other things, the report faults the maintenance schedule.
7. The residue of the estate was split *amongst* the surviving cousins.

**See also:** [amidst](#amidst), [betwixt](#betwixt), [between](03-place-position.md#between), [inter](#inter)

### betwixt

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwɪkst/ &middot; *bih-TWIKST*
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** archaic; alive in American English inside one fixed phrase only
**Complement:** two coordinated noun phrases joined by *and*, or one plural noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. In the fixed phrase *betwixt and between* it takes **no object at all** and is no longer a preposition.
**Pattern:** *betwixt* [noun phrase] and [noun phrase] &middot; *betwixt* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *betwixt and between* (fixed adverbial, objectless)
**Contrast:** The archaic twin of [between](03-place-position.md#between), from the same root as *two* and *twin*, and nothing separates them but age. The single place it is still ordinary American English is *betwixt and between*, meaning "neither one thing nor the other, undecided" — and there it has stopped governing anything. Its clipped poetic form is ['twixt](#twixt).
**Variants:** *'twixt* — the clipped poetic form; *between* — the ordinary modern word; *betwixt and between* — the one live American survival

Where you meet it: Shakespeare, the King James Bible ("that there be no strife betwixt me and thee"), hymnody, and mock-antique prose. A modern American who writes *betwixt* is quoting or joking, with the one exception of the fixed phrase.

**1. Between — the archaic preposition, in all the senses of *between*.** There was little love *betwixt* the two brothers.
**2. In the fixed objectless phrase *betwixt and between*: neither one thing nor the other; undecided.** He is *betwixt and between*.

1. There was little love *betwixt* the two brothers.
2. Let there be no strife *betwixt* thee and me.
3. The cabin sat *betwixt* the river and the ridge.
4. Nothing ever came *betwixt* him and his work.
5. He is *betwixt and between* — too old for the draft, too young for the pension.
6. She has been *betwixt and between* about the move since February.

**See also:** ['twixt](#twixt), [between](03-place-position.md#between), [amongst](#amongst), [inter](#inter)

### forby

**Pronunciation:** /fɔrˈbaɪ/ &middot; also /ˈfɔrbaɪ/ &middot; *for-BY*
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** archaic and regional — Scots and northern English; effectively unknown in America
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. In the "moreover" sense it stands alone as an **adverb** with no object, usually at the end of the clause.
**Pattern:** *forby* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], and [noun phrase] *forby*. (adverbial)
**Contrast:** The two senses split cleanly and were taken over by two different English words: "besides, in addition to" went to [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides) and [in addition to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-addition-to), while the older spatial "past, close by" went to [past](04-movement-direction.md#past) and [by](02-core-relational.md#by). Having lost both jobs, *forby* had nothing left to do. And do not let the modern heir be confused with its own near-twin: [beside](03-place-position.md#beside) is spatial, "at the side of," while *besides* is additive, "in addition to" — *she sat beside him* against *nobody besides him showed up*.
**Variants:** *forbye* — the commoner Scots spelling; *forbys* in some northern texts

Where you meet it: Burns, Scott, Stevenson, Hogg, and Hardy. That is the whole list. It is here for completeness, not because any American will ever need to write it.

**1. Besides; in addition to.** He kept a dozen sheep, *forby* the cattle.
**2. Past; alongside — the older spatial sense.** She walked *forby* the kirkyard without looking in.

1. He kept a dozen sheep, *forby* the cattle.
2. There were six of us at table, *forby* the children.
3. *Forby* the rent, there was coal to be paid for.
4. She walked *forby* the kirkyard without once looking in.
5. The road runs *forby* the mill and over the burn.
6. He is a good enough fiddler, and a joiner *forby*.

**See also:** [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides), [in addition to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-addition-to), [past](04-movement-direction.md#past), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### 'gainst

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɛnst/ &middot; also /ɡeɪnst/ &middot; *GENST*
**Type:** simple (a clipped form of *against*) &middot; **Register:** poetic and archaic; it exists to save a syllable
**Complement:** noun phrase or a pronoun in the objective case — never a clause, never a gerund. Identical to [against](04-movement-direction.md#against) in every respect except length, and like *against* it is a preposition only: it has none of the adverbial use that [o'er](#oer) keeps, so it can never stand without an object.
**Pattern:** *'gainst* [noun phrase] — in verse, wherever the meter wants one syllable where *against* would give two.
**Contrast:** Not a separate word at all: a metrical contraction of [against](04-movement-direction.md#against), the first syllable elided and the apostrophe marking the loss. Set it beside its companions in the same trick — ['twixt](#twixt), [o'er](#oer), ['neath](#neath), and *e'er* for *ever*. The dialect spelling *agin* (*I'm agin it*) is a different thing: it records a pronunciation of the full word, not a contraction, and belongs to rural American speech rather than to poetry.
**Variants:** *gainst* without the apostrophe in older printings; *agin* is the dialectal respelling of full *against*, not a contraction

Where you meet it: Shakespeare, Milton, Keats, the metrical psalms, and the hymnbook. Anywhere a line had to scan and *against* was one syllable too long.

1. He set his shoulder *'gainst* the door.
2. The rain beat *'gainst* the pane the whole night through.
3. Sworn *'gainst* his own kin, he would not speak.
4. *'Gainst* such odds no army could have held the field.
5. She leaned *'gainst* the gate and waited for the light.
6. They marched *'gainst* wind and weather to the shore.

**See also:** [against](04-movement-direction.md#against), ['twixt](#twixt), [o'er](#oer), [neath](#neath)

### maugre

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɔɡər/ &middot; also /ˈmɑɡər/ &middot; *MAW-gur*
**Type:** simple (from Old French *maugré*, "ill will") &middot; **Register:** archaic; dead in every register of modern American English
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. It cannot be strengthened by *of*: ✗ *maugre of his pride*.
**Pattern:** *Maugre* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *maugre* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** It means what *despite* and [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of) mean, and it lost to both. What it had that they lack is contempt: the literal sense is "in the ill will of, in defiance of," so *maugre all thy pride* is not a neutral concession but a taunt. Compare [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), the one concessive of this vintage that survived, and survived by moving into law.
**Variants:** *mauger*, *maulgre*, *malgre* in older texts; modern French *malgré* is the same word

Where you meet it: Chaucer, Spenser's *Faerie Queene*, and Shakespeare — Olivia in *Twelfth Night* declares her love "maugre all thy pride." Nineteenth-century antiquarians revived it for a decade or two, and then it stopped for good.

1. *Maugre* all his boasting, the man never once left the harbor.
2. She loved him, *maugre* her father's rage.
3. *Maugre* the storm, the fleet stood out to sea.
4. He held the pass *maugre* every assault they made on it.
5. *Maugre* his years, the old knight rode first into the field.
6. I will have my say, *maugre* the whole council.

**See also:** [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of), [regardless of](11-complex-multiword.md#regardless-of), [without prejudice to](#without-prejudice-to)

### midst

**Pronunciation:** /mɪdst/ &middot; also /mɪtst/ &middot; *MIDST*
**Type:** simple as a preposition; a noun in its ordinary modern use &middot; **Register:** poetic as a preposition; the noun inside *in the midst of* is ordinary formal English
**Complement:** as a preposition, a noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. Note carefully that in the far commoner phrase *in the midst of*, *midst* is a **noun**, and *in* and *of* are the prepositions doing the work.
**Pattern:** *midst* [noun phrase] (poetic) &middot; *in the midst of* [noun phrase] (the ordinary construction) &middot; in our / their *midst*
**Contrast:** The bare preposition *midst* belongs to verse; prose uses *in the midst of*, a complex preposition built on the noun and parallel to [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of). Against [amidst](#amidst) and *amid*, its longer relatives — same root, same sense, different register. The shortest member of the family is bare poetic *mid* (*mid the roses*), which no one has written seriously in a century.
**Variants:** *'midst* — the commoner poetic spelling; *mid* — the shortest poetic form; *amid* and *amidst* — the prose forms; *in the midst of* — where *midst* is a noun

Where you meet it: hymns, the Book of Common Prayer ("In the midst of life we are in death"), nineteenth-century verse, and the still-current phrase *a traitor in our midst*, in which it is a noun.

**1. Poetic preposition: in the middle of; amid.** *Midst* the din no one heard the bell.
**2. Noun, inside *in the midst of* and *in our midst* — where the prepositional work is done by *in* and *of*, not by this word.** There is a traitor in our *midst*.

1. *Midst* the din no one heard the bell.
2. He stood *'midst* the ruins of his own house.
3. A stranger appeared *'midst* the dancers and was gone again.
4. There is a traitor in our *midst*.
5. In the *midst* of life we are in death.
6. She resigned in the *midst* of the investigation.

**See also:** [amidst](#amidst), [amongst](#amongst), [in the middle of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-middle-of), [neath](#neath)

### neath

**Pronunciation:** /niθ/ &middot; *NEETH*
**Type:** simple (a clipped form of *beneath*) &middot; **Register:** poetic and archaic — hymnbooks, nineteenth-century verse, and old college songs
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. It must have an object: unlike [o'er](#oer), it kept none of the adverbial use of its parent, so ✗ *the valley lay 'neath* is not English.
**Pattern:** *'neath* [noun phrase] — in verse, wherever the meter wants one syllable where *beneath* would give two.
**Contrast:** A metrical clipping of [beneath](03-place-position.md#beneath), exactly as ['gainst](#gainst) is of [against](04-movement-direction.md#against) and [o'er](#oer) is of [over](04-movement-direction.md#over). Modern prose uses [under](04-movement-direction.md#under), *beneath*, or *below*. The place an American is likeliest to run into it is a hymn ("'Neath the shadow of Thy throne") or an alma mater written in the 1890s, when the college-song idiom was stiff with these forms.
**Variants:** *'neath* with the apostrophe is the standard spelling; *neath* without it appears in later printings; *beneath* is the full form

Nothing but a shorter *beneath*. It has no meaning of its own, only a syllable count, and that is the entire reason it exists.

1. *'Neath* the shadow of the elms we sang the old songs.
2. He sleeps *'neath* the sod of his own field.
3. The valley lay *'neath* a sheet of cloud all morning.
4. *'Neath* her window he waited until dawn.
5. Buried *'neath* the church floor, the founder rests there still.
6. The colors passed *'neath* the arch to the sound of drums.

**See also:** [o'er](#oer), ['gainst](#gainst), [under](04-movement-direction.md#under), [midst](#midst)

### o'er

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr/ &middot; also disyllabic /ˈoʊər/ &middot; *OR*
**Type:** simple (a contracted form of *over*) &middot; **Register:** poetic and archaic — and yet every American hears it several times a year
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund. Like [over](04-movement-direction.md#over), it can also be an **adverb** with no object at all (*the battle o'er*), and in verse that objectless use is often postposed.
**Pattern:** *o'er* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun phrase] *o'er* (adverbial, postposed in verse)
**Contrast:** Nothing but a one-syllable [over](04-movement-direction.md#over). Where *over* would give a poet two syllables and the line needs one, *o'er* appears. Its family is the whole set of poetic elisions — *e'er* for *ever*, *ne'er* for *never*, *heav'n*, *ev'ry* — and the compound *o'erhead*. It has no separate meaning whatever, and treating it as more solemn than *over* is a misreading.
**Variants:** *oer* without the apostrophe in some older printings; *o'erhead*, *o'ercome*, *o'erthrow* in verse compounds

Where you meet it: "O'er the ramparts we watched" and "O'er the land of the free" — the national anthem alone keeps this word in the ear of the entire country, which makes it the one archaic preposition every American knows without knowing it. Beyond that, hymns, Poe, Longfellow, and the metrical Psalms.

**1. Above or across — the spatial senses of *over*, and the ones the anthem uses.** *O'er* the ramparts we watched.
**2. Throughout; all over — of sound, light, or anything that spreads across a whole area.** The bells rang out *o'er* the sleeping town.
**3. Adverb, with no object at all: over, finished — often postposed in verse.** The battle *o'er*, they buried their dead.

1. *O'er* the ramparts we watched, were so gallantly streaming.
2. The banner yet waves *o'er* the land of the free.
3. He wandered lonely *o'er* the hills that summer.
4. A shadow passed *o'er* her face and was gone.
5. The bells rang out *o'er* the sleeping town.
6. *O'er* moor and fen, *o'er* crag and torrent, lead thou me on.
7. The battle *o'er*, the field was left to the crows.
8. The long night at last was *o'er*.
9. Word of the surrender spread *o'er* the whole county before nightfall.

**See also:** [over](04-movement-direction.md#over), [neath](#neath), ['gainst](#gainst), ['twixt](#twixt)

### sith

**Pronunciation:** /sɪθ/ &middot; *SITH*
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** archaic; extinct since about 1650
**Complement:** as a preposition, a noun phrase naming a time (*sith that day*). It was far commoner as a **conjunction** taking a full finite clause (*sith you will not hear me*), and nearly every surviving example is of that kind. It never takes a gerund.
**Pattern:** *sith* [noun phrase] (preposition) &middot; *sith* [clause] (conjunction — the usual case)
**Contrast:** The ancestor of [since](05-time.md#since), which absorbed both of its jobs — the temporal "from that time onward" and the causal "seeing that" — and drove it out by about 1650. It has no connection whatever to the *Sith* of *Star Wars*, an invented name, though that is now the only place most Americans have seen the letters in that order.
**Variants:** *sithence*, *sithen*, *sithens* in Middle and early Modern English; *since* is the modern reflex

Where you meet it: Shakespeare — Claudius asks the courtiers to stay "sith nor th'exterior nor the inward man resembles that it was" — together with Spenser, Sidney, and the Elizabethan sonneteers. Nothing later than the seventeenth century.

**1. Temporal: from that time; since.** *Sith* that hour he has not spoken.
**2. Causal: seeing that; inasmuch as.** *Sith* you will not hear me, I will say no more.

1. *Sith* that hour he has not spoken a word to any of us.
2. *Sith* the death of the old king, no council has been called.
3. Much has changed *sith* our last meeting.
4. *Sith* you will not hear me, I will say no more.
5. *Sith* it must be so, let it be done quickly.
6. *Sith* he is dead, the quarrel dies with him.

**See also:** [since](05-time.md#since), [ere](05-time.md#ere), [afore](#afore), [betwixt](#betwixt)

### 'twixt

**Pronunciation:** /twɪkst/ &middot; *TWIKST*
**Type:** compound (clipped from *betwixt*) &middot; **Register:** poetic and archaic
**Complement:** two coordinated noun phrases joined by *and*, or one plural noun phrase — never a clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *'twixt* [noun phrase] and [noun phrase] &middot; *'twixt* [plural noun phrase]
**Contrast:** A metrical clipping of [betwixt](#betwixt), which is itself the archaic twin of [between](03-place-position.md#between) — three steps, then, from the ordinary word. Its companions in verse are ['gainst](#gainst), ['neath](#neath), and [o'er](#oer). Modern prose uses *between*, and the two proverbs below are the only strings most Americans have ever heard it in.
**Variants:** *twixt* without the apostrophe in later printings; *betwixt* is the full form; *between* is the modern word

Where you meet it: two proverbs — *'twixt the devil and the deep blue sea* and *there's many a slip 'twixt cup and lip* — plus Shakespeare, Milton, and the hymnbook. It has no meaning of its own beyond *between* and a shorter line.

1. He was caught *'twixt* the devil and the deep blue sea.
2. There is many a slip *'twixt* cup and lip.
3. There is little enough to choose *'twixt* the two roads.
4. He stood *'twixt* his father and the door.
5. Peace was made *'twixt* the clans that winter.
6. *'Twixt* dusk and dark the whole valley went silent.

**See also:** [betwixt](#betwixt), [between](03-place-position.md#between), ['gainst](#gainst), [o'er](#oer)

### withal

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɔl/ &middot; also /wɪˈθɔl/ &middot; *with-AWL*
**Type:** postpositive as a preposition; adverb in its commoner use &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary
**Complement:** **its object comes first and is separated from it.** *Withal* is a stranded [with](02-core-relational.md#with) whose object has already appeared earlier in the clause: *the tools he worked withal* means "the tools he worked with." It can never be followed by a noun phrase (✗ *withal a knife*) and never takes a clause. In the adverbial use it has no object at all.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] ... *withal* (postpositive preposition) &middot; [clause], [adjective] *withal* (adverb) &middot; *withal*, [clause] (adverb, sentence-initial)
**Contrast:** The single most misread word in this file. Modern writers use it as though it were "with all," a vague intensifier meaning something like "altogether" — it is not. It is either a postposed [with](02-core-relational.md#with), its object already gone by, or an adverb meaning "besides, moreover" or "nevertheless." Compare *therewith*, *wherewith*, and *herewith*, which are the same preposition fused to a pointing word instead of stranded, and note the parallel with modern stranding: *the tools he worked with* is the living construction that replaced it.
**Variants:** *wherewithal* is the same element, now a noun meaning "the means"; *therewith*, *herewith*, *wherewith* are the fused relatives

A survival of the Old English habit of letting a preposition follow its object. Its three uses shade into one another, and only the first is genuinely prepositional.

**1. Postpositive preposition: with, its object already stated earlier in the clause.** He had no weapon to defend himself *withal*.
**2. Adverb: besides, moreover, in addition.** The room was small, and cold *withal*.
**3. Adverb: nevertheless, for all that.** He is a hard man, and honest *withal*.

1. He had no weapon to defend himself *withal*.
2. She gave him money to buy bread *withal*.
3. The tools he worked *withal* were his father's before him.
4. The room was small, and cold *withal*.
5. He is a hard man, and honest *withal*.
6. She spoke plainly, and *withal* kindly.
7. It was a long book, and a dull one *withal*.
8. The terms were harsh, and *withal* the only ones on offer.

**See also:** [with](02-core-relational.md#with), [hereunder](#hereunder), [thereunder](#thereunder), [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding)

### without (= outside)

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/ &middot; also /wɪˈθaʊt/ &middot; weak /wəˈðaʊt/ &middot; *with-OUT*
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in one legal collocation
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a place or a boundary — never a clause, never a gerund. It also occurs as an **adverb** with no object at all, chiefly as a stage direction (*a noise without*). This is a different sense from the everyday privative *without*, which has its own entry.
**Pattern:** *without* [place or boundary] &middot; within and *without* [noun phrase] (the surviving legal pair) &middot; [noise / knocking] *without* (adverbial stage direction)
**Contrast:** Everything turns on this: the living [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without) means "lacking" (*without money*), while this archaic one means "on the outside of" (*without the walls*) and is the exact opposite of [within](03-place-position.md#within). English lost the spatial sense around 1800 and now says [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of) — but the old sense is why *within* and *without* still pair off in statutes and insurance policies, and why *the outside world* was once *the world without*. The Scots preposition *outwith* keeps the spatial sense alive north of the border, and it is not a variant an American can borrow.
**Variants:** the spelling is identical to the privative *without* — only context separates them; *outwith* is the living Scots equivalent, not standard American

Where you meet it: the King James Bible ("they led him without the camp"), the hymn "There is a green hill far away, without a city wall," Shakespeare's stage directions, and the statutory phrase *within and without the State*. Never assume a modern American reader will take it correctly — most will read it as "lacking" and be confused for a line or two.

**1. Outside of; on the outer side of — the lost spatial sense, and the exact opposite of *within*.** They led him forth *without* the gate.
**2. In the statutory pair *within and without*, of a state or other jurisdiction.** Business conducted within and *without* the state.
**3. Adverb, with no object at all: outside — chiefly a stage direction.** *A noise without.*

1. There is a green hill far away, *without* a city wall.
2. They led him forth *without* the gate and stoned him there.
3. The suburbs lay *without* the old walls.
4. He waited *without* the door for the better part of an hour.
5. Business conducted within and *without* the state is taxed at the same rate.
6. All the ground *without* the fortifications had been cleared for a mile.
7. The stage direction reads simply *A noise without*, and the whole scene turns on it.
8. Income earned within and *without* the United States is reported on the same return.
9. The scene opens with drums *without* and the alarm bell ringing.

**See also:** [without](08-accompaniment-exception.md#without), [within](03-place-position.md#within), [outside of](11-complex-multiword.md#outside-of), [sine](#sine), [intra](#intra)
