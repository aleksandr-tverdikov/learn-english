# The core three: in, on, at

> The three highest-frequency and highest-error prepositions in English — the container, the surface, and the point — and the whole system of time and place they divide between them.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

*In*, *on*, and *at* are the first prepositions a learner meets and the last ones a learner gets right. Underneath all three lies one geometric idea — **a container gets *in*, a surface or a line gets *on*, a point gets *at*** — and that idea, projected onto time, produces the ladder *at 6:15* → *on Tuesday* → *in March*, and projected onto space produces *at 1215 Bryden Road* → *on Bryden Road* → *in Columbus*. The geometry will carry you a long way, but it will not carry you all the way: each of these three words has a dozen or more further senses (state, medium, membership, ratio, topic, rate, target) that were metaphorical once and are frozen idiom now, and a handful of cases — *at night*, *on the weekend*, *in the hospital* — where the pattern simply breaks and the phrase has to be memorized.

Two more facts run through all three entries. First, **these are the most heavily reduced words in the language**: in ordinary speech all three collapse to a schwa, so *at*, *in*, and *on* sound like "uht," "uhn," and "uhn," which is why learners hear a blur where a preposition should be. Second, *in* and *on* each have an adverb-particle twin (*Come in*, *The lights are on*) that looks identical and is not a preposition, while *at* has no adverb life at all — it always has an object, even a stranded one. The two tables in the middle of this file, [TIME](#time-at-on-in) and [PLACE](#place-at-on-in), collect the full contrast and every standard exception, and a closing section gives full entries to the three compound twins — [into](#into), [onto](#onto), and [upon](#upon) — which are built out of *in* and *on* and cannot be explained apart from them.

## The three entries

### at

**Pronunciation:** /æt/ &middot; *AT*; weak /ət/ &middot; *uht*
The weak form is the normal one — *at* takes full stress only under contrast (*I said AT the store, not IN it*). Between vowels the /t/ flaps to /ɾ/ — an American quick *d*, not an *r* — which is what makes it so hard to hear: *at all* comes out "uh-DAWL," *at eight* "uh-DATE," *at a time* "uh-duh-TIME." Before a consonant the /t/ is unreleased or gone altogether, so *at school* is "uh-SKOOL" and *at that point* is "uh-THAT point." The one thing that never happens is loss of the whole word: *at* reduces to a schwa but does not delete, which is why *Where are you at?* still has an audible tail.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*at him*, *at us*, *at whom*) &middot; gerund (*good at cooking*, *no good at explaining things*) &middot; *wh*-clause (*shocked at what it cost*, *good at what she does*) &middot; a bare article-less noun in frozen idiom only (*at work*, *at school*, *at home*, *at night*, *at war*, *at sea*). **Never a bare infinitive** (*good at to swim*) and **never a finite that-clause** — *surprised at that he left* is impossible; you must write *surprised that he left* or *surprised at the fact that he left*. The gerund may carry its own subject (*surprised at his leaving so early*, *annoyed at the neighbors parking there*), and a *wh*-word is fine (*at what point*, *at whatever price*) while *whether* is not — *it depends at whether* is impossible, because that frame belongs to [on](#on). With the reaction adjectives the three patterns divide cleanly: *shocked at the price* takes a noun phrase, *shocked that the price had doubled* takes the bare *that*-clause with no preposition at all, and *shocked by* is the ordinary passive-agent alternative. Unlike *in* and *on*, *at* has **no adverb use whatsoever**: it always has an object, even when that object has moved away.
**Pattern:** *at* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *at* [noun phrase] &middot; [adjective] *at* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *at* [noun phrase] (*a look at the numbers*) &middot; stranded: *What are you looking* **at**? &middot; *the motel we stayed* **at** &middot; pied-piped (formal): *At which point did you notice?*
**Contrast:** The point member of the *in / on / at* system — see the [TIME](#time-at-on-in) and [PLACE](#place-at-on-in) tables for the full grid. Against [in](#in): *arrive at* a building or an event but *arrive in* a city or country; *at school* is where you are standing, *in school* is enrolled; *at the store* is the errand, *in the store* is the interior. Against [on](#on): *at the corner* is a meeting point, *on the corner* is a building's address, *in the corner* is the inside angle of a room. Against [to](02-core-relational.md#to): *throw it at him* means he is the target and may get hit, *throw it to him* is a pass; *shout at* is anger, *shout to* is distance. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by) in the spatial sense: *at the door* is the point you are stationed at and implies purpose (you are answering it, waiting at it), while *by the door* only says "beside," with no claim about what you are doing there. On the calendar *at* takes the smallest unit of the three: *at 6:15* is a tick, *on Tuesday* a day, *in March* a container.

The preposition of the point. Whatever it governs is treated as dimensionless — a dot on a map, a tick on a clock, a value on a scale — and that single idea generates a spatial sense, a temporal sense, a target sense, and a rate sense. What it never does is treat its object as a space you can be inside; the moment a location has an interior worth mentioning, English switches to *in*.

**1. A point in space.** A location seen as a dot rather than an area — an intersection, a doorway, a desk, a street address. This sense also carries the *at the* [top / bottom / front / back / end / edge] *of* frame, which picks a single point out of a larger object: *at the top of the page*, *at the bottom of the stairs*, *at the end of the street*, *at the back of the room*.

1. She's waiting *at* the bus stop on Fourth.
2. Turn left *at* the second light.

**2. A place named by what happens there.** Institutions, workplaces, and events, where the activity matters and the building does not.

3. He's *at* work until six.
4. We met *at* a wedding in Tulsa.

**3. Engaged in an activity.** The article-less frame *at work, at play, at prayer, at study, at lunch, at table*, plus the bare *at it* — where the phrase names an occupation rather than a place. *He's at work* is ambiguous between this sense and sense 2; *at work on a new draft* can only be this one.

5. The two of them were *at* it again by Thursday.
6. She's been *at* work on the same chapter since April.

**4. Where someone can be reached.** Phone numbers, extensions, email addresses, handles. The @ sign is read aloud as *at* in an address, but the symbol was not invented for it: @ is the old commercial *at* of sense 12, and *12 crates @ $4* meant "at the rate of" for centuries before it meant "at this domain."

7. You can reach me *at* 555-0143.
8. Questions go to the help desk *at* support@example.com.

**5. A clock time or an exact moment.**

9. The flight boards *at* 6:15.
10. *At* midnight the fireworks started.

**6. An age or a stage.**

11. She published her first novel *at* nineteen.
12. *At* that point in the season, nobody expected a comeback.

**7. A target.** The thing an action, a look, or a weapon is aimed at. With verbs of throwing and hitting, *at* implies the target may be missed and the intent is often hostile.

13. Stop staring *at* me.
14. Some kid threw a rock *at* the window.

**8. An attempt.** The thing an effort is pointed at, in the noun frame *a try / a shot / a stab / a crack / a go at* and with the verbs *guess at, work at, pick at, hint at, grasp at*. Like the target sense, it leaves open whether the aim succeeded.

15. Take a crack *at* the first two problems and skip the rest.
16. Don't guess *at* the dosage — call the pharmacy.

**9. The stimulus behind a reaction.** The thing that caused a feeling, after *surprised, shocked, amazed, delighted, annoyed, angry*.

17. I was shocked *at* how much the repair cost.
18. She was annoyed *at* being left off the list.

**10. Prompted by, or on a given signal.** The request, order, or cue that sets something off: *at your request, at his insistence, at the sound of the bell, at the first sign of trouble, at gunpoint, at your convenience*.

19. The hearing was rescheduled *at* the mayor's request.
20. *At* the first sign of smoke, the whole floor emptied.

**11. Skill or ability.** The activity someone is good, bad, or hopeless at.

21. He's hopeless *at* directions.
22. She's gotten very good *at* saying no.

**12. Rate, price, speed, or level.** A reading on a scale — the commercial sense the @ sign stands for.

23. Gas is *at* four dollars a gallon this week.
24. Bake it *at* 350 for forty minutes.

**13. Distance, range, or remove.** How far off something is held, seen, or kept: *at a distance, at close range, at arm's length, at a depth of, at thirty thousand feet*.

25. The photo was taken *at* close range.
26. She keeps everybody *at* arm's length.

**14. A state or condition.** A closed set of article-less idioms: *at war, at peace, at rest, at risk, at fault, at odds, at large, at ease, at a loss, at liberty, at issue*.

27. The two countries have been *at* war since spring.
28. Half the neighborhood is *at* risk of flooding.

**15. Fixed adverbial phrases of degree and manner.** *At least, at most, at best, at worst, at once, at last, at length, at random, at any rate, at all*, plus the *at its* + superlative frame (*the garden is at its best in June*). These are frozen; *at all* appears only in negatives, questions, and conditionals.

29. *At* least twenty people showed up.
30. I didn't like the ending *at* all.

**Stranding and position.** *At* strands freely and naturally in American English: *What are you looking* ***at***? / *the motel we stayed* ***at*** / *That's the one she's mad* ***at***. Pied-piping is available but formal (*At which point did you notice?*), and it is flatly impossible after *that* or a zero relative — *the motel that we stayed at*, never *the motel at that we stayed*. Because *at* has no adverb use, it can never be left pointing at nothing: *Where are you at?* is common in casual speech but doubles the location that *where* already supplies, and edited prose drops it.

**See also:** [in](#in), [on](#on), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of)

### in

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn/ &middot; *IN*; weak /ən/, frequently reduced to a syllabic /n̩/ &middot; *uhn*
Before /p, b, m/ it assimilates: *in place* is "im-place," *in bed* "im-bed." The vowel stays full only when *in* is the adverb (*Come IN*) or under contrast.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*in it*, *in them*) &middot; gerund (*interested in going*, *succeeded in getting the permit*) &middot; *wh*-clause (*a difference in what they charge*, *no say in who gets hired*) &middot; a bare noun or adjective in frozen idiom only (*in short, in general, in full, in private, in public*). **Never a bare infinitive** (*interested in to go*) and **never a finite that-clause**. The sequence *in that* + clause does exist (*It's unusual in that it has no engine*), but that is a fixed two-word subordinator meaning "insofar as" — a conjunction, not this preposition taking a clause.
**Pattern:** *in* [noun phrase] &middot; *in* [verb-ing] &middot; [verb] *in* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in* [noun phrase] (*a change in policy*) &middot; [adjective] *in* [noun phrase] (*rich in iron*) &middot; stranded: *the town I grew up* **in** &middot; *This bed has been slept* **in** &middot; pied-piped (formal): *the town in which I grew up*
**Contrast:** The container member of the *in / on / at* system — see the [TIME](#time-at-on-in) and [PLACE](#place-at-on-in) tables. Against [at](#at): *in the store* is inside the building, *at the store* is the errand; *in school* is enrolled, *at school* is present. Against [on](#on): enclosure versus surface — *in the car* but *on the bus*; *in bed* but *on the bed*. Against [into](#into): *in* is position and *into* is the crossing of the boundary — *He was in the pool* is not *He jumped into the pool* — though after ordinary verbs of motion American English uses plain *in* freely (*jump in the pool*, *put it in the drawer*); when the crossing itself is the news, or when the two-word reading would change the meaning, use *into*. Against [within](05-time.md#within): with a future interval, *in an hour* means when the hour is up and *within an hour* means before it is; with a boundary, *within the city limits* is strictly inside a perimeter while *in the city* merely says where. *Within* is the precise, legalistic word, it never reduces to a schwa, and it has none of *in*'s other senses — no *within cash*, no *within Spanish*, no *within love*. Against [during](12-marginal-participial.md#during): both locate an event inside a period, but *during* insists on the span while *in* merely names the container. Against [inside](03-place-position.md#inside): *inside* is the emphatic form that insists on the boundary and contrasts with an outside — *inside the box*, *inside the city limits*, *inside an hour* — while *in* is the neutral default that makes no such point; *He's in the house* is where he is, *He's inside the house* answers someone who thought he was in the yard. Against [for](02-core-relational.md#for) in duration: after a negative or a superlative, American English reaches for *in* where British English keeps *for* — *I haven't seen him in years*, *the best game in a decade* — and both varieties still allow *for*. Against the adverb particle *in*: the preposition always has an object (*Come in the house*), the particle never does (*Come in*).

The preposition of the container. Its object is a bounded space you are inside — physically, geographically, temporally, or metaphorically — and every one of its many senses is a projection of that one image onto a different kind of boundary: a room, a country, a month, a language, a profession, a mood. It runs neck and neck with *on* for the most polysemous of the three, and it carries the most fixed collocations of any preposition in the language.

**1. Enclosure.** Inside something with walls, sides, or a lid.

1. The scissors are *in* the top drawer.
2. She waited *in* the car with the engine running.

**2. An area or region.** Cities, states, countries, and any space large enough to be inside rather than at.

3. They lived *in* Cleveland for six years.
4. There's still snow *in* the mountains.

**3. Inside a text, an image, or a performance.**

5. It's explained *in* chapter four.
6. That's her *in* the second row of the photo.

**4. Months, seasons, years, and long stretches of time.** Also the general parts of the day — *in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening* — though not *at night*.

7. The lease is up *in* August.
8. He started teaching *in* the nineties.

**5. Time until something happens, and time taken to finish.** With a future event, *in an hour* means after the hour has gone by; with a completed action it means the hour was enough.

9. The bus leaves *in* twenty minutes.
10. She read the whole thing *in* one sitting.

**6. Elapsed time after a negative or a superlative.** The distinctively American use: *in* takes over from *for* after *not, never, hardly*, and after a superlative — *I haven't seen him in years*, *the coldest week in a decade*. British English keeps *for* in the first frame and *in* is spreading there too; in American writing *in* is the unmarked choice.

11. We haven't had a summer like this *in* twenty years.
12. That's the first straight answer I've gotten out of him *in* months.

**7. Membership and participation.**

13. Both her sons are *in* the Navy.
14. He plays bass *in* a cover band.

**8. A state, a condition, or a set of circumstances.** *In love, in trouble, in pain, in danger, in debt, in a hurry, in good shape, in ruins.*

15. They're *in* trouble with the county over the fence.
16. The whole building was *in* ruins by the time we got there.

**9. Ambient conditions.** Weather, light, and temperature treated as a medium you stand inside: *in the rain, in the snow, in the dark, in the sun, in the cold, in the heat, in the fog, in the wind*. English says *in the rain* where it says *under the sun* only in the ornamental sense — the ordinary weather frame is always *in*.

17. They stood *in* the rain for two hours waiting for the gates to open.
18. Don't read *in* the dark.

**10. Manner.** How something is done: *in silence, in a whisper, in haste, in detail, in this way*.

19. The kids filed out *in* silence.
20. He said it *in* a way that ended the conversation.

**11. Medium, material, or form.** Languages, substances, currencies, and formats.

21. The contract was drafted *in* Spanish.
22. He pays *in* cash and keeps every receipt.

**12. Clothing.** What someone has on.

23. The man *in* the gray coat never sat down.
24. She showed up *in* jeans and a blazer.

**13. Proportion and ratio.**

25. One *in* five households lost power.
26. The odds are about one *in* a thousand.

**14. A field or a line of work.**

27. She has a degree *in* microbiology.
28. He's been *in* insurance his whole career.

**15. The respect in which something is true.** The dimension a statement applies to: *six feet in length, rich in iron, an increase in rent, similar in tone, differ in appearance*.

29. The room is about twelve feet *in* width.
30. There's been a sharp increase *in* applications.

**16. Arrangement.** The shape or grouping something takes.

31. Line up *in* twos, please.
32. Cut the sandwich *in* half.

**17. *In* + *-ing*: in the very act of.** Formal, and close in force to *by* or *when*.

33. *In* signing the waiver, you give up the right to sue.
34. *In* trying to fix the leak, he cracked the pipe.

**18. The domain of a superlative or a ranking.** The field a "best," "biggest," or "only" is measured across. English uses *in* here where many languages reach for *of*. With a countable group or a time span, *of* is available too (*the best of the four*, *the best movie of the year*), but with a place or an institution *in* is the only choice: *the tallest building in Ohio*, never *the tallest building of Ohio*.

35. It's the oldest bar *in* Cincinnati.
36. She was the only sophomore *in* the entire program to place.

**19. Fixed adverbial and discourse phrases.** A large closed set that behaves as single words: *in fact, in general, in short, in particular, in addition, in other words, in any case, in effect, in the meantime, in the first place, in my opinion, in a sense, in return, in vain, in person, in charge*. Most take no article and resist modification — *in fact* cannot become *in an important fact*.

37. *In* fact, the second estimate was lower than the first.
38. She delivered the paperwork *in* person rather than mailing it.

**Stranding and position.** *In* strands freely: *the town I grew up* ***in***, *What state was she born* ***in***?, *This bed has been slept* ***in***. Pied-piping (*the town in which I grew up*) is available and formal. Keep the preposition apart from its adverb-particle twin, and use the object test to tell them apart: **the preposition has an object, the adverb does not.** *Come in* (no object — adverb) versus *Come in the house* (object — preposition); *Fill it in* versus *Fill it in the box*; *Get in* versus *Get in the car*; *The tide is in*, *Breathe in*, *Hand it in*, *Drop in sometime* — all adverbs, all objectless. American English also uses *in* as an informal adjective (*the in crowd*, *plaid is in this year*), as a noun (*He has an in with the county*), and inside the fixed sequence *in that* as a subordinating conjunction (*It's unusual in that it has no engine*). None of those is the preposition.

### on

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn/ &middot; *AHN* — /ɔn/ &middot; *AWN* for the many American speakers who keep the *cot / caught* distinction; weak /ən/ &middot; *uhn*
*On* resists reduction slightly more than *in* and *at*, because it so often carries contrastive stress (*ON the table, not under it*), but in ordinary running speech *on the* is simply /ənðə/.
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*on it*, *on them*) &middot; gerund (*insist on paying*, *on arriving*) &middot; *wh*-clause (*it depends on what she says*, *a guide on how to file*). **Never a bare infinitive** (*insist on to pay*) and **never a finite that-clause** — *depends on that he comes* is impossible. Use *whether* (*depends on whether he comes*) or insert a noun: *on the grounds that*, *on the assumption that*, *on the condition that*.
**Pattern:** *on* [noun phrase] &middot; *on* [verb-ing] &middot; [verb] *on* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *on* [noun phrase] (*a tax on imports*, *a book on ethics*) &middot; stranded: *What are you working* **on**? &middot; *the shelf it was* **on** &middot; pied-piped (formal): *the assumption on which the whole plan rests*
**Contrast:** The surface member of the *in / on / at* system — see the [TIME](#time-at-on-in) and [PLACE](#place-at-on-in) tables. Against [in](#in): surface versus enclosure — *on the bus, the train, the plane* (you can stand up in it) but *in the car, the cab, a canoe* (you sit down inside it). Against [at](#at): *on the corner* is a building located at an intersection, *at the corner* is the meeting point itself. Against [onto](#onto): position versus the act of crossing onto a surface — *The cat was on the roof* is not *The cat jumped onto the roof* — though American English uses plain *on* freely with motion verbs (*get on the bus*, *put it on the shelf*). Against the two-word *on to*: keep the words apart whenever *on* belongs to the verb — *hold on to the railing*, *move on to the next item*, *log on to the portal* — and write one word only where "to a position on top of" fits (*climb onto the railing*). Against [upon](#upon): *upon* is the formal, ceremonial, and literary twin (*once upon a time*, *upon arrival*) and adds register but no meaning. Against [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about): a book *on* Roman history is systematic and expert; a book *about* Roman history is looser and more general. Against [over](05-time.md#over) with the weekend and holidays: *on the weekend* names the block as a dated unit (*we're closing on the weekend*), *over the weekend* spreads an event across it (*it rained all over the weekend*) — and only *over* works with a stretch you pass through (*over Thanksgiving*, *over the summer*). Against [in](#in) for media, the split is idiomatic rather than logical: a text is a container (*in the paper*, *in the report*) but a broadcast or a network is a surface you travel over (*on TV*, *on the radio*), so the same story is *in the Times* and *on Channel 4*. Against the adverb particle *on*: the preposition has an object (*Put your coat on the chair*), the particle does not (*Put your coat on*).

The preposition of the surface and the line. Its object supports something, is traced along, or is the medium something travels over — and from that come contact, routes, floors, calendars, broadcasts, and the great American set of *on the team*, *on the board*, *on strike*, *on vacation*. It shares a stretch of territory with *at* on the calendar (*on Tuesday* / *at noon*) and with *in* almost everywhere in space, so most of the learner's difficulty with *on* is really a difficulty about which of the three to pick.

**1. Contact with a surface.** Resting on, stuck to, or hanging from the face of something.

1. Your keys are *on* the counter.
2. There's a smudge *on* the screen.

**2. A line, an edge, a border, or a route.** Streets, rivers, coasts, boundaries, and paths.

3. Their place is *on* Lake Erie.
4. We stopped for gas *on* the way back.

**3. A floor, a page, a list, or a position in a layout.**

5. Radiology is *on* the third floor.
6. The footnote is *on* page 212.

**4. Aboard.** Anything you can stand up in or sit astride — bus, train, plane, ship, subway, bike, horse — plus *on foot*.

7. I left my umbrella *on* the train.
8. She crossed the whole state *on* a bicycle.

**5. A day or a date.** Named days, calendar dates, days combined with a part of the day, and — in American English — *on the weekend*.

9. The closing is *on* March 3rd.
10. He works late *on* Thursdays.

**6. At the moment of.** Formal, with a noun or a gerund: *on arrival, on hearing the news, on his return, on further review*.

11. *On* arrival, please check in at the front desk.
12. *On* hearing the verdict, half the room stood up.

**7. Topic.** What a book, a talk, a report, or a comment is about.

13. She wrote the standard textbook *on* soil chemistry.
14. The senator refused to comment *on* the report.

**8. Medium or device.** The channel something travels over — broadcast, wire, or instrument.

15. It was *on* the news all morning.
16. I'll be *on* the phone for another minute.

**9. Networks, platforms, and apps.** The modern extension of sense 8, and now one of the highest-frequency uses of the word: *on the internet, on the web, on email, on Instagram, on Zoom, on Slack, on my laptop, on the app*. The platform is treated as a surface you appear on, which is why *on Facebook* beats *in Facebook*; a document, by contrast, is still a container (*in the spreadsheet*, *in the thread*).

17. Half the class only knows her *on* Instagram.
18. We'll do the walkthrough *on* Zoom at four.

**10. Membership of a body you are picked or appointed to.** Distinctively American with teams and committees.

19. She's *on* the school board now.
20. Both twins made it *on* the team.

**11. Basis, dependence, or fuel.** What something rests on, runs on, or lives on.

21. The whole argument rests *on* one email.
22. The generator runs *on* propane.

**12. A state, a process, or an activity under way.** *On fire, on sale, on strike, on duty, on call, on leave, on hold, on the run, on the rise, on the mend.*

23. The nurses have been *on* strike for two weeks.
24. Everything in the store is *on* sale through Sunday.

**13. A trip, an errand, or an assignment.** A journey or a posting treated as a road you are out on: *on vacation, on a trip, on the road, on tour, on business, on assignment, on patrol, on a hike, on an errand, on a break*.

25. He's *on* the road three weeks out of every month.
26. She's *on* assignment in Phoenix until the end of the quarter.

**14. Medication and regimens.**

27. He's *on* antibiotics for another five days.
28. She's been *on* a low-sodium diet since March.

**15. Paying for it.**

29. Dinner's *on* me tonight.
30. The first round is *on* the house.

**16. Directed at, and often against.** Taxes, attacks, restrictions, and betrayals.

31. The council imposed a new tax *on* short-term rentals.
32. Don't be too hard *on* yourself.

**17. The person inconvenienced.** Very common in American speech and almost invisible in grammars: an *on* phrase naming whoever suffers the event. The pronoun is not the target of the verb but the person left holding the bag — *The car died on me* does not mean the car died on top of me.

33. My laptop quit *on* me halfway through the exam.
34. She hung up *on* him twice.

**18. Carried on one's person.** With *have*, *carry*, and *bring*, the surface is the body itself.

35. Do you have a pen *on* you?
36. I never carry cash *on* me anymore.

**19. Fixed adverbial and discourse phrases.** A closed set, frozen and mostly article-less: *on purpose, on average, on the whole, on the other hand, on the contrary, on balance, on time, on hand, on end, on the spot, on second thought, on the record, on the house*. Like *in fact* and *at least*, these are learned whole.

37. He did it *on* purpose, and everyone knew it.
38. *On* the whole, the first draft held up better than we expected.

**Stranding and position.** *On* strands freely: *the shelf it was* ***on***, *What are you working* ***on***?, *That's the option we settled* ***on***. Pied-piping is formal but common in writing (*the assumption on which the plan rests*). As with *in*, do not confuse the preposition with the adverb particle, and use the object test: **the preposition has an object, the particle does not.** *Put your coat on* (no object — particle) versus *Put your coat on the chair* (object — preposition); *Drive on*, *The lights are on*, *later on*, *from then on*, *and so on* are all objectless and therefore not prepositions. Note also the two-word *on to* versus the one-word *onto*: *Hold on to the railing* (particle + preposition) is not *Climb onto the railing* (single preposition). *On* is also a noun-modifying adjective in *the on switch* and *an on year*, and it is never a conjunction — *on that he arrived* is not English.

## TIME: at, on, in

**The principle: points get *at*, days and dates get *on*, containers of time get *in*.** Zoom out from a clock time to a day to a month and the preposition climbs the ladder with you — *at 6:15* → *on Tuesday* → *in March* → *in 2027*.

| | Takes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | clock times, precise moments, points in a process, mealtimes, holidays treated as a season | at 6:15, at noon, at midnight, at dawn, at sunset, at that moment, at the same time, at lunch, at the start, at the end, at present, at the age of ten, **at night**, at Christmas |
| **on** | days, dates, named days, a day plus a part of the day, anniversaries, and (American) the weekend | on Tuesday, on July 4th, on Christmas Day, on my birthday, on Tuesday morning, on the night of the 12th, on New Year's Eve, on the first of the month, **on the weekend** |
| **in** | months, seasons, years, decades, centuries, long periods, the general parts of the day, and future intervals | in March, in the fall, in 1998, in the '90s, in the twentieth century, **in the morning / afternoon / evening**, in an hour, in the meantime, in the past, in the future |

#### The exceptions you have to memorize

| Case | Rule |
|---|---|
| **at night** — but *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening* | The one real hole in the pattern. *In the night* exists, but it means one particular night: *I woke up twice in the night.* |
| **on Monday morning**, **on the night of the 12th** | A named day outranks the part of the day. Once a specific day is present, *on* wins over *in* and *at*. |
| **on the weekend** (American) vs. *at the weekend* (British) | The best-known transatlantic split. American English also says *on weekends* and *over the weekend*; it does not say *at the weekend*. |
| **at Christmas / at Thanksgiving** vs. **on Christmas Day / on Thanksgiving** | The holiday as a season is a point (*at*); the single dated day is a day (*on*). |
| **in the summer of 2019** vs. **on the summer solstice** | A stretch takes *in*; a dated day takes *on*. |
| **at the beginning / at the end** vs. **in the beginning / in the end** | *At the end of the movie* locates a moment in the sequence; *in the end* means "finally, after all." The pairs line up: *at first* goes with *at last* (points in a sequence), *in the beginning* goes with *in the end* (a summing-up of the whole stretch). |
| **on Fridays** vs. **on Friday** | The plural is habitual (*The library is closed on Mondays*); the singular is one occasion (*The library is closed on Monday*). *Every Friday* takes no preposition at all. |
| **at present, at the moment** vs. **in the past, in the future** | The current instant is a point; past and future are stretches. |
| **in the future** (American) vs. *in future* (British) | Meaning "from now on," American English keeps the article — *In the future, please call ahead.* The article-less *in future* is British and reads as an error in American copy. |
| **in years, in ages, in a decade** after a negative or a superlative | *I haven't seen him in years*, *the coldest week in a decade*. This is the American default where British English says *for years*. Both varieties accept *for*; only American English makes *in* the unmarked choice. |
| **in time** vs. **on time** | *In time* is soon enough, with room to spare; *on time* is exactly at the scheduled moment. |
| **in ten minutes** vs. **within ten minutes** | *In* means after the ten minutes have elapsed; *within* means before they are up. Casual American speech often lets *in* do both jobs, but formal writing keeps them apart. |
| **No preposition at all**: *next Friday, last year, this morning, every day, all week, tomorrow, yesterday, tonight* | *This / that / next / last / every / all* + a time noun blocks the preposition outright. Never *in next week*, never *on last Friday*. |
| **Days with the *on* dropped**: *I'll call you Monday.* *We're closed Sundays.* | Standard in American speech and informal writing. Keep the *on* in formal prose. |
| **at the age of ten** vs. **in his thirties** | A single age is a point; a decade of life is a container. |
| Fixed and unanalyzable: *at once, at last, at the last minute, on the dot, on schedule, in a minute, in the nick of time, in advance* | Memorize as whole phrases. |

## PLACE: at, on, in

**The principle: a point gets *at*, a surface or a line gets *on*, an enclosure or an area gets *in*.** Zoom in on a map and the preposition walks back down the ladder: *in the United States* → *in Ohio* → *in Columbus* → *on Bryden Road* → *at 1215 Bryden Road*. The smaller and more precise the location, the likelier *at*.

| | Takes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | a point on the map, a street address, a door or desk or counter, an institution seen as its function, an event | at the corner, at 400 Elm, at the door, at the front desk, at work, at school, at church, at the airport, at the game, at a party, at home, at the top of the page, at sea |
| **on** | a surface, a line, a border, a route, a floor, a page, a list, public transportation, a medium | on the table, on the wall, on the ceiling, on the third floor, on Main Street, on the coast, on the border, on the way, on page 12, on the bus, on TV, on the list, on the farm, on the left |
| **in** | a container, a room, a building seen as an interior, a city or state or country, a large open area, a liquid, a text | in the box, in the kitchen, in the building, in Chicago, in Texas, in the mountains, in the water, in the yard, in the picture, in the car, in bed, in line |

#### The contrasting pairs

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| **at the corner** / **on the corner** / **in the corner** | Where two streets meet (a point) / a building whose address is that intersection / the inside angle of a room. |
| **at the store** / **in the store** | Out doing the errand / physically inside the building. |
| **at school** / **in school** | At the building right now / enrolled as a student. British English uses *at school* for both. |
| **at the hospital** / **in the hospital** | Visiting or working there / a patient. British English drops the article: *in hospital*. |
| **at home** / **in the house** | Not out — the default American phrase, and *home* alone after motion verbs (*go home*, never *go to home*) / literally indoors. |
| **on the bus, the train, the plane, the subway** / **in the car, the cab, the truck, a canoe** | If you can stand up and walk around in it, it takes *on*; if you sit down inside it, it takes *in*. |
| **in bed** / **on the bed** | Under the covers, sleeping / on top of it, probably still dressed. |
| **in the picture** / **on the picture** | One of the things shown in it / stuck to its surface. |
| **on the wall** / **in the wall** | Hanging on the face of it / embedded inside it. |
| **at the table** / **on the table** | Seated for a meal or a negotiation / lying on the top surface. |
| **in the water** / **on the water** | Swimming or submerged / floating, boating, or out on the surface. |
| **on the team, the committee, the board, the jury, the staff** / **in the band, the choir, the army, the cast** | American English takes *on* for bodies you are picked or appointed to and *in* for groups you simply belong to. British English says *in the team*. |
| **in line** / **on line** | *Wait in line* is standard across the United States; *wait on line* is New York City and its immediate suburbs. Neither is *online*, which means on the internet. |
| **on the street** / **in the street** | American English prefers *on the street* for ordinary location (*the kids play on the street*, *I live on Maple*); *in the street* suggests out in the roadway itself. British English prefers *in* for both. |
| **at the front of the room** / **in front of the building** | Inside it, at its forward part / outside it, facing it. |
| **at the door** / **by the door** / **in the doorway** | Stationed there with a purpose — answering it, waiting at it / merely beside it / standing in the opening itself, blocking it. |
| **at the airport** / **in the airport** / **on the plane** | The point on your itinerary (*I'm at the airport*, said from the curb) / physically inside the terminal / aboard the aircraft. The same three-way split works for *at / in the station* and *on the train*. |
| **in college**, **at college** / British *at university* | American English says *in college* for enrolled and *at college* for on campus, and uses *college* for undergraduate study generally. *At university* and article-less *at uni* are British; so is *read* for "major in." |
| **arrive at** / **arrive in** / **reach** / **get to** | *Arrive at* a building, an address, a desk, or an event; *arrive in* a city, a state, or a country. *Reach* takes no preposition at all (*reach the airport*, never *reach to the airport*), and the everyday American verb is *get to*, which covers both sizes (*get to Denver*, *get to the office*). Never *arrive to*. |
| **in the newspaper, in the book, in the report** / **on TV, on the radio, on the internet, on the news** | A text is a container you read inside; a broadcast or a network is a medium something travels over. Both are standard, and the split is idiomatic, not logical — the same story is *in the Times* and *on Channel 4*. |

#### One note on movement

Strictly, *in* and *on* are static and *into* and *onto* mark the crossing of a boundary. American English does not enforce this after ordinary motion verbs — *jump in the pool*, *get on the bus*, *put it in the drawer* are all completely standard — but the compound forms remain the safer choice whenever the crossing itself is the point, and in a few cases the two-word and one-word versions mean genuinely different things: *He walked into the wall* (collision) is not *He walked in the wall*, and *Hold on to the railing* (verb particle + preposition) is not *Climb onto the railing* (one preposition).

## The compound twins: into, onto, upon

Three prepositions in this class are *in* and *on* with a second word welded on: **into** = *in* + *to*, **onto** = *on* + *to*, **upon** = *up* + *on*. The first two add motion — the object is not where you are but where you end up — and the third adds nothing but register. None of them has an adverb life: unlike bare *in* and *on*, all three always take an object, which is one quick way to tell *Come in* (adverb) from *Come into the kitchen* (preposition). Each is also treated in the movement family; see [into](04-movement-direction.md#into), [onto](04-movement-direction.md#onto), and [upon](04-movement-direction.md#upon) in [Movement and direction](04-movement-direction.md) for their place among *toward*, *out of*, and *off*.

### into

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪn.tu/ &middot; *IN-too*; weak /ˈɪn.tə/ &middot; *IN-tuh*; casual /ˈɪn.ə/ &middot; *IN-uh*
Stress sits on the first syllable and only the *to* reduces; the /t/ flaps or drops between vowels, so *into it* comes out "IN-uh-wit" and *get into a fight* is "geh-DIN-uh-fight." Spoken *into* and spoken *in to* are near-identical in running speech, which is why the one-word / two-word choice has to be settled by the grammar and never by the ear.
**Type:** compound (*in* + *to*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*into it*, *into them*) &middot; gerund (*talked her into staying*, *got into arguing about money*, *ran into trouble finding parking*) &middot; *wh*-clause, chiefly in the inquiry sense (*an investigation into what went wrong*, *I'll look into how that happened*, *no insight into why she left*). **Never a bare infinitive** — *talked her into to stay* is impossible — and **never a finite that-clause**: *an inquiry into that the money vanished* has to become *an inquiry into the fact that the money vanished* or simply *an inquiry into the missing money*. *Into* has **no adverb use at all**; like *at*, it always has an object, even a stranded one.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *into* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *into* [noun phrase] &middot; *into* [verb-ing] &middot; *into* [*wh*-clause] &middot; stranded: *the room she walked* **into**, *What have you gotten yourself* **into**? &middot; pied-piped (formal): *the state into which the house had fallen*
**Contrast:** Against [in](#in): **position versus crossing** — *He was in the pool* is not *He jumped into the pool*. This is the single most useful distinction in the pair, though American English lets plain *in* do the motion job after everyday verbs (*jump in the pool*, *throw it in the trash*). Against the two-word *in to*: keep them apart whenever *in* belongs to the verb — *turned himself* ***in to*** *the police* (he surrendered) is not *turned himself* ***into*** *the police* (he became them); likewise *came in to talk*, *dropped in to say hi*, *tuned in to the broadcast*. Against [onto](#onto): interior versus surface, exactly as with *in* and *on* — *fell into the pool* but *fell onto the deck*. Against [to](02-core-relational.md#to): *to* names a destination reached, *into* insists that a boundary was crossed — *drove to the garage* stops at it, *drove into the garage* ends up inside. The two-word sequence has its own entry at [in to](04-movement-direction.md#in-to).

The general preposition of entry: motion from outside to inside, with the boundary crossed. Every other sense is that image applied to something that is not a room — a wall you hit, a state you turn into, a night you work deep into, a subject you look inside.

**1. Motion to a position inside.**

1. She walked *into* the kitchen without knocking.
2. Pour the batter *into* the pan and level it off.

**2. Motion ending in collision or forced contact.** The "inside" collapses to the surface itself.

3. He backed *into* a mailbox on the first try.
4. The truck slid *into* the guardrail.

**3. Change of state, form, or language.** After *turn, change, translate, convert, break, grow, make*.

5. The argument turned *into* a shouting match.
6. Translate the whole section *into* Spanish by Friday.

**4. Entry into an action or an outburst.** The start of an activity treated as a space you break into: *break into a run, burst into tears, launch into a speech, dive into the paperwork, go into detail, get into an argument, fall into the habit*. The action begins abruptly, which is why *broke into a run* is not *began to run*.

7. He broke *into* a run before the light changed.
8. She burst *into* tears in the middle of the toast.

**5. Penetration into a period or a distance.** How far in you get.

9. They kept rehearsing *into* the early morning.
10. We were three weeks *into* the semester before anyone noticed.

**6. Inquiry or investigation.** After *look, inquiry, investigation, probe, research, delve*.

11. The board opened an investigation *into* the missing funds.
12. I'll look *into* what happened with your order.

**7. Persuasion or pressure.** *Talk someone into*, *push into*, *bully into*, *guilt into* — always with a gerund, never an infinitive.

13. They talked her *into* running for the seat.
14. Nobody pushed him *into* selling the house.

**8. Enthusiasm for (informal).** With *be*, and only predicatively.

15. He's been *into* woodworking since he retired.
16. She's not *into* horror movies at all.

**9. Chance encounter.** With *run* and *bump*.

17. I ran *into* my old landlord at the hardware store.
18. We bumped *into* each other outside the courthouse.

**10. Division (arithmetic).**

19. Four *into* twenty goes five times.
20. Divide the class *into* groups of three.

**Stranding and position.** *Into* strands normally: *the mess we got* ***into***, *What did she change* ***into***? Pied-piping is formal and slightly stiff (*the state into which the house had fallen*). Because *into* has no particle twin, a stranded *into* is always the preposition — but watch the reverse error, splitting a real *into* into two words: *He got into trouble*, never *He got in to trouble*.

**See also:** [in](#in), [onto](#onto), [upon](#upon), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [in to](04-movement-direction.md#in-to), [out of](04-movement-direction.md#out-of), [through](04-movement-direction.md#through)

### onto

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑn.tu/ &middot; *AHN-too*; also /ˈɔn.tu/ &middot; *AWN-too* for speakers who keep the *cot / caught* distinction; weak /ˈɑn.tə/ &middot; *AHN-tuh*
As with *into*, stress stays on the first syllable and only the *to* reduces, so *onto* and *on to* are distinguishable in careful speech and usually not in fast speech — which is why the spelling has to be decided by the grammar, not by the ear.
**Type:** compound (*on* + *to*, fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*onto it*, *onto them*) &middot; *wh*-clause in the informal "aware of" sense only (*they're onto what he's been doing*, *she's onto how the scheme works*) — everywhere else a clause is impossible. **Gerunds are marginal at best** — *onto winning* is not idiomatic, and where a gerund is wanted writers use the two-word *on to* instead (*moved on to fixing the roof*). This is the one clear complement difference between *onto* and [into](#into), which takes gerunds readily. **Never a bare infinitive** and **never a finite that-clause**: *onto that he was lying* is impossible, and the repair is *onto the fact that he was lying*. *Onto* has **no adverb use**: it always has an object.
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *onto* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] [object] *onto* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the ledge he climbed* **onto**, *the flash drive I copied it* **onto**
**Contrast:** Against [on](#on): **position versus crossing** — *The cat was on the roof* is not *The cat jumped onto the roof*. Against the two-word *on to*: this is the error American writers actually make. **Test: if "to a position on top of" fits, write one word.** If *on* belongs to the verb — *hold on*, *move on*, *pass on*, *hang on*, *log on*, *sign on*, *catch on*, *go on* — keep two: *hold* ***on to*** *the rail* (gripped it) versus *climbed* ***onto*** *the rail* (got up on it); *log* ***on to*** *the server*, not *log onto the server*. The one genuine gray area is *be on to someone* ("have figured out what they are up to"), written both ways in edited American prose with *onto* now slightly commoner. Against [into](#into): surface versus interior — *loaded it onto the truck* (on the bed) but *loaded it into the truck* (in the cab).

The surface counterpart of [into](#into): motion that ends with the moving thing resting on top of something. The extensions treat a drive, a bill, or a person's scheme as a surface you land on.

**1. Motion to a position on a surface.**

1. She stepped *onto* the platform just as the doors closed.
2. The cat leapt *onto* the counter the second we left.

**2. Transfer onto a surface or a medium.**

3. Copy the files *onto* a flash drive before you wipe the laptop.
4. Load the boxes *onto* the truck first.

**3. Emergence or outlook onto an open space.** With *open*, *look*, *give*, *back*.

5. The kitchen door opens *onto* the alley.
6. Their balcony looks *onto* the parking lot.

**4. Adding to an existing base.** Charges, additions, extensions.

7. The hotel tacked a resort fee *onto* the bill.
8. They bolted a sunroom *onto* the back of the house.

**5. Shifting a burden or a feeling onto a person.** The person becomes the surface the load lands on: *offload it onto, dump it onto, palm it off onto, pin the blame onto, project his anxiety onto*. This is the psychological cousin of sense 2, and it is one of the few places where *onto* clearly beats plain *on*.

9. He projects every bad mood *onto* whoever's nearest.
10. They dumped the whole reconciliation *onto* the new hire.

**6. Aware of, having caught on to (informal).**

11. Don't bother lying — they've been *onto* you for weeks.
12. I think she's *onto* something with that theory.

**Stranding and position.** *Onto* strands, but reluctantly (*the shelf I put it* ***onto*** — most speakers prefer *the shelf I put it on*), and pied-pipes almost never — *the ledge onto which he climbed* is stiff enough that writers rephrase. Note that where the surface is already established, plain *on* is usually the more natural American choice: *put it on the shelf* beats *put it onto the shelf*.

**See also:** [on](#on), [into](#into), [upon](#upon), [to](02-core-relational.md#to), [off](04-movement-direction.md#off), [on to](04-movement-direction.md#on-to)

### upon

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑn/ &middot; *uh-PAHN*; also /əˈpɔn/ &middot; *uh-PAWN*; the first syllable is always a schwa and never takes stress
**Type:** compound (*up* + *on*, fused) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or fixed-phrase — in plain modern American prose [on](#on) is the default and *upon* is the marked choice
**Complement:** noun phrase &middot; pronoun in the objective case (*upon it*, *upon them*) &middot; **gerund** (*upon signing the contract*, *upon arriving at the site*) — this is the one place *upon* genuinely beats *on* in modern writing &middot; *wh*-clause, exactly as with *on* (*it depends upon what the auditor finds*, *acted upon what he had been told*, *seized upon whichever number helped*). **Never a bare infinitive** (*upon to sign*) and **never a finite that-clause**: *upon that he arrived* is impossible, and the clause version is *when he arrived* or *once he had arrived*. *Upon* has **no adverb use**; the objectless *on* in *later on* can never be *later upon*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *upon* [noun phrase] &middot; *Upon* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [main clause] &middot; [noun] *upon* [the same noun] (*row upon row*) &middot; stranded only marginally: *the fate they had stumbled* **upon**
**Contrast:** Against [on](#on): the same meaning at a higher register. *Upon* is longer, heavier, and more formal, and reaching for it to sound elegant is the classic overcorrection — *sit upon the couch* is simply worse than *sit on the couch*. Reserve it for the four places it earns its keep: after certain verbs (*depend upon*, *call upon*, *seize upon*, *act upon*), in the temporal *upon* + gerund, in the doubling pattern (*row upon row*), and in frozen phrases (*once upon a time*). Against [onto](#onto): in its motion sense *upon* is a near-synonym of *onto* with a sudden or forceful edge — armies *descend upon* a town, not *onto* it.

The elevated twin of *on*, useful in exactly the places where its extra weight buys something. Its most genuinely modern job is temporal: *upon arrival* and *upon signing* compress a whole clause into two words, which is why contracts, instructions, and signage are full of it.

**1. On a surface (formal or literary).**

1. A heavy snow lay *upon* the fields.
2. The inscription *upon* the stone had worn away.

**2. Motion onto, often sudden or forceful.**

3. Winter descended *upon* the valley all at once.
4. The dogs fell *upon* the scraps before anyone could stop them.

**3. Immediately after, with a noun or a gerund.** The contract-and-signage sense.

5. *Upon* arrival, report to the front desk.
6. *Upon* signing, you'll receive a copy of the agreement.

**4. Discourse frames of reconsideration.** A small set of sentence adverbials in which *upon* is the standard word and plain *on* sounds wrong: *upon reflection, upon further review, upon closer inspection, upon consideration, upon due deliberation*. (Sports broadcasting is the exception that keeps *on further review*.)

7. *Upon* reflection, I'd rather not put my name on it.
8. *Upon* closer inspection, the signature turned out to be stamped.

**5. Chance encounter.** With *come*, *happen*, *stumble*.

9. I happened *upon* the letter while cleaning out the attic.
10. They stumbled *upon* the wreck by accident.

**6. Doubling for quantity.** [Noun] *upon* [the same noun], repeated with no article — singular in *mile upon mile*, plural in *thousands upon thousands*.

11. Mile *upon* mile of the same brown grass.
12. Thousands *upon* thousands of people showed up.

**7. Fixed verb and noun collocations.** *Depend upon, call upon, seize upon, act upon, insist upon, look upon, impose upon, prevail upon* — every one of which also works with *on*, and most of which are commoner with it.

13. The whole plan depends *upon* the county approving the variance.
14. She was called *upon* to testify twice.

**Stranding and position.** *Upon* strands only reluctantly (*the discovery they had stumbled* ***upon***); most writers either pied-pipe (*the discovery upon which the case rests*) or drop to *on*. Note the frozen sentence-initial *Upon* + gerund is the only place where *upon* is clearly better than *on*, and that *once upon a time* is unanalyzable.
