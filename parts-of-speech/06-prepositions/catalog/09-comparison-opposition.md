# Comparison, similarity, contrast, opposition, and support

> The prepositions that hold two things up against each other — to say they are alike, to say they are different, to name the benchmark one of them is measured by, or to take a side.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file is two-place in a way the others are not: it needs a thing in the main clause and a benchmark after the preposition, and the sentence only works if those two things are the same kind of thing. That single requirement produces most of the errors in the group — *Unlike the 1970s, today's inflation is mild* compares a decade with an inflation rate, and *Compared to Chicago, our winters are short* compares a city with a season. Two grammatical traps run alongside it. The first is **like versus as**: *like* takes a noun and asserts a resemblance, *as* names a role or heads a clause, and *like I said* is still marked in careful American prose. The second is **than**, which is a preposition when a noun follows (*taller than me*) and a conjunction when a clause follows (*taller than I am*) — and which one you have decides the case of the pronoun.

Several members of this family are housed elsewhere because their other senses dominate. The comparison-to-a-norm use of [for](02-core-relational.md#for) — *tall for his age*, *warm for November*, *cheap for a downtown lot* — is treated with the core relational prepositions and is not repeated here. Where a word in this file also has spatial, temporal, or topical senses — *as*, *against*, *over*, *next to*, *relative to*, *vis-à-vis* — the entry covers only its comparison-and-opposition work and links to the file that owns the rest.

## Likeness and difference

The plainest members of the group. They assert that two things are alike, or that they are not, and nothing else. *Like* and *as* are the pair that has to be kept straight, and the rest of the section is arranged around them: *similar to* and *akin to* are the analytic and the impressionistic versions of *like*, and *unlike*, *different from*, and *in common with* mark out the negative and the group-membership edges of the same idea. Above all of them sits *the same as*, which claims not resemblance but identity, and which is the one member of the section whose *as* will also take a clause.

### a far cry from

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌfɑr ˈkraɪ frʌm/ &middot; weak /ə ˌfɑr ˈkraɪ frəm/ &middot; *uh-far-KRY frum*
**Русский:** далеко не то, что — сильно уступает обещанному или образцу, с оттенком разочарования
**Type:** complex (article + noun phrase + fixed *from*; wholly idiomatic — the *cry* is the old sense "shouting distance," so the phrase literally measures a gap) &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational and journalistic
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*a far cry from hers*), gerund (*a far cry from running a newsroom*), or a free relative (*a far cry from what the contract described*) — **never a finite clause**. It is also **predicative only**: it follows *be*, *seem*, or *remain*, and it cannot be hung on a noun as a modifier. ***A far cry than* and *a far cry to* are errors**; the preposition is *from*, exactly as in *different from*.
**Pattern:** [be/seem/remain] *a far cry from* [noun phrase] &middot; *still a far cry from* [noun phrase] &middot; *a long way from* — the plain paraphrase
**Contrast:** Against *different from*, which reports unlikeness neutrally, *a far cry from* measures the distance and calls it large. It is also **directional**: what follows the phrase is the standard, and the subject is nearly always the thing that falls short of it — *the finished building is a far cry from the drawings* faults the building, not the drawings. Against *out of sync with*, which reports a mismatch of timing or alignment, this one reports a mismatch of degree.

Says two things are the same kind of thing and very far apart in quality, degree, or fidelity. It nearly always registers a disappointment: what follows the phrase is the promise, the original, or the standard, and the subject is what actually turned up.

1. The finished building is *a far cry from* the drawings the council approved.
2. Her second album is *a far cry from* the bluegrass she started with.
3. Twelve dollars an hour is *a far cry from* a living wage in this county.
4. Running a two-person shop is *a far cry from* running a newsroom.
5. What the contractor delivered was *a far cry from* what the contract described.
6. The river is cleaner than it was in 1975, but it is still *a far cry from* the trout stream the old-timers describe.

### akin to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɪn tu/ &middot; weak /əˈkɪn tə/ &middot; *uh-KIN too*
**Русский:** сродни; близко к — того же рода, но не то же самое
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal — *akin* survives almost nowhere else in modern English) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; common in journalism and criticism
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*akin to admitting defeat*), or a *wh*-free relative (*akin to what happened in 2008*) — never a finite clause. ***Akin with* is an error.**
**Pattern:** *be akin to* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *akin to* [noun phrase] &middot; *something akin to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Weaker than *the same as* and stronger than *reminiscent of*: *akin to* claims a family resemblance without claiming identity. Against *similar to*, which invites the reader to compare features, *akin to* is reached for when the writer wants an approximation and does not intend to defend the details — which is why *something akin to* is by far its commonest frame.

Says two things belong to the same family without saying they are the same thing. The adjective *akin* is a fossil, contracted from *of kin*, and outside this phrase it barely exists, which is why the whole string behaves as a single preposition.

1. What she felt was *akin to* relief, though she would not have called it that.
2. The charge is *akin to* a fee for using the road.
3. Signing that waiver is *akin to* giving up the claim entirely.
4. He greeted the news with something *akin to* panic.
5. Their dialect is more *akin to* Portuguese than to Spanish.

### along the lines of

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ ðə ˈlaɪnz əv/ &middot; weak /əˈlɔŋ ðə ˈlaɪnz ə/ &middot; also /əˈlɑŋ/ wherever *cot* and *caught* have merged &middot; *uh-long-thuh-LYNZ-uv*
**Русский:** в духе; примерно как — приблизительный образец, говорящий не ручается за детали
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational, business, and reported speech
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*along the lines of raising the fee*), a free relative (*along the lines of what we did last year*), or a **quoted or paraphrased utterance** (*something along the lines of "we'll be in touch"*) — **never a bare finite clause.** *Along the lines that the state would pay half* is possible in formal writing, but there the *that*-clause is hung on the noun *lines* and most editors rewrite it.
**Pattern:** *something along the lines of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *along the lines of* [noun phrase] &middot; *thinking along the lines of* [noun phrase] &middot; *along these/those/the same lines* — adverbial, no complement
**Variants:** *on the lines of* is British and does not belong in American writing. Bare *along those lines* and *along similar lines* take no object at all and are adverbials, not this preposition.
**Contrast:** Against *like* and *similar to*, this phrase is deliberately **approximate**: it says a model is being followed loosely and declines to be pinned to the details, which is why *something along the lines of* is the standard American way to report words you cannot quote exactly. Against *in the vein of*, which places a work in an artistic tradition, *along the lines of* points at a plan, a proposal, or a form of words. Against *in the manner of*, it claims no imitation of style at all.

Names a rough model without committing to it. The phrase is a hedge in prepositional form: whatever follows is offered as the general shape of the thing, and the speaker is explicitly reserving the right to have the details wrong.

1. He said something *along the lines of* "we'll let you know Friday."
2. The committee wants a program *along the lines of* the one Ohio runs.
3. I was thinking *along the lines of* a smaller deck and a bigger porch.
4. They floated a settlement *along the lines of* what the union got in 2019.
5. She is looking for work *along the lines of* what she did before the layoff.
6. The state is considering a surcharge *along the lines of* raising the registration fee by twenty dollars.

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ &middot; weak /əz/, and in rapid speech nearly /z/ after a vowel &middot; *AZ / uhz*
**Русский:** как, в качестве; такой же, как — второй член сравнения as … as и указание на роль или тождество
**Type:** simple — the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time, cause, and manner, the second half of the correlative *as … as*, and a relative pronoun after *such* and *the same* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in comparison, a noun phrase or an **objective-case pronoun** (*as tall as me*), and an adjective or adverb inside the correlative frame (*as quickly as*). It takes a **finite clause** only when it is doing conjunction duty (*as I said*), and the presence of a subject and verb after it is exactly what tells you which word you have. Never a gerund in the comparison sense, and never a *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** *as* [adjective/adverb] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *the same* … *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *such* … *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *as many/much/few/little as* [quantity] &middot; frozen adverbials: *as usual*, *as always*, *as a rule*, *as follows*
**Variants:** none. The two-word *as if* and *as though* take clauses and are conjunctions, not prepositions.
**Contrast:** ***Like* versus *as* is the central rule of this file.** *Like* takes a noun and asserts a resemblance; *as* names a role or heads a clause. *Like your lawyer, I'd settle* says the speaker is someone else who agrees with the lawyer; *As your lawyer, I'd settle* says the speaker **is** the lawyer. Before a clause, careful American prose uses *as* — *as I said*, not *like I said* — even though the *like* version is universal in speech. The role-and-capacity *as* (*works as a nurse*, *regarded as unreliable*) is treated with the prepositions of role at [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as); this entry covers *as* in comparison and manner.

The comparison *as* asserts equality or sameness of kind: that two things measure the same, that they are the same one, or that one belongs to the set named by the other. It is among the highest-frequency words in English, and its constant traffic with the identically spelled conjunction is what makes it the hardest word in this file to use confidently.

**1. The second *as* of a comparison of equality.** After a pronoun, American speech takes the objective case (*as tall as me*); formal writing usually restores the verb (*as tall as I am*).

1. The new engine is *as* quiet *as* the old one and half the weight.
2. She can throw *as* far *as* anybody on the team.
3. He's nearly *as* stubborn *as* me — formally, *as stubborn as I am*.

**2. After *the same*, asserting identity of kind.**

4. Her schedule is exactly *the same as* mine.
5. That's *the same* truck *as* the one parked out front all week.

**3. After *such*, introducing an example.**

6. Root vegetables *such as* turnips and parsnips keep all winter in sand.
7. Delays *such as* these are unavoidable in February.

**4. Frozen adverbials of manner and expectation.**

8. The 8:10 was late, *as* usual.
9. *As* always, the trailhead lot was full by seven.
10. Payment is due on the first, *as* a rule.

**5. *As many as*, *as much as*, *as few as* — the quantity frame.** The correlative closes on a number, and the effect is to offer that number as a striking maximum: *as many as forty* means "up to forty, and forty is a lot."

11. *As many as* forty families may have to move before the levee work starts.
12. The repair could run *as much as* nine thousand dollars, and that estimate is two weeks old.
13. The clinic sees *as few as* three patients on a Monday in August.

**6. Not a preposition — the conjunction, with a whole clause after it.** This is the form careful writing uses where speech reaches for *like*.

14. Fold the corners in, *as* the diagram shows.
15. *As* I said on the phone, the estimate is only good for thirty days.

**See also:** [like](#like), [than](#than), [similar to](#similar-to), [the same as](#the-same-as), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [as](10-topic-reference-role.md#as)

### different from

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪfrənt frʌm/ &middot; weak /ˈdɪfərənt frəm/ &middot; three syllables, /ˈdɪfərənt/, in careful speech &middot; *DIF-runt frum*
**Русский:** отличный от; не такой, как — стандартный предлог при different (не to, по-британски)
**Type:** complex (adjective + preposition; **marginal** — *different* is a fully live adjective, and the pair is listed here because choosing its preposition is the problem, not because the two words have fused) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*different from me*), gerund (*different from driving a truck*), or a free relative (*different from what I expected*). **Never a bare *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [be/seem/look] *different from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *different from* [noun phrase] &middot; *no different from* [noun phrase] &middot; *differently from* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** ***different than*** is standard American English and is the better choice before a clause, where *from* forces a clumsy free relative: *The house looked different than I remembered* beats *different from what I remembered*. ***Different to*** is British and does not belong in American writing. *Different from* is never wrong and is the safe default.
**Contrast:** The pronoun test separates the two American forms: *from* is a preposition and takes the objective case (*different from her*), while *than* can be either preposition or conjunction, so *different than she is* and *different than her* are both current. Note that the verb is *differ from*, never *differ than*, and the adverb is *differently from* or *differently than*.

The workhorse expression of unlikeness, and the one that draws the most usage mail in American English. The rule that matters is short: *from* always works, *than* works and is often smoother before a clause, and *to* is British.

1. Her second draft is completely *different from* the first.
2. Growing up in Fairbanks is *different from* anything I can describe to you.
3. The new packaging is no *different from* the old except for the color.
4. It tasted *different from* what the menu had promised.
5. He votes *differently from* the rest of his delegation on almost everything.
6. The building looks *different than* it did in the photographs — before a clause, *than* is the smoother choice.

**See also:** [unlike](#unlike), [as distinct from](#as-distinct-from), [than](#than), [similar to](#similar-to), [the same as](#the-same-as), [other than](08-accompaniment-exception.md#other-than)

### in common with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑmən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in KAH-mun with*
**Русский:** как и; наравне с — подобно другим членам той же группы; книжн., только письменно
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; written English almost exclusively
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** *In common with* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [subject], *in common with* [noun phrase], [verb]
**Contrast:** Only the fronted, comma-set use is a preposition. The far commoner *have something in common with* is a verb plus its object plus an ordinary *with*-phrase, and the two cannot be swapped: *In common with her sister, she plays the cello* means "like her sister," while *She has the cello in common with her sister* means they share it as an interest. Against *like*, this phrase is stiffer and nearly always fronted; against *along with*, it asserts resemblance rather than accompaniment, and like *along with* it leaves the verb agreeing with the original subject alone.

Marks the subject of the main clause as one more member of a group that behaves the same way. Because it is bulky and almost always fronted, it belongs to written English — a reporter writes it and nobody says it.

1. *In common with* most western states, Nevada holds its primary in June.
2. *In common with* her colleagues, she had no warning that the lab would close.
3. The bank, *in common with* every lender in the state, tightened its terms that spring.
4. *In common with* many veterans of that campaign, he refused to discuss it.
5. *In common with* the older engines, this one still needs leaded fuel.

**See also:** [like](#like), [akin to](#akin-to), [similar to](#similar-to), [along with](08-accompaniment-exception.md#along-with)

### in the manner of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈmænər əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈmænər ə/ &middot; *in-thuh-MAN-ur-uv*
**Русский:** в манере; в стиле кого-л. — книжн.: подражание известному стилю, часто с намёком на копию
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to literary; standard in art criticism, music, and cooking
**Complement:** noun phrase, usually a proper name or a style label — never a finite clause; a gerund is grammatical but rare
**Pattern:** [noun] *in the manner of* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *in the manner of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In the manner of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *after the manner of* is the older and more literary form; *in the style of* is the plain one; the borrowed *à la* /ɑ ˈlɑ/ (*à la* Hitchcock) is informal in English. In museum and auction cataloguing, *in the manner of* is a term of art meaning "by an unknown follower," as against *attributed to* and *studio of*.
**Contrast:** Against *like*, which simply asserts a resemblance, this phrase names a deliberate imitation of a recognized style. Against *as*, it never assigns a role: a fresco *in the manner of* Giotto was emphatically not painted by Giotto, while a fresco *by* Giotto was.

Says the thing was made in imitation of a known style. In auction catalogues the phrase carries a specific and unflattering meaning — the work is by a follower, not the master — and that hedged quality travels with it into ordinary prose.

1. A courtyard fountain *in the manner of* the Alhambra.
2. He wrote three novels *in the manner of* Dashiell Hammett and one that was entirely his own.
3. The sauce is finished *in the manner of* a classic beurre blanc.
4. She ran the department *in the manner of* a ship's captain.
5. The lithograph is catalogued *in the manner of* Daumier, which is the polite way of saying it is not one.

**See also:** [like](#like), [as](#as), [akin to](#akin-to), [in the guise of](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-the-guise-of)

### in the vein of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈveɪn əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈveɪn ə/ &middot; *in-thuh-VAYN uhv*
**Русский:** в духе; в стиле — о книге, фильме, пластинке в традиции названного образца
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; criticism and reviewing — books, records, films, and restaurant menus
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a proper name or a genre label (*in the vein of Raymond Carver*, *in the vein of early punk*), or a free relative (*in the vein of what she showed in Santa Fe*) — **never a finite clause**, and a gerund only awkwardly: *in the vein of writing for children* is grammatical and nobody writes it.
**Pattern:** [noun] *in the vein of* [noun phrase] &middot; *something in the vein of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in a lighter/darker/similar vein*, *in the same vein* — the adjectival frames, which take no complement at all
**Variants:** *in a … vein* (*in a lighter vein*) and *in the same vein* are the older constructions and are adverbials, not this preposition; *in the vein of* is the prepositional development of them. *In the style of* and *in the manner of* are the near-synonyms. The *vein* is the mining one — a seam of a particular material — not the anatomical one.
**Contrast:** Against [in the manner of](#in-the-manner-of), which says a style was deliberately imitated, *in the vein of* only places a work in a tradition: a novel *in the vein of* Chandler need not be trying to sound like Chandler, and no forgery is implied. Against *along the lines of*, which points at a plan or a form of words, this points at an artistic kind. Against *like*, it is the reviewer's register and always names something with a recognized style attached to it.

Places a work in the tradition of a named model. It is the standard shorthand of reviewing — three words that tell a reader what shelf a thing belongs on — and it commits the writer to far less than *in the manner of* would.

1. It is a quiet domestic novel *in the vein of* Anne Tyler.
2. The band's third record is *in the vein of* early Fleetwood Mac.
3. He told a long story *in the vein of* the ones his grandfather used to tell.
4. The gallery wants something *in the vein of* what she showed in Santa Fe.
5. The report closes on a warning, *in the vein of* every county study since 1998.
6. She is writing satire *in the vein of* the eighteenth century, footnotes and all.

**See also:** [in the manner of](#in-the-manner-of), [along the lines of](#along-the-lines-of), [akin to](#akin-to), [like](#like), [similar to](#similar-to)

### like

**Pronunciation:** /laɪk/ &middot; a stressed word with no weak form, which is one reason it is so audible in American speech &middot; *LYKE*
**Русский:** как; подобно; вроде — сходство; требует существительного, тогда как as вводит роль или придаточное
**Type:** simple — the same spelling is also a verb (*I like it*), an adjective (*of like mind*), a suffix (*childlike*), a quotative (*he was like, no*), and a discourse particle. Only the preposition takes an object and means "resembling." &middot; **Register:** neutral as a preposition; the clause-taking and approximating uses are informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, **objective-case pronoun** (*like me*, never *like I*), gerund (*It's like driving on ice*), or a *wh*-free relative (*like what happened last year*). **A finite clause after *like* — *like I said*, *like you told me* — is informal**; careful American writing uses *as* or *as if* there. Never a *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** [be/look/sound/feel/smell/taste/seem] *like* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *like* [noun phrase] &middot; *Like* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *just/much/something/more like* [noun phrase] &middot; *It is (not) like* [person] *to* [verb]
**Contrast:** **The *like*/*as* rule.** *Like* takes a noun and states a resemblance; *as* takes a role or a clause. *He works like a mule* is a comparison; *He works as a mule* would mean the animal is his job. Before a clause, formal prose wants *as* (*as I said*) or *as if* (*It looks as if it will rain*). Against *such as*, some editors reserve *like* for resemblance and *such as* for actual instances (*poets such as Frost*), but that distinction is not observed in general American usage and *like* introduces examples freely.

The general-purpose word of resemblance, and the busiest preposition in spoken American English. Everything it does reduces to a single operation — hold X up against Y and assert that they are alike — but the range of things that can fill those two slots is enormous, which is why the entry runs long.

**1. Resembling; in the same way as.**

1. The new bridge looks *like* a harp from the river.
2. She drives *like* a professional and parks *like* a tourist.
3. Nobody else on the crew works *like* him.

**2. Typical or untypical of a person — always with *be*.**

4. It's not *like* Marcus to miss a deadline.
5. That is exactly *like* her: she paid for everyone and slipped out.

**3. Introducing an example.**

6. Bring something sturdy, *like* a tarp or a moving blanket.
7. Root crops *like* beets and carrots store well in damp sand.

**4. After verbs of perception and impression.**

8. The whole house smelled *like* woodsmoke for a week.
9. It felt *like* the first real morning of fall.

**5. Approximately — informal, before a quantity.**

10. There were *like* forty people crammed into that kitchen.
11. It took *like* two hours just to get through security.

**6. Not a preposition — the quotative *be like* and the discourse particle,** both conversational, neither taking an object.

12. And she was *like*, "I already sent it twice."
13. It was, *like*, the third time that week — the discourse particle, with nothing after it that it governs.

**See also:** [as](#as), [unlike](#unlike), [similar to](#similar-to), [akin to](#akin-to), [than](#than)

### similar to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪmələr tu/ &middot; weak /ˈsɪmələr tə/ &middot; *SIM-uh-lur too*
**Русский:** похожий на; сходный с — нейтральный, более технический аналог like
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*similar to running a small business*), or a *wh*-free relative (*similar to what we saw last spring*) — never a finite clause. ***Similar with* and *similar than* are errors**; the preposition is always *to*.
**Pattern:** [be/look/sound] *similar to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *similar to* [noun phrase] &middot; *broadly/roughly/strikingly similar to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against *like*, *similar to* is the analytic word: *like* asserts a likeness the reader can feel, *similar to* asserts one the writer could itemize, which is why lab reports and appraisals prefer it. Against *the same as*, it explicitly stops short of identity. Note that the noun takes different prepositions depending on the frame — *a similarity **to** the original*, but *similarities **between** the two*.

States a measured resemblance. It is the neutral technical alternative to *like*, and its natural home is a sentence in which the two things compared are of the same type and the differences are going to matter.

1. The symptoms are *similar to* those of ordinary flu.
2. This year's model is *similar to* last year's except for the transmission.
3. Running a school board turned out to be *similar to* running a small business.
4. Her handwriting is strikingly *similar to* her father's.
5. The plan is broadly *similar to* one the county rejected in 2019.

**See also:** [like](#like), [akin to](#akin-to), [comparable to](#comparable-to), [the same as](#the-same-as), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [different from](#different-from), [as](#as)

### the likes of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlaɪks əv/ &middot; weak /ðə ˈlaɪks ə/ &middot; *the* is /ði/ before a vowel &middot; *thuh-LYKES uhv*
**Русский:** такие, как; подобные — вводит примеры; в зависимости от контекста с восхищением или презрением
**Type:** complex (definite article + plural noun + fixed *of*; the noun *likes* survives in almost nothing else) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the example-listing sense; the *the likes of us* sense is informal and rarely neutral in tone
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*the likes of us*, *the likes of him*), or a list of names — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** The **verb agrees with what follows *of***, not with the plural *likes*: *the likes of Ford and GM have moved on*, but *the likes of Babe Ruth comes along once a century*.
**Pattern:** *the likes of* [name or list] &middot; *never seen the likes of* [noun phrase] &middot; *not for the likes of us* &middot; *and the like* — the fixed tail phrase, which is **not** this preposition and takes no object
**Variants:** the singular ***the like of*** (*the like of which*) is older and now chiefly formal; *the likes of which* is the everyday American version. ***And the likes of that***, for "and that sort of thing," is dialectal and does not belong in writing.
**Contrast:** Against [such as](11-complex-multiword.md#such-as), which is colorless, *the likes of* always carries an attitude — admiration, dismissal, or awe — and it names people and institutions rather than kinds of thing: *poets such as Frost*, but *the likes of Frost and Stevens*. Against *like*, it is heavier and always plural in form. And note that the tone flips with the frame: after a negative it is awe (*never seen the likes of it*), while *the likes of us* and *the likes of him* mark a social boundary and are never neutral.

Names people or institutions of a recognized kind, and colors them as it does so. All three of its uses do the same grammatical work — they head a comparison class — and they differ entirely in tone, from a flat list of examples to open contempt.

**1. Such as — introducing prominent examples.**

1. Grants went to *the likes of* Carnegie Mellon and Georgia Tech.
2. She has beaten *the likes of* three former champions this season.
3. The street lost *the likes of* the hardware store and the shoe repair within a year.

**2. After a negative — nothing comparable, the awe frame.**

4. Nobody on that crew had ever seen *the likes of* the storm that came through in June.
5. There will never be *the likes of* her in that department again.

**3. *The likes of us* — the class marker.**

6. That club was never built for *the likes of us*.
7. He wasn't going to take advice from *the likes of* a night clerk.

**See also:** [such as](11-complex-multiword.md#such-as), [as](#as), [like](#like), [similar to](#similar-to), [in the vein of](#in-the-vein-of)

### the same as

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈseɪm æz/ &middot; weak /ðə ˈseɪm əz/, and in fast speech /ðə ˈseɪm z/ after a vowel &middot; *the* is /ði/ before a vowel &middot; *thuh-SAME az*
**Русский:** такой же, как; то же самое, что — тождество; второе слово именно as, а не that
**Type:** complex (determiner + adjective + fixed *as*; marginal — *same* is a fully live adjective, and the string is listed because choosing its second word is the problem) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, **objective-case pronoun** (*the same as me*), gerund (*the same as driving at night*), or a *wh*-free relative (*the same as what we had before*). A **finite clause** can follow (*the same as it always was*), but there the *as* has gone back to conjunction duty, exactly as it does at [as](#as). **Never a *that*-clause.** ***The same than* and *the same like* are errors.**
**Pattern:** [be] *the same as* [noun phrase] &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] — the two words split around the noun &middot; *much/exactly/about/roughly the same as* [noun phrase] &middot; *not the same as* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** ***the same that*** in the split frame (*the same house that we rented*) is possible but reads as a relative clause, not as this preposition; American English uses *as*. Bare *the same*, with no complement (*I'll have the same*), is a noun phrase and not a preposition at all. ***Same as* without the article** (*Same as last year*) is idiomatic in speech and in headlines and is clipped, not wrong.
**Contrast:** The top of the likeness ladder. *The same as* claims identity; *on a par with* claims equal standing; *comparable to* claims that a comparison is legitimate; *similar to* claims a resemblance with differences left over; *akin to* claims only a family likeness. Against *equal to*, which compares amounts, *the same as* compares kind as well as amount — *the same as* a hundred dollars and *equal to* a hundred dollars are not quite the same claim. And note the split frame: this is the only member of the section that can put a noun between its two halves.

Says two things are one thing, or that they match in every way currently being counted. It is the plainest identity statement English has, and every error it attracts is an error in the second word.

**1. Identical to; matching in every relevant respect.**

1. Her schedule this fall is *the same as* it was in the spring.
2. The replacement part is *the same as* the one that failed.
3. My rent is about *the same as* yours, and I have half the space.

**2. Split around the noun — *the same* [noun] *as*.**

4. We used *the same* contractor *as* the church did.
5. She went to *the same* high school *as* both her parents.

**3. Amounting to; equivalent in effect — the evaluative use.**

6. Saying nothing at the hearing was *the same as* agreeing to it.
7. Twenty dollars an hour here is not *the same as* twenty dollars an hour in Seattle.
8. Deferred maintenance is *the same as* a tax, only nobody votes on it.

**See also:** [as](#as), [similar to](#similar-to), [comparable to](#comparable-to), [equal to](#equal-to), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [like](#like), [different from](#different-from)

### unlike

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈlaɪk/ &middot; *un-LYKE*
**Русский:** в отличие от — отрицает сходство, которое читатель мог предположить; также «не свойственно кому-л.»
**Type:** simple (historically *un-* + *like*, no longer analyzed as two pieces) — also an adjective (*two unlike quantities*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*unlike me*), or gerund (*unlike driving in the city*) — never a finite clause. *Unlike I said* is not English; *unlike what I said* is the repair.
**Pattern:** *Unlike* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *unlike* [noun phrase] &middot; [be] *unlike* [noun phrase] &middot; *not unlike* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The negative of prepositional *like*, and the one-word equivalent of *in contrast to*. The **parallelism trap** is worse here than anywhere else in this file because *unlike* is usually fronted: whatever follows it must match the subject of the main clause. *Unlike the 1990s, today's market is volatile* compares a decade with a market; *Unlike the market of the 1990s, today's is volatile* repairs it. Note the litotes *not unlike*, which means "rather like" and is a deliberate hedge, not a double negative to be canceled out.

Denies a resemblance the reader might otherwise have assumed. It does the work of a whole clause in one word, and because it nearly always sits at the front of the sentence, it puts an unusually heavy burden on the complement to be the right kind of thing.

**1. Different from.**

1. *Unlike* her brother, she never learned to swim.
2. *Unlike* most of the coast, the peninsula gets almost no fog.
3. The second season, *unlike* the first, was filmed entirely on location.

**2. Uncharacteristic of a person.**

4. It is *unlike* him to leave without saying anything.
5. That kind of carelessness is completely *unlike* her.

**3. *Not unlike* — the hedged resemblance.**

6. The sound was *not unlike* a train going over a trestle.
7. His method is *not unlike* the one the old county surveyor used.

**See also:** [like](#like), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [different from](#different-from), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to)

## Explicit comparison and proportion

These name a benchmark and measure something against it. The whole section is governed by one rule — the two things compared must be of the same kind — and by one stylistic fact: the phrase gets longer as the register gets more formal, running from *than* through *compared with* to *in comparison with*, with no change in meaning along the way. Four members make a claim about the comparison itself rather than reporting its result: *comparable to* says the two things are alike enough that measuring them against each other is legitimate, *on a par with* says the measurement came out even, *commensurate with* says one is fitting for the other, and *equal to* says the arithmetic came out the same. The comparison-to-a-norm sense of [for](02-core-relational.md#for), as in *tall for his age*, belongs to the same semantic field but is housed with the core relational prepositions.

### as against

**Pronunciation:** /əz əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; full /æz əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *uhz-uh-GENST*
**Русский:** по сравнению с (о цифрах); юр. в отношении такой-то стороны — книжн.
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; sense 1 statistical and journalistic, sense 2 legal
**Complement:** noun phrase, very often a bare figure — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** [figure] *as against* [figure] &middot; [clause] *as against* [noun phrase] &middot; *as against* [party] — in legal use
**Contrast:** In sense 1 it is a compact *compared with*, and it is nearly always attached to numbers; if the sentence has no figures in it, *compared with* or *as opposed to* reads better. Sense 2 is a term of art and is not a comparison at all: *valid as against third parties* means "enforceable in relation to them," not "opposed to them." Against plain [against](#against), the *as* signals measurement rather than hostility.

Sets two figures or two positions opposite each other. In general prose it is a compact and slightly old-fashioned comparison word; in law it is a term of art meaning "in relation to a particular party," and there it says nothing about conflict at all.

**1. Compared with — chiefly between two figures.**

1. Revenue came in at $41 million *as against* $37 million a year earlier.
2. Turnout was 61 percent, *as against* 48 in the last off-year election.
3. Three inspectors now cover the whole county, *as against* nine in 2010.

**2. Considered in relation to; enforceable with respect to — legal.**

4. The lien is valid *as against* subsequent purchasers.
5. The waiver has no effect *as against* the insurer.
6. Her claim is stronger *as against* the estate than *as against* the trust.

**See also:** [compared with](#compared-with), [versus](#versus), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [against](#against), [relative to](#relative-to)

### as between

**Pronunciation:** /əz bɪˈtwin/ &middot; full /æz bɪˈtwin/ &middot; weak /əz bəˈtwin/ &middot; *uhz-bih-TWEEN*
**Русский:** в отношениях между (сторонами) — юр.: правило действует только для названных сторон
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; **legal** — an American meets it in a contract's risk-allocation clause, an insurance policy, or a judicial opinion, and almost nowhere else
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming **two or more parties**, normally two joined by *and* (*as between the buyer and the seller*) or a plural noun (*as between the partners*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** Because *between* is itself a preposition, a pronoun in the complement is objective: *as between you and me*, never *as between you and I*.
**Pattern:** *As between* [party] *and* [party], [clause] &middot; [clause] *as between* [parties] &middot; *as between themselves*
**Variants:** *as among* is used where there are more than two parties and a drafter wants to be exact; in practice *as between* covers both. Bare *between*, in its ordinary senses, is at [between](03-place-position.md#between).
**Contrast:** **Not a comparison.** *As between* does not mean "compared with"; it confines a statement to the internal relations of the named parties and says nothing about anyone else. *As between the buyer and the seller, the seller bears the loss* allocates the loss between those two and leaves the insurer's rights exactly where they were — which is precisely why drafters use it. Against [as against](#as-against) sense 2, which names the one party a right is good against, *as between* names the two parties whose relations are being fixed. Against [in privity with](#in-privity-with), which asserts that a legal relationship exists at all, this one takes the relationship for granted and divides something up inside it.

Confines a rule to the parties named and to no one else. It is allocation language: the clause that follows settles who bears a loss, a cost, or a priority as between the two of them, while the outside world's rights stay untouched.

1. *As between* the buyer and the seller, risk of loss passes on delivery.
2. The indemnity decides who pays *as between* the contractor and the owner.
3. *As between* the two lienholders, the bank recorded first and takes priority.
4. *As between* the partners, profits are split evenly, whatever the tax return shows.
5. The clause governs *as between* the parties only; third parties keep whatever rights they had.
6. The court divided the fee *as between* the two firms that had worked the case.

**See also:** [as against](#as-against), [in privity with](#in-privity-with), [versus](#versus), [relative to](#relative-to), [between](03-place-position.md#between)

### at a discount to

**Pronunciation:** /ət ə ˈdɪskaʊnt tu/ &middot; weak /ət ə ˈdɪskaʊnt tə/ &middot; full /æt ə ˈdɪskaʊnt tu/ &middot; *at-uh-DISS-kownt too*
**Русский:** с дисконтом к; дешевле, чем — фин.: ниже названного эталона цены
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; **finance** — securities analysis, earnings coverage, and appraisal, where an American meets it in a research note or on a business page
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **benchmark that carries a price or a value** — a peer group, an index, book value, net asset value, par, an appraisal, a trailing average. **Never a finite clause and never a gerund**, and never an adjective: *at a discount to cheap* is not English, because the complement has to be something you could put a number on.
**Pattern:** [trade/sell/change hands/be valued] *at a discount to* [benchmark] &middot; *at a* [figure] *percent discount to* [benchmark] &middot; *at a discount* — adverbial, no complement
**Variants:** bare *at a discount*, with nothing after it, means only "below the usual price" and is an adverbial rather than this preposition. Note the two prepositions: the purchase you get cheaply takes *on* (*a discount on the fare*), while the benchmark you fall below takes *to*.
**Contrast:** The exact mirror of [at a premium to](#at-a-premium-to), and the direction is the whole point: **the complement is the reference, and the subject is the cheap one.** *The stock trades at a discount to its peers* says the stock is the bargain. Against plain *below*, which just reports a smaller number, *at a discount to* implies the two are comparable assets and quietly invites a judgment about whether the gap is deserved. Against *relative to*, which only normalizes one quantity by another, this one states which way the gap runs.

Prices one thing below a named benchmark. It is relative-value language: nobody who uses it is interested in the absolute number, only in the size and the direction of the gap between that number and whatever is doing the measuring.

1. The shares have traded *at a discount to* book value since the writedown.
2. Regional banks are still cheap *at a discount to* the sector as a whole.
3. The bonds were issued *at a discount to* par and redeemed at face value.
4. Closed-end funds routinely sell *at a discount to* net asset value.
5. The county sold the parcels *at a discount to* the appraised price to get them built on.
6. Its multiple sits *at a fifteen percent discount to* every comparable name in the index.

**See also:** [at a premium to](#at-a-premium-to), [relative to](#relative-to), [compared with](#compared-with), [as against](#as-against), [in proportion to](#in-proportion-to)

### at a premium to

**Pronunciation:** /ət ə ˈprimiəm tu/ &middot; weak /ət ə ˈprimiəm tə/ &middot; full /æt ə ˈprimiəm tu/ &middot; *at-uh-PREE-mee-um too*
**Русский:** с премией к; дороже, чем — фин.: выше названного эталона цены
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; **finance** — merger coverage, securities analysis, and real estate
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **benchmark price or value** — a closing price, a trailing average, a peer group, net asset value, the conventional price. **Never a finite clause and never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [trade/sell/bid/value X] *at a premium to* [benchmark] &middot; *at a* [figure] *percent premium to* [benchmark] &middot; *pay a premium for* [thing] — a different frame with a different preposition
**Variants:** bare *at a premium*, with no complement, is a separate idiom meaning "scarce and therefore dear" (*parking downtown is at a premium*) and has nothing to do with a benchmark. Note the two prepositions: what you pay extra **for** takes *for* (*paid a premium for waterfront*), while the level you exceed takes *to*. ***At a premium over*** is common in speech and is not wrong, but *to* is the house form in financial writing.
**Contrast:** The exact mirror of [at a discount to](#at-a-discount-to), with the same directional rule: **the complement is the reference, and the subject is the expensive one.** Against *above*, which reports a bigger number and nothing more, *at a premium to* asserts that the two are comparable and that somebody is paying extra by choice. Against *on a par with*, which says the measurement came out even, it says it did not.

Prices one thing above a named benchmark. It is the language of takeover bids and of anything sold on reputation, and its whole content is the gap: how far above the reference the price sits, and whether that gap is deserved.

1. The offer values the company *at a premium to* Friday's closing price.
2. Waterfront lots sell *at a premium to* anything two blocks inland.
3. The bid came in *at a thirty percent premium to* the thirty-day average.
4. Short-dated paper is trading *at a premium to* the rest of the curve.
5. Organic milk moves *at a premium to* the conventional price all year.
6. The fund has traded *at a premium to* net asset value since the spring.

**See also:** [at a discount to](#at-a-discount-to), [relative to](#relative-to), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [compared with](#compared-with), [over](#over)

### by comparison with

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ kəmˈpɛrəsən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *by kum-PAIR-uh-sun with*
**Русский:** по сравнению с — книжн., более развёрнутый вариант compared with
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a *wh*-free relative (*by comparison with what the company earned*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *by comparison with* [noun phrase] &middot; *By comparison with* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *by comparison to* is equally acceptable in American English. The bare *by comparison*, with no complement, is an adverbial rather than a preposition: *The second half was dull by comparison.*
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *in comparison with* and slightly lighter on the page. Both are longer than *compared with*, which does identical work in two syllables — reach for the phrase only when the benchmark is long or when the comparison needs to be fronted.

Puts the main clause beside a benchmark and invites the reader to notice the gap. Its real advantage over *compared with* is rhythmic: it can carry a heavy complement without stalling the sentence.

1. *By comparison with* the drought years, this spring has been generous.
2. The fine looks trivial *by comparison with* what the company earned on the contract.
3. Our overhead is modest *by comparison with* firms of the same size.
4. *By comparison with* the original recording, the remaster is almost harsh.
5. The delay was minor *by comparison with* what the other agencies went through.

**See also:** [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [compared with](#compared-with), [relative to](#relative-to), [next to](#next-to)

### commensurate with

**Pronunciation:** /kəˈmɛnsərət wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /kəˈmɛnʃərət/ &middot; *kuh-MEN-sur-ut with*
**Русский:** соразмерный; соответствующий — книжн.: величина под стать другой (зарплата по опыту)
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) &middot; **Register:** formal; standard in job postings, compensation policy, and sentencing
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*commensurate with what the job demands*) — **never a finite clause**; a gerund is grammatical but rare (*commensurate with running a department that size*)
**Pattern:** [noun] *commensurate with* [noun phrase] &middot; *be commensurate with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***Commensurate to* is an error** in current American usage; the preposition is *with*. Where *in proportion to* states an arithmetic relation, *commensurate with* states a judgment of fitness: a salary *commensurate with* experience is a fair one, not one computed from a formula. Against *in line with*, it is more evaluative and considerably more formal.

Says one quantity is fitting for another — large enough, serious enough, or long enough to match it. The compensation cliché *salary commensurate with experience* is where most Americans meet the word, and what it means is "we will pay what your résumé justifies."

1. Salary *commensurate with* experience.
2. The sentence should be *commensurate with* the harm done.
3. She wanted a title *commensurate with* the work she was already doing.
4. The reserve is not *commensurate with* the risk the fund is carrying.
5. Federal aid was never *commensurate with* the scale of the damage.

**See also:** [in proportion to](#in-proportion-to), [in line with](#in-line-with), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [comparable to](#comparable-to), [equal to](#equal-to), [relative to](#relative-to)

### comparable to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑmpərəbəl tu/ &middot; weak /ˈkɑmpərəbəl tə/ &middot; also /kəmˈpɛrəbəl/, with the stress pulled onto the second syllable — now common in American speech and still objected to by some &middot; *KAHM-pur-uh-bul too*
**Русский:** сопоставимый с; сравнимый с — достаточно схожи, чтобы сравнение было правомерным
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in appraisal, statistics, and compensation
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*comparable to hers*), gerund (*comparable to running a small hotel*), or a *wh*-free relative (*comparable to what the coasts pay*) — never a finite clause. ***Comparable than* is an error**, and ***comparable as* is not English.**
**Pattern:** [be/seem] *comparable to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *comparable to* [noun phrase] &middot; *roughly/broadly/directly comparable to* [noun phrase] &middot; *not comparable to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *comparable with* is the older analytic form and survives in technical writing, on the same logic that keeps *compared with* alive; *comparable to* is far commoner in American English and is safe everywhere. The related noun takes either preposition — *a comparison with* or *a comparison to*. In real-estate work the clipped noun *comps* (*we pulled three comps*) is standard and is not this preposition.
**Contrast:** Against *similar to*, which says two things resemble each other, *comparable to* says they are close enough in kind and in size that setting them side by side proves something — which is why an appraiser's *comparable* sale is a measurement rather than an impression. Against *on a par with*, it is descriptive rather than evaluative: a salary *comparable to* the market measures the same, while a salary *on a par with* the market is being judged adequate. Against *the same as*, it explicitly allows a gap. The negative *not comparable to* is the standard American way of refusing a comparison outright, and it is a stronger move than saying the two things merely differ.

Says two things are alike enough to be measured against each other. Its claim is about the legitimacy of the comparison rather than about how the comparison came out, which is exactly why appraisers, statisticians, and compensation committees reach for it.

1. Starting salaries here are *comparable to* what the state pays for the same work.
2. The two districts are not *comparable to* each other in size or in wealth.
3. A studio in this neighborhood rents for a figure *comparable to* a two-bedroom upstate.
4. The appraiser found three recent sales *comparable to* the subject property.
5. Running the county fair is *comparable to* running a small hotel for a week.
6. This year's storm damage is *comparable to* what the coast saw in 2011.
7. Nothing in the record is *comparable to* the penalty the board handed down.

**See also:** [similar to](#similar-to), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [the same as](#the-same-as), [compared with](#compared-with), [commensurate with](#commensurate-with), [relative to](#relative-to)

### compared to

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈpɛrd tu/ &middot; weak /kəmˈpɛrd tə/ &middot; *kum-PAIRD too*
**Русский:** по сравнению с — самый частый вариант; также «уподобляя чему-л. другому по природе»
**Type:** complex (a fossilized past participle — it needs no subject to control it, which is what makes it a preposition rather than a dangling modifier) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*compared to me*), gerund (*compared to driving*), or a free relative (*compared to what we paid last year*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *compared to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Compared to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [noun] *compared to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *as compared to* and *as compared with* are heavier forms carrying no extra meaning.
**Contrast:** **The traditional rule:** *compare to* likens two unlike things (*compared the city to an anthill*), while *compare with* sets two like things side by side to weigh them (*compared the 2019 figures with the 2020 figures*). That distinction is real in the **verb** and is still worth keeping there. In the **prepositional phrase**, American usage has largely collapsed it and *compared to* now serves both jobs, with *compared with* surviving as the more careful choice in analytical writing. Neither is an error. What *is* an error is a mismatched complement: *Compared to last year, sales are up* is fine; *Compared to last year, the company is bigger* compares a year with a company.

Sets the main clause against a benchmark — most often an earlier period, a rival, or the alternative that was not taken. It is the commonest comparison preposition in American English and the least formal of the multi-word set.

**1. Measured against a benchmark or an earlier state.**

1. *Compared to* last winter, this one has been mild.
2. Tuition is low *compared to* comparable private colleges.
3. Our commute is nothing *compared to* hers.

**2. Held up against something of a different kind, as a way of characterizing it — the likening use, where only *to* will do.**

4. *Compared to* a Broadway house, the theater is tiny.
5. *Compared to* what her parents went through, the move was easy.
6. A half marathon, *compared to* the full distance, is almost sociable.

**See also:** [compared with](#compared-with), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [than](#than), [relative to](#relative-to), [versus](#versus)

### compared with

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈpɛrd wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *kum-PAIRD with*
**Русский:** по сравнению с — при сопоставлении однородных величин; норма газетной и финансовой прозы
**Type:** complex (fossilized past participle) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the house style of most American newspapers and of financial reporting
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a free relative (*compared with what we paid last year*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *compared with* [noun phrase] &middot; *Compared with* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [figure], *compared with* [figure]
**Variants:** *as compared with* adds length and nothing else.
**Contrast:** The analytical member of the pair. *Compared with* implies that the two things are of the same kind and that you are weighing the differences between them; *compared to* is looser and can also liken unlike things. Where a sentence carries two figures, *compared with* is the traditional and still-preferred choice: *sales of $4.1 million, compared with $3.8 million a year ago*.

Puts two comparable things side by side so that the difference between them can be read off. In edited American prose it is the number-against-number comparison word, which is why it dominates earnings coverage and statistical writing.

1. The company earned $4.1 million, *compared with* $3.8 million a year earlier.
2. *Compared with* the coastal counties, the interior lost population.
3. Fuel use is down nine percent *compared with* the same month last year.
4. *Compared with* her earlier books, this one is tightly plotted.
5. The failure rate is negligible *compared with* the older design.
6. Emergency-room visits rose sharply *compared with* the previous quarter.

### equal to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈikwəl tu/ &middot; weak /ˈikwəl tə/ &middot; *EE-kwul too*
**Русский:** равный, равен — о величине; также «способный справиться» (equal to the task)
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal — *equal* is also a live adjective, a noun, and a verb) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in mathematics, finance, and contracts
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*equal to hers*), or a free relative (*equal to what the state contributes*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**. In the *equal to the task* frame the complement is always a noun naming a demand. ***Equal than* is an error**, and *equal with* survives only inside the frozen *on equal terms with*.
**Pattern:** [quantity] *is equal to* [quantity] &middot; [payment/share] *equal to* [amount] &middot; *be equal to* [the task / the occasion / the challenge] &middot; *equal in* [respect] *to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *equivalent to* is the near-synonym and the better choice when two things match in value but not in kind (*equivalent to three years' salary*). The **verb** *equal* takes no preposition at all (*the total equals nine hundred*), and *equate* takes *with* or *to* (*equating cost with quality*).
**Contrast:** Against *the same as*, which claims identity of kind as well as of amount, *equal to* compares quantity and nothing else: a payment *equal to* one month's rent need not be rent. Against *on a par with*, which judges standing, it measures. Against *comparable to*, it reports that the measurement came out even rather than that the comparison is legitimate. And keep the two senses apart — *equal to the task* has stopped being arithmetic and means "adequate for."

Says two quantities come out the same, or that a person or a thing is adequate to what is being asked of it. The first sense is the arithmetic one and is how contracts state amounts; the second is a fixed evaluative frame that takes a demand rather than a number.

**1. The same in amount, value, or measure.**

1. Each heir receives a share *equal to* one-fifth of the estate.
2. The deposit is *equal to* two months' rent.
3. Sales this quarter were almost exactly *equal to* last year's.
4. One hectare is *equal to* about two and a half acres.

**2. Adequate for; up to — the *equal to the task* frame.**

5. Nobody was sure she would be *equal to* the job, and she was.
6. The old furnace is not *equal to* a winter like this one.
7. He proved *equal to* the occasion, which is more than anyone expected.

### in comparison with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəmˈpɛrəsən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in kum-PAIR-uh-sun with*
**Русский:** в сравнении с — книжн., самый длинный вариант того же сравнения
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a free relative (*in comparison with what a new building would have cost*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *in comparison with* [noun phrase] &middot; *In comparison with* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *in comparison to* is equally standard in American English; *with* leans toward weighing two like things and *to* toward likening, but the two are freely interchanged. Bare *in comparison* is an adverbial, not a preposition.
**Contrast:** A heavier *compared with* and a fuller *by comparison with*. Where an ordinary comparative would carry the sentence — *This one is cheaper* — the phrase is padding, and its legitimate use is a long or fronted benchmark. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in comparison with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-comparison-with).

Sets the main clause beside a benchmark at full length. Everything it does, *compared with* does in fewer syllables; what it buys in exchange is weight at the front of a sentence and room for a complement too long to trail comfortably.

1. *In comparison with* the states that expanded coverage, ours has done badly.
2. The renovation was cheap *in comparison with* what a new building would have cost.
3. *In comparison with* his brother, he is practically a recluse.
4. Our staffing is thin *in comparison with* districts of the same enrollment.
5. The second storm was mild *in comparison with* the one that came through in January.

**See also:** [by comparison with](#by-comparison-with), [compared with](#compared-with), [relative to](#relative-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [in comparison with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-comparison-with)

### in proportion to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən tə/ &middot; *in pruh-POR-shun too*
**Русский:** пропорционально; соразмерно — одна величина меняется вместе с другой
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*out of all proportion to what actually happened*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in proportion to* [noun phrase] &middot; *in direct/inverse proportion to* [noun phrase] &middot; *out of (all) proportion to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in proportion with* occurs but is much rarer. *Out of proportion to* is the standard negative form, often intensified to *out of all proportion to*.
**Contrast:** Against *relative to*, which merely normalizes one quantity by another, *in proportion to* asserts that the two rise and fall together — which is why it is the natural English for a mathematical relation. Against *commensurate with*, it is arithmetic rather than evaluative: taxes *in proportion to* income are calculated, taxes *commensurate with* income are judged fair.

States that one quantity varies with another. In technical writing it means precisely that; in ordinary prose it turns up most often in the negative, where *out of all proportion to* is a complaint that the response did not match the provocation.

1. Aid is distributed *in proportion to* enrollment, not to need.
2. Drag increases *in proportion to* the square of the speed.
3. Members' dues rise *in proportion to* revenue.
4. The coverage was *out of all proportion to* the size of the protest.
5. Punishment should stand *in proportion to* the offense, not to the offender's record.
6. Their share of the cost is small *in proportion to* the benefit they receive.

**See also:** [commensurate with](#commensurate-with), [relative to](#relative-to), [in line with](#in-line-with), [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to)

### next to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛkst tu/ &middot; casual /ˈnɛks tə/, the medial *t* usually dropping &middot; *NEKST too*
**Русский:** по сравнению с; на фоне — разг.: рядом с эталоном второе выглядит бледно
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational
**Complement:** in the comparative sense, a noun phrase, an objective-case pronoun (*next to her*), or a free relative (*next to what the coasts pay*) — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** *Next to* [noun phrase], [clause] — nearly always fronted, and nearly always followed by an evaluation
**Contrast:** This entry covers only the comparative *next to*, "when you set it beside." The spatial sense — *the pharmacy next to the hardware store* — is at [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to), and the limiting *next to nothing* and ranking *next to* are at [next to](08-accompaniment-exception.md#next-to). The comparative sense is recognizable by its position: fronted, comma-set, and followed by a clause containing a judgment. Against *compared to*, it is conversational and always slightly unfavorable to the thing in the main clause.

Puts something beside a standard and lets the standard win. The construction is close to formulaic — *Next to X, Y looks* plus a judgment — and it is the most idiomatic short comparison in spoken American English.

1. *Next to* her, everyone else on the panel looked unprepared.
2. *Next to* the original, the reissue sounds thin.
3. *Next to* what the coasts pay, our property taxes are nothing.
4. *Next to* a real Chicago winter, this is a mild inconvenience.
5. *Next to* his first restaurant, the new place feels corporate.

**See also:** [compared to](#compared-to), [by comparison with](#by-comparison-with), [against](#against), [next to](11-complex-multiword.md#next-to)

### on a par with

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ə ˈpɑr wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; casual /ɑnəˈpɑr wəθ/, the three words running together &middot; *on-uh-PAR with*
**Русский:** наравне с; на одном уровне с — признаёт две вещи равными по качеству
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a free relative (*on a par with what the hospitals offer*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** *be on a par with* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *on a par with* [noun phrase] &middot; *roughly/about/nowhere near on a par with* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *on par with* — without the article — is now very common in American English and appears in edited prose, but *on a par with* is the traditional form and the safer one in formal writing. The financial *at par*, meaning "at face value," is a different expression entirely.
**Contrast:** Says the two things are of equal quality or standing, which is more than *similar to* (merely alike) and less than *the same as* (identical). Against *comparable to*, it is evaluative rather than measurable: a *comparable* salary is one you can look up, a salary *on a par with* the market is one being judged adequate. Note the spelling — *par*, from golf and from finance, never *pair*.

Ranks two things as equals. It is almost always deployed to defend or to dispute a claim of quality, which is why it collocates so heavily with *roughly*, *about*, and *nowhere near*.

1. Their pitching staff is *on a par with* anybody's in the division.
2. The state's math scores are roughly *on a par with* the national average.
3. Our benefits package is not *on a par with* what the hospitals offer.
4. He puts that record *on a par with* anything the band did later.
5. The apprenticeship is *on a par with* a two-year degree in the trades.

**See also:** [commensurate with](#commensurate-with), [similar to](#similar-to), [equal to](#equal-to), [in line with](#in-line-with), [relative to](#relative-to)

### relative to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɛlətɪv tu/ &middot; weak /ˈrɛlətɪv tə/ &middot; *REL-uh-tiv too*
**Русский:** относительно; по отношению к — по сравнению с выбранной точкой отсчёта; фин., науч.
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; standard in finance, physics, and statistics
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a free relative (*relative to what the coasts pay*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *relative to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Relative to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [noun] *relative to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The normalizing comparison word: *relative to* implies that the benchmark is being used to **scale** the first quantity, not merely to sit beside it. *Wages fell relative to housing costs* says the ratio moved; *Wages fell compared with housing costs* is looser about what actually happened. The unrelated bureaucratic sense "concerning" — *questions relative to the timeline* — is stiff English for *about* and is treated with the complex prepositions at [relative to](11-complex-multiword.md#relative-to); in the comparison field only the quantitative sense is live.

Measures one quantity against another that has been chosen as the reference point. Physics gave the word its precision — motion is defined only *relative to* a frame — and finance borrowed it wholesale for benchmark comparisons.

1. The fund has underperformed *relative to* its benchmark for three straight years.
2. Wages have fallen sharply *relative to* the cost of housing.
3. *Relative to* its population, the county has an unusual number of small airports.
4. Velocity is meaningless except *relative to* a chosen frame of reference.
5. Our defect rate is low *relative to* the industry, and still falling.
6. The dollar strengthened *relative to* every major currency last quarter.

**See also:** [in proportion to](#in-proportion-to), [compared with](#compared-with), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [vis-à-vis](#vis-à-vis), [relative to](11-complex-multiword.md#relative-to)

### than

**Pronunciation:** /ðæn/ &middot; weak /ðən/, and in fast speech a syllabic /ðn̩/ — the weak form is the normal one, and the strong form appears only under emphasis or in citation &middot; *THAN / thun*
**Русский:** чем — присоединяет второй член сравнения после сравнительной степени (taller than me — выше меня)
**Type:** simple — **and this is the whole problem: *than* is also a subordinating conjunction**, spelled and pronounced identically &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** as a preposition, a noun phrase or an **objective-case pronoun** (*taller than me*); as a conjunction, a finite clause (*taller than I am*) or a clause reduced by ellipsis. A free relative also works (*more than what we budgeted*). The fixed *than whom* is always prepositional and always objective. **Never a *that*-clause**, and **never a bare infinitive** except in the two frozen frames *rather than* (*chose to walk rather than wait*) and *would rather … than* (*I'd rather walk than wait*) — note that *no sooner … than* takes a **finite clause**, not an infinitive.
**Pattern:** [comparative adjective/adverb] *than* [noun phrase] &middot; [comparative] *than* [clause] &middot; *more/less/fewer* [noun] *than* [noun phrase] &middot; *other than*, *rather than*, *no sooner … than*, *no less a figure than* [noun phrase], *than whom*
**Contrast:** **Preposition or conjunction — the test is what follows it.** A bare noun makes it a preposition and calls for the objective case: *She's faster than me.* A clause makes it a conjunction, and the pronoun is that clause's subject: *She's faster than I am*, or by ellipsis *She's faster than I*. Both are correct English; *than I* with no following verb sounds stiff in American speech but is standard in formal writing. Ambiguity is the real reason to care — *He likes her more than me* and *He likes her more than I* mean different things, and restoring the verb (*more than I do*) is the fix. Keep *than* apart from *then*. And note that *different than* is standard American (see [different from](#different-from)) while *superior than*, *inferior than*, and *prefer … than* are errors: those words take *to*.

The comparison word proper. Everything else in this file names a benchmark; *than* attaches directly to a comparative and completes it, which is why it cannot be dropped and cannot be moved. It is also, in exactly the same shape, a conjunction, and the whole difficulty of the word lies in telling the two apart.

**1. After a comparative adjective or adverb.**

1. The second offer was better *than* the first by about nine thousand dollars.
2. She got there faster *than* anyone expected.
3. He's a good deal taller *than* me now.

**2. After *more*, *less*, *fewer*, *other*, *rather*, *else*.**

4. Fewer *than* two hundred of these were ever built.
5. There was nothing on the table other *than* a jar of pennies.
6. He'd rather drive all night *than* pay for a motel.

**3. The frozen frames.** *No sooner … than* takes a clause; *than whom* is the one place where the preposition is unmistakable.

7. No sooner had the tarp gone up *than* the wind took it.
8. She cited Learned Hand, *than whom* no American judge is more quoted.

**4. Not a preposition — the conjunction, with a clause after it.** Restoring the verb also settles any question about who is doing what.

9. He likes the dog more *than* I do.
10. The trip cost more *than* we had budgeted for the whole quarter.

**See also:** [as](#as), [different from](#different-from), [rather than](#rather-than), [compared to](#compared-to), [other than](08-accompaniment-exception.md#other-than)

### vis-à-vis

**Pronunciation:** /ˌviz ə ˈvi/ &middot; also /ˌvi zɑ ˈvi/ &middot; both written *s* letters are silent &middot; *veez-ah-VEE*
**Русский:** по отношению к; в сравнении с — книжн., из фр. «лицом к лицу»; о взаимном положении сторон
**Type:** complex (from French, literally "face to face") &middot; **Register:** formal, and faintly self-conscious in American prose
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *vis-à-vis* [noun phrase] &middot; *Vis-à-vis* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [noun] *vis-à-vis* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *vis-a-vis* and *vis à vis* both appear; the grave accent is optional in American text and the hyphens are standard. All spellings are pronounced alike.
**Contrast:** **Not a synonym for *versus*.** *Versus* sets two parties in conflict; *vis-à-vis* only positions one thing in relation to another and is neutral about whether they are fighting. Against *relative to*, it is less quantitative and more political — it is the standard word for one party's standing with respect to another. Its separate "concerning, with regard to" sense is treated with the prepositions of topic at [vis-à-vis](10-topic-reference-role.md#vis-à-vis).

States where one thing stands when it is set opposite another. Diplomacy and economics keep it alive because it names a relative position — of a currency, a bargaining party, an institution — without committing to a number or to a quarrel.

1. The dollar has weakened *vis-à-vis* the euro for six straight months.
2. Nobody has explained the university's position *vis-à-vis* the union.
3. Our costs are high *vis-à-vis* the rest of the industry.
4. The treaty leaves the smaller states with little leverage *vis-à-vis* their neighbors.
5. *Vis-à-vis* the previous administration, the shift is one of tone rather than of policy.

**See also:** [relative to](#relative-to), [versus](#versus), [as against](#as-against), [vis-à-vis](10-topic-reference-role.md#vis-à-vis)

## Contrast and opposition

Comparison notices a difference; contrast insists on it; opposition takes a side. The three shade into one another, and the useful distinctions are these: *in contrast to* only reports that two things differ, *contrary to* claims that one contradicts the other, and *in defiance of* adds that somebody knew and did it anyway. A separate trio reports that two accounts fail to square — *at odds with* in a newspaper, *inconsistent with* in a brief or a lab report, *at variance with* in the most formal register of all — and none of the three says which side is wrong. *Versus* and *against* are the plain words of conflict, and *pro* and *anti* — the two Latin prepositions that Americans have almost entirely converted into prefixes — are treated at [pro](#pro) and [anti](#anti).

### against

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; also /əˈɡeɪnst/, a minority American pronunciation and commoner in British English &middot; *uh-GENST / uh-GAYNST*
**Русский:** против; вопреки; на фоне — противостояние, состязание или сверка с эталоном
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*argued against raising the fee*), or a free relative (*against what the committee recommended*) — **never a finite clause**. Write *against the claim that he knew*, not *against he knew*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *against* [noun phrase] &middot; *as against* [noun phrase] &middot; stranded: *the amendment she voted* **against**
**Contrast:** This entry covers *against* in opposition, competition, and benchmarking. Its physical senses — forcible contact (*hail against the skylight*), leaning (*a bike against the fence*), and motion into a force (*rowing against the current*) — are treated with the prepositions of movement at [against](04-movement-direction.md#against). Against *versus*, which is a labeling word joining two names, *against* is the ordinary verb-governed preposition: teams *play against* each other, but the marquee reads *Ohio State versus Michigan*. Against *opposed to*, it is shorter and less deliberative.

Sets one thing in opposition to another — in argument, in a contest, or as a measuring stick. All the senses below share a single geometry, two things facing each other, and they differ only in whether the facing is hostile, competitive, evaluative, or merely visual.

**1. In opposition to a proposal, a person, or a course of action.**

1. Every member of the delegation voted *against* the bill.
2. Her lawyer advised *against* saying anything publicly.
3. Two of the three commissioners came out *against* the rezoning.

**2. In competition with.**

4. The Wolverines play *against* a ranked opponent for the third week running.
5. She ran *against* an incumbent and lost by four hundred votes.

**3. Measured or checked against a standard.**

6. The peso lost ground *against* the dollar.
7. Check the transcript *against* the recording before you file it.
8. Earnings are up nine percent *against* the same quarter last year.

**4. As protection or insurance from.**

9. The clinic vaccinates *against* four strains.
10. We're insured *against* flood but not *against* wind.

**5. Seen with something behind it — the visual backdrop, where the contrast is what makes the thing visible.**

11. Black branches *against* a white sky.
12. Her voice stood out *against* the noise of the platform.

**See also:** [versus](#versus), [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [as against](#as-against), [counter to](#counter-to), [up against](#up-against), [against](04-movement-direction.md#against)

### anti

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæntaɪ/ &middot; also /ˈænti/ — the /aɪ/ pronunciation is the American default and the /i/ one is heard in British English and in some fixed compounds &middot; *AN-ty / AN-tee*
**Русский:** против — разг.; в американском английском почти всегда приставка анти-, а не самостоятельный предлог
**Type:** simple (marginal — from the Greek preposition; in American English it is overwhelmingly a **prefix**, and its free-standing prepositional use is rare) &middot; **Register:** informal, and **chiefly British as a preposition**; American English uses the hyphenated prefix (*anti-tax*, *anti-union*) or the ordinary preposition *against*
**Complement:** noun phrase only, with or without a determiner (*anti unions*, *anti the whole idea*) — never a clause, never a gerund. Note that *anti* is also a **noun** (*the antis picketed the hearing*) and an **adjective** (*Are you pro or anti?*), and in neither of those does it take an object.
**Pattern:** *be anti* [noun phrase] &middot; *anti-* [noun] as a prefix — the American norm &middot; *pro or anti* — predicative, no object
**Variants:** American style hyphenates the prefix before a proper noun or a capital (*anti-American*) and closes up long-established combinations (*antifreeze*, *antibiotic*, *antitrust*).
**Contrast:** The mirror of [pro](#pro), and the two behave identically. Both are genuinely prepositional only in casual speech and both are more current in British English in that use. In American prose the natural choices are *opposed to* or *against*: *She is against the merger*, not *She is anti the merger*.

Declares the speaker on the opposite side of something. It is one of a very small number of English prepositions that are also live prefixes, and in the United States the prefix has all but crowded out the preposition.

1. He's been *anti* the whole redevelopment scheme from the start. (informal, British-flavored)
2. Nobody in that department is *anti* technology exactly — they just want training first.
3. She isn't *anti* unions; she's *anti* this particular contract.
4. Anti-tax groups spent heavily on the referendum. (prefix — the normal American form)
5. Are you pro or *anti*? (no object — here it is an adjective, not a preposition)

**See also:** [pro](#pro), [against](#against), [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [counter to](#counter-to)

### as distinct from

**Pronunciation:** /əz dɪˈstɪŋkt frʌm/ &middot; weak /əz dɪˈstɪŋkt frəm/ &middot; *uhz-dih-STINKT-frum*
**Русский:** в отличие от; в противоположность — книжн.: разграничивает два смешиваемых понятия
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; academic, legal, and philosophical
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a free relative (*as distinct from what they tell pollsters*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *as distinct from* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *as distinct from* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *as distinguished from* is the legal and philosophical form and means exactly the same thing; *as opposed to* is the everyday equivalent.
**Contrast:** Its job is to head off a confusion the reader might otherwise fall into, so its complement is always something the first item could plausibly be mistaken for. Against *as opposed to*, which usually carries a preference, *as distinct from* is purely taxonomic — it draws a line without taking a side. Against *rather than*, which rejects one of two options, it keeps both and merely separates them.

Marks a boundary between two things that habitually get run together. It is the workhorse of definitions and of any argument whose first move is to say what is *not* being claimed.

1. We are talking about market value *as distinct from* replacement cost.
2. The statute governs custody *as distinct from* visitation.
3. She studies how people vote, *as distinct from* what they tell pollsters.
4. Chronic pain, *as distinct from* the acute kind, rarely responds to that class of drug.
5. This is a question of etiquette *as distinct from* one of law.

**See also:** [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [rather than](#rather-than), [other than](08-accompaniment-exception.md#other-than)

### as opposed to

**Pronunciation:** /əz əˈpoʊzd tu/ &middot; weak /əz əˈpoʊzd tə/ &middot; *uhz-uh-POHZD too*
**Русский:** в отличие от; а не — называет тот вариант, о котором речь как раз не идёт
**Type:** complex (fossilized past participle) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*as opposed to waiting*), or an adjective or prepositional phrase parallel to whatever precedes it (*careless as opposed to malicious*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [X] *as opposed to* [Y] &middot; [clause], *as opposed to* [noun phrase] &middot; *As opposed to* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *opposed to* without *as* is a predicative adjective (*I am opposed to the plan*) — a different construction with a different job, since that one states the speaker's position while this one contrasts two options.
**Contrast:** The everyday form of *as distinct from*, and unlike it this one usually carries a preference: whatever comes after *as opposed to* is the rejected option. That is the exact reverse of *in favor of*, where what follows is the option that won. Against *rather than*, which is nearly interchangeable, *as opposed to* more often clarifies a category and *rather than* more often reports a choice. Against *instead of*, which substitutes one thing for another, *as opposed to* only contrasts them.

Names the alternative you are *not* talking about, so the reader knows which of two readings to take. Its complement must be grammatically parallel to the thing contrasted with it — that parallelism is the whole discipline of the phrase.

1. We're hiring a contractor, *as opposed to* a full-time employee.
2. The problem is structural *as opposed to* cosmetic.
3. She wanted to be judged on the work, *as opposed to* on who recommended her.
4. He took the train *as opposed to* driving, which added two hours.
5. This is a recommendation *as opposed to* a requirement.
6. *As opposed to* the coastal route, the inland highway stays open all winter.

**See also:** [rather than](#rather-than), [as distinct from](#as-distinct-from), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [instead of](11-complex-multiword.md#instead-of)

### at loggerheads with

**Pronunciation:** /ət ˈlɔɡərhɛdz wɪθ/ &middot; weak /ət ˈlɔɡərhɛdz wəθ/ &middot; also /ˈlɑɡərhɛdz/ wherever *cot* and *caught* have merged &middot; full /æt/ &middot; *at-LOG-ur-hedz-with*
**Русский:** на ножах с; в затяжной ссоре с — разг./газетн., о зашедшем в тупик конфликте
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; journalistic — a headline and lead-paragraph phrase more than a conversational one
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a **person, a group, or an institution** — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**, and, unlike *at odds with*, **never a document, an account, or a set of figures**: *his testimony is at loggerheads with the log* is wrong, and the repair is *at odds with*. The subject of the quarrel is named with *over*, not with a second *with*.
**Pattern:** *be at loggerheads with* [person/body] &middot; *be at loggerheads over* [issue] &middot; *the two sides are at loggerheads* — adverbial, no complement
**Variants:** *at loggerheads* with no complement is the commonest form of all (*the two agencies have been at loggerheads all year*) and is an adverbial rather than this preposition. There is no singular *at loggerhead*; the noun is a seventeenth-century word of disputed origin and never appears here in the singular.
**Contrast:** The vivid cousin of [at odds with](#at-odds-with), and narrower in two ways. *At odds with* covers people and evidence alike; this one covers only parties. And *at loggerheads with* adds **deadlock**: the quarrel has been running a while and neither side is moving, which is why it collocates so heavily with *have been* and *remain*. Against *in opposition to*, which states a position formally taken, this reports a state of relations.

Says two parties are locked in a quarrel that is going nowhere. Reporters reach for it when the news is not that a disagreement exists but that it has hardened, which is why the phrase so often arrives with a stretch of time attached.

1. The mayor has been *at loggerheads with* the council since the budget vote.
2. Two trustees remain *at loggerheads with* the rest of the board over the endowment.
3. The union is *at loggerheads with* management on scheduling and on nothing else.
4. She spent a decade *at loggerheads with* the agency she now runs.
5. The two states are *at loggerheads with* each other over water rights that were supposed to be settled in 1922.
6. Both departments were *at loggerheads with* the contractor by the time the slab was poured.

**See also:** [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [at variance with](#at-variance-with), [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [up against](#up-against), [against](#against)

### at odds with

**Pronunciation:** /ət ˈɑdz wɪθ/ &middot; weak /ət ˈɑdz wəθ/ &middot; full /æt ˈɑdz wɪθ/ &middot; *at-ODZ-with*
**Русский:** в разладе с; не согласуется с — о ссорящихся людях и о противоречащих друг другу сведениях
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a free relative (*at odds with what the audit found*) — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** *be at odds with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at odds with* [noun phrase] &middot; *at odds over* [topic] — the subject of the quarrel takes *over*, not *with*
**Variants:** *at odds* alone, with no complement, means the parties disagree with one another: *The two agencies have been at odds all year.*
**Contrast:** Against *at variance with*, which is formal and used almost exclusively of statements and data, *at odds with* covers both people and evidence and is at home in a newspaper. Note the two prepositions in a single frame: you are *at odds with* a person *over* an issue, and swapping them is the usual error.

Says two things fail to fit together. What is at odds may be two people who cannot agree or two accounts that cannot both be true, and English uses one phrase for both.

**1. In conflict with a person or a body.**

1. The governor has been *at odds with* her own party since the special session.
2. He found himself *at odds with* every other member of the committee.
3. The two agencies are *at odds with* each other over who pays for the cleanup.

**2. Inconsistent with a fact, an account, or a principle.**

4. His testimony is *at odds with* the security footage.
5. The policy is *at odds with* everything the company says publicly.
6. That figure is badly *at odds with* the county's own estimate.

**See also:** [at variance with](#at-variance-with), [inconsistent with](#inconsistent-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [counter to](#counter-to), [consistent with](#consistent-with)

### at variance with

**Pronunciation:** /ət ˈvɛriəns wɪθ/ &middot; weak /ət ˈvɛriəns wəθ/ &middot; full /æt ˈvɛriəns wɪθ/ &middot; *at-VAIR-ee-unss-with*
**Русский:** расходится с; не согласуется с — книжн., юр. и науч.: два документа или результата не сходятся
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, scientific, and administrative
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*at variance with what the other witnesses said*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** *be at variance with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at variance with* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** none. In land-use law *a variance* is a permitted exception to a zoning rule and has nothing to do with this phrase.
**Contrast:** The formal twin of *at odds with*, and narrower: it is used of statements, records, and measurements, and only rarely of people. Against *inconsistent with*, it is more literary; against *contrary to*, it reports a mismatch rather than an act of defiance.

Reports that two accounts, records, or results fail to agree. It survives chiefly because auditors, judges, and scientists need a neutral way of saying that two things which ought to match do not.

1. The invoice totals are *at variance with* the shipping records.
2. His account of the meeting is *at variance with* the minutes.
3. The finding is *at variance with* every earlier study of the same population.
4. The building as constructed is *at variance with* the approved drawings.
5. Nothing in her statement is *at variance with* what the other witnesses said.

**See also:** [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [inconsistent with](#inconsistent-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with)

### contrary to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrɛri tu/ &middot; weak /ˈkɑntrɛri tə/ &middot; stress on the first syllable; the unrelated adjective meaning "perverse" is /kənˈtrɛri/, *kun-TRAIR-ee* &middot; *KAHN-trair-ee too*
**Русский:** вопреки; в противоречие с — не просто отличие, а опровержение правила или ожидания
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*contrary to what the ads say*) — **never a *that*-clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** *Contrary to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *contrary to* [noun phrase] &middot; *run contrary to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** none. *Evidence to the contrary* and *on the contrary* are fixed phrases built on the noun *contrary*, not on this preposition.
**Contrast:** Against *in contrast to*, which merely sets two things side by side, *contrary to* asserts that one **contradicts** the other: *in contrast to the forecast* means the weather differed from it, while *contrary to the forecast* means the forecast was wrong. Against *counter to*, which is nearly identical, *contrary to* is the commoner and slightly more formal. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [contrary to](11-complex-multiword.md#contrary-to).

Asserts that one thing contradicts another — a rule, an instruction, an expectation, or a widely held belief. It is stronger than difference: something *contrary to* the forecast does not merely differ from it, it shows the forecast to have been wrong.

**1. In conflict with a rule, an instruction, or an expectation.**

1. The valves were installed *contrary to* the manufacturer's instructions.
2. He acted *contrary to* the advice of every lawyer in the room.
3. The ruling runs *contrary to* forty years of practice in this circuit.

**2. The corrective opener — unlike what people generally assume.**

4. *Contrary to* popular belief, most avalanches are triggered by their victims.
5. *Contrary to* what the brochure implies, the shuttle runs only twice a day.
6. *Contrary to* expectations, the smaller school won by nineteen.

**See also:** [counter to](#counter-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [contrary to](11-complex-multiword.md#contrary-to)

### counter to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaʊntər tu/ &middot; weak /ˈkaʊntər tə/ &middot; *KOWN-tur too*
**Русский:** вразрез с; в противоположном направлении от — метафора механическая, а не моральная
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*counter to what the data show*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** *run counter to* [noun phrase] &middot; *fly counter to* [noun phrase] &middot; [be/act] *counter to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Almost always used with *run*: *run counter to* is the collocation, and the bare *counter to expectations* is a shortening of it. Against *contrary to*, it is a shade more physical, as if two forces were moving in opposite directions; against *in defiance of*, it implies no intent at all, since a fact can run *counter to* a theory without meaning anything by it. Distinguish the verb *counter* (*she countered the objection*) and the adverb (*the current runs counter*), neither of which governs an object with *to*.

Says that one thing works in the opposite direction from another. The metaphor is mechanical rather than moral, which makes it the natural word for evidence that cuts against a hypothesis.

1. The findings run *counter to* thirty years of received wisdom.
2. That policy runs *counter to* everything the agency was set up to do.
3. *Counter to* expectations, enrollment held steady.
4. Nothing in the report is *counter to* the union's position.
5. Selling into a panic runs *counter to* every instinct, which is exactly why it works.

**See also:** [contrary to](#contrary-to), [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [in defiance of](#in-defiance-of), [against](#against)

### despite

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈspaɪt/ &middot; weak /dəˈspaɪt/ &middot; *dih-SPYT*
**Русский:** несмотря на — уступка: названное обстоятельство должно было помешать, но не помешало
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*despite knowing better*) — **never a finite clause.** To put a clause after it you must expand to *despite the fact that*.
**Pattern:** *Despite* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *despite* [noun phrase] &middot; *despite* [verb-ing] &middot; *despite the fact that* [clause]
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *in spite of* in every context; *despite* is simply shorter, which is why editors prefer it. ***Despite of* is always an error** — it is a blend of the two, and it is the single most common mistake made with this word. Against *notwithstanding*, which is heavier, legal in flavor, and uniquely able to follow its object (*the objections notwithstanding*); *despite* can only precede. Against *regardless of*, which means "without taking into account" rather than "even though" — *she was hired regardless of her experience* says experience was not considered, while *despite her experience* says it counted against her. Note that the concessive **conjunctions** *although* and *even though* take the finite clause that *despite* refuses: see [although](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/07-concession.md#although).

Concedes a fact that would normally have prevented what the main clause reports. It subordinates that fact rather than balancing it, so whatever sits in the *despite* phrase is the obstacle and the main clause carries the point.

1. *Despite* the rain, the game started on time.
2. She got the job *despite* having no formal training.
3. The bridge held *despite* decades of deferred maintenance.
4. He kept his voice level *despite* being furious.
5. *Despite* everything that had happened that year, they stayed friends.
6. The bill passed *despite* the fact that no one had read it.

**See also:** [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of), [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), [regardless of](11-complex-multiword.md#regardless-of), [contrary to](#contrary-to)

### in contrast to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑntræst tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈkɑntræst tə/ &middot; the noun *contrast* is stressed on the first syllable; the verb *contrast* is /kənˈtræst/, *kun-TRAST* &middot; *in KAHN-trast too*
**Русский:** в отличие от; в противоположность — прямо противопоставляет два однородных предмета
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a free relative (*in contrast to what the survey found*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** *In contrast to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *in contrast to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in contrast with* is equally standard in American English. Bare *in contrast*, with no complement, is a conjunctive adverb rather than a preposition: *Sales fell. In contrast, margins improved.* ***In contrast of* is an error.**
**Contrast:** Against *unlike*, which is one word and does the same work, *in contrast to* is heavier and likelier to be fronted for emphasis. Against *contrary to*, it makes no claim that either side is mistaken — it only reports a difference. The **parallelism rule** is strict here: *In contrast to the 1990s, today's market is volatile* compares a decade with a market. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in contrast to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-contrast-to).

Sets its object against the main clause as the opposite case and warns the reader in advance to expect a difference. The two halves have to be the same kind of thing, or the sentence collapses into a false comparison.

1. *In contrast to* the coasts, the interior stayed dry all summer.
2. Her second novel, *in contrast to* the first, is almost cheerful.
3. *In contrast to* what the survey found, actual turnout was lowest among the youngest voters.
4. The northern district gained population, *in contrast to* every other part of the state.
5. *In contrast to* earlier models, this one runs almost silently.
6. Enrollment rose here, *in contrast with* a statewide decline of four percent.

**See also:** [unlike](#unlike), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [by comparison with](#by-comparison-with), [in contrast to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-contrast-to)

### in defiance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn dɪˈfaɪəns əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn dɪˈfaɪəns ə/ &middot; *in dih-FYE-unss uhv*
**Русский:** вопреки; в нарушение — сделано сознательно наперекор приказу, запрету или предупреждению
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; journalistic
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund, though the noun phrase is far commoner — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *in defiance of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In defiance of* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Unlike *contrary to* and *counter to*, this phrase attributes **intent**: you act *in defiance of* an order only if you knew about it. Against *in violation of*, which is the neutral legal statement that a rule was broken, *in defiance of* characterizes the actor's attitude. Against *notwithstanding* and *despite*, which merely concede an obstacle, it asserts deliberate refusal.

Says the act was done knowingly against an authority, an order, or a warning. It is a strong word and a judgment, which is why careful reporters spend it sparingly.

1. The vessel sailed *in defiance of* a Coast Guard order.
2. They kept the clinic open *in defiance of* the injunction.
3. *In defiance of* every forecast, the incumbent won going away.
4. The mural went up overnight *in defiance of* the historical commission.
5. She published the memo *in defiance of* a direct instruction from counsel.

**See also:** [counter to](#counter-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of)

### in despite of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn dɪˈspaɪt əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn dəˈspaɪt ə/ &middot; *in dih-SPYT uhv*
**Русский:** наперекор; несмотря на — устар., книжн.: старая полная форма despite (Шекспир, старые тексты)
**Type:** complex (the ancestor of the one-word *despite*, which is what was left after the frame wore away) &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** It is ordinary in Middle English and still current through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; by the nineteenth it is already a period effect, and in modern American prose it survives only in quotation, in historical narrative, or as deliberate flavor. **The living forms are *despite* and *in spite of*, and nothing in this entry should be read as ordinary modern usage.**
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — **never a finite clause.** In older texts the complement is very often a person or a personified force (*in despite of Fortune*, *in despite of the abbot*), and that is a good clue that the older, stronger sense is meant.
**Pattern:** *in despite of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in despite of* [person] — the older "in scorn of, to the vexation of" frame
**Variants:** the modern equivalents are [despite](#despite) and [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of). Note that ***despite of*** — the phrase with *in* dropped — **is an error in modern English**, and the reason writers keep producing it by accident is that it is a half-memory of this genuine older form. Middle English also had the bare *maugre*, now wholly obsolete.
**Contrast:** Two things separate it from modern *despite*. **Register:** nobody writes it today without meaning to sound old. **Meaning:** the older phrase leans toward *in defiance of*, because the noun *despite* originally meant contempt — *in despite of the law* meant "in scorn of it," not merely "even though it existed." Against *in spite of*, which is the same phrase worn smooth and now purely concessive, *in despite of* keeps the sting.

The old, long form of *despite*, and the one that still carries the word's original charge of contempt. A modern American meets it in Shakespeare, in the older strata of legal and devotional English, and in writing that is imitating them.

**1. In defiance of; in scorn of — the older and stronger sense.**

1. He held the pass *in despite of* every order to withdraw.
2. The charter was confirmed *in despite of* the abbot's protest.
3. She kept the old spelling *in despite of* her editors — a deliberate period joke in a modern sentence.

**2. Concessive: even though — the sense that survived into modern *despite*.**

4. *In despite of* wind and tide, they made the harbor before dark.
5. *In despite of* Fortune, the family prospered another eighty years.
6. He married her *in despite of* his father; a modern writer would say *despite his father's objections*.

### in opposition to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˌɑpəˈzɪʃən tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˌɑpəˈzɪʃən tə/ &middot; *in ah-puh-ZISH-un too*
**Русский:** против; в противовес — книжн., юр. и полит.: то, чему акт или документ противопоставлен
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; parliamentary, legal, and academic
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in opposition to raising the cap*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *in opposition to* [noun phrase] &middot; *In opposition to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [document] *in opposition to* [motion]
**Variants:** *in opposition* alone is a standing political phrase (*the party in opposition*) and is not this preposition.
**Contrast:** The formal counterpart of *against* and the exact mirror of [in support of](#in-support-of). It is the register of filings and floor speeches: a brief is filed *in opposition to* a motion, but a neighbor simply speaks *against* the permit. Against the predicative adjective *opposed to*, which describes a person's standing position, this phrase attaches to an act.

Names what an act, a document, or a movement is set against. It is the standard caption language of American motion practice — every *Opposition to Motion to Dismiss* is one — and it carries that deliberative weight into ordinary prose.

1. Plaintiffs filed a brief *in opposition to* the motion for summary judgment.
2. Three council members spoke *in opposition to* the variance.
3. *In opposition to* the leadership, a dozen members voted with the minority.
4. The letter was signed by sixty economists *in opposition to* the tariff.
5. She organized *in opposition to* the highway extension for eleven years.

### in the teeth of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈtiθ əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈtiθ ə/ &middot; *in-thuh-TEETH uhv*
**Русский:** наперекор; прямо навстречу — книжн., образн.: против ветра, сопротивления, очевидных фактов
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to literary; still live in journalism, where it is a heightened alternative to *in the face of*
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming **a force that is pushing back** — a gale, a boycott, an injunction, resistance, the evidence. **Never a finite clause and never a gerund**: *in the teeth of knowing better* is not English. The complement must be capable of opposing something, which is why *in the teeth of a mild afternoon* is nonsense while *in the teeth of a February gale* is not.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the teeth of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In the teeth of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *in the teeth of the wind/the gale* — the original nautical frame
**Variants:** [in the face of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-face-of) is the plain modern equivalent and is far commoner; *in the teeth of* keeps the picture of a headwind. There is no singular *in the tooth of*, and *into the teeth of* is a phrase of movement, not this one.
**Contrast:** Against *despite*, which merely concedes an obstacle, *in the teeth of* insists the obstacle was actively resisting — the metaphor is a boat beating to windward. Against *in defiance of*, which attributes an intent to disobey some authority, this attributes only persistence against a force, and the force need not be anybody's decision. Against *in the face of*, it is stronger and a shade literary, and it is the only one of the three that still works literally, of wind.

Says the thing was done straight into whatever was resisting it. The phrase comes off a sailing ship, and the picture of a vessel driving to windward is still faintly present every time it is used of a vote, a boycott, or a body of evidence.

**1. Against active opposition or contrary evidence — the ordinary modern use.**

1. The bill passed *in the teeth of* the strongest lobbying campaign in years.
2. She won the seat *in the teeth of* her own party's leadership.
3. *In the teeth of* the evidence, he went on insisting the numbers were fine.
4. They rebuilt the pier *in the teeth of* two more storms that winter.

**2. Straight into the wind — the literal nautical original.**

5. The little boat beat north *in the teeth of* a February gale.
6. The crew worked all night *in the teeth of* forty-knot winds.

**See also:** [in the face of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-face-of), [in defiance of](#in-defiance-of), [despite](#despite), [against](#against), [up against](#up-against)

### inconsistent with

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnkənˈsɪstənt wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in-kun-SISS-tunt with*
**Русский:** несовместимый с; противоречащий — книжн.: два утверждения не могут быть верны одновременно
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; legal, medical, scientific, and administrative
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*inconsistent with what the witness told police*) — **never a finite clause**; a gerund is possible but rare. ***Inconsistent to* and *inconsistent from* are errors** — the preposition is *with*.
**Pattern:** *be inconsistent with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *inconsistent with* [noun phrase] &middot; *wholly/materially/facially inconsistent with* [noun phrase] &middot; *to the extent inconsistent with* [instrument] — the contract formula
**Variants:** none. Distinguish the ordinary adjective *inconsistent* meaning "erratic" (*an inconsistent pitcher*), which takes no complement at all, and the noun *inconsistency*, which takes *in* (*an inconsistency in the record*) or *between* (*inconsistencies between the two accounts*).
**Contrast:** The exact negative of [consistent with](#consistent-with), and it inherits that phrase's modesty in reverse: it says two things cannot both be right and stops short of saying which one is wrong. Against *contrary to*, which claims that one side contradicts a rule or an expectation, *inconsistent with* reports a clash between two accounts and assigns no blame. Against *at odds with*, it is the more analytic and the likelier in a brief; against *at variance with*, it is far commoner in American English and covers people as well as records.

Says two things cannot both be true, or that one does not square with the other. It is the standard American negative in law, medicine, and audit, and its whole usefulness is that it identifies a conflict without characterizing anybody's conduct.

**1. Not reconcilable with an account, a record, or the evidence.**

1. His statement is *inconsistent with* the timeline in the dispatch log.
2. The injuries are *inconsistent with* a fall.
3. Nothing in the deposition is *inconsistent with* what she told the police that night.
4. The reading is *inconsistent with* everything the other sensors recorded.

**2. Not reconcilable with a rule, a policy, or a governing instrument.**

5. Any provision *inconsistent with* federal law is void.
6. The board's practice has been *inconsistent with* its own written policy for years.
7. State rules apply except to the extent they are *inconsistent with* the collective bargaining agreement.

**See also:** [consistent with](#consistent-with), [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [at variance with](#at-variance-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in violation of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-violation-of)

### opposite to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑpəzɪt tu/ &middot; weak /ˈɑpəzɪt tə/ &middot; *AH-puh-zit too*
**Русский:** противоположный чему-л. — обратное по смыслу; в значении «напротив» американцы говорят across from
**Type:** complex — though the bare preposition *opposite*, with no *to*, is the ordinary American form &middot; **Register:** formal; **in the spatial sense *opposite to* is chiefly British**, and American English says *across from* or bare *opposite*
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** [noun] *opposite to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun/clause] *opposite* [noun phrase] — the bare American form &middot; *play/star opposite* [person]
**Variants:** **bare *opposite*** (*the house opposite ours*, *she sat opposite him*) is standard in both varieties and is the normal American choice; *opposite from* is nonstandard; *across from* is the everyday American spatial equivalent.
**Contrast:** Three different things share this shape. The **spatial** *opposite* means "facing," and Americans usually say *across from*. The **abstract** *opposite to* means "the reverse of" and is fully current in American writing — *an effect opposite to the one intended*. The **theatrical** *opposite* means "as the co-star of" and never takes *to* at all. Against *in front of*, *opposite* requires the two things to face each other; against *contrary to*, it describes a relation rather than a conflict. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [opposite to](11-complex-multiword.md#opposite-to).

Places one thing facing another, literally or figuratively. Of the three constructions that share this shape, only the abstract "reverse of" sense is fully American; the other two either prefer *across from* or drop the *to* altogether.

**1. The reverse of — abstract, and the live American sense.**

1. The drug had an effect exactly *opposite to* the one intended.
2. Her conclusions are *opposite to* his in every particular.
3. The current runs *opposite to* the prevailing wind along this stretch.

**2. Facing; across from — spatial. Here *opposite to* is British and Americans say *across from* or bare *opposite*.**

4. The pharmacy is *opposite to* the courthouse. (British; American: *across from the courthouse*)
5. She sat *opposite* him for two hours and said nothing. (bare — standard American)

**3. As the co-star of — theater and film, always bare.**

6. He played *opposite* Katharine Hepburn in three pictures.
7. She was cast *opposite* an actor she had never met.

**See also:** [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [in front of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-front-of), [opposite to](11-complex-multiword.md#opposite-to)

### out of sync with

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊt əv ˈsɪŋk wɪθ/ &middot; weak /ˌaʊd ə ˈsɪŋk wəθ/, the medial *t* flapping and *of* reducing to a single vowel &middot; *owt-uv-SINK with*
**Русский:** не в такт с; рассогласован с — разг.: сбой в синхронности или согласованности, а не в истинности
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; conversational and journalistic
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or a free relative (*out of sync with what people said at the hearings*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** *Out of sync with running the meeting* is not English; write *out of sync with the way the meeting is run*.
**Pattern:** [be/fall/get/drift] *out of sync with* [noun phrase] &middot; *badly/wildly/increasingly out of sync with* [noun phrase] &middot; *out of sync* — adverbial, no complement
**Variants:** *out of synch with* is an accepted spelling and is losing ground. *Out of step with* is the near-synonym and is the better choice where the mismatch is one of politics or opinion rather than of timing.
**Contrast:** The everyday member of the mismatch family, and the one that survives best in speech. Against *inconsistent with*, which says two accounts cannot both be true, *out of sync with* says only that two things are not moving together — a schedule, a market, a mood. Against *at variance with*, it is not a finding: nobody writes it in an audit. Against *at odds with*, it implies no quarrel, only drift. Its positive is [in sync with](#in-sync-with), which is treated with the prepositions of alignment.

Reports that two things which ought to be moving together are not. What has gone wrong is timing or alignment rather than truth, which is why the phrase covers an audio track, a bus schedule, and a politician's instincts with no change of meaning.

1. The bus schedule is *out of sync with* the ferry, and always has been.
2. Their pay scale has fallen badly *out of sync with* the rest of the region.
3. The audio drifts *out of sync with* the picture about ten minutes in.
4. He sounded *out of sync with* his own party for the first time in years.
5. The council's priorities are wildly *out of sync with* what people said at the hearings.
6. Planting dates are increasingly *out of sync with* the last frost.

**See also:** [in sync with](#in-sync-with), [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [inconsistent with](#inconsistent-with), [at variance with](#at-variance-with), [in line with](#in-line-with)

### up against

**Pronunciation:** /ʌp əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *up-uh-GENST*
**Русский:** перед лицом чего-л.; столкнуться с — разг.: препятствие, которое надо преодолеть
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund. The idiom *up against it*, meaning "in serious trouble," takes only the fixed pronoun *it*.
**Pattern:** *be up against* [noun phrase] &middot; *come up against* [noun phrase] &middot; *be up against it* — fixed idiom
**Contrast:** Where *against* names an opponent neutrally, *up against* casts the subject as the underdog: *We're playing against Central* is a schedule, *We're up against Central* is a warning. The literal spatial *up against the wall*, meaning touching it, belongs with the contact senses of [against](04-movement-direction.md#against); in the sense treated here the object is always an obstacle, a deadline, or a stronger opponent.

Names the obstacle the subject has to get past. It is the idiom of tight deadlines, long odds, and better-funded opponents, and it carries a note of strain that plain *against* does not.

1. We're *up against* a Friday deadline and two people are out sick.
2. You have no idea what she was *up against* that first year.
3. Small growers are *up against* buyers who set the price.
4. The campaign came *up against* a spending limit it had not planned for.
5. After the second surgery they were really *up against* it.

**See also:** [against](#against), [at odds with](#at-odds-with), [in the face of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-face-of), [against](04-movement-direction.md#against)

### versus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɜrsəs/ &middot; commonly /ˈvɜrsəz/ in speech &middot; *VUR-suss*
**Русский:** против; в противовес — в состязаниях, судебных делах и при выборе из двух (сокр. vs., v.)
**Type:** simple (Latin, "turned against") &middot; **Register:** neutral; the abbreviations are the usual written forms
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a **bare** one with no determiner (*quality versus quantity*). It joins two parallel constituents of almost any kind — nouns, gerunds, adjectives, even prepositional phrases — and in headlines and titles it frequently joins them with no verb in the sentence at all. Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [X] *versus* [Y] &middot; [X] *vs.* [Y] &middot; [case name] *v.* [case name]
**Variants:** ***vs.*** is the general abbreviation and takes a period in American usage (*Yankees vs. Red Sox*); ***v.*** is reserved for the names of court cases (*Brown v. Board of Education*), where it is read aloud as "versus," or by many lawyers as "against" or simply "and." AP style uses *vs.*; some sports usage drops the period.
**Contrast:** **Not a synonym for *vis-à-vis*.** *Versus* asserts conflict or an either/or choice, while *vis-à-vis* only names a relative position. Against *against*, *versus* is a labeling word rather than a verb-governed preposition: you *play against* a team, but the poster reads *Duke versus Kentucky*. Against *as opposed to*, it is more schematic — it frames a clean binary, which is why it dominates headlines and slide titles. In running formal prose, spell it out rather than abbreviating.

Names the two sides of a contest, a case, or a choice. It is a labeling word rather than a sentence word — it can join two nouns with no verb anywhere in sight, which is exactly what headlines, dockets, and marquees need.

**1. Against, in a contest or a lawsuit.**

1. The regional final is Lincoln *versus* Grant, and it will be sold out.
2. The Court's decision in *Miranda v. Arizona* is more than half a century old.
3. It came down to the union *versus* the city, with the state watching.

**2. As opposed to — a choice between two options or two framings.**

4. The whole debate is speed *versus* accuracy.
5. She had to weigh a bigger salary *versus* another two years of training.
6. Renting *versus* buying looks very different at these rates.
7. What matters is what the policy covers *versus* what people think it covers.

**See also:** [against](#against), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [as against](#as-against), [vis-à-vis](#vis-à-vis), [rather than](#rather-than)

## Preference: choosing one thing over another

A small, tight family. All three name two options and tell you which one survived — and the position of the surviving option differs from word to word, which is where the errors come from. After *over* and *in favor of*, the winner follows the preposition; after *rather than*, *as opposed to*, and *in preference to*, the **loser** does.

### in preference to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈprɛfrəns tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈprɛfrəns tə/ &middot; *in PREF-runss too*
**Русский:** предпочтительнее, чем; вместо — книжн.; после него стоит отвергнутый вариант
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [choose/use/appoint X] *in preference to* [Y] &middot; [clause] *in preference to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Note that the **verb** *prefer* takes *to*, not *than*: *prefer coffee to tea*, never *prefer coffee than tea*. With infinitives, *rather than* is the idiom: *I'd prefer to walk rather than wait*. *In preference to* is the noun-phrase form of the same idea; against *in favor of*, it is about taste rather than about dropping one option to take up another.

Names the option that was passed over. It is stiffer than *rather than* and much stiffer than *over*, and its natural home is a policy document or a formal recommendation.

1. The committee chose the smaller site *in preference to* the riverfront parcel.
2. Use the hex bolts *in preference to* the screws that came in the box.
3. She was appointed *in preference to* two more senior candidates.
4. Most readers still take the printed edition *in preference to* the app.
5. The state funded rail *in preference to* widening the interstate.

**See also:** [rather than](#rather-than), [over](#over), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [instead of](11-complex-multiword.md#instead-of)

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; a stressed word with no weak form; the clipped *o'er* /ɔr/ is poetic only &middot; *OH-vur*
**Русский:** предпочесть чему-л.; выше, над — выбор в пользу первого, а также превосходство в ранге или счёте
**Type:** simple — also an adverb (*it tipped over*), a verb particle (*think it over*), an adjective (*the game is over*), and a noun in cricket &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*chose settling over going to trial*), or a free relative (*over what the state was offering*) — never a finite clause. **The preposition always has an object; the adverb and the verb particle never do** (*it tipped over*, *think it over*), and that is the test for which word you are looking at.
**Pattern:** [choose/pick/prefer/take/select X] *over* [Y] &middot; [advantage/edge/lead/priority/claim] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb of authority] *over* [noun phrase] &middot; not prepositional: [verb] *over* with no object
**Contrast:** This entry covers only *over* in preference and precedence. Its spatial senses are at [over](04-movement-direction.md#over), its duration sense at [over](05-time.md#over), and its "subject of a quarrel" sense at [over](10-topic-reference-role.md#over). In the preference sense it competes with *rather than* and *instead of*: *over* needs two noun phrases and a verb of choosing, *rather than* can take verbs as well, and *instead of* implies substitution rather than ranking. Note that the verb *prefer* traditionally takes *to* (*prefer tea to coffee*), though *prefer X over Y* is now common in American English and is no longer worth objecting to.

Ranks one thing above another. In every sense below two candidates are in play and *over* names the winner, whether the contest is a decision, a legal claim, a chain of command, or a scoreboard.

**1. Chosen in place of — after a verb of choosing.**

1. She took the teaching job *over* two offers that paid more.
2. The board picked the local firm *over* a national one.
3. Voters chose the bond measure *over* a sales-tax increase by nine points.

**2. Ahead of, in rank or in claim.**

4. Secured creditors are paid *over* everyone else.
5. Seniority gives him first claim *over* the summer shifts.
6. Safety takes priority *over* the schedule, and it says so on the wall.

**3. In authority above.**

7. She has three supervisors *over* her and nobody under her.
8. Congress has no authority *over* a state's ballot design in that respect.

**4. Superiority in a contest or a measurement — the margin sense.**

9. The Tigers took a four-run lead *over* Cleveland in the fifth.
10. The alloy's advantage *over* steel is weight, not strength.

**5. Not a preposition — the adverb, the verb particle, and the adjective.** None of these takes an object, which is exactly what separates them from the four senses above.

11. The canoe went *over* in the first set of rapids — adverb, no object.
12. Read it *over* once more before you sign, and then the whole thing is *over* — particle, then adjective.

**See also:** [rather than](#rather-than), [in preference to](#in-preference-to), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [ahead of](11-complex-multiword.md#ahead-of), [over](04-movement-direction.md#over)

### rather than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðər ðæn/ &middot; weak /ˈræðər ðən/ &middot; *RA-thur-thun*
**Русский:** а не; вместо того чтобы — называет отвергнутый вариант; берёт имя, герундий или инфинитив
**Type:** complex — and grammatically a hybrid: it behaves as a preposition before a noun phrase and as a coordinator before a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*rather than waiting*), **bare infinitive** when a *to*-infinitive precedes it (*chose to walk rather than wait*), adjective, or prepositional phrase — whatever is parallel to the item being contrasted. **Never a finite clause:** *rather than he should go* is not English.
**Pattern:** [X] *rather than* [Y] &middot; *Rather than* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause] &middot; [subject], *rather than* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; *would rather* [bare verb] *than* [bare verb] — a separate construction
**Variants:** *instead of* is the near-equivalent before nouns and gerunds but cannot take a bare infinitive: *rather than wait* is fine, *instead of wait* is not.
**Contrast:** **Verb agreement.** Like *as well as* and *along with*, this phrase does not build a compound subject: *The manager, rather than the clerks, **is** responsible.* **Parallelism** governs the complement: whatever follows must match the form of what precedes it — *to drive rather than fly*, *driving rather than flying*, never a mixture of the two. And keep it apart from the split frame *would rather … than*, in which the two words are separated and both verbs are bare: *I'd rather walk than wait.*

Names the option that was turned down. It is the most flexible of the preference words because it accepts a noun, a gerund, or a bare infinitive; the price of that flexibility is that its complement has to be matched carefully to whatever precedes it.

**1. Instead of, in place of — before a noun phrase.**

1. She asked for time off *rather than* a raise.
2. *Rather than* a fine, the court imposed community service.
3. The delay was a supply problem *rather than* a labor problem.

**2. Before a verb — gerund or bare infinitive, matching whatever precedes it.**

4. *Rather than* waiting for the ferry, we drove around the bay.
5. He chose to resign *rather than* answer the committee's questions.
6. The company would close the plant *rather than* renegotiate.

**3. Appended to a subject — the verb still agrees with the subject alone.**

7. The dispatcher, *rather than* the drivers, **decides** the route.
8. Cost, *rather than* safety, **was** the reason given.

**See also:** [instead of](11-complex-multiword.md#instead-of), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [over](#over), [in preference to](#in-preference-to), [than](#than)

## Support, alignment, and conformity

The positive half of the field. Four of these — *in favor of*, *in support of*, *in defense of*, *on the side of* — declare that you are backing something, and they are not interchangeable: you are *in favor of* a proposal (approval), you march *in support of* it (an act), you write *in defense of* it only once it has been attacked, and you are *on the side of* a party rather than of a question. The one-word member of that group is *behind* — *the union is solidly behind the proposal* — whose support sense is a straight extension of its spatial one and is treated with the prepositions of position at [behind](03-place-position.md#behind). The rest are the conformity prepositions, and they form a strength ladder: *in accordance with* binds, *in line with* matches, *in keeping with* merely fits the spirit, and *consistent with* claims only that nothing contradicts anything.

### consistent with

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɪstənt wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *kun-SISS-tunt with*
**Русский:** согласуется с; совместимо с — мед., юр.: не противоречит и укладывается в известную картину
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; heavily used in medicine, law, and engineering
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a free relative (*consistent with what arbitrators have done*) — never a finite clause. ***Consistent to* is an error.**
**Pattern:** *be consistent with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *consistent with* [noun phrase] &middot; *findings/injuries/results consistent with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The negatives are *inconsistent with* and *at odds with*. Against *in accordance with*, which reports obedience to a rule, *consistent with* reports only that two things do not conflict — which is exactly why the medical formula *injuries consistent with a fall* is so carefully weak: it says the evidence fits that story, not that the story is true. Against *in keeping with*, it is analytic rather than evaluative.

Says two things can both be true at once, or that a thing fits a known pattern. The whole value of the phrase is its modesty: it claims compatibility and stops there, which is precisely what an expert witness or a radiologist needs it to do.

1. The bruising is *consistent with* a fall from that height.
2. Her account is entirely *consistent with* the timeline in the dispatch log.
3. The readings are *consistent with* a slow leak rather than a rupture.
4. Please continue to act *consistent with* the policy until the board revises it.
5. Nothing we found is *consistent with* an electrical cause.
6. The award is *consistent with* what arbitrators have done in similar cases.

**See also:** [inconsistent with](#inconsistent-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [at variance with](#at-variance-with), [at odds with](#at-odds-with)

### in accordance with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈkɔrdəns wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in uh-KOR-dunss with*
**Русский:** в соответствии с; согласно — книжн., юр.: действие отвечает норме или предписанию
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, regulatory, and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*in accordance with what the statute requires*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in accordance with* [noun phrase] &middot; *In accordance with* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Variants:** *in conformity with*, *in compliance with*, and the one-word *pursuant to* are the near-synonyms of the same register; *under* and *as required by* are the plain-English replacements. ***In accordance to* is an error**, and so is *in accordance of*.
**Contrast:** ***In accordance to* is always an error** — the noun *accordance* takes *with*. Against *according to*, which reports what a source says, this reports conformity to a rule: *According to the manual, the valve closes* (the manual says so) versus *The valve was installed in accordance with the manual* (the installation followed it). Against *in keeping with*, it binds — rules require accordance, traditions only invite keeping. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in accordance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-accordance-with).

States that an act conformed to a governing rule, standard, or instrument. It is compliance language, and in ordinary prose *under*, *following*, or *as required by* is nearly always the better choice.

1. The audit was conducted *in accordance with* generally accepted auditing standards.
2. Ballots will be counted *in accordance with* state law.
3. The device must be installed *in accordance with* the manufacturer's specifications.
4. *In accordance with* your instructions, we canceled the order on Friday.
5. Benefits are calculated *in accordance with* the schedule in Appendix B.

**See also:** [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to)

### in conformity with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˈfɔrməti wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in kun-FOR-muh-tee with*
**Русский:** в соответствии с; сообразно — книжн., юр. и бухг.: сделано по установленному стандарту
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; **legal, regulatory, and accounting** — an American meets it in an auditor's report, a building code, a consent decree, or a will
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*in conformity with what the code requires*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** *In conformity with installing the meter* is not English: the complement names the standard, never the act being measured against it.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in conformity with* [rule/standard/instrument] &middot; *In conformity with* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *prepared/audited/constructed in conformity with* [standard]
**Variants:** *in conformity to* occurs and is not an error, but *with* is far commoner in American English; the bare noun takes either (*conformity with the statute*, *conformity to type*). ***In conformity of* is an error.** The near-synonyms of the same register are [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [in compliance with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-compliance-with), and [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to); *under* and *as required by* are the plain-English replacements.
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *in accordance with* in nearly every sentence, and a shade stiffer. Against *in compliance with*, which reports that a specific obligation was discharged, *in conformity with* reports that a thing matches a standard's shape — which is why an accountant's statements are prepared *in conformity with* generally accepted accounting principles rather than in compliance with them. Against *consistent with*, which claims only that nothing conflicts, it claims positive agreement. Its negative is the noun phrase *nonconformity with*, not a preposition.

States that a thing was made, done, or drawn up to match a governing standard. It is the auditor's and the code official's word, and in ordinary prose *under*, *following*, or *as required by* nearly always reads better.

1. The statements were prepared *in conformity with* generally accepted accounting principles.
2. All wiring must be *in conformity with* the state electrical code before the meter goes in.
3. *In conformity with* the settlement, the district rewrote its discipline policy over the summer.
4. The trustees distributed the residue *in conformity with* the terms of the will.
5. Nothing about the addition is *in conformity with* what the historic district requires.
6. The samples were collected *in conformity with* the protocol filed with the agency.

**See also:** [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [pursuant to](11-complex-multiword.md#pursuant-to)

### in defense of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn dɪˈfɛns əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn dɪˈfɛns ə/ &middot; *in dih-FENSS uhv*
**Русский:** в защиту кого/чего-л. — предполагает, что объект под угрозой или подвергся нападкам
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (*in defense of doing nothing*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *in defense of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In defense of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *in* [possessive] *defense* — the short alternative frame
**Variants:** ***in defence of*** is the British spelling; American English uses *defense*. The possessive frame *in her defense* is idiomatic and far commoner than *in defense of her*. Note the stress: the sports noun meaning "the defending side" is /ˈdifɛns/, *DEE-fenss*, but inside this phrase the stress stays on the second syllable.
**Contrast:** Against *in support of*, which backs a proposal, *in defense of* answers an attack — there has to be a criticism, a charge, or a threat somewhere in the picture. Against *on behalf of*, it implies no authorization: you may defend someone who never asked you to. In athletics, *in defense of the title* means the holder is trying to keep it.

Names what is being protected or argued for. Something has to be under threat for the phrase to make sense — a reputation, a flock, a championship — and that requirement is what separates it from the other support prepositions.

**1. Arguing or acting to protect something under attack.**

1. He wrote a long letter *in defense of* the department's record.
2. She spoke *in defense of* a colleague nobody else would defend.
3. *In defense of* the old system, it never once lost a file.

**2. Physically protecting.**

4. The dog was shot *in defense of* the flock.
5. Two firefighters were injured *in defense of* the outbuildings.

**3. Defending a title or championship.**

6. She returns to Melbourne in January *in defense of* her title.
7. The team travels to Denver *in defense of* a nine-game winning streak.

### in favor of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfeɪvər əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ˈfeɪvər ə/ &middot; *in FAY-vur uhv*
**Русский:** в пользу; за — одобрение; выбранный вариант; юр. в чью-либо пользу
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (*in favor of raising the cap*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** *be in favor of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *vote in favor of* [noun phrase] &middot; [drop/abandon X] *in favor of* [Y] &middot; *rule/decide/find in favor of* [party]
**Variants:** ***in favour of*** is British; American English drops the *u*. *In X's favor* is the possessive alternative: *the ruling went in her favor*.
**Contrast:** Three senses a reader has to keep apart. Sense 1 is approval, the opposite of *against*. Sense 2 is preference, and here the thing after the phrase is the option that **won** — the exact reverse of *as opposed to*, where what follows is the option rejected. Sense 3 is legal and financial and means "to the benefit of." Against plain *for*, sense 1 is the deliberative register: *I'm for it* in the hallway, *I am in favor of the amendment* on the record. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in favor of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-favor-of).

Declares approval of something, names the option that won a choice, or assigns a benefit. All three senses turn on the same idea of favor, but they attach to very different kinds of sentence and are easy to run together.

**1. Supporting; approving of.**

1. Nine members voted *in favor of* the measure.
2. Are you *in favor of* moving the meeting to Thursday?
3. Public opinion has shifted sharply *in favor of* the plan.

**2. Chosen in place of — the surviving option follows the phrase.**

4. The city dropped the streetcar plan *in favor of* dedicated bus lanes.
5. She turned down the corporate offer *in favor of* a research fellowship.

**3. To the legal or financial benefit of.**

6. The court ruled *in favor of* the tenants.
7. Make the check payable *in favor of* the trust, not the individual.

**See also:** [in support of](#in-support-of), [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [on the side of](#on-the-side-of), [over](#over), [in favor of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-favor-of)

### in keeping with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkipɪŋ wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in KEE-ping with*
**Русский:** в духе; в соответствии с — согласуется с традицией, характером или волей кого-л.
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*in keeping with what neighboring districts have offered*) — **never a finite clause**; a gerund is grammatical but awkward
**Pattern:** [clause], *in keeping with* [noun phrase] &middot; *In keeping with* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Where *in accordance with* means conformity to a rule that binds, *in keeping with* means consistency with a spirit, a tradition, or a character — nobody is obliged. *In accordance with the statute* is legally required; *in keeping with the family's wishes* is fitting but optional. *In line with* sits between the two, and *consistent with* is the analytic version of the same idea. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in keeping with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-keeping-with).

Says the act fits the tone or the precedent of whatever came before it. It is evaluative rather than regulatory, which is why it collocates so strongly with *tradition*, *spirit*, *character*, and *wishes*.

1. *In keeping with* tradition, the outgoing chair gave the closing toast.
2. The addition is *in keeping with* the character of the block.
3. *In keeping with* the family's wishes, the service was private.
4. His decision was entirely *in keeping with* everything he had said publicly.
5. The raise is *in keeping with* what neighboring districts have offered.

**See also:** [in line with](#in-line-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [in the manner of](#in-the-manner-of), [in keeping with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-keeping-with)

### in line with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪn wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in LYNE with*
**Русский:** в соответствии с; совпадает с — о результате, совпавшем с прогнозом, планом или нормой
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; business and journalism
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative headed by *what* (*in line with what the committee recommended*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in line with* [noun phrase] &middot; *In line with* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *bring/come/fall in line with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Weaker than *in accordance with*, which implies obligation, and more quantitative than *in keeping with*, which is about spirit: earnings come in *in line with* expectations, but a memorial service is *in keeping with* someone's wishes. Keep the frozen phrase apart from the literal *in line with the doorframe*, meaning physically aligned, which uses the same three words. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [in line with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-line-with).

Reports that something matched a forecast, a policy, or a standard. It is the standard verb-free way for analysts and reporters to say "as predicted," and *bring into line with* is its transitive twin.

1. Third-quarter results came in *in line with* analysts' forecasts.
2. Wage growth is now roughly *in line with* inflation.
3. *In line with* company policy, expenses over fifty dollars need a receipt.
4. The new rules bring the state *in line with* federal standards.
5. The proposal is broadly *in line with* what the committee recommended.

**See also:** [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [on a par with](#on-a-par-with), [in line with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-line-with)

### in privity with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈprɪvəti wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in PRIV-uh-tee with*
**Русский:** юр. в договорных или иных правовых отношениях с — термин: между сторонами есть права и обязанности
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** **legal; a term of art.** An American meets it in a warranty claim against a manufacturer, a lease or covenant dispute, or an opinion about whether an earlier judgment binds somebody — and essentially nowhere else. Outside law it reads as a malapropism for *privy to*.
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a **party** — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** *In privity with buying the house* is not English. What the two parties share is named with *of* inside the phrase rather than by a second complement: *in privity of contract with the manufacturer*, *in privity of estate with the original lessee*.
**Pattern:** *be in privity with* [party] &middot; *in privity of contract with* [party] &middot; *in privity of estate with* [party] &middot; *not in privity with* [party] — the negative is the commoner form, because the point is usually that the plaintiff is a stranger to the deal
**Variants:** the bare noun takes *of* (*privity of contract*) or *between* (*privity between the parties*). The adjective *privy to*, meaning "let in on a secret," shares the Latin root and nothing else and is not this phrase.
**Contrast:** **Not a comparison and not an alignment of opinion.** It asserts that a legal relationship exists close enough for one party to enforce, or be bound by, what the other did. Against [as between](#as-between), which takes the relationship for granted and divides something up inside it, *in privity with* is the prior question of whether there is a relationship at all. Against being *a party to* a contract, which means you signed it, privity reaches successors who never did: a later buyer can be in privity with the original covenantor without having signed anything.

Says two parties stand in a legal relationship close enough to carry rights and duties between them. Whether privity exists is the threshold question in a whole class of American lawsuits, and the phrase is almost always doing that work — deciding who is allowed to sue whom.

**1. Privity of contract — the warranty and third-party frame.**

1. The subcontractor was never *in privity with* the owner and has to sue the general contractor instead.
2. Most states have dropped the rule that a buyer must be *in privity with* the manufacturer to bring a warranty claim.
3. The insurer is not *in privity with* the repair shop and cannot enforce the estimate against it.

**2. Privity of estate and of succession — leases, covenants, and preclusion.**

4. The tenant is *in privity of estate with* the landlord for as long as the lease runs.
5. Because the assignee stands *in privity with* the original lessee, the covenant runs with the land.
6. The court held that the second plaintiff was not *in privity with* the first, so the earlier judgment did not bind her.

**See also:** [as between](#as-between), [as against](#as-against), [in conformity with](#in-conformity-with), [on behalf of](#on-behalf-of), [in violation of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-violation-of)

### in support of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn səˈpɔrt əv/ &middot; weak /ɪn səˈpɔrt ə/ &middot; *in suh-PORT uhv*
**Русский:** в поддержку; в подтверждение — о поступке, документе или доказательствах, а не просто о мнении
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in support of raising the minimum wage*) — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *in support of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In support of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [document] *in support of* [motion] &middot; *evidence in support of* [proposition]
**Contrast:** The exact mirror of [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), and the two form the standard caption pair in American motion practice. Against *in favor of*, which states approval, *in support of* names something you are actively backing with an act, a document, or evidence: you are *in favor of* a bill, but you march *in support of* it. Against *on behalf of*, no authorization is implied.

Names what an act, a document, or a body of evidence is backing. Unlike *in favor of*, which reports an opinion, this phrase attaches to something that was actually done.

**1. Backing a proposal, a cause, or a person.**

1. Two hundred people rallied *in support of* the striking nurses.
2. She testified *in support of* the bill at Thursday's hearing.
3. *In support of* the application, the college submitted four letters.

**2. Offered as evidence for.**

4. There is very little data *in support of* that claim.
5. The affidavit was filed *in support of* the motion to compel.

**3. Providing assistance to an operation — military and logistical.**

6. Two cutters were dispatched *in support of* the search.
7. Guard units deployed *in support of* the county's flood response.

**See also:** [in opposition to](#in-opposition-to), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [in defense of](#in-defense-of), [on behalf of](#on-behalf-of), [on the side of](#on-the-side-of)

### in sync with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈsɪŋk wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; *in SINK with*
**Русский:** в такт с; синхронно с; заодно с — разг.: совпадение по ритму, графику или пониманию
**Type:** complex (clipped from *in synchronization with*) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; conversational and technical, and increasingly at home in business writing
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*in sync with him*), or a free relative (*in sync with what the rest of the team is doing*) — **never a finite clause and never a gerund.** *In sync with running the meeting* is not English; write *in sync with the way the meeting is run*.
**Pattern:** [be/stay/get/fall] *in sync with* [noun phrase] &middot; *bring* [X] *in sync with* [Y] &middot; *in sync* — adverbial, no complement
**Variants:** ***in synch with*** is an accepted spelling and is losing ground; *in sync* is now the dominant American form. The full *in synchronization with* is technical and rare.
**Contrast:** The informal member of the conformity family. Against *in line with*, which reports that a figure matched a forecast, *in sync with* reports that two things are moving together in time or in intention — two departments can be in sync without ever having discussed anything. Against *in agreement with*, it is not about opinion at all. Its negative, [out of sync with](#out-of-sync-with), is much the commoner of the two and is treated with the prepositions of contrast, exactly as *inconsistent with* is treated apart from *consistent with*.

Says two things are running together — on the same beat, the same schedule, or the same understanding. The metaphor is mechanical and reached general English from film and audio work, which is why it still fits best wherever timing is involved.

1. Payroll is finally *in sync with* the new accounting calendar.
2. The two cameras have to stay *in sync with* each other to the frame.
3. Her instincts are *in sync with* what the neighborhood actually wants.
4. Wage growth is roughly *in sync with* inflation for the first time in three years.
5. Get your calendar *in sync with* the shared one before Monday.
6. The lights were never *in sync with* the traffic on that stretch of road.

### on behalf of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn bɪˈhæf əv/ &middot; weak /ɑn bɪˈhæf ə/ &middot; *on bih-HAF uhv*
**Русский:** от имени; в интересах кого-л. — говорящий или действующий выступает как представитель
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** [clause] *on behalf of* [noun phrase] &middot; *On behalf of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *on* [possessive] *behalf* — the possessive frame
**Variants:** *in behalf of* was traditionally distinguished in American usage — *on behalf of* = "as the agent of," *in behalf of* = "for the benefit of" — but the distinction has collapsed and *on behalf of* now covers both. The reflexive is *on my own behalf*, never *on behalf of myself*. British usage is identical.
**Contrast:** Against *for*, this specifies representation rather than mere benefit: a lawyer files *on behalf of* a client, but you buy a gift *for* a friend. Against *in the name of*, which invokes an authority, *on behalf of* names a principal. Against *in support of* and *in defense of*, which require no authorization, this one implies that you were asked. Its listing among the complex prepositions generally is at [on behalf of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-behalf-of).

Names the person or body an actor is representing. It is the standard opening of formal remarks and the standard signature line of counsel and agents, and its core claim is agency: somebody else's interest is being carried.

**1. As the authorized agent or representative of.**

1. The attorney filed the motion *on behalf of* her client.
2. The union bargains *on behalf of* eleven thousand members.
3. He signed the lease *on behalf of* the corporation, not personally.

**2. Speaking for a group ceremonially or courteously.**

4. *On behalf of* the entire staff, thank you for twenty years.
5. He accepted the award *on behalf of* his late father.

**3. For the benefit of — the older *in behalf of* sense, now absorbed into this one.**

6. Neighbors organized *on behalf of* a family that lost everything in the fire.
7. She has lobbied *on behalf of* rural hospitals for a decade.

### on the side of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈsaɪd əv/ &middot; weak /ɑn ðə ˈsaɪd ə/ &middot; *on-thuh-SIDE uhv*
**Русский:** на стороне кого-л. — в споре; в обороте err on the side of — сознательно склониться к (осторожности)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; in the *err on the side of* frame, an abstract noun naming a quality (*caution*, *generosity*, *disclosure*) — never a finite clause, never a gerund
**Pattern:** *be on the side of* [noun phrase] &middot; *come down on the side of* [noun phrase] &middot; *err on the side of* [quality] &middot; *on* [possessive] *side* — the short frame
**Variants:** *on X's side* (*history is on our side*, *the wind was on their side*) is the ordinary short form. The literal spatial *on the side of the hill* is an ordinary *of*-phrase, not this preposition.
**Contrast:** Sense 1 declares an alliance and answers the question *whose side are you on?*. Sense 2, *err on the side of*, is a fixed idiom meaning "when in doubt, take too much of this rather than too little," and its complement is always a virtue, never a person. Against *in favor of*, sense 1 is about loyalty rather than about a position on a question; against *in support of*, it names a camp rather than an act.

Names the camp you belong to. Sense 1 is about loyalty in a dispute; sense 2 is a frozen idiom for deliberate overcorrection, and it takes a virtue rather than a party as its object.

**1. Allied with; favoring in a dispute.**

1. The building inspector was plainly *on the side of* the developer.
2. The court came down *on the side of* the tenants.
3. Time is *on the side of* whoever can afford to wait longest.
4. He liked to say he was *on the side of* the angels, which nobody believed.

**2. *Err on the side of* — deliberately overshooting a virtue.**

5. When in doubt, *err on the side of* caution.
6. The policy tells reporters to *err on the side of* disclosure.
7. She erred *on the side of* generosity in every estimate she ever made.

### pro

**Pronunciation:** /proʊ/ &middot; *PROH*
**Русский:** за; сторонник чего-л. — разг. и редк. как самостоятельный предлог; обычно приставка про-
**Type:** simple (marginal — from the Latin preposition; in American English overwhelmingly a **prefix**) &middot; **Register:** informal as a free-standing preposition and **more current in British English** than American; the prefixed form is neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase only, usually bare (*pro union*, *pro the merger*) — never a clause, never a gerund. Distinguish the **noun** *pro*, meaning "a professional" or "an argument in favor" (*the pros and cons*), and the **adjective** (*Is she pro or anti?*), neither of which takes an object.
**Pattern:** *be pro* [noun phrase] &middot; *pro-* [noun] as a prefix — the American norm &middot; *pro and con*
**Variants:** American style hyphenates the prefix before a capital or wherever the closed form would be misread (*pro-American*, *pro-labor*). The Latin phrases *pro bono*, *pro rata*, *pro forma*, and *pro tem* carry their own Latin objects and are not this preposition in ordinary English use.
**Contrast:** The mirror of [anti](#anti). Neither is fully naturalized as a free preposition in American English, where *in favor of* and *against* do the work. The frozen pair *pro and con* — from Latin *pro et contra* — has become a noun idiom (*the pros and cons*) rather than a prepositional one.

Declares the speaker on the same side as something. It is the shortest word in English for taking a position, which is exactly why it thrives as a prefix and struggles as a preposition.

1. Nobody in that district is *pro* new taxes, whatever the polling says. (informal)
2. She's *pro* the merger but wants the timeline slowed down. (informal, British-flavored)
3. Pro-labor candidates swept the county races. (prefix — the normal American form)
4. The mayor is *pro* development in a way her predecessor never was.
5. We spent an hour on the pros and cons and settled nothing. (noun, not a preposition)
