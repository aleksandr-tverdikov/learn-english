Comparison, similarity, contrast, opposition, and support#

The prepositions that hold two things up against each other — to say they are alike, to say they are different, to name the benchmark one of them is measured by, or to take a side.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file is two-place in a way the others are not: it needs a thing in the main clause and a benchmark after the preposition, and the sentence only works if those two things are the same kind of thing. That single requirement produces most of the errors in the group — Unlike the 1970s, today's inflation is mild compares a decade with an inflation rate, and Compared to Chicago, our winters are short compares a city with a season. Two grammatical traps run alongside it. The first is like versus as: like takes a noun and asserts a resemblance, as names a role or heads a clause, and like I said is still marked in careful American prose. The second is than, which is a preposition when a noun follows (taller than me) and a conjunction when a clause follows (taller than I am) — and which one you have decides the case of the pronoun.

Several members of this family are housed elsewhere because their other senses dominate. The comparison-to-a-norm use of for — tall for his age, warm for November, cheap for a downtown lot — is treated with the core relational prepositions and is not repeated here. Where a word in this file also has spatial, temporal, or topical senses — as, against, over, next to, relative to, vis-à-vis — the entry covers only its comparison-and-opposition work and links to the file that owns the rest.

Likeness and difference#

The plainest members of the group. They assert that two things are alike, or that they are not, and nothing else. Like and as are the pair that has to be kept straight, and the rest of the section is arranged around them: similar to and akin to are the analytic and the impressionistic versions of like, and unlike, different from, and in common with mark out the negative and the group-membership edges of the same idea. Above all of them sits the same as, which claims not resemblance but identity, and which is the one member of the section whose as will also take a clause.

Type
complex (article + noun phrase + fixed from; wholly idiomatic — the cry is the old sense "shouting distance," so the phrase literally measures a gap)
Register
neutral; conversational and journalistic
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (a far cry from hers), gerund (a far cry from running a newsroom), or a free relative (a far cry from what the contract described) — never a finite clause. It is also predicative only: it follows be, seem, or remain, and it cannot be hung on a noun as a modifier. A far cry than and a far cry to are errors; the preposition is from, exactly as in different from.
Pattern
[be/seem/remain] a far cry from [noun phrase] · still a far cry from [noun phrase] · a long way from — the plain paraphrase
Contrast
Against different from, which reports unlikeness neutrally, a far cry from measures the distance and calls it large. It is also directional: what follows the phrase is the standard, and the subject is nearly always the thing that falls short of it — the finished building is a far cry from the drawings faults the building, not the drawings. Against out of sync with, which reports a mismatch of timing or alignment, this one reports a mismatch of degree.

Says two things are the same kind of thing and very far apart in quality, degree, or fidelity. It nearly always registers a disappointment: what follows the phrase is the promise, the original, or the standard, and the subject is what actually turned up.

  1. The finished building is a far cry from the drawings the council approved.
  2. Her second album is a far cry from the bluegrass she started with.
  3. Twelve dollars an hour is a far cry from a living wage in this county.
  4. Running a two-person shop is a far cry from running a newsroom.
  5. What the contractor delivered was a far cry from what the contract described.
  6. The river is cleaner than it was in 1975, but it is still a far cry from the trout stream the old-timers describe.

Type
complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal — akin survives almost nowhere else in modern English)
Register
formal to neutral; common in journalism and criticism
Complement
noun phrase, gerund (akin to admitting defeat), or a wh-free relative (akin to what happened in 2008) — never a finite clause. Akin with is an error.
Pattern

Contrast

Says two things belong to the same family without saying they are the same thing. The adjective akin is a fossil, contracted from of kin, and outside this phrase it barely exists, which is why the whole string behaves as a single preposition.

  1. What she felt was akin to relief, though she would not have called it that.
  2. The charge is akin to a fee for using the road.
  3. Signing that waiver is akin to giving up the claim entirely.
  4. He greeted the news with something akin to panic.
  5. Their dialect is more akin to Portuguese than to Spanish.

  2. The committee wants a program along the lines of the one Ohio runs.
  3. I was thinking along the lines of a smaller deck and a bigger porch.
  4. They floated a settlement along the lines of what the union got in 2019.
  5. She is looking for work along the lines of what she did before the layoff.
  6. The state is considering a surcharge along the lines of raising the registration fee by twenty dollars.

as#

Pronunciation
/æz/ · weak /əz/, and in rapid speech nearly /z/ after a vowel · AZ / uhz
Русский
как, в качестве; такой же, как — второй член сравнения as … as и указание на роль или тождество
Type
simple — the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time, cause, and manner, the second half of the correlative as … as, and a relative pronoun after such and the same
Register
neutral
Complement
in comparison, a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (as tall as me), and an adjective or adverb inside the correlative frame (as quickly as). It takes a finite clause only when it is doing conjunction duty (as I said), and the presence of a subject and verb after it is exactly what tells you which word you have. Never a gerund in the comparison sense, and never a that-clause.
Pattern
as [adjective/adverb] as [noun phrase] · the same … as [noun phrase] · such … as [noun phrase] · as many/much/few/little as [quantity] · frozen adverbials: as usual, as always, as a rule, as follows
Variants
none. The two-word as if and as though take clauses and are conjunctions, not prepositions.
Contrast
Like versus as is the central rule of this file. Like takes a noun and asserts a resemblance; as names a role or heads a clause. Like your lawyer, I'd settle says the speaker is someone else who agrees with the lawyer; As your lawyer, I'd settle says the speaker is the lawyer. Before a clause, careful American prose uses as — as I said, not like I said — even though the like version is universal in speech. The role-and-capacity as (works as a nurse, regarded as unreliable) is treated with the prepositions of role at as; this entry covers as in comparison and manner.

The comparison as asserts equality or sameness of kind: that two things measure the same, that they are the same one, or that one belongs to the set named by the other. It is among the highest-frequency words in English, and its constant traffic with the identically spelled conjunction is what makes it the hardest word in this file to use confidently.

1. The second as of a comparison of equality. After a pronoun, American speech takes the objective case (as tall as me); formal writing usually restores the verb (as tall as I am).

  1. The new engine is as quiet as the old one and half the weight.
  2. She can throw as far as anybody on the team.
  3. He's nearly as stubborn as me — formally, as stubborn as I am.

2. After the same, asserting identity of kind.

  1. Her schedule is exactly the same as mine.
  2. That's the same truck as the one parked out front all week.

3. After such, introducing an example.

  1. Root vegetables such as turnips and parsnips keep all winter in sand.
  2. Delays such as these are unavoidable in February.

4. Frozen adverbials of manner and expectation.

  1. The 8:10 was late, as usual.
  2. As always, the trailhead lot was full by seven.
  3. Payment is due on the first, as a rule.

5. As many as, as much as, as few as — the quantity frame. The correlative closes on a number, and the effect is to offer that number as a striking maximum: as many as forty means "up to forty, and forty is a lot."

  1. As many as forty families may have to move before the levee work starts.
  2. The repair could run as much as nine thousand dollars, and that estimate is two weeks old.
  3. The clinic sees as few as three patients on a Monday in August.

6. Not a preposition — the conjunction, with a whole clause after it. This is the form careful writing uses where speech reaches for like.

  1. Fold the corners in, as the diagram shows.
  2. As I said on the phone, the estimate is only good for thirty days.

different from#

Pronunciation
/ˈdɪfrənt frʌm/ · weak /ˈdɪfərənt frəm/ · three syllables, /ˈdɪfərənt/, in careful speech · DIF-runt frum
Русский
отличный от; не такой, как — стандартный предлог при different (не to, по-британски)
Type
complex (adjective + preposition; marginal — different is a fully live adjective, and the pair is listed here because choosing its preposition is the problem, not because the two words have fused)
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (different from me), gerund (different from driving a truck), or a free relative (different from what I expected). Never a bare that-clause.
Pattern
[be/seem/look] different from [noun phrase] · [noun] different from [noun phrase] · no different from [noun phrase] · differently from [noun phrase]
Variants
different than is standard American English and is the better choice before a clause, where from forces a clumsy free relative: The house looked different than I remembered beats different from what I remembered. Different to is British and does not belong in American writing. Different from is never wrong and is the safe default.
Contrast
The pronoun test separates the two American forms: from is a preposition and takes the objective case (different from her), while than can be either preposition or conjunction, so different than she is and different than her are both current. Note that the verb is differ from, never differ than, and the adverb is differently from or differently than.

The workhorse expression of unlikeness, and the one that draws the most usage mail in American English. The rule that matters is short: from always works, than works and is often smoother before a clause, and to is British.

  1. Her second draft is completely different from the first.
  2. Growing up in Fairbanks is different from anything I can describe to you.
  3. The new packaging is no different from the old except for the color.
  4. It tasted different from what the menu had promised.
  5. He votes differently from the rest of his delegation on almost everything.
  6. The building looks different than it did in the photographs — before a clause, than is the smoother choice.

Русский
как и; наравне с — подобно другим членам той же группы; книжн., только письменно
Type
complex
Register
formal; written English almost exclusively
Complement
noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund
Pattern

Contrast
Only the fronted, comma-set use is a preposition. The far commoner have something in common with is a verb plus its object plus an ordinary with-phrase, and the two cannot be swapped: In common with her sister, she plays the cello means "like her sister," while She has the cello in common with her sister means they share it as an interest. Marks the subject of the main clause as one more member of a group that behaves the same way. Because it is bulky and almost always fronted, it belongs to written English — a reporter writes it and nobody says it.

  1. In common with most western states, Nevada holds its primary in June.
  2. In common with her colleagues, she had no warning that the lab would close.
  3. The bank, in common with every lender in the state, tightened its terms that spring.
  4. In common with many veterans of that campaign, he refused to discuss it.
  5. In common with the older engines, this one still needs leaded fuel.

Says the thing was made in imitation of a known style. In auction catalogues the phrase carries a specific and unflattering meaning — the work is by a follower, not the master — and that hedged quality travels with it into ordinary prose.

  1. A courtyard fountain in the manner of the Alhambra.

  3. The sauce is finished in the manner of a classic beurre blanc.
  4. She ran the department in the manner of a ship's captain.
  5. The lithograph is catalogued in the manner of Daumier, which is the polite way of saying it is not one.

complex

neutral to formal; criticism and reviewing — books, records, films, and restaurant menus
Complement

[noun] in the vein of [noun phrase] · something in the vein of [noun phrase] · in a lighter/darker/similar vein, in the same vein — the adjectival frames, which take no complement at all
Variants

Contrast
Against in the manner of, which says a style was deliberately imitated, in the vein of only places a work in a tradition: a novel in the vein of Chandler need not be trying to sound like Chandler, and no forgery is implied. Against along the lines of, which points at a plan or a form of words, this points at an artistic kind. Against like, it is the reviewer's register and always names something with a recognized style attached to it.

Places a work in the tradition of a named model. It is the standard shorthand of reviewing — three words that tell a reader what shelf a thing belongs on — and it commits the writer to far less than in the manner of would.

  1. It is a quiet domestic novel in the vein of Anne Tyler.
  2. The band's third record is in the vein of early Fleetwood Mac.
  3. He told a long story in the vein of the ones his grandfather used to tell.
  4. The gallery wants something in the vein of what she showed in Santa Fe.
  5. The report closes on a warning, in the vein of every county study since 1998.
  6. She is writing satire in the vein of the eighteenth century, footnotes and all.

like#

Pronunciation
/laɪk/ · a stressed word with no weak form, which is one reason it is so audible in American speech · LYKE
Русский
как; подобно; вроде — сходство; требует существительного, тогда как as вводит роль или придаточное
Type
simple — the same spelling is also a verb (I like it), an adjective (of like mind), a suffix (childlike), a quotative (he was like, no), and a discourse particle. Only the preposition takes an object and means "resembling."
Register
neutral as a preposition; the clause-taking and approximating uses are informal
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (like me, never like I), gerund (It's like driving on ice), or a wh-free relative (like what happened last year). A finite clause after like — like I said, like you told me — is informal; careful American writing uses as or as if there. Never a that-clause.
Pattern
[be/look/sound/feel/smell/taste/seem] like [noun phrase] · [verb] like [noun phrase] · Like [noun phrase], [clause] · just/much/something/more like [noun phrase] · It is (not) like [person] to [verb]
Contrast
The like/as rule. Like takes a noun and states a resemblance; as takes a role or a clause. He works like a mule is a comparison; He works as a mule would mean the animal is his job. Before a clause, formal prose wants as (as I said) or as if (It looks as if it will rain). Against such as, some editors reserve like for resemblance and such as for actual instances (poets such as Frost), but that distinction is not observed in general American usage and like introduces examples freely.

The general-purpose word of resemblance, and the busiest preposition in spoken American English. Everything it does reduces to a single operation — hold X up against Y and assert that they are alike — but the range of things that can fill those two slots is enormous, which is why the entry runs long.

1. Resembling; in the same way as.

  1. The new bridge looks like a harp from the river.
  2. She drives like a professional and parks like a tourist.
  3. Nobody else on the crew works like him.

2. Typical or untypical of a person — always with be.

  1. It's not like Marcus to miss a deadline.
  2. That is exactly like her: she paid for everyone and slipped out.

3. Introducing an example.

  1. Bring something sturdy, like a tarp or a moving blanket.
  2. Root crops like beets and carrots store well in damp sand.

4. After verbs of perception and impression.

  1. The whole house smelled like woodsmoke for a week.
  2. It felt like the first real morning of fall.

5. Approximately — informal, before a quantity.

  1. There were like forty people crammed into that kitchen.
  2. It took like two hours just to get through security.

6. Not a preposition — the quotative be like and the discourse particle, both conversational, neither taking an object.

  1. And she was like, "I already sent it twice."
  2. It was, like, the third time that week — the discourse particle, with nothing after it that it governs.

/ˈsɪmələr tu/ · weak /ˈsɪmələr tə/ · SIM-uh-lur too
Русский
похожий на; сходный с — нейтральный, более технический аналог like
Type
complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal)
Register
neutral to formal
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (similar to running a small business), or a wh-free relative (similar to what we saw last spring) — never a finite clause. Similar with and similar than are errors; the preposition is always to.
Pattern
[be/look/sound] similar to [noun phrase] · [noun] similar to [noun phrase] · broadly/roughly/strikingly similar to [noun phrase]

Against like, similar to is the analytic word: like asserts a likeness the reader can feel, similar to asserts one the writer could itemize, which is why lab reports and appraisals prefer it. States a measured resemblance. It is the neutral technical alternative to like, and its natural home is a sentence in which the two things compared are of the same type and the differences are going to matter.

  1. The symptoms are similar to those of ordinary flu.
  2. This year's model is similar to last year's except for the transmission.
  3. Running a school board turned out to be similar to running a small business.
  4. Her handwriting is strikingly similar to her father's.
  5. The plan is broadly similar to one the county rejected in 2019.

the likes of#

Pronunciation
/ðə ˈlaɪks əv/ · weak /ðə ˈlaɪks ə/ · the is /ði/ before a vowel · thuh-LYKES uhv
Русский
такие, как; подобные — вводит примеры; в зависимости от контекста с восхищением или презрением
Type
complex (definite article + plural noun + fixed of; the noun likes survives in almost nothing else)
Register
neutral in the example-listing sense; the the likes of us sense is informal and rarely neutral in tone
Complement
noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (the likes of us, the likes of him), or a list of names — never a finite clause and never a gerund. The verb agrees with what follows of, not with the plural likes: the likes of Ford and GM have moved on, but the likes of Babe Ruth comes along once a century.
Pattern
the likes of [name or list] · never seen the likes of [noun phrase] · not for the likes of us · and the like — the fixed tail phrase, which is not this preposition and takes no object
Variants
the singular the like of (the like of which) is older and now chiefly formal; the likes of which is the everyday American version. And the likes of that, for "and that sort of thing," is dialectal and does not belong in writing.
Contrast
Against such as, which is colorless, the likes of always carries an attitude — admiration, dismissal, or awe — and it names people and institutions rather than kinds of thing: poets such as Frost, but the likes of Frost and Stevens. Against like, it is heavier and always plural in form. And note that the tone flips with the frame: after a negative it is awe (never seen the likes of it), while the likes of us and the likes of him mark a social boundary and are never neutral.

Names people or institutions of a recognized kind, and colors them as it does so. All three of its uses do the same grammatical work — they head a comparison class — and they differ entirely in tone, from a flat list of examples to open contempt.

1. Such as — introducing prominent examples.

  1. Grants went to the likes of Carnegie Mellon and Georgia Tech.
  2. She has beaten the likes of three former champions this season.
  3. The street lost the likes of the hardware store and the shoe repair within a year.

2. After a negative — nothing comparable, the awe frame.

  1. Nobody on that crew had ever seen the likes of the storm that came through in June.
  2. There will never be the likes of her in that department again.

3. The likes of us — the class marker.

  1. That club was never built for the likes of us.
  2. He wasn't going to take advice from the likes of a night clerk.

noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (the same as me), gerund (the same as driving at night), or a wh-free relative (the same as what we had before). Contrast
The top of the likeness ladder. Explicit comparison and proportion#

These name a benchmark and measure something against it. The whole section is governed by one rule — the two things compared must be of the same kind — and by one stylistic fact: the phrase gets longer as the register gets more formal, running from than through compared with to in comparison with, with no change in meaning along the way. Four members make a claim about the comparison itself rather than reporting its result: comparable to says the two things are alike enough that measuring them against each other is legitimate, on a par with says the measurement came out even, commensurate with says one is fitting for the other, and equal to says the arithmetic came out the same. The comparison-to-a-norm sense of for, as in tall for his age, belongs to the same semantic field but is housed with the core relational prepositions.

Contrast and opposition#

Comparison notices a difference; contrast insists on it; opposition takes a side. The three shade into one another, and the useful distinctions are these: in contrast to only reports that two things differ, contrary to claims that one contradicts the other, and in defiance of adds that somebody knew and did it anyway. A separate trio reports that two accounts fail to square — at odds with in a newspaper, inconsistent with in a brief or a lab report, at variance with in the most formal register of all — and none of the three says which side is wrong. Versus and against are the plain words of conflict, and pro and anti — the two Latin prepositions that Americans have almost entirely converted into prefixes — are treated at pro and anti.

The positive half of the field. Four of these — in favor of, in support of, in defense of, on the side of — declare that you are backing something, and they are not interchangeable: you are in favor of a proposal (approval), you march in support of it (an act), you write in defense of it only once it has been attacked, and you are on the side of a party rather than of a question. The one-word member of that group is behind — the union is solidly behind the proposal — whose support sense is a straight extension of its spatial one and is treated with the prepositions of position at behind. The rest are the conformity prepositions, and they form a strength ladder: in accordance with binds, in line with matches, in keeping with merely fits the spirit, and consistent with claims only that nothing contradicts anything.
