Comparison, similarity, contrast, opposition, and support#

The prepositions that hold two things up against each other — to say they are alike, to say they are different, to name the benchmark one of them is measured by, or to take a side.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file is two-place in a way the others are not: it needs a thing in the main clause and a benchmark after the preposition, and the sentence only works if those two things are the same kind of thing. That single requirement produces most of the errors in the group — Unlike the 1970s, today's inflation is mild compares a decade with an inflation rate, and Compared to Chicago, our winters are short compares a city with a season. Two grammatical traps run alongside it. The first is like versus as: like takes a noun and asserts a resemblance, as names a role or heads a clause, and like I said is still marked in careful American prose. The second is than, which is a preposition when a noun follows (taller than me) and a conjunction when a clause follows (taller than I am) — and which one you have decides the case of the pronoun.

Several members of this family are housed elsewhere because their other senses dominate. The comparison-to-a-norm use of for — tall for his age, warm for November, cheap for a downtown lot — is treated with the core relational prepositions and is not repeated here. Where a word in this file also has spatial, temporal, or topical senses — as, against, over, next to, relative to, vis-à-vis — the entry covers only its comparison-and-opposition work and links to the file that owns the rest.

Likeness and difference#

The plainest members of the group. They assert that two things are alike, or that they are not, and nothing else. Like and as are the pair that has to be kept straight, and the rest of the section is arranged around them: similar to and akin to are the analytic and the impressionistic versions of like, and unlike, different from, and in common with mark out the negative and the group-membership edges of the same idea. Above all of them sits the same as, which claims not resemblance but identity, and which is the one member of the section whose as will also take a clause.

akin to#

Pronunciation: /əˈkɪn tu/ · weak /əˈkɪn tə/ · uh-KIN too Type: complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal — akin survives almost nowhere else in modern English) · Register: formal to neutral; common in journalism and criticism Complement: noun phrase, gerund (akin to admitting defeat), or a wh-free relative (akin to what happened in 2008) — never a finite clause. Akin with is an error. Pattern: be akin to [noun phrase / verb-ing] · [noun] akin to [noun phrase] · something akin to [noun phrase] Contrast: Weaker than the same as and stronger than reminiscent of: akin to claims a family resemblance without claiming identity. Against similar to, which invites the reader to compare features, akin to is reached for when the writer wants an approximation and does not intend to defend the details — which is why something akin to is by far its commonest frame.

Says two things belong to the same family without saying they are the same thing. The adjective akin is a fossil, contracted from of kin, and outside this phrase it barely exists, which is why the whole string behaves as a single preposition.

  1. What she felt was akin to relief, though she would not have called it that.
  2. The charge is akin to a fee for using the road.
  3. Signing that waiver is akin to giving up the claim entirely.
  4. He greeted the news with something akin to panic.
  5. Their dialect is more akin to Portuguese than to Spanish.

as#

Pronunciation: /æz/ · weak /əz/, and in rapid speech nearly /z/ after a vowel · AZ / uhz Type: simple — the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time, cause, and manner, the second half of the correlative as … as, and a relative pronoun after such and the same · Register: neutral Complement: in comparison, a noun phrase or an objective-case pronoun (as tall as me), and an adjective or adverb inside the correlative frame (as quickly as). It takes a finite clause only when it is doing conjunction duty (as I said), and the presence of a subject and verb after it is exactly what tells you which word you have. Never a gerund in the comparison sense, and never a that-clause. Pattern: as [adjective/adverb] as [noun phrase] · the same … as [noun phrase] · such … as [noun phrase] · as many/much/few/little as [quantity] · frozen adverbials: as usual, as always, as a rule, as follows Variants: none. The two-word as if and as though take clauses and are conjunctions, not prepositions. Contrast: Like versus as is the central rule of this file. Like takes a noun and asserts a resemblance; as names a role or heads a clause. Like your lawyer, I'd settle says the speaker is someone else who agrees with the lawyer; As your lawyer, I'd settle says the speaker is the lawyer. Before a clause, careful American prose uses as — as I said, not like I said — even though the like version is universal in speech. The role-and-capacity as (works as a nurse, regarded as unreliable) is treated with the prepositions of role at as; this entry covers as in comparison and manner.

The comparison as asserts equality or sameness of kind: that two things measure the same, that they are the same one, or that one belongs to the set named by the other. It is among the highest-frequency words in English, and its constant traffic with the identically spelled conjunction is what makes it the hardest word in this file to use confidently.

1. The second as of a comparison of equality. After a pronoun, American speech takes the objective case (as tall as me); formal writing usually restores the verb (as tall as I am).

  1. The new engine is as quiet as the old one and half the weight.
  2. She can throw as far as anybody on the team.
  3. He's nearly as stubborn as me — formally, as stubborn as I am.

2. After the same, asserting identity of kind.

  1. Her schedule is exactly the same as mine.
  2. That's the same truck as the one parked out front all week.

3. After such, introducing an example.

  1. Root vegetables such as turnips and parsnips keep all winter in sand.
  2. Delays such as these are unavoidable in February.

4. Frozen adverbials of manner and expectation.

  1. The 8:10 was late, as usual.
  2. As always, the trailhead lot was full by seven.
  3. Payment is due on the first, as a rule.

5. As many as, as much as, as few as — the quantity frame. The correlative closes on a number, and the effect is to offer that number as a striking maximum: as many as forty means "up to forty, and forty is a lot."

  1. As many as forty families may have to move before the levee work starts.
  2. The repair could run as much as nine thousand dollars, and that estimate is two weeks old.
  3. The clinic sees as few as three patients on a Monday in August.

6. Not a preposition — the conjunction, with a whole clause after it. This is the form careful writing uses where speech reaches for like.

  1. Fold the corners in, as the diagram shows.
  2. As I said on the phone, the estimate is only good for thirty days.

different from#

Pronunciation: /ˈdɪfrənt frʌm/ · weak /ˈdɪfərənt frəm/ · three syllables, /ˈdɪfərənt/, in careful speech · DIF-runt frum Type: complex (adjective + preposition; marginal — different is a fully live adjective, and the pair is listed here because choosing its preposition is the problem, not because the two words have fused) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (different from me), gerund (different from driving a truck), or a free relative (different from what I expected). Never a bare that-clause. Pattern: [be/seem/look] different from [noun phrase] · [noun] different from [noun phrase] · no different from [noun phrase] · differently from [noun phrase] Variants: different than is standard American English and is the better choice before a clause, where from forces a clumsy free relative: The house looked different than I remembered beats different from what I remembered. Different to is British and does not belong in American writing. Different from is never wrong and is the safe default. Contrast: The pronoun test separates the two American forms: from is a preposition and takes the objective case (different from her), while than can be either preposition or conjunction, so different than she is and different than her are both current. Note that the verb is differ from, never differ than, and the adverb is differently from or differently than.

The workhorse expression of unlikeness, and the one that draws the most usage mail in American English. The rule that matters is short: from always works, than works and is often smoother before a clause, and to is British.

  1. Her second draft is completely different from the first.
  2. Growing up in Fairbanks is different from anything I can describe to you.
  3. The new packaging is no different from the old except for the color.
  4. It tasted different from what the menu had promised.
  5. He votes differently from the rest of his delegation on almost everything.
  6. The building looks different than it did in the photographs — before a clause, than is the smoother choice.

in common with#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈkɑmən wɪθ/ · weak /wəθ/ · also /wɪð/ before a vowel · in KAH-mun with Type: complex · Register: formal; written English almost exclusively Complement: noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a finite clause, never a gerund Pattern: In common with [noun phrase], [clause] · [subject], in common with [noun phrase], [verb] Contrast: Only the fronted, comma-set use is a preposition. The far commoner have something in common with is a verb plus its object plus an ordinary with-phrase, and the two cannot be swapped: In common with her sister, she plays the cello means "like her sister," while She has the cello in common with her sister means they share it as an interest. Against like, this phrase is stiffer and nearly always fronted; against along with, it asserts resemblance rather than accompaniment, and like along with it leaves the verb agreeing with the original subject alone.

Marks the subject of the main clause as one more member of a group that behaves the same way. Because it is bulky and almost always fronted, it belongs to written English — a reporter writes it and nobody says it.

  1. In common with most western states, Nevada holds its primary in June.
  2. In common with her colleagues, she had no warning that the lab would close.
  3. The bank, in common with every lender in the state, tightened its terms that spring.
  4. In common with many veterans of that campaign, he refused to discuss it.
  5. In common with the older engines, this one still needs leaded fuel.

in the manner of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ðə ˈmænər əv/ · weak /ɪn ðə ˈmænər ə/ · in-thuh-MAN-ur-uv Type: complex · Register: formal to literary; standard in art criticism, music, and cooking Complement: noun phrase, usually a proper name or a style label — never a finite clause; a gerund is grammatical but rare Pattern: [noun] in the manner of [noun phrase] · [clause] in the manner of [noun phrase] · In the manner of [noun phrase], [clause] Variants: after the manner of is the older and more literary form; in the style of is the plain one; the borrowed à la /ɑ ˈlɑ/ (à la Hitchcock) is informal in English. In museum and auction cataloguing, in the manner of is a term of art meaning "by an unknown follower," as against attributed to and studio of. Contrast: Against like, which simply asserts a resemblance, this phrase names a deliberate imitation of a recognized style. Against as, it never assigns a role: a fresco in the manner of Giotto was emphatically not painted by Giotto, while a fresco by Giotto was.

Says the thing was made in imitation of a known style. In auction catalogues the phrase carries a specific and unflattering meaning — the work is by a follower, not the master — and that hedged quality travels with it into ordinary prose.

  1. A courtyard fountain in the manner of the Alhambra.
  2. He wrote three novels in the manner of Dashiell Hammett and one that was entirely his own.
  3. The sauce is finished in the manner of a classic beurre blanc.
  4. She ran the department in the manner of a ship's captain.
  5. The lithograph is catalogued in the manner of Daumier, which is the polite way of saying it is not one.

like#

Pronunciation: /laɪk/ · a stressed word with no weak form, which is one reason it is so audible in American speech · LYKE Type: simple — the same spelling is also a verb (I like it), an adjective (of like mind), a suffix (childlike), a quotative (he was like, no), and a discourse particle. Only the preposition takes an object and means "resembling." · Register: neutral as a preposition; the clause-taking and approximating uses are informal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (like me, never like I), gerund (It's like driving on ice), or a wh-free relative (like what happened last year). A finite clause after like — like I said, like you told me — is informal; careful American writing uses as or as if there. Never a that-clause. Pattern: [be/look/sound/feel/smell/taste/seem] like [noun phrase] · [verb] like [noun phrase] · Like [noun phrase], [clause] · just/much/something/more like [noun phrase] · It is (not) like [person] to [verb] Contrast: The like/as rule. Like takes a noun and states a resemblance; as takes a role or a clause. He works like a mule is a comparison; He works as a mule would mean the animal is his job. Before a clause, formal prose wants as (as I said) or as if (It looks as if it will rain). Against such as, some editors reserve like for resemblance and such as for actual instances (poets such as Frost), but that distinction is not observed in general American usage and like introduces examples freely.

The general-purpose word of resemblance, and the busiest preposition in spoken American English. Everything it does reduces to a single operation — hold X up against Y and assert that they are alike — but the range of things that can fill those two slots is enormous, which is why the entry runs long.

1. Resembling; in the same way as.

  1. The new bridge looks like a harp from the river.
  2. She drives like a professional and parks like a tourist.
  3. Nobody else on the crew works like him.

2. Typical or untypical of a person — always with be.

  1. It's not like Marcus to miss a deadline.
  2. That is exactly like her: she paid for everyone and slipped out.

3. Introducing an example.

  1. Bring something sturdy, like a tarp or a moving blanket.
  2. Root crops like beets and carrots store well in damp sand.

4. After verbs of perception and impression.

  1. The whole house smelled like woodsmoke for a week.
  2. It felt like the first real morning of fall.

5. Approximately — informal, before a quantity.

  1. There were like forty people crammed into that kitchen.
  2. It took like two hours just to get through security.

6. Not a preposition — the quotative be like and the discourse particle, both conversational, neither taking an object.

  1. And she was like, "I already sent it twice."
  2. It was, like, the third time that week — the discourse particle, with nothing after it that it governs.

similar to#

Pronunciation: /ˈsɪmələr tu/ · weak /ˈsɪmələr tə/ · SIM-uh-lur too Type: complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) · Register: neutral to formal Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (similar to running a small business), or a wh-free relative (similar to what we saw last spring) — never a finite clause. Similar with and similar than are errors; the preposition is always to. Pattern: [be/look/sound] similar to [noun phrase] · [noun] similar to [noun phrase] · broadly/roughly/strikingly similar to [noun phrase] Contrast: Against like, similar to is the analytic word: like asserts a likeness the reader can feel, similar to asserts one the writer could itemize, which is why lab reports and appraisals prefer it. Against the same as, it explicitly stops short of identity. Note that the noun takes different prepositions depending on the frame — a similarity to the original, but similarities between the two.

States a measured resemblance. It is the neutral technical alternative to like, and its natural home is a sentence in which the two things compared are of the same type and the differences are going to matter.

  1. The symptoms are similar to those of ordinary flu.
  2. This year's model is similar to last year's except for the transmission.
  3. Running a school board turned out to be similar to running a small business.
  4. Her handwriting is strikingly similar to her father's.
  5. The plan is broadly similar to one the county rejected in 2019.

the same as#

Pronunciation: /ðə ˈseɪm æz/ · weak /ðə ˈseɪm əz/, and in fast speech /ðə ˈseɪm z/ after a vowel · the is /ði/ before a vowel · thuh-SAME az Type: complex (determiner + adjective + fixed as; marginal — same is a fully live adjective, and the string is listed because choosing its second word is the problem) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (the same as me), gerund (the same as driving at night), or a wh-free relative (the same as what we had before). A finite clause can follow (the same as it always was), but there the as has gone back to conjunction duty, exactly as it does at as. Never a that-clause. The same than and the same like are errors. Pattern: [be] the same as [noun phrase] · the same [noun] as [noun phrase] — the two words split around the noun · much/exactly/about/roughly the same as [noun phrase] · not the same as [noun phrase] Variants: the same that in the split frame (the same house that we rented) is possible but reads as a relative clause, not as this preposition; American English uses as. Bare the same, with no complement (I'll have the same), is a noun phrase and not a preposition at all. Same as without the article (Same as last year) is idiomatic in speech and in headlines and is clipped, not wrong. Contrast: The top of the likeness ladder. The same as claims identity; on a par with claims equal standing; comparable to claims that a comparison is legitimate; similar to claims a resemblance with differences left over; akin to claims only a family likeness. Against equal to, which compares amounts, the same as compares kind as well as amount — the same as a hundred dollars and equal to a hundred dollars are not quite the same claim. And note the split frame: this is the only member of the section that can put a noun between its two halves.

Says two things are one thing, or that they match in every way currently being counted. It is the plainest identity statement English has, and every error it attracts is an error in the second word.

1. Identical to; matching in every relevant respect.

  1. Her schedule this fall is the same as it was in the spring.
  2. The replacement part is the same as the one that failed.
  3. My rent is about the same as yours, and I have half the space.

2. Split around the noun — the same [noun] as.

  1. We used the same contractor as the church did.
  2. She went to the same high school as both her parents.

3. Amounting to; equivalent in effect — the evaluative use.

  1. Saying nothing at the hearing was the same as agreeing to it.
  2. Twenty dollars an hour here is not the same as twenty dollars an hour in Seattle.
  3. Deferred maintenance is the same as a tax, only nobody votes on it.

unlike#

Pronunciation: /ʌnˈlaɪk/ · un-LYKE Type: simple (historically un- + like, no longer analyzed as two pieces) — also an adjective (two unlike quantities) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (unlike me), or gerund (unlike driving in the city) — never a finite clause. Unlike I said is not English; unlike what I said is the repair. Pattern: Unlike [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause], unlike [noun phrase] · [be] unlike [noun phrase] · not unlike [noun phrase] Contrast: The negative of prepositional like, and the one-word equivalent of in contrast to. The parallelism trap is worse here than anywhere else in this file because unlike is usually fronted: whatever follows it must match the subject of the main clause. Unlike the 1990s, today's market is volatile compares a decade with a market; Unlike the market of the 1990s, today's is volatile repairs it. Note the litotes not unlike, which means "rather like" and is a deliberate hedge, not a double negative to be canceled out.

Denies a resemblance the reader might otherwise have assumed. It does the work of a whole clause in one word, and because it nearly always sits at the front of the sentence, it puts an unusually heavy burden on the complement to be the right kind of thing.

1. Different from.

  1. Unlike her brother, she never learned to swim.
  2. Unlike most of the coast, the peninsula gets almost no fog.
  3. The second season, unlike the first, was filmed entirely on location.

2. Uncharacteristic of a person.

  1. It is unlike him to leave without saying anything.
  2. That kind of carelessness is completely unlike her.

3. Not unlike — the hedged resemblance.

  1. The sound was not unlike a train going over a trestle.
  2. His method is not unlike the one the old county surveyor used.

Explicit comparison and proportion#

These name a benchmark and measure something against it. The whole section is governed by one rule — the two things compared must be of the same kind — and by one stylistic fact: the phrase gets longer as the register gets more formal, running from than through compared with to in comparison with, with no change in meaning along the way. Four members make a claim about the comparison itself rather than reporting its result: comparable to says the two things are alike enough that measuring them against each other is legitimate, on a par with says the measurement came out even, commensurate with says one is fitting for the other, and equal to says the arithmetic came out the same. The comparison-to-a-norm sense of for, as in tall for his age, belongs to the same semantic field but is housed with the core relational prepositions.

as against#

Pronunciation: /əz əˈɡɛnst/ · full /æz əˈɡɛnst/ · uhz-uh-GENST Type: complex · Register: formal; sense 1 statistical and journalistic, sense 2 legal Complement: noun phrase, very often a bare figure — never a finite clause, never a gerund Pattern: [figure] as against [figure] · [clause] as against [noun phrase] · as against [party] — in legal use Contrast: In sense 1 it is a compact compared with, and it is nearly always attached to numbers; if the sentence has no figures in it, compared with or as opposed to reads better. Sense 2 is a term of art and is not a comparison at all: valid as against third parties means "enforceable in relation to them," not "opposed to them." Against plain against, the as signals measurement rather than hostility.

Sets two figures or two positions opposite each other. In general prose it is a compact and slightly old-fashioned comparison word; in law it is a term of art meaning "in relation to a particular party," and there it says nothing about conflict at all.

1. Compared with — chiefly between two figures.

  1. Revenue came in at $41 million as against $37 million a year earlier.
  2. Turnout was 61 percent, as against 48 in the last off-year election.
  3. Three inspectors now cover the whole county, as against nine in 2010.

2. Considered in relation to; enforceable with respect to — legal.

  1. The lien is valid as against subsequent purchasers.
  2. The waiver has no effect as against the insurer.
  3. Her claim is stronger as against the estate than as against the trust.

See also: compared with, versus, as opposed to, against, relative to

by comparison with#

Pronunciation: /baɪ kəmˈpɛrəsən wɪθ/ · weak /wəθ/ · also /wɪð/ before a vowel · by kum-PAIR-uh-sun with Type: complex · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a wh-free relative (by comparison with what the company earned) — never a finite clause Pattern: [clause] by comparison with [noun phrase] · By comparison with [noun phrase], [clause] Variants: by comparison to is equally acceptable in American English. The bare by comparison, with no complement, is an adverbial rather than a preposition: The second half was dull by comparison. Contrast: Interchangeable with in comparison with and slightly lighter on the page. Both are longer than compared with, which does identical work in two syllables — reach for the phrase only when the benchmark is long or when the comparison needs to be fronted.

Puts the main clause beside a benchmark and invites the reader to notice the gap. Its real advantage over compared with is rhythmic: it can carry a heavy complement without stalling the sentence.

  1. By comparison with the drought years, this spring has been generous.
  2. The fine looks trivial by comparison with what the company earned on the contract.
  3. Our overhead is modest by comparison with firms of the same size.
  4. By comparison with the original recording, the remaster is almost harsh.
  5. The delay was minor by comparison with what the other agencies went through.

See also: in comparison with, compared with, relative to, next to

commensurate with#

Pronunciation: /kəˈmɛnsərət wɪθ/ · weak /wəθ/ · also /kəˈmɛnʃərət/ · kuh-MEN-sur-ut with Type: complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) · Register: formal; standard in job postings, compensation policy, and sentencing Complement: noun phrase, or a free relative headed by what (commensurate with what the job demands) — never a finite clause; a gerund is grammatical but rare (commensurate with running a department that size) Pattern: [noun] commensurate with [noun phrase] · be commensurate with [noun phrase] Contrast: Commensurate to is an error in current American usage; the preposition is with. Where in proportion to states an arithmetic relation, commensurate with states a judgment of fitness: a salary commensurate with experience is a fair one, not one computed from a formula. Against in line with, it is more evaluative and considerably more formal.

Says one quantity is fitting for another — large enough, serious enough, or long enough to match it. The compensation cliché salary commensurate with experience is where most Americans meet the word, and what it means is "we will pay what your résumé justifies."

  1. Salary commensurate with experience.
  2. The sentence should be commensurate with the harm done.
  3. She wanted a title commensurate with the work she was already doing.
  4. The reserve is not commensurate with the risk the fund is carrying.
  5. Federal aid was never commensurate with the scale of the damage.

comparable to#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɑmpərəbəl tu/ · weak /ˈkɑmpərəbəl tə/ · also /kəmˈpɛrəbəl/, with the stress pulled onto the second syllable — now common in American speech and still objected to by some · KAHM-pur-uh-bul too Type: complex (adjective + fixed preposition; marginal) · Register: neutral to formal; standard in appraisal, statistics, and compensation Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (comparable to hers), gerund (comparable to running a small hotel), or a wh-free relative (comparable to what the coasts pay) — never a finite clause. Comparable than is an error, and comparable as is not English. Pattern: [be/seem] comparable to [noun phrase] · [noun] comparable to [noun phrase] · roughly/broadly/directly comparable to [noun phrase] · not comparable to [noun phrase] Variants: comparable with is the older analytic form and survives in technical writing, on the same logic that keeps compared with alive; comparable to is far commoner in American English and is safe everywhere. The related noun takes either preposition — a comparison with or a comparison to. In real-estate work the clipped noun comps (we pulled three comps) is standard and is not this preposition. Contrast: Against similar to, which says two things resemble each other, comparable to says they are close enough in kind and in size that setting them side by side proves something — which is why an appraiser's comparable sale is a measurement rather than an impression. Against on a par with, it is descriptive rather than evaluative: a salary comparable to the market measures the same, while a salary on a par with the market is being judged adequate. Against the same as, it explicitly allows a gap. The negative not comparable to is the standard American way of refusing a comparison outright, and it is a stronger move than saying the two things merely differ.

Says two things are alike enough to be measured against each other. Its claim is about the legitimacy of the comparison rather than about how the comparison came out, which is exactly why appraisers, statisticians, and compensation committees reach for it.

  1. Starting salaries here are comparable to what the state pays for the same work.
  2. The two districts are not comparable to each other in size or in wealth.
  3. A studio in this neighborhood rents for a figure comparable to a two-bedroom upstate.
  4. The appraiser found three recent sales comparable to the subject property.
  5. Running the county fair is comparable to running a small hotel for a week.
  6. This year's storm damage is comparable to what the coast saw in 2011.
  7. Nothing in the record is comparable to the penalty the board handed down.

See also: similar to, on a par with, the same as, compared with, commensurate with, relative to

compared to#

Pronunciation: /kəmˈpɛrd tu/ · weak /kəmˈpɛrd tə/ · kum-PAIRD too Type: complex (a fossilized past participle — it needs no subject to control it, which is what makes it a preposition rather than a dangling modifier) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (compared to me), gerund (compared to driving), or a free relative (compared to what we paid last year) — never a finite clause Pattern: [clause] compared to [noun phrase] · Compared to [noun phrase], [clause] · [noun] compared to [noun phrase] Variants: as compared to and as compared with are heavier forms carrying no extra meaning. Contrast: The traditional rule: compare to likens two unlike things (compared the city to an anthill), while compare with sets two like things side by side to weigh them (compared the 2019 figures with the 2020 figures). That distinction is real in the verb and is still worth keeping there. Contrast and opposition#

Comparison notices a difference; contrast insists on it; opposition takes a side. The three shade into one another, and the useful distinctions are these: in contrast to only reports that two things differ, contrary to claims that one contradicts the other, and in defiance of adds that somebody knew and did it anyway. A separate trio reports that two accounts fail to square — at odds with in a newspaper, inconsistent with in a brief or a lab report, at variance with in the most formal register of all — and none of the three says which side is wrong. Versus and against are the plain words of conflict, and pro and anti — the two Latin prepositions that Americans have almost entirely converted into prefixes — are treated at pro and anti.

Preference: choosing one thing over another#

A small, tight family. All three name two options and tell you which one survived — and the position of the surviving option differs from word to word, which is where the errors come from. After over and in favor of, the winner follows the preposition; after rather than, as opposed to, and in preference to, the loser does.

Support, alignment, and conformity#

The positive half of the field. Four of these — in favor of, in support of, in defense of, on the side of — declare that you are backing something, and they are not interchangeable: you are in favor of a proposal (approval), you march in support of it (an act), you write in defense of it only once it has been attacked, and you are on the side of a party rather than of a question. The one-word member of that group is behind — the union is solidly behind the proposal — whose support sense is a straight extension of its spatial one and is treated with the prepositions of position at behind. The rest are the conformity prepositions, and they form a strength ladder: in accordance with binds, in line with matches, in keeping with merely fits the spirit, and consistent with claims only that nothing contradicts anything.
