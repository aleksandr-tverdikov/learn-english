Marginal, participial, and postpositive prepositions#

The words that are prepositions only by the job they do — verbs, adjectives, and adverbs drafted into prepositional service, plus the handful that follow their object instead of preceding it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Nothing in this file started life as a preposition. These are verbs (considering, given, pending), adjectives (absent, effective, worth), and adverbs (ago, aside, hence) that have been recruited into prepositional service, and each keeps just enough of its old behavior to trip you up. Two tests separate the preposition from the participle it came from: a true preposition needs no subject and cannot dangle (Considering the weather, the turnout was good is fine, while Walking to work, the rain started is not), and its object is a plain noun phrase that a pronoun replaces in the objective case (including me, never including I). The postpositive group at the end breaks the one rule everyone thinks prepositions have — the object comes first, and the order is fixed rather than stylistic: three weeks ago, the objections notwithstanding, present company excepted. One trap runs through the whole file: almost none of these words accepts a finite clause (✗ regarding he was late, ✗ following she resigned), and the four that genuinely do — considering that, given that, granted that, and legal notwithstanding that — are exactly the ones careful writers avoid out of misplaced caution.

The complement rule that governs the whole class. A preposition takes a noun phrase, and when a verb has to fill that slot the verb becomes a gerund, never an infinitive. This is true here (worth seeing, ✗ worth to see; aside from being late, ✗ aside from to be late), and it is true of every other to in English that is a preposition rather than an infinitive marker: look forward to hearing (✗ look forward to hear), object to being told (✗ object to be told), accustomed to working nights (✗ accustomed to work nights), used to getting up early, opposed to raising rates, committed to hiring locally, devoted to restoring the house, in addition to paying the fine, prior to leaving, with a view to selling. The test is substitution — if a plain noun fits after the word (look forward to the weekend, accustomed to the noise, opposed to the plan), the to is a preposition and the verb after it must end in -ing. Two more restrictions travel with the class and are worth stating once: the concessives and causals take a noun phrase or a gerund and never a that-clause (despite the rain, despite being late, ✗ despite it rained, ✗ despite that it rained; in spite of the delay, in spite of arriving late, ✗ in spite of that it was delayed; owing to the strike, owing to being short-staffed, ✗ owing to that they struck; due to fog, due to fog closing the field, ✗ due to that it was foggy) — for a clause you must switch words entirely, to although, even though, or because. And a pronoun object is always objective (including me, compared to her, ✗ between you and I).

Reduced pronunciations. Two things weaken in this file, and neither shows in the spelling. First, every -ing ending drops to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (considerin', includin', dependin'). Second — the part learners never hear — the small word welded onto the end of a two-word member reduces to a schwa exactly as it does everywhere else in English: of /əv/ → /ə/, to → /tə/ (owing to /ˈoʊɪn tə/, compared to /kəmˈpɛrd tə/), for → /fɚ/ (allowing for /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/), at → /ət/, from → /frəm/ (aside from /əˈsaɪd frəm/), on → /ən/ (based on /beɪst ən/, depending on /dəˈpɛndɪn ən/), by → /bə/ (judging by /ˈdʒʌdʒɪn bə/), and with /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/, which is what you hear buried inside notwithstanding /ˌnɑtwəθˈstændɪn/. The strong form is what you say in isolation or under stress; the weak form is what you say in a sentence, and a learner who pronounces every one of these at full value sounds wrong in a way that is hard to place.

Near-neighbor rules that touch this file. Several confusable pairs cataloged elsewhere in this book decide sentences built on words from this file, so the rules are worth having in one place. By names a deadline for one completed act (file it by Friday), until names how long a state lasts (we're closed until Friday), and pending names the event instead of the date (closed pending inspection). At the front of a sentence American English wants except for, not bare except (Except for the ending, it's great), while after a noun either works (everyone except him). In names a location or a period, within an upper bound — in an hour can mean after an hour has passed, within an hour cannot. Between takes two parties, or several considered pairwise; among takes an undifferentiated mass. Beside means alongside, besides means in addition to. Like takes a noun phrase and as takes a clause (she works like a machine, she works as I do). Different from is the safe American form, different than is tolerated before a clause, and different to is British. In is static where into is directional, exactly as on is static where onto is directional. And toward is the American default against British towards — the same split that gives amid/amidst, among/amongst, forward/forwards, and American around against British round.

American forms in this file. Where the two standards split, this book gives the American form first and labels the other as British — never the reverse. In this file that means aside and aside from are the American choices and apart and apart from the British-leaning ones; Monday through Friday is the American inclusive range and has no British equivalent shorter than Monday to Friday inclusive; bar ("bar a miracle") is British and survives in American English only in bar none; and thru is an American spelling but a signage-only one. One pair is often mislabeled and is worth correcting here: long since is not British. It is ordinary formal American English, and it differs from long ago by tense rather than by nationality — long since pairs with the perfect (the mill has long since closed), long ago with the simple past (the mill closed long ago). The same American-first rule governs the pairs catalogued elsewhere: toward, amid, among, and forward are the American defaults, and towards, amidst, amongst, and forwards are the British forms; round is British where American English uses around (all year round is the one American holdout).

Participial prepositions in -ing#

The core of the group: present participles that lost their subject. Every one of them reduces its ending to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (considerin', includin'), so the written -ing is rarely what you hear. None of them agrees with anything, and none of them needs a subject. Only considering takes a that-clause freely; notwithstanding that is confined to legal drafting; every other word in this section rejects a finite clause outright, which is the single trap to watch. Most of them do take a gerund, and that is the repair when a clause tempts you: not ✗ following she resigned but following her resignation, not ✗ owing to that costs rose but owing to rising costs. The topic members (concerning, regarding, pertaining to, depending on, judging by) additionally accept a wh-nominal — regarding what you asked, depending on whether it rains — which is a clause in shape but a noun phrase in job, and is the one clause-like thing they allow.

allowing for#

Pronunciation: /əˈlaʊɪŋ fɔr/ · weak /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/ · uh-LOW-ing fer Type: marginal/participial (two-word) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ allowing for the traffic was heavy → allowing for traffic, or allowing for the fact that traffic was heavy). The for is part of the word: dropping it gives you the plain verb, and allowing the traffic means something else entirely. Pattern: Allowing for [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] allowing for [noun phrase]. Contrast: Three words adjust a figure and they are not interchangeable. Allowing for X builds X into the estimate and keeps it in view; excluding X takes X out of the arithmetic; discounting X dismisses X as not worth counting. Barring is different again — it names a future spoiler rather than a known factor. Against given, which accepts a premise whole, allowing for concedes only a partial adjustment.

Makes room in a claim for a factor the speaker already knows about. It is the honest hedge of estimates, budgets, and forecasts.

1. Adjusting a figure for a known factor. The quantitative use. 2. Making allowance for a person's circumstances. The excusing use, close to considering.

  1. Allowing for inflation, the raise is closer to two percent.
  2. Allowing for traffic, the drive takes an hour and a half.
  3. Allowing for rounding, the two totals match.
  4. The schedule holds, allowing for one weather day.
  5. Even allowing for his inexperience, the mistake was serious.
  6. Allowing for being new to the system, she has picked it up fast.

See also: discounting, excluding, depending on, considering, given

barring#

Pronunciation: /ˈbɑrɪŋ/ · casual /ˈbɑrɪn/ · BAR-ing (not BAIR-ing, which is bearing) Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral, faintly bookish Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ barring that the weather holds). Pronoun objects take the objective case: barring him. Pattern: Barring [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] barring [noun phrase]. Contrast: Barring is hypothetical and forward-looking — it names the one thing that could still spoil a prediction. Excepting and excluding subtract something that actually exists. The bare form bar (bar a miracle) is chiefly British; American English keeps bar only in the fixed phrase bar none. Variants: bar (British; in American English only in bar none)

Frozen from the verb bar, "to block." It sets one obstacle in front of an otherwise confident claim and is overwhelmingly a sentence-opener.

1. Unless this one thing intervenes. The default use, always about the future. 2. With the exception of. A weaker, static use, close to apart from.

  1. Barring a last-minute appeal, the sale closes Friday.
  2. Barring bad weather, we should be in Denver by six.
  3. The team makes the playoffs barring another injury at quarterback.
  4. Barring a few typos, the manuscript is clean.
  5. Barring the second chapter, I liked the book.
  6. She has the votes, barring something nobody has thought of yet.
  7. Barring being called in to cover a shift, I'm free all weekend.

See also: excepting, excluding, failing, pending, absent

bating#

Pronunciation: /ˈbeɪtɪŋ/ · flapped /ˈbeɪɾɪŋ/ · BAY-ting (a homophone of baiting) Type: marginal/participial · Register: archaic; alive only in legal and antiquarian prose Complement: noun phrase — never a finite clause in modern use. Do not write bating that. Pattern: bating [noun phrase] · Bating [noun phrase], [main clause]. Contrast: A dead synonym of excepting and barring. Clipped from abating, from the verb abate, "to subtract." Do not confuse it with baiting (luring) or with bated in with bated breath, which is the same root in a different sense — "restrained."

Subtracts an item from a total or a claim. Any modern sentence that uses it is either quoting an old document or making a joke about one; except for is the living equivalent.

  1. Bating a few errors of arithmetic, the accounts were in order.
  2. The whole estate passed to the widow, bating the mill.
  3. Bating his temper, he was an excellent tenant.
  4. Every witness agreed, bating the clerk.
  5. She was, bating one bad season, the best pitcher in the league.

See also: excepting, saving, barring, wanting

beginning#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈɡɪnɪŋ/ · weak /bəˈɡɪnɪn/ · bih-GIN-ing Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal; the standard word in notices and official announcements Complement: a time expression only — a date, a day, or a prepositional phrase of time (beginning in July, beginning at noon). Never a finite clause, and never an ordinary object (✗ beginning the project in this sense, which is the plain verb). Pattern: Beginning [date], [main clause]. · [main clause] beginning [date]. Contrast: Starting is the everyday equivalent and is what Americans say out loud; beginning is the version that gets printed on the sign. As of can look backward as well as forward (as of Tuesday, we had no answer); beginning only looks forward. From needs a matching endpoint or an on to feel complete.

A pure scheduling word. It fixes the first day of a new arrangement and says nothing about when the arrangement ends.

  1. Beginning Monday, the clinic opens at seven.
  2. Rates increase beginning January 1.
  3. Beginning in the fall, all freshmen live on campus.
  4. The library will close at six beginning next week.
  5. Beginning with the third episode, the show finds its footing.

See also: starting, effective, following, pending, as of

concerning#

Pronunciation: /kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ · casual /kənˈsɜrnɪn/ · kun-SUR-ning Type: marginal/participial · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or wh-nominal (concerning what happened) — never a finite that-clause. To take a clause you need concerning the fact that, which is worse than starting over. Pattern: concerning [noun phrase] · Concerning [noun phrase], [main clause]. Contrast: Concerning, regarding, respecting, touching, and re all mean "about," but only the first two are still in ordinary use, and concerning is the heavier of those two — it belongs to correspondence and reports. In speech, plain about is almost always the better word. Note that the adjective concerning — "a concerning trend," meaning worrying — is a separate, much newer American usage and is not this word.

The formal topic preposition: it names what a document, question, or complaint is about.

  1. I have a question concerning the deadline.
  2. The memo says nothing concerning severance.
  3. Concerning your invoice, we never received it.
  4. She wrote to the board concerning the audit.
  5. Rumors concerning the merger have been circulating for weeks.
  6. There is a statute concerning exactly this situation.

See also: regarding, respecting, touching, pertaining to, re, with regard to

considering#

Pronunciation: /kənˈsɪdərɪŋ/, syncopated /kənˈsɪdrɪŋ/ · casual /kənˈsɪdrɪn/ · kun-SID-uh-ring Type: marginal/participial; also a subordinating conjunction with that, and an absolute adverb · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a finite clause with or without that — considering (that) she had never flown before. It is the one word in this file that freely takes a clause in ordinary American English. It can also stand alone with no complement at all: She did well, considering. Pattern: Considering [noun phrase], [main clause]. · Considering (that) [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause], considering. Contrast: Against given: both accept a fact and reason from it, but given is clipped and treats the fact as a settled premise, while considering implies weighing and leans toward excusing what follows. Only considering can trail a sentence alone (✗ She did well, given). Do not confuse it with concerning — considering the evidence means in view of it, concerning the evidence means about it.

The workhorse of the group, and the only one that has kept a foot in three word classes at once.

1. In view of; taking into account. With a noun phrase. 2. Seeing that. With a full clause, functioning as a conjunction. 3. All things considered. Standing alone at the end of a sentence, as an adverb.

  1. Considering her age, she recovered remarkably fast.
  2. The price is fair considering the condition of the roof.
  3. Considering the hour, the crowd was enormous.
  4. Considering that he had two hours' notice, the speech was excellent.
  5. Considering you've never done this before, that's impressive.
  6. Considering having to learn the software from scratch, she was quick.
  7. The turnout was good, considering.
  8. It went well, considering — nobody walked out.

See also: given, granted, based on, concerning, notwithstanding

counting#

Pronunciation: /ˈkaʊntɪŋ/ · casual /ˈkaʊnɪn/ · KOWN-ting Type: marginal/participial · Register: informal to neutral; a spoken word Complement: noun phrase or gerund (not counting waiting for a table) — never a finite clause (✗ not counting that the kids came). Almost always negated: not counting. Pattern: counting [noun phrase] · not counting [noun phrase] · [main clause], not counting [noun phrase]. Contrast: Counting is the conversational including, and it is restricted to tallies — you can say counting the dog about a headcount, but not counting Chapter 4 about a table of contents. Negative not counting is the spoken excluding.

The arithmetic preposition of everyday speech. It adds or, more often, withholds an item from a running total.

1. Including in a total. Positive, usually about people or money. 2. Excluding from a total. The far more common negative, not counting.

  1. There are nine of us, counting the kids.
  2. Counting tips, she clears about forty thousand.
  3. It's a two-hour drive, not counting traffic.
  4. We've lost three games, not counting the exhibition.
  5. The room sleeps six, not counting the couch.
  6. Counting the garage, the place is nineteen hundred square feet.
  7. It's ninety minutes at that place, not counting waiting for a table.

See also: including, excluding, discounting, included, not counting

depending on#

Pronunciation: /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ˈɑn/, /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ˈɔn/ · casual /dəˈpɛndɪn ən/ · dih-PEN-ding awn Type: marginal/participial (two-word) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a wh-nominal — and the wh-nominal is the everyday form: depending on what he says, depending on whether it rains, depending on how many show up. Never a bare that-clause (✗ depending on that it rains). ✗ Depending of and ✗ depending from are errors; the adjective is dependent on or dependent upon, never dependent of. Pattern: Depending on [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause], depending on [noun phrase or wh-clause]. Contrast: Three near neighbors sort by what the object names. Depending on names the variable that will decide the outcome; based on names the evidence a conclusion rests on; according to names the source of a report. So the price varies depending on the season (variable), we chose based on the reviews (evidence), according to the reviews, it's good (source). Keep the preposition apart from the plain verb: the outcome depends on the vote is a verb with a subject, while depending on the vote, we may adjourn has no subject and is a preposition. Variants: depending upon (formal)

Names the thing that has not been settled yet and that will settle everything else. It is the standard way American English hedges a quantity, a schedule, or a decision.

1. Determined by; varying with. With a noun phrase — the range use. 2. Conditional on the answer to a question. With a wh- or whether-nominal.

  1. Depending on the weather, the game starts at six or not at all.
  2. Tickets run $40 to $90, depending on the seat.
  3. Depending on what the lab says, we may operate tonight.
  4. Depending on whether the bill passes, the rate changes in July.
  5. She works from home two or three days, depending on the week.
  6. Depending on how you count, there are eight planets or nine.

See also: based on, allowing for, judging by, according to, regardless of

discounting#

Pronunciation: /dɪsˈkaʊntɪŋ/ · casual /dɪsˈkaʊnɪn/ · dis-KOWN-ting Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal; common in analysis and argument Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause. Pattern: Discounting [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] discounting [noun phrase]. Contrast: Excluding removes an item as a matter of arithmetic; discounting dismisses it as not worth weighing. The difference is judgment: excluding the outliers is a method, discounting the outliers is an argument. Not counting is the casual version of excluding, never of this.

Sets something aside on the grounds that it should not count — because it is unreliable, unrepresentative, or interested. It carries an implicit claim, which is why it belongs to persuasion rather than bookkeeping.

  1. Discounting the outliers, the results are consistent.
  2. Discounting his own testimony, there is no evidence at all.
  3. Sales rose eight percent, discounting the effect of the promotion.
  4. Discounting inflation, wages have been flat for a decade.
  5. Even discounting the hype, the device is impressive.
  6. Discounting having played at home all month, the streak still means something.

See also: excluding, counting, barring, regardless of

during#

Pronunciation: /ˈdʊrɪŋ/, widely /ˈdɜrɪŋ/ (rhyming with stirring) · casual /ˈdɜrɪn/ · British keeps the yod: /ˈdjʊərɪŋ/ · DOOR-ing or DUR-ing Type: marginal/participial by origin, now a fully grammaticalized simple preposition · Register: neutral Complement: a noun phrase naming a period or an event — never a finite clause (✗ during I was there → while I was there), never a gerund (✗ during eating → while eating), and never a measure of duration (✗ during three hours → for three hours). Pattern: during [noun phrase] · During [noun phrase], [main clause]. Contrast: The big one is during vs. for: for answers "how long" and takes a quantity (for two weeks), while during answers "when" and takes a named span (during the strike). for · While is the clause form; throughout insists on the entire span, where during leaves the point inside it vague.

Historically a participle — the object of the obsolete verb dure, "to last," in an absolute construction (during the king's life once meant "the king's life enduring"). Nothing of that survives in the modern word except its membership in this file.

1. At some point inside a period. The event is located somewhere within the span. 2. Throughout a period. The event occupies the whole span.

  1. She called during the meeting.
  2. The power went out twice during the storm.
  3. He worked two jobs during college.
  4. During the strike, the plant produced nothing.
  5. Nobody speaks during the reading.
  6. During the sixties, the town lost half its population.

See also: for, pending, following, beginning

excepting#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈsɛptɪŋ/, /ɛkˈsɛptɪŋ/ · casual /ɪkˈsɛptɪn/ · ik-SEP-ting Type: marginal/participial · Register: formal and dated; standard only after not and always Complement: noun phrase — never a finite clause in modern American English. The old conjunction excepting that is archaic. Pattern: not excepting [noun phrase] · always excepting [noun phrase] · Excepting [noun phrase], [main clause]. Contrast: Plain except is the ordinary word, and using excepting where except would do sounds stilted or legalistic. Between the two live forms, the rule is positional: at the front of a sentence American English requires except for (Except for the ending, the movie is great; ✗ Except the ending, the movie is great), while after the noun either works (everyone except him, everyone except for him). Excepting can front on its own, which is the one thing it still does that except cannot. Excepting otherwise survives in two fixed frames: not excepting (which insists someone is included) and always excepting (a legal reservation). The postposed participle excepted is alive and well.

An older, heavier except. Note that not excepting means the opposite of what learners expect — it does not exclude anyone; it refuses to exclude them.

  1. Everyone signed, not excepting the chairman.
  2. The rule applies to all staff, always excepting the medical team.
  3. Excepting the last chapter, the book is a delight.
  4. All fees are refundable, excepting the deposit.
  5. Every state ratified it, not excepting the two that had sued.

See also: excepted, excluding, saving, barring, except for

excluding#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈskludɪŋ/ · casual /ɪkˈskludɪn/ · ik-SKLOO-ding Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral; the default in commercial, technical, and statistical writing Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause. Pattern: [noun phrase] excluding [noun phrase] · Excluding [noun phrase], [main clause]. Contrast: The exact opposite of including, and the neutral partner of not counting. Against except for: excluding is arithmetical and unemotional, while except for often implies the exception is remarkable. Exclusive of is the formal contract-drafting version. Against discounting: excluding removes, discounting dismisses.

Subtracts a specified item from a set, a total, or a price. It makes no claim about why.

  1. The fare is $340, excluding taxes and fees.
  2. Excluding weekends, that leaves eleven working days.
  3. Enrollment rose in every division, excluding the law school.
  4. The study covered adults, excluding anyone over eighty.
  5. Excluding the two pilot sites, results were flat.
  6. The job takes four hours, excluding driving out and back.

See also: including, excluded, excepting, discounting, exclusive of

failing#

Pronunciation: /ˈfeɪlɪŋ/ · casual /ˈfeɪlɪn/ · FAY-ling Type: marginal/participial · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase, typically abstract (an agreement, a payment, a response) or the pronoun that — never a finite clause (✗ failing that they agree → failing agreement). Pattern: Failing [noun phrase], [main clause]. · Failing that, [main clause]. Contrast: Failing and barring are mirror images. Barring X means "unless X happens," where X is the obstacle; failing X means "if X does not happen," where X is the thing you were hoping for. Absent and in the absence of are the near-synonyms; failing that is the fixed phrase that carries the whole word in everyday writing.

Introduces the fallback. It names the thing that was supposed to occur and quietly concedes that it might not.

1. If the named thing does not happen. With an abstract noun phrase, and always fronted. 2. Failing that. The fixed phrase, which carries the word in everyday writing and is the only form most Americans ever use.

  1. Failing an agreement by Friday, the case goes to trial.
  2. Failing payment within thirty days, the account goes to collections.
  3. Failing any response from the seller, the buyer may cancel.
  4. Ask your supervisor; failing that, put it in writing to HR.
  5. We'll take the early flight, or failing that, drive.
  6. Try the main number, and failing that, email the clerk.

See also: absent, lacking, barring, pending, wanting

following#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ · casual /ˈfɑləwɪn/ · FAH-loh-ing Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal; heavy in journalism and official statements Complement: noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ following she resigned → after she resigned, or following her resignation). A gerund is grammatical but nearly always clumsy (following being notified → following notification), so the repair for a tempting clause is a nominalization, not an -ing form. The demonstrative pronoun is fine: following that, we adjourned. Pattern: Following [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] following [noun phrase]. Contrast: After is the plain word, it can take a clause, and it is almost always better. Following implies the second event came because of the first, which is exactly why press releases prefer it — and exactly why it should be resisted when no such link exists. Subsequent to is worse than either. Do not let it collide with the noun the following (please read the following).

The journalist's after. It attaches an event to the trigger that preceded it, and it dangles easily: Following the vote, the room emptied is fine, but Following the vote, applause broke out invites the reading that the applause did the following.

1. After, in plain sequence. No causal claim — the second event simply came later. 2. In the wake of; as a result of. The loaded use, and the reason press offices reach for the word.

  1. Following a brief recess, the hearing resumed.
  2. Following his release, he moved to Arizona.
  3. Following the storm, half the county was without power.
  4. Three executives resigned following the audit.
  5. The bill was withdrawn following pressure from both parties.
  6. Following that, we adjourned until Thursday.

See also: preceding, pending, during, beginning, prior to, subsequent to

going on#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈɑn/, /ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈɔn/ · casual /ˈɡoʊɪn ɔn/ · goh-in-ON Type: marginal/participial (two-word) · Register: informal, spoken Complement: a number or a number-headed time or age expression — never a clause, never a person or an object (✗ going on the meeting). Pattern: [be] going on [number] · [noun phrase] going on [number] Contrast: Nearly and almost are adverbs and go with anything; going on attaches only to numbers, and only from below — you cannot be going on an age you have already passed. The idiom nine going on thirty-five (a knowing child) is a fixed joke built on the same frame.

Says you are approaching a number — an age, a clock time, or a length of service — without having reached it.

  1. She's going on forty and still won't fly.
  2. It's going on midnight; we should head back.
  3. He's been at the paper going on twenty years.
  4. The kid is six going on thirty.
  5. They've been engaged going on three years now.

See also: ago, back, hence, worth

including#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈkludɪŋ/ · casual /ɪnˈkludɪn/ · in-KLOO-ding Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ including that he lied). Pronoun objects must be objective: including me, including her and me — ✗ including my wife and I, which is the single most common error made with this word. Pattern: [noun phrase], including [noun phrase] · Including [noun phrase], [main clause]. · Postposed counterpart: [noun phrase] included. Contrast: Including opens a partial list; namely and that is introduce a complete one, so including must never be followed by the whole set. Against counting: including works for any set, counting only for tallies. In contracts, including alone can be read as exhaustive, which is why drafters write including but not limited to.

Names one or more members of a set already mentioned, leaving the rest implied.

1. Naming examples from a larger set. The list is partial by definition. 2. Counting an amount into a total. Usually money, weight, or time.

Prepositions from past participles#

Four forms that shed a subject and a be and became prepositions outright. They split on the complement, and the split is the thing to memorize: given and granted accept a that-clause, which puts them in a tiny minority, while based on and compared to take a noun phrase, a gerund, or a wh-nominal and reject a finite clause outright (✗ based on he told me, ✗ compared to she was faster). In the two-word members the second word is welded on — you cannot drop the on or the to, and you cannot swap in another preposition.

  2. Granted that the data are thin, the pattern is still suggestive.
  3. Granted, the seats are terrible, but the sound is perfect.
  4. It's a risk, granted, and we're taking it anyway.
  5. Granting his sincerity, the argument still fails.
  6. Granted the premise, the conclusion follows.

See also: given, considering, notwithstanding, in spite of

Marginal prepositions from other word classes#

Words that were never verbs at all, yet take a bare object exactly the way a preposition does. Four of them are catalogued in the files that own their meaning rather than here, because what they do matters more than where they came from: the adjective absent (absent a court order) with the exception prepositions, the fossilized subjunctive come (come Monday) and the adjective effective (effective July 1) with the time prepositions, and the Latin ablative re (re your email) with the topic prepositions. One stays here, because no other file has a claim on it and because it takes a direct object without being a verb.

worth#

Pronunciation: /wɜrθ/ · run together before a weak object: worth it /ˈwɜrθ ɪt/ → /ˈwɜrθət/ · WURTH Type: marginal (traditionally labeled an adjective, but it takes a bare object like a preposition) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause and never a to-infinitive: ✗ worth to see, ✗ worth to be seen → worth seeing. This is one of the most common learner errors in English. The object may be a pronoun: worth it. Pattern: [subject] is worth [noun phrase] · [subject] is worth [verb-ing] · it is worth [verb-ing] · for what it's worth Contrast: Worth takes its object bare; worthy requires of — worth praise but worthy of praise; ✗ worth of praise is the error that comes of mixing them. And the gerund after worth is passive in meaning even though it is active in form: the house is worth fixing up means worth being fixed up, and adding being is wrong (✗ worth being fixed up). Keep the preposition apart from the identical noun, which follows a possessive and takes of: ten dollars' worth of gas, get your money's worth. Note the apostrophe — the measure is possessive, and ten dollars worth is a misspelling.

The reason worth sits in this file is that it is the one common English word that takes a direct object without being a verb or a preposition by traditional labeling. Every modern grammar that reclassifies it makes it a preposition.

1. Having a stated monetary value. 2. Deserving the time, money, or trouble. Typically with a gerund — and the gerund is where learners reach for the infinitive and go wrong. 3. In fixed phrases. For what it's worth, for all it's worth. 4. Cross-class: the noun. Preceded by a possessive and followed by of, which the preposition never is: a dollar's worth of trouble.

  1. The house is worth about four hundred thousand.
  2. That coin is worth more than the car it came in.
  3. The drive is long, but the view is worth it.
  4. This place is worth visiting twice.
  5. It's worth asking before you assume.
  6. The old boiler is not worth repairing a third time.
  7. For what it's worth, I'd have made the same call.
  8. The agent played up the school district for all it was worth.
  9. We got twenty dollars' worth of gas and our money's worth of advice.

See also: going on, absent, come, effective

Postpositive and deferred prepositions#

The small group that follows its object. The order is fixed grammar, not style: three weeks ago can never become ago three weeks, and present company excepted can never be fronted. Because the object comes first, the safest way to think about these is as prepositions turned around — the noun phrase is still the complement, and it is still what the word is doing its work on. Most of them are also cross-class, and the test is always the same: the postpositive has a noun phrase in front of it, and the look-alike adverb has nothing at all (a while back against come back, money aside against step aside, two years hence against hence the detour). Two words covered elsewhere in this file belong to the group as well: notwithstanding, which is the only English preposition equally natural in both orders, and the postposed adverbials built on core prepositions, such as ten years on and the world over, which are frozen idioms rather than a productive pattern.

ago#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡoʊ/ · uh-GOH Type: postpositive · Register: neutral Complement: precedes it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (three weeks, a moment, long). It can take nothing after it, ever, and never a clause. The measure phrase is obligatory: ✗ He left ago. Pattern: [measure of time] ago · ✗ ago [measure of time] Contrast: Two rules. First, ago counts back from now and therefore demands the simple past: ✗ He has left three days ago → He left three days ago. Before counts back from a point already in the past: He had left three days before. Second, ago is not interchangeable with for — ago locates an event, for measures a duration (she left three days ago, she was gone for three days). And long since is not a British substitute for long ago, as it is sometimes labeled: it is standard formal American English, and it differs by tense, taking the perfect that ago forbids (the shop has long since closed, ✗ the shop has closed long ago).

Descended from the past participle of Old English āgān, "to pass by," so it is a participial preposition too — one that finished its journey centuries earlier than the rest of this file and ended up on the wrong side of its object.

  1. Three weeks ago we had no idea.
  2. She called a few minutes ago.
  3. That happened long ago.
  4. The factory closed twenty years ago.
  5. I saw him not five minutes ago.
  6. A century ago, none of this existed.

See also: back, hence, going on, prior to

apart#

Pronunciation: /əˈpɑrt/ · uh-PART Type: postpositive · Register: neutral; slightly British in this construction Complement: precedes it — a noun phrase or gerund. Never a clause. Pattern: [noun phrase] apart, [main clause]. Contrast: Aside is the American default in this frame: joking aside is idiomatic in the U.S., while joking apart reads as British. Do not confuse the postposed apart with the fronted complex preposition apart from, which means "except for" or "in addition to," is itself the British-leaning counterpart of aside from, and lives with the multi-word prepositions. Cross-class, the test is whether anything is being set aside: the postpositive has an object in front of it (cost apart), while the plain adverb has no object at all (the two men drifted apart, I can't tell them apart).

Sets a topic to one side so the sentence can get to the real point. Almost always fronted as a clause-opening phrase with a comma.

  1. Cost apart, the plan is workable.
  2. Joking apart, we need an answer by noon.
  3. Politics apart, the two men were friends for forty years.
  4. That one incident apart, the trip was flawless.
  5. Style apart, the argument is sound.

See also: aside, aside from, excepted, apart from

aside#

Pronunciation: /əˈsaɪd/ · uh-SYD Type: postpositive · Register: neutral; the American preference over apart Complement: precedes it — a noun phrase or gerund. Never a clause. Pattern: [noun phrase] aside, [main clause]. Contrast: Same job as postposed apart, but this is the form Americans use. Note the direction change when it moves to the front: postposed aside only sets something to one side, while fronted aside from can mean either "except for" or "in addition to." Cross-class, the postpositive has an object in front of it (money aside); the ordinary adverb has none (step aside, set it aside), and there is also the theatrical noun an aside.

Brackets an objection or a topic and moves past it. It is the standard way to signal that you know about the thing you are not going to discuss.

  1. Money aside, the schedule alone kills the project.
  2. Joking aside, that was dangerous.
  3. All modesty aside, I fixed it in ten minutes.
  4. The lighting aside, the room is perfect.
  5. Legal questions aside, is this the right thing to do?

See also: aside from, apart, excepted, notwithstanding

aside from#

Pronunciation: /əˈsaɪd frʌm/ · weak /əˈsaɪd frəm/ · uh-SYD frum Type: complex, built on the postpositive aside · Register: neutral; the American counterpart to British-leaning apart from Complement: noun phrase or gerund — never a bare finite clause; use aside from the fact that if you must have one. Pattern: Aside from [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] aside from [noun phrase]. Contrast: The ambiguity is the point of the entry. Aside from has two opposite readings — subtractive ("except for") and additive ("in addition to") — and only the following clause tells you which: Aside from the salary, the job is great subtracts, while Aside from the salary, there are benefits adds. When it matters, write except for for the first and besides or in addition to for the second. Apart from carries exactly the same double reading and is the British preference.

The most useful and most treacherous member of this group, because both of its meanings are common and neither is marked.

1. Except for. Subtractive: it takes the named thing out of the claim. 2. In addition to. Additive: it puts the named thing alongside what follows.

  1. Aside from the ending, the movie is terrific.
  2. Aside from a sore shoulder, he came through fine.
  3. Aside from the tuition, there are books and fees.
  4. Aside from being late, she brought the wrong file.
  5. Nobody knew, aside from the two of us.
  6. Aside from Nashville, the tour hits four cities in Texas.

See also: aside, apart, excluding, apart from, except for, in addition to

back#

Pronunciation: /bæk/ · BAK Type: postpositive · Register: informal; spoken American English Complement: precedes it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (a few years, a while). Never a clause, and no object after it. Pattern: [measure of time] back Contrast: It is a casual ago, and it is vaguer — a few years back invites less precision than three years ago. Do not confuse it with the adverbial back in 1998, which comes before its object and is a different construction entirely. Cross-class, the postpositive needs a measure phrase in front of it (a while back), while the ordinary directional adverb needs nothing (come back, she called back), and back is also a common noun and a verb. In writing above the level of a blog post, use ago.

The conversational alternative to ago, restricted to loose measures of past time.

  1. A few years back, this was all farmland.
  2. I ran into him a while back.
  3. Two summers back, the river came up over the road.
  4. She mentioned it a couple of weeks back.
  5. Some time back, they stopped answering the phone.

excepted#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈsɛptɪd/ · casual /ɪkˈsɛpɾɪd/ · ik-SEP-tid Type: postpositive participial · Register: formal; one fixed phrase is conversational Complement: precedes it — a noun phrase. Never a clause, never anything after it. Pattern: [noun phrase] excepted, [main clause]. · [main clause], [noun phrase] excepted. Contrast: The postposed partner of prepositive excepting, and the negative counterpart of postposed included. Against excluded: excepted lifts a person or case out of a general statement, usually as a courtesy or a legal reservation, while excluded removes an item from a price, a set, or a policy. The fixed phrase present company excepted is the one use every American speaker knows.

Takes someone or something out from under a rule that has just been stated.

  1. Everyone in that office is useless, present company excepted.
  2. All fees are refundable, the application fee excepted.
  3. Sundays excepted, the desk is staffed from eight to six.
  4. The rule binds every member, the treasurer excepted.
  5. Two counties excepted, the state went the other way.

excluded#

Pronunciation: /ɪkˈskludɪd/ · casual /ɪkˈskluɾɪd/ · ik-SKLOO-did Type: postpositive participial · Register: neutral; commercial and technical Complement: precedes it — a noun phrase. Never a clause. Pattern: [noun phrase] excluded, [main clause]. · [main clause], [noun phrase] excluded. Contrast: The mirror of postposed included and the postposed partner of excluding. Against excepted: excluded is arithmetic — it takes an item out of a total or a coverage list — while excepted is a concession about people or cases. On a price tag, tax excluded and excluding tax say the same thing; tax excepted does not exist.

Removes an item from a price, a set, or a scope of coverage, after the fact.

  1. The rate is $220 a night, taxes excluded.
  2. All finishes are covered, custom colors excluded.
  3. Delivery is free nationwide, Alaska and Hawaii excluded.
  4. Every department gained headcount, contractors excluded.
  5. The warranty covers parts and labor, wear items excluded.

hence#

Pronunciation: /hɛns/ · HENS Type: postpositive · Register: formal and literary Complement: precedes it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (three days, a week, a fortnight). Never a clause, nothing after it. Pattern: [measure of time] hence Contrast: The forward-pointing twin of ago: three days ago looks back, three days hence looks forward. In ordinary American English the phrase is three days from now; hence in this sense is a deliberate literary choice. Keep it apart from the far more common conjunctive adverb hence meaning "therefore," which is the same spelling doing an entirely different job. The test is the measure phrase: the postpositive has one in front of it (two years hence), and the connective adverb has none and instead points back at a whole clause (The road was closed; hence the detour). A third, obsolete sense is spatial and means "from here," surviving only in get thee hence and in henceforth.

An archaic-flavored survivor. Almost every modern American use of temporal hence is either legal drafting or a bid for gravity.

1. Postpositive: a stated span from now. A measure of time comes first, and nothing follows. 2. Cross-class: the connective adverb. Meaning "therefore," with no measure phrase and no object — not a preposition at all.

  1. The lease expires two years hence.
  2. A century hence, none of these arguments will matter.
  3. The court will reconvene thirty days hence.
  4. What we decide today will be judged a generation hence.
  5. Six months hence, the program will be reviewed.
  6. The bridge was out; hence the four-hour detour.
  7. She had never been told; hence her surprise.

included#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈkludɪd/ · casual /ɪnˈkluɾɪd/ · in-KLOO-did Type: postpositive participial · Register: neutral Complement: precedes it — a noun phrase. Never a clause. Case matters: myself included and me included are both idiomatic; ✗ I included is not. Pattern: [noun phrase] included, [main clause]. · [main clause], [noun phrase] included. Contrast: The postposed partner of including, and it is often the more natural order when the object is a pronoun — everyone, myself included beats everyone, including myself. Its negatives are excluded for items and excepted for people.

Folds a member back into a set that has just been described, usually with a small note of surprise or self-implication.

  1. Everyone laughed, the judge included.
  2. We all missed it, myself included.
  3. The price is $1,800, shipping included.
  4. Every account was affected, the founders' included.
  5. Four states voted against, ours included.

inclusive#

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈklusɪv/ · in-KLOO-siv Type: postpositive (traditionally labeled an adjective, but it follows and governs the range in front of it) · Register: formal; contracts, statutes, and specifications Complement: precedes it — a stated range, normally two numbers or dates joined by to (1 to 30 inclusive). Never a clause, and nothing may follow it. When something does follow, the word is the prepositive complex preposition inclusive of, which is a different construction (a fee inclusive of tax). Pattern: [start] to [end] inclusive · [range] inclusive Contrast: This is the British repair for exactly the ambiguity American English fixes with through. Monday to Friday inclusive and Monday through Friday name the same five days, but the American form is shorter and is the default in the United States — so an American writes inclusive only in a contract or a specification where nothing may be left to inference. Its opposite postposes the same way: pages 40 to 62 exclusive leaves out page 62. Keep it apart from the ordinary attributive adjective, which stands in front of its noun and means something entirely different (an inclusive definition, an inclusive community); the postpositive has a range in front of it and the attributive has a noun behind it.

The belt-and-suspenders word. It exists because to does not say whether the last item is in or out, and sometimes that has to be settled on paper.

  1. Sections 4 to 9 inclusive are hereby repealed.
  2. The offer is open December 1 to December 24 inclusive.
  3. Answer questions 1 to 20 inclusive.
  4. Rates apply Monday to Friday inclusive.
  5. The survey covers the years 1990 to 2000 inclusive.
  6. Read chapters 3 to 7 inclusive before the seminar.

through#

Pronunciation: /θru/ · weak /θrə/ · THROO Type: simple preposition; in the inclusive-range sense a distinctly American connector; postpositive in fixed adverbials; also an adverb, a verb particle, and a predicate adjective · Register: neutral Complement: in the range sense, a second date, day, or number, with the first one preceding: Monday through Friday, pages 40 through 62. Never a clause. In the postposed adverbial, the noun phrase comes first: the whole night through. As an adverb and as a particle it takes no object at all — that absence is the test. Pattern: [start] through [end] · [noun phrase] through (postposed adverbial) · [verb] through (adverb or particle, no object) · be through with [noun phrase] Contrast: This is the most useful Americanism in the whole file. American Monday through Friday includes Friday. Monday to Friday is ambiguous and British-leaning, and until Friday or till Friday excludes it — I'm here until Friday means you leave on Friday, I'm here through Friday means you leave after it. British writers reach for Monday to Friday inclusive to get the same certainty an American gets from through. Cross-class, the test is the object: the preposition has one (we drove through the tunnel, open Monday through Friday), the adverb does not (we drove through, let me through), the particle binds to the verb and can be separated from it (read it through, see it through, think this through), and the predicate adjective means finished (Are you through?, I'm through with him). The path sense (through the tunnel) belongs with the movement prepositions. Variants: thru (informal American spelling; signage, menus, and drive-thru — never in edited prose)

Three things qualify it for this file: the inclusive endpoint, which is a genuine grammatical resource American English has and British English does not; the postposed adverbial, a frozen relic in which the object comes first; and the clean cross-class split between the preposition and the object-less adverb.

1. Inclusive range. From the first named point to the second, with the second one counted in. 2. Postposed: for the entire span. Fixed and literary — the whole night through. 3. Cross-class: adverb, particle, predicate adjective. No object anywhere.

  1. The office is open Monday through Friday.
  2. Read pages 40 through 62 for tomorrow.
  3. The exhibit runs June 3 through September 8.
  4. Applications are accepted March through May.
  5. The baby cried the whole night through.
  6. She stayed loyal to him his life through.
  7. The gate was jammed, so we squeezed through.
  8. Read the contract through before you sign it.
  9. Two hours in, I was through with the whole argument.

Look-alikes that are not prepositions#

Four groups get mistaken for members of this file, and each fails a test the entries above pass.

Words that take a clause and are therefore conjunctions. Assuming, provided, providing, seeing, and supposing look exactly like participial prepositions but require a clause and cannot take a bare noun phrase: assuming that the check clears, not ✗ assuming the check's clearance in the same sense. Considering, given, and granted are the reverse — they take both, which is why they appear above.

Absolute constructions. Weather permitting, God willing, all things being equal, and present circumstances allowing keep their own subject, and that subject is what makes them absolutes rather than prepositions. Remove the subject and the phrase collapses.

Reduced relative clauses. The man standing there, the report filed on Tuesday, and the quarter ending June 30 look participial because they are — they modify the noun in front of them and can be expanded with who is or which is. A participial preposition modifies the clause, not the noun, and no relative pronoun can be inserted.

Real danglers. Basing, walking, hoping, and every other live participle still needs its subject to be the subject of the main clause. ✗ Walking to work, the rain started is wrong because the rain was not walking. That test does not apply to considering, regarding, given, following, judging by, depending on, compared to, or based on, because those have stopped being verbs — which is the entire reason this file exists.
