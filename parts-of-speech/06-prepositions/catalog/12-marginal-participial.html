Marginal, participial, and postpositive prepositions#

The words that are prepositions only by the job they do — verbs, adjectives, and adverbs drafted into prepositional service, plus the handful that follow their object instead of preceding it.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Nothing in this file started life as a preposition. These are verbs (considering, given, pending), adjectives (absent, effective, worth), and adverbs (ago, aside, hence) that have been recruited into prepositional service — along with a few imports and abbreviations that arrived already recruited (alias, née, d/b/a) — and each keeps just enough of its old behavior to trip you up. Two tests separate the preposition from the participle it came from: a true preposition needs no subject and cannot dangle (Considering the weather, the turnout was good is fine, while Walking to work, the rain started is not), and its object is a plain noun phrase that a pronoun replaces in the objective case (including me, never including I). The postpositive group at the end breaks the one rule everyone thinks prepositions have — the object comes first, and the order is fixed rather than stylistic: three weeks ago, the objections notwithstanding, present company excepted. One trap runs through the whole file: almost none of these words accepts a finite clause (✗ regarding he was late, ✗ following she resigned), and the four that genuinely do — considering that, given that, granted that, and legal notwithstanding that — are exactly the ones careful writers avoid out of misplaced caution.

The complement rule that governs the whole class. A preposition takes a noun phrase, and when a verb has to fill that slot the verb becomes a gerund, never an infinitive. This is true here (worth seeing, ✗ worth to see; aside from being late, ✗ aside from to be late), and it is true of every other to in English that is a preposition rather than an infinitive marker: look forward to hearing (✗ look forward to hear), object to being told (✗ object to be told), accustomed to working nights (✗ accustomed to work nights), used to getting up early, opposed to raising rates, committed to hiring locally, devoted to restoring the house, in addition to paying the fine, prior to leaving, with a view to selling. The test is substitution — if a plain noun fits after the word (look forward to the weekend, accustomed to the noise, opposed to the plan), the to is a preposition and the verb after it must end in -ing. Two more restrictions travel with the class and are worth stating once: the concessives and causals take a noun phrase or a gerund and never a that-clause (despite the rain, despite being late, ✗ despite it rained, ✗ despite that it rained; in spite of the delay, in spite of arriving late, ✗ in spite of that it was delayed; owing to the strike, owing to being short-staffed, ✗ owing to that they struck; due to fog, due to fog closing the field, ✗ due to that it was foggy) — for a clause you must switch words entirely, to although, even though, or because. And a pronoun object is always objective (including me, compared to her, ✗ between you and I).

Reduced pronunciations. Two things weaken in this file, and neither shows in the spelling. First, every -ing ending drops to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (considerin', includin', dependin'). Second — the part learners never hear — the small word welded onto the end of a two-word member reduces to a schwa exactly as it does everywhere else in English: of /əv/ → /ə/, to → /tə/ (owing to /ˈoʊɪn tə/, compared to /kəmˈpɛrd tə/), for → /fɚ/ (allowing for /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/), at → /ət/, from → /frəm/ (aside from /əˈsaɪd frəm/), on → /ən/ (based on /beɪst ən/, depending on /dəˈpɛndɪn ən/), by → /bə/ (judging by /ˈdʒʌdʒɪn bə/), and with /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/, which is what you hear buried inside notwithstanding /ˌnɑtwəθˈstændɪn/. The strong form is what you say in isolation or under stress; the weak form is what you say in a sentence, and a learner who pronounces every one of these at full value sounds wrong in a way that is hard to place.

Near-neighbor rules that touch this file. Several confusable pairs cataloged elsewhere in this book decide sentences built on words from this file, so the rules are worth having in one place. By names a deadline for one completed act (file it by Friday), until names how long a state lasts (we're closed until Friday), and pending names the event instead of the date (closed pending inspection). At the front of a sentence American English wants except for, not bare except (Except for the ending, it's great), while after a noun either works (everyone except him). In names a location or a period, within an upper bound — in an hour can mean after an hour has passed, within an hour cannot. Between takes two parties, or several considered pairwise; among takes an undifferentiated mass. Beside means alongside, besides means in addition to. Like takes a noun phrase and as takes a clause (she works like a machine, she works as I do). Different from is the safe American form, different than is tolerated before a clause, and different to is British. In is static where into is directional, exactly as on is static where onto is directional. And toward is the American default against British towards — the same split that gives amid/amidst, among/amongst, forward/forwards, and American around against British round.

American forms in this file. Where the two standards split, this book gives the American form first and labels the other as British — never the reverse. In this file that means aside and aside from are the American choices and apart and apart from the British-leaning ones; Monday through Friday is the American inclusive range and has no British equivalent shorter than Monday to Friday inclusive; bar ("bar a miracle") is British and survives in American English only in bar none; and thru is an American spelling but a signage-only one. One pair is often mislabeled and is worth correcting here: long since is not British. It is ordinary formal American English, and it differs from long ago by tense rather than by nationality — long since pairs with the perfect (the mill has long since closed), long ago with the simple past (the mill closed long ago). The same American-first rule governs the pairs catalogued elsewhere: toward, amid, among, and forward are the American defaults, and towards, amidst, amongst, and forwards are the British forms; round is British where American English uses around (all year round is the one American holdout).

Participial prepositions in -ing#

The core of the group: present participles that lost their subject. Every one of them reduces its ending to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (considerin', includin'), so the written -ing is rarely what you hear. None of them agrees with anything, and none of them needs a subject. Only considering takes a that-clause freely; notwithstanding that is confined to legal drafting; every other word in this section rejects a finite clause outright, which is the single trap to watch. Most of them do take a gerund, and that is the repair when a clause tempts you: not ✗ following she resigned but following her resignation, not ✗ owing to that costs rose but owing to rising costs. The topic members (concerning, regarding, relating to, pertaining to, depending on, judging by) additionally accept a wh-nominal — regarding what you asked, depending on whether it rains — which is a clause in shape but a noun phrase in job, and is the one clause-like thing they allow.

A second family sits at the far edge of the section and is worth naming, because a reasonable reader will ask why it is here at all: the noun-postmodifiers. Some take a measure phrase (exceeding, totaling, numbering, weighing, measuring, spanning) and some have simply been bleached of the verb they came from (featuring, involving, surrounding, where nothing is featured, involved, or surrounded in any literal sense). They look like the reduced relative clauses cataloged at the end of this file, and they are not, because they fail that group's own test: a reduced relative expands with who is or which is (the report [which was] filed on Tuesday), and these will not — ✗ a career which is spanning four decades, ✗ a fine which is exceeding five hundred dollars. They answer only to a simple-present relative, they take a quantity or a topic rather than a true object, and none of them can be put in the progressive. That is the line between the two groups, and it is the only line that holds.

accruing to#

Pronunciation
/əˈkruɪŋ tu/ · weak /əˈkruɪn tə/ · uh-KROO-ing too
Русский
начисляемый в пользу — юр./фин. о правах и доходах, накапливающихся у названной стороны
Type
marginal/participial (two-word)
Register
formal; legal, financial, and actuarial. An American meets it in a trust instrument, a royalty statement, or a municipal bond prospectus.
Complement
a noun phrase naming the party a benefit lands on, or a wh-nominal (accruing to whoever holds the policy) — never a finite clause (✗ accruing to the estate owns the mineral rights). The to is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, so a verb after it must be a gerund: ✗ accruing to receive interest, and in practice the object is a person or an entity rather than an action at all.
Pattern
[benefit] accruing to [party] · the rights accruing to [party] under [instrument]
Contrast
Three words send a benefit to somebody and they are not the same. Accruing to says the benefit is building up over time, which is why it takes interest, royalties, and credits; inuring to says the benefit legally lands on a named party, which is why deeds use it; attributable to merely assigns a cause. Keep the preposition apart from the plain verb, which needs a subject (interest accrues at five percent).

Names the party a benefit, a right, or a liability is piling up for. Its home is the paragraph of a contract that says who ends up better off.

1. Rights and benefits building up to a holder. The legal use, usually with under naming the instrument. 2. Interest, dividends, and other money accumulating. The accounting use.

  1. All royalties accruing to the estate are paid quarterly.
  2. The tax credit accruing to first-time buyers expires in December.
  3. Interest accruing to the account is posted on the last business day.
  4. The report tallies the savings accruing to the city under the new contract.
  5. Any advantage accruing to the incumbent is small and getting smaller.
  6. Benefits accruing to whoever holds the policy pass to the heirs.

adjusting for#

Pronunciation
/əˈdʒʌstɪŋ fɔr/ · weak /əˈdʒʌstɪn fɚ/ · uh-JUS-ting fer
Русский
с поправкой на — пересчитав цифру так, чтобы искажающий фактор больше не влиял
Type
marginal/participial (two-word)
Register
neutral to formal; the standard word of economics, statistics, and data journalism
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (adjusting for having closed nine stores) — never a finite clause (✗ adjusting for prices rose → adjusting for the rise in prices). The for is welded on, and swapping it changes the word entirely: adjust to means get used to (adjusting to the altitude), which is not this.
Pattern
Adjusting for [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause], adjusting for [noun phrase].
Contrast
Three near neighbors do three different jobs to a number. Adjusting for X recomputes the figure so X no longer distorts it; controlling for holds X constant so some other effect can be seen; allowing for merely concedes X in an estimate. Discounting is different again — it throws the item out as not worth counting. The past-participial adjusted for modifies a noun and is the form on the chart label (wages, adjusted for inflation).

The honest-arithmetic word: it announces that a raw number has been reworked, and names the distortion that was taken out.

1. Recomputing a figure to remove a distorting factor. The quantitative use. 2. Making an allowance in a judgment. The looser use, close to allowing for.

  1. Adjusting for inflation, a ticket cost more in 1975 than it does now.
  2. Median pay is flat adjusting for overtime.
  3. Adjusting for population, the state ranks fourth in traffic deaths.
  4. The chart shows returns adjusting for fees and taxes.
  5. Adjusting for the extra week in the quarter, sales actually fell.
  6. Adjusting for having closed nine stores, revenue held steady.
See also
controlling for, allowing for, discounting, as measured by, indexed to

allowing for#

Pronunciation
/əˈlaʊɪŋ fɔr/ · weak /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/ · uh-LOW-ing fer
Русский
с учётом, делая скидку на — заранее закладывает известный фактор в оценку
Type
marginal/participial (two-word)
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ allowing for the traffic was heavy → allowing for traffic, or allowing for the fact that traffic was heavy). The for is part of the word: dropping it gives you the plain verb, and allowing the traffic means something else entirely.
Pattern
Allowing for [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] allowing for [noun phrase].
Contrast
Three words adjust a figure and they are not interchangeable. Allowing for X builds X into the estimate and keeps it in view; excluding X takes X out of the arithmetic; discounting X dismisses X as not worth counting. Barring is different again — it names a future spoiler rather than a known factor. Against given, which accepts a premise whole, allowing for concedes only a partial adjustment.

Makes room in a claim for a factor the speaker already knows about. It is the honest hedge of estimates, budgets, and forecasts.

1. Adjusting a figure for a known factor. The quantitative use. 2. Making allowance for a person's circumstances. The excusing use, close to considering.

  1. Allowing for inflation, the raise is closer to two percent.
  2. Allowing for traffic, the drive takes an hour and a half.
  3. Allowing for rounding, the two totals match.
  4. The schedule holds, allowing for one weather day.
  5. Even allowing for his inexperience, the mistake was serious.
  6. Allowing for being new to the system, she has picked it up fast.
See also
discounting, excluding, depending on, considering, given

arising out of#

Pronunciation
/əˈraɪzɪŋ aʊt ʌv/ · weak /əˈraɪzɪn aʊɾ ə/ · uh-RY-zing owt-uv
Русский
вытекающий из, возникший из — юр. связывает требование с породившим его событием
Type
marginal/participial (three-word)
Register
formal and legal; an American meets it in an insurance policy, an arbitration clause, and the first paragraph of a complaint
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (arising out of being denied coverage) — never a finite clause (✗ arising out of he crashed the truck → arising out of the collision). The whole three-word string is fixed: ✗ arising of, ✗ arising out from.
Pattern
[claims or disputes] arising out of [event or instrument] · arising out of or relating to [noun phrase] — the standard arbitration formula
Contrast
The three phrases that drafters chain together are ranked by breadth, and the ranking is litigated constantly. Arising out of needs a causal origin — the claim grew out of the named thing. Arising under needs the claim to be created by a document or a statute, which is narrower. Relating to needs only a connection, which is the widest of the three and the reason it is always added last. Plain arising from is the same word in ordinary prose; caused by is narrower than all of them.

The origin clause of American legal drafting. It ties a claim to the event that produced it without saying how tight the link has to be — which is exactly why courts spend so much time on it.

1. Growing out of a specified event. The insurance and tort use. 2. In the arbitration formula. Paired with relating to to cover everything.

  1. The policy excludes injuries arising out of the use of a motor vehicle.
  2. Any dispute arising out of this Agreement shall be settled by arbitration.
  3. She sued for damages arising out of the collapse of the walkway.
  4. Costs arising out of the recall exceeded a hundred million dollars.
  5. The board reviewed every complaint arising out of the merger.
  6. Claims arising out of being denied coverage go to the state commission.
See also
arising under, relating to, arising from, resulting from, in connection with

arising under#

Pronunciation
/əˈraɪzɪŋ ˈʌndɚ/ · weak /əˈraɪzɪn ʌndɚ/ · uh-RY-zing UN-der
Русский
возникающий на основании — юр. требование, порождённое названным законом или договором
Type
marginal/participial (two-word)
Register
formal and legal; a term of art. An American meets it in Article III of the Constitution, in the federal-question statute, and in the dispute clause of a contract.
Complement
a noun phrase naming an instrument — a constitution, a statute, a treaty, an agreement, a plan. Never a finite clause, and a gerund is impossible (✗ arising under signing the contract).
Pattern
[cases or claims] arising under [instrument]
Contrast
Against arising out of, the difference is the source of the claim rather than its subject matter: a claim arises under a contract when the contract itself creates it, and arises out of the contract when it merely grew out of the dealings. In federal practice that distinction decides the courthouse — a case arises under federal law when federal law creates the cause of action, which is what gives a district court federal-question jurisdiction. Pursuant to points at the authority for an act; arising under points at the source of a right.

The jurisdictional preposition. It says that the claim exists because some named instrument says it does.

  1. The judicial power extends to all cases arising under this Constitution.
  2. District courts have original jurisdiction of civil actions arising under federal law.
  3. Any claim arising under Section 4 must be filed within one year.
  4. Benefits arising under the plan are not assignable.
  5. Disputes arising under the treaty go to a joint commission.
  6. Rights arising under the earlier lease survived the sale.
See also
arising out of, accruing to, inuring to, pursuant to, hereunder

barring#

Pronunciation
/ˈbɑrɪŋ/ · casual /ˈbɑrɪn/ · BAR-ing (not BAIR-ing, which is bearing)
Русский
если не помешает, если не считать — называет единственное возможное препятствие
Type
marginal/participial
Register
neutral, faintly bookish
Complement
noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ barring that the weather holds). Pronoun objects take the objective case: barring him.
Pattern
Barring [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause] barring [noun phrase].
Contrast
Barring is hypothetical and forward-looking — it names the one thing that could still spoil a prediction. Excepting and excluding subtract something that actually exists. The bare form bar (bar a miracle) is chiefly British; American English keeps bar only in the fixed phrase bar none.
Variants
bar (British; in American English only in bar none)

Frozen from the verb bar, "to block." It sets one obstacle in front of an otherwise confident claim and is overwhelmingly a sentence-opener.

1. Unless this one thing intervenes. The default use, always about the future. 2. With the exception of. A weaker, static use, close to apart from.

  1. Barring a last-minute appeal, the sale closes Friday.
  2. Barring bad weather, we should be in Denver by six.
  3. The team makes the playoffs barring another injury at quarterback.
  4. Barring a few typos, the manuscript is clean.
  5. Barring the second chapter, I liked the book.
  6. She has the votes, barring something nobody has thought of yet.
  7. Barring being called in to cover a shift, I'm free all weekend.
See also
excepting, excluding, failing, pending, absent

A dead synonym of excepting and barring. Clipped from abating, from the verb abate, "to subtract." Do not confuse it with baiting (luring) or with bated in with bated breath, which is the same root in a different sense — "restrained."

Subtracts an item from a total or a claim. Any modern sentence that uses it is either quoting an old document or making a joke about one; except for is the living equivalent.

  1. Bating a few errors of arithmetic, the accounts were in order.
  2. The whole estate passed to the widow, bating the mill.
  3. Bating his temper, he was an excellent tenant.
  4. Every witness agreed, bating the clerk.
  5. She was, bating one bad season, the best pitcher in the league.

Русский

Type

Complement
noun phrase, gerund, or wh-nominal (concerning what happened) — never a finite that-clause. To take a clause you need concerning the fact that, which is worse than starting over.

Concerning, regarding, respecting, touching, and re all mean "about," but only the first two are still in ordinary use, and concerning is the heavier of those two — it belongs to correspondence and reports. The formal topic preposition: it names what a document, question, or complaint is about.

  1. I have a question concerning the deadline.
  2. The memo says nothing concerning severance.
  3. Concerning your invoice, we never received it.
  4. She wrote to the board concerning the audit.
  5. Rumors concerning the merger have been circulating for weeks.
  6. There is a statute concerning exactly this situation.

considering#

Pronunciation
/kənˈsɪdərɪŋ/, syncopated /kənˈsɪdrɪŋ/ · casual /kənˈsɪdrɪn/ · kun-SID-uh-ring
Русский
учитывая, принимая во внимание; с придаточным — «ввиду того что»; отдельно — «в общем-то»
Type
marginal/participial; also a subordinating conjunction with that, and an absolute adverb
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase, gerund, or a finite clause with or without that — considering (that) she had never flown before. It is the one word in this file that freely takes a clause in ordinary American English. It can also stand alone with no complement at all: She did well, considering.
Pattern
Considering [noun phrase], [main clause]. · Considering (that) [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause], considering.
Contrast
Against given: both accept a fact and reason from it, but given is clipped and treats the fact as a settled premise, while considering implies weighing and leans toward excusing what follows. Only considering can trail a sentence alone (✗ She did well, given). Do not confuse it with concerning — considering the evidence means in view of it, concerning the evidence means about it.

The workhorse of the group, and the only one that has kept a foot in three word classes at once.

1. In view of; taking into account. With a noun phrase. 2. Seeing that. With a full clause, functioning as a conjunction. 3. All things considered. Standing alone at the end of a sentence, as an adverb.

  1. Considering her age, she recovered remarkably fast.
  2. The price is fair considering the condition of the roof.
  3. Considering the hour, the crowd was enormous.
  4. Considering that he had two hours' notice, the speech was excellent.
  5. Considering you've never done this before, that's impressive.
  6. Considering having to learn the software from scratch, she was quick.
  7. The turnout was good, considering.
  8. It went well, considering — nobody walked out.
See also
given, granted, based on, concerning, notwithstanding

controlling for#

Pronunciation
/kənˈtroʊlɪŋ fɔr/ · weak /kənˈtroʊlɪn fɚ/ · kun-TROH-ling fer
Русский
с поправкой на, при контроле — стат. удерживая мешающий фактор постоянным
Type
marginal/participial (two-word)
Register
formal; the standard term in statistics and the social and medical sciences, and now common in newspaper explainers
Complement
an abstract noun phrase naming a variable (controlling for age), or a gerund naming a condition (controlling for having children) — never a finite clause (✗ controlling for the subjects were older → controlling for age). The for cannot be dropped or swapped: ✗ controlling age is the plain verb and means something else.
Pattern
[main clause], controlling for [variable]. · Controlling for [variable], [main clause].
Contrast
Adjusting for recomputes the number; controlling for holds a variable steady so a different effect can be isolated; allowing for is the everyday concession and claims no method at all. In a methods section controlling for is a promise that a model was fitted, and using it loosely in prose — where no model exists — is a quiet overclaim. Keep it apart from the attributive adjective in a controlling interest, which takes no object.

The word that says a confound has been taken out of the picture. It is the most-borrowed phrase in social-science writing, and the most often borrowed without the arithmetic behind it.

  1. Controlling for age, the difference disappears.
  2. The gap narrows but does not close, controlling for education and income.
  3. Controlling for prior convictions, sentences still varied by county.
  4. Even controlling for square footage, the north side is more expensive.
  5. The study found no effect controlling for hours slept.
  6. Controlling for having children, the wage gap is about six percent.

Русский
считая, с учётом; чаще в отрицании not counting — «не считая» — разг. при подсчёте
Type
marginal/participial
Register
informal to neutral; a spoken word
Complement
noun phrase or gerund (not counting waiting for a table) — never a finite clause (✗ not counting that the kids came). Almost always negated: not counting.
Pattern
counting [noun phrase] · not counting [noun phrase] · [main clause], not counting [noun phrase].
Contrast
Counting is the conversational including, and it is restricted to tallies — you can say counting the dog about a headcount, but not counting Chapter 4 about a table of contents. Negative not counting is the spoken excluding.

The arithmetic preposition of everyday speech. It adds or, more often, withholds an item from a running total.

1. Including in a total. Positive, usually about people or money. 2. Excluding from a total. The far more common negative, not counting.

  1. There are nine of us, counting the kids.
  2. Counting tips, she clears about forty thousand.
  3. It's a two-hour drive, not counting traffic.
  4. We've lost three games, not counting the exhibition.
  5. The room sleeps six, not counting the couch.
  6. Counting the garage, the place is nineteen hundred square feet.
  7. It's ninety minutes at that place, not counting waiting for a table.

/ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ/, /ˌnɑtwɪðˈstændɪŋ/ · casual /ˌnɑtwəθˈstændɪn/ · naht-with-STAN-ding

Type
marginal/participial; also postpositive; also a sentence adverb
Register
formal and legal
Complement
noun phrase or gerund; in legal drafting only, a finite that-clause (notwithstanding that the parties have agreed). In ordinary prose, keep it to a noun phrase.
Pattern

Contrast
It means despite, but with three properties despite lacks. First, it can follow its object — the objections notwithstanding — which no other concessive preposition in English can do. Second, it heads the override formula that makes one contract or statute clause defeat another. Despite and in spite of are the everyday words, and all three take a noun phrase or a gerund and never a that-clause outside legal drafting: ✗ despite he was tired, ✗ in spite of that it rained → despite being tired, in spite of the rain. Also ✗ notwithstanding of is an error, exactly like ✗ despite of.

A calque of Latin non obstante, "not standing in the way," and still the only common English preposition that is equally comfortable before or after its object.

1. Despite (before the object). The ordinary formal use. 2. Despite (after the object). The postpositive use, distinctly literary or legal. 3. The legal override. Notwithstanding any other provision — this clause wins over conflicting ones. 4. Cross-class: the bare adverb. No object at all, meaning "all the same." The preposition takes an object; the adverb does not.

  1. Notwithstanding the delay, the shipment arrived intact.
  2. She took the job notwithstanding the pay cut.
  3. The objections notwithstanding, the vote proceeded.
  4. His reputation notwithstanding, the jury acquitted him.
  5. Notwithstanding any other provision of this Agreement, Section 9 survives termination.
  6. Notwithstanding that the tenant has cured the default, the landlord retains the right of entry.
  7. Notwithstanding having signed the waiver, she sued anyway.
  8. Every engineer on the project objected; the launch went ahead notwithstanding.

Prepositions from past participles#

Forms that shed a subject and a be and became prepositions outright. They split on the complement, and the split is the thing to memorize: given and granted accept a that-clause, which puts them in a tiny minority, while every other member — based on, compared to, followed by, predicated on, and the rest — takes a noun phrase, a gerund, or a wh-nominal and rejects a finite clause outright (✗ based on he told me, ✗ compared to she was faster, ✗ followed by they left). In the two-word members the second word is welded on: you cannot drop the on or the to, and you cannot swap in another preposition.

Four smaller families live inside the section. The as-framed ones (as cited in, as determined by, as evidenced by, as measured by) attach a source, an authority, a proof, or a yardstick to a claim, and they are the working joints of legal and scientific prose. The destination ones (bound for, destined for, headed for) put a thing on a course, and differ only in how sure the arrival is. The benchmark ones (indexed to, pegged to, referenced to) tie a number to another number. And the sequence pair (followed by, preceded by) fixes an order — with the standing warning that whatever follows followed by comes second and whatever follows preceded by came first, which is the single most reversible error in the group.

The same meaning, with a full finite clause — one of only three words in this file that allows one in ordinary prose, alongside considering and granted; the fourth, legal notwithstanding that, is confined to drafting. 3. In mathematics and logic: assumed as a starting condition. 4. Cross-class: the adjective and the noun. A given moment, a given. No object, no clause — and no longer a preposition.

  5. Given that no one objected, the motion carried.

Marginal prepositions from other word classes#

Words that were never verbs at all, yet take a bare object exactly the way a preposition does. Four of them are catalogued in the files that own their meaning rather than here, because what they do matters more than where they came from: the adjective absent (absent a court order) with the exception prepositions, the fossilized subjunctive come (come Monday) and the adjective effective (effective July 1) with the time prepositions, and the Latin ablative re (re your email) with the topic prepositions. Postpositive and deferred prepositions#

The small group that follows its object. The order is fixed grammar, not style: three weeks ago can never become ago three weeks, and present company excepted can never be fronted. Because the object comes first, the safest way to think about these is as prepositions turned around — the noun phrase is still the complement, and it is still what the word is doing its work on. Most of them are also cross-class, and the test is always the same: the postpositive has a noun phrase in front of it, and the look-alike adverb has nothing at all (a while back against come back, money aside against step aside, two years hence against hence the detour). Two words covered elsewhere in this file belong to the group as well: notwithstanding, which is the only English preposition equally natural in both orders, and the postposed adverbials built on core prepositions, such as ten years on and the world over, which are frozen idioms rather than a productive pattern.

Second, ago is not interchangeable with for — ago locates an event, for measures a duration (she left three days ago, she was gone for three days). And long since is not a British substitute for long ago, as it is sometimes labeled: it is standard formal American English, and it differs by tense, taking the perfect that ago forbids (the shop has long since closed, ✗ the shop has closed long ago).

Descended from the past participle of Old English āgān, "to pass by," so it is a participial preposition too — one that finished its journey centuries earlier than the rest of this file and ended up on the wrong side of its object.

  1. Three weeks ago we had no idea.
  2. She called a few minutes ago.
  3. That happened long ago.
  4. The factory closed twenty years ago.
  5. I saw him not five minutes ago.
  6. A century ago, none of this existed.

Look-alikes that are not prepositions#

Nine groups get mistaken for members of this file, and each fails a test the entries above pass. The first four are ordinary English doing something other than prepositional work. The last five are flagged rather than entered: they are dialect, archaism, or a plain word wearing a costume, and a learner who meets one in the wild should be able to name what it is and then leave it alone.

Words that take a clause and are therefore conjunctions. Assuming, provided, providing, seeing, and supposing look exactly like participial prepositions but require a clause and cannot take a bare noun phrase: assuming that the check clears, not ✗ assuming the check's clearance in the same sense. Considering, given, and granted are the reverse — they take both, which is why they appear above.

Absolute constructions. Weather permitting, God willing, all things being equal, and present circumstances allowing keep their own subject, and that subject is what makes them absolutes rather than prepositions. Remove the subject and the phrase collapses.

Reduced relative clauses. The man standing there, the report filed on Tuesday, and the quarter ending June 30 look participial because they are — they modify the noun in front of them and can be expanded with who is or which is. A participial preposition modifies the clause, not the noun, and no relative pronoun can be inserted.

Real danglers. Basing, walking, hoping, and every other live participle still needs its subject to be the subject of the main clause. ✗ Walking to work, the rain started is wrong because the rain was not walking. That test does not apply to considering, regarding, given, following, judging by, depending on, compared to, or based on, because those have stopped being verbs — which is the entire reason this file exists.

Adverbs, because they take no object. Thence, whence, whither, alow, aloft, athwartships, betimes, and aback are often shelved with prepositions because they look like athwart and among and because they turn up in the same old sentences. They are adverbs, and the test is the one that runs through this whole file: nothing follows them. He sailed thence is complete; ✗ he sailed thence the harbor is not. Their registers differ — thence, whence, and whither are archaic or legal (whence means "from where," which is why ✗ from whence has been criticized for three centuries, though writers from the King James translators onward have used it anyway); aloft, alow, and athwartships are nautical, meaning up in the rigging, below, and across the ship; betimes is archaic for "early," and its modern use for "occasionally" is a misunderstanding of the word; and aback survives only in taken aback. Their prepositional cousins are real and are cataloged elsewhere — athwart takes an object, and aloft never does.

Archaic conjunctions, because they take a clause. Or ever ("before ever," as in or ever the silver cord be loosed), forasmuch as, insomuch as, against in the sense "by the time that" (have it ready against the guests arrive), and an meaning "if" (an it please you) all govern a finite clause, which makes them conjunctions no matter how prepositional they look. The register is Elizabethan to eighteenth-century, and an is Shakespearean; none is available for modern use. Note that against is a perfectly good preposition in its other senses, cataloged with place, movement, and opposition — it is only the clause-taking temporal sense that is a conjunction and only that sense that is dead.

Objectless with, because it has no object. Come with, can I go with?, do you want to ride with? — the construction is real, it is regional American, and it is not a preposition, because a preposition needs a complement and this has none. It belongs to the Upper Midwest and Chicago, where German and Scandinavian settlement left behind the separable-verb pattern of mitkommen and gå med; a Minnesotan says You want to come with? the way a German says Kommst du mit? By the file's own test the with here is an adverb or a particle. In writing meant for a national audience, finish it: come with me, come along.

For to, because it is an infinitive marker. I came for to see you is not for governing a noun; it is a two-part infinitive marker that was ordinary Middle English, is still alive in ballads (for to admire and for to see), in Appalachian and some Southern speech, and in Irish English, and is otherwise gone from standard American writing. The modern equivalent is a bare to-infinitive (I came to see you), and the one place for legitimately precedes an infinitive is the for-plus-subject frame, which is a different construction entirely: for him to see it.

On line, because it is ordinary on plus a noun. Waiting on line for waiting in line is a New York City regionalism, not a preposition of its own; both are the core prepositions cataloged in in, on, at, and the choice is a fact about American dialect geography rather than about grammar. Outside metropolitan New York the national form is in line, and on line in that sense reads as regional. Keep it separate from online, one word, which is about the internet and is now the standard spelling.
