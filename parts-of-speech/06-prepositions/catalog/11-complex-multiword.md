# Complex (multi-word) prepositions

> The fixed groups of two, three, and four words that behave as a single preposition — *in front of*, *according to*, *because of*, *with regard to* — and the rules that keep them frozen.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

A complex preposition is a phrase that has stopped being a phrase. *In front of* looks like preposition + noun + preposition, but you cannot say *in a front of*, *in the fronts of*, or *in front upon*: the internal parts have fused, and the whole thing takes a single object the way *near* or *before* does. That frozenness is the test that separates a real complex preposition from a literal string that merely looks like one — compare frozen *in the face of criticism* (meaning "despite") with literal *in the face of the statue* (meaning a face, on a statue).

**Three traps run through the entire class, and they account for nearly every error learners make with it.**

**First, none of these takes a *that*-clause.** A preposition governs a noun phrase, and a *that*-clause is not one: *in the event of* takes a noun phrase and *in the event that* takes a clause; *in case of* takes a noun phrase and *in case* takes a clause; *on the basis of* takes a noun phrase and *on the basis that* takes a clause; and *despite the fact that* and *in spite of the fact that* exist only because *despite* and *in spite of* cannot swallow a *that*-clause on their own. **But do not overcorrect.** A *free relative* — a nominal *wh*-clause like *what he said*, *whether it rains*, *how the money is spent* — is a **noun-phrase equivalent**, and a large share of these prepositions take one freely: *in light of what we learned*, *contrary to what you may have heard*, *regardless of whether you attend*, *because of who he knows*, *in line with what the committee recommended*. The rule to memorize is therefore narrower and sharper than "no clauses": **no *that*-clause, but a *wh*-clause is a noun phrase and is usually fine.** Each entry below states which of the two it accepts.

**Second, the last word is fixed and unforgiving** — it is *with regard to*, never *with regards to*; *in accordance with*, never *in accordance to*; *in exchange for*, never *in exchange of*; and *as regards*, which takes no *to* at all.

**Third, where the phrase ends in *to*, that *to* is a preposition and not an infinitive marker**, so what follows is a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a bare verb: *with a view to reducing costs* (not *to reduce*), *prior to signing* (not *to sign*), *accustomed to working nights* (not *to work*), *subject to being audited*, *opposed to raising taxes*. The same rule governs the prepositional verbs that sit just outside this class — *look forward to hearing from you*, *object to being questioned*, *get used to living alone* — and it is the single most common preposition error in learner English.

## The *in ___ of* family

The largest and most productive frame in the class: *in* + a bare noun (or *the* + noun) + *of*. The noun in the middle is what carries the meaning, and it is almost always article-less — *in case of*, *in light of*, *in search of*, *in advance of*, *in violation of* — which is itself the sign of a frozen unit, since English nouns do not normally appear bare in the singular. Where the article is present it is fixed too: *in the event of* keeps its *the*, *in view of* refuses one. Note the shared pronunciation fact: the closing *of* is unstressed in every member and reduces to /əv/ and then to a bare /ə/, so *in front of it* is *in FRUN-tuh-vit* and *in light of that* is *in LY-tuh-that*.

### in advance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ədˈvæns əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ədˈvæn sə/ &middot; *in ud-VANSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; scheduling, logistics, and news writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in advance of filing*). **Never a *that*-clause.** A nominal *wh*-clause is barely possible in sense 1 but is entirely normal in sense 2 (*well in advance of what anyone expected*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in advance of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In advance of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** *Before* plus foresight — it says not merely that one thing preceded another but that the earlier thing was done in preparation for the later one, which is why it collocates with deadlines, storms, votes, and deliveries. Against bare *before*, it is longer and adds that implication of deliberateness; against *prior to*, which adds nothing at all, it at least earns the extra words. Against *ahead of*, it is the more formal of the two and lacks *ahead of*'s competitive sense 3. **Bare *in advance* is an adverbial and takes no object**: *Pay in advance*, *Let me know in advance* — where the preposition always needs one.

**1. Earlier than, and in preparation for.** The county opened shelters *in advance of* the storm.
**2. Further developed than; ahead of (a standard or era).** The design was years *in advance of* anything else on the market.

1. The county opened four shelters *in advance of* the storm.
2. Ballots must be requested *in advance of* the October 20 deadline.
3. *In advance of* the vote, both sides released their whip counts.
4. She read the entire file *in advance of* meeting the family.
5. Payment is required *in advance of* delivery, not on receipt.
6. The design was years *in advance of* anything else on the market.
7. His thinking on the subject was well *in advance of* the profession's.

**See also:** [prior to](#prior-to), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [as of](#as-of), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [before](05-time.md#before)

### in anticipation of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃən əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ænˌtɪsəˈpeɪʃə nə/ &middot; *in an-tiss-uh-PAY-shun uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; news writing, business, and scheduling
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in anticipation of moving abroad*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the bare noun *anticipation* can take one (*the anticipation that prices would fall*), but the frozen preposition cannot, and *anticipating that* or plain *expecting* is how to write a clause. A free relative is fine (*in anticipation of what the exhibit would draw*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in anticipation of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In anticipation of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Against [*in advance of*](#in-advance-of), which treats the later event as settled fact, *in anticipation of* puts the reason inside the actor's head: **the expected thing may never arrive, and the sentence is still true.** That is the whole difference between *shelters opened in advance of the storm* (the storm came) and *shelters opened in anticipation of a storm* (they expected one). Against *ahead of*, it is the more explicitly motivated of the two. **Bare *in anticipation* is an adverbial and takes no object**: *She waited in anticipation* — where the preposition always needs one.

Names the expected event that explains a preparation. Because the motive is an expectation rather than a fact, the phrase survives intact even when the anticipated thing never happens.

1. The city stockpiled salt *in anticipation of* a hard winter.
2. *In anticipation of* the ruling, both firms had statements drafted.
3. Shares rose *in anticipation of* strong holiday sales.
4. She sold the second car *in anticipation of* moving abroad.
5. *In anticipation of* heavy turnout, the county added six machines.
6. The museum extended its hours *in anticipation of* what the exhibit would draw.

**See also:** [in advance of](#in-advance-of), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [prior to](#prior-to), [in the event of](#in-the-event-of), [with a view to](#with-a-view-to)

### in case of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkeɪs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈkeɪs ə/ &middot; *in KAYSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a strong pull toward signage and instructions
**Complement:** noun phrase only, and characteristically a bare article-less noun (*in case of fire*). **Never a *that*-clause and never a *wh*-clause** — for a clause you need the conjunction *in case* (*Take an umbrella in case it rains*) or *in the event that*.
**Pattern:** *In case of* [noun phrase], [imperative or clause]. &middot; [clause] *in case of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *In case of* + noun phrase names the emergency; *in case* + clause names a precaution. *In case of fire, use the stairs* tells you what to do when there is a fire; *Bring a flashlight in case the power goes out* tells you to prepare for one. Substituting one for the other is the single most common error with this phrase.

Names the situation that would trigger the instruction attached to it. It is the fixed formula of public safety notices and contract clauses, which is why it survives with a bare noun where ordinary English would demand an article.

1. *In case of* fire, use the stairs.
2. *In case of* emergency, break the glass.
3. *In case of* a tie, the chair casts the deciding vote.
4. The policy explains what happens *in case of* cancellation.
5. *In case of* rain, the ceremony moves indoors.
6. Passengers should remain seated *in case of* turbulence.

**See also:** [in the event of](#in-the-event-of), [in the face of](#in-the-face-of), [barring](12-marginal-participial.md#barring), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in)

### in charge of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈtʃɑrdʒ əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈtʃɑrdʒ ə/ &middot; *in CHARJ uhv* — the *of* reduces to a bare schwa, so *in charge of it* comes out *in CHARJ uh-vit*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*in charge of hiring*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in charge of what goes into the report*). **Never a *that*-clause.** Frequently stranded or used with no complement at all, in which case *of* drops: *Who's in charge here?*
**Pattern:** [person] *is in charge of* [noun phrase] &middot; the [person] *in charge of* [noun phrase] &middot; [person] *put someone in charge of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***In charge of* and *in the charge of* reverse the roles.** *The nurse in charge of the ward* runs the ward; *the ward in the charge of the nurse* is the thing being run. The single word *the* flips who has the authority, and this is a genuine trap in formal writing.

Assigns responsibility and authority over what follows. The noun *charge* here is the old sense "a duty entrusted to someone," now surviving almost nowhere else, which is what makes the phrase frozen: you cannot say *in a charge of* or *in full charge of* and keep the same meaning.

1. She is *in charge of* the entire western region.
2. Who was *in charge of* locking up last night?
3. They put a twenty-six-year-old *in charge of* a hundred-million-dollar budget.
4. The agent *in charge of* the investigation refused to comment.
5. He is *in charge of* nothing and complains about everything.
6. I want to speak to whoever is *in charge of* customer accounts.

**See also:** [on behalf of](#on-behalf-of), [in the name of](#in-the-name-of), [on top of](#on-top-of)

### in danger of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈdeɪndʒər əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈdeɪndʒə rə/ &middot; *in DAYN-jur uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or **gerund**, and the gerund is much the commoner (*in danger of losing the house*, *in danger of collapse*). Because *of* is a preposition, ***in danger of to lose the house* is wrong**, and so is ***in danger to fall***. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** *be in danger of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *in danger of* [verb-ing] &middot; *in real/serious/imminent danger of* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** The bad outcome named after *of* is one the **subject** would suffer or commit, which is what keeps *The bridge is in danger of collapsing* right and *The bridge is in danger of the flood* wrong — the flood is the threat, not the outcome. For the threat itself the phrase is *in danger from* (*in danger from rising water*). Against *at risk of*, which takes identical complements, this is the more vivid and less statistical of the two; against [*on the verge of*](#on-the-verge-of), which is neutral about whether the outcome is welcome, *in danger of* is always unwelcome. **Bare *in danger* takes no object**: *She knew she was in danger.*

States a bad outcome the subject may be about to suffer or cause. It is one of the few members of this family whose default complement is a gerund rather than a noun, and it takes a degree adverb inside it — *in real danger of*, *in serious danger of* — the way *in need of* does.

1. Two of the schools are *in danger of* losing accreditation.
2. The seawall is *in danger of* collapse within the decade.
3. He was *in danger of* missing the filing deadline entirely.
4. Nobody here is *in any danger of* being overpaid.
5. The species is *in danger of* disappearing from the state altogether.
6. She was *in real danger of* talking herself out of the job.

**See also:** [on the verge of](#on-the-verge-of), [close to](#close-to), [short of](#short-of), [subject to](#subject-to), [in need of](#in-need-of)

### in defiance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn dɪˈfaɪəns əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn dɪˈfaɪən sə/ &middot; *in dih-FYE-unss uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; news writing and legal reporting
**Complement:** noun phrase — an order, ban, law, or authority — or a gerund (*in defiance of being told twice*), or a free relative (*in defiance of what the court had ordered*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in defiance of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In defiance of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** The deliberate twin of [*in violation of*](#in-violation-of). *In violation of* reports that a rule was broken and says nothing about intent; *in defiance of* asserts that the breach was open and meant as a challenge, which is why it attaches to *orders*, *bans*, *sanctions*, and *warnings* rather than to section numbers. Against *in spite of* and *despite*, which merely concede an obstacle, this one requires an authority that was deliberately refused. It always carries a judgment — admiration or condemnation, depending on who is telling the story.

Reports that the action was taken in open refusal of an authority. Because the phrase asserts intent, it is the wrong word wherever the actor may simply not have known the rule.

1. The march went ahead *in defiance of* the mayor's order.
2. Three ships sailed *in defiance of* the blockade.
3. *In defiance of* the ban, the paper published the transcript in full.
4. She kept the clinic open *in defiance of* two cease-and-desist letters.
5. The council raised the levy *in defiance of* what the state auditor had recommended.
6. He stayed on the ballot *in defiance of* his own party's leadership.

**See also:** [in violation of](#in-violation-of), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in spite of](#in-spite-of), [regardless of](#regardless-of), [in defiance of](09-comparison-opposition.md#in-defiance-of)

### in excess of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ɪkˈsɛ sə/ &middot; *in ik-SESS uhv* — the noun here is /ɪkˈsɛs/, stressed on the second syllable, not the /ˈɛksɛs/ of *excess baggage*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; financial, legal, and technical
**Complement:** **a quantity expression only** — a number, a sum, a measure, or a noun naming a limit (*in excess of $2 million*, *in excess of the posted speed*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [quantity or clause] *in excess of* [quantity] &middot; *be in excess of* [quantity] &middot; *amounts in excess of* [figure]
**Contrast:** A four-word *more than*, and outside drafting *more than* is better: *damages in excess of $50,000* is *damages over $50,000*. It earns its length only where the threshold is legally operative, which is why it is the standard phrasing of jurisdictional minimums, coverage caps, and permit limits. Against *over*, it has the advantage of being unmistakable — *over $50,000* can be misread as spatial or temporal in a crowded sentence, and *in excess of* cannot. Against [*short of*](#short-of), it is the exact opposite: one passes the line, the other stops before it.

States that a figure passes a stated threshold. It is a threshold word rather than a comparison word: the object has to be a limit, not simply another quantity being ranked against it.

1. The plaintiff seeks damages *in excess of* $75,000.
2. Repairs are expected to run *in excess of* two million dollars.
3. Loads *in excess of* four tons require a permit.
4. The fund has held cash *in excess of* its stated target for six quarters.
5. Any hours *in excess of* forty are paid at time and a half.
6. Rainfall was *in excess of* the seasonal average for the third year running.

**See also:** [short of](#short-of), [up to](#up-to), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [in excess of](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#in-excess-of), [in the amount of](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#in-the-amount-of)

### in favor of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfeɪvər əv/ &middot; casual /ɪn ˈfeɪvərə/ &middot; *in FAY-vur uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*in favor of raising the cap*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in favor of whatever gets the building open*). **Never a *that*-clause** — for that you need *in favor of the proposition that*. The predicative pattern *be in favor of* is the commonest form.
**Pattern:** *be in favor of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [reject/drop X] *in favor of* [noun phrase] &middot; *rule/decide in favor of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in favour of* is the British spelling; American English uses *favor* without the *u*.
**Contrast:** Against *for*, this is the deliberative register — *I'm for it* in conversation, *I am in favor of the amendment* in a meeting. Against *instead of*, sense 2 adds a note of preference and judgment: you drop one thing *in favor of* a better one, but you simply do one thing *instead of* another. **Bare *in favor* is not this preposition** — with no complement it is an adverbial phrase meaning "supporting the motion" (*Three voted in favor, two against*), and the possessive *in his favor* is a different construction again (*The ruling went in his favor*).

**1. Supporting.** Nine members voted *in favor of* the measure.
**2. Choosing one thing over another.** The city dropped the streetcar plan *in favor of* dedicated bus lanes.
**3. To the legal or financial advantage of.** The court ruled *in favor of* the tenants.

1. Nine members voted *in favor of* the measure.
2. Are you *in favor of* moving the meeting to Thursday?
3. The city dropped the streetcar plan *in favor of* dedicated bus lanes.
4. She turned down the corporate offer *in favor of* a research fellowship.
5. The court ruled *in favor of* the tenants.
6. Write the check *in favor of* the trust, not the individual.
7. Public opinion has shifted sharply *in favor of* the proposal.

**See also:** [instead of](#instead-of), [in place of](#in-place-of), [in lieu of](#in-lieu-of), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### in front of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfrʌnt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈfrʌn(t)ə/ &middot; *in FRUNT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **noun phrase or objective-case pronoun** (*in front of me*, never *in front of I*). **No gerund** — *in front of leaving* is impossible, because the object has to be a thing or a person that occupies space. **Never a *that*-clause.** A free relative is fine when it names such a thing (*in front of what used to be the courthouse*), since a free relative is a noun phrase; a *wh*-clause that names a proposition is not (*in front of whether he agreed* is nonsense).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in front of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In front of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *right/directly/just in front of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***In front of* and *in the front of* are different prepositions.** *In front of the bus* is outside it, ahead of it; *in the front of the bus* is inside it, in the forward section. Against *before*, *in front of* is the ordinary spatial word — *before* in a spatial sense is formal or literary (*He knelt before the altar*). Against *opposite* and *across from*, *in front of* does not require the two things to face each other. **Bare *in front* is an adverb, not this preposition**, and takes no object: *the car in front* and *He sat in front* are complete, where *in front of* demands one — *the car in front of us*.

**1. Positioned ahead of something, facing it or blocking it.** A truck was parked *in front of* the hydrant.
**2. In the presence of an audience, especially an inhibiting one.** Don't say that *in front of* the children.

1. A truck was parked *in front of* the hydrant.
2. Meet me *in front of* the library at six.
3. She spilled the whole cup right *in front of* him.
4. Don't say that *in front of* the children.
5. He had to defend the budget *in front of* the entire board.
6. There were forty people *in front of* us in line.

**See also:** [ahead of](#ahead-of), [in the middle of](#in-the-middle-of), [on top of](#on-top-of), [next to](#next-to), [close to](#close-to)

### in lieu of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlu əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈlu ə/ &middot; *in LOO uhv* (British English often /ɪn ˈljuː əv/, *in LYOO uhv*)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, financial, and funeral-notice English
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in lieu of paying cash*); a nominal *wh*-clause is possible but formal (*in lieu of what was promised*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in lieu of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In lieu of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Semantically identical to *instead of* and *in place of*, but three registers higher. *In lieu of* belongs in wills, leases, and obituaries; using it in casual speech reads as stiff or as a mistake for *in view of*, which it is often confused with and which means something completely different ("considering").

Substitutes one thing for another that was expected or owed. *Lieu* is a fossil — a French word for "place" that survives in English in this phrase and essentially nowhere else — so the expression admits no modification at all.

1. *In lieu of* flowers, the family asks for donations to the food bank.
2. He accepted stock *in lieu of* a year-end bonus.
3. The company offered store credit *in lieu of* a refund.
4. Employees may take compensatory time *in lieu of* overtime pay.
5. The tenant paid two months' rent *in lieu of* notice.
6. *In lieu of* a formal hearing, the parties agreed to arbitration.

**See also:** [instead of](#instead-of), [in place of](#in-place-of), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [in view of](#in-view-of)

### in light of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪt əv/ &middot; flapped /ɪn ˈlaɪɾ əv/ &middot; casual /ɪn ˈlaɪɾ ə/ &middot; *in LYTE uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in light of what we learned yesterday*). **Never a *that*-clause** — write *in light of the fact that* if you need one.
**Pattern:** *In light of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in light of* [noun phrase].
**Variants:** *in the light of* is the dominant British form; American English overwhelmingly drops the article.
**Contrast:** Near-identical to *in view of* and *given*, but *in light of* implies new information that reframes what came before — you reconsider something *in light of* fresh evidence. *In view of* is neutral about novelty. Do not confuse with *in lieu of*, which means "instead of."

Introduces the consideration that explains or justifies the decision in the main clause. The metaphor is illumination: the object shines on the situation and changes how it looks.

1. *In light of* the new evidence, the charges were dropped.
2. The board reconsidered the layoffs *in light of* record third-quarter earnings.
3. *In light of* recent events, the reception has been postponed.
4. Her comments look very different *in light of* what we learned yesterday.
5. *In light of* his age, the judge suspended the sentence.
6. The guidance was rewritten *in light of* the audit findings.

**See also:** [in view of](#in-view-of), [in the face of](#in-the-face-of), [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given), [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering), [because of](#because-of)

### in need of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈnid əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈni də/ &middot; *in NEED uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, very often an abstract bare noun (*in need of repair*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, and never an infinitive** — *in need of to rest* is impossible, and even the gerund is awkward: *in need of rest*, not *in need of resting*.
**Pattern:** *be in need of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in need of* [noun phrase] &middot; *badly/urgently/sorely in need of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *In need of* is the adjectival, describing form; plain *need* is the verb. *The roof needs repair* and *the roof is in need of repair* mean the same thing, but the phrase is more formal and, unlike the verb, can modify a noun directly: *a roof in need of repair*.

States a deficiency as a property of the subject. This is one of the few complex prepositions that accepts a degree adverb inside it — *badly in need of*, *sorely in need of* — because *need* here retains enough of its nominal life to be measured.

1. The building is *in need of* serious structural work.
2. He arrived tired, hungry, and *in need of* a shower.
3. Families *in need of* assistance should call the county office.
4. The argument is sound but badly *in need of* editing.
5. She is *in need of* nothing so much as a week off.
6. This is a neighborhood *in need of* investment, not surveillance.

**See also:** [in search of](#in-search-of), [devoid of](#devoid-of), [short of](#short-of)

### in place of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈpleɪs əv/ &middot; casual /ɪn ˈpleɪsə/ &middot; *in PLAYSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in place of what was there before*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in place of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In place of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *instead of* in most contexts, but *in place of* keeps a stronger sense of physical or structural substitution — one thing occupying the slot the other vacated. *Instead of* is the everyday word and the only one of the three that comfortably takes a gerund in speech; *in lieu of* is the formal one. Do not confuse with the literal string *in the place of*, which means "in that location."

Marks a replacement filling a role. The substitute and the original are understood to be comparable — same job, different filler.

1. Use olive oil *in place of* butter.
2. The deputy chaired the meeting *in place of* the director.
3. *In place of* the annual review, managers will hold quarterly check-ins.
4. They planted native grasses *in place of* the lawn.
5. A photograph hung *in place of* the mirror that had broken.
6. He offered an apology *in place of* an explanation.

### in receipt of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈsit əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn rɪˈsi də/ &middot; *in ruh-SEET uhv* (the *p* is silent)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; commercial and bureaucratic correspondence
**Complement:** noun phrase; **never a gerund and never a *that*-clause**. A free relative is possible but stiff and rare (*in receipt of what the court had ordered*).
**Pattern:** *be in receipt of* [noun phrase] &middot; *upon being in receipt of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** A four-word way of saying *have received*, and almost always worse. *We are in receipt of your letter* says exactly what *We received your letter* says, at twice the length. Keep it only where a formal record is genuinely wanted — benefit determinations, acknowledgments of legal service.

Marks the subject as having taken delivery of something. It survives almost entirely in fixed openings of business letters and in social-services language (*households in receipt of benefits*).

1. We are *in receipt of* your letter of March 3.
2. Households *in receipt of* federal assistance are exempt from the fee.
3. The clerk confirmed that the office was *in receipt of* the filing.
4. Once *in receipt of* payment, we will release the shipment.
5. Any employee *in receipt of* such a notice should contact HR immediately.

### in respect of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈspɛkt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn rɪˈspɛk tə/ &middot; *in ruh-SPEKT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; British and legal. **Flagged: this is not the ordinary American form.**
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in respect of what was paid*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in respect of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *in respect to* is a chiefly American variant, itself less common than *with respect to*, which is the form American writers actually use. British and Commonwealth legal drafting prefers *in respect of*.
**Contrast:** *In respect of* means "concerning, with regard to" — it has nothing to do with admiration. American editors will usually change it to *with respect to*, *regarding*, or plain *about* or *for*. Note that *with respect to* is neutral and standard in American usage while *in respect of* reads as an importation.

Introduces the matter the statement is limited to. In British legal and accounting prose it is the default way to attach a topic to a claim; in American prose it is a marked, faintly foreign choice.

1. No claim may be brought *in respect of* losses arising before the policy date.
2. The company made provision *in respect of* deferred tax.
3. Payments *in respect of* prior years must be listed separately.
4. She was questioned *in respect of* three separate incidents.
5. Nothing is owed *in respect of* the canceled order.

### in search of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈsɜrtʃ əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈsɜrtʃ ə/ &middot; *in SURCH uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a literary tilt
**Complement:** noun phrase, and never a gerund, since what is sought must be a thing (*in search of work*, not *in search of working*). **Never a *that*-clause.** A free relative naming a thing is fine and is common (*in search of whatever work they could find*, *in search of what was left of the wreck*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in search of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In search of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** *In search of* states a purpose and implies motion or effort; *looking for* is the plain equivalent. Compare *in need of*, which states a lack without implying that anyone is doing anything about it.

Names what a journey or effort is aimed at obtaining. Almost always follows a verb of motion or of striving, and it is the standard headline formula for a quest.

1. They moved north *in search of* work.
2. He spent the afternoon *in search of* a parking space.
3. Whales travel thousands of miles *in search of* food.
4. *In search of* quiet, she rented a cabin for the winter.
5. Reporters descended on the town *in search of* a story.
6. The expedition set out *in search of* the wreck.
7. They spent two seasons *in search of* whatever the first party had left behind.

### in spite of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈspaɪt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈspaɪ də/ &middot; *in SPYTE uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in spite of being warned*). **Never a *that*-clause** — ***in spite of that he was warned* is impossible**, and for a clause you must write *in spite of the fact that* or switch to the conjunction *although*. A nominal *wh*-clause is marginal here and best avoided.
**Pattern:** *In spite of* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in spite of* [noun phrase / verb-ing].
**Contrast:** **Fully interchangeable with *despite*, which has exactly the same complement rule**: *despite* takes a noun phrase (*despite the forecast*) or a gerund (*despite being warned*) and **never a *that*-clause** — *despite that he was warned* is wrong, and *despite the fact that he was warned* is the workaround. *Despite* is one word shorter and marginally more formal; the only real difference is length and rhythm. Two errors to avoid: the blend ***despite of***, which is never correct in either direction, and ***in spite of that*-clause**. *In spite of* has no connection to the noun *spite* meaning malice — nobody is being spiteful.

Concedes a fact that would ordinarily have prevented what the main clause reports. The concession and the outcome are both asserted as true, which distinguishes this from a conditional.

1. *In spite of* the forecast, we went ahead with the picnic.
2. She finished the race *in spite of* a torn hamstring.
3. *In spite of* everything, the two of them stayed close.
4. The bridge held *in spite of* decades of deferred maintenance.
5. He signed the contract *in spite of* being warned by three lawyers.
6. *In spite of* its size, the truck handles like a sedan.

### in support of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn səˈpɔrt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn səˈpɔr də/ &middot; *in suh-PORT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*in support of raising the cap*), or a free relative (*in support of what the union had asked for*). **Never a *that*-clause** — for a clause the drafting form is *in support of the proposition that*, and ordinary prose uses *to support*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in support of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In support of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [evidence / testimony / a brief] *in support of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against [*in favor of*](#in-favor-of), which reports a position held, *in support of* reports an action taken to advance something: you *are in favor of* the bill, and you march, testify, or file a brief *in support of* it. Substituting one for the other turns an opinion into an act or an act into an opinion. Against plain *for*, it is the formal, active choice and is the fixed term of court filings (*a brief in support of the motion*). The literal engineering sense the phrase grew out of is still live — a beam *in support of* a floor.

Names the cause an action is meant to advance. The complement is what benefits; the main clause supplies the effort.

1. Thousands marched *in support of* the striking teachers.
2. She filed a brief *in support of* the plaintiffs.
3. *In support of* the application, the school submitted four years of test data.
4. He spoke *in support of* raising the cap on class size.
5. The company donated equipment *in support of* the cleanup.
6. Nothing in the record was offered *in support of* what the witness claimed.

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈtɜrmz əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈtɜrmz ə/ &middot; *in TURMZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral in sense 1; sense 2 is criticized filler
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in terms of what we should do*, *in terms of how it is funded*). **Never a *that*-clause**: *in terms of what we should do* is fine because *what we should do* is a noun-phrase equivalent, but *in terms of that we should act* is impossible.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in terms of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *In terms of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Sense 1 is precise and useful; sense 2 is a vague topic marker that usually replaces a sharper preposition. *Costs rose in terms of labor* almost always means *labor costs rose*. Where *in terms of* can be swapped for *about*, *for*, *as for*, or nothing at all, cut it.

**1. Measured in the units of; expressed by reference to.** The budget is written *in terms of* constant 2019 dollars.
**2. As regards (a vague topic marker).** *In terms of* staffing, we're in decent shape.

1. The budget is written *in terms of* constant 2019 dollars.
2. Thinking about distance *in terms of* travel time changes the map completely.
3. Explain the result *in terms of* the underlying chemistry.
4. *In terms of* staffing, we're in decent shape.
5. *In terms of* cost, the two proposals are indistinguishable.
6. She's improved a lot *in terms of* accuracy, less so *in terms of* speed.

### in the absence of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈæbsəns əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ði ˈæbsəns ə/ &middot; *in thee AB-sunss uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase only. **Never a *that*-clause and never a *wh*-clause; never a gerund.** The workaround for a clause is the conjunction *unless* or *if there is no*.
**Pattern:** *In the absence of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the absence of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *In the absence of* means "when there is no X" and is a near-synonym of *without* and *failing*. *Without* is the everyday word; *in the absence of* is the drafting word and is preferred where the absence is a formally relevant condition. Do not confuse the frozen phrase with the literal *in the absence of the chairman* meaning "while the chairman is away," which is also idiomatic and uses the same words.

Supplies the negative condition under which the main clause holds. It is the standard opening of default rules in statutes, contracts, and scientific reasoning.

1. *In the absence of* evidence, the committee declined to act.
2. *In the absence of* a written agreement, state law governs.
3. Plants grown *in the absence of* light lose their color within days.
4. The vice chair presides *in the absence of* the chair.
5. *In the absence of* any objection, the motion carries.

### in the case of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈkeɪs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈkeɪ sə/ &middot; *in thuh KAYSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative (*in the case of what the auditors flagged*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** *In the case of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the case of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** ***In the case of* and [*in case of*](#in-case-of) are different prepositions, and the article is the whole difference.** *In case of fire* names an emergency and attaches an instruction to it; *in the case of the Hartford plant* names one instance already under discussion and says something about it. The first is a contingency, the second an example — and *In the case of fire, use the stairs* is not idiomatic English. Against *as for* and *with regard to*, this one singles out one member of a set the reader already knows; against plain *for*, it is the longer form and is usually cuttable, since *in the case of older buildings* is normally just *for older buildings*.

Singles out one instance from a set and restricts the statement to it. It presupposes that the set has already been established, which is why it so often follows a general claim rather than opening one.

1. *In the case of* older buildings, the inspection is more thorough.
2. The rule is different *in the case of* part-time employees.
3. *In the case of* the Hartford plant, the layoffs began in March.
4. Refunds are automatic, except *in the case of* tickets bought through a reseller.
5. *In the case of* twins, the benefit is paid twice.
6. Nothing was done *in the case of* what the auditors flagged last spring.

### in the course of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈkɔrs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈkɔrs ə/ &middot; *in thuh KORSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in the course of reviewing the file*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the object has to name an activity, not a proposition, though a free relative that names one is fine (*in the course of what turned into a four-hour meeting*).
**Pattern:** *In the course of* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the course of* [noun phrase / verb-ing].
**Contrast:** A formal *during* that adds the idea of an extended process rather than a mere span of time. *During the meeting* is neutral; *in the course of the meeting* implies things unfolded. The employment-law fixed phrase is *in the course of employment*, where no substitution is possible.

Locates an event inside a longer activity, usually one that had its own momentum. Because *course* means a run or progression, the complement is nearly always an activity noun, not a point in time.

1. *In the course of* the investigation, three more accounts surfaced.
2. She mentioned it only once *in the course of* a two-hour interview.
3. Injuries sustained *in the course of* employment are covered.
4. *In the course of* reviewing the file, the auditor found the discrepancy.
5. Languages change enormously *in the course of* a few centuries.

### in the event of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ɪˈvɛnt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ði ɪˈvɛn də/ &middot; *in thee ih-VENT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; contracts, policies, and announcements
**Complement:** noun phrase only, and a gerund at a pinch (*in the event of the tenant's defaulting*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the clause-taking form is the conjunction *in the event that* (*in the event that the flight is canceled*), which is a different item.
**Pattern:** *In the event of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the event of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *In the event of* and *in case of* both take a bare noun and both name a triggering situation; *in case of* is the shorter, more public-facing one (signs and instructions), while *in the event of* is the contract-drafting one. Neither takes a clause. Against *if*, both are nominal: *if the flight is canceled* versus *in the event of cancellation*.

Names a contingency and attaches the rule that applies when it occurs. The pairing with *in the event that* is the cleanest illustration in the whole class of the noun-phrase-versus-clause split.

1. *In the event of* cancellation, tickets will be refunded in full.
2. *In the event of* a power failure, the generators start automatically.
3. The lease terminates *in the event of* the tenant's death.
4. *In the event of* a dispute, the matter goes to arbitration.
5. Coverage continues *in the event of* a temporary layoff.

### in the face of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈfeɪs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈfeɪs ə/ &middot; *in thuh FAYSS uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal, rhetorical
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically an abstract noun of difficulty (*criticism*, *adversity*, *opposition*); a gerund is possible but rare (*in the face of being outvoted*). **Never a *that*-clause.** A free relative is fine (*in the face of what the auditors found*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the face of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In the face of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Frozen *in the face of* means "confronted with, and despite"; the literal string *in the face of the cliff* means an actual face or surface, and the two are told apart only by whether the complement is abstract. Against *in spite of*, this one insists that the obstacle was live and pressing at the time, not merely a background fact.

Sets the main clause against active opposition or danger. It nearly always carries admiration or defiance, which is why it collocates so heavily with *courage*, *stood firm*, *pressed on*, and *held*.

1. She kept her composure *in the face of* relentless questioning.
2. *In the face of* mounting evidence, the company finally issued a recall.
3. The crew stayed calm *in the face of* real danger.
4. He refused to resign *in the face of* enormous pressure.
5. *In the face of* a shrinking budget, the library extended its hours anyway.

### in the form of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈfɔrm əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈfɔr mə/ &middot; *in thuh FORM uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a gerund naming an activity (*in the form of matching what employees give*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the form of* [noun phrase]. &middot; [abstract noun] *in the form of* [concrete noun]
**Contrast:** It specifies the shape a thing turned out to have, and **its object must be a kind of the thing already named**: *help arrived in the form of a loan* works because a loan is a kind of help, and the sentence collapses where that is not true (*help arrived in the form of Tuesday*). Against *as*, which does the same work in one word (*arrived as a loan*), the phrase is heavier but clearer when the two nouns are far apart in the sentence. Against [*such as*](#such-as), which offers one member of a class as an example, *in the form of* identifies the whole of the thing rather than a sample of it.

Names the concrete shape an abstract thing took. The pattern is almost always abstract noun first, concrete noun second, and reversing it produces nonsense.

1. Help arrived *in the form of* a state loan.
2. The bonus is paid *in the form of* stock, not cash.
3. Relief came *in the form of* three days of steady rain.
4. Opposition surfaced *in the form of* a petition with four thousand signatures.
5. The city offers support *in the form of* matching what residents raise themselves.
6. The evidence was circumstantial, mostly *in the form of* phone records.

### in the interest of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈɪntrəst əv/ &middot; also /ˈɪntərəst/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ði ˈɪntrə stə/ &middot; *in thee IN-trust uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in the interest of getting this finished*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** *In the interest of* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the interest of* [noun phrase].
**Variants:** The plural *in the interests of* is equally standard and is the commoner form in British English; American writers lean to the singular, and *in the interest of time* is fixed in the singular.
**Contrast:** Two readings that pull apart on the object. With an abstract value — *time*, *clarity*, *fairness*, *safety* — it names a goal being served, and it is very often a polite way of cutting something short: *in the interest of time, let's move on*. With a person or body it names a beneficiary, and there it competes with *on behalf of*, which claims an agency this phrase does not: a trustee acts *on behalf of* the estate and *in the interest of* the heirs. Against [*for the sake of*](#for-the-sake-of), the two are close, but *for the sake of* leans toward people and *in the interest of* toward procedural values.

Names the value or party the action is meant to serve. It presents the action as justified rather than merely explained, which is why it is standard in rulings, minutes, and public statements.

1. *In the interest of* time, we'll take two more questions.
2. The names were withheld *in the interest of* privacy.
3. *In the interest of* fairness, both sides got the same twenty minutes.
4. The board acted *in the interest of* the shareholders, not the founders.
5. *In the interest of* getting this finished before June, I've cut the second phase.
6. The records were sealed *in the interest of* protecting the children involved.

### in the midst of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈmɪdst əv/ &middot; casual /ɪn ðə ˈmɪs əv/ — the *d* routinely drops &middot; *in thuh MIDST uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral, with a literary tilt
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in the midst of moving*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the midst of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In the midst of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Against [*in the middle of*](#in-the-middle-of), which is the everyday word, *in the midst of* sits a register higher and takes a different kind of object: *the middle of* prefers a single bounded thing (*the middle of the room*, *the middle of dinner*), while *the midst of* prefers a plural, a mass, or an abstraction — *in the midst of strangers*, *in the midst of a recession*, *in the midst of all that noise*. It also lacks the interruption that *in the middle of dinner* carries: this phrase says "surrounded by," not "broken into." Against *amid*, it is the three-word equivalent and means the same thing. **Bare *midst* is not a preposition in modern English**; apart from this phrase it survives only in *in our midst*.

Places the subject inside something extended and surrounding — a crowd, a crisis, a stretch of turbulent time. The object is nearly always plural, mass, or abstract.

1. She stood *in the midst of* a crowd that would not move.
2. The company hired forty people *in the midst of* a hiring freeze everywhere else.
3. *In the midst of* the worst drought in decades, the district approved a golf course.
4. He resigned *in the midst of* an investigation he had asked for.
5. *In the midst of* all that noise, nobody heard the alarm.
6. They were *in the midst of* moving when the offer came through.

### in the name of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈneɪm əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈneɪm ə/ &middot; *in thuh NAYM uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; sense 2 is rhetorical
**Complement:** noun phrase in sense 1; **noun phrase or gerund in sense 2** (*in the name of protecting the public*, *in the name of keeping costs down*), which is where the phrase is most productive. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the name of* [noun phrase] &middot; *In the name of* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause/imperative].
**Contrast:** Against *on behalf of*, which means "as the agent of" and is neutral, *in the name of* carries authority or invocation — you act *on behalf of* a client but *in the name of* the law. Sense 2 is almost always critical: things done *in the name of* progress are things the speaker doubts were progress. Note that only sense 2 takes the gerund — *He signed in the name of the United States* (sense 1) cannot become *in the name of signing*.

**1. Using the authority of; invoking.** Open up, *in the name of* the law.
**2. Under the justification of (usually with an implication that the justification is false).** Whole neighborhoods were leveled *in the name of* urban renewal.

1. Open the door, *in the name of* the law.
2. The property is registered *in the name of* her late husband.
3. He signed the treaty *in the name of* the United States.
4. Whole neighborhoods were leveled *in the name of* urban renewal.
5. Terrible things have been done *in the name of* efficiency.
6. *In the name of* transparency, they released a document with half the pages blacked out.
7. The library cut its hours *in the name of* protecting the endowment.
8. *In the name of* keeping the peace, she said nothing for eleven years.

### in the process of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈprɑsɛs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈprɑsɛs ə/ &middot; *in thuh PRAH-sess uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **gerund above all** (*in the process of moving*), or a process noun (*in the process of renovation*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, and never an infinitive** — ***in the process of to move* is wrong**, and so is *in the process to move*.
**Pattern:** *be in the process of* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause], *and in the process of* [verb-ing], [clause]
**Contrast:** Nearly always a wordier progressive: *We are in the process of reviewing your application* says what *We are reviewing your application* says. It survives because it signals that the process is long and the speaker is not finished — which is why it is the natural language of customer service and of construction schedules. **Bare *in the process* is an adverbial, not this preposition**: *They rebuilt the engine and wrecked the transmission in the process* has no object and cannot take one.

Places the subject partway through an ongoing activity. It is the one member of the *in the ___ of* family whose default complement is a gerund rather than a noun.

1. We are *in the process of* reviewing your application.
2. The company is *in the process of* relocating its headquarters.
3. She was *in the process of* changing careers when the offer came.
4. The building is *in the process of* renovation and will reopen in the fall.
5. They are still *in the process of* deciding whether to appeal.

**See also:** [in the middle of](#in-the-middle-of), [in the course of](#in-the-course-of), [pending](12-marginal-participial.md#pending)

### in the wake of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈweɪk əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ðə ˈweɪ kə/ &middot; *in thuh WAYK uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; the standard idiom of news writing
**Complement:** noun phrase, almost always naming a disruptive event; a gerund is possible but uncommon (*in the wake of losing the contract*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the workaround is *after* + clause. A free relative naming the event is fine (*in the wake of what happened in March*).
**Pattern:** *In the wake of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in the wake of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** More than *after*: it asserts consequence, not just sequence. *After the hurricane* is chronology; *in the wake of the hurricane* says the hurricane caused what follows. The metaphor is a ship's wake, so the complement should be something big enough to leave one — *in the wake of lunch* is a joke.

Marks the aftermath of a major event as the cause or setting of the main clause. It combines the temporal work of *after* with the causal work of *because of*, which is exactly why journalists reach for it.

1. *In the wake of* the recall, sales fell by a third.
2. Three executives resigned *in the wake of* the audit.
3. *In the wake of* the storm, half the county was without power.
4. New disclosure rules were adopted *in the wake of* the scandal.
5. *In the wake of* her departure, the department reorganized twice.

**See also:** [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [because of](#because-of), [following](12-marginal-participial.md#following), [in the face of](#in-the-face-of)

### in view of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈvju əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˈvju ə/ &middot; *in VYOO uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal in sense 1; neutral in sense 2
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause in sense 1 (*in view of what happened last year*). **Never a *that*-clause** — use *in view of the fact that*. Sense 2, the literal spatial one, takes a noun phrase only.
**Pattern:** *In view of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in view of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *be in view of* [noun phrase] (literal)
**Contrast:** Sense 1 is a formal *because of* and a close synonym of *in light of* and *given*; sense 2 is the literal, spatial reading. Do not confuse with *in lieu of* ("instead of") — the two are frequently swapped by writers who half-remember the phrase, and the resulting sentence means the opposite of what was intended.

**1. Considering; because of.** *In view of* the delay, the fee has been waived.
**2. Within sight of (literal).** The cabin stands *in view of* the lake.

1. *In view of* the delay, the registration fee has been waived.
2. The sentence was reduced *in view of* his cooperation.
3. *In view of* what happened last year, the policy seems reasonable.
4. The cabin stands *in view of* the lake.
5. They camped *in view of* the summit and waited for the weather to clear.
6. *In view of* rising costs, the district postponed the project.

### in violation of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˌvaɪəˈleɪʃən əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɪn ˌvaɪəˈleɪʃə nə/ &middot; *in vye-uh-LAY-shun uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, regulatory, and news writing
**Complement:** noun phrase — a statute, rule, order, permit, or agreement — or a free relative standing in for one (*in violation of what the order plainly required*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in violation of* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in violation of* [noun phrase] &middot; *be in violation of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The negative counterpart of *in accordance with* and *pursuant to*: those report that the action followed the rule, this alleges that it broke one. **The object must be an instrument that could be named and cited** — which is why the phrase is so often followed by a section number — and that is what separates it from *contrary to*, which covers mere advice and expectations: you act *contrary to* advice, but *in violation of* a statute. Against plain *against*, it is the formal, citable form. Milder alternatives that stop short of alleging a breach are *inconsistent with* and *at variance with*.

Alleges that the action broke a specific rule that governed it. Because the claim is legally loaded, careful writers use it only where the instrument can be identified and the breach is being asserted, not merely suspected.

1. The fence was built *in violation of* the setback ordinance.
2. Three trades were made *in violation of* the firm's own policy.
3. The court held that the search was *in violation of* the Fourth Amendment.
4. Dumping *in violation of* the permit carries a fine for each day it continues.
5. She was dismissed *in violation of* the collective bargaining agreement.
6. *In violation of* the cease-fire, shelling resumed before dawn.

**See also:** [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [subject to](#subject-to)

## The *in ___ to*, *in ___ with*, and *in ___ for* family

The same *in* + noun frame, but closed by a different preposition. The choice of that final preposition is completely fixed and is where nearly all the errors happen: *in accordance with* (never *in accordance to*), *in association with* (never *in association to*), *in proportion to* (never *in proportion with*), *in contrast to* or *in contrast with* (both live), *in exchange for* (never *in exchange of*). Several of these also govern subject–verb agreement traps, because a phrase like *in addition to* attaches extra material without making the subject plural. And in every member closed by *to*, that *to* is prepositional: *in addition to running the shop*, never *in addition to run the shop*. The closing *with* reduces to /wəθ/ in ordinary speech throughout the group.

### in accordance with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈkɔrdəns wɪθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; weak /wəθ/, /wəð/ &middot; *in uh-KOR-dunss with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, regulatory, and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in accordance with what the permit requires*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in accordance with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In accordance with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** ***In accordance to* is always an error** — the noun *accordance* takes *with*. Against *according to*, which reports what a source says, *in accordance with* reports conformity to a rule: *According to the manual, the valve closes* (the manual says so) versus *The valve was installed in accordance with the manual* (the installation followed it).

States that the action conformed to a governing rule, standard, or instrument. It is the workhorse of compliance language and is almost never the right choice in ordinary prose, where *under*, *following*, or *as required by* will do.

1. The audit was conducted *in accordance with* generally accepted standards.
2. Ballots will be counted *in accordance with* state law.
3. *In accordance with* your instructions, we have canceled the order.
4. The device must be installed *in accordance with* the manufacturer's specifications.
5. Benefits are calculated *in accordance with* the schedule in Appendix B.

**See also:** [according to](#according-to), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of)

### in addition to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tə/ &middot; *in uh-DISH-un too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*in addition to running the shop*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in addition to what the grant covers*). **Never a *that*-clause** and — because this *to* is a preposition, not an infinitive marker — **never a bare infinitive**: ***in addition to run the shop* is wrong**, and *in addition to running the shop* is the only correct form.
**Pattern:** *In addition to* [noun phrase / verb-ing], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in addition to* [noun phrase / verb-ing].
**Contrast:** **Verb agreement trap.** *In addition to* does not create a compound subject: *The manager, in addition to two clerks, **was** present* — singular, because only *the manager* is the subject. Coordinating *and* would make it *were*. The same rule governs *as well as*, *along with*, and *together with*. Against *besides*, this is the fuller, more formal version; against *plus*, the more written one. **Bare *in addition* is a conjunctive adverb, not this preposition**, and takes no object: *The fee covers materials. In addition, there is a lab charge.*

Adds a second item to what the main clause already says. The added material is presented as supplementary, which is why it does not count toward the subject.

1. *In addition to* the tuition, students pay a lab fee.
2. She speaks Portuguese *in addition to* Spanish and French.
3. *In addition to* running the shop, he teaches two nights a week.
4. The senator, *in addition to* three staffers, was subpoenaed.
5. *In addition to* being cheaper, the new part is easier to install.
6. The package includes breakfast *in addition to* airport transfers.

**See also:** [as well as](#as-well-as), [along with](#along-with), [together with](#together-with), [apart from](#apart-from), [on top of](#on-top-of)

### in association with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˌsoʊsiˈeɪʃən wɪθ/ &middot; also /əˌsoʊʃiˈeɪʃən/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in uh-soh-see-AY-shun with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; institutional in sense 1, scientific in sense 2
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in association with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *in association with* [noun phrase] &middot; *presented in association with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Two lives, and the register tells them apart. In credit lines and event billing it is a politer, looser [*in conjunction with*](#in-conjunction-with): institutions co-present things *in association with* each other, where machines and medicines are used *in conjunction with* each other. In research writing it is a deliberately weak causal word — ***in association with* claims co-occurrence and nothing more**, which is exactly why journals prefer it to *caused by* or *because of*. A reader who treats sense 2 as a causal claim has misread it, and a writer who uses it hoping the reader will is being slippery.

**1. Jointly with; in partnership with.** The festival is presented *in association with* the city arts commission.
**2. Occurring together with; correlated with (technical, and expressly not causal).** Elevated readings were observed *in association with* long-term use.

1. The festival is presented *in association with* the city arts commission.
2. The report was produced *in association with* two university laboratories.
3. The exhibit travels *in association with* the national gallery.
4. *In association with* the merger, four executives took early retirement.
5. Elevated readings were observed *in association with* long-term use.
6. The rash appears *in association with* the second dose, not the first.
7. Fatigue was reported *in association with* several of the older drugs.

**See also:** [in conjunction with](#in-conjunction-with), [together with](#together-with), [along with](#along-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [by means of](#by-means-of)

### in comparison with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəmˈpɛrəsən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in kum-PAIR-uh-sun with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in comparison with what we paid last year*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in comparison with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In comparison with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** *in comparison to* is equally standard in American English; *with* is slightly preferred when the two things are being measured against each other, *to* when one is being likened to the other. Both are acceptable.
**Contrast:** A heavier *compared with* or *than*. Where a simple comparative will carry the sentence (*This one is cheaper*), the phrase is padding. Against *relative to*, which is neutral and quantitative, *in comparison with* is discursive. **Bare *in comparison* is an adverbial**, not this preposition: *Last year was mild. This winter, in comparison, has been brutal.*

Sets the main clause's subject beside a benchmark. Because it is bulky, it earns its place mainly when the benchmark is long or when the comparison needs to be fronted for emphasis.

1. *In comparison with* last year, enrollment is up nine percent.
2. The salary looks generous *in comparison with* the regional average.
3. Our losses were trivial *in comparison with* theirs.
4. *In comparison with* the original, the remake feels weightless.
5. The drug performed well *in comparison with* the placebo.

**See also:** [relative to](#relative-to), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to)

### in compliance with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəmˈplaɪəns wɪθ/ &middot; also /wɪð/ before a vowel &middot; weak /wəθ/, /wəð/ &middot; *in kum-PLY-unss with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; regulatory, legal, and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase — a rule, order, permit, standard, or agreement — or a free relative standing in for one (*in compliance with what the order required*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** *be in compliance with* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *in compliance with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In compliance with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** ***In compliance to* is an error** — the noun *compliance* takes *with*, exactly as *accordance* does. The negative is *out of compliance with*, the standard regulatory phrasing for a lapse that is being corrected rather than prosecuted.
**Contrast:** Three phrases worth learning as a set: *in compliance with* reports that a **duty was met**, [*in accordance with*](#in-accordance-with) reports that a **procedure was followed**, and [*in violation of*](#in-violation-of) alleges that a **rule was broken**. *In compliance with* presupposes an obligation the subject was under and usually implies that somebody checks, which is why it belongs to inspections, audits, and consent decrees rather than to ordinary description. Against *under*, which is shorter and better in ordinary prose, it earns its length only where compliance itself is the thing being asserted.

States that the subject has met an obligation imposed on it. The phrase is evaluative in a way *in accordance with* is not: somebody was required to do this, and did.

1. The facility is now *in compliance with* federal emissions limits.
2. All three buildings were brought *in compliance with* the fire code.
3. Records are retained *in compliance with* the state's retention schedule.
4. *In compliance with* the court's order, the agency released the remaining pages.
5. Contractors must certify that their subcontractors are *in compliance with* the wage rules.
6. The district reported that it was *in compliance with* what the settlement required.

**See also:** [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [in violation of](#in-violation-of), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [subject to](#subject-to)

### in conjunction with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˈdʒʌŋkʃən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in kun-JUNK-shun with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; institutional and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in conjunction with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In conjunction with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Means "jointly with" or "used together with," not merely "and." Against *along with* and *together with*, which just add an item, *in conjunction with* asserts that the two things operate together as a unit. Against [*in association with*](#in-association-with), it is the more mechanical of the two: things are used *in conjunction with* each other, institutions do things *in association with* each other.

Marks joint operation or joint sponsorship. The two things named are doing something together, which is why the phrase is standard on instructions (*use in conjunction with*) and on event billing (*presented in conjunction with*).

1. The exhibit was organized *in conjunction with* the state historical society.
2. This medication should not be taken *in conjunction with* alcohol.
3. *In conjunction with* the new sensors, the software can detect leaks earlier.
4. The training runs *in conjunction with* the annual conference.
5. Read this section *in conjunction with* the warranty terms on page 12.

**See also:** [in association with](#in-association-with), [together with](#together-with), [along with](#along-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [by means of](#by-means-of)

### in connection with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəˈnɛkʃən wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in kuh-NEK-shun with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, police, and corporate language
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*in connection with filing the claim*), or a free relative (*in connection with what happened on the tenth*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *in connection with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In connection with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** **A deliberate vagueness word, and the vagueness is the point.** *Arrested in connection with the robbery* reports that a person has been linked to it without saying how — which is exactly why police statements and news reports use it, and why an editor should leave it alone there and cut it everywhere else, where *about*, *over*, or *for* does the job in one word. Against [*in association with*](#in-association-with), which claims that two things co-occurred, *in connection with* claims only that something is relevant to a matter.

Links the statement to a matter without specifying the link. Its usefulness and its emptiness come from the same source: it names a relation and declines to say which one.

1. Two men were arrested *in connection with* the break-in.
2. She was questioned *in connection with* a series of unpaid invoices.
3. *In connection with* the merger, the company disclosed four side agreements.
4. Costs incurred *in connection with* the audit are reimbursable.
5. No charges have been filed *in connection with* what happened on the tenth.

**See also:** [in relation to](#in-relation-to), [in association with](#in-association-with), [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [in respect of](#in-respect-of), [in connection with](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-connection-with)

### in contrast to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑntræst tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *in KAHN-trast too* (noun stress on the first syllable; the verb *contrast* is /kənˈtræst/)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in contrast to what the ads suggest*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and because the *to* is a preposition, **never a bare infinitive**.
**Pattern:** *In contrast to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause], *in contrast to* [noun phrase].
**Variants:** *in contrast with* is equally standard. The bare adverbial *in contrast* (with no complement) is a conjunctive adverb, not a preposition: *Sales fell. In contrast, margins improved.*
**Contrast:** Against *unlike*, which is a single-word preposition doing the same job more economically, and against *as opposed to*, which adds a note of rejection or correction. Note that the complement must be grammatically parallel to what it is contrasted with, or you get a false comparison: *In contrast to the 1990s, today's market is volatile* compares a decade to a market.

Sets its object against the main clause as an opposite case. It signals the reader to expect difference, so the two halves must be genuinely comparable.

1. *In contrast to* the coasts, the interior stayed dry all summer.
2. Her second novel, *in contrast to* the first, is almost cheerful.
3. *In contrast to* earlier models, this one runs silently.
4. The northern district gained population, *in contrast to* every other part of the state.
5. *In contrast to* what the ads suggest, the plan has a deductible.

**See also:** [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [other than](#other-than)

### in exchange for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ɪksˈtʃeɪndʒ fɔr/ &middot; weak /fər/ &middot; *in iks-CHAYNJ for*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*in exchange for dropping the suit*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a nominal *wh*-clause is possible but rare (*in exchange for whatever they could salvage*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in exchange for* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In exchange for* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** ***In exchange of* is an error** — the noun *exchange* takes *for*, not *of*. Against *in return for*, which is nearly identical, *in exchange for* leans toward a transaction with two concrete sides, *in return for* toward a favor answered. Against plain *for*, both are explicit about reciprocity. **Bare *in exchange* is an adverbial** and takes no object: *He gave up the patent and got nothing in exchange.*

Names what is given back in a trade. Both halves of the exchange are stated, which is what distinguishes it from a simple *for* of price.

1. He agreed to testify *in exchange for* immunity.
2. *In exchange for* the land, the developer will build a park.
3. She tutors his kids *in exchange for* studio space.
4. The company got tax breaks *in exchange for* keeping the plant open.
5. They released the documents *in exchange for* dropping the lawsuit.

**See also:** [in return for](#in-return-for), [in lieu of](#in-lieu-of), [instead of](#instead-of), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### in keeping with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkipɪŋ wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in KEE-ping with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in keeping with what other districts have offered*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a gerund is possible but awkward.
**Pattern:** [clause], *in keeping with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In keeping with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Where *in accordance with* means conformity to a rule that binds, *in keeping with* means consistency with a spirit, tradition, or character — nobody is obliged. Compare *in accordance with the statute* (legally required) with *in keeping with the family's wishes* (fitting, not mandated). *In line with* sits between the two.

Says the action fits the tone or precedent of what it follows. It is evaluative rather than regulatory, which is why it collocates with *tradition*, *spirit*, *character*, and *wishes*.

1. *In keeping with* tradition, the outgoing chair gave the closing toast.
2. The renovation is *in keeping with* the character of the neighborhood.
3. *In keeping with* the family's wishes, the service was private.
4. His decision was entirely *in keeping with* everything he had said before.
5. The pay increase is *in keeping with* what other districts have offered.

**See also:** [in line with](#in-line-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [according to](#according-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to)

### in line with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪn wɪθ/ &middot; weak /wəθ/ &middot; *in LYNE with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; business and journalism
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in line with what the committee recommended*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a gerund is rare.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in line with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In line with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Weaker than *in accordance with* (no obligation) and more quantitative than *in keeping with* — earnings come in *in line with* expectations. Do not confuse the frozen phrase with the literal *in line with the doorframe*, meaning physically aligned, which uses the same words and is also current.

Reports that something matched an expectation, forecast, or policy. It is the standard verb-free way for analysts and reporters to say "as predicted."

1. Third-quarter results came in *in line with* analysts' forecasts.
2. The proposal is broadly *in line with* what the committee recommended.
3. *In line with* company policy, all expenses over $50 require a receipt.
4. Wage growth is now roughly *in line with* inflation.
5. The new rules bring the state *in line with* federal standards.

**See also:** [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [according to](#according-to), [relative to](#relative-to)

### in proportion to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən tə/ &middot; *in pruh-POR-shun too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; technical, financial, and statistical
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative (*in proportion to what each partner contributed*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in proportion to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In proportion to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *directly / inversely in proportion to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** ***In proportion with* is an error** — the noun *proportion* takes *to*. The negative counterpart is *out of proportion to* (*a fine out of all proportion to the offense*), which says the ratio is wrong rather than fixed.
**Contrast:** It asserts a **ratio**, which *relative to* and *compared with* do not: *wages fell relative to rents* says only that one moved against the other, while *wages rose in proportion to hours worked* says the two move together at a steady rate. Against sense 3 of [*according to*](#according-to) (*aid distributed according to need*), the two overlap, but *in proportion to* commits to a measurable ratio where *according to* commits only to a criterion. The pair to know from science writing is *directly in proportion to* and *inversely in proportion to*.

States that one quantity varies with another at a steady rate. Because it makes a quantitative claim, it should not be reached for loosely where *in line with* is meant.

1. Dues are set *in proportion to* each member's revenue.
2. Air resistance rises *in proportion to* the square of the speed.
3. The penalty should be *in proportion to* the harm actually done.
4. Seats are allocated *in proportion to* population, not land area.
5. Payouts are calculated *in proportion to* what each partner contributed.
6. Their share of the costs falls *in proportion to* the space they actually use.

**See also:** [relative to](#relative-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [in relation to](#in-relation-to), [according to](#according-to), [in proportion to](09-comparison-opposition.md#in-proportion-to)

### in relation to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈleɪʃən tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *in ruh-LAY-shun too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in relation to what was agreed in June*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in relation to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In relation to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Sense 1 overlaps *with regard to* and *concerning*; sense 2 overlaps *relative to* and *compared with*. The two readings can collide — *small in relation to the budget* is a comparison, *questions in relation to the budget* is a topic — and only the surrounding words disambiguate. Where sense 1 is meant, *about* or *regarding* is almost always tighter.

**1. Concerning; on the subject of.** He was questioned *in relation to* the break-in.
**2. In proportion to; compared with.** The fee is small *in relation to* the total cost.

1. He was questioned *in relation to* a burglary in the same block.
2. The report makes no findings *in relation to* the second complaint.
3. *In relation to* staffing, nothing has been decided.
4. The fee is trivial *in relation to* the total cost of the project.
5. Their salaries have fallen sharply *in relation to* the cost of living.
6. The moon's size *in relation to* Earth is unusual among planets and satellites.

**See also:** [relative to](#relative-to), [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [as to](#as-to), [regarding](12-marginal-participial.md#regarding)

### in response to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈspɑns tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *in ruh-SPAHNSS too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*in response to what the auditors found*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and because this *to* is a preposition, **never a bare infinitive** — *in response to receiving the notice*, not *in response to receive*.
**Pattern:** *In response to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *in response to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** Causal like *because of*, but it requires that the object be something addressed to the subject — a question, a request, a crisis that demanded an answer. *Because of the rain we canceled* is a bare cause; *in response to the rain* would be odd unless the rain had made a demand. **Bare *in response* is an adverbial** with no object: *They filed suit. In response, the city rewrote the ordinance.*

Marks the stimulus that the main clause answers. The relation is reactive rather than merely causal, which is why it is standard in official statements and in the methods sections of reports.

1. *In response to* the complaints, the airline added two more flights.
2. Prices rose sharply *in response to* the supply disruption.
3. She wrote a four-page memo *in response to* a two-line email.
4. *In response to* your inquiry, the position has been filled.
5. The agency issued new guidance *in response to* the court's ruling.

**See also:** [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [because of](#because-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [further to](#further-to)

### in return for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈtɜrn fɔr/ &middot; weak /fər/ &middot; *in ruh-TURN for*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause**; a nominal *wh*-clause is possible but uncommon (*in return for whatever help he could give*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *in return for* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *In return for* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *in exchange for* in most sentences; *in return for* is the better fit when the second party is repaying a favor or a service rather than trading goods. The bare adverbial *in return* (no complement) is not a preposition: *She helped him, and he said nothing in return.*

Names what is given back as compensation or reciprocation. It presupposes that the first party gave something first.

1. *In return for* her help, he fixed the transmission.
2. The union accepted a wage freeze *in return for* job guarantees.
3. She asks nothing *in return for* the work she does at the shelter.
4. *In return for* the endorsement, the campaign promised a speaking slot.
5. They got free housing *in return for* managing the property.

**See also:** [in exchange for](#in-exchange-for), [in lieu of](#in-lieu-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

## The *by ___ of* and *with ___ to* family

Two small frames that share a job: naming the instrument, authority, or topic of a statement. The *by ___ of* group is instrumental and comes down to us largely from legal Latin; the *with ___ to* group is topical and is the formal register's substitute for *about*. Both are rigidly frozen, and the *with* group carries the most common written error in this whole file: *with regards to*.

### by dint of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈdɪnt əv/ &middot; connected speech /baɪ ˈdɪn də/ &middot; *by DINT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, slightly old-fashioned
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*by dint of working nights*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *by dint of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *By dint of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Means "by means of, through the force of," and is almost always followed by a noun of effort — *hard work*, *sheer persistence*, *repetition*. Against *by virtue of*, which cites a qualification or status, *by dint of* cites exertion. *Dint* is a fossilized word for "a blow, force" and appears in no other living phrase.

Attributes an achievement to sustained effort. It is a small, self-consciously literary preposition, and in modern American prose it is close to a set piece.

1. *By dint of* sheer persistence, she finally got the records released.
2. He passed the bar *by dint of* six months of eighteen-hour days.
3. The team stayed in contention *by dint of* an absurd defensive record.
4. *By dint of* repetition, the phrase had lost all meaning.
5. She rose to partner *by dint of* out-working everyone around her.

**See also:** [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [by means of](#by-means-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [through](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#through), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### by means of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈminz əv/ &middot; connected speech /baɪ ˈminz ə/ &middot; *by MEENZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; technical and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *by means of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *By means of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** An explicit *by* of instrument. Where plain *by* would be ambiguous between agent and instrument (*The lock was opened by the technician* versus *by a magnet*), *by means of* forces the instrument reading. In ordinary prose, *by*, *with*, or *using* is shorter and just as clear.

Names the instrument or method that made the action possible. The plural *means* is invariable here; there is no *by mean of*.

1. The sample is heated *by means of* an induction coil.
2. Access is controlled *by means of* a keycard system.
3. They crossed the ravine *by means of* a rope bridge.
4. The message was transmitted *by means of* a simple substitution cipher.
5. *By means of* a series of small loans, the cooperative rebuilt the mill.

**See also:** [by way of](#by-way-of), [by dint of](#by-dint-of), [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### by reason of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈrizən əv/ &middot; connected speech /baɪ ˈrizə nə/ &middot; *by REE-zun uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal; largely confined to statutes, pleadings, and verdicts
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically an abstract one naming a condition or a failure (*by reason of insanity*, *by reason of age*, *by reason of nonpayment*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.** A gerund is possible in drafting (*by reason of having failed to appear*) but the nominal is preferred.
**Pattern:** [clause] *by reason of* [noun phrase] &middot; *not guilty by reason of* [noun phrase] (fixed) &middot; *By reason of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** A legal *because of*, and outside legal writing that is what to use. Against [*by virtue of*](#by-virtue-of), which grounds an entitlement in a status the subject **holds**, *by reason of* names a cause of liability, exemption, or incapacity — which is why the two split so cleanly in practice: you chair the board *by virtue of* your office and are excused *by reason of* illness. The verdict formula *not guilty by reason of insanity* is fixed and admits no variation; *by reason whereof* is its archaic drafting relative.

Attributes a legal consequence to the fact that caused it. Its survival is purely professional: it says nothing *because of* does not say, and it says it two words longer.

1. He was found not guilty *by reason of* insanity.
2. The lease may be terminated *by reason of* nonpayment.
3. No employee may be dismissed *by reason of* jury service.
4. *By reason of* their age, two of the defendants were tried separately.
5. The claim failed *by reason of* the plaintiff's own delay.

**See also:** [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [because of](#because-of), [on the grounds of](#on-the-grounds-of), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [by reason of](06-cause-purpose.md#by-reason-of)

### by virtue of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu əv/ &middot; connected speech /baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu ə/ &middot; *by VUR-choo uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and academic
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*by virtue of being a resident*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a nominal *wh*-clause is possible but rare (*by virtue of what the charter grants him*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *by virtue of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *By virtue of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Cites a status, office, or inherent property as the reason something holds — *by virtue of her office*, *by virtue of the contract*. Against *because of*, it is narrower: the cause must be something the subject possesses or occupies. Against *by dint of*, which credits effort, this credits standing. *Virtue* here means "power, efficacy," not moral goodness.

Grounds an entitlement or a consequence in a qualifying fact. It is the standard formula for automatic, definitional consequences in legal writing.

1. *By virtue of* her office, the mayor chairs the redevelopment board.
2. Children born here are citizens *by virtue of* the Fourteenth Amendment.
3. He inherited the title *by virtue of* being the eldest son.
4. The clause is void *by virtue of* the statute of frauds.
5. *By virtue of* its size alone, the company sets the market price.

**See also:** [by dint of](#by-dint-of), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [on the basis of](#on-the-basis-of), [in the name of](#in-the-name-of)

### by way of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈweɪ əv/ &middot; casual /baɪ ˈweɪə/ &middot; *by WAY uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *by way of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Three distinct senses, and they are told apart only by the complement: a place name gives sense 1, an abstract noun of function gives sense 2, an instrument gives sense 3. Against *via*, sense 1 is the plain-English equivalent. Sense 2 has no one-word substitute — *by way of apology* means "as an apology," and *as* alone would not carry the tentativeness.

**1. Traveling through; via.** We flew to Lisbon *by way of* Newark.
**2. As a form of; serving as.** He sent flowers *by way of* apology.
**3. By means of.** The data reaches the server *by way of* an encrypted tunnel.

1. We flew to Lisbon *by way of* Newark.
2. The pipeline reaches the coast *by way of* three pumping stations.
3. He sent flowers *by way of* apology.
4. She offered a shrug *by way of* explanation.
5. *By way of* introduction, let me say how the committee was formed.
6. The data reaches the server *by way of* an encrypted tunnel.

**See also:** [by means of](#by-means-of), [through](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#through), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to)

### with a view to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ə ˈvju tu/ &middot; weak /wəð ə ˈvju tə/ &middot; *with uh VYOO too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, diplomatic, and business
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund only.** The *to* here is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, so ***with a view to increase costs* is wrong** — it must be *with a view to increasing costs*. This is the single most common error with the phrase. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *with a view to* [verb-ing / noun phrase]
**Variants:** *with a view toward* and *with an eye to* are American alternatives; *with a view to* is the standard drafting form. British writers also use *with a view towards*.
**Contrast:** Means "with the aim of." Against *in order to*, which takes a bare infinitive (*in order to reduce costs*), *with a view to* takes a gerund (*with a view to reducing costs*). Mixing the two produces the classic error.

States the purpose behind an action that is only a first step toward it. It implies the goal is intended but not yet achieved, which is why it is so common in negotiation and planning language.

1. The parties met *with a view to* settling the dispute.
2. The land was purchased *with a view to* future expansion.
3. They reorganized the department *with a view to* cutting overhead.
4. The bill was drafted *with a view to* surviving a court challenge.
5. She took the position *with a view to* eventually running the program.

**See also:** [for the sake of](#for-the-sake-of), [in search of](#in-search-of), [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [to](02-core-relational.md#to)

### with reference to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθ ˈrɛfrəns tu/ &middot; weak /wəθ ˈrɛfrəns tə/ &middot; *with REF-runss too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; correspondence and technical writing
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*with reference to what you raised on Tuesday*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**.
**Pattern:** *With reference to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *with reference to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The letter-opening member of the *with ___ to* group: sense 1 points back at a specific document or previous communication, where *with regard to* and *with respect to* point at a topic generally. Sense 2 is the technical one and is the reason the phrase survives at all — *measured with reference to sea level* means "using it as the baseline," which *with regard to* cannot mean. Note also ***with references to* is an error** in the topic sense, exactly parallel to *with regards to*.

**1. Concerning; further to (a document or previous message).** *With reference to* your letter of April 2, the shipment left on Monday.
**2. Measured or defined by taking something as the baseline (technical).** All elevations are given *with reference to* mean sea level.

1. *With reference to* your letter of April 2, the shipment left on Monday.
2. *With reference to* the matter we discussed, I have spoken to counsel.
3. *With reference to* what you raised on Tuesday, the committee has no position yet.
4. All elevations are given *with reference to* mean sea level.
5. The clause must be read *with reference to* the definitions in Section 1.
6. Velocity is meaningless except *with reference to* some frame.
7. The tolerances are specified *with reference to* the finished surface, not the blank.

**See also:** [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [further to](#further-to), [relative to](#relative-to), [regarding](12-marginal-participial.md#regarding)

### with regard to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθ rɪˈɡɑrd tu/ &middot; weak /wəθ rɪˈɡɑrd tə/ &middot; *with ruh-GARD too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*with regard to what the statute requires*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**: *with regard to hiring*, not *with regard to hire*.
**Pattern:** *With regard to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *with regard to* [noun phrase].
**Variants:** *in regard to* and *as regards* are equally standard; *regarding* is the one-word form.
**Contrast:** ***With regards to* is an error.** *Regards* with an *s* means greetings (*give my regards to your mother*), and it belongs at the bottom of a letter, not in the middle of a sentence. The singular *regard* is the noun meaning "attention, consideration." The same rule kills *in regards to* and *as regards to*. Note also that *as regards* takes no *to* at all.

Introduces the topic the statement is about. It is the formal register's *about*, and it is nearly always compressible — *with regard to the budget* is usually just *on the budget* or *about the budget*.

1. *With regard to* the budget, no decision has been made.
2. The report says nothing *with regard to* enforcement.
3. *With regard to* your second question, the deadline has not changed.
4. Policies differ sharply *with regard to* part-time employees.
5. She has been careless *with regard to* deadlines all semester.

**See also:** [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [as regards](#as-regards), [in relation to](#in-relation-to), [as to](#as-to), [regarding](12-marginal-participial.md#regarding)

### with respect to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪθ rɪˈspɛkt tu/ &middot; weak /wəθ rɪˈspɛkt tə/ &middot; *with ruh-SPEKT too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; the standard American form in legal, technical, and academic prose
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*with respect to whether the filing was timely*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**: *with respect to filing*, not *with respect to file*.
**Pattern:** *With respect to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *with respect to* [noun phrase].
**Variants:** Abbreviated *w.r.t.* in mathematics and engineering. *In respect of* is the British counterpart; *in respect to* is a less common American variant.
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *with regard to* as a topic marker, but *with respect to* also has a technical sense that *with regard to* lacks: *differentiate with respect to x*, *symmetric with respect to the origin* — "taking x as the variable or reference." Note that the phrase has nothing to do with deference; *with all due respect* is a different expression entirely. And as with *with regard to*, ***with respects to* is an error** — the plural belongs only in *pay one's respects*.

**1. On the subject of; as far as X is concerned (topic marker).** *With respect to* the second charge, the jury could not agree.
**2. Taking X as the variable, baseline, or axis (technical).** Differentiate the function *with respect to* time.

1. *With respect to* the second charge, the jury could not agree.
2. The statute is silent *with respect to* electronic filings.
3. *With respect to* scheduling, please copy the front office on everything.
4. The parties remain apart *with respect to* whether the notice was timely.
5. Differentiate the function *with respect to* time.
6. The two designs are identical *with respect to* power draw.
7. The curve is symmetric *with respect to* the origin.

**See also:** [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [in respect of](#in-respect-of), [relative to](#relative-to), [as to](#as-to), [in terms of](#in-terms-of)

### with the exception of

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ði ɪkˈsɛpʃən əv/ &middot; weak /wəð ði ɪkˈsɛpʃən ə/ &middot; *with thee ik-SEP-shun uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a gerund at a pinch (*with the exception of having to repaint*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause** — the clause-taking form is the conjunction *except that*.
**Pattern:** *With the exception of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *with the exception of* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The heaviest member of the exception family. *Except for*, *apart from*, *aside from*, and bare *except* all do the same work in fewer words. It is worth the length only when the exception needs to be foregrounded, or in drafting where *except* alone might be read as attaching to the wrong element.

Subtracts a named item from an otherwise universal claim. The claim must be a genuine generalization; subtracting from a specific statement produces nonsense.

1. *With the exception of* Tuesday, the whole week is booked.
2. Every state, *with the exception of* Nebraska, has a bicameral legislature.
3. The building was undamaged, *with the exception of* two broken windows.
4. All fees are refundable *with the exception of* the application charge.
5. *With the exception of* one abstention, the vote was unanimous.

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [save for](#save-for), [other than](#other-than), [excepting](12-marginal-participial.md#excepting)

### with the help of

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ðə ˈhɛlp əv/ &middot; weak /wəð ðə ˈhɛl pə/ &middot; *with thuh HELP uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a person, body, or instrument; a gerund is possible but uncommon. **Never a *that*-clause.** A free relative is fine (*with the help of whoever happened to be around*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *with the help of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *With the help of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** *with the aid of* is the formal twin and is preferred for instruments (*with the aid of a magnifying glass*); *with the assistance of* is the most formal of the three; *with help from* is the looser conversational version. **The *of* is not optional, and *with the help from* is an error.**
**Contrast:** Against [*by means of*](#by-means-of), which names a pure instrument, *with the help of* usually names a collaborator and always implies that the subject did the work too — the helper assisted, and did not do it alone. That is also the difference from [*thanks to*](#thanks-to), which hands the credit over entirely: *We finished thanks to Maria* makes her contribution decisive, *We finished with the help of Maria* makes it partial.

Names a contributor to an effort the subject remains responsible for. The main clause keeps the agency; the phrase only shares it.

1. *With the help of* two neighbors, they had the roof patched by dark.
2. She finished the application *with the help of* a legal aid clinic.
3. The map was reconstructed *with the help of* nineteenth-century tax records.
4. *With the help of* a state grant, the library stayed open on Sundays.
5. He got through the first year *with the help of* whoever would tutor him for free.

**See also:** [by means of](#by-means-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [courtesy of](#courtesy-of), [together with](#together-with), [with the aid of](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#with-the-aid-of)

## The *___ of* and *___ from* family

Units that close in *of* or *from* and are built on something other than *in*: usually a bare adverb, adjective, or noun, and in a few cases a small *at ___ of*, *on ___ of*, or *for ___ of* frame of their own. This is the group where the frozen/literal distinction bites hardest, because the first word usually leads an independent life as an adverb: *ahead* and *out* and *inside* and *apart* all stand alone without an object, and adding *of* or *from* is precisely what turns them into prepositions. **The test is the object**: *He walked out* is an adverb; *He walked out of the room* is a preposition with a complement. Two members of the group carry live usage disputes — *inside of* and *outside of*, where the *of* is optional and often edited out, and *regardless of*, whose deformation *irregardless* is the most notorious nonstandard word in American English.

### ahead of

**Pronunciation:** /əˈhɛd əv/ &middot; connected speech /əˈhɛ də/ &middot; *uh-HED uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.** Sense 2 also takes a gerund (*ahead of leaving*), which sense 1 does not.
**Pattern:** [clause] *ahead of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Ahead of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *well/far/just ahead of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against *in front of*, sense 1 implies a shared line of travel or a queue — *the car ahead of us* is going the same way, *the car in front of us* may be parked crosswise. Against *before*, sense 2 is the journalistic word for anticipation: *ahead of the vote* rather than *before the vote*, with an implication of preparation. Bare *ahead* with no object is an adverb, not this preposition: *Go on ahead.*

**1. In front of, in a line or sequence.** There were six people *ahead of* us.

### at the hands of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə ˈhændz əv/ &middot; weak /ət ðə ˈhændz ə/ — *at* reduces to /ət/ &middot; *at thuh HANDZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; news writing, history, and sports reporting
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a person, group, or institution — **never an inanimate force**. **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.** A free relative naming an agent is fine (*at the hands of whoever was guarding him*).
**Pattern:** [noun of harm or defeat] *at the hands of* [person or body] &middot; [clause] *at the hands of* [person or body]
**Contrast:** An agentive phrase with a restriction built into it: **what happens at somebody's hands is always something bad.** *A beating at the hands of the guards* and *a defeat at the hands of an unranked team* are idiomatic; *a gift at the hands of her uncle* is not, and plain *by* is the neutral form for everything else. ### at the mercy of

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə ˈmɜrsi əv/ &middot; weak /ət ðə ˈmɜrsi ə/ — *at* reduces to /ət/ &middot; *at thuh MUR-see uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a person, body, or uncontrollable force. **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.** A free relative is fine and is common (*at the mercy of whatever the wholesalers decide to pay*).

**Contrast:** It asserts **powerlessness, not cruelty** — *at the mercy of the weather* accuses the weather of nothing, and the phrase is fully idiomatic with forces that have no intentions at all. 1. Small growers are *at the mercy of* whatever the wholesalers decide to pay.

4. The schedule leaves the whole project *at the mercy of* a single supplier.
5. Renters here are completely *at the mercy of* a market with no vacancies.
6. He refused to be *at the mercy of* a committee that met twice a year.

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkɔz əv/ or /bɪˈkʌz əv/ &middot; casual /bɪˈkʌzə/, /ˈkʌzə/ (*'cause of*) &middot; *bih-KUZ uhv*

**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*different from me*, never *different from I*), gerund (*different from working nights*), or a free relative (*different from what I expected*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [X] *is different from* [Y] &middot; *very / completely / no different from* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *different from* [noun phrase]

**Contrast:** Synonymous with *regardless of*, which is far more common in American English; *irrespective of* is the formal, legal-flavored twin. Note that *irrespective* is the correct word with the *ir-* prefix — the nonstandard *irregardless* is a blend of these two and should never be written.

Dismisses a factor as irrelevant to what the main clause asserts. It is the drafting word for non-discrimination clauses and eligibility rules.

1. The benefit is available to all employees *irrespective of* length of service.
2. *Irrespective of* cost, the repair has to be done this month.
3. Admission is open to applicants *irrespective of* nationality.
4. The rule applies *irrespective of* what the parties intended.
5. *Irrespective of* the outcome, the process was worth it.

**See also:** [regardless of](#regardless-of), [in spite of](#in-spite-of), [notwithstanding](12-marginal-participial.md#notwithstanding), [in the face of](#in-the-face-of)

### on account of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn əˈkaʊnt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn əˈkaʊn də/ &middot; *on uh-KOWNT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal in standard use; the clause-taking version is regional and nonstandard
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*on account of being sick*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*on account of what he said*). **A *that*-clause or a bare finite clause after it — *on account of he was sick* — is nonstandard** in writing, though it is well attested in Southern and Appalachian speech and in dialogue.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on account of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *On account of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** A three-word *because of*, slightly more formal in writing and slightly folksier in speech, depending on the surrounding register. Against *due to*, it is safely adverbial. Note the unrelated literal phrase *on account* meaning "as partial payment" (*$50 on account*), which is accounting language, not this preposition.

Gives the reason for the main clause. In edited American prose *because of* is the default and *on account of* is a stylistic choice; in speech it often signals a homelier register.

1. The game was called *on account of* rain.
2. She retired early *on account of* her back.
3. *On account of* the holiday, the office closes at noon.
4. He was excused *on account of* being the only caregiver at home.
5. The road is closed *on account of* flooding.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [in view of](#in-view-of)

### on behalf of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn bɪˈhæf əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn bɪˈhæ və/ &middot; *on bih-HAF uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, naming a person or body; **never a gerund and never a *that*-clause**. A free relative naming a person is fine (*on behalf of whoever ends up holding the lease*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *on behalf of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *On behalf of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** *in behalf of* was traditionally distinguished in American usage — *on behalf of* = "as the agent of," *in behalf of* = "for the benefit of" — but the distinction has largely collapsed and *on behalf of* now covers both. *In behalf of* is safe to avoid.
**Contrast:** Against *for*, this specifies representation rather than mere benefit. Against *in the name of*, which invokes authority, *on behalf of* names a principal you are acting for. The reflexive form is *on my own behalf*, not *on behalf of myself*.

Marks the person or body the speaker or actor is representing. It is the standard opening of formal remarks and the standard signature line of counsel and agents.

1. *On behalf of* the entire staff, thank you for twenty years.
2. The attorney filed the motion *on behalf of* her client.
3. He accepted the award *on behalf of* his late father.
4. The union bargains *on behalf of* eleven thousand members.
5. *On behalf of* the mayor, I'd like to welcome you to the city.

### on the basis of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈbeɪsɪs əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈbeɪsɪ sə/ &middot; *on thuh BAY-siss uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal wh-clause (*on the basis of what he saw*). Never a *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the basis of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *On the basis of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** *on the basis that* is the clause-taking companion form and is a conjunction, not this preposition: *on the basis that the contract had lapsed*.
**Contrast:** Names the evidence or criterion a judgment rests on, where *because of* names a cause. *Hired on the basis of her portfolio* describes the grounds for a decision; *hired because of her portfolio* describes what made it happen. Non-discrimination law makes heavy use of *on the basis of* precisely because "grounds" is the legally relevant idea.

Identifies what a decision, claim, or classification was grounded in. It is the standard formula in statutes, methods sections, and administrative findings.

1. Applicants are ranked *on the basis of* test scores and interviews.
2. Discrimination *on the basis of* age is prohibited.
3. *On the basis of* the sample, the estimate is within two points.
4. The claim was denied *on the basis of* a paperwork error.
5. She was hired *on the basis of* what her references said, not her résumé.

### on the grounds of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈɡraʊndz əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈɡraʊnd zə/ &middot; *on thuh GROWNDZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and administrative
**Complement:** noun phrase naming a recognized ground — *age*, *race*, *national origin*, *cost*, *ill health*, *national security* — or a free relative (*on the grounds of what the inspector found*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the grounds of* [noun phrase] &middot; *refuse / object / dismiss / discriminate on the grounds of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *on grounds of*, without the article, is common in headlines and drafting; the singular *on the ground of* is older and now rare in American use. **The clause-taking companion is *on the grounds that* + a full clause** (*on the grounds that the notice was late*), which is a conjunction and not this preposition — the same split as *in case of* / *in case* and *on the basis of* / *on the basis that*.
**Contrast:** Exactly parallel to [*on the basis of*](#on-the-basis-of), and the two are near-interchangeable in discrimination law, but *on the grounds of* leans toward a reason given for refusing, objecting, or excluding, while *on the basis of* leans toward a criterion used for deciding: you are *hired on the basis of* your record and *dismissed on the grounds of* misconduct. Against *because of*, this one claims the reason was formally stated, not merely operative.

Names the stated reason for a decision, an objection, or a refusal. The reason is presented as one the speaker is prepared to defend, which is why the phrase belongs to rulings and appeals rather than to ordinary explanation.

1. The application was refused *on the grounds of* cost.
2. Discrimination *on the grounds of* age is unlawful in every state.
3. He objected *on the grounds of* relevance.
4. She retired early *on the grounds of* ill health.
5. The documents were withheld *on the grounds of* national security.
6. The appeal succeeded *on the grounds of* what the trial transcript actually showed.

### on the heels of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈhilz əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈhil zə/ &middot; *on thuh HEELZ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a standard idiom of news writing
**Complement:** noun phrase naming an event; a gerund is possible but uncommon (*on the heels of losing the contract*). **Never a *that*-clause.** A free relative naming the event is fine (*on the heels of what happened in March*).
**Pattern:** *come/follow/arrive on the heels of* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *on the heels of* [noun phrase] &middot; *hard/hot on the heels of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *hard on the heels of* and *hot on the heels of* are the intensified forms and mean "immediately after." *Heels* is invariable — there is no *on the heel of*.
**Contrast:** Against [*in the wake of*](#in-the-wake-of), the phrase it is most often confused with, **this one claims closeness in time and nothing more**: *in the wake of the scandal* says the scandal caused what followed, while *on the heels of the scandal* says only that the second thing came fast behind the first. Reach for *in the wake of* when you mean consequence and *on the heels of* when you mean the very next thing. Against plain *after*, both add speed and neither is neutral.

Marks an event as following hard upon another. The metaphor is pursuit, so the two events have to be close enough together for the second to be treading on the first.

1. The layoffs came *on the heels of* a record quarter.
2. *On the heels of* the recall, two more suppliers suspended shipments.
3. The announcement arrived hard *on the heels of* the mayor's resignation.
4. A second storm followed *on the heels of* the first.
5. *On the heels of* losing the contract, the firm closed its Denver office.
6. The bill passed *on the heels of* three years of failed attempts.

### on the part of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈpɑrt əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈpɑr də/ &middot; *on thuh PART uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, academic, and report writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming a person or a body — **never a thing**. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [noun of conduct or attitude] *on the part of* [person or body] &middot; *There was no* [noun] *on the part of* [person].
**Contrast:** Its one job is to attach an actor to an abstract noun of behavior — *negligence*, *reluctance*, *failure*, *bad faith*, *misunderstanding* — where the nominalization has lost the subject a verb would have carried: *negligence on the part of the contractor* is *the contractor was negligent*, kept nominal. That is also its weakness, since nine times in ten a *by* phrase or a plain verb is shorter. **The complement must be animate** — *a failure on the part of the software* is a misuse for *a failure of the software*. Do not confuse it with *for my part* ("as far as I'm concerned") or *take the part of* ("side with"), which are separate fixed phrases.

Assigns an abstract behavior to the person or body responsible for it. It exists because English nominalizations drop their subjects, and this is the phrase that puts one back.

1. There was no bad faith *on the part of* either side.
2. The report found negligence *on the part of* two supervisors.
3. Some reluctance *on the part of* the family is understandable.
4. It was a genuine misunderstanding *on the part of* the clerk.
5. Any failure *on the part of* the tenant to give notice voids the clause.
6. The delay reflects real indecision *on the part of* the board.

**See also:** [on behalf of](#on-behalf-of), [in the name of](#in-the-name-of), [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [on the part of](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#on-the-part-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### on the strength of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈstrɛŋkθ əv/ &middot; also /ˈstrɛŋθ/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈstrɛŋk θə/ &middot; *on thuh STRENGKTH uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative (*on the strength of what the scouts reported*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *on the strength of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *On the strength of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Against [*on the basis of*](#on-the-basis-of), which names a criterion neutrally, *on the strength of* adds that the thing named was **persuasive enough to carry the decision**: *hired on the basis of her portfolio* says the portfolio was what they looked at, *hired on the strength of her portfolio* says it was what convinced them. Against *because of*, it confines the cause to evidence, testimony, or a record that somebody actually weighed. It resists a weak object — *on the strength of a rumor* reads as deliberate irony, and that irony is the proof of the rule.

Names the evidence or achievement that carried a decision. It presupposes that somebody was persuaded, which is why it attaches to hirings, loans, verdicts, promotions, and signings.

1. The loan was approved *on the strength of* two years of receipts.
2. She was promoted *on the strength of* a single quarter.
3. *On the strength of* his testimony, the charges were refiled.
4. The band was signed *on the strength of* a four-song demo.
5. The town rebuilt its budget *on the strength of* what the new plant paid in taxes.
6. He got the fellowship *on the strength of* one unpublished chapter.

### on the verge of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈvɜrdʒ əv/ &middot; connected speech /ɑn ðə ˈvɜr dʒə/ &middot; *on thuh VURJ uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund** (*on the verge of collapse*, *on the verge of collapsing*). Because *of* is a preposition, ***on the verge of to collapse* is wrong**, and so is ***on the verge to collapse***. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** *be on the verge of* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *bring/push someone to the verge of* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *on the brink of* is the near-twin and is preferred for large disasters (*on the brink of war*); *on the point of* is the more formal, British-leaning version and takes the same gerund. **The *of* is not optional** — the elliptical *She was on the verge* survives only where the complement has just been said.
**Contrast:** Against sense 3 of [*close to*](#close-to) (*close to tears*), which is gradable and vague about the outcome, *on the verge of* names a **threshold about to be crossed**, and it takes only outcomes, never places: *close to the airport* is fine, *on the verge of the airport* is nonsense. Against [*in danger of*](#in-danger-of), it is neutral about whether the outcome is welcome — you can be *on the verge of* a breakthrough as easily as a breakdown, where *in danger of* is always bad.

Places the subject one step short of a change of state. Because the change has not happened, the phrase stays true even if it never does.

1. The company was *on the verge of* bankruptcy when the order came in.
2. She was *on the verge of* tears and would not look up.
3. Researchers say they are *on the verge of* a usable test.
4. He was *on the verge of* quitting when the transfer came through.
5. Two of the three species are *on the verge of* disappearing from the river.
6. The talks are *on the verge of* collapse for the third time this year.

### on top of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ˈtɑp əv/ &middot; casual /ɑnˈtɑpə/ &middot; *on TOP uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; sense 3 informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund in sense 2 (*on top of paying rent*). Sense 3 also takes a nominal *wh*-clause (*on top of what the auditors want*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *on top of* [noun phrase] &middot; *On top of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *right on top of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Sense 1 is literal and takes intensifiers (*right on top of*); senses 2 and 3 are frozen idioms. Against *in addition to*, sense 2 is the informal equivalent and usually carries a complaint — things pile *on top of* each other. Against *above*, sense 1 requires contact: a lamp *above* the table is not touching it; a book *on top of* the table is. **Bare *on top* is an adverb, not this preposition**, and takes no object: *The cake had berries on top* is complete; *on top of the cake* needs its object.

**1. Resting on the upper surface of.** The keys are *on top of* the refrigerator.
**2. In addition to (usually burdensome).** *On top of* everything else, the car wouldn't start.

1. The keys are *on top of* the refrigerator.
2. The truck stopped right *on top of* the crosswalk.
3. Stack the folders *on top of* the printer for now.
4. *On top of* everything else, the furnace quit.
5. *On top of* paying rent, they cover their own utilities.

**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — one of the most frequent prepositions in the language
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund in sense 5 only (*out of not wanting to offend anyone*). In senses 4 and 5 the complement is characteristically a bare mass or abstract noun (*out of milk*, *out of curiosity*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a nominal *wh*-clause is possible in sense 2 (*out of what little was left*).
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *out of* [noun phrase] &middot; *be out of* [noun phrase] &middot; [number] *out of* [number] &middot; *made out of* [material]
**Contrast:** *Out* alone is an adverb or a verb particle and takes no object (*She went out*, *Cross it out*); *out of* is the preposition and requires one. Against *from*, sense 1 insists on prior enclosure — you come *out of* a building but *from* a city. **6. Beyond the limits of (a range or state).** The plane was already *out of* sight.
**7. Absent from (a place the subject belongs to or is expected at).** She's *out of* the office until Monday.

1. She walked *out of* the room without a word.

8. The team is *out of* contention for the first time in years.
9. He apologized *out of* habit rather than conviction.
10. I only asked *out of* curiosity.
11. The plane was already *out of* sight.
12. Your call is *out of* the ordinary, and so is the answer.
13. She made supper *out of* what little was left in the pantry.
14. He said nothing, *out of* not wanting to make it worse.
15. She's *out of* the office until Monday and unreachable before that.
16. He has been *out of* the country since June.

**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral in sense 1 (with the *of* often edited out); informal and **chiefly American** in sense 2

**1. Beyond the limits of (spatial or figurative; the *of* is dispensable).** The plant is *outside of* the city limits.

### regardless of

**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — the standard American form
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal wh-clause (*regardless of what happens*, *regardless of whether he agrees*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *regardless of* [noun phrase / wh-clause]. &middot; *Regardless of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** ***Irregardless* is not a word in standard English** — it is a blend of *irrespective* and *regardless*, and although dictionaries record it, it is stigmatized everywhere and should never be written. Against *in spite of* and *despite*, *regardless of* dismisses a factor as irrelevant rather than conceding it as an obstacle: *in spite of the cost* admits the cost was a problem, *regardless of the cost* refuses to weigh it. ## The *___ to* family

An adjective or participle plus *to*. Every member shares one pronunciation fact worth learning before anything else: **the *to* is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, and it reduces to /tə/**, so *prior to* is *PRY-ur tuh* and *according to* is *uh-KOR-ding tuh*. That same fact produces the family's defining grammatical trap — because the *to* is prepositional, what follows must be a noun phrase or a gerund, never a bare verb. *Prior to submitting the form* is correct; *prior to submit the form* is not.

**This is the single most productive error in learner English, and it reaches well past this file.** The same prepositional *to* governs a whole set of adjectives and prepositional verbs that behave identically, and all of them demand an *-ing* form where the learner reaches for an infinitive:

| Correct | Wrong |
| --- | --- |
| I *look forward to* **hearing** from you. | ~~I look forward to hear from you.~~ |
| They *object to* **being** questioned twice. | ~~They object to be questioned twice.~~ |
| She is *accustomed to* **working** nights. | ~~She is accustomed to work nights.~~ |
| He got *used to* **living** alone. | ~~He got used to live alone.~~ |
| We are *opposed to* **raising** the fee. | ~~We are opposed to raise the fee.~~ |
| The offer is *subject to* **being** approved. | ~~The offer is subject to be approved.~~ |
| The plan is *devoted to* / *committed to* **cutting** waste. | ~~committed to cut waste~~ |

The test is mechanical: **try substituting a plain noun.** If *I look forward to your letter* works, the *to* is a preposition and the verb after it must be *-ing*. If a noun cannot go there — *I want to your letter* is nonsense — the *to* is an infinitive marker and a bare verb is correct.

Several members of the family are also disputed on usage grounds: *due to* has the oldest live controversy in English usage, and *prior to* and *subsequent to* are bureaucratic padding for *before* and *after*.

### according to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tu/ &middot; weak /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tə/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal wh-clause (*according to what she told me*). **Never a *that*-clause** and never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** *According to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause], *according to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** ***According to me* is not idiomatic** — the phrase attributes a claim to an outside source, so a speaker attributing a claim to himself must say *in my opinion* or *I think*. Against *in accordance with*, which reports conformity to a rule, *according to* reports what a source says. Sense 3 (*according to need*) is a distinct, older sense meaning "in proportion to."

**1. As stated or reported by (a source).** *According to* the coroner, death was instantaneous.
**2. In conformity with (a plan, rule, or standard).** Everything went *according to* plan.
**3. In proportion to.** Aid is distributed *according to* need.

1. *According to* the National Weather Service, the storm turns north tonight.
2. He's leaving in June, *according to* two people familiar with the decision.
3. *According to* what she told me, the deal fell through in March.
4. Everything went *according to* plan until the second act.
5. Sort the files *according to* date, not alphabetically.
6. Aid is distributed *according to* need rather than population.
7. Members are taxed *according to* their ability to pay.

**See also:** [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [as per](#as-per), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [in line with](#in-line-with), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with)

### accustomed to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkʌstəmd tu/ &middot; weak /əˈkʌstəmd tə/ &middot; *uh-KUSS-tumd too*
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund only.** The *to* is a preposition, not an infinitive marker, so ***accustomed to work nights* is wrong** — it must be *accustomed to working nights*. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.** The reflexive verb form *accustom oneself to* follows exactly the same rule: *accustom yourself to waiting*, never *to wait*.
**Pattern:** *be/grow/become accustomed to* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *accustomed to* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; *quite/hardly/not yet accustomed to* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *used to* is the everyday equivalent and takes the identical complement — *used to living alone*, never *used to live alone* in this sense. **Beware the trap:** *used to* + bare infinitive (*He used to live alone*) is a completely different item, a past-habitual auxiliary, and the collision of the two is the reason learners produce *I'm used to live here*.
**Contrast:** This is the cleanest test case in the file for the prepositional *to*. **Substitute a plain noun**: *accustomed to the noise* is perfectly good, which proves the *to* is a preposition and forces the *-ing* on any verb that follows. The same test convicts *look forward to hearing* and *object to being questioned*. Against *given to* (*given to exaggeration*), which reports a tendency, *accustomed to* reports habituation through repeated exposure. Against *familiar with*, which is about knowledge, this is about tolerance.

Says the subject has been worn into acceptance of something by exposure to it. It is freely gradable — *more accustomed*, *not yet accustomed*, *thoroughly accustomed* — which is what still marks *accustomed* as a live adjective rather than a fully frozen particle.

1. She is *accustomed to* working nights and sleeps through the afternoon.
2. He never grew *accustomed to* the cold.
3. Readers *accustomed to* the old layout complained for months.
4. They are not *accustomed to* being questioned.
5. It took a full year to become *accustomed to* the altitude.
6. A jury *accustomed to* long trials would not have found this one unusual.
7. He is quite *accustomed to* having his calls returned within the hour.

**See also:** [subject to](#subject-to), [opposed to](#opposed-to), [obedient to](#obedient-to), [close to](#close-to), [up to](#up-to)

### close to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkloʊs tu/ &middot; weak /ˈkloʊs tə/ &middot; *KLOHSS too* — **the *s* is /s/, not /z/**; /kloʊz/ is the verb *close*
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund in sense 3 (*close to quitting*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*close to what we expected*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — never a bare infinitive: *close to finishing*, not *close to finish*.
**Pattern:** *be close to* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *close to* [noun phrase] &middot; *very/quite/awfully close to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Against *near*, which is a one-word preposition doing the same spatial job, *close to* is more freely gradable (*much closer to*, *closest to*). Against *next to*, which requires adjacency with nothing in between, *close to* only requires proximity. Sense 2 with a number means "approximately" and is not spatial at all, and sense 4 is not spatial either — *close to her sister* is about intimacy, not distance, and it is the one sense that cannot take a measure phrase. **Bare *close* is an adjective or adverb and takes no object** — *Stay close*, *That was close* — and it is pronounced the same /kloʊs/; only *close to* is the preposition.

**1. Near in space.** They live *close to* the airport.
**2. Nearly (with a quantity).** *Close to* four hundred people showed up.
**3. On the verge of.** She was *close to* tears.
**4. Emotionally attached to.** She has always been *close to* her grandmother.

1. They live *close to* the airport, which is why the rent is low.
2. Pull the chair *close to* the table.
3. The two candidates finished remarkably *close to* each other.
4. *Close to* four hundred people showed up for a room that seats two hundred.
5. The repair will run *close to* three thousand dollars.
6. She was *close to* tears by the end of the hearing.
7. He came *close to* quitting twice that year.
8. She has always been *close to* her grandmother.
9. He is not especially *close to* anyone in the family.

**See also:** [next to](#next-to), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [up to](#up-to), [away from](#away-from), [in front of](#in-front-of)

### contrary to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrɛri tu/ &middot; weak /ˈkɑntrɛri tə/ &middot; *KAHN-trair-ee too* (stress on the first syllable; the adjective meaning "perverse" is /kənˈtrɛri/, *kun-TRAIR-ee*)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause — and the *wh*-clause is one of its commonest patterns (***contrary to what you may have heard***, *contrary to what the label says*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund**, and — the *to* being a preposition — never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** *Contrary to* [noun phrase / wh-clause], [clause]. &middot; [clause], *contrary to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** Sense 2 is the fixed rhetorical opener *contrary to popular belief*, which corrects the reader; sense 1 is a plainer statement of conflict with a rule or expectation. Against *in contrast to*, which merely sets two things side by side, *contrary to* asserts that one contradicts the other. Note the stress difference between this word and the adjective *contrary* meaning "stubborn." **The bare adverbials are separate items:** *on the contrary* rejects what was just said (*On the contrary, sales rose*), and *to the contrary* modifies a noun (*evidence to the contrary*); neither takes an object the way *contrary to* does.

**1. In conflict with (a rule, instruction, or fact).** The parts were installed *contrary to* the manufacturer's instructions.
**2. Unlike what is generally supposed.** *Contrary to* popular belief, the building is not the tallest in the city.

1. The parts were installed *contrary to* the manufacturer's instructions.
2. He acted *contrary to* the advice of every lawyer in the room.
3. Nothing in the record is *contrary to* her account.
4. *Contrary to* popular belief, most avalanches are triggered by the victims.
5. *Contrary to* expectations, the smaller school won by nineteen.
6. The evidence runs *contrary to* the prosecution's theory.
7. *Contrary to* what you may have heard, the branch is not closing.
8. *Contrary to* what the label says, the product contains no juice at all.

**See also:** [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [other than](#other-than)

### due to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdu tu/ &middot; weak /ˈdu tə/ &middot; *DOO too* (British /ˈdjuː/, *DYOO*)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a live usage dispute over sense 2
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*due to being understaffed*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*due to what the inspector found*). **Never a *that*-clause** — write *due to the fact that* if you need one, though *because* is better. Because this *to* is a preposition, **never a bare infinitive** in the causal sense: *due to needing repairs*, not *due to need repairs*.
**Pattern:** *be due to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *due to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Due to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** **The classic test:** *due to* is traditionally adjectival, so it should be substitutable by *attributable to* or *caused by* and should attach to a noun — *The delay was due to fog* (fine, *due to* modifies *delay*). Using it adverbially to modify a whole clause — *Due to fog, the flight was canceled* — was condemned for most of the twentieth century, and *because of* or *owing to* was prescribed instead. That adverbial use is now standard in American English and appears in edited prose everywhere, but conservative editors still flag it, so *because of* is the safe choice in formal writing. Note separately that *be due to* + bare infinitive (*The train is due to arrive at six*) is a different construction entirely — adjective *due* plus a true infinitive, meaning "scheduled."

**1. Caused by; attributable to (adjectival, after *be* or modifying a noun).** The delay was *due to* fog.
**2. Because of (adverbial, modifying the clause — standard but contested).** *Due to* fog, the flight was canceled.

1. The delay was *due to* fog over the bay.
2. Most of the increase is *due to* a change in how the data is collected.
3. Absences *due to* illness must be reported by 9 a.m.
4. *Due to* the holiday, there will be no pickup on Monday.
5. *Due to* being understaffed all summer, the clinic cut its hours.
6. Errors *due to* rounding may cause the totals to differ slightly.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to)

### next door to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛkst ˈdɔr tu/ &middot; casual /ˈnɛks ˈdɔr tə/ (the first *t* usually drops) &middot; *NEKST DOR too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; sense 2 informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun (*next door to us*); **never a gerund and never a *that*-clause**. A free relative naming premises is fine (*next door to what is now a nail salon*). Sense 2 takes only an adjective or an abstract noun of extremity (*next door to impossible*, *next door to a miracle*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *next door to* [noun phrase] &middot; *be next door to* [adjective / noun]
**Contrast:** Against *next to*, this asserts the specific relation of adjacent premises: *next door to the bakery* is the building beside it, where *next to the bakery* could be a parked car or a mailbox. Sense 2 is a fixed intensifier meaning "very nearly," and it survives only with words at the end of a scale — *next door to impossible* works, *next door to difficult* does not. **Bare *next door* is an adverb and takes no object**: *They live next door*, *Ask next door* — where the preposition always requires one.

**1. In the building or unit immediately adjacent to.** They live *next door to* the elementary school.
**2. Very nearly; barely short of (informal).** Getting a permit that fast is *next door to* impossible.

1. They live *next door to* the elementary school.
2. The pharmacy is *next door to* the hardware store, not across from it.
3. We moved in *next door to* her parents and stayed nine years.
4. The office is *next door to* mine, which is how I hear everything.
5. Getting a permit that fast is *next door to* impossible.
6. His third album is *next door to* unlistenable.

**See also:** [next to](#next-to), [close to](#close-to), [in front of](#in-front-of), [on top of](#on-top-of), [alongside](03-place-position.md#alongside)

### next to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛkst tu/ &middot; casual /ˈnɛks tə/ (the first *t* usually drops) &middot; *NEKST too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; **never a gerund and never a *that*-clause**. A free relative naming a place or a thing is fine (*next to what used to be the bakery*). Sense 2 takes only a small set of negative quantifiers (*nothing*, *no one*, *never*, *impossible*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *next to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Next to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *next to* [negative quantifier]
**Contrast:** Against *beside*, which means the same thing and is one word shorter, *next to* is the more common in American speech. **Do not confuse *beside* with *besides***: *beside* is the spatial word, "at the side of," while *besides* means "in addition to" or "apart from" (*Besides the rent, there's insurance*). The fixed idiom is ***beside the point***, meaning irrelevant — and ***besides the point* is simply an error for it**, not a phrase with a meaning of its own. Against *close to*, *next to* requires immediate adjacency — nothing between the two things. Sense 2, *next to nothing*, is a fixed idiom meaning "almost none" and has no spatial content; sense 3 is comparative and means "compared with." **Bare *next* is a determiner or an adverb and takes no object** — *next week*, *Who's next?*, *the house next door* — where the preposition always has one.

**1. Immediately beside.** She sat *next to* the window.
**2. Almost (with a negative).** He knows *next to* nothing about cars.
**3. Compared with.** *Next to* last year's model, this one feels cheap.

1. She sat *next to* the window the whole flight.
2. The pharmacy is *next to* the hardware store.
3. Put the recycling bin *next to* the garage, not in front of it.
4. He knows *next to* nothing about cars.
5. The repair cost *next to* nothing.
6. *Next to* last year's model, this one feels cheap.
7. *Next to* her, everyone else on the panel looked unprepared.

**See also:** [close to](#close-to), [next door to](#next-door-to), [in front of](#in-front-of), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [alongside](03-place-position.md#alongside), [on top of](#on-top-of)

### obedient to

**Pronunciation:** /oʊˈbidiənt tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *oh-BEE-dee-unt too*
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition; **marginal** — it is closer to an adjective governing its complement than to a true unit) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, legal
**Complement:** noun phrase only. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund** — and, the *to* being a preposition, never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** *be obedient to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *obedient to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** Listed here because it patterns like *contrary to* and *pursuant to*, but it is the weakest member of the family: *obedient* is a fully live adjective and *to* is simply the preposition it takes, so the two do not fuse the way *according to* does. Compare [*subject to*](#subject-to), which has fused much further and has developed meanings the adjective *subject* no longer carries on its own. In modern American prose, *obeys* or *complies with* is nearly always better.

Says the subject submits to an authority, a law, or a principle. It is the vocabulary of scripture, oaths, and nineteenth-century prose, and outside those settings it sounds antique.

1. The design is *obedient to* the constraints of the site.
2. A citizen is *obedient to* the law, not to the officer enforcing it.
3. The prose is *obedient to* a rhythm that never varies.
4. He remained *obedient to* the vows he had taken at twenty.
5. Every part of the machine is *obedient to* a single cam.

**See also:** [subject to](#subject-to), [accustomed to](#accustomed-to), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [according to](#according-to)

### opposed to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpoʊzd tu/ &middot; weak /əˈpoʊzd tə/ &middot; *uh-POHZD too*
**Type:** complex (participial adjective + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund only.** The *to* is a preposition, so ***opposed to raise the fee* is wrong** — it must be *opposed to raising the fee*. **Never a *that*-clause**; a nominal *wh*-clause is marginal and best avoided.
**Pattern:** *be/remain opposed to* [noun phrase / verb-ing] &middot; [noun] *opposed to* [noun phrase] &middot; *strongly/bitterly/firmly/implacably opposed to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **Distinguish this sharply from [*as opposed to*](#as-opposed-to)**, which is a different item doing a different job: *She is opposed to the plan* states a position, while *a grant, as opposed to a loan* draws a definitional line and states no position at all. Dropping the *as* changes the sentence from a clarification into an assertion of belief, and the two are confused constantly. Against plain *against*, which is shorter and says the same thing (*She is against the plan*), *opposed to* is the formal register's choice and grades more freely. Against *in favor of*, it is the exact antonym and takes the same complements.

States that the subject holds a position of resistance. Because it is a participial adjective rather than a verb, it reports a settled stance rather than an act of opposing, which is why committees and editorial pages reach for it.

1. Two members are firmly *opposed to* the merger.
2. She is *opposed to* raising the fee in an election year.
3. The union is not *opposed to* automation as such.
4. He remained bitterly *opposed to* the settlement until the last day.
5. Voters *opposed to* the measure outnumbered its supporters two to one.
6. They are *opposed to* being surveyed at all.
7. Nobody on the board is *opposed to* the idea in principle.

**See also:** [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in favor of](#in-favor-of), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [subject to](#subject-to), [accustomed to](#accustomed-to)

### opposite to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑpəzɪt tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *AH-puh-zit too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; **chiefly British in the spatial sense**
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause in the abstract sense (*opposite to what the designers intended*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [noun/clause] *opposite to* [noun phrase / wh-clause]
**Variants:** In American English the ordinary spatial word is bare *opposite* (*the house opposite ours*) or, far more often, *across from* (*the house across from ours*). *Opposite to* survives in American usage mainly in the abstract sense "the reverse of."
**Contrast:** Bare *opposite* is itself a preposition and needs no *to*; adding *to* is a British habit in the spatial sense and is redundant in American prose. Against *in front of*, *opposite* requires the two things to face each other across a space. Against *contrary to*, which asserts conflict, *opposite to* asserts inversion.

Places one thing facing or inverting another. American writers should prefer *across from* for places and *the opposite of* for abstractions.

1. The effect was exactly *opposite to* what the designers intended.
2. Their conclusions are *opposite to* ours in every particular.
3. The pharmacy sits *opposite to* the courthouse. *(chiefly British; American: across from)*
4. Warm air moves *opposite to* the surface current.
5. Her instinct runs *opposite to* his on almost every question.

**See also:** [in front of](#in-front-of), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [next to](#next-to)

### owing to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊɪŋ tu/ &middot; weak /ˈoʊɪŋ tə/ &middot; *OH-ing too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*owing to what the survey found*). **Never a *that*-clause** — *owing to the fact that* is the clause-taking workaround. The *to* is a preposition, so **never a bare infinitive**: *owing to being closed*, not *owing to be closed*.
**Pattern:** *Owing to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *owing to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *Owing to* is the preposition that traditionalists prescribe **instead of** adverbial *due to*: *Owing to fog, the flight was canceled* has never been disputed, while *Due to fog, ...* has. The two are otherwise synonymous, and both mean *because of*. In American English *owing to* is noticeably more formal and less frequent than in British English.

Names the cause of the situation in the main clause. Its safety in the sentence-adverbial position is its whole reason for surviving alongside *because of*.

1. *Owing to* a scheduling conflict, the hearing was moved to Thursday.
2. The shortfall is *owing to* lower-than-expected enrollment.
3. *Owing to* the drought, the district imposed watering restrictions.
4. Several flights were delayed *owing to* a systems failure at the tower.
5. *Owing to* being closed for renovation, the branch is not accepting returns.

**See also:** [due to](#due-to), [because of](#because-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to)

### prior to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpraɪər tu/ &middot; weak /ˈpraɪər tə/ &middot; *PRY-ur too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; bureaucratic, legal, and technical
**Complement:** noun phrase or **gerund** (*prior to signing*). **Never a bare infinitive** — *prior to sign the lease* is wrong — and **never a *that*-clause or a *wh*-clause**.
**Pattern:** [clause] *prior to* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *Prior to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** ***Prior to* means *before*, and *before* is almost always better.** It is the most reliably flagged padding in this whole family; usage guides from Fowler onward have called it a needless formality. Keep it only where *before* would be ambiguous with the spatial sense, or in legal drafting where it is conventional. Note that *prior* alone is an adjective (*a prior conviction*), not a preposition.

Places an event earlier than another. Its one real advantage over *before* is that it cannot be misread as spatial.

1. All applications must be received *prior to* the March 1 deadline.
2. *Prior to* signing, read the arbitration clause carefully.
3. She had no experience in the field *prior to* 2019.
4. Employees hired *prior to* the merger keep their original benefits.
5. *Prior to* the storm, the reservoir was already at capacity.

**See also:** [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [as of](#as-of), [in advance of](#in-advance-of), [pending](12-marginal-participial.md#pending), [before](05-time.md#before)

### pursuant to

**Pronunciation:** /pərˈsuənt tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *pur-SOO-unt too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal — essentially confined to statutes, contracts, and court filings
**Complement:** noun phrase only, almost always a legal instrument. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund**, and never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** [clause] *pursuant to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Pursuant to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Means "under, in accordance with, as authorized by." Against *in accordance with*, it is narrower — the object must be a rule or authority that empowers the action, not merely a standard the action matched. Plain-language drafting guides recommend *under* in nearly every case: *under Section 12* says what *pursuant to Section 12* says.

Grounds an action in the legal authority that permits it. It is one of the few prepositions in English whose register is essentially a single profession.

1. The subpoena was issued *pursuant to* Rule 45.
2. *Pursuant to* the settlement agreement, the payments begin in January.
3. Records are released *pursuant to* a valid public-records request.
4. The officer conducted the search *pursuant to* a warrant.
5. *Pursuant to* state law, the notice must run for three consecutive weeks.

**See also:** [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [by virtue of](#by-virtue-of), [subject to](#subject-to), [under](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#under), [on the basis of](#on-the-basis-of), [in receipt of](#in-receipt-of)

### relative to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɛlətɪv tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *REL-uh-tiv too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; technical in sense 1, bureaucratic in sense 2
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*relative to what we paid last year*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** [clause] *relative to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Relative to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** Sense 1 is quantitative and is the standard scientific and financial word for a benchmark comparison. Sense 2 means "concerning" and is stiff bureaucratic English for *about* — the same criticism that applies to *in terms of* and *with regard to*. Against *compared with*, sense 1 implies a proportional or normalized comparison, not just a side-by-side one.

**1. In comparison with; measured against.** Wages have fallen *relative to* housing costs.
**2. Concerning; with regard to.** The memo raises questions *relative to* the timeline.

1. Wages have fallen sharply *relative to* housing costs.
2. The fund underperformed *relative to* its benchmark for the third year running.
3. Motion is only meaningful *relative to* a chosen frame of reference.
4. The city's crime rate is low *relative to* comparable metros.
5. The memo raises several questions *relative to* the proposed timeline.
6. Please forward any documents *relative to* the 2023 audit.

### similar to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪmələr tu/ &middot; weak /ˈsɪmələr tə/ &middot; *SIM-uh-lur too* — **not /ˈsɪmjələr/**; there is no *y* sound in it
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*similar to mine*), gerund (*similar to running a small business*), or a free relative (*similar to what we saw last year*). **Never a *that*-clause**, and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**: *similar to filing*, not *similar to file*.
**Pattern:** [X] *is similar to* [Y] &middot; *very / broadly / roughly / strikingly similar to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *similar to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***Similar to* takes *to*; [*different from*](#different-from) takes *from*** — two exact opposites with opposite prepositions, and both ***similar with* and *similar than* are errors**. Against *like*, the one-word preposition doing the same job (*a car like mine*), *similar to* is the written, hedged form and grades freely in a way *like* cannot: *broadly similar to* has no *like* equivalent. Against *the same as*, it deliberately stops short of identity — things *similar to* each other are not claimed to match.

Asserts resemblance without asserting identity. Because it is gradable, the real information is usually in the adverb in front of it rather than in the phrase itself.

1. The new policy is *similar to* one Ohio adopted in 2019.
2. Her handwriting is strikingly *similar to* her mother's.
3. Running a clinic is *similar to* running a small business, only with worse hours.
4. The symptoms are broadly *similar to* what we saw last winter.
5. This one is *similar to* mine but a generation older.
6. The two proposals are *similar to* each other in everything but price.

**See also:** [different from](#different-from), [close to](#close-to), [relative to](#relative-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [similar to](09-comparison-opposition.md#similar-to), [like](07-agent-instrument-manner.md#like)

### subject to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tu/ &middot; weak /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə/ &middot; *SUB-jikt too* — **adjective stress on the first syllable**; the verb *subject* is /səbˈdʒɛkt/, *sub-JEKT*
**Type:** complex (adjective + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; sense 3 is the language of contracts and offers
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund.** The *to* is a preposition, so ***subject to be approved* is wrong** — write *subject to being approved*, or better, the nominal *subject to approval*. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** *be subject to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun phrase], *subject to* [noun phrase] &middot; *subject to change / approval / availability / audit* (fixed collocations)
**Contrast:** Three senses a reader must keep apart, because only the third makes the surrounding statement provisional: *The offer is final, subject to board approval* is **not** a final offer, and the whole force of the sentence sits in those three words. Sense 1 is liability, sense 2 is disposition, sense 3 is conditionality. Watch the stress and the part of speech — *subjected to* is the passive verb and means "made to undergo" (*subjected to questioning*), which is not this item. Against [*obedient to*](#obedient-to), which never fused, *subject to* has developed meanings the adjective *subject* no longer carries alone; against *pursuant to*, it names a condition rather than an authorizing rule.

**1. Liable to; exposed to (a charge, penalty, or requirement).** Late filings are *subject to* a fifty-dollar penalty.
**2. Prone to; repeatedly affected by.** The valley is *subject to* flash flooding.
**3. Conditional on; not final until (contract language).** The sale is *subject to* financing.

1. Late filings are *subject to* a fifty-dollar penalty.
2. All imports are *subject to* inspection at the port of entry.
3. Contractors are *subject to* the same conduct code as employees.
4. The valley is *subject to* flash flooding every spring.
5. He has been *subject to* migraines since his twenties.
6. Prices are *subject to* change without notice.
7. The sale is *subject to* financing and a clean inspection.
8. The appointment is *subject to* being confirmed by the full council.

**See also:** [obedient to](#obedient-to), [accustomed to](#accustomed-to), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [in violation of](#in-violation-of), [depending on](#depending-on)

### subsequent to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbsəkwənt tu/ &middot; weak /tə/ &middot; *SUB-suh-kwunt too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; bureaucratic and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a bare infinitive** — *subsequent to filing*, not *subsequent to file*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *subsequent to* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *Subsequent to* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** The mirror image of *prior to*, and subject to the same criticism: ***after* is shorter and clearer in nearly every sentence.** It is genuinely useful only in accounting and legal contexts where *subsequent events* is a term of art. Note that *subsequent* alone is an adjective and takes no complement.

Places an event later than another. Like *prior to*, its persistence owes more to the gravity of official registers than to any distinction it draws.

1. *Subsequent to* the merger, three regional offices were closed.
2. No changes were made *subsequent to* the final review.
3. Claims filed *subsequent to* the deadline will not be considered.
4. *Subsequent to* filing the report, the auditor discovered a second error.
5. The property was rezoned *subsequent to* the 2019 general plan update.

**See also:** [prior to](#prior-to), [in the wake of](#in-the-wake-of), [as of](#as-of), [following](12-marginal-participial.md#following)

### thanks to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθæŋks tu/ &middot; weak /ˈθæŋks tə/ &middot; *THANKSS too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*thanks to being warned early*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*thanks to what the neighbors did*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the workaround is *because*.
**Pattern:** *Thanks to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *thanks to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *no thanks to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** A causal preposition that normally credits a **welcome** cause, which is what separates it from neutral *because of*. Its ironic use — *Thanks to you, we missed the exit* — is extremely common and depends entirely on tone; the fixed *no thanks to* (*We finished on time, no thanks to him*) makes the blame explicit. Do not confuse with the plural noun in *give thanks to* or with the greeting *thanks to everyone who came*, where *thanks* is a noun and *to* marks its recipient — the test is whether the sentence has a separate outcome being explained.

Attributes an outcome to a person or factor, with an evaluative charge that plain *because of* lacks. Read the surrounding sentence to know whether the charge is gratitude or sarcasm.

**1. Because of (a welcome cause); with credit to.** *Thanks to* a last-minute donation, the shelter stayed open.
**2. Because of (an unwelcome cause) — ironic, and equally common.** *Thanks to* you, we missed the exit.
**3. *no thanks to* — despite the failure of (fixed).** We finished on time, no *thanks to* the detour signs.

1. *Thanks to* a last-minute donation, the shelter stayed open.
2. She finished the degree in three years *thanks to* summer courses.
3. The team is in the playoffs *thanks to* a kicker nobody had heard of in August.
4. *Thanks to* being warned a day early, we moved the equipment in time.
5. *Thanks to* you, we missed the exit and drove twenty miles the wrong way.
6. *Thanks to* one clause nobody read, the lease renews automatically.
7. We got there on time, no *thanks to* the detour signs.
8. The roof held, no *thanks to* the contractor who installed it.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [courtesy of](#courtesy-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of)

### up to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌp tu/ &middot; weak /ˈʌp tə/ &middot; *UP too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; senses 4 and 6 informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund in sense 5 (*up to running a marathon*), or a nominal *wh*-clause in sense 6 (*up to whoever signs off on it*). **Never a *that*-clause.** In sense 4 the complement is nearly always a vague or negative noun (*something*, *no good*, *what*). Note that sense 6 also licenses a following *to*-infinitive on a separate subject — *It's up to the board to decide* — which is a different slot, not this preposition's complement.
**Pattern:** *up to* [quantity] &middot; [verb of motion] *up to* [place] &middot; *be up to* [noun phrase] &middot; *It's up to* [person] *to* [verb] &middot; *be up to* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *Up* alone is an adverb or a particle (*Stand up*, *Look it up*); *up to* is the preposition and always has an object. Sense 1's "maximum" reading is a persistent source of advertising complaints: *save up to 50%* guarantees nothing, because it names a ceiling, not a floor. Sense 2 is the plain spatial one and is the sense the others are built out of — a limit reached and not passed. Sense 6, *it's up to you*, is one of the most frequent uses in speech and is invisible to learners who only know sense 1.

**1. As many or as much as; not exceeding (a ceiling).** The theater holds *up to* four hundred people.
**2. As far as; right up to (a point reached in space).** She walked *up to* the counter and asked for a refund.
**3. Until (a point in time).** Everything was fine *up to* the last week of rehearsals.
**4. Engaged in (usually something suspect).** What are those two *up to*?
**5. Capable of; equal to.** He isn't *up to* the job.
**6. The responsibility or choice of.** It's *up to* the board now.

1. The theater holds *up to* four hundred people.
2. Fines run *up to* five thousand dollars per violation.
3. She walked *up to* the counter and asked for a refund.
4. The water came *up to* my knees before I turned back.
5. Everything went smoothly *up to* the last week of rehearsals.
6. *Up to* that point, she had never missed a shift.
7. What are those two *up to* in the garage?
8. He's been *up to* no good since he got here.
9. She wasn't *up to* a five-mile hike two months after surgery.
10. Nobody thought the rookie would be *up to* the pressure.
11. It's *up to* the board now, not to me.
12. Whether we go at all is *up to* the weather.

## The *as ___* family

*As* leads a triple life — preposition (*as a doctor*), conjunction (*as I said*), and adverb of degree (*as tall as*) — and every phrase built on it inherits some of that instability. Three members, *as far as*, *as well as*, and *as to*, flip between preposition and conjunction depending on what follows them, and the flip is where the errors come from. The rest are rigidly frozen: *as regards* takes no *to*, *as of* takes nothing but a time expression, and *as per* is a redundancy that survives only because commercial writing is fond of it.

### as a result of

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə rɪˈzʌlt əv/ &middot; weak /əz ə rɪˈzʌlt əv/ &middot; *az uh rih-ZULT uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*as a result of being understaffed*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as a result of what the audit turned up*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the clause-taking form is *as a result of the fact that*, which is worth avoiding, or simply *because*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *as a result of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *As a result of* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** **The bare adverbial *as a result* is not this preposition.** With no complement it is a conjunctive adverb joining two sentences: *Sales fell. As a result, we cut hours.* The preposition requires an object. Against *because of*, this is heavier and frames the object as a cause whose consequences are being traced; against *due to*, it is adverbial and therefore never disputed.

Names the event or condition whose consequence the main clause reports. It is the causal preposition of reports and investigations, where the point is usually to establish a chain rather than simply assign a reason.

1. Two hundred jobs were lost *as a result of* the merger.
2. *As a result of* the audit, three policies were rewritten.
3. She missed a month of school *as a result of* the surgery.
4. Premiums rose sharply *as a result of* last year's claims.
5. *As a result of* being understaffed, the clinic cut its weekend hours.
6. The bridge was closed *as a result of* damage found during a routine inspection.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [in the wake of](#in-the-wake-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to)

### as far as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɑr æz/ &middot; weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *az FAR az*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **Three different grammars.** As a preposition of distance it takes a noun phrase naming a place or point (*as far as Denver*). As a topic marker it takes a noun phrase **that must be closed by *is concerned*, *goes*, or a similar verb** — *as far as the budget is concerned*. As a conjunction it takes a full finite clause (*as far as I know*). **It never takes a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *as far as* [place] &middot; *As far as* [noun phrase] *is concerned*, [clause]. &middot; *as far as* [clause] (conjunction)
**Contrast:** **The truncation trap.** In speech Americans routinely drop the completing verb — *As far as money, we're fine* — and editors routinely restore it. The phrase that needs no verb is *as for*: *As for money, we're fine* is correct as it stands. The two are one syllable apart and are constantly swapped. The distance sense is treated with the movement prepositions and the full topic formula with the topic prepositions; both are linked below.

**1. To the point of; up to (distance).** We drove *as far as* Santa Fe and turned around.
**2. To the extent or limit of (degree).** The offer goes *as far as* free replacement, but no further.
**3. On the subject of (topic; requires *is concerned* or *goes*).** *As far as* the schedule is concerned, nothing has changed.

1. We drove *as far as* Santa Fe and then turned around.
2. The trail runs *as far as* the second ridge.
3. Fires were reported *as far as* forty miles from the coast.
4. The company will go *as far as* a partial refund and no further.
5. *As far as* the schedule is concerned, nothing has changed.
6. *As far as* the money goes, we're covered through March.
7. *As far as* I know, the meeting is still on.

**See also:** [as for](#as-for), [as to](#as-to), [up to](#up-to), [short of](#short-of), [as far as](04-movement-direction.md#as-far-as), [as far as X is concerned](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-far-as-x-is-concerned)

### as for

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɔr/ &middot; weak /əz fər/ &middot; *az FOR*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*as for me*, never *as for I*), gerund (*as for finding a replacement*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as for what happens next*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** *As for* [noun phrase], [clause]. — overwhelmingly sentence-initial; the postposed *[clause] as for [noun phrase]* is unidiomatic.
**Contrast:** *As for* needs no completing verb, where *as far as* does: *As for the budget, we're fine* / *As far as the budget is concerned, we're fine*. Against *as to*, which introduces an uncertainty or a question, *as for* introduces a contrasting participant that has already been mentioned — it presupposes prior context and cannot open a conversation cold. Against *regarding*, it is conversational rather than bureaucratic.

Shifts the topic to one item already in play, usually to say something different about it from what was just said about the others. The characteristic rhythm is a list followed by an exception: everyone else did X; *as for* him, he did Y.

1. *As for* me, I'd rather stay home.
2. The others left at six. *As for* Dana, nobody knows.
3. *As for* the money, we'll figure that out later.
4. *As for* your second question, I don't have an answer yet.
5. *As for* finding a replacement, the search starts Monday.
6. He's furious. *As for* the rest of us, we're just tired.

**See also:** [as to](#as-to), [as far as](#as-far-as), [as regards](#as-regards), [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [as for](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-for)

### as of

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈʌv/ &middot; weak /əz əv/ &middot; *az UHV*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in business, legal, and news writing
**Complement:** **a time expression only** — a date, a clock time, or a temporal noun (*as of Monday*, *as of noon*, *as of today*, *as of this writing*). **Never a clause of any kind, never a gerund, and never a non-temporal noun**: *as of the merger* is wrong unless the merger is being treated as a dated event.
**Pattern:** *As of* [time], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *as of* [time].
**Contrast:** Two readings, and **the tense of the main verb decides which**. A present or future verb gives "starting from": *As of Monday, the rate goes up.* A past or perfect verb gives "at the point of, and up to": *As of Friday, no one had called.* Against plain *from*, *as of* fixes an official effective moment rather than a starting edge; against *by*, it names a snapshot rather than a deadline. British *as from* is rare in American English.

**1. Starting from; with effect from (a future or announced point).** *As of* January 1, the fee increases.
**2. At the moment of; up to and including (a snapshot of a state).** *As of* last night, forty units were still unsold.

1. *As of* January 1, the parking fee increases to twelve dollars.
2. The new policy takes effect *as of* Monday morning.
3. She is, *as of* this week, the longest-serving member of the board.
4. *As of* Friday, no one had returned the call.
5. *As of* last night, forty units were still unsold.
6. The balance *as of* the close of business was $4,318.
7. *As of* this writing, the vote count has not been certified.

**See also:** [prior to](#prior-to), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [up to](#up-to), [as of](05-time.md#as-of), [effective](05-time.md#effective), [from](02-core-relational.md#from)

### as opposed to

**Pronunciation:** /æz əˈpoʊzd tu/ &middot; weak /əz əˈpoʊzd tə/ &middot; *az uh-POHZD too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund (*as opposed to waiting*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as opposed to what most people assume*). **Never a *that*-clause** and — the *to* being a preposition — **never a bare infinitive**: *as opposed to wait* is wrong. The complement must be grammatically parallel to the item it corrects.
**Pattern:** [X] *as opposed to* [Y] &middot; [clause], *as opposed to* [noun phrase / verb-ing].
**Contrast:** It does not merely contrast, it **corrects** — it names the thing that is *not* the case, usually because a reader might have assumed it was. Against *instead of*, which reports a substitution that actually took place (*We took the train instead of driving*), *as opposed to* draws a definitional line (*a commuter train, as opposed to a freight line*). Against *rather than*, it is heavier and more explicitly clarifying; against *unlike*, it does not compare qualities.

Marks the alternative that is being ruled out. Because its job is to head off a misreading, it is at its most useful when the two options are genuinely confusable.

1. This is a recommendation, *as opposed to* an order.
2. She works in public health, *as opposed to* clinical medicine.
3. We're talking about the 1980 election, *as opposed to* the primary.
4. The grant funds research, *as opposed to* construction.
5. He chose to negotiate, *as opposed to* walking away.
6. The figure is annual, *as opposed to* monthly.

**See also:** [instead of](#instead-of), [rather than](#rather-than), [opposed to](#opposed-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [other than](#other-than)

### as per

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈpɜr/ &middot; weak /əz pər/ &middot; *az PUR*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** **commercial and bureaucratic; widely disliked in edited prose**
**Complement:** noun phrase only. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *as per* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As per* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** **A redundancy.** Latin *per* already means "according to," so the *as* adds nothing: *per your request* and *according to your request* both say what *as per your request* says, in fewer words. Usage guides across the board recommend *per*, *according to*, *in accordance with*, or *following*. The one form with a life of its own is the jocular fixed phrase *as per usual*, which nobody intends seriously.

Cites an instruction, agreement, or standard as the authority for what was done. It is boilerplate — the language of purchase orders and shipping notices — and it signals institutional register more than it carries meaning.

1. The goods were shipped *as per* your instructions.
2. Payment is due in thirty days *as per* the contract.
3. *As per* the attached schedule, the site closes at four.
4. Invoices will be processed *as per* company policy.
5. He was late again, *as per* usual.
6. The panels were installed *as per* the manufacturer's drawings.

**See also:** [according to](#according-to), [in accordance with](#in-accordance-with), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [further to](#further-to)

### as regards

**Pronunciation:** /æz rɪˈɡɑrdz/ &middot; weak /əz rɪˈɡɑrdz/ &middot; *az rih-GARDZ*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, somewhat old-fashioned
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as regards what was agreed*). **Never a *that*-clause.** And note what cannot follow it at all: ***as regards to* is an error** — the phrase takes no *to*.
**Pattern:** *As regards* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *as regards* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** ***With regard to* needs its *to*; *as regards* refuses one.** The reason is grammatical history: in *with regard to*, *regard* is a noun that requires the preposition *to*, while in *as regards* the *-s* is the third-person verb ending of an old impersonal clause ("as [it] regards"), and a verb takes its object directly. Learners who know one form contaminate the other, producing *as regards to* and *with regards to*, both wrong.

Introduces the topic of the statement. It is the most formal of the topic markers and reads as slightly archaic in American prose, where *regarding*, *on*, or *about* almost always fits better.

1. *As regards* the second contract, negotiations are ongoing.
2. The report is silent *as regards* enforcement.
3. *As regards* staffing, the department has made no decision.
4. Nothing has changed *as regards* eligibility.
5. *As regards* funding the expansion, the board remains divided.

### as to

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈtu/ &middot; weak /əz tə/ &middot; *az TOO*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; very heavy in legal writing
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as to whether the signature is genuine*, *as to what happened next*). **Never a *that*-clause** — *as to that he was late* is impossible.
**Pattern:** *As to* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [noun of uncertainty] *as to* [wh-clause] &middot; [clause] *as to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** Two jobs with different fates. Fronted, it is a topic-shifter and a stiffer twin of *as for*. Mid-sentence after a noun of uncertainty — *question*, *doubt*, *dispute*, *evidence*, *confusion* — it introduces the content of that uncertainty, and there it is very often deletable: *no question as to whether he signed* is simply *no question whether he signed*. Against *about*, it is the formal register's choice and is usually padding outside legal drafting.

Attaches a topic or a specification to what precedes it. Its survival in American English is almost entirely a legal phenomenon; in ordinary prose *about*, *on*, or *of* replaces it without loss.

**1. On the subject of (fronted topic-shifter).** *As to* the second charge, the defense concedes nothing.
**2. Introducing the content of a question, doubt, or dispute.** There is real doubt *as to* whether the signature is genuine.

1. *As to* the second charge, the defense concedes nothing.
2. *As to* your fee, we can discuss it after the hearing.
3. There is real doubt *as to* whether the signature is genuine.
4. The parties disagree *as to* the meaning of Section 4.
5. She gave no hint *as to* what she would decide.
6. The statute is silent *as to* remedies.
7. Opinions differ sharply *as to* how the money should be spent.

### as well as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈwɛl æz/ &middot; weak /əz ˈwɛl əz/ &middot; *az WELL az*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*as well as what the grant covers*). **Never a *that*-clause.** When the added item is a verb and the main clause already has a finite verb of its own, **the gerund is obligatory**: *She runs the department as well as teaching two sections*, not *as well as teach*. (The pattern *He writes as well as directs*, with two finite verbs sharing one subject, is *as well as* working as a coordinating conjunction, not as this preposition.)
**Pattern:** [subject], *as well as* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; [clause] *as well as* [noun phrase / verb-ing].
**Contrast:** **Two traps, both common.** First, **verb agreement**: *as well as* does not build a compound subject, so *The manager, as well as two clerks, **was** present* — singular, where *and* would give *were*. The same holds for *along with*, *together with*, and *in addition to*. Second, **it is not a synonym for *and***: it subordinates, presenting the material after it as the lesser or already-known item, so *He plays guitar as well as piano* foregrounds the guitar. Distinguish also the ordinary comparative *as well as* meaning "as skillfully as": *She sings as well as her sister* is a comparison of ability, not an addition.

Adds a second item while keeping it grammatically and rhetorically subordinate to the first. That subordination is the whole point, and it is why writers who mean plain coordination should use *and*.

1. The fee covers materials *as well as* labor.
2. The senator, *as well as* two aides, was subpoenaed.
3. She runs the department *as well as* teaching two sections a semester.
4. He speaks Korean *as well as* Japanese.
5. *As well as* the main gallery, there's a sculpture garden out back.
6. The storm knocked out power in three counties *as well as* half of the next state.
7. She sings *as well as* her sister does, which is saying something.

**See also:** [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [along with](#along-with), [together with](#together-with), [on top of](#on-top-of), [as well as](08-accompaniment-exception.md#as-well-as), [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides)

## Remaining fixed phrases

The leftovers of the class, grouped only by the fact that they fit no frame. Four of them — *along with* and *together with* here, *as well as* and *in addition to* above — share the single most consequential grammatical fact in this file: **they do not create compound subjects**, so the verb still agrees with the original subject alone. The rest are individually idiosyncratic. *But for* demands a counterfactual main clause. *Rather than* is the only member of the entire class that accepts a bare infinitive. *Such as* introduces examples where *like* introduces resemblances. *For fear of* is the cleanest surviving example of the noun-phrase-versus-clause split, since it keeps a separate conjunction, *for fear that*, for the clause. And *depending on* takes *wh*-clauses as freely as *regardless of* does, which almost nothing else in this file will do.

### along with

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ wɪθ/ &middot; also /əˈlɑŋ wɪð/ &middot; weak /əˈlɔŋ wəθ/ &middot; *uh-LAWNG with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund (*along with paying the fine*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*along with whatever else you need*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [subject], *along with* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; [clause] *along with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Along with* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** **Verb agreement trap**, identical to *as well as*, *together with*, and *in addition to*: *The coach, along with two players, **was** fined.* The parenthetical material never joins the subject. Against plain *with*, *along with* stresses that the two share the action or the fate rather than merely being present together. Bare *along* is a different word — an adverb (*Bring the kids along*) or a preposition of path (*a walk along the river*).

Adds a second participant to whatever the subject is doing or undergoing. It is the neutral, everyday member of the addition group, less formal than *together with* and less rhetorically loaded than *as well as*.

**1. In the company of; accompanying.** She arrived *along with* the rest of the delegation.
**2. In addition to; at the same time as.** The bill raises fees *along with* penalties.

1. She arrived *along with* the rest of the delegation.
2. Send the signed form *along with* two forms of ID.
3. The coach, *along with* two assistants, was placed on leave.
4. Three warehouses burned, *along with* everything inside them.
5. *Along with* paying the fine, he lost his license for a year.
6. Her name appeared on the list *along with* forty others.

**See also:** [together with](#together-with), [as well as](#as-well-as), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [along with](08-accompaniment-exception.md#along-with), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### at odds with

**Pronunciation:** /æt ˈɑdz wɪθ/ &middot; weak /ət ˈɑdz wəθ/ — *at* reduces to /ət/ &middot; *at ODZ with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, or a free relative (*at odds with what the data show*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** *be at odds with* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *at odds with* [noun phrase] &middot; *sharply/fundamentally/wildly at odds with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The negative counterpart of [*in keeping with*](#in-keeping-with) and [*in line with*](#in-line-with): those report that two things fit, this reports that they do not. It stops well short of [*in violation of*](#in-violation-of) — **being at odds with something alleges inconsistency, not breach** — which is why it is the safe word for a claim that clashes with the evidence rather than with a rule. With a person as the object it means an ongoing quarrel (*at odds with her own caucus*), and there both sides are animate and the relation is mutual. **Bare *at odds*, with no complement, is a predicate phrase and takes no object**: *The two of them have been at odds for years.* The *odds* here is invariable and has nothing to do with betting.

Reports that two things are inconsistent with each other. The subject and the complement are treated as equals, which is why the phrase can be reversed without changing what it claims.

1. His account is sharply *at odds with* the security footage.
2. The policy is *at odds with* everything the mayor campaigned on.
3. She has been *at odds with* her own caucus since January.
4. The findings are *at odds with* three earlier studies.
5. The new wing is wildly *at odds with* the rest of the block.
6. Their numbers are *at odds with* what the state reported for the same month.

**See also:** [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [in violation of](#in-violation-of), [at odds with](09-comparison-opposition.md#at-odds-with)

### but for

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt ˈfɔr/ &middot; weak /bət fər/ &middot; *but FOR*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause and never a *wh*-clause** — the clause equivalent is the conjunction *if it had not been for the fact that*, or simply *if ... had not*.
**Pattern:** *But for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would*, *would have*, or *could have*]. — the counterfactual verb is not optional.
**Contrast:** **It requires a conditional main clause.** *But for* means "if it had not been for," so the sentence must state what would otherwise have happened: *But for the delay, we would have made the connection.* A plain indicative after it (*But for the delay, we missed the connection*) is wrong. Against *except for*, which subtracts a real exception from a real situation, *but for* names a cause that did operate and asks the reader to imagine it away. The *but* here is the old preposition meaning "except," not the conjunction.

Introduces the one thing whose absence would have changed the outcome. Legal writing has made it a term of art: the *but-for* test asks whether the injury would have occurred without the defendant's act.

1. *But for* the seatbelt, she would not have survived.
2. *But for* a last-minute donation, the shelter would have closed.
3. *But for* his testimony, the case would never have gone to trial.
4. The building would be gone *but for* a single preservation lawsuit.
5. *But for* the weather, we would have finished the roof on Tuesday.
6. *But for* one dissenting vote, the measure would have passed unanimously.

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [save for](#save-for), [short of](#short-of), [in the absence of](#in-the-absence-of), [but for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#but-for), [but](08-accompaniment-exception.md#but)

### depending on

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ɑn/ &middot; casual /dɪˈpɛndɪn ən/ &middot; *dih-PEN-ding on*
**Type:** complex (participial + fixed preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or a nominal *wh*-clause — *depending on what you order*, *depending on whether it rains*, *depending on how far you drive*. **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause], *depending on* [noun phrase / wh-clause]. &middot; *Depending on* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** *depending upon* is the formal variant and means exactly the same thing.
**Contrast:** It has frozen completely: it needs no subject and cannot dangle, unlike the live participle in *Depending on his brother for rides, he never bought a car*, where *depending* still belongs to *he*. Against *according to*, which reports what a source says, *depending on* names a variable that determines the outcome. Its free acceptance of *wh*-clauses puts it in a very small group with *regardless of*, *irrespective of*, and *as to*.

Makes the main clause conditional on a factor that may vary. It is the standard way English states a range of outcomes without writing a conditional sentence.

1. The trip takes two or three hours, *depending on* traffic.
2. *Depending on* the weather, the ceremony will be inside or out.
3. Fees range from $40 to $120 *depending on* the size of the lot.
4. *Depending on* what the inspector finds, we may have to start over.
5. She'll fly or drive, *depending on* whether the fare drops.
6. Turnout could double or halve *depending on* how the case is decided.

**See also:** [according to](#according-to), [regardless of](#regardless-of), [in the event of](#in-the-event-of), [on the basis of](#on-the-basis-of), [considering](12-marginal-participial.md#considering)

### except for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt fɔr/ &middot; weak /ɪkˈsɛpt fər/ &middot; *ik-SEPT for*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun (*except for me*), gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*except for what he told the police*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the clause form is the conjunction *except that* (*The house is perfect except that it has no garage*).
**Pattern:** *Except for* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; [clause] *except for* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** ***Except for* versus bare *except***. Use *except for* when the exception is fronted (*Except for Tuesday, I'm free*) and when it subtracts from a whole rather than from a matching list item; use bare *except* when the exception is parallel to something explicit in the sentence (*Everyone came except Dave*). Against *apart from* and *aside from*, which can also mean "in addition to," *except for* **only subtracts** — which makes it the right choice whenever the exception reading must be unmistakable.

**1. Apart from; with the exception of.** *Except for* two typos, the manuscript is clean.
**2. If it were not for (a factor that prevented something).** *Except for* the rain, we would have finished the roof.

1. *Except for* two typos, the manuscript is clean.
2. The office is empty *except for* the night guard.
3. I like everything on the menu *except for* the oysters.
4. *Except for* Tuesday, I'm free all week.
5. *Except for* the rain, we would have finished the roof.
6. The team would be in first place *except for* a bad October.

**See also:** [with the exception of](#with-the-exception-of), [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [save for](#save-for), [but for](#but-for), [other than](#other-than), [except for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except-for)

### for fear of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈfɪr əv/ &middot; weak /fər ˈfɪr ə/ &middot; *for FEER uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or **gerund** (*for fear of waking the baby*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the clause-taking companion is the conjunction *for fear that* (*for fear that the baby would wake*), which is a different item, exactly as *in case* is to [*in case of*](#in-case-of). A free relative is fine, since it is a noun phrase (*for fear of what the neighbors would say*), and **never an infinitive**: ***for fear of to wake the baby* is wrong**.
**Pattern:** [negative or cautious clause] *for fear of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; [clause] *for fear that* [clause] — the conjunction, not this preposition.
**Variants:** *out of fear of* is the looser, more psychological variant. **Do not confuse it with *in fear of***, which means "afraid of" and names a state rather than a motive: *living in fear of deportation* is how someone feels, *he said nothing for fear of deportation* is why he acted.
**Contrast:** It gives the feared outcome as the reason something was **not** done, so the main clause is characteristically negative, hedged, or evasive — *said nothing*, *never reported it*, *kept the door locked*. Against *because of*, the feared thing need never have happened at all, which is exactly the difference between a cause and a motive. Against *to avoid*, which takes the same gerund and names the same purpose, *for fear of* adds the emotion and concedes the timidity. Its structural neighbor is [*for lack of*](#for-lack-of), which blames an absence where this one blames an anticipation.

Names the outcome whose prospect stopped the subject from acting. The phrase reports a motive rather than a cause, and the motive is always avoidance.

1. She said nothing *for fear of* making it worse.
2. Nobody reported the leak *for fear of* losing the contract.
3. Witnesses would not come forward *for fear of* retaliation.
4. They moved the horses inside *for fear of* the storm.
5. He avoided the subject entirely *for fear of* another argument.
6. The family kept the diagnosis quiet *for fear of* what the neighbors would say.

**See also:** [for lack of](#for-lack-of), [because of](#because-of), [in case of](#in-case-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [for fear of](06-cause-purpose.md#for-fear-of)

### for lack of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈlæk əv/ &middot; weak /fər ˈlæ kə/ &middot; *for LAK uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, characteristically a bare abstract or mass noun (*for lack of evidence*, *for lack of funding*). **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for lack of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *for lack of anything better* &middot; *for lack of a better* [noun]
**Variants:** ***For want of*** is the older and more literary twin — *For want of a nail the shoe was lost* — and is somewhat commoner in British English; *for lack of* is the ordinary American form. Both take the same complement, and neither puts an article on the noun unless the noun demands one.
**Contrast:** It states a cause that consists in an absence, which is exactly what separates it from *because of*: *dismissed because of the evidence* and *dismissed for lack of evidence* say opposite things. Against [*in the absence of*](#in-the-absence-of), which supplies a condition and looks forward (*in the absence of an agreement, state law governs*), *for lack of* supplies a reason and looks back at something that has already failed. The apologetic formula *for lack of a better word* is fixed and cannot be varied.

Gives the missing thing as the reason for the outcome, and the outcome is nearly always a failure, a refusal, or a collapse. Because the phrase blames an absence, it cannot take an object that was present.

1. The case was dismissed *for lack of* evidence.
2. The project died *for lack of* funding.
3. *For lack of* a better word, the meeting was a disaster.
4. Two positions went unfilled *for lack of* qualified applicants.
5. The garden failed *for lack of* water, not for lack of attention.

**See also:** [in the absence of](#in-the-absence-of), [because of](#because-of), [short of](#short-of), [devoid of](#devoid-of), [for lack of](06-cause-purpose.md#for-lack-of), [for want of](06-cause-purpose.md#for-want-of)

### for the purpose of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ðə ˈpɜrpəs əv/ &middot; weak /fər ðə ˈpɜrpə sə/ &middot; *for thuh PUR-puss uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, regulatory, and technical
**Complement:** **noun phrase or gerund only** (*for the purpose of raising revenue*). Because *of* is a preposition, ***for the purpose of to raise revenue* is wrong**, and so is ***for the purpose to raise revenue***. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [clause] *for the purpose of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *For the purposes of* [this section], [definition].
**Variants:** The plural *for the purposes of* is the standard opening of a definition clause — *For the purposes of this Act, "employee" means…* — and is not interchangeable with the singular, which states an aim rather than a scope.
**Contrast:** Against the plain infinitive of purpose, which says the same thing in two words, this is nearly always padding: *for the purpose of reducing costs* is *to reduce costs*. Keep it where the purpose has to appear as a formal element — tax law, permits, definitions — and cut it everywhere else. Against [*with a view to*](#with-a-view-to), which takes the same gerund but implies the goal has not been reached, *for the purpose of* simply names the aim; against [*for the sake of*](#for-the-sake-of), which names a beneficiary or a value, this names an objective.

States the aim an action was designed to serve. Its survival is entirely institutional: it marks the purpose as officially declared rather than merely intended.

1. The land was acquired *for the purpose of* building a transfer station.
2. The account exists *for the purpose of* holding client funds and nothing else.
3. *For the purposes of* this section, "resident" means a person domiciled in the state.
4. Records may be retained *for the purpose of* audit.
5. He formed the company *for the purpose of* limiting his personal liability.

**See also:** [with a view to](#with-a-view-to), [for the sake of](#for-the-sake-of), [in the interest of](#in-the-interest-of), [for the purpose of](06-cause-purpose.md#for-the-purpose-of), [in order to](06-cause-purpose.md#in-order-to)

### for the sake of

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ðə ˈseɪk əv/ &middot; weak /fər ðə ˈseɪk əv/ &middot; casual /fər ðə ˈseɪ kə/ &middot; *for thuh SAYK uhv*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*for the sake of keeping the peace*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a free relative is possible but uncommon (*for the sake of whoever comes after us*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *for the sake of* [noun phrase / verb-ing]. &middot; *For the sake of* [noun phrase], [clause]. &middot; *for* [possessive] *sake* (fixed: *for heaven's sake*)
**Variants:** *for the sake of argument* and the possessive pattern *for argument's sake* are both current; the possessive drops both *the* and *of*.
**Contrast:** The two senses below are told apart by whether the object is animate. Against *on behalf of*, which means "as the agent of," this means "for the benefit of," and confusing them makes a speaker claim authority they do not have. The possessive forms — *for heaven's sake*, *for old times' sake*, *for goodness' sake* — are a separate frozen construction and take no *of*.

Names the person served or the end pursued. The purpose reading is the commoner one in writing, where it introduces a stylistic or argumentative goal rather than a beneficiary.

**1. For the benefit of (a person or group).** They stayed together *for the sake of* the children.
**2. In order to achieve or preserve (a purpose or quality).** *For the sake of* clarity, let me restate the question.

1. They stayed together *for the sake of* the children.
2. She took the fall *for the sake of* the team.
3. He gave up the appeal *for the sake of* his mother, who could not face another hearing.
4. *For the sake of* clarity, let me restate the question.
5. He conceded the point *for the sake of* argument.
6. *For the sake of* keeping the peace, nobody mentioned the will.
7. Don't argue *for the sake of* arguing.

### further to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɜrðər tu/ &middot; weak /ˈfɜrðər tə/ &middot; *FUR-thur too*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** **formal correspondence; markedly more British than American**
**Complement:** noun phrase only — a document, a message, or a prior exchange. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause, never a gerund**, and never a bare infinitive.
**Pattern:** *Further to* [noun phrase], [clause]. — almost always the opening words of a letter or an email.
**Contrast:** A letter-opening formula meaning "following up on." In American business writing it reads as imported; *following up on*, *regarding*, or plain *about* is the native equivalent. Against *with reference to*, which points at a document as a source, *further to* points at a prior exchange and promises to continue it. Do not confuse with the comparative adverb *further* (*We went further than planned*), which takes no object.

Links the present message to an earlier one. It is nearly pure register: it carries no meaning that the reader would miss if the phrase were cut.

1. *Further to* your email of March 3, the position remains open.
2. *Further to* our conversation, I've attached the revised figures.
3. *Further to* the committee's request, the data is enclosed.
4. *Further to* my previous letter, the deadline has been extended.
5. *Further to* the notice of April 12, no further action is required.

### other than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðər ðæn/ &middot; weak /ˈʌðər ðən/ &middot; *UH-thur than*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** unusually wide — a noun phrase, an objective-case pronoun (*other than her*), a gerund, an adverb (*other than briefly*), a whole prepositional phrase (*other than by mail*), or a nominal *wh*-clause (*nothing other than what he said*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [negative or universal clause] *other than* [complement] &middot; *Other than* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** It is most idiomatic **after a negative or a universal** — *nothing other than*, *anyone other than*, *no way other than*. After a plain positive statement it sounds stiff. Against *except for*, it is more formal and far more willing to take adverbial complements: *other than by mail* is fine, *except for by mail* is not. Against *besides*, which can add a second item, *other than* only subtracts. **Neither half is this word on its own**: *other* is a determiner or adjective (*the other door*), and *than* by itself is the comparative — a conjunction before a clause (*taller than I am*) and a preposition before a pronoun (*taller than me*). And it is *other than*, never *other then*.

**1. Apart from; besides.** We have no evidence *other than* his statement.
**2. In any way but; differently from.** You cannot reach the island *other than* by ferry.

1. We have no evidence *other than* his own statement.
2. *Other than* a sore shoulder, he came through it fine.
3. Nobody *other than* the two of us has the code.
4. She has never lived anywhere *other than* Ohio.
5. You cannot reach the island *other than* by ferry.
6. He has never spoken about it *other than* briefly, and never on the record.

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [rather than](#rather-than), [other than](08-accompaniment-exception.md#other-than), [besides](08-accompaniment-exception.md#besides)

### over and above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvər ənd əˈbʌv/ &middot; casual /ˌoʊvər ən əˈbʌv/ &middot; *OH-vur und uh-BUV*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*over and above meeting the minimum*). **Never a *that*-clause**; a free relative is fine (*over and above what the contract requires*).
**Pattern:** [clause] *over and above* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Over and above* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Contrast:** A four-word [*in addition to*](#in-addition-to) with one extra claim: that what came before was already enough. *Over and above her salary* presents the salary as the expected part and the rest as surplus, where *in addition to her salary* is neutral about sufficiency. Against *besides*, it is heavier and more emphatic. **Neither half carries the meaning alone** — *over* and *above* are ordinary spatial prepositions elsewhere, and the frozen pair is not spatial at all; nor can the two words be separated. The similar-sounding *above and beyond* is a different fixed phrase and describes effort rather than amount.

Adds something to a total that was already complete. The baseline has to be recoverable from the sentence, or the phrase has nothing to measure the surplus against.

1. She receives a housing allowance *over and above* her salary.
2. The fee is charged *over and above* the standard shipping rate.
3. *Over and above* the fine, he lost his license for a year.
4. They asked for two more inspectors *over and above* what the budget allowed.
5. The volunteers put in three hundred hours *over and above* running the shelter itself.

**See also:** [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [as well as](#as-well-as), [on top of](#on-top-of), [together with](#together-with), [over and above](08-accompaniment-exception.md#over-and-above)

### rather than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðər ðæn/ &middot; weak /ˈræðər ðən/ &middot; *RA-thur than*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** **the freest complement in this file.** A noun phrase (*rather than the bus*), a gerund (*rather than complaining*), **a bare infinitive** (*rather than complain*), an adjective (*structural rather than personal*), an adverb, a prepositional phrase, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*rather than what was proposed*). **Never a *that*-clause.** After a *to*-infinitive it takes a bare infinitive: *He chose to walk rather than wait.*
**Pattern:** [X] *rather than* [Y] — X and Y parallel &middot; *Rather than* [Y], [clause]. &middot; [subject], *rather than* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb]
**Contrast:** **The bare-infinitive privilege is what separates it from *instead of***, which must nominalize: *rather than complain* is correct, *instead of complain* is not. Verb agreement follows the *as well as* rule: *The manager, rather than the clerks, **is** responsible.* Note also that *rather than* is often a conjunction rather than a preposition — the test is what it joins: parallel phrases make it a preposition, full clauses make it a conjunction.

**1. In preference to; instead of.** We took the train *rather than* the bus.
**2. And not; as opposed to (correcting a possible reading).** The problem is structural *rather than* personal.

1. We took the train *rather than* the bus.
2. *Rather than* complain, she rewrote the whole section herself.
3. He chose to negotiate *rather than* walk away.
4. The problem is structural *rather than* personal.
5. The change was gradual *rather than* sudden.
6. They fund research *rather than* construction.
7. The board, *rather than* the members, decides.
8. *Rather than* raising taxes, the city cut three programs.

### save for

**Pronunciation:** /seɪv ˈfɔr/ &middot; weak /seɪv fər/ &middot; *sayv FOR*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal and literary
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*save for what little was left*). **Never a *that*-clause** — the clause-taking form is the conjunction *save that*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *save for* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Save for* [noun phrase], [clause].
**Variants:** bare *save* (*The room was empty save one chair*) is older and even more literary; *saving* is archaic; *save that* is the clause-taking conjunction.
**Contrast:** A consciously elevated *except for*. In American prose it reads as a deliberate stylistic choice rather than a neutral one, and using it in ordinary business writing draws attention to itself. Against *but for*, which is counterfactual and demands a conditional main clause, *save for* simply subtracts from a real situation. Do not confuse the preposition with the verb *save*; the preposition never has a subject of its own.

Removes one item from an otherwise complete picture. Its natural home is descriptive narrative, where the exception is meant to stand out against an emptiness or a uniformity.

1. The street was silent *save for* a dog barking two blocks over.
2. The house was untouched *save for* a cracked window at the back.
3. *Save for* one abstention, the vote was unanimous.
4. He remembered nothing of the evening *save for* the drive home.
5. Every ship in the harbor was lost, *save for* the two in dry dock.
6. *Save for* an hour on Sunday, she worked the entire week.

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [but for](#but-for), [with the exception of](#with-the-exception-of), [apart from](#apart-from), [save for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#save-for), [save](08-accompaniment-exception.md#save)

### such as

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ ˈæz/ &middot; weak /sətʃ əz/ &middot; *such AZ*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase (usually a list) or gerund. **Never a *that*-clause, never a *wh*-clause.** The complement must be a genuine member of the class named before it — *fruits such as apples*, not *fruits such as sweetness*.
**Pattern:** [general noun] *such as* [example], [example] &middot; [clause], *such as* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** ***Such as* introduces examples; *like* introduces resemblances.** The traditional rule reserves *like* for comparison and *such as* for exemplification: *a writer like Hemingway* means someone who resembles him, *a writer such as Hemingway* means him, offered as an instance. Speech ignores the distinction; edited prose still keeps it. Note the related split in the same pair: **as a preposition *like* takes a noun phrase (*He works like a machine*), while *as* introduces a role (*He works as a machine operator*) and, as a conjunction, a full clause (*Do as I say*)** — *like I say* is spoken English that edited prose still changes to *as I say*. Two mechanical points on *such as*: no comma before it when the examples restrict the class (*fruits such as apples and pears*), a comma when they merely illustrate; and **do not end the list with *etc.***, since *such as* already announces that the list is partial. **Neither half stands alone**: bare *such* is a determiner (*such a mess*) and bare *as* is a different preposition or a conjunction; only the pair is this item.

Offers one or more members of a class as illustrations of it. The class must be stated first, which is why *such as* cannot open a sentence the way *like* sometimes can.

1. Root vegetables *such as* carrots and parsnips keep for months.
2. Heavy industries *such as* steel and shipbuilding left the region entirely.
3. She avoids stimulants *such as* coffee and black tea.
4. The grant covers direct costs *such as* travel, equipment, and printing.
5. Small tasks *such as* returning calls pile up fast.
6. Cities *such as* Tucson and Phoenix face the same water problem.

**See also:** [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in the way of](10-topic-reference-role.md#in-the-way-of), [including](08-accompaniment-exception.md#including), [as](09-comparison-opposition.md#as), [like](09-comparison-opposition.md#like)

### together with

**Pronunciation:** /təˈɡɛðər wɪθ/ &middot; also /təˈɡɛðər wɪð/ &middot; weak /təˈɡɛðər wəθ/ &middot; *tuh-GETH-ur with*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; common in drafting and in formal reports
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a nominal *wh*-clause (*together with whatever documents you have*). **Never a *that*-clause.**
**Pattern:** [subject], *together with* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; [clause], *together with* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** **Verb agreement trap**, the same one as *along with*, *as well as*, and *in addition to*: the added item is parenthetical, so the verb agrees with the original subject alone — *The report, together with the appendices, **was** filed Monday.* Against *along with*, it is the more formal of the two and the one that appears in contracts and filings; against *and*, it deliberately refuses to build a compound subject. Bare *together* is an adverb and takes no object: *We went together.*

Attaches supplementary material to a subject without merging the two. Its usefulness in drafting comes precisely from that refusal to merge: the primary item stays primary.

**1. In the company of; accompanied by.** *Together with* her brother, she inherited the farm.
**2. In addition to (adding an item without joining it to the subject).** The application, *together with* the filing fee, must reach the office by Friday.

1. The application, *together with* the filing fee, must reach the office by Friday.
2. The deed was recorded *together with* two easements.
3. *Together with* her brother, she inherited the farm.
4. The report, *together with* all supporting exhibits, was filed Monday.
5. Send the transcript *together with* three letters of recommendation.
6. The principal, *together with* accrued interest, comes to just over $9,000.
7. She arrived *together with* two lawyers and said almost nothing.

### up against

**Pronunciation:** /ʌp əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; also /ʌp əˈɡeɪnst/ &middot; weak /əp əˈɡɛn(t)s/ — *up* reduces to /əp/ and the final *t* routinely drops &middot; *up uh-GENST*
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; **never a gerund and never a *that*-clause**. A free relative is idiomatic in sense 2 (*up against whatever the league can put on the field*). In the fixed idiom *up against it* the complement is the invariable pronoun *it* and nothing else may replace it.
**Pattern:** *be up against* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *up against* [noun phrase] &middot; *be up against it* (fixed)
**Contrast:** Sense 1 is not really a unit at all — it is the adverb *up* plus the ordinary preposition *against*, and you can drop the *up* (*the ladder against the wall*). Sense 2 is the frozen idiom, and there the *up* cannot go: *We're up against a deadline* is not *We're against a deadline*. Against *facing*, sense 2 always implies difficulty and usually implies an unfair contest.

**1. Leaning on and touching (literal).** He propped the ladder *up against* the gutter.
**2. Confronted by, as an obstacle or an opponent.** We're *up against* a hard deadline and a short staff.
**3. *up against it* — in serious trouble, usually financial (fixed).** They've been *up against it* since the mill closed.

1. He propped the ladder *up against* the gutter.
2. She pushed the desk *up against* the far wall.
3. We're *up against* a hard deadline and two people short.
4. The defense is *up against* the best offense in the league.
5. Nobody realized what the small growers were *up against*.
6. You're *up against* twenty years of institutional habit.
7. The family has been *up against it* since the mill closed.
