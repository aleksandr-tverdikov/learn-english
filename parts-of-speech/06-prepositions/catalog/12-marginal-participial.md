# Marginal, participial, and postpositive prepositions

> The words that are prepositions only by the job they do — verbs, adjectives, and adverbs drafted into prepositional service, plus the handful that follow their object instead of preceding it.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Nothing in this file started life as a preposition. These are verbs (*considering*, *given*, *pending*), adjectives (*absent*, *effective*, *worth*), and adverbs (*ago*, *aside*, *hence*) that have been recruited into prepositional service, and each keeps just enough of its old behavior to trip you up. Two tests separate the preposition from the participle it came from: a true preposition needs no subject and cannot dangle (*Considering the weather, the turnout was good* is fine, while *Walking to work, the rain started* is not), and its object is a plain noun phrase that a pronoun replaces in the objective case (*including me*, never *including I*). The postpositive group at the end breaks the one rule everyone thinks prepositions have — the object comes first, and the order is fixed rather than stylistic: *three weeks ago*, *the objections notwithstanding*, *present company excepted*. One trap runs through the whole file: almost none of these words accepts a finite clause (*✗ regarding he was late*, *✗ following she resigned*), and the four that genuinely do — *considering that*, *given that*, *granted that*, and legal *notwithstanding that* — are exactly the ones careful writers avoid out of misplaced caution.

**The complement rule that governs the whole class.** A preposition takes a noun phrase, and when a verb has to fill that slot the verb becomes a **gerund**, never an infinitive. This is true here (*worth seeing*, ✗ *worth to see*; *aside from being late*, ✗ *aside from to be late*), and it is true of every other *to* in English that is a preposition rather than an infinitive marker: *look forward to hearing* (✗ *look forward to hear*), *object to being told* (✗ *object to be told*), *accustomed to working nights* (✗ *accustomed to work nights*), *used to getting up early*, *opposed to raising rates*, *committed to hiring locally*, *devoted to restoring the house*, *in addition to paying the fine*, *prior to leaving*, *with a view to selling*. The test is substitution — if a plain noun fits after the word (*look forward to the weekend*, *accustomed to the noise*, *opposed to the plan*), the *to* is a preposition and the verb after it must end in *-ing*. Two more restrictions travel with the class and are worth stating once: the concessives and causals take a noun phrase or a gerund and **never** a *that*-clause (*despite the rain*, *despite being late*, ✗ *despite it rained*, ✗ *despite that it rained*; *in spite of the delay*, *in spite of arriving late*, ✗ *in spite of that it was delayed*; *owing to the strike*, *owing to being short-staffed*, ✗ *owing to that they struck*; *due to fog*, *due to fog closing the field*, ✗ *due to that it was foggy*) — for a clause you must switch words entirely, to *although*, *even though*, or *because*. And a pronoun object is always objective (*including me*, *compared to her*, ✗ *between you and I*).

**Reduced pronunciations.** Two things weaken in this file, and neither shows in the spelling. First, every *-ing* ending drops to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (*considerin'*, *includin'*, *dependin'*). Second — the part learners never hear — the small word welded onto the end of a two-word member reduces to a schwa exactly as it does everywhere else in English: *of* /əv/ → /ə/, *to* → /tə/ (*owing to* /ˈoʊɪn tə/, *compared to* /kəmˈpɛrd tə/), *for* → /fɚ/ (*allowing for* /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/), *at* → /ət/, *from* → /frəm/ (*aside from* /əˈsaɪd frəm/), *on* → /ən/ (*based on* /beɪst ən/, *depending on* /dəˈpɛndɪn ən/), *by* → /bə/ (*judging by* /ˈdʒʌdʒɪn bə/), and *with* /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/, which is what you hear buried inside *notwithstanding* /ˌnɑtwəθˈstændɪn/. The strong form is what you say in isolation or under stress; the weak form is what you say in a sentence, and a learner who pronounces every one of these at full value sounds wrong in a way that is hard to place.

**Near-neighbor rules that touch this file.** Several confusable pairs cataloged elsewhere in this book decide sentences built on words from this file, so the rules are worth having in one place. *By* names a deadline for one completed act (*file it by Friday*), *until* names how long a state lasts (*we're closed until Friday*), and *pending* names the event instead of the date (*closed pending inspection*). At the front of a sentence American English wants *except for*, not bare *except* (*Except for the ending, it's great*), while after a noun either works (*everyone except him*). *In* names a location or a period, *within* an upper bound — *in an hour* can mean after an hour has passed, *within an hour* cannot. *Between* takes two parties, or several considered pairwise; *among* takes an undifferentiated mass. *Beside* means alongside, *besides* means in addition to. *Like* takes a noun phrase and *as* takes a clause (*she works like a machine*, *she works as I do*). *Different from* is the safe American form, *different than* is tolerated before a clause, and *different to* is British. *In* is static where *into* is directional, exactly as *on* is static where *onto* is directional. And *toward* is the American default against British *towards* — the same split that gives *amid/amidst*, *among/amongst*, *forward/forwards*, and American *around* against British *round*.

**American forms in this file.** Where the two standards split, this book gives the American form first and labels the other as British — never the reverse. In this file that means *aside* and *aside from* are the American choices and *apart* and *apart from* the British-leaning ones; *Monday through Friday* is the American inclusive range and has no British equivalent shorter than *Monday to Friday inclusive*; *bar* ("bar a miracle") is British and survives in American English only in *bar none*; and *thru* is an American spelling but a signage-only one. One pair is often mislabeled and is worth correcting here: *long since* is **not** British. It is ordinary formal American English, and it differs from *long ago* by tense rather than by nationality — *long since* pairs with the perfect (*the mill has long since closed*), *long ago* with the simple past (*the mill closed long ago*). The same American-first rule governs the pairs catalogued elsewhere: *toward*, *amid*, *among*, and *forward* are the American defaults, and *towards*, *amidst*, *amongst*, and *forwards* are the British forms; *round* is British where American English uses *around* (*all year round* is the one American holdout).

## Participial prepositions in *-ing*

The core of the group: present participles that lost their subject. Every one of them reduces its ending to /ɪn/ in casual American speech (*considerin'*, *includin'*), so the written *-ing* is rarely what you hear. None of them agrees with anything, and none of them needs a subject. Only *considering* takes a *that*-clause freely; *notwithstanding that* is confined to legal drafting; every other word in this section rejects a finite clause outright, which is the single trap to watch. Most of them do take a gerund, and that is the repair when a clause tempts you: not ✗ *following she resigned* but *following her resignation*, not ✗ *owing to that costs rose* but *owing to rising costs*. The topic members (*concerning*, *regarding*, *pertaining to*, *depending on*, *judging by*) additionally accept a *wh*-nominal — *regarding what you asked*, *depending on whether it rains* — which is a clause in shape but a noun phrase in job, and is the one clause-like thing they allow.

### allowing for

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlaʊɪŋ fɔr/ &middot; weak /əˈlaʊɪn fɚ/ &middot; *uh-LOW-ing fer*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *allowing for the traffic was heavy* → *allowing for traffic*, or *allowing for the fact that traffic was heavy*). The *for* is part of the word: dropping it gives you the plain verb, and *allowing the traffic* means something else entirely.
**Pattern:** *Allowing for* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *allowing for* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** Three words adjust a figure and they are not interchangeable. *Allowing for* X builds X into the estimate and keeps it in view; *excluding* X takes X out of the arithmetic; *discounting* X dismisses X as not worth counting. *Barring* is different again — it names a future spoiler rather than a known factor. Against *given*, which accepts a premise whole, *allowing for* concedes only a partial adjustment.

Makes room in a claim for a factor the speaker already knows about. It is the honest hedge of estimates, budgets, and forecasts.

**1. Adjusting a figure for a known factor.** The quantitative use.
**2. Making allowance for a person's circumstances.** The excusing use, close to *considering*.

1. *Allowing for* inflation, the raise is closer to two percent.
2. *Allowing for* traffic, the drive takes an hour and a half.
3. *Allowing for* rounding, the two totals match.
4. The schedule holds, *allowing for* one weather day.
5. Even *allowing for* his inexperience, the mistake was serious.
6. *Allowing for* being new to the system, she has picked it up fast.

**See also:** [discounting](#discounting), [excluding](#excluding), [depending on](#depending-on), [considering](#considering), [given](#given)

### barring

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɑrɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈbɑrɪn/ &middot; *BAR-ing* (not *BAIR-ing*, which is *bearing*)
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral, faintly bookish
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *barring that the weather holds*). Pronoun objects take the objective case: *barring him*.
**Pattern:** *Barring* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *barring* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *Barring* is hypothetical and forward-looking — it names the one thing that could still spoil a prediction. *Excepting* and *excluding* subtract something that actually exists. The bare form *bar* (*bar a miracle*) is chiefly British; American English keeps *bar* only in the fixed phrase *bar none*.
**Variants:** [*bar*](08-accompaniment-exception.md#bar) (British; in American English only in *bar none*)

Frozen from the verb *bar*, "to block." It sets one obstacle in front of an otherwise confident claim and is overwhelmingly a sentence-opener.

**1. Unless this one thing intervenes.** The default use, always about the future.
**2. With the exception of.** A weaker, static use, close to *apart from*.

1. *Barring* a last-minute appeal, the sale closes Friday.
2. *Barring* bad weather, we should be in Denver by six.
3. The team makes the playoffs *barring* another injury at quarterback.
4. *Barring* a few typos, the manuscript is clean.
5. *Barring* the second chapter, I liked the book.
6. She has the votes, *barring* something nobody has thought of yet.
7. *Barring* being called in to cover a shift, I'm free all weekend.

**See also:** [excepting](#excepting), [excluding](#excluding), [failing](#failing), [pending](#pending), [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent)

### bating

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbeɪtɪŋ/ &middot; flapped /ˈbeɪɾɪŋ/ &middot; *BAY-ting* (a homophone of *baiting*)
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** archaic; alive only in legal and antiquarian prose
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause in modern use. Do not write *bating that*.
**Pattern:** *bating* [noun phrase] &middot; *Bating* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** A dead synonym of *excepting* and *barring*. Clipped from *abating*, from the verb *abate*, "to subtract." Do not confuse it with *baiting* (luring) or with *bated* in *with bated breath*, which is the same root in a different sense — "restrained."

Subtracts an item from a total or a claim. Any modern sentence that uses it is either quoting an old document or making a joke about one; *except for* is the living equivalent.

1. *Bating* a few errors of arithmetic, the accounts were in order.
2. The whole estate passed to the widow, *bating* the mill.
3. *Bating* his temper, he was an excellent tenant.
4. Every witness agreed, *bating* the clerk.
5. She was, *bating* one bad season, the best pitcher in the league.

**See also:** [excepting](#excepting), [saving](#saving), [barring](#barring), [wanting](#wanting)

### beginning

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈɡɪnɪŋ/ &middot; weak /bəˈɡɪnɪn/ &middot; *bih-GIN-ing*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the standard word in notices and official announcements
**Complement:** a time expression only — a date, a day, or a prepositional phrase of time (*beginning in July*, *beginning at noon*). Never a finite clause, and never an ordinary object (✗ *beginning the project* in this sense, which is the plain verb).
**Pattern:** *Beginning* [date], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *beginning* [date].
**Contrast:** *Starting* is the everyday equivalent and is what Americans say out loud; *beginning* is the version that gets printed on the sign. *As of* can look backward as well as forward (*as of Tuesday, we had no answer*); *beginning* only looks forward. *From* needs a matching endpoint or an *on* to feel complete.

A pure scheduling word. It fixes the first day of a new arrangement and says nothing about when the arrangement ends.

1. *Beginning* Monday, the clinic opens at seven.
2. Rates increase *beginning* January 1.
3. *Beginning* in the fall, all freshmen live on campus.
4. The library will close at six *beginning* next week.
5. *Beginning* with the third episode, the show finds its footing.

**See also:** [starting](#starting), [effective](05-time.md#effective), [following](#following), [pending](#pending), [as of](05-time.md#as-of)

### concerning

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ &middot; casual /kənˈsɜrnɪn/ &middot; *kun-SUR-ning*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or *wh*-nominal (*concerning what happened*) — never a finite *that*-clause. To take a clause you need *concerning the fact that*, which is worse than starting over.
**Pattern:** *concerning* [noun phrase] &middot; *Concerning* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Concerning*, *regarding*, *respecting*, *touching*, and *re* all mean "about," but only the first two are still in ordinary use, and *concerning* is the heavier of those two — it belongs to correspondence and reports. In speech, plain *about* is almost always the better word. Note that the adjective *concerning* — "a concerning trend," meaning worrying — is a separate, much newer American usage and is not this word.

The formal topic preposition: it names what a document, question, or complaint is about.

1. I have a question *concerning* the deadline.
2. The memo says nothing *concerning* severance.
3. *Concerning* your invoice, we never received it.
4. She wrote to the board *concerning* the audit.
5. Rumors *concerning* the merger have been circulating for weeks.
6. There is a statute *concerning* exactly this situation.

**See also:** [regarding](#regarding), [respecting](#respecting), [touching](#touching), [pertaining to](#pertaining-to), [re](10-topic-reference-role.md#re), [with regard to](11-complex-multiword.md#with-regard-to)

### considering

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɪdərɪŋ/, syncopated /kənˈsɪdrɪŋ/ &middot; casual /kənˈsɪdrɪn/ &middot; *kun-SID-uh-ring*
**Type:** marginal/participial; also a subordinating conjunction with *that*, and an absolute adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, **or a finite clause with or without *that*** — *considering (that) she had never flown before*. It is the one word in this file that freely takes a clause in ordinary American English. It can also stand alone with no complement at all: *She did well, considering.*
**Pattern:** *Considering* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Considering (that)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *considering*.
**Contrast:** Against [given](#given): both accept a fact and reason from it, but *given* is clipped and treats the fact as a settled premise, while *considering* implies weighing and leans toward excusing what follows. Only *considering* can trail a sentence alone (✗ *She did well, given*). Do not confuse it with *concerning* — *considering the evidence* means in view of it, *concerning the evidence* means about it.

The workhorse of the group, and the only one that has kept a foot in three word classes at once.

**1. In view of; taking into account.** With a noun phrase.
**2. Seeing that.** With a full clause, functioning as a conjunction.
**3. All things considered.** Standing alone at the end of a sentence, as an adverb.

1. *Considering* her age, she recovered remarkably fast.
2. The price is fair *considering* the condition of the roof.
3. *Considering* the hour, the crowd was enormous.
4. *Considering* that he had two hours' notice, the speech was excellent.
5. *Considering* you've never done this before, that's impressive.
6. *Considering* having to learn the software from scratch, she was quick.
7. The turnout was good, *considering*.
8. It went well, *considering* — nobody walked out.

**See also:** [given](#given), [granted](#granted), [based on](#based-on), [concerning](#concerning), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding)

### counting

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaʊntɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈkaʊnɪn/ &middot; *KOWN-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; a spoken word
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*not counting waiting for a table*) — never a finite clause (✗ *not counting that the kids came*). Almost always negated: *not counting*.
**Pattern:** *counting* [noun phrase] &middot; *not counting* [noun phrase] &middot; [main clause], *not counting* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *Counting* is the conversational *including*, and it is restricted to tallies — you can say *counting the dog* about a headcount, but not *counting Chapter 4* about a table of contents. Negative [*not counting*](08-accompaniment-exception.md#not-counting) is the spoken *excluding*.

The arithmetic preposition of everyday speech. It adds or, more often, withholds an item from a running total.

**1. Including in a total.** Positive, usually about people or money.
**2. Excluding from a total.** The far more common negative, *not counting*.

1. There are nine of us, *counting* the kids.
2. *Counting* tips, she clears about forty thousand.
3. It's a two-hour drive, *not counting* traffic.
4. We've lost three games, *not counting* the exhibition.
5. The room sleeps six, *not counting* the couch.
6. *Counting* the garage, the place is nineteen hundred square feet.
7. It's ninety minutes at that place, *not counting* waiting for a table.

**See also:** [including](#including), [excluding](#excluding), [discounting](#discounting), [included](#included), [not counting](08-accompaniment-exception.md#not-counting)

### depending on

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ˈɑn/, /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ˈɔn/ &middot; casual /dəˈpɛndɪn ən/ &middot; *dih-PEN-ding awn*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a *wh*-nominal — and the *wh*-nominal is the everyday form: *depending on what he says*, *depending on whether it rains*, *depending on how many show up*. Never a bare *that*-clause (✗ *depending on that it rains*). ✗ *Depending of* and ✗ *depending from* are errors; the adjective is *dependent on* or *dependent upon*, never *dependent of*.
**Pattern:** *Depending on* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *depending on* [noun phrase or *wh*-clause].
**Contrast:** Three near neighbors sort by what the object names. *Depending on* names the **variable** that will decide the outcome; [*based on*](#based-on) names the **evidence** a conclusion rests on; [*according to*](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to) names the **source** of a report. So *the price varies depending on the season* (variable), *we chose based on the reviews* (evidence), *according to the reviews, it's good* (source). Keep the preposition apart from the plain verb: *the outcome depends on the vote* is a verb with a subject, while *depending on the vote, we may adjourn* has no subject and is a preposition.
**Variants:** *depending upon* (formal)

Names the thing that has not been settled yet and that will settle everything else. It is the standard way American English hedges a quantity, a schedule, or a decision.

**1. Determined by; varying with.** With a noun phrase — the range use.
**2. Conditional on the answer to a question.** With a *wh*- or *whether*-nominal.

1. *Depending on* the weather, the game starts at six or not at all.
2. Tickets run $40 to $90, *depending on* the seat.
3. *Depending on* what the lab says, we may operate tonight.
4. *Depending on* whether the bill passes, the rate changes in July.
5. She works from home two or three days, *depending on* the week.
6. *Depending on* how you count, there are eight planets or nine.

**See also:** [based on](#based-on), [allowing for](#allowing-for), [judging by](#judging-by), [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to), [regardless of](11-complex-multiword.md#regardless-of)

### discounting

**Pronunciation:** /dɪsˈkaʊntɪŋ/ &middot; casual /dɪsˈkaʊnɪn/ &middot; *dis-KOWN-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in analysis and argument
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** *Discounting* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *discounting* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *Excluding* removes an item as a matter of arithmetic; *discounting* dismisses it as not worth weighing. The difference is judgment: *excluding the outliers* is a method, *discounting the outliers* is an argument. *Not counting* is the casual version of *excluding*, never of this.

Sets something aside on the grounds that it should not count — because it is unreliable, unrepresentative, or interested. It carries an implicit claim, which is why it belongs to persuasion rather than bookkeeping.

1. *Discounting* the outliers, the results are consistent.
2. *Discounting* his own testimony, there is no evidence at all.
3. Sales rose eight percent, *discounting* the effect of the promotion.
4. *Discounting* inflation, wages have been flat for a decade.
5. Even *discounting* the hype, the device is impressive.
6. *Discounting* having played at home all month, the streak still means something.

**See also:** [excluding](#excluding), [counting](#counting), [barring](#barring), [regardless of](11-complex-multiword.md#regardless-of)

### during

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʊrɪŋ/, widely /ˈdɜrɪŋ/ (rhyming with *stirring*) &middot; casual /ˈdɜrɪn/ &middot; British keeps the yod: /ˈdjʊərɪŋ/ &middot; *DOOR-ing* or *DUR-ing*
**Type:** marginal/participial by origin, now a fully grammaticalized simple preposition &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period or an event — never a finite clause (✗ *during I was there* → *while I was there*), never a gerund (✗ *during eating* → *while eating*), and **never a measure of duration** (✗ *during three hours* → *for three hours*).
**Pattern:** *during* [noun phrase] &middot; *During* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The big one is *during* vs. *for*: *for* answers "how long" and takes a quantity (*for two weeks*), while *during* answers "when" and takes a named span (*during the strike*). [*for*](02-core-relational.md#for) &middot; *While* is the clause form; *throughout* insists on the entire span, where *during* leaves the point inside it vague.

Historically a participle — the object of the obsolete verb *dure*, "to last," in an absolute construction (*during the king's life* once meant "the king's life enduring"). Nothing of that survives in the modern word except its membership in this file.

**1. At some point inside a period.** The event is located somewhere within the span.
**2. Throughout a period.** The event occupies the whole span.

1. She called *during* the meeting.
2. The power went out twice *during* the storm.
3. He worked two jobs *during* college.
4. *During* the strike, the plant produced nothing.
5. Nobody speaks *during* the reading.
6. *During* the sixties, the town lost half its population.

**See also:** [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [pending](#pending), [following](#following), [beginning](#beginning)

### excepting

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛptɪŋ/, /ɛkˈsɛptɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ɪkˈsɛptɪn/ &middot; *ik-SEP-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal and dated; standard only after *not* and *always*
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause in modern American English. The old conjunction *excepting that* is archaic.
**Pattern:** *not excepting* [noun phrase] &middot; *always excepting* [noun phrase] &middot; *Excepting* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** Plain *except* is the ordinary word, and using *excepting* where *except* would do sounds stilted or legalistic. Between the two live forms, the rule is positional: at the **front** of a sentence American English requires *except for* (*Except for the ending, the movie is great*; ✗ *Except the ending, the movie is great*), while after the noun either works (*everyone except him*, *everyone except for him*). *Excepting* can front on its own, which is the one thing it still does that *except* cannot. *Excepting* otherwise survives in two fixed frames: *not excepting* (which insists someone is included) and *always excepting* (a legal reservation). The postposed participle *excepted* is alive and well.

An older, heavier *except*. Note that *not excepting* means the opposite of what learners expect — it does not exclude anyone; it refuses to exclude them.

1. Everyone signed, *not excepting* the chairman.
2. The rule applies to all staff, *always excepting* the medical team.
3. *Excepting* the last chapter, the book is a delight.
4. All fees are refundable, *excepting* the deposit.
5. Every state ratified it, *not excepting* the two that had sued.

**See also:** [excepted](#excepted), [excluding](#excluding), [saving](#saving), [barring](#barring), [except for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except-for)

### excluding

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskludɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ɪkˈskludɪn/ &middot; *ik-SKLOO-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default in commercial, technical, and statistical writing
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *excluding* [noun phrase] &middot; *Excluding* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The exact opposite of *including*, and the neutral partner of *not counting*. Against *except for*: *excluding* is arithmetical and unemotional, while *except for* often implies the exception is remarkable. *Exclusive of* is the formal contract-drafting version. Against *discounting*: *excluding* removes, *discounting* dismisses.

Subtracts a specified item from a set, a total, or a price. It makes no claim about why.

1. The fare is $340, *excluding* taxes and fees.
2. *Excluding* weekends, that leaves eleven working days.
3. Enrollment rose in every division, *excluding* the law school.
4. The study covered adults, *excluding* anyone over eighty.
5. *Excluding* the two pilot sites, results were flat.
6. The job takes four hours, *excluding* driving out and back.

**See also:** [including](#including), [excluded](#excluded), [excepting](#excepting), [discounting](#discounting), [exclusive of](11-complex-multiword.md#exclusive-of)

### failing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfeɪlɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈfeɪlɪn/ &middot; *FAY-ling*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, typically abstract (an agreement, a payment, a response) or the pronoun *that* — never a finite clause (✗ *failing that they agree* → *failing agreement*).
**Pattern:** *Failing* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Failing that*, [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Failing* and *barring* are mirror images. *Barring X* means "unless X happens," where X is the obstacle; *failing X* means "if X does not happen," where X is the thing you were hoping for. *Absent* and *in the absence of* are the near-synonyms; *failing that* is the fixed phrase that carries the whole word in everyday writing.

Introduces the fallback. It names the thing that was supposed to occur and quietly concedes that it might not.

**1. If the named thing does not happen.** With an abstract noun phrase, and always fronted.
**2. *Failing that*.** The fixed phrase, which carries the word in everyday writing and is the only form most Americans ever use.

1. *Failing* an agreement by Friday, the case goes to trial.
2. *Failing* payment within thirty days, the account goes to collections.
3. *Failing* any response from the seller, the buyer may cancel.
4. Ask your supervisor; *failing that*, put it in writing to HR.
5. We'll take the early flight, or *failing* that, drive.
6. Try the main number, and *failing that*, email the clerk.

**See also:** [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent), [lacking](#lacking), [barring](#barring), [pending](#pending), [wanting](#wanting)

### following

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈfɑləwɪn/ &middot; *FAH-loh-ing*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; heavy in journalism and official statements
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ *following she resigned* → *after she resigned*, or *following her resignation*). A gerund is grammatical but nearly always clumsy (*following being notified* → *following notification*), so the repair for a tempting clause is a **nominalization**, not an *-ing* form. The demonstrative pronoun is fine: *following that, we adjourned*.
**Pattern:** *Following* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *following* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** *After* is the plain word, it can take a clause, and it is almost always better. *Following* implies the second event came *because of* the first, which is exactly why press releases prefer it — and exactly why it should be resisted when no such link exists. *Subsequent to* is worse than either. Do not let it collide with the noun *the following* (*please read the following*).

The journalist's *after*. It attaches an event to the trigger that preceded it, and it dangles easily: *Following the vote, the room emptied* is fine, but *Following the vote, applause broke out* invites the reading that the applause did the following.

**1. After, in plain sequence.** No causal claim — the second event simply came later.
**2. In the wake of; as a result of.** The loaded use, and the reason press offices reach for the word.

1. *Following* a brief recess, the hearing resumed.
2. *Following* his release, he moved to Arizona.
3. *Following* the storm, half the county was without power.
4. Three executives resigned *following* the audit.
5. The bill was withdrawn *following* pressure from both parties.
6. *Following* that, we adjourned until Thursday.

**See also:** [preceding](#preceding), [pending](#pending), [during](#during), [beginning](#beginning), [prior to](11-complex-multiword.md#prior-to), [subsequent to](11-complex-multiword.md#subsequent-to)

### going on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈɑn/, /ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈɔn/ &middot; casual /ˈɡoʊɪn ɔn/ &middot; *goh-in-ON*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken
**Complement:** a number or a number-headed time or age expression — never a clause, never a person or an object (✗ *going on the meeting*).
**Pattern:** [be] *going on* [number] &middot; [noun phrase] *going on* [number]
**Contrast:** *Nearly* and *almost* are adverbs and go with anything; *going on* attaches only to numbers, and only from below — you cannot be *going on* an age you have already passed. The idiom *nine going on thirty-five* (a knowing child) is a fixed joke built on the same frame.

Says you are approaching a number — an age, a clock time, or a length of service — without having reached it.

1. She's *going on* forty and still won't fly.
2. It's *going on* midnight; we should head back.
3. He's been at the paper *going on* twenty years.
4. The kid is six *going on* thirty.
5. They've been engaged *going on* three years now.

**See also:** [ago](#ago), [back](#back), [hence](#hence), [worth](#worth)

### including

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkludɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ɪnˈkludɪn/ &middot; *in-KLOO-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *including that he lied*). **Pronoun objects must be objective:** *including me*, *including her and me* — ✗ *including my wife and I*, which is the single most common error made with this word.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], *including* [noun phrase] &middot; *Including* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; Postposed counterpart: [noun phrase] *included*.
**Contrast:** *Including* opens a partial list; *namely* and *that is* introduce a complete one, so *including* must never be followed by the whole set. Against *counting*: *including* works for any set, *counting* only for tallies. In contracts, *including* alone can be read as exhaustive, which is why drafters write *including but not limited to*.

Names one or more members of a set already mentioned, leaving the rest implied.

**1. Naming examples from a larger set.** The list is partial by definition.
**2. Counting an amount into a total.** Usually money, weight, or time.

1. Several people, *including* the director, objected.
2. The package covers five cities, *including* Nashville.
3. Everyone was invited, *including* me.
4. That's $40, *including* tax.
5. The flight takes six hours, *including* the layover.
6. All costs, *including* shipping, are itemized on page two.

**See also:** [included](#included), [excluding](#excluding), [counting](#counting), [excepting](#excepting), [in addition to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-addition-to)

### judging by

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʒʌdʒɪŋ baɪ/ &middot; weak /ˈdʒʌdʒɪn bə/ &middot; *JUH-jing by*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or *wh*-nominal (*judging by what he wrote*, *judging by how fast they settled*) — never a finite *that*-clause (✗ *judging by he was late*). *Judging from* takes exactly the same complements.
**Pattern:** *Judging by* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Judging from* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *judging by* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** This is the word the traditionalists recommend in place of fronted [*based on*](#based-on), and the recommendation is worth understanding: nobody expects a *judging* to have a subject, so the fronted phrase raises no dangling objection even from readers who dislike *Based on the data, we canceled*. Against *according to*, which reports what a source said, *judging by* draws the speaker's own inference from evidence — *according to the reviews* quotes them, *judging by the reviews* reasons from them. *Judging by* and *judging from* are freely interchangeable in American English; *to judge by* and *to judge from* are the stiffer infinitival variants.
**Variants:** *judging from*, *to judge by*, *to judge from*

Marks an inference and its evidence in one stroke, and quietly signals that the speaker could be wrong.

**1. Inferring from physical or documentary evidence.**
**2. Inferring from a report or a manner.** Usually with *judging from*.

1. *Judging by* the line outside, the reviews were good.
2. *Judging from* her voice, she had been crying.
3. *Judging by* what he wrote, he never opened the report.
4. The house is older than the deed says, *judging by* the wiring.
5. *Judging by* how fast they settled, the evidence was bad.
6. *Judging from* two quarters of data, the change is holding.

### lacking

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlækɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈlækɪn/ &middot; *LAK-ing*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal to literary
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause, and no gerund (✗ *lacking having a quorum*).
**Pattern:** *Lacking* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Lacking* is the living, slightly literary form; *wanting* is its archaic twin; *absent* is what an American lawyer writes; *failing* is used for an act that was not performed rather than a thing that is not there. Keep the preposition apart from the adjective in *the report is lacking in detail*, which requires *in*.

States the absence that forced the outcome. Almost always fronted, and almost always followed by a consequence.

1. *Lacking* a quorum, the board adjourned.
2. *Lacking* any evidence of intent, prosecutors dropped the charge.
3. *Lacking* an heir, he left the farm to the county.
4. *Lacking* better options, we took the red-eye.
5. *Lacking* federal money, the project stalled for a decade.

**See also:** [wanting](#wanting), [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent), [failing](#failing), [barring](#barring)

### notwithstanding

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ/, /ˌnɑtwɪðˈstændɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˌnɑtwəθˈstændɪn/ &middot; *naht-with-STAN-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial; also **postpositive**; also a sentence adverb &middot; **Register:** formal and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund; in legal drafting only, a finite *that*-clause (*notwithstanding that the parties have agreed*). In ordinary prose, keep it to a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** *notwithstanding* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun phrase] *notwithstanding* (postposed) &middot; *Notwithstanding* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** It means *despite*, but with three properties *despite* lacks. First, it can follow its object — *the objections notwithstanding* — which no other concessive preposition in English can do. Second, it heads the override formula that makes one contract or statute clause defeat another. Third, it can drop its object altogether and stand as a sentence adverb meaning "all the same" (*He was warned; he went notwithstanding*), where *despite* is impossible. *Despite* and *in spite of* are the everyday words, and all three take a noun phrase or a gerund and **never** a *that*-clause outside legal drafting: ✗ *despite he was tired*, ✗ *in spite of that it rained* → *despite being tired*, *in spite of the rain*. Also ✗ *notwithstanding of* is an error, exactly like ✗ *despite of*.

A calque of Latin *non obstante*, "not standing in the way," and still the only common English preposition that is equally comfortable before or after its object.

**1. Despite (before the object).** The ordinary formal use.
**2. Despite (after the object).** The postpositive use, distinctly literary or legal.
**3. The legal override.** *Notwithstanding any other provision* — this clause wins over conflicting ones.
**4. Cross-class: the bare adverb.** No object at all, meaning "all the same." The preposition takes an object; the adverb does not.

1. *Notwithstanding* the delay, the shipment arrived intact.
2. She took the job *notwithstanding* the pay cut.
3. The objections *notwithstanding*, the vote proceeded.
4. His reputation *notwithstanding*, the jury acquitted him.
5. *Notwithstanding* any other provision of this Agreement, Section 9 survives termination.
6. *Notwithstanding* that the tenant has cured the default, the landlord retains the right of entry.
7. *Notwithstanding* having signed the waiver, she sued anyway.
8. Every engineer on the project objected; the launch went ahead *notwithstanding*.

**See also:** [given](#given), [granted](#granted), [considering](#considering), [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of), [regardless of](11-complex-multiword.md#regardless-of)

### owing to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊɪŋ tu/ &middot; weak /ˈoʊɪn tə/ &middot; *OH-ing too*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause. For a clause use *because*; *owing to the fact that* is grammatical but wordy.
**Pattern:** *Owing to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *owing to* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The traditional rule pairs it against *due to*: *owing to* was the adverbial (*Owing to fog, the flight was canceled*) and *due to* the adjectival, licensed only after a linking verb (*The cancellation was due to fog*). American editors have largely abandoned the distinction and fronted *due to* is now standard, but conservative style guides still flag it — so *owing to* remains the safe choice in that position. *Because of* is safer still and sounds like a human wrote it.

Assigns a cause, at a slightly elevated pitch. Note that bare *owing* is an adjective meaning unpaid (*the balance owing*), a different word from this two-word preposition.

1. *Owing to* the weather, all flights are grounded.
2. The museum closed early *owing to* a power failure.
3. *Owing to* an oversight, your invoice was never sent.
4. Attendance was down, *owing to* the strike.
5. *Owing to* rising costs, the program was scaled back.
6. *Owing to* being short-staffed all summer, the branch cut its hours.
7. The route was closed *owing to* the bridge having washed out — ✗ *owing to that the bridge washed out*.

### pending

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɛndɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈpɛndɪn/ &middot; *PEN-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and administrative
**Complement:** noun phrase naming an event, decision, or process — never a finite clause (✗ *pending the board decides* → *pending the board's decision*), and gerunds are awkward (write *pending receipt of the report*, not *pending receiving the report*).
**Pattern:** *pending* [noun phrase] &middot; *Pending* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Until* takes a clause and a point in time; *pending* takes an event as a noun. The *by*/*until* rule sits right next to it and is worth having straight: *by* names a deadline for a single completed action (*file it by Friday*), *until* names how long a state goes on (*we're closed until Friday*), and *pending* names the event that will end the state rather than the date (*we're closed pending the inspection*). *Awaiting* is the near-synonym but keeps a stronger sense of active waiting. Keep the preposition apart from the adjective *pending* (*a pending case*, *patent pending*), which is postposed and means "not yet decided."

Borrowed from French *pendant*, "hanging." It suspends the main clause until the named event resolves, and it carries a strong implication that the current state is temporary.

**1. Until (an event occurs).** The forward-looking sense.
**2. While awaiting; during the course of.** The overlapping sense, common in legal phrases like *pending trial* and *pending appeal*.
**3. Cross-class: the adjective.** Meaning "not yet decided," used attributively (*a pending case*) and postpositively (*patent pending*). It takes **no object**, which is the whole test — *pending the appeal* is the preposition, *the appeal is pending* is the adjective.

1. The decision is *pending* review by the board.
2. She was suspended with pay *pending* the investigation.
3. *Pending* further notice, the office stays closed.
4. He was held *pending* trial.
5. Payments continue *pending* the outcome of the appeal.
6. *Pending* congressional approval, the funds cannot be released.
7. Two claims are still *pending*, and the patent is *pending* as well.

### pertaining to

**Pronunciation:** /pɚˈteɪnɪŋ tu/ &middot; weak /pɚˈteɪnɪn tə/ &middot; *pur-TAY-ning too*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word) &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, bureaucratic, and archival
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or *wh*-nominal (*pertaining to what was filed*) — never a finite *that*-clause (✗ *pertaining to he was hired* → *pertaining to his hiring*). The *to* is welded on: ✗ *pertaining of*, ✗ *pertaining with*, and bare *pertaining* is not a preposition at all.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *pertaining to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Pertaining to* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The heaviest of the topic prepositions. Ranked by weight: plain *about* is what a person says, *regarding* is the business default, *concerning* is a step up, and *pertaining to* is the top of the scale — it belongs to records requests, statutes, and discovery demands (*all documents pertaining to the transaction*). *Relating to* is the same construction one degree lighter and is what most American drafters now write. Against *regarding*, which merely announces a topic, *pertaining to* claims that the object **belongs** to the matter, which is why it survives in law: relevance is exactly what it asserts. Keep it apart from the plain verb *pertain*, which needs a subject and its own *to* (*the rule pertains to contractors*).
**Variants:** *relating to* (lighter; the current American drafting default), *appertaining to* (archaic)

Ties a document, a rule, or a record to the matter it belongs to. Its natural home is the filing cabinet, and outside that setting it reads as padding.

**1. Belonging to the subject of; relevant to.** The legal and archival use.
**2. On the topic of.** The weakened bureaucratic use, where *about* would do.

1. Please produce all records *pertaining to* the settlement.
2. The statute *pertaining to* easements was amended in 1998.
3. Questions *pertaining to* benefits go to Human Resources.
4. *Pertaining to* your second point, the committee has no view.
5. She testified only *pertaining to* what she saw that night.
6. Documents *pertaining to* hiring before 2015 were destroyed.
7. The clause *relating to* arbitration survives termination.

**See also:** [regarding](#regarding), [concerning](#concerning), [respecting](#respecting), [touching](#touching), [re](10-topic-reference-role.md#re), [with respect to](11-complex-multiword.md#with-respect-to)

### preceding

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈsidɪŋ/ &middot; casual /prəˈsidɪn/ &middot; *prih-SEE-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal; minutes, reports, and legal recitals
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause (✗ *preceding she spoke* → *preceding her remarks*, or plain *before she spoke*). Gerunds are as clumsy here as after *following*, and a bare measure of time is impossible: ✗ *preceding three days* → *in the three days preceding the vote*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *preceding* [noun phrase] &middot; *Preceding* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The mirror image of [following](#following), and it inherits every one of *following*'s problems. *Before* is the plain word, it takes a clause, and it is almost always the better choice; *prior to* is stiffer still and worse than either. The live trap is cross-class: the far more common **attributive adjective** in *the preceding paragraph* and *the preceding year* modifies the noun it stands in front of and takes no object, while the preposition sits between two noun phrases and relates them (*the year preceding the merger*). Most readers meet only the adjective, which is why the preposition reads as officialese.

Places one event immediately in front of another and asserts nothing about cause — which is the one respect in which it is cleaner than *following*.

**1. Immediately before, in sequence.** Between two noun phrases.
**2. Fronted, opening a clause.** The formal adverbial use.

1. *Preceding* the vote, the chair read the resolution aloud.
2. In the week *preceding* the storm, nobody could buy a generator.
3. The quarter *preceding* the recall was the company's best.
4. *Preceding* the merger, both firms had already cut staff.
5. The paragraph *preceding* this one defines the term.
6. *Preceding* that, there had been no complaints at all.

### regarding

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈɡɑrdɪŋ/ &middot; weak /rəˈɡɑrdɪn/ &middot; *rih-GAR-ding*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the standard business-correspondence word
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or *wh*-nominal (*regarding what you asked*) — never a finite *that*-clause.
**Pattern:** *regarding* [noun phrase] &middot; *Regarding* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The trap is the plural. **With regard to** is correct; ✗ *with regards to* is an error — *regards* belongs only in *give my regards*. *As regards* (no *to*) is also correct but stiff. Among the topic words, *regarding* is the neutral business default, *concerning* is heavier, *respecting* is dated, and plain *about* or *on* is usually the best of all.

Announces the subject of the sentence or the document. In American office writing it has almost entirely displaced *concerning*.

1. I'm writing *regarding* your application.
2. *Regarding* the budget, we'll take that up in March.
3. He had nothing to say *regarding* the settlement.
4. Please see the attached memo *regarding* parking.
5. *Regarding* what you asked yesterday, the answer is no.
6. There are new rules *regarding* overtime.

### respecting

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈspɛktɪŋ/ &middot; weak /rəˈspɛktɪn/ &middot; *rih-SPEK-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal and dated; alive in statutes and constitutional text
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** *respecting* [noun phrase] &middot; *Respecting* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** A synonym of *concerning* and *regarding* that has fallen out of ordinary use. It survives chiefly because it appears in famous legal language — *Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion* — where it means "about," not "showing respect for." That collision with the modern verb *respect* is the reason to avoid it in new prose.

The oldest of the surviving topic prepositions. Use it only when you are quoting or deliberately writing in a legal register.

1. Congress shall make no law *respecting* an establishment of religion.
2. The treaty is silent *respecting* fishing rights.
3. *Respecting* the second charge, the defense offered no evidence.
4. Rules *respecting* the conduct of members were adopted in 1974.
5. He would say nothing *respecting* his sources.

### saving

**Pronunciation:** /ˈseɪvɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈseɪvɪn/ &middot; *SAY-ving*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in legal drafting and set courtesies
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause in modern use. The old conjunction *saving that* is archaic.
**Pattern:** *saving* [noun phrase] &middot; *Saving* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** The bare preposition [*save*](08-accompaniment-exception.md#save) is the surviving literary form (*all save one*); *saving* is one step further back. In law it means "without prejudice to," which is why contracts have a *savings clause* — *saving the rights of third parties* preserves those rights rather than excluding them. The two readings, "except" and "preserving," are close enough that modern drafters prefer *except for* or *without prejudice to*.

An old *except* with a legal afterlife.

1. All the crew survived, *saving* the second mate.
2. *Saving* your presence, ma'am, the man is a fraud.
3. Nothing was left standing, *saving* the chimney.
4. The order is affirmed, *saving* the appellant's right to seek review.
5. Every provision remains in force, *saving* Section 12.

### speaking of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈspikɪŋ ʌv/ &middot; weak /ˈspikɪn ə/ &middot; *SPEE-king uv*
**Type:** marginal/participial (two-word); a conversational discourse connector &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; overwhelmingly spoken
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*speaking of getting there early*) — never a finite clause (✗ *speaking of he is late* → *speaking of his being late*). One extra restriction no other word in this file has: the object must be something already said or obviously in the air. *Speaking of* cannot introduce a topic out of nowhere, which is why it is a connector and not just a preposition.
**Pattern:** *Speaking of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Speaking of which*, [main clause]. — always fronted, always with a comma.
**Contrast:** *As for* and *as to* also switch topics, but they are neutral about whether the topic came up before; *speaking of* insists that it did, and that insistence is the entire meaning of the word. *Regarding* announces a subject in writing; *speaking of* picks one up in conversation, and using it in a memo sounds like a transcript. *Talking of* is the British counterpart and sounds foreign in American speech. The fixed form *speaking of which* is what you use when the trigger was the whole preceding clause rather than a nameable thing. Do not confuse any of this with *generally speaking*, *strictly speaking*, and *roughly speaking*, which are sentence adverbs, take **no object at all**, and are not prepositions.
**Variants:** *speaking of which*, *talking of* (British)

The pivot of American conversation: it grabs a word someone just said and turns the talk toward it, while pretending the turn was accidental.

**1. Picking up a topic just mentioned.** With a noun phrase repeated from the previous turn.
**2. *Speaking of which*.** With the whole preceding clause as the trigger.

1. *Speaking of* the weather, did you hear about the storm Friday?
2. *Speaking of* money, we never settled the check.
3. *Speaking of* your brother, is he still in Denver?
4. *Speaking of* being late, where is Dana?
5. We need a new roof — *speaking of which*, the estimate came in.
6. *Speaking of* Nashville, that reminds me of the last tour.

**See also:** [regarding](#regarding), [concerning](#concerning), [pertaining to](#pertaining-to), [as for](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-for), [as to](10-topic-reference-role.md#as-to)

### starting

**Pronunciation:** /ˈstɑrtɪŋ/ &middot; flapped and casual /ˈstɑrɾɪn/ &middot; *STAR-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; the spoken counterpart of *beginning*
**Complement:** a time expression — a date, a day, a clock time, or a prepositional phrase of time. Never a finite clause, and never an ordinary object in this sense.
**Pattern:** *Starting* [time], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *starting* [time].
**Contrast:** *Starting* is what Americans say; *beginning* is what they print; *effective* is what the HR notice says; *as of* is the neutral written form and can also look backward. One redundancy to avoid: *starting from Monday* — use *starting Monday* or *from Monday on*, not both.

Fixes the first day of a new state of affairs. Purely temporal — it never takes a person or a thing.

1. *Starting* Monday, we're on the new schedule.
2. The pool is open *starting* Memorial Day.
3. *Starting* at nine, calls go straight to voicemail.
4. Deliveries run twice a week *starting* in June.
5. *Starting* next semester, the class meets online.

**See also:** [beginning](#beginning), [effective](05-time.md#effective), [pending](#pending), [as of](05-time.md#as-of)

### touching

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtʌtʃɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈtʌtʃɪn/ &middot; *TUH-ching*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** archaic; ecclesiastical, legal, and literary
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** *touching* [noun phrase] &middot; *Touching* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Contrast:** Another dead synonym of *concerning*. The living modern word spelled the same way is the adjective (*a touching tribute*) and the verb (*touching the wire*), so a modern reader hits *touching the matter of the will* and stumbles. Use *concerning* or *about*.

Survives almost entirely in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century prose and in deliberate pastiche of it.

1. *Touching* the matter of the estate, I have written to counsel.
2. He was examined *touching* his knowledge of the conspiracy.
3. *Touching* these accusations, the bishop was silent.
4. Nothing in the record is decisive *touching* the question of intent.
5. *Touching* your late letter, I can add little.

**See also:** [concerning](#concerning), [respecting](#respecting), [regarding](#regarding), [re](10-topic-reference-role.md#re)

### wanting

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɑntɪŋ/, /ˈwɔntɪŋ/ &middot; casual /ˈwɑnɪn/ &middot; *WAHN-ting*
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** archaic to formal-literary
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a finite clause, never a gerund.
**Pattern:** *Wanting* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [quantity] *wanting* [quantity].
**Contrast:** *Lacking* is the modern equivalent for the first sense; *minus* or *less* covers the subtractive second sense. Keep the preposition apart from the predicative adjective in *he was found wanting* and from the ordinary verb *want*, "desire," which is the meaning most learners assume and which is never right here.

The older sibling of *lacking*, from the original sense of *want*, "to be missing."

**1. In the absence of; without.** The concessive-causal use.
**2. Less; minus.** A subtractive use, now confined to old prose and set arithmetic.

1. *Wanting* a quorum, the assembly adjourned.
2. *Wanting* better evidence, the coroner recorded an open verdict.
3. *Wanting* a will, the estate passed to the next of kin.
4. A hundred *wanting* three is ninety-seven.
5. He served forty years *wanting* two months.
6. It was a full crew *wanting* one deckhand.
7. *Wanting* any word from headquarters, the captain acted on his own.

**See also:** [lacking](#lacking), [failing](#failing), [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent), [bating](#bating), [minus](08-accompaniment-exception.md#minus), [less](08-accompaniment-exception.md#less)

## Prepositions from past participles

Four forms that shed a subject and a *be* and became prepositions outright. They split on the complement, and the split is the thing to memorize: *given* and *granted* accept a *that*-clause, which puts them in a tiny minority, while *based on* and *compared to* take a noun phrase, a gerund, or a *wh*-nominal and reject a finite clause outright (✗ *based on he told me*, ✗ *compared to she was faster*). In the two-word members the second word is welded on — you cannot drop the *on* or the *to*, and you cannot swap in another preposition.

### based on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbeɪst ˈɑn/, /ˌbeɪst ˈɔn/ &middot; weak /beɪst ən/ &middot; *bayst-ON*
**Type:** marginal/participial (past participle, two-word) &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in American journalism and business writing
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a *wh*-nominal (*based on what he told us*) — never a finite *that*-clause (✗ *based on that he told us*). For a clause, write *because* or *on the ground that*.
**Pattern:** *Based on* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *based on* [noun phrase]. &middot; [clause], *based on* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The famous dangling-modifier fight. Traditionalists object to the sentence-opening use (*Based on the data, we canceled the launch*) because *we* are not based on anything, and prescribe *on the basis of* or [*judging by*](#judging-by) instead. American practice has moved: fronted *based on* is now standard in edited prose. The real participle *basing* still needs its subject and still dangles (✗ *Basing the estimate on last year, the budget was wrong*). Against [*depending on*](#depending-on): *based on* names the evidence behind a settled conclusion, *depending on* names the variable that has not resolved yet.

The most contested word in this file, and the clearest case of a participle finishing its journey into a preposition. When it modifies a noun (*a film based on a novel*) nobody objects; the argument is only about the fronted adverbial.

**1. Deriving from a source.** Attached to a noun, uncontroversially participial.
**2. On the evidence of; according to.** The fronted preposition.

1. The film is *based on* a novel nobody read.
2. They built a whole product line *based on* one patent.
3. *Based on* the polling, the race is a toss-up.
4. *Based on* what she told me, I'd take the offer.
5. We rejected the bid *based on* price alone.
6. *Based on* three quarters of data, the trend looks real.
7. *Based on* having watched two seasons, I'd say it holds up.

**See also:** [judging by](#judging-by), [depending on](#depending-on), [considering](#considering), [given](#given), [according to](11-complex-multiword.md#according-to), [on the basis of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-the-basis-of)

### compared to

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈpɛrd tu/ &middot; weak /kəmˈpɛrd tə/ &middot; *kum-PAIRD too*
**Type:** marginal/participial (past participle, two-word) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause (✗ *compared to he ran faster* → *compared to him*, *compared to his time*). The two things compared must be **parallel**, and this is where the sentence usually breaks: ✗ *Her salary is low compared to a manager* compares a salary to a person → *compared to a manager's* or *compared with that of a manager*. A pronoun object takes the objective case: *compared to me*, ✗ *compared to I*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *compared to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Compared to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *compared with* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The old rule assigned *compare to* to likening unlike things (*shall I compare thee to a summer's day*) and *compare with* to measuring like against like (*compare this quarter with last*). In the fixed participial preposition the distinction has collapsed in American English, and *compared to* now covers both; *compared with* survives as the slightly more formal and more statistical of the two. *In comparison with* is the heavy version, *relative to* the technical one. *Than* is a conjunction and is not an alternative here: ✗ *cheaper compared than*, either *cheaper than* or *cheap compared to*. And *versus* merely juxtaposes two things, where *compared to* asserts that a measurement has been made.
**Variants:** *compared with* (more formal, more common in statistical writing)

Sets one thing beside another so the difference registers. It is the standard American way to frame a number as good or bad news.

**1. Measured against.** The quantitative use, the common one.
**2. Likened to.** The figurative use, where the two things are of different kinds.

1. *Compared to* last year, sales are up nine percent.
2. The rent is cheap *compared to* Boston.
3. *Compared to* the original, the remake is tame.
4. Enrollment fell four percent, *compared with* six percent nationally.
5. Two hours is nothing *compared to* what that drive used to be.
6. *Compared to* being on call every night, this job is a vacation.

**See also:** [based on](#based-on), [given](#given), [in comparison with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-comparison-with), [relative to](11-complex-multiword.md#relative-to), [contrary to](11-complex-multiword.md#contrary-to)

### given

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪvən/ &middot; syllabic /ˈɡɪvn̩/ &middot; *GIV-un*
**Type:** marginal/participial (past participle) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, **or a finite clause with *that*** — *given that the deadline had passed*. It cannot stand alone as an adverb (✗ *She did well, given*).
**Pattern:** *Given* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Given that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *given* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** Against [considering](#considering): *given* states a premise and moves on, while *considering* weighs and often excuses. Against [granted](#granted): *given* takes a fact you both accept; *granted* concedes a point you are about to argue past. Only *considering* and *granted* can trail a sentence with no complement at all; *given* always needs an object. Cross-class, three other words are spelled the same and none of them is this preposition: the plain past participle of *give* (*the money was given anonymously*), the attributive adjective meaning "specified" or "prone to" (*at any given moment*, *given to exaggeration*), and the count noun (*that's a given*). The preposition is the one that takes a bare object and modifies the whole clause.

Treats what follows as established and reasons from it. Its origin is visible in the mathematical use, where the *given* is literally what you are handed at the start of a proof.

**1. In view of; taking as established.** The everyday use, with a noun phrase.
**2. Given that (with a clause).** The same meaning, with a full finite clause — one of only three words in this file that allows one in ordinary prose, alongside *considering* and *granted*; the fourth, legal *notwithstanding that*, is confined to drafting.
**3. In mathematics and logic: assumed as a starting condition.**
**4. Cross-class: the adjective and the noun.** *A given moment*, *a given*. No object, no clause — and no longer a preposition.

1. *Given* the circumstances, the sentence was lenient.
2. *Given* his record, the promotion surprised nobody.
3. *Given* the shortage of nurses, the wait is no surprise.
4. *Given that* she had two days to prepare, the presentation was remarkable.
5. *Given that* no one objected, the motion carried.
6. *Given* two points, construct the perpendicular bisector.
7. *Given* a constant rate of decay, the age can be calculated.
8. At any *given* moment, half the fleet is in the shop — and that the schedule slips is now a *given*.

**See also:** [granted](#granted), [considering](#considering), [based on](#based-on), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [in view of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-view-of)

### granted

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd/ &middot; casual /ˈɡrænɪd/ &middot; *GRAN-tid*
**Type:** marginal/participial (past participle); also a standalone concessive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, gerund, or a finite clause with or without *that* — *granted (that) the price is high*. It also takes **no complement at all** as a sentence adverb: *Granted, it's expensive.*
**Pattern:** *Granted* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Granted (that)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Granted*, [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Given* accepts a premise as neutral fact; *granted* concedes a point that works against the speaker, and the sentence that follows almost always pushes back — *Granted, it's expensive, but...*. As a bare adverb it takes a comma and functions like *admittedly*; *given* can never do this. The *-ing* variant *granting (that)* is slightly more formal and less common.
**Variants:** *granting*, *granting that*

The concessive of the pair. It hands the other side a point and then takes the sentence back.

**1. Conceding a stated point (with an object or clause).**
**2. Standing alone as *admittedly*.** No complement, comma after.

1. *Granted* the difficulty of the terrain, the survey was fast work.
2. *Granted that* the data are thin, the pattern is still suggestive.
3. *Granted*, the seats are terrible, but the sound is perfect.
4. It's a risk, *granted*, and we're taking it anyway.
5. *Granting* his sincerity, the argument still fails.
6. *Granted* the premise, the conclusion follows.

**See also:** [given](#given), [considering](#considering), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [in spite of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-spite-of)

## Marginal prepositions from other word classes

Words that were never verbs at all, yet take a bare object exactly the way a preposition does. Four of them are catalogued in the files that own their meaning rather than here, because what they do matters more than where they came from: the adjective [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent) (*absent a court order*) with the exception prepositions, the fossilized subjunctive [come](05-time.md#come) (*come Monday*) and the adjective [effective](05-time.md#effective) (*effective July 1*) with the time prepositions, and the Latin ablative [re](10-topic-reference-role.md#re) (*re your email*) with the topic prepositions. One stays here, because no other file has a claim on it and because it takes a direct object without being a verb.

### worth

**Pronunciation:** /wɜrθ/ &middot; run together before a weak object: *worth it* /ˈwɜrθ ɪt/ → /ˈwɜrθət/ &middot; *WURTH*
**Type:** marginal (traditionally labeled an adjective, but it takes a bare object like a preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or **gerund** — never a finite clause and **never a *to*-infinitive**: ✗ *worth to see*, ✗ *worth to be seen* → *worth seeing*. This is one of the most common learner errors in English. The object may be a pronoun: *worth it*.
**Pattern:** [subject] *is worth* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject] *is worth* [verb-ing] &middot; *it is worth* [verb-ing] &middot; *for what it's worth*
**Contrast:** *Worth* takes its object bare; *worthy* requires *of* — *worth praise* but *worthy of praise*; ✗ *worth of praise* is the error that comes of mixing them. And the gerund after *worth* is passive in meaning even though it is active in form: *the house is worth fixing up* means worth **being** fixed up, and adding *being* is wrong (✗ *worth being fixed up*). Keep the preposition apart from the identical **noun**, which follows a possessive and takes *of*: *ten dollars' worth of gas*, *get your money's worth*. Note the apostrophe — the measure is possessive, and *ten dollars worth* is a misspelling.

The reason *worth* sits in this file is that it is the one common English word that takes a direct object without being a verb or a preposition by traditional labeling. Every modern grammar that reclassifies it makes it a preposition.

**1. Having a stated monetary value.**
**2. Deserving the time, money, or trouble.** Typically with a gerund — and the gerund is where learners reach for the infinitive and go wrong.
**3. In fixed phrases.** *For what it's worth*, *for all it's worth*.
**4. Cross-class: the noun.** Preceded by a possessive and followed by *of*, which the preposition never is: *a dollar's worth of trouble*.

1. The house is *worth* about four hundred thousand.
2. That coin is *worth* more than the car it came in.
3. The drive is long, but the view is *worth* it.
4. This place is *worth* visiting twice.
5. It's *worth* asking before you assume.
6. The old boiler is not *worth* repairing a third time.
7. For what it's *worth*, I'd have made the same call.
8. The agent played up the school district for all it was *worth*.
9. We got twenty dollars' *worth* of gas and our money's *worth* of advice.

**See also:** [going on](#going-on), [absent](08-accompaniment-exception.md#absent), [come](05-time.md#come), [effective](05-time.md#effective)

## Postpositive and deferred prepositions

The small group that follows its object. The order is fixed grammar, not style: *three weeks ago* can never become *ago three weeks*, and *present company excepted* can never be fronted. Because the object comes first, the safest way to think about these is as prepositions turned around — the noun phrase is still the complement, and it is still what the word is doing its work on. Most of them are also cross-class, and the test is always the same: the postpositive has a noun phrase in front of it, and the look-alike adverb has nothing at all (*a while back* against *come back*, *money aside* against *step aside*, *two years hence* against *hence the detour*). Two words covered elsewhere in this file belong to the group as well: [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), which is the only English preposition equally natural in both orders, and the postposed adverbials built on core prepositions, such as *ten years* [**on**](01-core-in-on-at.md#on) and *the world over*, which are frozen idioms rather than a productive pattern.

### ago

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *uh-GOH*
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (*three weeks*, *a moment*, *long*). It can take nothing after it, ever, and never a clause. The measure phrase is obligatory: ✗ *He left ago.*
**Pattern:** [measure of time] *ago* &middot; ✗ *ago* [measure of time]
**Contrast:** Two rules. First, *ago* counts back from **now** and therefore demands the simple past: ✗ *He has left three days ago* → *He left three days ago*. *Before* counts back from a point already in the past: *He had left three days before*. Second, *ago* is not interchangeable with *for* — *ago* locates an event, *for* measures a duration (*she left three days ago*, *she was gone for three days*). And *long since* is not a British substitute for *long ago*, as it is sometimes labeled: it is standard formal American English, and it differs by tense, taking the perfect that *ago* forbids (*the shop has long since closed*, ✗ *the shop has closed long ago*).

Descended from the past participle of Old English *āgān*, "to pass by," so it is a participial preposition too — one that finished its journey centuries earlier than the rest of this file and ended up on the wrong side of its object.

1. Three weeks *ago* we had no idea.
2. She called a few minutes *ago*.
3. That happened long *ago*.
4. The factory closed twenty years *ago*.
5. I saw him not five minutes *ago*.
6. A century *ago*, none of this existed.

**See also:** [back](#back), [hence](#hence), [going on](#going-on), [prior to](11-complex-multiword.md#prior-to)

### apart

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑrt/ &middot; *uh-PART*
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** neutral; slightly British in this construction
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a noun phrase or gerund. Never a clause.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *apart*, [main clause].
**Contrast:** *Aside* is the American default in this frame: *joking aside* is idiomatic in the U.S., while *joking apart* reads as British. Do not confuse the postposed *apart* with the fronted complex preposition [*apart from*](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from), which means "except for" or "in addition to," is itself the British-leaning counterpart of *aside from*, and lives with the multi-word prepositions. Cross-class, the test is whether anything is being set aside: the postpositive has an object in front of it (*cost apart*), while the plain adverb has no object at all (*the two men drifted apart*, *I can't tell them apart*).

Sets a topic to one side so the sentence can get to the real point. Almost always fronted as a clause-opening phrase with a comma.

1. Cost *apart*, the plan is workable.
2. Joking *apart*, we need an answer by noon.
3. Politics *apart*, the two men were friends for forty years.
4. That one incident *apart*, the trip was flawless.
5. Style *apart*, the argument is sound.

**See also:** [aside](#aside), [aside from](#aside-from), [excepted](#excepted), [apart from](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from)

### aside

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsaɪd/ &middot; *uh-SYD*
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** neutral; the American preference over *apart*
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a noun phrase or gerund. Never a clause.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *aside*, [main clause].
**Contrast:** Same job as postposed *apart*, but this is the form Americans use. Note the direction change when it moves to the front: postposed *aside* only sets something to one side, while fronted [aside from](#aside-from) can mean either "except for" or "in addition to." Cross-class, the postpositive has an object in front of it (*money aside*); the ordinary adverb has none (*step aside*, *set it aside*), and there is also the theatrical noun *an aside*.

Brackets an objection or a topic and moves past it. It is the standard way to signal that you know about the thing you are not going to discuss.

1. Money *aside*, the schedule alone kills the project.
2. Joking *aside*, that was dangerous.
3. All modesty *aside*, I fixed it in ten minutes.
4. The lighting *aside*, the room is perfect.
5. Legal questions *aside*, is this the right thing to do?

**See also:** [aside from](#aside-from), [apart](#apart), [excepted](#excepted), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding)

### aside from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsaɪd frʌm/ &middot; weak /əˈsaɪd frəm/ &middot; *uh-SYD frum*
**Type:** complex, built on the postpositive *aside* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the American counterpart to British-leaning *apart from*
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a bare finite clause; use *aside from the fact that* if you must have one.
**Pattern:** *Aside from* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *aside from* [noun phrase].
**Contrast:** The ambiguity is the point of the entry. *Aside from* has two opposite readings — subtractive ("except for") and additive ("in addition to") — and only the following clause tells you which: *Aside from the salary, the job is great* subtracts, while *Aside from the salary, there are benefits* adds. When it matters, write *except for* for the first and *besides* or *in addition to* for the second. *Apart from* carries exactly the same double reading and is the British preference.

The most useful and most treacherous member of this group, because both of its meanings are common and neither is marked.

**1. Except for.** Subtractive: it takes the named thing out of the claim.
**2. In addition to.** Additive: it puts the named thing alongside what follows.

1. *Aside from* the ending, the movie is terrific.
2. *Aside from* a sore shoulder, he came through fine.
3. *Aside from* the tuition, there are books and fees.
4. *Aside from* being late, she brought the wrong file.
5. Nobody knew, *aside from* the two of us.
6. *Aside from* Nashville, the tour hits four cities in Texas.

**See also:** [aside](#aside), [apart](#apart), [excluding](#excluding), [apart from](11-complex-multiword.md#apart-from), [except for](08-accompaniment-exception.md#except-for), [in addition to](11-complex-multiword.md#in-addition-to)

### back

**Pronunciation:** /bæk/ &middot; *BAK*
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** informal; spoken American English
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (*a few years*, *a while*). Never a clause, and no object after it.
**Pattern:** [measure of time] *back*
**Contrast:** It is a casual *ago*, and it is vaguer — *a few years back* invites less precision than *three years ago*. Do not confuse it with the adverbial *back in 1998*, which comes **before** its object and is a different construction entirely. Cross-class, the postpositive needs a measure phrase in front of it (*a while back*), while the ordinary directional adverb needs nothing (*come back*, *she called back*), and *back* is also a common noun and a verb. In writing above the level of a blog post, use *ago*.

The conversational alternative to *ago*, restricted to loose measures of past time.

1. A few years *back*, this was all farmland.
2. I ran into him a while *back*.
3. Two summers *back*, the river came up over the road.
4. She mentioned it a couple of weeks *back*.
5. Some time *back*, they stopped answering the phone.

**See also:** [ago](#ago), [hence](#hence), [going on](#going-on)

### excepted

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛptɪd/ &middot; casual /ɪkˈsɛpɾɪd/ &middot; *ik-SEP-tid*
**Type:** postpositive participial &middot; **Register:** formal; one fixed phrase is conversational
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a noun phrase. Never a clause, never anything after it.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *excepted*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], [noun phrase] *excepted*.
**Contrast:** The postposed partner of prepositive *excepting*, and the negative counterpart of postposed [included](#included). Against [excluded](#excluded): *excepted* lifts a person or case out of a general statement, usually as a courtesy or a legal reservation, while *excluded* removes an item from a price, a set, or a policy. The fixed phrase *present company excepted* is the one use every American speaker knows.

Takes someone or something out from under a rule that has just been stated.

1. Everyone in that office is useless, present company *excepted*.
2. All fees are refundable, the application fee *excepted*.
3. Sundays *excepted*, the desk is staffed from eight to six.
4. The rule binds every member, the treasurer *excepted*.
5. Two counties *excepted*, the state went the other way.

**See also:** [excepting](#excepting), [excluded](#excluded), [included](#included), [saving](#saving)

### excluded

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskludɪd/ &middot; casual /ɪkˈskluɾɪd/ &middot; *ik-SKLOO-did*
**Type:** postpositive participial &middot; **Register:** neutral; commercial and technical
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a noun phrase. Never a clause.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *excluded*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], [noun phrase] *excluded*.
**Contrast:** The mirror of postposed [included](#included) and the postposed partner of *excluding*. Against [excepted](#excepted): *excluded* is arithmetic — it takes an item out of a total or a coverage list — while *excepted* is a concession about people or cases. On a price tag, *tax excluded* and *excluding tax* say the same thing; *tax excepted* does not exist.

Removes an item from a price, a set, or a scope of coverage, after the fact.

1. The rate is $220 a night, taxes *excluded*.
2. All finishes are covered, custom colors *excluded*.
3. Delivery is free nationwide, Alaska and Hawaii *excluded*.
4. Every department gained headcount, contractors *excluded*.
5. The warranty covers parts and labor, wear items *excluded*.

**See also:** [excluding](#excluding), [included](#included), [excepted](#excepted), [exclusive of](11-complex-multiword.md#exclusive-of)

### hence

**Pronunciation:** /hɛns/ &middot; *HENS*
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** formal and literary
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a measure-of-time noun phrase (*three days*, *a week*, *a fortnight*). Never a clause, nothing after it.
**Pattern:** [measure of time] *hence*
**Contrast:** The forward-pointing twin of [ago](#ago): *three days ago* looks back, *three days hence* looks forward. In ordinary American English the phrase is *three days from now*; *hence* in this sense is a deliberate literary choice. Keep it apart from the far more common **conjunctive adverb** *hence* meaning "therefore," which is the same spelling doing an entirely different job. The test is the measure phrase: the postpositive has one in front of it (*two years hence*), and the connective adverb has none and instead points back at a whole clause (*The road was closed; hence the detour*). A third, obsolete sense is spatial and means "from here," surviving only in *get thee hence* and in *henceforth*.

An archaic-flavored survivor. Almost every modern American use of temporal *hence* is either legal drafting or a bid for gravity.

**1. Postpositive: a stated span from now.** A measure of time comes first, and nothing follows.
**2. Cross-class: the connective adverb.** Meaning "therefore," with **no** measure phrase and no object — not a preposition at all.

1. The lease expires two years *hence*.
2. A century *hence*, none of these arguments will matter.
3. The court will reconvene thirty days *hence*.
4. What we decide today will be judged a generation *hence*.
5. Six months *hence*, the program will be reviewed.
6. The bridge was out; *hence* the four-hour detour.
7. She had never been told; *hence* her surprise.

**See also:** [ago](#ago), [back](#back), [going on](#going-on), [pending](#pending)

### included

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkludɪd/ &middot; casual /ɪnˈkluɾɪd/ &middot; *in-KLOO-did*
**Type:** postpositive participial &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a noun phrase. Never a clause. **Case matters:** *myself included* and *me included* are both idiomatic; ✗ *I included* is not.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *included*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], [noun phrase] *included*.
**Contrast:** The postposed partner of *including*, and it is often the more natural order when the object is a pronoun — *everyone, myself included* beats *everyone, including myself*. Its negatives are [excluded](#excluded) for items and [excepted](#excepted) for people.

Folds a member back into a set that has just been described, usually with a small note of surprise or self-implication.

1. Everyone laughed, the judge *included*.
2. We all missed it, myself *included*.
3. The price is $1,800, shipping *included*.
4. Every account was affected, the founders' *included*.
5. Four states voted against, ours *included*.

**See also:** [including](#including), [excluded](#excluded), [excepted](#excepted), [counting](#counting), [inclusive](#inclusive)

### inclusive

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈklusɪv/ &middot; *in-KLOO-siv*
**Type:** postpositive (traditionally labeled an adjective, but it follows and governs the range in front of it) &middot; **Register:** formal; contracts, statutes, and specifications
**Complement:** **precedes** it — a stated range, normally two numbers or dates joined by *to* (*1 to 30 inclusive*). Never a clause, and nothing may follow it. When something does follow, the word is the prepositive complex preposition *inclusive of*, which is a different construction (*a fee inclusive of tax*).
**Pattern:** [start] *to* [end] *inclusive* &middot; [range] *inclusive*
**Contrast:** This is the British repair for exactly the ambiguity American English fixes with [through](#through). *Monday to Friday inclusive* and *Monday through Friday* name the same five days, but the American form is shorter and is the default in the United States — so an American writes *inclusive* only in a contract or a specification where nothing may be left to inference. Its opposite postposes the same way: *pages 40 to 62 exclusive* leaves out page 62. Keep it apart from the ordinary **attributive adjective**, which stands in front of its noun and means something entirely different (*an inclusive definition*, *an inclusive community*); the postpositive has a range in front of it and the attributive has a noun behind it.

The belt-and-suspenders word. It exists because *to* does not say whether the last item is in or out, and sometimes that has to be settled on paper.

1. Sections 4 to 9 *inclusive* are hereby repealed.
2. The offer is open December 1 to December 24 *inclusive*.
3. Answer questions 1 to 20 *inclusive*.
4. Rates apply Monday to Friday *inclusive*.
5. The survey covers the years 1990 to 2000 *inclusive*.
6. Read chapters 3 to 7 *inclusive* before the seminar.

**See also:** [through](#through), [included](#included), [excluded](#excluded), [exclusive of](11-complex-multiword.md#exclusive-of), [up to](11-complex-multiword.md#up-to)

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; weak /θrə/ &middot; *THROO*
**Type:** simple preposition; in the inclusive-range sense a distinctly **American** connector; postpositive in fixed adverbials; **also an adverb, a verb particle, and a predicate adjective** &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in the range sense, a second date, day, or number, with the first one preceding: *Monday through Friday*, *pages 40 through 62*. Never a clause. In the postposed adverbial, the noun phrase comes first: *the whole night through*. As an adverb and as a particle it takes **no object at all** — that absence is the test.
**Pattern:** [start] *through* [end] &middot; [noun phrase] *through* (postposed adverbial) &middot; [verb] *through* (adverb or particle, no object) &middot; *be through with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** This is the most useful Americanism in the whole file. **American *Monday through Friday* includes Friday.** *Monday to Friday* is ambiguous and British-leaning, and *until Friday* or *till Friday* excludes it — *I'm here until Friday* means you leave on Friday, *I'm here through Friday* means you leave after it. British writers reach for *Monday to Friday inclusive* to get the same certainty an American gets from *through*. Cross-class, the test is the object: the **preposition** has one (*we drove through the tunnel*, *open Monday through Friday*), the **adverb** does not (*we drove through*, *let me through*), the **particle** binds to the verb and can be separated from it (*read it through*, *see it through*, *think this through*), and the **predicate adjective** means finished (*Are you through?*, *I'm through with him*). The path sense (*through the tunnel*) belongs with the [movement prepositions](04-movement-direction.md#through).
**Variants:** *thru* (informal American spelling; signage, menus, and *drive-thru* — never in edited prose)

Three things qualify it for this file: the inclusive endpoint, which is a genuine grammatical resource American English has and British English does not; the postposed adverbial, a frozen relic in which the object comes first; and the clean cross-class split between the preposition and the object-less adverb.

**1. Inclusive range.** From the first named point to the second, with the second one counted in.
**2. Postposed: for the entire span.** Fixed and literary — *the whole night through*.
**3. Cross-class: adverb, particle, predicate adjective.** No object anywhere.

1. The office is open Monday *through* Friday.
2. Read pages 40 *through* 62 for tomorrow.
3. The exhibit runs June 3 *through* September 8.
4. Applications are accepted March *through* May.
5. The baby cried the whole night *through*.
6. She stayed loyal to him his life *through*.
7. The gate was jammed, so we squeezed *through*.
8. Read the contract *through* before you sign it.
9. Two hours in, I was *through* with the whole argument.

**See also:** [inclusive](#inclusive), [ago](#ago), [pending](#pending), [beginning](#beginning), [up to](11-complex-multiword.md#up-to), [as of](05-time.md#as-of), [until](05-time.md#until)

## Look-alikes that are not prepositions

Four groups get mistaken for members of this file, and each fails a test the entries above pass.

**Words that take a clause and are therefore conjunctions.** *Assuming*, *provided*, *providing*, *seeing*, and *supposing* look exactly like participial prepositions but require a clause and cannot take a bare noun phrase: *assuming that the check clears*, not ✗ *assuming the check's clearance* in the same sense. *Considering*, *given*, and *granted* are the reverse — they take both, which is why they appear above.

**Absolute constructions.** *Weather permitting*, *God willing*, *all things being equal*, and *present circumstances allowing* keep their own subject, and that subject is what makes them absolutes rather than prepositions. Remove the subject and the phrase collapses.

**Reduced relative clauses.** *The man standing there*, *the report filed on Tuesday*, and *the quarter ending June 30* look participial because they are — they modify the noun in front of them and can be expanded with *who is* or *which is*. A participial preposition modifies the clause, not the noun, and no relative pronoun can be inserted.

**Real danglers.** *Basing*, *walking*, *hoping*, and every other live participle still needs its subject to be the subject of the main clause. ✗ *Walking to work, the rain started* is wrong because the rain was not walking. That test does not apply to *considering*, *regarding*, *given*, *following*, *judging by*, *depending on*, *compared to*, or *based on*, because those have stopped being verbs — which is the entire reason this file exists.
