Movement and direction#

The prepositions that trace a path — where motion starts, what route it takes, and where it ends up.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

Every preposition in this file answers which way? rather than where? They attach to verbs of motion (run, drive, throw, climb, push) and they name one of three things: a source (out of, off, away from), a path (across, through, along, past, via), or a goal (into, onto, toward, up to). The single rule that runs through the whole group is the position/motion split: in, on, and at mark where something already is, while into, onto, and to mark where something is going. He was in the pool and He jumped into the pool are not stylistic variants — they describe different events, and choosing the static form for a dynamic verb is the most common spatial error learners make.

The second thing to watch is that almost every word here moonlights as an adverb or a verb particle. Up, down, over, through, past, around, off, and out all have a life outside this file. The test is the object: a preposition always has a noun phrase after it (ran up the hill), while an adverb or particle stands alone (stand up) or belongs to the verb rather than the noun (turned the offer down). That test also settles the two nastiest spelling traps in American English — into vs. in to and onto vs. on to — because when in or on belongs to the verb, the words stay apart.

Finally, the complement rule, which is where the real errors live. Not one of these prepositions takes a that-clause. You cannot write into he was late or past she arrived — the same rule that blocks despite he was late and due to he was late over in cause and purpose. What they do take is noun phrases and objective-case pronouns (past me, toward them). A subset noted entry by entry also takes a gerund (advised against going, a step toward solving it, talked me out of quitting, upon signing), and a gerund is what these prepositions want wherever a verb is called for — never a bare infinitive. A few — into, over, and up to in one extraposed frame — take a nominal wh-clause (look into what happened, a fight over who pays, it's up to you how we split it), which is a noun phrase in disguise rather than an exception to the ban. Pronoun case matters here and is frequently botched in coordination: walked past Ellen and me, never and I.

Owned by other files. Seven movement-capable prepositions have their homes elsewhere and are cross-referenced rather than repeated: to (the destination preposition proper — toward aims, to arrives), from (the source preposition proper), by (motion past something), for (destination of a departure — left for the airport, bound for Denver), at (a target, as in threw a rock at the window), in, and on. The purely static vertical prepositions — above, below, beneath, underneath — belong to the location group; only under and atop appear here, because those are the two members of that set that take motion freely (swam under the bridge, scrambled atop the boulder). Two more words are split between files rather than duplicated: beyond is here in its spatial sense and in the time file for beyond midnight, and after in its pursuit sense (ran after the bus) is noted with the sequencing preposition after. Multi-word phrases that are only incidentally directional — by way of, next to, on top of — live in complex and multi-word prepositions; the three whose whole job is direction, en route to, in the direction of, and out from under, are written out here.

Goal and destination#

Where the motion is headed, and what it hits when it gets there.

aboard#

Pronunciation: /əˈbɔrd/ · uh-BORD Type: compound (a- "on" + board, fused — the board is a ship's side) · Register: neutral, but tied to transport; formal or ceremonial outside it Complement: noun phrase only — a vehicle or vessel. No gerunds, no clauses, no ordinary places: aboard the terminal is wrong. It is also a bare adverb with no object at all (All aboard!, Welcome aboard), which is the usual test for telling the two uses apart. Pattern: [verb of motion] aboard [vessel / vehicle] · [verb] [object] aboard [vessel / vehicle] · aboard [vessel / vehicle], [main clause] Contrast: Aboard covers both the motion and the resulting position, so it does the work of onto and on at once — but only for craft. Everyday American English says get on the plane; climb aboard the plane is the heightened or official register (announcements, journalism, nautical writing). The spelling trap: the figurative "in agreement" sense is two words — get everyone on board with the plan, never onboard or aboard — while onboard as one word is an adjective for equipment (onboard navigation).

The vessel preposition. It began as sailors' vocabulary and spread to trains, buses, planes, and spacecraft, keeping its slightly formal flavor the whole way. Its figurative use — joining a crew, and so joining a company or a project — is now common in business English, but almost always as the adverb (welcome aboard) rather than with an object.

1. Motion onto a vessel or vehicle. The passengers filed aboard the ferry. 2. Position on a vessel or vehicle. There were nineteen people aboard the flight. 3. Joining a crew, staff, or undertaking (figurative). She came aboard the campaign in June.

  1. We climbed aboard the trawler before sunrise. (1)
  2. They loaded the horses aboard the transport one at a time. (1)
  3. Everyone aboard the train felt the jolt. (2)
  4. The instruments aboard the probe are still returning data. (2)
  5. Two of the three crew members aboard the vessel were rescued. (2)
  6. He came aboard the firm as a junior partner. (3)
  7. She brought two of her old analysts aboard the project with her. (3)
  8. Compare the two-word idiom: we need the union on board before we announce anything. (agreement — not this preposition)

against#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡɛnst/ · uh-GENST; also /əˈɡeɪnst/ uh-GAYNST (minority American, standard British); casual /əˈɡɛns/ · uh-GENS, with the final t dropped before a consonant (against the wall) Type: simple · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (advised against going) — never a finite clause. Write against the idea that he was lying, not against he was lying. Pattern: [verb of motion] against [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] against [noun phrase] · against [verb-ing] · stranded: the wall he threw it against Contrast: Against is the contact-with-force preposition; on and onto are neutral about force. Put the ladder on the wall is odd; lean it against the wall is right, because against supplies the pressure. For a target you aim at but may miss, use at — threw a rock at the window (aim) vs. threw a rock against the window (it landed).

Motion that terminates in forcible contact, or motion that pushes back on an opposing force. The static "leaning" sense is really the resting state left behind by that motion, which is why the same word covers both. From there the meaning generalizes to opposition of every kind — political, legal, and physical — and finally to a backdrop, where one thing is seen pressed up against another.

1. Motion ending in forcible contact. The hail rattled against the skylight. 2. Resting contact under pressure. He propped the bike against the fence. 3. Motion opposing a force. We were rowing against the current. 4. Opposition, hostility, competition. She voted against the measure. 5. Seen with something behind it. Black branches against a white sky. 6. Protection or insurance from. Vaccinated against measles.

  1. The rain beat against the windshield the whole way home. (1)
  2. He kicked the ball against the garage door until his mother made him stop. (1)
  3. She leaned her shoulder against the doorframe and waited. (2)
  4. Two rakes stood against the shed. (2)
  5. Walking against that wind was like walking uphill. (3)
  6. The salmon fight their way against the current for miles. (3)
  7. Every senator from the state voted against the bill. (4)
  8. Her lawyer advised against saying anything publicly. (4)
  9. The lighthouse showed white against the storm clouds. (5)
  10. His accent stood out against everyone else's. (5)
  11. The clinic vaccinates against four strains. (6)
  12. We're insured against flood but not against fire. (6)

See also: toward, into, onto, at, on

as far as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈfɑr æz/ · az-FAR-az; weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ · uhz-FAR-uhz Type: complex (three words) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun only. With a clause it stops being a preposition and becomes a conjunction (as far as I know, as far as anyone can tell) — a different construction with a different meaning. Pattern: [verb of motion] as far as [noun phrase] · [measurement] as far as [noun phrase] Contrast: As far as marks the far end of a distance actually covered; up to marks a ceiling that may not be reached (up to fifty people means fifty at most). We walked as far as the bridge says the bridge is where we stopped. Note also the disputed truncation as far as money, we're fine — standard edited English wants as far as money is concerned or simply as for money.

Names the limit of a movement or an extent: the point where the walking, the driving, or the seeing stops. The literal spatial sense extends easily to abstract reach (as far as the evidence goes). Beware the clause version, which is a completely different animal: as far as I know hedges a statement and has nothing to do with distance.

1. The farthest point of a motion. We drove as far as Flagstaff and turned back. 2. The limit of an extent or a view. Wheat as far as the horizon. 3. The limit of an abstract reach. The rules apply as far as the county line, and no further.

  1. She walked as far as the mailbox and had to sit down. (1)
  2. The bus goes as far as Ninth Street, then loops back. (1)
  3. Corn as far as the eye could see. (2)
  4. The flood reached as far as the second step. (2)
  5. His authority runs as far as the state line. (3)
  6. The grant covers tuition, but only as far as the first two years. (3)

See also: up to, to, toward, down to, back to

atop#

Pronunciation: /əˈtɑp/ · uh-TAHP; also /əˈtɔp/ · uh-TAWP in the regions where the cot–caught merger settles on the rounded vowel — the same variation you hear in on, onto, and upon. The vowel is the one in top, so speakers who keep the two apart use /ɑ/ here Type: compound (a- "on" + top, fused — the same a- that built aboard, ashore, astride, away) · Register: neutral to formal; commonest in journalism, sports writing, and physical description Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun only — no gerunds, no clauses of any kind. It is one of the most restricted prepositions in this file: it wants a concrete object that has a top. Unlike aboard, around, and along, it has no bare adverb use — he stood atop with nothing after it is not English. Pattern: [verb of motion] atop [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] atop [noun phrase] · [noun] atop [noun phrase] (the flag atop the dome) · sit / perch / sit atop [noun phrase] Variants: on top of — the ordinary spoken equivalent and the safer choice in plain prose; see on top of. The older a-top of is obsolete. Contrast: Atop is onto and on compressed into one word with a summit added: it asserts that the object has a high point and that the thing is at it. A book on the table is neutral; a book atop the table is faintly odd, because a table's top is not a summit. Reserve it for things with a real high point — a hill, a roof, a pole, a stack, a standings table. Against upon: both are raised in register, but upon is about contact and atop is about height.

The summit preposition. It was built the same way as aboard and ashore — a worn-down on fused to a noun — and it does in one word what on top of does in three, which is exactly why headline writers and sportswriters reach for it. Its figurative life is almost entirely about rankings and hierarchies: a team sits atop the standings, an executive sits atop an organization.

1. Position on the top of. A weather vane atop the barn. 2. Motion to the top of. He scrambled atop the boulder. 3. Highest in a ranking or a hierarchy (figurative). They finished the season atop the division. 4. Piled on; added to what is already there. Another delay atop the two we already had.

  1. A wind sock hung atop the pole at the end of the runway. (1)
  2. Snow was still sitting atop the ridge in June. (1)
  3. She climbed atop the stepladder to reach the smoke detector. (2)
  4. The cat hauled itself atop the refrigerator and stayed there all afternoon. (2)
  5. They finished the week atop the conference for the first time since 2011. (3)
  6. He spent twelve years atop the agency before anyone questioned the budget. (3)
  7. A second rate hike atop the one in March would break the deal. (4)
  8. The fee sits atop everything else the school already charges. (4)

See also: onto, upon, on, over, aboard, up

back to#

Pronunciation: /ˈbæk tu/ · BAK-too; weak /ˈbæk tə/ · BAK-tuh Type: complex (adverb back + preposition to, fixed as a unit) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (back to working nights) — never a finite clause. Pattern: [verb of motion] back to [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] back to [noun phrase] · back to [verb-ing] · dates back to [year] Contrast: Plain to is neutral about whether you have been there before; back to asserts you have. Go to Denver is a trip; go back to Denver is a return. Do not confuse the temporal sense (dates back to 1890) with since, which measures forward from a start point rather than reaching backward from now.

Return motion — to a place, a state, an activity, or a point in the past. The back carries all the "again" meaning; to supplies the destination. In its temporal sense it reaches from the present into the past, which is why it pairs with date, go, trace, and reach.

1. Return to a place. She flew back to Seattle on Sunday. 2. Return to a state or condition. Things went back to normal within a week. 3. Return to an activity or topic. Let's get back to the schedule. 4. Reaching into the past. The building dates back to 1904.

  1. We drove back to the hotel in the dark. (1)
  2. He moved back to Ohio after the divorce. (1)
  3. The swelling went back to normal overnight. (2)
  4. After the layoffs, nothing went back to the way it was. (2)
  5. She went back to teaching after ten years in software. (3)
  6. Getting back to your question — no, we haven't decided. (3)
  7. The family's records go back to the 1830s. (4)
  8. The feud traces back to an argument over a fence. (4)

See also: to, down to, up to, away from, from

down to#

Pronunciation: /ˈdaʊn tu/ · DOWN-too; weak /ˈdaʊn tə/ · DOWN-tuh Type: complex (two words) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (came down to choosing between two) — never a finite clause. Pattern: [verb of motion] down to [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] down to [noun phrase] · everything down to [noun phrase] Contrast: Directional down to mirrors up to and, like it, has an "as far as and including" sense — but down to the last detail scans the list from big to small, while up to fifty counts from zero to a ceiling. The British sense "attributable to" (it's down to you, that's down to the weather) is not American; Americans say up to you for responsibility and because of or due to for cause.

Motion to a lower place, a farther-along point, or a smaller amount. The "lower" is often social or geographic rather than literal — Americans go down to the city, down to Florida, and down to the store without any change in elevation.

1. Motion to a lower or farther point. We walked down to the water. 2. Reduction to a level. Trim it down to four minutes. 3. As far as and including the smallest item. She remembers it down to the color of his tie.

  1. He went down to the basement to reset the breaker. (1)
  2. They drove down to Austin for the weekend. (1)
  3. The doctor got her dosage down to five milligrams. (2)
  4. We cut the guest list down to forty. (2)
  5. The set is period-accurate down to the light switches. (3)
  6. Everything was accounted for down to the last penny. (3)

See also: down, up to, back to, as far as, to

en route to#

Pronunciation: /ˌɑn ˈrut tu/ · ahn-ROOT-too; also /ˌɛn ˈrut tu/ · en-ROOT-too. Weak /ˌɑn ˈrut tə/ · ahn-ROOT-tuh, with the same to → /tə/ reduction as up to and back to. The route here is /rut/ — ROOT, never the American /raʊt/ ROWT. ✗en ROWT is the pronunciation error to avoid Type: complex (three words — the French borrowing en route plus English to) · Register: neutral to formal; standard in travel, shipping, aviation, and news writing Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun — a destination or an outcome. A gerund is idiomatic in the figurative "on the way to achieving" sense that sportswriting made common (en route to winning her third title), but never a finite clause: en route to he arrived is not English. Strip the to and bare en route is an adverb with no object at all (the parts are en route), which is the test for telling the two uses apart. Pattern: [be / verb of motion] en route to [destination] · [clause], en route to [destination] · en route to [verb-ing] (figurative) Variants: ✗enroute — the closed spelling is common in aviation and military writing and is still treated as an error in edited American prose. Keep the two words. Contrast: En route to insists that the trip has started and has not finished, which is what separates it from plain to and from toward: flying to Denver reports the whole trip, en route to Denver reports only the part in progress. Against via: via names the waypoint and en route to names the destination not yet reached, so the two sit at opposite ends of the same journey and often appear in one sentence (en route to Santa Fe via Amarillo).

The in-transit preposition. English already owns to, toward, and on the way to, but none of them states as flatly that the motion is under way, which is why dispatchers, airlines, and delivery notifications run on this one. Sportswriting then borrowed it for a figurative sense — the points a player scores en route to a win are the ones scored along the way — and that use is now at least as common in American print as the literal one.

1. In transit to a destination. The tanker is en route to Galveston. 2. Stopping or passing through, on the way somewhere else. We spent a night in Amarillo en route to Santa Fe. 3. On the way to an outcome or an achievement (figurative). She broke two records en route to the title.

  1. The ambulance was en route to the hospital when the call was canceled. (1)
  2. Your package is en route to the sorting facility in Memphis. (1)
  3. Two of the crates were damaged en route to the warehouse. (1)
  4. The delegation stopped in Reykjavik en route to Brussels. (2)
  5. We refueled twice en route to the coast. (2)
  6. He threw for four touchdowns en route to a 38–10 win. (3)
  7. She set a personal best en route to winning her third state title. (3)
  8. The bill picked up nine amendments en route to the governor's desk. (3)

See also: to, toward, via, as far as, on to, by way of

in the direction of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ðə dəˈrɛkʃən əv/ · in-thuh-duh-REK-shun-uv; weak /ɪn ðə dəˈrɛkʃən ə/ · in-thuh-duh-REK-shun-uh — the final of reduces to a bare schwa in every ordinary use, exactly as it does in out of and off of. Both /dəˈrɛkʃən/ and /daɪˈrɛkʃən/ are standard American, and the unstressed /də-/ is the commoner one inside this phrase Type: complex (four words: preposition + determiner + noun + preposition) · Register: neutral, but four syllables longer than the word it replaces; in political and official prose it is usually a hedge Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun — the object belongs to the final of. It also takes a nominal wh-clause (in the direction of where the shots came from), which is a noun phrase in disguise; it never takes a that-clause. A gerund is grammatical but graceless (a move in the direction of settling) — write toward settling. Pattern: [verb of motion] in the direction of [noun phrase] · point / gesture / nod / look / turn in the direction of [noun phrase] · a step / a move in the direction of [noun phrase] Contrast: This is toward in four words, and toward is nearly always the better choice. The phrase earns its length in exactly two places: when the direction is being indicated rather than traveled (he nodded in the direction of the door — he did not move, so toward would mislead), and when a writer wants to name a bearing without claiming a destination (a move in the direction of reform promises less than a move toward reform). Against as far as: that names a limit actually reached; this names a bearing only. If toward fits, use toward.

The long form of toward, useful mainly for pointing rather than going. Because it names a bearing and commits to nothing else, it is also the standard hedge in official and political language — nobody who calls a policy a step in the direction of something has promised to arrive. Learners meet it constantly in reading and should recognize it on sight, but should reach for the one-word preposition when writing.

1. Moving on a bearing toward something. The smoke drifted in the direction of the highway. 2. Pointing, looking, or gesturing toward, with no motion of the pointer. She nodded in the direction of the back office. 3. Partial or hedged progress toward a goal (figurative). The vote is a step in the direction of full repeal.

  1. The herd moved slowly in the direction of the river. (1)
  2. He drove off in the direction of the interstate without saying where he was going. (1)
  3. Rescuers headed in the direction of where the last signal came from. (1)
  4. She tipped her chin in the direction of the man by the window. (2)
  5. The trooper pointed in the direction of the exit ramp. (2)
  6. Everyone in the diner turned in the direction of the noise. (2)
  7. The settlement is a small step in the direction of real oversight. (3)
  8. Nothing in the budget moves in the direction of the reforms they promised. (3)

in to#

Pronunciation: /ˌɪn ˈtu/ · in-TOO; weak /ɪn tə/ · in-tuh Type: complex (two words) — strictly the adverb/particle in plus the preposition or infinitive marker to, not a preposition in its own right · Register: neutral Complement: whatever the following to takes — a noun phrase or objective pronoun when to is a preposition (turned it in to the clerk), or a bare verb when to is the infinitive marker (came in to talk). Never a finite clause. Pattern: [verb + in] to [noun phrase] · [verb + in] to [bare verb] · [verb] [object] in to [noun phrase] Contrast: The mirror image of on to, and the test is the same. If "to a position inside" fits, write into as one word. If in belongs to the verb — turn in, hand in, drop in, come in, stop in, check in, log in, give in, cave in, tune in, sit in, call in — the words stay apart. He turned himself in to the police means he surrendered; he turned himself into the police means he became them.

Not a preposition, but the counterpart the onto / on to pair makes everyone expect — and the error it prevents is funnier and commoner than its sibling's. Two distinct things hide behind the same two words: to as a real preposition after a particle verb (hand it in to the office) and to as an infinitive marker of purpose (stopped in to say hello). Neither can ever be spelled into.

  1. She turned the paperwork in to the registrar on Friday. (particle verb: turn in)
  2. He dropped in to see how the remodel was going. (particle verb: drop in + infinitive)
  3. Log in to the payroll site with your employee number. (particle verb: log in)
  4. They finally gave in to the union's last demand. (particle verb: give in)
  5. I'll come in to sign the forms tomorrow morning. (particle verb: come in + infinitive)
  6. Turn your keys in to the front desk when you check out. (particle verb: turn in)
  7. Compare: she walked into the registrar's office. (true preposition — one word)

into#

Pronunciation: /ˈɪntu/ · IN-too; weak /ˈɪntə/ · IN-tuh; casual /ˈɪnə/ · IN-uh Type: compound (in + to, fused) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (got into arguing about it), or a wh-clause in the inquiry sense (an investigation into what went wrong, look into how this happened) — never a that-clause. Pattern: [verb of motion] into [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] into [noun phrase] · into [wh-clause] · stranded: the room she walked into; what did you get yourself into? Contrast: in is position, into is motion — He was in the pool vs. He jumped into the pool. This is the central contrast of the whole file. Separately, write in to as two words when in belongs to the verb: turned himself in to the police (he surrendered), came in to talk, dropped in to say hi. Turned himself into the police would mean he became them.

The general preposition of entry — motion from outside to inside, with the boundary crossed. From there it fans out into some of the widest polysemy in English: collision (where the "inside" is contact), transformation (where the thing enters a new state), penetration into a time span, inquiry, division, and the informal enthusiasm sense.

1. Motion to a position inside. She walked into the kitchen. 2. Motion ending in collision. He backed into a mailbox. 3. Change of state or form. The caterpillar turned into a moth. 4. Penetration into a period or a distance. They worked into the night. 5. Inquiry, investigation. An inquiry into the crash. 6. Enthusiasm for (informal). She's really into bluegrass. 7. Chance encounter (with run, bump). I ran into Marcus at the airport. 8. Division (arithmetic). Three into twelve is four.

  1. Pour the batter into the pan and level it off. (1)
  2. The dog bolted into the woods and didn't come back for an hour. (1)
  3. She backed into a pole in the parking garage. (2)
  4. He walked straight into the screen door. (2)
  5. Translate the whole thing into Spanish by Friday. (3)
  6. The argument turned into a shouting match. (3)
  7. We were three weeks into the semester before anyone noticed. (4)
  8. They kept rehearsing into the early morning. (4)
  9. The board opened an investigation into the missing funds. (5)
  10. I'll look into what happened with your order. (5)
  11. He's been into woodworking since he retired. (6)
  12. She's not into horror movies at all. (6)
  13. I ran into my old landlord at the hardware store. (7)
  14. We bumped into each other outside the courthouse. (7)
  15. Four into twenty goes five times. (8)
  16. Divide the class into groups of three. (8)

on to#

Pronunciation: /ˌɑn ˈtu/ · ahn-TOO; weak /ˈɑn tə/ · AHN-tuh Type: complex (two words) — strictly the adverb/particle on plus the preposition to, not a preposition in its own right · Register: neutral Complement: whatever to takes — noun phrase, objective pronoun, or gerund (moved on to fixing the roof) — never a finite clause. Pattern: [verb + on] to [noun phrase] · hold on to [noun phrase] · pass [object] on to [noun phrase] · move on to [noun phrase] Contrast: The whole entry exists for one test. If you can substitute "to a position on top of," write onto as one word. If on belongs to the verb — hold on, move on, pass on, hang on, sign on, log on, catch on — keep the two words apart. He held on to the rail (he gripped it) vs. He climbed onto the rail (he got up on it). Log on to the server, not log onto the server.

Not a preposition, but the single most useful non-entry in this file: American writers get onto and on to wrong constantly, and the error is always in the same direction — collapsing a particle verb into a false preposition. Read the sentence with on attached to the verb; if it still makes sense, the words stay apart. One genuine gray area: be on to someone ("have discovered what they're doing") is written both ways in edited American prose, with onto now slightly commoner.

  1. Hold on to the handrail — those steps are slick. (particle verb: hold on)
  2. Let's move on to the second item. (particle verb: move on)
  3. She passed the message on to her supervisor. (particle verb: pass on)
  4. Log on to the portal with your employee ID. (particle verb: log on)
  5. Hang on to that receipt in case it doesn't fit. (particle verb: hang on)
  6. He signed on to the project in March. (particle verb: sign on)
  7. Compare: she stepped onto the platform. (true preposition — one word)

onto#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑntu/ · AHN-too; also /ˈɔntu/ AWN-too; weak /ˈɑntə/ · AHN-tuh Type: compound (on + to, fused) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase or objective pronoun. Gerunds are marginal — onto winning is not idiomatic. Never a finite clause. Pattern: [verb of motion] onto [noun phrase] · [verb] [object] onto [noun phrase] · stranded: the ledge he climbed onto Contrast: on is position, onto is motion — The cat was on the roof vs. The cat jumped onto the roof. Against by for means: by is the ordinary word (by car, by mail), via is heavier and suggests a routed path. Against through: interchangeable for intermediaries (through a friend / via a friend), with via the more clinical choice. The full form by way of means the same and lives in the complex prepositions file.

The route preposition: it marks the waypoint between a start and a finish. Its second sense, a channel of transmission, is now the commoner one in everyday American writing — email, text, and courier all travel via something.

1. By way of a route or a stopping point. We flew to Lisbon via Newark. 2. By means of a channel, medium, or intermediary. Send the files via the shared drive.

  1. The train runs to Sacramento via Stockton. (1)
  2. They shipped it via Memphis to save two days. (1)
  3. Confirm your appointment via the patient portal. (2)
  4. I got the news via my sister-in-law. (2)
  5. The virus spreads via contaminated water. (2)
  6. Payment can be made via check or wire transfer. (2)

Directional forms that are not prepositions#

Three groups of words look like they belong in this file and do not. The test is the same as everywhere else: a preposition has an object; these do not.

The -ward forms. Homeward, upward, downward, forward, backward, onward, inward, outward, seaward, skyward, northward, heavenward are adverbs and adjectives, not prepositions. You can write the ship turned homeward, but not the ship turned homeward the harbor — with a named destination you need a real preposition (toward home, to the harbor). On the -s: American English prefers the bare -ward form for the adverb (moved forward, glanced upward, stepped backward), while British English leans to -wards; both are acceptable in the United States, but be consistent. As an adjective the -s is always wrong: an upward trend, never an upwards trend. The one member of the family that did become a preposition is toward, which took an object and never let go.

The bare adverbs and particles. Up, down, over, through, past, around, off, out, back, along, alongside, aboard, aside, apart, away, forth all appear with no object at all — sit down, the deal fell through, a truck rumbled past, come along, step aside. In that position they are adverbs, or particles belonging to the verb. The moment a noun phrase follows, check which it is: He ran up the hill (preposition — up the hill is a unit, and you can front it: Up the hill he ran) versus He ran up a big tab (particle — you can split it: He ran a big tab up). That movement test is the reliable one.

The archaic direction adverbs. Hence, thence, whence mean "from here, from there, from where," and hither, thither, whither mean "to here, to there, to where." They already contain the preposition, which is why from whence is a redundancy that has been criticized since the eighteenth century (though it appears in the King James Bible and in Shakespeare). None of them takes an object. In modern American English they survive only in hence meaning "therefore," in henceforth, and in the fixed phrase hither and thither.
