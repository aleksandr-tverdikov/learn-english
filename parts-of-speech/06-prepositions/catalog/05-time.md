# Time

> The prepositions that put an event on the clock and the calendar — when it happened, how long it lasted, and what it came before, after, or inside of.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file answers one of three questions: **when** (a point — *before noon*, *around six*), **how long** (a span — *during the war*, *throughout the winter*), or **by when** (a boundary — *until Friday*, *within an hour*). The trap that runs through the whole group is that the words do not cross between those three jobs. *During* names a period and cannot measure one, so *during three hours* is an error for *for three hours*; *since* names a starting point and cannot measure a stretch, so *since three years* is an error for *for three years*; *ago* measures a stretch and cannot name a point, so *Monday ago* is not English at all.

A second trap is grammatical rather than semantic. *After*, *before*, *since*, *till*, *until*, and archaic *ere* are prepositions when a noun phrase follows and conjunctions when a whole clause follows — *after the show* against *after the show ended*. Several more of them — *after*, *before*, *since*, *past*, *through*, *over*, *around*, *about*, *behind*, *beyond*, *throughout* — are adverbs or verb particles when nothing follows at all. **The preposition takes an object; the adverb does not.** *We ate soon after* is the adverb, *we ate after the show* is the preposition, and the difference is the noun phrase.

A third trap is the **-ing** form, and it is the one that produces the single commonest preposition error learners make. *After*, *before*, *since*, *upon*, *prior to*, *subsequent to*, *ahead of*, *in advance of*, *at the time of*, *in the course of*, and *in the middle of* all take a gerund — *after eating*, *upon signing*, *prior to filing*, *in the middle of moving*. *Through* and *into* take one after a measure phrase (*halfway through cooking dinner*, *ten minutes into filming*), and *between* takes two inside its paired frame (*between finishing school and starting the job*). *During* and *until* never take one at all: ***during eating*** and ***until leaving*** are errors, and the repairs are *while eating* or *in the middle of eating*, and *until he left*. That is why the **Complement** line is the first thing to read in every entry below.

The nine core prepositions carry a heavy share of the time load, but they have their own homes: *in*, *on*, and *at* live in [the core: in, on, at](01-core-in-on-at.md), and *of*, *to*, *for*, *from*, *by*, and *with* in [the core relational prepositions](02-core-relational.md). The contrasts that involve them — *by* vs. *until*, *for* vs. *since* vs. *ago*, *during* vs. *for*, *in* vs. *within*, and the American *quarter of three* — are worked out in the entries below and gathered again at the end of this file.


## Sequence: what comes before and what comes after

The words that order two events on a line. Every one of them has a plain register and a heavy one: *before* and *after* are the neutral pair, *prior to*, *subsequent to*, and *in advance of* the bureaucratic ones, *following* and *on the eve of* the journalistic ones, *upon* the contractual one, *post* the finance-desk one, and *ere* the archaic one. Four of these are also conjunctions, so check what follows: a noun phrase means preposition, a subject-and-verb means conjunction.

### after

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftər/ &middot; *AF-ter* &middot; no true weak form — the vowel keeps its quality even when unstressed, which makes *after* one of the few prepositions a learner can always hear
**Русский:** после — позже точки или события; амер. о минутах после часа: ten after six
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (*after eating*) — **never a finite clause**. *After the show ended* is the conjunction, a different word class doing a different job. It does take the relative pronoun in *after which* (*the vote, after which he resigned*). *After* is also an adverb (*we ate soon after*) and an attributive adjective (*the after party*); the preposition always has an object.
**Pattern:** *after* [noun phrase] &middot; *after* [verb-ing] &middot; [measure phrase] *after* [noun phrase] &middot; *just*, *right*, *soon*, *shortly*, or *long after* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *after which* [clause]
**Contrast:** *After* is the plain word; *following* is its newsroom dress, *subsequent to* its legal one, *post* its finance-desk one, and none of the three is clearer. Against *since*: *after* names one later point, while *since* opens a stretch that runs up to now — *after 2019* is any time later, *since 2019* is the whole span. On the clock, *after* is the standard American word for minutes past the hour (*ten after six*) where British English says only *ten past six*.

Places the main event later on the line than whatever follows *after*. It is the most general sequencing word in English and carries no meaning of its own beyond order — no cause, no consequence, no judgment about the size of the gap.

**1. Later than a point in time.** The pharmacy closes *after* nine.
**2. Later than an event.** *After* the surgery, she needed six weeks off.
**3. Repeated succession, in the frame [noun] after [same noun].** day *after* day
**4. Clock time: minutes past the hour (American).** a quarter *after* three
**5. In pursuit of, or in search of.** The dog took off *after* a squirrel.
**6. In imitation of, or in honor of.** She was named *after* her grandmother.

Senses 5 and 6 are not temporal at all, but they belong to the same word and appear constantly. Sense 6 is the American idiom; British English also says *named for*, which Americans use too but hear as slightly formal.

1. The pharmacy closes *after* nine.
2. Call me any time *after* Tuesday.
3. *After* the surgery, she needed six weeks off.
4. He apologized only *after* being asked twice.
5. Day *after* day, the same gray sky.
6. One delay *after* another pushed the launch to March.
7. It was a quarter *after* three when the power came back.
8. The shuttle leaves at ten *after* the hour, every hour.
9. The dog took off *after* a squirrel and didn't come back for an hour.
10. Reporters have been *after* him for a comment all week.
11. She was named *after* her grandmother.
12. It's a courtyard *after* the Spanish style.

### antecedent to

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæntəˈsidənt tu/ &middot; *an-tuh-SEE-duhnt TOO* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ and the whole phrase flattens under the sentence stress — *an-tuh-SEE-duhnt tuh*
**Русский:** до, прежде — юр./книжн.: предшествующий по времени, причине или очерёдности прав
**Type:** complex (the adjective *antecedent* + *to*) &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, philosophical, and older academic prose; dated everywhere else
**Complement:** a noun phrase (*antecedent to the sale*) or, far more rarely, a gerund (*antecedent to filing*) — **never a finite clause**. There is no *antecedent to he filed*; for a clause, use *before*. The gerund is grammatical on the model of *prior to filing*, but it is thin in real American usage and a noun phrase is the safe choice. The bare adjective (*the antecedent agreement*) and the grammarian's noun (*the antecedent of the pronoun*) are separate jobs for the same word; the preposition always has an object after *to*.
**Pattern:** *antecedent to* [noun phrase] &middot; [noun] *antecedent to* [noun phrase] &middot; *logically antecedent to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Antecedent to* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Three phrases in this file mean *before* in a suit — [prior to](#prior-to), [previous to](#previous-to), and *antecedent to* — and this is the heaviest of the three and the only one with an extra idea in it. *Antecedent to* leans toward priority in a chain, not merely earlier on a clock, which is why a cause is *antecedent to* its effect and a recorded lien is *antecedent to* a later judgment. Its mirror is [subsequent to](#subsequent-to). Do not confuse the preposition with the grammar term *antecedent* (the noun a pronoun points back to), and do not reach for it where *before* will do: in modern American prose it reads as period costume.

Earlier than — with the suggestion that the earlier thing also comes first in rank or in reasoning. An American meets it in property and evidence law, in philosophy, and in nineteenth-century treatises. In a memo or an email it is dead weight, and *before* is the word.

**1. Earlier in time than a stated event, in formal or legal prose.** The easement was recorded *antecedent to* the sale.
**2. Earlier in a chain of cause or reasoning, and not merely on the clock.** Every effect has a cause *antecedent to* it.
**3. Ranking ahead of a later claim, in the law of liens and priorities.** A mortgage *antecedent to* the judgment takes first position.

Sense 2 is the only one that earns five syllables, because *before* cannot carry the idea of logical priority — *the duty is before the contract* says something else. Sense 1 is *before* in a frock coat, and every American editor cuts it.

1. The easement was recorded *antecedent to* the sale and survived it.
2. *Antecedent to* the hearing, both sides exchanged exhibit lists.
3. Every effect must have a cause *antecedent to* it.
4. The duty of care is *antecedent to* any contract between them.
5. A lien *antecedent to* the judgment takes priority over it.
6. *Antecedent to* 1868, the state imposed no such requirement.
7. Nothing in the record shows an agreement *antecedent to* the closing.

**See also:** [prior to](#prior-to), [previous to](#previous-to), [preparatory to](#preparatory-to), [before](#before), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [ere](#ere)

### before

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *bih-FOR* &middot; weak /bəˈfɔr/ &middot; *buh-FOR*
**Русский:** до, перед — раньше точки или события; также «перед» инстанцией (testify before)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund (*before leaving*) — **never a finite clause**. *Before you leave* is the conjunction. *Before* is also an adverb with no object at all (*I've seen this movie before*), which is the surest test of the difference.
**Pattern:** *before* [noun phrase] &middot; *before* [verb-ing] &middot; [measure phrase] *before* [noun phrase] &middot; *just*, *shortly*, or *long before* [noun phrase] &middot; *before long*
**Contrast:** *Before* vs. *by*: *before Friday* excludes Friday, *by Friday* includes it — a deadline written with *before* is a day tighter than most writers intend. *Before* vs. *ago*: *ago* counts backward from now and takes the simple past (*three days ago*), *before* counts backward from a point already in the past and goes with the past perfect (*he had left three days before*). *Before* vs. *prior to*: identical in meaning, and *before* is a syllable shorter. *Before* vs. *until*: *finish it before Friday* is a deadline, *wait until Friday* is a duration.

Places the main event earlier on the line than whatever follows *before* — the exact mirror of *after*.

**1. Earlier than a point in time.** We should be there *before* noon.
**2. Earlier than an event.** Sign the lease *before* the walk-through.
**3. Earlier in an ordering or a queue, where the sequence is not literally temporal.** B comes *before* C.
**4. In the presence of a person or body with authority to judge.** She testified *before* the committee.
**5. Facing, or still ahead of, someone.** The whole summer lay *before* them.

Sense 4 is formal and mostly spatial — you appear *before* a judge, a court, a board, or a congregation — but it inherits the ordering logic of senses 1 through 3: the person judged comes first in line. Sense 5 is literary and always has a stretch of time or an opportunity as its subject.

1. The mail usually comes *before* noon.
2. Get here *before* eight or you'll miss the opening.
3. Read the whole contract *before* signing anything.
4. *Before* the storm, nobody had bothered to trim the trees.
5. She quit two weeks *before* the merger was announced.
6. In the alphabet, B comes *before* C.
7. Your name comes *before* mine on the list.
8. *Before* long, the whole office knew.
9. He appeared *before* the zoning board three times.
10. The bill goes *before* the full Senate on Thursday.
11. At nineteen, she had the whole thing *before* her.

**See also:** [after](#after), [prior to](#prior-to), [ahead of](#ahead-of), [ere](#ere), [until](#until), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [before (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#before)

### ere

**Pronunciation:** /ɛr/ &middot; *AIR* &middot; no weak form — the word is short, stressed, and identical in sound to *air*
**Русский:** устар./поэт. прежде, до — книжная замена before
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary
**Complement:** noun phrase — and, in older texts, a finite clause, where it is a conjunction instead (*ere the sun set*). A gerund is vanishingly rare and reads as pastiche; the period pattern is a bare noun phrase. No modern American writes any of it except for deliberate period effect.
**Pattern:** *ere* [noun phrase] &middot; *ere long* &middot; *ere* [the] [time noun]
**Contrast:** A dead-plain synonym of *before*, with nothing added but age. Do not confuse it with the homophone *e'er* (a contraction of *ever*), with *err* /ɜr/ ("to make a mistake"), or with *air*, which it is pronounced exactly like.

Earlier than. You will meet it in hymns, in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in nineteenth-century verse, and in modern writing that is reaching for one of those. It survives in living American speech only in the fixed phrase *ere long*, and even that is bookish.

**1. Earlier than a point in time.** They were on the road *ere* dawn.
**2. Earlier than an event.** Not a word was spoken *ere* the reading of the will.
**3. In the fixed adverbial *ere long*, before much time has passed.** *Ere* long, the whole village had heard.

Sense 3 is the only one a modern American is likely to write on purpose, and even it is a costume. Senses 1 and 2 are read, not written.

1. They were on the road *ere* dawn.
2. He swore he would return *ere* nightfall.
3. *Ere* long, the whole village had heard.
4. Not a word was spoken *ere* the reading of the will.
5. *Ere* the year's end, three of them were dead.
6. *Ere* the last bell, the church was already full.
7. *Ere* the frost came, the last of the hay was in.

**See also:** [before](#before), [prior to](#prior-to), [nigh](#nigh), [hence](#hence), [ere (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#ere)

### following

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ &middot; *FAH-loh-ing* &middot; no weak form — the stress stays on the first syllable and nothing reduces, which is part of why the word feels heavy next to *after*
**Русский:** после, вслед за — нейтр.-книжн., газетный оборот вместо after
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — heavily journalistic
**Complement:** a noun phrase, characteristically one naming an event (*following the announcement*). A gerund is grammatical but clumsy, and a finite clause is impossible — there is no *following the vote was counted*. Note that the same string is also a present participle (*the dog following me*) and an adjective (*the following week*).
**Pattern:** *Following* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *following* [noun phrase] &middot; *immediately following* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *After* means the same thing and is never ambiguous; *following* can be misread as the verb, which is why *Following the mayor, the reporters filed out* stalls a reader for half a second. Against *subsequent to*, *following* at least sounds like English. Reserve it for cases where the noun really is an event and *after* has already been used twice in the paragraph.

After, in time, with a faint suggestion that the second event grew out of the first.

**1. Later in time than a named event.** *Following* the vote, the chairman resigned.
**2. In the wake of, shading into cause.** Three stores closed *following* the flood.

That second sense is why press releases like it: *following* lets a writer imply a connection without claiming one. In careful prose that is a reason to distrust the word rather than a reason to use it.

1. *Following* the vote, the chairman resigned.
2. A press conference is scheduled *following* the hearing.
3. Three stores on Main closed *following* the flood.
4. *Following* two years of losses, the company sold its warehouse.
5. The reception begins immediately *following* the ceremony.
6. *Following* his release, he moved to Tucson and said nothing more about it.
7. *Following* the recall, the company replaced its entire safety team.

**See also:** [after](#after), [prior to](#prior-to), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [post](#post), [pending](#pending), [given](12-marginal-participial.md#given)

### in advance of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ədˈvæns əv/ &middot; *in ad-VANSS uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *in ad-VANSS uh*
**Русский:** заранее до, в преддверии — офиц.: до события, часто с подготовкой к нему
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — business, official, and journalistic
**Complement:** noun phrase or **gerund** (*in advance of filing*) — **never a finite clause**. There is no *in advance of he arrived*; for a clause, use *before*. The bare adverbial *in advance* (*pay in advance*, *a week in advance*) takes no object at all and is the commoner form by far.
**Pattern:** *in advance of* [noun phrase] &middot; *in advance of* [verb-ing] &middot; *In advance of* [event], [clause] &middot; [quantity of time] *in advance* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** *In advance of* is not a free synonym of *before*. It carries the idea of **preparation** — you do something early so as to be ready for what is coming. *She trained in advance of the race* is idiomatic because the training served the race; *she was born in advance of the war* is not, because birth prepares for nothing. Plain *before* has no such restriction, which is why it fits everywhere this phrase does and many places it does not. Against *ahead of*: the two overlap almost completely in the run-up sense, and *ahead of* is shorter. Against *prior to*: *prior to* is pure sequence with no preparation in it.

Earlier than a coming event, and usually in preparation for it. The phrase is four syllables where *before* is two, so it earns its place only when the preparation is the point.

**1. Early, in preparation for a coming event.** Crews sandbagged the levee *in advance of* the storm.
**2. In the run-up to a scheduled event, without the preparation sense.** Both sides filed briefs *in advance of* the hearing.

Sense 1 is the one that justifies the phrase. Sense 2 is the newsroom use, where it competes directly with *ahead of* and loses on length.

1. Crews sandbagged the levee *in advance of* the storm.
2. Please submit your questions *in advance of* the meeting.
3. She trained for six months *in advance of* the race.
4. *In advance of* filing, the firm circulated a draft to the client.
5. Both sides filed briefs *in advance of* Tuesday's hearing.
6. The hotel needs a deposit two weeks *in advance of* your stay.
7. *In advance of* the audit, the department rebuilt its whole ledger.

**See also:** [ahead of](#ahead-of), [before](#before), [prior to](#prior-to), [on the eve of](#on-the-eve-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by)

### in the aftermath of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈæftərˌmæθ əv/ &middot; *in thee AF-ter-math uhv* &middot; *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *aftermath*, and *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *in thee AF-ter-math uh*
**Русский:** после, в период после — о последствиях катастрофы или потрясения
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — journalistic and historical
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **disaster, a shock, or an upheaval** — *the flood*, *the shooting*, *the crash*, *the war*. **No gerund**: *in the aftermath of resigning* is possible but stiff, and *in the aftermath of his resignation* is the idiomatic repair. **No finite clause.** The article is fixed — *in aftermath of* is not English — and the noun *aftermath* also stands alone (*the aftermath was worse than the storm*), where it is not a preposition at all.
**Pattern:** *In the aftermath of* [event], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in the aftermath of* [event] &middot; *in the immediate aftermath of* [event]
**Contrast:** Against [in the wake of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-wake-of), its close twin: both mean "in the period following, and shaped by," and *in the wake of* is the wider of the two — a policy can follow *in the wake of* a report, where *the aftermath of a report* is odd unless the report did damage. **The restriction worth memorizing is the event: an aftermath is the bad stretch after something bad.** *In the aftermath of the wedding* is a joke; *in the aftermath of the fire* is not. Against [after](#after) and [following](#following), which are neutral about consequence, and against [on the heels of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-the-heels-of), which stresses how fast the second thing came rather than how much wreckage the first left.

In the stretch of time that follows a destructive event and is still shaped by it. The word is a farming term — *math* is an old word for a mowing, and the *aftermath* was the second growth that came up after the first cut — and that is exactly the image: what comes up in ground the event has already gone over.

**1. In the period following a disaster or a shock.** *In the aftermath of* the flood, half the block moved out.
**2. In the period following a political or institutional upheaval, with the consequences as the point.** *In the aftermath of* the scandal, three directors resigned.
**3. In the first hours or days after a disaster, in the narrowed frame with "immediate."** *In the immediate aftermath of* the crash, nobody knew how many were aboard.

Sense 3 is the one that pins the phrase down. Without *immediate* the stretch floats: the aftermath of a storm is a week, the aftermath of a war can be a decade, and the phrase itself never says which.

1. *In the aftermath of* the flood, half the block never came back.
2. *In the aftermath of* the shooting, the school stayed closed a week.
3. Insurance rates doubled *in the aftermath of* the fire.
4. *In the immediate aftermath of* the crash, nobody knew how many were aboard.
5. *In the aftermath of* the scandal, three board members resigned within a month.
6. *In the aftermath of* the war, the city rebuilt the whole downtown in brick.
7. The rule was written *in the aftermath of* a collapse nobody had planned for.

**See also:** [after](#after), [following](#following), [post](#post), [on the eve of](#on-the-eve-of), [in the wake of](11-complex-multiword.md#in-the-wake-of), [on the heels of](11-complex-multiword.md#on-the-heels-of)

### on the eve of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˈiv əv/ &middot; *ahn thee EEV uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/; *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *eve*
**Русский:** накануне — в день перед событием или в последний период перед ним
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to journalistic — heavily used in news writing and history
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming an **event**, and essentially nothing else (*on the eve of the election*, *on the eve of war*). **No gerund** — *on the eve of signing* is possible but stiff, and *on the eve of the signing* is the idiomatic repair. No finite clause. The article is fixed: *on eve of* is headline clipping, not prose.
**Pattern:** *on the eve of* [event noun] &middot; *On the eve of* [event], [clause] &middot; [clause] *on the eve of* [event]
**Contrast:** Literally it means the night or the day before, and in *on the eve of Christmas* it still does; figuratively it has loosened to mean "in the last stretch before," which can be a week or a year. That drift is why editors distrust it. Against *ahead of* and *in advance of*, both of which are neutral, *on the eve of* is the dramatic option — it exists to make the coming event sound momentous, so it wants a war, an election, a launch, or a verdict, not a dentist appointment.

Immediately before a significant event, with the event's importance built into the phrase. The noun *eve* is a worn-down *even*, the old word for evening, which survives independently in *Christmas Eve* and *New Year's Eve*.

**1. On the day or night immediately before, literally.** They arrived *on the eve of* the wedding.
**2. In the last stretch before a momentous event, figuratively.** *On the eve of* the election, the polls tightened.

Sense 2 has no fixed length and takes all the weight. If the event is not genuinely momentous, the phrase reads as inflation and *before* is the honest word.

1. They arrived *on the eve of* the wedding.
2. *On the eve of* the election, the polls finally tightened.
3. The company announced layoffs *on the eve of* the holidays.
4. *On the eve of* war, the embassy burned its files.
5. He resigned *on the eve of* the vote he was certain to lose.
6. *On the eve of* the launch, a sensor failed and cost them the window.
7. *On the eve of* the trial, the state offered a plea.

**See also:** [ahead of](#ahead-of), [in advance of](#in-advance-of), [before](#before), [prior to](#prior-to), [upon](#upon)

### post

**Pronunciation:** /poʊst/ &middot; *POHST* &middot; no weak form — the diphthong survives even unstressed, which is one reason the word still sounds like a prefix that got loose
**Русский:** после — деловой и газетный жаргон: post the merger «после слияния»
**Type:** simple (a preposition clipped out of the Latin prefix *post-*) &middot; **Register:** informal-to-neutral in business, finance, tech, and journalism; still rejected by conservative editors
**Complement:** a noun phrase, nearly always one naming an event or an era (*post the merger*, *post pandemic*). No gerund, no clause, and no article is required. It is far commoner as the hyphenated prefix (*postwar*, *post-9/11*, *post-op*), and that is the safe form in edited prose.
**Pattern:** *post* [event noun] &middot; *post* [era noun] &middot; *post-*[noun] as an attributive modifier
**Contrast:** It is *after*, in one syllable, borrowed out of a prefix. Against *following* and *subsequent to* — which are at least whole English words — *post* still reads as in-group jargon: *post the acquisition* is finance-speak for *after the acquisition*. The mirror form *pre* (*pre the merger*) exists but is rarer and sounds more British. When in doubt, hyphenate it onto a noun and it stops being controversial.

After a named event or era. The usage grew out of *postwar* and *post-Watergate*, where the prefix was doing real work, and then broke free of the hyphen sometime in the late twentieth century. American editors split on it: it is unremarkable in an earnings call and conspicuous in a magazine.

**1. After a specific event.** Margins recovered *post* the merger.
**2. After an era, usually with no article.** *Post* pandemic, nobody wants five days in the office.

1. Margins recovered *post* the merger.
2. *Post* pandemic, nobody wants five days a week in the office.
3. Head count is down twelve percent *post* the reorganization.
4. We'll revisit the forecast *post* earnings.
5. *Post* the recall, the stock never fully came back.
6. Everything in that neighborhood went up *post*-war.
7. *Post* the split, the two halves were worth more than the whole had been.

**See also:** [after](#after), [following](#following), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [prior to](#prior-to)

### preparatory to

**Pronunciation:** /prɪˈpærəˌtɔri tu/ &middot; *prih-PAIR-uh-tor-ee TOO* &middot; weak /prəˈpærəˌtɔri tə/ — the first syllable reduces to /prə/ and *to* to /tə/ — *pruh-PAIR-uh-tor-ee tuh*
**Русский:** перед тем как, в порядке подготовки к — офиц./воен.: как шаг к действию
**Type:** complex (the adjective *preparatory* + *to*) &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, military, procedural, and dated business prose
**Complement:** a noun phrase or **a gerund** (*preparatory to filing*, *preparatory to moving out*) — **never a finite clause**. There is no *preparatory to he filed*. The gerund is in fact the commoner complement, and that is what separates this phrase from [previous to](#previous-to) and [prior to](#prior-to), where the noun phrase dominates. The bare adjective (*preparatory work*, *a preparatory school*) drops the *to* and is a different job.
**Pattern:** *preparatory to* [verb-ing] &middot; *preparatory to* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *preparatory to* [verb-ing] &middot; *Preparatory to* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** It is *before* plus a purpose: whatever comes first is done **in order to get ready** for what follows, which is the same restriction [in advance of](#in-advance-of) carries and the one [prior to](#prior-to) does not. *He resigned preparatory to running for office* says the resignation served the campaign; *he resigned prior to running for office* says only that it came first. Against *in preparation for*, which is plainer, commoner in American writing, and takes a noun phrase only — the gerund frame is the one place *preparatory to* still earns its keep. Never use it where nothing is being prepared for: ***she was born preparatory to the war*** is nonsense.

Before, and as a preparation for. Nineteenth-century business and legal prose used the phrase freely; modern American writing keeps it in military orders, procedural manuals, and formal narration, where one step is being logged as a step toward the next.

**1. Before a stated action, as a step toward it, with a gerund.** He cleared his desk *preparatory to* leaving.
**2. Before a stated event, in preparation for it, with a noun phrase.** Troops moved south *preparatory to* the landing.

Sense 1 is where the phrase does work that nothing shorter does, because *in preparation for* cannot take a verb. In sense 2, with a plain noun after it, *in preparation for* is the more natural American choice and *ahead of* is shorter than either.

1. He cleared his desk *preparatory to* leaving for good.
2. The crew rigged the lines *preparatory to* casting off.
3. Two divisions were moved south *preparatory to* the landing.
4. She sold the house *preparatory to* moving to Phoenix.
5. *Preparatory to* filing, counsel circulated a draft of the complaint.
6. The company took a charge *preparatory to* the spin-off.
7. He cleared his throat *preparatory to* saying something he had clearly rehearsed.

### previous to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpriviəs tu/ &middot; *PREE-vee-uhs TOO* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ — *PREE-vee-uhs tuh*
**Русский:** до, прежде — книжн. и устаревающее; то же, что before, но тяжеловеснее
**Type:** complex (the adjective *previous* + *to*) &middot; **Register:** formal and dated — legal, historical, and older American prose
**Complement:** a noun phrase or a gerund (*previous to signing*) — **never a finite clause**. There is no *previous to he signed*; for a clause, use *before*. In the fixed *previous to that* the object is a pronoun pointing back at the last thing said. The adjective *previous* by itself takes no *to* (*the previous tenant*), and the adverb *previously* takes no object at all.
**Pattern:** *previous to* [noun phrase] &middot; *previous to* [verb-ing] &middot; *Previous to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *previous to that*
**Contrast:** An exact synonym of [prior to](#prior-to), which is itself an exact synonym of *before*. Of the three, *before* is the one to write, *prior to* is the one American legal and medical drafting actually uses, and *previous to* is the one that sounds like 1890 — rare enough now that a reader notices it. Usage guides have been striking it out since Fowler. Note that the adverb *previously* is alive and unremarkable (*he had previously worked in Boston*); it is only the preposition that is dated.

Earlier than. It carries nothing that *before* does not, and in American English it survives mainly in historical writing and in the fixed backward-pointing *previous to that*.

**1. Earlier than a stated event or date.** *Previous to* 1920, women could not vote in most states.
**2. Earlier than a stated action, with a gerund.** *Previous to* enlisting, he had never left the county.
**3. In the fixed backward-pointing idiom, before the time just mentioned.** She taught in Ohio; *previous to that*, she had been in the Navy.

Sense 3 is the only one an American is likely to say out loud, and even it competes with the plainer *before that*. Note that senses 1 and 2 both pull the past perfect after them, because the sentence is already standing at one past point and looking further back.

1. *Previous to* 1920, women could not vote in most state elections.
2. *Previous to* the hearing, neither side had seen the report.
3. He had held two other pastorates *previous to* this one.
4. *Previous to* enlisting, he had never been out of the county.
5. Nothing in the file is dated *previous to* the closing.
6. She runs a print shop; *previous to that*, she taught school.
7. *Previous to* the fire, the building had housed a bank.

**See also:** [prior to](#prior-to), [antecedent to](#antecedent-to), [preparatory to](#preparatory-to), [before](#before), [subsequent to](#subsequent-to), [ere](#ere)

### prior to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpraɪər tu/ &middot; *PRY-er TOO* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ — *PRY-er tuh*
**Русский:** до, ранее — офиц.-юр. канцелярский эквивалент before
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, bureaucratic
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund (*prior to signing*) — **never a finite clause**. For a clause you need *before*, since there is no *prior to he arrived*.
**Pattern:** *prior to* [noun phrase] &middot; *prior to* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *prior to* [noun phrase] &middot; *Prior to* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** It means *before*, exactly and only, in three syllables instead of two, and every American usage guide tells you to cut it. Keep it in contracts, protocols, and medical charts, where it is house style and where *prior* pairs with *subsequent*. Do not stretch it to mean [in advance of](#in-advance-of) in the sense of preparation — *she trained in advance of the race* is not *she trained prior to the race*.

Earlier than. The phrase is built on the adjective *prior* ("earlier, previous"), which is why it attracts writers who want a sentence to sound procedural. In ordinary prose it adds length, not precision: *prior to the meeting* and *before the meeting* are the same statement.

**1. Earlier than a stated event.** All materials must be submitted *prior to* the deadline.
**2. Earlier than a date or a year, as a background marker.** *Prior to* 2018, the building had no sprinkler system.
**3. Earlier than a stated action, with a gerund.** *Prior to* signing, review the indemnification clause.

Sense 3 is the one that carries real weight in drafting, because it attaches a requirement to an act rather than to a clock: *prior to signing* binds whoever signs, whenever that happens.

1. All materials must be submitted *prior to* the deadline.
2. *Prior to* 2018, the building had no sprinkler system.
3. Patients should fast for eight hours *prior to* the procedure.
4. *Prior to* signing, review the indemnification clause.
5. He held two other posts *prior to* his appointment as chief.
6. Any defect reported *prior to* delivery is the seller's problem.
7. *Prior to* dispensing the medication, the pharmacist must verify the allergy list.

### since

**Pronunciation:** /sɪns/ &middot; *SINSS* &middot; weak /səns/ in fast speech
**Русский:** с (такого-то момента) — от точки в прошлом до сих пор; длительность не мерит (ср. for)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **point** in time (*since Monday*, *since 2019*, *since the accident*), a time adverb (*since then*, *since yesterday*), or a gerund (*since leaving school*). **Never a length of time** — *since three years* is the commonest single error in this file, and the word for a length is *for*. With a finite clause it is a conjunction (*since I moved here*), and with no object at all an adverb (*I haven't seen her since*).
**Pattern:** [perfect verb] ... *since* [point in time] &middot; *since* [verb-ing] &middot; *ever since* [point in time] &middot; *Since* [point in time], [perfect clause] &middot; *since when* (fixed)
**Contrast:** **for / since / ago** is the triangle: *for* takes a length (*for three years*), *since* takes a starting point (*since 2022*), and *ago* takes a length and counts backward from now (*three years ago*). *Since* also demands the perfect in the main clause — *She has lived here since 2019*, never *she lives here since 2019*. The causal *since* ("because") is the conjunction, not this word, and *since* meaning "afterward" (*he has since retired*) is an adverb.

Opens a stretch of time that starts at a named point and runs up to the present moment. The "runs to now" part is built into the word, and it is why the main verb must be a perfect: the stretch is still open.

**1. From a past point up to now.** We've been waiting *since* eight.
**2. From a past point up to some other past point, with the past perfect.** By March he had not written *since* the funeral.

1. We've been waiting *since* eight o'clock.
2. She's lived in Tucson *since* 2019.
3. Nothing has been the same *since* the fire.
4. He hasn't touched a piano *since* leaving school.
5. Ever *since* the surgery, he's walked with a cane.
6. By March, she had not heard from him *since* the funeral.
7. The building had stood empty *since* the previous tenant's bankruptcy.

### subsequent to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbsɪkwənt tu/ &middot; *SUB-si-kwuhnt TOO* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ — *SUB-si-kwuhnt tuh*
**Русский:** после — юр./офиц. канцелярский эквивалент after
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — **never a finite clause**. There is no *subsequent to he resigned*; use *after* with a clause.
**Pattern:** *subsequent to* [noun phrase] &middot; *subsequent to* [verb-ing] &middot; [clause] *subsequent to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The mirror of *prior to*, and heavier still: five syllables for a word of two. It exists mainly so that legal drafting can pair *prior to* with something symmetrical. Outside contracts and audit reports, write *after*.

After. The adjective *subsequent* means "coming later," and the phrase inherits that and nothing more. It carries no implication of consequence, despite the family resemblance to *consequent*: *subsequent to the audit* says only that something happened later, not that the audit caused it.

**1. After a stated event.** *Subsequent to* the audit, three controls were rewritten.
**2. After a date or a cutoff, in claims, warranties, and financial statements.** Any claim arising *subsequent to* the closing date is the buyer's.
**3. After a stated action, with a gerund.** *Subsequent to* filing, the plaintiff amended the complaint twice.

Sense 2 is the one with a technical life of its own: accountants call anything that happens after the balance-sheet date a *subsequent event*, and the phrase is the standard label for it. Senses 1 and 3 are ordinary *after* in a suit.

1. *Subsequent to* the audit, three controls were rewritten.
2. Any claim arising *subsequent to* the closing date is the buyer's.
3. *Subsequent to* filing, the plaintiff amended the complaint twice.
4. The defect was discovered *subsequent to* delivery.
5. *Subsequent to* his resignation, the department was reorganized.
6. Two findings were disclosed *subsequent to* the balance-sheet date.
7. *Subsequent to* receiving notice, the tenant has ten days to cure.

### upon

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑn/ &middot; *uh-PAHN* &middot; also /əˈpɔn/ &middot; *uh-PAWN* &middot; the first syllable is already reduced to /ə/ and never takes stress, so there is no further weak form
**Русский:** по, при — книжн.: upon receipt «по получении», upon signing «при подписании»
**Type:** compound (*up* + *on*, fused) &middot; **Register:** formal — contracts, notices, and fixed phrases
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming an event (*upon arrival*, *upon receipt*) or **a gerund** (*upon signing*, *upon hearing the verdict*) — never a finite clause. The gerund is the construction that keeps the word alive in modern American writing.
**Pattern:** *upon* [event noun] &middot; *upon* [verb-ing] &middot; *Upon* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *on* or *upon* [receipt, arrival, completion, delivery]
**Contrast:** In the time sense *upon* means "at the moment of, and immediately after," compressing a whole clause: *upon arrival* is *as soon as he arrived* in two words. Plain *on* does the same job and is shorter (*on arrival*, *on receipt*); the two are interchangeable here and *upon* is the drafting choice. Against *after*, which allows any interval: *after signing* could be a week later, *upon signing* is that instant. The contact and movement senses live in [upon](04-movement-direction.md#upon).

At the moment of the stated event, and therefore immediately after it. This is the one job in modern American English where *upon* is not merely a dressed-up *on* — it is the standard way contracts, invoices, and official notices attach an obligation to a triggering event.

**1. At the moment a stated event occurs.** Payment is due *upon* receipt.
**2. With a gerund: as soon as someone does something.** *Upon* signing, you have three days to cancel.
**3. The frozen storytelling opener.** Once *upon* a time...

1. Payment is due *upon* receipt.
2. *Upon* arrival, please check in at the front desk.
3. The estate passes to her children *upon* her death.
4. *Upon* signing, you have three business days to cancel.
5. *Upon* hearing the verdict, he sat back down without a word.
6. The balance becomes payable *upon* completion of the work.
7. Once *upon* a time, this was all farmland.
8. Once *upon* a time there were three sawmills on this river.

## Duration: spans, stretches, and containment

These say how long, or inside what stretch. The rule that governs the section is that a **period** and a **length** are different things grammatically: *during*, *over*, *throughout*, and *through* take a named period (*the war*, *the summer*, *Tuesday*), while a measured length (*three hours*) needs *for* — or, when the point is a deadline rather than a duration, *within*. The three complex phrases in this section — *at the time of*, *in the course of*, and *in the middle of* — are the ones that will take a gerund where *during* cannot, and they are the honest repair for ***during eating***.

### at the age of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ði ˈeɪdʒ əv/ &middot; *uht thee AYJ uhv* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/, *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *age*, and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *uht thee AYJ uh*
**Русский:** в возрасте (скольких-то лет) — указывает возраст в момент события
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a number, and in ordinary use nothing else** — *at the age of six*, *at the age of ninety-two*. A noun follows only in a closed set of fixed legal and religious terms (*at the age of consent*, *at the age of majority*, *at the age of reason*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** The article is fixed: ***at age of six*** is not English, though the clipped *at age six* — no article, no *of* — is standard American and is commoner in print than the full phrase.
**Pattern:** *at the age of* [number] &middot; *At the age of* [number], [clause] &middot; *at age* [number] (the clipped American form) &middot; *at the age of* [consent, majority, reason] (fixed terms)
**Variants:** *at age* [number] (the standard American clipping — *at age fifteen*), *aged* [number] (chiefly British and newspaper style — *a boy aged fifteen*)
**Contrast:** **The live choice here is length, not meaning: *at the age of six*, *at age six*, and plain *at six* all say the same thing.** American writing uses the middle one most; the full phrase survives wherever a bare number would be misread, since *at six* also means six o'clock. Against *by the age of*, which is a deadline rather than a point — *she was reading by the age of four* claims four **or earlier**. Against *from the age of*, which opens a span (*from the age of twelve he worked summers*). And note [going on](12-marginal-participial.md#going-on), the colloquial hedge for an age not quite reached (*going on eighty*).

Fixes an event to a point in a life rather than to a point on the calendar. It is the standard frame of obituaries, biographies, medical histories, and legal findings, and it is one of the very few time phrases in English whose object must ordinarily be a number.

**1. At the point in a life named by a number.** She published her first book *at the age of* twenty-three.
**2. At a legally or ritually defined stage, in fixed terms.** The trust pays out *at the age of* majority.

The clipping is worth a note of its own. *At age fifteen* is not casual English — it is the form American newspapers, medical charts, and court opinions prefer, and it drops both the article and the *of*. What it cannot do is stand at the head of a sentence as comfortably: *At the age of fifteen, he started at the mill* reads better than *At age fifteen, he started at the mill*.

1. She published her first novel *at the age of* twenty-three.
2. He started at the mill *at the age of* fifteen and stayed forty years.
3. My grandmother learned to drive *at the age of* sixty.
4. *At the age of* four, he could already read the sports page out loud.
5. The diagnosis usually comes *at the age of* seven or eight.
6. The trust distributes the principal *at the age of* majority.
7. She died *at the age of* ninety-one, in the house she was born in.

**See also:** [at the time of](#at-the-time-of), [by the end of](#by-the-end-of), [come](#come), [going on](12-marginal-participial.md#going-on), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at)

### at the beginning of/at the start of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə bɪˈɡɪnɪŋ əv/, /ət ðə ˈstɑrt əv/ &middot; *uht thuh bih-GIN-ing uhv, uht thuh START uhv* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/ and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *uht thuh bih-GIN-ing uh*, *uht thuh START uh*
**Русский:** в начале — в первый момент периода, события или процесса
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral — *at the start of* a shade more spoken, *at the beginning of* a shade more written
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **period or a process with a shape** — *the year*, *the semester*, *the meeting*, *her career*. **A gerund is grammatical but clumsy and rare**: *at the beginning of writing the report* is repaired either to *when he began writing the report* or to *at the beginning of the report*. **No finite clause.** The article is fixed; bare *at start of* is form-field clipping. Note that *at first*, *to start with*, and *in the beginning* are adverbials with no object at all.
**Pattern:** *at the beginning of* [period] &middot; *at the start of* [period] &middot; *At the start of* [period], [clause] &middot; *right at the start of* [period] &middot; *from the start of* [period]
**Variants:** *at the start of* and *at the beginning of* are interchangeable in nearly every context; *at the outset of* is the formal third (*at the outset of the trial*); *at the top of* is the broadcast and business idiom for the near edge of an hour or a meeting (*at the top of the hour*, *at the top of the call*); *at the head of* is spatial, not temporal
**Contrast:** Its mirror is [at the end of](#at-the-end-of), and the two frames behave identically. Against [near](#near) and [toward](#toward), which both back off the edge: *at the start of the month* is the first day or two, *toward the beginning of the month* is looser and still moving. Against *from the start of*, which is the difference between a point and a span — *at the start of the season* names a moment, *from the start of the season* opens a stretch that runs on. And against the objectless *at first*, which cannot name the period it belongs to.

Pins an event to the near edge of a period or a process. Everything the frame does is edge-work: it says the event sat at the opening of the span, not somewhere inside it.

**1. At the opening of a period on the calendar.** Rent is due *at the beginning of* the month.
**2. At the opening of an event or a process already under way.** *At the start of* the meeting, she handed out the numbers.
**3. At the first stage of a career, a life, or a long undertaking.** *At the beginning of* his career he covered high school sports.

Choosing between the two wordings is a matter of rhythm and register, not of meaning. *Start* is the shorter, blunter, more spoken word and dominates in sports, work, and scheduling; *beginning* is the one that fits a life, a book, or a century. Neither can be shortened to *at the beginning* when a period is named — the *of* is not optional.

1. Rent is due *at the beginning of* the month, not the end.
2. *At the start of* the meeting, she handed out the revised numbers.
3. The furnace quit *at the beginning of* the coldest week of the year.
4. *At the start of* the season nobody picked them to finish above .500.
5. *At the beginning of* his career he covered high school football for forty dollars a game.
6. We heard about the change right *at the start of* the shift.
7. *At the beginning of* the semester the class had thirty students in it.

**See also:** [at the end of](#at-the-end-of), [at the time of](#at-the-time-of), [near](#near), [toward](#toward), [beginning](12-marginal-participial.md#beginning), [starting](12-marginal-participial.md#starting), [from](02-core-relational.md#from)

### at the end of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ði ˈɛnd əv/ &middot; *uht thee END uhv* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/, *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *end*, and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *uht thee END uh*
**Русский:** в конце — в последний момент периода; at the end of the day «в конечном счёте»
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period or a process (*the month*, *the meeting*, *his life*). **No gerund** — ***at the end of eating*** is not English, and the repair is *when he finished eating*. **No finite clause.** The article is fixed. The same frame is also spatial (*at the end of the hall*) and idiomatic (*at the end of the day*), and both take a noun phrase in exactly the same way.
**Pattern:** *at the end of* [period] &middot; *At the end of* [period], [clause] &middot; *at the very end of* [period] &middot; *at the end of the day* (fixed idiom)
**Variants:** *at the close of* (formal — *at the close of business*, *at the close of the century*), *at the tail end of* (informal, and always a little grudging)
**Contrast:** **Against [by the end of](#by-the-end-of), which is the contrast to drill: *at the end of the month* names a moment, *by the end of the month* names a deadline that anything earlier also satisfies.** A report due *at the end of* the month is wanted on the 30th; due *by the end of* the month, it can land on the 3rd. Against [near](#near) and [toward](#toward), which stop short of the edge. Against the objectless *in the end*, which means "finally" and names no period. And note *at the end of the day*, which in American English is nearly always the figurative filler meaning "when everything is considered" — a phrase about reasoning, not about five o'clock.

Pins an event to the far edge of a period or a process — the exact mirror of *at the beginning of*.

**1. At the closing moment of a period.** The lease is up *at the end of* June.
**2. At the close of an event or a process.** *At the end of* the interview, they asked for references.
**3. At the last stage of a life, a career, or a long run.** *At the end of* his life he moved back to the farm.
**4. In the fixed idiom "at the end of the day": when everything is taken into account.** *At the end of the day*, the numbers still have to work.

Sense 4 has no time in it at all. It is the American workplace's favorite filler, and a reader who hears it as a clock reference will misparse the sentence — which is the argument for cutting it and writing *ultimately*, or nothing.

1. The lease is up *at the end of* June.
2. *At the end of* the interview they asked for three references.
3. We settle up *at the end of* every month.
4. *At the end of* the season half the roster was gone.
5. *At the end of* his life he moved back to the farm he grew up on.
6. Her name is in the credits *at the very end of* the reel.
7. *At the end of the day*, the numbers still had to work.

### at the time of

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˈtaɪm əv/ &middot; *uht thuh TYME uhv* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/ and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *uht thuh TYME uh*
**Русский:** во время, на момент — в момент указанного события или действия
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — legal, insurance, journalistic, and academic
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming an event or a period (*at the time of the accident*), or **a gerund** (*at the time of writing*, *at the time of filing*) — **never a finite clause**. There is no *at the time of he arrived*; for a clause, use *when*. The frame *at the time that* [clause] is not an exception, since the clause there sits inside the noun phrase *the time*. The article is fixed in prose: bare *at time of* survives only as form-field clipping (*at time of purchase*, *at time of service*), which is why you meet it on receipts and nowhere else.
**Pattern:** *at the time of* [event noun] &middot; *at the time of* [verb-ing] &middot; *At the time of* [event], [clause] &middot; *at time of* [purchase, service, sale] (fixed transactional clipping)
**Contrast:** Against *during*, which spreads an event across a whole period: an accident has no inside, so *during the accident* is odd where *at the time of the accident* pins a single moment. Against *when*, which is shorter but needs a subject and a tense — *at the time of writing* is the phrase's one indispensable use, because *when I write this* says something else. Against [as of](#as-of): *as of this writing* dates the claim from outside and is the commoner American business form; *at the time of writing* puts the writer inside the moment.

Pins one event to the moment another event happened. It is the phrase that legal, insurance, and academic writing reach for when exact simultaneity is the thing at issue — a policy in force, an age, an address, a state of knowledge.

**1. At the moment a stated event occurred.** She was twenty-six *at the time of* the crash.
**2. With a gerund, at the moment of doing something.** *At the time of* writing, the bill had not been signed.
**3. In the transactional frame, at the moment a transaction takes place.** Payment is collected *at the time of* booking.

Sense 3 is the one that drops its article on forms and signs. *At time of purchase* is standard on a receipt and wrong in a sentence; write *at the time of purchase* in prose.

1. She was twenty-six *at the time of* the crash.
2. *At the time of* writing, the bill still had not been signed.
3. *At the time of* the merger, the company had nine hundred employees.
4. The policy had lapsed *at the time of* the fire.
5. Payment is collected *at the time of* booking, not at check-in.
6. He was living in Denver *at the time of* his father's death.
7. *At the time of* filing, neither party had counsel.

### between

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwin/ &middot; *bih-TWEEN* &middot; weak /bəˈtwin/
**Русский:** между — промежуток, ограниченный двумя моментами: between 1 and 2 «с часу до двух»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase, or the paired frame *between* [X] *and* [Y] — the second half must be joined by *and*, never by *or* and never by *to*. ***Between 2 to 4* is always an error**; write *between 2 and 4* or *from 2 to 4*. **Gerunds are fine in the paired frame** — *between finishing school and starting the job* — and so is a bare plural or mass noun with no *and* at all (*between meetings*). **No finite clause**: *between I got home and dinner* is an error, and the repair is a noun phrase on each side. Pronouns take the objective case: *between you and me*, never *between you and I*. *Between* is also an adverb in *in between* (*there's a week in between*), where it has no object.
**Pattern:** *between* [time A] *and* [time B] &middot; *between* [verb-ing] *and* [verb-ing] &middot; *between* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *between now and* [future time] &middot; *in between* [noun phrase] &middot; *somewhere between* [A] *and* [B]
**Variants:** *betwixt* (archaic and dialectal; alive only in *betwixt and between*), *'tween* (poetic)
**Contrast:** *Between* vs. *among*: the old rule that *between* is only for two is a myth — *between* is correct for any number of individually named points (*between 1990, 1995, and 2000*), and *among* is for an undifferentiated mass viewed collectively (*among the war years*). In time expressions *among* is almost never right. *Between … and* vs. *from … to*: *from 2 to 4* is a run you occupy, *between 2 and 4* is a window with slack in it — *I'll call between 2 and 4* promises one call, not two hours of talking. Against *within*: *between now and Friday* names two edges, *within two days* measures one interval from now.

Names the stretch that falls inside two stated edges, or in the gap that separates two recurring events.

**1. A window bounded by two stated times.** The pharmacy is closed *between* 1 and 2.
**2. The interval separating two recurring events.** She grades papers *between* classes.
**3. The stretch from now to a stated future edge, in the fixed frame *between now and* [time].** We have three inspections *between* now and closing.

Sense 2 needs a plural or a mass noun and no *and*: *between meetings*, *between semesters*, *between jobs*. It names the empty stretch that falls in the gaps, which is why *between jobs* is a polite way of saying unemployed. Sense 3 is the everyday planning frame, and it is the one place where *between* takes a single edge plus the present moment.

1. The pharmacy is closed *between* one and two.
2. I'll call you sometime *between* Tuesday and Friday.
3. *Between* 1994 and 2001, the population doubled.
4. *Between* finishing school and starting the job, she spent a summer in Maine.
5. She grades papers *between* classes.
6. The house sat empty *between* tenants for most of that year.
7. He wrote three novels *between* teaching jobs.
8. There's a lot to do *between* now and Friday.
9. Two meetings ran long, and I ate lunch somewhere in *between*.
10. *Between* you and me, the deadline was never real.

### during

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʊrɪŋ/ &middot; *DOOR-ing* &middot; also /ˈdɜrɪŋ/ &middot; *DUR-ing* &middot; no weak form — the stressed vowel keeps its quality, so *during* is always audible
**Русский:** во время, в ходе — внутри названного периода; длительность не измеряет (ср. for)
**Type:** simple (historically the participle of the dead verb *dure*, "to last") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **a noun phrase only** — a noun naming a period or an event (*during the war*, *during recess*). **Never a gerund** (*during eating* is an error for *while eating*), **never a finite clause** (*during I was there* is an error for *while I was there*), and **never a bare measurement of length** (*during three hours* is an error for *for three hours*). Unlike most of the words in this file, *during* is **only** ever a preposition — no adverb, no particle, no conjunction — so it must always have a noun phrase after it and nothing else will do.
**Pattern:** *during* [the period or event] &middot; *During* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *during* [noun phrase] &middot; *at some point during* [period]
**Contrast:** **during vs. for** is the big one: *during* answers *when* and takes a named period, *for* answers *how long* and takes a measured length. *It rained during the night* (when) against *it rained for six hours* (how long). **during vs. while**: *while* takes a clause. **during vs. throughout**: *throughout* insists on the entire span with no gaps, *during* only says somewhere inside it, which is why *he coughed during the concert* does not mean he coughed for two hours.

Locates an event inside a named stretch of time without saying where inside.

**1. At some unspecified point within a period.** The alarm went off *during* the meeting.
**2. Across the whole of a period, when the verb or the context forces that reading.** *During* the war she worked in a shipyard.

Which sense you get is decided by the verb, not by *during*: a punctual verb (*went off*, *called*) gives sense 1, a durative one (*worked*, *lived*) gives sense 2. The preposition itself is neutral between them.

1. The fire alarm went off *during* the meeting.
2. Somebody's phone rang twice *during* the second act.
3. *During* the war, she worked in a shipyard.
4. Nothing much grows here *during* the winter.
5. Please hold your questions *during* the presentation.
6. *During* my first year in Chicago, I didn't own a coat.
7. Three windows broke *during* the storm.
8. *During* the last two minutes, the arena never sat down.

### for the duration of

**Pronunciation:** /fər ðə dəˈreɪʃən əv/ &middot; *fer thuh duh-RAY-shuhn uhv* &middot; *for* reduces to /fər/ and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *fer thuh duh-RAY-shuhn uh*
**Русский:** на весь срок — офиц.: на всю продолжительность договора или события
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, military, insurance, and official
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **period or an event that has a term** (*the lease*, *the war*, *the investigation*, *his sentence*). **No gerund** — ***for the duration of eating*** is not English, and *for the duration of the meal* is the repair. **No finite clause.** The phrase also lives with no object at all, as the fixed adverbial *for the duration* (*he was interned for the duration*), and there it is not a preposition.
**Pattern:** *for the duration of* [period or event] &middot; *For the duration of* [event], [clause] &middot; *for the duration* (fixed adverbial, no object)
**Contrast:** Against [during](#during), which puts something at some point inside a period and says nothing about coverage: *he was quiet during the meeting* allows him a sentence or two, *he was quiet for the duration of the meeting* does not. That makes it a near-synonym of [throughout](#throughout); the difference is register and the object, since *throughout* takes any period at all while *for the duration of* wants one with a defined term — which is why it belongs to leases, policies, and orders. Against plain [for](02-core-relational.md#for), which takes a measured length: ***for the duration of three hours*** is padding for *for three hours*.

For the whole length of a stated period, start to finish. It is the drafting phrase for an obligation that is coextensive with a term — insurance that runs as long as a lease, a restriction that binds as long as an employment lasts.

**1. For the entire length of a stated term or agreement.** The tenant must carry insurance *for the duration of* the lease.
**2. For the entire length of an event or a condition.** Power stayed out *for the duration of* the storm.
**3. In the objectless fixed adverbial, for as long as the present emergency lasts.** They boarded up the storefront and left *for the duration*.

Sense 3 is a war fossil. Americans enlisted "for the duration of the war" in 1917 and again in 1941, the object wore off, and what was left was a phrase that assumes everyone knows which emergency is meant. Used today it is either historical or dryly funny.

1. The tenant must carry insurance *for the duration of* the lease.
2. Power stayed out *for the duration of* the storm.
3. The pass road is closed *for the duration of* the repairs.
4. He was barred from the building *for the duration of* the investigation.
5. *For the duration of* his term, the second seat stays empty.
6. The clause binds both parties *for the duration of* the agreement and two years after.
7. They boarded up the storefront and moved to Ohio *for the duration*.

### for the space of

**Pronunciation:** /fər ðə ˈspeɪs əv/ &middot; *fer thuh SPAYSS uhv* &middot; *for* reduces to /fər/ and *of* to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *fer thuh SPAYSS uh*
**Русский:** устар./книжн. в течение, на протяжении — о длительности в высоком стиле
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary — scripture, eighteenth- and nineteenth-century narrative, and deliberate pastiche
**Complement:** **a measured length of time, and nothing else** — *for the space of three days*, *for the space of an hour*. **Not a named period**: *for the space of the war* is not idiomatic, and [for the duration of](#for-the-duration-of) is the phrase for that. **No gerund, no finite clause.**
**Pattern:** *for the space of* [measured length] &middot; [clause] *for the space of* [measured length] &middot; *For the space of* [measured length], [clause]
**Variants:** *by the space of* and *about the space of* (both in older biblical English), *in the space of* (**a live modern phrase with a different meaning** — see the contrast below)
**Contrast:** It is plain [for](02-core-relational.md#for) with three extra words, and the extra words buy period flavor and nothing else: *for the space of three days* and *for three days* say exactly the same thing. A modern American meets it in scripture, in Defoe and Scott, and in writing imitating them. **Do not confuse it with *in the space of*, which is alive and means something different** — *in the space of a year* stresses how little time something took (*the whole thing collapsed in the space of a year*), where *for the space of a year* merely measures one out.

For a stretch of time of a stated length. The noun *space* here carries its old sense of an interval of time rather than of room, a sense that survives in *a short space*, *in the space of*, and *breathing space*.

**1. For a stated length of time — the archaic measuring use.** The crowd cried out *for the space of* two hours.
**2. In narrative, for a stretch through which nothing changed.** *For the space of* a week, no word came.

The two senses are one meaning in two settings: the phrase measures, and older narrative liked it because a measured stretch of nothing happening is easier to feel than a bare *for*. In modern American prose, write *for* — and if the point is how fast something happened, write *in the space of*.

1. The crowd cried out *for the space of* two hours and would not be quieted.
2. He stood in the doorway *for the space of* a full minute and said nothing.
3. *For the space of* three days the ship lay becalmed.
4. She was gone *for the space of* an hour and came back soaked.
5. The fever held the town *for the space of* a summer.
6. *For the space of* a week, no word came from the fort.
7. They kept the fast *for the space of* forty days.

**See also:** [for the duration of](#for-the-duration-of), [during](#during), [throughout](#throughout), [ere](#ere), [for](02-core-relational.md#for)

### in the course of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈkɔrs əv/ &middot; *in thuh KORSS uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *in thuh KORSS uh*
**Русский:** в ходе, в течение — офиц.: в какой-то момент внутри идущего процесса
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, official, and academic
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a process or a stretch (*in the course of the investigation*), or **a gerund** (*in the course of reviewing the file*) — **never a finite clause**. The gerund is the whole reason to keep the phrase: *during* refuses one, so ***during reviewing the file*** is an error and *in the course of reviewing the file* is the repair.
**Pattern:** *in the course of* [process noun] &middot; *in the course of* [verb-ing] &middot; *In the course of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *over the course of* [measured span] &middot; *in the ordinary course of* [business]
**Variants:** *over the course of* (the commoner form with a measured span — *over the course of three years*), *during the course of* (redundant; editors cut *the course of* and leave *during*), *in the ordinary course* and *in the course of employment* (fixed legal phrases that cannot be shortened)
**Contrast:** *In the course of* and *during* both put something inside a stretch, and *during* is three syllables shorter — which is why usage guides call the longer phrase padding. It earns its place in exactly two spots: in front of a gerund, where *during* cannot go, and in *in the course of employment*, where a whole doctrine of workplace liability hangs on the words. Against *over the course of*: *in* prefers a process (*in the course of the audit*), *over* prefers a measured span (*over the course of six months*). Against *throughout*, which claims every moment of the span: *in the course of* claims only some moment in it.

Somewhere inside a process or a stretch that is under way. The image is the older sense of *course* — a run, a path traveled — so the phrase suits things that unfold: an investigation, a career, a negotiation, an illness.

**1. At some point inside a process already under way.** *In the course of* the audit, two more accounts turned up.
**2. With a gerund, while doing something.** *In the course of* reviewing the file, she found the missing page.
**3. Across a measured span, in the frame *over the course of*.** Attendance doubled *over the course of* three seasons.

Sense 3 is where the phrase is genuinely useful in ordinary prose: *over the course of* spreads a change out along a span in a way that bare *over* leaves flat. Sense 1 is the one to cut whenever *during* will do the same work.

1. *In the course of* the investigation, two more accounts turned up.
2. *In the course of* reviewing the file, she found the missing page.
3. Nothing like it came up again *in the course of* his career.
4. The injury occurred *in the course of* employment, which is why the claim was paid.
5. Attendance doubled *over the course of* three seasons.
6. *Over the course of* six months, the debt was paid off entirely.
7. *In the course of* a single afternoon the whole plan fell apart.

### in the middle of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈmɪdəl əv/ &middot; *in thuh MID-ul uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/, and in fast speech the tail flattens to a single syllable — *in thuh MIDD-luh*
**Русский:** посреди, в середине — разг.: внутри периода или занятия, часто некстати
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — the everyday spoken form
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period or an activity (*in the middle of the night*, *in the middle of dinner*) or **a gerund** (*in the middle of eating*, *in the middle of moving*) — **never a finite clause**. *In the middle of I was eating* is an error and the gerund is the repair. That gerund is what makes this the everyday spoken substitute for *while*, and it is the reason ***during eating*** has an idiomatic fix at all.
**Pattern:** *in the middle of* [period] &middot; *in the middle of* [activity noun] &middot; *in the middle of* [verb-ing] &middot; *right in the middle of* [noun phrase] &middot; *be in the middle of* [something]
**Contrast:** Against *during*, which takes a noun phrase and stops there: *during dinner* and *in the middle of dinner* both work, but only the second can carry on into a verb — *in the middle of eating dinner*. Against *halfway through*, which actually measures: *halfway through the movie* is a position on the clock, *in the middle of the movie* usually means only "while it was running, at a bad moment." Against *at the time of*, which is neutral: this phrase almost always brings interruption with it.

Inside a period or an activity already under way, most often at the moment something cuts in. Literal centering is rare — *in the middle of the night* is any dark hour, not 12:00 sharp.

**1. At some point inside a period, characteristically an inconvenient one.** The phone rang *in the middle of* the night.
**2. Inside an activity already under way, with a noun.** He walked out *in the middle of* the ceremony.
**3. With a gerund, in the act of doing something.** I was *in the middle of* eating when they knocked.

Sense 3 is the one worth drilling. *During eating* is an error and *while eating* is the textbook repair, but what Americans actually say is *in the middle of eating* — and unlike *while*, it needs no subject and no tense.

1. The phone rang *in the middle of* the night.
2. He walked out *in the middle of* the ceremony.
3. We lost power right *in the middle of* dinner.
4. I was *in the middle of* eating when they knocked.
5. She's *in the middle of* moving and can't find anything.
6. *In the middle of* all this, the landlord raised the rent.
7. Don't call me *in the middle of* the workday unless something is on fire.

### into

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪntu/ &middot; *IN-too* &middot; weak /ˈɪntə/ before a consonant &middot; *IN-tuh*; casual /ˈɪnə/ &middot; *IN-uh*
**Русский:** вплоть до, вглубь периода — продолжение за границу: into the night «до глубокой ночи»
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period, nearly always with a modifier of extent in front of it (*far into the night*, *well into his eighties*, *three weeks into the semester*). **After a measure phrase a gerund is idiomatic too** — *ten minutes into filming*, *two years into running the shop*. **No finite clause**: *into he arrived* is not English. Note that *into* is only ever a preposition; it has no adverb or particle use, which is what separates it from bare *in* — *come in* is the adverb with no object, while *come into the room* still has one and is the preposition.
**Pattern:** [verb] *into* [the period] &middot; *far*, *well*, *deep*, or *late into* [the period] &middot; [measure phrase] *into* [period] &middot; [measure phrase] *into* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** **in vs. into** is the contrast to memorize here. *In* locates something inside a period; *into* adds motion, carrying the event across a boundary and onward — *they talked in the night* is a location, *they talked into the night* is a trespass past bedtime. Against *through*: *through the night* covers the night end to end, *into the night* only says the activity kept going past the point where it should have stopped. Against *within*: *three weeks into the semester* counts forward from the semester's start, *within three weeks* counts forward from now. The movement senses (*into the room*) live in [into](04-movement-direction.md#into).

Carries an activity across the edge of a period and keeps it going inside — or measures how far inside a period something happened.

**1. Continuing past a boundary and on into a period.** The argument went on *into* the night.
**2. At a stated distance inside a period already under way.** Ten minutes *into* the flight, an engine light came on.
**3. Deep inside a long stretch or a stage of life, with *well*, *far*, or *deep*.** She kept teaching well *into* her eighties.

Sense 1 almost always carries a note of excess or persistence — you work *into* the night, not into the afternoon. Sense 2 is a measuring use and is perfectly neutral; it is the standard American way to time-stamp something inside an event, and it is why sportscasters say *four minutes into the third quarter*. Sense 3 needs its extent adverb: *into his eighties* alone is odd, *well into his eighties* is idiomatic.

1. They argued *into* the night and settled nothing.
2. The renovation dragged on *into* a second winter.
3. The party ran late *into* Sunday morning.
4. Ten minutes *into* the flight, an engine light came on.
5. Three weeks *into* the semester, half the class had dropped.
6. Twenty minutes *into* filming, the generator quit.
7. Two years *into* running the shop, she still opens it herself.
8. She kept teaching well *into* her eighties.
9. The drought stretched far *into* the following spring.

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/ &middot; *OH-ver* &middot; no weak form — the second syllable is already a reduced /ɚ/ and the first keeps its diphthong; the poetic contraction *o'er* /ɔr/ is dead outside verse
**Русский:** в течение, за — о периоде целиком: over the weekend «за выходные»; также «свыше» о сроке
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period (*over the summer*), an activity or a meal (*over lunch*), a medium (*over the phone*), a quantity of time (*over an hour*), or a condition being left behind (*over the flu*). **No finite clause**, and **no gerund** — *over eating* is not this preposition but the compound verb *overeating*, a different word. The one place a gerund turns up nearby is the frame *over the course of writing it*, where the gerund is the object of *of* and not of *over* at all. *Over* is also an adverb (*it's over*, *come over*), an adjective (*the game is over*), and a verb particle (*think it over*, *the car flipped over*); **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *the meeting is over* with *we talked it out over lunch*.
**Pattern:** *over* [the period] &middot; *over the past / last* [measured stretch] &middot; *over* [meal, activity, or medium] &middot; *over* [quantity of time] &middot; *over the course of* [period] &middot; *be over* [an illness or a feeling]
**Contrast:** *Over* vs. *during*: *over* prefers a span you survey from outside, especially a recent one (*over the past decade*), while *during* prefers a named event. *Over* vs. *through*: *over the weekend* surveys the span from outside, *through the weekend* runs it end to end without a gap. *Over* vs. *for*: *over three hours* means "more than," *for three hours* means "that long"; the opposite of that *over* is *under* (*in under an hour*). *Over the weekend* and *on the weekend* are standard American; British *at the weekend* is not.

Spreads an event across a stretch of time that the speaker is looking at whole, from the outside. Every sense below is that same image applied to a different kind of stretch: a season, a measured run of years, a meal, a quantity, or an illness.

**1. Across the whole of a named period, viewed as a span.** We're driving to Duluth *over* the long weekend.
**2. Across a measured stretch counted back from now, in the frame *over the past / last* [quantity].** Attendance fell *over* the last five years.
**3. In the course of an activity, especially a meal, or by way of a medium.** We settled it *over* coffee.
**4. More than, with a quantity of time.** The delay lasted *over* an hour.
**5. Finished with, past the worst of — with an illness, a feeling, or a person.** She's *over* the flu.

Sense 3 is the one learners miss. *Over dinner* does not mean above the food; it means the conversation and the dinner occupied the same stretch, which is why *over drinks*, *over lunch*, and *over the phone* all work the same way. Sense 5 is the same reach-across image applied to a condition rather than a clock, and it is the sense that produces the flat American *I'm over it*.

1. We're driving to Duluth *over* the long weekend.
2. The shop stays open *over* the holidays.
3. Enrollment dropped sharply *over* the last five years.
4. *Over* the past decade the neighborhood has turned completely.
5. They worked out the whole deal *over* coffee.
6. She told me *over* dinner that she was quitting.
7. We hashed it out *over* the phone instead of flying anybody in.
8. The outage lasted *over* an hour.
9. He's been with the company *over* twenty years.
10. She was *over* the flu by Thursday and back at work Friday.
11. He said he was *over* it, and for once he sounded like he meant it.

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; *THROO* &middot; weak /θrə/ before a consonant in fast speech — *thruh* (*through the door* → /θrə ðə dɔr/)
**Русский:** по … включительно; на протяжении — Monday through Friday «с понедельника по пятницу»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase: a day, a date, a period, or the second element of a range. **After a measure phrase it also takes a gerund** — *halfway through cooking dinner*, *partway through reading it*. **No finite clause**: *through the movie ended* is not English. *Through* is also an adverb (*let me through*), a predicative adjective (*I'm through with it*), and a verb particle (*see it through*); **the preposition takes an object and the adverb does not** — compare *we're through* with *we're through Friday*.
**Pattern:** [start] *through* [end] &middot; *from* [start] *through* [end] &middot; *all through* [period] &middot; *through* [period] &middot; *halfway* or *partway through* [period or verb-ing] &middot; *get through* [a hard stretch]
**Variants:** *thru* (informal spelling — signs, notes, and menus only, never in prose)
**Contrast:** **This is the American inclusive-endpoint word, and it is one of the clearest AmE/BrE differences in the language.** *Monday through Friday* includes Friday. British English writes *Monday to Friday*, which many Americans read as possibly stopping at the start of Friday. Against *till* and *until*, which stop *at* the boundary rather than including it: *open until Friday* is genuinely ambiguous, *open through Friday* is not. Against *throughout*, which claims the whole span emphatically and takes no range: there is no *Monday throughout Friday*. In contracts the belt-and-suspenders form is *up to and including*. The path senses (*through the tunnel*) live in [through](04-movement-direction.md#through).

Runs an event along a stretch of time from one end to the other — and, in the American range use, includes the far end.

**1. Up to and including the stated endpoint of a range.** The office is open Monday *through* Friday.
**2. From the beginning to the end of a period, with no gaps.** He slept all *through* the movie.
**3. At a measured distance inside an activity, with *halfway*, *partway*, or a fraction.** We left halfway *through* the second act.
**4. Out the far side of a hard stretch, usually with *get*, *make it*, or *come*.** She got *through* the winter on savings.

Sense 1 is the workhorse, and it is why American schedules, store hours, and government notices are less ambiguous than British ones. Sense 2 usually wants *all* in front of it and overlaps with *throughout*. Sense 4 always implies difficulty: you get *through* a winter, a diagnosis, or a divorce, not through a vacation.

1. The clinic is open Monday *through* Friday.
2. Tickets are on sale *through* the end of March.
3. The exhibit runs June 3 *through* September 12.
4. Coverage lasts *through* December 31 and not a day longer.
5. He slept all *through* the second half.
6. She practiced *through* the whole summer without a break.
7. We kept the shop open *through* the holidays.
8. We left halfway *through* the second act.
9. Partway *through* cooking dinner, the power went out.
10. She got *through* the winter on savings and a lot of soup.

### throughout

**Pronunciation:** /θruˈaʊt/ &middot; *throo-OWT* &middot; weak first syllable /θrəˈaʊt/ &middot; *thruh-OWT* — the stress is always on *-out*
**Русский:** на протяжении всего, всё время — без перерывов через весь период
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period (or, in the spatial sense, a place). No gerund, no finite clause. It is also an adverb with no object at all (*the mood stayed tense throughout*), and that stranded use is common — **the preposition has an object, the adverb ends the clause bare.**
**Pattern:** *throughout* [the period] &middot; *Throughout* [period], [clause] &middot; [clause] *throughout* (adverb, no object)
**Contrast:** *Throughout* vs. *during*: *throughout* claims the **entire** span with no gaps, *during* is silent about coverage. *He was quiet during the meeting* allows him a sentence or two; *he was quiet throughout the meeting* does not. *Throughout* vs. *all through*: same meaning, and *all through* is the plainer, more spoken form.

For the whole of a period, from one end to the other, without interruption. The word is emphatic by construction — *through* plus *out* — so it is wasted on short spans: *throughout the afternoon* is normal, *throughout the minute* is not.

**1. For the whole of a period, with a state that holds without a break.** It rained *throughout* the weekend.
**2. At every point across a period, with events that keep recurring.** Interruptions came *throughout* the hearing.
**3. Everywhere inside a place — the spatial sense, listed here only so the split is visible.** The company has offices *throughout* the Southwest.

Senses 1 and 2 are the same claim of total coverage seen from two angles: sense 1 says a condition never lapsed, sense 2 says events kept coming and no stretch was spared. The verb picks the sense — a stative verb (*rained*, *held*, *stayed*) gives 1, a countable event (*came*, *cried out*, *rang*) gives 2. Sense 3 belongs with the place prepositions and has its own entry there.

1. It rained *throughout* the weekend.
2. *Throughout* the 1970s, the plant ran three shifts.
3. She stayed calm *throughout* the entire ordeal.
4. Prices held steady *throughout* the year.
5. *Throughout* his presidency, the same three advisers kept their jobs.
6. The baby cried *throughout* the flight.
7. Interruptions came *throughout* the hearing, and the chair finally cleared the room.
8. The same warning is repeated *throughout* the manual.

### within

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɪn/ &middot; *wih-THIN* &middot; weak /wəˈðɪn/ — the *th* is voiced, as in *this*
**Русский:** в пределах, не позднее чем через — within 48 hours «в течение 48 часов»
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a length of time (*within an hour*) or a bounded period (*within the fiscal year*); also the frame *within* [length] *of* [event]. **No gerund directly after *within*** — but the *of*-frame takes one freely, since the gerund there is the object of *of*: *within a week of moving in*. **No finite clause.** In literary use *within* is also an adverb or a noun (*a voice from within*), where it has no object.
**Pattern:** *within* [length of time] &middot; *within* [length] *of* [event] &middot; *within* [the bounded period]
**Contrast:** **in vs. within** is the contrast worth memorizing. *I'll be there in an hour* means at the end of the hour — sixty minutes from now. *I'll be there within an hour* means at any point before the hour is up. For a deadline, *within* is the unambiguous word and *in* is not. Informal American also says *inside of an hour* for exactly the *within* meaning. Against *by*: *by noon* fixes a clock time, *within two hours* fixes an interval measured from now.

Draws a boundary and puts the event inside it, before the far edge is reached. Everything *within* does is about a limit not crossed, which is why it is the deadline word of American contracts, warranties, statutes, and return policies.

**1. Before a stated amount of time has elapsed.** Reply *within* 48 hours.
**2. Not more than a stated distance in time from some other event.** She died *within* a week of her husband.
**3. At some point inside a bounded named period.** *Within* the fiscal year, the department has to spend it or lose it.

Sense 2 works in both directions — *within a week of the wedding* can be the week before or the week after, and only context settles it. Sense 3 comes closest to plain *in*, and it survives as a separate sense only because a fiscal year, a term, or a legislative session has hard edges to be inside of.

1. Please reply *within* forty-eight hours.
2. The package should arrive *within* three business days.
3. He was back on his feet *within* a month.
4. Cancel *within* thirty days and the fee is refunded in full.
5. She died *within* a week of her husband.
6. The lawsuit has to be filed *within* two years of the injury.
7. *Within* the fiscal year, the department has to spend it or lose it.
8. *Within* the first two weeks of the session, every bill has to be introduced.

## Boundaries: deadlines, endpoints, and starting lines

The words that put a wall somewhere on the timeline. Two rules govern the whole section. The first is the **by/until** split: *by* is a deadline for one finished action, *until* marks something that keeps going and then stops. The second is the **inclusive/exclusive** question — does *until Friday* include Friday? — which ordinary American English settles with *through* and legal drafting settles with *no later than* or *up to and including*.

### ahead of

**Pronunciation:** /əˈhɛd əv/ &middot; *uh-HED uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/ — *uh-HED uh*
**Русский:** раньше, накануне — раньше срока (ahead of schedule) или в преддверии события
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; sense 2 is journalistic
**Complement:** a noun phrase or a gerund (*ahead of signing the deal*) — never a finite clause. The fixed forms *ahead of schedule* and *ahead of time* take no article.
**Pattern:** *ahead of* [noun phrase] &middot; *ahead of* [verb-ing] &middot; *ahead of schedule*, *ahead of time* &middot; *Ahead of* [event], [clause]
**Contrast:** Against plain *before*: *ahead of* adds either "earlier than the plan called for" (*ahead of schedule*) or the newsroom sense "in the run-up to" (*ahead of Tuesday's vote*), which has spread fast since about 2000 and which some editors still resist. Note that *ahead of time* is idiomatic and *before time* is not English. The opposite is *behind*.

Earlier than a point that was already fixed. The word doing the work is *already*: there has to be a schedule, a due date, or a scheduled event for something to be ahead of, which is exactly the restriction plain *before* does not have.

**1. Earlier than a scheduled or expected point.** The bridge opened three months *ahead of* schedule.
**2. In the period leading up to an event.** Traders sold off *ahead of* the Fed announcement.

Sense 1 is old and universal. Sense 2 is newer, is everywhere in journalism, and is the one to use sparingly: *before the vote* says the same thing without sounding like a wire report. The same newsroom slot is filled by [on the eve of](#on-the-eve-of) when the writer wants drama and by [in advance of](#in-advance-of) when the point is preparation.

1. The bridge reopened three months *ahead of* schedule.
2. Pay the invoice *ahead of* the due date and you get two percent off.
3. Call me *ahead of* time if you're bringing anyone.
4. Traders sold off *ahead of* the Fed announcement.
5. *Ahead of* Tuesday's hearing, both sides filed briefs.
6. She resigned *ahead of* being formally charged.
7. *Ahead of* the holiday weekend, every flight out was already full.

### as at

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæz ət/ &middot; *AZ uht* &middot; weak /əz ət/ — *uhz uht*; both words reduce, so in speech the phrase is nearly a single unstressed foot
**Русский:** по состоянию на — брит./бухг.: на отчётную дату; в амер. английском пишут as of
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — **chiefly British and Commonwealth accounting**; in American English a foreign-office usage to recognize, not to write
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date**, characteristically a reporting date (*as at 31 December 2024*, *as at the balance sheet date*, *as at year end*). No clock time in ordinary use, **no gerund, no finite clause**. It **cannot look forward**: there is no *as at July 1 the rate goes up*, and that is the one grammatical difference from *as of*.
**Pattern:** *as at* [date] &middot; [figure] *as at* [date] &middot; *as at the balance sheet date* &middot; *as at year end*
**Contrast:** **The American equivalent is [as of](#as-of), and an American should write *as of*.** The two are not fully interchangeable even in Britain: *as at* is a snapshot and looks only backward, while *as of* does that **and** can point forward to a change taking force. *As at December 31* reports a balance; *as of January 1* can announce a new rate. Against [with effect from](#with-effect-from) and [as from](#as-from), which are the forward-facing Commonwealth phrases — the trio splits neatly, *as at* backward, *as from* and *with effect from* forward.

States the date at which a figure or a state of affairs is measured. It is the standard heading language of accounts prepared under IFRS and under UK, Canadian, Australian, and Indian practice — *Statement of Financial Position as at 31 December* — and an American meets it in a cross-border audit report, a foreign subsidiary's accounts, an IFRS filing, or a contract drafted in London.

**1. Measured at a stated reporting date — the accounting use.** Cash *as at* December 31 was $4.2 million.
**2. As things stood at a stated date, in official and legal prose more generally.** The register *as at* the date of this notice lists forty-one members.

American practice writes *as of* for both. Recognize *as at*, leave it alone when you are editing an IFRS statement to house style, and do not import it into American copy — to an American ear it reads as a typo for *as of*.

1. Cash and equivalents *as at* December 31 were $4.2 million.
2. The statement of financial position *as at* 31 March 2025 was signed by two directors.
3. The register of members *as at* the date of this notice lists forty-one names.
4. Inventory *as at* year end had already been written down twice.
5. Head count *as at* the reporting date was 1,180.
6. The valuation was prepared *as at* the last day of the quarter.
7. *As at* the close of business Friday, the account was still overdrawn.

**See also:** [as of](#as-of), [as from](#as-from), [with effect from](#with-effect-from), [effective](#effective), [at the time of](#at-the-time-of)

### as from

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæz frʌm/ &middot; *AZ FRUM* &middot; weak /əz frəm/ — *uhz fruhm*; in running speech both words reduce and the stress lands on the date
**Русский:** начиная с — брит. офиц.: с указанной даты; амер. эквивалент — as of
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — **chiefly British and Commonwealth**; in American English a Britishism to recognize, not to write
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date or a starting point** (*as from July 1*, *as from the date of this letter*, *as from the commencement date*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** It looks **forward only**: *as from* starts a clock, so there is no *as from last year we had four hundred orders*.
**Pattern:** *As from* [date], [clause] &middot; [clause] *as from* [date] &middot; *as from the date of* [document]
**Contrast:** The American forms are [as of](#as-of) and [effective](#effective): *as of July 1, the rate goes up*, or *effective July 1, the rate goes up*. British drafting also uses [with effect from](#with-effect-from), which is the same idea at greater length. Against [as at](#as-at), the backward-looking snapshot: *as from* opens a period, *as at* reads one. Against plain [from](02-core-relational.md#from), which does the same job in every register and is what most British writers now use anyway — the *as* adds only officialness.

Starting on a stated date. It announces that something takes force from that date onward, and it belongs to British and Commonwealth contracts, employment letters, and official notices.

**1. Taking force from a stated future date.** *As from* September 1, the office closes at four.
**2. Backdated — taking force from a stated past date.** Her salary is increased *as from* January 1.

Sense 2 is the one that carries money. A raise *as from* a date already past is a backdated raise, and arrears follow; American letters say *effective January 1* or *retroactive to January 1* and mean the same thing.

1. *As from* September 1, the office closes at four.
2. Her salary is increased *as from* January 1, and the arrears will be paid this month.
3. *As from* the date of this letter, you are no longer authorized to sign for the company.
4. The tariff applies *as from* the first shipment of the year.
5. *As from* next term, the fee covers materials.
6. The lease runs for five years *as from* the commencement date.
7. *As from* Monday, all deliveries go to the rear entrance.

### as of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæz əv/ &middot; *AZ uhv* &middot; weak /əz əv/ or /əz ə/ — *uhz uh*
**Русский:** с, по состоянию на — с указанной даты вперёд или на указанную дату как срез
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — business, legal, journalistic
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a date or a time point (*as of Monday*, *as of this writing*, *as of yesterday's close*). No gerund, no clause.
**Pattern:** *As of* [date], [clause] &middot; [clause] *as of* [date] &middot; *as of* [date], [figure]
**Variants:** *as from* (British; Americans write *as of* or *effective*), *as at* (British accounting, for a balance-sheet date)
**Contrast:** *As of* looks both directions, and that is its whole trick. Pointed forward it announces a change that takes force at that moment (*as of July 1, the rate goes up*); pointed backward it reports the state of things at that moment (*as of Friday, 400 tickets were left*). *Effective* can only do the first. *Since* opens a stretch; *as of* names its edge and says nothing about what follows.

Names a date and pins a statement to it. Which way the sentence points — forward to a change or backward to a state — is decided by the tense of the verb, not by *as of*, and that is why the phrase can do both jobs with the same two words.

**1. Starting from a stated date, looking forward.** *As of* Monday, masks are optional.
**2. Measured at a stated date, as a snapshot, looking backward.** *As of* Friday, we had 412 registrations.

The forward sense is an announcement; the backward sense is a reading off a meter. Sense 2 is why *as of this writing* and *as of now* are so useful — they date a claim without committing the writer to what happens next.

1. *As of* Monday, masks are optional in the building.
2. *As of* January 1, the deposit goes up to two hundred dollars.
3. *As of* Friday, we had 412 registrations.
4. The account balance *as of* yesterday's close was negative.
5. *As of* this writing, no arrests have been made.
6. She is, *as of* last month, the longest-serving member of the board.
7. *As of* six o'clock, the roads through the pass were still closed.

### behind

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈhaɪnd/ &middot; *bih-HYND* &middot; weak /bəˈhaɪnd/
**Русский:** позади, с отставанием от — отставая от графика или соперника; также «в прошлом»
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in the temporal sense, a small and mostly fixed set of noun phrases — *schedule*, *time*, *plan*, or a competitor (*behind the leader*). Related but distinct is *behind on* + an obligation, where *on* is required and **the object may be a noun or a gerund**: *behind on rent*, *behind on filing her taxes*. **No gerund directly after bare *behind***, and **no finite clause**. *Behind* is also an adverb (*he stayed behind*, *we fell behind*), a noun, and a place preposition; **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *we fell behind* with *we fell behind schedule*.
**Pattern:** *behind schedule* &middot; *run*, *fall*, or *get behind* [noun phrase] &middot; *behind* [competitor] &middot; *behind on* [obligation]
**Variants:** *behind time* (chiefly British; American English says *behind schedule*)
**Contrast:** The mirror of *ahead of*. Do not confuse the schedule sense with the place sense (*behind the garage*) or with *behind the times*, which means old-fashioned rather than late. Against *past*: *past the deadline* says the wall has been crossed, *behind schedule* says you are losing ground against a plan that is still running.

Puts something later than the standard it is being measured against — the mirror of *ahead of*. Like *ahead of*, it needs a plan, a clock, or a competitor for the lateness to be measured from; there is no *behind* in the abstract.

**1. Later than a plan or a schedule required.** The project is six weeks *behind* schedule.
**2. Trailing a competitor at a given moment.** He's a full lap *behind* the leader.
**3. In the past and finished with, from the speaker's point of view.** Those years are *behind* us now.

Sense 3 always has the period as subject and a form of *be* — *the worst is behind us* — and it carries a note of relief that the neutral *in the past* does not.

1. The project is running six weeks *behind* schedule.
2. We fell *behind* schedule the day the inspector showed up.
3. The train pulled in twenty minutes *behind* schedule. (British copy would say *behind time*.)
4. He's a full lap *behind* the leader.
5. Her fundraising is *behind* where it was at this point last cycle.
6. All of that is *behind* us now.
7. Two more weeks and the whole audit is *behind* us.
8. She's *behind* on rent for the second month in a row.
9. He's badly *behind* on filing his quarterly reports.

### beyond

**Pronunciation:** /biˈjɑnd/ &middot; *bee-YAHND* &middot; weak /bəˈjɑnd/ &middot; *buh-YAHND*
**Русский:** за пределами, после — позже указанного времени или предела: beyond midnight «за полночь»
**Type:** compound (*be-* + *yond*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a point in time or a limit; a gerund only in a closed set of idioms (*beyond caring*, *beyond saving*, *beyond repairing*). Never a clause. *Beyond* is also an adverb (*the fields beyond*) and a noun (*the great beyond*); **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *the years beyond* with *beyond the next two years*. The spatial senses belong with the place prepositions.
**Pattern:** *beyond* [point in time] &middot; *beyond* [a stated limit] &middot; *go*, *run*, *stretch*, or *extend beyond* [time] &middot; *beyond* [verb-ing] (closed set) &middot; *well beyond* [time]
**Contrast:** *Beyond midnight* and *past midnight* both report a line crossed; *beyond* is the more literary of the two and is likelier when the crossing is unwelcome, surprising, or beyond anyone's plan. Against *after*, which is neutral about limits: *the meeting ran beyond six* says six was supposed to be the end, and *the meeting ran after six* is not idiomatic at all. Against *over*: *over an hour* counts a quantity, *beyond an hour* pushes past a stated ceiling.

Puts an event on the far side of a limit, with the limit still in view. Where *after* names a later time and says nothing about it, *beyond* implies that the time it names was supposed to be an end.

**1. Later than a stated time or limit.** The talks went on *beyond* midnight.
**2. Longer than a period was meant to last.** Nothing on the schedule extends *beyond* Friday.
**3. Past the point of, in a closed set of idioms.** By then she was *beyond* caring.

Sense 3 is the evaluative one and it is always about exhaustion or ruin: *beyond help*, *beyond repair*, *beyond saving*, *beyond caring*. It is the same reach-past-a-limit image as sense 1 applied to a condition instead of a clock.

1. The talks went on *beyond* midnight.
2. Nothing on the schedule extends *beyond* Friday.
3. The lease runs *beyond* the end of the fiscal year.
4. He stayed at the paper well *beyond* retirement age.
5. Coverage does not continue *beyond* the warranty period.
6. By two in the morning she was *beyond* caring who won.
7. The car was *beyond* saving after the second winter.

### by the end of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ði ˈɛnd əv/ &middot; *by thee END uhv* &middot; *by* has no weak form of its own and keeps /baɪ/ even unstressed; *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *end*, and *of* reduces to /əv/ or bare /ə/ — *by thee END uh*
**Русский:** к концу — не позже последнего момента периода: by the end of the week «к концу недели»
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a period or a process (*the week*, *the quarter*, *the trial*). **No gerund** — ***by the end of eating*** is not English. **No finite clause.** The article is fixed.
**Pattern:** *by the end of* [period] &middot; *By the end of* [period], [clause] &middot; *by the end of the day* &middot; *by the end of business* [Friday]
**Variants:** *by close of business* and *by COB* (the office abbreviation), *by the close of* [period] (formal)
**Contrast:** **This is [at the end of](#at-the-end-of) turned into a deadline, and the two are not interchangeable.** *At the end of the month* names the last day; *by the end of the month* names the last acceptable day and takes any earlier one. It inherits everything from [by](02-core-relational.md#by): a punctual verb (*file*, *finish*, *pay*), a completed action, and no claim about what happens afterward. Against *before the end of*, which excludes the last day and is a day tighter. Against [no later than](#no-later-than), which is the same deadline in contract dress, and [within](#within), which measures an interval from now instead of naming a period.

Names a period and makes its far edge the deadline. The action may land anywhere inside the period; what the phrase forbids is landing outside it.

**1. On or before the last moment of a stated period — a deadline.** Have the draft to me *by the end of* the week.
**2. By the time a process has run its course — a vantage point rather than a deadline.** *By the end of* the trial, three jurors had been replaced.

Sense 2 is not a deadline at all, and it is worth separating because it changes the tense: it looks back from the far edge and takes the past perfect — *by the end of the war, half the town had moved away*. Sense 1 looks forward and takes a plain future or an imperative.

1. Have the draft to me *by the end of* the week.
2. The permits have to be filed *by the end of* the quarter.
3. *By the end of* the day, everybody had heard some version of it.
4. We should know *by the end of* the month whether the grant came through.
5. *By the end of* the trial, three jurors had been replaced.
6. *By the end of* the first winter, half the settlers were gone.
7. Get me an answer *by the end of* business Friday.

### from and after

**Pronunciation:** /frəm ənd ˈæftər/ &middot; *frum uhnd AF-ter* &middot; *from* and *and* both reduce hard — /frəm ən/ — so the phrase is heard as a single run, *frum-un-AF-ter*
**Русский:** юр. начиная с и после — дублет в договорах: с указанной даты и далее
**Type:** complex (a legal doublet) &middot; **Register:** legal — deeds, indentures, contracts, statutes, and municipal ordinances
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date or a dated event** (*from and after the Closing Date*, *from and after the effective date of this ordinance*, *from and after the date hereof*). **No gerund, no finite clause.**
**Pattern:** *from and after* [date] &middot; *from and after* [named event] &middot; [obligation] *from and after* [date] &middot; *from and after the date hereof*
**Variants:** *on and after* (the same doublet with the same meaning, and three syllables shorter; modern drafting manuals prefer it), *from and after the date hereof* (the fixed self-referential form)
**Contrast:** It is a doublet — two words doing one word's work, like *give and bequeath* or *null and void* — and it exists to close a gap that plain *from* might be argued to leave open: does a period running *from* June 1 include June 1? *From and after June 1* answers that it starts **on** June 1 and runs onward. Modern drafting guides call it surplusage and tell you to write *on and after* or simply *beginning*. Against [as of](#as-of), which dates a statement rather than starting an obligation running; against [with effect from](#with-effect-from), its Commonwealth cousin; against [on or after](#on-or-after), which sorts events into a window rather than starting a clock.

Beginning on the stated date and continuing thereafter. The phrase marks the moment an obligation, a title, or a rule starts to run, and the doubled words are there to make the starting day itself unarguable. An American meets it in a deed, a bond indenture, the effective-date clause of an ordinance, and the transition provisions of a state statute.

**1. Beginning on a stated date and continuing indefinitely.** Interest accrues *from and after* the Closing Date.
**2. Beginning at a named event.** *From and after* the recording of this deed, the covenants run with the land.

Both senses are one idea: a start line that includes its own day. The reason the doublet survived is that the money question — who owns the interest that accrued on the closing day itself — has actually been litigated, and *from* alone has lost it.

1. Interest accrues on the unpaid balance *from and after* the Closing Date.
2. *From and after* the effective date of this ordinance, no permit shall issue for such use.
3. The covenants run with the land *from and after* the recording of this deed.
4. Buyer assumes all liabilities *from and after* the Closing.
5. Rent shall be payable monthly *from and after* the Commencement Date.
6. *From and after* the date hereof, the guarantor is released.
7. Coverage applies to claims made *from and after* the retroactive date.

### in or about

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ər əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *IN er uh-BOWT* &middot; *or* reduces to /ər/ and *in* takes no stress, so the phrase runs together as *in-er-uh-BOWT*
**Русский:** юр. в или около — в указанном месяце или году либо примерно в это время
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal — pleadings, indictments, affidavits, and investigative reports
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **stretch rather than a day**: a month, a season, a year, or a vague period (*in or about June 2019*, *in or about the summer of 2020*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** The same documents use the phrase of place as well (*in or about the vehicle*), which is a different sense of *in* and not a time expression at all.
**Pattern:** *in or about* [month and year] &middot; *in or about* [year] &middot; *in or about* [the season or period] &middot; [clause] *in or about* [period]
**Contrast:** **It is the period-sized twin of [on or about](#on-or-about), and the two split by the object: *on or about* takes a date (*on or about June 3*), *in or about* takes a month, a season, or a year (*in or about June 2019*).** Pleaders keep the same hedge and swap the governing preposition to match the size of the object, exactly as ordinary English does with *on Tuesday* against *in June*. In ordinary prose write *around* or *sometime in* — *sometime in June 2019* is what a newspaper prints.

In the stated month, season, or year, or near enough to it that the difference is immaterial. Like *on or about*, it exists to keep an allegation alive when a witness's memory is imprecise, and it is a fixture of American charging documents and civil complaints.

**1. In a stated month or year, or near it — the pleading use.** *In or about* March 2018, the defendant opened the account.
**2. In a stated season or vague stretch.** The payments stopped *in or about* the fall of 2020.

The hedge runs both directions and has no stated width, which is exactly what a pleader wants and exactly what makes the phrase useless outside a legal document. Written anywhere else it reads as evasion rather than as care.

1. *In or about* March 2018, the defendant opened an account in a false name.
2. The complaint alleges that the payments stopped *in or about* the fall of 2020.
3. She was hired *in or about* January 2016 and terminated two years later.
4. *In or about* 1997, the company began storing the drums behind the plant.
5. The witness recalls a conversation *in or about* the week of the storm.
6. Defendant resided at that address *in or about* the period charged in Count Two.
7. The parties began negotiating *in or about* the summer, though nobody wrote anything down.

### no later than

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈleɪtər ðən/ &middot; *noh LAY-ter thuhn* &middot; *than* always reduces to /ðən/ — never a full *THAN*
**Русский:** не позднее — крайний срок, названная дата или время включаются
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — contracts, procurement, military, official notices
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a point in time: a date, a clock time, or a named event (*no later than close of business*). No gerund, no finite clause. **Not a length of time** — *no later than three hours* is an error for *within three hours*.
**Pattern:** [obligation] *no later than* [point in time] &middot; *No later than* [date], [clause] &middot; *NLT* [date] (military and government shorthand)
**Variants:** *not later than* (the older form, standard in statutes and regulations), *NLT* (abbreviation, US military and federal usage), *no earlier than* (the mirror form, which opens a window instead of closing one; a pair of them fixes both edges)
**Contrast:** It is *by* with the ambiguity drafted out. *By Friday* leaves a reader wondering whether Friday itself counts; *no later than Friday* includes Friday and closes the question, which is exactly why contracts use the longer phrase. Against *before*, which excludes the stated day and is therefore a day tighter. Against *within*, which measures an interval from now rather than naming a fixed date.

The deadline phrase of American legal and administrative English. It names the last acceptable moment and includes it. Because it is explicit about inclusion, it is the safe form whenever the difference between two adjacent days has money or a right attached to it.

**1. By a stated date or clock time, that date included.** Proposals must be received *no later than* 5 p.m. on March 3.
**2. By a point measured off another event.** The tenant shall give notice *no later than* thirty days before the lease ends.

Sense 2 is the drafting workhorse, and it is where learners slip: the measured phrase is still naming a **point**, not a length. *No later than thirty days before closing* picks a square on the calendar; *no later than thirty days* picks nothing and should be *within thirty days*.

1. Proposals must be received *no later than* 5 p.m. on March 3.
2. The tenant shall give notice *no later than* thirty days before the lease ends.
3. Please submit the reimbursement form *no later than* the end of the quarter.
4. Report to the gate *no later than* forty minutes before departure.
5. *No later than* Friday, we need a decision from the board.
6. Payment is due *not later than* the fifteenth of each month.
7. Bids may be filed *no earlier than* March 1 and *no later than* March 15.

### on or about

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑn ər əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *AHN er uh-BOWT* &middot; *or* reduces to /ər/
**Русский:** юр. такого-то числа или около того — когда точная дата несущественна
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** legal — pleadings, indictments, affidavits, contracts
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date**, and essentially nothing else. No clock time in ordinary use, no gerund, no clause.
**Pattern:** *on or about* [date] &middot; [clause] *on or about* [date] &middot; *On or about* [date], [clause]
**Contrast:** It is *about* with a legal safety net. *About June 3* hedges and leaves June 3 itself sounding excluded; *on or about June 3* hedges while conceding that the exact day may have been June 3 after all. Pleaders use it so that a date proved to be off by a day does not defeat the allegation. In ordinary prose *around June 3* says the same thing without sounding like a subpoena.

On the stated date, or close enough to it that the difference does not matter legally. It is one of the few phrases in English whose entire purpose is to preserve a claim against an imprecise memory, and it is nearly invariable in American criminal charging documents.

**1. On a stated past date, or near enough that the difference is immaterial — the pleading use.** *On or about* June 3, the defendant deposited the check.
**2. On a scheduled future date, allowing for slippage — the contract use.** Delivery is scheduled to occur *on or about* the first of the month.

Sense 1 protects an allegation from a witness's imperfect memory; sense 2 protects a promise from a carrier's imperfect logistics. Both are hedges, and neither belongs outside a legal document — in ordinary prose, write *around*.

1. *On or about* June 3, the defendant deposited the check.
2. The parties entered into the agreement *on or about* January 15, 2019.
3. She was last seen *on or about* the evening of the fourteenth.
4. Delivery is scheduled to occur *on or about* the first of the month.
5. *On or about* that date, the account was closed and the funds withdrawn.
6. Closing shall occur *on or about* October 15, subject to the lender's schedule.
7. The complaint alleges that the calls began *on or about* the first week of April.

### on or after

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɑn ər ˈæftər/ &middot; *AHN er AF-ter* &middot; *or* reduces to /ər/ and the two content words share the stress — *AHN-er-AF-ter*
**Русский:** такого-то числа или позднее — юр. формула включающей даты вступления в силу
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal and legal — statutes, regulations, contracts, insurance policies, and tax rules
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date or a dated event** (*on or after January 1*, *on or after the effective date*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** **Not a length of time**: ***on or after thirty days*** is an error for *no earlier than thirty days after* or *thirty days or more after*.
**Pattern:** [rule] *on or after* [date] &middot; [event] *occurring on or after* [date] &middot; *for tax years beginning on or after* [date] &middot; *on or after the effective date of this act*
**Contrast:** It opens a window where [on or before](#on-or-before) closes one, and a pair of them fixes both edges. Against plain [after](#after), which leaves the named day itself in doubt: a policy covering losses *after* January 1 invites an argument about January 1 that *on or after January 1* forecloses. That is the entire reason the phrase exists, and it is the same instinct that produced [no later than](#no-later-than) and *up to and including*. Against [from and after](#from-and-after), the deed-and-indenture doublet, which starts an obligation running rather than sorting events into a window.

On the stated day, or any day later than it. American statutes and regulations use it to fix the first day a new rule bites; insurance and tax drafting use it to sort events into the old regime or the new one along a bright line.

**1. Starting on a stated day, that day included — the effective-date use.** This section applies to returns filed *on or after* January 1.
**2. Of an event, occurring on the stated day or later.** The policy covers claims first made *on or after* the retroactive date.

The two senses differ only in what is being sorted — a rule in sense 1, an event in sense 2 — and in both the whole work is done by the two words *on or*. Strike them and the boundary day becomes arguable, which in a tax year or a claims-made policy is worth real money.

1. The amendment applies to tax years beginning *on or after* January 1, 2026.
2. The policy covers claims first made *on or after* the retroactive date.
3. Any purchase made *on or after* the closing is for the buyer's account.
4. Tickets bought *on or after* the price change are nonrefundable.
5. *On or after* the effective date of this act, no new licenses shall be issued.
6. Employees hired *on or after* July 1 are in the new plan.
7. Deliveries scheduled *on or after* the fifteenth were canceled.

**Русский:** не позднее такого-то числа включительно — юр./офиц. формула срока

**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date or a dated event** (*on or before April 15*, *on or before the tenth day of each month*, *on or before the expiration date*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** **Not a length of time**: ***on or before thirty days*** is an error for *within thirty days*.

**Contrast:** It is the deadline phrase of American drafting and means exactly what [no later than](#no-later-than) means; the two are interchangeable, with *on or before* commoner in tax, court, and contract language and *no later than* commoner in procurement and government notices. Against plain [before](#before), which **excludes the stated day** and is therefore a full business day tighter — *file before April 15* and *file on or before April 15* are different deadlines. Against [by](02-core-relational.md#by), which ordinary readers take to include the day but which has never been proof against a lawyer, and that gap is why the longer phrase exists. On the stated day or any day earlier — a deadline with the last day explicitly counted in.

**1. By a stated calendar date, that date included.** Returns must be filed *on or before* April 15.

**3. By a date fixed off another event, in court orders and procedural rules.** The exhibit list is due *on or before* the tenth day before trial.

Sense 3 is where learners slip, and the slip is the same one [no later than](#no-later-than) invites: the measured phrase is still naming a **point** on the calendar, not a length. *On or before the tenth day before trial* picks a square; *on or before ten days* picks nothing and should be *within ten days*.

1. Federal returns must be filed *on or before* April 15.

### past

**Pronunciation:** /pæst/ &middot; *PAST* &middot; no weak form of its own, but in the fixed *half past* fast speech clips it to /pəs/, so *half past six* comes out *haf-puss-six* &middot; identical in sound to *passed*, which is the trap
**Русский:** после, за — минут после часа: twenty past four «двадцать минут пятого»; позже предела
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase — a clock hour, a time point, or a limit. A gerund appears only in a closed set of idioms (*past caring*, *past praying for*, *past believing*). No clause. *Past* is also an adverb (*he drove past*), an adjective (*the past week*), and a noun (*the past*); the preposition has an object.
**Pattern:** [minutes] *past* [the hour] &middot; *half past* [hour] &middot; *past* [time point] &middot; *past* [a limit or a peak] &middot; *way* or *well past* [time]
**Contrast:** On the clock, American English prefers *after* for minutes past the hour (*ten after six*) where British English uses only *past* — but *half past* and *quarter past* are alive and well in American speech, so *past* is no Briticism. British colloquial *gone six* ("just after six") has no American equivalent. Beware the homophone *passed*, which is the verb: *we drove past the exit* but *we passed the exit*. Against *after*: *past midnight* stresses that a boundary was crossed, *after midnight* just names a later time. The movement sense (*drove past the house*) lives in [past](04-movement-direction.md#past).

Puts a time on the far side of a line: the minute hand past a number, the clock past midnight, a thing past the end of its useful life. The line is always there in the meaning, which is what separates *past* from the flatly neutral *after*.

**1. Minutes later than the stated hour.** It's twenty *past* four.
**2. Later than a stated time, with the sense of a line crossed.** It was *past* midnight when they got in.
**3. Beyond the limit of a period, a condition, or a capacity.** He's *past* his prime.

Sense 3 is where the word turns evaluative: *past the deadline* is a failure, *past caring* is exhaustion, *past its expiration date* is a warning. That judgment lives in *past* and not in *after*.

1. It's twenty *past* four.
2. The meeting starts at half *past* nine.
3. It was *past* midnight when they finally got in.
4. The kids were up way *past* their bedtime.
5. The milk is a week *past* its expiration date.
6. He's *past* his prime as a starter, but he can still relieve.

### pending

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɛndɪŋ/ &middot; *PEN-ding* &middot; no weak form — the stressed first syllable holds its vowel
**Русский:** до, в ожидании — офиц./юр.: до вынесения решения; также «при условии»
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, business, official

**2. Subject to, conditional on — a shading of the same sense.** The sale is final, *pending* inspection.

1. The officer was suspended *pending* the outcome of the review.

**Русский:** до — разг. вариант until; регион. амер. о часах: ten till six «без десяти шесть»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a point in time, or — as a conjunction — a finite clause (*till the rain stops*). **Never a length of time**: *till three hours* is an error for *for three hours*. No gerund.
**Pattern:** *till* [point in time] &middot; *from* [time] *till* [time] &middot; *not ... till* [point in time] &middot; *up till* [point in time]
**Variants:** *'til* (a back-formation from *until*; common but disliked by editors), *til* (nonstandard), *until* (the fuller and slightly more formal word)
**Contrast:** **Contrary to almost universal belief, *till* is not a shortened *until*** — it is the older word by several centuries, and *until* was built out of it. ### until

**Pronunciation:** /ʌnˈtɪl/ &middot; *un-TIL* &middot; weak /ənˈtɪl/, /ənˈtəl/ &middot; *uhn-TIL*
**Русский:** до — действие длится вплоть до момента и там прекращается; not … until «только тогда»
**Type:** compound &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **point** in time, or — as a conjunction — a finite clause (*until she calls*). **Never a length of time** (*until three hours* is an error for *for three hours*) and **never a gerund** (*until leaving* is not English; write *until he left*).
**Pattern:** *until* [point in time] &middot; *from* [time] *until* [time] &middot; *not ... until* [point in time] &middot; *Until* [point in time], [clause] &middot; *up until* [point in time]
**Variants:** *till* (the older, slightly more spoken form), *up until* and *up till* (emphatic), *'til* (nonstandard but common)
**Contrast:** **by vs. until is the most useful contrast in this file.** *By* is a deadline for a single completed action: *Finish the report by Friday.* *Until* marks a state or an activity that continues up to that point and then stops: *Stay until Friday.* The verb decides — punctual verbs (*finish*, *submit*, *arrive*, *pay*) take *by*; durative ones (*wait*, *stay*, *keep*, *sleep*) take *until*. *Wait by Friday* and *finish until Friday* are both broken. Against *through*: *until Friday* may or may not include Friday, *through Friday* certainly does.

Marks the far end of a state or an activity that runs up to it and then stops.

**1. Continuing up to a point in time, and stopping there.** The store is open *until* nine.
**2. In the *not … until* frame, the point at which something finally happens.** He didn't call *until* Tuesday.
**3. The whole stretch leading up to a turning point, in the emphatic *up until*.** *Up until* the recount, she thought she'd won.

Sense 2 is worth practicing, because the negative is doing real work: *he didn't call until Tuesday* says the call happened on Tuesday. Without the negative the sentence means something else entirely. Fronted for emphasis, the frame inverts: *Not until Tuesday did he call.* Sense 3 adds nothing to the meaning of sense 1 except emphasis on the length of the run-up, and *up till* is its more spoken twin.

1. The store is open *until* nine on weeknights.
2. We stayed *until* the last song.
3. *Until* last year, the road was gravel.
4. The offer stands from now *until* the end of the month.
5. He didn't call *until* Tuesday.
6. Nothing was decided *until* well after midnight.
7. She didn't find out *until* the movers were already loading the truck.
8. Up *until* the recount, she thought she'd won.
9. *Up until* about 1990, every one of those storefronts was a hardware store.

**See also:** [till](#till), [through](#through), [up to](#up-to), [pending](#pending), [no later than](#no-later-than), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [until (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/03-time.md#until)

### up to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌp tu/ &middot; *UP TOO* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ — *UP tuh*
**Русский:** вплоть до; не более чем — up to now «до сих пор», up to ten days «максимум десять дней»
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a time point (*up to now*, *up to last Friday*) or a quantity of time (*up to six weeks*). No clause. Note that *up to* + gerund belongs to a different idiom entirely (*up to no good*, *what have you been up to*), as does the "your decision" sense (*it's up to you*).
**Pattern:** *up to* [time point] &middot; *up to* [quantity of time] &middot; *up to and including* [date] &middot; *up to now*, *up to this point*
**Contrast:** As a boundary, *up to* means *until*, but it is the natural choice with *now* and with measured quantities, where *until* cannot go: *up to three hours* is fine, *until three hours* is not. Against *within*: *up to six weeks* states a maximum, *within six weeks* promises a deadline. In contracts, *up to and including* settles the inclusive-endpoint question that ordinary American prose settles with *through*.

Runs a stretch out to a stated edge and stops there — either the edge of a span already traveled or the ceiling on one still to come. Which of the two you get depends on whether the object is a point or a quantity.

**1. Until a stated point, especially the present moment.** *Up to* now, the plan has worked.
**2. No more than — a stated maximum length.** Delivery takes *up to* ten business days.
**3. In the drafting phrase *up to and including*, out to a stated endpoint with that endpoint counted in.** The lease runs *up to and including* December 31.

Sense 2 is the one that ends up in advertising and warranties, and it is deliberately weak: *up to 40% off* promises nothing except a ceiling. Read it as an upper bound, never as an estimate. Sense 3 is what contracts write instead of trusting *through*, and it is the only form in this file that states inclusion in so many words.

1. *Up to* now, the plan has worked.
2. Everything was fine *up to* last Friday.
3. *Up to* the very end he insisted the numbers were fine.
4. Delivery takes *up to* ten business days.
5. The battery holds a charge for *up to* eighteen hours.
6. The lease runs *up to and including* December 31.
7. Coverage continues *up to and including* the last day of the month.
8. *Up to* the day he retired, he opened the shop himself.

**See also:** [until](#until), [till](#till), [through](#through), [within](#within), [as of](#as-of), [no later than](#no-later-than), [up to (complex)](11-complex-multiword.md#up-to)

### with effect from

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ɪˈfɛkt frəm/ &middot; *with ih-FEKT frum* &middot; *with* takes no stress and is /wɪð/ or /wɪθ/; *from* reduces to /frəm/, so only *effect* keeps a full vowel — *with-ih-FEKT-frum*
**Русский:** начиная с (о вступлении в силу) — брит. офиц.; амер. эквивалент — effective
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal — **chiefly British and Commonwealth** official, HR, and contract prose
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **date or a dated event** (*with effect from 1 April*, *with effect from the date of this letter*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** The objectless cousin is *with immediate effect*, which takes nothing at all and corresponds to American *effective immediately*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *with effect from* [date] &middot; *With effect from* [date], [clause] &middot; *w.e.f.* [date] (Commonwealth abbreviation) &middot; *with immediate effect* (no object)
**Variants:** *w.e.f.* (the abbreviation, standard in Indian and other Commonwealth official writing and not used in American English), *with effect on and from* (the belt-and-suspenders drafting form)
**Contrast:** The American equivalents are [effective](#effective) and [as of](#as-of): an American memo says *effective April 1, the rate goes up* where a British one says *with effect from 1 April, the rate will increase*. It is three words where *effective* is one, and it does nothing *effective* cannot do. Against [as from](#as-from), the shorter Britishism for the same job. Against [as at](#as-at), which looks backward and cannot start anything. An American meets this phrase in a multinational's policy memo, an academic appointment letter, or a contract drafted outside the United States.

Taking force from the stated date. It names the moment a rule, an appointment, a rate, or a resignation begins to operate, and it is the standard formula of British and Commonwealth official writing.

**1. Taking force from a stated future date.** The fee increases *with effect from* 1 April.
**2. Backdated — taking force from a stated past date.** She was promoted *with effect from* January 1.
**3. In the objectless form "with immediate effect": at once.** He was suspended *with immediate effect*.

Sense 3 is the one Americans do borrow, and it is worth noticing why: *with immediate effect* sounds colder and more institutional than *effective immediately*, which is why it turns up in press statements about firings. The rest of the phrase stays British.

1. The subscription rate increases *with effect from* 1 April.
2. She was appointed to the chair *with effect from* the start of the academic year.
3. *With effect from* Monday, the rear entrance will be closed to visitors.
4. His resignation was accepted *with effect from* 31 December.
5. The allowance is payable *with effect from* the date of transfer.
6. Salaries are revised *w.e.f.* 1 July each year.
7. He was suspended *with immediate effect* pending the inquiry.

**See also:** [effective](#effective), [as of](#as-of), [as from](#as-from), [as at](#as-at), [from and after](#from-and-after), [from](02-core-relational.md#from)


## Approximation: roughly when

Seven ways to hedge a time. *About* and *around* are interchangeable in American English and cover everything; *circa* is restricted to dates; *near* is a static position close to a point; *toward* means "as it approaches, and not yet arrived"; *nigh* survives only in *nigh on*; and *long about* is the regional one, Southern and Midland, that says *around* in a particular voice. The legal hedges are [on or about](#on-or-about) and [in or about](#in-or-about), and the colloquial one for durations and ages is [going on](12-marginal-participial.md#going-on) (*going on three years*, *going on eighty*).

### about

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *uh-BOWT* &middot; clipped to /baʊt/ in casual speech — *'bout*
**Русский:** около, примерно — приблизительное время или срок: about seven «около семи»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in this sense, a noun phrase naming a clock time, a date, or a quantity of time. **No gerund and no finite clause in the time sense.** (*About* + gerund exists — *a book about running* — but that is the topic sense, a different job for the same word, treated in [about](10-topic-reference-role.md#about). *About to* + bare infinitive, as in *about to leave*, is a near-future construction and not a prepositional phrase at all.) *About* is also an adverb of degree (*just about finished*, *about right*) and, in older or regional use, an adverb of place (*he wandered about*); **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *we're about done* with *we're leaving about six*.
**Pattern:** *about* [clock time] &middot; *at about* [clock time] &middot; *about* [quantity of time] &middot; *about* [date or period] &middot; *about this time* [last year, next week]
**Contrast:** *About* and *around* are freely interchangeable for time in American English. The mild tendency: *around* is a touch more casual and much commoner with holidays and seasons (*around Christmas*), *about* a touch commoner with measured amounts (*about three weeks*). *Approximately* is the formal register and belongs in lab reports, not conversation. British *round about six* is not American.

**1. Approximately, with a clock time or a date.** Come by *about* seven.
**2. Approximately, with a measured stretch of time.** It took *about* three weeks.

Americans routinely stack *at* in front of it — *at about six* — which some style guides call redundant and which every American says anyway. It is standard spoken usage.

1. Come by *about* seven.
2. The show starts at *about* eight thirty.
3. It took *about* three weeks to hear back.
4. She's been gone *about* an hour.
5. *About* this time last year, we were still in the old office.
6. The whole thing lasted *about* as long as a sitcom.
7. Give it *about* ten minutes and try the door again.

**See also:** [around](#around), [circa](#circa), [near](#near), [toward](#toward), [nigh](#nigh), [on or about](#on-or-about), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [about (topic)](10-topic-reference-role.md#about)

### around

**Pronunciation:** /əˈraʊnd/ &middot; *uh-ROWND* &middot; clipped to /raʊnd/ in casual speech — *'round*
**Русский:** около, примерно — разг.; о часе, дате или празднике: around six «часов в шесть»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a clock time, a date, a holiday, or a period. **No gerund, no finite clause.** The frame *around the time* [clause] is not an exception: the clause there is a relative clause inside the noun phrase *the time*, and *around* still has a noun phrase as its object. *Around* is also an adverb (*she's been around*, *turn around*) and a verb particle (*hang around*, *stick around*, *come around*); **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *he's not around* with *he got here around six*. The spatial and movement senses (*around the corner*, *around the lake*) live in [around](04-movement-direction.md#around).
**Pattern:** *around* [clock time] &middot; *around* [date or holiday] &middot; *around* [period] &middot; *right around* [time] &middot; *around the time* [that-clause inside the noun phrase]
**Variants:** *'round* (informal American, as in *'round midnight*), *round* (**the British form** — in American English *round* is a noun, verb, or adjective, not this preposition)
**Contrast:** See *about*, with which it is interchangeable here. The thing to flag is the spelling: British writers use *round* where Americans must use *around*, and *round about tea time* is not an American sentence. *Around* is also the American choice in *year-round*, *around the clock*, and *the other way around*.

**1. Approximately, with a clock time.** Let's say *around* six.
**2. Approximately, with a date, a season, or a holiday.** We'll be back *around* Labor Day.

The holiday use is the one with no good substitute: *around Christmas* covers a fuzzy week or two in a way that *on* and *at* cannot, and *about Christmas* would be heard as the topic sense.

1. Let's say *around* six.
2. He usually gets in *around* nine thirty.
3. We'll be back *around* Labor Day.
4. *Around* the holidays, nothing ships on time.
5. She moved to Denver *around* 2015.
6. The rent went up right *around* the time the new owners took over.
7. *Around* midnight the power finally came back on.

**See also:** [about](#about), [circa](#circa), [near](#near), [toward](#toward), [on or about](#on-or-about), [at](01-core-in-on-at.md#at), [around (movement)](04-movement-direction.md#around)

### circa

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɜrkə/ &middot; *SUR-kuh* &middot; no weak form — it carries its own stress, and the abbreviations *c.* and *ca.* are read aloud as the full word
**Русский:** около, ок. — книжн., перед годом или датой: circa 1450 «ок. 1450 г.»
**Type:** simple (a Latin borrowing, "around") &middot; **Register:** formal — scholarly, curatorial, genealogical
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **year or a date, and nothing else**. Never a clock time, never a gerund, never a clause, and — this is the error to avoid — **never a quantity of time**: *circa three weeks* is wrong for *about three weeks*.
**Pattern:** *circa* [year] &middot; *c.* [year] &middot; *circa* [decade] &middot; *circa-*[year] [noun] (hyphenated modifier)
**Variants:** abbreviated *c.* or *ca.* before a year (*c. 1450*, *ca. 1920*); *c.* is the commoner American abbreviation
**Contrast:** *About* and *around* work with any time expression; *circa* is licensed only for dating, and using it for durations marks a writer as reaching. In loose journalistic use it has spread to mean "in the era of" (*circa-1975 Springsteen*), which is a stylistic flourish rather than the dating word, and it is usually hyphenated when it modifies a noun that way.

Approximately, before a date whose exact value is unknown or disputed. It is the standard notation of museum labels, catalog entries, genealogy, and academic history, and its point is honesty: *circa 1450* says the object was dated by inference rather than by record.

**1. Approximately, before a year or a date.** A Flemish altarpiece, *circa* 1450.
**2. Loosely, "in the era of," as a hyphenated modifier in front of a noun.** He showed up in a *circa*-1975 leather jacket.

Sense 1 is the real word and it belongs to scholarship. Sense 2 is journalistic drift — *circa-1975 Springsteen* is a period label, not a dating claim — and it is the only place the word gets hyphenated.

1. A Flemish altarpiece, *circa* 1450.
2. The photograph was taken *circa* 1918.
3. He was born *circa* 1770 in what is now Vermont.
4. The manuscript dates from *c.* 900.
5. *Circa* 1890, the whole street was boarding houses.
6. The whole neighborhood went up *circa* the postwar boom.
7. He showed up in a *circa*-1975 leather jacket.

**See also:** [about](#about), [around](#around), [near](#near), [on or about](#on-or-about), [in](01-core-in-on-at.md#in)

### long about

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɔŋ əˌbaʊt/ &middot; *LAWNG uh-bowt* &middot; the spelling *'long about* is the same sounds with the lost *a-* marked; in running speech the phrase flattens to /lɔŋ ə/ before the stressed time word — *lawng-uh MID-nite*
**Русский:** диал./разг. (южн. амер.) этак около — примерно в такой-то час или время суток
**Type:** complex (a worn-down *along about*) &middot; **Register:** **regional and informal American — Southern, Midland, and rural**; also literary dialect
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a time: a clock hour, a part of the day, a month, or a season (*long about midnight*, *long about suppertime*, *long about March*). **No gerund, no finite clause.** **Not a measured length**: *long about three hours* is not the idiom, and plain *about three hours* is the phrase for that.
**Pattern:** *long about* [clock time] &middot; *long about* [part of the day] &middot; *long about* [month or season] &middot; *'long about* [time] (the eye-dialect spelling)
**Variants:** *'long about* (the commoner spelling in written dialect, with an apostrophe for the lost first syllable), *along about* (the full form, itself informal), *long toward* and *along toward* (the same construction pointed at an approaching time — *along toward evening*)
**Contrast:** It means [around](#around) or [about](#about) and adds nothing to them but voice: *long about midnight* is *around midnight*, spoken by somebody the reader is meant to place. That is why it lives in fiction, country lyrics, and oral history and essentially never in edited prose. Against *about*, which can also hedge a quantity (*about three weeks*) — *long about* cannot. Do not confuse it with the adjective *long* in front of a noun (*a long about-face*), and do not use it in writing where the regional coloring is not wanted.

Around, before a time of day, a month, or a season. The phrase is *along about* with the first syllable worn off, and the *along* in it is the same weakened *along* that turns up in *along toward evening*.

**1. At approximately a stated hour.** *Long about* midnight the wind came up.
**2. At approximately a stated part of the day, month, or season.** *Long about* suppertime he'd walk down to the store.

The label is the whole entry. In edited American prose write *around*; reproduce *long about* only in quoted speech, in fiction where the voice is the point, or in transcription, and expect it to date and place the speaker as surely as a vowel would.

1. *Long about* midnight the wind came up and took the porch screen with it.
2. He'd come by *'long about* suppertime, every day that summer.
3. *Long about* the first frost, the whole hillside turns.
4. We got there *long about* four in the morning.
5. *'Long about* March you start hearing the frogs again.
6. She quit calling *long about* the time he sold the farm.
7. *Long about* then, the money ran out.

**See also:** [about](#about), [around](#around), [near](#near), [toward](#toward), [nigh](#nigh), [come](#come)

### near

**Pronunciation:** /nɪr/ &middot; *NEER* &middot; no weak form — the vowel keeps its quality, and the word is normally stressed
**Русский:** ближе к, около — у края периода: near the end of the month «ближе к концу месяца»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a point in time, and above all the edge of a period in the frame *near the end*, *the beginning*, *the close*, or *the middle of* [period]. **No gerund, no finite clause.** The comparative and superlative still work as prepositions (*nearer the holidays*, *nearest the deadline*). *Near* is also an adjective (*the near future*, *a near miss*), an adverb (*don't come near*), and a verb (*as the deadline neared*); **the preposition has an object and the adverb does not** — compare *the deadline is near* with *we finished near the deadline*.
**Pattern:** *near* [time point] &middot; *near the end / beginning / close / middle of* [period] &middot; *nearer* [time] &middot; *nowhere near* [quantity of time]
**Variants:** *near to* (chiefly British in this sense; American English drops the *to* — *near the end of the year*, not *near to the end of the year*)
**Contrast:** *Near* vs. *toward*: *near the end of the month* is a static position on the calendar, *toward the end of the month* has drift in it — something is still moving. *Near* vs. *about* and *around*: those two hedge a stated time on both sides (*around six* covers 5:50 and 6:10), while *near* leans to just before, and it is much rarer with bare clock times — an American says *around six*, not *near six*, but *near midnight* is idiomatic because midnight is an edge. Note *nowhere near*, which is the emphatic negative of quantity (*nowhere near an hour*) and is much commoner in speech than the positive.

Close to a point in time without reaching it, and most at home at the edges of a named period. The temporal use is the spatial one carried straight over: *near the door* and *near the end of the year* are the same relation on different maps.

**1. Close to the edge of a period, in the *near the end of* frame.** Sales pick up *near* the end of the month.
**2. Close to a named moment, especially one that is itself a boundary.** They finally left *near* midnight.
**3. In *nowhere near*: far short of a stated quantity of time.** The repair took *nowhere near* two weeks.

Sense 1 is where the word earns its keep — American writing uses it constantly for the last stretch of a month, a quarter, a term, or a century. Sense 2 is narrower than learners expect: *near* wants a moment that already reads as a boundary (*midnight*, *dawn*, *closing*), which is why *near three o'clock* sounds off where *around three* does not.

1. Sales pick up *near* the end of the month.
2. *Near* the close of the century the mills all shut down.
3. They finally left *near* midnight.
4. He was hired *near* the beginning of her second term.
5. It rains here only *near* the end of summer.
6. *Nearer* the holidays, the parking gets impossible.
7. The whole repair took *nowhere near* the two weeks they quoted.

**See also:** [toward](#toward), [around](#around), [about](#about), [on the eve of](#on-the-eve-of), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [near (place)](03-place-position.md#near)

### nigh

**Pronunciation:** /naɪ/ &middot; *NY* &middot; no weak form of its own; in *nigh on* the *on* reduces to /ən/ — *NY-un*
**Русский:** устар./диал. почти, около — чаще в nigh on «без малого»: nigh on forty years
**Type:** marginal &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, or regional
**Complement:** in living usage, *nigh on(to)* + a measured quantity of time (*nigh on thirty years*). The bare preposition meaning "near" (*nigh the river*) is archaic. No gerund, no clause. *Nigh* is also an adjective and an adverb (*the end is nigh*, *well-nigh impossible*), and those are the forms most Americans actually meet.
**Pattern:** *nigh on* [quantity of time] &middot; *nigh onto* [quantity of time] &middot; *well-nigh* [adjective]
**Variants:** *nigh on*, *nigh onto* (the only forms in living American use)
**Contrast:** It means *almost*, and *almost* is the word to use unless you are deliberately reaching for a backcountry or a biblical tone. The everyday American equivalent for durations is *going on* — *going on forty years*. Do not confuse it with *neigh* (what a horse does) or *nay* (the old-fashioned "no"); all three sound alike.

Nearly, before a stretch of time. In American English it survives almost entirely in *nigh on*, in speech that is either rural, elderly, or putting on a voice. As an adverb it is healthier: *well-nigh impossible* is still current in formal writing.

**1. In *nigh on* or *nigh onto*: nearly, before a measured stretch of time.** He's worked that farm *nigh on* forty years.
**2. Near, before a point in time — the bare preposition, archaic outside deliberate pastiche.** It was *nigh* midnight before the last of them left.

Sense 2 is the original preposition and it is dead in ordinary American writing. What survives is sense 1, and it survives as a hedge on a quantity rather than as a time preposition proper — *nigh on forty years* is doing the work of *almost*, not of *near*.

1. He's worked that farm *nigh on* forty years.
2. It's been *nigh onto* a decade since anybody lived there.
3. *Nigh on* two hundred people showed up.
4. The old bridge stood *nigh on* a century.
5. We waited *nigh on* an hour before anyone came out.
6. *Nigh on* three weeks passed before anybody answered.
7. It was *nigh* midnight before the last of them left.

**See also:** [about](#about), [around](#around), [ere](#ere), [going on](12-marginal-participial.md#going-on)

### toward

**Pronunciation:** /tɔrd/ &middot; *TORD* &middot; also /təˈwɔrd/ &middot; *tuh-WORD* — the one-syllable form is the ordinary American pronunciation and is itself the reduced form, so there is no further weak form; the two-syllable version already has a schwa in the first syllable and is the deliberate, careful one
**Русский:** к, ближе к — по мере приближения времени: toward evening «к вечеру»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a time of day or a period, very often in the frame *toward the end*, *the middle*, or *the close of* [period]. No gerund, no clause. The direction senses (*toward the door*) live in [toward](04-movement-direction.md#toward).
**Pattern:** *toward* [time of day] &middot; *toward the end*, *the middle*, or *the close of* [period] &middot; *toward* [period]
**Variants:** *towards* (**the British preference** — both spellings are correct English and mean exactly the same thing, but American English writes *toward*; recognize *towards* in British copy and do not reproduce it. Pick one and hold to it through a document.)
**Contrast:** *Toward evening* means as evening comes on and has not arrived; *by evening* means at or before it; *around evening* means approximately at it. The difference is that *toward* keeps the motion in the sentence — something is still approaching. Against [near](#near): *near the end of the month* is a static position in time, *toward the end of the month* implies drift.

Approaches a time without arriving at it. Where *at* pins a moment and *around* hedges one, *toward* keeps something in motion on the way there, and that motion is the whole of what it adds.

**1. As a stated time approaches, without reaching it.** *Toward* evening the wind died down.
**2. In the later part of a period.** *Toward* the end of the semester everyone gives up.

The word imports its spatial meaning wholesale: just as *toward the door* does not mean you got there, *toward midnight* does not mean midnight arrived.

1. *Toward* evening the wind finally died down.
2. *Toward* midnight the noise from the street let up.
3. *Toward* dawn the rain finally stopped.
4. *Toward* the end of the semester, everybody stops reading.
5. Sales pick up *toward* the middle of December.
6. He grew quieter *toward* the end of his life.
7. *Toward* the close of the fiscal year, every department starts spending.

## Postpositive and marginal time words

Five oddities. *Ago* and *hence* are the two English prepositions that must follow their object rather than precede it, and they point in opposite directions. *Come* is a fossilized verb form doing a preposition's work, *effective* is an adjective that business writing drafted into the same job, and *of a* is a fossilized genitive that turns a period into a habit — *of an evening* means "in the evenings, as a rule."

### ago

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡoʊ/ &middot; *uh-GOH* &middot; no separate weak form — the first syllable is already a schwa and never takes stress
**Русский:** (тому) назад — ставится после срока, отсчёт назад от настоящего: six years ago
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** **it follows its object, and the object must be a measured length of time** — *three days ago*, *a moment ago*, *a long time ago*. Never a point in time (*Monday ago* is not English), never a gerund, never a clause, and never in front of its object (*ago three days* is not English). It also **requires the simple past** in the clause it modifies: *He left three days ago*, never *he has left three days ago*.
**Pattern:** [quantity of time] *ago* &middot; [quantity of time] *ago*, [past-tense clause] &middot; *long ago* &middot; *how long ago* &middot; *not long ago*
**Variants:** *agone* (archaic; found in older verse and in some rural speech)
**Contrast:** **for / since / ago**: *for three years* measures a stretch, *since 2022* names a starting point, *three years ago* names a point by counting backward from now. **ago vs. before**: *ago* counts back from the moment of speaking and takes the simple past; *before* counts back from a point already in the past and takes the past perfect — *he had left three days before*. This is why *ago* cannot survive the tense shift of reported speech: *she said she had quit three years earlier*, not *three years ago*. **ago vs. hence** is the forward-backward pair.

Locates a point in the past by measuring backward from the present moment. It is the commonest preposition in modern English that comes after its object, a leftover from the Old English past participle *āgān*, "gone by" — literally *three days gone by*.

**1. Counting back from now to a past point.** We moved here six years *ago*.
**2. In vague measures, a general past.** That was a long time *ago*.

1. We moved here six years *ago*.
2. The package shipped two days *ago*.
3. She called about ten minutes *ago*.
4. That was a long time *ago*, and nobody remembers it the same way.
5. Long *ago*, this was all orchard.
6. How long *ago* did you two meet?
7. Not long *ago* you couldn't get a coffee on this block.

**See also:** [since](#since), [hence](#hence), [before](#before), [past](#past), [for](02-core-relational.md#for), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [ago (participial)](12-marginal-participial.md#ago)

### come

**Pronunciation:** /kʌm/ &middot; *KUM* &middot; weak /kəm/ &middot; *kuhm* — in this prepositional use it is almost always unstressed, so the weak form is the ordinary one
**Русский:** разг. когда наступит, к — come Monday «когда настанет понедельник»
**Type:** marginal (a fossilized subjunctive of the verb *come*) &middot; **Register:** informal — folksy and regional in flavor, but understood everywhere
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **future** time point — a day, a month, a season, a holiday, or an age (*come Monday*, *come spring*, *come her next birthday*). Never a past time (*come last Monday* is not English), never a gerund, never a clause.
**Pattern:** *Come* [future time], [clause] &middot; [clause] *come* [future time] &middot; [subject] *will be* [age] *come* [time]
**Contrast:** It means *when [X] comes* or *by the time [X] arrives*, and it is warmer and more colloquial than either. Unlike *by*, it sets no deadline — *come Monday* announces what will be true then, not what must be done before then. Its one hard restriction is direction: *come* can only look forward.

When the stated future time arrives. The construction is a leftover subjunctive — *come Monday* is *[when] Monday come[s]* — which is why it takes no *-s* and resists any tense marking at all. It gives a sentence a settled, almost prophetic tone, and it is at home in country lyrics, farm talk, and political prediction.

**1. When a stated future day, month, or season arrives.** *Come* Monday, everything changes.
**2. By the time a stated future point arrives — with an age or a length of service.** She'll be ninety *come* August.

Sense 2 is where the construction is most alive in ordinary American speech. It is the standard way to state an age or a tenure that has not been reached yet, and nothing shorter does the job: *she'll be ninety in August* loses the sense of a milestone waiting on the calendar.

1. *Come* Monday, everything changes.
2. She'll be ninety *come* August.
3. *Come* spring, the whole hillside turns yellow.
4. *Come* November, none of this will matter.
5. He'll have been at the paper thirty years *come* the fall.
6. *Come* Tuesday, the rates go back up.
7. It'll be forty years *come* December since the mill closed.

**See also:** [as of](#as-of), [until](#until), [by](02-core-relational.md#by), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [starting](12-marginal-participial.md#starting)

### effective

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈfɛktɪv/ &middot; *ih-FEK-tiv* &middot; weak /əˈfɛktɪv/
**Русский:** начиная с — офиц.: вступает в силу с указанной даты или момента
**Type:** marginal (an adjective pressed into prepositional service) &middot; **Register:** formal — business, legal, official notices
**Complement:** a date, or a time adverb standing in for one (*effective January 1*, *effective immediately*, *effective at midnight*, *effective next Monday*). No gerund, no clause. It introduces only the moment at which a rule, an appointment, or a change takes force; it is not a general-purpose time preposition.
**Pattern:** *Effective* [date], [clause] &middot; [clause], *effective* [date] &middot; *effective immediately* &middot; *effective with* [the next billing cycle]
**Contrast:** *Effective* looks forward only: it names the moment something starts to have force. *As of* does that and can also look backward to report a state — *as of Friday, we had 400 orders*, which *effective Friday* could not say. *Beginning* and *starting* do the same job in plainer registers, and British *as from* is the equivalent Britishism.

Taking force from the stated moment. The word is an adjective everywhere else in the language (*an effective argument*), and it earns a place in this dictionary because in official writing it takes a bare date as its object and sits exactly where a preposition sits — usually fronted, with a comma.

**1. Taking force from a stated date.** *Effective* January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars.
**2. Taking force from a moment named by an adverb or a cycle rather than a date.** *Effective* immediately, all travel requires written approval.

Sense 2 is why the word survives at all: *effective immediately* has no one-word substitute, because *now* is a description and this is an instrument. Note that the object may be a bare date with no preposition of its own (*effective Monday*), which is the clearest sign the word has stopped behaving like an adjective.

1. *Effective* January 1, the deposit increases to two hundred dollars.
2. *Effective* immediately, all travel requires written approval.
3. She resigned from the board, *effective* at the end of the month.
4. The new rates are *effective* Monday.
5. *Effective* with the next billing cycle, paper statements cost two dollars.
6. The appointment is *effective* the first of the month.
7. *Effective* at midnight, the contract is void.

**See also:** [as of](#as-of), [upon](#upon), [pending](#pending), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [on](01-core-in-on-at.md#on), [beginning](12-marginal-participial.md#beginning)

### hence

**Pronunciation:** /hɛns/ &middot; *HENSS* &middot; no weak form — it keeps its stress even trailing a phrase, as in *two years hence*
**Русский:** книжн. спустя, через — ставится после срока, отсчёт вперёд: two years hence
**Type:** postpositive &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, legal
**Complement:** **it follows its object**, and the object must be a measured length of time (*three days hence*, *a century hence*). Never a point in time, never a gerund, never a clause, and nothing at all may follow it.
**Pattern:** [quantity of time] *hence* &middot; [clause], [quantity of time] *hence*
**Contrast:** The forward-facing twin of *ago*: *three days ago* counts back from now, *three days hence* counts forward. Ordinary American English says *three days from now* and leaves *hence* to contracts, sermons, and speeches. Do not confuse it with the far commoner conjunctive adverb *hence* meaning "therefore" (*the road was closed; hence the detour*), which takes no time phrase and is a different word entirely, or with the archaic adverb of place (*get thee hence*).

Counting forward from the present moment to a future point. It is one of the handful of English prepositions that follow their object — *ago*, *hence*, and *notwithstanding* are the everyday examples — and where *ago* is neutral, *hence* is a deliberate reach for gravity — which is why it survives mostly in legal drafting and in sentences about the judgment of history.

**1. Counting forward from now by a measured stretch.** The lease expires two years *hence*.
**2. In vague or grand measures, a general future.** What we build now will be judged a century *hence*.

The split is the same one *ago* makes in the other direction: sense 1 is a real interval a reader could put on a calendar, sense 2 is rhetorical distance. Sense 2 is the commoner one in speeches, and it is the reason the word sounds like a peroration.

1. The lease expires two years *hence*.
2. A decade *hence*, none of these arguments will matter.
3. The court will reconvene thirty days *hence*.
4. What we build now will be judged a century *hence*.
5. Six months *hence*, the pilot program comes up for review.
6. The vote is set for two weeks *hence*.
7. A generation *hence*, nobody will remember which side won.

**See also:** [ago](#ago), [ere](#ere), [until](#until), [from](02-core-relational.md#from), [hence (participial)](12-marginal-participial.md#hence)

### of a

**Pronunciation:** /əv ə/ &middot; *uhv uh* &middot; both words are unstressed and *of* is often bare /ə/, so *of an evening* comes out *uh-vuhn EEV-ning* and *of a Sunday* as *uh-vuh SUN-day* — the phrase is never stressed and is easy to miss entirely
**Русский:** устар./диал. по (вечерам, субботам) — о привычном действии: of an evening «по вечерам»
**Type:** marginal (a fossilized adverbial genitive) &middot; **Register:** **archaic in general American; alive as a regional and folksy usage — Southern, Appalachian, and rural Northern**; also British and Irish dialect
**Complement:** **a singular noun naming a recurring time, with *a* or *an*, and nothing else** — *of an evening*, *of a morning*, *of a Sunday*, *of a winter night*. **No plural** (*of evenings* is a separate and older adverbial), **no gerund, no finite clause**, and **no definite article**: *of the evening* is an ordinary possessive and means something else entirely (*the cool of the evening*). The construction is frozen and will not extend — ***of a meeting***, ***of a birthday*** are not English.
**Pattern:** [habitual clause] *of an evening* &middot; [habitual clause] *of a* [day of the week] &middot; *Of a* [Sunday afternoon, winter night], [clause]
**Variants:** *of a morning*, *of an evening*, *of a Sunday* (the fixed members of the set), *of a night* (regional), *of an afternoon* (rarer)
**Contrast:** **It does not name one occasion; it names a habit, and that is the whole of it.** *He read of an evening* means he read in the evenings, as a rule, which is why the construction pulls *would*, *used to*, or a plain habitual past after it. Compare *in the evening*, which can be either one evening or a habit, and *on Sundays*, which is the plain modern way to say this. Against *on a Sunday*, which names one unspecified Sunday: *he'd fish of a Sunday* is a standing custom, *he went fishing on a Sunday* is one trip. Do not read the *of* as partitive — it is the last trace of an Old English genitive of time, the same one that survives in *nowadays* (*now-a-days*) and in *he works nights*.

In the course of a typical one of these — habitually, on evenings, mornings, or Sundays taken as a class. The construction is an Old English adverbial genitive gone fossil, and in American English it survives in speech that is regional, elderly, or reaching for one of the two.

**1. Habitually in the evenings or in the mornings.** He'd sit out on the porch *of an evening*.
**2. Habitually on a named day.** They'd drive into town *of a Saturday*.
**3. In an extended noun phrase naming a kind of occasion.** *Of a winter night* you could hear the trains from the house.

Every one of these has a plain modern equivalent — *in the evenings*, *on Saturdays*, *on winter nights* — and the plain form is what to write. The test that the construction is doing its job is the verb: it wants *would*, *used to*, or a habitual past. A single event breaks it, which is why *he called of an evening* cannot mean he called once.

1. He'd sit out on the porch *of an evening* and not say much.
2. *Of a Saturday* they'd drive into town for supplies.
3. She still bakes *of a Sunday*, though nobody comes for it anymore.
4. *Of a morning* the fog sits in the low fields until nine.
5. We used to walk down to the landing *of an evening*.
6. *Of a winter night* you could hear the trains all the way from the house.
7. He'd take the truck out *of a Sunday* afternoon and just drive.

## Telling time on the clock

The half hour splits American English from British English twice over, and it splits American English from itself once.

**Minutes after the hour.** Americans say *ten after six* or *ten past six*; both are standard, and *after* is the more distinctly American of the two. British English uses only *past*. *Half past six* and *quarter past six* are alive on both sides of the Atlantic; the British colloquial *half six* (meaning 6:30) has no American equivalent and will be misheard as 5:30 or as a mistake.

**Minutes before the hour.** Here American English has three options where British English has one.

| Form | Example | Range |
|---|---|---|
| *to* | *ten to six* | general American, and the only British form |
| *of* | *a quarter of three* | American, strongest in the Northeast and among older speakers |
| *till* | *a quarter till three* | American, Midland and Southern |

All three are standard American. *Of* is the one that reliably confuses non-Americans, because nothing else in the language uses *of* for time — *a quarter of three* means 2:45, not 45 minutes of some three-hour thing. If you are writing for a mixed audience, the digits are safer than any of them.

All three also **reduce to nothing much in speech**, which is the other half of the difficulty. *To* is /tə/ (*ten tuh six*), *of* is /əv/ and usually bare /ə/ (*a quarter uh three*), and *till* is /təl/ (*a quarter tuhl three*). A listener who is not expecting the reduced *of* hears an unstressed syllable and no word at all, which is why this is the American clock form that most often has to be repeated.

**And the endpoint problem.** *Open until Friday* does not tell a reader whether Friday counts. American English fixed this with [through](#through) — *Monday through Friday* includes Friday — and legal drafting fixed it with [no later than](#no-later-than) and *up to and including*. British English has no equivalent of the *through* solution, which is why British schedules say *Monday to Friday inclusive*.

## British time forms to recognize but not to write

| British | American |
|---|---|
| *at the weekend* | *on the weekend*, *over the weekend* |
| *Monday to Friday* (inclusive) | *Monday through Friday* |
| *half six* (= 6:30) | *six thirty*, *half past six* |
| *gone six* (= just after six) | *past six*, *after six* |
| *round about six* | *around six*, *about six* |
| *behind time* | *behind schedule* |
| *as from July 1* | *as of July 1*, *effective July 1* |
| *with effect from 1 July* | *effective July 1*, *as of July 1* |
| *with immediate effect* | *effective immediately* |
| *as at 31 December* (accounting) | *as of December 31* |
| *towards evening* | *toward evening* |
| *amongst*, *amidst* | *among*, *amid* |
| *whilst the meeting ran* | *while the meeting ran* |
| *in future* | *in the future*, *from now on* |
| *Tuesday week* (= a week from Tuesday) | *a week from Tuesday* |
| *near to the end of the year* | *near the end of the year* |
| *a fortnight* | *two weeks* |

The pattern in the last block is the one to internalize: **American English prefers the shorter member of each pair** — *toward* over *towards*, *among* over *amongst*, *amid* over *amidst*, *while* over *whilst*, *around* over *round about* — while British English is comfortable with either. The one place the direction reverses is the inclusive endpoint, where American writing spends an extra word on *Monday through Friday* to buy the precision that *Monday to Friday* does not have.
