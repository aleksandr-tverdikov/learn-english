# Accompaniment, addition, exception, and restriction

> The prepositions that add something to a set, subtract something from it, or report that a piece of it is missing.

[← The grammar of prepositions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every preposition in this file does arithmetic on a set. Some widen it — *along with*, *as well as*, *including*, *plus* — some narrow it — *except*, *but*, *excluding*, *minus*, *save* — and some declare a piece of it absent altogether — *without*, *absent*, *sans*. Two traps run through the whole group. The first is grammatical: **in the uses catalogued here, not one of these words is a conjunction** — they are prepositions taking an object — so not one of them changes the number of the subject: *The coach, as well as three players, **was** ejected*, never *were*. (Several of them, *but* and *except* and *save* among them, do double as conjunctions elsewhere, and each entry says where the line falls.) The second is semantic: *apart from*, *aside from*, *besides*, and *outside of* can mean either "in addition to" or "except for", and nothing but the polarity of the sentence around them decides which.

## Accompaniment and addition

The base preposition of accompaniment is [with](02-core-relational.md#with), which has its own entry with the other core relational prepositions. The words below are the additive specialists built on top of it: two complex prepositions that mean only accompaniment and never instrument (*along with*, *together with*), two that mean only addition (*as well as*, *in addition to*), the participial of inclusion (*including*), its commercial twin (*inclusive of*) and its contractual belt-and-suspenders (*including without limitation*), the arithmetic *plus* and its emphatic *over and above*, the causal *coupled with*, the four partnership formulas (*in collaboration with*, *in cooperation with*, *in concert with*, *in tandem with*), the strictly human *in the company of*, the outfitting *complete with*, the aggrieved *on top of*, the rhetorical pair *not to mention* and *to say nothing of*, which add an item by pretending to leave it out, and *let alone*, which reverses their polarity and adds an item only to place it out of reach.

The agreement rule applies to all of them. A phrase introduced by any of these words is a **supplement**, not a second subject. It sits inside commas, it can be lifted out without breaking the sentence, and the verb continues to agree with the original subject alone.

### along with

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ wɪð/ (also /əˈlɑŋ/ with the *cot–caught* merger; *with* also /wɪθ/) &middot; weak /əˈlɔŋ wəð/, /əˈlɔŋ wəθ/ &middot; *uh-LAWNG with*
**Русский:** вместе с, наряду с — присоединяет спутника или ещё один пункт; число глагола не меняет
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause, never a bare or *to*-infinitive
**Pattern:** [clause] *along with* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject], *along with* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; *Along with* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** Interchangeable with *together with*, which is the more written of the two. Neither can do the instrumental work of plain [with](02-core-relational.md#with) — you cut bread *with* a knife, not *along with* one. And *along with* is a preposition, not *and*: it does **not** make a singular subject plural.

Joins a second party or a second item to what has already been named, either as company on the way or as one more thing in the pile. It is the everyday additive of American speech and writing, equally at home in a shipping manifest and in conversation.

**1. In the company of.** Two people or things moving or acting together.

1. She rode out to the site *along with* two state inspectors.
2. The dog came *along with* us on every trip that summer.

**2. In addition to.** One more item added to a list, a price, or a transaction.

3. The sale includes the appliances *along with* all the patio furniture.
4. Bring a photo ID *along with* a copy of the lease.

**3. Appended to a subject.** The verb still agrees with the original subject alone.

5. The governor, *along with* three of her aides, **was** subpoenaed.
6. Interest, *along with* late fees, **is** added to the balance every month.

**See also:** [together with](#together-with), [as well as](#as-well-as), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [coupled with](#coupled-with), [complete with](#complete-with), [in collaboration with](#in-collaboration-with), [in the company of](#in-the-company-of), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### as well as

**Pronunciation:** full /æz ˈwɛl æz/ &middot; weak /əz ˈwɛl əz/ — both instances of *as* reduce, and the weak form is what you actually hear &middot; *uhz-WEL-uhz*
**Русский:** а также, равно как и — добавляет второй пункт вторым планом; глагол согласуется с первым
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund; a **bare infinitive** when the main verb is itself a bare or *to*-infinitive (*She can act as well as sing*) — never a *that*-clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *as well as* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject], *as well as* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb] &middot; [verb-ing] … *as well as* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *As well as* is not *and*. *And* builds a compound subject and takes a plural verb; *as well as* subordinates the second item, so the verb agrees with the first subject alone — *The pitcher, as well as two relievers, **was** ejected*. Keep it apart from the comparative adverbial *as well as* meaning "as skillfully as" (*He plays as well as she does*), which is a different construction with a clause after it.

Adds a second item while keeping the first one in the foreground. This asymmetry is the whole point of the word: *A and B* gives the two equal billing, *A as well as B* makes B the afterthought — which is exactly why the verb never notices it.

**1. In addition to a noun.**

1. The clinic treats adults *as well as* children.
2. She speaks Portuguese *as well as* Spanish.

**2. Linking two verbs.** The second verb goes to *-ing*, unless a modal or a *to*-infinitive already governs it.

3. He runs the front desk *as well as* handling the schedule.
4. The grant covers tuition *as well as* paying for books.
5. She wanted to direct *as well as* act.

**3. Appended to a subject.** Singular subject, singular verb.

6. The mayor, *as well as* two council members, **was** invited.
7. Fuel, *as well as* labor, **accounts** for most of the increase.

**See also:** [along with](#along-with), [together with](#together-with), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [besides](#besides), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention)

### complete with

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈplit wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) &middot; weak /kəmˈplit wəð/, /kəmˈplit wəθ/ &middot; *kum-PLEET with*
**Русский:** в комплекте с — вещь идёт со всем оснащением; часто иронично о нежеланном довеске
**Type:** complex (adjective + *with*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; a fixture of advertising, real-estate copy, and description
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — no gerund, no finite clause, no infinitive
**Pattern:** [noun] *complete with* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *complete with* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Complete with* is not *including*. *Including* names a member of a set that has already been counted; *complete with* presents the extra as equipment the whole thing arrives furnished with. It is also positionally restricted where *including* is not: it postmodifies a noun or trails a clause and **cannot open a sentence** — ✗ *Complete with a canoe, the cabin rents by the week*. Against *together with*, which merely adds, *complete with* implies the addition finishes the picture, and that implication is what makes it the standard vehicle for a dry joke.

Presents a thing along with everything that outfits it. It sells hard, and because it sells so hard, half its working life is spent being sarcastic.

**1. Listing what comes with a thing.**

1. The cabin rents *complete with* linens, firewood, and a canoe.
2. They delivered the grill *complete with* a cover and a full tank of propane.

**2. In descriptions of costume, staging, and setting.**

3. He showed up in a three-piece suit, *complete with* pocket watch.
4. The lobby has been restored *complete with* the original brass mailboxes.

**3. Sarcastically, for an unwelcome extra.**

5. We got the whole presentation, *complete with* the slideshow.
6. It was a classic family dinner, *complete with* an argument about the will.

**See also:** [including](#including), [together with](#together-with), [along with](#along-with), [coupled with](#coupled-with), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### coupled with

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkʌpəld wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) &middot; weak /ˈkʌpəld wəð/, /ˈkʌpəld wəθ/ &middot; *KUP-uld with*
**Русский:** в сочетании с, вкупе с — два фактора действуют совместно и дают названный результат
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the analyst's and the reporter's word
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], *coupled with* [noun phrase], [verb agreeing with the first noun] &middot; *Coupled with* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; *coupled with* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *combined with*, *paired with*, *allied to* — the same construction under different metaphors
**Contrast:** Where *along with* and *together with* merely add a second item, *coupled with* claims the two **work on each other** to produce the result the main clause names. It is the causal member of the additive family, and swapping it for *and* loses that claim. Its agreement behavior is the family rule: *Drought, coupled with two failed harvests, **was** enough to empty the valley* — singular, because *coupled with* is a preposition and not a conjunction.

Joins two factors and reports what they do together. Reporters and analysts reach for it whenever one cause alone would be too simple an explanation.

**1. Naming two factors behind one result.**

1. Low inventory, *coupled with* cheap credit, pushed prices past any honest appraisal.
2. *Coupled with* the new tariffs, the shipping delays wiped out the quarter.

**2. Adding a condition to an obligation or an offer.**

3. The grant carries a five-year commitment *coupled with* an annual audit.
4. A guilty plea *coupled with* full restitution usually keeps a first offender out of prison.

**3. With a gerund.**

5. *Coupled with* having to retrain the whole staff, the software change cost them a season.
6. The rent increase, *coupled with* losing two tenants, put the building underwater.

**4. Appended to a subject.** The verb agrees with the original subject alone.

7. Fatigue, *coupled with* poor visibility, **was** listed as the probable cause.
8. The deductible, *coupled with* the monthly premium, **makes** the policy a bad deal.

**See also:** [along with](#along-with), [together with](#together-with), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [complete with](#complete-with), [plus](#plus)

### in addition to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tu/ &middot; weak /ɪn əˈdɪʃən tə/ &middot; *in-uh-DISH-un too*
**Русский:** в дополнение к, помимо — to здесь предлог, поэтому дальше герундий, а не инфинитив
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or **gerund** — the *to* here is a preposition, so a bare infinitive is an error: *in addition to working*, never *in addition to work full time*. No finite clause.
**Pattern:** *In addition to* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in addition to* [noun phrase] &middot; *in addition to* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** The *to*-trap it shares with *look forward to*, *object to*, and *be used to*: the *to* is a preposition and demands *-ing*. Against *as well as*, it is heavier and more likely to open a sentence; against *besides*, it is unambiguous — *besides* can also mean "except for", and *in addition to* never can.

Names something extra that stands alongside what the main clause says, with no suggestion that either item is the more important. Because it is transparently compositional, it survives in formal writing where *plus* and *besides* would sound casual.

**1. Adding a noun.**

1. *In addition to* the fine, he lost his license for ninety days.
2. The apartment comes with a parking space *in addition to* a storage locker.

**2. Adding an activity — always *-ing*.**

3. *In addition to* working full time, she takes two night classes.
4. *In addition to* running the shop, he does all the bookkeeping himself.

**3. Appended to a subject.** The verb agrees with the subject alone.

5. The principal, *in addition to* four teachers, **was** placed on leave.
6. *In addition to* the tuition, students **pay** a lab fee and a parking permit.

**See also:** [as well as](#as-well-as), [along with](#along-with), [besides](#besides), [plus](#plus), [over and above](#over-and-above), [not to mention](#not-to-mention)

### in collaboration with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəˌlæbəˈreɪʃən wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) &middot; weak /ən kəˌlæbəˈreɪʃən wəð/ &middot; *in-kuh-lab-uh-RAY-shun with*
**Русский:** в сотрудничестве с, совместно с — называет партнёра по совместной работе
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; institutional — bylines, credit lines, press releases, grant reports
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, normally a **named party** — a person, an agency, a company, a university. No finite clause, no infinitive, and for three of the four no gerund: ✗ *in collaboration with rebuilding the archive*. *In tandem with* is the exception and takes a gerund freely (*in tandem with rebuilding the archive*), because what it pairs is two processes rather than two parties.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in collaboration with* [named party] &middot; *In collaboration with* [named party], [clause] &middot; [subject], *in collaboration with* [party], [**singular** verb]
**Variants:** *in cooperation with* /ɪn koʊˌɑpəˈreɪʃən wɪð/, *in-koh-op-uh-RAY-shun with* — the institutional side of the same relation, and the standard wording on a government or nonprofit credit line; *in concert with* /ɪn ˈkɑnsərt wɪð/, *in-KON-surt with* — coordinated and simultaneous, the choice when the point is timing; *in tandem with* /ɪn ˈtændəm wɪð/, *in-TAN-dum with* — two things working as a linked pair, and the only one of the four that takes a process as comfortably as a person. *In partnership with* and *in conjunction with* belong to the same family.
**Contrast:** All four are periphrastic [with](02-core-relational.md#with), and plain *with* would carry the bare meaning in every sentence below; what the longer forms buy is a claim about **how** the two parties are joined. *In collaboration with* claims joint creative or professional work — the partner actually contributed. *In cooperation with* claims institutional consent and assistance, which is why it appears on permits and credit lines and why it can be literally true when the partner did almost nothing. *In concert with* claims **simultaneity and coordination**, and it is the one that survives in adversarial contexts: *acting in concert with* is a term of art in American securities law for parties whose holdings are counted as one block. *In tandem with* claims a **linked pair**, and takes an abstract process where the others want a partner. Against [in conjunction with](11-complex-multiword.md#in-conjunction-with), the colorless "together with" of this group, which makes no claim at all about who did what. Like everything else in this file they are prepositions and not conjunctions, so the verb keeps agreeing with the original subject.

Names the partner a piece of work was done with, and says something about what kind of partnership it was. American writing reaches for these wherever a bare *with* would be ambiguous between "accompanied by", "by means of", and "jointly with" — a byline, a grant credit, and a press release cannot afford that ambiguity.

**1. Joint work, with a named partner.** The core use of *in collaboration with*.

1. The exhibit was mounted *in collaboration with* the tribal historic preservation office.
2. She wrote the score *in collaboration with* her brother.
3. *In collaboration with* researchers at Michigan, the lab published three papers in a year.

**2. Institutional sponsorship or consent.** Where *in cooperation with* is the fixed form.

4. The clinic operates *in cooperation with* the county health department.
5. The festival is presented *in cooperation with* the National Park Service.

**3. Coordinated and simultaneous action.** Where *in concert with* is the fixed form, including in its adversarial legal sense.

6. Local police executed the warrant *in concert with* federal agents.
7. The two funds bought the shares *in concert with* one another and had to file as a group.

**4. Two things moving as a linked pair.** Where *in tandem with* is the fixed form, and the only one of the four that readily takes a gerund.

8. Wages have risen *in tandem with* rents, so nobody is any better off.
9. The rollout will proceed *in tandem with* retraining the entire dispatch staff.

**5. Appended to a subject.** The verb agrees with the original subject alone.

10. The museum, *in collaboration with* two universities, **is** digitizing the collection.
11. The state, *in cooperation with* four counties, **was** named in the consent decree.

**See also:** [together with](#together-with), [along with](#along-with), [coupled with](#coupled-with), [in the company of](#in-the-company-of), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### in the company of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈkʌmpəni ʌv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ðə ˈkʌmpəni əv/, reduced /ɪn ðə ˈkʌmpəniə/ &middot; *in-thuh-KUM-puh-nee-uv*
**Русский:** в обществе кого-л., в компании кого-л. — только о людях: среди кого человек находился
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; literary, biographical, and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun naming **people** — no gerund, no finite clause, and normally no inanimate object: *in the company of strangers*, not ✗ *in the company of a suitcase*. The determiner is frozen: never ✗ *in a company of*, never ✗ *in company of*.
**Pattern:** [clause] *in the company of* [person or people] &middot; *In the company of* [person or people], [clause] &middot; last seen *in the company of* [person]
**Variants:** *in company with* — dated, and chiefly nautical or commercial (*sailed in company with the Ariel*). Bare *in company*, with nothing after it, is an **adverb** and not this preposition at all (*he behaves better in company*): it names no one, and the object is the whole test.
**Contrast:** A periphrasis of accompanying [with](02-core-relational.md#with), and the only member of the accompaniment family restricted to **human** company. Where *along with* and *together with* add a second item to a list, *in the company of* describes the situation of the subject — it says whom he was among, and it very often carries a judgment about whether that was good for him. Against [among](03-place-position.md#among), which is neutral about the relationship and takes a group rather than a set of named people; against *accompanied by*, which points at a subordinate escort where *in the company of* points at the surrounding party. Its natural homes are the police blotter, the biography, and any sentence about one person's influence on another.

Places a person among other people and lets that fact color the sentence. It is what English reaches for when the point is not that two people were both present but that one of them was inside the other's orbit.

**1. Reporting whom somebody was with.** Common in police, court, and news writing.

1. He was last seen *in the company of* two men in a gray pickup.
2. The senator spent the evening *in the company of* the plaintiffs' lawyers.

**2. Describing the effect of the company kept.**

3. She is a different person *in the company of* her sisters.
4. *In the company of* strangers he hardly says a word.

**3. In praise or in judgment — the evaluative use.**

5. Six months *in the company of* people who read put a dent in his television habit.
6. He spent that whole summer *in the company of* a crowd his mother could not stand.

**4. Figuratively, of a thing set among its peers.** A learned extension, and the one place the object need not be alive.

7. The painting hangs *in the company of* three Vermeers.
8. The novel belongs *in the company of* the best work of that decade.

**5. Not a preposition — bare in company.** Nothing follows it, no one is named, and it is an adverb meaning "among other people".

9. He behaves much better *in company*.
10. The two frigates sailed *in company* as far as the Azores.

**See also:** [along with](#along-with), [together with](#together-with), [in collaboration with](#in-collaboration-with), [without](#without), [with](02-core-relational.md#with)

### including

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkludɪŋ/ &middot; *in-KLOO-ding*
**Русский:** включая, в том числе — называет отдельных членов множества; список не считается полным
**Type:** marginal/participial (fully grammaticalized — it needs no subject to control it) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a ***wh*-clause** (*including how the money was spent*, *including whether the lease can be broken*) — one of the very few prepositions in this file that takes one. Never a *that*-clause and never a tensed clause on its own: ✗ *including it was raining*.
**Pattern:** [total set], *including* [member], … &middot; [amount] *including* [component] &middot; *including* but not limited to [list]
**Contrast:** The mirror of *excluding*. Against *inclusive of*, which is a commercial and contractual word attaching to a noun, *including* attaches loosely to a whole clause and is unrestricted in register. Against postposed *included* (*tax included*), which follows its noun rather than preceding it. Note that *including* names a **sample**, not the whole list — which is why American drafters write *including without limitation* when they need to say so out loud.

Picks out one or more members of a set that has just been named, or folds a component into a total. It does not claim the list that follows is complete, and in careful writing it should not be used as if it were: if the set really is exhausted, write *namely* or *consisting of*.

**1. Naming members of a set.** A pronoun after it goes in the objective case — *including me*, never *including I*.

1. Nine passengers, *including* the pilot, walked away from the crash.
2. Several states, *including* Ohio and Nevada, have already certified.
3. Everyone *including* me had to retake the certification.

**2. Counting something into a total.**

4. The rate is $180 a night, *including* breakfast.
5. That comes to $42 *including* tax and delivery.

**3. The drafting formula.** Used when the list must be read as open-ended.

6. All benefits, *including* but not limited to health and dental coverage, continue through June.
7. The tenant pays all utilities, *including* without limitation gas, electric, water, and trash.

**4. Before a *wh*-clause.** The complement is a question folded into a noun slot.

8. The audit covers everything, *including* how the reserve fund was spent.
9. Several questions remain, *including* whether the lease can be broken at all.

**5. With a gerund.**

10. The job has its costs, *including* driving ninety minutes each way.
11. She listed her objections, *including* having to sign a five-year lease.

**See also:** [including without limitation](#including-without-limitation), [inclusive of](#inclusive-of), [excluding](#excluding), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [not counting](#not-counting), [plus](#plus)

### including without limitation

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkludɪŋ wɪˌðaʊt ˌlɪmɪˈteɪʃən/ &middot; weak /ɪnˈkludɪŋ wəˌðaʊt ˌlɪmɪˈteɪʃən/ &middot; *in-KLOO-ding with-OWT lim-ih-TAY-shun*
**Русский:** включая, но не ограничиваясь — юр. формула, открывающая заведомо неполный перечень
**Type:** complex (a frozen drafting formula built on [including](#including)) &middot; **Register:** formal; American legal drafting almost exclusively
**Complement:** whatever bare *including* takes — noun phrase, list of noun phrases, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — because *without limitation* is a parenthetical tag on *including* and not a preposition of its own. Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [total set], *including without limitation* [list] &middot; [total set], *including but not limited to* [list] &middot; [total set], *including, without limitation,* [list]
**Variants:** *including but not limited to* — the same formula, and by a wide margin the commoner of the two in American contracts; *including without limiting the generality of the foregoing* is the long-winded third form, now mostly confined to older boilerplate. British drafting prefers *including without limitation*; American drafting splits between the first two.
**Contrast:** Bare [including](#including) already names a sample rather than the whole list, so in ordinary prose the tag adds nothing and an editor will cut it. It survives because courts do not reliably read *including* that way: under the interpretive canon **ejusdem generis** — Latin, "of the same kind", and something an American meets only in a brief or an opinion — a following list can be read as narrowing the general word that introduced it, and drafters add *without limitation* to shut that reading down. Against [inclusive of](#inclusive-of), which declares what a stated price already contains rather than opening a list; against [to the exclusion of](#to-the-exclusion-of), which is not about lists at all. Note the punctuation split: American drafters write *including without limitation* unpunctuated in a short list and *including, without limitation,* inside commas when the list runs long.

Opens a list and says out loud that the list is only a sample. It is the most common belt-and-suspenders phrase in American contract drafting, and outside a contract it is almost always four words too many.

**1. In contracts, opening a non-exhaustive list.**

1. Tenant shall pay all utilities, *including without limitation* gas, electric, water, and trash removal.
2. The release covers all claims, *including without limitation* claims for wrongful termination.

**2. The commoner American variant.**

3. Contractor is responsible for all permits, *including but not limited to* electrical and plumbing permits.
4. The policy excludes intentional acts, *including but not limited to* arson and fraud.

**3. Set off by commas, where the list runs long.**

5. Licensee may not modify the software, *including, without limitation,* by decompiling, disassembling, or reverse engineering it.
6. The estate passes all personal property, *including, without limitation,* furniture, vehicles, and the contents of any safe deposit box.

**4. Outside a contract — where it should be cut.** The tag buys nothing in ordinary prose, and editors delete it on sight.

7. The grant covers travel, *including without limitation* airfare and lodging. *(edit to* including*)*
8. She asked about the fees, *including but not limited to* the origination charge. *(edit to* including*)*

**See also:** [including](#including), [inclusive of](#inclusive-of), [excluding](#excluding), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [to the exclusion of](#to-the-exclusion-of), [not counting](#not-counting)

### inclusive of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈklusɪv ʌv/ &middot; weak /ɪnˈklusɪv əv/, reduced /ɪnˈklusɪvə/ before a consonant &middot; *in-KLOO-siv uv*
**Русский:** офиц. включая, с учётом — что уже входит в названную цену или величину
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; chiefly commercial, contractual, and hospitality writing
**Complement:** noun phrase only — no gerund, no clause
**Pattern:** [noun] *inclusive of* [noun phrase] &middot; [price] **is** *inclusive of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The exact opposite of *exclusive of*, and the stiff twin of *including*. *Including* can float anywhere in a sentence; *inclusive of* almost always postmodifies a noun or follows the verb *be*. Do not confuse the preposition with the adjective *inclusive* in a date range (*March 3 through March 9 inclusive*), which takes no *of* — and which American English rarely needs, since *through* is already inclusive where British *to* is not.

Declares what a stated price, figure, or period already contains. It exists because contracts and invoices need a word that cannot be misread, and it buys that precision at the cost of sounding like a contract.

**1. Of a price, a rate, or a fee.** The commonest use by far.

1. All quoted prices are *inclusive of* shipping and handling.
2. Rent is $2,150 a month, *inclusive of* water, sewer, and trash.
3. The flat fee is *inclusive of* all filing costs.

**2. Of a figure, a headcount, or a package.**

4. Salaries are stated *inclusive of* the employer's retirement contribution.
5. The package is *inclusive of* airfare, transfers, and two guided excursions.
6. Headcount stands at 240, *inclusive of* contractors and seasonal staff.

**3. Of a span of dates.** Where American English usually says *through* instead.

7. The blackout period runs March 3 to March 9, *inclusive of* both dates.
8. Coverage is *inclusive of* the first and the last day of the term.

**See also:** [including](#including), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [excluding](#excluding), [plus](#plus)

### let alone

**Pronunciation:** /lɛt əˈloʊn/ &middot; reduced /lɛɾ əˈloʊn/ — the *t* flaps between vowels &middot; *let-uh-LOHN*
**Русский:** не то что, не говоря уже о — только после отрицания: то, что тем более невозможно
**Type:** marginal (a frozen imperative that has become a connective) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, adjective, adverb, prepositional phrase, gerund, or a **bare infinitive** matched to the verb before it (*can't walk, let alone run*) — never a finite clause, and never a *to*-infinitive when the verb before it is bare: ✗ *can't walk, let alone to run*.
**Requires:** a **negative, near-negative, or question** earlier in the sentence — *not*, *never*, *nobody*, *nothing*, *hardly*, *barely*, *too … to*, *can't*. Without one it is ungrammatical: ✗ *He can drive, let alone fly a plane*.
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *let alone* [phrase parallel to what preceded] &middot; can't [bare verb], *let alone* [bare verb] &middot; not [noun], *let alone* [noun]
**Variants:** *much less* — fully interchangeable in American English; *never mind* (informal) and *still less* (formal) do the same work.
**Contrast:** *Let alone* is the mirror image of *not to mention* and *to say nothing of*. All three add a second item at the end, but *not to mention* **upgrades** — what follows is the heavier item actually in play — while *let alone* **downgrades**: what follows is the more extreme thing that is even further out of reach. *He can't drive, let alone fly a plane* says flying is the harder claim; reverse the two and the sentence collapses. Note also that whatever follows must be **parallel** to what preceded it: a bare verb answers a bare verb, a noun answers a noun.

Adds a second item only to say that it lies even further beyond whatever was just denied. It is a scaling device rather than a true addition: the first item marks the floor and *let alone* points past it.

**1. After a negative, before a noun phrase.**

1. There wasn't enough money for repairs, *let alone* a new roof.
2. He doesn't have a driver's license, *let alone* a car.

**2. Before a bare infinitive matched to the verb before it.**

3. She can't walk to the mailbox, *let alone* run a 10K.
4. Most of them couldn't find the building, *let alone* get past the front desk.

**3. Before an adjective, an adverb, or a prepositional phrase.**

5. The road isn't paved, *let alone* plowed.
6. Nobody goes down there in daylight, *let alone* after dark.

**4. In questions and after near-negatives.**

7. Who has time for lunch, *let alone* a two-hour meeting?
8. The team barely made the playoffs, *let alone* a deep run.

**See also:** [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of), [besides](#besides), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [short of](#short-of)

### not to mention

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt tə ˈmɛnʃən/ &middot; reduced /ˌnɑʔ tə ˈmɛnʃən/ — the *t* of *not* glottalizes and *to* is always the weak /tə/, never /tu/ &middot; *naht-tuh-MEN-shun*
**Русский:** не говоря уже о — добавляет более весомый пункт под видом умолчания; только в конце фразы
**Type:** marginal (a frozen infinitival that has become a preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, **adjective**, or a *wh*-clause. A *that*-clause only through *the fact that* (*not to mention the fact that nobody asked*). Never a bare finite clause.
**Pattern:** [clause], *not to mention* [noun phrase] &middot; [adjective], *not to mention* [adjective] &middot; [clause], *not to mention* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of) — a shade more formal and fully interchangeable. Distinguish [let alone](#let-alone), which reverses the polarity: it needs a negative before it and **downgrades** what follows (*He can't drive, let alone fly a plane*), where *not to mention* upgrades.
**Contrast:** Against *in addition to* and *as well as*, which are neutral, *not to mention* is **rhetorical**: it pretends to omit the item while naming it, so whatever follows is always the heavier or the more damning of the two. It is also positionally fixed — it comes at the end of what it adds to and **never opens a sentence**: ✗ *Not to mention the cost, the plan is impractical*; write *The plan is impractical, not to mention ruinously expensive.*

Adds one last item under the pretense of skipping it. The pretense is the whole device: what follows lands harder than what came before precisely because the speaker claimed not to be bringing it up.

**1. Adding a heavier item at the end of a list.**

1. The repair took three weeks and two thousand dollars, *not to mention* a rental car.
2. She runs the department and teaches two seminars, *not to mention* a research group of nine.

**2. Adding an adjective.** A construction almost no other preposition allows.

3. The apartment is tiny, *not to mention* four flights up.
4. The plan is expensive, *not to mention* flatly illegal.

**3. With a gerund.**

5. He had to redo three years of taxes, *not to mention* explaining the whole thing to his brother.
6. Moving in February means ice and a truck deposit, *not to mention* taking a week off work.

**4. With *the fact that*, where a clause is unavoidable.**

7. The lease is a bad deal, *not to mention* the fact that the building is for sale.
8. He missed three deadlines, *not to mention* the fact that nobody could reach him for a week.

**See also:** [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of), [let alone](#let-alone), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [as well as](#as-well-as), [besides](#besides), [over and above](#over-and-above), [plus](#plus)

### on top of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ˈtɑp ʌv/ &middot; weak /ɑn ˈtɑp əv/, reduced /ɑnˈtɑpə/ before a consonant — the *of* loses its /v/ exactly as it does in *out of* &middot; *on-TOP-uv*
**Русский:** вдобавок к, сверх — разг., с оттенком жалобы: ещё одна тягота поверх прежних
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; the additive sense is conversational and nearly always aggrieved
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or **gerund** (*on top of paying rent*); a nominal ***wh*-clause** is possible in the additive sense (*on top of what the movers charged*). Never a *that*-clause and never a *to*-infinitive: ✗ *on top of pay rent*. Bare *on top* is an **adverb** and takes no object — *the berries go on top* — and that is the test: the preposition must have something after it.
**Pattern:** *On top of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *on top of* [noun phrase] &middot; *on top of* [verb-ing] &middot; *on top of* everything else
**Contrast:** The literal sense — *the keys are on top of the refrigerator* — is a spatial preposition and is covered with the [complex prepositions](11-complex-multiword.md#on-top-of); what belongs in this file is the additive one. Against *in addition to*, which is neutral and formal, *on top of* is informal and carries a complaint built in: the second item is a burden piled on the first, which is why *on top of the rent* is idiomatic and *on top of the bonus* sounds odd. Against *besides*, it can never be read as "except for". A third idiom, *on top of* meaning "in full control of" (*she's on top of every deadline*), is a separate sense again.

Piles one more demand onto a load that was already heavy. It is the American complaint word of the additive family, and the only one that presupposes the addition is unwelcome.

**1. In addition to — with the second item as a burden.**

1. *On top of* the rent, they pay their own utilities and trash.
2. The clinic charges a facility fee *on top of* the doctor's bill.

**2. With a gerund.**

3. *On top of* working two jobs, she's finishing a degree at night.
4. *On top of* having to repaint, we found rot under the siding.

**3. The fixed phrase *on top of everything else*.**

5. *On top of* everything else, the furnace quit on the coldest night of the year.
6. She lost the contract and, *on top of* that, the client wants the deposit back.

**4. Before a *wh*-clause.**

7. *On top of* what the movers charged, we paid two weeks of storage.
8. *On top of* whatever the insurer covers, there's a $2,000 deductible.

**See also:** [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [as well as](#as-well-as), [besides](#besides), [over and above](#over-and-above), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention)

### over and above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvər ənd əˈbʌv/ &middot; weak /ˌoʊvɚ ən əˈbʌv/ — the *and* is always reduced to /ən/ or /n̩/ &middot; *OH-vur-un-uh-BUV*
**Русский:** сверх, помимо — прибавка поверх уже выполненного обязательства или нормы
**Type:** complex (frozen) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; standard in compensation, contracts, and budgets
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund is possible but uncommon (*over and above having to repay the advance*). Never a finite clause.
**Pattern:** [amount] *over and above* [amount] &middot; [clause] *over and above* [noun phrase] &middot; *Over and above* [noun phrase], [clause]
**Contrast:** *Over and above* is *in addition to* with one extra claim built in: the addition sits **on top of a baseline that has already been satisfied**. A bonus over and above base salary means the salary was already paid in full; an hour over and above the minimum means the minimum was already met. Where *in addition to* is neutral about the first item, *over and above* certifies it. Do not read the phrase compositionally — it is frozen, and neither *over* nor *above* keeps its spatial meaning inside it.

Names what is added once an obligation has already been discharged. It is the standard phrase for a bonus, an overage, and a duty gone beyond.

**1. Money added to an amount already owed.**

1. The bonus is paid *over and above* base salary.
2. Any expenses *over and above* the retainer must be approved in writing.

**2. Effort or service beyond a required minimum.**

3. She did that work *over and above* anything the job actually required.
4. The district demands sixty hours of training *over and above* the state minimum.

**3. Adding a further consideration to an argument.**

5. *Over and above* the cost, there's the question of whether anyone would use it.
6. The building is unsafe, and *over and above* that, it isn't even zoned for offices.

**See also:** [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [as well as](#as-well-as), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [besides](#besides)

### plus

**Pronunciation:** /plʌs/ &middot; weak /pləs/ in rapid speech &middot; *PLUHS*
**Русский:** плюс — прибавляет число или пункт; предлог, поэтому глагол остаётся в единственном числе
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral as a preposition; the sentence-initial *Plus,* is informal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund is possible but rare (*plus having to repaint*). Never a finite clause. The word is also an adjective (*a plus sign*, *an A plus*), a noun (*that's a real plus*), and an informal sentence adverb (*Plus, it's cheaper*) — in those uses it has no object and is not a preposition.
**Pattern:** [number] *plus* [number] &middot; [amount] *plus* [noun phrase] &middot; *Plus*, [clause] *(adverb — informal)*
**Contrast:** *Plus* is not *and*. *And* makes a compound subject and takes a plural verb; *plus* is a preposition, so *The base salary plus the bonus **was** more than he expected* keeps the singular. In arithmetic, *Two plus two is four* is the standard form. Against *minus*, its arithmetic opposite; against *in addition to*, its formal equivalent — a job application should not begin a sentence with *Plus*.

Adds one quantity or item to another. It began life as the Latin word for "more" in arithmetic and spread outward from there into prices, packages, and finally into casual speech as a discourse connector.

**1. Arithmetic addition.**

1. Six *plus* four is ten.
2. The perimeter is the length *plus* the width, doubled.

**2. With the addition of.** A cost, a component, an extra.

3. Rent is $1,400 a month *plus* utilities.
4. At closing you'll owe the principal *plus* accrued interest.

**3. And also.** An extra item, slightly informal.

5. He brought his cousin *plus* two friends nobody had met.
6. She got the promotion *plus* a corner office.

**4. As a sentence adverb — not a preposition.** No object follows; informal.

7. It's too far to drive. *Plus*, the parking is impossible.
8. The seats are terrible, and *plus* they cost twice as much.

**See also:** [minus](#minus), [give or take](#give-or-take), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [on top of](#on-top-of), [over and above](#over-and-above), [as well as](#as-well-as), [including](#including), [not to mention](#not-to-mention)

### to say nothing of

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋ ʌv/ &middot; weak /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋ əv/, reduced /tə ˈseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋə/ before a consonant — the *to* is always the weak /tə/, never /tu/ &middot; *tuh-SAY-NUTH-ing-uv*
**Русский:** не говоря уже о — книжн. двойник not to mention: добавляет более веский довод
**Type:** complex (a frozen infinitival that has become a preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or **adjective**; a *that*-clause only through *the fact that*. Never a bare finite clause, and — because the *of* is a preposition — never a bare infinitive: *to say nothing of paying for it*, not ✗ *to say nothing of pay for it*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *to say nothing of* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *to say nothing of* [verb-ing] &middot; [adjective], *to say nothing of* [adjective]
**Variants:** [not to mention](#not-to-mention) — the spoken equivalent, and interchangeable in almost every context
**Contrast:** The formal twin of *not to mention*; the difference is register, not sense. *To say nothing of* is what you meet in edited prose, *not to mention* is what you hear. Both are **rhetorical** rather than neutral — whatever follows is the heavier item, named under the pretense of being skipped — and both are **positionally fixed** at the end of what they add to: ✗ *To say nothing of the cost, the plan is impractical*. Against [let alone](#let-alone), which reverses the polarity and downgrades what follows, *to say nothing of* always upgrades.

Names one more item while claiming to pass over it. Like *not to mention*, the pretense is the whole device, and register is the only thing that separates the two.

**1. Adding a heavier item at the end of a list.**

1. The delay cost them the season, *to say nothing of* the money.
2. Two of the exhibits were mislabeled, *to say nothing of* the one that never arrived.

**2. With a gerund.**

3. He'd have to sell the house, *to say nothing of* explaining it to his sister.
4. Rewriting the code took a month, *to say nothing of* testing it.

**3. Adding an adjective or a further judgment.**

5. The proposal is expensive, *to say nothing of* legally doubtful.
6. The trip was long and cold, *to say nothing of* pointless.

**4. With *the fact that*, where a clause is unavoidable.**

7. The deal is bad on its face, *to say nothing of* the fact that nobody on the board has read it.
8. She was unqualified, *to say nothing of* the fact that she had been fired twice.

**See also:** [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [let alone](#let-alone), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [besides](#besides), [over and above](#over-and-above), [as well as](#as-well-as)

### together with

**Pronunciation:** /təˈɡɛðər wɪð/ (also /wɪθ/) &middot; weak /təˈɡɛðɚ wəð/, /təˈɡɛðɚ wəθ/ &middot; *tuh-GETH-ur with*
**Русский:** вместе с, совместно с — офиц. вариант along with; на число глагола не влияет
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; standard in legal and conveyancing language
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [clause] *together with* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject], *together with* [noun phrase], [**singular** verb]
**Contrast:** The formal twin of *along with*, and the two are freely interchangeable in meaning. Neither is instrumental — that is plain [with](02-core-relational.md#with). Like every word in this section, it is a preposition and does **not** pluralize a singular subject.

Attaches a second item to the first in writing that wants to sound deliberate. It is the standard formula in deeds and contracts, where *together with all appurtenances* has been boilerplate for centuries, and it carries a trace of that formality into ordinary prose.

**1. Accompanying an item in a transaction or a submission.**

1. The deed conveys the parcel *together with* all easements and rights of way.
2. Please return the signed form *together with* a check for the filing fee.

**2. Adding one factor to another.**

3. Heat, *together with* the humidity, makes the afternoons unbearable.
4. Send the transcript *together with* three letters of recommendation.

**3. Appended to a subject.** Singular verb.

5. The chairman, *together with* two board members, **was** named in the suit.
6. The principal, *together with* any unpaid interest, **becomes** due on demand.

## Exception, exclusion, and subtraction

The largest family in the file, and the one with the most rules. It splits four ways: the everyday exception words (*except*, *except for*, *apart from*, *aside from*, *besides*, *other than*, *outside of*, *but*, and the ponderous *with the exception of*); the participials and hypotheticals (*barring*, *excluding*, *excepting*, *failing*, *leaving aside*, *not counting*, *short of*, and the counterfactual pair *but for* and *if not for*); the arithmetic and commercial subtractors (*less*, *minus*, and the two-way *give or take*); and a literary and archaic layer that American readers still meet in older books and in contracts (*save*, *save for*, *saving*, *bar*, *bating*). One further entry, *to the exclusion of*, is filed here by spelling rather than by sense: it does not take a member out of a set but names what got crowded out when something else took up all the room.

One rule governs the first group and is worth stating before you reach the entries. **Bare *except* and bare *but* need an all-inclusive word in front of them** — *all*, *every*, *everyone*, *everything*, *everywhere*, *nothing*, *no one*, *nowhere*, *any-*, a superlative, or a *wh-* question word. Without one, the sentence needs *except for*: *Except the noise, it's fine* is an error, *Except for the noise, it's fine* is correct, and *Everything except the noise is fine* is correct because *everything* is doing the work.

### apart from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpɑrt frʌm/ &middot; weak /əˈpɑrt frəm/, /əˈpɑrt frm̩/ &middot; *uh-PART frum*
**Русский:** кроме, если не считать — но при отрицании или с also, наоборот, «помимо, вдобавок к»
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a ***wh*-clause** (*apart from what the neighbors told us*, *apart from whether it's legal*) — never a *that*-clause and never a tensed clause on its own
**Pattern:** *Apart from* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *apart from* [noun phrase] &middot; *apart from* [verb-ing] &middot; *apart from* [*wh*-clause]
**Contrast:** *Apart from* and *aside from* mean the same thing; *aside from* is the more American of the two and *apart from* the more British, though both are current in the United States. Both are ambiguous between "except for" and "in addition to", and only context settles it: after a plain positive claim they subtract, but before *also*, *too*, or *as well*, or alongside a negative, they add.

Sets an item outside the claim being made — either by exempting it from a generalization or, in the additive reading, by putting it beside one. The literal reading, "at a distance from", is still live and is the source of both.

**1. Except for.** Subtracting a qualification.

1. *Apart from* a scratch on the bumper, the car is in perfect shape.
2. Everyone *apart from* the two interns had already gone home.

**2. In addition to.** Adding one more item, usually signaled by *also* or a negative.

3. *Apart from* being the cheapest option, it's also the fastest.
4. *Apart from* the tuition, there are lab fees and a parking permit.

**3. Before a *wh*-clause.**

5. *Apart from* what the neighbors told us, we know nothing about the family.
6. *Apart from* whether the roof will hold, there's the wiring to think about.

**4. Separate from.** The literal spatial sense, and the source of the other three.

7. He stood *apart from* the rest of the group, saying nothing.
8. Keep the recyclables *apart from* the regular trash.

**See also:** [aside from](#aside-from), [besides](#besides), [except for](#except-for), [other than](#other-than), [outside of](#outside-of)

### aside from

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsaɪd frʌm/ &middot; weak /əˈsaɪd frəm/, /əˈsaɪd frm̩/ &middot; *uh-SYD frum*
**Русский:** кроме, если не считать; также «помимо, вдобавок к» — амер. вариант apart from
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral; chiefly North American
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a ***wh*-clause** (*aside from what it costs*, *aside from whether it's legal*) — never a *that*-clause and never a tensed clause on its own
**Pattern:** *Aside from* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *aside from* [noun phrase] &middot; *aside from* [verb-ing] &middot; *aside from* [*wh*-clause]
**Contrast:** Identical in meaning to *apart from*; the difference is geography, not sense. American writers reach for *aside from* first, British writers for *apart from*. Like *apart from*, it swings between subtraction and addition on the polarity of the sentence around it. Against *except for*, it is looser — *except for* commits to subtraction, *aside from* does not.

The default American way to fence off an exception before making a general claim. It opens sentences more comfortably than almost anything else in this file, which is why it turns up constantly in reviews, summaries, and complaints.

**1. Except for.**

1. *Aside from* a few typos, the draft is ready to send.
2. Nobody *aside from* the two of us knows the combination.

**2. In addition to.**

3. *Aside from* running the shop, he coaches a Little League team.
4. *Aside from* being illegal, it's a terrible idea.

**3. Before a *wh*-clause.**

5. *Aside from* whether it's legal, it's a terrible way to treat people.
6. *Aside from* what the inspector wrote down, nobody has said a word to us.

**4. Opening a concession before the main point.**

7. *Aside from* the cost, there's the question of whether it will even fit.
8. *Aside from* one bad winter, we've never had trouble with the pipes.

**See also:** [apart from](#apart-from), [besides](#besides), [except for](#except-for), [other than](#other-than), [outside of](#outside-of)

### bar

**Pronunciation:** /bɑr/ &middot; *BAR*
**Русский:** кроме, за исключением — в амер. англ. живо лишь в обороте bar none «без исключений»
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** formal and chiefly British in free use; standard in American English only inside the fixed phrase *bar none*
**Complement:** noun phrase — no gerund, no clause
**Pattern:** [superlative claim], *bar* none &middot; all *bar* [number] *(chiefly British)* &middot; *bar* [noun phrase] *(chiefly British)*
**Contrast:** In American English *bar* survives almost entirely in *bar none*, a fixed tag meaning "with no exceptions at all" that follows a superlative. Where a British writer uses *bar* freely, an American writes *barring* or *except for*. Do not confuse the preposition with the verb *bar* (*barred from the premises*) or the several nouns.

Built on the verb *bar* exactly as *barring* is, and now nearly frozen. In its one thriving American use it does no grammatical work beyond intensifying a superlative that has already been made.

**1. The fixed American phrase.** After a superlative: with no exceptions.

1. It's the best barbecue in the state, *bar* none.
2. She is the finest trial lawyer in this building, *bar* none.

**2. General exception — chiefly British.** An American ear hears this as an import.

3. Every seat was sold, *bar* a handful in the last row.
4. All the runners finished *bar* one, who pulled up at mile eighteen.
5. *Bar* a late collapse, they'll take the division. *(an American would write* barring*)*

**3. Not a preposition — the verb and the noun.** The preposition takes an object and means "except"; the verb means "to shut out" and takes a direct object of its own, and the nouns are unrelated to both.

6. The court *barred* him from the property for a year. *(verb)*
7. They met at a *bar* two blocks from the courthouse. *(noun)*

**See also:** [barring](#barring), [except for](#except-for), [save](#save), [but](#but)

### barring

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɑrɪŋ/ &middot; *BAR-ing*
**Русский:** если не будет, за исключением — оговорка к прогнозу: называет возможную помеху
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral; a staple of forecasting and sports and business journalism
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — **never a finite clause**. Not *barring it rains*; write *barring rain* or switch to the conjunction *unless*.
**Pattern:** *Barring* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *barring* [noun phrase] &middot; *barring* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** *Barring* names a future contingency that would spoil an otherwise confident prediction; *except for* subtracts a real member from a set that already exists. *Barring an injury* looks forward, *except for the injury* looks back. Against *failing*, which is its near mirror: *barring X* frames X as the unwelcome disruption, *failing X* frames X as the outcome you were hoping for.

Fences off the one thing that could go wrong and then makes the prediction anyway. It is the single most useful hedge in American reporting, because it lets a writer commit to a forecast while naming its escape hatch.

**1. Excluding a contingency from a prediction.**

1. *Barring* a late snowstorm, the roads should be clear by Friday.
2. *Barring* injuries, this is the deepest roster they've had in years.
3. *Barring* something unforeseen, closing is set for the fifteenth.

**2. Plain exception.** Slightly formal, less common than sense 1.

4. Every member voted yes, *barring* two who abstained.
5. The archive is complete, *barring* a handful of volumes lost in the fire.

**3. With a gerund, and with an object plus a gerund.**

6. *Barring* getting called in, I'm free all weekend.
7. *Barring* the river coming up again, the crews will be finished by Thursday.

**See also:** [bar](#bar), [failing](#failing), [short of](#short-of), [except for](#except-for), [absent](#absent)

### bating

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbeɪtɪŋ/ &middot; *BAY-ting*
**Русский:** устар. за исключением, не считая — встречается только в книгах XVIII–XIX вв.
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Complement:** noun phrase — never a clause
**Pattern:** *Bating* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *bating* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** An archaic cousin of *excepting* and *saving*, formed from the verb *abate*, "to take away". No living American writes it; you meet it only in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose, where it means "leaving out of account". Modern equivalents: *excepting*, *apart from*, *leaving aside*. Do not confuse it with *baiting* (luring), and note that the *bated* of *with bated breath* is the same root but a frozen idiom, not this preposition.

Sets a small deduction aside before endorsing the whole. It is included here for readers of older books, not as a word to use.

**1. Setting a small deduction aside from a record or an agreement.**

1. *Bating* a few small errors of transcription, the manuscript is faithful to the original.
2. The account is complete, *bating* the two pages torn from the ledger.
3. The terms were agreed, *bating* the question of the boundary line.

**2. Excepting one person or one trait from a judgment.**

4. *Bating* his temper, he was as fine a foreman as the yard ever had.
5. All the company came away satisfied, *bating* one gentleman who had lost his hat.

**See also:** [excepting](#excepting), [saving](#saving), [save](#save), [excluding](#excluding)

### besides

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsaɪdz/ &middot; weak /bəˈsaɪdz/ &middot; *bih-SYDZ*
**Русский:** кроме, помимо — «вдобавок к», а после отрицания «за исключением»; ср. beside «рядом с»
**Type:** compound (historically *be-* + *side* + adverbial *-s*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the adverb use is conversational
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause. A *what*-phrase after it (*besides what he told us*) is a **fused relative**, which is a noun phrase and not a clause; the test is that a plain noun can replace it (*besides his statement*). As an **adverb** it takes no object at all (*Besides, it's too late*), and that is its commonest use in speech.
**Pattern:** *Besides* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [negative or question] *besides* [noun phrase] &middot; *besides* [verb-ing] &middot; *Besides*, [clause] *(adverb)*
**Contrast:** **Beside vs. besides.** *Beside* without the *-s* means "at the side of" and belongs with the position prepositions, along with the two frozen idioms *beside the point* and *beside himself*. *Besides* with the *-s* means "in addition to" or "except for", and is also an adverb meaning "anyway". *Sit beside me* / *Nobody besides me came*. Writing *besides the point*, or *beside the salary there's a bonus*, is a real and frequent error. Against *except for* and *in addition to*, *besides* is the ambiguous one — careful writers pick the unambiguous word when the reading matters.

Puts one more item next to what has been said, or — after a negative — carves one out. It is the oldest of the additive prepositions and the only one that has also become a discourse marker, which is now what most Americans use it for.

**1. In addition to.**

1. *Besides* the mortgage, they're carrying two car loans.
2. Who else is coming *besides* your brother?

**2. Except for.** Nearly always after a negative or an all-inclusive word.

3. No one *besides* the surgeon was allowed in the room.
4. There's nothing in the refrigerator *besides* condiments.

**3. With a gerund.**

5. *Besides* teaching, she edits a quarterly journal.
6. *Besides* costing twice as much, the new system is slower.

**4. As an adverb — not a preposition.** No object; means "anyway" or "moreover".

7. It's too far, and *besides*, I don't have the money.
8. I'm not going. *Besides*, I wasn't invited.

**See also:** [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [in addition to](#in-addition-to), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [other than](#other-than), [except for](#except-for)

### but

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt/ &middot; weak /bət/ &middot; *BUHT*
**Русский:** кроме — только после all, everyone, nothing и т. п.: everyone but me «все, кроме меня»
**Type:** simple; the same form is the coordinating conjunction *but* (see the [conjunctions dictionary](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#but)) and an adverb in *all but* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase or pronoun (objective case in American usage), an adverb or prepositional phrase (*anywhere but here*, *any time but now*), a **bare infinitive** after a *do*-phrase or after *cannot* (*nothing to do but wait*, *one cannot but admire it*), or a ***to*-infinitive** after *choice*, *option*, or *alternative* (*no choice but to resign*). **The two infinitives are not interchangeable:** ✗ *nothing to do but to wait*, ✗ *no choice but resign*. **Never a finite clause** — *but that* is the conjunction, not this preposition, and *but* never takes a gerund.
**Requires:** an all-inclusive or negative word earlier in the sentence — *all*, *every*, *everyone*, *everything*, *everywhere*, *no one*, *nothing*, *nowhere*, *any-*, a superlative, or a *wh-* question word. Without one it is ungrammatical: not *I saw the movie but the ending*, but *I saw everything but the ending*.
**Pattern:** [universal/negative word] *but* [noun phrase] &middot; nothing *but* [noun phrase] &middot; nothing to do *but* [bare infinitive] &middot; no choice *but to* [verb] &middot; cannot *but* [bare infinitive]
**Contrast:** Prepositional *but* is the tightly restricted twin of *except*, and where *except* can stand at the head of a sentence with *for*, *but* cannot — there is no ✗ *but for the noise* in this sense (*but for* is a separate, counterfactual preposition). Against the conjunction *but*, which joins two clauses of equal rank and reduces to /bət/: the preposition takes an object and is usually stressed.

Subtracts one member from a set that has already been declared total. Because it depends entirely on that earlier totalizing word, it feels less like a preposition and more like a second half of a construction — which is why it also lives inside a handful of frozen quantifying phrases.

**1. Except, after a universal or a negative.**

1. Everyone *but* Marcus signed the petition.
2. The store is open every day *but* Sunday.
3. Nobody *but* you would have noticed.

**2. Before an adverb or a prepositional phrase.**

4. He'd go anywhere *but* back to that office.
5. Any weekend *but* this one would work.

**3. Before an infinitive — bare after a *do*-phrase, *to* after *choice*.** This is the one place learners of English go wrong with *but*, and the two patterns do not mix.

6. There was nothing to do *but* wait for the tow truck.
7. There is nothing left to say *but* congratulations.
8. They had no choice *but* to postpone the wedding.
9. With the bridge closed there was no option *but* to double back.

**4. In frozen quantifying phrases.** *Nothing but* = only; *all but* = almost; *cannot but* = cannot help.

10. The season was nothing *but* injuries and rain delays.
11. The bill is all *but* dead in committee.
12. One cannot *but* admire the nerve of it. *(formal)*

**5. Not a preposition — the conjunction and the adverb.** The preposition takes an object; the conjunction joins two units of equal rank and takes none, and the adverbial *but* means "only".

13. I called twice, *but* nobody picked up. *(conjunction — two clauses, no object)*
14. The paint is dry *but* still tacky in one corner. *(conjunction — two adjectives)*
15. The tank was *but* half full when they set out. *(adverb, "only" — formal and dated)*

**Case note:** American usage takes the objective pronoun — *everyone but me*, *no one but him*. The traditional rule that a subject-position *but*-phrase takes the nominative (*No one but he knew*) is now bookish; *No one but him knew* is what educated Americans write and say.

**See also:** [except](#except), [except for](#except-for), [but for](#but-for), [other than](#other-than), [save](#save)

### but for

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʌt fɔr/ &middot; weak /ˈbʌt fɚ/ (also written /fər/), and in rapid speech /ˈbʌʔ fɚ/ with a glottal *t* &middot; *BUHT fur*
**Русский:** если бы не — юр. и книжн.; главное предложение обязательно с would (have)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; standard in legal English
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a clause. To take a clause, write *but for the fact that*.
**Requires:** a **counterfactual** main clause, with *would*, *would have*, *might have*, or *could have*.
**Pattern:** *But for* [noun phrase], [subject] *would (have)* [verb] &middot; [subject] *would (have)* [verb] *but for* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *But for* is not *except for*. *Except for* subtracts a real item from a real situation (*Except for the dessert, the meal was excellent*); *but for* deletes an item and reports what would have followed (*But for the dessert, the meal would have been forgettable*). *Without* says the same thing more plainly, and the everyday American equivalent is [if not for](#if-not-for), which requires exactly the same counterfactual main clause. It is unrelated to prepositional *but*.

Names the one thing that stood between the world as it is and the world it would otherwise have been. Its home is American tort law, where **but-for causation** — the injury would not have occurred but for the defendant's conduct — is the standard test, and it carries that argumentative weight into ordinary prose.

**1. Naming the decisive cause of a good outcome.**

1. *But for* your testimony, the case would have been dismissed.
2. She would have drowned *but for* the lifeguard on the north jetty.

**2. Naming the decisive obstacle to one.**

3. *But for* a flat tire, we'd have been there by noon.
4. *But for* the pandemic, the restaurant might still be open.

**3. In legal and financial argument.**

5. The company would be insolvent *but for* the parent's guarantee.
6. *But for* the officer's failure to secure the scene, the evidence would have been admissible.

**See also:** [if not for](#if-not-for), [without](#without), [except for](#except-for), [absent](#absent), [failing](#failing), [but](#but)

### except

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt/ (also /ɛkˈsɛpt/) &middot; weak /əkˈsɛpt/ &middot; *ik-SEPT*
**Русский:** кроме, за исключением — требует в предложении all/every/nothing; иначе нужно except for
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, prepositional phrase (*except in emergencies*), adverb (*except here*, *except yesterday*), **bare infinitive** after *do* (*nothing to do except wait*), or *to*-infinitive (*no choice except to agree*). A finite clause is possible **only** through a following conjunction — *except that*, *except when*, *except if*, *except where* — and at that point *except* is a conjunction, not this preposition.
**Requires:** an all-inclusive word already in the sentence — *all*, *every*, *everyone*, *everything*, *everywhere*, *always*, *nothing*, *no one*, *the whole*. Without one, use *except for*.
**Pattern:** [totalizing word] *except* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *except* [adverbial] &middot; nothing to do *except* [bare infinitive] &middot; *except that* [clause] *(conjunction)*
**Contrast:** **Except vs. except for vs. excepting.** Use *except* when a totalizing word is already present (*everyone*, *all*, *nothing*, *every day*). Use *except for* when there is no such word, when the phrase opens the sentence, or when the thing subtracted is not the same kind of thing as the set — *Except for a few typos, the essay is clean*, because typos are not essays. *Excepting* is formal and now confined to *not excepting* and *always excepting*. Sentence-initial bare *except* — *Except the noise, it's fine* — is an error. The test for the conjunction is the one used everywhere else in this file: the preposition takes an object, while *except that*, *except when*, and *except if* take a whole finite clause. And do not confuse any of it with the verb *accept*, which is a different word entirely.

The plain, unmarked word for taking one member out of a stated whole. Everything else in this section is a stylistic or grammatical variation on it.

**1. Subtracting a member from a total set.**

1. Everyone *except* the two of us went home early.
2. She remembered nothing *except* the sound of the brakes.

**2. Before an adverbial — a time, a place, a condition.**

3. The museum is open daily *except* Monday.
4. The rule applies everywhere *except* on federal land.

**3. Before an infinitive.**

5. There was nothing to do *except* apologize and start over.
6. He had no option *except* to resign.

**4. Before a clause — with *that*, or in informal speech without it.** Here it is a conjunction.

7. I'd have told you *except that* it wasn't mine to tell.
8. I'd go, *except* I'm broke until Friday. *(informal)*

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [with the exception of](#with-the-exception-of), [excepting](#excepting), [but](#but), [other than](#other-than), [apart from](#apart-from)

### except for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt fɔr/ &middot; weak /əkˈsɛpt fɚ/ (also written /fər/), and before a consonant often /əkˈsɛp fɚ/ &middot; *ik-SEPT fur*
**Русский:** кроме, если не считать — не требует обобщающего слова и может открывать предложение
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** *Except for* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *except for* [noun phrase] &middot; *except for* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** Where *except* needs a totalizing word already in place, *except for* supplies its own frame and can open a sentence on its own. *Except for* is also the right choice when the thing subtracted is a different kind of thing from the set — a typo is not an essay, a squeak is not a car. Against *but for*, which is counterfactual and demands a *would*-clause; against *apart from* and *aside from*, which are looser and may be read additively.

Withdraws a qualification from a claim that would otherwise be sweeping. It is the workhorse of the exception family in American English and the safe default when you are unsure whether bare *except* will hold.

**1. Subtracting a flaw from a general endorsement.**

1. *Except for* a cracked screen, the phone works fine.
2. The team played well *except for* the third quarter.

**2. Naming the only thing left in an otherwise empty scene.**

3. The house is empty *except for* a folding chair in the kitchen.
4. The lot was dark *except for* one security light over the door.

**3. Opening a sentence, where bare *except* cannot.**

5. *Except for* Tuesdays, my afternoons are open.
6. *Except for* having to change planes in Dallas, it was an easy trip.

**See also:** [except](#except), [with the exception of](#with-the-exception-of), [but for](#but-for), [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [save for](#save-for)

### excepting

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛptɪŋ/ &middot; weak /əkˈsɛptɪŋ/ &middot; *ik-SEP-ting*
**Русский:** устар./юр. исключая — живо лишь в not excepting «включая даже» и always excepting
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, dated
**Complement:** noun phrase — no gerund, no clause
**Pattern:** not *excepting* [noun phrase] &middot; always *excepting* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *excepting* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Excepting* is what *except for* replaced. Outside the two fixed emphatics *not excepting* ("including even") and *always excepting* ("with the standing exception of"), it sounds stiff or antique in American prose. *Excluding* is the current neutral participial; *saving* and *bating* are the archaic ones.

Marks an exemption with more ceremony than the situation usually requires. Its two surviving phrases pull in opposite directions: *not excepting* insists that nothing is exempt, *always excepting* insists that one thing permanently is.

**1. *Not excepting* — including even.**

1. All employees, *not excepting* senior partners, must complete the training.
2. The delegates voted unanimously, *not excepting* the two who had objected in committee.

**2. *Always excepting* — with a standing carve-out.**

3. The policy covers every kind of loss, *always excepting* acts of war.
4. The fee schedule applies to all filings, *always excepting* emergency petitions.

**3. Plain participial exception.** Dated in American writing.

5. Every parcel on the block, *excepting* the corner lot, was rezoned.
6. All charges were dropped, *excepting* the count of criminal trespass.

**See also:** [except](#except), [except for](#except-for), [excluding](#excluding), [saving](#saving), [bating](#bating)

### excluding

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskludɪŋ/ &middot; weak /əkˈskludɪŋ/ &middot; *ik-SKLOO-ding*
**Русский:** исключая, не считая — нейтрально-деловое: что не входит в сумму или множество
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in pricing, statistics, and specifications
**Complement:** noun phrase or gerund — never a finite clause
**Pattern:** [total] *excluding* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *excluding* [noun phrase] &middot; *excluding* [verb-ing]
**Contrast:** The exact mirror of *including*, and the modern neutral replacement for *excepting*. Against *except for*, it is colder and more quantitative — *excluding* belongs on an invoice, *except for* belongs in a sentence. *Exclusive of* is its formal-contractual variant, *not counting* its conversational one. Against *less* and *minus*, which perform a real arithmetic subtraction: *excluding* only declares something out of scope.

Draws a line around a figure or a set and says what is outside it. Because it is neutral and precise, it is the safe word wherever a number has to be defended.

**1. Removing a component from a price.**

1. The list price is $34,900, *excluding* destination charges and tax.
2. The estimate covers labor only, *excluding* hauling away the old unit.

**2. Removing a group from a statistic.**

3. Enrollment rose four percent, *excluding* transfer students.
4. Revenue grew nine percent *excluding* currency effects.

**3. Removing a member from a set.**

5. Everyone on the roster, *excluding* the two on injured reserve, made the trip.
6. The building sleeps twelve, *excluding* the finished basement.

**See also:** [including](#including), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [not counting](#not-counting), [except for](#except-for), [less](#less), [to the exclusion of](#to-the-exclusion-of)

### exclusive of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsklusɪv ʌv/ &middot; weak /əkˈsklusɪv əv/, reduced /əkˈsklusɪvə/ before a consonant &middot; *ik-SKLOO-siv uv*
**Русский:** офиц. не включая, без учёта — что не входит в названную цену или величину
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; commercial and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase only — no gerund, no clause
**Pattern:** [noun] *exclusive of* [noun phrase] &middot; [price] **is** *exclusive of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The exact opposite of *inclusive of*, and the contractual twin of *excluding*. In ordinary American writing, *not including* or *plus tax* does the same work without the starch. Do not confuse it with the adjective *exclusive* meaning "restricted" or "sole" (*an exclusive contract*), which is a different sense of the same word.

States what a quoted figure leaves out, in language a court could read. It is the price-list word, and it postmodifies a noun or follows *be* rather than floating freely through a sentence.

**1. Of a price, a rate, or a fee.**

1. All prices are quoted *exclusive of* sales tax.
2. The rent is $1,900 a month, *exclusive of* utilities.
3. The retainer is *exclusive of* court costs and expert witness fees.

**2. Of a measurement or a headcount.**

4. The trailer measures forty feet, *exclusive of* the tongue.
5. Headcount stands at 212, *exclusive of* contractors and seasonal staff.
6. The bid covers materials only, *exclusive of* the general contractor's fee.

**3. Of a span of dates.**

7. The policy runs from the first of the month, *exclusive of* the day of purchase.
8. The notice period is fourteen days, *exclusive of* weekends and federal holidays.

**See also:** [inclusive of](#inclusive-of), [excluding](#excluding), [less](#less), [not counting](#not-counting), [free of](#free-of), [to the exclusion of](#to-the-exclusion-of)

### failing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfeɪlɪŋ/ &middot; *FAY-ling*
**Русский:** за неимением, если не выйдет — вводит запасной вариант: failing that «если не получится»
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase — most often the pronouns *that* and *which* (*failing that*, *failing which*), and otherwise an abstract noun of outcome (*a settlement*, *agreement*, *payment*). Never a finite clause, never a gerund, never a *to*-infinitive: ✗ *failing to reach a settlement, the case goes to trial* is a dangling participle of the verb *fail*, not this preposition.
**Pattern:** *Failing* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], or *failing that*, [alternative] &middot; [requirement], *failing which* [consequence]
**Contrast:** *Failing* and *barring* both introduce a condition, and both can often be swapped, but they take opposite attitudes to what they name. *Failing X* treats X as the outcome you wanted, and what follows is the fallback; *barring X* treats X as the accident that would spoil an expected result. *Absent X* is the legal register's version of *failing X*, and simple *without* the plain one.

Names the thing that has to happen, then supplies the plan for when it doesn't. The fixed phrase *failing that* is by far its commonest form and is the most efficient two words in English for "if that doesn't work".

**1. Introducing a fallback after a named prerequisite.**

1. *Failing* a settlement, the case goes to trial in October.
2. *Failing* agreement between the parties, the matter goes to arbitration.

**2. The fixed phrase *failing that*.**

3. Ask at the front desk; *failing that*, call the number posted on the door.
4. We'll fly out Thursday or, *failing that*, drive up on Friday.

**3. In procedural and contractual language.**

5. *Failing* any objection from the floor, the motion is adopted.
6. *Failing* a qualified buyer by June, the bank takes the property.

**4. The formula *failing which*.** A fixed relative that hangs a consequence on the failure of what was just required. Formal, and chiefly contractual.

7. Payment is due on the first, *failing which* the account goes to collections.
8. The tenant must cure the breach within ten days, *failing which* the lease terminates.

**See also:** [barring](#barring), [absent](#absent), [without](#without), [short of](#short-of), [but for](#but-for), [if not for](#if-not-for)

### give or take

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪv ɔr ˈteɪk/ &middot; weak /ˈɡɪv ɚ ˈteɪk/ — the *or* reduces to /ɚ/ and the three words run together as one &middot; *GIV-ur-TAYK*
**Русский:** плюс-минус, примерно — допуск в обе стороны от названной величины
**Type:** marginal (a frozen coordination that behaves as a preposition) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Complement:** a noun phrase naming a **quantity** — a number, a measure, a span of time. No gerund, no clause, and no ordinary object: ✗ *give or take the traffic*. It can also stand with **no object at all** (*forty people, give or take*), and in that use it is an adverb, not a preposition.
**Pattern:** [quantity], *give or take* [smaller quantity] &middot; [quantity], *give or take* *(adverb — no object)*
**Variants:** *plus or minus* — the technical equivalent, at home on a spec sheet where *give or take* would sound careless
**Contrast:** *Give or take* is the only member of this family that subtracts and adds at the same time: it names a margin of error on **both sides** of a figure, where *less* and *minus* deduct in one direction only. Against *about* and *around*, which hedge a figure without saying how far the hedge runs, *give or take* states the tolerance out loud. It attaches only to quantities — a figure with *give or take* after it is an estimate, never a count.

Puts a tolerance on a number. It is the spoken American way of saying "plus or minus", and its presence is what turns a stated figure into an admitted approximation.

**1. Naming a margin on a count.**

1. There were sixty people in the room, *give or take* a dozen.
2. The lot holds forty cars, *give or take* a few.

**2. Naming a margin on a measurement or a distance.**

3. It's about ninety miles from here, *give or take* five.
4. The ceiling is nine feet, *give or take* an inch.

**3. Naming a margin on time.**

5. The drive takes two hours, *give or take* twenty minutes.
6. They closed on the house in March of '09, *give or take* a month.

**4. With no object — the adverb.** Nothing follows, and the margin is left vague.

7. He's been at the paper thirty years, *give or take*.
8. The room seats two hundred, *give or take*.

**See also:** [plus](#plus), [minus](#minus), [less](#less), [short of](#short-of), [next to](#next-to)

### if not for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈnɑt fɔr/ &middot; weak /ɪf ˈnɑt fɚ/ (also written /fər/), reduced /ɪf ˈnɑʔ fɚ/ with a glottal *t* &middot; *if-NAHT fur*
**Русский:** если бы не — разг. эквивалент but for; главное предложение тоже с would (have)
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; the everyday American *but for*
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun — never a gerund and **never a finite clause**. For a clause, write *if it weren't for* or *if it hadn't been for*, which are full clauses with their own subject and verb and not this preposition.
**Requires:** a **counterfactual** main clause, with *would*, *would have*, *might have*, or *could have* — exactly what *but for* requires.
**Pattern:** *If not for* [noun phrase], [subject] *would (have)* [verb] &middot; [subject] *would (have)* [verb] *if not for* [noun phrase]
**Variants:** *if it weren't for*, *if it hadn't been for*, *were it not for* (formal) — clausal expansions of the same idea, and the route to take when a clause has to follow
**Contrast:** *If not for* is [but for](#but-for) in ordinary clothes: identical in meaning, identical in what it demands of the main clause, and different only in register — *but for* belongs to legal and formal writing, *if not for* to speech and general prose. Neither is *except for*. *Except for the dessert, the meal was good* reports the world as it is; *if not for the dessert, the meal would have been forgettable* reports a world that never happened.

Deletes one item from the record and reports what would have followed. It is the phrase most Americans reach for when the courtroom *but for* would sound stiff.

**1. Naming the decisive cause of a good outcome.**

1. *If not for* the neighbors, the fire would have taken the whole block.
2. She'd have missed the flight *if not for* a delay on the tarmac.

**2. Naming the decisive obstacle.**

3. *If not for* the rain, we'd have finished the roof on Saturday.
4. *If not for* one bad quarter, the company would have had a record year.

**3. In argument and explanation.**

5. The bill would have died in committee *if not for* two senators from farm states.
6. *If not for* the second surgery, he'd have been back at work by June.

**See also:** [but for](#but-for), [without](#without), [absent](#absent), [in the absence of](#in-the-absence-of), [failing](#failing)

### leaving aside

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlivɪŋ əˈsaɪd/ &middot; *LEE-ving uh-SYD*
**Русский:** оставляя в стороне, если отвлечься от — вопрос не отрицается, а откладывается
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a staple of argument and analysis
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, gerund, or a ***wh*-clause** (*leaving aside whether it's legal*, *leaving aside what it costs*) — never a *that*-clause and never a tensed clause on its own
**Pattern:** *Leaving aside* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *leaving aside* [noun phrase] &middot; *leaving aside* [*wh*-clause] &middot; *leaving aside* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *setting aside*, *putting aside*, *leaving out*, *leaving to one side* (more formal) — freely interchangeable
**Contrast:** Where *excluding* takes an item out of a **count** and *except for* takes it out of a **claim**, *leaving aside* takes it out of the **discussion**: the item is not denied, only postponed, which is what lets it govern a *wh*-clause where most of this family cannot. Against *apart from* and *aside from*, which can be read additively, *leaving aside* is unambiguously subtractive. It is a true preposition here and not a dangling participle — nobody in the main clause has to be doing the leaving.

Puts one issue to one side so that the argument can get on with the rest. It concedes that the item matters and declines, for the moment, to take it up.

**1. Postponing an issue before making a point.**

1. *Leaving aside* the cost, the schedule alone makes it impossible.
2. *Leaving aside* the two outliers, the results are remarkably consistent.

**2. Before a *wh*-clause.**

3. *Leaving aside* whether the policy is legal, it is certainly unpopular.
4. *Leaving aside* what the repairs will run, the car is still worth keeping.

**3. With a gerund.**

5. *Leaving aside* having to move twice, the year went about as well as we hoped.
6. *Leaving aside* paying for it, who would even run the program?

**4. Trailing a clause.**

7. The proposal is workable, *leaving aside* the question of who pays for it.
8. Enrollment held steady all year, *leaving aside* a dip in the spring semester.

**See also:** [excluding](#excluding), [apart from](#apart-from), [aside from](#aside-from), [except for](#except-for), [not counting](#not-counting), [excepting](#excepting)

### less

**Pronunciation:** /lɛs/ &middot; weak /ləs/ when it sits unstressed between two figures &middot; *LES*
**Русский:** за вычетом, минус — бухг. и юр.: вычет из суммы (less tax «за вычетом налога»)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** formal; financial, commercial, and legal
**Complement:** noun phrase naming an amount or a deduction — no gerund, no clause, no personal object (*less him* is not English)
**Pattern:** [amount] *less* [deduction] &middot; [amount] *less* any [deduction]
**Contrast:** *Less* and *minus* both subtract, but *less* is the accountant's word and takes an abstract deduction (*less tax*, *less depreciation*, *less attorney's fees*), while *minus* is the arithmetic and everyday word (*ten minus three*, *showed up minus his coat*). *Less* is never casual and never means "without". Keep the preposition apart from the quantifier *less* of the *fewer/less* dispute (*less traffic*, *less expensive*) — that *less* modifies a noun or an adjective directly and has no object of its own.

Subtracts one figure from another in the language of statements and settlements. It is the only word in this file that is genuinely restricted to money and measurable quantities.

**1. Deducting a charge from a sum.**

1. The settlement was $80,000, *less* attorney's fees.
2. She cleared the invoice amount *less* a two percent early-payment discount.
3. You'll get the deposit back *less* any charges for damage.

**2. In the standing formulas of accounting.** The deduction is a named line item and the whole phrase is a definition.

4. Net pay is gross salary *less* withholding and benefit contributions.
5. Revenue *less* cost of goods sold gives you gross profit.
6. Book value is original cost *less* accumulated depreciation.

**3. Deducting a period or a measure.** Chiefly legal and surveying language.

7. The lease runs for one year *less* a day.
8. The parcel is a full acre *less* the strip taken for the easement.
9. The estate distributes the remaining balance *less* administrative expenses.

**4. Not a preposition — the quantifier and the comparative adverb.** Here *less* modifies a noun or an adjective directly and has no object of its own; the preposition always has one.

10. There is *less* traffic on the bridge after nine. *(quantifier — no object)*
11. The second bid came in *less* expensive by about a third. *(comparative adverb)*

### minus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmaɪnəs/ &middot; *MY-nus*
**Русский:** минус — вычитание и «ниже нуля»; разг. «без, лишившись» (came back minus a suitcase)
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic and commerce; informal in the "without" sense
**Complement:** noun phrase or a number — no gerund, no clause. The word is also an adjective (*a minus sign*, *an A minus*) and a noun (*that's a real minus*); in those uses it has no object and is not a preposition.
**Pattern:** [number] *minus* [number] &middot; [amount] *minus* [deduction] &middot; *minus* [number] degrees &middot; [subject] [verb] *minus* [thing lost]
**Contrast:** Against *less*, which is the formal financial word for the same operation; against *without*, which is the neutral and unrestricted word for absence. Informal *minus* meaning "without" implies **loss** — you turn up *minus* something you should have had — which is why *minus his jacket* works and *coffee minus sugar* does not.

Takes one quantity away from another, and by extension takes a thing away from a person who ought to have it. The arithmetic sense is the oldest; the everyday "lacking" sense is a twentieth-century American extension and still carries a joking edge.

**1. Arithmetic subtraction.**

1. Nine *minus* four is five.
2. Take the total *minus* whatever you've already paid.

**2. Below zero on a scale.**

3. It was *minus* twelve at the airport this morning.
4. The forecast has us at *minus* five by Sunday night.

**3. Commercial deduction.**

5. Your share is the sale price *minus* closing costs.
6. The refund is the ticket price *minus* a $35 service fee.

**4. Informally, without — implying loss.**

7. He came back from the trip *minus* a suitcase and a phone.
8. She showed up to the interview *minus* a résumé.

**5. Not a preposition — the adjective and the noun.** No object follows either one.

9. She got an A *minus* on the final. *(adjective)*
10. The commute is the one real *minus* of the job. *(noun)*

### not counting

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ˈkaʊntɪŋ/ &middot; reduced /nɑʔ ˈkaʊnɪŋ/ — the *t* of *not* goes to a glottal stop before the *k*, and the medial *nt* of *counting* drops &middot; *naht-KOWN-ting*
**Русский:** не считая, без учёта — разговорная поправка к подсчёту
**Type:** marginal/participial &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause. A *what*-phrase after it (*not counting what the permits cost*) is a **fused relative**, which is a noun phrase and not a clause; the test is that a plain noun can replace it (*not counting the permit fees*).
**Pattern:** [count or total], *not counting* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause] *not counting* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *not including* — interchangeable and a shade more formal
**Contrast:** The plain-speech version of *excluding* and *exclusive of*. It differs from *except for* in what it operates on: *not counting the kids* adjusts a **number**, while *except for the kids* qualifies a **claim**. Because it is transparently a negated participle, it can be used anywhere the arithmetic is being done out loud.

Corrects a total by naming what was left out of it. Nothing else in the exception family is so comfortable in speech while remaining perfectly precise.

**1. Adjusting a head count.**

1. There were fourteen of us at dinner, *not counting* the kids.
2. She's read everything he wrote, *not counting* the early poems.

**2. Adjusting a measurement or a duration.**

3. The drive takes about six hours, *not counting* stops.
4. It's a two-bedroom, *not counting* the office in the back.

**3. Adjusting a sum or a span.**

5. We've spent around nine thousand, *not counting* what the permits cost.
6. He's been here twenty years, *not counting* the two he spent in Denver.

### other than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðər ðæn/ &middot; weak /ˈʌðər ðən/ &middot; *UH-thur than*
**Русский:** кроме, иной чем — подчёркивает инаковость; допускает и прилагательное, и наречие
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **adjective**, **adverb**, prepositional phrase, gerund, or a *to*-infinitive (*no choice other than to sue*) — never a finite clause
**Requires:** usually a negative, a question, or a totalizing word nearby, much as *but* does — though it is freer than *but* and tolerates more.
**Pattern:** [negative or question] *other than* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause], *other than* [adjective/adverb] &middot; no choice *other than to* [verb] &middot; *Other than that*, [clause]
**Contrast:** *Other than* and *except for* overlap heavily, but *other than* insists on **difference of kind**, which is what lets it take an adjective or an adverb where *except for* cannot: *other than briefly*, *other than legal*, *other than in an emergency*. Do not confuse it with *rather than*, which expresses a choice or a preference (*walk rather than drive*) and never an exception.

Rules out everything but the named case, or says that a thing is of a different sort from what was expected. Its stock phrase *other than that* is one of the most common ways an American speaker signals that a report is otherwise complete.

**1. Except, apart from.**

1. No one *other than* the account holder can request a copy.
2. The clinic accepts no payment *other than* cash.

**2. Before an adjective, adverb, or prepositional phrase.**

3. We've never used the cabin *other than* in the summer.
4. Any use *other than* residential requires a variance.

**3. Before a *to*-infinitive.**

5. He had no choice *other than* to take the plea.
6. There was nothing to do *other than* to wait it out.

**4. The discourse formula.**

7. She's fine, *other than* a bruised shoulder.
8. *Other than that*, the week was completely uneventful.

**Русский:** кроме, помимо (амер. разг.); а также «вне, за пределами» чьей-л. компетенции
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal in the exception sense; **chiefly American**
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, or gerund — never a finite clause. Bare *outside*, with no *of*, is also an **adverb** that takes no object at all (*she waited outside*, *let's eat outside*); there it is not a preposition, and the test is exactly that — the preposition has an object, the adverb has none.
**Pattern:** *Outside of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [negative] *outside of* [noun phrase] &middot; that's *outside of* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *outside* *(adverb — no object)*
**Variants:** *outside* — the *of* is optional and usually dropped in the spatial sense (*outside the courthouse*), but **obligatory** in the exception sense: *Outside a few typos* is not idiomatic.

In the spatial sense, American editors trim the *of* — *outside the building*, not *outside of the building* — and that spatial *outside* is covered with the position prepositions. Against *apart from* and *aside from*, *outside of* is the most conversational of the three.

Puts a case beyond the reach of the statement being made — either beyond a boundary, beyond a limit of competence, or beyond the scope of a general claim.

**1. Except for, apart from — chiefly American.**

1. *Outside of* a couple of late shifts, the schedule has been reasonable.
2. Nobody *outside of* the family knew he'd been sick.
3. *Outside of* Chicago, she's never lived in a big city.

**2. Beyond the limits or the scope of.**

4. That's *outside of* my area — you want the compliance desk.
5. Anything *outside of* routine maintenance needs written approval.
6. The pump is *outside of* warranty now.

**3. Spatially — where American editors cut the *of*.** Correct in speech, but blue-penciled in edited prose.

7. They were standing *outside of* the courthouse when the verdict came down. *(edit to* outside the courthouse*)*
8. Park anywhere *outside of* the loading zone. *(edit to* outside the loading zone*)*

**4. As an adverb — not a preposition.** No *of*, no object.

9. The dogs stayed *outside* all afternoon.
10. It's warm enough to eat *outside* tonight.

### save

**Pronunciation:** /seɪv/ &middot; *SAYV*
**Русский:** книжн. кроме, за исключением — литературный и юридический двойник except
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** literary, formal, legal
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, a prepositional phrase, or a **bare infinitive** after a *do*-phrase (*nothing to do save wait*); a finite clause only through *save that*, which is a conjunction. Never a gerund and never a *to*-infinitive.
**Pattern:** [negative or total] *save* [noun phrase] &middot; *save as* provided in [clause reference] *(legal)* &middot; nothing to do *save* [bare infinitive] &middot; *save that* [clause] *(conjunction)*
**Contrast:** *Save* is *except* in a good suit. In American prose it reads as consciously literary, and it survives most naturally in legal drafting, where *save as otherwise provided* is standard boilerplate. *Save for* is the version most writers reach for now. Do not confuse the preposition with the verb *save*, from which it is historically distinct — this one came in from French *sauf*, "except".

Names the single survivor of a general negation. Almost every idiomatic use follows *nothing*, *no one*, *all*, or a description of emptiness, and the effect is elegiac in a way that *except* is not.

**1. After a negative or a total.**

1. All the crew were rescued *save* the engineer.
2. Nothing remained of the settlement *save* a chimney and a well.
3. He would tell them nothing *save* his name and the name of his ship.

**2. In descriptions of emptiness or silence.**

4. The hall was dark *save* a single lamp at the far end.
5. Every window was shuttered *save* the one above the door.

**3. Before a bare infinitive.** Only after a *do*-phrase, and never with *to*.

6. There was nothing to do *save* wait for the tide to turn.
7. He could do nothing *save* watch the water come up over the sill.

**4. In legal drafting.** The boilerplate *save as otherwise provided* is its last productive home.

8. The rights of the tenant are unaffected, *save* as provided in section 9.
9. All implied warranties are excluded, *save* as required by applicable law.

**5. Not a preposition — the conjunction *save that*.** The preposition takes an object; *save that* takes a whole finite clause with its own subject and verb.

10. The two drafts are identical, *save that* the second one is dated. *(conjunction — a clause follows)*
11. He remembered nothing of the evening, *save that* it had rained. *(conjunction)*

**Contrast:** The participial twin of *save*, and the ancestor of the legal noun **saving clause** — the provision that preserves something the rest of a document would otherwise wipe out. Preserves one item from a sweeping provision. Where *save* subtracts a member from a set, *saving* protects a right or an interest from a rule that would otherwise reach it — which is exactly the job it still does in contracts.

**1. Preserving a right or a provision.**

**2. Preserving something from an older grant.**

3. The charter left the old privileges intact, *saving* the crown's right of appeal.

**Contrast:** *Short of* and *barring* both name a contingency, but *short of* names a **drastic step you are declining to take**, while *barring* names an accident you don't expect. 4. *Short of* firing him, there isn't much the board can do.
5. *Short of* a miracle, that bridge won't reopen this year.
6. Nothing *short of* a subpoena will get those records released.

**3. Lacking, running low on.**

### to the exclusion of

**Pronunciation:** /tə ði ɪkˈskluʒən ʌv/ &middot; weak /tə ði ɪkˈskluʒən əv/, reduced /tə ði ɪkˈskluʒənə/ &middot; *tuh-thee-ik-SKLOO-zhun-uv*
**Русский:** в ущерб всему остальному — не изъятие из множества, а вытеснение прочего
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; at home in criticism, in psychology, and in contracts
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, possessive plus noun, or **gerund** (*to the exclusion of sleeping*) — never a finite clause and never a *to*-infinitive: ✗ *to the exclusion of to sleep*. The *to* is a preposition, so the *-ing* is obligatory, exactly as it is after [in addition to](#in-addition-to).
**Pattern:** [verb phrase] *to the exclusion of* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb phrase] *to the exclusion of* [verb-ing] &middot; *to the exclusion of* all else
**Variants:** *to the exclusion of all else* and *to the exclusion of everything else* — the two fixed superlative tags, and the commonest shapes the phrase takes outside a contract.
**Contrast:** The one entry in this section that is **not** an exception word. *Excluding*, *except for*, and *with the exception of* take a member out of a set; *to the exclusion of* names what got **crowded out** because something else took up all the room. *She studies history excluding the war years* subtracts a period from her subject; *she studies the war years to the exclusion of everything else* says the war years have eaten her whole subject. It therefore attaches to a verb phrase rather than to a noun, and unlike the rest of this family it **cannot open a sentence**: ✗ *To the exclusion of her friends, she trained.* Against [instead of](11-complex-multiword.md#instead-of), which names a straight substitution and implies no excess; against [at the expense of](11-complex-multiword.md#at-the-expense-of), its closest neighbor, which stresses the price paid rather than the crowding out. In legal drafting it has a fourth, unemotional use — a grant *to the exclusion of all others* is a grant of exclusivity, with no criticism implied at all.

Names what has been squeezed out by whatever the main clause is doing. It is a phrase of imbalance: outside a contract, nearly every use of it is a complaint that one activity has taken over the space that belonged to something else.

**1. One pursuit crowding out everything else.** The everyday use, and usually a criticism.

1. He practiced *to the exclusion of* his schoolwork, his friends, and eventually his health.
2. The athletic department funds football *to the exclusion of* every other sport.

**2. With a gerund.**

3. She read that winter *to the exclusion of* seeing anybody at all.
4. The team optimized for speed *to the exclusion of* testing anything properly.

**3. The fixed superlative tags.**

5. For two years he worked on the manuscript *to the exclusion of* all else.
6. She talks about the lawsuit *to the exclusion of* everything else.

**4. Of a rule or a test that forces the alternatives out.**

7. The court applied the plain language of the statute *to the exclusion of* the legislative history.
8. Hiring on test scores alone, *to the exclusion of* everything else a résumé shows, is how you end up with this staff.

**5. In law and contracts — exclusivity, with no criticism implied.**

9. The agreement grants the distributor the territory *to the exclusion of* all others.
10. The federal statute governs *to the exclusion of* any conflicting state rule.

**See also:** [excluding](#excluding), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [with the exception of](#with-the-exception-of), [including without limitation](#including-without-limitation), [short of](#short-of), [instead of](11-complex-multiword.md#instead-of)

### with the exception of

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ði ɪkˈsɛpʃən ʌv/ &middot; weak /wəθ ði ɪkˈsɛpʃən əv/, reduced /wəθ ði ɪkˈsɛpʃənə/ &middot; *with-thee-ik-SEP-shun-uv*
**Русский:** за исключением — офиц. развёрнутое except for; выделяет один пункт из общего правила
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun; a gerund is possible but uncommon (*with the exception of having to change planes*). **Never a finite clause** — for a clause, write *except that*.
**Pattern:** *With the exception of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause], *with the exception of* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject], *with the exception of* [noun phrase], [verb agreeing with the subject]
**Variants:** *with the sole exception of* (emphatic), *with the possible exception of* (hedging) — the two standard expansions, and the reason the long form survives at all
**Contrast:** The longest word for the shortest job. *Except for*, *apart from*, *aside from*, *other than*, and bare *except* all subtract the same item in a third of the space, and in ordinary prose one of them is the better choice. Reach for the long form when the exception must be foregrounded, when a document has to be unmistakable about which element the exception attaches to, or when *with the possible exception of* is doing real hedging that no shorter phrase can do. Like everything else in this file it is a preposition, so it leaves the number of the verb alone: *Every state, with the exception of Nebraska, **has** two chambers.*

Lifts one named item out of an otherwise universal statement, slowly and on the record. The claim it modifies has to be a genuine generalization — subtracting from a statement about a single thing produces nonsense.

**1. Subtracting one member from a universal claim.**

1. Every state *with the exception of* Nebraska has a two-chamber legislature.
2. *With the exception of* Tuesday, the entire week is booked.

**2. In reports, findings, and inventories.**

3. The building came through the storm undamaged, *with the exception of* two broken windows.
4. All fees are refundable, *with the exception of* the application charge.

**3. The emphatic and hedged expansions.**

5. *With the sole exception of* the treasurer, no one on the board had seen the numbers.
6. *With the possible exception of* 1998, this is the wettest spring on record.

**4. With a gerund.** Grammatical, but heavy enough that *except for* is usually better.

7. The trip went smoothly, *with the exception of* having to change planes in Dallas.
8. The renovation stayed on budget, *with the exception of* having to replace the wiring on the second floor.

**See also:** [except for](#except-for), [except](#except), [apart from](#apart-from), [other than](#other-than), [save for](#save-for), [excepting](#excepting), [leaving aside](#leaving-aside)

## Absence and restriction

The last group does not subtract a member from a set — it reports that something is missing outright, or that a quantity has been squeezed down to almost nothing. *Without* is the center of it and the negative counterpart of [with](02-core-relational.md#with); the others are register variants of the same idea (*sans* is its literary pose, *absent* its legal one, *in the absence of* its regulatory one), the one member that reports an absence as good news (*free of*), or a limiter on degree (*next to*).

### absent

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæbsənt/ &middot; *AB-sunt* — first-syllable stress, unlike the verb *absent* /æbˈsɛnt/ (*he absented himself*)
**Русский:** юр., амер. при отсутствии, за неимением — называет недостающее условие (absent proof)
**Type:** simple (adjective-derived) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal; **chiefly American**
**Complement:** noun phrase, usually indefinite — a gerund is possible but rare even in legal writing, and a **finite clause is impossible**: write *absent evidence that…*, or switch to *unless*
**Pattern:** *Absent* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *absent* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The legal register's *without* or *in the absence of*. Against *barring*, which names an intervening event, *absent* names a **missing prerequisite**; against *but for*, which looks back at a counterfactual, *absent* looks forward to a condition that has not been met. British editors still flag it as an Americanism; American courts and regulators use it constantly.

Declares a required thing to be missing and states what follows from its absence. It is one of the few prepositions that entered general American usage straight out of judicial opinions, and it still carries their tone.

**1. Naming a missing document or authorization.**

1. *Absent* a written waiver, the filing deadline stands.
2. *Absent* an emergency, visits are limited to two hours.

**2. Naming a missing showing or proof.**

3. *Absent* some showing of prejudice, the court will not disturb the verdict.
4. *Absent* any objection from the neighbors, the variance will be granted.

**3. Naming a missing condition in policy or business writing.**

5. *Absent* new funding, the program shuts down in June.
6. *Absent* clear direction from the board, the staff did what seemed reasonable.

**See also:** [without](#without), [in the absence of](#in-the-absence-of), [failing](#failing), [barring](#barring), [but for](#but-for), [if not for](#if-not-for), [sans](#sans)

### free of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfri ʌv/ &middot; weak /ˈfri əv/, reduced /ˈfriə/ before a consonant — the *of* loses its /v/ as it does in *kind of* &middot; *FREE uv*
**Русский:** свободный от, без — об отсутствии нежелательного, как о благе: free of charge «бесплатно»
**Type:** complex (adjective + *of*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, usually with no determiner (*free of charge*, *free of debt*, *free of liens*), or a **gerund** (*free of having to report to anyone*) — never a finite clause and never a *to*-infinitive: ✗ *free of to pay*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *free of* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject] **is** *free of* [noun phrase] &middot; *free of* [verb-ing]
**Variants:** *free from* — the two overlap, but *free of* prefers a physical or financial contaminant (*free of dust*, *free of debt*) and *free from* prefers an oppression or a constraint (*free from fear*, *free from interference*). *Devoid of* is the cold formal version and is always a criticism.
**Contrast:** Against *without*, which merely reports that a thing is missing, *free of* reports the absence as a **benefit** — whatever is missing is something you would not have wanted. That is why *free of charge* is idiomatic and ✗ *free of a roof* is not, and it is the one respect in which this preposition is not interchangeable with *without*, *sans*, or *minus*. Against *exclusive of*, which is bookkeeping for what a figure leaves out rather than a claim about anybody's good fortune.

Reports that an unwelcome thing is missing and counts that as good news. It is the positive face of the absence family and the only member with approval built into it.

**1. Free of a cost or a charge.**

1. Delivery within the county is *free of* charge.
2. The account is *free of* monthly maintenance fees.

**2. Free of a contaminant or a defect.**

3. The lot is *free of* debris and ready for the foundation.
4. The samples came back *free of* any sign of contamination.

**3. Free of an obligation or an encumbrance.**

5. The title is *free of* liens and easements.
6. They retired *free of* debt for the first time in thirty years.

**4. With a gerund.**

7. For one summer she was *free of* having to answer to anybody.
8. The new job leaves him *free of* commuting three hours a day.

**See also:** [without](#without), [sans](#sans), [absent](#absent), [in the absence of](#in-the-absence-of), [exclusive of](#exclusive-of), [minus](#minus)

### in the absence of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈæbsəns ʌv/ &middot; weak /ɪn ði ˈæbsəns əv/, reduced /ɪn ði ˈæbsənsə/ &middot; *in-thee-AB-suns-uv*
**Русский:** офиц. при отсутствии, за неимением — условие, при котором действует правило по умолчанию
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, scientific, and administrative
**Complement:** noun phrase or objective-case pronoun, usually indefinite or non-count. No gerund, and **never a finite clause**: write *in the absence of any evidence that…*, or switch to the conjunction *unless*.
**Pattern:** *In the absence of* [noun phrase], [clause] &middot; [clause] *in the absence of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The unfolded, fully explicit *without*, and the drafting register's *absent*. Three registers of one idea: *without a permit* is what a person says, *absent a permit* is what a court writes, *in the absence of a permit* is what a regulation writes. Against *failing*, which frames the missing thing as a prerequisite somebody was supposed to supply, *in the absence of* is neutral about fault. Keep the frozen preposition apart from the live phrase in *The vice chair presides in the absence of the chair*, where *absence* still means somebody is away — the same five words doing an entirely different job.

States the negative condition under which the main clause holds. It is the standard opening of a default rule: this is what happens when the thing that ought to be there isn't.

**1. Naming a missing document, rule, or authority.**

1. *In the absence of* a written agreement, state law governs.
2. *In the absence of* any objection, the motion carries.

**2. Naming missing evidence or information.**

3. *In the absence of* evidence, the committee declined to act.
4. *In the absence of* a diagnosis, the insurer refused to pay the claim.

**3. In scientific and technical description.** Here the absence is a controlled condition, not a failure.

5. Plants grown *in the absence of* light lose their color within days.
6. *In the absence of* oxygen, the reaction stops cold.

**See also:** [without](#without), [absent](#absent), [failing](#failing), [but for](#but-for), [sans](#sans)

### next to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛkst tu/ &middot; weak /ˈnɛks tə/ (the medial *t* usually drops) &middot; *NEKST too*
**Русский:** почти, практически (next to nothing «почти ничего»); а также «уступая только кому-л.»
**Type:** complex &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** in the limiting sense, only *nothing*, *no* + noun, or an adjective — **not an ordinary noun phrase**. *Next to nothing* means "hardly anything"; *next to money* means nothing at all. In the ranking sense ("second only to") it does take an ordinary noun phrase or a gerund — *next to her sister*, *next to sleeping*. Never a finite clause in either sense. The spatial *next to*, which takes any noun phrase, is covered with the position prepositions.
**Pattern:** *next to* nothing &middot; *next to* no [noun] &middot; *next to* [adjective] &middot; *Next to* [noun phrase], [superlative claim] *(ranking)*
**Contrast:** In the limiting sense it is a near-synonym of *all but* (*all but impossible*) and *little short of*, and it cannot be swapped for the spatial *next to* without changing the word class of what follows: *next to the bank* is a place, *next to nothing* is a quantity. British and American usage agree completely here.

Squeezes a quantity or a quality down to the edge of zero. The ranking sense — "second only to" — is the same geometry applied to a list rather than to a scale.

**1. Almost nothing, hardly any.**

1. The car sold for *next to* nothing at the county auction.
2. He knows *next to* nothing about wiring.
3. There's *next to* no parking on that block after five.

**2. Practically, all but — before an adjective.**

4. Getting a permit downtown is *next to* impossible.
5. The insulation in this place is *next to* useless.

**3. Second only to — ranking.**

6. *Next to* her sister, she's the fastest swimmer on the team.
7. *Next to* sleeping, reading is his favorite way to kill an afternoon.

**See also:** [short of](#short-of), [but](#but), [minus](#minus), [without](#without)

### sans

**Pronunciation:** /sænz/ &middot; *SANZ* — anglicized; the French /sɑ̃/ only when the speaker is being deliberately French
**Русский:** книжн./шутл. без — нарочитый вариант without; всегда с голым существительным, без артикля
**Type:** simple &middot; **Register:** literary, jocular, or design jargon
**Complement:** a **bare** noun phrase, usually with no determiner — *sans makeup*, not *sans the makeup*. Never a finite clause, and a gerund only as a joke.
**Pattern:** [clause] *sans* [bare noun] &middot; *sans* [noun], *sans* [noun], *sans* [noun] *(the rhetorical series)*
**Contrast:** A self-conscious *without*. Borrowed from French in the fourteenth century, dropped, and revived as a mannerism, it now signals wit or style rather than plain absence. Note that *sans serif* is a frozen typographic compound, not a live preposition. Where *without* is invisible, *sans* is always noticed — which is the only reason to use it.

Reports absence with an arched eyebrow. Americans meet it most often in Shakespeare's catalogue of old age and in the type specimen book, and use it themselves mainly for effect.

**1. Plain absence, delivered as a wink.** Always a bare noun, never *sans the*.

1. He took the meeting *sans* tie, which for him counted as rebellion.
2. A martini *sans* olive is just cold gin.
3. The remake is the original *sans* charm.

**2. The rhetorical series.** Repeated for cumulative effect — the construction Shakespeare fixed in the language.

4. She showed up to the gala *sans* jewelry, *sans* makeup, *sans* apology.
5. Jaques ends his speech "*sans* teeth, *sans* eyes, *sans* taste, *sans* everything."

**3. In headline, menu, and catalog copy.** Where brevity rather than wit is the motive.

6. It's a Western *sans* horses, which is either brave or a mistake.
7. A weeknight lasagna *sans* the four-hour sauce. *(the determiner here is a copywriter's liberty)*

**See also:** [without](#without), [minus](#minus), [absent](#absent), [short of](#short-of)

### without

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/ (also /wɪˈθaʊt/, common in American speech) &middot; weak /wəˈðaʊt/ &middot; *with-OWT*
**Русский:** без — отсутствие; с герундием «не делая чего-л.»; а также «если бы не» в сослагательном
**Type:** compound (historically *with* + *out*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Complement:** noun phrase, objective-case pronoun, **gerund**, or object + gerund (*without anyone noticing*). It can also stand alone as an **adverb** with no object after *do*, *go*, and *manage* (*we'll have to do without*). It can **never** take a finite clause and never a *to*-infinitive: not *without to pay*, not *without he agrees*.
**Pattern:** *without* [noun phrase] &middot; *without* [verb-ing] &middot; *without* [noun phrase] [verb-ing] &middot; do / go / manage *without* *(no object)*
**Contrast:** The negative counterpart of [with](02-core-relational.md#with), and the center the whole absence family orbits: *sans* is its literary pose, *minus* its informal one, *absent* its legal one, *in the absence of* its bureaucratic one, and *free of* the one that treats the missing thing as good riddance. Against *but for*, *without* covers both plain absence (*coffee without sugar*) and counterfactual absence (*without you, I'd have quit*), where *but for* can only do the second. The regional conjunction *without* meaning "unless" (*I won't go without you come along*) is **nonstandard** in written American English.

The most heavily used preposition in this file and the most versatile. Its range runs from a missing spoonful of sugar to a counterfactual that reorganizes the whole sentence, and its gerund construction — *without saying goodbye* — is the standard English way of saying "and did not".

**1. Not having, lacking.**

1. She drinks her coffee *without* sugar.
2. A house *without* a roof isn't worth much.

**2. Not accompanied by.**

3. He walked out *without* his keys.
4. They went to the game *without* me.

**3. Followed by a gerund — "and not doing the thing named".**

5. He left *without* saying goodbye to anyone.
6. You can't cancel *without* giving thirty days' notice.

**4. Followed by an object and a gerund.**

7. The bill passed *without* a single senator reading it.
8. We finished the job *without* anyone getting hurt.

**5. Counterfactually — "if there were not" or "if there had not been".**

9. *Without* your help, we'd still be arguing about the budget.
10. *Without* the levee, half the town would have flooded.

**6. As an adverb, with no object.** After *do*, *go*, and *manage*.

11. There's no coffee left, so we'll have to do *without*.
12. They went *without* for most of that winter.

**7. Archaically, "outside".** The original sense, now confined to older writing and hymn texts. This is the *with* + *out* the word was built from.

13. The old road still runs *without* the city gates.
14. "There is a green hill far away, *without* a city wall."
