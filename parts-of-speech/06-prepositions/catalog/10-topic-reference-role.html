Topic, reference, respect, role, and capacity#

The prepositions that name what something is about, restrict a claim to one domain, or say which hat a person is wearing.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

These words answer two questions: what is this about? and in what character is this person acting? Nearly every one of them is a long-form substitute for about, on, or as, which is why this is the group where padding collects — in terms of, with regard to, as to, and in reference to each take three or four words to do what one word does, and American editors cut them on sight. Two mechanical rules run through the whole file: first, ✗ none of these takes a finite that*-clause, so you can write concerning the delay, concerning what happened, or concerning the fact that it was late, but never concerning that it was late; second, ✗ the noun is regard*, not regards — with regard to, in regard to, without regard to. Regards with an s means greetings and belongs at the bottom of an email, the sole exception being as regards, where regards is a verb and the s is agreement.

A third rule worth stating separately: a wh-clause is not a that-clause. Most of these words take a fused wh-nominal quite happily — concerning who authorized it, as to whether the deal closes, in terms of what it costs — and the ban is on the finite that-clause alone. Where an entry below says "never a clause," read it as "never a clause of any kind," and where it says "never a finite that-clause," the wh-nominal is open to you.

A fourth rule, and the one that produces the most errors: after every preposition in this file a verb goes in -ing, never in the bare infinitive. Seven of the phrases here end in to — as to, in regard to, with regard to, in relation to, in reference to, with reference to, with respect to — and that to is a preposition, not the infinitive marker, so a gerund follows it: with regard to filing late, never ✗ with regard to file late; as to paying the balance, never ✗ as to pay the balance. The same trap runs through the rest of the language wherever a fixed phrase ends in a prepositional to — look forward to hearing from you, object to being asked, accustomed to working nights, with a view to selling — and the test never changes: if a noun can stand in that slot (look forward to the weekend, object to the fee), the to is a preposition and the verb must take -ing. The only wh-exception is the infinitival indirect question, which is a wh-nominal and not a bare infinitive: no guidance as to how to proceed is correct, ✗ no guidance as to proceed is not.

Owned by other files. Several words that do heavy topic and role work have their full entries elsewhere and are cross-referenced rather than repeated here: on (the scholarly topic preposition — a book on bees, a lecture on Keats), of (think of, speak of, news of the layoffs), to (testify to, attest to, speak to the question), for (known for, mistaken for, famous for), by, with, from, and at. Over keeps an entry below because the topic-of-conflict sense is this file's business, but its spatial, covering, authority, medium, and quantitative senses live in movement and direction and its "spanning a period" sense in time. Four more that a reader will look for here are one file over: like, which is the resemblance word that as is constantly confused with; relative to and in comparison with, which are comparative rather than topical; and on behalf of, which assigns a representative capacity rather than a role. On the part of (attributing conduct to a party) and bare per (the shorter and now commoner form of as per) are likewise elsewhere, as are in the name of, in light of, for the purpose of, such as, and cum. According to, owing to, due to, regardless of, and irrespective of are in complex and multi-word prepositions, and the participial pair considering and given is in marginal and participial prepositions.

American against British. With respect to is the American form and in respect of the British and Commonwealth one; as regards and so far as X is concerned carry a British flavor where regarding and as far as X is concerned carry none; talking of is British and speaking of American; and about in the "here and there" sense (papers lying about the room) is British, where American English says around. Two rules from elsewhere in the dictionary hold here too, and they are worth stating because writers reach for the wrong member when they want to sound formal: the American defaults are toward, amid, among, and around, and towards, amidst, amongst, and round are the British forms. The extra -s and the extra -st buy no formality in American prose; they read as affectation.

Reduced forms. Every multi-word phrase below reduces in ordinary speech, and the reduction lands on the function words at the edges, never on the noun in the middle. Final of goes /əv/ → /ə/ (in terms of → /ɪn ˈtɜrmz ə/, by way of → /baɪ ˈweɪ ə/); final to goes /tu/ → /tə/ (with regard to → /wəθ rɪˈɡɑrd tə/); initial with goes /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/; initial as goes /æz/ → /əz/; initial for goes /fɔr/ → /fɚ/ (also written /fər/); and the neighbors these phrases keep company with reduce the same way — at /ət/, from /frəm/, of /əv/ → /ə/, to /tə/. The stressed noun — regard, respect, reference, terms, matter — always keeps its full vowel, which is why with respect to comes out /wəθ rɪˈspɛkt tə/ and never the other way around. The two-syllable and Latin members (over, anent, apropos, qua, pace, vis-à-vis, in re, re) have no weak form at all.

Topic and subject matter#

The words that put a subject in front of you. About is the workhorse and nearly everything else in this section is either more formal, more legal, or more angry than about is. Two members do something else: abreast of names not the subject of a statement but the subject somebody is trying to keep up with, and of and concerning is a single element of American defamation law wearing the shape of a preposition.

abreast of#

Pronunciation
/əˈbrɛst əv/ · weak /əˈbrɛst ə/ · uh-BREST-uv · the stress sits on -breast and never moves; the a- reduces to /ə/ in every register, and final of goes /əv/ → /ə/ before a consonant
Русский
в курсе (чего-л.) — только с keep/stay: следить за тем, что меняется
Type
complex (the adjective abreast + of)
Register
neutral to formal
Complement
noun phrase, gerund (abreast of filing on time — grammatical but stiff), or a fused wh-nominal (abreast of what the competition is charging); never a finite that-clause (✗ abreast of that the rules changed → abreast of the change in the rules). The phrase is an adjectival complement rather than a free adverbial, so a verb of maintaining almost always stands in front of it — keep, stay, remain, get, or bring someone — and ✗ I abreast of the news is not a sentence.
Pattern
[keep / stay / remain / get] abreast of [noun phrase] · [keep / bring] [object] abreast of [noun phrase] · [noun] abreast of [noun phrase]
Contrast
Against about and on, which merely name a subject, abreast of names a subject somebody is staying current on. It entails a moving target and a continuing effort, which is why keep abreast of the litigation works and ✗ keep abreast of the Battle of Hastings does not — the battle has stopped changing. The informal American equivalent is up on (up on the news); the formal one is current with. Cross-class test — the object: with an object it is this preposition; with nothing after it, abreast is a bare adverb of position (they rode two abreast) and governs nothing at all.
Senses treated elsewhere
this entry covers only the "informed about" sense. The spatial one — a moving thing drawing level with a fixed one, as in the tug came abreast of the pier — belongs with the prepositions of place and position.

The preposition of staying current. Its object is not the subject of a statement but the subject somebody is trying to keep up with, which is why it lives among verbs of maintenance and almost never stands alone.

  1. Part of the job is keeping abreast of changes in the tax code.
  2. She reads two trade journals to stay abreast of the industry.
  3. The Friday memo exists to keep the board abreast of the negotiations.
  4. He has never been abreast of what his own department is doing.
  5. Regulators struggled to remain abreast of the new products.
  6. Nobody in the room was abreast of the overnight filings.

about#

Pronunciation
/əˈbaʊt/ · casual clipped form /ˈbaʊt/ ('bout) · uh-BOWT · the stressed second syllable never reduces; only the unstressed first one drops
Русский
о, об — тема книги, разговора, жалобы; перед числом — «около, примерно»
Type
simple (historically a fusion of Old English on + būtan "outside") — also an adverb (there was nobody about), a degree modifier (about forty), and a verb particle (bring it about, come about)
Register
neutral
Complement
noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (about starting the job), or a wh-clause (about what happened, about whether to go, about how it works) — never a bare that-clause (✗ I told her about that the meeting moved). To carry a that-clause you must insert a noun: about the fact that she left. As an adverb or particle it takes no object at all.
Pattern
[verb] about [noun phrase] · [noun] about [noun phrase] · [adjective] about [noun phrase] · stranded: the thing I told you about · fronted and formal: the matter about which we spoke
Contrast
Against on, about is the general, conversational topic word and on is the scholarly one: a book about bees is for anyone, a book on bees is a treatise. On also implies sustained, organized treatment, which is why you can give a lecture on Keats but only a quick word about your weekend. Against over, about is neutral where over implies a fight. The "scattered here and there" sense (papers lying about the room) is British; American English says around, and British round is not an American option. Cross-class test — the object: we talked about the merger is the preposition (object the merger); there was nobody about and about forty people are adverbs with nothing after them to govern; bring it about is a particle, where the object it belongs to bring; and be about to is a fixed idiom in which about governs nothing and cannot be moved or stranded.

The most useful preposition in this file and the default for every job the others do. It is also one of the most polysemous words in English, and only the first three senses below are topic senses.

1. Subject matter. What a book, conversation, film, or complaint is on.

  1. It's a documentary about two brothers who rebuilt a lighthouse.
  2. She wouldn't tell me anything about the interview.

2. The target of a feeling or attitude. What the emotion is pointed at.

  1. He's still upset about the parking ticket.
  2. I'm excited about starting the new job.

3. The essential point or nature of something. Usually in a corrective sentence.

  1. This isn't about the money — it's about being taken seriously.
  2. Good teaching is about knowing when to stay quiet.

4. Occupied with, engaged in. A limited, slightly old-fashioned use.

  1. Go about your business and don't stare.
  2. While you're about it, could you refill the ice tray?

5. Here and there within an area. Chiefly British; American English uses around, and British round is not an American option at all.

  1. There were toys lying about the living room.
  2. He spent the morning wandering about the museum.

6. Not a preposition — the degree modifier "approximately." It has no object; it just sits in front of a number or a measurement.

  1. There were about forty people in the room.
  2. We got there about nine.
  3. The trip takes about an hour and a half.

7. Not a preposition — the adverb and the verb particle. Nothing follows for it to govern, and in be about to it is frozen into an idiom of imminence.

  1. There was nobody about when we pulled in.
  2. Nobody knows how the change came about.
  3. She was about to leave when the phone rang.

concerning#

Pronunciation
/kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ · kun-SUR-ning · no weak form — like the other participial prepositions (regarding, respecting, touching) it keeps its stressed second syllable in every register; only the prefix vowel reduces, to /kən-/
Русский
касательно, относительно — офиц. предлог темы; омоним — прилагательное «тревожный»
Type
marginal/participial (grammaticalized from the verb concern)
Register
formal
Complement
noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (concerning failing to file on time), or a fused wh-nominal (concerning who authorized the transfer) — never a finite that-clause. ✗ Concerning that he resigned is impossible; write concerning his resignation or concerning the fact that he resigned.
Pattern
[clause] concerning [noun phrase] · Concerning [noun phrase], [clause]
Contrast
Regarding is the neutral business default; concerning is heavier and gravitates toward inquiries, complaints, allegations, and official correspondence. Cross-class test — the object: the preposition always has one and never follows a linking verb (a complaint concerning the instructor), while the modern adjective meaning "worrying" has no object and sits happily after is (that's concerning, a concerning trend). If you can drop very in front of it, you have the adjective.

Introduces the matter an official communication is about. Unlike the participial prepositions of cause (considering, given), it has lost every trace of its verb: no subject is implied and nothing is doing any concerning.

1. The subject of an inquiry, complaint, or official communication. The core sense, and the reason the word survives in correspondence at all.

  1. I'm writing concerning the invoice dated March 4.
  2. The committee received two complaints concerning the same instructor.
  3. Concerning your request for an extension, the deadline is fixed by statute.

2. The matter a document, rule, or statute covers — or conspicuously fails to cover. Very common after silent, vague, no documentation, no provision.

  1. The statute is silent concerning leases signed before 1998.
  2. There is no documentation concerning who authorized the transfer.
  3. She refused to answer questions concerning her time at the agency.

3. Not a preposition — the modern adjective meaning "worrying." It governs nothing, follows a linking verb freely, and takes degree modifiers the preposition cannot.

  1. The trend in the third quarter is concerning.
  2. Two of the lab values came back concerning enough to repeat.

of and concerning#

Pronunciation
/əv ənd kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ · weak /ə ən kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ · uv-und-kun-SUR-ning · both function words at the front reduce almost to nothing — /ə(v) ən/ — and the whole weight of the phrase sits on -cern-
Русский
юр. «о и в отношении» — в делах о диффамации: что высказывание указывало именно на истца
Type
complex (a legal binomial: two near-synonyms coordinated and frozen together)
Register
legal — a term of art in American defamation law, and effectively nonexistent outside it
Complement
a noun phrase naming a person or an entity, namely the plaintiff. No gerund and no clause of any kind: ✗ of and concerning that he took the money, ✗ of and concerning defaming him. The object is always a who and never a what, which is the sharpest difference between this phrase and ordinary concerning.
Pattern
[statement, article, broadcast, publication] of and concerning [the plaintiff] · the statement was of and concerning the plaintiff
Contrast
Not an ornamental way of writing about or concerning. It names one element of the tort: a defamation plaintiff must prove that the statement was of and concerning him, meaning an ordinary reader would have understood it to point at him in particular, and a complaint that never pleads the phrase invites a motion to dismiss. Modern drafters cut legal doublets — give, devise and bequeath, null and void — as redundant, but this one has been fixed by a century of case law and cannot be shortened to about without losing the term of art. Where an American meets it: a libel or slander complaint, a motion to dismiss, and the pattern jury instruction read aloud at the end of the trial.

The defamation element that hardened into a preposition. It asks not what a statement was about but whom it pointed at, and a statement about a large enough group points at nobody.

  1. The complaint fails to allege that the broadcast was of and concerning the plaintiff.
  2. A statement of and concerning a group of two hundred employees rarely defames any one of them.
  3. The jury was asked whether the column was of and concerning the sheriff.
  4. Nothing in the review is of and concerning the restaurant's owner personally.
  5. The court held that the caption alone made the article of and concerning her.
  6. Plaintiffs must plead facts showing that the post was of and concerning them.

Pronunciation

simple — also an adverb and adjective (the meeting was over), a verb particle (hand it over, think it over), and a directional adverb (come over)

noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (sued over failing to disclose the defect), or a fused wh-nominal in the dispute sense (a fight over who gets the house, disagreement over whether to appeal) — never a finite that-clause (✗ they argued over that the rent was late → they argued over the late rent). Senses treated elsewhere
this entry covers only the topic senses. The spatial ("above," "across"), covering, authority, medium, and "more than" senses are in movement and direction; the "spanning a period" sense (over the weekend) is in time.

The preposition of things people quarrel about. Whenever the head noun or verb involves conflict, grief, deliberation, or consequence, English reaches for over rather than about — and the choice is idiomatic rather than logical, so it has to be learned verb by verb.

1. The matter two parties are fighting about. After the nouns argument, dispute, fight, quarrel, row, battle, standoff, litigation, deadlock and the verbs argue, fight, quarrel, clash, split, fall out, deadlock, haggle.

  1. The strike began as an argument over scheduling software.
  2. Two families have been in court over forty feet of shoreline.
  3. Congress deadlocked over the disaster funding bill.
  4. There is still disagreement over who signs the release.

2. The trigger of a strong reaction — a resignation, a lawsuit, an arrest, a firing.

  1. The deputy director resigned over the handling of the complaint.
  2. The city was sued over the condition of the pumping station.
  3. Two contractors were charged over the falsified inspection reports.

3. The subject of worry, grief, fuss, or slow deliberation. After agonize, brood, fret, fuss, obsess, pore, mull, labor, grieve, cry.

  1. He's still beating himself up over one bad quarter.
  2. She pored over the lease for the better part of an hour.
  3. Nobody is going to cry over a broken plate.

4. Not a preposition — the adverb, adjective, and verb particle. No object follows it.

  1. The meeting was over by four.
  2. He handed over the keys without a word.
  3. Come over whenever you like.

The subject line of the English language. It labels what a document is about, and it does not belong in the body of one.

1. As a heading, followed by a colon. The overwhelmingly commoner use, and the only one that is above criticism.

  2. The memo was headed Re: Q3 headcount and ran to nine pages.

2. Inside running text or a file label — jargon, and worth replacing with about or regarding.

  1. Please reply to my note re the Thursday deposition.
  2. Every filing in the folder was marked re the same policy number.
  3. He sent one line re the Henderson closing and nothing since.

regarding#

Pronunciation
/rɪˈɡɑrdɪŋ/ · rih-GAR-ding · no weak form — the stress on -gard- is fixed, and the -ing may go /-ɪn/ in casual speech but the vowel of the stressed syllable never reduces
Русский
относительно, по поводу — стандартный деловой предлог темы в амер. английском
Type
marginal/participial
Register
neutral to formal; the standard word in American business writing
Complement
noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (regarding filing after the deadline), or a fused wh-nominal (regarding what the auditors found, regarding whether to appeal) — never a finite that-clause (✗ regarding that the auditors found an error → regarding the auditors' finding).
Pattern
[clause] regarding [noun phrase] · Regarding [noun phrase], [clause]
Contrast
The shortest member of the regard family and the only one that is a single word — prefer it to with regard to, in regard to, and as regards, which say the same thing in three words. There is no s: regardings and as regarding are not English, and regarding to is an error. Cross-class test — the subject: the preposition implies no subject and can open a sentence with nothing to attach to (Regarding your application, a decision is due Friday); the identically spelled present participle of the verb regard must share its subject with the main clause (Regarding the painting closely, she noticed the repair — she is doing the regarding). If the -ing word has an agent, it is the participle.

The neutral formal word for "about." It has no attitude, no implication of complaint, and no legal flavor, which is what makes it the safe default in correspondence.

1. Concerning — the plain topic sense.

  1. Regarding your application, we expect to decide by the fifteenth.
  2. I have a question regarding the deductible.
  3. The email said nothing regarding payment.

2. Attaching a topic to a noun or adjective of communication. After question, silence, guidance, correspondence, and after cagey, evasive, forthcoming, explicit.

  1. Several parents have written regarding the change to the bus route.
  2. She was cagey regarding her plans after graduation.
  3. The department has issued no guidance regarding remote work.

3. With a fused wh-nominal, where the topic is itself an open question.

  1. Regarding who should attend, that's the director's call.
  2. Regarding what the auditors found, nobody will say anything on the record.

The regard, reference, and relation family#

Eighteen ways to say about in two, three, or four words, and four legal cross-reference markers keeping them company. The eighteen are near-perfect synonyms of each other, they are all more formal than they are useful, and the errors in this section — in regards to, as regards to, in accordance to — are among the most frequently made in American business writing. Three of them break out of the pattern: in relation to and vis-à-vis carry a genuine comparative sense the others lack, and as per claims conformity rather than mere reference. On the score of is the dated member that has drifted halfway into cause. Apart from those, the choice among them is a choice of tone alone. The four outliers — as defined in, as provided in, as set forth in, and as used in — are not synonyms of about at all: each points at a named text and borrows its authority, and together they are the connective tissue of American contract and statutory drafting.

Limiting the frame#

These do not simply name a topic — they fence one off. Each says "within this domain and no other," which is why they so often carry an unstated but elsewhere the answer would be different. Three of them started life as clauses and hardened into prepositions — as far as X is concerned, when it comes to, and, in its fixed phrases, as a matter of — and two, for purposes of and in the case of, are the workhorses of American legal drafting, where fencing a rule off is the whole job. As versus like is the central rule. As your doctor, I'd advise against it means the speaker is the doctor; Like your doctor, I'd advise against it means the speaker is somebody else who happens to agree. In careful American English, as takes a role and like takes a resemblance. Two corollaries: such as introduces genuine members of a set (fruit such as pears) and informal like does the same job in speech; and like used as a conjunction (like I said, like I told you) is thoroughly established in American speech but still cut in edited prose, where the conjunction is as. Against for: mistaken for a doctor means misidentified, taken as a compliment means interpreted. Cross-class test — the object: She works as a nurse is the preposition (object a nurse, a noun phrase) against She works as I do (conjunction, whole clause); as tall as I am is the comparative correlative; and in such delays as we saw, as is a relative pronoun. Anything headed by as that is completed by a clause is the conjunction, and that includes the compounds as if, as though, as soon as, as long as, and as far as — none of them takes an object, and none of them may be stranded.

The role preposition, and one of the highest-frequency words in English. Senses 1 through 5 are all prepositional; sense 6 is included because the same spelling covers a conjunction that learners constantly confuse with it.

1. The job, office, or role a person holds.

  1. She spent eleven years as a public defender.
  2. As treasurer, he signs every check over five thousand dollars.
  3. He was hired as a consultant and stayed as head of the department.

2. The function a thing is put to.

  1. We used a milk crate as a nightstand for most of that year.
  2. The gym doubles as a storm shelter.

3. In the character of — costume, casting, or disguise.

  1. He came to the party as a bag of jelly beans.
  2. She was cast as Lady Macbeth three seasons running.

4. At a stage of life, or in a stated condition. Here the noun phrase names not an office but a period or a state.

  1. As a child, she spent every summer on the lake.
  2. As a young attorney he took whatever walked through the door.

5. The status a verb of classification assigns. After regard, see, describe, treat, define, count, cite, dismiss, know — and note that this is the one sense that admits an adjective or a participle in place of a noun.

  1. The letter was dismissed as a hoax within a day.
  2. Most economists regard the figure as unreliable.
  3. He's known as the only person who can fix that machine.
  4. Skipping the deposition would count as defaulting.

6. Not a preposition — the conjunction of time and cause, which takes a whole clause and governs no object.

  1. The lights went out as she reached the landing.
  2. As the roads were closed, we stayed another night.
