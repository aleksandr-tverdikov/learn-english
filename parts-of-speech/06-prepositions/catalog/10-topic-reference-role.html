Topic, reference, respect, role, and capacity#

The prepositions that name what something is about, restrict a claim to one domain, or say which hat a person is wearing.

← The grammar of prepositions · All categories

These words answer two questions: what is this about? and in what character is this person acting? Nearly every one of them is a long-form substitute for about, on, or as, which is why this is the group where padding collects — in terms of, with regard to, as to, and in reference to each take three or four words to do what one word does, and American editors cut them on sight. Two mechanical rules run through the whole file: first, none of these takes a finite that-clause, so you can write concerning the delay, concerning what happened, or concerning the fact that it was late, but never concerning that it was late; second, the noun is regard, not regards — with regard to, in regard to, without regard to. Regards with an s means greetings and belongs at the bottom of an email, the sole exception being as regards, where regards is a verb and the s is agreement.

A third rule worth stating separately: a wh-clause is not a that-clause. Most of these words take a fused wh-nominal quite happily — concerning who authorized it, as to whether the deal closes, in terms of what it costs — and the ban is on the finite that-clause alone. Where an entry below says "never a clause," read it as "never a clause of any kind," and where it says "never a finite that-clause," the wh-nominal is open to you.

A fourth rule, and the one that produces the most errors: after every preposition in this file a verb goes in -ing, never in the bare infinitive. Seven of the phrases here end in to — as to, in regard to, with regard to, in relation to, in reference to, with reference to, with respect to — and that to is a preposition, not the infinitive marker, so a gerund follows it: with regard to filing late, never ✗ with regard to file late; as to paying the balance, never ✗ as to pay the balance. The same trap runs through the rest of the language wherever a fixed phrase ends in a prepositional to — look forward to hearing from you, object to being asked, accustomed to working nights, with a view to selling — and the test never changes: if a noun can stand in that slot (look forward to the weekend, object to the fee), the to is a preposition and the verb must take -ing. The only wh-exception is the infinitival indirect question, which is a wh-nominal and not a bare infinitive: no guidance as to how to proceed is correct, ✗ no guidance as to proceed is not.

Owned by other files. Several words that do heavy topic and role work have their full entries elsewhere and are cross-referenced rather than repeated here: on (the scholarly topic preposition — a book on bees, a lecture on Keats), of (think of, speak of, news of the layoffs), to (testify to, attest to, speak to the question), for (known for, mistaken for, famous for), by, with, from, and at. Over keeps an entry below because the topic-of-conflict sense is this file's business, but its spatial, covering, authority, medium, and quantitative senses live in movement and direction and its "spanning a period" sense in time. Four more that a reader will look for here are one file over: like, which is the resemblance word that as is constantly confused with; relative to and in comparison with, which are comparative rather than topical; and on behalf of, which assigns a representative capacity rather than a role. On the part of (attributing conduct to a party) and bare per (the shorter and now commoner form of as per) are likewise elsewhere, as are in the name of, in light of, for the purpose of, such as, and cum. According to, owing to, due to, regardless of, and irrespective of are in complex and multi-word prepositions, and the participial pair considering and given is in marginal and participial prepositions.

American against British. With respect to is the American form and in respect of the British and Commonwealth one; as regards and so far as X is concerned carry a British flavor where regarding and as far as X is concerned carry none; talking of is British and speaking of American; and about in the "here and there" sense (papers lying about the room) is British, where American English says around. Two rules from elsewhere in the dictionary hold here too, and they are worth stating because writers reach for the wrong member when they want to sound formal: the American defaults are toward, amid, among, and around, and towards, amidst, amongst, and round are the British forms. The extra -s and the extra -st buy no formality in American prose; they read as affectation.

Reduced forms. Every multi-word phrase below reduces in ordinary speech, and the reduction lands on the function words at the edges, never on the noun in the middle. Final of goes /əv/ → /ə/ (in terms of → /ɪn ˈtɜrmz ə/, by way of → /baɪ ˈweɪ ə/); final to goes /tu/ → /tə/ (with regard to → /wəθ rɪˈɡɑrd tə/); initial with goes /wɪθ/ → /wəθ/; initial as goes /æz/ → /əz/; initial for goes /fɔr/ → /fɚ/ (also written /fər/); and the neighbors these phrases keep company with reduce the same way — at /ət/, from /frəm/, of /əv/ → /ə/, to /tə/. The stressed noun — regard, respect, reference, terms, matter — always keeps its full vowel, which is why with respect to comes out /wəθ rɪˈspɛkt tə/ and never the other way around. The two-syllable and Latin members (over, anent, apropos, qua, pace, vis-à-vis, in re, re) have no weak form at all.

Topic and subject matter#

The words that put a subject in front of you. About is the workhorse and everything else in this section is either more formal, more legal, or more angry than about is.

about#

Pronunciation: /əˈbaʊt/ · casual clipped form /ˈbaʊt/ ('bout) · uh-BOWT · the stressed second syllable never reduces; only the unstressed first one drops Type: simple (historically a fusion of Old English on + būtan "outside") — also an adverb (there was nobody about), a degree modifier (about forty), and a verb particle (bring it about, come about) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (about starting the job), or a wh-clause (about what happened, about whether to go, about how it works) — never a bare that-clause (✗ I told her about that the meeting moved). To carry a that-clause you must insert a noun: about the fact that she left. As an adverb or particle it takes no object at all. Pattern: [verb] about [noun phrase] · [noun] about [noun phrase] · [adjective] about [noun phrase] · stranded: the thing I told you about · fronted and formal: the matter about which we spoke Contrast: Against on, about is the general, conversational topic word and on is the scholarly one: a book about bees is for anyone, a book on bees is a treatise. On also implies sustained, organized treatment, which is why you can give a lecture on Keats but only a quick word about your weekend. Against over, about is neutral where over implies a fight. The "scattered here and there" sense (papers lying about the room) is British; American English says around, and British round is not an American option. Cross-class test — the object: we talked about the merger is the preposition (object the merger); there was nobody about and about forty people are adverbs with nothing after them to govern; bring it about is a particle, where the object it belongs to bring; and be about to is a fixed idiom in which about governs nothing and cannot be moved or stranded.

The most useful preposition in this file and the default for every job the others do. It is also one of the most polysemous words in English, and only the first three senses below are topic senses.

1. Subject matter. What a book, conversation, film, or complaint is on.

  1. It's a documentary about two brothers who rebuilt a lighthouse.
  2. She wouldn't tell me anything about the interview.

2. The target of a feeling or attitude. What the emotion is pointed at.

  1. He's still upset about the parking ticket.
  2. I'm excited about starting the new job.

3. The essential point or nature of something. Usually in a corrective sentence.

  1. This isn't about the money — it's about being taken seriously.
  2. Good teaching is about knowing when to stay quiet.

4. Occupied with, engaged in. A limited, slightly old-fashioned use.

  1. Go about your business and don't stare.
  2. While you're about it, could you refill the ice tray?

5. Here and there within an area. Chiefly British; American English uses around, and British round is not an American option at all.

  1. There were toys lying about the living room.
  2. He spent the morning wandering about the museum.

6. Not a preposition — the degree modifier "approximately." It has no object; it just sits in front of a number or a measurement.

  1. There were about forty people in the room.
  2. We got there about nine.
  3. The trip takes about an hour and a half.

7. Not a preposition — the adverb and the verb particle. Nothing follows for it to govern, and in be about to it is frozen into an idiom of imminence.

  1. There was nobody about when we pulled in.
  2. Nobody knows how the change came about.
  3. She was about to leave when the phone rang.

See also: on, over, regarding, concerning, on the subject of, of

concerning#

Pronunciation: /kənˈsɜrnɪŋ/ · kun-SUR-ning · no weak form — like the other participial prepositions (regarding, respecting, touching) it keeps its stressed second syllable in every register; only the prefix vowel reduces, to /kən-/ Type: marginal/participial (grammaticalized from the verb concern) · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (concerning failing to file on time), or a fused wh-nominal (concerning who authorized the transfer) — never a finite that-clause. ✗ Concerning that he resigned is impossible; write concerning his resignation or concerning the fact that he resigned. Pattern: [clause] concerning [noun phrase] · Concerning [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: Regarding is the neutral business default; concerning is heavier and gravitates toward inquiries, complaints, allegations, and official correspondence. Cross-class test — the object: the preposition always has one and never follows a linking verb (a complaint concerning the instructor), while the modern adjective meaning "worrying" has no object and sits happily after is (that's concerning, a concerning trend). If you can drop very in front of it, you have the adjective.

Introduces the matter an official communication is about. Unlike the participial prepositions of cause (considering, given), it has lost every trace of its verb: no subject is implied and nothing is doing any concerning.

1. The subject of an inquiry, complaint, or official communication. The core sense, and the reason the word survives in correspondence at all.

  1. I'm writing concerning the invoice dated March 4.
  2. The committee received two complaints concerning the same instructor.
  3. Concerning your request for an extension, the deadline is fixed by statute.

2. The matter a document, rule, or statute covers — or conspicuously fails to cover. Very common after silent, vague, no documentation, no provision.

  1. The statute is silent concerning leases signed before 1998.
  2. There is no documentation concerning who authorized the transfer.
  3. She refused to answer questions concerning her time at the agency.

3. Not a preposition — the modern adjective meaning "worrying." It governs nothing, follows a linking verb freely, and takes degree modifiers the preposition cannot.

  1. The trend in the third quarter is concerning.
  2. Two of the lab values came back concerning enough to repeat.

in re#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈreɪ/ · also /ɪn ˈri/ · in-RAY (also in-REE) · no weak form — the re carries the stress and keeps its full vowel, and the Latin in is not the reduced English in of in a minute Type: complex (Latin, ablative of rēs "thing, matter") · Register: legal Complement: a noun phrase, almost always a proper name or the name of an estate, matter, or proceeding. No gerund, and no clause of any kind — not a that-clause and not a wh-nominal. Pattern: In re [noun phrase] — as a case caption, docket heading, or file label Variants: sub nom. (short for sub nomine, "under the name of") — the companion caption tag used when the same matter was decided below under a different name; In the Matter of, the English rendering, which some state courts prefer in full Contrast: Re is the clipped commercial descendant of this phrase and belongs in memos, not pleadings. In re survives in American practice mainly to caption a matter that has no opposing party — a bankruptcy, an estate, a juvenile proceeding — where there is no v. to write.

Latin for "in the matter of," and used for exactly that. Outside case captions it is affected.

  1. In re Gault remains the foundational juvenile due-process case.
  2. The clerk filed it under In re Estate of Margaret Voss.
  3. In re Marriage of Hoffman was decided the following term.
  4. Counsel cited three bankruptcy opinions, all captioned in re the same debtor.
  5. The docket entry read simply In re Grand Jury Subpoena.

See also: re, in the matter of, in point of, concerning

in the matter of#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ðə ˈmætər əv/ · weak /ɪn ðə ˈmætər ə/ · in-thuh-MAT-ur-uv Type: complex · Register: formal, legal Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a fused wh-nominal (in the matter of who pays for repairs); never a finite that-clause (✗ in the matter of that the funds are missing). Pattern: In the matter of [noun phrase] · [clause] in the matter of [noun phrase] Contrast: The English of in re, and the form you want when you are writing for readers rather than for a docket. Against on the subject of, it is heavier and implies a proceeding, a decision, or a formal position rather than a conversation.

Names the business at hand with deliberate ceremony. In running prose it signals that something official is being disposed of.

  1. In the matter of the disputed easement, the court found for the defendant.
  2. The board has reached no conclusion in the matter of the missing funds.
  3. In the matter of your suspension, you may request a hearing within ten days.
  4. She was called to testify in the matter of the 2019 audit.
  5. In the matter of who pays for repairs, the lease is unambiguous.

See also: in re, on the subject of, concerning, regarding

on the subject of#

Pronunciation: /ɑn ðə ˈsʌbdʒɪkt əv/ · weak /ɑn ðə ˈsʌbdʒɪkt ə/ · ahn-thuh-SUB-jikt-uv Type: complex · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a fused wh-nominal (on the subject of who pays); never a finite that-clause. Pattern: On the subject of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] on the subject of [noun phrase] Variants: on the topic of (interchangeable); on the question of (leans toward a matter genuinely in dispute); on the matter of (heavier, and drifting toward in the matter of) Contrast: Fronted, it works like speaking of — a bridge from what was just said. Mid-sentence it is simply an expanded on, and the expansion buys you the ability to attach a long noun phrase without ambiguity: a chapter on the subject of nineteenth-century tariffs cannot be misread the way a chapter on nineteenth-century tariffs occasionally can.

Announces the topic explicitly, either to open it or to change to it.

  1. On the subject of money, we should talk before the meeting.
  2. He has written three books on the subject of memory.
  3. The senator has been notably quiet on the subject of the pipeline.
  4. On the subject of who pays for the venue, nothing has been decided.
  5. She had strong opinions on the subject of letting children lose at games.
  6. On the subject of dinner — is anyone actually cooking?

over#

Pronunciation: /ˈoʊvər/ · OH-vur · no weak form — unlike the one-syllable prepositions around it, over keeps its stress and its full first vowel even in fast speech; the clipped o'er /ɔr/ is poetic only Type: simple — also an adverb and adjective (the meeting was over), a verb particle (hand it over, think it over), and a directional adverb (come over) · Register: neutral Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (sued over failing to disclose the defect), or a fused wh-nominal in the dispute sense (a fight over who gets the house, disagreement over whether to appeal) — never a finite that-clause (✗ they argued over that the rent was late → they argued over the late rent). As an adverb or particle it takes no object at all, which is the test that separates the two. Pattern: [noun of conflict] over [noun phrase] · [verb of conflict, distress, or deliberation] over [noun phrase] · over [wh-nominal] · stranded: what exactly are they fighting over? Contrast: Against about, over adds friction: you talk about the merger but you fight over it, and a discussion about the budget and a dispute over the budget differ in temperature, not in subject. Against on, which frames a topic for study, over frames it for combat. Against in connection with, over names the cause outright where that phrase deliberately declines to. Cross-class test — the object: they argued over the bill is the preposition (object the bill); the argument was over is the adjective, with nothing after it; hand it over is a particle, where the object it belongs to hand and over governs nothing; and come over sometime is a bare directional adverb. Senses treated elsewhere: this entry covers only the topic senses. The spatial ("above," "across"), covering, authority, medium, and "more than" senses are in movement and direction; the "spanning a period" sense (over the weekend) is in time.

The preposition of things people quarrel about. Whenever the head noun or verb involves conflict, grief, deliberation, or consequence, English reaches for over rather than about — and the choice is idiomatic rather than logical, so it has to be learned verb by verb.

1. The matter two parties are fighting about. After the nouns argument, dispute, fight, quarrel, row, battle, standoff, litigation, deadlock and the verbs argue, fight, quarrel, clash, split, fall out, deadlock, haggle.

  1. The strike began as an argument over scheduling software.
  2. Two families have been in court over forty feet of shoreline.
  3. Congress deadlocked over the disaster funding bill.
  4. There is still disagreement over who signs the release.

2. The trigger of a strong reaction — a resignation, a lawsuit, an arrest, a firing.

  1. The deputy director resigned over the handling of the complaint.
  2. The city was sued over the condition of the pumping station.
  3. Two contractors were charged over the falsified inspection reports.

3. The subject of worry, grief, fuss, or slow deliberation. After agonize, brood, fret, fuss, obsess, pore, mull, labor, grieve, cry.

  1. He's still beating himself up over one bad quarter.
  2. She pored over the lease for the better part of an hour.
  3. Nobody is going to cry over a broken plate.

4. Not a preposition — the adverb, adjective, and verb particle. No object follows it.

  1. The meeting was over by four.
  2. He handed over the keys without a word.
  3. Come over whenever you like.

re#

Pronunciation: /ri/ · also /reɪ/ · REE (also RAY) · no weak form — it is a stressed monosyllable on the rare occasions it is read aloud Type: simple (a clipping of Latin in re) · Register: business and legal shorthand — written only, and effectively never spoken in running prose Complement: a noun phrase. No gerund, and no clause of any kind; a pronoun object is possible only in the fixed heading Re: yours of the 14th and is otherwise avoided. Pattern: Re: [noun phrase] — as a memo or letter heading · re [noun phrase] — inside a docket line or file label Contrast: Re is not an abbreviation of regarding or of reference, so it takes no period. In a heading it is conventionally followed by a colon; inside a sentence it looks like jargon, and regarding or about is better. Not to be confused with the prefix re- of rewrite and reissue, which is bound and hyphenated where it is written at all, or with the re of do re mi, which is a different word entirely — the preposition is free-standing and takes neither hyphen nor period.

The subject line of the English language. It labels what a document is about, and it does not belong in the body of one.

1. As a heading, followed by a colon. The overwhelmingly commoner use, and the only one that is above criticism.

  1. Re: Termination of Lease, 41 Colfax Avenue.
  2. The memo was headed Re: Q3 headcount and ran to nine pages.
  3. Re: your voicemail — the shipment left Tuesday.

2. Inside running text or a file label — jargon, and worth replacing with about or regarding.

  1. Please reply to my note re the Thursday deposition.
  2. Every filing in the folder was marked re the same policy number.
  3. He sent one line re the Henderson closing and nothing since.

See also: in re, regarding, with reference to, about

regarding#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈɡɑrdɪŋ/ · rih-GAR-ding · no weak form — the stress on -gard- is fixed, and the -ing may go /-ɪn/ in casual speech but the vowel of the stressed syllable never reduces Type: marginal/participial · Register: neutral to formal; the standard word in American business writing Complement: noun phrase, objective pronoun, gerund (regarding filing after the deadline), or a fused wh-nominal (regarding what the auditors found, regarding whether to appeal) — never a finite that-clause (✗ regarding that the auditors found an error → regarding the auditors' finding). Pattern: [clause] regarding [noun phrase] · Regarding [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: The shortest member of the regard family and the only one that is a single word — prefer it to with regard to, in regard to, and as regards, which say the same thing in three words. There is no s: regardings and as regarding are not English, and regarding to is an error. Cross-class test — the subject: the preposition implies no subject and can open a sentence with nothing to attach to (Regarding your application, a decision is due Friday); the identically spelled present participle of the verb regard must share its subject with the main clause (Regarding the painting closely, she noticed the repair — she is doing the regarding). If the -ing word has an agent, it is the participle.

The neutral formal word for "about." It has no attitude, no implication of complaint, and no legal flavor, which is what makes it the safe default in correspondence.

1. Concerning — the plain topic sense.

  1. Regarding your application, we expect to decide by the fifteenth.
  2. I have a question regarding the deductible.
  3. The email said nothing regarding payment.

2. Attaching a topic to a noun or adjective of communication. After question, silence, guidance, correspondence, and after cagey, evasive, forthcoming, explicit.

  1. Several parents have written regarding the change to the bus route.
  2. She was cagey regarding her plans after graduation.
  3. The department has issued no guidance regarding remote work.

3. With a fused wh-nominal, where the topic is itself an open question.

  1. Regarding who should attend, that's the director's call.
  2. Regarding what the auditors found, nobody will say anything on the record.

See also: concerning, with regard to, as regards, respecting, about

respecting#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈspɛktɪŋ/ · rih-SPEK-ting · no weak form — identical in this respect to the noun phrase with respect to, where respect also keeps its full stressed vowel while the words around it reduce Type: marginal/participial · Register: formal and dated; still productive in legal drafting Complement: noun phrase or gerund (respecting selling the timber); a fused wh-nominal is grammatical but rare (respecting who may vote); never a finite that-clause (✗ respecting that the trust was funded). Pattern: [clause] respecting [noun phrase] · Respecting [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: The reason this word is fading is that it collides head-on with the participle of the verb respect. A law respecting religion means "a law about religion" in the First Amendment and "a law that shows deference to religion" in ordinary modern reading — and nothing in the sentence disambiguates. Use regarding or about unless you are quoting.

The eighteenth-century sibling of regarding, preserved mostly by constitutional and statutory text.

1. Concerning, about — the prepositional sense.

  1. Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion.
  2. The trustee has broad discretion respecting the sale of estate property.
  3. Rules respecting the admission of new members are set out in Article IV.
  4. He would say nothing respecting the terms of the settlement.
  5. The manual is vague respecting overtime.

2. Not a preposition — the present participle of the verb respect, which is the collision that is killing the word. The participle has a subject; the preposition does not.

  1. Respecting her wishes, the family held no service.
  2. A statute respecting tribal sovereignty may be one that concerns it or one that defers to it, and the sentence will not tell you which.

See also: regarding, concerning, touching, with respect to, in respect of

touching#

Pronunciation: /ˈtʌtʃɪŋ/ · TUCH-ing · no weak form — stress falls on the first syllable and stays there Type: marginal/participial · Register: archaic and literary Complement: noun phrase or gerund; a fused wh-nominal is possible in older text (touching what was owed him); never a finite that-clause. Pattern: [clause] touching [noun phrase] · Touching [noun phrase], [clause] Variants: touching on, touching upon — these are the living verb-plus-particle construction (the report touches on funding) and are a different structure from the preposition, which needs no on. Contrast: Identical in meaning to concerning and three hundred years older in feel. It also has an unrelated adjective twin meaning "moving" (a touching tribute), so a modern reader hits touching the estate and stalls.

The Renaissance word for "concerning," now confined to deliberate period style, older legal instruments, and church documents.

1. Concerning — the prepositional sense.

  1. Touching the matter of the boundary wall, the parties are agreed.
  2. He left instructions touching the disposal of his library.
  3. Touching your inquiry of the tenth, I can say little.
  4. There is an ancient canon touching the fitness of candidates.
  5. She wrote at length touching her father's debts.

2. Not a preposition — the adjective meaning "moving" and the participle of the verb touch. Neither governs a topic, and the adjective governs nothing at all.

  1. It was a touching tribute, and nobody had expected it.
  2. He read the whole letter without touching the envelope again.

The regard, reference, and relation family#

Seventeen ways to say about in two, three, or four words. They are near-perfect synonyms of each other, they are all more formal than they are useful, and the errors in this section — in regards to, as regards to, in accordance to — are among the most frequently made in American business writing. Three of them break out of the pattern: in relation to and vis-à-vis carry a genuine comparative sense the others lack, and as per claims conformity rather than mere reference. On the score of is the dated member that has drifted halfway into cause. Apart from those, the choice among them is a choice of tone alone.

anent#

Pronunciation: /əˈnɛnt/ · uh-NENT · no weak form — the stressed second syllable keeps its full vowel and only the unstressed a- reduces Type: simple · Register: archaic; alive in Scots law and in deliberately antique or jocular writing Complement: noun phrase only. A gerund is grammatical but unidiomatic, and no clause of any kind will go here — not a that-clause and not a wh-nominal. Pattern: [clause] anent [noun phrase] · Anent [noun phrase], [clause] Contrast: Means precisely concerning and adds nothing but age. Readers who do not know it will assume you meant ancient or mistyped something, which is a good reason to leave it alone outside Scotland.

A survival from Middle English, kept in circulation by Scottish legal usage and by writers reaching for a period effect.

  1. Anent the disposition of the estate, the sheriff has issued no interlocutor.
  2. He wrote a long, cranky letter to the editor anent the new parking scheme.
  3. There is an old rule anent the grazing of sheep on common land.
  4. Anent your question, I have consulted the minutes.
  5. The pamphlet sets out the author's views anent the theater.

apropos of#

Pronunciation: /ˌæprəˈpoʊ əv/ · weak /ˌæprəˈpoʊ ə/ · ap-ruh-POH-uv (the s is silent) Type: complex (from French à propos de) · Register: formal to literary Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a fused wh-nominal (apropos of what you just said); never a finite that-clause. As an adjective (sense 3) it takes no complement at all. Pattern: Apropos of [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause], apropos of [noun phrase] Variants: à propos of (with the accent; older and now uncommon in American text); bare apropos used directly as a preposition (apropos your email) — widespread, but conservative usage guides still want the of. Contrast: Not a general synonym for about. It means "prompted by" or "arising out of," so it needs something already on the table to arise from. It is also an adjective meaning "apt" (an apropos remark), where it takes no object at all.

Marks a remark as occasioned by something just mentioned. Its single most common appearance in American English is the fixed phrase apropos of nothing, which announces the opposite — a remark with no connection to anything.

1. Prompted by, arising out of.

  1. Apropos of your point about staffing, we lost two nurses last month.
  2. She mentioned, apropos of the renovation, that the wiring was original.

2. The fixed phrase apropos of nothing.

  1. Apropos of nothing, my brother once met him in an airport.
  2. He announced, apropos of nothing at all, that he was learning Portuguese.

3. As an adjective — no object, and no of.

  1. Her question was brief and entirely apropos.
  2. The timing of the announcement was hardly apropos.

as concerns#

Pronunciation: /æz kənˈsɜrnz/ · weak /əz kənˈsɜrnz/ · az-kun-SURNZ Type: complex · Register: formal Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or a wh-clause; never a finite that-clause. Pattern: As concerns [noun phrase], [clause] · [clause] as concerns [noun phrase] Contrast: Built exactly like as regards, with a verb agreeing with an implied subject, which is why the -s is fixed: as concern is an error, and there is no as concerns to. Rarer and stiffer than as regards; regarding beats both.

These do not simply name a topic — they fence one off. Each says "within this domain and no other," which is why they so often carry an unstated but elsewhere the answer would be different. Three of them started life as clauses and hardened into prepositions — as far as X is concerned, when it comes to, and, in its fixed phrases, as a matter of — and two, for purposes of and in the case of, are the workhorses of American legal drafting, where fencing a rule off is the whole job.

Role, capacity, and character#

The prepositions that assign a hat. As does almost all of this work; the rest are formal, legal, theatrical, or skeptical variants of it — in the capacity of is jurisdictional, in the role of theatrical, in the form of neutral, in the guise of accusatory, under color of the legal test for a hat worn without authority, in the person of grand, and qua analytical. as#

Pronunciation: /æz/ · weak /əz/ · AZ, reduced to uhz Type: simple — the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time and cause (as I was leaving), half of the comparative correlative as … as, and a relative pronoun after such and the same (such delays as we saw) · Register: neutral Complement: a noun phrase, bare (as chair, as treasurer) or with an article (as a nurse), with any pronoun in the objective case (cast as me, never ✗ as I). Never a finite clause — a clause after as means you have the conjunction, not the preposition — and never a bare gerund in the role sense: ✗ He works as driving a bus → He works as a bus driver. Pattern: [verb] as [noun phrase] · As [noun phrase], [clause] · [verb] [object] as [noun phrase or adjective phrase], with verbs of classification: regard/see/describe/treat/define/cite/use/dismiss X as Y Contrast: As versus like is the central rule. As your doctor, I'd advise against it means the speaker is the doctor; Like your doctor, I'd advise against it means the speaker is somebody else who happens to agree. In careful American English, as takes a role and like takes a resemblance. Two corollaries: such as introduces genuine members of a set (fruit such as pears) and informal like does the same job in speech; and like used as a conjunction (like I said, like I told you) is thoroughly established in American speech but still cut in edited prose, where the conjunction is as. Against for: mistaken for a doctor means misidentified, taken as a compliment means interpreted. Cross-class test — the object: She works as a nurse is the preposition (object a nurse, a noun phrase) against She works as I do (conjunction, whole clause); as tall as I am is the comparative correlative; and in such delays as we saw, as is a relative pronoun. Anything headed by as that is completed by a clause is the conjunction, and that includes the compounds as if, as though, as soon as, as long as, and as far as — none of them takes an object, and none of them may be stranded.

The role preposition, and one of the highest-frequency words in English. Senses 1 through 5 are all prepositional; sense 6 is included because the same spelling covers a conjunction that learners constantly confuse with it.

1. The job, office, or role a person holds.

  1. She spent eleven years as a public defender.
  2. As treasurer, he signs every check over five thousand dollars.
  3. He was hired as a consultant and stayed as head of the department.

2. The function a thing is put to.

  1. We used a milk crate as a nightstand for most of that year.
  2. The gym doubles as a storm shelter.

3. In the character of — costume, casting, or disguise.

  1. He came to the party as a bag of jelly beans.
  2. She was cast as Lady Macbeth three seasons running.

4. At a stage of life, or in a stated condition. Here the noun phrase names not an office but a period or a state.

  1. As a child, she spent every summer on the lake.
  2. As a young attorney he took whatever walked through the door.

5. The status a verb of classification assigns. After regard, see, describe, treat, define, count, cite, dismiss, know — and note that this is the one sense that admits an adjective or a participle in place of a noun.

  1. The letter was dismissed as a hoax within a day.
  2. Most economists regard the figure as unreliable.
  3. He's known as the only person who can fix that machine.
  4. Skipping the deposition would count as defaulting.

6. Not a preposition — the conjunction of time and cause, which takes a whole clause and governs no object.

  1. The lights went out as she reached the landing.
  2. As the roads were closed, we stayed another night.

speaking of#

Pronunciation: /ˈspikɪŋ əv/ · weak /ˈspikɪŋ ə/ · SPEE-king-uv Type: marginal/participial · Register: informal to neutral Complement: noun phrase, gerund, or the fused relative which (speaking of which); never a finite clause. Pattern: Speaking of [noun phrase], [clause] · Speaking of which, [clause] — essentially always sentence-initial Variants: talking of (chiefly British); speaking of which (fixed); which reminds me (the non-prepositional equivalent) Contrast: It is a topic-shift device, not a framing preposition: it claims that the object was just mentioned and uses that mention as a bridge to something new. As for also shifts topic, but it returns to one item out of a set already under discussion rather than following an association. By origin this is a dangling participle — nobody is doing the speaking — but it has grammaticalized completely, and no usage authority requires a matching subject.

The conversational hinge. It licenses a change of subject by pretending the change was inevitable.

  1. Speaking of the weather, did you ever get the gutters cleaned?
  2. Speaking of which, your brother called twice.
  3. Speaking of people who never answer email — has anyone heard from Dana?
  4. She's in Denver now. Speaking of Denver, the flights are absurd.
  5. Speaking of being late, we should probably leave.
  6. Speaking of money, the deposit hasn't cleared.
