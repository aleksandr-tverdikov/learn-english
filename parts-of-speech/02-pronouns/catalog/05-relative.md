# Relative pronouns

> The words that hook a clause onto a noun already standing in the sentence, pointing back at it and filling a slot inside the new clause at the same time.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

A relative pronoun does two jobs in one word: it points back at a noun already in the sentence — its **antecedent** — and it fills a grammatical slot, subject or object or possessor, inside the clause it introduces. Which relative you reach for is settled by three questions, asked in this order: is the antecedent a person or a thing, does the clause **narrow down** which one you mean or merely **comment** on something already identified, and what job does the pronoun do inside its own clause. The second question carries the punctuation with it: a clause that identifies is **restrictive** and takes no commas (*the car that needs repairs*), while a clause that only adds is **nonrestrictive** and is fenced off by commas on both sides (*my car, which needs repairs*). Nearly every error in this category is a failure to answer one of those three questions before choosing the word.

---

## The core relatives

Six items do almost all the work: *that*, *which*, *who*, *whom*, *whose*, and the zero relative — the one you get by leaving the pronoun out. Four rules govern the whole set, and they are worth stating before the entries.

**The comma rule is a meaning rule.** No commas means the clause is doing identifying work and cannot be removed without changing what the sentence is about. Commas mean the clause is a parenthesis and could be lifted out. *The passengers who had tickets were allowed to board* says some passengers had tickets; *The passengers, who had tickets, were allowed to board* says all of them did. Same words, different fact.

**Which vs. that, stated honestly.** Half of the famous rule is real and half is house style. The real half: ***that* can never introduce a nonrestrictive clause** — ✗ *my car, that needs repairs, is in the lot* is simply wrong. The house-style half: restrictive *which* (*the car which needs repairs*) is perfectly grammatical, is the ordinary British choice, and appears throughout American legal and technical prose — but American copy editors following Fowler, Strunk, and the Chicago Manual will change it to *that*, so writing *that* in restrictive clauses is the low-friction choice, not a moral obligation.

**Whom is nearly gone, except after a preposition.** In American speech *who* has taken over the object slot almost entirely, and *the man who I met* is unremarkable. The one place *whom* is still obligatory is immediately after a preposition — *to whom, for whom, with whom, several of whom* — and there it is produced without effort even by people who never otherwise use it. Related to this: **stranding a preposition at the end of a clause is normal English**, not an error. *The man I spoke to* and *the man to whom I spoke* are both correct; the first is speech, the second is formal writing, and the prohibition on final prepositions was invented in the seventeenth century on a Latin analogy that does not apply.

**Two case decisions, two different tests.** A relative pronoun takes the case of the slot it fills in **its own** clause, and nothing outside that clause can change the answer; the tool for it is the **he/him test** (*we hired **him*** → *the agent **whom** we hired*). A personal pronoun sitting in a **coordination** inside that same clause is settled separately, by the **deletion test**: take the other person out and read what is left aloud. *The clients whom my partner and **I** signed last year* — delete *my partner and*, and ***I** signed them* is what your ear wants, so the subject form is right. *The clients who signed with my partner and **me*** — delete it and you get *with **me***, an object after a preposition. The test catches two opposite errors, and both are common. On one side the nonstandard subject: ✗ *Me and him went* (✗ ***Me** went*, ✗ ***him** went*); the objection is to the case, not to the order, so the repair is *He and I went*, exactly as *She and I went* is right because ***I** went* is. On the other side the hypercorrections, produced by people over-applying the correction they were given as children: ✗ *between you and I* (✗ *between … **I***) and ✗ *They invited my wife and I* (✗ *They invited … **I***). The object form is required in both — *between you and **me***, *He gave it to Bob and **me*** — because a preposition and a transitive verb each take an object, whatever else is standing in the slot with it. This is personal-pronoun business, worked out in full in [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it); it is stated here because a relative clause is the commonest place to lose track, being the one structure where both decisions are running at once.

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðət/ &middot; *thut* &middot; the weak form **is** the normal form here; the strong /ðæt/ *THAT* shows up only when the word is being quoted or contrasted. This is the reliable way to hear the difference between relative *that* and the demonstrative: *the car **that** broke down* /ðət/ versus ***that** car* /ðæt/.
**Русский:** который — универсальное относительное о людях и о вещах, в ограничительных придаточных без запятых
**Type:** relative pronoun (some modern grammars call it a relativizer rather than a true pronoun) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; it has no genitive and can never follow a preposition &middot; **Register:** neutral, at every level from conversation to Supreme Court opinions
**Person / number:** invariant. Third person by default; number and person are supplied by the antecedent and surface on the verb, never on *that*.
**Refers to:** people, animals, things, and abstractions alike — *the woman that called*, *the dog that bit him*, *the report that arrived*. It cannot take a whole clause as its antecedent; for that use [which](#which). It is the strongly preferred relative after superlatives (*the best movie that*), after *all, any, every, only, no, few, much, little*, after indefinite pronouns (*everything that*, *anyone that*), and in *it*-clefts (*it was Marie that noticed*).
**Agreement:** when *that* is the subject of its clause, the verb agrees with the **antecedent**: *the file **that is** missing*, *the files **that are** missing*. In *one of the [plural] that*, the antecedent is the plural noun, so the verb is plural — *one of the few people **that know** the code*. Only when *the only* is added does it go singular: *the only one of the guards **that was** awake*. When the antecedent is itself a pronoun or a quantified noun, that antecedent's own number decides, and the classes are fixed: the compound indefinites *everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, no one, nobody* are **singular** (*everyone that **wants** a copy*, *somebody that **knows** the system*, *nobody that **has** read it*), and so are *each*, *every*, *either*, and *neither* (*each report that **is** filed*, *neither copy that **was** returned*); *both*, *several*, *few*, and *many* are **plural** (*both files that **were** attached*, *the few that **remain***); *all*, *some*, and *most* take the number of what they actually refer to (*all the pages that **were** missing* against *all the gravel that **was** delivered*); and *none* is the one word here that goes **either way**, taking a singular or a plural verb with both fully standard — *none of the reports that came in **was** useful* presses the "not one" reading, *none of the reports that came in **were** useful* is the commoner choice in edited American prose.
**Pattern:** [noun] *that* [verb] … — subject of its clause &middot; [noun] *that* [subject] [verb] — object of its clause &middot; [noun] *that* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *the office that I called from* &middot; never ✗ [preposition] *that* &middot; never ✗ *,* *that*
**Contrast:** Against *which*: in edited American prose *that* takes the restrictive clauses and *which* the nonrestrictive ones, but only one direction of that rule is absolute — *that* is impossible in a nonrestrictive clause, while restrictive *which* is merely something a copy editor will change. Against *who*: *that* with a human antecedent (*the man that hired me*) is standard and has been since Old English; a minority of readers dislike it, so *who* is the safer choice when the antecedent is a named individual. Against the complementizer *that* (*he said that he'd come*): the relative fills a slot inside its clause — take it away and there is a hole (*the report [that] arrived* → *…arrived* has no subject); the complementizer fills no slot — take it away and the clause is still complete.

The workhorse of the English relative system, and the only relative that is at home with both people and things. It marks off the clause that tells you *which one*.

**1. Subject of its own clause.** The gap is the subject; *that* cannot be dropped here.

1. The bill *that* passed last night wasn't the one anybody had read.
2. He's one of the few engineers *that* have read the original specification.

**2. Object of its own clause.** The gap is the object; here *that* is optional and speakers usually leave it out.

3. The apartment *that* we looked at on Tuesday is already rented.
4. That's the folder *that* I pulled the numbers from.

**3. After superlatives, quantifiers, and indefinite pronouns.** The frame where *that* beats *which* and *who* even for writers who otherwise mix them freely.

5. Everything *that* could go wrong on that drive did.
6. She's the only reporter *that* got the mayor on the record.
7. Nothing *that* he said afterward changed a single vote.

**4. In *it*-clefts.** The construction that pushes one element to the front for emphasis.

8. It was my sister *that* first noticed the smell of gas.

### which

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ &middot; *WICH* &middot; weak /wətʃ/. For most Americans *which* and *witch* are homophones; older speakers and many in the South and Appalachia keep the breathy /ʍɪtʃ/ that distinguishes them. The *wh-* here is the same Old English *hw-* that gives *who* its /h/.
**Русский:** который — относительное о вещах; в придаточных с запятыми или отсылка ко всему предложению
**Type:** relative pronoun; also interrogative, and a determiner when it precedes a noun &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; the genitive is [whose](#whose) or [of which](#of-which) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the only option in a nonrestrictive clause about a thing, and the formal option after a preposition
**Person / number:** invariant, third person; singular or plural entirely according to the antecedent
**Refers to:** things, animals, institutions, and abstractions — **never a person**. ✗ *the woman which called* was ordinary English in 1611 (*Our Father which art in heaven*) and is a plain error now. Companies, teams, and committees count as things in American English (*the company, which*), though *the band who* is heard when the group is being thought of as its members. Among the ordinary relatives only *which* can take **an entire clause** as its antecedent; the one other word that does the same job is sentential [as](#as), which is confined to a narrower frame and, unlike *which*, may also stand in front of the clause it comments on (*As everyone expected, the vote was postponed*).
**Agreement:** as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent — *the box, **which is** heavy*; *the boxes, **which are** heavy*. With a whole clause as antecedent the verb is singular: *He said nothing, **which was** answer enough.* With a collective antecedent American English takes the singular: *the committee, **which has** met twice*.
**Pattern:** [noun], *which* [verb] … &middot; [noun], *which* [subject] [verb] … &middot; [preposition] *which* [subject] [verb] — pied-piped, as in *the shelf on which it sits* &middot; [quantifier] *of which* — *three of which, all of which* &middot; [clause], *which* [verb] — the sentential relative
**Contrast:** Against *that*: see the honest version above — *that* is barred from nonrestrictive clauses absolutely, while restrictive *which* is grammatical but will be edited. Against *who*: things versus people, with no overlap. Against the fused relative *what*: *which* needs an antecedent sitting in the sentence, *what* contains its own (*I heard what you said* = *the thing that you said*).

The relative for things, and the everyday choice when the antecedent is a whole idea rather than a noun.

**1. Nonrestrictive relative.** Commas on both sides, and the clause could be lifted out without changing what the sentence is about.

1. Her second novel, *which* took nine years, sold about four hundred copies.
2. The engine, *which* had been rebuilt twice, finally seized on the interstate.

**2. Restrictive relative.** No commas. Grammatical, standard in British English, common in American legal and technical writing — and routinely changed to *that* by American editors.

3. The provisions *which* survive termination are listed in Schedule C.

**3. After a preposition.** The pied-piping frame. Here *that* is impossible, the zero relative is impossible, and no editor objects to restrictive *which*.

4. The file cabinet in *which* the originals were stored burned with the building.
5. That's the argument to *which* I keep coming back.

**4. Sentential relative.** The antecedent is the whole preceding clause, not any noun in it. Always preceded by a comma. The trap: if the reader can find a noun that might be the antecedent, they will try it first, so keep the clause short and the reference obvious.

6. He turned down the offer, *which* everyone else thought was insane.
7. She never explained why she left, *which* is her right.

**5. In a partitive phrase.** With a quantifier or a number in front of it.

8. There were forty-two proposals, six of *which* missed the deadline outright.

**6. Object of the verb in its own clause.** The gap is the object rather than the subject, and *which* keeps the same form either way — it has no *whom*.

9. The bylaws, *which* nobody had read since 1974, turned out to settle the question.

### who

**Pronunciation:** /hu/ &middot; *HOO* &middot; weak /(h)ʊ/ — the vowel shortens in running speech (*the man who called*), but unlike *him* and *her* the /h/ normally survives. *Who's* /huz/ is *who is* or *who has*, and is pronounced exactly like *whose*.
**Русский:** который, кто — относительное о людях, обычно подлежащее своего придаточного
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject (nominative) in the paradigm — object form [whom](#whom), genitive [whose](#whose) — but in present-day American English *who* also fills the **object** slot everywhere except directly after a preposition: *the agent **who** we hired*, *the agent **who** I gave it **to***, but never ✗ *the agent to **who** I spoke* &middot; **Register:** neutral as a subject; as an object it is the ordinary spoken form and increasingly the written one
**Person / number:** invariant in form; any person, either number. It transmits the **person** of its antecedent to the verb: *I who **am** …*, *you who **are** …*
**Refers to:** people, and animals granted personal status (*our dog, who hates the mail carrier*). Not things: ✗ *the report who arrived*. American English treats collective nouns as things and uses *that* or *which* for them (*the committee that voted*), though *the band who* turns up when the members rather than the body are meant.
**Agreement:** as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent in number **and person**: *the neighbor **who lives** upstairs*, *the neighbors **who live** upstairs*, *It is I **who am** to blame* (formal). The classic trap: *one of those people who **do** this* takes the plural, because *who* points at *those people*; *the only one of my friends who **has** a truck* takes the singular, because *who* points at *the only one*. With an **indefinite-pronoun antecedent** the verb inside the relative clause is singular — *everyone who **wants** a copy*, *somebody who **knows** the system*, *nobody who **was** on the call* — even though the pronoun that picks that antecedent up later in the sentence is normally singular *they*: *Everyone who wants a copy should raise **their** hand.* Singular *they* is standard American English, accepted by the AP, Chicago, MLA, and APA manuals with differences only of scope (see [they](01-personal.md#they)), and it leaves the relative clause's own verb singular. One honest footnote to *It is I who am to blame*: that is the formal pattern, and the reason person agreement is worth knowing at all, but the ordinary American sentence is *It's **me** who's to blame* or *It's me that's to blame*. ***It's me* is standard English**, not a lapse — the subject form after *be* is a Latin-modeled rule that American usage has left behind (see [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it)).
**Pattern:** [person noun] *who* [verb] … — subject &middot; [person noun] *who* [subject] [verb] — object, standard in speech: *the guy who I met* &middot; [person noun] *who* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded: *the guy who I gave it to* &middot; [person noun], *who* [verb] … , — nonrestrictive &middot; never ✗ [preposition] *who*
**Contrast:** *who* vs. *whom* is decided by the **he/him test**: rebuild the relative clause as a sentence and put *he* or *him* in the gap. *The agent who handled the sale* → *he handled the sale* → *who*. *The agent whom we hired* → *we hired him* → *whom*. Do not let a parenthetical clause fool you: in *the candidate who we thought would win*, the gap belongs to *would win* (*he would win*), so it is *who*, and ✗ *whom we thought would win* is a hypercorrection — now a more common error than the one it was meant to prevent. *who's* vs. *whose*: expand it. If *who is* or *who has* fits, it is *who's*; otherwise *whose*. Against *that*: both are standard for people in restrictive clauses, but only *who* can appear in a nonrestrictive one. One case decision the relative does **not** make: a coordination sitting inside the relative clause is settled on its own by the **deletion test** — *the clients who my partner and **I** signed* (delete *my partner and*: ✗ *me signed*), *the clients who signed with my partner and **me*** (✗ *with I*). The two failures are the familiar pair, ✗ *Me and him went* on one side and the hypercorrections ✗ *between you and I* and ✗ *They invited my wife and I* on the other; the correct forms are *between you and **me*** and *He gave it to Bob and **me***, and the whole subject belongs to [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it).

The relative for people, filling the subject slot of its clause — and, in everything short of formal writing, the object slot as well.

**1. Subject of its own clause, restrictive.** No commas; the clause says which person.

1. The nurse *who* took my blood pressure had been on shift for fourteen hours.
2. Anyone *who* still has a paper ticket should come to the front.
3. There's nobody in this office *who* knows how to reset the alarm.

**2. Subject of its own clause, nonrestrictive.** Commas; the person is already identified.

4. My grandmother, *who* never learned to drive, walked everywhere until she was eighty.
5. It was Ruiz *who* filed the original complaint, not his partner.

**3. Subject across an intervening clause.** *We thought*, *they said*, *everyone assumed* — these do not change the case.

6. The candidate *who* we all assumed would win finished third.

**4. Object of its own clause.** Where formal writing wants *whom*, and where speech does not.

7. He's the one *who* I gave the spare key to.
8. The contractor *who* she hired last spring never finished the deck.

### whom

**Pronunciation:** /hum/ &middot; *HOOM* &middot; weak /(h)ʊm/, and in rapid speech reduced all the way to /(h)əm/ — *to whom* comes out /tə əm/ and *several of whom* /ˈsɛvrəl ə əm/. The /h/-dropping is the ordinary unstressed-syllable process that also gives *him* /ɪm/, *her* /ɚ/, *them* /əm/, *you* /jə/, and *he* /i/; the full table is under [weak forms](01-personal.md#weak-forms-what-you-actually-hear).
**Русский:** которого, которому — объектная форма who; книжн., обязательна после предлога
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** formal in the bare relative use; neutral and unavoidable after a preposition
**Person / number:** invariant; any person, either number; people only
**Refers to:** human antecedents only, plus animals treated as persons. Things take *which*: *the agency to which he reported*, not ✗ *to whom*.
**Agreement:** none of its own. As an object it never controls a verb, so the verb of the relative clause agrees with that clause's own subject: *the officers **whom** the board **has** cited* — *has* agrees with *board*.
**Pattern:** [person noun] [preposition] *whom* [subject] [verb] — pied-piped: *the agent to whom I spoke* &middot; [person noun] *whom* [subject] [verb] — plain object: *the agent whom I called* &middot; [person noun], [quantifier] *of whom* [verb] … — *four of whom, all of whom* &middot; *than whom* — a fossil, as in *a scholar than whom none was more careful* &middot; never ✗ *that whom*, never ✗ *whom* as a subject
**Contrast:** *whom* is the object form of [who](#who), and the **he/him test** settles every case: put *he* or *him* in the gap and see which one fits — *we hired **him*** → *the agent **whom** we hired*. Two honest observations about how much of it survives. First, outside prepositional position American English has very largely replaced it with *who*, and *the agent who we hired* is standard in speech and increasingly in print; using *whom* there is a register choice, not a correctness requirement. Second, immediately after a preposition *whom* is still obligatory in writing — ✗ *to who* is an error — and this is where even *whom*-avoiders produce it naturally. The one thing to avoid outright is the hypercorrection ✗ *whom shall I say is calling*, ✗ *the candidate whom we thought would win*: the pronoun there is the subject of the inner verb, so it must be *who*.

The object form, used where the person is on the receiving end of the verb or the preposition.

**1. After a preposition.** The pied-piped frame — formal, and the last stronghold of the word.

1. The attorney to *whom* the case was reassigned had never tried a jury case.
2. The people with *whom* we shared the cabin turned out to be cousins of my father's.
3. He's the sort of man about *whom* nobody has a strong opinion.

**2. Object of its own clause.** Formal writing; ordinary speech uses *who* or nothing at all.

4. The candidate *whom* everyone had written off won by nine points.
5. She wrote to the senator, *whom* she had met exactly once, and got an answer in a week.

**3. In a partitive phrase after a quantifier.** Nonrestrictive, always after a comma, and the frame where no substitute exists.

6. Several of the workers, three of *whom* had been there since the plant opened, refused to sign.
7. He thanked the volunteers, most of *whom* had driven in from out of state.

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ* &middot; weak /(h)ʊz/ &middot; a perfect homophone of *who's* (= *who is*, *who has*), which is the whole reason the spelling gets confused
**Русский:** чей, которого — притяжательная форма относительного, о людях и о вещах
**Type:** relative genitive — the possessive of both *who* and *which*; also interrogative. Inside its clause it behaves like a determiner, since a noun always follows it, but it is the element that links the clause, and grammars have called it a relative pronoun for four centuries &middot; **Case:** possessive (genitive) &middot; **Register:** neutral with human antecedents; neutral with inanimate ones in every register except the most conservative copydesk
**Person / number:** invariant. The **following noun** controls number, not the antecedent.
**Refers to:** **both people and things** — *a violinist whose bow arm is failing*, *a house whose roof leaks*, *a statute whose intent is obvious*, *a dog whose owner never showed up*.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the noun that *whose* introduces, not with the antecedent: *a house **whose windows are** boarded up* (plural verb after a singular antecedent), *houses **whose roof is** shared* (singular verb after a plural one).
**Pattern:** [noun] *whose* [noun] [verb] … — the *whose*-phrase is the subject of the relative clause &middot; [noun] *whose* [noun] [subject] [verb] — the *whose*-phrase is its object: *the writer whose books I collect* &middot; [noun] [preposition] *whose* [noun] [subject] [verb] — pied-piped: *a company in whose warehouse the crates sat for a year* &middot; [noun], *whose* [noun] [verb] … , — nonrestrictive
**Contrast:** *whose* vs. *who's* — expand the contraction; if *who is* or *who has* works, write *who's*, and if it does not, write *whose*. *whose* vs. *of which* for inanimate possessors — both are correct and mean the same thing, but *whose* is almost always the better sentence: *a house whose roof leaks* against the stilted *a house the roof of which leaks*. The rule that *whose* may refer only to people is a **zombie rule**: inanimate *whose* has been in continuous use since Middle English, appears in Shakespeare, Milton, and the King James Bible, and is endorsed by Fowler, Garner, and Merriam-Webster's usage dictionary alike. This entry covers the relative use; for the question word before a noun (*Whose coat is this?*) see the determiner file.

The one relative that marks possession, and the one that does not care whether its antecedent is human.

**1. With a human antecedent.**

1. The novelist *whose* letters were auctioned last year had asked that they be burned.
2. Any student *whose* name appears on this list should see the registrar before Friday.
3. She's the one *whose* car got towed on Tuesday.

**2. With an inanimate antecedent.** Standard, and lighter than any alternative.

4. We finally bought a house *whose* roof doesn't leak.
5. It's an argument *whose* logic falls apart the moment you check the numbers.
6. There's a bill in committee *whose* effects nobody has bothered to model.

**3. In a nonrestrictive clause.**

7. The company, *whose* founder resigned in March, has lost half its engineering staff.

**4. After a preposition.** *Whose* pied-pipes like *which* and *whom*, carrying both the preposition and the following noun to the front of the clause.

8. They finally located the contractor in *whose* warehouse the crates had sat for a year.

**See also:** [who](#who), [whom](#whom), [which](#which), [of which](#of-which), [whose (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whose), [of which (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#of-which), [whosever](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever), [who's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whos-not-a-determiner)

### zero relative

**Pronunciation:** none — the pronoun is not pronounced because it is not there. What a listener actually hears is a bare juncture: two noun phrases butted together with no pause and no /ðət/ between them — *the book I read* /ðə ˈbʊk aɪ ˈrɛd/. Pausing at the gap is what makes a learner sound like they have lost their place. Written with the symbol **Ø** in grammars; written with nothing at all in real life.
**Русский:** нулевое относительное — пропуск «который» в роли дополнения: the book I read
**Type:** zero relative, also called the contact clause — a relative pronoun omitted rather than replaced &middot; **Case:** the missing element is an object or a complement, never a subject in edited writing &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; ordinary in speech and in all but the most formal prose. It is not a contraction, not slang, and not an error.
**Person / number:** no form, so nothing to inflect.
**Refers to:** anything *that* could refer to — people, things, ideas — but **only in restrictive clauses**. Never in a nonrestrictive one: ✗ *my car, I bought in June, is already dead*.
**Agreement:** none of its own. The relative clause keeps its own subject, and that subject controls the verb: *the emails Ø **she sent** are missing*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *Ø* [subject] [verb] — *the movie we saw* &middot; [noun] *Ø* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, and the commonest shape of all: *the guy I told you about* &middot; [noun] *Ø* [subject] [verb] [complement] — *the man he was* &middot; never ✗ [noun] *Ø* [verb] in edited writing &middot; never ✗ [preposition] *Ø*
**Contrast:** Against *that*: fully interchangeable whenever the relative is an object — *the book that I read* and *the book I read* differ only in weight. The test for whether you may drop it is mechanical: **look at what comes right after the gap. Another subject? Drop it. A verb? Keep it.** *The file [that] I sent* — *I* follows, so it goes. *The file that arrived* — a verb follows, so ✗ *the file arrived was corrupt* is broken English. It also cannot be dropped from a nonrestrictive clause, and there is nothing to drop when the preposition has been pied-piped (*the man to whom I spoke*). One recognized exception runs the other way: after existential *there* and in clefts, spoken American English does drop a **subject** relative — *There's a man Ø wants to see you*, *It was my brother Ø told me* — which is widespread, old, and still changed by every copy editor.

The relative pronoun you get by leaving the relative pronoun out. English has allowed it since the Middle Ages, and in conversation it is more common than *that*.

**1. Object of the verb in its clause.**

1. The apartment *Ø* we looked at on Tuesday is already rented.
2. Everything *Ø* she owns fits in the back of one car.
3. The one thing *Ø* nobody mentioned was what it would cost.

**2. Object of a stranded preposition.**

4. That's the guy *Ø* I was telling you about.
5. The years *Ø* we spent in Tucson were the best ones we had.

**3. Complement of *be*.**

6. He's not the man *Ø* he was ten years ago.

**4. Subject after existential *there* — spoken only.**

7. There's a woman downstairs *Ø* says she has an appointment.

**See also:** [that](#that), [which](#which), [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [zero *that*](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#zero-that)

## Relatives inside a preposition or a quantifier phrase

When the relative has to carry a preposition or a quantifier along with it to the front of its clause, the field narrows sharply. *That* and the zero relative drop out entirely — neither can follow a preposition, and neither can appear after *all of*, *several of*, or *the last of* — and so does plain *who*, since ✗ *to who* is an error. What is left is *which* for things, *whom* for people, and [whose](#whose) for either when what the preposition governs is a possessor (*a company in whose warehouse the crates sat for a year*). These are the frames where *whom* is not a stylistic flourish but the only grammatical option, and where American writers who avoid it everywhere else still produce it.

### all of which, some of which, none of which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl əv ˈwɪtʃ/, /ˌsʌm əv ˈwɪtʃ/, /ˌnʌn əv ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *awl-uhv-WICH, sum-uhv-WICH, nun-uhv-WICH* &middot; weak: *of* reduces to /ə/, giving /ˌɔl ə ˈwɪtʃ/ — the stress sits on *which*
**Русский:** все из которых, некоторые из которых, ни один из которых — партитив о вещах
**Type:** quantifier + partitive relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** object of *of* &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning written
**Person / number:** third person. The number of the verb is set by the **quantifier** when the quantifier is one that fixes number on its own (*one, each, either, neither* → singular; *both, several, many, few* → plural) and by the **antecedent** when it is not (*all, some, most*). *None* belongs to neither list: it goes **either way**, and both the singular and the plural verb are standard.
**Refers to:** things, animals, and abstractions gathered in a set. People take *of whom*. The antecedent is a plural or a mass noun phrase.
**Agreement:** four classes, and it is worth keeping them apart. **Always singular** — *one of which **is** broken*, *each of which **is** numbered*, *either of which **works***, *neither of which **was** under warranty*; *were* is common in speech after *neither*, but careful writing keeps the singular. **Always plural** — *both of which **were** returned*, *several of which **have** failed*, *few of which **survive***, *many of which **are** duplicates*. **Number taken from the antecedent** — *all*, *some*, and *most* agree with what they refer back to, so a plural count antecedent gives a plural verb (*the boxes, all of which **were** wet*; *the pages, most of which **are** missing*) and a mass antecedent gives a singular one (*two tons of gravel, most of which **was** washed away*; *the testimony, some of which **was** sealed*). **Either way** — *none of which* takes a singular or a plural verb and both are fully standard: the singular presses the "not one" reading (*none of which **was** mentioned*), the plural is the commoner choice in edited American prose (*none of which **were** mentioned*).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], [quantifier] *of which* [verb] … &middot; [noun phrase], [quantifier] *of which* [subject] [verb] … &middot; [number or superlative] *of which* — *three of which*, *the largest of which* &middot; always after a comma: this construction is nonrestrictive by nature
**Contrast:** *of which* for things against *of whom* for people is the only distinction that matters inside the frame, and it is absolute. What actually goes wrong is punctuation: writers reach for *of them* instead and produce a comma splice — ✗ *He made three promises, none of them were kept.* Fix it either by relativizing (*none of **which** were kept*) or by breaking the sentence (*; none of **them** were kept*). Note also that *that* is impossible here: ✗ *three of that*.

A quantifier and a relative pronoun joined by *of*, letting a second clause comment on part of what the first clause introduced.

1. She left behind eleven boxes of papers, most of *which* had never been opened.
2. The bill adds four new fees, none of *which* was mentioned in the summary. (*were* is equally standard here)
3. He gave three reasons for the delay, all of *which* turned out to be false.
4. The house came with two furnaces, neither of *which* was under warranty.
5. They tested sixty samples, some of *which* had been sitting in a garage since 1998.
6. The company operates twelve plants, the largest of *which* employs nine hundred people.
7. He filed five motions, two of *which* the judge dismissed before lunch. (here the *of which* phrase is the **object** of *dismissed*, not the subject of its clause)

**See also:** [which](#which), [of which](#of-which), [both of whom, several of whom, many of whom](#both-of-whom-several-of-whom-many-of-whom), [by which, in which, on which](#by-which-in-which-on-which)

### both of whom, several of whom, many of whom

**Pronunciation:** /ˌboʊθ əv ˈhum/, /ˈsɛvrəl əv ˈhum/, /ˌmɛni əv ˈhum/ &middot; *bohth-uhv-HOOM, SEV-ruhl-uhv-HOOM, men-ee-uhv-HOOM* &middot; weak: *of* goes to /ə/ and *whom* often loses its /h/ — /ˌboʊθ ə ˈum/
**Русский:** оба из которых, несколько из которых, многие из которых — партитив о людях
**Type:** quantifier + partitive relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** object of *of* &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the one frame in which ordinary American writing still uses *whom* without self-consciousness
**Person / number:** third person, people only; a plural antecedent, or exactly two for *both*, *neither*, and *either*
**Refers to:** human antecedents only. Things take *of which*.
**Agreement:** the same four classes as with *of which*. **Always plural** — *both of whom **were** hired*, *several of whom **have** left*, *many of whom **were** veterans*, *few of whom **remember** it*. **Always singular** — *each of whom **is** responsible*, *one of whom **is** now his boss*, *either of whom **is** qualified*, *neither of whom **was** licensed*; *were* is common in speech, the singular is what careful writing uses. **Number taken from the antecedent** — *all* and *most of whom* agree with the group they point back at, which with people is a plural count noun and therefore a plural verb (*the volunteers, all of whom **were** interviewed*; *the tenants, most of whom **have** lived here for years*). **Either way** — *none of whom* takes a singular or a plural verb, exactly as *none of which* does, and both are standard.
**Pattern:** [plural person noun], [quantifier] *of whom* [verb] … &middot; [plural person noun], [quantifier] *of whom* [subject] [verb] … &middot; [number] *of whom* — *two of whom* &middot; always after a comma; the clause is nonrestrictive
**Contrast:** ✗ *of who* is an error — after a preposition the object form is required, and this is precisely the position where speakers who never otherwise say *whom* still say it. ✗ *of that* is impossible. And the comma-splice trap is identical to the *of which* one: ✗ *I called four references, two of them never called back* needs *two of **whom** never called back*, or a semicolon.

The people-side twin of *all of which* — a quantifier reaching back into a group and commenting on part of it.

1. He has two sisters, both of *whom* live in Portland.
2. The panel included six economists, several of *whom* had advised the previous administration.
3. She interviewed thirty veterans, many of *whom* had never talked about the war to anyone.
4. They hired two contractors, neither of *whom* was licensed in this state.
5. The seminar has forty students, each of *whom* has to present twice.
6. He named four people in the deposition, one of *whom* is now his boss.
7. The firm has three partners, two of *whom* the client has never met. (here the *of whom* phrase is the **object** of *met*, not the subject of its clause)

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [all of which, some of which, none of which](#all-of-which-some-of-which-none-of-which), [who](#who), [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom)

### by which, in which, on which

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈwɪtʃ/, /ɪn ˈwɪtʃ/, /ɑn ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *by-WICH, in-WICH, ahn-WICH* &middot; weak: the preposition reduces (/ən ˈwɪtʃ/, /ət ˈwɪtʃ/) while *which* keeps the stress
**Русский:** которым, в котором, на котором — предлог перед which, о вещах и местах
**Type:** pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** object of the preposition &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; the stranded version is the ordinary spoken equivalent
**Person / number:** invariant, third person
**Refers to:** things, places, times, processes, and abstractions. People take *to whom*, *for whom*, *with whom*. When the antecedent is a place or a time, *where* and *when* usually do the same job with less machinery: *the town in which he grew up* = *the town where he grew up*.
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb agrees with the relative clause's own subject — *the rate at which prices **rise***, *the terms on which they **were** hired*.
**Pattern:** [noun] [preposition] *which* [subject] [verb] — *the drawer in which I keep it* &middot; the stranded twin: [noun] [*that* / Ø] [subject] [verb] … [preposition] — *the drawer I keep it in* &middot; [clause], [preposition] *which* [subject] [verb] — sentential, as in *…, after which nobody spoke*
**Contrast:** Pied-piping (carrying the preposition to the front) against stranding (leaving it at the end) is a **register** choice, not a correctness one. Both are correct English; stranding is the unmarked American option in speech and most writing, and the belief that a clause may not end with a preposition is a seventeenth-century invention based on a Latin analogy that does not hold. The real constraint runs the other way: with *that* and with the zero relative, stranding is the **only** option, since ✗ *the drawer in that I keep it* is impossible. So pied-piping forces you into *which* or *whom*, and stranding opens the whole field back up.

A preposition dragged to the front of the clause along with its relative pronoun — the formal alternative to leaving it stranded at the end.

1. The speed at *which* the fire moved caught everyone off guard.
2. This is the shelf on *which* the original negatives were stored.
3. He described the process by *which* the bones are cleaned and cataloged.
4. There were four grounds on *which* the appeal could have been filed, and she used none of them.
5. The room in *which* we waited had one window and no clock.
6. The manner in *which* the vote was taken is now itself under review.
7. The meeting ran until eleven, after *which* nobody had the energy to argue. (sentential — the antecedent is the whole preceding clause, not a noun in it)

**See also:** [which](#which), [of which](#of-which), [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom), [zero relative](#zero-relative), [whereupon, whereon, whereto, wherewith, whereat, whereunto](#whereupon-whereon-whereto-wherewith-whereat-whereunto), [wherein](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#wherein), [whereby](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#whereby), [where](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#where), [when](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#when)

### of which

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *uhv-WICH* &middot; weak /ə ˈwɪtʃ/ — *of* is almost never given its full form here
**Русский:** которого, которой — родительный падеж о вещах: the windows of which — окна которого
**Type:** preposition + relative pronoun, doing the work of a genitive &middot; **Case:** object of *of* &middot; **Register:** formal, and frequently stiff
**Person / number:** invariant, third person
**Refers to:** things and abstractions. People take *of whom*.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the head noun of the *of which* phrase, not with the antecedent: *a building the windows of which **are** original*; *a building the roof of which **is** original*.
**Pattern:** [noun] *the* [noun] *of which* [verb] … — *a house the roof of which leaks* &middot; [noun] *the* [noun] *of which* [subject] [verb] … — *a contract the terms of which nobody read* &middot; [noun], [quantifier] *of which* … — the partitive frame &middot; the heavier inversion [noun] *of which the* [noun] [verb] is possible and rare
**Contrast:** Against *whose*: identical in meaning when the possessor is inanimate, and *whose* wins on every count of readability — *a house whose roof leaks* against *a house the roof of which leaks*. *Of which* earns its place in two situations: legal and technical writing, where the possessor phrase is long and precision about what modifies what matters; and after a quantifier or number (*three of which*, *the first of which*), where *whose* cannot be used at all.

The heavy, formal genitive of *which* — a possessive relative built out of a preposition instead of an inflection.

1. It's an old contract, the terms of *which* nobody in the office can explain.
2. They bought a warehouse the roof of *which* had already partly collapsed.
3. The report makes forty recommendations, the first of *which* is to fire the consultant.
4. She published two studies, the conclusions of *which* have since been retracted.
5. He belongs to a club the rules of *which* are unwritten and strictly enforced.
6. This is a disease the cause of *which* was unknown until the early 1980s.

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [which](#which), [all of which, some of which, none of which](#all-of-which-some-of-which-none-of-which), [whereupon, whereon, whereto, wherewith, whereat, whereunto](#whereupon-whereon-whereto-wherewith-whereat-whereunto), [of which (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#of-which), [whereof](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#whereof)

### to whom, for whom, with whom

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈhum/, /fɚ ˈhum/, /wɪð ˈhum/ &middot; *tuh-HOOM, fer-HOOM, with-HOOM* &middot; weak: the preposition reduces to schwa and *whom* can lose its /h/ at speed — /tə ˈum/
**Русский:** которому, для которого, с которым — предлог перед whom, только о людях
**Type:** pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun; the same frame serves as an interrogative (*To whom am I speaking?*) &middot; **Case:** object of the preposition &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral in writing; in speech the stranded version with *who* or nothing is the ordinary form
**Person / number:** invariant; people only
**Refers to:** human antecedents only. Things take *to which*, *for which*, *with which*.
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb agrees with the relative clause's own subject — *the neighbors for whom we **collect** the mail*.
**Pattern:** [person noun] [preposition] *whom* [subject] [verb] — *the agent to whom I spoke* &middot; the stranded twin: [person noun] [*who* / *that* / Ø] [subject] [verb] … [preposition] — *the agent I spoke to* &middot; [person noun], [preposition] *whom* [subject] [verb], … — nonrestrictive &middot; fixed phrases: *to whom it may concern*, *for whom the bell tolls*
**Contrast:** This is the last position in American English where *whom* is not optional: directly after a preposition it is the only correct form, and ✗ *the agent to who I spoke* is an error. Strand the preposition and the picture flips — *the agent **who** I spoke **to*** is unremarkable in speech and fine in most writing, while *the agent **whom** I spoke **to*** is correct but sounds like someone is being watched. Both structures are grammatical; what changes is how formal the sentence sounds and how far the preposition has traveled.

The one *whom* that survives in ordinary use, held in place by the preposition standing in front of it.

1. The counselor to *whom* she was assigned had a six-week backlog.
2. He couldn't remember the name of the officer with *whom* he had filed the report.
3. These are the neighbors for *whom* we've been collecting mail all month.
4. There's a woman in accounting from *whom* I've learned more than from any manual.
5. My uncle, about *whom* the less said the better, showed up anyway.
6. The letter began "To *whom* it may concern," which told her everything she needed to know.

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [by which, in which, on which](#by-which-in-which-on-which), [both of whom, several of whom, many of whom](#both-of-whom-several-of-whom-many-of-whom), [zero relative](#zero-relative)

## Restricted, archaic, and nonstandard relatives

Nine entries that are alive only inside a particular frame, in a particular century, or in a particular dialect — four single relatives, two frozen phrases, one cross-reference formula, one dialect frame that takes three different relatives inside it, and one family of six *where-* compounds. You will meet every one of them in reading. Only *as* belongs in ordinary writing, and only in the frames that license it — after *such* or *the same*, or as a comment on a whole clause; the one other survivor is *whereupon*, and it survives as a narrator's word for "and then." What is marked **archaic** below is dated rather than wrong, and what is marked **nonstandard** is marked that way on purpose and with the region named: these are regular, rule-governed forms in the varieties that use them, and they are still edited out of American prose.

**The nonstandard neighbors, labeled.** Dialect features do not arrive one at a time, so the relatives below turn up in sentences carrying other marked pronouns along with them. Each of those forms is regular and rule-governed in the variety that uses it, each is nonstandard in edited American prose, and none of them is a relative pronoun — so each is catalogued in the file that owns it, and named here only so a reader meeting it in one of the examples below knows what it is. **Demonstrative *them*** — ***them** apples*, ***them** boxes*, and the subject use in *Them as has, gets* — stands in for *those* and is widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech; the pronoun use is at [them](04-demonstrative.md#them), the determiner use (*them apples*) in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them), and the frame it makes with a relative hung on it at [them that / them as / them what](#them-that--them-as--them-what). Keep it apart from the reduced pronunciation /əm/ of *I saw 'em*, which is ordinary General American and carries no dialect marking at all. **The vernacular reflexives *hisself* and *theirselves*** — general in Southern, Appalachian, and rural white vernaculars and in African American English — are in fact more regular than the *himself* and *themselves* they replace, since they build on the possessive stem exactly as *myself* and *yourself* do; see [hisself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself) and [theirselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#theirselves). **Plural *youse*** belongs to New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its American distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration, and it stands beside [y'all](01-personal.md#yall) (Southern, and standard within African American English), [you'uns](01-personal.md#youuns) and *yinz* (Appalachia and Pittsburgh), and [you guys](01-personal.md#you-guys) (national, informal rather than nonstandard); see [youse](01-personal.md#youse). **The double negative** — *There ain't nobody **what** knows nothing about it* — is **negative concord**, in which the second and third negatives agree with the first instead of canceling it, exactly as in Spanish *no vi a nadie* or French *ne … personne*; it was ordinary English in Chaucer and Shakespeare, and it is systematic today in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernacular speech. It is nevertheless nonstandard in edited American English, which allows one negative per clause: *There isn't anybody who knows anything about it.* See [nothing (negative concord)](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord). And the two case failures this file's opening section works through — ✗ *Me and him went*, general vernacular American and the most-corrected error in American classrooms, and the hypercorrections ✗ *between you and I* and ✗ *They invited my wife and I*, which run *upward* through the social scale rather than downward — are catalogued at [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#nonstandard-pronoun-forms-recognize-them-label-them-dont-write-them).

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ &middot; *AZ* &middot; weak /əz/ — the weak form is the usual one in this use, since the stress falls on *such* or *same*
**Русский:** который; как — относительное только после such и the same, а также о целом предложении
**Type:** relative pronoun after *such* and *the same* (marginally after *so many*, *so much*); elsewhere a conjunction or a preposition &middot; **Case:** none — it serves as subject or object of its clause &middot; **Register:** neutral in the *such* and *the same* frames, neutral in the sentential use, nonstandard in the general-relative dialect use (rural Southern and Midland American; northern and Midland English, Scots, and Irish)
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** whatever *such* or *the same* points at — people or things — and, in the sentential use, an entire clause.
**Agreement:** as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent: *such people **as are** willing*, *the same problems **as were** reported last year*. In the sentential use the verb is singular, since the antecedent is a whole proposition: *as **is** usual*, *as **was** expected*.
**Pattern:** *such* [noun] *as* [verb] — *such help as remains* &middot; *such* [noun] *as* [subject] [verb] — *such help as he could offer* &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [subject] [verb] &middot; [clause], *as* [verb] — sentential: *…, as was predicted* &middot; *As* [verb], [clause] — fronted sentential: *As is often the case, …*
**Contrast:** Against *that* after *the same*: both are used — *the same car as I drove* and *the same car that I drove* — and the traditional distinction is that *as* points to a matching item while *that* points to the identical one. The distinction is real but soft, and no reader will be misled either way. Against *which* in the sentential use: *which* can only follow its clause (*He was late, which surprised us*), while *as* can also come first (*As we all know, he was late*) and carries a flavor of "in accordance with what."

The relative that needs a host: it appears only after *such* or *the same*, or as a comment on a whole clause.

**1. After *such*.**

1. He offered such help *as* he could and then went home.
2. Such of the records *as* survive are in the county archive.

**2. After *the same*.**

3. We ran into the same problems *as* were reported in the pilot study.
4. She wore the same coat *as* her sister had worn to the funeral.

**3. Sentential relative.** The antecedent is the whole clause, and *as* may come before or after it.

5. The deadline was moved twice, *as* everyone expected.
6. *As* is often the case with sealed bids, the low number was the least reliable.

**4. General relative — nonstandard.** In older rural and working-class speech — the American South and Midland, and northern England, the English Midlands, Scotland, and Ireland — *as* replaces *that* in ordinary relative clauses. Dialect only; recognize it, do not write it. *Them as has, gets* carries two further nonstandard features along with the relative — the demonstrative [them](04-demonstrative.md#them) standing in for *those*, and the dialect singular verb after a plural subject (*them … has*).

7. Them *as* has, gets. (nonstandard)
8. The man *as* sold me the truck has left town. (nonstandard)

### but

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt/ &middot; *BUT* &middot; weak /bət/ — in this use it is unstressed and easy to miss
**Русский:** который не — устар. отрицательное относительное: no one but knows — нет никого, кто не знал бы
**Type:** negative relative pronoun, archaic and literary; elsewhere a coordinating conjunction or a preposition meaning *except* &middot; **Case:** none — it functions as the subject of its clause &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, proverbial. Read it; do not write it except in deliberate pastiche.
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** people or things, and only when the main clause is negative or near-negative — after *no one*, *not a*, *nobody*, *never*, *scarcely*, *few*
**Agreement:** as the subject of its clause it takes a verb agreeing with the antecedent: *there is no one but **knows** the story*; *there were few but **knew** the risk*.
**Pattern:** [negative expression] *but* [verb] … — *no man but knows* &middot; *there is / was no* [noun] *but* [verb] &middot; the modern rewrite is *who does not* or *that does not*
**Contrast:** It means "**who … not**" or "**that … not**" all by itself — the negative is built into the word, which is why the clause that follows contains no *not*. Two negatives are therefore correct here and only look wrong: *There is no one but knows the story* means *There is no one who does **not** know the story*, that is, everybody knows it. Adding a second negative (✗ *no one but doesn't know*) reverses the meaning by accident. Keep it apart from the coordinating conjunction *but* (*small but sturdy*), from the preposition *but* meaning *except* (*everyone but me*, with the object case), from the fixed *but that* (*I don't doubt but that he'll come*), and — the one that actually gets miscounted — from the **conjunction** *but* after *so*: in *There was never a sermon so short but someone complained*, *but* is followed by its own subject (*someone*) and means "that … not," so nothing is left for a pronoun to do. The test is the one that separates relative *that* from complementizer *that*: the relative *but* **is** the subject of its clause, so the clause has no other subject. Put a noun or pronoun after it and you have left this entry — *no one but **her** knows the whole story* is the preposition *but* meaning *except*: *her* is its object, and the subject of *knows* is the whole phrase *no one but her*. One case note while we are here, since this is the entry where it bites: after prepositional *but* the **object** form is the ordinary American choice — *everyone but **me***, *nobody but **him***, *no one but **her*** — and the older *no one but **she** knows*, in which the pronoun copies the case of *no one* because *but* is being read as a conjunction, is a formal variant rather than an error. What is not a variant is the coordination rule the opening section of this file is built on: *but* is a preposition here, so ✗ *everyone but my wife and I* is the same hypercorrection as ✗ *between you and I*, and the deletion test settles it — *everyone but **me*** → *everyone but my wife and **me***.

The negative relative: a single word carrying both the pronoun and the *not*, standard in English through the nineteenth century and now confined to proverbs and period prose.

1. There is scarcely a family in the county *but* has lost someone to the mine.
2. No one hears that recording *but* wonders how it survived at all.
3. Not a man among them *but* would have gone back for her.
4. There was not a window in the old schoolhouse *but* was broken.
5. Few of his students *but* remembered that lecture forty years later.
6. Scarcely a week passes *but* brings another complaint from the neighbors.

### q.v. (quod vide) / which see

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkju ˈvi/ &middot; *kyoo-VEE* &middot; the abbreviation is read out as its two letter names and never as the Latin behind it. The unabbreviated *quod vide* is /kwɑd ˈwiˌdeɪ/ in the restored classical pronunciation and /kwɑd ˈvaɪdi/ *kwod-VY-dee* in the traditional English pronunciation of Latin, which is what an older American lawyer or librarian says. The English gloss *which see* is /ˌwɪtʃ ˈsi/ *wich-SEE*, and it does have a weak form: *which* is unstressed in the phrase and reduces to /wətʃ/, giving /wətʃ ˈsi/ with every bit of the stress on *see*. The plural *qq.v.* is /ˌkju ˌkju ˈvi/. The abbreviation itself has no weak form, since it is spelled out letter by letter.
**Русский:** см. там же — книжн. отсылка к статье в той же книге; по-русски просто «см.»
**Type:** a relative clause frozen into a cross-reference formula — *which* is the **object** of the imperative *see*, fronted ahead of its own verb, so the two words together mean "see this one." *Q.v.* abbreviates Latin *quod vide*, in which *quod* is the same relative pronoun standing in the accusative. Style manuals file it with the Latin reference tags; it is entered here because the word inside it is a genuine relative pronoun with its antecedent sitting in the sentence &middot; **Case:** object — *which* is what *see* takes, which is the whole grammatical point of the phrase and the reason the Latin uses accusative *quod* rather than nominative *quid* &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly, and dated. Its home is the reference work — encyclopedias, dictionaries, gazetteers, legal and scientific treatises, and the scholarly editions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, where an American student still meets it constantly. American editing now prefers plain *see* with a page number, or a link; *which see* written out reads as deliberate antiquing.
**Person / number:** invariant, third person, with no number of its own. Latin marks the plural — *qq.v.*, for *quae vide*, when two or more terms are being pointed at — but the English *which see* never changes shape: *the hen harrier and the marsh harrier, which see*.
**Refers to:** the term just named, of whatever kind — a word, a person, a place, a doctrine, a species, a movement. The antecedent is always the noun phrase immediately in front of it, never a whole clause (that is ordinary sentential [which](#which)) and never the reader being addressed. And it points **inside the same work**: *q.v.* is a promise that this book has an entry of its own for the term.
**Agreement:** none whatever. *Which* is an object here, so it never controls a verb, and the verb it belongs to is an imperative, which does not inflect for person or number. *Which see* stays *which see* after a singular antecedent, a plural one, or two of them at once — never ✗ *which sees*, never ✗ *which are seen*.
**Pattern:** [term] (*q.v.*) — the standard shape: parentheses, immediately after the term, no comma inside &middot; [term], *q.v.* — unparenthesized, after a comma, closing the sentence &middot; [term] *and* [term] (*qq.v.*) — the plural, for two cross-references at once &middot; [term], *which see* — the English gloss written out &middot; *see* [term] — the modern rewrite &middot; never ✗ separated from its antecedent by intervening words &middot; never ✗ followed by a comma when it stands inside parentheses
**Contrast:** Against *cf.* (Latin *confer*, "compare"): *cf.* sends you to something that **differs** from what you have just read and asks you to notice the difference, while *q.v.* tells you that the work in your hands **has its own entry** for the term just named. Against *ibid.* and *op. cit.*, which point back at a source already cited rather than sideways at an entry in the same book. Against ordinary relative [which](#which): the pronoun and its object function are identical, but everything around it is frozen — the verb is fixed in the imperative, nothing can be added to the clause, and no substitution is available, so ✗ *that see* and ✗ *whom see* are impossible and the zero relative is impossible too, the pronoun being the only thing doing the pointing. Against the entry in the conjunction dictionary: same phrase, different question — [quod vide](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#quod-vide) covers the punctuation and the company it keeps (*i.e.*, *e.g.*, *cf.*, *n.b.*), while this entry covers the relative pronoun inside it.
**Variants:** *qq.v.* (plural, from Latin *quae vide*); the unabbreviated *quod vide*; the English calque *which see*; and the plain modern instructions *see*, *see also*, and *see under*. The *vide* of it — the imperative "see" — is the same verb that gives *vide supra* ("see above"), *vide infra* ("see below"), and *videlicet* (*viz.*, "namely").

A whole relative clause frozen into a cross-reference: *which* is the object, *see* is an imperative, and the two words together say "go look this one up in this same book." Latin wrote it *quod vide*, American reference works abbreviate it *q.v.*, and almost nobody spells the English out any more.

**1. The standard parenthetical.** Directly after the term, inside parentheses, with no comma between the term and the parenthesis and none after the abbreviation.

1. The doctrine descends from the old writ of assumpsit (*q.v.*).
2. Her early canvases owe everything to the Nabis (*q.v.*), and she never quite admitted it.
3. At a distance the bird is regularly mistaken for the northern harrier (*q.v.*).

**2. Unparenthesized, after a comma.** Older, slightly more literary, and it closes the sentence rather than sitting inside it.

4. On the history of the tenure, see the entry for seisin, *q.v.*
5. The whole argument turns on the older meaning of consideration, *q.v.*

**3. The plural qq.v.** Two or more cross-references pointed at in one breath.

6. The reader should consult the entries for estoppel and laches (*qq.v.*) before going further.

**4. The English gloss written out.** *Which see* — the calque, and the shape that makes the grammar visible: a relative pronoun standing as the object of an imperative verb.

7. The style begins with the Aesthetic Movement, *which see*, and ends in the poster art of the nineties. (dated)
8. Compare the entry Habeas Corpus, *which see*, for the procedural history. (dated)

**5. What an American editor writes instead.** The reason this is a reading item rather than a writing one.

9. The doctrine descends from the old writ of assumpsit; see "Assumpsit," page 214. (the modern rewrite of example 1)
10. See also the entries for estoppel and laches. (the modern rewrite of example 6)

### such as (fused relative)

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *SUCH-uhz* &middot; weak: *as* is /əz/ here and never the strong /æz/, since the stress sits on *such*; the fixed phrase runs together as /ˌsʌtʃ əz ɪt ˈɪz/ *such as it is*
**Русский:** те, кто; то, что — книжн. слитное относительное без антецедента: such as remain — те, кто остался
**Type:** fused relative — *such* supplies the antecedent and *as* does the relative work, so the two words together fill a noun slot &middot; **Case:** none; the phrase as a whole serves as subject, object, or complement, and *as* inside the clause is that clause's own subject or object &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and literary, archaic-leaning in speech. The productive use (*such as wish to attend*) is nineteenth-century in flavor; what survives in ordinary American English is the fixed concessive *such as it is / such as they are* and the drafting formula *such as may be required*.
**Person / number:** invariant, third person. Plural by default, since the fused antecedent is normally a set — *such as remain* = *those that remain*; singular only when the reference is to a mass or to a single thing.
**Refers to:** people and things alike, and always by **fused** reference: no antecedent stands anywhere in the sentence for the phrase to point back at, because *such* carries its own inside it. *Such as wish to be heard* = *those who wish to be heard*; *such as survive* = *the ones that survive*.
**Agreement:** the verb inside the clause is plural whenever the fused antecedent is a set of countable people or things — *such as **are** willing*, *such as **remain***, *such as **were** left behind* — and singular when it is a mass or a single item: *such as **is** needed*, *such as **was** left of the estate*. In the fixed phrase the verb follows the pronoun sitting inside it: *such as it **is***, *such as they **are***.
**Pattern:** *such as* [verb] … — fused subject: *such as remain* &middot; *such as* [subject] [verb] — fused object: *such as he could offer* &middot; [verb] *such as* [subject] [modal] [verb] — drafting: *furnish such as may be required* &middot; *such as it is*, *such as they are* — fixed, appended to a noun already given &middot; never ✗ *such as* [noun] in this sense: with a noun after it the phrase is the everyday "for example" one, and not a relative at all
**Contrast:** Against the everyday *such as* meaning "for example" — *a citrus fruit such as a lemon* — the test is what comes next. A noun phrase after it means "for example," and there is an antecedent sitting in front of it; a bare verb, or a subject and a verb, means the fused relative, and there is no antecedent anywhere in the sentence. Against [as](#as) in this file: plain relative *as* needs a noun for *such* to modify (*such help **as** he could give*), while in the fused version the noun is gone and *such* stands on its own (*such **as** he could give*). Against *those who* and *those that*: those are the modern equivalents, and unless the period flavor is the point they make the better sentence — *Such as wish to attend* is *Those who wish to attend*. Against the other fused relatives, [what](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative) and [whoever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever): the mechanism is identical and only the coverage differs — *what* fuses "the thing that," *whoever* fuses "anyone who," and *such as* fuses "those who" with an added sense of "as many of them as there are." And the legal *such* of *such defects* and *such party* is a **determiner**, since a noun has to follow it; it belongs to the determiner dictionary and is not this entry.
**Variants:** *such … as* with the noun left in (*such records as survive*) is the unfused parent construction and is treated at [as](#as); *such of … as* (*such of the records as survive*) is the partitive version of the same thing.

A fused relative assembled out of two ordinary words: *such* carries the antecedent, *as* does the relative work, and the pair stands where a whole noun phrase would.

**1. Fused subject of its own clause.** The commonest shape, and the one that sounds most like a proclamation.

1. *Such as* wish to be heard should sign the sheet by the door before nine. (formal)
2. *Such as* survive of the original letters are kept in the county archive.
3. He talked to *such as* would listen, and there were never many of them.

**2. Fused object of its own clause.** The gap is inside the clause rather than in front of it.

4. He gave *such as* he had and apologized for the rest.
5. She wrote down *such as* she could remember of the conversation.

**3. The fixed such as it is.** A concession hung on a noun already named — "for what little it is worth." The verb agrees with the pronoun inside the phrase, so a plural referent gives *such as they are*.

6. The furniture, *such as it is*, comes with the apartment.
7. Her savings, *such as they are*, won't cover one semester.

**4. In legal drafting.** Still written, and the reason a paralegal meets the construction at all.

8. The contractor shall replace *such as* are found defective within thirty days. (drafting)

### than whom

**Pronunciation:** /ðæn ˈhum/ &middot; *than-HOOM* &middot; weak /ðən ˈhum/ — *than* is unstressed in this phrase and reduces to /ðən/ or to syllabic /ðn̩/, and *whom* drops its /h/ at speed, giving /ðən ˈum/. The full /ðæn/ turns up only when someone is reading the phrase aloud as a curiosity.
**Русский:** лучше которого никто — устар. книжн. оборот превосходства о человеке
**Type:** fossilized preposition + relative pronoun — the one construction in modern English where *than* is unmistakably a preposition taking an object &middot; **Case:** object of *than* &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, and frankly archaic. Milton and Johnson wrote it; an American meets it today in tributes, award citations, obituaries, and jokes about tributes, award citations, and obituaries.
**Person / number:** invariant; people only. The antecedent may be singular or plural — *a scholar than whom*, *two editors than whom* — and *whom* does not change either way.
**Refers to:** a human antecedent just named and now being ranked against everyone else in its class. The clause always carries a comparison, and almost always a negative that turns the comparison into a superlative: *than whom **no one** is better qualified*. The inanimate counterpart is *than which* (see Variants); otherwise things take an ordinary comparative — *a tower higher than any other*, not a relative clause.
**Agreement:** none of its own. As the object of *than* it never controls a verb; the verb of the clause agrees with that clause's own subject, which is normally the negative quantifier — *a scholar than whom **none was** more careful*, *two editors than whom **none were** stricter*.
**Pattern:** [person noun], *than whom* [*no one* / *nobody* / *none* / *few*] [verb] [comparative], [rest of the main clause] &middot; [person noun], *than whom* [subject] [verb] [*no one* / *nothing*] *more*, … &middot; the inanimate twin: [noun], *than which* [*nothing* / *few*] [verb] [comparative], … &middot; commas on both sides, always — the clause is supplementary and can never be restrictive &middot; never ✗ *than who* &middot; never as a free relative: ✗ *than whom I spoke to*
**Contrast:** This is the sentence that keeps the *than* argument alive. Read as a **conjunction**, *than* introduces a clause and a pronoun after it takes subject case — *He is taller than **I** am*, and elliptically *than **I***. Read as a **preposition**, it takes an object — *He is taller than **me***, which is ordinary standard American English and not an error. *Than whom* exists only on the prepositional reading: nobody has ever written ✗ *than who*, which is why the phrase gets quoted in every argument about the word. Against [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom): those frames are living, productive, and neutral in register, and they will take any clause you put after them; *than whom* is one frozen shape with the comparison built into it and no other use. Against the plain modern sentence: *a scholar than whom none was more careful* is *a scholar more careful than anyone else*, and outside deliberate formality the plain version is the better one.
**Variants:** *than which* — the same fossil with an inanimate antecedent (*a doctrine than which nothing was more contested*), rarer still, and the only way the construction will accept a thing.

The last relative use of *than*: a fossil that ranks the person just named above everyone else in the field, in two words and a pair of commas.

**1. Ranking a person above all others.** The core use, and the reason the phrase survives at all.

1. The chair called on Dr. Whitfield, *than whom* nobody in the room had read the file more closely.
2. She learned the trade from an old pressman, *than whom* no one alive knew more about lead type.

**2. In a tribute or a citation.** Award programs, endowed chairs, and eulogies keep it in print.

3. The prize goes to Ellen Park, *than whom* this department has produced no finer teacher.
4. He dedicated the book to his mother, *than whom* he had known no more patient reader.

**3. The inanimate variant than which.** Same frame, different antecedent, and rarer still.

5. The bridge, *than which* few structures in the state have aged better, reopened last spring.
6. They cut the one paragraph, *than which* nothing in the draft had cost more argument.

**4. As deliberate archaism.** Modern writers reach for it when they want the eighteenth century in the room, or when they are joking.

7. He signed off as "your obedient servant, *than whom* none is more obedient," and thought it was very funny.
8. Readers usually meet the construction in Milton, where Beelzebub is the one *than whom*, Satan excepted, none higher sat.

### the which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh-WICH* &middot; weak /ðə wətʃ/
**Русский:** который — устар. форма which с артиклем, из Библии короля Якова и Шекспира
**Type:** archaic relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** archaic — common from Middle English through the seventeenth century, then gone. It survives in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, and in deliberate antiquing.
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** things chiefly, and people in the older texts, from the period when plain *which* could still take a human antecedent (*Our Father which art in heaven*)
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the antecedent, exactly as with plain *which*
**Pattern:** [noun] *the which* [verb] … &middot; [noun] [preposition] *the which* [subject] [verb] — *by the which he was condemned*
**Contrast:** Nothing separates it from plain [which](#which) except four hundred years. The article is a leftover from a period when *which* still patterned like a noun and could take a determiner in front of it — the same instinct that produced the determiner *the which* (*the which cause*). It is a reading item, not a writing item, and using it in modern prose reads as costume.

The medieval definite-article form of *which*, kept alive only by the texts that were written before it died.

1. He left her a farm, *the which* she sold within the year.
2. They came at last to a river, *the which* they could not cross before nightfall.
3. She spoke of a promise, *the which* he had long since forgotten.
4. It was a law of long standing, by *the which* many a family had been ruined.
5. He carried a letter, *the which* he would show to no one.
6. There stood a chapel, in *the which* the family had been buried for three centuries.

### them that / them as / them what

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛm ˈðæt/, /ˌðɛm ˈæz/, /ˌðɛm ˈwʌt/ &middot; *them-THAT, them-AZ, them-WUT* &middot; the weak forms are what a listener actually gets, because the whole phrase is unstressed machinery sitting in front of the verb that carries the point: *them* reduces to /ðəm/ and after a consonant to /əm/, and the relative reduces with it, giving /ðəm ðət/, /ðəm əz/, /ðəm wət/. *Them that has, gets* comes out /ðəm ət ˈhæz ˈɡɛts/ at speed, which is why dialect writing spells it *them 'at has*. Do not read the reduction as the dialect: the /əm/ of *I saw 'em* is ordinary General American and marks nothing at all.
**Русский:** те, кто — прост., диал. вместо those who; в роли подлежащего нестандартно
**Type:** fused-relative frame, **nonstandard** in its main use — the dialect demonstrative *them* standing in for *those* as the head, with a relative pronoun hung on it; the three members differ only in which relative fills the slot, and all three mean *those who* &middot; **Case:** the head *them* is historically the **object** form of *they*, and the dialect use puts it in subject position as well (*Them that has, gets*); the relative itself has no case &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** as a subject, and the region differs by member. ***Them that*** is the widest — Southern and South Midland American, Appalachian, and rural and working-class speech generally, plus African American English; it is the version an American is most likely to hear spoken. ***Them as*** carries the dialect relative *as*, which belongs to the American South and Midland and, in Britain, to northern England, the English Midlands, Scotland, and Ireland; an American now meets it mostly in print — Hardy, George Eliot, Lawrence — and in older rural speech. ***Them what*** is the most heavily marked of the three, the stock form of Cockney and southern English dialect writing (Dickens, the music hall) and of American dialect writing after it (Twain, Faulkner). In the **object** slot the same string is not dialect at all: see the Contrast.
**Person / number:** third person plural, and generic rather than pointing at anyone in particular. The singular members of the same frame — *him that*, *her that*, *he that* — are archaic rather than regional.
**Refers to:** people, almost always: an open class of them, whoever does or has what the relative clause says. Things turn up occasionally (*them that's left over go back in the box*), but the frame is built for persons. It is never used for one identified individual; for that these same varieties say *the one that*.
**Agreement:** two things are going on at once and only one of them belongs to the pronoun. The relative clause normally takes the *-s* form of the verb whatever the number — *them that **has***, *them as **knows***, *them what **wants*** — which is dialect verb agreement (the Northern Subject Rule and its American descendants) riding along with the pronoun, not a fact about *them*. The main clause follows the same varieties' habits: *Them that comes late **gets** nothing.* The standard equivalent is plural throughout: *those who **have***, *those who **come** late **get** nothing*.
**Pattern:** *them that* / *them as* / *them what* [verb] … + [main verb] — the fused subject, and the marked use &middot; [verb or preposition] *them that* [verb] — the object slot: *for them that mourn* &middot; *them that's* — with *is* or *has* contracted onto the relative &middot; the standard rewrites: *those who*, *those that*, *the ones who*, *whoever* &middot; never ✗ *them* + [noun] in this entry: put a noun after it (*them apples*, *them boxes*) and *them* is a **determiner**, which belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them) and not here
**Contrast:** Three separate things get run together here, and they come apart cleanly. **Position decides the label.** *Them that* in the **object** slot — *the Lord pitieth them that fear him*, *a fund for them that lost everything* — is the object case doing exactly what an object case should, and it is period-standard English rather than dialect: it is the oblique member of the old *he that* / *they that* frame, ordinary through the seventeenth century, preserved in the King James Bible, and dated but never regional today. *Them that* in the **subject** slot (*Them that has, gets*) puts an object form where a subject belongs, and that is the nonstandard part — the same shape of thing as ✗ *Me and him went*, and marked for the same reason. **The head is not the relative.** What is nonstandard in *them that* is *them*, standing in for *those*; the *that* is the ordinary standard relative of this whole file. In *them as* and *them what* both halves are marked, since [as](#as) and [what](#what-nonstandard-relative) are themselves dialect relatives, which is why those two sound heavier. **The pronunciation is not the use.** Reduced /əm/ is standard American speech; demonstrative *them* is not. Against *those who* and *those that*: those are the standard equivalents in every register, and they are what an editor will write. Against [he who, he that, she who, they that](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#he-who-he-that-she-who-they-that): the identical frame with a nominative head, archaic rather than dialect. Against *whoever*: the living one-word equivalent — *Them that wants seconds* is *Whoever wants seconds*.
**Variants:** *them 'at* (eye-dialect spelling of the reduced *that*); *them as what* and *them what has* with a doubled relative, in the broadest varieties; the singular *him that* and *her that*; and the standard and archaic members of the same frame — *he that*, *they that*, *those that*, *those who* — treated at [he that](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#he-that) and [those who](04-demonstrative.md#those-who).

*Those who*, with the dialect demonstrative *them* as the head and any of three relatives hung on it. One frame, three fillers, and an honest split running through it: in the object slot it is period-standard English, in the subject slot it is dialect. Like most dialect material it does not travel alone, and the examples below carry the nonstandard *-s* verb and, in one case, negative concord along with the pronoun; each is labeled.

**1. them that — the fused subject.** The commonest of the three, and the one an American will actually hear.

1. *Them that* has, gets. (nonstandard — proverbial; the dialect *-s* verb rides along with the pronoun)
2. *Them that* come late don't get fed. (nonstandard)
3. *Them that* wrote the rules never had to live under them. (nonstandard)

**2. them as — Southern and Midland American, and northern British.** The relative *as* doing the work *that* does elsewhere.

4. *Them as* knows, knows. (nonstandard)
5. *Them as* wants seconds can go around again. (nonstandard)

**3. them what — the heaviest marked of the three.** Cockney and southern English dialect writing above all, and American dialect writing after it.

6. *Them what* pays the piper calls the tune. (nonstandard)
7. *Them what* ain't got nothing got nothing to lose. (nonstandard — negative concord riding along: *ain't … nothing* is a single negation in these varieties, not two)

**4. In the object slot, where it is not dialect at all.** After a verb or a preposition the head is simply in the case the sentence calls for. This is the archaic-but-standard use, and the King James Bible is where an American meets it.

8. "The LORD pitieth *them that* fear him." (Psalm 103:13, King James Version — object of *pitieth*, and standard for its period)
9. "Remember *them that* are in bonds, as bound with them." (Hebrews 13:3, King James Version)
10. The fund was set up for *them that* had lost everything in the fire. (dated in flavor, but the case is ordinary)

**5. Quoted by standard speakers.** The proverb travels much further than the dialect does, and people who would never say *them* for *those* produce this one intact.

11. "*Them that* has, gets" — which is the whole theory of the tax code in four words. (quoting the proverb)

### what (nonstandard relative)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/, also /wɑt/ &middot; *WUT* &middot; weak /wət/ — unstressed and usually reduced in this use
**Русский:** который — прост., диал. вместо that: the man what owns the place; в грамотной речи недопустимо
**Type:** relative pronoun, **nonstandard** &middot; **Case:** none — subject or object of its clause &middot; **Register:** nonstandard. Regional and working-class speech — Southern, Appalachian, and other rural American varieties; in Britain, Cockney, East Anglian, and other southern English ones — together with the dialect writing that records it (Twain, Faulkner, Cockney and Southern dialogue). Never correct in edited prose.
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** people and things alike, exactly like the *that* it replaces
**Agreement:** as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent: *the man what **owns** the place*; and in the dialects that use it, nonstandard agreement often comes along with it (*the men what owns the place*)
**Pattern:** [noun] *what* [verb] … &middot; [noun] *what* [subject] [verb] … &middot; in the broadest varieties, doubled with a resumptive pronoun: *the man what I told you about him*
**Contrast:** Do not confuse it with the **fused relative** *what*, which is completely standard: *I heard what you said* means *the thing that you said*, and *what* carries its own antecedent inside it. The tell is simple — the nonstandard relative has an antecedent sitting right in front of it (*the thing what you said*), and the fused relative has none. Standard English uses *that*, *which*, *who*, or nothing in the slot this dialect *what* fills; the dialect uses one word for all four, which is why it is so easy to understand and so unmistakably marked.

The all-purpose relative of several English dialects, included here so a reader recognizes it on the page and knows what it is doing. Dialect rarely arrives one feature at a time, and four of the examples below carry a second marked form along with the relative: *them boxes* is the nonstandard demonstrative [them](04-demonstrative.md#them) for *those*, *he done it* is the dialect past tense of *do*, *I told you about him* leaves a **resumptive** pronoun sitting in the gap the relative was supposed to empty, and *ain't nobody … nothing* is [negative concord](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord) — the double negative, in which each further negative agrees with the first instead of canceling it, systematic in Southern, Appalachian, and African American English and nonstandard in edited American prose. All four are labeled, and none of them is a typo.

**1. Subject of its own clause.** The commonest shape by far.

1. The fella *what* fixed my truck moved to Tulsa. (nonstandard)
2. That's the woman *what* called about the dog. (nonstandard)
3. It was my own brother *what* told them where to look. (nonstandard)
4. Every man *what* worked that shift got sick inside a week. (nonstandard)

**2. Object of its own clause.** The same one word, doing the job standard English splits between *that*, *which*, *whom*, and nothing.

5. The truck *what* he sold me broke down inside a month. (nonstandard — *what* is the object of *sold*)
6. The only thing *what* she kept was her mother's ring. (nonstandard)

**3. Carrying another dialect feature along.**

7. Them boxes *what* came yesterday are still sitting on the porch. (nonstandard — *them* for *those*)
8. He's the one *what* done it, and everybody knew it. (nonstandard — *done* for *did*)
9. There ain't nobody around here *what* knows nothing about it. (nonstandard — negative concord: *ain't … nobody … nothing* is a single negation in these varieties, not three)

**4. With a resumptive pronoun.** The broadest varieties fill the gap back in instead of leaving it open.

10. That's the man *what* I told you about him. (nonstandard)

### whereupon, whereon, whereto, wherewith, whereat, whereunto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrəˈpɑn/, /wɛrˈɑn/, /wɛrˈtu/, /wɛrˈwɪθ/, /wɛrˈæt/ &middot; *wair-uh-PON, wair-ON, wair-TOO, wair-WITH, wair-AT* &middot; and *whereunto* /ˌwɛrənˈtu/, *wair-un-TOO*. The stress falls on the preposition in every one of them, never on *where-*, and *wherewith* is heard with either /θ/ or /ð/. Weak forms: the first syllable reduces in running speech to /wɚ/ — *whereupon* /ˌwɚəˈpɑn/, *whereat* /wɚˈæt/ — which is why these words slide past a listener when a clerk is reading minutes aloud.
**Русский:** на котором, к которому, чем — устар., юр. сращения «where- + предлог»; whereupon «после чего»
**Type:** relative pro-adverbs — *where-* stands in for the relative and the welded preposition governs it, so each word is exactly one pied-piped preposition plus *which*: *whereon* = *on which*, *whereto* = *to which*, *wherewith* = *with which*, *whereat* = *at which*, *whereunto* = *unto which*, *whereupon* = *upon which*. Grammars are divided over whether to file them as pronouns or as adverbs; they are entered here because they fill exactly the slot [by which, in which, on which](#by-which-in-which-on-which) fills, and each one's full treatment as a connector is in the conjunction dictionary &middot; **Case:** none — the *where-* element is the object of the attached preposition, but nothing inflects &middot; **Register:** archaic to legal, and it differs word by word. ***Whereupon*** is the live one: "and immediately after that," ordinary in court transcripts, board minutes, and formal narrative, and the only member an American is likely to write. ***Whereat*** is archaic-literary, the reaction word of nineteenth-century fiction. ***Whereon***, ***whereto***, and ***wherewith*** are archaic outside deeds, wills, and scriptural echo — the King James Bible of 1611 and the prose written in imitation of it. ***Whereunto*** is the deadest of the six; the *-unto* shape survives in American English only in its demonstrative cousin *hereunto*, still typed into signature blocks (*have hereunto set their hands*).
**Person / number:** invariant. No person, no number, no case, nothing to inflect.
**Refers to:** things, places, documents, and abstractions — and, for *whereupon* and *whereat*, a whole preceding clause together with the event it names. **Never a person** — ✗ *the man whereto I wrote*; people take *to whom*. In drafting the antecedent is normally a document or one of its parts — *the schedule whereto the exhibits are attached*.
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb agrees with the subject of the clause the word introduces — *the terms whereon the parties **have** agreed*, *the table whereon the papers **lie***. With *whereupon* the antecedent is the whole preceding clause, and the clause that follows brings its own subject: *…, whereupon the meeting **adjourned***.
**Pattern:** [noun] *whereon* / *whereto* / *wherewith* / *whereunto* [subject] [verb] — *the rock whereon it stands* &middot; [noun] *wherewith* / *whereon* *to* [verb] — *no money wherewith to pay* &middot; [clause], *whereupon* [subject] [verb] — the living use, and it always takes a comma in front of it &middot; [clause], *whereat* [subject] [verb] — the reaction frame &middot; the modern equivalents: *on which*, *to which*, *with which*, *at which*, *upon which*, and the stranded version at the end of the clause (*the rock it stands on*) &middot; never ✗ with a human antecedent &middot; never ✗ opening a sentence — every one of these points back at something already on the page
**Contrast:** Against [by which, in which, on which](#by-which-in-which-on-which): identical in meaning, opposite in register — *the shelf on which it stood* is ordinary formal English, *the shelf whereon it stood* is costume. Against the *here-* and *there-* families: all three are built the same way and used differently — *where-* relativizes and hooks a clause on, *hereto* and *hereunder* point at the document being signed, *thereon* and *thereto* point back at something already named, and it is the *there-* words, not these, that American lawyers still write every day (see [thereon](10-archaic-dialectal.md#thereon), [thereto](10-archaic-dialectal.md#thereto)). Against *wherefore*: not a member of this set at all — it means "for which reason," and Juliet's *wherefore art thou Romeo* asks why, not where. Against the two-word *where at* of *Where's he at?*: that is the dialect question word and has nothing to do with this entry (see [where at](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#where-at)).
**Variants:** the rest of the family is built on the same pattern — *wherein* (= *in which*), *whereby* (= *by which*), *whereof* (= *of which*), and the rarer *wherefrom* and *whereinto*; the three that stand in for a noun phrase have their own entries at [wherein](10-archaic-dialectal.md#wherein), [whereby](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereby), and [whereof](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereof). *Whereto* and *whereunto* are the same word, *unto* being the older and heavier *to*. *Wherewith* survives in ordinary American English only as the noun it fathered, *wherewithal* — *the wherewithal to pay*.

Six survivals of a pattern that was once general: *where-* welded onto a preposition to make a single word meaning "[preposition] which." Five of them are words to read rather than to write. *Whereupon* is still in use.

**1. whereupon — immediate sequence.** The living member, and the one to punctuate carefully: it is a relative, not a conjunctive adverb, so it takes a comma before it and can never begin a sentence.

1. The witness refused a third time, *whereupon* the judge cleared the courtroom.
2. She named a figure, *whereupon* the whole table went quiet.

**2. whereat — the reaction.** Archaic-literary: *at which point*, with the sense that somebody responded to what just happened.

3. The chairman ruled her out of order, *whereat* half the room stood up and left. (archaic)

**3. whereon and whereto — place, and destination.** Deeds, wills, and land descriptions, plus the scriptural echo.

4. The land *whereon* the mill stood has been a parking lot since 1961. (archaic, legal)
5. Exhibit C is the schedule *whereto* the easement description is attached. (archaic, legal)

**4. wherewith — the means, and usually the missing means.** Nearly always followed by *to* and an infinitive.

6. They were left with nothing *wherewith* to pay the workmen. (archaic)
7. He had the plans for the house but never the money *wherewith* to build it. (archaic)

**5. whereunto — the King James register.** A purpose, an office, or a calling that something is directed toward.

8. He was ordained to the ministry *whereunto* he believed himself called. (archaic)
9. The trust names the purposes *whereunto* its income may be applied. (archaic, legal)
