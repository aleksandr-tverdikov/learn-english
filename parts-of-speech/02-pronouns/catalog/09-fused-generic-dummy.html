Fused relatives, generic pronouns, and dummy pronouns#

The pronouns that point at nobody in particular — the ones that swallow their own antecedent, the ones that stand for people in general, and the ones that fill a subject slot English refuses to leave empty.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Every other pronoun in this dictionary works by pointing back at a noun phrase already in the sentence. The three groups collected here do not. A fused relative — what, whoever, whichever — contains its own antecedent, so what I need already means "the thing that I need" and there is nothing left for it to point at. A generic pronoun — one, you, they, we, and the dated generic he — stands for people in general, with the speaker included or excluded as the occasion requires. A dummy pronoun — it, there — refers to nothing whatsoever and is present only because an English finite clause must have a subject.

Two rules cover most of the errors in the file. In a fused relative, the case comes from the inside clause, not the outside one: Give it to whoever answers is right because whoever is the subject of answers, no matter what preposition stands in front of the clause — ✗ to whomever answers is a hypercorrection, and it is now more common than the error it was meant to prevent. With a dummy, the verb agrees with what follows, not with the dummy itself: there is a problem, there are problems — except in the it-cleft, where the copula stays singular however plural the focus (It was the neighbors who complained).

The third theme is register. Generic you is what Americans actually say; generic one is formal and tips easily into stiffness; whom, whomever, and the whole -soever family survive mainly in contracts and scripture. This file says plainly which forms are alive, which are museum pieces, and which will get marked by a conservative editor even though everyone says them.

Case, once, for the whole file. A pronoun takes the case its own slot requires, and the thing that most often disguises the slot is a coordination. The fix is the deletion test: take the other person out and read what is left. He gave it to Bob and me → He gave it to me ✓ · between you and me → between … me ✓ · She and I went → She went, I went ✓. It catches the error in both directions. The plain error is ✗ Me and him went → ✗ Me went, ✗ Him went, so the repair is He and I went — and note that the case is what is wrong there, not the order. The hypercorrections run the other way: ✗ between you and I → ✗ between … I, and ✗ They invited my wife and I → ✗ They invited … I, which is the one that survives a proofread, because there is no preposition in the sentence to warn the writer — a coordinated direct object takes the object form for exactly the same reason a prepositional object does. Two things this file adds to that. After the copula, It's me is standard American English and is not an error to be repaired into It is I (see it (cleft)). And in a fused relative the case is read off the inner clause and never off the preposition standing in front of it (see whoever). The full table and the rest of the coordination cases are in Case: the rule, the test, and where Americans actually break it.

Agreement, once, for the whole file. Six facts, and everything below follows them. A subject what-clause and every -ever and -soever form take a singular verb — What matters is the deadline, Whichever comes first is fine, Whoever wants seconds should say so. The one fused relative that goes the other way is as many as, which is plural — As many as want to come are welcome (see as many as). Generic one takes a singular verb (one is, one has), and so does the dated generic he; generic you, we, and they take the plural forms (you are, we were, they say). Dummy it is always singular in every one of its jobs, including the cleft, where the copula stays singular however plural the focus (It was the neighbors who complained). Existential there has no number of its own and takes it from the noun that follows (There is a problem, There are problems). The indefinite pronouns that raise the other agreement questions are not in this file, and the short version of each is: everyone, somebody, and nobody take a singular verb (with singular they standard for the follow-up pronoun); each is singular; either and neither are singular in careful use, though plural verbs after them are common in speech; both, several, and a few are always plural; none takes either number — singular with a mass referent, and with a plural count set the plural is older, commoner, and the ordinary default, the "none means not one" rule being an eighteenth-century invention; and all, some, and most agree with whatever they refer to. They are entered in indefinite compounds and quantifying indefinites.

Where this file stands on disputed usage. Five things stated plainly, so that nothing below reads as a rule it is not. It's me is standard — the object form after be has been ordinary English for centuries; It is I is defensible grammar and elevated register, not a correction. Whom and whomever survive mainly right after a preposition — to whom, for whom, to whomever it may concern — and everywhere else American English has handed the object slot to who and whoever; Invite whoever you want is a register choice, not an error (see whom). Singular they is standard American English — entered in Merriam-Webster, accepted by the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style, and attested since the fourteenth century — so Whoever left this should come get their coat needs no repair. That versus restrictive which is an editing convention, not grammar: the one hard rule in that neighborhood is that that cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause (see which). The antecedent rules underneath all of that are settled and are not in dispute: who is for people, which is for things, that is for both (the woman that called, the report that arrived), and whose is for both as well — a book whose cover was torn has been ordinary English since Middle English, and the objection to it is a superstition rather than a rule (see who, which, that, whose). What this file does not soften: ✗ to whomever answers the phone, ✗ the thing what I need, ✗ Whatever are left, ✗ Is raining with no subject, ✗ There's two things in edited prose, ✗ between you and I, and ✗ Me and him went are all still marked wrong in edited American English. Being honest about what has changed is not permission to pretend that nothing is an error.

The nonstandard neighbors, labeled. Five forms stand next to the pronouns in this file, and every one of them is labeled here rather than left to be guessed at. Relative what — the man what fixed the roof — is regular in London and other British vernaculars and in Southern, Appalachian, and rural American speech, where it is rule-governed; it is nonstandard in writing, and its entry is what (nonstandard relative). The vernacular reflexives hisself and theirselves are nonstandard — general in Southern, Appalachian, and rural white vernaculars and in African American English — and are in fact more regular than the standard himself and themselves they replace; see hisself and theirselves. Demonstrative them — them apples, them boys — is nonstandard for those, widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech, and it is a determiner rather than a pronoun, so it belongs to the determiner dictionary; keep it separate from the reduced pronunciation /əm/ of I saw 'em, which is ordinary General American and carries no dialect marking at all. Plural youse is nonstandard — New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration (youse) — and it stands beside y'all (Southern and African American English, standard within those varieties), you'uns and yinz (nonstandard; Appalachia and Pittsburgh), and you guys (national, informal rather than nonstandard); all of them are address forms, not generics. The double negative — There ain't nobody home, they never told nobody — is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernaculars, and was ordinary English in Chaucer and Shakespeare, but it is nonstandard in edited American English, where the repair is one negative per clause: There isn't anybody home. See nothing (negative concord).

Fused relatives#

A fused relative — also called a free relative or a nominal relative — is a wh- word doing two jobs at once: it is the relative pronoun and its own antecedent. The whole clause it opens fills a noun slot, so it can be a subject, an object, or a complement. The test is substitution: if the thing(s) that, anyone who, or any one that can be dropped in, the word is a fused relative.

Because the antecedent is already inside the word, it must never be propped on a second one: ✗ the thing what I need, ✗ anyone whoever wants it. And because these clauses sit inside larger sentences, they raise the case question that gives the group its reputation — resolve it by looking inward, at the job the pronoun does in its own clause.

The -ever forms have a second life as concessives, meaning "no matter what / who / which." Those sit outside the main clause and are set off by a comma. The -soever forms are emphatic archaisms, alive today only in legal boilerplate, hymnody, and the King James Bible.

One older construction is entered with this group although nothing about it is fused: he who, he that, she who, they that, where the antecedent and the relative are still two separate words drawing their case from two different clauses — give it to him who asks. It survives in proverbs, scripture, and deliberate archaism, and whoever is the word that replaced it.

A second construction is entered with this group on the same footing: as many as, the old plural fused relative meaning "all those that" — as many as want to come are welcome. It is the one member of the family that takes a plural verb, and it has to be kept apart from the as many as that counts (as many as forty flights were canceled), which stands in front of a numeral and is not a pronoun at all.

Pronunciation
/ˌæz ˈmɛni əz/ · az-MEN-ee-uz · both instances of as are ordinarily weak /əz/, so the phrase runs to [əz ˈmɛni əz] with the only real accent on man-; the first as recovers its full /æz/ only under contrastive stress. many keeps its /n/ in every register — there is no reduction to ✗ [ˈmɛi]
Русский

Type
fused relative pronoun — the plural member of the group
Case
none — one invariant form for subject and object
Register
split. The "all those who" use is archaic, biblical, and formal, and an American meets it in scripture, in older prose, and in contract boilerplate; the everyday take as many as you need is neutral and unremarkable
Person / number
third person, invariant in form, and plural for agreement — the only fused relative in this file that is.
Refers to
the whole set that satisfies the clause — people (as many as were willing) or things (as many as we could carry). Unlike whoever it is not restricted to people, and unlike whatever it is not restricted to things; what it does insist on is that the set be countable and plural. For a mass quantity the phrase is as much as.
Agreement
plural verb — As many as want to come are welcome, ✗ As many as wants to come is welcome. This is the single agreement exception in the fused-relative group, and it comes from many, which is plural by nature. Compare the singular whoever, which says the same thing one person at a time: Whoever wants to come is welcome.
Pattern
as many as [plural verb] — as subject, as many as were present signed it · [verb] as many as [subject] [verb] — as object, take as many as you need · [preposition] as many as [clause] · as many as [subject] want / like / need / can carry · the counting frame as many as [numeral] [noun] — not this pronoun
Contrast
two constructions share the spelling and only one of them is here. As a pronoun it fills a noun slot and means "all those that": As many as received the notice have already replied. As a degree modifier it stands in front of a number and means "up to, and surprisingly so": As many as forty people were turned away — nothing there is filling a noun slot, the numeral is, and that use belongs with the quantifiers. The test is substitution: if all those who or all the ones that drops in cleanly, it is the pronoun. Against whoever, the difference is number and range — whoever is singular and people only, as many as is plural and takes things as well. Against as much as, the difference is count against mass: as many as you need of chairs, as much as you need of coffee. Against such as (fused relative), which is the same archaic move made with such — such as were present is the older sibling of as many as were present. And put a noun straight behind it (as many as fifty applicants) and it has stopped being a pronoun altogether.
Variants
as many standing alone with the clause understood (take as many), the mass counterpart as much as, and the archaic sibling such as

'All those that' — the plural fused relative, and the one that takes a plural verb.

1. All those that (fused relative). The archaic core, alive in scripture and in formal drafting: As many as were willing came forward. 2. As many as you need. The everyday use, where the as-clause sets a limit instead of naming a set: Take as many as you want. 3. Not this word at all. As many as forty people is a degree modifier standing in front of a numeral, and no pronoun is involved.

  1. As many as want to come are welcome to come.
  2. Take as many as you need — there's a whole case of them in the back.
  3. As many as received the notice have already written back.
  4. We carried out as many as we could before the smoke got bad.
  5. The clause binds as many as sign it, and nobody else. (contract boilerplate, and the archaic use)
  6. As many as were present that evening still remember the noise.
  7. The headline said as many as forty flights were canceled — a count in front of a numeral, not a pronoun.

The w of who has been silent for centuries, so the word begins with a plain /h/
Русский
тот, кто; та, кто; те, кто — устар., книжн.; в новой прозе заменено на whoever
Type
antecedent pronoun plus relative pronoun — the two-word ancestor of the fused relative, entered here beside the forms that replaced it
Case
split — the antecedent takes the case of the outer clause (he, object him), the relative takes the case of the inner one (who, object whom)
Register
archaic; alive in proverbs, scripture, hymns, and deliberate formality. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in maxims, and in nineteenth-century prose, and essentially never in new writing
Person / number
third person; he who, she who, and he that are singular, they that and they who plural.
Refers to
a person defined by the clause that follows — "anyone who," "the one who." He who was the traditional generic and now reads as generic masculine, which is why edited American prose has replaced it with whoever, anyone who, or those who; she who names a woman, or is used deliberately as a rhetorical counterweight.
Agreement
the verb agrees with the antecedent pronoun: singular with he who, she who, and he that — He who hesitates is lost — and plural with they that and they who — They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. Older texts carry the archaic inflection inside the relative clause: he that hath ears, she that doeth good.
Pattern
he who [verb] … [main verb] · he that [verb] · [verb or preposition] him who [verb] — the split-case form, give it to him who asks · she who [verb] · they that [verb] · the frozen proverb frames He who hesitates…, He who laughs last…
Contrast
this is the construction whoever replaced, and setting them side by side shows what fusion bought. He who keeps the two case jobs in two separate words, so the outer clause can pull the antecedent into the object case while the relative stays a subject: Give it to him who asks — him is the object of to, who the subject of asks. Whoever has one word for both jobs and settles it on the inner clause alone: Give it to whoever asks, never ✗ to whomever asks. Against those who: that is the modern plural replacement and what an American editor now reaches for, since it carries no masculine default. Against that which: the same shape for things instead of people. He which was ordinary in 1611 (Our Father which art in heaven) and is a plain error today — for a person the relative is who or that. And the case inside the clause is still read off that clause: in he who I met, the relative is the object of met, so a frame this deliberately archaic wants he whom I met for consistency of register. Be exact about what that is — object who is standard American English everywhere else (see whom), so this is a register slip inside an antique construction, not a grammatical error. In ordinary English the whole thing is the man I met.
Variants
he that, she that, they who, him who, him that, and the single-word archaisms whoso and whosoever

'Anyone who' — the antecedent and the relative kept as two separate words, the way English said it before whoever fused them.

1. The proverb frame. Quoted whole rather than built fresh: He who hesitates is lost. 2. Scripture and hymn. He that hath ears to hear, let him hear. 3. Deliberate archaism. A modern writer reaching for weight or for a joke: she who must be obeyed. 4. The split-case form. him who, him that — antecedent in the object case, relative still a subject.

  1. He who hesitates is lost. (a frozen proverb, quoted rather than produced)
  2. He that hath ears to hear, let him hear. (King James Bible, with the archaic -th inside the clause)
  3. Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.
  4. The prize goes to him who finishes first. (split case: object of to, subject of finishes; modern English to whoever finishes first)
  5. She who must be obeyed has already decided where we are eating. (jocular archaism)
  6. They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. (plural antecedent, plural verb)
  7. In a new policy, write those who apply late, not he who applies late.

whoever, whomever, whosoever, he (generic), those who, that which, whoso

/wʌt/, also /wɑt/ · WUT · weak /wət/, and the t flaps into a following vowel, so what I need runs to [wəɾ aɪ ˈnid] and what's to [wəts]. Conservative and many Southern speakers keep an /hw-/ onset: /hwʌt/
Русский
то, что — содержит собственный антецедент; только о предметах, не о людях
Type
fused relative pronoun
Case
none — one invariant form for subject and object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person, invariant. The what-clause counts as singular for agreement.
Refers to
things, substances, ideas, events, and whole situations — never to a specific person. For people use whoever. The complement of a pseudo-cleft may still name people (What we need is more nurses), because what names the need, not the nurses.
Agreement
a subject what-clause takes a singular verb by default — What matters is the deadline, ✗ What matter is the deadline. In the pseudo-cleft the copula is singular by default too (What I need is a vacation); before a plural complement both What we need is more volunteers and What we need are more volunteers are current in educated American English and neither is worth correcting. The verb inside the clause goes plural only when the clause is itself plainly plural, which in practice means with a linking or raising verb in it: What appear to be errors are actually rounding differences.
Pattern
what [subject] [verb] — as clause subject · [verb] what [subject] [verb] — as object · [preposition] what [clause] · What [clause] is [phrase] — the pseudo-cleft · [phrase] is what [clause] — the reverse pseudo-cleft
Contrast
what means "the thing(s) that," so it must never sit on top of an antecedent: ✗ the thing what I need — write either the thing that I need or what I need, never both. That is exactly what separates it from relative that and which, which require an antecedent already standing in the sentence. Against interrogative what, the test is whether an answer is being requested: I know what he wants reports a thing, I asked what he wants embeds a question.
Nonstandard
what as a plain relative pronoun — the man what fixed the roof — is regular in London and other British vernaculars and in Southern, Appalachian, and rural American speech, and it is rule-governed there; it is not standard written English. Its entry is what (nonstandard relative).

'The thing that' — a noun phrase and its relative clause fused into a single word.

1. The thing(s) that. The core use: the clause names a thing without ever naming it. 2. The pseudo-cleft. Fronting a what-clause and parking the news at the end (What I need is coffee) is one of the main ways English moves emphasis, and it is why so many spoken sentences begin with What I'm saying is… or What happened was…

  1. What I need right now is about ten hours of sleep.
  2. She got exactly what she paid for.
  3. What happened at the meeting is still not clear to me.
  4. Just tell them what you told me.
  5. That is not what I meant, and you know it.
  6. He never spends what he earns.
  7. What worries the committee is the cost of the second phase.

fused relative pronoun, and concessive pronoun

Register
neutral; the standalone dismissive Whatever. is informal only
Person / number
third person, invariant, treated as singular.
Refers to
things and situations, with the range left open — "anything at all that." Not people (use whoever). In the concessive use its scope can be a whole clause.
Agreement
singular verb — Whatever is left goes in the fridge, ✗ Whatever are left. Even where the sense is plainly plural, the verb stays singular: Whatever remains is yours.

Contrast
two distinct jobs, and the comma tells them apart. As a fused relative it fills a noun slot: I'll eat whatever you cook (object of eat). As a concessive it is an adverbial sitting outside the main clause and paraphrasable as "no matter what": Whatever you cook, I'll eat it. Against whichever: whatever leaves the range open (take whatever you want — anything at all), whichever picks from a set already on the table (take whichever you want — one of these). Spaced what ever is a third thing entirely, an intensified question: What ever were you thinking? And the emphasizer in no evidence whatsoever is not this pronoun — nor, strictly, a determiner: it is a postposed negative-polarity emphasizer belonging to the noun phrase, which is why it stands after the noun and never in the determiner slot (see whatsoever (determiner)).

'Anything that,' with no limit put on the range.

1. Anything that (fused relative). Fills a noun slot in the sentence. 2. No matter what (concessive). An adverbial clause, normally fronted and followed by a comma. 3. Dismissal. Standalone Whatever. means "I decline to argue" — informal, and read as rude in most workplaces.

  1. Take whatever you need from the supply closet.
  2. Whatever she decides, the rest of us will go along with it.
  3. He believes whatever the last person told him.
  4. Whatever is left over goes home with you.
  5. I'll pay whatever it costs to get the car back on the road.
  6. They can call it whatever they like; it's still a tax.

'Any one that' — picks a single member out of a set the sentence has already established.

1. The one selected (fused relative). We'll take whichever is cheapest. 2. No matter which (concessive). Whichever you pick, someone will complain.

  2. The warranty runs three years or 36,000 miles, whichever comes first.
  3. Whichever of the two routes you pick, expect traffic.
  4. She'll be happy with whichever they send.
  5. Whichever is cheapest is the one we'll book.
  6. We can meet Tuesday or Thursday, whichever works better for you.

Generic and impersonal pronouns#

English has no dedicated impersonal pronoun the way French has on or German man. It improvises with ordinary pronouns pressed into generic service — one, you, they, we, and, for most of the twentieth century, he — plus one option that uses no pronoun at all: the passive.

They are not interchangeable, and the difference is mostly register and who is included. Generic you is the ordinary American choice and includes the hearer. Generic one is formal, includes the speaker, and tips into stiffness fast. Generic they is for what unnamed others do or say. Generic we claims a shared stake. Generic he is the one the older style guides prescribed and edited American English has now abandoned in favor of singular they; it is entered below so that it can be recognized in an old contract and not imitated in a new one. The passive removes the person entirely — It is said that…, Mistakes were made — which is why it is both the formal solution and the classic evasion.

Several more things do the same work without being pronouns in the strict sense, and they are entered here because this is where a reader goes looking for them. The noun-phrase generics a person, a guy, and a fella are ordinary nouns pressed into generic service — warmer than one and commoner than it in speech. The placeholders such and such and so-and-so name a slot instead of a value, one for a thing and one for a person. The impersonal self-reference the present writer puts the author of a page into the third person. And the paired forms he or she, its object him or her, and the written-only s/he are the repairs that were tried on generic he before singular they settled the question; each is entered with an honest label on its register and on whom it leaves out. Four families join them, on the same terms. The placeholder names Joe Blow, Joe Schmo, John Q. Public, and the average Joe stand for the ordinary person, and every Tom, Dick, and Harry for everybody indiscriminately — all of them male in form, and each carrying its own load of disparagement. The sworn self-reference deponent and affiant is the present writer in legal dress, and it is what an American meets at the head of an affidavit. The citation pro-forms ibid. and idem point back at a source rather than at a person, and live only in footnotes and briefs. And jocular yours truly is me with a wink.

Two rules hold across the whole group. First, agreement never changes: generic one and generic he take singular verbs, generic you, we, and they take plural forms, exactly as they do in their ordinary uses. Second, do not switch generics mid-passage: ✗ When one is tired, you make mistakes. Pick one and stay with it.

One family that looks as though it belongs here and does not. Neopronouns — ze/hir, ze/zir, xe/xem/xyr, ey/em/eir (the Spivak set), ae/aer, ne/nem/nir, per/pers, ve/ver/vis, fae/faer, co/cos, hu/hum, and the nounself sets such as bun, kit, and star — are third-person singular pronoun sets, each built on the ordinary five slots of he / him / his / his / himself: subject, object, possessive determiner, independent possessive, reflexive (ze / hir / hir / hirs / hirself; xe / xem / xyr / xyrs / xemself; ey / em / eir / eirs / emself). Two facts place them outside this section. They take a singular verb — ze is on the schedule, xe was early — where singular they keeps the plural (they are on the schedule). And each is used for a particular person who has stated it, never for people in general and never for a hypothetical one — those are the jobs generic they and singular they do, and no neopronoun does either. The paradigms and the individual entries are at Neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets, the independent possessives at possessive pronouns, and the reflexives at neopronoun reflexives.

Dummy and expletive pronouns#

An English finite clause must have a subject, even when there is nothing for the subject to be. Nothing is raining in It's raining; there names no place in There's a problem. Both words are placeholders, and both are grammatically required: ✗ Is raining, ✗ Has a problem with the invoice. Speakers of Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, Russian, and Mandarin lose more ground to this rule than to almost anything else in the language, because their own grammars let the subject go.

Which dummy you need is decided by what the sentence is doing. Use it for the weather, the clock, the calendar, a distance, a temperature, an ambient condition — and for a clause you want to move to the end (It is clear that…, I find it odd that…) or an element you want to spotlight (It was Maria who called). Use there to assert that something exists (There is a problem). The pair It's windy and There's a strong wind shows the split cleanly: no noun takes it, a noun takes there.

Agreement is where the group bites. Dummy it is always singular. Existential there borrows its number from the noun that follows — and in speech there's runs in front of plurals constantly, which is the most-corrected honest fact in this file.

Type
dummy subject of the it-cleft
Case
subject
Register
neutral — the cleft is ordinary in both speech and writing
Person / number
third person singular, invariant, and it stays singular even when the spotlighted element is plural.
Refers to
nothing. The cleft splits one clause in two so a single element can be spotlighted: Maria called becomes It was Maria who called.
Agreement
the copula agrees with the dummy, so it is singular whatever the focus — It was the neighbors who complained, ✗ It were the neighbors. The verb inside the cleft clause agrees with the focused element instead: It is I who am responsible (formal), It's the kids who are the problem.
Pattern
It + be + [focused noun phrase] + who/that [clause] · It + be + [focused adverbial] + that [clause] — It was on Tuesday that… · negative: It wasn't the money that bothered him · question: Was it Maria who called? · It wasn't until [time] that [clause]
Contrast
the cleft is also where the old case question gets settled. It was I who broke it is the formal nominative version; It's me is ordinary standard American English and has been for centuries — write It's me, not It is I, unless the register is deliberately elevated. That honesty has a limit, though: in a coordination the case is still decided by the slot the pronoun sits in, and the deletion test decides it — between you and me, They invited my wife and me, She and I went, against the error ✗ Me and him went and the hypercorrection ✗ between you and I (worked through at we (generic) and in full at Case). Inside the cleft clause, who is for people, which for things, and that for both — It was Maria who called and It was Maria that called are equally good — while whose covers people and things alike. And remember that the American preference for restrictive that over restrictive which is an editing convention, not a rule of grammar; the one hard rule there is that that cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause. Against it (extraposition), the test is the gap: in a cleft the material after who/that is missing the focused element (It was Maria who ___ called), while in extraposition the that-clause is complete (It is clear that she was right).

Splits a sentence in two to put one element under the spotlight.

  1. It was Maria who called, not her brother.
  2. It's the humidity that gets to me, not the heat.
  3. It wasn't until Thursday that anyone noticed the error.
  4. It was the neighbors who complained about the noise.
  5. Was it you who left the back door open?
  6. It's not the falling that hurts; it's the landing.
  7. It is in the second paragraph that the argument falls apart.

it (extraposition)#

Pronunciation
/ɪt/ · IT · weak /ət/; it is runs to [ɪɾ ɪz], it's to [əts]
Русский
формальное подлежащее it — место придаточного-подлежащего, вынесенного в конец
Type
dummy (anticipatory) subject
Case
subject
Register
neutral — the default way to build these sentences
Person / number
third person singular, invariant.
Refers to
nothing on its own. It is a placeholder for a subject clause — a that-clause, an infinitive, or an -ing clause — that has been moved to the end because English prefers heavy material last.
Agreement
singular verb, always — It is clear that the numbers were wrong, ✗ It are clear. The verb stays singular no matter how many plurals the extraposed clause contains: It is obvious that the results were fabricated.
Pattern
It [be] [adjective] that [clause] · It [be] [noun phrase] to [verb] · It [verb] [object] that [clause] — It bothers me that… · It seems / appears / turns out / happens that… · It's no use [verb-ing] · It is said / believed / thought that… — the impersonal passive
Contrast
the unextraposed version is grammatical but almost never chosen: That she was right is clear against It is clear that she was right. What is not available is dropping the subject — ✗ Is clear that she was right — the standard interference error for speakers of Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, and Russian. Against it (cleft): here the that-clause is complete, while a cleft leaves a gap where the focused element belongs. Against existential there: it introduces a proposition, there introduces the existence of a thing — It is a problem that nobody checked versus There is a problem. This construction is also the polite formal substitute for generic they: They say the bridge will close → It is said that the bridge will close.

Holds the subject slot for a clause that has been moved to the end of the sentence.

  1. It is clear that nobody actually read the contract.
  2. It's important to arrive at least an hour early.
  3. It turns out the flight was canceled before we even left the house.
  4. It bothers me that she never apologized.
  5. It's no use complaining about it now.
  6. It seems that the two departments never spoke to each other.
  7. It has been decided that the office will close on Fridays.

Type
dummy object in fixed expressions
Case
object
Register
informal to neutral, depending on the idiom
Person / number
third person singular, invariant, and frozen — it cannot be pluralized, questioned, replaced by a noun, or promoted to subject by a passive.
Refers to
nothing at all. The it is part of the idiom's spelling, not a pronoun with a job.

none.
Pattern

the test that separates this it from referential it is whether a what-question can reach it. He fixed it invites "Fixed what?" — referential. Because the it is frozen, the idiom will not passivize or pluralize: ✗ It was made by him in the "arrived" sense, ✗ They lorded them over us. And the spelling trap runs through every one of these: its is the possessive, it's is it is or it has. If you can expand it to it is, it takes the apostrophe.

A meaningless it welded into a fixed expression, where no noun could ever stand.

  1. We barely made it to the gate before they closed the door.

  3. He's been lording it over the rest of the team since the promotion.
  4. Let's call it a day and pick this up tomorrow.
  5. They roughed it for a week with no hot water.
  6. I hadn't prepared anything, so I just winged it.
  7. We're going to have to tough it out until payday.

Русский
безличное it: о погоде, времени, расстоянии — в русском подлежащего нет вовсе
Type
dummy (ambient) subject
Case
subject
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular, invariant.
Refers to
nothing — there is no answer to "what is raining?" This it covers the weather, the clock, the calendar, the temperature, a distance, a duration, and the general state of the surroundings, none of which the sentence has named.
Agreement
singular verb, always — It is, It was, It gets, It has been. ✗ It are.
Pattern
It [be] [weather adjective] — It's cold · It [be] [verb-ing] — It's snowing · It [be] [clock time / day / date] — It's ten after four, It's Tuesday · It [be] [distance or duration] to/from [place] — It's three miles to the airport · It [verb] — It rained all night, It gets dark by five
Contrast
this is the hardest dummy for speakers of languages that drop the subject or build these sentences with a possession verb. English demands the pronoun (✗ Is raining, ✗ Rains a lot here), and it demands it specifically — not there and not have: ✗ There is raining, ✗ It has three miles to the airport, ✗ There are three miles to the airport (compare Spanish hay, French il y a). Existential there asserts that a thing exists, so There's a storm coming is fine because a storm is a noun, while It is storming describes the ambient condition with no noun at all. The minimal pair to remember is It's windy against There's a strong wind.

Fills the subject slot for the weather, the clock, the calendar, and the space around you.

It's three miles to the airport, It's twenty minutes by train. 4. Ambient conditions. It's noisy in here, It smells like smoke in the hallway.

  2. What time is it? — It's almost quarter to six.
  3. It's only about twenty minutes from here if the traffic cooperates.
  4. It gets dark around five o'clock in December.
  5. It was ninety-four degrees on the tarmac when we landed.
  6. It's freezing in this office.
  7. It's Tuesday, so the trash goes out tonight.

Pronunciation
/ðɛr/ · THAIR · existential there is nearly always weak — /ðɚ/, so there are two runs to [ðɚ ɚ ˈtu] and there's to [ðɚz]. The strong /ðɛr/ belongs to the locative adverb; if you hear a full vowel, the word means "in that place"
Русский
оборот there is/are — «есть, имеется, существует»; места не обозначает
Type
existential (expletive) subject
Case
subject
Register
neutral
Person / number
none of its own — it borrows both from the noun phrase that follows the verb.
Refers to
nothing, and in particular not to a place. It exists so the sentence can announce that something exists or is present, with the real subject postponed until after the verb.
Agreement
the verb agrees with the noun phrase that follows, not with there — There is a problem, There are problems, There was one applicant, There were three applicants. Two honest qualifications: before a coordinated list the verb almost always matches the first noun (There is a fork and two knives on the table), and before an amount treated as a single block it is singular (There is ten dollars in the drawer).
Pattern
There [be] [indefinite noun phrase] ([place or time phrase]) · There [be] [noun phrase] to [verb] — There's a lot to do · There [modal] be [noun phrase] — There must be a reason · formal, with other verbs: There arose, There remains, There exists, There seems to be · question: Is there…?, Are there any…? · tag: There's a problem, isn't there?
Contrast
existential there against locative there — both can appear in one sentence, which settles it: There's a bus stop there. The first is unstressed and means nothing; the second is stressed and means "at that spot." Against dummy it: there asserts existence, it describes an ambient condition or identifies something — There is a problem introduces the problem, It is a problem comments on something already in view. The noun after existential there is normally indefinite, which is why ✗ There is my wallet on the table is wrong as an existential (rewrite as My wallet is on the table) and only works if you are pointing, in which case there is the locative. The tag question is the proof that this there is a real grammatical subject — the tag copies it the way it would copy any subject: There were no messages, were there?
Nonstandard
negative concord in the existential — There ain't nobody home, There wasn't nothing we could do — is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernacular speech, and it was ordinary English before the eighteenth century; it is nevertheless nonstandard in edited American English, which allows one negative per clause: There isn't anybody home, There was nothing we could do. See nothing (negative concord).

  3. There was nobody at the desk when I came in.
  4. Is there any coffee left?
  5. There must be a simpler way to do this.
  6. There used to be a hardware store on that corner.
  7. There were fewer complaints this quarter than last.
  8. There's a lot to talk about, isn't there?

there's, there're#

Pronunciation
there's /ðɛrz/, weak [ðɚz] · THAIRZ · there're /ˈðɛrɚ/ collapses in ordinary speech to [ðɛɚ] or plain [ðɛr], barely distinguishable from there alone — one reason it is so rarely written. Also there've /ðɛrv/, there'll /ðɛrl/, there'd /ðɛrd/
Русский
стяжённые there is / there are — «есть, имеется»; разг.
Type
contracted existential subject plus verb
Case
subject
Register
informal to neutral in speech; there's before a plural is informal in writing
Person / number
there's contracts a singular verb (there is, there has); there're contracts a plural one (there are).
Refers to
nothing — see there (existential).
Agreement
this is the whole point of the entry. In edited writing the verb must match the following noun: There's a problem / There are problems. In speech, there's before a plural is near-universal at every level of American English — there's two things I want to say, there's a lot of people here — and it is best understood as a fixed opener rather than an agreement decision made in real time. It is still marked as an error in edited prose, and a conservative editor will change every instance. There're is grammatically correct but awkward to say and rare on paper; most writers use the full there are.
Pattern
There's [singular noun phrase] · There're [plural noun phrase] · There've been [plural noun phrase] · There'll be [noun phrase] · There'd been [noun phrase] · spoken only: There's [plural noun phrase]
Contrast
keep there's apart from theirs and from the homophone set. The apostrophe in there's marks the missing letter of is or has; theirs, being a possessive pronoun, never takes one. In the three-way there / their / they're, there is the place or the dummy, their is the possessive determiner, they're is they are — and in the existential frame only there is possible: ✗ Their's a problem, ✗ They're are two options.

The contracted existential — the form the construction actually takes in speech, agreement problems and all.

  1. There's a package for you by the front door.
  2. There've been three complaints about that intersection this month.
  3. There'll be plenty of time to talk about it after dinner.
  4. There're a few things we should go over first.
  5. There's two things I want to say before we start. (ordinary speech; edited to there are)
  6. There'd been no warning at all before the layoffs.
