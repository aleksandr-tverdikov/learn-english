Interrogative pronouns#

The five wh- words that stand in for the answer you are asking for — who, whom, whose, what, which — plus how many and how much, and the emphatic and elliptical forms built on them.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

An interrogative pronoun stands where the answer will stand, and then moves to the front: Someone called becomes Who called?, and You saw someone becomes Who did you see? That single operation — put a wh- word in the gap, front it — generates every question in this file, and it explains the group's two signature rules. If the wh- word is the subject, nothing else moves and there is no do: Who called? — ✗Who did call? is wrong as a neutral question, though emphatic did comes back when a denial is being contradicted (Somebody called, so who did call?), and the negative takes do as usual (Who didn't call?); if the wh- word is anything else, the auxiliary inverts around the subject: What did you say? And when the question is embedded in a larger sentence, the inversion unwinds and statement order comes back — I asked what he said, never ✗I asked what did he say.

Two choices run the length of the file. What asks from an open set and which from a closed one the hearer can already see: What do you want for dinner? against Which do you want, the soup or the salad? And who asks for a person's identity where what asks for their role — Who is she? wants a name, What is she? wants an occupation.

The five core forms#

The whole class fits in five words, and only one pair of them marks case: who is the subject form and whom the object form, whose is the only possessive, and what and which never change shape at all. Pronunciation is uniform too. Most Americans have the wine–whine merger, so what and which begin with a plain /w/ and which is a homophone of witch; many Southern and older speakers keep an /hw-/ onset. Who, whom, and whose went the other way centuries ago — their w is silent and they begin with /h/. As the focus of the question these words carry the sentence accent and keep their full vowels; it is in embedded clauses that they weaken, which is why I know what I want comes out with a reduced [wət].

what#

Pronunciation
/wʌt/, also /wɑt/ · WUT / WAHT · weak /wət/ whenever it is not the accented word (I know what I want → [wət]); the t flaps into a following vowel, so what if is [wʌˈɾɪf] and what are you runs together as whaddaya; plain /w/ for most Americans, /hwʌt/ for speakers who keep the older onset
Русский
что — спрашивает о предмете, событии или действии из открытого, не заданного круга
Type
interrogative pronoun (also a fused relative; the exclamative What a mess! is a determiner — see the contrast)
Case
none — one form for subject and object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person, invariant; no plural form, and it is construed as singular unless something in the sentence forces otherwise
Refers to
things, events, actions, ideas, amounts, and whole clauses — anything except a person's name. Pointed at a person it asks for the role or category rather than the identity (What is she? — An anesthesiologist.); for the identity, use who. It is open-ended: what assumes no list. For a choice out of a set both speakers can see, use which.
Agreement
as subject it takes a singular verb by default — What is in the box?, What happens next? A plural verb appears only when the complement is plainly plural: What are those?, What were the results? Where the answer could go either way, edited American English prefers the plural with a plural complement: What are the remaining options?
Pattern
What [verb]? (subject question — no do) · What [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (object question) · What [subject] [verb] [preposition]? (What are you looking at?) · [clause] what [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · [subject] [verb] what? (echo, in place) · What? / What about it? (elliptical)
Contrast
what against which is the file's central choice: which selects from a set already on the table, what asks with no set assumed. What did you order? invites any answer; Which did you order? means one of the things in front of us. What against who for a person turns on identity versus role: Who is he? gets my brother-in-law, What is he? gets a structural engineer — and asking What is he? about a person can sound cold, so What does he do? is the ordinary way to put it. Three other whats are not this one: the fused relative (What you said was true = "the thing that"), covered with the fused relatives; the exclamative (What a mess!), which is a determiner; and the determiner what in what color, what time, covered in the determiner dictionary. The test is the same one that runs through both dictionaries: a noun immediately after it makes it a determiner, standing alone makes it a pronoun.

Asks for the identity of a thing, an event, or an action, out of an open field of possibilities.

1. Things, events, and actions. The default use: What happened?, What do you want?, What's in the bag? The answer can be anything at all, which is what separates it from which.

2. A person's role or category. What is she? asks for an occupation, a nationality, a category — not a name. Beside it, Who is she? asks for the identity.

3. Amount, price, and measure. What did it cost?, What does the whole thing weigh? Here what is doing the work of how much.

4. Standing for a whole clause. What happened next? and What's going on? take an entire event as the answer, not a noun.

5. As a request for repetition. Bare What? means "say that again", and in American English it is blunt — Sorry?, Excuse me?, and I'm sorry, what? are the polite versions, Huh? the casual one. For the incredulous You did what?, see what (echo).

  1. What happened to the second box of files?
  2. What did she say when you told her the price?
  3. What are you looking at?
  4. He asked me what I wanted for my birthday.
  5. What was your father before he retired, a machinist?
  6. What does the whole thing cost with tax and delivery?
  7. What's in the trunk? Nothing that won't fit in the hall closet.
  8. Nobody could tell us what the delay was about.

which#

Pronunciation
/wɪtʃ/ · WICH · a homophone of witch for most Americans; /hwɪtʃ/ for Southern and older speakers who keep the wh- onset; weak /wətʃ/ in an embedded clause (I forget which one it was)
Русский
который, какой из — просит выбрать из закрытого набора, известного обоим собеседникам
Type
interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun)
Case
none — one form for subject and object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person, invariant; construed as singular or plural according to the answer expected
Refers to
things and people alike, but only from a known, bounded set — one that is visible, has just been mentioned, or is listed in the question itself. With people it asks which member of a group (Which of you is Dr. Reyes?), and it never asks for a stranger's identity: ✗Which is she? → Who is she? The set need not be small, only bounded and mutually known (Which state has the longest coastline?).
Agreement
the verb agrees with the number of the expected answer, not with the of-phrase: Which is yours? (one expected) beside Which are yours? (more than one), and Which of these boxes is yours? beside Which of these boxes are ready to ship? With which of us / you / them, a singular verb is the default: Which of us is going to tell him?
Pattern
Which [verb]? (subject question — no do) · Which [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · Which of [determiner + noun]? · Which [subject] [verb] [preposition]? · [clause] which [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · Which? (elliptical, set understood)
Contrast
open set versus closed set is the whole story. What's your favorite movie? leaves the field open to every movie ever made; Which is your favorite? means one of the three on the table. Choosing what when a list has already been given invites an answer from outside the list, and choosing which with no list leaves the hearer hunting for one. Against who for people: who identifies, which selects — Who called? asks for a name, Which of them called? asks you to pick from a group already named. The relative which is a different job (the report, which arrived late) and lives with the relative pronouns — and the famous which/that rule belongs to that entry, not this one: half of it is real (that cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause) and half is an American editing convention (restrictive which is perfectly grammatical, and a copy editor will still change it to that). The determiner which takes a noun directly (which flight) and lives in the determiner dictionary. Adding of always makes it the pronoun: which flight determiner, which of the flights pronoun.

Asks the hearer to pick one member — or several — out of a set both speakers already have in view.

  1. Which do you want, the aisle or the window?
  2. Which of these chargers is mine?
  3. Which is faster this time of day, driving or the train?
  4. I couldn't tell you which of the twins I spoke to.
  5. Which of you has the parking pass?
  6. Which are we taking, my car or yours?
  7. Which did you end up ordering?
  8. She never said which she had picked.

what, who, which of, whichever, which (relative), which (determiner)

who#

Pronunciation
/hu/ · HOO · the w is silent. No true weak form — as the question word it carries the accent — but it shortens in embedded position, and the contractions are ordinary: who's /huz/, who'd /hud/, who'll /hul/, who're /ˈhuɚ/. Who's is a homophone of whose.
Русский
кто — спрашивает об имени, личности человека; форма подлежащего
Type
interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun)
Case
subject — and, in everyday American English, object as well (Who did you call?)
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person in agreement, whatever person the answer turns out to be; invariant in form, and singular by default no matter how many people the answer names
Refers to
people only — including a pet spoken of as a person and a team, band, or company understood as the people in it (Who won?). For a thing use what or which; to pick from a group already named use which of.
Agreement
singular by default, even when several people are expected: Who wants coffee?, Who is coming tonight?, Who's got the keys? A plural verb appears only when a plural complement forces it (Who are those men?, Who are the finalists?). Who has no gender, so the pronoun that picks it up afterward is singular they — Who left their coat on the porch?, Whoever called, they didn't leave a message. That is standard American English and not a compromise; see they.
Pattern
Who [verb]? (subject question — no do in a neutral question: ✗Who did call?) · Who [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (object question: Who did you call?) · Who [subject] [verb] [preposition]? (stranded: Who did you give it to?) · Who [auxiliary] not [verb]? (negative subject question, where do does appear: Who didn't sign?) · [clause] who [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · Who else, Who all (regional), Says who?
Contrast
whom is the object form, and the test is he/him: answer the question and see which fits — Who called? — He called → who; Whom did you see? — I saw him → whom. In real American usage who has taken over the fronted object slot, so Who did you see? is standard rather than an error, and whom survives mainly right after a preposition (to whom, for whom, with whom). Against what for a person: Who is he? asks identity, What is he? asks occupation. Against which: who identifies out of the blue, which selects from a set. And the two do-support facts that trip learners: a positive subject question takes no do (Who called?), but a negative one does (Who didn't get paid?), and emphatic did returns when a denial is being challenged (Somebody called — so who did call?). Who's with an apostrophe is who is / who has, never possessive: see whose. Answering a who question is where case actually shows itself in speech, and the facts are these. The bare answer takes the object form — Who wants coffee? — Me, not ✗I — and It's me is standard English, with It is I now stiff outside the telephone formula This is she. When the answer is built out into a full subject the subject form returns (Who went? — She and I went), and in an object slot the object form returns (Who did they invite? — They invited my wife and me). The hypercorrections are the mirror image of the schoolroom error: ✗between you and I, ✗They invited my wife and I, ✗He gave it to Bob and I — every one of those is an object slot, two of them after a preposition (between, to) and one after a verb (invited), and the object form is what belongs in all three: between you and me, They invited my wife and me, He gave it to Bob and me. The deletion test settles them: drop the other person and read what is left aloud — He gave it to me ✓ against ✗He gave it to I, They invited me ✓ against ✗They invited I, I went ✓ against ✗Me went, which is also what rules out ✗Me and him went. Between is the one place the test needs a second step, because deleting the partner leaves the phrase incomplete; put a plural in instead, and between us shows the case plainly — never ✗between we. Full treatment with me and I.

Asks for the identity of a person — the one who did it, or the one it happened to.

  1. Who left the porch light on all night?
  2. Who did you talk to at the bank?
  3. Who's bringing dessert on Thursday?
  4. I still don't know who she voted for.
  5. Who wants to go first?
  6. Who are those people in the driveway?
  7. Who didn't get their parking pass yet?
  8. Who do you think is going to end up paying for it?

whom#

Pronunciation
/hum/ · HOOM · the w is silent. It keeps its full vowel almost everywhere, because it is usually being chosen deliberately; the one place it genuinely weakens is inside a fixed prepositional phrase in fast speech, where to whom can flatten to [tə ˈhəm]. The w stays silent even there — there is no /w/ sound in whom at any speed, so ✗[təˈwəm] is not a pronunciation of it.
Русский
кого, кому — объектная форма who, при глаголе или предлоге; формальн., книжн.
Type
interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun)
Case
object
Register
formal
Person / number
any person, singular or plural; people only, and invariant in form
Refers to
human referents only, in an object slot — the object of a verb (Whom did they hire?) or of a preposition (For whom is this?). For things the object form is simply what or which, neither of which marks case.
Agreement
none of its own. Being an object, it never controls the verb; the verb agrees with the clause's own subject — Whom did the committee choose? has committee as subject.
Pattern
Whom [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · [preposition] whom [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · [clause] whom [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · To whom? / For whom? (elliptical) · the fixed salutation To Whom It May Concern
Contrast
the he/him test decides it: if the answer takes him, the question takes whom. Beyond that, be honest about the state of the language. In ordinary American speech and in most published prose, who has replaced whom in fronted object position — Who did you see? is standard American English, not a mistake, and Whom did you see? sounds careful or stiff depending on the room. Where whom is still genuinely required is immediately after a preposition: ✗to who did you speak will be corrected everywhere, while Who did you speak to? is fine because the preposition has been left at the end. The trap now runs the other way: writing whom where the word is the subject of an embedded clause is a hypercorrection and is more common than the error it was meant to prevent — ✗Whom shall I say is calling? (test: he is calling) and ✗the candidate whom we thought would win. Intervening words like we thought and shall I say never change the case.

Marks the person who is the object of the verb or of a preposition in the question.

  1. Whom did the board finally appoint?
  2. To whom should I address the complaint?
  3. Whom are we meeting at four?
  4. For whom was the second reservation made?
  5. She wouldn't say whom she had consulted before signing.
  6. Whom do you trust with a key to the building?
  7. Whom did they notify first, the family or the press?

whose#

Pronunciation
/huz/ · HOOZ · the w is silent, and it is a perfect homophone of who's (= who is / who has). No weak form; as a question word it takes the accent.
Русский
чей — спрашивает о владельце, авторе или ответственном за предмет
Type
interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun; and a determiner in whose coat)
Case
possessive
Register
neutral
Person / number
invariant — it asks about a possessor of any person, number, or gender at once
Refers to
possessors. As an interrogative the possessor asked about is almost always human (Whose is this?); the wider power of whose to take inanimate possessors (a building whose roof leaks) belongs to its relative use. What it points at is the owner, never the thing owned.

the verb agrees with the thing possessed, not with whose: Whose is this?, Whose are these?, Whose was the idea? Nothing about whose itself is singular or plural.
Pattern
Whose [be] [noun phrase]? (Whose is this jacket?) · Whose [be] [demonstrative]? (Whose are those?) · Whose [be] it? · [clause] whose [subject] [verb] (embedded: I asked whose it was) · Whose? (elliptical)
Contrast
whose against who's is the commonest spelling error in this whole class, and the test is expansion: if who is or who has fits, it takes the apostrophe (Who's coming?), and if the sentence is about ownership it does not (Whose is this?). The apostrophe form is never possessive — no possessive pronoun in English has one, which is the same fact behind its/it's and your/you're. Determiner against pronoun is the other line: a noun right after it makes it a determiner (Whose coat is this? — see the determiner dictionary), and standing alone makes it the pronoun (Whose is this coat?, Whose is this?). Note also that whose has no partitive: ✗whose of these does not exist, and the job goes to which of — Which of these is yours?

Asks who owns, wrote, made, or is otherwise responsible for the thing in question.

  1. Whose is the truck blocking the driveway?
  2. Whose are these sunglasses?
  3. Somebody left a phone on the bench and nobody knows whose it is.
  4. Whose was the idea to leave at four in the morning?
  5. Whose is this — yours or your brother's?
  6. She wouldn't tell me whose it was.
  7. Whose are we taking, since mine is in the shop?

Partitive, prepositional, and complex forms#

The core five rarely stand completely bare. They take a preposition in front (to whom), a partitive of-phrase behind (which of these), or a modifier stuck on the end (what else, and regionally who all). Two rules govern the whole section. First, of makes it a pronoun: which flight is a determiner, which of the flights is a pronoun, and the noun inside the of-phrase keeps its own determiner, so ✗which of flights is impossible. Second, a fronted preposition takes whom, a stranded one takes who in ordinary American use — To whom did you speak? and Who did you speak to? are both correct, and the mixed ✗To who did you speak? is the only version that is not. (Whom did you speak to?, stranding the preposition but keeping the object form, is grammatical as well; it is simply the stiffest of the three and rare in speech.) Three entries here are not built on the core five and belong with them anyway: how many and how much, the quantifier pair that heads its own noun phrase and takes the same partitive of; whether, the archaic which of two that which long ago swallowed; and wherefore, the fossil that fuses a wh- word onto for and asks why. One more, whomst, is not a form of English at all but an internet joke made out of whom, and it is labelled as such where it stands.

how many, how much#

Pronunciation
/ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ/ · how-MEN-ee, how-MUCH · standing alone the accent sits on many or much, because the noun that would otherwise carry it has been left out; put a noun back and the accent shifts forward onto how (HOW many PEOPLE came?). How keeps its full vowel everywhere and has no weak form, but the second word reduces when the phrase is unaccented in an embedded clause — I don't know how many are left runs to [haʊ məni], how much of it to [haʊ mətʃ] — and of is the weak /əv/, often bare [ə], so how many of them is [ˈhaʊ ˌmɛni ə ðəm]
Русский
сколько — how many о счётных предметах, how much о количестве, мере или цене
Type
interrogative pronoun — a quantifier standing as the head of its own noun phrase; the same two words in front of a noun are determiners
Case
none — one form for subject and object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person, invariant in form; how many is plural and how much singular, and the two split the field between them by countability
Refers to
a number (how many) or an amount, extent, or price (how much) of something already named or plain from the situation — How many are coming? counts the people under discussion, How much is it? asks a price. What is counted in units takes how many, what is measured takes how much. Neither asks for identity; that is what or which.
Agreement
the understood noun controls the verb, not the phrase: How many are left? against How much is left? In the partitive the noun inside the of-phrase decides — How many of the files were deleted? beside How much of the paint was wasted? — and with how many of us / you / them the plural is the normal choice: How many of us are actually going?
Pattern
How many [verb]? (subject question — no do: How many showed up?) · How much [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much is [noun phrase]? (the price question) · How many of / How much of [determiner + noun]? · [clause] how many [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · How many? / How much? (elliptical) · never directly in front of a noun in this use — a noun there makes it a determiner
Contrast
the noun test settles the part of speech here exactly as it does for what and which: how many chairs has a noun after it and is a determiner (see how many and how much in the determiner dictionary), while How many do we need? stands alone and is the pronoun in this entry. Adding of makes it a pronoun as well — how many of the chairs — and the noun inside the of-phrase keeps its own determiner, so ✗how many of chairs is impossible; that is the same of-rule that governs which of and what of. Countability is where learners actually go wrong: ✗how much people → how many people, ✗how many money → how much money, and a measure word brings the countable form back (how many gallons of gas). Against what, English asks a price with how much and not with what as the subject — ✗What costs the jacket? is a direct translation that never sounds native, though What did it cost? is ordinary. And a third how much is neither pronoun nor determiner but a degree adverb, with no noun anywhere in the sentence: How much do you care?, How much longer? Nothing counted or measured is expected as the answer there.

Asks for the number or the amount, leaving the thing counted or measured to be understood.

  1. How many are coming to the walkthrough on Saturday?
  2. How much is the gray one in the window?
  3. "We'll take the shrimp." "How many?"
  4. How much of the deposit do we get back if we cancel now?
  5. Nobody could tell me how many had already shipped.
  6. How many of you have actually read the lease?
  7. She asked how much it would run us to have the tree taken down.

object, governed by the preposition

object of about

The emphatic -ever forms#

Adding -ever to a question word turns it into an emphatic question expressing astonishment: Whatever do you mean?, Whoever told you that? Two things need saying up front. First, in American English this construction is dated and reads as literary or British — the everyday American intensifiers are on earth, in the world, the heck, and the hell (What on earth do you mean?), which get their own entry at the end of this section. Second, careful writers spell the emphatic question as two words (What ever happened to Ted?) to keep it apart from the fused relative whatever (Take whatever you need), which is a different word doing a different job and is covered with the fused relatives. Nothing here is wrong; it is a register choice, and only whoever is still fully at home in ordinary American speech.

Echo questions, ellipsis, and fixed expressions#

These are questions with most of the sentence taken away. An echo question leaves the wh- word exactly where the missing information was — You did what? — with no fronting, no do-support, and a hard rise at the end; it asks the speaker to repeat or confirm what they just said. The rest of the section is ellipsis that has frozen: What for?, So what?, Who cares?, Now what?, …or what? Each began as a full question, lost everything but the question word and one companion, and hardened into a fixed expression with its own meaning and its own intonation — several of them, like So what? and Who cares?, take a question mark by convention while expecting no answer at all. Three of them — what's what, which is which, and who's who — have gone further still and become noun phrases, and two more — what have you and whatnot — have stopped asking anything at all and become ways of ending a list. Where a full clause does follow one of these, it keeps statement word order, the same rule that governs every indirect question: Who knows what she paid for it?, never ✗who knows what did she pay. That rule is set out in full in Reported questions.
