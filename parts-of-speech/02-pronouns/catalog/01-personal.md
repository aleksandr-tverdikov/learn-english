# Personal pronouns

> The twelve forms that point at the speaker, the listener, and everyone else — with *who/whom*, the last words in English that still change shape depending on their job in the sentence.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Personal pronouns encode three things at once: **person** (who is speaking, who is spoken to, who is spoken about), **number** (one or more than one), and, in the third-person singular, **gender**. What sets them apart from every other word class is **case** — English nouns lost their case endings centuries ago, but *I/me*, *he/him*, *she/her*, *we/us*, and *they/them* still mark the difference between a subject and an object. The single rule running through the whole group is that **case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause**, and the single place Americans most reliably get it wrong is inside a coordination. It goes wrong in both directions: *between you and I*, *he gave it to Bob and I*, and *they invited my wife and I* are **hypercorrections** — the object slot demands the object form — while *me and him went* is **nonstandard** in the opposite direction, an object form doing a subject's job. One one-second test fixes every case of it: **delete the other person and listen.** *He gave it to Bob and **me*** (because *he gave it to me*), *They invited my wife and **me*** (because *they invited me*), ***She** and **I** went* (because *she went*, *I went*). The second thing to know is that these words are almost never stressed, so the form a learner actually hears is the weak form: *him* is /ɪm/, *her* is /ɚ/, *them* is /əm/, *he* is /i/, and *you* is /jə/.

The possessive forms (*my/mine, your/yours, his, her/hers, its, our/ours, their/theirs*) and the reflexives (*myself … themselves*) belong to the same paradigm but are dictionaried separately — the possessive determiners in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md), the standalone possessives in [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md) and the reflexives in [reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md).

The entries below cover those twelve in alphabetical order, plus the reduced forms that a listener actually hears in place of the full words in most casual American sentences — ['em](#em), which is a separate word historically rather than a lazy spelling of *them*, [ya](#ya), which is simply *you* said at speed, and ['im, 'er, 'is](#im-er-is), which are *him*, *her*, and *his* with the /h/ gone — together with the coordinated pair [he or she, him or her, s/he](#he-or-she-him-or-her-she-himself-or-herself), the formal epicene that singular *they* has largely replaced, and the two counted phrases [the two of us](#the-two-of-us) and [you two](#you-two), which are the only way English has of saying how many people a *we* or a *you* covers. None of the reduced forms is regional and none is nonstandard in speech; all are informal on the page. Three further sections follow the twelve: the [regional, nonstandard, and archaic forms](#regional-nonstandard-and-archaic-forms), the [honorific address pronominals](#honorific-address-pronominals) that address a person in the third person, and the [neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets](#neopronouns-and-chosen-pronoun-sets).

### 'em

**Pronunciation:** /əm/ &middot; *uhm* &middot; also a syllabic /m̩/ after a consonant (*tell 'em* [ˈtɛl m̩]). It is never stressed and has no strong form; if the word carries stress it is *them* /ðɛm/.
**Русский:** их, им — редуцированное them в живой речи; на письме разг.
**Type:** personal pronoun, reduced (clitic) &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech, informal in writing — normal in dialogue, transcription, and advertising copy, out of place in a formal document
**Person / number:** third person plural, and also singular where it is the reduced object form of singular [they](#they). Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** exactly what [them](#them) refers to — any plural antecedent, people or things, and a single person of unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary gender. It carries no dialect marking of its own: *'em* in *I saw 'em* is what educated General American speakers produce in ordinary conversation, not a regional feature.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb.
**Pattern:** [verb] *'em* &middot; [verb] *'em* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *'em* &middot; *all/both/some of 'em* &middot; fixed phrases: *give 'em hell*, *let 'em have it*, *sock it to 'em*, *round 'em up*
**Contrast:** Against [them](#them): same word in function, different register in *writing* only — you may always write *them* where you hear *'em*, and in edited prose you generally should, outside quoted speech. The apostrophe is standard and points backward at the missing sound. And the etymology is the real surprise — *'em* is **not** a clipping of *them*. It descends from Middle English *hem* (Old English *heom*), the native third-person plural object pronoun, which the Norse-borrowed *them* displaced in the written language while the old form survived unstressed in the mouth. So *them* and *'em* are two different words that ended up sharing one job.

The form of *them* that ordinary speech actually produces. A learner who listens for /ðɛm/ and hears nothing is hearing /əm/ perfectly well and not recognizing it.

1. Just tell *'em* the shipment is delayed.
2. I saw *'em* leaving as I came in.
3. Round *'em* up and get *'em* on the truck.
4. Both of *'em* said the same thing.
5. There's nothing left in the box — somebody took all of *'em*.
6. Give *'em* hell tomorrow.

**See also:** [them](#them), [they](#they), [him](#him), [themselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themselves)

### he

**Pronunciation:** /hi/ &middot; *HEE* &middot; weak /i/ — the /h/ drops whenever *he* is unstressed and not at the start of an utterance: *is he* /ˈɪzi/, *did he* /ˈdɪdi/, *would he* /ˈwʊdi/, *has he* /ˈhæzi/
**Русский:** он — о мужчине или о животном, чей пол известен; о предметах — it
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine
**Refers to:** a male human being, or an animal whose sex is known and matters (a family pet, a named racehorse). Not things — for objects, abstractions, plants, and animals whose sex is unknown or irrelevant, use [it](#it). Some religious writing capitalizes *He* for God. Generic *he* covering a person of unspecified gender (*Every applicant must submit his fingerprints*) was the traditional rule and is now outdated in American English; use singular [they](#they) or recast in the plural.
**Agreement:** third-person singular verb forms — *he **is**, he **was**, he **has**, he **does**, he **goes**, he **doesn't***. Never *he are*. The bare present without *-s* (*he go*, *he don't*) is systematic in African American English and in some Southern varieties, but it is not standard written English.
**Pattern:** *He* [verb] &middot; *He* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *he* [verb]? &middot; [name] *and he* [plural verb] &middot; *than he* [auxiliary] &middot; *It was he who* [verb] (formal)
**Contrast:** *He* is the subject form, [him](#him) the object form. Test by putting the pronoun alone in the slot: *He arrived* / *I saw him* — whichever sounds right alone is right in a coordination too (*Marcus and **he** arrived*, *I saw Marcus and **him***). Do not confuse *he* with the possessive *his*, which never stands as a subject. After *than* and *as*, both cases are current: *faster than **he** [is]* is the formal conjunction reading, *faster than **him*** the ordinary spoken one.

Points at a specific male person already identified, or one the listener can identify from context. It is subject-only: it can head a sentence, follow an auxiliary in a question, or sit in a coordinated subject, but it can never follow a verb or a preposition.

1. *He* left the office before anyone could stop him.
2. Did *he* say when the shipment would arrive?
3. My brother and *he* were roommates for two years in Chicago.
4. *He* hasn't answered a single email since Thursday.
5. She types faster than *he* does, but he catches more mistakes.
6. Neither the landlord nor *he* had a key to the back door.

**See also:** [him](#him), [she](#she), [they](#they), [it](#it), [his](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his), [himself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#himself)

### he or she, him or her, s/he, himself or herself

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhi ɚ ˈʃi/ &middot; *hee-er-SHEE* &middot; the *or* is a weak /ɚ/ in ordinary speech, and in *him or her* /ˌhɪm ɚ ˈhɚ/ both /h/s drop: [ˌhɪm ɚ ˈɚ]. ***S/he*** has no pronunciation of its own — a reader says *he or she* aloud; the slash exists only on the page, and so do the older *he/she* and *(s)he*.
**Русский:** он или она — книжн. формула для лица неизвестного пола; вытеснена единственным they
**Type:** coordinated pronoun pair, epicene — gender-inclusive by listing both &middot; **Case:** *he or she* subject, *him or her* object, *himself or herself* reflexive &middot; **Register:** formal and dated — the house solution of American statutes, contracts, employee handbooks, insurance policies, and academic prose from roughly 1970 to 2010
**Person / number:** third person singular, and grammatically singular in every slot, which is exactly what it was built to be.
**Refers to:** one unidentified person of unstated gender — the *each applicant*, *every employee*, *a juror* of institutional prose. Never a specific person already named, and never a group. Two things pushed it out. It names **two** genders, so it excludes anyone who is neither and cannot be used for a person who has stated *they* or a neopronoun; and it doubles the length of every pronoun slot, which collapses after two repetitions — *Each applicant must sign his or her form and bring it with him or her when he or she reports.*
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *he or she **is** responsible*, *he or she **has** thirty days*. That is the cleanest difference from singular [they](#they), which takes a plural verb even for one person (*they **are** responsible*). The possessive determiner *his or her* precedes a noun and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her); the standalone possessive is [his or hers](02-possessive.md#his-or-hers).
**Pattern:** *he or she* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *him or her* &middot; [preposition] *him or her* &middot; [verb] *himself or herself* &middot; *s/he* (writing only) &middot; *he or she, as the case may be* (legal drafting) &middot; *he or she who* [verb]
**Variants:** *he/she*, *(s)he*, *s/he*, *she or he*, *her or him*, *herself or himself*, *he or she, as the case may be*; the possessive halves *his or her*, *his/her*, and *his or hers*
**Contrast:** Against singular [they](#they), which is shorter, standard in edited American English, accepted by the major style guides, covers people of every gender, and takes a plural verb. Against generic [he](#he), which is dated in the other direction. In practice American editors now reach past both pronouns for a third repair — **make the noun plural** (*All applicants must sign their forms*) or **cut the pronoun** (*Each applicant must submit a complete file*) — because those remove the problem instead of managing it. The halves are not interchangeable across cases: ✗*Give the form to he or she* needs the object pair, *him or her*. And the reflexive still has to match a subject in its own clause, exactly as plain *himself* does: *No employee may approve a payment to **himself or herself***.

The compromise American editing reached in the 1970s, when generic *he* stopped being acceptable and singular *they* had not yet been accepted back. It is grammatically unimpeachable and stylistically expensive, and it now marks a document as a generation old — which is where a learner meets it: leases, policies, bylaws, statutes, and older textbooks.

1. Each applicant is responsible for making sure that *he or she* has submitted a complete file.
2. Notify the account holder in writing before *he or she* is charged an overdraft fee.
3. The plan shall pay the participant the amount due *him or her* within sixty days of the request.
4. If a passenger's bag is lost, *s/he* should file a claim at the counter before leaving the airport.
5. No employee may approve a reimbursement to *himself or herself*.
6. A juror may not discuss the case with anyone, including *his or her* spouse. (the determiner half, which belongs with the determiners)
7. The same sentence as an American editor would write it today: *All applicants are responsible for submitting a complete file.*

**See also:** [he](#he), [she](#she), [they](#they), [them](#them), [his or hers](02-possessive.md#his-or-hers), [his or her (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her), [himself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#himself), [herself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#herself), [themself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themself)

### her

**Pronunciation:** /hɚ/ &middot; *HUR* &middot; weak /ɚ/ — the /h/ drops when unstressed and non-initial: *tell her* /ˈtɛlɚ/, *give her* /ˈɡɪvɚ/, *ask her* /ˈæskɚ/, *call her* /ˈkɔlɚ/ (homophonous with *caller*)
**Русский:** её, ей — объектная форма she; притяжательное her (her car) сюда не входит
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, feminine
**Refers to:** a female human being, or an animal whose sex is known. The same word *her* is also the possessive determiner (*her car*) — that use belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#her) and is not covered here. This entry is only the object pronoun: *I called her.*
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. When *her* follows *be* as a complement, the verb agrees with the subject, not with *her*: *That's **her***, *It was **her** who called* (informal) / *It was **she** who called* (formal).
**Pattern:** [verb] *her* &middot; [verb] *her* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *her* &middot; [name] *and her* &middot; *That's her* &middot; *than her*
**Contrast:** Three words look alike and do different jobs. **her** = object pronoun (*I saw her*) or possessive determiner (*her keys*); **hers** = standalone possessive (*The keys are hers*); **her's** = not a word in any variety of English, ever. The test for object vs determiner is what follows: a noun after it makes it a determiner (*her office*), nothing after it makes it the object pronoun (*I called her*). Against [she](#she): *she* is the subject (*She called*), *her* the object (*I called her*).

The object counterpart of *she* — it takes the direct object, indirect object, and object-of-preposition slots. It is also what Americans actually say after *be* (*That's her*), where traditional grammar prescribes *she*.

1. I called *her* twice from the airport and got voicemail both times.
2. The committee offered *her* the position on Friday afternoon.
3. This envelope is for *her*, not for me.
4. Nobody had told *her* that the meeting was moved.
5. Give the spare key to Dana and *her* before you leave.
6. That's *her* in the second row, next to the window.

**See also:** [she](#she), [him](#him), [them](#them), [hers](02-possessive.md#hers), [her (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#her), [herself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#herself)

### him

**Pronunciation:** /hɪm/ &middot; *HIM* &middot; weak /ɪm/, sometimes /əm/ — *tell him* /ˈtɛlɪm/, *get him* /ˈɡɛɾɪm/, *ask him* /ˈæskɪm/. The /h/ survives only under stress or after a pause.
**Русский:** его, ему — объектная форма he
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine
**Refers to:** a male human being, or an animal whose sex is known and matters. Not things. Generic *him* for a person of unspecified gender (*Ask each student to bring his own laptop*) is outdated in American English — use singular [them](#them).
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. After *be*, the verb agrees with the subject: *That's **him***, *It was **him*** (standard American) / *It was **he*** (formal, now stilted).
**Pattern:** [verb] *him* &middot; [verb] *him* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *him* &middot; [name] *and him* &middot; *That's him* &middot; *than him*
**Contrast:** [He](#he) is the subject, *him* the object: *He called me* / *I called him*. Inside a coordination, delete the other name and the right form becomes obvious — *The prize went to Marcus and him* passes because *The prize went to him* passes, while ✗*The prize went to Marcus and he* fails the same test. Against *his*: *his* is possessive and never fills an object slot on its own (*I called his* is impossible). Against *himself*: use the reflexive only when the object is the same person as the subject of the same clause — *He blamed **himself*** vs. *Marco said the manager would call **him*** (different clauses, so the plain object form).

The object counterpart of *he*. It fills every non-subject slot, and in ordinary American speech it also fills the complement slot after *be*, where traditional grammar wants *he*.

1. Tell *him* I'll be about ten minutes late.
2. We haven't seen *him* since the reunion in June.
3. The letter was addressed to *him*, but it came to my box.
4. Is that *him* in the blue jacket, or am I thinking of his brother?
5. She gave Marcus and *him* exactly the same instructions.
6. I've worked with *him* longer than anyone else on this floor.

**See also:** [he](#he), [her](#her), [them](#them), [his](02-possessive.md#his), [himself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#himself)

### I

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ/ &middot; *EYE* &middot; no true weak form — the vowel keeps its quality even unstressed, though the offglide shortens toward [ɑ] in fast speech (*I dunno* [ɑ dəˈnoʊ]) and across much of the South (*I* [aː]). The reductions happen in the contractions instead: *I'm* /aɪm/, *I'll* /aɪl/, *I've* /aɪv/, *I'd* /aɪd/.
**Русский:** я — только говорящий; в отличие от русского, опустить нельзя и пишется с заглавной
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person singular. Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** the speaker or writer, and nobody else. In quoted or reported speech it shifts to whoever is doing the speaking, which is why *"I'll pay," she said* becomes *She said **she** would pay*.
**Agreement:** *I* is the only pronoun in English that takes ***am***. Otherwise: *I **was**, I **have**, I **do**, I **go*** — the bare present, no *-s*. In the counterfactual subjunctive the form is *were*: *If I **were** you*.
**Pattern:** *I* [verb] &middot; *I* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *I* [verb]? &middot; [name] *and I* [plural verb] &middot; *than I* [auxiliary] &middot; *It is I* (formal, archaic-sounding)
**Contrast:** *I* is subject-only; [me](#me) takes every other slot. The trap runs in both directions. The **hypercorrection** puts *I* into an object slot, and it is not confined to prepositions: ✗*between you and I* and ✗*for my wife and I* are objects of a preposition, but ✗*They invited my wife and I* and ✗*She thanked Marcus and I* are plain **direct objects**, and they are wrong for the same reason. This over-correction is now more common in educated American speech than the error it was invented to avoid. In the other direction, ✗*Me and my friend are studying* is nonstandard as a subject. The fix for both is the same: **delete the other person.** *Send the invoice to Karen and ___* → *Send the invoice to **me***. *They invited my wife and ___* → *They invited **me***. *___ and my friend are studying* → ***I** am studying*. Orthographically, *I* is the only English word capitalized wherever it appears.

The speaker's own subject form. It heads a clause, inverts with an auxiliary in questions, and joins coordinated subjects — where politeness, not grammar, puts it last: *My brother and I*, not *I and my brother*.

1. *I* disagree with the report's central conclusion.
2. My sister and *I* drove up to Vermont on Saturday morning.
3. Am *I* on the list, or did the registration never go through?
4. She has been at the firm longer than *I* have.
5. *I* have rewritten the opening paragraph three times and it still isn't right.
6. Neither the auditors nor *I* could find the missing invoice.
7. She and *I* went over the numbers twice before the meeting.

### 'im, 'er, 'is

**Pronunciation:** /ɪm/ &middot; *im* &middot; ***'er*** is /ɚ/ (*ur*) and ***'is*** is /ɪz/ (*iz*). All three are weak forms and nothing else — they are never stressed, and under stress the words are *him* /hɪm/, *her* /hɚ/, *his* /hɪz/. Linking and flapping across the boundary are normal: *get 'im* /ˈɡɛɾɪm/, *tell 'er* /ˈtɛlɚ/, *what's 'is name* → *whatsisname* /ˈwʌtsɪzˌneɪm/.
**Русский:** его, её, его (притяж.) — слабые формы him/her/his с выпадением /h/; разг. написание
**Type:** personal pronoun, reduced (weak forms) &middot; **Case:** *'im* and *'er* object; *'is* possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech, informal in writing — dialogue, song lyrics, transcription, and advertising copy
**Person / number:** third person singular — *'im* masculine object, *'er* feminine object, *'is* masculine possessive.
**Refers to:** exactly what [him](#him), [her](#her), and *his* refer to. **The h-dropping is not a dialect feature.** In American English the /h/ of an unstressed, non-initial pronoun deletes for every speaker in every region: *I told him* is [aɪ ˈtoʊldɪm], *Ask her* is [ˈæskɚ], *That's his car* is [ˈðæts ɪz ˈkɑr]. It is the ordinary output of General American at conversational speed, and it is not the British h-dropping of *'ouse* and *'ammer*, which deletes /h/ from full lexical words and is socially marked in England. Only the apostrophe spelling is informal; the sound is universal.
**Agreement:** none of their own — *'im* and *'er* are objects, and an object controls no verb; *'is* is a possessive.
**Pattern:** [verb] *'im* &middot; [preposition] *'er* &middot; fixed phrases: *tell 'im*, *get 'im*, *let 'er rip*, *give 'er hell*, *whatsisname*, *whatshername* &middot; *'is* [noun] (the determiner use)
**Variants:** written without the apostrophe as *im*, *er*, *is* in some novelists' eye dialect; *'er* also spells the reduced possessive determiner *her* (*'er car*)
**Contrast:** Against ['em](#em): the same reduction one cell over in the paradigm, with one difference — *'em* is a separate word historically, the descendant of Old English *heom*, while *'im*, *'er*, and *'is* are simply *him*, *her*, and *his* minus the /h/. Against the page: spelling these forms in one character's mouth while writing *him* and *her* in the narrator's tells a reader that the character talks differently, when the narrator would reduce them too — that is the eye-dialect trap, and it is why editors cut these spellings from everything but deliberate dialogue. And the determiner line runs straight through this entry: ***'is* before a noun is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *'is car* is the reduced possessive determiner [his](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his), dictionaried with the determiners; only the standalone use (*That one's 'is*) is the pronoun [his](02-possessive.md#his).

What a listener actually receives in place of *him*, *her*, and *his*. A learner who cannot find the pronoun in *Did you tell him?* is not missing it — there is no /h/ there to hear.

1. Tell *'im* I'll call back after five.
2. I gave *'er* the keys and she drove off before I could explain the gate code.
3. Get *'im* out of the sun and bring 'im some water.
4. What's *'is* name — the guy who used to run the Denver office?
5. Let *'er* rip. (a fixed phrase, where the referent is a machine, not a woman)
6. Somebody call *'im* and find out whether the truck ever left the yard.
7. Ask *'er* whether she wants the last of the coffee.

### it

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; frequently ends in an unreleased or glottal [ɪʔ] before a pause (*Forget it* [fɚˈɡɛɾ ɪʔ]). Flapping across the boundary is normal: *get it* /ˈɡɛɾɪt/, *put it* /ˈpʊɾɪt/. The contraction *it's* is /ɪts/.
**Русский:** он, она, оно — о неодушевлённом и о животных; также формальное подлежащее без перевода
**Type:** personal pronoun; also dummy (expletive) pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, neuter. Identical in both cases; no plural of its own — the plural is [they](#they)/[them](#them).
**Refers to:** things, places, abstractions, substances, and animals whose sex is unknown or irrelevant; also a whole preceding clause or situation (*The vendor missed three deadlines, and **it** cost us the contract*). **It cannot refer to a specific human being** except in identification questions before the person is known (*Who is **it**? — Someone's at the door*) — and except where a person has chosen *it/its* as their own pronoun, which is a separate entry: [it (as a chosen personal pronoun)](#it-as-a-chosen-personal-pronoun). Using *it* for a baby was once ordinary and now reads as cold; use *he*, *she*, or *they*. Traditional English called ships, boats, cars, and countries *she*; American style has largely moved to *it*, and Lloyd's List switched its own house style to *it* in 2002. In its dummy uses *it* refers to nothing at all — it simply fills the subject slot that English syntax insists on.
**Agreement:** third-person singular verb forms — *it **is**, it **was**, it **has**, it **does**, it **seems***. This holds even in the dummy uses, and even when what follows is plural: *It **is** the deadlines that worry me*, though ***It's** the deadlines* is what people say. A collective noun takes *it* in American English (*The committee released **its** findings*) where British English often takes *they*.
**Pattern:** *It* [verb] &middot; [verb] *it* &middot; [preposition] *it* &middot; *It is* [adjective] *that* [clause] &middot; *It was* [focused element] *that/who* [clause] &middot; [verb] *it* [adjective] *that* [clause] &middot; *It's* [weather / time / distance]
**Contrast:** **it** (possessive-free pronoun) vs. **its** (possessive determiner, no apostrophe, *the dog and its bone*) vs. **it's** (contraction of *it is* or *it has*). The test never fails: if you can expand the word to *it is* or *it has*, write *it's*; otherwise write *its*. Against dummy *there*: both fill an empty subject slot, but *there* introduces the existence of something and hands agreement to the noun that follows (*There **are** two options*), while *it* keeps singular agreement always (*It **is** two miles*). Against [they](#they): a plural antecedent takes *they*, and in American English a collective noun takes *it*.

*It* does more distinct jobs than any other pronoun in English, and only the first of them involves referring to anything.

**1. Referential.** Stands for a thing, animal, or abstraction already named. *The package arrived. **It** was damaged.*

**2. Clausal / situational.** Points back at an entire clause or an unnamed state of affairs. *She quit without notice, and **it** put the whole project three weeks behind.*

**3. Dummy subject.** Weather, time, date, distance, temperature, and ambient conditions, where there is no subject to be had. ***It** is snowing. **It** is almost midnight. **It** is three miles to the trailhead.*

**4. Anticipatory subject.** Holds the subject slot while the real subject, a clause, waits at the end. ***It** is obvious that she was right* (= *That she was right is obvious*).

**5. Anticipatory object.** The same trick in object position, after verbs like *find, make, think, consider*. *I find **it** strange that nobody called.*

**6. Cleft focus.** Splits a sentence to put the spotlight on one element. ***It** was Daniel who signed the lease.*

**7. Empty idiomatic object.** A fixed object with no referent at all: *make it, cool it, wing it, beat it, knock it off, lord it over someone, rough it, hoof it.*

1. The printer jammed again this morning, and *it* took an hour to clear.
2. *It* has been raining since Tuesday and the field is unplayable.
3. *It* is unfortunate that the deadline fell on a holiday.
4. I find *it* strange that nobody from the office has called.
5. *It* was in Miami, not Atlanta, that they signed the agreement.
6. Don't overthink the presentation — just wing *it*.
7. Who is *it*? I wasn't expecting anyone.
8. The committee published *its* findings, and *it* stands by them.

### me

**Pronunciation:** /mi/ &middot; *MEE* &middot; no schwa weak form — unstressed *me* simply shortens (*Tell me* /ˈtɛl mi/, *gimme* /ˈɡɪmi/). The vowel stays /i/ in every position.
**Русский:** меня, мне, мной — объектная форма I
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person singular. Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** the speaker or writer, and nobody else — the same referent as [I](#i), in a different grammatical slot.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. When *me* follows *be*, the verb agrees with the subject: ***It's** me*, ***That was** me*. If a relative clause follows, the two registers split cleanly: *It's **me** who's responsible* (normal American) vs. *It is **I** who **am** responsible* (formal, and note the verb goes to *am*).
**Pattern:** [verb] *me* &middot; [verb] *me* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *me* &middot; [name] *and me* &middot; *It's me* &middot; *than me* &middot; *Me* — (standalone answer) &middot; *Me,* [clause] (topic)
**Contrast:** [I](#i) is subject-only, *me* takes everything else. The two errors are mirror images. ✗*Between you and I* / ✗*Send it to Karen and I* / ✗*They invited my wife and I* — every one of those slots is an object slot (the first two after a preposition, the third a direct object), so the *I* is pure hypercorrection. ✗*Me and him went to the concert* — a subject slot takes the subject form. The **deletion test** settles both — remove the other person and read the sentence aloud. *He gave it to Bob and ___* → *He gave it to **me*** ✓, *He gave it to **I*** ✗. *They invited my wife and ___* → *They invited **me*** ✓, *They invited **I*** ✗. ***I** went to the concert* ✓, ***Me** went to the concert* ✗. Against *myself*: ✗*Please contact Sara or myself* — a reflexive needs a matching subject in the same clause, and *myself* is not a more polite *me*. One thing to unlearn: ***It's me* is standard English**, not an error. *It is I* is grammatically defensible and sounds stilted outside set phrases.

The speaker's object form, and the default first-person form everywhere except the subject slot — including as a one-word answer, after *be*, and as a fronted topic.

1. The manager called *me* into her office before I'd even taken my coat off.
2. Give the extra ticket to Priya and *me*.
3. "Who wants the last slice?" — "*Me*."
4. Somebody knocked. "It's *me*," she said through the door.
5. Between you and *me*, that deal was dead a month ago.
6. Nobody warned *me* about the construction on Route 9.
7. *Me*, I'd have waited another week before signing anything.

### she

**Pronunciation:** /ʃi/ &middot; *SHEE* &middot; no schwa weak form and no /h/ to drop — unstressed *she* just shortens (*Did she* /ˈdɪd ʃi/, often [ˈdɪtʃi] with assimilation). Contractions: *she's* /ʃiz/, *she'll* /ʃil/, *she'd* /ʃid/.
**Русский:** она — о женщине или о животном, чей пол известен; о предметах — it
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, feminine
**Refers to:** a female human being, or an animal whose sex is known and matters. Not things, with one shrinking exception: ships, boats, cars, engines, and countries were traditionally *she* in English, and that convention now sounds dated in American prose — most style guides and most speakers use *it*. Hurricanes carried only female names until 1979 and alternating names since; either way a storm is *it*.
**Agreement:** third-person singular verb forms — *she **is**, she **was**, she **has**, she **does**, she **goes***. Never *she are*.
**Pattern:** *She* [verb] &middot; *She* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *she* [verb]? &middot; [name] *and she* [plural verb] &middot; *than she* [auxiliary] &middot; *This is she* (telephone formula)
**Contrast:** *She* is the subject form, [her](#her) the object form: *She called me* / *I called her*. In a coordination, test each half alone — *Dana and **she** were hired the same week* passes because *She was hired* passes. Do not confuse the subject *she* with the possessive determiner *her* or the standalone possessive *hers*. The one place *she* survives against the tide is the telephone formula *This is she* — a fossil of the old rule requiring the subject case after *be*, and the only context where most Americans still produce it.

The feminine third-person subject form. It heads a clause, inverts in questions, and joins coordinated subjects.

1. *She* runs the entire East Coast operation out of a two-room office.
2. When did *she* get back from Denver?
3. *She* and her husband bought the building in 2019.
4. *She* hasn't returned any of my calls this week.
5. My mother reads faster than *she* does, but retains less.
6. "May I speak with Dr. Alvarez?" — "This is *she*."

**See also:** [her](#her), [he](#he), [they](#they), [it](#it), [hers](02-possessive.md#hers), [herself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#herself), [this is he / this is she](04-demonstrative.md#this-is-he--this-is-she)

### the two of us

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈtu əv ʌs/ &middot; *thuh-TOO-uv-uss* &middot; one stress, on the numeral, and everything around it reduces — *the* is /ðə/, *of* falls to /ə/ or disappears, and the whole phrase runs together as [ðə ˈtuəvəs]. ***The both of us*** is /ðə ˈboʊθ əv ʌs/ (*thuh-BOHTH-uv-uss*) and ***the three of them*** /ðə ˈθri əv ðəm/ (*thuh-THREE-uv-thum*).
**Русский:** мы вдвоём, мы двое — точное указание числа группы: the three of you «вы втроём»
**Type:** pronoun phrase (partitive) — *the* + a numeral or *both* + *of* + a personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike; the phrase takes whatever slot it stands in, and the pronoun inside it is **always** the object form — *of us*, *of you*, *of them*, never ✗*of we* or ✗*of they* &middot; **Register:** neutral, and usable in any register including a contract
**Person / number:** first, second, or third person, and always plural in reference — *the two of us*, *the three of you*, *the four of them*. The numeral is doing arithmetic: it names the size of the group exactly, which is the one thing a bare personal pronoun cannot do.
**Refers to:** a specific group already identified, counted out in full. *The two of us* is the speaker plus one named or understood other; *the three of them* is a group of exactly three. It never introduces a group — the people have to be recoverable from context. People are the ordinary referent; *the two of them* can point at things (*I bought two lamps and returned the two of them*), but there it reads as clumsy beside plain *both*.
**Agreement:** **plural verb forms**, and the verb agrees with the numeral rather than with the pronoun inside: *The two of us **are** driving up*, *The three of them **were** waiting at the gate*, *The both of us **were** out sick*. Never ✗*The two of us is*. The reflexive matches too — *the two of us blamed **ourselves***, *the three of you should help **yourselves***.
**Pattern:** *the* [numeral] *of us/you/them* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *the* [numeral] *of us/you/them* &middot; [preposition] *the* [numeral] *of us/you/them* &middot; *just the two of us* &middot; *between the two of us* &middot; *the both of us*
**Variants:** *the three of us*, *the four of them*, *the two of you*, and on up through the numerals; ***the both of us***, with *both* in the numeral slot, is the one member of the family that carries a register marking — it is informal, commonest in Southern and Irish-influenced American speech, and some editors cut the *the*; plain *both of us*, *all three of us*, and *the two of us* are unmarked everywhere
**Contrast:** Against bare [we](#we), [you](#you), and [them](#them), which leave the number open. That is not a small difference: the [we](#we) entry names this phrase as the ordinary repair for the inclusive/exclusive ambiguity English has no grammar for — *We're going to lunch* is either an invitation or a dismissal, while *The two of us are going to lunch* is neither, because it has already said who. Against [you two](#you-two): same count, same people, different frame — *you two* is the **appositive** (pronoun + numeral), *the two of you* the **partitive** (*the* + numeral + *of* + pronoun). Both are standard; the partitive is a shade more formal on the page, and the appositive is the natural vocative (*You two, over here* beats *The two of you, over here*). Against *both of us* and *all of us*, which are the same construction with a quantifier instead of a numeral and are dictionaried with the [quantifying pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#both). And the case rule inside the phrase is absolute: *of* is a preposition, so what follows it is an object form, whatever the phrase as a whole is doing in the sentence — ***The two of us** signed* has a subject-slot phrase built on the object pronoun *us*, and that is correct.

The only way English has of saying how many people a *we*, a *you*, or a *they* covers. It is a phrase rather than a word, but it fills a pronoun's slot, takes a pronoun's agreement, and does a job no single pronoun in the language does.

1. *The two of us* signed the lease in March and moved in three weeks later.
2. The manager called *the three of us* into her office before we'd taken our coats off.
3. This stays between *the two of us* — don't bring it up at the meeting.
4. *The four of them* have been working the night shift since the plant reopened.
5. Can *the two of you* be at the airport by six, or should I go on ahead?
6. It's just *the two of us* for dinner tonight, so don't set the long table.
7. *The both of us* were out sick that week, which is why nobody answered the phone. (informal; *both of us* is the unmarked form)

### them

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛm/ &middot; *THEM* &middot; weak /ðəm/, reducing further to /əm/ and to a syllabic /m̩/ — *get them* /ˈɡɛɾəm/, *tell them* /ˈtɛləm/, *give them* /ˈɡɪvəm/. Written informally as ***'em*** ([entry](#em)), which is not a lazy spelling of *them* but the direct descendant of Middle English *hem* (Old English *heom*) — the native object pronoun that the Norse-borrowed *them* replaced on the page while it survived unstressed in the mouth.
**Русский:** их, им — объектная форма they; также об одном лице неизвестного или небинарного пола
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; plural, and also singular when it is the object form of singular [they](#they). Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** any plural antecedent — people, animals, things, abstractions, mixed groups. Also a single person of unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary gender (*Somebody left their umbrella; put it aside for **them***). Also unspecified authorities or institutions in the generic sense (*I called the city and **they** put me on hold; I never got **them** back on the line*).
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. After *be*, the verb agrees with the subject: *It was **them***, *That's **them** now*. In the quantifier patterns the verb agrees with the quantifier, not with *them*: *Both of them **are** here*, *Each of them **is** here*, and *none of them* takes **either** a singular or a plural verb — *None of them **fits*** (not one does) and *None of them **fit*** (no members do) are both standard, the plural being the older and commoner choice. See [none](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#none).
**Pattern:** [verb] *them* &middot; [verb] *them* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *them* &middot; *all/both/some/none of them* &middot; [name] *and them* &middot; *That's them*
**Contrast:** [They](#they) is the subject, *them* the object: *They called me* / *I called them*. Do not confuse the pronoun *them* with the **nonstandard demonstrative** *them* (*them apples*, *them boys*, *Them are the ones*) — that use is **nonstandard**, widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech and marked wrong in all edited writing, and the standard form is *those*: [them (demonstrative pronoun)](04-demonstrative.md#them), [them (demonstrative determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them), [those](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#those). Keep the **use** separate from the **pronunciation**: the reduced /əm/ of *I saw 'em* carries no dialect marking at all and is ordinary General American. Against *their/there/they're*: *them* is never a possessive; the possessive determiner is *their* and the standalone possessive is *theirs*, neither of which takes an apostrophe.

The object form of *they*, for plural referents and for singular *they* alike. It is also the form that survives in the reduced clitic *'em*, which is what you will actually hear in nearly every casual sentence.

1. I mailed *them* the signed contract on Tuesday.
2. We ran into *them* at the farmers' market on Saturday.
3. Ask *them* to call me back after four.
4. None of *them* fit, so I returned the whole box.
5. That's *them* pulling into the driveway now.
6. Somebody left an umbrella in the lobby — I'll hold it for *them*.
7. Just tell 'em the shipment is delayed.

### they

**Pronunciation:** /ðeɪ/ &middot; *THAY* &middot; no schwa weak form; the diphthong shortens toward [ðe] when unstressed. The contractions matter more than the reduction: *they're* is /ðɛr/ — identical to *there* and *their* in General American, and the one contraction that changes the vowel outright — while *they'll* /ðeɪl/, *they've* /ðeɪv/, and *they'd* /ðeɪd/ keep the diphthong, shortening it to [ðe-] only when unstressed.
**Русский:** они — также об одном лице неизвестного или небинарного пола, но глагол всегда во мн. ч.
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; plural, and also singular in the epicene use. Invariant for gender in every use.
**Refers to:** any plural antecedent, human or not (*The reports arrived late; **they** were incomplete*). Also a single person whose gender is unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary. Also unidentified people in general (***They** say the winter will be mild*) and unnamed institutions (***They** raised the rates again*).
**Agreement:** **always plural verb forms, including in the singular use** — *they **are**, they **were**, they **have**, they **do***. ✗*They is* and ✗*they was* are nonstandard in every variety of edited English, and singular *they* does not change this: *Alex said **they were** coming*, never *they was coming*. The reflexive is *themselves*, or *themself* when the referent is explicitly one person.
**Pattern:** *They* [plural verb] &middot; *They* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *they* [verb]? &middot; *They say that* [clause] &middot; [singular antecedent] … *they* [plural verb]
**Contrast:** *They* is the subject, [them](#them) the object. The homophone set is the most-misspelled trio in English: **their** (possessive determiner — *their car*), **there** (place, or the dummy subject — *there is*), **they're** (contraction of *they are*). All three are /ðɛr/ in speech; only the spelling separates them. Against *he or she*: **singular *they* is standard American English, including in edited prose**, and it is attested continuously from the late 1300s — Chaucer, Shakespeare, Austen — so it is not a recent invention. Merriam-Webster had long recorded the generic singular and added the specific nonbinary sense in 2019. The major American style guides accept it, with different scope: the APA *Publication Manual* endorses it outright and **requires** it for a person who uses it; the *MLA Handbook* accepts it generally; the *Chicago Manual of Style* accepts it without reservation for a person whose pronoun it is, and for a generic antecedent in all but the most formal registers; the *AP Stylebook* accepts it for people who use it and, in limited cases, generically, while still preferring a recast where one is natural. *He or she* now reads as clunky, and generic *he* reads as dated. What a conservative editor may still flag is inconsistency, not *they* itself: pick one strategy per passage and keep it.

The plural third-person subject form, and the general-purpose third-person singular when gender is unknown or not to be specified.

**1. Plural referential.** Points back at a plural noun phrase of any kind. *The files were water-damaged, so **they** had to be rescanned.*

**2. Singular epicene.** One person of unknown or unspecified gender. *If a customer complains, ask **them** to fill out a form.*

**3. Singular specific.** One named person whose pronouns are *they*. *Alex said **they** would email the draft tonight.*

**4. Generic / hearsay.** Unidentified people in general. ***They** say it never rains here in July.*

**5. Institutional.** An organization the speaker does not bother to name. ***They** changed the parking rules again.*

1. The contractors finished early, and *they* left the site spotless.
2. *They* have been renovating that building for almost two years.
3. Whoever ordered this should confirm that *they* still want it.
4. Alex texted to say *they* were running late.
5. *They* say the bridge will be closed all summer.
6. Are *they* still planning to open the second location?
7. Every applicant must sign the waiver before *they* can start.

### us

**Pronunciation:** /ʌs/ &middot; *UHSS* &middot; weak /əs/, reducing to bare /s/ in the fixed contraction *let's* /lɛts/ (= *let us*). Flapping across the boundary is normal: *got us* /ˈɡɑɾəs/.
**Русский:** нас, нам, нами — объектная форма we; включён ли собеседник, грамматически не показано
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person plural. Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** the speaker together with at least one other person. Whether the listener is included is **not marked grammatically** — English has no separate inclusive and exclusive forms, so *He invited us* leaves it to context whether you are in the group.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. After *be*, the verb agrees with the subject: *It was **us***, *That's **us** in the back row*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *us* &middot; [verb] *us* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *us* &middot; *all/both/some/none of us* &middot; *us* [plural noun] (appositive) &middot; *Let's* [verb] &middot; *than us*
**Contrast:** [We](#we) is the subject, *us* the object: *We hired them* / *They hired us*. The appositive pair is worth memorizing because both forms are correct in different slots: ***We** editors work late* (subject) but *They gave **us** editors a deadline* (object) — delete the noun and the case is obvious. ✗*Us guys went to the game* is nonstandard for the same reason. *Let's* is *let us* contracted, and the doubled ✗*let's us go* is dialectal. In British and Irish English *us* can informally mean *me* (*Give us a minute*); this is marginal in American speech and best avoided in writing.

The object form of *we*, filling every non-subject slot. It also appears in the appositive pattern *us* + noun, and inside the fossilized imperative *let's*.

1. The neighbors invited *us* to dinner on Sunday.
2. Give *us* five more minutes and we'll have an answer.
3. The rezoning affects all of *us* on this side of the street.
4. They handed *us* editors a deadline nobody could have met.
5. Nobody had told *us* that the road was closed at the county line.
6. *Let's* get out of here before the lot fills up.

### we

**Pronunciation:** /wi/ &middot; *WEE* &middot; no schwa weak form; the vowel shortens when unstressed. The contractions do reduce: *we're* is often /wɚ/ in fast speech, which is why *we're* and *were* collide (*We're going* [wɚ ˈɡoʊɪn]); *we'll* /wil/ or /wɪl/, *we've* /wiv/, *we'd* /wid/.
**Русский:** мы — говорящий и другие; «мы с тобой» и «мы без тебя» не различаются
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person plural. Invariant for gender.
**Refers to:** the speaker plus at least one other. English does **not** grammatically distinguish inclusive *we* (speaker + listener) from exclusive *we* (speaker + others, not you), unlike Tagalog, Cherokee, or Mandarin's *zánmen*. *We're going to lunch* is genuinely ambiguous between an invitation and an announcement, and speakers disambiguate with a following phrase — *we all*, *the two of us*, *you and I*.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *we **are**, we **were**, we **have**, we **do***, and the bare present with no *-s* (*we go*). This holds even in the singular-reference uses: the royal *we* and the editorial *we* still take *we are*, never *we is*.
**Pattern:** *We* [plural verb] &middot; *We* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *we* [verb]? &middot; *We* [plural noun] [verb] (appositive) &middot; *Let's* [verb] &middot; *than we* [auxiliary]
**Contrast:** *We* is the subject, [us](#us) the object. The appositive test is the one to keep: ***We** editors work late* / *They gave **us** editors a deadline* — delete the noun and the case falls out. Against the possessives: *our* precedes a noun (*our office*), *ours* stands alone (*The corner office is ours*), and neither takes an apostrophe.

Beyond its ordinary plural sense, *we* carries five special uses. Four of them — the editorial, the authorial, the royal, and the nurse's *we* — are plural in grammar but singular in what they actually refer to; the fifth, the generic, is wider than any group the speaker could point at. All six take a plural verb, without exception: the royal *we* and the editorial *we* are *we are*, never *we is*.

**1. Ordinary plural.** The speaker and at least one other. ***We** signed the lease in March.*

**2. Generic / universal.** Humanity, or everyone in a shared situation. ***We** still don't know what causes most migraines.*

**3. Editorial.** A publication or an institution speaking in its own voice, whoever typed the sentence: an unsigned newspaper editorial (***We** endorse the bond measure*), a magazine's house style, a court speaking for itself in an opinion (***We** granted certiorari to resolve the conflict among the circuits*). One person writes it; the institution says it, which is why the byline is absent and the verb is still plural.

**4. Authorial.** A single author using the plural in academic, scientific, or technical prose. *In this paper **we** argue that the effect is overstated.* It is a convention of the genre rather than a claim about how many people did the work, and the mathematician's version of it — ***we** now differentiate both sides* — sweeps the reader in alongside the author. Several American style guides, the APA manual among them, now tell a sole author to write *I*.

**5. Royal (the majestic plural).** A monarch, and formerly a pope, speaking as the institution rather than as a person. ***We** are not amused*, the line traditionally attributed to Queen Victoria and probably never said by her. Archaic in American English outside historical writing, satire, and the joke about having a mouse in one's pocket.

**6. The nurse's we (the patronizing or paternal we).** A caregiver, teacher, or clinician using *we* for a *you* that is doing all the work. *How are **we** feeling today?* *Are **we** ready to try a few steps?* Warm and inclusive when the rapport is there, condescending when it isn't — many patients dislike it, and it is worth recognizing rather than adopting.

1. *We* signed the lease in March and moved in three weeks later.
2. Are *we* still meeting at eight, or has that shifted?
3. *We* still don't understand why the effect disappears at low temperatures.
4. *We* Midwesterners have a complicated relationship with February.
5. In this paper *we* argue that the reported gains were an artifact of the sampling.
6. *We* endorse the bond measure and urge a yes vote in November. (the paper's editorial, written by one person and signed by none)
7. *We* granted certiorari to resolve a conflict among the circuits. (a court speaking as itself)
8. "And how are *we* feeling this morning?" the nurse asked, reaching for the chart.
9. *We* have not yet decided whether to grant the petition.

**See also:** [us](#us), [I](#i), [they](#they), [ours](02-possessive.md#ours), [ourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#ourselves), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one)

### ya

**Pronunciation:** /jə/ &middot; *yuh* &middot; never stressed and with no strong form of its own — if the word carries stress it is *you* /ju/. After /t/ or /d/ it does not surface as a separate syllable at all but fuses with the word before it: *did ya* /ˈdɪdʒə/, *don't ya* /ˈdoʊntʃə/, *got ya* → *gotcha* /ˈɡɑtʃə/, *what do ya* → *whaddaya* /ˈwʌɾəjə/.
**Русский:** ты, вы — редуцированное you; разг. написание, не диалект
**Type:** personal pronoun, reduced (weak form) &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech, informal in writing — normal in dialogue, lyrics, advertising copy, and transcription, out of place in a formal document
**Person / number:** second person, **singular and plural, subject and object alike** — it inherits every cell of [you](#you) and, like *you*, marks neither number nor case.
**Refers to:** exactly what [you](#you) refers to — the person or people being addressed, and, in the generic sense, anybody at all (*Ya never know*). It carries **no dialect marking of its own**: /jə/ is what educated General American speakers produce for unstressed *you* in ordinary conversation, and the spelling *ya* only writes that down. It is not Southern, not working-class, and not a plural.
**Agreement:** **plural verb forms, even for one person**, exactly as with *you* — *ya **are**, ya **were**, ya **have**, ya **do***, never ✗*ya is*. Reducing the pronoun changes nothing about the conjugation.
**Pattern:** [verb] *ya* &middot; [preposition] *ya* &middot; *Ya* [plural verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *ya* [verb]? &middot; fixed phrases: *See ya*, *Catch ya later*, *How ya doin'?*, *Ya know?*, *Gotcha*
**Contrast:** Against [you](#you): the same word in its weak form, differing in *writing* only — you may always write *you* where you hear *ya*, and in edited prose you generally should, outside quoted speech. Against ['em](#em): the same relationship, one person up the paradigm, except that *'em* is a distinct word historically while *ya* is nothing but *you* at speed. Against [y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse), and [you'uns](#youuns): those are **built** forms — plural-only, and regionally or socially marked; *ya* is neither built nor plural nor marked. And it is never a possessive: the reduced possessive is *yer*/*your* /jɚ/ (*Watch yer step*), and the reduced *you are* is *y'r* or plain *you're*.

The weak form of *you*, written down. A learner listening for /ju/ and hearing nothing has heard /jə/ perfectly well without recognizing it — and most often has not even heard that, because the sound has been swallowed into the /dʒ/ or /tʃ/ at the end of the previous word.

1. *See ya* Monday — I'll leave the keys at the front desk.
2. I'll call *ya* as soon as the flight lands.
3. *Ya* never know what the market is going to do.
4. This one's for *ya*, and don't say I never gave you anything.
5. How *ya* doin'? I haven't seen you since the spring.
6. Did anybody tell *ya* the meeting moved to Thursday?
7. *Ya* got a minute, or should I come back later?

**See also:** [you](#you), ['em](#em), [y'all](#yall), [you all](#you-all), [yours](02-possessive.md#yours), [your (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your), [yourself, yourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#yourself)

### you

**Pronunciation:** /ju/ &middot; *YOO* &middot; weak /jə/ and /jʊ/ — and, more than any other pronoun, *you* fuses with the word before it: *did you* /ˈdɪdʒə/, *don't you* /ˈdoʊntʃə/, *would you* /ˈwʊdʒə/, *what do you* → *whaddaya* /ˈwʌɾəjə/, *got you* → *gotcha* /ˈɡɑtʃə/. Learners who cannot hear *you* are usually hearing it perfectly well as the /dʒ/ or /tʃ/ at the end of the previous word. The weak form is spelled ***ya*** in dialogue and casual writing (*See ya*, *How ya doin'?*, *Thank ya*) — that spelling records the ordinary reduction of standard *you* and marks no region and no dialect; it is informal on the page, not nonstandard in the mouth. It has its own entry: [ya](#ya).
**Русский:** ты, вы — к одному и ко многим; различия ты/вы и ед./мн. ч. в английском нет
**Type:** personal pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** second person, **singular and plural, subject and object — one form for all four**. Only the reflexive distinguishes number: *yourself* (one person) vs. *yourselves* (more than one).
**Refers to:** the person or people being addressed. Also, in the generic sense, anybody at all (*You never know what the market will do*), where it is the informal counterpart of formal *one*.
**Agreement:** **always plural verb forms, even for one person** — *you **are**, you **were**, you **have**, you **do***, never ✗*you is*. This is a historical fossil: *you* was originally the plural, and it drove out the singular *thou* in the seventeenth century, taking the plural verb with it. ✗*You was* was ordinary educated English in the eighteenth century and survives today as a systematic feature of Southern American English, African American English, and working-class varieties nationwide, alongside *we was* and *they was*; all of them are **nonstandard** in edited English. The counterfactual is *if you were*.
**Pattern:** *You* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you* &middot; [preposition] *you* &middot; [auxiliary] *you* [verb]? &middot; *You* [verb]! (emphatic imperative) &middot; *you* [plural noun] (appositive: *you people*) &middot; *You never know* (generic)
**Contrast:** The apostrophe pair is the trap: **your** is the possessive determiner (*your keys*), **you're** is the contraction of *you are* (*you're late*), and **yours** — no apostrophe, ever — is the standalone possessive (*the blue one is yours*). Expand it: if *you are* fits, write *you're*. Against *one*: generic *you* is normal American English in speech and ordinary writing (*You have to file by April 15*), while *one* is the formal register (*One must file by April 15*) — the error is drifting between them in a single passage. Against the plural workarounds below: none of *y'all, you guys, youse, yinz* belongs in formal writing; when the plural genuinely needs marking there, write *all of you*, *you all*, *both of you*, or name the group.

One word doing four jobs, which is why American English keeps inventing plural forms for it. *You* is the subject, the object, the singular, and the plural, and nothing in the form itself tells you which.

**1. Singular addressee.** One person. ***You** left your badge on the counter.*

**2. Plural addressee.** A group, unmarked. ***You** all need to sign in at the front desk.*

**3. Generic.** Anyone, people in general. ***You** can't get a permit without an appointment.*

**4. Emphatic / vocative in imperatives.** Normally the imperative has no subject, but *you* can be inserted for contrast or force. ***You** go first. Don't **you** touch that.*

1. *You* left your badge on the kitchen counter again.
2. Did *you* two ever hear back from the insurance company?
3. I'll email *you* the revised numbers before Friday.
4. *You* can't get a building permit in this county without an appointment.
5. This one is for *you* and this one is for your sister.
6. *You* go first — I'll hold the door.
7. If *you* were in charge, what would you change?

**See also:** [ya](#ya), [yours](02-possessive.md#yours), [your (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your), [yourself, yourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#yourself), [y'all](#yall), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [youse](#youse), [you'uns](#youuns), [thou](#thou), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one)

### you two

**Pronunciation:** /juˈtu/ &middot; *yoo-TOO* &middot; weak /jəˈtu/ — *yə-TOO*; the stress falls on the numeral and the two words are said as one unit, exactly as in [you guys](#you-guys). ***You both*** is /juˈboʊθ/ (*yoo-BOHTH*) and ***you three*** /juˈθri/ (*yoo-THREE*).
**Русский:** вы двое, вы оба — you с числительным; нейтрально, годится и для письма
**Type:** personal pronoun (phrasal) — the appositive [you](#you) + a numeral, the same frame as *you guys*, *you people*, and *you editors* &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike, like *you* itself &middot; **Register:** neutral — with [you all](#you-all), one of the only two plural-*you* forms that survive editing unchanged; it is not regional, not informal, and not gendered
**Person / number:** second person plural, and **exactly counted**. *You two* is two people and *you three* is three, which is what separates this frame from every other plural *you* in the file: *y'all*, *you guys*, *youse*, *you'uns*, and *you all* all leave the number open.
**Refers to:** the people being addressed, counted. In the vocative it does a second job — singling a pair out of a larger room (*You two, over here*) — and in that use the count is doing the pointing. It is not a group label the way *you guys* is, and it carries none of the freight of [you people](#you-people).
**Agreement:** **plural verb forms** — *you two **are**, you two **were**, you two **have**, you two **do***, never ✗*you two is*. Since the referent is more than one, the reflexive is ***yourselves***, never ✗*yourself*: *Help **yourselves** to the coffee.*
**Pattern:** *You two* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you two* &middot; [preposition] *you two* &middot; *You two!* (vocative) &middot; *you both* &middot; *you three*, *you four* &middot; *you two's* [noun] (informal possessive)
**Variants:** *you both*, *you three*, *you four*, and on up; the partitive equivalents [*the two of you*, *the three of you*](#the-two-of-us) and *both of you*; the possessive *you two's* (*Is that you two's car?*) is spoken rather than written, and edited prose recasts it as *your* or *the two of you*
**Contrast:** Same appositive frame as [you guys](#you-guys) and [you people](#you-people) — [you](#you) plus a nominal, taking the case of its slot, exactly like the *us editors* pattern under [us](#us). What makes it different is that it is **unmarked**: *you guys* is informal and under pressure for being gendered, *you people* lands as hostile, [y'all](#yall) and [youse](#youse) are regional, and *you two* is none of these. Against the partitive [the two of you](#the-two-of-us): same count and same people, both standard, and the choice is register and position — the partitive reads slightly more formally as a subject, the appositive is what a speaker actually says in the vocative. Against singular *you*: the number is the whole point, so *You two need to sign* says something *You need to sign* does not, and it is the cheapest way to disambiguate an American *you* without reaching for a regional form.

The plural *you* that a document can use. English lost its second-person plural when [thou](#thou) died out, and every regional replacement in the next section is marked in some way; *you* plus a numeral is the repair that costs nothing, and it has the bonus of saying exactly how many.

1. Did *you two* ever hear back from the insurance company?
2. I'll meet *you two* at the gate about a half hour before boarding.
3. *You two* — over here, please. The rest can wait in the hall.
4. Have *you both* signed the acknowledgment at the bottom of page three?
5. This applies to *you three* as well, not just to the two who were in the room.
6. Help *yourselves* to the coffee; there's more than enough for *you two*.
7. Is that *you two's* car in the loading zone? (informal; in writing, *the two of you*)

**See also:** [you](#you), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [you people](#you-people), [the two of us](#the-two-of-us), [y'all](#yall), [us](#us), [yourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#yourselves)

## Regional, nonstandard, and archaic forms

With one exception named below, nothing in this section is standard written American English, and none of it should appear in formal writing. The forms are here because a learner will meet every one of them — *y'all* and *you guys* daily, *thou* and *ye* in scripture, hymns, and Shakespeare — and needs to recognize what they mean and who says them. Almost every one exists for the same reason: **standard English has no plural *you*, and speakers keep building one.** Two are exceptions to that. [We'uns](#weuns) fills no gap at all — *we* was never ambiguous — and is built by analogy with *you'uns* to mark a boundary rather than a number; and [yo](#yo) fills a different gap altogether, the missing gender-neutral third-person singular, which is the hole singular *they* fills for everybody else.

Two items here are odd in a different way, and neither is nonstandard. [You all](#you-all) is the exception to the paragraph above: it is nationwide, unmarked, and — with the counted [you two](#you-two), which is filed with the standard forms — one of the only two plural-*you* forms that survive editing unchanged. It sits here because it is the phrase *y'all* contracted from and reads most naturally beside it, not because an editor would strike it. [You people](#you-people) is likewise nationwide rather than regional, and perfectly grammatical — the ordinary appositive *you* plus a noun. It sits in this section because of what it does to a listener rather than because an editor would mark it wrong.

**Read the labels literally.** *Archaic* means no living American produces it outside quotation, ritual, or pastiche. *Regional* means real speakers use it as their ordinary form in a place that can be named, and the name is given. *Nonstandard* means an editor, a teacher, or a standardized test will mark it wrong however common it is in speech. The one form drifting out of this section is *y'all*, which is fully standard **within** Southern American English and African American English and is spreading nationally as ordinary informal speech. These forms are treated at greater length in [archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns](10-archaic-dialectal.md).

### thee

**Pronunciation:** /ði/ &middot; *THEE* &middot; no weak form in modern liturgical reading
**Русский:** тебя, тебе — устар., объектная форма thou; книжн., церк., поэт.
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** archaic, liturgical, poetic
**Person / number:** second person singular
**Refers to:** one person being addressed — historically an intimate, a subordinate, a child, or God. Never a group; the plural was [ye](#ye) and later *you*.
**Agreement:** none of its own as an object. In Quaker plain speech, where *thee* was extended to subject position, it took the **third-person** singular verb: *Thee **is** right*, *Thee **has** forgotten* — not the *-est* ending that goes with [thou](#thou).
**Pattern:** [verb] *thee* &middot; [preposition] *thee* &middot; *thee* [third-person verb] (Quaker usage only)
**Contrast:** *Thou* is the subject, *thee* the object — exactly the *I/me* and *he/him* split: ***Thou** art late* / *I saw **thee***. The possessives are *thy* before a consonant, *thine* before a vowel or standing alone (*thy hand*, *thine eyes*, *the fault is thine*). Do not use *thee* as a subject when imitating Shakespeare or the King James Bible; that pattern belongs specifically to nineteenth- and twentieth-century Quaker speech, not to Early Modern English.

The object form of *thou*, driven out of general English by *you* in the seventeenth century. It survived in Quaker plain speech into the twentieth century, in the King James Bible, in hymns and prayers, and in poetry reaching for an older register.

1. I will not leave *thee*, nor forsake *thee*.
2. The Lord bless *thee* and keep *thee*.
3. How do I love *thee*? Let me count the ways.
4. Get *thee* to a nunnery.
5. The Lord make his face shine upon *thee*. (object of a preposition)
6. *Thee* is welcome to stay as long as thee likes, Friend. (Quaker usage)

**See also:** [thou](#thou), [ye](#ye), [you](#you)

### thou

**Pronunciation:** /ðaʊ/ &middot; *THOW* (rhymes with *now*)
**Русский:** ты — устар., фамильярное ед. ч. при вежливом you, как франц. tu; книжн., церк.
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** archaic, liturgical, poetic
**Person / number:** second person singular
**Refers to:** one person being addressed. In Early Modern English it was the **familiar** singular — used to intimates, children, servants, and God — while *you* was the plural and the polite singular, exactly like French *tu* and *vous*. Using *thou* to a social superior was a calculated insult; the verb *to thou* meant precisely that.
**Agreement:** takes its own verb ending, ***-est*** or ***-st***: *thou **goest**, thou **hast**, thou **art**, thou **wilt**, thou **shalt**, thou **didst**, thou **canst***. This ending exists nowhere else in the language and is the surest marker of the register.
**Pattern:** *Thou* [verb + *-est*] &middot; *Thou art* [complement] &middot; [auxiliary + *-st*] *thou* [verb]?
**Contrast:** *Thou* is the subject and [thee](#thee) the object, the same split as *he/him*. Its disappearance is why modern *you* takes plural agreement (*you are*) even for one person — *you* won, and it brought the plural verb with it. Standard American English has no trace of *thou*; the second-person singular/plural distinction it once carried is now rebuilt informally as [y'all](#yall) and [you guys](#you-guys).

The old singular subject *you*, lost from general English around 1650 and preserved now only in scripture, hymns, prayer, poetry, and deliberate archaism. It survives in living speech in parts of northern England, not in the United States.

1. *Thou* shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbor.
2. *Thou* art the man.
3. Shall I compare *thee* to a summer's day? *Thou* art more lovely and more temperate.
4. Where *wilt thou* go, and what *wilt thou* do there?
5. *Thou* hast forgotten who gave thee that ring.

**See also:** [thee](#thee), [ye](#ye), [you](#you)

### we'uns

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwiənz/ &middot; *WEE-unz* &middot; also written *we-uns*; the object form is *us'uns* /ˈʌsənz/ &middot; *USS-unz*
**Русский:** мы — диал. (Аппалачи), в противопоставлении you'uns; вне литературной нормы
**Type:** personal pronoun, regional &middot; **Case:** subject *we'uns*, object *us'uns* — the one built form in this section that keeps a case distinction &middot; **Register:** nonstandard, strongly regionally marked, receding
**Person / number:** first person plural. From *we ones*, built exactly like [you'uns](#youuns) from *you ones*, and carried by the same Scots-Irish settlement.
**Refers to:** the speaker together with the group the speaker belongs to — a family, a household, a hollow, a side — almost always in explicit or implied contrast with some other group, typically *you'uns*. That contrastive force is the whole point of the form, since standard *we* already covers the plural perfectly well.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *we'uns **are**, we'uns **were**, we'uns **have**, we'uns **do***. *Us'uns*, being an object, controls no verb at all.
**Pattern:** *We'uns* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *us'uns* &middot; [preposition] *us'uns* &middot; *we'uns and you'uns*
**Contrast:** *We'uns* is the first-person mate of [you'uns](#youuns), and the pair is the reason this section is not entirely about the second person. The two are not parallel in what they accomplish: *you'uns* fills a real hole in the standard paradigm, since English has no plural *you*, while *we'uns* fills nothing and survives on contrast alone — which is why it is receding faster. Both are Appalachian and South Midland, both are nonstandard in writing, and *we'uns* in particular reads as an imitation of the region in anyone who did not grow up with it. Against [us](#us): *us* is the standard object form and takes every slot *us'uns* takes.

The Appalachian and South Midland first-person plural, built from *we ones* on the same pattern as *you'uns*. It is the rarest of the built pronouns in this file and is now heard mainly from older speakers.

1. *We'uns* have farmed this hollow for four generations.
2. Are *you'uns* driving up, or should *we'uns* come down to meet you?
3. He never had much use for *us'uns*.
4. *We'uns* were finished before the rain started.
5. That strip of bottomland belongs to *us'uns*, not to the county.

**See also:** [you'uns](#youuns), [we](#we), [us](#us), [y'all](#yall), [we'uns (archaic and dialectal)](10-archaic-dialectal.md#weuns)

### y'all

**Pronunciation:** /jɔl/ or /jɑl/ &middot; *YAWL* &middot; one syllable; the two-syllable /jəˈɔl/ is [you all](#you-all), a different item
**Русский:** вы, вы все — южн. и афроам. англ.; часто «ты и твои», разг. вне Юга
**Type:** personal pronoun, regional &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal; standard within Southern American English and African American English, informal elsewhere
**Person / number:** second person plural. Contracted from *you all*, and the apostrophe marks the missing letters of *you* — ***y'all***, not ✗*ya'll*.
**Refers to:** the group being addressed. Often **associative** as well as plural: *y'all* can mean "you and the people who go with you" — *Is y'all coming Sunday?* said to one person can mean "you and your family." Whether *y'all* is ever truly singular is genuinely disputed; linguists document singular uses, most native speakers report only the plural and associative ones, and outsiders who use it as a singular mark themselves immediately.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *y'all **are**, y'all **were**, y'all **have***.
**Pattern:** *Y'all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *y'all* &middot; [preposition] *y'all* &middot; *all y'all* (intensified, unambiguously the whole group) &middot; *y'all's* [noun] (possessive)
**Contrast:** *Y'all* is the Southern and African American English plural *you*; [you guys](#you-guys) is the national informal equivalent; [youse](#youse) is the northeastern urban one; [you'uns](#youuns) and *yinz* are western Pennsylvanian. All four fill the gap left when [thou](#thou) died and *you* absorbed both numbers. *Y'all* is spreading well beyond the South, partly because it is the only one of the four that is not gendered and not regionally stigmatized outside its home range. Its possessive is *y'all's*; the plural-of-a-plural *all y'all* is standard within the dialect and is not redundant — it forces the reading "every one of you."

The Southern and African American English second-person plural, and the most widely adopted of the American plural-*you* forms. Within those varieties it is unremarkable, fully standard speech; outside them it is informal but no longer marked as uneducated.

1. *Y'all* coming to the cookout on Saturday?
2. I'll see *y'all* at the church at six.
3. This one's for *y'all* — don't open it till Christmas.
4. Is that *y'all's* truck blocking the driveway?
5. All *y'all* need to be on the bus by seven sharp.

**See also:** [you](#you), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [youse](#youse), [you'uns](#youuns)

### ye

**Pronunciation:** /ji/ &middot; *YEE*
**Русский:** вы — устар., мн. ч. подлежащего; книжн., библ., поэт.
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** originally subject; later subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** archaic, liturgical, poetic
**Person / number:** second person plural (later also the polite singular)
**Refers to:** the group being addressed. In the oldest layer of the system *ye* was the plural subject and *you* the plural object (*Ye shall know the truth* / *I say unto you*); by the sixteenth century the two had blurred, and *you* eventually took over both.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *ye **are**, ye **have**, ye **shall*** — never the *-est* ending, which belongs to [thou](#thou).
**Pattern:** *Ye* [plural verb] &middot; *Hear ye* &middot; [imperative] *ye*
**Contrast:** *Ye* is the plural of [thou](#thou), so the full archaic set is *thou/thee* (one person) against *ye/you* (more than one). **The *Ye* in *Ye Olde Shoppe* is a completely different word.** It is not the pronoun at all — it is the definite article *the*, written with the Old English letter thorn (þ) that early printers, lacking the type, replaced with a *y*. It was always pronounced /ðə/, and the modern spelling-pronunciation /ji ˈoʊldi/ is a joke the sign painters started.

The archaic plural subject *you*. It survives in scripture, hymns, proclamations, and set phrases, and in the mock-antique register of tavern signs.

1. *Ye* shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.
2. Gather *ye* rosebuds while *ye* may.
3. O come, all *ye* faithful.
4. Hear *ye*, hear *ye*: this court is now in session.
5. *Ye* of little faith, why are *ye* so fearful?

**See also:** [thou](#thou), [thee](#thee), [you](#you)

### yo

**Pronunciation:** /joʊ/ &middot; *YOH* &middot; no weak form — it is one short syllable and normally carries at least secondary stress. It is a perfect homophone of two other words spelled the same way: the nationwide interjection *yo* (*Yo! Over here*) and the vernacular possessive determiner *yo* (*yo mama*), which is *your* with the /r/ gone.
**Русский:** он, она — диал. (Балтимор), о присутствующем лице без указания пола; вне нормы
**Type:** personal pronoun, regional and nonstandard — a spontaneously arisen third-person singular &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** nonstandard and tightly regional — Baltimore-area vernacular, chiefly adolescent and chiefly within African American English. It is a spoken form only: it does not appear in edited writing, it is not part of adult standard speech in any region, and outside Baltimore most Americans have never heard it used this way.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral, invariant for case.
**Refers to:** one person, ordinarily one who is present or in sight — the recorded uses are overwhelmingly about somebody in the room, across the hall, or down the block, and often about somebody whose gender the speaker knows perfectly well and simply does not mark. Never a group, never a thing, and never a hypothetical or generic person.
**Agreement:** **third-person singular** verb forms — *yo **is**, yo **was**, yo **has**, yo **does***. That is the sharpest difference from singular [they](#they), which keeps a plural verb even for one person (*they **are***).
**Pattern:** *Yo* [singular verb] &middot; *Yo* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *yo* [verb]? &middot; [verb] *yo* &middot; [preposition] *yo*
**Variants:** none recorded; the study that documented it found no possessive of its own — speakers filled that slot with the ordinary *his*, *her*, or *their* (*Yo was tuckin' in **his** shirt*), which is one reason it is described as a partial paradigm rather than a full set
**Contrast:** Three different words share the spelling, and only one of them is this pronoun. The **interjection** *yo* — *Yo, hold the door* — is nationwide, informal, and fills no noun-phrase slot at all. And *yo* meaning *your* precedes a noun, which makes it a **determiner**, not a pronoun: *yo car* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your). Against singular [they](#they): the same job, a different verb (*yo is* against *they are*), and an incomparably smaller reach — *they* is standard nationwide and centuries old, *yo* is a local vernacular form used by a few thousand speakers in one city. Against the [neopronouns](#neopronouns-and-chosen-pronoun-sets): those are proposals, adopted deliberately by people who state them, and *yo* is the opposite case — nobody coined it, nobody advocates for it, and no speaker announces it. That is exactly why it drew attention. Dennis Baron's survey of the record turns up more than two hundred invented gender-neutral pronouns since the 1780s and not one general success; *yo* arrived on its own. It is still a form to recognize rather than to produce: it belongs in no document, and an outsider using it marks themselves the way *yinz* does outside Pittsburgh.

Linguists working in Baltimore middle schools in the mid-2000s noticed students using *yo* where standard English needs *he* or *she*, and described it in *American Speech* in 2007. It remains local, adolescent, and spoken, and it is the rarest thing in this whole file: a gender-neutral third-person singular that appeared in ordinary American speech with no campaign behind it.

1. *Yo* was tuckin' in his shirt. (the recorded pattern — the pronoun is neutral, the possessive that follows it is not)
2. *Yo* threw a thumbtack at me and then acted like nothing happened.
3. Ask *yo* whether the late bus already came.
4. *Yo* is in my math class, second period.
5. I gave the hall pass back to *yo* after lunch.
6. *Yo* wasn't even in the building when it happened.
7. Look at *yo* — *yo* got new shoes on.

**See also:** [they](#they), [he](#he), [she](#she), [y'all](#yall), [neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets](#neopronouns-and-chosen-pronoun-sets), [your (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your)

### you all

**Pronunciation:** /juˈɔl/, reduced /jəˈwɔl/ &middot; *yoo-AWL* &middot; two syllables, stress on *all*
**Русский:** вы все — мн. ч. от you, подчёркнуто без исключений; допустимо на письме
**Type:** personal pronoun (phrasal) &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; the least marked of the plural-*you* forms, and one of only two — with the counted [you two](#you-two) — usable in careful writing
**Person / number:** second person plural
**Refers to:** everyone in the group being addressed, with no exceptions — the *all* is doing real work, which is why the phrase reads as inclusive rather than merely plural.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *you all **are**, you all **were***.
**Pattern:** *You all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you all* &middot; *all of you* (the inverted variant, more formal) &middot; *Thank you all*
**Contrast:** *You all* is the uncontracted source of [y'all](#yall), but the two are not interchangeable in register: *you all* is unmarked nationwide, while *y'all* is regionally and socially marked. The near-synonym *all of you* is the most formal of the set and is the one to use when a document genuinely has to address a group (*This applies to all of you*). None of *y'all*, *you guys*, *youse*, or *yinz* would do there.

The plain, unmarked American plural *you*, and, beside the counted [you two](#you-two), the member of the family that survives editing. It is also the source of *y'all*, though speakers who use both keep them distinct.

1. *You all* need to be at the gate by six.
2. Thank *you all* for coming out on such short notice.
3. I'd like *you all* to look at the second page.
4. This policy applies to all of *you*, without exception.
5. Are *you all* riding together, or should I hold two spaces?

**See also:** [you](#you), [y'all](#yall), [you guys](#you-guys), [you two](#you-two), [the two of us](#the-two-of-us)

### you guys

**Pronunciation:** /juˈɡaɪz/, reduced /jəˈɡaɪz/ &middot; *yoo-GYZE*
**Русский:** вы, ребята — разг., о группе любого пола; не для письменной речи
**Type:** personal pronoun (phrasal) &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal, nationwide; not for writing
**Person / number:** second person plural
**Refers to:** the group being addressed, of **any gender composition** — *you guys* is routinely used for all-female and mixed groups across the United States, which is why it is not simply the plural of *guy*. That said, a growing number of speakers and institutions avoid it precisely because the singular is gendered, and substitute *you all*, *folks*, *everyone*, or *team*.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *you guys **are**, you guys **were***.
**Pattern:** *You guys* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you guys* &middot; [preposition] *you guys* &middot; *you guys'* [noun] (possessive)
**Contrast:** *You guys* is the national informal plural *you*; [y'all](#yall) is the Southern one, [youse](#youse) the northeastern urban one, [you'uns](#youuns)/*yinz* the Pittsburgh one. Of these, *you guys* has the widest geographic reach and the shakiest future — it is the only one under active pressure for being gendered, and *y'all* and *you all* are the usual replacements. Its possessive *you guys'* is written with the apostrophe after the *s*, which is one sign of how far the phrase has traveled from being a real noun phrase.

The default informal American plural *you*, current in every region and almost every register short of writing. Grammatically it has fused: it takes a possessive as a unit, and speakers apply it without reference to the gender of anyone in the group.

1. Are *you guys* ready to order, or do you need another minute?
2. I'll meet *you guys* downstairs in ten.
3. Did anyone bring *you guys* the updated schedule?
4. Is this *you guys'* car in the loading zone?
5. *You guys* have been incredibly patient with all of this.

**See also:** [you](#you), [you all](#you-all), [y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse)

### you people

**Pronunciation:** /juˈpipəl/ &middot; *yoo-PEE-pul* &middot; weak /jəˈpipəl/ — *yə-PEE-pul*; the stress falls on *people*, and the two words are said as one unit
**Русский:** вы, эти ваши — разг., звучит враждебно: обращение к людям как к категории
**Type:** personal pronoun (phrasal) — the appositive *you* + noun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal, nationwide, and **pragmatically loaded** — grammatically it is nothing but the plural *you*, and in most American contexts it lands as hostile
**Person / number:** second person plural.
**Refers to:** the group being addressed — but, unlike [you all](#you-all) or [you guys](#you-guys), it addresses them as a **category** rather than as the people in the room. The noun does the damage: *people* invites the hearer to ask which people, and in the United States the answer supplied is usually race, ethnicity, religion, immigration status, class, or party. That reading is now the default one whatever the speaker intended, which is why the phrase turns up so often in coverage of public apologies. The neutral reading survives mainly when a modifier names a harmless category outright: *you people in the back row*, *you people who ordered the fish*.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *you people **are**, you people **were**, you people **have***.
**Pattern:** *You people* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you people* &middot; [preposition] *you people* &middot; *you people who* [verb] &middot; *you people in* [place]
**Contrast:** Grammatically it is the same frame as *you guys*, *you folks*, *you two*, *you editors* — [you](#you) plus a noun in apposition, taking the case of its slot, exactly like the *us editors* pattern under [us](#us). Pragmatically it is nothing like them. *You all* and *y'all* address a group as a group; *you people* addresses it as a type, and American listeners hear the type being named even when the speaker had none in mind. The most-cited instance is Ross Perot's 1992 address to the NAACP convention, where his repeated *you people* drew objections from the floor and took over the coverage. If the plural is all that is wanted, [you all](#you-all), *all of you*, *folks*, *everyone*, or the name of the group does the same work with none of the freight. Against *your people*, which is a different construction — possessive determiner plus noun — meaning the people belonging to or associated with you.

The one American plural *you* whose difficulty is not its grammar. Recognize it, understand why it lands the way it does, and reach for a different plural.

1. "*You people* don't understand how this neighborhood works," he said, and the room went quiet.
2. Every time a candidate says *you people* in a speech, the next day's coverage is about the phrase instead of the policy.
3. *You people* in the back — can you hear me all right?
4. She caught herself halfway through: "*You people* — sorry, *all of you* — need to sign in at the desk."
5. He insisted he meant nothing by *you people*, which is the whole difficulty: a speaker does not get to decide alone how it lands.
6. The newsroom style guide tells reporters to quote *you people* exactly when a source says it and never to use it in their own voice.

**See also:** [you](#you), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse), [us](#us), [your (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your)

### you'uns

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjuənz/ &middot; *YOO-unz* &middot; two syllables, built like [we'uns](#weuns) /ˈwiənz/; it also reduces to a one-syllable /junz/ (*YOONZ*), and the contracted Pittsburgh form ***yinz*** is /jɪnz/ (*YINZ*) — the same word one step further along
**Русский:** вы — диал. (Аппалачи, Питтсбург, где также yinz); вне литературной нормы
**Type:** personal pronoun, regional &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** nonstandard, strongly regionally marked
**Person / number:** second person plural. From *you ones*, by way of Scots-Irish settlement.
**Refers to:** the group being addressed. Geographically it is one of the tightest markers in American English: *you'uns* through Appalachia and the southern Midlands, *yinz* in and around Pittsburgh, where a native of the city is a *Yinzer* and the dialect itself is *Yinzer* or *Pittsburghese*.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *yinz **are**, yinz **were***.
**Pattern:** *Yinz* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *yinz* &middot; *Yinz guys* &middot; *yinz'* [noun]
**Contrast:** Same job as [y'all](#yall), [you guys](#you-guys), and [youse](#youse), different territory. *You'uns* and *yinz* are the most regionally restricted of the four and the most immediately identifying — using *yinz* outside western Pennsylvania reads as an imitation of Pittsburgh, not as a plural pronoun. Both are nonstandard in writing. The same dialects build a first person the same way: [we'uns](#weuns), with the object form *us'uns*.

The Appalachian and western Pennsylvanian plural *you*, built from *you ones*. Locally it is an emblem of identity; outside its range it is unintelligible or heard as a joke.

1. *Yinz* going down to the game on Sunday?
2. I'll save *yinz* a couple of seats.
3. Do *you'uns* need a ride back over the mountain?
4. *Yinz* guys want anything from the store?
5. Is that *yinz'* dog out in the yard?

**See also:** [you](#you), [y'all](#yall), [you guys](#you-guys), [youse](#youse), [we'uns](#weuns)

### youse

**Pronunciation:** /juz/ &middot; *YOOZ* &middot; also spelled *yous*
**Русский:** вы — прост., городское рабочее просторечие (Нью-Йорк, Филадельфия, Бостон)
**Type:** personal pronoun, regional &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** nonstandard, working-class urban
**Person / number:** second person plural. Formed by adding the regular noun plural *-s* to *you*.
**Refers to:** the group being addressed. Associated with New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, and with Irish, Scottish, and Australian English — its American distribution tracks nineteenth-century Irish immigration closely.
**Agreement:** plural verb forms — *youse **are**, youse **were***. The doubled *youse guys* is common and is treated as a single unit.
**Pattern:** *Youse* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *youse* &middot; *youse guys* &middot; *youse guys'* [noun]
**Variants:** ***yous*** (the commoner spelling in Philadelphia, and the usual one in Irish and Australian English), ***youse guys***, ***yous guys*** — the doubled forms are one unit, plural twice over, and take a possessive as a unit (*youse guys' car*); *youse* and *yous* differ only in spelling
**Contrast:** Same function as [y'all](#yall), [you guys](#you-guys), and [you'uns](#youuns), different region and a heavier social marking than any of them — *youse* carries a working-class urban stereotype in the United States that *y'all* has largely shed. It is nonstandard everywhere in writing.

The northeastern urban plural *you*, built the obvious way: take *you* and add an *-s*. Recognize it; do not write it.

1. *Youse* better hurry or you'll miss the train.
2. I told *youse* twice already.
3. What are *youse* guys doing out here?
4. This whole thing is on *youse*, not on me.
5. Are *youse* coming up for the holiday or not?
6. Are *yous* two coming, or should I hold the table?
7. *Youse guys* want anything from the deli?

**See also:** [you](#you), [y'all](#yall), [you guys](#you-guys), [you'uns](#youuns)

## Honorific address pronominals

Standard English has one second-person pronoun and no way to mark deference with it — no *vous*, no *usted*, no *Sie*. American English builds the missing polite form out of a frozen noun phrase instead: *Your Honor*, *Your Majesty*, *His Excellency*, and behind them *Mr. President*, *Madam Secretary*, *Your Holiness*, *Your Grace*. Each is a noun phrase in shape and a pronoun in use, and each does something no ordinary pronoun does — it addresses the person standing in front of you in the **third** person. One entry covers the frame; the rest of the set is built the same way.

### your Honor, Your Majesty, His Excellency

**Pronunciation:** /jɔr ˈɑnɚ/ &middot; *yor-ON-er* &middot; weak /jɚ ˈɑnɚ/ — *y'r ON-er*, which is what a courtroom actually sounds like. ***Your Majesty*** is /jɔr ˈmædʒəsti/ (*yor-MAJ-uh-stee*) and ***His Excellency*** is /hɪz ˈɛksələnsi/ (*hiz-EK-suh-len-see*), the *h* dropping when unstressed. *Your Honour* is the same word in British and Canadian spelling.
**Русский:** Ваша честь, Ваше Величество, Его Превосходительство — обращение по титулу вместо you
**Type:** honorific pronominal — a fixed noun phrase used in place of a pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike; being a noun phrase, it fills any nominal slot &middot; **Register:** formal, institutional, ceremonial
**Person / number:** third person singular in **grammar**, second person in **reference** — the strangest thing about these forms. *Your Honor* is spoken to the judge and takes the verb of a third person: *Your Honor **is** correct*, *if Your Honor **wishes***. The *Your-* forms address the person; the *His-* and *Her-* forms speak about them. Plurals exist and take a plural verb: *Their Majesties **were** received*, *Their Honors **have** ruled*.
**Refers to:** the holder of an office, addressed by the office rather than by name or by *you*. In American practice *Your Honor* is the only one of the three an ordinary person is likely to say: it is the standard address to a judge in any American courtroom, and in some cities to a mayor. *Your Majesty* belongs to reigning monarchs and reaches Americans through British news, film, and history rather than through domestic life. *His* or *Her Excellency* is used of ambassadors and, in diplomatic correspondence, of heads of state and government, with *Your Excellency* as the vocative.
**Agreement:** third-person singular verb forms — *Your Honor **has** the exhibit*, *His Excellency **was** received at noon*. The possessive is formed on the noun, like any other noun phrase: *Your Honor's ruling*, *His Excellency's residence*.
**Pattern:** *Your Honor,* [clause] (vocative, set off by commas) &middot; *if Your Honor* [third-person singular verb] &middot; *May it please the Court* / *May it please Your Honor* &middot; *His Excellency* [third-person singular verb] &middot; *Their Majesties* [plural verb] &middot; *Your Honor's* [noun]
**Variants:** *Your Honour* (British and Canadian spelling); *Your Excellency* as the vocative of *His/Her Excellency*; the parallel frames *Your Holiness*, *Your Grace*, *Your Eminence*, *Mr. President*, *Madam Speaker*, *Mr. Chief Justice*
**Contrast:** The *your* and *his* inside these phrases are **possessive determiners**, and as words they belong to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your) — what acts as a pronoun here is the whole frozen phrase, exactly as with [you guys](#you-guys) and [you all](#you-all). Against plain [you](#you): both address the person in front of you, but the honorific converts the address into the third person, and that conversion is the grammar of deference — the same move as *Would the gentleman yield?* on the House floor. Capitalization is the written signal: *Your Honor* addressing the judge is capitalized, *a judge of great honor* is not. And the American set is narrower than the British one — *My Lord*, *Your Worship*, and *Your Ladyship* are not used in American courts, and in the U.S. Supreme Court advocates ordinarily say *Justice Sotomayor* and *Mr. Chief Justice*, with *Your Honor* also heard.

A pronoun made out of a noun phrase, and the only place in modern English where a speaker addresses somebody in the third person on purpose. A learner meets it first in a courtroom, then in every costume drama ever filmed.

1. *Your Honor*, the defense has no further questions for this witness.
2. If *Your Honor* is inclined to grant the continuance, we can be ready by the fourteenth.
3. May it please *Your Honor*, the exhibit was produced in discovery last spring.
4. *His Excellency* the Ambassador of Ireland will present his credentials on Tuesday.
5. *Their Majesties* were greeted at the airport by the governor and both senators.
6. Counsel rose and began, "*Your Honor*, may I approach the bench?"
7. The clerk read out the order, and *Your Honor's* signature was on the last page.

**See also:** [you](#you), [he](#he), [she](#she), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [your (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your), [his (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his)

## Neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets

English has been proposing gender-neutral third-person singular pronouns for well over two centuries. Dennis Baron's survey of the record (*What's Your Pronoun?*, 2020) turns up more than two hundred coinages since the 1780s — *thon*, *e*, *ne*, *hiser*, *ip*, *heer* — and not one of them became a word of the general language. The form that filled the gap needed no coining at all: singular [they](#they), continuously attested since the 1300s and standard in edited American English today.

The entries below are the coinages still in live use, plus two related items — the chosen personal use of [it](#it-as-a-chosen-personal-pronoun), which is not a coinage but the ordinary English word, and the [pronoun-set label](#theythem-sheher-hehim-zehir) that names any of these sets on a badge or in a signature line. One family inside the section is built differently from the rest: the **nounself** sets — [bun](#bun--bunself), [kit](#kit--kitself), [star](#star--starself), and [fae / faer](#fae--faer) — take an ordinary noun instead of an existing pronoun as their base, and live almost entirely online. Six things hold across the whole section, and they are the six a learner needs.

**They belong to particular people, not to the language at large.** A neopronoun is used for a specific person who has stated it. Nobody writes *xe* for a hypothetical applicant — that is singular *they*'s job, and it is the job none of these forms does.

**They take singular agreement.** *Ze **is** on the schedule*, *xe **was** late*, *ey **has** the file* — unlike singular *they*, which keeps its plural verb (*they **are** on the schedule*) even when it refers to one person. A minority of speakers use plural agreement with these forms; the person's own practice settles it.

**Each set has five slots**, on the model of *he / him / his / his / himself*: subject, object, possessive determiner, independent possessive, reflexive. The determiner column is a determiner and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md); the independent possessives have their own entries in [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md#eirs).

**Spelling and pronunciation vary by user, and the paradigms are not settled.** *Ze* pairs with *hir* for some speakers and *zir* for others; *xyr* is also written *xir*; *ve* takes *vis* in one paradigm and *vers* in another. Where a set has more than one shape, the entry says so, and where a person states a pronunciation, that is the pronunciation.

**How common they are.** Far rarer than singular *they*. The Trevor Project's 2020 national survey of about 40,000 LGBTQ young people in the United States found roughly a quarter using *they/them* and about four percent using a neopronoun set such as *ze/zir*, *xe/xem*, or *fae/faer*. Americans meet them mainly in email signatures, profile fields, name badges, and class rosters rather than in print.

**What the style guides say.** The major American guides take up singular *they* and prescribe no particular coinage; their common instruction is to use the forms a person states, to gloss an unfamiliar one at first mention, and to recast the sentence where that reads more clearly than either.

### ae / aer

**Pronunciation:** /eɪ/ &middot; *AY* &middot; ***aer*** is /ɛr/ (*AIR*); a minority of users say /i/ (*EE*) for *ae* and /ˈeɪɚ/ (*AY-er*) for *aer*, and the person's own reading governs. Neither form has a schwa weak form; unstressed, they simply shorten, exactly as [they](#they) does.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — гендерно-нейтральный набор ae/aer, с 2010-х
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *ae / aer / aers / aerself* set &middot; **Case:** *ae* subject, *aer* object and possessive determiner &middot; **Register:** limited; one of the newer sets, in circulation in American online writing since the 2010s and met chiefly in profiles, signature lines, and rosters
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ae* (subject), *aer* (object), *aer* (determiner), *aers* (independent possessive), *aerself* (reflexive) — the same shape as *fae / faer*, with which it is easily confused.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ae / aer* — human only, never a group, and never a hypothetical or generic person, which is singular [they](#they)'s job.
**Agreement:** singular verb forms — *ae **is**, ae **was**, ae **has**, ae **does***. Not ✗*ae are*: this is where the coined sets differ from singular *they*, which keeps the plural verb.
**Pattern:** *Ae* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *aer* &middot; [preposition] *aer* &middot; *aer* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *aers* (standalone possessive) &middot; *aerself*
**Variants:** cited *ae/aer* or *ae/aer/aers*; some listings give *aers* in the determiner slot as well
**Contrast:** Against [fae / faer](#fae--faer), built on exactly the same endings one letter over, and against [ey](#ey) and [xe](#xe), which clip *they/them* rather than rhyming with it. The trap is homophony: *aer* is said like *air* and *ae* like the letter A, so *Ae asked for the file* reads for a beat as a typo — which is why writers who use the set gloss it once at first mention.

One of the newer coinages, and one of the two — with *fae / faer* — that an American reader is likelier to meet in an online profile than anywhere in print.

1. *Ae* joined the lab in September and has run the night shift ever since.
2. I sent *aer* the revised schedule on Tuesday and haven't heard back.
3. The badge reads "Wren Alcott (ae/aer), Facilities."
4. *Ae* is the only person on the crew who has read the manual all the way through.
5. Leave the packet with *aer* at the front desk.
6. Everything in the blue folder is *aers*; the red one is mine.

**See also:** [fae / faer](#fae--faer), [ey](#ey), [ze](#ze), [xe](#xe), [they](#they), [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md)

### bun / bunself

**Pronunciation:** /bʌn/ &middot; *BUN* &middot; no weak form — one short syllable, and a perfect homophone of the ordinary noun *bun*. The possessive ***buns*** is /bʌnz/ (*BUNZ*) and the reflexive ***bunself*** is /bʌnˈsɛlf/ (*bun-SELF*), stressed on *-self* exactly as *himself* and *themself* are.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — nounself-набор от bunny «зайчик», интернет-среда
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — a **nounself** set, built out of the noun *bunny* rather than out of an existing pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike, like *you* and *it* &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; internet-native, met in online profiles, bios, and pronoun fields, and essentially never in speech, in print, or on workplace and campus forms
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The full paradigm runs *bun* (subject), *bun* (object), *buns* (possessive determiner), *buns* (independent possessive), *bunself* (reflexive) — five slots on the model of *he / him / his / his / himself*, with one form covering both cases and one covering both possessives.
**Refers to:** one specific person who has stated *bun/bunself* for themselves. Human only, never a group, and never a hypothetical or generic person — that is singular [they](#they)'s job, and it is the job no set in this section does. Nounself sets are introduced the way any other set is, and there is no way to infer one: a profile line, a bio, a signature, or an introduction (*I'm Wren — bun/bunself*).
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *bun **is**, bun **was**, bun **has**, bun **does***. Not ✗*bun are*: like every coined set here, and unlike singular [they](#they), it takes the singular verb.
**Pattern:** *Bun* [singular verb] &middot; *Bun* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *bun* [verb]? &middot; [verb] *bun* &middot; [preposition] *bun* &middot; *buns* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *buns* (standalone possessive) &middot; *bunself*
**Variants:** cited *bun/bunself*, *bun/buns*, or in full *bun/bun/buns/bunself*; some users write the set out as *bunny/bunnyself*, and the reflexive is occasionally *bunnself*
**Contrast:** Against [fae / faer](#fae--faer), the best-known nounself set and the only one shaped to look like a pronoun — *fae* rhymes with *they*, while *bun* does not disguise the noun it came from at all. Against [star / starself](#star--starself) and [kit / kitself](#kit--kitself), the same construction on other nouns. Against the common noun *bun*, with which it is both homophone and homograph: *Bun brought the folder* stops a reader who does not know the set, which is why writers who use one name it once at first mention and then let it run. **Before a noun *buns* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *buns notebook* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md), and only the standalone use (*the notebook is buns*) is the pronoun.

The nounself sets are the newest and rarest layer of this section, and *bun* is the one most often given as their example. They are not part of any traditional English paradigm and are not used by most speakers; where a person has stated one, it inflects across the same five slots as the older coined sets and takes the same singular verb.

1. *Bun* has been running the server's art channel since it opened.
2. I asked *bun* to look over the draft before it went out to the group.
3. The bio line reads "Wren Alcott (bun/bunself), moderator."
4. *Bun* is the only one of us who remembered to bring a charger.
5. Leave the sketchbook with *bun* — *bun* asked for it back on Friday.
6. That's *buns* notebook on the table; don't move it. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)
7. *Bun* built the whole site *bunself* over one weekend.

**See also:** [star / starself](#star--starself), [kit / kitself](#kit--kitself), [fae / faer](#fae--faer), [ze](#ze), [xe](#xe), [they](#they), [reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md)

### co

**Pronunciation:** /koʊ/ &middot; *KOH* &middot; no schwa weak form; unstressed it shortens without changing quality. Homophonous with the prefix *co-* and with the abbreviation *Co.*, which is the one practical difficulty it has on the page.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — старейший из живых наборов, с 1970-х
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *co / co / cos / coself* set &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike, like *you* and *it* &middot; **Register:** limited; the oldest of the sets still in daily use, and the only one with a documented offline home
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. Invariant for case — *Co called* and *I called co*. The possessive *cos* covers both the determiner and the standalone slot; the reflexive is *coself*.
**Refers to:** one specific person. *Co* was coined by Mary Orovan in 1970 and has been in continuous use since the 1970s at Twin Oaks Community, an intentional community in Louisa County, Virginia, whose bylaws and everyday speech use it as the ordinary third-person pronoun for everybody — which makes it the rare neopronoun an American might hear spoken all day in one place rather than read in a profile.
**Agreement:** singular verb forms — *co **is**, co **was**, co **has**, co **does***.
**Pattern:** *Co* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *co* &middot; [preposition] *co* &middot; *cos* [noun] &middot; *coself*
**Variants:** the possessive is also written *co's*; the set is cited *co/cos*
**Contrast:** Against [per](#per), the other case-invariant set of the 1970s — neither marks subject against object, so both behave like *you* rather than like *he/him*. Against [it](#it), which is also invariant across cases but carries a history *co* does not. In print the difficulty is the abbreviation: *Co* opening a sentence can read as a truncated company name, which is why documents that use it often set it in italics the first time. **Before a noun *cos* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *cos bicycle* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md); only the standalone use (*the bicycle is cos*) is the pronoun.

The one coined pronoun in American English with a town's worth of native speakers rather than a scattering of individual ones.

1. *Co* has been on the garden crew for three years.
2. I asked *co* to swap shifts with me on Thursday.
3. That's *cos* bicycle leaning against the dining hall. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)
4. *Co* is teaching the beekeeping class this spring.
5. Nobody told *co* the meeting had moved to seven.
6. *Co* built the shelves *coself*, out of scrap from the woodshop.

**See also:** [per](#per), [it (as a chosen personal pronoun)](#it-as-a-chosen-personal-pronoun), [ze](#ze), [they](#they), [pers](02-possessive.md#pers)

### em (Spivak object)

**Pronunciation:** /ɛm/ &middot; *EM* &middot; weak /əm/ — and in that weak form it is a **homophone of ['em](#em)**, the reduced *them*. The resemblance is not an accident: the set is *they / them / their* with the *th-* clipped off the front.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — объектная форма набора ey/em (местоимения Спивака)
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the object form of the *ey / em / eir* set, usually called the Spivak pronouns &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** limited; in circulation since the mid-1970s, and met by Americans in online writing, in some science fiction and academic prose, and in pronoun introductions
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ey* (subject), **em** (object), *eir* (determiner), *eirs* (independent possessive), *emself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ey / em* — human only, never a group.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. The subject form takes a singular verb (*ey **is***), where singular *they* takes *are*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *em* &middot; [verb] *em* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *em* &middot; *eir* [noun] &middot; *eirs* (standalone possessive) &middot; *emself*
**Variants:** capitalized *Em* in Michael Spivak's original *E / Em / Eir*; the lowercase *ey / em / eir* is Christine Elverson's 1975 revision, which won a contest for a gender-neutral pronoun and is the form in general use; the reflexive is written *emself* and, less often, *eirself*
**Contrast:** Against ['em](#em) — one sound, two unrelated words. *'em* is the thirteen-hundred-year-old survival of Old English *heom*, unstressed and available to every English speaker; *em* is a deliberate coinage of the 1970s and 1980s, used of one specific person. Speech does not distinguish them at all, and the apostrophe does all the work on the page. Against [them](#them): *em* is what is left of *them* once the *th-* comes off, which is also why *emself* looks the way it does.

The object form of the Spivak set — the family built by subtraction rather than invention. Michael Spivak used *E / Em / Eir* in his TeX manual in the 1980s and gave the set its usual name; the early-1990s text world LambdaMOO offered "Spivak" as a gender setting, which is where a great many American internet users first met it.

1. I sent *em* the draft on Friday and got notes back the same night.
2. Ask *em* whether the lab still needs a second reader.
3. The package on the porch is for *em*, not for the downstairs apartment.
4. We hired *em* out of the Denver office in 2019.
5. Give the extra ticket to Priya and *em*.
6. Said aloud, *tell em* and *tell 'em* are the same sentence; only the writing says whether one person or a crowd is meant.

**See also:** [ey](#ey), ['em](#em), [them](#them), [eirs](02-possessive.md#eirs), [they](#they)

### ey

**Pronunciation:** /eɪ/ &middot; *AY* &middot; no schwa weak form; the diphthong shortens when unstressed, exactly as in [they](#they), from which the form was made by dropping the *th-*.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — подлежащная форма набора ey/em, местоимения Спивака
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the subject form of the *ey / em / eir* set, the Spivak pronouns &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** limited; in circulation since the mid-1970s, commoner in online and academic writing than in speech
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs **ey** (subject), *em* (object), *eir* (determiner), *eirs* (independent possessive), *emself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ey / em* — human only, never a group, and not a generic epicene.
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *ey **is**, ey **was**, ey **has**, ey **does***. This is where the Spivak set parts company with the *they* it was clipped from: *they* keeps the plural verb even for one person, *ey* takes the singular. Some users say *ey are* by analogy; the person's own practice settles it.
**Pattern:** *Ey* [singular verb] &middot; *Ey* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *ey* [verb]? &middot; *eir* [noun] &middot; *emself*
**Variants:** capitalized *E* (Michael Spivak's spelling); lowercase *ey* (Christine Elverson's 1975 revision, and the usual modern form)
**Contrast:** Against [they](#they): same origin, different agreement — the whole point of the set was a singular pronoun that looks singular and takes a singular verb. Against [ae](#ae--aer), which sounds like the letter A and belongs to a different set entirely. In running speech *ey* is close enough to the interjection *eh* that writers name the set once at first mention and then let it run.

The subject form of the Spivak pronouns, and the most-used of the coinages built by clipping an existing word rather than inventing a new one.

1. *Ey* runs the reading room on Tuesdays and Thursdays.
2. Did *ey* say when the transcript would be ready?
3. *Ey* has been with the firm longer than anyone else on this floor.
4. My co-author and *ey* presented the paper together in Boston.
5. *Ey* wrote the whole grant application *emself* in four days.
6. When *ey* is on call, the schedule shows "L. Ahmadi (ey/em)."

### fae / faer

**Pronunciation:** /feɪ/ &middot; *FAY* &middot; ***faer*** is /fɛr/ (*FAIR*); neither has a weak form, and both simply shorten when unstressed.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — nounself-набор от fae «фея»
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *fae / faer / faers / faerself* set &middot; **Case:** *fae* subject, *faer* object and possessive determiner &middot; **Register:** limited; an internet-era coinage in circulation since the 2010s, met in profiles, fan communities, and rosters rather than in print
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *fae* (subject), *faer* (object), *faer* (determiner), *faers* (independent possessive), *faerself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *fae / faer* — human only. The form is built from the noun *fae*, a fairy, in the older spelling *fay*, which puts it in the group sometimes called **nounself pronouns**: sets built out of an ordinary noun rather than out of an existing pronoun. That origin is why it is likelier to be met in an online community than in a workplace directory.
**Agreement:** singular verb forms — *fae **is**, fae **was**, fae **has***.
**Pattern:** *Fae* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *faer* &middot; [preposition] *faer* &middot; *faer* [noun] &middot; *faers* (standalone possessive) &middot; *faerself*
**Variants:** cited *fae/faer* or *fae/faer/faers*; the spellings *fey* and *feyr* are occasionally used for the same set
**Contrast:** Against [ae / aer](#ae--aer), built on identical endings — a letter apart in writing, clearly distinct in speech. Against the common noun *fae*, which is what a reader who does not know the set will take it for. Against [ze](#ze) and [xe](#xe), which are older, commoner, and built as pronouns from the start rather than out of a noun.

The best-known of the nounself sets, and a form a learner is far likelier to meet online than at work.

1. *Fae* has been moderating the forum since it opened.
2. I told *faer* the meeting had moved to Thursday.
3. That's *faers* — the green notebook with the sticker on the cover.
4. *Fae* is bringing the extra chairs, so we don't have to rent any.
5. Ask *faer* to send the photos when fae gets home.
6. The roster shows "Ari Delacroix (fae/faer)" in the pronoun column.

### hir

**Pronunciation:** /hɪr/ &middot; *HEER* &middot; also /hɚ/ (*HUR*) for a minority of users, which makes it a perfect homophone of *her*; most say /hɪr/ precisely to keep the two apart. Weak /(h)ɪr/ — the /h/ drops when the word is unstressed and non-initial, exactly as it does in *him* and *her*: *tell hir* [ˈtɛl ɪr].
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — объектная и притяжательная форма набора ze/hir
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the object form of the *ze / hir* and *sie / hir* sets &middot; **Case:** object, and also the possessive determiner (*hir coat*) &middot; **Register:** limited; the object form of the 1990s wave of coinages, met in writing from that period, in trans and genderqueer communities, and wherever a person states it
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ze*, *zie*, or *sie* (subject), **hir** (object and determiner), *hirs* (independent possessive), *hirself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ze / hir* — human only, never a group. The pairing was current in American trans and genderqueer writing and on the early internet in the 1990s; the writer Leslie Feinberg used *ze* and *hir* in print in that decade, which is where many readers first met the set.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. The subject form takes a singular verb (*ze **is***).
**Pattern:** [verb] *hir* &middot; [verb] *hir* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *hir* &middot; *hir* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *hirs* (standalone possessive) &middot; *hirself*
**Variants:** *hir* also fills the independent possessive slot for some users (*the coat is hir*), on the model of *his*, though *hirs* is the regularized form; the subject form is written *ze*, *zie*, and *sie*
**Contrast:** Against [zir](#zir): the same slot in a different pairing — *ze/hir* is the older set, *ze/zir* the one that regularized everything around *z-*, and which of the two a person uses is a fact about the person, not about the words. Against *her*: one letter apart on paper, and for speakers who say /hɪr/ clearly distinct in speech; for the minority who say /hɚ/ the two are homophones and only the writing separates them. **Before a noun *hir* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *hir coat* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md), and the standalone possessive is [hirs](02-possessive.md#hirs).

The object form of the first neopronoun set to reach a wide American readership, and still the one most often met in writing from the 1990s and 2000s.

1. I called *hir* twice from the airport and got voicemail both times.
2. The committee offered *hir* the position on Friday afternoon.
3. This envelope is for *hir*, not for me.
4. Nobody had told *hir* that the deadline moved.
5. Give the spare key to Dana and *hir* before you leave.
6. Ze wrote the report *hirself*, over one weekend.

### hu / hum

**Pronunciation:** /hju/ &middot; *HYOO* &middot; homophonous with *hue* and with the name *Hugh*. ***Hum*** is /hjum/ (*HYOOM*) and ***hus*** /hjuz/ (*HYOOZ*) for users who keep the vowel of *human*, the word the set is clipped from; others read the spellings by ordinary English rules and say /hʌm/ (*HUM*) and /hʌs/ (*HUSS*). All three drop the /h/ when unstressed and non-initial, exactly as *him*, *her*, and *his* do — *tell hum* [ˈtɛl (j)əm] — which puts the object form within a hair of ['em](#em) at conversational speed.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — набор hu/hum, усечение от human (1982), редк.
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *hu / hum / hus / humself* set, clipped from *human* &middot; **Case:** *hu* subject, *hum* object, *hus* possessive determiner and independent possessive alike &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; a proposal of the early 1980s that stayed in the pronoun listings without ever reaching general use, met today in those listings, in profiles, and occasionally in fiction rather than in print or in speech
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *hu* (subject), *hum* (object), *hus* (determiner and independent possessive), *humself* (reflexive) — four distinct forms across five slots, the possessive doing double duty the way *his* does.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *hu / hum* — human only, which is the whole point of a form clipped from *human*, and never a group or a generic person. The set is usually credited to Sasha Newborn, who published it in Santa Barbara in 1982, and it belongs to the long American line of gender-neutral coinages Dennis Baron catalogues in *What's Your Pronoun?* — more than two hundred since the 1780s, almost none of which entered the general language.
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms for the subject — *hu **is**, hu **was**, hu **has**, hu **does***, not ✗*hu are*. *Hum* and *hus*, being an object and a possessive, control no verb at all.
**Pattern:** *Hu* [singular verb] &middot; *Hu* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *hu* [verb]? &middot; [verb] *hum* &middot; [preposition] *hum* &middot; *hus* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *hus* (standalone possessive) &middot; *humself*
**Variants:** cited *hu/hum*, *hu/hus*, or in full *hu/hum/hus/humself*; the reflexive is written *humself* and, less often, *huself*; a reduced paradigm appears in some listings with *hu* covering subject and object alike
**Contrast:** Against [per](#per) and [co](#co), the other sets pulled out of an ordinary word rather than invented from nothing — *per* from *person*, *hu* from *human* — and, like them, far rarer in American pronoun listings than [ze](#ze) or [xe](#xe). Against *him* and *his*, which *hum* and *hus* sit one letter away from and collapse into once the /h/ goes: unstressed, *hum* and *him* are both [əm], and only the writing separates them. Against ['em](#em) for the same reason. **Before a noun *hus* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *hus coat* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md); only the standalone use (*the coat is hus*) is the pronoun.

One of the two-hundred-odd gender-neutral third-person pronouns proposed in English since the 1780s, and one of the handful still carried in current listings. It is not part of any traditional paradigm and is not used by most speakers; where it is used, it is used for one person who has stated it, and it inflects across all five slots like every other set in this section.

1. *Hu* has taught the Thursday seminar for four years running.
2. Did *hu* say whether the transcript would be ready before the hearing?
3. I left the packet with *hum* at the front desk.
4. *Hu* is the only person on the committee who read the appendix.
5. That's *hus* bicycle chained to the rail outside. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)
6. The green folder is *hus*; the blue one is mine.
7. *Hu* wrote the entire proposal *humself* in a week.

### it (as a chosen personal pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/ — identical in every respect to the ordinary [it](#it); nothing in the sound distinguishes the two uses.
**Русский:** оно — обычное it как выбранное самим человеком местоимение; иначе звучит как оскорбление
**Type:** personal pronoun, in its chosen personal use &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** limited, and conditional on consent — neutral when it is the pronoun a person has stated, an insult when it is not
**Person / number:** third person singular. The paradigm is the ordinary one: *it* (subject and object), *its* (determiner and independent possessive), *itself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who has stated *it/its* for themselves. A small number of people — mostly young, mostly nonbinary — use *it/its*, sometimes alone and sometimes paired with another set (*it/they*, *it/she*). This is the one item in this section that is not a coinage at all: it is the existing English *it*, applied to a person by that person's own choice.
**Agreement:** third-person singular verb forms — *it **is**, it **was**, it **has***, exactly as with the ordinary *it*.
**Pattern:** *It* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *it* &middot; [preposition] *it* &middot; *its* [noun] &middot; *itself* &middot; the roster form *it/its*
**Variants:** listed as *it/its*, and often paired — *it/they*, *it/she*, *it/he* — where a person uses more than one set
**Contrast:** Against the ordinary [it](#it), from which it differs in nothing grammatical: same forms, same agreement, same slots. The entire difference is the referent and the permission. English's ordinary rule is that *it* cannot refer to a human being, and the reason that rule matters is that calling a person *it* has been a standard way of denying that they are one; that history is exactly why the chosen use turns on the person having asked for it, and why it does not generalize to anyone else. Against [they](#they), which is what an American writer uses when a person's pronoun is unknown.

The one pronoun in this section that was already in the dictionary. Everything about it is ordinary except who is entitled to apply it.

1. The directory entry reads "Robin Ash (it/its), staff photographer."
2. Robin asked the team to use *it*, so that is what the team uses.
3. *It* had already read the whole packet before the meeting started.
4. I left the revised print with *it* at the front desk.
5. The blue camera bag by the door is *its*.
6. Some people use two sets, and the roster shows *it/they* — either one is right.

**See also:** [it](#it), [they](#they), [itself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#itself), [its (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its), [ze](#ze)

### kit / kitself

**Pronunciation:** /kɪt/ &middot; *KIT* &middot; no weak form; a single short syllable and a perfect homophone of the ordinary noun *kit*. The possessive ***kits*** is /kɪts/ (*KITS*) — indistinguishable in speech from the plural *kits* and from the noun's own possessive *kit's* — and the reflexive ***kitself*** is /kɪtˈsɛlf/ (*kit-SELF*), stressed on *-self* like *itself* and *himself*.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — nounself-набор от kit «лисёнок, крольчонок»
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — a **nounself** set, built out of the noun *kit*, a young fox, rabbit, or ferret &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; internet-native, met in online profiles and pronoun fields rather than in speech, in print, or on institutional forms
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *kit* (subject), *kit* (object), *kits* (possessive determiner), *kits* (independent possessive), *kitself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who has stated *kit/kitself*. Human only, never a group, and never a generic or hypothetical person — singular [they](#they) covers that and no coined set does. Like the other nounself sets it is stated rather than inferred, and it is stated in the ordinary places: a profile field, a bio line, an introduction.
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *kit **is**, kit **was**, kit **has**, kit **does***, not ✗*kit are*.
**Pattern:** *Kit* [singular verb] &middot; *Kit* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *kit* [verb]? &middot; [verb] *kit* &middot; [preposition] *kit* &middot; *kits* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *kits* (standalone possessive) &middot; *kitself*
**Variants:** cited *kit/kitself* or *kit/kits*; the possessive is also written *kit's*, which buys back the distinction from the plural noun at the cost of colliding with the noun's own possessive
**Contrast:** Against [bun / bunself](#bun--bunself) and [star / starself](#star--starself), the same construction on different nouns. Against the common noun *kit* — a toolkit, a drum kit, a model kit — which is the commoner word by an enormous margin, so *Kit is ready* is a sentence about a person only for a reader who already knows the set. Against the given name *Kit*, short for Christopher or Katherine, which is the other thing a reader assumes first. That double collision is the practical argument for naming the set at first mention wherever it appears in running prose. **Before a noun *kits* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *kits headset* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md).

A nounself set built on *kit*, the word for a young fox or rabbit. Like the rest of that family it is not part of any traditional English paradigm and is not used by most speakers; it inflects across the same five slots as the older coined sets, takes a singular verb, and belongs to whoever has stated it.

1. *Kit* joined the forum two years ago and has moderated it ever since.
2. I sent *kit* the revised map on Sunday night and got notes back by morning.
3. Everything in the shared folder came from *kit*.
4. *Kit* is the only person here who can still read the old file format.
5. Hold the door for *kit* — *kit* is carrying both boxes.
6. That's *kits* headset on the desk; leave it where it is. (the determiner use)
7. *Kit* finished the translation *kitself*, without a dictionary.

### ne / nem

**Pronunciation:** /ni/ &middot; *NEE* &middot; ***nem*** is /nɛm/ (*NEM*), weak /nəm/ — the same reduction *them* takes, and for the same reason: the set is modeled on *they / them*.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — набор ne/nem/nir, редк.
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *ne / nem / nir / nirs / nemself* set &middot; **Case:** *ne* subject, *nem* object &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; met in online pronoun listings and profiles rather than in print
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ne* (subject), *nem* (object), *nir* (determiner), *nirs* (independent possessive), *nemself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ne / nem* — human only, never a group.

**Pattern:** *Ne* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *nem* &middot; [preposition] *nem* &middot; *nir* [noun] &middot; *nirs* (standalone possessive) &middot; *nemself*
**Variants:** cited *ne/nem* or *ne/nem/nir*; a nineteenth-century *ne / nis / nim* appears among the older proposals and shares only the subject form
**Contrast:** Against [ey](#ey) and [em (Spivak object)](#em-spivak-object), built the same way — take *they / them / their* and replace the *th-* — and much commoner. Against *em*: *nem* and *em* are one consonant apart and belong to two different sets, which is the practical argument for naming a set at first mention. **Before a noun *nir* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *nir office* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md); only the standalone *nirs* is the pronoun.

A set built on the *they/them* frame, and a reminder that the same idea has been tried more than once: a *ne* turns up among the nineteenth-century proposals as well as in the current listings.

1. *Ne* teaches the Tuesday section and grades the Thursday one.
2. I passed *nem* in the hallway and forgot to ask about the schedule.
3. *Nir* office is the one at the end with the plant in the window. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)
4. We asked *nem* to look at the numbers before the board meeting.
5. *Ne* has been rewriting the intake form *nemself* since March.

### per

**Pronunciation:** /pɚ/ &middot; *PUR* &middot; no separate weak form. Homophonous with the preposition *per* (*per diem*, *miles per hour*) and with *purr*, which is the one real difficulty the form has.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — набор per, усечение от person (М. Пирси, 1976)
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the *per / per / pers / perself* set, clipped from *person* &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike, like *you* and *it* &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; an American meets it chiefly in feminist science fiction, and occasionally where a person states it
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. One form covers subject, object, and — for many users — the determiner; the independent possessive is *pers* and the reflexive *perself*.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *per* — human only, which is the point of a form clipped from *person*. The set entered general circulation through Marge Piercy's 1976 novel *Woman on the Edge of Time*, where it is the ordinary pronoun of the future the book imagines, and that novel is where most American readers have met it.
**Agreement:** singular verb forms — *per **is**, per **was**, per **has**, per **does***.
**Pattern:** *Per* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *per* &middot; [preposition] *per* &middot; *pers* [noun] &middot; *perself*
**Variants:** *per* also fills the possessive determiner slot in many listings (*per coat*), in which case the set is given as *per / per / per / perself*; *pers* is the regularized independent possessive
**Contrast:** Against [co](#co), the other case-invariant set of the 1970s — neither marks subject against object, so both work like *you* rather than like *he/him*. Against the Latin preposition *per*, a different word entirely that is never a pronoun, though it does make *per coat* readable two ways for a beat. Against [ze](#ze) and [xe](#xe), which mark case and are far commoner in American pronoun listings. **Before a noun *per* or *pers* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *pers jacket* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md); only the standalone use (*the jacket is pers*) is the pronoun.

The one coinage that came into English through a novel, and the one whose homophone problem is with a preposition rather than with another pronoun.

1. *Per* took the early train and got in ahead of the storm.
2. I gave *per* the keys to the shop on Friday.
3. That's *pers* jacket on the back of the chair. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)
4. *Per* is the one who noticed the error in the totals.
5. Nobody had told *per* that the route changed.
6. In Piercy's novel everyone in the village is *per*, and the word stops being strange after a page.

### star / starself

**Pronunciation:** /stɑr/ &middot; *STAR* &middot; no weak form. The possessive ***stars*** is /stɑrz/ (*STARZ*), homophonous with the plural noun *stars* and with the noun's possessive *star's*; the reflexive ***starself*** is /stɑrˈsɛlf/ (*star-SELF*), stressed on *-self*.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — nounself-набор от star «звезда»
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — a **nounself** set, built out of the ordinary noun *star* &middot; **Case:** subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** limited and rare; internet-native, met in profiles, bios, and pronoun fields rather than in speech or in print
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *star* (subject), *star* (object), *stars* (possessive determiner), *stars* (independent possessive), *starself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who has stated *star/starself*. Human only, never a group, and never a generic or hypothetical person. As with the other nounself sets, the noun the form is built from is part of why a person chose it, and the set is announced rather than deduced.
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *star **is**, star **was**, star **has**, star **does***, not ✗*star are*.
**Pattern:** *Star* [singular verb] &middot; *Star* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *star* [verb]? &middot; [verb] *star* &middot; [preposition] *star* &middot; *stars* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *stars* (standalone possessive) &middot; *starself*
**Variants:** cited *star/starself* or *star/stars*; the possessive is also written *star's*
**Contrast:** Against [bun / bunself](#bun--bunself) and [kit / kitself](#kit--kitself), built the same way on other nouns, and against [fae / faer](#fae--faer), the nounself set old enough and common enough to have its own following. Against the ordinary noun *star*, which carries two everyday senses at once — the object in the sky and the celebrity — so *Star was late* is readable three ways until context settles it. The possessive is the harder collision: spoken *stars* is the plural noun, the noun's possessive, and the pronoun's determiner all at the same time, which is why writers using the set often spell it *star's* to buy one distinction back. **Before a noun *stars* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *stars notes* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md).

A nounself set built on *star*. It is not part of any traditional English paradigm and is not used by most speakers; where a person has stated it, it inflects across the same five slots as the older coined sets and takes a singular verb, and it is introduced the same way every set in this section is — on a badge, in a bio, or in a sentence of introduction.

1. *Star* has been running the Thursday reading group since last winter.
2. I asked *star* to send the photos over when *star* got home.
3. The introduction went, "This is Rowan — *star/starself*, if you're taking notes."
4. *Star* is bringing the projector, so nobody has to rent one.
5. Leave the packet with *star* at the front desk.
6. Everything on the second shelf is *stars*.
7. *Star* wrote the grant application *starself* over two weekends.

### they/them, she/her, he/him, ze/hir

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðeɪ ˈðɛm/ &middot; *thay-THEM* &middot; the slash is never read aloud — *she/her* is said *she her*, *ze/hir* is said *zee heer*, and a three-part label (*xe/xem/xyr*) is read the same way. In writing, the slash is the whole convention.
**Русский:** «она/её», «они/их» — не местоимение, а запись через слэш названия набора форм
**Type:** citation label — a pronoun set named by two or three of its forms, used as the set's **name** rather than as a pronoun &middot; **Case:** none; the label is a noun phrase (*my pronouns are they/them*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in email signatures, name badges, class rosters, staff directories, conference programs, and spoken introductions
**Person / number:** not applicable — the label names a paradigm rather than pointing at anybody. The person it belongs to is named separately, right beside it.
**Refers to:** the whole set of forms a person uses. *She/her* stands for *she, her, hers, herself*; *they/them* for *they, them, their, theirs, themself*; *ze/hir* for *ze, hir, hirs, hirself*. Two forms — subject and object — are the American norm, and a third is added where the possessive is not predictable from the first two (*ze/hir/hirs*, *xe/xem/xyr*). A slash between two whole sets (*she/they*, *it/they*) means the person uses both.
**Agreement:** the label behaves as a fixed name, and the verb follows the sentence rather than the pronoun inside the label: *My pronouns **are** they/them*, *"She/her" **is** what the badge says*.
**Pattern:** *my pronouns are* [set] &middot; [name] *(they/them)* &middot; *Pronouns: she/her* &middot; *goes by* [set] &middot; [set] */* [set] (two sets, either acceptable) &middot; *I'm Marcus, he/him*
**Variants:** two-form labels (*he/him*), three-form labels (*xe/xem/xyr*), paired sets (*she/they*), and the older *preferred pronouns*, now generally shortened to *pronouns*
**Contrast:** **The label is not the pronoun.** *She/her* in a signature block names a paradigm; *she* in a sentence does a pronoun's work, and putting the label where a pronoun belongs is a category error — ✗*I asked she/her to send the file* is *I asked **her** to send the file*. American institutional style has largely dropped *preferred pronouns* for plain *pronouns*, on the reasoning that a pronoun is not a preference. And a label naming two sets is an invitation rather than a puzzle: either one is correct.

The convention that lets a whole paradigm be named in a badge-sized space. It is a piece of American workplace and campus orthography rather than a word of the language, and it is the form in which most Americans first meet any pronoun set they did not grow up with.

1. The signature line reads "Dana Whitlock (they/them), Director of Admissions."
2. "Hi, I'm Marcus, *he/him* — I'll be running the workshop this morning."
3. The registration form takes any set: *she/her*, *he/him*, *they/them*, *ze/hir*, or free text.
4. Her card says *she/her*, his says *he/him*, and the new analyst's says *ze/zir*.
5. Some people list two sets — *she/they* means either one is fine.
6. The form used to say "preferred pronouns" and now says just "pronouns."

**See also:** [they](#they), [she](#she), [he](#he), [ze](#ze), [hir](#hir), [xe](#xe), [it (as a chosen personal pronoun)](#it-as-a-chosen-personal-pronoun), [themself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themself)

### ze

**Pronunciation:** /zi/ &middot; *ZEE* &middot; also /zeɪ/ (*ZAY*) for a minority of users. The variant spellings ***zie*** /zi/ and ***sie*** /si/ belong to the same set. No schwa weak form; like [she](#she) it simply shortens when unstressed. For most speakers *ze* and [xe](#xe) are homophones.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — самый частый; подлежащная форма ze/hir и ze/zir
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the subject form of the *ze / hir* and *ze / zir* sets &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** limited; the most widely used of the American neopronouns, met in pronoun introductions, email signatures, name badges, class rosters, and profiles
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. Two paradigms share this subject form: *ze / hir / hirs / hirself*, the older pairing, and *ze / zir / zirs / zirself*, which regularized everything around *z-*.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ze* — human only, never a group, and never a hypothetical or generic person, which is singular [they](#they)'s job. The set emerged in American trans and genderqueer writing and on the early internet in the 1990s; the writer Leslie Feinberg used *ze* and *hir* in print in that decade.
**Agreement:** **singular** verb forms — *ze **is**, ze **was**, ze **has**, ze **does***. Singular *they* keeps its plural verb; *ze* does not.
**Pattern:** *Ze* [singular verb] &middot; *Ze* [auxiliary] [verb] &middot; [auxiliary] *ze* [verb]? &middot; *hir* or *zir* [noun] &middot; *hirself* or *zirself*
**Variants:** *zie*, *sie*; the object form is *hir* for some users and *zir* for others, and the pairing is a fact about the person rather than about the word
**Contrast:** Against [xe](#xe): the same sound, a different spelling tradition, and a different set — *ze/hir* and *xe/xem/xyr* are not interchangeable in writing. Against singular [they](#they), which is standard, far commoner, and takes a plural verb. Against [hir](#hir) and [zir](#zir), the two object forms this subject form pairs with; the entry for each says which set it belongs to.

The most-used of the coined sets, and the one an American is likeliest to meet on a badge or in a signature line.

1. *Ze* has run the community garden for six years.
2. Did *ze* say when the grant report is due?
3. *Ze* is teaching the Wednesday workshop this term.
4. My neighbor and *ze* built the whole fence over one weekend.
5. *Ze* hasn't answered an email since the conference.
6. The directory lists "Sam Reyes (ze/hir), Program Coordinator."

**See also:** [hir](#hir), [zir](#zir), [hirs](02-possessive.md#hirs), [zirs](02-possessive.md#zirs), [xe](#xe), [they](#they)

### zir

**Pronunciation:** /zɪr/ &middot; *ZEER* &middot; also /zɚ/ (*ZUR*); weak /zɚ/. The variant spelling ***xir*** is pronounced the same way and belongs with the *xe* spelling of the set.
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — объектная и притяжательная форма набора ze/zir
**Type:** personal pronoun; neopronoun — the object form of the *ze / zir* set &middot; **Case:** object, and also the possessive determiner (*zir coat*) &middot; **Register:** limited; one of the more frequently used American neopronoun forms, met in signatures, badges, rosters, and profiles
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ze* or *zie* (subject), **zir** (object and determiner), *zirs* (independent possessive), *zirself* (reflexive).
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ze / zir* — human only, never a group.
**Agreement:** none of its own — an object controls no verb. The subject form takes a singular verb (*ze **is***).
**Pattern:** [verb] *zir* &middot; [verb] *zir* [direct object] &middot; [preposition] *zir* &middot; *zir* [noun] (the determiner use) &middot; *zirs* (standalone possessive) &middot; *zirself*
**Variants:** *xir*, the spelling used alongside *xe*, and *zer*; *zir* also fills the independent possessive slot for some users, on the model of *his*, though *zirs* is the regularized form
**Contrast:** Against [hir](#hir): the same slot in the older pairing, and the choice between them belongs to the person, not to the grammar. Against [xem](#xem), the object form of the *xe* set. Against *sir*, with which it rhymes for speakers who say /zɚ/ — a real source of mishearing on the telephone. **Before a noun *zir* is a determiner, not a pronoun** — *zir coat* belongs with [the possessive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md), and the standalone possessive is [zirs](02-possessive.md#zirs).

The object form of the regularized *z-* set, and the neopronoun most often paired with *ze* on American forms today.

1. I emailed *zir* the schedule on Monday.
2. Ask *zir* whether the studio is free on Saturday.
3. The package on the counter is for *zir*.
4. We met *zir* at the conference in Portland two years ago.
5. Give the extra badge to Priya and *zir*.
6. That's *zir* jacket on the hook by the door. (the determiner use, which belongs with the determiners)

**See also:** [ze](#ze), [hir](#hir), [zirs](02-possessive.md#zirs), [xem](#xem), [xe](#xe), [they](#they)

## Case: the rule, the test, and where Americans actually break it

**The rule.** A pronoun takes the case its own job in its own clause requires. Subject of a verb → subject case. Direct object, indirect object, object of a preposition → object case.

| Function | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| subject of a verb | I, he, she, we, they | ***She** called.* |
| direct object | me, him, her, us, them | *I called **her**.* |
| indirect object | me, him, her, us, them | *Give **him** the receipt.* |
| object of a preposition | me, him, her, us, them | *This is for **us**.* |
| complement of *be* | *me, him, her, us, them* in practice | *It's **me**.* |

*You* and *it* have one form for every row above, which is why they never enter into any of this. Everything below concerns the ten forms that do change.

**The test.** Nearly every real-world case error happens inside a coordination, and one move fixes all of them: **delete the other person and read the sentence aloud.** Your ear has never once produced *to I* or *me went*, and it will not start now.

| Sentence | Delete the other person | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| *He gave it to Bob and **me**.* | *He gave it to **me**.* | ✓ correct |
| ✗*He gave it to Bob and **I**.* | ✗*He gave it to **I**.* | ✗ hypercorrection |
| *between you and **me*** | *between … **me*** | ✓ correct |
| ✗*between you and **I*** | ✗*between … **I*** | ✗ hypercorrection |
| *They invited my wife and **me**.* | *They invited **me**.* | ✓ correct |
| ✗*They invited my wife and **I**.* | ✗*They invited **I**.* | ✗ hypercorrection |
| ***She** and **I** went.* | ***She** went.* / ***I** went.* | ✓ correct |
| ✗*Her and me went.* | ✗***Her** went.* / ✗***Me** went.* | ✗ nonstandard |
| *Bob and **I** finished the report.* | ***I** finished the report.* | ✓ correct |
| ✗*Me and Bob finished the report.* | ✗***Me** finished the report.* | ✗ nonstandard |
| ✗*Me and him went to the concert.* | ✗***Me** went.* / ✗***Him** went.* | ✗ nonstandard |

Note what the middle rows show: **the hypercorrection is not only a preposition problem.** *They invited my wife and I* has no preposition anywhere in it — the coordination is a plain direct object, and the object form is required there for exactly the same reason.

**Where the breaks happen, and what they mean.**

- ***Between you and I*** is a **hypercorrection** — an error produced by over-applying a rule. Speakers are corrected so often for *me and Bob* that they come to hear *and me* as suspect anywhere, and start writing *and I* wherever a coordination appears, including the object slots where only the object form is possible. It is common in educated American speech, it appears in Shakespeare, and it is still marked as an error by every American style guide.
- ***They invited my wife and I*** is the same hypercorrection in a direct object, and it is the version most likely to survive a proofread, because the writer is watching for prepositions. Delete *my wife* and the sentence answers itself.
- ***Me and him went*** is nonstandard in the other direction. It is extremely common in casual American speech and will be corrected in any classroom or edited document. Note that the objection is to the case, not to the order: fixing it means ***He and I** went*, not *Him and me went* rearranged.
- ***It's me*** is **standard English**, full stop. Traditional grammar prescribed the subject case after the linking verb *be* (*It is I*, *That is he*), on the model of Latin. Modern American usage overwhelmingly uses the object form, and *It is I* now sounds stiff outside the telephone formula *This is she*. Both are defensible; only one is what people say.
- **Appositive pronouns** take the case of the slot, not of the noun beside them: ***We** editors work late* (subject) but *They gave **us** editors a deadline* (object). Delete the noun and the ear decides.
- **After *but* and *except* meaning "other than,"** the object case is normal: *Everyone but **me** had read it.* (*Everyone but I* is defended by some traditionalists on the grounds that *but* is a conjunction; almost nobody says it.)
- **Before a gerund**, formal writing prefers the possessive and speech uses the object form: *I appreciate **your** taking the time* (formal) / *I appreciate **you** taking the time* (ordinary). Both are current; only the first is safe in a conservative document.

**The same rule reaches *who* and *whom*.** *Who* is the subject form and *whom* the object form, exactly as *he* is to *him* — and the substitution test is the same one: if *he/she/they* fits the slot, write *who*; if *him/her/them* fits, *whom* is the traditional form. What has changed is the honest description of where *whom* still lives. In American English **it survives mainly right after a preposition** — *to whom it may concern*, *for whom the bell tolls*, *with whom I studied*, *the tenant to whom we sent the notice* — and there it is genuinely required in edited prose. Everywhere else, and especially at the front of a question or clause where the preposition has drifted to the end, *who* is normal and unremarkable in standard American English: *Who did you give it to?* is what educated speakers say and write, and *Whom did you give it to?* is stiff at best and, at worst, sounds like a person who has just learned the rule. The one thing to avoid is hypercorrection in the other direction — ✗*Whom shall I say is calling?*, where the pronoun is the subject of *is calling* and must be *who*. Full treatment in [relative pronouns](05-relative.md#whom) and [interrogative pronouns](06-interrogative.md).

## Case after *than* and *as*

Both cases are established English, and which one is "correct" depends on whether you read *than* as a conjunction introducing a reduced clause or as a preposition.

> She is taller than ***I*** [am]. — conjunction reading; formal, and the one traditionalists require
> She is taller than ***me***. — preposition reading; normal in speech and accepted in most writing

The safest formal solution keeps the verb: *taller than **I am***. That is unimpeachable in any register and never sounds stilted.

But there is one situation where the case is not a style choice, because the two forms mean different things:

> He likes pizza more than ***I*** [do]. — I like pizza less than he does.
> He likes pizza more than ***me***. — He likes pizza more than he likes me.

The same split applies to *as*: *She works as hard as **he** [does]* vs. *as hard as **him***. When ambiguity is possible, supply the verb and the problem disappears.

## The order of pronouns in a coordination

Putting yourself last is a convention of politeness, not a rule of grammar — but it is expected in careful writing, and violating it reads as brusque.

> *My brother and **I** drove to Chicago.* — preferred over *I and my brother*
> *The award went to her and **me**.* — preferred over *me and her*
> *You, Marcus, and **I** are on the same team.* — second person, then third, then first

The order carries no grammatical weight, and it does not change the case. In an object slot *me and Bob* is already correct and only impolite (*He thanked me and Bob* → *He thanked Bob and me*). In a subject slot it is wrong because of the **case**, not the order, so reordering it to *Bob and me* fixes nothing — the repair is *Bob and **I***.

## Agreement: the verb each pronoun takes

Only one row of this table is genuinely irregular, and it is the first one. Everything else follows the ordinary present-tense rule: the third-person singular takes *-s* (*is, has, does, goes*), and every other person and number takes the bare form (*are, have, do, go*).

| Pronoun | *be* (present) | *be* (past) | *have* | *do* | ordinary verb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | am | was | have | do | go |
| you (sg. or pl.) | are | were | have | do | go |
| he, she, it | is | was | has | does | goes |
| singular *they* | **are** | **were** | have | do | go |
| we | are | were | have | do | go |
| they (pl.) | are | were | have | do | go |

Three things this table is saying out loud:

- ***I am*** is unique. No other pronoun in the language takes *am*, and *I* takes nothing else.
- ***You are*** is correct for one person. *You* was originally the plural and kept the plural verb when it swallowed the singular; ✗*you was* is a real and systematic feature of Southern American English, African American English, and working-class varieties nationwide, and it is nonstandard in edited English.
- **Singular *they* keeps the plural verb.** *Alex said **they were** running late* — never ✗*they was*. Adopting singular *they* changes the pronoun, not the conjugation. Its reflexive is *themselves*, or *themself* when the referent is explicitly one person.

**Object pronouns control no verb at all.** *Me, him, her, us, them* never trigger agreement, because agreement is a subject's job. After *be*, the verb still agrees with the **subject**, not with the pronoun sitting in the complement slot: *It **is** them*, *That **was** us*, *It **is** me*.

**Quantified pronoun phrases agree with the quantifier, not with the pronoun.** These belong to the [indefinite and quantifying pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md), and only the *of them / of us / of you* patterns touch this file:

| Phrase | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *each of them/us/you* | singular | *Each of them **is** on the list.* |
| *either of them*, *neither of them* | singular | *Neither of them **has** replied.* |
| *both of them*, *several of them*, *a few of them* | plural | *Both of them **are** late.* |
| *none of them* | **either**, with a note | *None of them **fits*** (not one) / *None of them **fit*** (no members) — both standard, plural the older and commoner |
| *all/some/most of them* | matches the referent | *All of it **is** gone* / *All of them **are** gone* |

Strip the *of them* and nothing changes: the quantifier still decides the verb. *Each **is** numbered.* *Either **is** fine.* *Neither **has** replied.* *Both **are** late.* *Several **were** missing.* *A few **have** arrived.* *None **fits*** or *None **fit***, on the same terms as above. *All **is** lost* (mass) / *All **are** present* (count). Full entries in [indefinite and quantifying pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md).

And the corresponding indefinite antecedents, which are what most often feed a personal pronoun: [*everyone*, *everybody*, *someone*, *somebody*, *anyone*, *anybody*, *nobody*, and *no one*](07-indefinite-compound.md) all take a **singular verb** — *Everyone **is** here*, *Somebody **has** left a bag*, *Nobody **has** called*, never ✗*everyone are* — while the pronoun that points back at them is ordinarily **singular *they***: *Everyone **is** here, and **they** all brought something*; *Somebody left **their** umbrella*. That mismatch is not an error; it is the settled shape of modern English, and it is the reason singular *they* exists.

## Antecedents: what each pronoun may point at

A personal pronoun is only as clear as the noun it points back at. The rules are short.

| Antecedent | Pronoun | Note |
|---|---|---|
| one person, gender known and relevant | *he/him*, *she/her* | a named person's own pronouns govern, whatever they are |
| one person, gender unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary | *they/them* | standard American English; see [they](#they) |
| one person who uses a coined set | that person's own set — *ze/hir*, *xe/xem*, *ey/em*, *fae/faer* | singular verb (*ze **is***); see [neopronouns](#neopronouns-and-chosen-pronoun-sets) |
| a thing, place, abstraction, or substance | *it* | |
| an animal whose sex is known and matters | *he/him*, *she/her* | a pet, a named racehorse |
| an animal whose sex is unknown or irrelevant | *it* | |
| a whole preceding clause or situation | *it* | *…and **it** cost us the contract* |
| a collective noun (*committee, team, company*) | *it* in American English | British English often takes *they* |
| any plural noun phrase | *they/them* | people or things alike |
| no antecedent at all — weather, time, distance, extraposition | dummy *it* | *It is raining. It is three miles.* |

Two things this table deliberately does **not** cover, because they are relative pronouns and belong to [relative pronouns](05-relative.md). The first is which relative pronoun matches which antecedent:

| Antecedent | Relative pronoun | Example and note |
|---|---|---|
| a person | *who* — *whom* in object slots | *the tenant **who** signed the lease*; *the tenant to **whom** we sent the notice* |
| a thing, an animal, an idea | *which* | *the clause **which** we struck* — not used for people |
| **either** — person or thing | *that* | *the tenant **that** signed*, *the clause **that** we struck*; standard for both, and for people it is older than *who* |
| **either** — person or thing | *whose* | *a woman **whose** car was towed*, *a house **whose** roof leaks* — *whose* is the possessive relative for things as well as people, and the workaround *of which* is stiffer, not more correct |

The second is the much-repeated *that*-restrictive/*which*-nonrestrictive split, an **American editing convention** rather than a rule of the language: *which* has introduced restrictive clauses in good English writing for centuries, and *Chicago* and the *AP Stylebook* recommend the split as house practice rather than reporting it as grammar. The commas are the part that is genuinely obligatory.

## Second person: one form, two numbers

Standard English lost its singular/plural distinction in the second person when [thou](#thou) died out around 1650 and *you* absorbed both jobs — bringing the plural verb with it, which is why *you are* is correct for one person. American English has been rebuilding the missing plural ever since, and every region has its own solution.

| Form | Region | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [you all](#you-all) | nationwide | informal to neutral | unmarked everywhere; with *you two*, one of the two usable in careful writing |
| *all of you* | nationwide | neutral to formal | the safe choice in documents |
| [you two](#you-two), *you both* | nationwide | neutral | the only frame that names an exact number, and standard in writing; the partitive [the two of you](#the-two-of-us) says the same thing a shade more formally |
| [you guys](#you-guys) | nationwide | informal | used for mixed and all-female groups; increasingly avoided as gendered |
| [you people](#you-people) | nationwide | informal, and usually heard as hostile | grammatically the plain plural, pragmatically loaded; see the entry |
| [y'all](#yall) | South, African American English | standard within those varieties | spreading nationally; possessive *y'all's*; intensified *all y'all* |
| [youse](#youse) | NYC, Philadelphia, Boston, Chicago, northern NJ | nonstandard | tracks Irish immigration |
| [you'uns](#youuns), *yinz* | Appalachia; Pittsburgh | nonstandard | from *you ones*; a Pittsburgher is a *Yinzer* |
| *you lot* | British, not American | informal | listed only so it is not mistaken for an Americanism |

None of the marked forms belongs in formal writing. When a document must genuinely address a group, write *all of you*, *you all*, *both of you*, or name the group.

## *We*: inclusive, exclusive, editorial, royal, and the nurse's *we*

English marks none of these grammatically. *We* is one word doing the seven jobs below, and only context separates them.

| Use | Who it actually means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **inclusive** | speaker + addressee (+ others) | *Shall **we** get started?* |
| **exclusive** | speaker + others, not the addressee | ***We**'re going to lunch — see you at two.* |
| **generic** | humanity, or everyone in a shared situation | ***We** still don't know what causes migraines.* |
| **editorial** | one writer, speaking as the publication or the institution | ***We** endorse the bond measure.* |
| **authorial** | one author, plural by convention of the genre | *In this paper **we** argue …* |
| **royal (majestic)** | one monarch, speaking as the institution | ***We** are not amused.* |
| **nurse's (patronizing)** | the addressee alone, doing all the work | *How are **we** feeling today?* |

The inclusive/exclusive ambiguity is the one that causes real trouble, since *We're going to lunch* is either an invitation or a dismissal. English has no fix built in — speakers disambiguate by adding *we all*, *the two of us*, *you and I*, or *the rest of us*. Languages that mark the distinction grammatically include Tagalog, Cherokee, and Mandarin (*zánmen* inclusive vs. *wǒmen* general).

The **nurse's *we*** deserves a warning label. Used by a clinician, teacher, or caregiver, it converts a *you* into a shared *we* the speaker is not actually part of. With rapport it lands as warmth; without it, it lands as condescension, and many patients specifically object to it. Recognize it; think twice before producing it.

## Weak forms: what you actually hear

Personal pronouns carry old information, so they are almost never stressed, and almost never pronounced in full. This table is the difference between reading English and hearing it.

| Pronoun | Strong | Weak | What it sounds like in a sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | /aɪ/ | — | offglide shortens: *I dunno* [ɑ dəˈnoʊ] |
| me | /mi/ | /mi/ (short) | no schwa form: *gimme* /ˈɡɪmi/ |
| you | /ju/ | /jə/, /jʊ/ | *did you* /ˈdɪdʒə/, *don't you* /ˈdoʊntʃə/, *got you* → *gotcha* /ˈɡɑtʃə/; written [*ya*](#ya) |
| he | /hi/ | /i/ | *is he* /ˈɪzi/, *did he* /ˈdɪdi/ |
| him | /hɪm/ | /ɪm/, /əm/ | *tell him* /ˈtɛlɪm/, *get him* /ˈɡɛɾɪm/ |
| she | /ʃi/ | /ʃi/ (short) | no /h/ to drop and no schwa form |
| her | /hɚ/ | /ɚ/ | *tell her* /ˈtɛlɚ/, *call her* = *caller* |
| it | /ɪt/ | /ət/, [ɪʔ] | *get it* /ˈɡɛɾɪt/, *forget it* [fɚˈɡɛɾ ɪʔ] |
| we | /wi/ | /wi/ (short) | *we're* often /wɚ/, colliding with *were* |
| us | /ʌs/ | /əs/, /s/ | *let us* → *let's* /lɛts/ |
| they | /ðeɪ/ | [ðe] | *they're* = *there* = *their* /ðɛr/ |
| them | /ðɛm/ | /ðəm/, /əm/, /m̩/ | *get them* → *get 'em* /ˈɡɛɾəm/ |

Two patterns account for most of it. **H-dropping**: *he, him, her* lose their /h/ whenever they are unstressed and not at the start of an utterance — which is nearly always. **Coalescence**: /t/ or /d/ plus the /j/ of *you* fuses into /tʃ/ or /dʒ/, which is why *Did you eat?* comes out as *Dja eat?* and why learners hear no *you* at all.

Reduction is **not** dialect. *'em*, *ya*, *tellim*, *whaddaya* are what standard General American sounds like at speed, in any register of speech; only the spelling is informal. The forms in the next section are a different matter.

## Nonstandard pronoun forms: recognize them, label them, don't write them

Everything here is **nonstandard** in edited American English — it will be marked wrong by an editor, a teacher, or a test — and everything here is also **extremely common in speech**, produced by tens of millions of native speakers as their ordinary form. Both halves of that sentence are true at once, and a dictionary that hides either half is lying. Where a form belongs to a nameable region or variety, the name is given; where it is general vernacular English with no single home, that is said too.

| Form | Standard equivalent | Where it lives | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***hisself*** | *himself* | Southern American English, Appalachian English, African American English, and general vernacular English across the country; also long-standing in rural British dialect | **Regularization.** *Myself, yourself, ourselves* are all *possessive + self*; *hisself* simply completes the pattern that *himself* breaks. It is the more logical form, and it is still nonstandard. |
| ***theirselves*** (also *theirself*) | *themselves* | the same varieties as *hisself*, and they usually travel together | Same regularization: *their + selves*, on the model of *ourselves* and *yourselves*. |
| ***them apples***, ***them boys*** | *those apples*, *those boys* | rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech nationwide | The object pronoun *them* doing a demonstrative's job. Frozen as a joke even for standard speakers in *Them's the breaks* and *Them's fightin' words*. |
| ***Them are the ones*** | *Those are the ones* | same as above | The same *them*, now in subject position. |
| ***youse***, ***yous*** | *you* (plural) | New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, Chicago; Irish, Scottish, and Australian English | A plural *you* built with the regular noun *-s*. Carries a heavier working-class marking in the US than *y'all* does. Full entry above: [youse](#youse). |
| ***we'uns***, ***us'uns*** | *we*, *us* | Appalachia and the South Midlands, receding | Built from *we ones* on the model of *you'uns*, and the only **first-person** form in this table. It is also the only built pronoun here that fills no gap at all — *we* was never ambiguous — so it exists purely to draw a line between our group and yours. Full entry above: [we'uns](#weuns). |
| ***yo*** as a third-person singular (*Yo was tuckin' in his shirt*) | *he*, *she*, or singular *they* | Baltimore, chiefly adolescent and chiefly African American English | A gender-neutral third-person singular that arose in ordinary speech rather than being proposed by anybody — the one item in this file that filled the gap the two-century record of coinages never filled. Takes a **singular** verb (*yo is*), where singular *they* takes a plural one. Full entry above: [yo](#yo). |
| ***Me and him went***, ***Us guys went*** | *He and I went*, *We guys went* | general vernacular American speech, all regions | Object forms in a subject slot. The most-corrected error in American classrooms. |
| ***between you and I***, ***They invited my wife and I*** | *between you and me*, *They invited my wife and me* | educated American speech and writing — this one runs *upward* through the social scale | Hypercorrection, not vernacular. See [the case section](#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it). |
| ***you was***, ***we was***, ***they was*** | *you were, we were, they were* | Southern American English, African American English, working-class varieties nationwide | Leveling *was* across the whole past-tense paradigm. |
| ***I didn't see nobody***, ***He don't know nothing*** | *I didn't see anybody*, *He doesn't know anything* | African American English, Southern American English, Appalachian English, and working-class varieties in every region | **Negative concord**, below. |
| ***let's us go*** | *let's go* | Southern and Midland American speech | A doubled *us* — *let (us) us go*. |
| ***hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn*** | *his, hers, ours, yours, theirs* | Appalachian and South Midland speech, receding | Possessives built on the *mine/thine* model. See [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md#hisn-hern-ourn-yourn-theirn). |

**On the double negative.** *I didn't see nobody* does not mean "I saw somebody." In the varieties that use it, the second negative **agrees** with the first rather than canceling it — the technical name is **negative concord** — and the sentence means exactly what *I didn't see anybody* means in standard English. This is how negation works in French (*ne … personne*), in Spanish (*no vi a nadie*), in Russian, and in Old and Middle English, where Chaucer stacked four negatives across two lines of the *General Prologue* and no reader took him to have said something positive. The "two negatives make a positive" argument is imported arithmetic, invented by eighteenth-century grammarians, and it is not a fact about English.

None of which makes it standard. Negative concord is one of the most reliably stigmatized features of American English, and edited writing allows **one** negative per clause. There are two ways to get there: negate the verb and use the *any-* series (*I didn't see **anybody***, *He doesn't know **anything***), or leave the verb positive and let the pronoun carry the negation (*I saw **nobody***, *He knows **nothing***). Both are standard; what is not standard is using both at once. Recognize the double negative, understand that it is rule-governed rather than sloppy, and do not write it outside quoted speech.

The same disclaimer covers the whole table. These forms are systematic, ancient in several cases, and often more regular than the standard forms that displaced them. They are still nonstandard, and a learner needs to be able to hear them without producing them. The reflexive forms have full entries in [reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself) and in [archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns](10-archaic-dialectal.md#hisself).
