Possessive pronouns#

The words that answer whose? with no noun attached — mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs, and every noun phrase in English that can take 's and then stand on its own.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

A possessive pronoun replaces an entire possessive noun phrase: my coat becomes mine, the Smiths' house becomes the Smiths'. That replacement is the whole line between this file and the determiner dictionary — a possessive determiner comes before a noun (my coat), a possessive pronoun has nothing after it (the coat is mine) — and the slot test settles every word in the file. Two facts run through all of them. First, no personal possessive pronoun takes an apostrophe: ours, yours, hers, theirs, its, and relative whose, which is why ✗ our's is always wrong and why its and it's are the most-corrected pair in written English. The genitives built on nouns and indefinites do take one (John's, the children's, nobody's, y'all's), and that split is the whole reason the error happens. Second, when a possessive pronoun is the subject, the verb agrees with the thing possessed, never with the possessor — Mine is on the table for one bag, Mine are on the table for several.

Every pronoun catalogued in this file is in the possessive (genitive) case — the apostrophe impostors at the end (it's, there's, they're, who's, you're) are a subject plus a verb and carry no case at all, which is exactly why they are filed as impostors. None of the genuine possessives ever does a subject's or an object's work — that is what she, her, and hers are three different words for — and the coordination traps that wreck case elsewhere (✗ Me and him went, ✗ between you and I) have their own possessive version here in ✗ my wife and I's. All of it is settled by the same deletion test, laid out in Notes and tricky cases below.

The personal possessive pronouns#

Seven forms, one for each person and number, each pairing with a possessive determiner that cannot stand alone (my ~ mine, your ~ yours, her ~ hers, our ~ ours, their ~ theirs). His is the one word that serves as both. Its is the hole in the paradigm: the determiner is everywhere, the standalone pronoun essentially never occurs. None of them takes an apostrophe, and none of them changes shape for the number of the thing possessed.

hers#

Pronunciation
/hɝz/, weak /ɚz/ · HURZ, weak erz · the weak form belongs to the double genitive — a friend of hers /ə frɛnd əv ɚz/, where the h drops entirely
Русский
её — принадлежащее ей, без существительного: «эта книга — её»; форма неизменяемая
Type
possessive pronoun (independent genitive)
Case
possessive
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person, singular possessor, female — or anyone referred to as she. Invariant: the form does not change with the number of the thing possessed (that book is hers, those books are hers).
Refers to
something belonging to, produced by, or associated with a female person or animal already identified. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (a cousin of hers), or inanimate (the blue one is hers); the possessor must be animate and definite. For a possessor of unknown or nonbinary gender, use theirs.
Agreement
carries no ending of its own. When hers is the subject, the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, not the possessor: Hers is on the table (one bag), Hers are on the table (several). When hers is a subject complement, the subject has already fixed the verb: The seats by the door are hers.
Pattern
[noun phrase] be hers — that desk is hers · hers [verb] — hers arrived first · [verb] hers — take hers · [preposition] hers — park next to hers · [noun] of hers — a colleague of hers · comparison — bigger than hers, the same as hers · coordination — hers and mine
Contrast
Her precedes a noun (her coat); hers stands alone (the coat is hers). ✗ her's is never correct — no possessive pronoun in English takes an apostrophe. Keep it apart from object her as well: I called her (object of a verb) versus That call was hers (possessive). Before an -ing form the determiner is required, not the pronoun: ✓ We appreciated her coming, ✗ We appreciated hers coming.

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to her.”

  1. That desk by the window is hers.
  2. My commute is forty minutes; hers is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — is)
  3. Hers were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — were)
  4. She lent me a charger of hers and I never gave it back. (double genitive)
  5. Take mine — hers is out of ink.
  6. Isn't the gray coat hers? (question)
  7. The mistake wasn't hers. (negative)

  4. She's an old teacher of his from middle school. (double genitive)
  5. Which locker is his? (question)
  6. That call was never his to make. (negative)
  7. He swore the truck was his, but the title said otherwise.

its#

Pronunciation
/ɪts/ — no weak form · ITS · like mine, whose, and thine, its never reduces, and it is pronounced exactly like it's, which is why the two are confused only in writing
Русский
его, её (о предмете, животном) — пустая клетка: самостоятельно почти не употребляется, вместо него its own
Type
possessive pronoun — the empty cell in the paradigm: grammars list the form, but it almost never stands alone
Case
possessive
Register
neutral as a determiner; as a standalone pronoun, rare enough that most Americans hear it as an error
Person / number
third person singular, non-personal. Invariant.
Refers to
something belonging to a thing, place, animal of unstated sex, organization, or abstraction — never to a named person. Where the standalone slot is needed, English fills it with its own (a life of its own) or with a noun genitive (that bowl is the dog's).
Agreement
in practice there is nothing to agree with, because the independent form does not occur. Use one of the substitutes and the verb then follows the head noun: The dog's is the red bowl (singular), The dog's are in the hall (plural).
Pattern
✗ [noun phrase] be its — not English · the substitutes: [noun] of its own — a life of its own; [noun] all its own — a character all its own; noun genitive — that collar is the dog's · determiner use, which belongs to the determiner dictionary — its [noun]
Contrast
Its versus it's is the most-corrected error in written English. Its is the possessive and never takes an apostrophe (the dog wagged its tail); it's is the contraction of it is or it has (it's raining, it's been a week). The test: if the word can be expanded to it is or it has, write it's; otherwise write its. The reason the error is so common is that every other possessive in English — John's, the company's, somebody else's — is built with an apostrophe, so the hand adds one by reflex; the personal possessives are precisely the forms that refuse it. And ✗ its' does not exist in English in any sense at all.

The third-person non-personal possessive — one of the two places where the possessive-pronoun paradigm has a hole in it, the other being generic one's.

1. The ordinary possessive with a noun after it (its tail, its lease) is a determiner, and it is not catalogued here — it is a cross-reference into Possessive and genitive determiners, and it appears in the examples below only so it can be told apart from the pronoun. 2. The standalone pronoun slot, which English simply leaves empty: ✗ That collar is its is not a sentence anyone says, and comparisons come out as than theirs or than the company's.

  1. The city has a rhythm all its own. (its own — the ordinary way English fills the empty slot)
  2. Once the rumor started, it took on a life of its own. (of its own stands in for the impossible ✗ a life of its)
  3. Each department manages a budget of its own.
  4. The dog wagged its tail the whole way home. (determiner use, no apostrophe — the form that gets misspelled ✗ it's tail)
  5. ✗ That collar is its. — not English. Say That collar is the dog's.
  6. ✗ Our revenue is higher than its. — say than theirs, or than that company's.
  7. ✗ Its is the red bowl. → ✓ The dog's is the red bowl. (subject position — the repair is a noun genitive)
  8. If it expands to it is or it has, it isn't its: It's been a long week, but The company lost its lease.

first person, singular possessor — always the speaker. Invariant: that seat is mine, those seats are mine.

  4. He's a colleague of mine from the Denver office. (double genitive)
  5. Your numbers and mine don't match. (coordination)
  6. Which of these coats is mine? (question)
  7. The fault was mine, not hers. (negative contrast)

Gender-neutral third-person possessives#

English has never had a settled third-person singular possessive for a person whose gender is unstated, unknown, or neither male nor female, and it has filled the gap four different ways. Singular theirs is the one that won: it is standard American English in speech and in edited prose, AP and Chicago both endorse it, and it is catalogued above with the rest of the personal forms. His or hers is the formal coordination that American editing used before that, and it now reads as dated. Generic his was the rule until the 1970s and is dated in the other direction; it needs no separate entry, because the form is simply his. The rest of this section is the coined possessives — aers, cos, eirs, faers, hirs, nirs, pers, thons, vers, xyrs, zirs — each the independent member of a set built on the model of he / him / his / his / himself, and used by particular people for themselves rather than as general-purpose words of the language. The oldest of them is thons, coined in 1884 and carried in American dictionaries for two-thirds of the twentieth century, which is a useful reminder that the problem these forms address is not a recent one.

Three facts hold across all of them. Each is the independent form of its set, so the slot test that runs through this whole file applies unchanged: zir coat has a noun after it and is a determiner, the coat is zirs has nothing after it and is the pronoun. None takes an apostrophe — ✗ zir's, ✗ eir's — for exactly the reason hers and theirs do not. And the verb still agrees with the thing possessed: Zirs is on the table for one bag, Zirs are on the table for several. These forms are younger than the rest of the file's stock and their paradigms are not uniform; where a set has more than one attested shape, the entry says which. American style guides do not list them among the ordinary pronouns of English, and direct writers instead to use the forms a person states for themselves, glossing an unfamiliar one at first mention or recasting the sentence where that reads more clearly.

Pronunciation
/ɛrz/ — no established weak form · AIRZ (rhymes with CHAIRS) · a homophone of airs and heirs; unstressed inside a phrase it drifts toward /ɚz/, which is where unstressed hers and theirs also land, so a friend of eirs is hard to place by ear alone
Русский
неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора ey/em/eir (местоимения Спивака)
Type
possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the ey / em / eir set, usually called the Spivak pronouns
Case
possessive
Register
limited. In circulation since the mid-1970s and 1980s, and an American meets it in online writing, in some science fiction and academic prose, and in pronoun introductions; it is far rarer than singular theirs.
Person / number
third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs ey (subject), em (object), eir (determiner), eirs (pronoun), emself (reflexive) — they / them / their / theirs / themself with the th- clipped off the front. Invariant: the form does not change with the number of the thing possessed.
Refers to
one specific person who uses ey / em / eir for themselves — human only, and never a group. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (a cousin of eirs), or inanimate (the blue folder is eirs).
Agreement
the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, exactly as with hers and theirs: Eirs is on the table (one bag), Eirs are on the table (several). In its own clause the subject form ey normally takes singular agreement — ey is, ey has — where singular they takes are; usage there is not fully settled, and none of it touches the verb after eirs.
Pattern
[noun phrase] be eirs — the corner desk is eirs · eirs [verb] — eirs arrived first · [verb] eirs — take eirs · [preposition] eirs — park next to eirs · [noun] of eirs — a colleague of eirs · comparison — older than eirs · coordination — eirs and mine · the determiner use, which belongs to the determiner dictionary — eir [noun]
Contrast
Eir precedes a noun, eirs stands alone — the same split as their ~ theirs, which is precisely where the pair comes from. It takes no apostrophe: ✗ eir's, for the same reason ✗ their's is wrong. On paper the commonest difficulty is that a reader who has not met the form takes it for a typo for theirs or heirs, which is why a gloss at first mention earns its space. Against zirs and xyrs the difference is which set a person uses, not what the word means.
Variants
E, Em, Eir, Eirs, Emself with initial capitals — Michael Spivak's spelling, which is why the whole set is known as the Spivak pronouns; the lowercase ey / em / eir spelling is Christine Elverson's, formed by clipping they / them / their. Both are current, and eirs is the independent possessive in either.

The independent possessive of the ey / em / eir set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to em.”

  1. The corner desk by the window is eirs.
  2. My commute is forty minutes; eirs is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — is)
  3. Eirs were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — were)
  4. She's an old colleague of eirs from the Denver office. (double genitive)
  5. Which locker is eirs? (question)
  6. The mistake wasn't eirs. (negative)
  7. Clip your form to the board next to eirs. (object of a preposition)
  8. ✗ Eir's is the one with the cracked screen. → ✓ Eirs is the one with the cracked screen. (no possessive pronoun in English takes an apostrophe)

his or hers#

Pronunciation
/ˌhɪz ɚ ˈhɝz/, weak /ɪz ɚ ˈhɝz/ · hiz-er-HURZ, weak iz-er-HURZ · the stress lands on hers; unstressed, the h of his drops entirely, exactly as it does in a friend of his
Русский
его или её — книжн., устар.: о лице неуказанного пола; сегодня вытеснено theirs
Type
coordinated possessive pronoun (independent genitive) — the standalone counterpart of the determiner his or her
Case
possessive
Register
formal and dated. The house solution of American edited prose from roughly the late 1970s through the 1990s, when it was the standard repair for generic his; an American still meets it in contracts, employee handbooks, insurance policies, and statutes drafted in those decades. Singular theirs has since replaced it almost everywhere.
Person / number
third person singular, gender unspecified. Never for a plural possessor — ✗ the employees … his or hers takes theirs — and, because the phrase names exactly two possibilities, not the form to use for a person who has stated they or a neopronoun.
Refers to
one unidentified person of unstated or irrelevant gender — the each employee, every applicant, a juror of institutional prose. Human only. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable.
Agreement
the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: His or hers is the only signature missing (one signature), His or hers were the only forms returned (several). Separately, the antecedent that licenses the phrase is grammatically singular and takes a singular verb of its own: ✓ Each employee is responsible for his or hers, ✗ Each employee are responsible for his or hers.
Pattern
[noun phrase] be his or hers — the last locker is his or hers · his or hers [verb] — his or hers is missing · [verb] his or hers — use his or hers · [preposition] his or hers — file it with his or hers · comparison — later than his or hers · the determiner, which belongs to the determiner dictionary — his or her [noun]
Contrast
Against singular theirs, which is shorter, standard, endorsed by AP and Chicago, and covers a person who uses they — which a phrase naming two options cannot. Against generic his (every applicant should sign his), which is dated in the other direction. In practice the best repair is usually neither one: make the noun plural — All employees are responsible for their own equipment. Note also that both halves have to be the independent forms: ✓ his or hers, and ✗ his or her with nothing after it, which is the determiner left stranded. ✗ his or her's is wrong twice over.
Variants
his/hers, her or his, hers or his, and the three-way his, hers, or theirs; the matching determiners are his or her, his/her, and her or his.

The formal coordination that stands in for a single possessor of unstated gender — “the one belonging to him, or to her.”

  1. The last locker in the row is his or hers, not mine. (subject complement)
  2. His or hers is the only signature still missing from the form. (subject, singular referent — is)
  3. His or hers were the only forms returned on time. (subject, plural referent — were)
  4. Every applicant submits a transcript, and his or hers has to reach us by Friday.
  5. A juror may not discuss the case with a spouse of his or hers. (double genitive — and a fair sample of the clumsiness that drove the phrase out of use)
  6. Is that badge his or hers? (question)
  7. The error was not his or hers. (negative)
  8. Rewritten the way an American editor would do it today: Theirs is the only signature still missing.

Genitive noun phrases standing alone#

The genitive is productive: any noun phrase at all can take 's and then drop the noun after it, leaving the genitive to do a pronoun's work. Grammarians call this the fused or elliptical genitive — the coats are the children's is the children's coats with the head noun understood. Two things follow. The verb still agrees with the understood noun, not with the possessor; and where the possessor is singular, the written form is also, on paper, a possible contraction of is or has — John's late, nobody's home, the company's been sold. A plural possessor blocks that second reading, which is why the children's and the Smiths' can only ever be genitives. The same ellipsis is what puts one's own and you and yours in this section: bring your own and you and yours both leave a head noun unspoken, and in the second of them yours means your family rather than your belongings.

Interrogative and relative possessives#

Whose is the possessive of who — and, contrary to a persistent zombie rule, of which as well. Standing alone with no noun after it, it is a pronoun; with a noun after it, it is a determiner and belongs to the other dictionary.

The relatives divide their work by antecedent, and whose is the one that refuses to divide: who takes people, which takes things, that takes both, and whose takes both — it is the only genitive relative English has, so the same word serves the engineer whose design won and a novel whose ending nobody likes. Two further honesties about the family. Whom is the object form, and in American English it survives mainly right after a preposition (to whom, for whom, several of whom), where it is still required in writing; in the bare relative slot the agent who we hired is standard, and whom there is a register choice. And the that / which split — that for restrictive clauses, which for nonrestrictive — is an editing convention, not a rule of the language: house styles enforce it, careful writers have used restrictive which for four hundred years, and no reader is misled by it. The comma is what actually carries the meaning. All of these are catalogued in Relative pronouns.

Notes and tricky cases#

The slot test decides which dictionary a word is in. A noun after it makes it a determiner (my coat, whose car, John's house, y'all's truck); nothing after it makes it a pronoun (the coat is mine, whose is this?, the house is John's, that truck is y'all's). The determiner forms are catalogued in Possessive and genitive determiners and are not repeated here.

The verb agrees with the thing possessed. This is the agreement rule for the whole class, and it catches people out because the pronoun looks plural when the possessor is plural. Ours is the blue house — one house, singular verb, even though ours stands for a whole family. Theirs was the only bid under budget — one bid. Mine are still in the dryer — several shirts. The possessor never touches the verb.

Agreement when a quantifier gets in front of the possessive. The rule above is easy to lose inside [quantifier] of ours, where the quantifier, not the possessive, decides the verb. Each, either, and neither are singular: Each of ours is late, Neither of theirs was ready. So are the indefinite compounds — everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, nobody — and their genitives with them: Everyone's is different, Nobody's was finished. Both, several, and few are plural: Both of ours are late, Several of theirs were damaged, Few of hers were usable. None takes either verb and always has — None of ours is late and None of ours are late are both standard English, the plural being the commoner in ordinary prose, and the "none = not one, therefore singular" rule is a nineteenth-century invention. All, some, and most simply follow the referent: All of ours are gone (things you count), All of ours is gone (something you measure). The quantifiers themselves are catalogued in Indefinite and quantifying pronouns.

The personal possessives take no apostrophe. Ever. Hers, ours, yours, theirs, its, whose — six forms, no apostrophes, no exceptions. The genitives built on nouns and indefinites all take one (John's, the children's, the Smiths', nobody's, y'all's), and that split is precisely why the pronouns attract the error.

Case in coordination, and the one test that settles all of it. Every form in this file is a genitive, and English keeps its three cases apart by using three different words for the same person — she went, I called her, the coat is hers. Case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause, and the single place Americans reliably lose track of it is inside a coordination, where it goes wrong in both directions. Delete the other conjunct and listen — one second, every time:

  • Subject slot, subject form. ✓ She and I went (because she went, I went). The opposite error, ✗ Me and him went, is an object form doing a subject's job: nonstandard, and universally corrected in writing.
  • Object slot, object form. ✓ between you and me, ✓ He gave it to Bob and me, ✓ They invited my wife and me — because between… me, to me, invited me. The I versions of all three — ✗ between you and I, ✗ to Bob and I, ✗ they invited my wife and I — are hypercorrections: and does not change what a preposition or a verb demands, and sounding careful is not the same as being right.
  • Possessive slot, neither one. ✗ my husband and I's anniversary, ✗ a friend of my wife and I — English has no way to hang a genitive ending on a coordinated pronoun, and the same deletion test exposes it, since ✗ I's anniversary is not English. The repairs are my husband's and my anniversary, my wife's and mine, or, far better, our anniversary.

One honest exception to the object rule, because the folk correction runs the other way: after be, ✓ It's me is standard American English at every level, and so are That's her, It was them. It is I is grammatical but stiff enough to sound like a joke, and anyone who corrects It's me is enforcing a Latin rule that English never had. The full subject and object paradigm is in Personal pronouns.

Singular they takes theirs. Somebody left their phone, and it must be theirs is standard modern American English, in speech and in edited prose; AP and Chicago both endorse it, and it is also the correct possessive for a person who uses they. The alternatives are his or hers (formal, clumsy when repeated), generic his (dated), and a plural rewrite (All the guests brought their own), which is usually the best of the three. For a person who uses a coined set instead of they, the independent possessives are catalogued in Gender-neutral third-person possessives — aers, cos, eirs, faers, hirs, nirs, pers, thons, vers, xyrs, and zirs.

Two cells of the paradigm are empty. There is no usable standalone its (✗ that collar is its) and no standalone possessive for generic one (✗ the choice is one's). English fills both gaps the same way — with one's own (a life of its own, a room of one's own) or with a noun genitive (that collar is the dog's). Weak forms, and the h that disappears. The possessives carry stress more often than the personal pronouns do, because they usually hold the new information in the sentence — but they reduce all the same, and the h-forms drop the h outright whenever they are unstressed inside a phrase: a friend of his /ə frɛnd əv ɪz/, a friend of hers /ə frɛnd əv ɚz/, a friend of theirs /ə frɛnd əv ðɚz/, a friend of yours /ə frɛnd əv jɚz/, with of itself down to /əv/ or /ə/. Mine, its, whose, and thine have no weak form at all — they keep their vowel however fast the sentence goes. The personal pronouns these pair with reduce much further, and they are the forms a learner actually hears: him /ɪm/, her /ɚ/, them /əm/, you /jə/, he /i/, catalogued in Personal pronouns. Put the two together and give him his comes out /ɡɪv ɪm ɪz/, three words and not one h among them.

Nonstandard forms, and where each one is catalogued. This file carries the nonstandard and regional possessives, each labeled and each placed: hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn (Appalachian, Ozark, and older Southern and Midland speech), mines (African American English and some urban varieties), y'all's (Southern and South Midland, and now heard far outside it), and you guys' (the Northern and general-American plural). The rest of the nonstandard pronoun stock belongs to other classes and is labeled there: the reflexives hisself and theirselves (Southern, South Midland, and African American English) in Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns; the plural-you forms youse (New York, New Jersey, Philadelphia), yinz (Pittsburgh), you'uns, and the general you guys in Personal pronouns; demonstrative them before a noun — them apples, widespread in vernacular speech with no single home region — in the determiner dictionary; and the double negative (It isn't nobody's, ain't nobody's business), ordinary in African American English and in Southern and working-class white vernaculars, where a second negative intensifies rather than cancels, in Indefinite compound pronouns. Every one of them is systematic in its own variety and marked in edited American prose; recognize them, label them, and don't write them.

The reciprocal possessives live with the reciprocals. Each other's and one another's nearly always have a noun after them (they wore each other's coats), and they are catalogued in Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns and in the determiner dictionary. Note the apostrophe placement either way: ✓ each other's names, ✗ each others' names — each other is a fixed singular-shaped phrase.

The absolute forms are old. Mine and thine are the original absolute possessives, and the -s of yours, hers, ours, and theirs was added later by analogy with the noun genitive. That history explains both directions of regularization still heard today: the Appalachian -n forms (hisn, ourn, yourn) extend the mine pattern, and mines extends the yours pattern. Both are logical, and both are nonstandard.
