# Possessive pronouns

> The words that answer *whose?* with no noun attached — *mine*, *yours*, *his*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*, and every noun phrase in English that can take *'s* and then stand on its own.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

A possessive pronoun replaces an entire possessive noun phrase: *my coat* becomes *mine*, *the Smiths' house* becomes *the Smiths'*. That replacement is the whole line between this file and the determiner dictionary — a possessive **determiner** comes before a noun (*my coat*), a possessive **pronoun** has nothing after it (*the coat is mine*) — and the slot test settles every word in the file. Two facts run through all of them. First, **no personal possessive pronoun takes an apostrophe**: *ours*, *yours*, *hers*, *theirs*, *its*, and relative *whose*, which is why ✗ *our's* is always wrong and why *its* and *it's* are the most-corrected pair in written English. The genitives built on nouns and indefinites do take one (*John's*, *the children's*, *nobody's*, *y'all's*), and that split is the whole reason the error happens. Second, when a possessive pronoun is the subject, the verb agrees with the **thing possessed**, never with the possessor — *Mine is on the table* for one bag, *Mine are on the table* for several.

Every pronoun catalogued in this file is in the **possessive (genitive) case** — the apostrophe impostors at the end (*it's*, *there's*, *they're*, *who's*, *you're*) are a subject plus a verb and carry no case at all, which is exactly why they are filed as impostors. None of the genuine possessives ever does a subject's or an object's work — that is what *she*, *her*, and *hers* are three different words for — and the coordination traps that wreck case elsewhere (✗ *Me and him went*, ✗ *between you and I*) have their own possessive version here in ✗ *my wife and I's*. All of it is settled by the same deletion test, laid out in [Notes and tricky cases](#notes-and-tricky-cases) below.

## The personal possessive pronouns

Seven forms, one for each person and number, each pairing with a possessive determiner that cannot stand alone (*my* ~ *mine*, *your* ~ *yours*, *her* ~ *hers*, *our* ~ *ours*, *their* ~ *theirs*). *His* is the one word that serves as both. *Its* is the hole in the paradigm: the determiner is everywhere, the standalone pronoun essentially never occurs. None of them takes an apostrophe, and none of them changes shape for the number of the thing possessed.

### hers

**Pronunciation:** /hɝz/, weak /ɚz/ &middot; *HURZ, weak erz* &middot; the weak form belongs to the double genitive — *a friend of hers* /ə frɛnd əv ɚz/, where the *h* drops entirely
**Русский:** её — принадлежащее ей, без существительного: «эта книга — её»; форма неизменяемая
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, singular possessor, female — or anyone referred to as *she*. Invariant: the form does not change with the number of the thing possessed (*that book is hers*, *those books are hers*).
**Refers to:** something belonging to, produced by, or associated with a female person or animal already identified. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a cousin of hers*), or inanimate (*the blue one is hers*); the possessor must be animate and definite. For a possessor of unknown or nonbinary gender, use [theirs](#theirs).
**Agreement:** carries no ending of its own. When *hers* is the **subject**, the verb agrees with the number of the **thing possessed**, not the possessor: *Hers is on the table* (one bag), *Hers are on the table* (several). When *hers* is a subject complement, the subject has already fixed the verb: *The seats by the door are hers.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **hers** — *that desk is hers* &middot; **hers** [verb] — *hers arrived first* &middot; [verb] **hers** — *take hers* &middot; [preposition] **hers** — *park next to hers* &middot; [noun] *of* **hers** — *a colleague of hers* &middot; comparison — *bigger than hers*, *the same as hers* &middot; coordination — *hers and mine*
**Contrast:** *Her* precedes a noun (*her coat*); *hers* stands alone (*the coat is hers*). ✗ *her's* is never correct — no possessive pronoun in English takes an apostrophe. Keep it apart from object *her* as well: *I called her* (object of a verb) versus *That call was hers* (possessive). Before an *-ing* form the determiner is required, not the pronoun: ✓ *We appreciated her coming*, ✗ *We appreciated hers coming*.

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to her.”

1. That desk by the window is *hers*.
2. My commute is forty minutes; *hers* is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Hers* were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She lent me a charger of *hers* and I never gave it back. (double genitive)
5. Take mine — *hers* is out of ink.
6. Isn't the gray coat *hers*? (question)
7. The mistake wasn't *hers*. (negative)

### his

**Pronunciation:** /hɪz/, weak /ɪz/ &middot; *HIZ, weak iz* &middot; as a pronoun it usually carries some stress and keeps its /h/; the weak form shows up in the double genitive — *a friend of his* /ə frɛnd əv ɪz/
**Русский:** его — принадлежащее ему, без существительного: «эта машина — его»
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); identical in form to the possessive determiner &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, singular possessor, male — or anyone referred to as *he*. Invariant.
**Refers to:** something belonging to, produced by, or associated with a male person or animal already identified. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a student of his*), or inanimate (*the red truck is his*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *His is missing* (one form), *His are missing* (several). As a subject complement it never drives the verb: *Those tools are his.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **his** — *the blue folder is his* &middot; **his** [verb] — *his came back marked up* &middot; [verb] **his** — *bring his* &middot; [preposition] **his** — *sit next to his* &middot; [noun] *of* **his** — *an old teacher of his* &middot; comparison — *worse than his* &middot; coordination — *his and hers*
**Contrast:** *His* is the one possessive whose determiner and pronoun are spelled the same, so the test is what follows it: a noun after it makes it a determiner (*his car*), nothing after it makes it a pronoun (*the car is his*). Compare *her* ~ *hers*, *your* ~ *yours*, *our* ~ *ours*, *their* ~ *theirs*, where the pronoun adds *-s*. Generic *his* for a person of unstated gender (*Every applicant should sign his*) was standard until the 1970s and now reads as dated in American writing; use *theirs*, or rewrite in the plural.

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to him.”

1. The blue folder on the counter is *his*.
2. My handwriting is bad; *his* is worse.
3. *His* were the only signatures missing from the form. (plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old teacher of *his* from middle school. (double genitive)
5. Which locker is *his*? (question)
6. That call was never *his* to make. (negative)
7. He swore the truck was *his*, but the title said otherwise.

### its

**Pronunciation:** /ɪts/ — no weak form &middot; *ITS* &middot; like *mine*, *whose*, and *thine*, *its* never reduces, and it is pronounced exactly like *it's*, which is why the two are confused only in writing
**Русский:** его, её (о предмете, животном) — пустая клетка: самостоятельно почти не употребляется, вместо него its own
**Type:** possessive pronoun — the empty cell in the paradigm: grammars list the form, but it almost never stands alone &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral as a determiner; as a standalone pronoun, rare enough that most Americans hear it as an error
**Person / number:** third person singular, non-personal. Invariant.
**Refers to:** something belonging to a thing, place, animal of unstated sex, organization, or abstraction — never to a named person. Where the standalone slot is needed, English fills it with *its own* (*a life of its own*) or with a noun genitive (*that bowl is the dog's*).
**Agreement:** in practice there is nothing to agree with, because the independent form does not occur. Use one of the substitutes and the verb then follows the head noun: *The dog's is the red bowl* (singular), *The dog's are in the hall* (plural).
**Pattern:** ✗ [noun phrase] *be* **its** — not English &middot; the substitutes: [noun] *of* **its own** — *a life of its own*; [noun] *all* **its own** — *a character all its own*; noun genitive — *that collar is the dog's* &middot; determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its) — **its** [noun]
**Contrast:** ***Its* versus *it's* is the most-corrected error in written English.** *Its* is the possessive and **never** takes an apostrophe (*the dog wagged its tail*); *it's* is the contraction of *it is* or *it has* (*it's raining*, *it's been a week*). The test: if the word can be expanded to *it is* or *it has*, write *it's*; otherwise write *its*. The reason the error is so common is that every other possessive in English — *John's*, *the company's*, *somebody else's* — is built with an apostrophe, so the hand adds one by reflex; the personal possessives are precisely the forms that refuse it. And ✗ *its'* does not exist in English in any sense at all.

The third-person non-personal possessive — one of the two places where the possessive-pronoun paradigm has a hole in it, the other being generic *one's*.

**1.** The ordinary possessive with a noun after it (*its tail*, *its lease*) is a **determiner**, and it is not catalogued here — it is a cross-reference into [Possessive and genitive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its), and it appears in the examples below only so it can be told apart from the pronoun. **2.** The standalone **pronoun** slot, which English simply leaves empty: ✗ *That collar is its* is not a sentence anyone says, and comparisons come out as *than theirs* or *than the company's*.

1. The city has a rhythm all *its* own. (*its own* — the ordinary way English fills the empty slot)
2. Once the rumor started, it took on a life of *its* own. (*of its own* stands in for the impossible ✗ *a life of its*)
3. Each department manages a budget of *its* own.
4. The dog wagged *its* tail the whole way home. (determiner use, no apostrophe — the form that gets misspelled ✗ *it's tail*)
5. ✗ *That collar is its.* — not English. Say *That collar is the dog's.*
6. ✗ *Our revenue is higher than its.* — say *than theirs*, or *than that company's*.
7. ✗ *Its is the red bowl.* → ✓ *The dog's is the red bowl.* (subject position — the repair is a noun genitive)
8. If it expands to *it is* or *it has*, it isn't *its*: *It's been a long week*, but *The company lost its lease*.

**See also:** [it's (not a possessive pronoun)](#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [its (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its), [one's own](#ones-own), [theirs](#theirs), [the company's](#the-companys), [it](01-personal.md#it)

### mine

**Pronunciation:** /maɪn/ — no weak form &middot; *MYNE (rhymes with LINE)* &middot; the determiner *my* reduces to /mə/ in ordinary speech, but *mine* keeps its full diphthong even unstressed
**Русский:** мой, моя, моё, мои — принадлежащее говорящему, без существительного: «это моё»
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person, singular possessor — always the speaker. Invariant: *that seat is mine*, *those seats are mine*.
**Refers to:** something belonging to, produced by, or associated with the speaker. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a friend of mine*), or inanimate (*the gray suitcase is mine*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, not with the speaker: *Mine is still in the dryer* (one shirt), *Mine are still in the dryer* (several). As a subject complement the subject fixes the verb: *These are mine.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **mine** — *that jacket is mine* &middot; **mine** [verb] — *mine came back damaged* &middot; [verb] **mine** — *use mine* &middot; [preposition] **mine** — *park behind mine* &middot; [noun] *of* **mine** — *a colleague of mine* &middot; comparison — *older than mine* &middot; coordination — *yours and mine* &middot; fixed frame — *what's mine is yours*
**Contrast:** *My* comes before a noun, *mine* stands alone: *my seat* versus *that seat is mine*. Before an *-ing* form use the determiner: ✓ *They objected to my leaving early*, ✗ *to mine leaving early*. Prenominal *mine* (*mine eyes*, *mine host*) is the archaic shape of the **determiner** and belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#mine). ✗ *mines* is nonstandard — see [mines](#mines). And in coordination the possessive keeps its own form: ✓ *my wife's and mine*, ✗ *my wife and I's* — delete the other conjunct and the error is obvious, since ✗ *I's car* is not English.

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to me.”

1. That gray jacket is *mine*.
2. *Mine* is the one with the cracked screen. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Mine* are still in the dryer. (subject, plural referent — *are*)
4. He's a colleague of *mine* from the Denver office. (double genitive)
5. Your numbers and *mine* don't match. (coordination)
6. Which of these coats is *mine*? (question)
7. The fault was *mine*, not hers. (negative contrast)
8. What's *mine* is yours.

**See also:** [my (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#my), [ours](#ours), [yours](#yours), [of mine (the double genitive)](#of-mine-the-double-genitive), [mines](#mines)

### ours

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊɚz/, /aʊrz/, casual /ɑrz/ &middot; *OW-erz / OWRZ, casual arz* &middot; in relaxed speech it flattens to /ɑrz/ and lands on top of *hours*, which is the one place *ours* gets misheard
**Русский:** наш, наша, наше, наши — принадлежащее нам, без существительного
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person plural — a group that includes the speaker. Invariant. English does not distinguish an inclusive *ours* (yours and mine) from an exclusive one (my side's, not yours); only context does.
**Refers to:** something belonging to, produced by, or associated with the speaker's group — a family, a team, a company, a household. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, never with the plural possessor: *Ours is the blue house* (one house, singular verb, even though *ours* means several people), *Ours were delivered late* (several items).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **ours** — *the house on the corner is ours* &middot; **ours** [verb] — *ours came in first* &middot; [verb] **ours** — *take ours* &middot; [preposition] **ours** — *next to ours* &middot; [noun] *of* **ours** — *a neighbor of ours* &middot; comparison — *newer than ours* &middot; coordination — *theirs and ours*
**Contrast:** *Our* precedes a noun, *ours* stands alone: *our table* versus *the table is ours*. ✗ *our's* is never correct. Because casual *ours* /ɑrz/ collides with *hours*, careless writing produces ✗ *a friend of hours*. Note also that *ours* cannot be used before a gerund: ✓ *They resented our leaving*, ✗ *They resented ours leaving*.

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to us.”

1. The house on the corner is *ours*.
2. *Ours* is the blue one with the porch. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Ours* were delivered a day late. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. They're old neighbors of *ours*. (double genitive)
5. Their deadline is Friday; *ours* was yesterday.
6. Is this table *ours* or theirs? (question)
7. The decision was never *ours* to make. (negative)

**See also:** [our (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#our), [mine](#mine), [yours](#yours), [theirs](#theirs), [her's, our's, your's, their's, its'](#hers-ours-yours-theirs-its)

### theirs

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrz/, weak /ðɚz/ &middot; *THAIRZ, weak therz* &middot; homophonous with *there's* in ordinary American speech; the weak form turns up in the double genitive — *a client of theirs*
**Русский:** их — принадлежащее им; тж. об одном человеке неизвестного или небинарного пола
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person. Either a **plural** possessor (*the neighbors*, *the Smiths*) or a **singular** person referred to as *they*. Singular *they* is standard American English, in speech and in edited prose — AP and Chicago both endorse it — and *theirs* is its possessive pronoun. Invariant.
**Refers to:** something belonging to two or more people, or to one person of unknown or nonbinary gender. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable, human (*a client of theirs*) or inanimate (*the corner office is theirs*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, not with the possessor: *Theirs was the only bid under budget* (one bid — singular verb, though *theirs* points at a whole company), *Theirs are flat this quarter* (several figures). Singular *they* takes a plural verb in its own clause (*they are*), and that has no effect at all on the verb that follows *theirs*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **theirs** — *the corner office is theirs* &middot; **theirs** [verb] — *theirs arrived first* &middot; [verb] **theirs** — *use theirs* &middot; [preposition] **theirs** — *parked beside theirs* &middot; [noun] *of* **theirs** — *a client of theirs* &middot; comparison — *flatter than theirs* &middot; coordination — *ours and theirs*
**Contrast:** Five spellings cluster here, in two sound groups. ***Theirs*** is the possessive pronoun (*the car is theirs*), and ***there's*** — *there is* or *there has* (*there's a problem*) — is its exact homophone, both of them /ðɛrz/. The other three are /ðɛr/, with no final /z/: ***they're*** (*they are* — *they're late*), the determiner ***their*** (*their car*), and the place word ***there***. Only *there's* can be mistaken for this word by ear; the rest are confused on paper alone. Expand the contraction to test: if *there is* fits, it takes an apostrophe. ✗ *their's* is never correct. Against the determiner *their*: a noun after it makes it a determiner (*their car*), nothing after it makes it the pronoun (*the car is theirs*).

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to them.”

1. The corner office is *theirs*.
2. Our numbers are up; *theirs* are flat. (subject, plural referent — *are*)
3. *Theirs* was the only bid that came in under budget. (subject, singular referent — *was*)
4. He's a client of *theirs*. (double genitive)
5. Somebody left a phone on the seat — I think it's *theirs*. (singular *they*, standard American usage)
6. Whose turn is it, ours or *theirs*? (question)
7. The mistake wasn't *theirs*. (negative)

### yours

**Pronunciation:** /jʊrz/, /jɔrz/, weak /jɚz/ &middot; *YOORZ / YORZ, weak yerz* &middot; the weak form is what you actually hear in *a friend of yours* /ə frɛnd əv jɚz/
**Русский:** твой, ваш (твоя, ваше, ваши) — принадлежащее собеседнику, без существительного
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** second person, **singular or plural** — one addressee or many, with no change in form. Regional Southern speech supplies a separate plural, [y'all's](#yalls).
**Refers to:** something belonging to, produced by, or associated with the person or people being addressed. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a fan of yours*), or inanimate (*this seat is yours*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Yours is at nine* (one flight), *Yours are the only comments left* (several). As a subject complement it never drives the verb: *These tickets are yours.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **yours** — *is this seat yours?* &middot; **yours** [verb] — *yours came back first* &middot; [verb] **yours** — *bring yours* &middot; [preposition] **yours** — *next to yours* &middot; [noun] *of* **yours** — *a fan of yours* &middot; comparison — *later than yours* &middot; coordination — *yours and mine* &middot; fixed closings — *Yours truly*, *Sincerely yours*
**Contrast:** *Your* precedes a noun, *yours* stands alone: *your seat* versus *the seat is yours*. ✗ *your's* is never correct, and ✗ *you're seat* confuses the possessive with the contraction of *you are* — expand it to test. Note the frozen letter closings *Yours truly* and *Sincerely yours*, and the jocular self-reference *yours truly*, which means *me*: *Guess who got stuck cleaning up? Yours truly.*

Stands for a whole possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to you.”

1. Is this seat *yours*?
2. My flight is at six; *yours* is at nine. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Yours* are the only comments I haven't read yet. (subject, plural referent — *are*)
4. She's a big fan of *yours*. (double genitive)
5. The choice is *yours*.
6. That coat isn't *yours* — check the label. (negative)
7. Sincerely *yours*, / *Yours* truly (fixed letter closings)

## Gender-neutral third-person possessives

English has never had a settled third-person singular possessive for a person whose gender is unstated, unknown, or neither male nor female, and it has filled the gap four different ways. **Singular *theirs*** is the one that won: it is standard American English in speech and in edited prose, AP and Chicago both endorse it, and it is catalogued above with the rest of the personal forms. ***His or hers*** is the formal coordination that American editing used before that, and it now reads as dated. **Generic *his*** was the rule until the 1970s and is dated in the other direction; it needs no separate entry, because the form is simply [his](#his). The rest of this section is the **coined** possessives — *aers*, *cos*, *eirs*, *faers*, *hirs*, *nirs*, *pers*, *thons*, *vers*, *xyrs*, *zirs* — each the independent member of a set built on the model of *he / him / his / his / himself*, and used by particular people for themselves rather than as general-purpose words of the language. The oldest of them is [thons](#thons), coined in 1884 and carried in American dictionaries for two-thirds of the twentieth century, which is a useful reminder that the problem these forms address is not a recent one.

Three facts hold across all of them. Each is the **independent** form of its set, so the slot test that runs through this whole file applies unchanged: *zir coat* has a noun after it and is a determiner, *the coat is zirs* has nothing after it and is the pronoun. **None takes an apostrophe** — ✗ *zir's*, ✗ *eir's* — for exactly the reason *hers* and *theirs* do not. And the **verb still agrees with the thing possessed**: *Zirs is on the table* for one bag, *Zirs are on the table* for several. These forms are younger than the rest of the file's stock and their paradigms are not uniform; where a set has more than one attested shape, the entry says which. American style guides do not list them among the ordinary pronouns of English, and direct writers instead to use the forms a person states for themselves, glossing an unfamiliar one at first mention or recasting the sentence where that reads more clearly.

### aers

**Pronunciation:** /ɛrz/, also /eɪrz/ — no established weak form &middot; *AIRZ, also AYRZ* &middot; the set is introduced as “*ae* like the letter, *aer* like *air*,” which makes the possessive a homophone of *airs*, of *heirs*, and of [eirs](#eirs)
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — самостоятельная притяжательная форма набора ae/aer
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *ae / aer* set &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited and rare. A twenty-first-century online coinage; an American meets it in pronoun introductions, profiles, email signatures, and fan writing, and effectively never in edited prose. Singular *theirs* is vastly commoner in every one of those settings.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ae* (subject), *aer* (object), *aer* (determiner), **aers** (pronoun), *aerself* (reflexive). Invariant: the form does not change with the number of the thing possessed.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ae / aer* for themselves — human only, and never a group. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a colleague of aers*), or inanimate (*the gray suitcase is aers*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, not with the possessor: *Aers is on the table* (one bag), *Aers are on the table* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *ae is*, *ae has* — where singular *they* takes *are*; that difference stops at the clause boundary and has no effect on the verb after *aers*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **aers** — *the gray suitcase is aers* &middot; **aers** [verb] — *aers arrived first* &middot; [verb] **aers** — *take aers* &middot; [preposition] **aers** — *park next to aers* &middot; [noun] *of* **aers** — *a colleague of aers* &middot; comparison — *older than aers* &middot; coordination — *aers and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **aer** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Aer* precedes a noun, *aers* stands alone — the same split as *their* ~ *theirs*. No apostrophe: ✗ *aer's*, for the same reason ✗ *their's* is wrong. Said aloud, *aers* and [eirs](#eirs) are one word, and both of them sound like *airs* and *heirs*; on paper they are separate things belonging to separate sets, which is why a writer using either one glosses it at first mention. Against [faers](#faers), which is built the same way on a different stem.
**Variants:** *aer* used as the independent possessive as well as the determiner, on the pattern of *his*, in which case the set is listed *ae / aer / aer / aerself* with no separate *aers* at all.

The independent possessive of the *ae / aer* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to aer.”

1. The gray suitcase by the door is *aers*.
2. My commute is forty minutes; *aers* is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Aers* were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old colleague of *aers* from the Denver office. (double genitive)
5. Which locker is *aers*? (question)
6. The mistake wasn't *aers*. (negative)
7. Clip your form to the board next to *aers*. (object of a preposition)
8. Said aloud, *aers* and *eirs* are the same word; written down, each belongs to its own set. (the homophone problem)

### cos

**Pronunciation:** /koʊz/ — no established weak form &middot; *KOHZ (rhymes with TOES)* &middot; the vowel is the /oʊ/ of *co-op* and *co-worker*, not the /ɑ/ of *cos lettuce* or of the trigonometric *cos*
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма местоимения co (М. Орован, 1970)
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the genitive of the *co* set, coined by Mary Orovan in 1970 &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited, and limited in a particular way. The *co* set is house usage in several American intentional communities — Twin Oaks in Virginia and the rest of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities — where it has been unremarkable since the 1970s and turns up in newsletters, work schedules, and ordinary conversation. Outside them an American is unlikely ever to meet it.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *co* (subject), *co* (object), **cos** (determiner and independent pronoun alike), *coself* (reflexive) — one of the shortest paradigms of any coined set, with a single genitive doing both jobs, exactly as *his* does. Invariant.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *co* for themselves — human only, and never a group. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a neighbor of cos*), or inanimate (*the blue bicycle is cos*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Cos is in the shed* (one bicycle), *Cos are in the shed* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *co is*, *co has*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **cos** — *the blue bicycle is cos* &middot; **cos** [verb] — *cos came back first* &middot; [verb] **cos** — *take cos* &middot; [preposition] **cos** — *park next to cos* &middot; [noun] *of* **cos** — *a neighbor of cos* &middot; comparison — *newer than cos* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **cos** [noun] — *cos shift*, *cos room*
**Contrast:** This is the one set in the section where the slot test changes nothing, because a single form fills both slots: *cos bicycle* and *the bicycle is cos* are both *cos*, the way *his car* and *the car is his* are both *his*. No apostrophe: ✗ *co's* — which matters more here than elsewhere, since *co's* would read as the contraction of *co is*. Three unrelated words share the spelling — the trigonometric *cos* /kɑs/, the *cos* of *cos lettuce* /kɑs/, and British *cos* meaning *because* /kəz/ — and none of them is a pronoun.
**Variants:** some listings give the object form as *cos* rather than *co*, making the set *co / cos / cos / coself*; ✗ *co's* is written for the possessive by people unfamiliar with the paradigm, which takes no apostrophe anywhere.

The genitive of the *co* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to co.”

1. The blue bicycle in the shed is *cos*.
2. *Cos* is the small room at the top of the stairs. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Cos* were the only hours nobody wanted on the schedule. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old neighbor of *cos* from the farm. (double genitive)
5. Which shift is *cos* this week? (question)
6. The decision was never *cos* to make. (negative)
7. Leave the tools on the bench beside *cos*. (object of a preposition)
8. One form does both jobs: *cos* shift starts at six, and the shift after it is *cos* too. (determiner, then pronoun — the *his* pattern)

### eirs

**Pronunciation:** /ɛrz/ — no established weak form &middot; *AIRZ (rhymes with CHAIRS)* &middot; a homophone of *airs* and *heirs*; unstressed inside a phrase it drifts toward /ɚz/, which is where unstressed *hers* and *theirs* also land, so *a friend of eirs* is hard to place by ear alone
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора ey/em/eir (местоимения Спивака)
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *ey / em / eir* set, usually called the Spivak pronouns &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited. In circulation since the mid-1970s and 1980s, and an American meets it in online writing, in some science fiction and academic prose, and in pronoun introductions; it is far rarer than singular *theirs*.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ey* (subject), *em* (object), *eir* (determiner), **eirs** (pronoun), *emself* (reflexive) — *they / them / their / theirs / themself* with the *th-* clipped off the front. Invariant: the form does not change with the number of the thing possessed.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ey / em / eir* for themselves — human only, and never a group. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a cousin of eirs*), or inanimate (*the blue folder is eirs*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, exactly as with *hers* and *theirs*: *Eirs is on the table* (one bag), *Eirs are on the table* (several). In its own clause the subject form *ey* normally takes singular agreement — *ey is*, *ey has* — where singular *they* takes *are*; usage there is not fully settled, and none of it touches the verb after *eirs*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **eirs** — *the corner desk is eirs* &middot; **eirs** [verb] — *eirs arrived first* &middot; [verb] **eirs** — *take eirs* &middot; [preposition] **eirs** — *park next to eirs* &middot; [noun] *of* **eirs** — *a colleague of eirs* &middot; comparison — *older than eirs* &middot; coordination — *eirs and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **eir** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Eir* precedes a noun, *eirs* stands alone — the same split as *their* ~ *theirs*, which is precisely where the pair comes from. It takes no apostrophe: ✗ *eir's*, for the same reason ✗ *their's* is wrong. On paper the commonest difficulty is that a reader who has not met the form takes it for a typo for *theirs* or *heirs*, which is why a gloss at first mention earns its space. Against [zirs](#zirs) and [xyrs](#xyrs) the difference is which set a person uses, not what the word means.
**Variants:** *E*, *Em*, *Eir*, *Eirs*, *Emself* with initial capitals — Michael Spivak's spelling, which is why the whole set is known as the Spivak pronouns; the lowercase *ey / em / eir* spelling is Christine Elverson's, formed by clipping *they / them / their*. Both are current, and *eirs* is the independent possessive in either.

The independent possessive of the *ey / em / eir* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to em.”

1. The corner desk by the window is *eirs*.
2. My commute is forty minutes; *eirs* is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Eirs* were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old colleague of *eirs* from the Denver office. (double genitive)
5. Which locker is *eirs*? (question)
6. The mistake wasn't *eirs*. (negative)
7. Clip your form to the board next to *eirs*. (object of a preposition)
8. ✗ *Eir's* is the one with the cracked screen. → ✓ *Eirs* is the one with the cracked screen. (no possessive pronoun in English takes an apostrophe)

### faers

**Pronunciation:** /fɛrz/ — no established weak form &middot; *FAIRZ* &middot; a homophone of *fairs* and *fares*; the set is usually introduced as “*fae* rhymes with *day*, *faer* with *air*”
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора fae/faer, от fae «фея»
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *fae / faer* set &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited and rare. A twenty-first-century online coinage built on the word *fae* (fairy), which puts it at the edge of the **nounself** pronouns — sets taken from a noun a person identifies with rather than from an existing pronoun. An American meets it in pronoun introductions, profiles, and fan writing, and effectively never in edited prose.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *fae* (subject), *faer* (object), *faer* (determiner), **faers** (pronoun), *faerself* (reflexive). Invariant.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *fae / faer* for themselves — human only, and never a group. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a cousin of faers*), or inanimate (*the corner office is faers*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Faers is on the table* (one), *Faers are on the table* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *fae is*, *fae has*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **faers** — *the corner office is faers* &middot; **faers** [verb] — *faers arrived first* &middot; [verb] **faers** — *use faers* &middot; [preposition] **faers** — *parked beside faers* &middot; [noun] *of* **faers** — *a cousin of faers* &middot; comparison — *bigger than faers* &middot; coordination — *faers and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **faer** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Faer* precedes a noun, *faers* stands alone. No apostrophe: ✗ *faer's*. The practical difficulty is the homophony with the ordinary nouns *fairs* and *fares* — *the tickets are faers* has to earn its reading from context, and a reader meeting the word cold will hear the fairground or the bus. Against [aers](#aers), the same shape without the *f-*, belonging to a different set.
**Variants:** *fae / faer / faers / faerself* is the standard listing; *faeself* is also found in the reflexive slot, and the subject form is occasionally spelled *fey*.

The independent possessive of the *fae / faer* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to faer.”

1. The corner office at the end of the hall is *faers*.
2. My handwriting is bad; *faers* is worse. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Faers* were the only drawings that survived the move. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. He's a cousin of *faers* from out of state. (double genitive)
5. Is the gray coat *faers* or mine? (question)
6. That call was never *faers* to make. (negative)
7. Park behind *faers* and leave the hydrant clear. (object of a preposition)
8. Out loud it is *fairs*, *fares*, and *faers* all at once, so the word is glossed at first mention and the sentence built to carry it. (the homophone problem)

### hirs

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrz/, also /hɝz/ — no established weak form &middot; *HEERZ, also HURZ* &middot; the commoner reading rhymes with *hears*; speakers who say /hɝz/ make it a perfect homophone of *hers*, and only the spelling then keeps the two apart
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма наборов ze/hir и sie/hir
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *ze / hir* and *sie / hir* sets &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited. It belongs chiefly to the 1990s and 2000s wave of coinages, and an American meets it in writing from that period, in academic and fan communities, and where a person states it for themselves; the *ze / zir* spelling and singular *theirs* are both commoner today.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ze*, *zie*, or *sie* (subject), *hir* (object), *hir* (determiner), **hirs** (pronoun), *hirself* (reflexive). Invariant.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ze / hir* for themselves — human only. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a cousin of hirs*), or inanimate (*the gray suitcase is hirs*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Hirs is in the drawer* (one), *Hirs are in the drawer* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *ze is*, *ze has* — which has no bearing on the verb after *hirs*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **hirs** — *the gray suitcase is hirs* &middot; **hirs** [verb] — *hirs came back marked up* &middot; [verb] **hirs** — *bring hirs* &middot; [preposition] **hirs** — *sit next to hirs* &middot; [noun] *of* **hirs** — *an old teacher of hirs* &middot; comparison — *worse than hirs* &middot; coordination — *hirs and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **hir** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Hir* precedes a noun, *hirs* stands alone — but this set is the one that blurs that line, because many users write *hir* in the independent slot too, on the pattern of *his*, which does both jobs. *That desk is hir* and *that desk is hirs* are both attested; *hirs* is the regularized form, built with the *-s* of *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs*. No apostrophe: ✗ *hir's*. The practical difficulty is the near-collision with [hers](#hers) — identical in speech for many speakers, one letter apart on paper — which is why *hirs* is easy to lose to a spell-checker.
**Variants:** *hir* used as the independent possessive as well as the determiner; the subject form appears as *ze*, *zie*, and *sie*, all of them taking the same *hir / hirs*.

The independent possessive of the *ze / hir* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to hir.”

1. The gray suitcase by the door is *hirs*.
2. *Hirs* is the one with the cracked screen. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Hirs* were the only signatures missing from the form. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. He's an old teacher of *hirs* from middle school. (double genitive)
5. Is the blue coat *hirs* or mine? (question)
6. That call was never *hirs* to make. (negative)
7. Park behind *hirs* and leave the hydrant clear. (object of a preposition)
8. *Hirs* and mine came back on the same day. (coordination)

### his or hers

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪz ɚ ˈhɝz/, weak /ɪz ɚ ˈhɝz/ &middot; *hiz-er-HURZ, weak iz-er-HURZ* &middot; the stress lands on *hers*; unstressed, the *h* of *his* drops entirely, exactly as it does in *a friend of his*
**Русский:** его или её — книжн., устар.: о лице неуказанного пола; сегодня вытеснено theirs
**Type:** coordinated possessive pronoun (independent genitive) — the standalone counterpart of the determiner [his or her](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **formal and dated.** The house solution of American edited prose from roughly the late 1970s through the 1990s, when it was the standard repair for generic *his*; an American still meets it in contracts, employee handbooks, insurance policies, and statutes drafted in those decades. Singular *theirs* has since replaced it almost everywhere.
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, gender unspecified. Never for a plural possessor — ✗ *the employees … his or hers* takes *theirs* — and, because the phrase names exactly two possibilities, not the form to use for a person who has stated *they* or a neopronoun.
**Refers to:** one unidentified person of unstated or irrelevant gender — the *each employee*, *every applicant*, *a juror* of institutional prose. Human only. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *His or hers is the only signature missing* (one signature), *His or hers were the only forms returned* (several). Separately, the antecedent that licenses the phrase is grammatically singular and takes a singular verb of its own: ✓ *Each employee is responsible for his or hers*, ✗ *Each employee are responsible for his or hers*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **his or hers** — *the last locker is his or hers* &middot; **his or hers** [verb] — *his or hers is missing* &middot; [verb] **his or hers** — *use his or hers* &middot; [preposition] **his or hers** — *file it with his or hers* &middot; comparison — *later than his or hers* &middot; the determiner, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her) — **his or her** [noun]
**Contrast:** Against singular [theirs](#theirs), which is shorter, standard, endorsed by AP and Chicago, and covers a person who uses *they* — which a phrase naming two options cannot. Against generic *his* (*every applicant should sign his*), which is dated in the other direction. In practice the best repair is usually neither one: make the noun plural — *All employees are responsible for their own equipment*. Note also that both halves have to be the **independent** forms: ✓ *his or hers*, and ✗ *his or her* with nothing after it, which is the determiner left stranded. ✗ *his or her's* is wrong twice over.
**Variants:** *his/hers*, *her or his*, *hers or his*, and the three-way *his, hers, or theirs*; the matching determiners are *his or her*, *his/her*, and *her or his*.

The formal coordination that stands in for a single possessor of unstated gender — “the one belonging to him, or to her.”

1. The last locker in the row is *his or hers*, not mine. (subject complement)
2. *His or hers* is the only signature still missing from the form. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *His or hers* were the only forms returned on time. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. Every applicant submits a transcript, and *his or hers* has to reach us by Friday.
5. A juror may not discuss the case with a spouse of *his or hers*. (double genitive — and a fair sample of the clumsiness that drove the phrase out of use)
6. Is that badge *his or hers*? (question)
7. The error was not *his or hers*. (negative)
8. Rewritten the way an American editor would do it today: *Theirs* is the only signature still missing.

**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *per* for themselves — human only, which is the point of a form clipped from *person*. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a cousin of pers*), or inanimate (*the red truck is pers*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Pers is on the table* (one), *Pers are on the table* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *per is*, *per has*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **pers** — *the red truck is pers* &middot; **pers** [verb] — *pers came in first* &middot; [verb] **pers** — *take pers* &middot; [preposition] **pers** — *next to pers* &middot; [noun] *of* **pers** — *a neighbor of pers* &middot; comparison — *newer than pers* &middot; coordination — *pers and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **per** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Per* precedes a noun, *pers* stands alone. The collision to watch is with the Latin preposition *per* — *per diem*, *per capita*, *miles per hour*, *as per your email* — a different word entirely that is never a possessive, and one that makes *per coat* readable two ways for a beat. Many listings give *per* rather than *pers* in the independent slot, on the pattern of *his*; *pers* is the regularized form, built with the *-s* of *hers* and *theirs*. No apostrophe: ✗ *per's*.
**Variants:** *per* used as the independent possessive as well as the determiner, in which case the set is listed simply as *per / per / per / perself* with no separate *pers* at all.

The independent possessive of the *per* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to per.”

1. The red truck in the driveway is *pers*.
2. *Pers* is the small desk by the window. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Pers* were the only drawings that survived the move. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old neighbor of *pers* from the co-op. (double genitive)
5. Which of these coats is *pers*? (question)
6. The decision was never *pers* to make. (negative)
7. Set the box down beside *pers*. (object of a preposition)
8. Note the two words that look alike: the mileage is figured *per* day, and the truck is *pers*. (the Latin preposition against the pronoun)

### thons

**Pronunciation:** /ðɑnz/ &middot; *THAHNZ (rhymes with JOHNS)* &middot; the *th* is the voiced one of *that*, not the voiceless one of *thin*, because the word is a blend of *that one*
**Русский:** устар. — притяжательная форма thon, первого гендерно-нейтрального местоимения (1884)
**Type:** possessive pronoun — the genitive of *thon*, the first gender-neutral pronoun to reach an American dictionary; determiner and independent pronoun alike &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **archaic.** Coined in 1884 by Charles Crozat Converse, an American lawyer and hymn composer, carried in Funk &amp; Wagnalls' *Standard Dictionary* from 1897 and in Merriam-Webster's *New International* of 1934, and dead in ordinary use by the middle of the twentieth century. An American meets it now in histories of the pronoun problem and nowhere else.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *thon* (subject and object alike), **thons** (determiner and pronoun alike), *thonself* (reflexive) — a three-form paradigm blended from *that one*. Invariant.
**Refers to:** one person of unstated, unknown, or irrelevant gender — the *every applicant*, *each employee*, *a juror* of institutional prose, which is exactly what Converse invented it for. Human only.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Thons is the signature still missing* (one), *Thons were returned late* (several). The subject form took singular agreement in its own clause — *thon is*, *thon has*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **thons** — *the last locker is thons* &middot; **thons** [verb] — *thons is missing* &middot; [verb] **thons** — *use thons* &middot; [noun] *of* **thons** — *a colleague of thons* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **thons** [noun] — *thons signature*
**Contrast:** *Thons* is the ancestor of everything else in this section: the earliest of the American gender-neutral coinages, the only one ever given a long run in a major dictionary, and the plain evidence that the problem is a century and a half old rather than a recent one. Against [theirs](#theirs), which solved the same problem with a word English already had, and against [his or hers](#his-or-hers), which solved it by naming both options. Do not read the *th-* as the *th-* of *theirs*: the blend is *that one*, and the two words are unrelated.
**Variants:** *thon's* in some nineteenth-century printings; *thon* itself is occasionally given in the genitive slot, and the reflexive appears as *thonself* and as *thon's self*.

The genitive of *thon*, an 1884 American coinage — “the one, or the ones, belonging to thon.”

1. The last locker in the row is *thons*, not mine.
2. *Thons* is the only signature still missing from the form. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Thons* were the only forms returned on time. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. Every applicant submits a transcript, and *thons* has to reach us by Friday. (the institutional sentence the word was coined for)
5. A juror may not discuss the case with a spouse of *thons*. (double genitive)
6. Is that badge *thons*? (question)
7. Rewritten the way an American would write it today: *Theirs* is the only signature still missing.
8. The *th-* is the *th-* of *that one*, which the word is blended from — not the *th-* of *theirs*.

**See also:** [theirs](#theirs), [his or hers](#his-or-hers), [cos](#cos), [pers](#pers), [possessive and genitive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md)

### vers

**Pronunciation:** /vɝz/ — no established weak form &middot; *VURZ* &middot; the vowel of *her*; a near-homophone of *verse*, differing only in the voicing of the last consonant
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора ve/ver (научная фантастика)
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *ve / ver* set &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited and rare. The *ve* set is met mainly in science fiction — Greg Egan's novels are where most American readers have run into it — and in online pronoun listings.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ve* (subject), *ver* (object), *ver* (determiner), **vers** (pronoun), *verself* (reflexive). Invariant.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *ve / ver* for themselves — human only. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a client of vers*), or inanimate (*the corner office is vers*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Vers was the only bid under budget* (one bid), *Vers are flat this quarter* (several figures). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *ve is*, *ve has*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **vers** — *the corner office is vers* &middot; **vers** [verb] — *vers arrived first* &middot; [verb] **vers** — *use vers* &middot; [preposition] **vers** — *parked beside vers* &middot; [noun] *of* **vers** — *a client of vers* &middot; comparison — *flatter than vers* &middot; coordination — *ours and vers* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **ver** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Ver* precedes a noun, *vers* stands alone. This is the one set in the file whose two shapes genuinely compete: *ve / ver / vis / vis / verself* builds the possessive as *vis*, on the model of *his*, and is the paradigm Greg Egan's fiction uses; *ve / ver / vers / vers / verself* builds it on *ver* with the regular *-s*, and *vers* is its independent form. A reader who has met one will not necessarily recognize the other, so the set is worth naming at first mention. No apostrophe: ✗ *ver's*.
**Variants:** *vis*, the possessive of the *ve / ver / vis* paradigm, which covers both the determiner and the pronoun (*vis coat*, *the coat is vis*); *ver* is also found in the determiner slot alongside *vis*.

The independent possessive of the *ve / ver* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to ver.”

1. The corner office is *vers*.
2. Our numbers are up; *vers* are flat. (subject, plural referent — *are*)
3. *Vers* was the only bid that came in under budget. (subject, singular referent — *was*)
4. He's a client of *vers*. (double genitive)
5. Whose turn is it, ours or *vers*? (question)
6. The mistake wasn't *vers*. (negative)
7. The truck parked beside *vers* has been there all week. (object of a preposition)
8. In Egan's paradigm the same sentence reads *the corner office is vis*, which is the other half of the split. (the competing shape of the set)

### xyrs

**Pronunciation:** /zɪrz/, also /zɝz/ — no established weak form &middot; *ZEERZ, also ZURZ* &middot; the *x* spells /z/, so the word starts like *zebra*, not like *ex*
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора xe/xem/xyr
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *xe / xem / xyr* set &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited. In American online writing since the 1990s, and now met mainly in pronoun introductions, profiles, email signatures, and class rosters; one of the more frequently listed neopronoun sets, alongside *ze / zir* and *ey / em*, and far rarer than singular *theirs* in every one of those settings.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *xe* (subject), *xem* (object), *xyr* (determiner), **xyrs** (pronoun), *xemself* (reflexive). Invariant.
**Refers to:** one specific person who uses *xe / xem / xyr* for themselves — human only. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, uncountable, human (*a student of xyrs*), or inanimate (*the blue folder is xyrs*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed: *Xyrs is missing* (one form), *Xyrs are missing* (several). The subject form takes singular agreement in its own clause — *xe is*, *xe has* — unlike singular *they*, which takes *are*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **xyrs** — *the blue folder is xyrs* &middot; **xyrs** [verb] — *xyrs came back marked up* &middot; [verb] **xyrs** — *bring xyrs* &middot; [preposition] **xyrs** — *sit next to xyrs* &middot; [noun] *of* **xyrs** — *an old student of xyrs* &middot; comparison — *worse than xyrs* &middot; coordination — *xyrs and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **xyr** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Xyr* precedes a noun, *xyrs* stands alone. Because *x-* here spells /z/, *xyrs* and [zirs](#zirs) are **homophones** — the same word in speech, told apart only by which spelling tradition the writer is following, and not interchangeable on paper, since each belongs to its own set. No apostrophe: ✗ *xyr's*. The spelling is the practical difficulty: a reader meeting the form cold will try /ks/ or /ɛks/ at the front of it, which is why writers who use it often gloss the pronunciation once.
**Variants:** *xirs*, from the *xe / xem / xir* spelling of the same set; the set is also written *ze / zem / zyr*, in which case the independent possessive is *zyrs*.

The independent possessive of the *xe / xem / xyr* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to xem.”

1. The blue folder on the counter is *xyrs*.
2. My handwriting is bad; *xyrs* is worse. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Xyrs* were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. She's an old student of *xyrs* from the seminar. (double genitive)
5. Which locker is *xyrs*? (question)
6. That call was never *xyrs* to make. (negative)
7. Leave your badge on the hook next to *xyrs*. (object of a preposition)
8. Said aloud, *xyrs* and *zirs* are the same word; written down, they are two, and each belongs to its own set. (the homophone problem)

### zirs

**Pronunciation:** /zɪrz/, also /zɝz/ — no established weak form &middot; *ZEERZ, also ZURZ* &middot; a homophone of [xyrs](#xyrs), which is the same word in a different spelling tradition
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — притяжательная форма набора ze/zir
**Type:** possessive pronoun (independent genitive); neopronoun — the independent possessive of the *ze / zir* set &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** limited. One of the more frequently used neopronoun sets in American English, met in pronoun introductions, email signatures, name badges, class rosters, and online profiles, chiefly among nonbinary and genderqueer speakers and the people writing about them. Singular *theirs* is far commoner in all of those settings.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. The set runs *ze* or *zie* (subject), *zir* (object), *zir* (determiner), **zirs** (pronoun), *zirself* (reflexive). Some speakers pair *ze* with *hir* instead — see [hirs](#hirs). Invariant.

**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the number of the thing possessed, never with the possessor: *Zirs is on the table* (one bag), *Zirs are on the table* (several). **Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **zirs** — *the corner office is zirs* &middot; **zirs** [verb] — *zirs arrived first* &middot; [verb] **zirs** — *use zirs* &middot; [preposition] **zirs** — *parked beside zirs* &middot; [noun] *of* **zirs** — *a colleague of zirs* &middot; comparison — *bigger than zirs* &middot; coordination — *zirs and mine* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **zir** [noun]
**Contrast:** *Zir* precedes a noun, *zirs* stands alone — though this set, like *ze / hir*, is often listed with *zir* doing both jobs on the pattern of *his*, so *the coat is zir* and *the coat is zirs* are both attested; *zirs* is the regularized independent form, built with the *-s* of *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs*. **Variants:** *zir* used as the independent possessive as well as the determiner; the subject form is written *ze* and *zie*; and the *ze / hir* pairing gives [hirs](#hirs) in this slot instead.

The independent possessive of the *ze / zir* set — “the one, or the ones, belonging to zir.”

1. The corner office is *zirs*.
2. My commute is forty minutes; *zirs* is closer to ninety. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. *Zirs* were the only comments the editor kept. (subject, plural referent — *were*)
4. He's a colleague of *zirs* from the Denver office. (double genitive)
5. Is the gray coat *zirs*? (question)
6. The mistake wasn't *zirs*. (negative)
7. Park behind *zirs* and leave room for the truck. (object of a preposition)
8. Take mine — *zirs* is out of ink. (coordination against another possessive)

## The double genitive

The one construction that lets a possessive share a noun phrase with an article, a number, or a demonstrative. It looks redundant and is not.

### of mine (the double genitive)

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈmaɪn/, weak /ə ˈmaɪn/ &middot; *uv MYNE* &middot; *of* is nearly always the weak /əv/ or /ə/ here, which is why the phrase is sometimes written *a friend o' mine*
**Русский:** один из моих — двойной генитив: a friend of mine = один из моих друзей
**Type:** construction — *of* plus a possessive pronoun; also called the oblique genitive or post-genitive &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** any person and number — *of mine*, *of yours*, *of his*, *of hers*, *of ours*, *of theirs*, and with a genitive noun phrase *of John's*, *of my sister's*. There is no *its* form: ✗ *a page of its* → *one of its pages*.
**Refers to:** the possessor named by the pronoun. The head noun is normally countable — singular after *a/an* or a demonstrative (*a friend of mine*, *that car of his*), plural after a number or quantifier (*two cousins of hers*, *several clients of ours*). With an uncountable noun it is limited to demonstrative and evaluative uses: ✓ *that attitude of yours*, ✗ *a money of mine*.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **head noun**, never with the possessor: *A friend of mine is coming*, *Two friends of mine are coming*, *Several clients of ours have asked*.
**Pattern:** [*a/an* / number / demonstrative / quantifier] [noun] *of* **mine / yours / his / hers / ours / theirs** &middot; [same] [noun] *of* [name]*'s* — *a friend of John's* &middot; with *own* — *a place of her own*
**Contrast:** It is not redundant, because English allows exactly one determiner in the central slot: ✗ *a my friend* and ✗ *that your car* are impossible, so the possessive gets pushed to the end. It also is not synonymous with a plain *of*-phrase — *a picture of my father* has him in it, while *a picture of my father's* belongs to him or was taken by him. With a demonstrative it usually carries an attitude: *that car of yours* is rarely neutral. And the complement of *of* must be a possessive **pronoun** or a genitive noun phrase — ✗ *a friend of me*, ✗ *a friend of my* are both wrong.

Combines possession with an indefinite, demonstrative, or quantified determiner — the workaround for a noun phrase that has room for only one determiner.

1. She's an old friend *of mine* from college. (*a/an* + singular countable)
2. That car *of his* has broken down three times this year. (demonstrative, and plainly not neutral)
3. Two cousins *of hers* flew in for the wedding. (number + plural countable)
4. Several clients *of ours* asked the same question. (quantifier + plural countable)
5. A painting *of my father's* hung over the mantel — he painted it. (compare *a painting of my father*, which would show him)
6. Is this book *of theirs* worth reading? (question)
7. I haven't met a single neighbor *of yours*. (negative)

**See also:** [mine](#mine), [hers](#hers), [theirs](#theirs), [of mine (determiner dictionary)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#of-mine), [John's](#johns)

## Genitive noun phrases standing alone

The genitive is productive: any noun phrase at all can take *'s* and then drop the noun after it, leaving the genitive to do a pronoun's work. Grammarians call this the **fused** or **elliptical genitive** — *the coats are the children's* is *the children's coats* with the head noun understood. Two things follow. The verb still agrees with the understood noun, not with the possessor; and where the possessor is singular, the written form is also, on paper, a possible contraction of *is* or *has* — *John's late*, *nobody's home*, *the company's been sold*. A plural possessor blocks that second reading, which is why *the children's* and *the Smiths'* can only ever be genitives. The same ellipsis is what puts [one's own](#ones-own) and [you and yours](#you-and-yours) in this section: *bring your own* and *you and yours* both leave a head noun unspoken, and in the second of them *yours* means your family rather than your belongings.

### another's

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnʌðɚz/ &middot; *uh-NUTH-erz* &middot; the *th* is voiced, as in *other*; the first syllable is a bare schwa and drops out altogether in fast speech — *'nother's*
**Русский:** другого, чужое — книжн.: принадлежащее иному лицу («свобода другого»)
**Type:** indefinite genitive pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral as a determiner; the standalone pronoun use is formal, literary, and proverbial
**Person / number:** third person **singular** — *another* is *an* + *other*, so it can only ever name one further person. There is no ✗ *anothers'*; the plural of the same idea is [others'](#others).
**Refers to:** one further person, not the one already under discussion — human only, and normally left unidentified. The thing possessed is supplied by context, and in the commonest use it is the noun that has just appeared: *one person's freedom ends where another's begins* = where another person's freedom begins.
**Agreement:** the **possessor** is always singular — *another* can only ever be one further person, and there is no plural ✗ *anothers'*. The **verb**, as everywhere in this file, follows the understood possessed noun and not the possessor: *Another's is the signature underneath* (one signature), *Another's are the gloves on the left* (several), and *where another's begins* takes *begins* because the understood noun (*freedom*) is singular.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **another's** — *the handwriting was another's* &middot; [noun]*'s* … **another's** — *one person's freedom … another's* &middot; comparison — *his own troubles and another's* &middot; [noun] *of* **another's** — *a manuscript of another's* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) — **another's** [noun] — *at another's expense*
**Contrast:** Against [others'](#others), the plural of the same idea — *another's* points at one further person, *others'* at several. Against *one another's*, which is **reciprocal** and points crosswise inside a group (*they wore one another's coats*), catalogued in [Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-others). And against the far commoner determiner use: a noun after it (*another's problem*, *at another's expense*) puts the phrase in the other dictionary. Note that *another's* is never a contraction — there is no short form of *another is* — so the apostrophe here is only ever a genitive, which is what makes it easier to read than *anyone's* or *nobody's*.

Names one further, unnamed possessor — “belonging to some other single person.”

1. One person's freedom ends where *another's* begins.
2. The handwriting on the envelope wasn't hers; it was *another's*.
3. He signed at the bottom, and beneath his name was *another's*.
4. It is easy enough to bear one's own troubles and impossible to bear *another's*. (comparison)
5. She refused to take credit that was *another's*. (relative clause)
6. Whose fault was it — yours, or *another's*? (question)
7. The manuscript he was carrying turned out to be *another's*, borrowed and never returned.
8. ✗ *Anothers'* is not a word: *another* is already singular, and the plural of the idea is *others'*.

**See also:** [others'](#others), [somebody else's](#somebody-elses), [anyone's](#anyones), [each other's](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-others), [another](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#another)

### anyone else's

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛniwʌn ˈɛlsɪz/ &middot; *en-ee-wun EL-siz* &middot; the *'s* lands on *else* and comes out /ɪz/ after the /s/, exactly as in *Chris's*; the whole phrase carries one main stress, on *else*
**Русский:** чей-либо ещё, чужое — принадлежащее любому другому человеку
**Type:** group genitive used as a pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person **singular** — the indefinite in front of *else* keeps its singular verb even where the sense takes in a whole group, so with the usual distributive singular understood, ✓ *Everyone else's is different*, ✗ *Everyone else's are different*. Where the understood possessed noun is itself plural, the verb follows it like any other fused genitive: *Mine is short; everyone else's are longer.* The frame is fully productive — *anybody else's*, *everyone else's*, *everybody else's*, *no one else's*, *nobody else's*, *someone else's*, *who else's*.
**Refers to:** a person other than the one already mentioned, human only. Which person depends on the indefinite in front of *else*: *anyone else's* opens it to anybody at all, *everyone else's* takes in all the others at once, *no one else's* shuts every other person out. The thing possessed comes from context.
**Agreement:** grammatically **singular**, like the indefinite it is built on: *Anyone else's is better than nothing*, *Everyone else's is already in*, *No one else's was ready*. With *no one else's* the verb stays **positive**, because the negation sits inside the phrase: ✓ *The fault was no one else's*, ✗ *wasn't no one else's*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **anyone else's** — *that seat isn't anyone else's* &middot; comparison — *as good as anyone else's*, *better than everyone else's* &middot; **no one else's** *but* [possessive] — *no one else's but hers* &middot; [noun] *of* **someone else's** — *a jacket of someone else's* &middot; short answer — *Whose problem is it now? Everyone else's.* &middot; the frame — [indefinite pronoun] *else's*
**Contrast:** The ending goes on *else*, not on the indefinite, because the English genitive attaches to a whole **phrase** and *else* is what ends it: ✓ *anyone else's*, ✗ *anyone's else*. The same mechanism gives ✓ *who else's* against the impossible ✗ *whose else*. With a noun after it (*anyone else's guess*) the phrase is a **determiner** and belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses). Against bare [anyone's](#anyones): *anyone's* opens the field to everybody, the person in front of you included, while *anyone else's* deliberately shuts that person out.
**Variants:** *anybody else's* /ˌɛnibɑdi ˈɛlsɪz/ &middot; *en-ee-bah-dee EL-siz* — the same phrase on the *-body* stem, identical in grammar and register and the commoner of the two in speech. *Everyone else's* and *everybody else's*, “belonging to all the other people.” *No one else's* and *nobody else's*, “belonging to no other person,” with the verb kept positive. *Someone else's* and *somebody else's*, catalogued separately at [somebody else's](#somebody-elses). And *who else's*, the interrogative member of the frame.

The *else* frame, which pushes the genitive ending onto *else* — “belonging to some other person than this one.”

1. That coat isn't mine, and it isn't *anyone else's* on this floor.
2. My guess is as good as *anyone else's*.
3. *Everyone else's* is already in; yours is the last one we're waiting on. (subject, singular verb — *is*)
4. The decision was *no one else's* but the mayor's. (the negation is inside the phrase, so the verb stays positive)
5. Her schedule is packed, but so is *everybody else's*. (comparison, the *-body* stem)
6. He turned up in a jacket of *someone else's* that he'd found in the closet. (double genitive)
7. Whose problem is it now? *Everyone else's*. (short answer)
8. ✗ *Anyone's else* → ✓ *anyone else's*: the ending goes on the end of the phrase, not on the pronoun.

### anyone's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniwʌnz/ &middot; *EN-ee-wunz* &middot; identical to the contraction *anyone is*; only context separates them
**Русский:** чей угодно — принадлежащее любому человеку; только о людях
**Type:** indefinite genitive pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular in form, though it points at an open set of people. The same pattern runs through *anybody's*, *someone's*, *everyone's*, *no one's*.
**Refers to:** any person at all, human only; the thing possessed is supplied by context (*the race is still anyone's* = anyone's race). It cannot point at a thing — for a non-human possessor use a noun genitive (*the company's*).
**Agreement:** grammatically singular: *Anyone's is better than nothing.* When it is a subject complement the subject fixes the verb: *The chances are anyone's.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **anyone's** — *the race is still anyone's* &middot; *as good as* **anyone's** &middot; *better than* **anyone's** &middot; [verb] **anyone's** — *it could be anybody's* &middot; the *else* variant — **anyone else's**
**Contrast:** The apostrophe carries two different jobs here: *Anyone's welcome to try* is *anyone is*, while *the decision is anyone's* is the possessive. Expand it to tell them apart. Keep it apart from the determiner use as well — *anyone's guess* has a noun after it and belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses). Note also *anyone's* (one indefinite person) against *any one's* (any single member of a stated set).
**Variants:** *anybody's* /ˈɛnibɑdiz/ &middot; *EN-ee-bah-deez* — the same pronoun on the *-body* stem, identical in grammar, reference, and register, and the commoner of the two in speech, where *anyone's* is the commoner in writing. Also the *else* forms *anyone else's* and *anybody else's*, and the two-word *any one's*, which picks a single member out of a stated set.

Names an unspecified possessor — “belonging to any person at all,” and by extension “up for grabs.”

1. With two laps to go, the race was still *anyone's*.
2. My guess is as good as *anyone's*.
3. Nobody claimed the wallet, so it could be *anybody's*.
4. His chances are better than *anyone's* on that roster.
5. Whose signature is missing? At this point it could be *anyone's*.
6. *Anyone's* is better than nothing at this point. (subject, singular verb — *is*)
7. Don't go by mine — go by *anyone's* on the committee. (object of a preposition)
8. *Anyone's* welcome to try. (not the possessive — this is *anyone is*)

### everyone's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriwʌnz/ &middot; *EV-ree-wunz* &middot; identical to the contraction *everyone is*; *everybody's* /ˈɛvribɑdiz/ behaves the same way in every respect
**Русский:** всех, каждого — принадлежащее всем сразу; только о людях
**Type:** indefinite genitive pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person **singular** in form, though the sense takes in the whole group — *everyone is*, *everybody is*, never ✗ *everyone are*, and the genitive keeps that singular agreement. The same frame gives *everybody's* and *everyone else's*.
**Refers to:** every member of the group, human only; the thing possessed is supplied by context (*the credit is everyone's* = everyone's credit). For a non-human possessor use a noun genitive (*the company's*).
**Agreement:** grammatically **singular**, and it stays singular even though the meaning is collective: ✓ *Everyone's is different*, ✗ *Everyone's are different*. This is the same singular that governs *everyone*, *everybody*, *someone*, *anyone*, and *nobody*. The verb still answers to the understood possessed noun, so a plural one pulls it plural — *Mine is short; everyone's are longer* — but the distributive singular is the ordinary case, and it is the one the ✗ above rules out. When a pronoun refers back to it, standard American English uses singular *they*: *Everyone's is different, and they all know it.* As a subject complement the subject fixes the verb: *The seats along the wall are everyone's.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **everyone's** — *the credit is everyone's* &middot; **everyone's** [verb] — *everyone's is different* &middot; comparison — *as bad as everyone's* &middot; short answer — *Whose problem is it? Everyone's.* &middot; the *else* variant — **everyone else's**
**Contrast:** One spelling, two words: *Everyone's here* is *everyone is here*, while *the responsibility is everyone's* is the possessive — expand it to test. *Everyone's guess* has a noun after it and is a **determiner**, catalogued in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses). Against [nobody's](#nobodys): *everyone's* spreads the ownership across the whole group, *nobody's* denies it to all of them, which is why the two pair off in *everyone's and nobody's*. Note also *everyone's* (the group as a whole) against *every one's* (each separate member of a stated set).
**Variants:** *everybody's* /ˈɛvribɑdiz/ &middot; *EV-ree-bah-deez* — the same pronoun on the *-body* stem, identical in grammar, reference, and register, and the commoner of the two in speech; it keeps the same singular agreement (✓ *Everybody's is different*, ✗ *Everybody's are different*). Also the *else* forms *everyone else's* and *everybody else's*, and the two-word *every one's*, which picks out each separate member of a stated set.

Names the whole group as possessor — “belonging to every person in it,” and by extension “shared by all of us.”

1. The credit was *everyone's*, not just the director's. (subject complement)
2. *Everyone's* is different — there's no single right answer. (subject, singular verb)
3. Whose problem is this? *Everyone's*. (short answer)
4. My schedule is packed, but so is *everybody's*. (comparison, the *-body* stem)
5. That call was never *everyone's* to make. (negative)
6. Is the courtyard *everyone's*, or just the ground-floor tenants'? (question)
7. *Everyone's* welcome to stay for dinner. (not the possessive — this is *everyone is*)

### John's

**Pronunciation:** /dʒɑnz/ &middot; *JAHNZ* &middot; the genitive *'s* is /s/ after a voiceless sound (*Pat's*), /z/ after a voiced one (*John's*), and /ɪz/ after a sibilant (*Chris's*) — the same three-way split as the plural
**Русский:** Джона — принадлежащее Джону; тж. «у Джона (дома)»
**Type:** genitive noun phrase used as a pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; singular possessor here, but the construction is fully productive — any noun phrase, of any person or number, can do this.
**Refers to:** whatever the context supplies. Three readings: the possessed thing (*the blue car is John's*), a place associated with the possessor (*dinner at John's* = at his house), and the possessor himself inside a double genitive (*a cousin of John's*).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the understood head noun, not with the possessor: *John's is the desk by the window* (one desk), *John's are in the hall closet* (several coats).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **John's** &middot; **John's** *be* [complement] — *John's is the red one* &middot; [preposition] **John's** — *at John's*, *to John's* (his house) &middot; [noun] *of* **John's** — *a cousin of John's* &middot; the same frame with any noun phrase — *Maria's*, *the doctor's*, *my sister's*
**Contrast:** *John's* the genitive and *John's* the contraction of *John is* or *John has* are spelled identically: *John's late* is *John is late*; *the car is John's* is possession. Expand it to test. And keep the two dictionaries apart with the slot test: *John's car* has a noun after it and is a **determiner**; *the car is John's* has nothing after it and is a **pronoun**. The locative use (*we're eating at John's*) is the same fused genitive with *house* or *place* understood, and it is what lies behind store names like *Macy's* and *Trader Joe's*.

A name plus *'s*, standing in for the whole possessive noun phrase — “the one belonging to John.”

1. The blue car in the driveway is *John's*.
2. *John's* is the desk by the window. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. We're having dinner at *John's* tonight. (locative genitive — *John's house*)
4. She's a cousin of *John's*. (double genitive)
5. Both signatures were *Maria's*, not *John's*.
6. Is that mug *John's* or mine? (question)
7. *John's* was the only application filed on time.

### nobody's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊbɑdiz/, /ˈnoʊbədiz/ &middot; *NO-bah-deez / NO-buh-deez* &middot; identical to the contraction *nobody is*, as in *nobody's home*
**Русский:** ничей — никому не принадлежащий; только о людях как владельцах
**Type:** indefinite genitive pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular. *No one's* is the same word in two words; *none's* does not exist.
**Refers to:** no person at all — it asserts that the thing has no owner. Human possessors only.
**Agreement:** grammatically **singular**, like the pronoun it is built on — *nobody is*, ✗ *nobody are*: ✓ *Nobody's was finished on time.* And the verb stays **positive**, because the negation is already inside the word: ✓ *The land is nobody's*, ✗ *The land isn't nobody's*. That second sentence is a **double negative** — not illogical, and entirely ordinary in African American English and in Southern and working-class white vernaculars, where the second negative intensifies rather than cancels — but it is nonstandard in edited American prose; see [negative concord](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **nobody's** — *the strip of land is nobody's* &middot; **nobody's** *but* [possessive] — *nobody's but hers* &middot; short answer — *Whose fault was it? Nobody's.* &middot; the *else* variant — **nobody else's**
**Contrast:** Two words, one spelling. *Nobody's home* is *nobody is home*; *the land is nobody's* is the possessive. Expand it to test. The frozen phrases *nobody's fool* and *nobody's business* have a noun after them and are therefore determiners, not pronouns. Against [anyone's](#anyones): *anyone's* says the thing is open to all comers, *nobody's* says it belongs to none of them.
**Variants:** *no one's* /ˈnoʊ wʌnz/ &middot; *NO-wunz* — the same pronoun written as two words on the *-one* stem, identical in grammar, reference, and register, and the form most edited American prose prefers in writing; ✗ *noone's* is not a spelling English uses, and ✗ *none's* does not exist at all. Also the *else* forms *nobody else's* and *no one else's*.

Names the absence of a possessor — “belonging to no one.”

1. The strip of land behind the fence is *nobody's*.
2. Whose fault was it? *Nobody's*, really.
3. That bike has sat in the rack for two years — it must be *nobody's*.
4. The credit was everyone's and *nobody's* at the same time.
5. The decision was *no one's* but the mayor's.
6. *Nobody's* was finished on time. (subject, singular verb — *was*)
7. The land is *nobody's*, not ✗ *isn't nobody's* — the negation is already inside the word. (double negative: ordinary in African American English and in Southern and working-class white vernaculars, nonstandard in edited prose)
8. *Nobody's* answering the phone. (not the possessive — this is *nobody is*)

### one's own

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnz ˈoʊn/ &middot; *wunz OHN* &middot; the stress falls on *own* throughout the paradigm, and the possessive in front of it reduces — *my own* /mə ˈoʊn/, *her own* /ɚ ˈoʊn/, *their own* /ðɚ ˈoʊn/
**Русский:** свой собственный — подчёркивает исключительность: bring your own «приноси своё»
**Type:** fused possessive — a possessive determiner plus the emphasizer *own*, standing where an independent possessive pronoun would &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral; the generic *one's* member of the paradigm is formal
**Person / number:** any person and number — *my own*, *your own*, *his own*, *her own*, *its own*, *our own*, *their own*, generic *one's own*, regional *y'all's own*. *One's own* is the citation form, exactly as the reflexive of generic *one* is cited as *oneself*.
**Refers to:** whatever the possessive names, with *own* adding exclusivity — “belonging to that person and to nobody else.” The thing possessed is left to context: *bring your own* means your own drink, chair, lunch, or whatever the occasion supplies.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the understood possessed noun, not with the possessor: *My own is still in the shop* (one car), *My own are still in the dryer* (several shirts). Where an indefinite licenses it, the indefinite keeps its own singular verb and *their own* does not disturb it: ✓ *Everyone brings their own*, ✗ *Everyone bring their own*.
**Pattern:** [verb] [possessive] **own** — *bring your own* &middot; [possessive] **own** [verb] — *my own is in the shop* &middot; [determiner] [noun] *of* [possessive] **own** — *a room of one's own*, *a place of his own* &middot; *all* [possessive] **own** — *a rhythm all its own* &middot; *on* [possessive] **own** — *she did it on her own* &middot; *hold* [possessive] **own** — *he held his own* &middot; *come into* [possessive] **own** — *the design came into its own*
**Contrast:** With a noun after it (*my own room*, *its own lease*) the phrase is a **determiner**, and *own* itself is catalogued as an emphasizer in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#own). Standing alone it is the pronoun — and it is the repair English uses for the two empty cells of the possessive paradigm: ✗ *that collar is its* becomes *a life of its own*, and ✗ *the choice is one's* becomes *a room of one's own*, which is why bare [one's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#ones) has no pronoun entry in this file at all. Against plain [mine](#mine) and [yours](#yours): *that's mine* names the owner and stops there, *that's my own* insists nobody else has a share in it.
**Variants:** *my own*, *your own*, *his own*, *her own*, *its own*, *our own*, *their own*, and regional *y'all's own*; generic *one's own* is the citation form. Informal speech also has *me own*, which is unstressed *my* rather than a separate word.

A possessive plus *own*, standing where a pronoun would — “the one belonging to that person and to nobody else.”

1. Don't take an office pen — bring *your own*.
2. Everyone at the potluck brought *their own*.
3. What she wanted most was a room of *her own*. (the *of*-frame)
4. The city has a rhythm all *its own* — the standard repair for the missing standalone *its*.
5. *My own* is still in the shop, so I borrowed his. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
6. He finished the whole thing on *his own*. (fixed frame — “unaided”)
7. She held *her own* against three older players. (fixed frame — “kept up”)
8. ✗ *The choice is one's* is not English; the repair is *the choice is one's own*.

**Русский:** других, чужое — принадлежащее прочим людям; книжн., в речи чаще other people's
**Type:** plural indefinite genitive pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral, and commoner in writing than in speech, where *other people's* usually takes its place
**Person / number:** third person **plural** — two or more further people. The singular of the same idea is [another's](#anothers). With the definite article, *the others'* names a specific remainder of a known group rather than an open set.
**Refers to:** people other than the ones already under discussion, human only. The thing possessed is understood from context, and is usually the noun that has just appeared: *he sees his own faults quickly and others' slowly*.

4. The blame was partly mine and partly *others'*. (coordination)
5. It is easier to forgive one's own failures than *others'*.
6. Whose signatures are missing — ours, or *others'*? (question)
7. That opinion was never *others'* to give. (negative)
8. Three spellings, one sound: *the others* left early, at *the other's* expense, at *others'* expense.

**Person / number:** third person singular. *Someone's* is the same word built on *one*; the *else* variant is [somebody else's](#somebody-elses).
**Refers to:** one unidentified person — human only; the thing possessed is supplied by context (*this coat is somebody's* = somebody's coat). For a non-human possessor English uses a noun genitive instead (*that collar is the dog's*).

Against [nobody's](#nobodys), which denies an owner outright.

Names an unidentified possessor — “belonging to some person, and we don't know who.”

1. That jacket on the chair is *somebody's* — don't throw it out. (subject complement)
2. *Somebody's* is missing from the pile; there are only nine. (subject, singular verb)
3. Whose keys are these? *Somebody's*, obviously. (short answer)

6. That call wasn't mine to make — it was *somebody's* higher up. (negative)
7. *Somebody's* been in my office. (not the possessive — this is *somebody has*)

**See also:** [somebody else's](#somebody-elses), [anyone's](#anyones), [everyone's](#everyones), [nobody's](#nobodys), [somebody](07-indefinite-compound.md#somebody)

### the children's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈtʃɪldrənz/ &middot; *thuh CHIL-drunz* &middot; an irregular plural takes the ordinary /z/ of the singular genitive, because the plural is not built with *-s* in the first place
**Русский:** детей, детское — принадлежащее детям (неправильное мн. ч. + 's)
**Type:** irregular plural genitive used as a pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, plural possessor, with a noun whose plural is irregular — *children*, *men*, *women*, *people*, *mice*.
**Refers to:** something belonging to the group named. The thing possessed is understood from context.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the understood possessed noun, not with the plural possessor: *The children's are in the hall* (several coats), *The children's is the small table* (one table).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **the children's** &middot; **the children's** *be* [complement] &middot; the frame — [irregular plural]*'s* — *the men's*, *the women's*, *the people's*
**Contrast:** Where the apostrophe goes depends on how the plural is built. A regular plural already ends in *-s*, so the apostrophe follows it (*the students'*, *the Smiths'*); an irregular plural does not, so it takes a full *'s* (*the children's*, *the women's*). Hence ✓ *the children's* and ✗ *the childrens'* — an apostrophe standing after the *-s* marks a plural genitive, so the second presupposes a plural ✗ *childrens*, and *children* is already the plural. The rule is mechanical: pluralize first, then add the apostrophe where the plural leaves off.

An irregular plural plus *'s*, standing alone for the possessive noun phrase — “the one, or the ones, belonging to the children.”

1. The coats in the front hall are *the children's*.
2. Every bedroom upstairs is *the children's*.
3. That schedule isn't ours to set — it's *the children's*.
4. *The children's* is the small table by the window. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
5. The loudest voices in the room were *the children's*.
6. Which locker is *the women's*? (question, same irregular pattern)
7. He's an old teacher of *the children's* from the school downtown. (double genitive)
8. Our coats went on the hook next to *the children's*. (object of a preposition)

**See also:** [the Smiths'](#the-smiths), [the company's](#the-companys), [the children's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#the-childrens), [s' (plural genitive)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#s-plural-genitive)

### the company's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈkʌmpəniz/ &middot; *thuh KUM-puh-neez* &middot; *the company's*, *the companies*, and *the companies'* are all pronounced /ˈkʌmpəniz/ — the difference exists only on paper
**Русский:** компании — принадлежащее организации; заменяет отсутствующее самостоятельное its
**Type:** genitive noun phrase used as a pronoun (fused genitive); institutional possessor &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; an organization treated as a **singular** possessor in American usage — *the company … its*, not *their*. British usage often takes such collectives as plural.
**Refers to:** an organization, agency, team, or other collective body. This is also the standard repair for the missing standalone *its*: where ✗ *higher than its* fails, *higher than the company's* works.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the understood possessed noun: *The company's is the bigger stake* (one stake), *The company's are the white trucks* (several). The possessor itself, being singular in American usage, pairs with *its* and with a singular verb: *The company has raised its offer.*
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **the company's** &middot; **the company's** *be* [complement] &middot; comparison — *higher than the company's* &middot; the frame — *the* [institution]*'s* — *the board's*, *the city's*, *the university's*
**Contrast:** Three spellings, one pronunciation: *the company's* (one company owns it), *the companies* (plain plural, no possession), *the companies'* (several companies own it). Only the apostrophe tells them apart, and only in writing. Against [its](#its): *the company's* is what English uses when it needs a standalone third-person non-personal possessive, because the pronoun slot is empty.

An institution's name or description plus *'s*, standing alone — “the one belonging to the company.”

1. The parking lot is *the company's*, not the city's.
2. Half the patents are *the company's*.
3. That call was *the board's*, not *the company's*.
4. The liability here is *the company's* alone.
5. Our margins are thin, but they're still better than *the company's* down the street. (the repair for ✗ *than its*)
6. Is the software *the company's* or the contractor's? (question)
7. *The company's* was the only bid that came in under budget.

**See also:** [the company's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#the-companys), [its](#its), [John's](#johns), [the children's](#the-childrens)

### the Smiths'

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈsmɪθs/ &middot; *thuh SMITHS* &middot; the plural genitive adds nothing you can hear — *the Smiths*, ✗ *the Smith's*, and *the Smiths'* are pronounced alike
**Русский:** Смитов — принадлежащее семье Смитов (мн. ч. + апостроф без s)
**Type:** regular plural genitive used as a pronoun (fused genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, plural possessor — a family, a couple, a group named collectively.
**Refers to:** something belonging to the named group. The thing possessed is understood from context.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the understood possessed noun, never with the plural possessor: *The Smiths' is the house with the red door* — one house, singular verb, though the possessor is a whole family.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **the Smiths'** &middot; **the Smiths'** *be* [complement] &middot; the frame — *the* [family name + plural]*'* — *the Joneses'*, *the Johnsons'*, *the neighbors'*
**Contrast:** Pluralize first, then add the apostrophe. The family is *the Smiths*, so the possessive is *the Smiths'* — ✗ *the Smith's* would mean one person named Smith. A name that already ends in a sibilant pluralizes with *-es* first: *Jones* → *the Joneses* → *the Joneses'*. And the plain plural takes no apostrophe at all: the holiday card is signed *the Smiths*, not ✗ *the Smith's*.

A plural family name plus the plural apostrophe, standing alone — “the one, or the ones, belonging to the Smiths.”

1. The house with the red door is *the Smiths'*.
2. That mail is *the Smiths'* — it landed in our box again.
3. Our yard ends at the fence; everything past it is *the Smiths'*.
4. *The Smiths'* is the only house on the block with a porch swing. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
5. The lawn mower isn't ours; it's *the neighbors'*. (negative)
6. Which boat is *the Joneses'*? (question — a name ending in a sibilant)
7. Our driveway is narrower than *the Smiths'*. (comparison)
8. She's a cousin of *the Smiths'* from out of state. (double genitive)

**See also:** [the children's](#the-childrens), [John's](#johns), [s' (plural genitive)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#s-plural-genitive), [the company's](#the-companys)

### you and yours

**Pronunciation:** /ˌju ən ˈjʊrz/ &middot; *yoo-un-YOORZ* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/, and the phrase carries one stress, on *yours*
**Русский:** вы и ваши близкие — формула поздравлений; здесь yours = люди, а не вещи
**Type:** fixed coordination — a personal pronoun plus an elliptical genitive, filling one noun-phrase slot between them &middot; **Case:** the first half takes whatever case the slot requires; the second is possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral and formulaic. Holiday cards, toasts, condolences, and the closing lines of letters are where it lives; outside those it sounds deliberately warm.
**Person / number:** second person here, singular or plural addressee. The frame runs through the whole paradigm — *me and mine*, *him and his*, *her and hers*, *us and ours*, *them and theirs* — and whatever the person, the phrase as a whole is **plural**.
**Refers to:** the person addressed **together with the people belonging to them** — family, household, dependents. This is the one place in the file where *yours* means people rather than possessions: *you and yours* is not “you and your things.”
**Agreement:** **plural**, always: ✓ *You and yours are in our thoughts*, ✗ *You and yours is in our thoughts*. The coordination has two heads and the second is understood as a plural noun (*your family*), so no singular reading is available even when one person is being addressed.
**Pattern:** *to* **you and yours** — *best wishes to you and yours* &middot; **you and yours** [plural verb] — *you and yours are welcome* &middot; *from* **me and mine** *to* **you and yours** &middot; [preposition] [pronoun] *and* [possessive] — *she'd do anything for her and hers*
**Contrast:** In *me and mine* and *him and his* the first half is the **object** form even when the phrase is the subject — *Me and mine are fine* — and that is a frozen idiom rather than the ordinary coordination error: the deletion test that condemns ✗ *Me and him went* has nothing to bite on here, because *I and mine* is not what anyone says either. It is still informal, and edited prose writes *my family and I*. Note also that *yours* in this phrase is the **elliptical** genitive with a human head understood — the same machinery as *the children's* — which is why *you and yours* means people while *is this yours?* means a coat.
**Variants:** *me and mine*, *him and his*, *her and hers*, *us and ours*, *them and theirs*; the archaic *thee and thine*; and the expanded *you and all yours*.

A fixed coordination of pronoun and possessive — “you, and the people who belong to you.”

1. Merry Christmas to *you and yours*.
2. *You and yours* are always welcome at our table. (plural verb — *are*)
3. Our thoughts are with *you and yours* this week. (condolence)
4. From *me and mine* to *you and yours*. (the first-person half of the frame)
5. *Him and his* cleared out two days before the storm hit. (object form frozen in subject position — informal)
6. She'd do anything for *her and hers*. (object of a preposition)
7. May the year be kind to *you and yours*. (toast)
8. The *yours* here means your family, not your belongings — compare *Is this coat yours?*, where it means the coat.

## Interrogative and relative possessives

*Whose* is the possessive of *who* — and, contrary to a persistent zombie rule, of *which* as well. Standing alone with no noun after it, it is a pronoun; with a noun after it, it is a determiner and belongs to the other dictionary.

The relatives divide their work by antecedent, and *whose* is the one that refuses to divide: ***who*** takes people, ***which*** takes things, ***that*** takes both, and ***whose*** takes both — it is the only genitive relative English has, so the same word serves *the engineer whose design won* and *a novel whose ending nobody likes*. Two further honesties about the family. ***Whom*** is the object form, and in American English it survives mainly right after a preposition (*to whom*, *for whom*, *several of whom*), where it is still required in writing; in the bare relative slot *the agent who we hired* is standard, and *whom* there is a register choice. And the ***that*** / ***which*** split — *that* for restrictive clauses, *which* for nonrestrictive — is an **editing convention**, not a rule of the language: house styles enforce it, careful writers have used restrictive *which* for four hundred years, and no reader is misled by it. The comma is what actually carries the meaning. All of these are catalogued in [Relative pronouns](05-relative.md).

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ* &middot; no weak form; identical to *who's*
**Русский:** чей, чьего — вопросительное и относительное; и о людях, и о предметах
**Type:** interrogative and relative pronoun (genitive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** any person or number. It asks about, or refers to, a possessor without specifying one.
**Refers to:** **both people and things.** *Whose* is the only genitive relative English has, so it does duty for *who* and for *which* alike: *the engineer whose design won*, *a novel whose ending nobody likes*. Set against the rest of the family — *who* takes people, *which* takes things, *that* takes both — *whose* patterns with *that*: it takes both, and always has. The claim that *whose* may not have an inanimate antecedent is an invented rule; the alternative, *of which*, is grammatical but stiff (*a novel the ending of which nobody likes*). As a standalone pronoun the possessor is normally human.
**Agreement:** takes no agreement of its own. In a question the verb agrees with the noun that follows *is/are*: *Whose is this?* (one thing), *Whose are these?* (several). In a relative clause the verb agrees with the clause's own subject, not with *whose*.
**Pattern:** **Whose** *is/are* [noun phrase]? — *Whose is this?* &middot; [verb] **whose** [clause] — *I know whose it is* &middot; [preposition] **whose** [clause] — *it depends on whose it is* &middot; the determiner use, which lives in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whose) — **whose** [noun] — *whose car is that?*
**Contrast:** ***Whose* versus *who's*.** *Whose* asks or states possession; *who's* is the contraction of *who is* or *who has*. Expand it: *Who's calling?* is *who is calling*, but *Whose call is it?* has no expansion, so it takes *whose*. Against [which](05-relative.md#which): *which* picks an item out of a set (*Which is yours?*), *whose* asks who owns it (*Whose is this?*). Against [whom](05-relative.md#whom): *whom* is the **object** form of *who*, not a possessive — ✓ *the client to whom I sent it*, ✓ *the client whose file I sent*; in American English *whom* survives mainly right after a preposition, and elsewhere *who* is standard. Note also the impossible ✗ *whose else* — the phrasal genitive requires *who else's*.

Asks, or states, who the possessor is, with the thing possessed already understood from context.

**1.** **Interrogative pronoun** — asks which person something belongs to, with no noun following: *Whose is this?* **2.** **Fused relative** — introduces a clause that supplies its own antecedent: *I don't know whose it is.*

1. *Whose* is this? (interrogative pronoun — nothing follows it)
2. *Whose* are these gloves on the counter? (plural referent — *are*)
3. I found a badge in the parking lot, but I have no idea *whose* it is.
4. Nobody could remember *whose* the idea had been originally.
5. ✓ *Whose* signature is missing? — determiner, because a noun follows; the pronoun is the bare *Whose is missing?*
6. It's an old house *whose* wiring needs replacing. (relative with a non-human antecedent — standard, despite the folk rule against it)
7. Half the coats were left unclaimed, and no one could say *whose*. (bare pronoun as the whole object of *say*)
8. Whether we send it back depends on *whose* it turns out to be. (fused relative as object of the preposition *on*)
9. ✗ *Whose* else is it? → ✓ *Who else's is it?*

**See also:** [who's (not a possessive pronoun)](#whos-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [whose (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whose), [whosever](#whosever), [who](05-relative.md#who), [whom](05-relative.md#whom), [of which](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#of-which)

### whosesoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhuz soʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hooz-so-EV-er* &middot; four syllables, main stress on *-ev-*; no weak form, and no modern speaker has occasion to reduce it
**Русский:** чей бы то ни было — устар., книжн. (библейский стиль); сам себе антецедент
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (genitive) — *whose* plus the emphatic suffix *-soever*; catalogued here for the standalone use, the prenominal use being a **determiner** &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **archaic and formal.** The King James Bible, older legal drafting, and deliberate period style. An American who meets this word meets it in John 20:23 or in a document written to sound like it.
**Person / number:** third person singular. It names one possessor, whatever the number of the things involved.
**Refers to:** a human possessor whose identity is unknown or deliberately left open — “whoever's, no matter whose.” Like [whosever](#whosever), it supplies its own antecedent, so no noun stands in front of it.
**Agreement:** the free relative names **one** person, so the main verb is singular even when a plural noun sits beside it: ✓ *Whosesoever these are needs to claim them.* The plural noun inside the clause takes its own plural verb (*these are*), and that has no effect on the main one.
**Pattern:** **Whosesoever** *it is*, [main clause] — *whosesoever it is, it has to move* &middot; [preposition] **whosesoever** [clause] — *give it back to whosesoever it is* &middot; the King James spelling, split around the noun — *whose soever* [noun] &middot; the prenominal frame, which is the determiner — **whosesoever** [noun] [verb]
**Contrast:** *-soever* is an intensifying suffix meaning “at all, no matter which,” the same one in *whatsoever*, *whosoever*, *wheresoever*, *whensoever*, and *howsoever*. Only *whatsoever* survives in ordinary American English, and chiefly in negatives (*no doubt whatsoever*); the rest, this one included, are archaic. Against [whosever](#whosever), which is merely rare and bookish rather than archaic, and against *whoever's*, which is what an American actually says. Note that the King James text splits the word around its noun — *Whose soever sins ye remit* — where modern printings close it up.
**Variants:** *whose soever*, the split King James spelling; *whosoever's*; and the archaic *whoseever*. The everyday equivalent is *whoever's*.

Introduces a clause that identifies a possessor without naming one, with the *-soever* of *whatsoever* on the end — “whoever's it may turn out to be.”

1. *Whosesoever* it is, it cannot stay in the hallway. (the bare pronoun)
2. *Whosesoever* these are needs to claim them by Friday. (bare pronoun, plural referent inside the clause — singular main verb *needs*)
3. I'll hand the wallet over to *whosesoever* it turns out to be. (object of a preposition)
4. *Whose soever* sins ye remit, they are remitted unto them. (John 20:23, King James Version — the split spelling)
5. *Whosesoever* name is on the deed will have to sign it. (determiner use inside the clause)
6. Said plainly today: *Whoever's* it is should say so now.
7. The suffix is the one in *whatsoever*, which is the only member of the family still in ordinary use: *no doubt whatsoever*.

**See also:** [whosever](#whosever), [whose](#whose), [whosever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever), [who](05-relative.md#who)

### whosever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈzɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-ZEV-er* &middot; no weak form; the everyday spoken equivalent is *whoever's* /huˈɛvɚz/
**Русский:** того, чей — книжн., редк.: «тот, чей»; существительное перед ним не нужно
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (genitive) — catalogued here for the standalone use, since the commoner prenominal use (*whosever car…*) is a **determiner** and belongs to the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** formal, rare — it sounds bookish in speech
**Person / number:** third person singular. It names one possessor, whatever the number of the things involved.
**Refers to:** a human possessor whose identity is unknown or beside the point. Unlike [whose](#whose), it supplies its own antecedent — *whosever* means “the person whose,” so no noun precedes it.
**Agreement:** the free relative names **one** person, so the main verb is singular even when a plural noun sits next to it: ✓ *Whosever keys these are needs to claim them.* Many speakers say *need* by attraction to *keys*; a careful editor will change it back.
**Pattern:** standalone, the pronoun use — **Whosever** *it is*, [main clause]: *Whosever it is, it needs to move* &middot; [preposition] **whosever** [clause] — *give it back to whosever it is* &middot; everyday spoken standalone — **whoever's** *it is* &middot; the prenominal frame, which is the determiner and lives in the other dictionary — **whosever** [noun] [verb] …
**Contrast:** In American speech people say *whoever's*: *Whoever's car that is needs to move it.* *Whosever* is the tidy written form and rare enough to sound stilted; *whosesoever* and *whoseever* are archaic. Do not confuse it with relative [whose](#whose), which needs an antecedent in front of it (*the man whose car…*) where *whosever* contains its own (*whosever car…*). Note too that *whoever's* is ambiguous on paper — it is also the contraction of *whoever is* (*Whoever's driving should stop*).

Introduces a clause that identifies a possessor without naming one — “the person whose.”

1. *Whosever* it is, it can't stay in the hallway. (the bare pronoun — the standalone use catalogued here)
2. *Whosever* these are, they need to be off the counter by noon. (bare pronoun, plural referent inside — *are*)
3. I'll give the wallet back to *whosever* it turns out to be. (object of a preposition)
4. *Whoever's* it is should say so now. (the ordinary spoken standalone form)
5. *Whosever* keys these are needs to come get them. (determiner inside the clause — note the plural noun beside it and the singular main verb *needs*)
6. *Whosever* car is blocking the driveway needs to move it. (determiner use, catalogued in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever))
7. *Whoever's* umbrella this is, they left it in the rain. (the spoken determiner form; singular *they* refers back to the unknown owner)
8. Leave the box at the desk and *whosever* it is can pick it up. (bare pronoun heading the subject of the second clause)
9. She'll hand the badge over to *whosever* it proves to be. (bare pronoun after a preposition)

## Archaic, dialectal, and nonstandard forms

These are here so you can recognize them, not so you can use them. *Thine* is four centuries old and survives in scripture and hymns; the *-n* forms and *mines* are alive in particular American speech communities and are marked in writing; *y'all's* is ordinary Southern speech and informal everywhere else, and *you guys'* is its Northern counterpart, equally ordinary in the mouth and equally absent from print.

### hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɪzn̩/, /hɝn/, /ɑrn/, /jɔrn/, /ðɛrn/ &middot; *HIZ-un, HURN, ARN, YORN, THAIRN* &middot; also written with an apostrophe — *his'n*, *her'n*, *our'n*, *your'n*, *their'n*
**Русский:** его, её, наш, ваш, их — диал., нестанд.: Аппалачи, Озарк, Юг и Мидленд США
**Type:** possessive pronouns, dialectal &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard.** Appalachian, Ozark, and older Southern and Midland American speech, and the northern English dialects those came from. Unremarkable locally, strongly marked in writing, and never appropriate in edited prose.
**Person / number:** third singular masculine (*hisn*), third singular feminine (*hern*), first plural (*ourn*), second singular or plural (*yourn*), third plural (*theirn*). There is no ✗ *minen* — *mine* already ends in the *-n* these were built from.
**Refers to:** exactly what the standard forms refer to; the meaning is identical. Only the register differs.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the thing possessed, as with the standard forms: *Ourn is the blue house*, *Ourn are in the barn*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **hisn** — *that truck is hisn* &middot; **ourn** [verb] — *ourn is the blue house* &middot; ✗ **hisn** [noun] — impossible; these forms never precede a noun. The determiner in the same dialects is the ordinary *his*, *her*, *our*, *your*, *their*.
**Contrast:** They were built by analogy with *mine* and *thine*, which is why they end in *-n*, and they behave like *mine*: they **stand alone** and never precede a noun. ✓ *That truck is his'n*, ✗ *his'n truck*. The slot test settles it in one move. Against the standard forms, the only difference is register — a reader will place the speaker geographically and socially, which is exactly why the forms belong in dialogue and nowhere else.

Dialectal possessive pronouns matching *his*, *hers*, *ours*, *yours*, *theirs*, formed on the pattern of *mine* and *thine*.

1. That truck out front is *hisn*.
2. *Ourn* is the blue house at the end of the lane.
3. Is this coat *yourn* or mine?
4. The bottom field used to be *theirn* before the road went through.
5. She said the recipe was *hern*, handed down from her grandmother.
6. ✗ *Hisn* truck is in the shop. → ✓ *His truck is in the shop.* (the form never precedes a noun, even in the dialects that use it)

**See also:** [mine](#mine), [thine](#thine), [his](#his), [theirs](#theirs), [mines](#mines)

### mines

**Pronunciation:** /maɪnz/ &middot; *MYNEZ* &middot; an added /z/ on the standard *mine*
**Русский:** моё — нестанд.: вариант mine в афроамериканском английском; на письме ошибка
**Type:** possessive pronoun, nonstandard &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard.** Common in African American English and in some urban and regional varieties, and ordinary within them; marked wrong by every editor and every standardized test. Do not use it in writing.
**Person / number:** first person singular — the same reference as *mine*, with no difference in meaning.
**Refers to:** something belonging to the speaker; identical in reference to [mine](#mine).
**Agreement:** the verb still agrees with the thing possessed, not with the *-s*: *That book is mines* has a singular referent despite the ending, and speakers who use the form do not treat it as a plural.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **mines** — *that seat is mines* &middot; ✗ **mines** [noun] — the form never precedes a noun, exactly like *mine*
**Contrast:** The *-s* is regularization, not a plural: *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs* all end in *-s*, so *mine* gets one too. That makes the form perfectly logical and still nonstandard. Standard English is ✓ *That's mine*. Note the parallel with the dialectal *-n* forms, which regularize in the opposite direction, adding the ending of *mine* to *his*, *her*, *our*, *your*, *their*.

A regularized form of *mine*, built by copying the *-s* of *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs*.

1. That seat is *mines*. (nonstandard; standard English is *That seat is mine*.)
2. Which one is *mines*? (nonstandard question form)
3. He said the jacket was *mines*, but I'd never seen it before.
4. *Mines* is the one on the end. (nonstandard subject use)
5. The logic behind it is plain: *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs* — so *mines*.
6. ✓ In writing, always *mine*: *That seat is mine.*

**See also:** [mine](#mine), [hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn](#hisn-hern-ourn-yourn-theirn), [yours](#yours), [ours](#ours)

### thine

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪn/ — no weak form &middot; *THYNE (rhymes with MINE)* &middot; the word only ever turns up stressed
**Русский:** твоё — устар.: библейское и поэтическое; в современном языке yours
**Type:** possessive pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **archaic.** The King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, Shakespeare, hymns, and deliberate period writing. A few conservative Quaker meetings still keep the plain speech (*thee*, *thy*, *thine*) as a religious practice; outside that, an American reads this word and never says it.
**Person / number:** second person **singular**, familiar — the possessive pronoun matching *thou* and *thee*. A plural addressee took *yours* even in Early Modern English, which is the distinction modern *your/yours* lost.
**Refers to:** something belonging to one familiar addressee. In modern English the whole range is covered by [yours](#yours).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the thing possessed: *the kingdom is thine* (singular), *these are thine* (plural).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **thine** — *the kingdom is thine* &middot; [noun] *of* **thine** — *a friend of thine* &middot; coordination — *what's mine is thine* &middot; the prenominal use, which is the **determiner** and lives in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thine) — *thine eyes*, *thine own self*
**Contrast:** Two words are spelled *thine*, and the usual test separates them: a noun after it makes it a **determiner** (*thine eyes*, and only before a vowel sound — *thy sword* before a consonant), nothing after it makes it a **pronoun** (*the kingdom is thine*). The first-person twin is *mine*, and the machinery is identical. The beginner's error is sprinkling *thine* on any noun to sound old: ✗ *thine sword*.

The possessive pronoun of the old familiar singular *thou* — “the one, or the ones, belonging to thee.”

1. For *thine* is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. (the Lord's Prayer, Matthew 6:13, King James Version)
2. Not my will, but *thine*, be done. (Luke 22:42, KJV)
3. All that I have is *thine*.
4. What's mine is *thine*, and what's thine is mine. (coordination)
5. He greeted an old friend of *thine* at the gate. (double genitive, Early Modern English)
6. ✗ *Thine* sword is the standard beginner's error: *sword* begins with a consonant, and a noun follows, so the phrase is *thy sword*.

**See also:** [thine (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thine), [thy (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thy), [mine](#mine), [yours](#yours), [thou](01-personal.md#thou)

### y'all's

**Pronunciation:** /jɔlz/ &middot; *YAWLZ* &middot; also spelled *y'alls*, *yall's*, and *ya'll's*; the last of these puts the apostrophe in the wrong place, since the contraction is *you all*
**Русский:** ваш, ваше — разг., южн.-амер.: принадлежащее нескольким собеседникам
**Type:** possessive pronoun, regional — the same form also serves as a determiner, which is catalogued in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#yalls) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **regional and informal.** Southern and South Midland American English, where it is entirely ordinary in speech, and increasingly heard nationally. Not used in formal writing.
**Person / number:** second person **plural** — the plural *your/yours* that standard English lacks. Related forms: *all y'all's* (emphatic plural), and the northern equivalents [you guys'](#you-guys) and *yinz's*.
**Refers to:** something belonging to the people being addressed, two or more of them. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the thing possessed: *Y'all's is the blue truck*, *Y'all's are in the cooler*.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **y'all's** — *that truck is y'all's* &middot; **y'all's** [verb] — *y'all's is the blue one* &middot; [noun] *of* **y'all's** — *a cousin of y'all's* &middot; emphatic — *all y'all's* &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the other dictionary — **y'all's** [noun]
**Contrast:** Unlike the standard possessives, *y'all's* is spelled **with** an apostrophe, because it is a genitive built on a contracted noun phrase (*you all* → *y'all* → *y'all's*), not one of the seven personal forms. It fills a real gap: standard *yours* cannot distinguish one addressee from several, and *y'all's* can. Against [yours](#yours), the difference is register and number, not meaning.
**Variants:** *all y'all's*, the emphatic plural — *the whole back row is all y'all's* — which marks off the whole group addressed from a smaller one already mentioned; the spellings *y'alls* and *yall's*, and the misplaced apostrophe of ✗ *ya'll's*; the Northern equivalent [you guys'](#you-guys); and Pittsburgh's *yinz's*.

The Southern second-person plural possessive — “the one, or the ones, belonging to you all.”

1. That truck in the driveway is *y'all's*.
2. *Y'all's* is the house with the blue shutters.
3. Is this cooler ours or *y'all's*? (question)
4. He's a cousin of *y'all's* on your mother's side. (double genitive)
5. The mess in the kitchen sure isn't mine — it's *y'all's*. (negative)
6. The whole back row is all *y'all's*. (emphatic plural)

**See also:** [yours](#yours), [you guys'](#you-guys), [y'all's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#yalls), [hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn](#hisn-hern-ourn-yourn-theirn), [you](01-personal.md#you)

### you guys'

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈɡaɪz/, also /ju ˈɡaɪzɪz/ &middot; *yoo GYZE, also yoo GY-ziz* &middot; the plain possessive adds nothing you can hear — *you guys* and *you guys'* are identical in speech — which is why many speakers pronounce the ending out as *you guys's*
**Русский:** ваш, ваше — разг.: то же о нескольких собеседниках, общеамериканский вариант
**Type:** possessive pronoun, regional and informal — the same form serves as a determiner, catalogued in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **informal.** The Northern, Western, and general-American answer to Southern *y'all's*: ordinary in speech across most of the country, and almost never written — partly because it is informal, partly because nobody agrees how to spell it.
**Person / number:** second person **plural** — the plural *your/yours* that standard English lacks. *You guys* is addressed to groups of any gender in most of the country, though some speakers avoid it for an all-female group and reach for *you all* instead.
**Refers to:** something belonging to the people being addressed, two or more of them. The thing possessed may be singular, plural, or uncountable.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the thing possessed, not with the plural addressee: *You guys' is the blue tent* (one tent), *You guys' are in the back seat* (several).
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *be* **you guys'** — *that cooler is you guys'* &middot; **you guys'** [verb] — *you guys' is the blue one* &middot; [noun] *of* **you guys'** — *a friend of you guys'* &middot; the pronounced-out genitive — **you guys's** &middot; the determiner use, which belongs to the other dictionary — **you guys'** [noun] — *you guys' car*
**Contrast:** The spelling is the whole difficulty, and it is unsettled: *you guys'*, *you guys's*, and *you guyses* are all written, because the ending is landing on a phrase whose last word already ends in /z/ — the same collision that produces *the Joneses'*. Southern [y'all's](#yalls) has no such problem, which is one reason it travels better into print. Against [yours](#yours) the difference is number and register: *yours* cannot tell one addressee from several, and this can. Note that *guys* here has been bleached of its noun meaning entirely — *you guys'* is a second-person plural pronoun, takes no article, and says nothing about anyone's gender.
**Variants:** *you guys's* (the pronounced-out genitive, /ju ˈɡaɪzɪz/) and *you guyses*; *youse guys'* and *yous guys'* (New York, New Jersey, Philadelphia); *yinz's* (Pittsburgh); *you-uns'* and *you'uns'* (Appalachian); and the Southern *y'all's* and *all y'all's*.

The Northern and general-American second-person plural possessive — “the one, or the ones, belonging to you guys.”

1. Is this cooler ours or *you guys'*?
2. *You guys'* is the blue tent at the end of the row. (subject, singular referent — *is*)
3. The seats in the back row are *you guys'*. (plural referent)
4. He's an old friend of *you guys'* from college. (double genitive)
5. The mess in the kitchen sure isn't mine — it's *you guys'*. (negative)
6. Most speakers say it with the extra syllable: *That car is you guys's.*
7. In the South the same sentence comes out *That car is y'all's.* (the regional counterpart)

## Apostrophe impostors — words that look or sound like possessive pronouns and are not

The personal possessives take no apostrophe; the contractions that sound like them take nothing else. Every one of these pairs is invisible in speech and unmistakable on paper, and the test never changes: **expand the contraction**. If the expansion is grammatical, the apostrophe belongs; if it is nonsense, you want the possessive.

### her's, our's, your's, their's, its'

**Pronunciation:** pronounced exactly like *hers*, *ours*, *yours*, *theirs*, *its* &middot; *HURZ, OW-erz, YOORZ, THAIRZ, ITS* &middot; the misspellings are inaudible — they exist only in writing
**Русский:** орфографическая ошибка — эти местоимения никогда не пишутся с апострофом
**Type:** misspellings — not words in any variety of English &middot; **Case:** none — a misspelling carries no case of its own; the forms these misspell are possessive &middot; **Register:** always an error
**Person / number:** not applicable.
**Refers to:** nothing. These are the correct pronouns with an apostrophe wrongly inserted.
**Agreement:** not applicable.
**Pattern:** ✗ *a friend of her's* → ✓ *of hers* &middot; ✗ *the house is our's* → ✓ *is ours* &middot; ✗ *is that your's?* → ✓ *yours* &middot; ✗ *the fault was their's* → ✓ *theirs* &middot; ✗ *its' tail* → ✓ *its tail*
**Contrast:** The rule has no exceptions: **personal possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe.** *Hers*, *ours*, *yours*, *theirs*, *its*, and *whose* are all apostrophe-free. The error is understandable, because every other possessive in English is built with one — *John's*, *the company's*, *somebody else's*, *the Smiths'* — so the hand adds it out of habit. The one to single out is ✗ *its'*, which is not a possible English word in any sense: there is no reading, no dialect, and no register that calls for it.

The apostrophe errors that the possessive pronouns attract — listed here only so they can be recognized and removed.

1. ✗ She's a friend of *her's*. → ✓ She's a friend of hers.
2. ✗ The blue house is *our's*. → ✓ The blue house is ours.
3. ✗ Is this seat *your's*? → ✓ Is this seat yours?
4. ✗ The mistake was *their's*. → ✓ The mistake was theirs.
5. ✗ The dog hurt *its'* paw. → ✓ The dog hurt its paw.
6. The one apostrophe that is correct here belongs to the noun genitives: ✓ *the coat is John's*, ✓ *the coats are the children's*.

**See also:** [hers](#hers), [ours](#ours), [yours](#yours), [theirs](#theirs), [it's (not a possessive pronoun)](#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun)

### it's (not a possessive pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪts/ &middot; *ITS* &middot; identical to *its*; the two are distinguishable only in writing
**Русский:** это, оно есть — сокращение it is / it has; притяжательное — its, без апострофа
**Type:** contraction of *it is* or *it has* &middot; **Case:** none — it is a subject plus a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing; spelled out in the most formal prose
**Person / number:** third person singular, non-personal — but as a subject and a verb, not as a possessive.
**Refers to:** nothing possessively. The *it* inside it is the ordinary dummy or referential subject pronoun.
**Agreement:** it **is** the agreement — the contracted verb is already third-person singular: *It's raining*, *It's been a week*.
**Pattern:** **It's** [adjective] — *It's cold.* &middot; **It's** [noun phrase] — *It's a shame.* &middot; **It's** [verb]*-ing* — *It's raining.* &middot; **It's been** [noun phrase] — *It's been a week.*
**Contrast:** ✗ *The dog hurt it's paw* → ✓ *its paw*: expand it and *the dog hurt it is paw* is nonsense, so the possessive is required. In the other direction, ✗ *Its raining* → ✓ *It's raining*. This is the most-corrected error in written English, and the test takes one second. And ✗ *its'* is not a word in any sense at all.

The contraction of *it is* or *it has* — a possessive-shaped trap with no possessive function whatsoever.

1. *It's* going to rain all weekend.
2. *It's* been three years since we moved. (*it has*)
3. I think *it's* your turn.
4. *It's* not the money, *it's* the principle. (negative)
5. Do you think *it's* too late to call? (question — and the test still works: *do you think it is too late*)
6. If *it's* broken, its warranty should cover the repair. (both words, one sentence)

**See also:** [its](#its), [who's (not a possessive pronoun)](#whos-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [you're (not a possessive pronoun)](#youre-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [it's (determiner dictionary)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its-not-a-determiner)

### there's (not a possessive pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrz/, weak /ðɚz/ &middot; *THAIRZ, weak therz* &middot; homophonous with *theirs* in ordinary American speech
**Русский:** есть, имеется — сокращение there is / there has; не притяжательное
**Type:** contraction of *there is* or *there has* &middot; **Case:** none — it is a dummy subject plus a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing
**Person / number:** not applicable. The *there* inside it is the existential dummy subject, which carries no person or number of its own.
**Refers to:** nothing. Existential *there* points at nothing at all; it fills the subject slot that English grammar requires.
**Agreement:** **this is where it bites.** Existential *there* takes its number from the noun that follows the verb, not from *there*: ✓ *There is one problem*, ✓ *There are three problems*. Spoken American English uses *there's* with plurals constantly (*there's two people here*), at every level of education and formality; in edited prose it is still changed to *there are*.
**Pattern:** **There's** [singular noun phrase] — *There's a problem.* &middot; **There's been** [noun phrase] — *There's been a mistake.* &middot; formal plural — *There are* [plural noun phrase] &middot; ✗ **there's** used possessively — impossible
**Contrast:** Two spellings share this sound, and two more only look as though they do. ***There's*** (*there is/has*) and ***theirs***, the possessive pronoun (*the car is theirs*), are the true homophones, both /ðɛrz/. ***They're*** (*they are*) and plain ***there*** (the place word or the dummy subject) are /ðɛr/ — they lack the final /z/ altogether, so they sound like *their*, not like this word. Expand the contraction to test: if *there is* fits, the apostrophe belongs. ✗ *The car is there's* is one of the more common possessive errors in casual writing.

The contraction of existential or locative *there* with *is* or *has* — a homophone of *theirs* with no possessive meaning at all.

1. *There's* a problem with the second invoice.
2. *There's* been a mistake. (*there has*)
3. *There's* nothing left in the fridge. (negative)
4. *There's* three people ahead of us. (ordinary speech; formal writing takes *There are three people*)
5. ✗ The blue car is *there's*. → ✓ The blue car is theirs.
6. *There's* their car, and the one behind it is theirs too. (all three words, one sentence)

**See also:** [theirs](#theirs), [they're (not a possessive pronoun)](#theyre-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [it's (not a possessive pronoun)](#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun)

### they're (not a possessive pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛr/, weak /ðɚ/ &middot; *THAIR, weak ther* &middot; homophonous with *their* and with *there*
**Русский:** они есть — сокращение they are; притяжательные — their, theirs
**Type:** contraction of *they are* &middot; **Case:** none — it is a subject plus a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing
**Person / number:** third person plural, or singular *they*.
**Refers to:** whatever *they* refers to — but as a subject, not as a possessor.
**Agreement:** it **is** the agreement: the contracted verb is plural *are*, and it stays *are* with singular *they* as well (*They're the applicant who called*).
**Pattern:** **They're** [adjective] — *They're late.* &middot; **They're** [noun phrase] — *They're the ones who called.* &middot; **They're** [verb]*-ing* — *They're leaving.* &middot; ✗ **they're** [noun] — impossible; the determiner is *their*
**Contrast:** The classic three-way pileup is ***their*** (the possessive determiner — *their car*), ***there*** (the place word or dummy subject), and ***they're*** (*they are*) — three spellings, one sound, /ðɛr/. ***Theirs***, the possessive pronoun (*the car is theirs*), is a fourth spelling in the same family, but it is not a homophone of these three: it carries a final /z/ and pairs by sound with *there's* instead. Expand it: ✗ *they're car* becomes *they are car*, which is nonsense, so the determiner *their* is required.

The contraction of *they are* — a homophone of *their* and *there*, and no kind of possessive.

1. *They're* running twenty minutes behind.
2. *They're* the ones who called about the apartment.
3. *They're* not going to make the deadline. (negative)
4. ✗ *They're* car is blocking the driveway. → ✓ Their car is blocking the driveway.
5. *They're* over there, and the blue one is theirs. (all three words, one sentence)
6. Somebody left a bag; *they're* going to want it back, and it's probably theirs. (singular *they*, standard)

**See also:** [theirs](#theirs), [there's (not a possessive pronoun)](#theres-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [their (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#their), [they](01-personal.md#they)

### who's (not a possessive pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ* &middot; identical to *whose*
**Русский:** кто есть — сокращение who is / who has; притяжательное — whose
**Type:** contraction of *who is* or *who has* &middot; **Case:** none — it is a subject plus a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing
**Person / number:** any person or number; the verb inside it is third-person singular *is* or *has*.
**Refers to:** a person, as the **subject** of a clause — never as a possessor.
**Agreement:** it **is** the agreement: *Who's coming?* is singular by default even when the answer is plural (*Who's coming? — The Smiths are*). Where a plural subject is explicit, use *who are*: *Who are these people?*
**Pattern:** **Who's** [verb]*-ing*? — *Who's calling?* &middot; **Who's** [noun phrase]? — *Who's the manager?* &middot; **Who's been** [verb]*-en*? — *Who's been here?* &middot; relative — *the one who's in charge*
**Contrast:** ✗ *Who's turn is it?* → ✓ *Whose turn is it?*: expand it and *who is turn* is nonsense, so the possessive is required. In the other direction, ✗ *Whose calling?* → ✓ *Who's calling?* The test is the same one that settles *its/it's* and *your/you're*.

The contraction of *who is* or *who has* — a homophone of *whose* with no possessive function.

1. *Who's* calling, please?
2. *Who's* been using my charger? (*who has*)
3. She's the one *who's* in charge of scheduling. (relative clause)
4. *Who's* not on the list yet? (negative)
5. ✗ *Who's* keys are these? → ✓ Whose keys are these?
6. *Who's* the owner, and whose is the truck out front? (both words, one sentence)

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [who](05-relative.md#who), [it's (not a possessive pronoun)](#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun), [who's (determiner dictionary)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whos-not-a-determiner)

### you're (not a possessive pronoun)

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr/, /jɔr/, weak /jɚ/ &middot; *YOOR / YOR, weak yer* &middot; in ordinary speech the weak /jɚ/ is identical to weak *your*, which is exactly why the two get swapped in writing
**Русский:** ты, вы есть — сокращение you are; притяжательные — your, yours
**Type:** contraction of *you are* &middot; **Case:** none — it is a subject plus a verb &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing
**Person / number:** second person, singular or plural.
**Refers to:** the addressee, as the **subject** of a clause — never as a possessor.
**Agreement:** it **is** the agreement: *you are* is invariably plural in form, for one addressee or many (*You're late*, said to one person or to six).
**Pattern:** **You're** [adjective] — *You're right.* &middot; **You're** [noun phrase] — *You're the manager.* &middot; **You're** [verb]*-ing* — *You're leaving already?* &middot; ✗ **you're** [noun] — impossible; the determiner is *your*
**Contrast:** ✗ *You're seat is in the back* → ✓ *Your seat*: expand it and *you are seat* is nonsense. In the other direction, ✗ *Your welcome* → ✓ *You're welcome*, which is the single most common form of this error. Note that the possessive pronoun is *yours*, with no apostrophe at all: ✓ *the seat is yours*, ✗ *your's*.

The contraction of *you are* — a homophone of *your* with no possessive function.

1. *You're* going to want a jacket.
2. *You're* the only one who answered.
3. *You're* not on the list. (negative)
4. ✗ *Your* welcome. → ✓ You're welcome.
5. ✗ *You're* seat is in the back row. → ✓ Your seat is in the back row.
6. *You're* early, your badge is at the desk, and the blue folder is yours. (all three words, one sentence)

## Notes and tricky cases

**The slot test decides which dictionary a word is in.** A noun after it makes it a **determiner** (*my coat*, *whose car*, *John's house*, *y'all's truck*); nothing after it makes it a **pronoun** (*the coat is mine*, *whose is this?*, *the house is John's*, *that truck is y'all's*). The determiner forms are catalogued in [Possessive and genitive determiners](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md) and are not repeated here.

**The verb agrees with the thing possessed.** This is the agreement rule for the whole class, and it catches people out because the pronoun looks plural when the possessor is plural. *Ours is the blue house* — one house, singular verb, even though *ours* stands for a whole family. *Theirs was the only bid under budget* — one bid. *Mine are still in the dryer* — several shirts. The possessor never touches the verb.

**Agreement when a quantifier gets in front of the possessive.** The rule above is easy to lose inside *[quantifier] of ours*, where the quantifier, not the possessive, decides the verb. *Each*, *either*, and *neither* are singular: *Each of ours is late*, *Neither of theirs was ready*. So are the indefinite compounds — *everyone*, *everybody*, *someone*, *somebody*, *anyone*, *nobody* — and their genitives with them: *Everyone's is different*, *Nobody's was finished*. *Both*, *several*, and *few* are plural: *Both of ours are late*, *Several of theirs were damaged*, *Few of hers were usable*. *None* takes **either** verb and always has — *None of ours is late* and *None of ours are late* are both standard English, the plural being the commoner in ordinary prose, and the "*none* = *not one*, therefore singular" rule is a nineteenth-century invention. *All*, *some*, and *most* simply follow the referent: *All of ours are gone* (things you count), *All of ours is gone* (something you measure). The quantifiers themselves are catalogued in [Indefinite and quantifying pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md).

**The personal possessives take no apostrophe. Ever.** *Hers, ours, yours, theirs, its, whose* — six forms, no apostrophes, no exceptions. The genitives built on nouns and indefinites all take one (*John's*, *the children's*, *the Smiths'*, *nobody's*, *y'all's*), and that split is precisely why the pronouns attract the error.

| Write | Not | Because |
|---|---|---|
| the coat is hers | ✗ her's | personal possessive pronouns take no apostrophe |
| the house is ours | ✗ our's | same rule |
| is this yours? | ✗ your's, ✗ you're | *you're* = *you are* |
| the fault was theirs | ✗ their's, ✗ there's | *there's* = *there is/has* |
| the dog hurt its paw | ✗ it's paw, ✗ its' paw | *it's* = *it is/has*; *its'* is not a word |
| whose turn is it? | ✗ who's turn | *who's* = *who is/has* |
| the coats are the children's | ✗ the childrens' | *children* is already plural |
| the house is the Smiths' | ✗ the Smith's (for a family) | pluralize first, then add the apostrophe |

**Before a gerund, use the determiner, not the pronoun.** ✓ *They objected to my leaving early*, ✗ *to mine leaving early*; ✓ *We appreciated her coming*, ✗ *hers coming*. The independent possessive can never precede anything, and an *-ing* form used as a noun counts as something. The live question with gerunds is *my leaving* against *me leaving* — the possessive is the formal choice and the safer one in edited prose, the object form is ordinary in speech and increasingly accepted in print, and with a long or inanimate subject the possessive is genuinely awkward (✓ *the chance of the plan failing*, stiff *the plan's failing*).

**Case in coordination, and the one test that settles all of it.** Every form in this file is a **genitive**, and English keeps its three cases apart by using three different words for the same person — *she* went, I called *her*, the coat is *hers*. Case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause, and the single place Americans reliably lose track of it is inside a coordination, where it goes wrong in both directions. **Delete the other conjunct and listen** — one second, every time:

- **Subject slot, subject form.** ✓ *She and I went* (because *she went*, *I went*). The opposite error, ✗ *Me and him went*, is an object form doing a subject's job: nonstandard, and universally corrected in writing.
- **Object slot, object form.** ✓ *between you and me*, ✓ *He gave it to Bob and me*, ✓ *They invited my wife and me* — because *between… me*, *to me*, *invited me*. The *I* versions of all three — ✗ *between you and I*, ✗ *to Bob and I*, ✗ *they invited my wife and I* — are **hypercorrections**: *and* does not change what a preposition or a verb demands, and sounding careful is not the same as being right.
- **Possessive slot, neither one.** ✗ *my husband and I's anniversary*, ✗ *a friend of my wife and I* — English has no way to hang a genitive ending on a coordinated pronoun, and the same deletion test exposes it, since ✗ *I's anniversary* is not English. The repairs are *my husband's and my anniversary*, *my wife's and mine*, or, far better, *our anniversary*.

One honest exception to the object rule, because the folk correction runs the other way: after *be*, ✓ **It's me** is standard American English at every level, and so are *That's her*, *It was them*. *It is I* is grammatical but stiff enough to sound like a joke, and anyone who corrects *It's me* is enforcing a Latin rule that English never had. The full subject and object paradigm is in [Personal pronouns](01-personal.md).

**Singular *they* takes *theirs*.** *Somebody left their phone, and it must be theirs* is standard modern American English, in speech and in edited prose; AP and Chicago both endorse it, and it is also the correct possessive for a person who uses *they*. The alternatives are [his or hers](#his-or-hers) (formal, clumsy when repeated), generic *his* (dated), and a plural rewrite (*All the guests brought their own*), which is usually the best of the three. For a person who uses a coined set instead of *they*, the independent possessives are catalogued in [Gender-neutral third-person possessives](#gender-neutral-third-person-possessives) — [aers](#aers), [cos](#cos), [eirs](#eirs), [faers](#faers), [hirs](#hirs), [nirs](#nirs), [pers](#pers), [thons](#thons), [vers](#vers), [xyrs](#xyrs), and [zirs](#zirs).

**Two cells of the paradigm are empty.** There is no usable standalone *its* (✗ *that collar is its*) and no standalone possessive for generic *one* (✗ *the choice is one's*). English fills both gaps the same way — with [one's own](#ones-own) (*a life of its own*, *a room of one's own*) or with a noun genitive (*that collar is the dog's*). Where a noun follows (*its own lease*, *one's own business*) the repair is a **determiner** construction, catalogued as [its](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its), [one's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#ones), and [own](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#own) in the determiner dictionary and cross-referenced, not duplicated, here. Bare *one's* gets no pronoun entry in this file for exactly that reason: it never stands alone.

**Weak forms, and the *h* that disappears.** The possessives carry stress more often than the personal pronouns do, because they usually hold the new information in the sentence — but they reduce all the same, and the *h*-forms drop the *h* outright whenever they are unstressed inside a phrase: *a friend of his* /ə frɛnd əv ɪz/, *a friend of hers* /ə frɛnd əv ɚz/, *a friend of theirs* /ə frɛnd əv ðɚz/, *a friend of yours* /ə frɛnd əv jɚz/, with *of* itself down to /əv/ or /ə/. *Mine*, *its*, *whose*, and *thine* have no weak form at all — they keep their vowel however fast the sentence goes. The personal pronouns these pair with reduce much further, and they are the forms a learner actually hears: *him* /ɪm/, *her* /ɚ/, *them* /əm/, *you* /jə/, *he* /i/, catalogued in [Personal pronouns](01-personal.md). Put the two together and *give him his* comes out /ɡɪv ɪm ɪz/, three words and not one *h* among them.

**Nonstandard forms, and where each one is catalogued.** This file carries the nonstandard and regional possessives, each labeled and each placed: [hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn](#hisn-hern-ourn-yourn-theirn) (Appalachian, Ozark, and older Southern and Midland speech), [mines](#mines) (African American English and some urban varieties), [y'all's](#yalls) (Southern and South Midland, and now heard far outside it), and [you guys'](#you-guys) (the Northern and general-American plural). The rest of the nonstandard pronoun stock belongs to other classes and is labeled there: the reflexives *hisself* and *theirselves* (Southern, South Midland, and African American English) in [Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself); the plural-*you* forms *youse* (New York, New Jersey, Philadelphia), *yinz* (Pittsburgh), *you'uns*, and the general *you guys* in [Personal pronouns](01-personal.md#youse); demonstrative *them* before a noun — *them apples*, widespread in vernacular speech with no single home region — in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them); and the double negative (*It isn't nobody's*, *ain't nobody's business*), ordinary in African American English and in Southern and working-class white vernaculars, where a second negative intensifies rather than cancels, in [Indefinite compound pronouns](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord). Every one of them is systematic in its own variety and marked in edited American prose; recognize them, label them, and don't write them.

**The reciprocal possessives live with the reciprocals.** *Each other's* and *one another's* nearly always have a noun after them (*they wore each other's coats*), and they are catalogued in [Reflexive and reciprocal pronouns](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-others) and in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#each-others). Note the apostrophe placement either way: ✓ *each other's names*, ✗ *each others' names* — *each other* is a fixed singular-shaped phrase.

**The absolute forms are old.** *Mine* and *thine* are the original absolute possessives, and the *-s* of *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs* was added later by analogy with the noun genitive. That history explains both directions of regularization still heard today: the Appalachian *-n* forms (*hisn*, *ourn*, *yourn*) extend the *mine* pattern, and [mines](#mines) extends the *yours* pattern. Both are logical, and both are nonstandard.
