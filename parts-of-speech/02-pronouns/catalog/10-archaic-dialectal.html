Archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns#

The pronouns no American writes in an email but every American runs into — thou and ye in scripture and Shakespeare, thereof and same in a contract, y'all and yinz and hisself in the speech of half the country.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Everything in this file sits outside the standard modern paradigm — behind it in time (thou, aught, whosoever, thereof), beside it in region and register (y'all, youse, yinz, hisself, theirselves), or off to one side of it entirely, deliberately invented to patch a hole the standard paradigm leaves open (thon, e). One thread runs through the whole group: English used to distinguish a singular you from a plural one, lost the distinction around 1600 when the polite plural swallowed the intimate singular, and has been patching the hole ever since. The archaic half of this file is the system before it collapsed; the dialectal half is a dozen independent American repairs to the same gap. The trap at the center is that thou was the intimate form and ye/you the formal one — the exact opposite of what nearly every English speaker assumes when they hear thou in a prayer.

A note on labels. Every entry below is marked, and the marks are not decoration. Archaic means no living American produces it except in quotation, pastiche, or ritual. Regional means real speakers use it as their ordinary form in a nameable place. Coined means somebody made it up on purpose and it never belonged to any older paradigm or any region — it is neither archaic nor an error, and the entries say who coined it and who uses it. Nonstandard means it will be marked wrong by an editor, a teacher, or a standardized test, however common it is in speech — and hisself, theirselves, and Me and him went are extremely common in speech. A learner needs to recognize all of them and produce almost none of them. The one exception is y'all, which is now so widespread and so useful that it is spreading well beyond the South into ordinary informal American English nationwide.

A note on what is not an error. Because so much of this file is marked, it is worth being equally plain about what is not. It's me is standard American English, in speech and in edited writing alike; It is I is not wrong either, but it is stiff enough now to read as a costume, and nobody should be corrected into it. Singular they is standard American English — recognized by Chicago, the AP stylebook, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster — both for an unknown or unspecified person (someone left their coat) and for a person who uses it; nothing in the theirselves or ourself entries below bears on that. Whom has not vanished, but it has retreated to one position: it survives mainly right after a preposition (to whom it may concern, for whom the bell tolls, the tenant with whom we spoke), and at the head of a clause or a question most careful American writers now use who. And the that / which split — that for restrictive clauses, which for nonrestrictive — is an American editing convention, not a rule of grammar: British editors ignore it, four centuries of good writers ignored it, and the part that actually carries meaning is the comma, not the word. See that and which. What antecedents each relative may take is a separate matter and is not a convention at all: who takes people, which takes things, that takes both, and whose takes both — the book whose cover is torn is correct English and has been for six hundred years, whatever you were told about of which. In this file, errors are marked ✗ and conventions are called conventions.

A note on pronunciation. Archaic pronouns are recited rather than spoken, so they keep full stressed vowels: thou /ðaʊ/, thee /ði/, ye /ji/ — no weak forms, because nobody uses them at conversational speed. The dialectal forms are the opposite. They are the most frequent words in their speakers' mouths and they reduce hard: y'all /jɔl/ flattens to /jl̩/ in y'all come back, you guys runs to /jəˈɡaɪz/, them is /ðəm/ → /əm/ even when it is doing demonstrative work. Where a weak form exists, it is given.

The standard pronouns that these forms compete with reduce just as hard, and five of those weak forms are worth having in your ear before you read any example in this file out loud — they are what the coordinated phrases in the last section actually sound like, and they are why me and him and between you and me are so easy to mishear:

The archaic second person: thou and ye#

Middle and Early Modern English had a full two-way second person, borrowed in structure from French tu/vous: thou for one person you were intimate with, close to, or socially above, and ye/you for several people, or for one person you owed deference to. Modern speakers get this backwards because the only place they meet thou is in the King James Bible and in hymns, where it addresses God — so it sounds elevated. It is not elevated. Addressing God as thou was the point: the KJV translators chose the intimate singular deliberately, exactly as a child addresses a parent. Using thou to a stranger or a superior was an insult, and Renaissance drama turns on it; Shakespeare's characters switch between thou and you to mark affection, contempt, and rank, sometimes inside a single speech.

Where an American actually meets these forms
the King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer, hymns and Christmas carols (O come, O come, Emmanuel… and ransom captive Israel), Shakespeare and Marlowe, the Declaration-era and Romantic poets, Quaker meetings and older Quaker families (in a distinctive reduced form — see thee), the Mormon practice of addressing God as thee/thou in prayer, Renaissance fairs, tattoo and monument inscriptions, fantasy novels, heavy-metal album titles, and jokey elevated register (What sayest thou?).

Contrast
thou and thee divide exactly as I/me and he/him do — thou is the subject, thee the object: Thou lovest me / I love thee. Against ye/you, the difference is not case but number and politeness: thou/thee to one intimate or inferior, ye/you to several people or to one superior. And thee is not a fancier you — it is a smaller, warmer, more familiar one.

The object form of the archaic singular you: the person spoken to, when that person is the object of a verb or a preposition.

  1. "How do I love thee? Let me count the ways." (Elizabeth Barrett Browning)
  2. "Get thee to a nunnery." (Hamlet)
  3. "The Lord bless thee, and keep thee." (Numbers 6:24, King James Version)
  4. I shall not tell thee twice, knave.
  5. Fare thee well — we sail at dawn.
  6. Friend, has thee signed the minute yet? (Quaker plain speech, with thee as subject and a third-person verb)

thine#

Pronunciation
/ðaɪn/ · THYNE (rhymes with mine)
Русский
твоё, твой — устар. самостоятельное притяжательное местоимение (ср. mine)
Type
possessive pronoun, archaic — this entry covers the standalone pronoun only; the determiner thine that stands before a vowel (thine eyes) is a separate entry in the determiner dictionary
Case
possessive, independent
Register
archaic, literary, religious
Person / number
second person singular only, invariant; it does not change for the number of the thing possessed (the kingdom is thine, these are thine).
Refers to
what belongs to the single person addressed. The possessor is always that one addressee; the thing possessed may be anything at all, count or mass, singular or plural, human or not.
Agreement
as a subject complement it takes no agreement of its own; as a subject, the verb agrees with what thine stands for — thine is the kingdom (singular), thine are the greater sins (plural).
Pattern
[noun phrase] is thine · thine is [noun phrase] · of thine (a friend of thine) · what is thine · [verb] thine (take thine and go) · [preposition] thine (with thine)
Contrast
thy and thine split the way my and mine do — with one extra wrinkle. Thy is the determiner before a consonant (thy name), thine the independent pronoun (it is thine), and thine is also the determiner before a vowel or h, exactly as older English wrote mine eyes and mine host. So thine covers two of the three jobs, and which one it is doing depends on whether a noun follows it. A noun follows → determiner, and that is a determiner entry, not a pronoun one: thine eyes, thine heart, and To thine own self be true are all covered in the determiner dictionary. No noun follows → the pronoun entry you are reading, and every example below is that. Modern yours replaces the pronoun, and never takes an apostrophe — ✗your's is as wrong as ✗thine's.

Stands alone for "the thing or things belonging to thee" — the archaic equivalent of yours.

  1. "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory." (the Lord's Prayer)
  2. A friend of thine waits at the gate. (after a preposition, the double genitive)
  3. My sword is thine, my lord.
  4. Take what is thine and go.
  5. Is this purse thine or the innkeeper's?
  6. What is mine is thine.
  7. The fault was thine, and thou knowest it.

second person singular only — one addressee, never more. Marked for intimacy or for the addressee's lower rank, not for reverence.

thou is the subject, thee the object — thou seest me, I see thee. Because the KJV addresses God as thou, the form now sounds solemn; historically, addressing a stranger as thou was rude enough to be actionable, and Shakespeare's characters use the switch as a weapon. Modern you descends from the polite plural, which is why the language keeps growing new plurals (y'all, youse, yinz) to fill the gap thou left.

The subject form of the archaic singular you: the one person being addressed, as the subject of a verb.

  1. "Thou shalt not steal." (Exodus 20:15, King James Version)
  2. "Thou art the man." (2 Samuel 12:7)
  3. "Thou art more lovely and more temperate." (Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 — thou to a beloved, the intimate use)
  4. What sayest thou to that, sir?
  5. Where art thou going with that lantern?
  6. Thou knowest well enough what I meant.

second person singular only. The plural is yourselves; the archaic singular has no plural of its own.

thyself is to thou/thee what yourself is to you. Do not use thyself for more than one person; that is yourselves.

The archaic singular reflexive: the addressee, when the addressee is both the subject and the object of the same clause. Also intensive, adding emphasis to thou.

  1. "Physician, heal thyself." (Luke 4:23, King James Version)
  2. Know thyself. (the Delphic maxim, in its standard English form)
  3. Thou hast said it thyself, and in front of witnesses.
  4. Trouble not thyself over so small a matter.
  5. Help thyself to the bread; there is plenty.
  6. Thou wilt only injure thyself by arguing further.

Archaic indefinites: aught, naught, and somewhat#

Three old indefinite pronouns — "anything," "nothing," and "something." All three have been driven out of ordinary use by their compound replacements (anything, nothing, something), and all three survive only in fixed phrases an American still hears: for aught I know, all for naught, somewhat of a. Two of them, aught and naught, also lead second lives as names for the digit zero, which is why the decade from 2000 to 2009 is the aughts. Four quantifying leftovers sit with them: divers, the older word for several; many another and many a one, which both say "a great many" while keeping a singular verb; and twain, the old form of two that survives in never the twain shall meet. Every one of them is a pronoun only when nothing follows it — put a noun after any of them and you have a determiner, which is a different dictionary.

Anything at all — the archaic ancestor of anything, now confined to a handful of set phrases.

2. Zero (numeral noun, not a pronoun). From a naught misheard as an aught: nineteen aught six for 1906, a thirty-aught-six, the aughts for the decade 2000–2009. Listed here only so the two are not confused; a numeral is not a pronoun.

  2. For aught I care, they can settle it in court.
  3. If aught should befall me, the letter explains everything.
  4. He would not say aught against his brother.
  5. Is there aught here worth carrying home?

Pronunciation
/ˌmɛni ə ˈwʌn/ · men-ee-uh-WUN · the main stress falls on one; many reduces to /mɛni/ and the article to /ə/, so at speed the whole phrase runs together as /mɛniəˈwʌn/ and the a disappears entirely
Русский
многие, не один — книжн., устар.; глагол при нём в ед. ч.
Type
indefinite pronoun phrase, archaic and literary — this entry covers the standalone pronoun only; many a in front of a singular noun (many a man, many a summer) is a determiner phrase and belongs to the determiner dictionary
Case
none — the same form serves as subject and object
Register
archaic and literary; ballads, hymns, sermons, and prose written for a formal cadence
Person / number
third person singular in form and in agreement, plural in sense — the same split that makes many a man was killed singular.
Refers to
a great many individual people, counted one at a time rather than gathered into a group; things are possible (many a one of these old clocks still runs) but people are the ordinary case. It never refers to a clause or a stretch of text.
Agreement
singular verb, always: many a one has tried, many a one was lost, ✗ many a one have tried. The singular is not a quirk but the point of the construction — it lets a writer say "a great many" one at a time, which is why the phrase belongs to elegy and sermon.
Pattern
many a one [singular verb] · [verb] many a one (object: she buried many a one) · many a one of [plural noun phrase] (many a one of them) · there is many a one [relative clause] (the existential, with the singular is) · many a one and many another
Contrast
its nearest neighbor is many another, and the two are not interchangeable: many another means "one more of the same kind" and needs the kind already named (the recipe calls for saffron; many another would do), while many a one is free-standing and simply means "a great many people." Both keep the singular verb that many a forces. Modern American English says a lot of people, plenty of them, or many; reaching for many a one in ordinary prose reads as pulpit costume. And keep the pronoun apart from the determiner: put a noun after many a and the dictionary line has been crossed.

A great many people, taken one at a time. The archaic indefinite that keeps a singular verb while meaning a crowd.

  1. Many a one has gone up that mountain and never come down again.
  2. Many a one of them would have given a year's wages for the chance.
  3. She had buried many a one she loved before that winter was out. (object of the verb)
  4. There is many a one in this town who remembers your father. (the existential, with the singular is)
  5. Many a one was lost that September whose name is on no stone.
  6. Modern English: A lot of people have gone up that mountain and never come down.

The -so and -soever forms#

Old English and Middle English built emphatic indefinite relatives by hanging -so and then -soever onto a wh- word: whoso, whomso, whatso, whosoever, whomsoever, whatsoever, whichsoever, whencesoever, wheresoever, whithersoever, whensoever, howsoever. They mean exactly what the modern -ever forms mean — whosoever is whoever, no more and no less — with the extra length doing the work of emphasis. An American meets them in the King James Bible, in hymns, in wills and deeds, and in the deliberately grand register of oaths and proclamations. The case trap in this section is the one that catches educated writers: the case of whosoever/whomsoever is fixed by the pronoun's job inside its own clause, never by the preposition sitting in front of it.

Verse contractions: 'tis, 'twas, and the -e'er forms#

English verse has always been allowed to squeeze a syllable out of a word to make a line scan, and two families of squeezed pronoun forms are common enough that an American meets them every December. The first fuses the pronoun it to the verb that follows — 'tis, 'twas, 'twere, 'twill, 'twould — running the two words together from the front instead of the back, which is the reverse of the modern habit that gives it's and it'll. The second knocks the v out of -ever, giving whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er, and the same elision produces the non-pronouns e'er, ne'er, and o'er that fill out a metrical foot in hymns.

Both families are archaic and metrical, not dialectal: nobody has ever said them at conversational speed in ordinary American English. They are read aloud, sung, and quoted. The apostrophe in each of them marks a letter that is genuinely gone, which is why these forms take one and the -n possessives (hisn, yourn) do not.

Frozen forms and formal survivals#

Ten leftovers that are not part of any living paradigm. A body is the generic pronoun that one and generic you pushed out, and it is still sitting inside anybody, everybody, nobody, and somebody. Methinks preserves a construction English abandoned six hundred years ago — a verb with a dative experiencer and no subject at all. Oneself is fully standard, formal but not archaic. The same, used as a pronoun, is the fossil that lawyers and shipping clerks kept alive after everyone else buried it. Around it stands the rest of the paperwork family — the aforesaid, the foregoing, the premises, and the undersigned — four noun phrases that have hardened into pro-forms and now do the work of it, that, the text above, and I. And such a one is the archaic way to say someone like that, while et seq. is the citation abbreviation that stands in for "and the sections that follow." Every one of them is a phrase rather than a word, and every one of them is a pronoun only when nothing follows it: the aforesaid vehicle is a determiner and lives in another dictionary.

The legal pronominals: hereof, thereof, whereof#

These are prepositions and pronouns welded into single words: thereof is "of it," herein is "in this document," whereof is "of which." Every American meets them, whether or not they notice — in a lease, an insurance policy, a mortgage, a warranty, a will, and in the Constitution. They are worth a section because they are perfectly regular once you see the pattern, and because the three prefixes are not interchangeable:

PrefixStands forPoints atExample
here-thisthe document you are holding — the contract, deed, or statute itselfthe terms set forth herein = in this agreement
there-it, thatthe noun phrase most recently named in the textthe roof and the shingles thereon = on that roof
where-which, whata relative antecedent, or a questionIn witness whereof = in witness of which

Combine each prefix with a preposition — of, in, to, on, under, by, after, with — and you can generate the whole family: hereof, herein, hereto, hereunder, hereby, hereinafter; thereof, therein, thereto, thereon, thereunder, thereafter, therewith; whereof, wherein, whereto, whereby, whereupon. Stress falls on the preposition in every one of them: there-OF, here-IN, where-OF, where-BY. The whole family is given below, because a reader meeting one of them needs to know which kind it is, and they are not all the same kind. Most of them genuinely stand in for a noun phrase — thereof is of it, wherewith is with which — and those are pronominal in the strict sense. A few point at an act rather than a thing (hereby, which performs the sentence it sits in), and a few point at a time (heretofore, thereupon, whereupon); those are adverbs wearing the family uniform, and each entry says so in its first line. Two members are ordinary formal American English rather than legalese and can be written without apology: whereby (by which) and thereby (by that means). The rarest members are in, because a reader who meets one has to be able to look it up: hereat and thereat, hereinbelow, thereinafter and thereinbefore, and the patent-drafting pair therebetween and therethrough. Thereafter and hereabouts are left out; they are adverbs of time and place with no pronoun inside them at all. So are the citation abbreviations supra and infra — "above" and "below" in a brief or a footnote (Smith, supra, at 415; see infra note 12). They look as though they belong with this family and they do not: they are the plain Latin adverbs, with no pronoun fused into them, and they never fill a noun-phrase slot the way thereof and wherewith do. For those, and for ibid., id., and et al., see the note under et seq..

The place pro-forms: hither, thither, whither#

The here- / there- / where- deixis of the last section runs through one more set of words, and this one is older and simpler than the legal compounds. English used to mark direction on its place words, three ways: a form for being somewhere, a form for going toward it, and a form for coming from it. The whole system fits in a box:

Position — in or atMotion toward — toMotion from
near the speakerherehitherhence
away from the speakertherethitherthence
which / what placewherewhitherwhence

Modern English collapsed the whole right-hand side into the left: we say come here, go there, where are you going, and let the verb carry the direction. Nothing was lost that a preposition cannot supply, which is why the six words died — but they died slowly and unevenly, and an American still meets every one of them. Hence is alive in a wholly different job, meaning "therefore." Thence is alive in surveying and in itineraries. Whence is alive in one psalm and in the phrase whence it came. Hither is alive inside come-hither, and hither and yon; thither inside hither and thither; whither inside a headline formula (Whither the euro?) and in the wedding reading from Ruth.

They are pro-adverbs, not pronouns in the strict sense — they stand in for a prepositional phrase (to that place) rather than for a noun phrase — and they are grouped here because the deixis, the register, and the wh- / th- alternation are the same as in the rest of the file. Each is archaic unless the entry says otherwise.

Dialectal second-person plurals#

When you swallowed thou, English was left with one word doing four jobs — singular and plural, subject and object — and no way to make clear that you are addressing the whole table rather than the person across from you. Every variety of American English has independently invented a repair, and the repairs are geographic enough to serve as a map: y'all in the South and in African American English, you guys almost everywhere else, youse and yous in the working-class Northeast, you'uns and y'uns in Appalachia and the Ohio Valley, and yinz in Pittsburgh alone. None of these is an error in the sense that hisself is an error; they are filling a real hole in the standard paradigm, which is exactly why every region built one. What they are is informal and regionally marked: they belong in speech and in writing that sounds like speech, and a job application is not the place for any of them. Y'all is the one that is genuinely moving upward and outward — it is now common in national informal writing as a gender-neutral alternative to you guys.

All of them take plural verbs: y'all are, youse were, yinz have. And all of them are invariant for case — the same form serves as subject and object (y'all should call me / I'll call y'all).

Dialectal interrogatives: who all and what all#

The same all that built y'all out of you also attaches to the question words, and for the same reason: it makes the question exhaustive. Who came? invites one name; Who all came? asks for the whole list, and a Southern speaker who gets one name back will ask the question again. Standard English has no single word for this and has to reach for who else, which people, or everyone who, which is why the construction is worth knowing even where it is not spoken. It is regional, not erroneous — Southern, South Midland, and Appalachian, and drifting into casual speech elsewhere — and like every regionalism in this file it belongs in speech rather than in a brief or a cover letter.

Invented pronouns: thon and the Spivak set#

English has no gender-neutral third-person singular that everybody agrees on, and for about a hundred and seventy years people have been proposing one. Dozens of sets have been coined — thon, e, ey, ze, xe, hir, ne, ve, per — and the ones in this section are here because an American is likelier to run into them than into the rest: thon because it spent sixty-six years in a major American dictionary, e because it was adopted by early online communities and is still in use, and heesh and hesh because they are the two blends that turn up in every published account of the attempts that failed. One entry here is not a coinage at all: ou is a genuine eighteenth-century English dialect pronoun that did this job by inheritance rather than by design, and it is included because it is the standing proof that the gap can be filled without anybody inventing anything.

Three factual notes frame them. First, the gap they were built to fill is real: English has no singular pronoun that is neutral as to gender and that everyone accepts, and writers have felt the lack since at least the 1850s. Second, the solution that actually spread is not on this list — it is singular they, which is a native form six hundred years old, is now recognized as standard by Chicago, AP, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster, and is what the overwhelming majority of American writers use both for an unknown person and for a person who asks for it. Third, the invented sets are nevertheless used: some people use them for themselves, some publications and online platforms support them, and a reader may meet them in fiction, in academic writing, and in email signatures. This section describes what the forms are and how they inflect. Whether to use any of them for a given person is settled the same way any other name is settled — by asking, and then by doing what you were told.

The coined entries below are marked coined rather than archaic or nonstandard, because neither label fits: they were deliberately made, they were never part of an older paradigm, and they are not errors in any dialect. None of them belongs to any traditional English paradigm and none is used by more than a very small number of speakers; each entry gives the forms, the inflection, and the history, and stops there. ou, the one inherited form in the group, is marked dialectal instead.

Other nonstandard forms#

The rest of the file covers forms that are not filling any gap in the paradigm — they are alternatives to a standard form that already exists, and using them will be noticed. Two of them, hisself and theirselves, are more logical than the standard forms they replace. The -n possessives (hisn, hern, ourn, theirn, yourn) are a whole rival paradigm, as regular in its own terms as the standard one, and mines finishes the -s paradigm that standard English left one word short. The doubled negative (I didn't do nothing) is the majority pattern across the world's Englishes and the minority pattern in this one. And two of them, the coordinated Me and him went and the hypercorrected between you and I, are opposite errors that come from the same anxiety about case. A handful are exceptions to the opening sentence and do fill a gap: the associative plural and them says "that person and their people" in three syllables, which standard English needs a whole phrase for, and them'uns, this'n and that'n, and t'other are the surviving singular and third-person members of the Appalachian -uns paradigm whose second-person member, you'uns, fills the biggest gap in the language. Three more are older than the standard forms they compete with rather than newer: hit is the Old English form of it with its h still attached, them that is the King James Bible's ordinary way of saying those who, and meself is nothing but the everyday weak pronunciation of myself written down. All of them are labeled honestly below, with the region named wherever there is one. Learn to recognize every one, and to produce none of them in writing.

Compare none, which is the one negative pronoun that takes either number — none of them is and none of them are are both standard, with the plural the ordinary default — while nobody and nothing are singular, always.
Pattern
[negated verb] + nothing/nobody (I didn't say nothing, he don't know nobody) · nobody/nothing + [negated verb] (nobody never called) · ain't nobody / ain't nothing + [predicate], with the auxiliary fronted (Ain't nobody home) · three or more markings in one clause (I ain't never told nobody nothing)
Contrast
Standard English marks the negative once, and it offers two ways to do it: negate the verb and use the nonassertive form (I didn't see anything), or leave the verb positive and let the pronoun carry the negation (I saw nothing). Choose one; using both is what gets marked. The schoolroom argument that "two negatives make a positive" is imported from logic and has never described English — I didn't see nothing has never once been understood as I saw something by any listener. The second negative is emphasis, and what it signals socially is which variety the speaker is using, which is exactly why it is worth recognizing, worth respecting in the varieties that own it, and worth keeping out of a cover letter. One lookalike is genuinely standard and does cancel: not uncommon, not unlike, not without merit, where a negative prefix under a negated verb yields a deliberately hedged positive. Another is not a double negative at all: I don't know nothing about it is concord, but I don't know anything about it is the standard equivalent, and I know nothing about it is its more formal twin.

Nothing; nobody — negated twice. The negative pronouns as they appear in negative concord, where the second negative reinforces the first instead of canceling it.

  2. He don't know nobody on that side of town. (nonstandard; standard doesn't know anybody)
  3. Nobody never said a word about the money. (nonstandard as subject; standard Nobody ever said)
  4. Ain't nobody home. (vernacular, with the auxiliary fronted; standard Nobody is home)
