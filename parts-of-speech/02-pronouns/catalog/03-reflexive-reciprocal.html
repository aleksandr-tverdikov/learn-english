Reflexive, intensive, and reciprocal pronouns#

The -self words and the two reciprocals — the pronouns that bend a sentence back onto its own subject, or pass the action around a group.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Every pronoun in this file needs something else in the sentence to lean on. A reflexive (she taught herself) is used when the object of a verb or preposition names the same person as the subject of that same clause; an intensive (I fixed it myself) is the identical word doing a different job — pure emphasis, deletable without changing the meaning. The reciprocals each other and one another need a plural or coordinated antecedent and distribute the action across it, so that they blamed each other and they blamed themselves describe two entirely different arguments. The single trap that runs through the whole file is the untriggered reflexive — please contact myself, Bob and myself attended — where a speaker reaches for an -self word as a politeness upgrade in a clause that has no I in it to license it, and where American style guides and editors mark it wrong.

The licensing test, applied to every entry here. Look inside the clause for the matching antecedent: myself needs an I, yourself needs a you, themselves needs a plural third person. No antecedent in the clause, no -self word — use the plain object pronoun (me, him, her, them) or the subject pronoun (I, he, she, they), whichever the slot calls for. Two consequences follow. First, no reflexive can be a subject: ✗Myself and Diane wrote the grant is Diane and I wrote the grant. Second, a preposition that merely locates something takes the plain pronoun, not the reflexive — she had the map beside her, he shut the door behind him, I have no cash on me — because those phrases place an object in space rather than naming a second participant. Beside herself is not a location; it means overcome with emotion.

Case: what the label means here, and the one place it goes wrong. The Case: line on every reflexive and intensive pronoun entry below reads reflexive, and what that means concretely is that the word fills an object slot — direct object (she cut herself), indirect object (I poured myself one), object of a preposition (he kept it to himself) — or stands as an appositive beside a noun already in the sentence (the mayor himself). There is no reflexive subject form, because there is no reflexive subject. The reciprocals each other and one another are labeled object for the same reason. When the slot wants an ordinary pronoun instead, the choice is between subject case (I, he, she, we, they) and object case (me, him, her, us, them), and case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause. That rule is broken in exactly one environment — a coordination, where the extra conjunct hides the job from the ear — and it is broken in both directions:

  • Subject slot takes the subject form. ✓She and I went to the closing. ✓My brother and he drove down together. The nonstandard version — ✗Me and him went, object forms doing a subject's job — is repaired as He and I went.
  • Object slot takes the object form. ✓He gave it to Bob and me. ✓They invited my wife and me. The hypercorrection ✗They invited my wife and I is the mirror image — an error manufactured by trying to avoid the first one.
  • After a preposition, the object form, always. ✓between you and me, ✓for Karen and me, ✓with him and her. ✗Between you and I is the most-repeated hypercorrection in American English. Between is a preposition, and no preposition in the language takes I.

The deletion test settles all of it in one second. Take the other person out of the sentence and read what is left. Bob and I signed leaves I signed, never me signed, so the coordination is Bob and I signed. He gave it to Bob and me leaves he gave it to me, never he gave it to I, so the coordination is to Bob and me. The one coordination the test cannot reach is between plus its two conjuncts, since removing either one leaves between with nothing to govern — there, fall back on the flat rule that a preposition takes the object form. Two footnotes. The order convention puts the first-person conjunct last (my wife and I, not I and my wife); that is courtesy, not grammar, and it changes no case. And myself is not an escape hatch from the choice: ✗Bob and myself signed, ✗please contact myself do not sidestep the case question, they add a second error on top of it — see myself.

Agreement, when the antecedent is an indefinite. A reflexive carries no verb agreement of its own; the verb agrees with the subject of its clause, and when that subject is an indefinite pronoun the two halves of the sentence can legitimately disagree in shape. The rows worth memorizing, because half of them are taught wrong:

  • everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody take a singular verb: Everyone is here, Somebody has left a coat, Nobody was hurt. Their reflexive is nevertheless normally themselves, or themself for one person: Everyone is expected to sign in themselves. Singular verb, plural-shaped reflexive — both halves standard, in the same sentence.
  • each takes a singular verb: Each of the applicants signs in. So do either and neither: Either is acceptable, Neither of them is ready. (Plural verbs after neither of them are common in speech; edited American prose keeps the singular.)
  • both, several, few, many, others take a plural verb, and their reflexive is themselves: Both were at fault, and both blamed themselves.
  • none takes either, and this is the row where the schoolroom rule is simply false. None of the crew was injured and None of the crew were injured are both standard; plural none is as old as English, and "none means not one, so it must be singular" is a nineteenth-century invention.
  • all, some, most, any agree with what they refer to: All of the water is gone but All of the guests have seated themselves; Most of the report is finished but Most of the pages are done.

The full entries for these words are in indefinite and quantifying pronouns; what matters here is which -self word they license, and the answer is almost always themselves or themself.

Antecedents inside relative clauses. A relative clause has a subject of its own, so it licenses a reflexive of its own: the woman who hurt herself, the machine that shut itself off, the account which balances itself monthly. Which relative word to use is a separate question with a short answer — who for people, which for things, that for both, whose for both people and things (the writer whose book sold itself, the statute whose wording contradicts itself) — and the full treatment, including how little of whom is left, is in relative pronouns.

Pronunciation, in two parts. These are among the very few pronouns that carry stress, and it always lands on -self / -selves, never on the first syllable: muh-SELF, er-SELF, thuhm-SELVZ, yer-SELVZ. The -self half therefore never reduces. The first half does, to the same weak forms the plain pronouns use when unstressed — him /ɪm/, her /ɚ/, them /əm/, you /jə/, and he /i/ (did 'e hurt 'imself? /dɪd i hɚt ɪmˈsɛlf/) — which gives himself /ɪmˈsɛlf/, herself /ɚˈsɛlf/, themselves /əmˈsɛlvz/, yourself /jəˈsɛlf/, and myself /məˈsɛlf/ in ordinary connected speech. The /h/ of him- and her- drops routinely inside a clause and is pronounced at the start of one. Hearing these reduced forms is most of what it takes to follow fast American speech.

Two smaller things worth noticing. The reflexives are the only corner of modern English where the second person still marks number — you covers both, but yourself and yourselves do not. And the possessive determiners my, your, his, her, its, our, their are not pronouns: they live in the determiner dictionary, while the standalone possessive pronouns mine, yours, hers, ours, theirs are in possessive pronouns. No determiner gets an entry in this file. The one entry below built on a determiner, one's self, is entered as what it is — the determiner one's in front of the ordinary noun self — because it is the form writers reach for when they want the pronoun oneself; the determiner itself lives at one's (determiner). Demonstrative them (them apples) is cross-referenced to the determiner dictionary rather than entered here, and the only genitives entered below — each other's and one another's — are inflected forms of the reciprocal pronouns, the one place this class inflects at all; for the determiner slot they fill in front of a noun, see each other's (determiner).

Where this file stands on disputed usage. Four things stated plainly, so that nothing below reads as a rule it is not. Singular they is standard American English, edited prose included — it is entered in Merriam-Webster, accepted by the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style, attested since the fourteenth century, and its reflexive is themself or themselves; neither is an error. It is not a concession to informality and not a workaround: it is the ordinary American form for one person of unknown, unstated, or nonbinary gender, and it appears as such in newspapers, court opinions, and university presses. It's me is standard, not a mistake to be repaired into It is I, which is defensible grammar and stilted English outside the telephone formula This is she (see me). Whom survives mainly after a preposition — to whom, for whom, several of whom — and elsewhere American English has largely handed the object slot to who; the agent who we hired is a register choice, not an error (see whom). That versus restrictive which is an editing convention, not grammar: the only hard rule in that neighborhood is that that cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause (see which). What this file does not soften: the untriggered myself, ✗between you and I, ✗Me and him went, and vernacular hisself / theirself / theirselves and the rare theyself are all still marked wrong in edited American English. Being honest about what has changed is not permission to pretend that nothing is an error. Neopronoun reflexives — aerself, coself, eirself, emself, faerself, hirself, nemself, perself, verself, xemself, zirself — get their own section at the end of the file, described rather than ruled on: they are the -self forms of pronoun sets used by a small number of speakers, most of them nonbinary, they take singular verb agreement where singular they takes plural, and the way to get one right is to use the set the person names.

Reflexive and intensive pronouns#

Ten forms, one paradigm. Each is built from a possessive or object base plus -self / -selves, and each does double duty: reflexive when it fills an object slot already occupied in meaning by the subject, intensive when it is an appositive tacked on for emphasis and could be deleted. The removal test separates them — delete the word, and if the sentence survives unchanged it was intensive. Two neighbors are entered alongside the ten: the coordinate phrase himself or herself, a reflexive assembled out of two of them, and one's self, which is not a pronoun at all but the noun self behind a possessive determiner — entered because it is what writers produce when they mean oneself.

Contrast
Three tests separate the noun phrase from the pronoun, and all three take one second. First, an adjective can slip in — one's true self, one's better self — and nothing can be inserted into oneself. Second, it pluralizes: one's earlier selves. Third, it can be a subject: one's self changes. When none of the three works, the word wanted is oneself: ✗one should behave one's self, ✗one owes it to one's self. The same split runs one person over between myself and the noun phrase my self, and it is why the emphatic my own self is a phrase rather than a pronoun.

Reciprocal pronouns#

Two forms and their possessives. A reciprocal takes a plural or coordinated antecedent and sends the action across the group rather than back into each member, which is the whole difference between they helped each other and they helped themselves. Neither form can be a subject — both are object-case forms — both require an antecedent in the same clause, and the traditional two-versus-more-than-two rule that is supposed to separate them has never matched what good writers do and is rejected by modern American usage guides.

Nonstandard, archaic, and disputed forms#

Eight forms a learner will meet and should be able to recognize. Three of them — hisself, theirself, theirselves — are vernacular formations that are, ironically, more regular than the standard words they replace: English builds myself, yourself, and ourselves from possessives, so his + self and their + selves are exactly what the pattern predicts, and standard himself and themselves, built from the object forms, are the exceptions. All three are nonstandard and do not belong in school, business, or published writing. A fourth, theyself, is nonstandard for a different reason: it is built on the subject form they, a base English uses for no other reflexive, and it is a recent and rare formation rather than an inherited dialect one. The other two are different cases and are labeled differently below: ourself is legitimate in the narrow royal and editorial uses and nonstandard everywhere else, and thyself is archaic rather than wrong — the right word four hundred years ago, a costume today. Two more are phrases rather than single words: his own self and my own self, possessive determiner plus the emphasizer own plus the noun self, the vernacular way of leaning on an intensive, and the source of the solid dialect spelling hisownself.

Three neighbors, labeled. The vernacular reflexives keep company with three other forms a learner will hear constantly and should be able to place. Demonstrative them — ✗ them apples, ✗ them boys — is nonstandard for those, and is widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech; note the line, though, because them after a verb or preposition (I ate them) is the ordinary object pronoun and completely standard. Demonstrative them is a determiner, not a pronoun, so its entry is in the determiner dictionary rather than here. Plural youse — nonstandard, and regional: New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration — is a personal pronoun and lives at youse, next to y'all (Southern), you'uns and yinz (Appalachian and Pittsburgh), and you guys (general, informal rather than nonstandard); every one of them takes yourselves. The double negative — he didn't hurt hisself none, they never told nobody — is nonstandard in edited American English and is marked wrong in school and business writing, but it is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernaculars, and it was ordinary English in Chaucer and Shakespeare before eighteenth-century grammarians ruled it out on a false analogy with arithmetic. The standard repair is one negative per clause: he didn't hurt himself, they never told anybody. One archaic neighbor while we are here: English once used plain object pronouns reflexively — I laid me down, he sat him down — a construction now confined to poetry, hymns, and set phrases.

Neopronoun reflexives#

Eleven -self forms belonging to ten pronoun sets outside the inherited paradigm — ten object-base or single-base forms plus eirself, the possessive-base alternative inside the Spivak set. Neopronouns are third-person singular sets — ze/hir, ze/zir, xe/xem/xyr, e/em/eir (the Spivak set), ae/aer, ne/nem/nir, per/pers, co/cos, ve/ver/vis, fae/faer — used by a small number of English speakers, most of them nonbinary people, to refer to themselves; among speakers who use a gender-neutral pronoun at all, singular they is by a wide margin the most common choice. Each set builds its reflexive the way the rest of English does, by adding -self to the object or the possessive base — hir + self, xem + self — and each takes the singular -self, never -selves, because each names one person. Some sets are cut down from ordinary English words rather than coined outright — per from person, co from the prefix meaning together, fae from the noun for a fairy — and the noun-derived ones are often grouped as nounself pronouns.

The one systematic difference from singular they is the verb. A neopronoun subject takes a singular verb — ze is here, xe was early, per has arrived — while singular they keeps the plural verb however many people it names (they are here). Three practical points beyond that. The sets are not interchangeable: someone who uses xe/xem is not covered by ze/hir, and reaching for the wrong set is the same order of mistake as using the wrong name. General American dictionaries enter few of these forms, and the major style guides do not rule them out — AP and Chicago both tell writers to use the pronouns a person uses and to explain an unfamiliar form once for readers who will not recognize it. And spellings and pronunciations vary between speakers: the ones given below are the most frequently cited, and a person's own pronunciation of their own pronoun settles the question.
