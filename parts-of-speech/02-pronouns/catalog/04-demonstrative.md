# Demonstrative pronouns

> The pointing pronouns — the words that stand in for a noun and at the same time say where it is: near the speaker or away from the speaker, in the room, in time, or in the conversation itself.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

English runs a two-way demonstrative system — near *this/these* against far *that/those* — crossed with number, and apart from the reflexives (*yourself* → *yourselves*) these are the only pronouns whose plural is the same word reshaped rather than a different word: *this* → *these* and *that* → *those*, against *I* → *we* and *he* → *they*. Case, the difficulty that runs through the rest of the pronoun class, does not exist here: *this*, *that*, *these*, and *those* have no subject or object forms, and one shape does every job. What sets the class apart is what it is allowed to point at. A demonstrative pronoun can stand not only for a thing but for a whole clause, a whole paragraph, or something the speaker has not said yet — *They cancelled the trip. That surprised me.* and *Listen to this:* — which no other pronoun except *it* does as freely. That power is also the trap: a demonstrative whose antecedent is an entire idea rather than a noun is the commonest source of vagueness in American academic and business prose, and the repair is always the same, which is to hang a noun on it (*That decision surprised me*). The boundary this file sits on is the second trap — every word here is also a determiner, and only the standalone use is a pronoun.

**A note on case.** Demonstratives are the one pronoun class with no case at all. *Those* is *those* as a subject (*Those are mine*), as an object (*I'll take those*), and after a preposition (*with those*), so the *I/me*, *he/him*, *who/whom* choices never come up. Case enters this file at two points, and at neither one is it the demonstrative that inflects. The first is the personal pronoun after *be* in the telephone formula [this is he / this is she](#this-is-he--this-is-she), where the traditional subject-case rule and ordinary American speech disagree, and where *It's me* is standard English rather than an error. The second is the nonstandard [them](#them) that replaces *those* (*Them are the ones*), where the object form of *they* has been pushed into a subject slot — the same shape of mistake as ✗ *Me and him went*, and nonstandard for the same reason. The coordination problems the subject/object rule creates belong to the [personal pronouns](01-personal.md), and they run in both directions. **The object form is required after a preposition** — ✓ *between you and **me***, ✓ *He gave it to Bob and **me*** (both *me*s are objects of *to* and *between*) — **and in a plain direct object**: ✓ *They invited my wife and **me***. That makes ✗ *between you and I* and ✗ *They invited my wife and I* **hypercorrections**: a subject form dropped into an object slot by a speaker trying to sound careful. ✗ *Me and him went* is the opposite error, an object form standing in a subject slot; the standard sentence is ✓ *He and I went*, and ✓ *She and **I** went*. One test settles every one of them — **delete the other person and listen**: *He gave it to ~~Bob and~~ **me*** ✓ (not ✗ *to I*), *They invited ~~my wife and~~ **me*** ✓ (not ✗ *invited I*), *~~She and~~ **I** went* ✓ (not ✗ *Me went*). See also [*It is I* vs. *It's me*](../README.md#subject-complement-case-it-is-i-vs-its-me).

**A note on stress and weak forms.** Nearly every pronoun in English reduces, and hearing the weak form is a learner's real problem: *him* /ɪm/ (*tell 'im*), *her* /ɚ/ (*give 'er the keys*), *them* /ðəm/ and, after a consonant, /əm/ (*take 'em*), *you* /jə/ (*did ya see it*), *he* /i/ (*where'd 'e go*). Demonstratives are the exception. They are pointing words, they carry the stress in their clause, and they keep their full vowels — /ðɪs/, /ðæt/, /ðiz/, /ðoʊz/ — even in fast speech. The reductions that belong in this file are on the words around them: *the* in *the same* is the ordinary weak /ðə/; *such* drops to /sətʃ/; *of* in *that of* drops to /əv/ and the /t/ in front of it flaps, [ˈðæɾəv]; and the dialect *them* of *them's the breaks* reduces to /ðəm/ or /əm/ precisely because it is the object pronoun doing a demonstrative's job.

**A note on the three *that*s.** Three different words are spelled *that*, and telling them apart is worth more than any other single fact in this file. The demonstrative pronoun is stressed, keeps its full vowel /ðæt/, and accepts *this* in its place: *That surprised me* → *This surprised me*. The relative pronoun (*the car that we sold*) and the conjunction (*I know that she left*) both reduce to /ðət/ and can usually be dropped outright — *the car we sold*, *I know she left* — which the demonstrative never can (✗ *surprised me*). Two tests, then: swap in *this*, and try deleting. The demonstrative is the only one of the three that takes *this*, and the only one that cannot be dropped. The deletion test has a limit worth knowing: a relative *that* can go only when it is the **object** of its own clause, so the subject relative in *the car that broke down* has to stay.

**A note on pointing at people.** A demonstrative pronoun refers to a person only while identifying or introducing one — *This is my sister*, *Who's that?*, *That's Dan in the blue jacket*, and on the telephone *This is she.* Once the person is established, English switches to a personal pronoun — *he*, *she*, or, for one person of unknown or unstated gender, the singular *they* that is now standard American English, in edited prose as much as in speech, and is accepted by the major American style guides, the AP Stylebook, Chicago, MLA, and APA among them, though AP and Chicago still prefer a recast where one comes easily: ✗ *That went home early* → *He went home early*, and *Somebody left a coat. **They** must be coming back.* (see [singular *they*](../README.md#singular-they)). The single place a demonstrative names people freely is in front of a relative clause, where *those who* means "the people who" — and there the antecedent rules of the relative pronouns take over: ***who*** **for people**, ***which*** **for things**, ***that*** **for both**, ***whose*** **for both as well** (*those **whose** names are called*, *a house **whose** roof leaks*), English never having built a separate possessive for things.

## The near set: *this* and *these*

The proximal pair: what is near the speaker in space, current in time, or still open in the conversation. Only number separates them, and the line is hard — *this* is singular, *these* is plural, and there is no third option.

### these

**Pronunciation:** /ðiz/ &middot; *THEEZ* &middot; no weak form — the vowel stays long even in fast speech, because the demonstrative carries the stress in its clause
**Русский:** эти — указательное местоимение мн. числа о близком; ставится только с мн. числом глагола
**Type:** demonstrative pronoun (plural, proximal) &middot; **Case:** none — one form serves as subject, object, and object of a preposition &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person plural; no gender distinction, no case forms
**Refers to:** more than one thing near the speaker — near in the room, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the conversation currently open. It points at objects freely; it points at people only in the introducing frame *These are my parents*, never as an ordinary subject for people already known (✗ *These are exhausted* → *They are exhausted*). Unlike singular *this*, it cannot take a single clause as its antecedent — one clause counts as one thing, so [this](#this) or [that](#that) handles it — though it can gather up two or more statements at once (*They cut the budget and froze hiring. **These** are the reasons we can't hire.*).
**Agreement:** plural verb, always — *These **are** the ones. These **were** on sale. These **don't** fit.* Never ✗ *these is*. If what you are pointing at is singular or uncountable, the pronoun changes rather than the verb: *This **is** the last of it*.
**Pattern:** *these* + [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *these* (*I'll take these*) &middot; [preposition] *these* (*with these*, *out of these*) &middot; *these are* [plural noun phrase] (identifying) &middot; *these* … *those* (contrastive pair) &middot; *these* + reduced relative (*these left over from Friday*)
**Contrast:** Two axes cross. **Number** — *these* is the plural of *this* and takes plural countable referents only. **Distance** — *these* is near (in space, in the current stretch of time, or in what is still live in the talk), where [those](#those) is away, past, or finished. **Pronoun against determiner** — the pronoun is the whole noun phrase (*these are taken*, *I'll take these*), the determiner leans on a noun ([these seats](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#these) *are taken*). Two smaller traps: the partitive idiom is *those of us / those of you* whatever the distance, never ✗ *these of us*; and *these ones*, ordinary in speech and in Canadian and British usage, is struck out by most American copy editors — write *these*, or *these two*, or *the red ones*.

Points at more than one thing near the speaker — in the room, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the discussion currently in hand.

1. *These* are the files you asked for.
2. Are *these* yours, or did somebody leave them behind?
3. I'll take *these* and leave the rest.
4. *These* were selling for twice as much last month.
5. *These* don't fit anymore.
6. *These* are my parents, Ray and Dolores.
7. Everything in the box is cracked except *these*.

### this

**Pronunciation:** /ðɪs/ &middot; *THIS* &middot; keeps its full vowel as a pronoun; the casual /ðəs/ heard in *this morning* belongs to the unstressed determiner, and a standalone *this* is normally the stressed word in its clause
**Русский:** это, этот — указывает на близкое, а также на целую высказанную или предстоящую мысль
**Type:** demonstrative pronoun (singular, proximal) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for every position &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular; no gender distinction, no case forms
**Refers to:** one thing near the speaker, or a mass of something (*This is the last of the coffee*); a stretch of time containing now; **a whole preceding clause or idea** (*They cut the budget again. This is why we can't hire.*); and, pointing forward, something the speaker is about to say or show (*Listen to this:*). With people it is limited to introducing and identifying — *This is my sister*, and on the phone *This is Karen* — never for a person already established (✗ *This is exhausted* → *She is exhausted*).
**Agreement:** singular verb, always — *This **is** mine. This **was** expensive. This **doesn't** work.* The verb agrees with *this* and with nothing downstream of it: *This **is** the reason our costs went up*, *This **is** what the neighbors complained about*. With an amount the singular holds even though the complement is plural in form — *This **is** forty dollars' worth*, *This **is** three hours of work*. But when the referent itself is plural and countable, the pronoun changes rather than the verb: *These **are** my keys*, not ✗ *This is my keys*.
**Pattern:** *this* + [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *this* (*Read this*) &middot; [preposition] *this* (*after this*, *because of this*) &middot; *this is* [noun phrase] (identifying or introducing) &middot; *this* pointing forward, followed by a colon and the thing itself (*It comes down to this:*) &middot; *this is* + [personal pronoun] on the telephone (*This is he*) &middot; *this* … *that* (contrastive pair)
**Contrast:** **Against *that*.** Proximity in space, but in discourse something subtler: *this* keeps a topic open and *that* closes it. Pointing back at the same fact, *That settles it* means the matter is done, while *This changes everything* means we now have to deal with it. Pointing forward, only *this* works — ✓ *Listen to this:*, ✗ *Listen to that:* about something not yet said. **Against *these*.** Number, and the boundary is absolute. **Pronoun against determiner.** The pronoun stands alone (*this is late*); the determiner leans on a noun ([this report](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#this) *is late*). **The writing trap.** A *this* whose antecedent is an entire clause is not ungrammatical — it is everywhere in speech and in good journalism — but in formal prose it makes the reader guess, and the standard repair is to attach a summary noun: not *This means we're behind*, but *This delay means we're behind*.

Points at a single thing — or a mass, a moment, or an idea — that is near the speaker: near in the room, near in time, or near in the flow of the conversation.

**1. In space.** The thing near me, or nearer than the alternative: *This is the one I meant, not that.*

**2. In time.** The stretch of time containing now, when it is the subject of comment: *This is the busiest week of the year.*

**3. Pointing back at a whole clause.** *They cut the budget again. This is why we can't hire.* Standard, and the source of the vagueness problem above.

**4. Pointing forward.** *Listen to this:* / *It comes down to this:* — the demonstrative announces something the hearer has not heard yet. *That* cannot do this.

**5. Identifying and introducing people.** Face to face, *This is my sister, Nadia*; on the telephone, *This is Karen* or, answering for oneself, *This is she* — see [this is he / this is she](#this-is-he--this-is-she).

1. *This* is the last of the coffee.
2. Take *this* and put it in the mail.
3. They cut the budget again. *This* is why we can't hire.
4. Listen to *this*: they want the whole report by Friday.
5. Is *this* yours?
6. *This* is my sister, Nadia.
7. *This* doesn't feel right to me.
8. I've had about enough of *this*.

## The far set: *that* and *those*

The distal pair, split by number on exactly the same line. Beyond distance they take on two jobs the near pair does not: closing off what has already been said, and standing in for a noun rather than repeating it.

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt/ &middot; *THAT* &middot; never reduced — the demonstrative pronoun always keeps the full vowel /ðæt/; the weak /ðət/ belongs to the two other words spelled *that*, the relative pronoun and the conjunction, and that difference is the fastest way to tell them apart by ear
**Русский:** то, тот — указывает на далёкое или прошедшее, а также на всё только что сказанное
**Type:** demonstrative pronoun (singular, distal) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for every position &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular; no gender distinction, no case forms
**Refers to:** one thing away from the speaker, or a mass, or a finished stretch of time; **a whole preceding clause, sentence, or run of discourse** (*They cancelled the trip. That surprised me.*), which is its most characteristic job and one no other pronoun but *it* does as cleanly; and, in [that of](#that-of), a noun already named. With people it is confined to identifying — *Who's that? — That's my brother* — and once the person is established English switches to *he*, *she*, or *they*: ✗ *That went home early*.
**Agreement:** singular verb, always — *That **is** enough. That **was** a mistake. That **doesn't** help.* Plural referents take [those](#those). A singular complement, and an amount, both keep the singular: *That **is** the reason the numbers moved*, *That **is** twenty dollars*. A plural complement is the one soft spot: *That was my parents at the door* is ordinary speech, but edited prose changes the pronoun along with the verb — *Those **were** my parents*.
**Pattern:** *that* + [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *that* (*Don't say that*) &middot; [preposition] *that* (*after that*, *besides that*, *with that*) &middot; *that's* [noun phrase] (identifying) &middot; *that of* [noun phrase] (formal substitution) &middot; *that which* [clause] (formal) &middot; frozen idioms: *at that*, *that is*, *that said*, *that's that*
**Contrast:** Three separate contrasts share this headword. **Against *this*:** distance and discourse status — *that* is away, past, finished, or the hearer's; *this* is near, current, mine, or still to come. Responding to something another person has just said, Americans overwhelmingly reach for *that* (*That's a good point*, *That's not what I heard*). **Against *those*:** number. **Against the other two *that*s:** the demonstrative pronoun is stressed /ðæt/ and can be replaced by *this*; the relative (*the car that we sold*) and the conjunction (*I know that she left*) reduce to /ðət/ and can usually be dropped. Apply both tests — swap in *this*, then try deleting — and the demonstrative is the only one that accepts *this* and the only one that cannot go. The deletion test has a limit: a relative *that* can be dropped only when it is the object of its own clause, so *the car that broke down* keeps it. And the same writing caution as *this*: pointing at a whole clause is standard, but in formal prose give it a summary noun (*That decision surprised everyone*).

Points at a single thing, a mass, a finished time, or a whole idea that is away from the speaker — across the room, back in the past, or already dealt with in the conversation.

**1. In space.** The thing farther off, or nearer the hearer: *Hand me that, would you?* A gesture or a glance usually goes with it.

**2. In time.** A stretch of time cut off from now: *That was the summer we lived in Denver.*

**3. Pointing back at a whole clause or idea.** *They cancelled the trip a week out. That surprised everyone.* The demonstrative treats what was said as finished and comments on it.

**4. Identifying.** *Who's that at the door?* / *That's my brother in the blue jacket.* This is the one frame in which a demonstrative pronoun may refer to a person.

**5. Substituting for a repeated noun.** In formal writing, *that of* replaces a singular or uncountable noun already named: *a climate like that of Spain*. See [that of](#that-of).

1. *That* is the best offer you're going to get.
2. They cancelled the trip a week out. *That* surprised everyone.
3. Who's *that* at the door?
4. Don't ever say *that* to your mother again.
5. After *that*, nobody brought it up again.
6. *That* wasn't what I meant.
7. Is *that* all you're taking?
8. *That's* my brother in the blue jacket.

### those

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊz/ &middot; *THOHZ* &middot; no weak form — full diphthong, and normally the stressed word in its clause
**Русский:** те — указательное местоимение мн. числа о далёком; также в оборотах those who, those of
**Type:** demonstrative pronoun (plural, distal) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for every position &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person plural; no gender distinction, no case forms
**Refers to:** more than one thing away from the speaker; plural items already mentioned; and, in the frames *those who*, *those that/which*, and *those of*, a class of people or things defined by whatever follows. Like *these*, it cannot take a single clause as its antecedent — that job belongs to singular *this* and *that* — though it can sum up two or more statements at once (*The plant closed and the road washed out. **Those** are the two facts that matter.*). It reaches people in two frames: identifying, exactly as *these* does (*Those are my cousins, over by the window*), and in front of a relative clause, where *those who apply early* means *the people who apply early*. For a person already established it is out: ✗ *Those are always late* → *They are always late*.
**Agreement:** plural verb — *Those **are** mine. Those **were** the days. Those **don't** match.* Inside the *those who* frame both verbs stay plural: *Those who **arrive** late **are** seated at intermission.* Two traps follow from that. After *those of us*, the relative clause agrees with *us*, first person plural: *those of us who **are** on call*, not ✗ *who is on call*. And *one of those … who* takes a plural verb, because the relative clause describes the plural noun: *He's one of those people who **never answer** email* — the singular *answers* is common in speech and is corrected in edited prose.
**Pattern:** *those* + [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *those* &middot; [preposition] *those* &middot; *those who* [clause] &middot; *those that / those which* [clause] &middot; *those of* [noun phrase] &middot; *those of us / you / them who* [clause] &middot; *those* + reduced relative or participle (*those left behind*, *those reported last year*, *those responsible*)
**Contrast:** **Against *these*:** distance. **Against *that*:** number. **Pronoun against determiner** — the determiner sits on a noun ([those people](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#those)), the pronoun replaces it (*those are the ones*). **Against a bare comparison** — when a plural noun is being compared, the substitution is obligatory in formal writing: ✗ *Our returns are higher than our competitors* (returns against people) → *than those of our competitors*, or in plain American English *than our competitors'*. **Against nonstandard [them](#them):** *Those are the ones* is standard, *Them are the ones* is dialect. And *those ones*, like *these ones*, is speech-only in American English; edited prose writes *those* or *the ones*.

Points at more than one thing away from the speaker, or picks out a class of people or things that the following clause or phrase then defines.

**1. In space and in plural pointing.** *Those are the files you requested.* The plural counterpart of pointing *that*.

**2. "The ones" — substituting for a repeated plural noun.** Followed by a participle or a phrase: *results consistent with those reported last year*. Formal, and it saves repeating the noun.

**3. "Those who" — the people who.** Names a group by what is true of them: *those who registered early*. See [those who](#those-who).

**4. "Those of us" and "those of you".** A partitive that includes the speaker or the hearer: *those of us who commute*. The idiom takes *those* no matter how near the group is — never ✗ *these of us*.

**5. Nostalgic and idiomatic.** *Those were the days.* Fixed, always plural, always past.

1. *Those* are the files you requested.
2. I'd rather have *those* than these.
3. The results are consistent with *those* reported last year.
4. *Those* who registered early get priority seating.
5. *Those* of us who commute know the 8:10 is never on time.
6. Are *those* still under warranty?
7. *Those* weren't the instructions we were given.
8. *Those* were the days.

## The demonstrative with the prop-word *one*: *this one*, *these ones*

Two entries for the construction that lets a demonstrative pick one item out of a set without naming the noun again: the demonstrative plus *one* or *ones*, which stands in for the noun and carries the stress. The singular pair is standard everywhere in English. The plural pair is the one place in this file where ordinary American speech and American editing disagree flatly, and where Canadian and British usage side with the speech.

### these ones / those ones

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðiz ˈwʌnz/, /ˌðoʊz ˈwʌnz/ &middot; *theez-WUNZ, thohz-WUNZ* &middot; no weak form — *ones* takes the stress and keeps its full vowel, and the /z/ of *these* runs straight into the /w/, so the phrase is one unit, [ˌðiˈzwʌnz]
**Русский:** эти, те (предметы) — разг.; в американской печати вычёркивают, пишут these / those
**Type:** demonstrative + the prop-word *ones* — a plural noun phrase with the noun left out &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** ordinary in American speech; **struck out of American edited prose**; unremarkable in Canadian and British usage, spoken and written
**Person / number:** third person plural
**Refers to:** more than one **countable** thing already named or in view, picked out by nearness (*these ones*) or by distance (*those ones*). The prop-word needs something countable behind it: there is no mass-noun version (✗ *these ones of coffee*), and no singular ✗ *this ones*. It points at objects; for people the identifying frame takes bare *these* and *those* (*Those are my cousins*), and *those ones* about people sounds dismissive.
**Agreement:** plural verb — *These ones **are** cracked. Those ones **were** on sale.* Never ✗ *these ones is*.
**Pattern:** *these ones* / *those ones* as subject or object &middot; [preposition] *these ones* &middot; *the ones* + relative clause (the standard substitute — *the ones we ordered*) &middot; *these two*, *those three* (the numeral substitute) &middot; *the red ones*, *the big ones* (the adjective substitute)
**Contrast:** **Against bare *these* and *those*** — the *ones* adds no meaning an American editor will pay for: *These are cracked* says everything *these ones are cracked* says, and this is one of the handful of things American copy editors delete on sight. It is worth being clear about what kind of objection that is: it is not a grammatical error and it is not dialect — it is normal in American speech, normal in Canada, normal in Britain, and simply not printed in American edited prose. **Against *the ones*** — *the ones* is standard everywhere and is the form to write when a relative clause follows (*the ones that came in Tuesday*), which bare *those* handles less comfortably. **Against [this one / that one](#this-one--that-one)** — the singular pair draws no objection at all, in any variety, which is the clearest evidence that the complaint is about the redundant plural and not about the prop-word. **A note on what is a determiner here** — inside the phrase, *these* is a determiner sitting on the noun-like *ones*; it is the whole phrase, not the demonstrative alone, that does the pronoun's work, which is why it is catalogued here and *these seats* is catalogued with the determiners.
**Variants:** *them ones* (**nonstandard** — rural, Southern, and Appalachian American speech, and northern British) stacks the dialect *them* on top of the contested *ones*: *Them ones ain't ripe yet.*

Picks out more than one countable thing by pointing at it — the plural of *this one*, ordinary in speech and edited out of American prose.

1. *These ones* are cracked; put them in the other box. (speech — edited prose: *These are cracked*)
2. Not the big ones — *these ones*, right here in front. (speech, contrastive)
3. Do you want *these ones* or the plain ones?
4. *Those ones* were on sale last week, and now they aren't. (speech)
5. Canadian and British writing takes it without comment: *Those ones* were sold at the fair.
6. American edited prose: *Those* were sold at the fair. / *The ones on the left* were sold at the fair.
7. Nonstandard: *Them ones* ain't ripe yet.

### this one / that one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ˈwʌn/, /ˌðæt ˈwʌn/ &middot; *this-WUN, that-WUN* &middot; no weak form — *one* carries the stress; in *that one* the /t/ is normally unreleased or a glottal stop before the /w/, [ˌðæʔˈwʌn], which is why the two words sound welded together
**Русский:** этот, вот этот; тот — один конкретный предмет, выбранный из нескольких
**Type:** demonstrative + the prop-word *one* — a singular noun phrase with the noun left out &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — standard in speech and in writing, in every variety of English
**Person / number:** third person singular
**Refers to:** **one countable thing** chosen out of a set already named or in view: *Which folder? — This one.* The prop-word needs a countable antecedent; with a mass noun English drops it and uses the bare demonstrative (✓ *This is the last of the coffee*, ✗ *this one of the coffee*). With people it is limited to the identifying frame the bare demonstrative allows, and it is blunter than the bare form — *That one, in the blue jacket* points a finger, where *he*, *she*, or a name is the ordinary choice.
**Agreement:** singular verb — *This one **fits**. That one **was** broken when it came.* The plural is bare *these* and *those* in edited prose, or [these ones / those ones](#these-ones--those-ones) in speech.
**Pattern:** *this one* / *that one* as subject or object &middot; [preposition] *this one* (*in this one*, *with that one*) &middot; *which one* (the interrogative partner) &middot; *this one here*, *that one over there* (the standard way to add the pointing that the dialect [this here](#this-here) wedges inside the phrase) &middot; *this one* … *that one* (contrastive pair) &middot; *the one that / the one we* + relative clause
**Contrast:** **Against bare *this* and *that*** — the prop-word narrows the reference to a single countable item out of several. *I'll take this* can mean the pile, the deal, or the coffee; *I'll take this one* can only mean one item chosen from a set, which is why a store counter runs on *this one*. **Against [these ones / those ones](#these-ones--those-ones)** — the singular is standard in American edited prose and the plural is not. **Against *the one*** — *the one* wants a defining clause behind it (*the one you ordered*), where *this one* does the defining by pointing. **Against the determiner** — *this box*, *that folder* keep the noun; *one* is precisely what stands in for it when the noun is dropped ([this (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#this)).

Picks out one countable thing from a set by pointing at it, with *one* standing in for the noun.

1. Which folder do you want? — *This one*.
2. *That one* has a chip in the rim; take *this one* instead.
3. I've read *this one* twice and never got through *that one*.
4. Is it *this one* here, or *that one* on the end of the shelf?
5. *That one* was my grandmother's — the small blue one on the left.
6. Every apartment we looked at was too small, but *this one* has a second bedroom.
7. With a mass noun the prop-word drops out: ✓ *This* is the last of the coffee, ✗ *this one* of the coffee.

**See also:** [these ones / those ones](#these-ones--those-ones), [this](#this), [that](#that), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one), [ones](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#ones), [which](06-interrogative.md#which), [this (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#this)

## Demonstratives that carry an *of*-phrase or a relative clause

Five formal constructions in which the demonstrative stops pointing and starts substituting. In each one *that* or *those* heads a noun phrase and something following it — an *of*-phrase or a relative clause — does the describing. This is the register where a repeated noun would be clumsy and a possessive would be too plain.

### that of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðæt əv/ &middot; *THAT-uv* &middot; the /t/ flaps between vowels and *of* reduces — the phrase runs to [ˈðæɾəv], and in rapid speech to [ˈðæɾə]
**Русский:** у, чей — заменяет повторяемое сущ. ед. числа в сравнении: than that of X = чем у X
**Type:** demonstrative pro-form (substitution for a repeated noun) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; a written construction, rare in speech
**Person / number:** third person singular; stands only for singular and uncountable nouns — the plural is [those of](#those-of)
**Refers to:** a **singular or uncountable noun already named in the same sentence**, repeated without repeating the word: *The climate of Portland is much like **that** of northern Spain* (= the climate of northern Spain). It stands for things and abstractions. For a person, ordinary American English uses the possessive instead — *than John's*, not *than that of John*.
**Agreement:** the phrase heads a singular noun phrase, so any verb it governs is singular: *That of the control group **was** unchanged.* It does not affect the verb of the clause it sits in, which agrees with that clause's own subject.
**Pattern:** [noun] *of* X … *than that of* Y &middot; *like that of* [noun phrase] &middot; *similar to that of* &middot; *comparable to that of* &middot; *that of the* [noun] &middot; *a fraction of that of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** The whole purpose of *that of* is to keep a comparison between like things. Leave it out and the sentence compares the wrong pair: ✗ *Her salary is higher than a surgeon* sets a salary against a person; ✓ *higher than that of a surgeon*, or, in plain American English, *higher than a surgeon's*. **Number must match** what is being replaced — singular *that of*, plural [those of](#those-of): ✗ *Our returns beat that of our competitors* → *those of*. **Do not stack it on a possessive** — ✗ *that of Chicago's*. And know the register — outside academic and technical prose the possessive or a simple repetition reads better, and *that of* in a short email sounds starched.

Stands in for a singular or uncountable noun just named, so that a comparison can be made without repeating the noun.

1. The climate of Portland is much like *that of* northern Spain.
2. Her handwriting is neater than *that of* anyone else in the office.
3. The company's revenue now exceeds *that of* its former parent.
4. The tone of the second letter is nothing like *that of* the first.
5. A nonsmoker's risk is a small fraction of *that of* a pack-a-day smoker.
6. Its texture is closer to *that of* clay than to *that of* sand.

**See also:** [those of](#those-of), [that](#that), [that which](#that-which), [the same](#the-same), [the former](#the-former), [the latter](#the-latter)

### that which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *that-WICH* &middot; both words keep full vowels; the stress falls on *which*
**Русский:** то, что — книжн., высокое; в современном языке обычно what
**Type:** fused relative built on a demonstrative pronoun — it contains its own antecedent &middot; **Case:** none — the phrase takes whatever role its slot in the main clause assigns &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, aphoristic; faintly archaic
**Person / number:** third person singular
**Refers to:** things and abstractions only, never people — *that which* means "the thing that." For people the parallel form is [those who](#those-who), or the dead *he who* of older prose. It takes no head noun and cannot be followed by one (✗ *that which book*); what follows is a relative clause.
**Agreement:** singular verb inside and out — *That which **remains is** ours.* The clause is singular even when its content is plural in sense: *That which was promised **has** still not arrived.*
**Pattern:** *that which* [relative clause] as subject &middot; [verb] *that which* [relative clause] &middot; [preposition] *that which* [relative clause] &middot; *all that which* (rare, emphatic)
**Contrast:** **Modern American English says *what*.** *What doesn't kill us* and *that which does not kill us* are the same sentence in two registers, and the second is a stylistic choice, not a grammatical requirement. Use it for deliberate weight — proverbs, ceremonial prose, scripture-flavored writing — and not twice in a paragraph. **Against [those who](#those-who):** singular and non-human against plural and human. **Against the plain relative *that*:** the relative attaches to a noun that is already there (*the thing that remains*), while *that which* supplies the noun itself.

Means "the thing that" — a formal written equivalent of *what*, chosen when a writer wants the weight of the demonstrative behind an abstraction.

1. *That which* does not kill us makes us stronger.
2. We are remembered for *that which* we leave behind.
3. He spent thirty years chasing *that which* he could not name.
4. The audit separates *that which* can be verified from *that which* is merely asserted.
5. Keep *that which* is good, and let the rest go.
6. *That which* was promised in March has still not arrived.

**See also:** [those which](#those-which), [those who](#those-who), [that](#that), [which](05-relative.md#which), [that which (determiner file)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#that-which)

### those of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðoʊz əv/ &middot; *THOHZ-uv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/, and before a consonant often to /ə/ — *those of us* runs to [ˈðoʊzəvəs]
**Русский:** те, что у…; те из нас, из вас — замена мн. сущ. в сравнении и выделение части группы
**Type:** demonstrative pro-form (substitution for a repeated plural noun); also the partitive *those of us / you / them* &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Person / number:** third person plural — except that *those of us* and *those of you* pull first- and second-person agreement into the relative clause that follows
**Refers to:** two things. **(1)** A plural noun already named, repeated without repeating the word: *Our margins are thinner than **those** of our competitors.* **(2)** With a personal pronoun, a subset of a group that includes the speaker or the hearer: *those of us who commute*, *those of you who registered online*.
**Agreement:** plural throughout — *Those of the samples that were contaminated **have** been discarded.* In the partitive, the relative clause agrees with the pronoun after *of*: *those of us who **are** on call*, *those of you who **have** questions* — not ✗ *those of us who is on call*. The main verb is plural too: *Those of us who work weekends **get** comp time.*
**Pattern:** [plural noun] … *than those of* [noun phrase] &middot; *like those of* / *similar to those of* &middot; *those of us / you / them* + relative clause &middot; *those of the* [plural noun] *that/who* [clause] &middot; [preposition] *those of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **Number** — plural *those of* against singular [that of](#that-of): ✗ *scores higher than that of the other schools* → *those of the other schools*. **Form** — the partitive idiom is *those of us / those of you*, fixed, whatever the distance involved; ✗ *these of us* and ✗ *we of us* are not English. **Register** — in ordinary American prose the possessive usually wins — *our competitors' margins* rather than *the margins of our competitors* or *those of our competitors*. And do not double the genitive: ✗ *those of our competitors'*.

Stands in for a plural noun just named — or, with a personal pronoun, singles out the part of a group that a following clause describes.

1. Our shipping costs are far below *those of* the national chains.
2. Her symptoms were identical to *those of* the earlier patients.
3. *Those of* you who registered online can skip the front desk.
4. *Those of* us who were there remember it differently.
5. The regulations are stricter than *those of* any neighboring state.
6. Only *those of* the applicants who submitted transcripts were considered.

**See also:** [that of](#that-of), [those](#those), [those who](#those-who), [those which](#those-which)

### those which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðoʊz ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thohz-WICH* &middot; full vowels in both words; stress on *which*
**Русский:** те, которые — о предметах; книжн. вариант обычного those that
**Type:** fused relative built on a demonstrative pronoun — the plural of *that which* &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal (*those which*); neutral (*those that*)
**Person / number:** third person plural
**Refers to:** things — a subset of a set already under discussion, defined by the relative clause that follows. It takes no head noun (✗ *those which boxes*). *Those that* stretches to people in careful prose, but [those who](#those-who) is the normal choice there.
**Agreement:** plural in both clauses — *Those which **remain are** damaged.* *Only those that **pass** inspection **get** a sticker.*
**Pattern:** *those which* [relative clause] &middot; *those that* [relative clause] &middot; *only those that* [clause] &middot; [preposition] *those which* [clause] &middot; *those* + zero relative when the relative is the **object** of its clause (*those we lost*)
**Contrast:** **Which against that.** *Those that* is the everyday American form; *those which* sounds legal or literary, and in a restrictive clause an American copy editor will change *which* to *that*. That preference is a house convention rather than a rule of grammar — it will still be enforced on you in most American publishing. **Dropping the relative.** You may omit it only when it is the object of its clause: ✓ *those we lost*, ✗ *those remain are damaged*. **People against things** — *those who* for people and *those which* for things, with *those that* available for either — though with people *those who* is the normal choice.

Means "the ones that" — picks out a subset of things already in play and defines it by the clause that follows.

1. Of the twelve proposals, we funded only *those which* had community backing.
2. Discard *those that* are cracked or warped.
3. The rule applies only to *those which* were signed before June.
4. I kept *those that* still fit and gave away the rest.
5. *Those that* pass inspection get a sticker on the base.
6. We are still missing *those* we ordered in April. (zero relative)

**See also:** [that which](#that-which), [those who](#those-who), [those](#those), [which](05-relative.md#which), [those which (determiner file)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#those-which)

### those who

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðoʊz ˈhu/ &middot; *thohz-HOO* &middot; *who* keeps its full vowel here; the /h/ survives because the word is stressed
**Русский:** те, кто — только о людях и только с придаточным: those who wait «кто ждёт»
**Type:** fused relative built on a demonstrative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none for *those*; the relative inside takes its own case (*those who apply*, formal *those whom we hired*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Person / number:** third person plural — but *those of us who* takes first person plural agreement and *those of you who* second person
**Refers to:** people only, always plural, always with a relative clause behind it. Bare *those* names people only when it is identifying somebody in view (*Those are my cousins*); it cannot otherwise stand for "people," which is what the relative clause is here for. *Who* is the form for people, *which* for things, *that* for either, and *whose* for either as well (*those **whose** names are called*, *a house **whose** roof leaks*), so *those who* is the human member of the set and [those which / those that](#those-which) the non-human one. It names a group by what is true of its members rather than by who they are, which is why it lives in rules, proverbs, and public announcements.
**Agreement:** plural verbs on both sides of the clause boundary — *Those who **wait are** rewarded.* Three traps. After *those of us who*, agreement is first person plural: *those of us who **are** on call*, ✗ *who is*. After *those of you who*, second person: *those of you who **have** questions*. And *one of those … who* takes a plural verb, because the clause describes the plural noun: *He's one of those people who **never answer** email* — the singular *answers* is common in speech and gets corrected in edited prose.
**Pattern:** *those who* [clause] + plural verb &middot; [preposition] *those who* [clause] &middot; *those of us / you / them who* [clause] &middot; *those whom* [clause] (formal, relative as object) &middot; *those* + reduced relative (*those left behind*, *those responsible*, *those present*)
**Contrast:** **People against things** — *those who* against *those which / those that*; *who* takes people, *which* takes things, *that* takes either, and *whose* takes either too (*those whose applications are complete*, *the one building whose roof leaks*) — English never developed a separate possessive for things, so *whose* covers both. ***Who* against *whom*:** the relative takes its own case inside the clause — *those who apply* (subject), *those whom we hired* (object) — but *whom* now survives mainly in the slot right after a preposition (*those **to whom** we wrote*, *those **with whom** she worked*). Everywhere else *those who we hired* is what Americans say and print, and only formal editing puts the *m* back. **Register** — *those who apply early* is a step up from *people who apply early* and two steps up from *anybody who applies early* — the demonstrative buys formality and loses warmth. **Against the singular archaisms** — *he who*, *she who*, *they who* are dead outside proverbs, and *he who* also reads as generic masculine, which is why *those who* has replaced it in American edited prose. **Against the determiner** — [those people](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#those) keeps the noun; *those who* replaces it.

Means "the people who" — names a group not by identity but by whatever the following clause says is true of them.

1. *Those who* arrive after seven will be seated at intermission.
2. The fund exists for *those who* need it most.
3. *Those who* can, do; *those who* can't, teach.
4. *Those of us who* commute are used to the delays.
5. To *those who* wrote in with corrections: thank you.
6. He's one of *those* people *who* never answer email.
7. *Those who* disagreed said nothing at the time.

**See also:** [those](#those), [those which](#those-which), [those of](#those-of), [that which](#that-which), [who](05-relative.md#who), [those who (determiner file)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#those-who)

## Demonstratives that point at the page: *the above*, *the former*, *the latter*

Seven phrases that do a demonstrative's work inside a document rather than inside a room. *This* and *that* point at what is near or far, current or finished; these point at what is earlier or later **on the page**, at the party or the passage already named, and at which of several things was named first or last. All seven are creatures of writing — say *the above* aloud and it has nothing to attach to — and every one of them asks the reader to stop and go back, which is why American style guides tell you to repeat the noun the moment the reader would have to hunt for it. Three of the seven — *the aforementioned*, *the foregoing*, and *the last-mentioned* — belong to legal and academic drafting and nowhere else, and an American meets them in a lease, a policy, a court filing, or a footnote.

### the above

**Pronunciation:** /ði əˈbʌv/ &middot; *thee-uh-BUV* &middot; *the* takes the /ði/ form used before a vowel, the one place its weak form changes shape instead of disappearing; the first syllable of *above* is the weak /ə/ and the stress falls on the second, and the phrase itself has no reduced form because it is barely ever said aloud
**Русский:** вышеизложенное, вышесказанное — юр., канц.; отсылка к месту выше в этом же тексте
**Type:** text-pointing (anaphoric) pro-form — *the* plus an adverb of place, used as a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **formal** — legal, contractual, academic, and technical writing, and the multiple-choice test; almost never spoken except in the fixed *all of the above*
**Person / number:** third person; number comes from what it stands for — singular for one passage or statement, plural for a list of items
**Refers to:** **the material already on the page** — a passage, a paragraph, a table, a list, or a statement printed earlier in the same document. The metaphor is the page read from the top down, and it is the whole of the word's meaning here: *above* means "earlier in this document," not "higher up" and not "over there." It reaches people only through something already written (*the above are hereby notified*, legal drafting, where the reader has to go up the page to learn who they are).
**Agreement:** the verb follows the referent, not the phrase — *The above **is** a summary of our position* (one passage) against *The above **are** the only exceptions the policy allows* (a list of items). Neither is more correct than the other; what decides is whether the writer is pointing at one block of text or at several things in it.
**Pattern:** *the above* as subject &middot; [preposition] *the above* (*nothing in the above*) &middot; [verb] *the above* (*initial the above*, *see the above*) &middot; *all of the above* / *none of the above* (the multiple-choice idiom, and the one use that reached ordinary speech) &middot; *as noted above*, *as stated above* (the adverb, far commoner than the pronoun) &middot; *the above-mentioned*, *the above-named* (determiners in practice — they lean on a noun)
**Contrast:** **Against [the following](#the-following)** — direction on the page, and nothing else: *the above* points back, *the following* points forward. **Against *this* and *that*** — the demonstratives point at ideas in a conversation and are the ordinary choice everywhere outside a document; *the above* in an email reads as a memo written by a lawyer, and *this* or *that* almost always does the job better. **Against the determiner** — *the above table*, *the above-mentioned clause*, and *the aforementioned exception* all sit on a noun and are catalogued in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned); only the bare standalone *the above* is a pronoun. **The one living idiom** — *all of the above* escaped the multiple-choice test and is now ordinary American speech for "every one of them" (*What went wrong? — All of the above*), and *none of the above* has gone the same way, onto ballots and into political commentary.
**Variants:** [the foregoing](#the-foregoing) (formal, chiefly legal and academic, and pointing at a whole preceding stretch rather than a spot: *The foregoing is subject to change without notice*); [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned) and *the aforesaid* (legal, and far commoner as determiners — *the aforesaid tenant*); *the above-mentioned* and *the above-named* (determiners); British legal writing adds *the undermentioned*, which an American meets only in imported documents. Every one of them is heavier than *this*, and none belongs in ordinary prose.

Points back at what is already written on the page — a passage, a table, or a list — rather than at anything in the world.

1. *The above* is a summary of the position we took in March. (memo)
2. Please initial *the above* and return one copy to the office. (form)
3. Nothing in *the above* should be read as an admission of liability. (contract)
4. *The above* are the only exceptions the policy allows. (plural referent)
5. Which of these caused the outage? — All of *the above*. (the spoken idiom)
6. *The foregoing* applies to renewals as well as to new accounts. (variant)
7. In ordinary prose: *That* is a summary of the position we took in March.

**See also:** [the following](#the-following), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned), [the former](#the-former), [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), [that](#that), [this](#this), [the same](#the-same), [aforementioned (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned), [aforesaid (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforesaid)

### the aforementioned

**Pronunciation:** /ði əˌfɔrˈmɛnʃənd/ &middot; *thee-uh-for-MEN-shund* &middot; *the* takes the /ði/ form used before a vowel; the main stress falls on *-men-* and a secondary one on *-fore-*, and the ending is the reduced /ʃənd/. Five syllables with nothing to shorten, which is part of why the phrase never left the page
**Русский:** вышеупомянутый — юр.; о лице или предмете, уже названном ранее в документе
**Type:** text-pointing (anaphoric) pro-form — a participial adjective standing as a whole noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **formal and legal** — contracts, leases, insurance policies, statutes, briefs, patent claims, and the occasional academic footnote; in speech it is either a lawyer at work or a joke
**Person / number:** third person; number comes from the referent — singular for one item or one party, plural for several (*The aforementioned **are** jointly liable*)
**Refers to:** **a person, a party, an item, or a document already named earlier in the same document**, pointed at without being named a second time. That is what separates it from its neighbors: [the foregoing](#the-foregoing) sweeps up a whole preceding stretch of text and [the above](#the-above) points at a place on the page, while *the aforementioned* points at one identified thing wherever it was introduced. It is also the one page-pointing phrase in this file that reaches people freely, because the parties to a contract are exactly what a draftsman keeps having to re-name.
**Agreement:** the verb follows the referent, not the phrase — *The aforementioned **is** attached as Exhibit B* (one document) against *The aforementioned **are** hereby notified* (several parties). Where an American actually meets it: signing a lease, reading an insurance rider, or being served with a court filing.
**Pattern:** *the aforementioned* as subject (*the aforementioned shall be liable*) &middot; [verb] *the aforementioned* &middot; [preposition] *the aforementioned* (*in the aforementioned*, *for the aforementioned*) &middot; *none of the aforementioned* &middot; *the aforementioned* + [noun] (*the aforementioned clause*, *the aforementioned defendant*) — the determiner use, and far the commoner one, catalogued in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned)
**Contrast:** **Against [the above](#the-above)** — a location on the page against a thing already introduced. **Against [the foregoing](#the-foregoing)** — one named item against the whole passage in front of it. **Against [the former](#the-former), [the latter](#the-latter), and [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned)** — those count position in a list, and this one does not count at all; it simply says "the one I already named." **The determiner is the real word** — nearly every occurrence in the wild has the noun present, and the bare pronoun is confined to drafting. **Register warning** — outside a legal document it reads as parody, and the American plain-English drafting movement has spent fifty years cutting it out of contracts; in ordinary prose write *this*, *that*, or the noun itself.
**Variants:** *the aforesaid* (the commonest legal sibling, and the one that still turns up bare — *the aforesaid shall be void*); *the aforenamed* and *the above-named*, used chiefly of parties; *the said* (*the said tenant*), a determiner and the most heavily marked legalism of the set; [the undersigned](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-undersigned), the drafting phrase for whoever signs, which behaves the same way and is treated with the [archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns](10-archaic-dialectal.md).

Points back at a person, a party, or a document already named earlier in the same document — the draftsman's way of not writing the name twice.

1. *The aforementioned* shall have thirty days from the date of service in which to respond. (court filing)
2. Liability for damage to *the aforementioned* rests with the lessee. (lease)
3. *The aforementioned* are jointly and severally liable under Section 4. (plural referent — several parties)
4. Nothing in this rider limits the coverage described for *the aforementioned*. (insurance)
5. *The aforesaid* is void if the property changes hands before closing. (variant, and the commoner bare form)
6. Determiner use, and the ordinary one: *the aforementioned* clause was struck at closing.
7. In plain English: *That* clause was struck at closing.

**See also:** [the above](#the-above), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [the following](#the-following), [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), [that](#that), [the aforesaid, the aforementioned (as legal style)](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-aforesaid-the-aforementioned), [the undersigned](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-undersigned), [aforementioned (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned), [aforesaid (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforesaid)

### the following

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ &middot; *thuh-FOL-oh-ing* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/ before the consonant; *following* keeps its first-syllable stress, and in running speech the *-ing* reduces to /ɪn/ and the middle vowel weakens to /ə/ with a linking /w/ — [ˈfɑləwɪn]
**Русский:** нижеследующее — то, что идёт дальше в тексте, обычно сразу после двоеточия
**Type:** text-pointing (cataphoric) pro-form — the forward-pointing partner of [the above](#the-above) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral — documents, instructions, agendas, announcements, and rosters; ordinary in business writing and rare in speech
**Person / number:** third person; number comes from what is about to be given — one passage or a list
**Refers to:** **what comes next in the document**: the list, the passage, the names, or the terms about to be set out, usually right after a colon. Like *the above* it lives on the page and dies in speech. It is the one page-pointing phrase that reaches people freely, because the list after the colon is so often a list of names — *The following have been promoted, effective Monday:*
**Agreement:** the verb takes the number of what actually follows — *The following **is** the text of the resolution* (one thing) against *The following **are** eligible to vote* (several). Both are right; the mistake is picking one out of habit and then contradicting it with the list. Note that *The following **is** a list of the eligible voters* is singular for a good reason: what follows is one list.
**Pattern:** *The following* + [verb] + colon &middot; [verb] *the following* (*consider the following*, *complete the following*) &middot; [preposition] *the following* &middot; *as follows* (frozen: *The terms are as follows:*) &middot; *the below* (business email — see Variants)
**Contrast:** **Against [the above](#the-above)** — direction on the page. **Against *this*** — pointing forward at something not yet said is exactly what *this* does in speech (*Listen to this:*), and *the following* is what a document does instead; the two are the same gesture in two registers. ***As follows* never inflects** — it is an old impersonal construction, frozen singular however many items come after it: ✓ *The rules are as follows*, ✗ *as follow*. **Against the determiner** — *the following morning*, *the following items* lean on a noun and are catalogued with the determiners under [subsequent, following](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#subsequent-following); only the standalone use is a pronoun.
**Variants:** *the below* (*Please review the below and confirm*) is corporate email English. It is spreading fast in American business writing and is still marked wrong by editors, on the grammatical ground that *below* had no noun use to begin with — write *the following*, *the text below*, or plain *this*. The British legal *the undermentioned* covers the same ground and is rare in the United States.

Points forward at what comes next in the document — the list, the names, or the terms about to be given.

1. *The following* are eligible to vote in the September election:
2. *The following* is the text of the resolution as amended.
3. Please read *the following* carefully before you sign anything.
4. Complete *the following* and return it with your deposit.
5. *The following* have been promoted, effective Monday: Ruiz, Delgado, and Park.
6. The payment terms are *as follows*: thirty days net, two percent for ten. (frozen — never ✗ *as follow*)
7. Business email: Please review *the below* and confirm. (edited prose: *the following*, or *this*)

**See also:** [the above](#the-above), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned), [the latter](#the-latter), [this](#this), [so](#so), [subsequent, following (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#subsequent-following)

### the foregoing

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈfɔrˌgoʊɪŋ/ &middot; *thuh-FOR-goh-ing* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/ before the consonant, and the compound takes its main stress on *fore-*, which is the whole difference between it and the verb *forgoing* /fɔrˈgoʊɪŋ/; nothing in the phrase reduces, because in the one register that uses it nothing is said quickly
**Русский:** вышеизложенное — книжн., юр.; всё сказанное выше целиком, а не отдельный пункт
**Type:** text-pointing (anaphoric) pro-form — a participial adjective standing as a whole noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **formal** — contracts, statutes, board resolutions, briefs, and academic prose; effectively never spoken
**Person / number:** third person; number comes from the referent — usually singular, because what it points at is one block of text, but plural when the block is a list
**Refers to:** **the whole of what has just been written** — the preceding sentence, paragraph, section, or run of recitals, taken as one lump rather than pinned to a spot. That sweep is the difference between it and its neighbors: [the above](#the-above) names a place on the page, [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned) names one item already introduced, and *the foregoing* takes in everything up to here. It reaches people only through the text, and only in drafting (*none of the foregoing shall be deemed employees*).
**Agreement:** the verb follows what the writer is pointing at — *The foregoing **is** a summary and does not constitute legal advice* (one passage) against *The foregoing **are** the only warranties given* (a list of items). Neither is more correct than the other; the mistake is choosing one and then contradicting it later in the same sentence.
**Pattern:** *The foregoing* + [verb] &middot; *notwithstanding the foregoing* (much the commonest frame in American contract drafting) &middot; *nothing in the foregoing* &middot; *none of the foregoing* &middot; *subject to the foregoing* &middot; *the foregoing paragraph*, *the foregoing provisions* (the determiner use, which needs a noun)
**Contrast:** **Against [the above](#the-above)** — the direction is the same and the scope is not: *the above* is a location, *the foregoing* is everything preceding. **Against [the following](#the-following)** — backward against forward. **Against [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned)** — a whole passage against a single named party or document. **Against *forgoing*** — one letter and one stressed syllable apart, and a completely different word: *the foregoing* means "what came before," while *forgoing* means "giving up" (*forgoing the bonus*). No American spell-checker catches the swap, and it is a standing embarrassment in contract drafts. **Register** — *notwithstanding the foregoing* is a genuine term of art and has survived every plain-English campaign aimed at it, but bare *the foregoing* in an email is a lawyer's tic; write *this*, *the above*, or the name of the thing.
**Variants:** *the preceding* (*the preceding applies to renewals as well*) is the plainer academic sibling and carries much less register cost; [the premises](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-premises), in the old legal sense of "the matters just recited," covers the same ground in a contract's recitals (*in consideration of the premises*) and nowhere else; British drafting adds *the aforegoing*, which an American meets only in imported documents.

Points back at the whole of what has just been written — a paragraph, a section, or a run of recitals taken as one block.

1. *The foregoing* is subject to change without notice. (contract)
2. Notwithstanding *the foregoing*, the tenant may terminate on sixty days' written notice. (the standard drafting frame)
3. Nothing in *the foregoing* creates a partnership between the parties.
4. *The foregoing* are the only warranties the manufacturer gives. (plural referent — a list)
5. None of *the foregoing* shall be deemed employees of the company. (people, reached through the text)
6. *The foregoing* summarizes the argument of Chapters 2 through 4. (academic)
7. Not this word: *forgoing* the bonus meant a smaller tax bill. (= giving up)

**See also:** [the above](#the-above), [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned), [the following](#the-following), [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), [that](#that), [the foregoing (as legal style)](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-foregoing), [the premises](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-premises), [aforementioned (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned)

### the former

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈfɔrmɚ/ &middot; *thuh-FOR-mer* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/; *former* takes the stress and keeps the r-colored /ɚ/ of General American, and has no weak form of its own
**Русский:** первый (из двух) — первое из двух только что названных; ровно двух, не больше
**Type:** text-pointing pro-form of order — the first of exactly two things just named &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; a written device, uncommon in speech
**Person / number:** third person; it takes the number of the noun it stands for — *The former **is** cheaper*, *The former **were** imported*
**Refers to:** **the first of exactly two** things named just before it, counted in the order they were named and not by distance, recency, or importance. Two is a hard limit: with three items the pair has nothing to hang on, and the repair is to name the thing (*oak is cheapest*) or to write *the first*. It stands for things, ideas, places, and — in careful writing — people, though repeating the person's name is friendlier to the reader.
**Agreement:** singular or plural according to the referent — *Of the two proposals, the former **is** cheaper*; *Of the two crews, the former **have** worked together before* is possible, though American English normally treats a collective as singular (*the former **has** worked together before*).
**Pattern:** *the former* … *the latter* (the pair, always in that order) &middot; *of the two, the former* &middot; *the former* + [verb] &middot; [preposition] *the former* (*in the former*, *for the former*) &middot; *the former of the two* &middot; *the former … the latter … * with a semicolon between the halves
**Contrast:** **Against [the latter](#the-latter)** — order of mention, nothing else. **Against *this* and *that*** — in older prose *that* sometimes marks the first-mentioned and *this* the second, on the logic that the first is further back in the text; almost no modern American reader parses that, and *former/latter* says it outright. **Against the adjective *former*** — *her former husband*, *the former president*, *the former Soviet Union* mean "previous," a completely different word that sits on a noun and belongs with the determiners ([former, latter](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#former-latter)). **The readability trap** — the pair forces the reader back up the sentence to count, and Chicago and Garner both advise repeating the noun whenever the antecedents are more than a line away, or whenever the two items are not plainly parallel. **Two only** — ✗ *We looked at oak, maple, and cherry; the former is cheapest.*
**Variants:** *the first* is the plain American alternative and carries no register cost (*the first is cheaper*); *the one … the other* does the same work when the point is contrast rather than counting.

Stands for the first of exactly two things just named, in the order they were named.

1. We can fly or drive; *the former* is faster, but the latter is cheaper.
2. She has a law degree and an MBA; *the former* has been the more useful of the two.
3. Of the two designs, *the former* came in under budget.
4. Both the sedan and the wagon are on the lot, and I'd take *the former*.
5. The report blames the weather and the contractor. *The former* is nobody's fault.
6. Not this word: her *former* husband, the *former* president. (adjective, = previous)
7. Plain American: We can fly or drive, and **flying** is faster.

**See also:** [the latter](#the-latter), [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), [the above](#the-above), [the following](#the-following), [that of](#that-of), [former, latter (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#former-latter)

### the last-mentioned

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlæst ˌmɛnʃənd/ &middot; *thuh-LAST-men-shund* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/; the compound takes its main stress on *last* and a secondary one on *-men-*, the ordinary American pattern for a hyphenated compound, and neither half has a weak form
**Русский:** последний из упомянутых — юр., книжн.; при перечне любой длины, в отличие от the latter
**Type:** text-pointing pro-form of order — the item named most recently, in a list of **any** length &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal — legal drafting, academic and technical prose, and reference writing; not spoken
**Person / number:** third person; it takes the number of what it stands for — *The last-mentioned **is** the cheapest*, *The last-mentioned **were** withdrawn*
**Refers to:** **whichever item in a preceding list was named last**, counted by order of mention and by nothing else — not by distance, recency in time, or importance. Its whole reason to exist is the two-item limit on [the latter](#the-latter): with three or more items *the latter* is an error, and this is the phrase that takes over. It stands for things, statutes, cases, and materials, and in legal and biographical writing for people as well (*the last-mentioned was called as a witness*).
**Agreement:** singular or plural according to the referent — *Of the three statutes, the last-mentioned **was** repealed in 1998*; *Of the four groups surveyed, the last-mentioned **were** the least likely to respond.* The phrase itself never inflects: there is no ✗ *the last-mentioneds* and no comparative.
**Pattern:** *of the three / four / five …, the last-mentioned* &middot; *the last-mentioned* + [verb] &middot; [preposition] *the last-mentioned* &middot; *the first-mentioned* … *the last-mentioned* (the pair, for a list of three or more) &middot; *the last-mentioned* + [noun] (*the last-mentioned case*) — the determiner use &middot; *the last-named*, *the first-named* (the forms preferred when the items are people)
**Contrast:** **Against [the latter](#the-latter)** — the count, and nothing else. *The latter* means the second of exactly two and is simply wrong with three; *the last-mentioned* means the most recent of any number, and it is available with two as well, though it sounds heavy there. **Against [the former](#the-former)** — the same relation at the other end of the list, where the partner form is *the first-mentioned*. **Against *the last*** — plain *the last* is the everyday American alternative and costs nothing in register: *of the three, the last is the most expensive.* **Against the adjective *last*** — *last week*, *the last person to leave* mean "final" or "most recent in time," where the counting here is over mentions on the page. **The readability trap, doubled** — the *former/latter* pair already sends the reader back up the sentence to count, and a list of five makes it worse; Chicago and Garner both advise naming the thing instead, and with anything longer than a short parallel list they are plainly right.
**Variants:** *the first-mentioned*, the other end of the list (*the first-mentioned of the four has since settled*); *the last-named* and *the first-named*, which legal and biographical writing prefers when the items are people; *the last* and *the first*, the plain American forms. British drafting writes *the lastmentioned* solid and *the last mentioned* open; American editing hyphenates.

Stands for whichever item in a list was named most recently — the phrase that takes over from *the latter* once there are more than two.

1. Of the three statutes, *the last-mentioned* was repealed in 1998.
2. The estate went to a brother, two cousins, and a charity, and *the last-mentioned* declined the bequest.
3. She cited Holmes, Brandeis, and Cardozo, and quoted *the last-mentioned* at length.
4. Copper, aluminum, and steel were all tested, and *the last-mentioned* failed at the lowest load.
5. *The first-mentioned* of the four defendants has since settled. (the partner form)
6. Where the items are people, legal writing prefers the *-named* form: *the last-named* was called as a witness.
7. Plain American: of the three, *the last* is the most expensive.

**See also:** [the latter](#the-latter), [the former](#the-former), [the above](#the-above), [the aforementioned](#the-aforementioned), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [former, latter (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#former-latter)

### the latter

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlæɾɚ/ &middot; *thuh-LAT-er* &middot; the /t/ between vowels is the ordinary American flap, so most Americans make *latter* and *ladder* sound alike; *the* is the weak /ðə/, and *latter* itself never reduces
**Русский:** второй, последний (из двух) — второе из двух только что названных
**Type:** text-pointing pro-form of order — the second of exactly two things just named &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; a written device, uncommon in speech
**Person / number:** third person; it takes the number of the noun it stands for
**Refers to:** **the second of exactly two** things named just before it, counted in the order of mention. With three or more the word is *the last*, not *the latter*. Like its partner it stands for things and ideas freely and for people only in careful writing.
**Agreement:** singular or plural according to the referent — *Of the two bids, the latter **was** withdrawn*; *Of the two teams, the latter **are** unbeaten* (British-leaning) against the ordinary American *the latter **is** unbeaten*.
**Pattern:** *the former* … *the latter* &middot; *of the two, the latter* &middot; *the latter* + [verb] &middot; [preposition] *the latter* &middot; *the latter two*, *the latter three* (postdeterminer — "the last two of the group," a different job) &middot; *the latter half*, *the latter part of the year* (determiner use)
**Contrast:** **Against [the former](#the-former)** — order of mention. **Against *later*** — the two words differ by one vowel, /ˈlæɾɚ/ against /ˈleɪɾɚ/, and ✗ *the later of the two* is a common American slip for *the latter of the two*: *later* means "further on in time," *latter* means "the second of two." **Against *the last*** — three or more items take *the last*, or the formal [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), and *the latter* with a three-item list is an error, not a stretch. **Against the determiner and postdeterminer uses** — *the latter half of the year* and *the latter two candidates* both lean on a noun; only the bare standalone form is the pronoun. **The readability trap** is the same as its partner's: if the reader has to go back and count, name the thing instead.
**Variants:** *the second* (plain American: *the second is cheaper*); *the other* when there are two and the first has already been dealt with.

Stands for the second of exactly two things just named, in the order they were named.

1. We can fly or drive; the former is faster, and *the latter* is cheaper.
2. He was offered Denver or Tulsa, and he took *the latter*.
3. Of the two witnesses, *the latter* is the one the jury believed.
4. The bill covers highways and bridges, and *the latter* got most of the money.
5. She writes fiction and criticism; *the latter* pays better and the former sells better.
6. Watch the vowel: ✗ *the later of the two* → *the latter of the two*.
7. Three items take *the last*: oak, maple, and cherry — *the last* is the most expensive.

**See also:** [the former](#the-former), [the last-mentioned](#the-last-mentioned), [the above](#the-above), [the following](#the-following), [that of](#that-of), [former, latter (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#former-latter)

## The other demonstrative pro-forms: *so*, *such*, *the same*, *the like*

Ten words and phrases that point the way a demonstrative points but stand for a proposition, its negation, a kind, an identity, an amount, a category, or a deliberate blank rather than for a thing. None of them inflects. Each attaches to its own short list of verbs and frames, and several of them — *as much*, *ditto*, *not*, *one and the same*, *such and such* — exist only inside those frames and cannot be moved out of them.

### as much

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *uz-MUCH* &middot; *as* is nearly always the weak /əz/ here and can devoice to /əs/ after a voiceless consonant; *much* carries the stress, so *I thought as much* runs to [aɪˈθɔɾəzˈmʌtʃ] with the /t/ of *thought* flapped
**Русский:** именно это, так и — заменяет целое высказывание: I thought as much «так я и думал»
**Type:** pro-form standing for a whole clause or proposition (a pro-clause), demonstrative in force &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; confined to a short list of fixed frames
**Person / number:** invariant — no person, number, or gender
**Refers to:** **the proposition just stated, or one plainly implied** — "that, that very thing." *I thought as much* means "that is what I thought," and *He said as much* means "that is what he said, in so many words." What it points at is always a whole statement, never a noun: ✗ *I'll take as much* in the pointing sense is not English, though it is a perfectly good quantity phrase in a comparison.
**Agreement:** almost none of its own. It is nearly always a complement rather than a subject, and it governs no verb — ✗ *As much surprised me* → *That surprised me.* The one exception is the formal fronted frame *As much **was** clear from the start*, where it is the subject and the verb is singular.
**Pattern:** *I thought as much* &middot; [verb of saying] + *as much* (*he said as much*, *she admitted as much*, *the report says as much*, *he told me as much*) &middot; *as much was clear from the start* (formal, and the one frame in which it fronts) &middot; **not this construction** — *as much as* + [noun phrase or clause] (comparison — *we paid as much as they did*; concession — *as much as I'd like to*)
**Contrast:** **Against [so](#so)** — the same job for a smaller set of verbs. *I think **so*** is idiomatic and ✗ *I think as much* is not; *He said **so*** and *He said **as much*** are both good, with *as much* the more formal and carrying "and now it is confirmed"; and *I thought **as much*** is the fixed phrase, where *I thought so* is the plainer everyday version of it. **Against [that](#that)** — *that* is a full noun phrase and can head a clause as its subject (*That surprised me*); *as much* is neither a noun phrase you can move around freely nor a possible subject. **Against the comparative *as much as*** — the tell is what comes after: the pro-form takes nothing (*He said as much.*), while the comparison demands a second term (*He paid as much as she did*). **Against *as such*** — a different phrase doing a different job, "in that capacity" (see [such](#such)).

Stands in for a whole statement just made or plainly implied — "that very thing."

1. He never admitted it outright, but he said *as much* to the auditors.
2. So you knew the deal was dead? — I thought *as much*.
3. The memo says *as much*, in the second paragraph.
4. She told me *as much* the day she resigned.
5. If the numbers are wrong, the footnote will say *as much*.
6. *As much* was clear from the first week. (formal, fronted)
7. Not the pro-form: We spent *as much as* they did. (a comparison of quantity)

**See also:** [so](#so), [not](#not), [such](#such), [the same](#the-same), [this much / that much](#this-much--that-much), [ditto](#ditto), [that](#that)

### ditto

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪtoʊ/ &middot; *DIT-oh* &middot; the /t/ between the vowels flaps in ordinary American speech — [ˈdɪɾoʊ] — and the stress is on the first syllable; no weak form, since the word is normally a whole utterance by itself
**Русский:** то же самое — разг.; в столбцах и списках заменяется знаком повтора 〃
**Type:** pro-form of identity — "the same again"; from Tuscan Italian *ditto* "said," taken into English in the 1620s as a bookkeeper's word &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal in speech; in writing a list-and-ledger convention, where the ditto mark 〃 does the work instead
**Person / number:** invariant, and always singular in force
**Refers to:** **what was just said or just written, without repeating it** — in a column, the entry directly above; in conversation, the whole of the other speaker's statement, which the speaker is seconding. It is also a verb (*He dittoed my objection*) and a noun (*the ditto marks*), and neither of those is a pronoun.
**Agreement:** none of its own. In the reply use it is a whole utterance and takes no verb, and in *Ditto for the second quarter* it heads a fragment; there is no ✗ *Ditto is true of the second quarter*, which needs [the same](#the-same).
**Pattern:** *Ditto.* (bare reply, seconding) &middot; *Ditto for* [noun phrase] &middot; *Ditto that* (American conversational) &middot; *and ditto* [noun phrase] (*the first quarter was revised, and ditto the second*) &middot; the ditto mark 〃 (or a pair of straight quotes) under a repeated line in a list or ledger
**Contrast:** **Against *same here*** — identical work in the same register, and *Same here* is the commoner American reply; *ditto* now carries a light jokiness that *same here* does not. **Against [the same](#the-same)** — *the same* is a full noun phrase and takes a verb (*The same is true of the other branches*); *ditto* is a fragment and cannot (✗ *Ditto is true of the other branches*). **Against *likewise*** — *likewise* is the one of the three that survives in formal writing (*Likewise, the second clause was struck*); *ditto* in a report reads as a lapse, and the fix is *the same*. **Not the duplicating machine** — the *ditto* an American over fifty remembers from grade school, and the purple-smelling sheets that came off it, is a trademark for a spirit duplicator and has nothing to do with the pronoun beyond the shared idea of copying.

Seconds what was just said, or marks a line in a list as a repeat of the line above it.

1. I'm skipping the Friday call. — *Ditto*.
2. The first-quarter numbers were revised, and *ditto* the second quarter's.
3. He hates the new logo, and *ditto* for the tagline.
4. She thinks the deadline is unrealistic. *Ditto* half the department.
5. In the ledger each repeated address is marked with a *ditto* rather than typed out again.
6. *Ditto that* — I wouldn't sign it either. (conversational)
7. In a report, write: *The same* is true of the second quarter.

**See also:** [the same](#the-same), [one and the same](#one-and-the-same), [as much](#as-much), [so](#so), [such](#such)

### not

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt/ &middot; *NAHT* &middot; no weak form in this use — the pro-clause *not* is the stressed word in its answer and keeps the full open /ɑ/ of General American, *I hope **not*** [aɪˈhoʊpˈnɑt]; the clitic *n't* of *don't* and *isn't* is the reduced negator and belongs to a different construction, and the two never trade places
**Русский:** нет — заменяет отрицаемое предложение целиком: I hope not «надеюсь, что нет»
**Type:** negative pro-clause — the negative counterpart of [so](#so) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant — no person, number, or gender
**Refers to:** **the negation of a whole clause** just stated or plainly in the air, never a noun. *Is the flight cancelled? — I hope not* means *I hope that it is not cancelled*, and the pronoun *it* cannot stand in its place (✗ *I hope it*, ✗ *I'm afraid it*). What it points at is the same kind of thing [so](#so) points at — a proposition — with the sign flipped.
**Agreement:** none of its own. It is a complement, never a subject, and it governs no verb: ✗ *Not surprised me* → *That didn't surprise me.* The verb in front of it agrees with that verb's own subject.
**Pattern:** [verb of hoping, guessing, or saying] + *not* (*I hope not*, *I'm afraid not*, *I guess not*, *I suppose not*, *she says not*) &middot; *I think not* (formal and clipped) &middot; *if not* &middot; *why not* &middot; *certainly not*, *absolutely not*, *of course not* (bare replies) &middot; *whether or not* &middot; **not this construction** — the clitic negator of *I don't think so*, which negates the verb and leaves *so* to carry the clause
**Contrast:** Which verbs take *not* and which push the negative onto the main verb is the whole difficulty, and the two lists barely overlap. **Verbs that take *not*:** *hope*, *guess*, *be afraid*, *say*, *suspect* — *I hope **not***, *I'm afraid **not***, *I guess **not*** — and for these the other pattern does not exist at all: ✗ *I don't hope so* is not English. **Verbs that push the negative onto the verb instead** — *think*, *believe*, *suppose*, *imagine*, *expect* — *I don't think so* is the ordinary American negative, and *I think not* survives only as a clipped, faintly starchy retort. **Against [so](#so):** exact counterparts filling the same slot after the same verbs, which is why the two are learned together. **Against the ordinary adverb *not*:** *He is not coming* negates a verb inside a clause that is fully present; the pro-clause *not* replaces the clause outright, which is why it can be a whole answer by itself. **Against *no*:** *No* answers the question and stops; *I hope not* answers it and says how the speaker feels about the answer.

Stands in for a whole clause and negates it in the same breath — the negative half of the pair whose affirmative half is *so*.

1. Is the meeting cancelled? — I hope *not*.
2. Will we get the deposit back? — I'm afraid *not*.
3. Did anybody call while I was out? — I guess *not*.
4. Are you planning to argue with her about it? — I think *not*. (clipped and formal; the everyday negative is *I don't think so*)
5. If *not*, we'll have to move the whole week.
6. Certainly *not* — I never agreed to any of that.
7. Wrong pattern with this verb: ✗ *I don't hope so* → I hope *not*.

**See also:** [so](#so), [as much](#as-much), [this much / that much](#this-much--that-much), [that](#that), [such](#such)

### one and the same

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ən ðə ˈseɪm/ &middot; *wun-un-thuh-SAYM* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n/ and *the* to the weak /ðə/, so the middle of the phrase all but disappears — [ˌwʌnən(d)ðəˈseɪm] — and the stress lands on *same*
**Русский:** одно и то же лицо, один и тот же — усиленное the same, требует двух названных предметов
**Type:** emphatic pro-form of identity — an intensified [the same](#the-same) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral; a set phrase, equally at home in speech and in writing
**Person / number:** invariant, and singular in force: it says that two names or descriptions have turned out to have exactly one referent
**Refers to:** **two things or people already named, asserted to be a single one** — *The complaining neighbor and the landlord were one and the same.* It points back at both of the noun phrases in front of it at once, which is why it needs two of them: ✗ *He is one and the same*, with nothing for him to be the same as, is not a sentence.
**Agreement:** it follows *be*, and the subject is coordinate or plural, so the verb is plural — *The two accounts **are** one and the same*; *The author and the translator **were** one and the same.* The phrase itself never changes shape: there is no ✗ *ones and the same* and no comparative.
**Pattern:** [coordinate subject] + *be* + *one and the same* &middot; *X and Y are one and the same* &middot; *are not one and the same* (the negative — the commonest use of all, because the phrase belongs to argument) &middot; *one and the same person / thing / man* (with the noun spelled out, which makes it a determiner phrase rather than the bare pronoun)
**Contrast:** **Against [the same](#the-same)** — *the same* matches a new instance to an old one (*She ordered the salmon and I'll have the same*); *one and the same* collapses two descriptions into a single referent (*the pen name and the professor were one and the same*). **Against *identical*** — *identical* allows two objects that match, and *one and the same* allows only one object with two names. That is the whole distinction arguments turn on: two identical keys are two keys, one and the same key is one. **Against *the selfsame*** — the literary determiner (*the selfsame afternoon*), which needs a noun behind it ([selfsame](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame)). **The empty use** — in loose speech it sometimes means only "much alike" (*To him, Tuesday and Wednesday are one and the same*), which is understood everywhere and quietly throws away the distinction the phrase exists to make.

Says that two things named separately have turned out to be a single thing.

1. The complaining neighbor and the landlord turned out to be *one and the same*.
2. For our purposes, the invoice number and the order number are *one and the same*.
3. Style and substance are not *one and the same*.
4. She wrote under three names, and no critic guessed that all three were *one and the same*.
5. The company that filed the patent and the company that sued over it were *one and the same*.
6. With the noun spelled out: they were *one and the same* person.
7. Looser, and weaker: to him, Saturday and Sunday are *one and the same*.

**See also:** [the same](#the-same), [ditto](#ditto), [such](#such), [same (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [selfsame (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame)

### so

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ &middot; *SOH* &middot; unstressed but not reduced — the diphthong survives, and *I think so* runs to [aɪˈθɪŋk soʊ]; the word has no weak form at all
**Русский:** так, да, это — заменяет целое предложение: I think so «думаю, да»
**Type:** pro-form standing for a whole clause (pro-clause), demonstrative in origin &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant — no person, number, or gender
**Refers to:** **a whole clause or proposition**, just stated or obvious from the situation — never a noun. *Is it raining? — I think so* means *I think that it is raining*. Because it stands for a clause and not a noun phrase, *it* cannot take its place: ✗ *I hope it*, ✗ *I'm afraid it*.
**Agreement:** none of its own — it is not a subject and never governs a verb; the verb in front of it agrees with that verb's own subject.
**Pattern:** [verb of thinking or saying] + *so* (*I think so*, *I hope so*, *I guess so*, *I suppose so*, *I'm afraid so*, *I told you so*, *She said so*) &middot; *if so* &middot; *do so* (replacing a whole verb phrase) &middot; *so* + inversion (*So do I*, *So it seems*) &middot; *more so*, *even more so*
**Contrast:** Which verbs take it, and how each one is negated, is the entire difficulty. **Verbs that take *so*:** *think, believe, suppose, expect, imagine, hope, guess, say, tell (someone), be afraid, seem, appear, hear*. **Verbs that do *not*:** *know* (✗ *I know so* → *I know*), *doubt* (✗ *I doubt so* → *I doubt it*), *be sure* (→ *I'm sure of it*). **Two negative patterns, not interchangeable:** with *think, believe, suppose, imagine, expect*, the negative goes on the main verb — *I don't think so* (the formal *I think not* survives, barely); with *hope, guess, be afraid, say*, the negative is *not* — *I hope **not***, *I'm afraid **not***, and ✗ *I don't hope so* is not English. **Against *that*:** *that* replaces a clause as a full noun phrase and can be a subject (*That surprised me*); *so* cannot be a subject (✗ *So surprised me*) and only follows certain verbs.

Stands in for a whole clause that has just been said or is obvious from the situation, so the speaker does not have to repeat it.

**1. Pro-clause after verbs of thinking and saying.** *Do you think it will rain? — I think so.* The commonest use by far, and the one that has to be learned verb by verb.

**2. "Do so" — replacing a verb phrase.** Formal and written: *Employees may park in Lot C; those who do so must display a permit.* In speech, *do it* or *do that*.

**3. "If so".** A whole conditional clause compressed to two words: *Are you driving? If so, I'll ride with you.*

**4. Confirming what someone has said.** *So it seems. So I hear. So they say.* The pro-form points back at the other speaker's sentence.

**5. Inversion after so, meaning "the same is true of".** *I'm exhausted. — So am I.* The negative counterpart is *neither*: *I don't drive. — Neither do I.*

1. Is the meeting still on? — I think *so*.
2. I hope *so*, but I wouldn't count on it.
3. I told you *so*.
4. If *so*, we'll need a bigger room.
5. She said *so* herself, in front of the whole committee.
6. Anyone wishing to appeal must do *so* within thirty days.
7. Is it going to rain all week? — I'm afraid *so*.
8. He's late again. — *So* I see.

**See also:** [not](#not), [such](#such), [the same](#the-same), [as much](#as-much), [this much / that much](#this-much--that-much), [that](#that), [this](#this)

### such

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ/ &middot; *SUCH* &middot; weak /sətʃ/ whenever it is unstressed, which is most of the time in running speech
**Русский:** таков, такое — книжн.: Such was the outcome «таков был итог»; as such «как таковой»
**Type:** demonstrative pro-form meaning "of that kind, that" &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to literary as a pronoun — the determiner *such a mess* is ordinary, the standalone pronoun is not; *and such* is informal; bare *such* in legal drafting is archaic
**Person / number:** invariant; the referent may be singular or plural, and the verb follows the referent, not the pronoun
**Refers to:** a kind of thing, or a state of affairs, just described: *Such was the outcome* (= that was the outcome, and that is what it was like). In *as such* it refers to the capacity or description just named — *He is the ranking officer and will be treated as such.* It reaches people only through a description, never by pointing at one.
**Agreement:** in the fronted pattern the verb agrees with the **real subject, which follows it**: *Such **is** life. Such **were** his last words. Such **was** the case in every office we visited.* Matching the verb to *such* instead of to the real subject (✗ *Such **was** his last words*) is the standard error here.
**Pattern:** *such* + [verb] + [subject] (*Such is life*, *Such was the plan*) &middot; *as such* &middot; *such as it is / such as they are* &middot; *and such* (informal, = and the like) &middot; *such* as a bare pronoun in legal drafting (*notify the tenant, and such shall have ten days to respond*)
**Contrast:** **Against the determiner [such](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such):** the determiner leans on a noun (*such a mess*, *such nonsense*), the pronoun stands alone (*Such is life*). **Against [so](#so):** *so* stands for a clause and follows a verb (*I think so*); *such* stands for a kind and usually sits in front of the verb (*Such was the plan*). **Against [that](#that):** *that* names the thing, *such* characterizes it — *That was the outcome* identifies, *Such was the outcome* says what sort of outcome it was. **The *as such* trap:** it means "in that capacity" or "strictly speaking," not "therefore." *He is the executor and, as such, signs the checks* ✓. *The vendor missed the deadline; as such, we cancelled* is common in American business writing and is still marked wrong by careful editors — write *therefore* or *so*.

Points back at the kind of thing or the state of affairs just described — "that, and that sort of thing."

1. *Such* is life.
2. *Such* was the plan, at least until the funding fell through.
3. He was elected treasurer and, as *such*, signs every check.
4. The house, *such as it is*, has been in the family a hundred years.
5. *Such* were his last words to the committee.
6. We stocked pens, paper, tape, and *such*. (informal)
7. The document is a draft and should be read as *such*.

**See also:** [so](#so), [the same](#the-same), [such and such](#such-and-such), [the like](#the-like), [that](#that), [such (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such)

### such and such

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *such-un-SUCH* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n/ and the first *such* weakens to /sətʃ/, so the phrase runs to [ˌsətʃn̩ˈsʌtʃ] with the stress on the second *such*
**Русский:** такой-то — заполнитель вместо намеренно не названных имени, даты, суммы
**Type:** pro-form standing for something the speaker is deliberately declining to name — a placeholder, not a pointer &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; chiefly spoken, and written *such-and-such* with hyphens
**Person / number:** invariant, and treated as singular
**Refers to:** **a name, a date, a place, or an amount that the speaker is leaving blank on purpose**, for the hearer to fill in with whatever the real case turns out to be: *They tell you the fee is such and such, and then the paperwork adds fifty dollars.* It points at nothing — that is the point of it — which makes it the odd member of this file: every other entry here has an antecedent. For people the partner form is *so-and-so*.
**Agreement:** singular verb — *Such-and-such **is** always the excuse.* There is no plural and no possessive.
**Pattern:** [verb or preposition] + *such and such* (*do such and such*, *at such and such*, *from such and such to such and such*) &middot; *such-and-such a* [noun] (the determiner use — *at such-and-such a time* — and much the commoner one; see [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a)) &middot; *Mr. So-and-so* (the person-shaped partner) &middot; *thus and so* (dated)
**Contrast:** **Against [such](#such)** — plain *such* points back at a kind of thing just described (*Such was the plan*); the doubled *such and such* points at nothing at all and holds a slot open. **Against *whatever* and *whatnot*** — those wave away the rest of a list; *such and such* keeps one slot empty and specific-shaped. **Against *so-and-so*** — things and quantities against people, and note that *so-and-so* has a second life as a mild American euphemism for a person you are not going to name politely (*the old so-and-so*). **The determiner is commoner** — most real occurrences are *such-and-such a time*, *such-and-such a form*, with the noun present; only the bare standalone use belongs in this dictionary. **The spelling** — American editing hyphenates it whenever it sits in front of a noun and leaves it open in the bare pronoun use, though both spellings turn up in both places.
**Variants:** *such-and-such* (hyphenated, the standard American written form); *so-and-so* for a person; *thus and so* and *thus-and-such*, both dated and now chiefly regional.

Holds a slot open for a name, date, or amount the speaker will not supply — a deliberate blank.

1. They tell you the fee is *such and such*, and then the paperwork adds another fifty.
2. He kept saying he served from *such and such* to *such and such*, and the dates never matched.
3. You fill in *such-and-such* on line 12 and the form does the rest.
4. It's always the same story: the check went out on *such and such* and the bank lost it.
5. Don't tell me it costs *such and such* — give me the number.
6. Determiner use, and the commoner one: be at *such-and-such* a gate by *such-and-such* a time.
7. For a person: Mr. *So-and-so* from the front office signed off on it.

**See also:** [such](#such), [the like](#the-like), [this and that](#this-and-that), [whatnot](07-indefinite-compound.md#whatnot), [such-and-such (a) (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a)

### the like

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlaɪk/ &middot; *thuh-LYKE* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/ before the consonant; *like* keeps its full diphthong and takes the stress, and the phrase has no reduced form
**Русский:** и тому подобное — закрывает перечисление, как русское «и т. п.»
**Type:** pro-form standing for "other things of the same kind" — a category-closer &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal in *and the like*; the fixed exclamation *Did you ever see the like?* is dated, and *suchlike* is dated in American English
**Person / number:** invariant; notionally plural, but it takes a singular verb in the rare frames where it is a subject
**Refers to:** **other things of the same kind as the ones just listed** — the demonstrative version of *et cetera*, closing off a category instead of pointing inside it: *bolts, washers, nuts, and the like*. In the older *the like of* it means "an equal, a match": *a crowd the like of which the town had never seen.*
**Agreement:** in its ordinary use it is the object of *and* or *or*, so no agreement question arises. As a subject — dated, and chiefly in the negative — it is singular: *The like **has** not been seen since.*
**Pattern:** [list]*, and the like* &middot; [list]*, or the like* &middot; *the like of which* [clause] (formal, literary) &middot; *the likes of* [noun phrase] (a different idiom — *the likes of us*, *the likes of Amazon*) &middot; *and suchlike* (variant) &middot; *Did you ever see the like?* (dated exclamation, still current in Irish and rural Southern American speech)
**Contrast:** **Against *and such*** — the same job, one register down: *pens, paper, tape, and such* is casual, *and the like* and *and so on* are neutral, and *etc.* is what the same sentence wears in a document. **Against *the likes of*** — the *s* changes the meaning completely: *the likes of us* means "people of our sort" and usually carries class feeling (*not for the likes of us*), where *and the like* only closes a list. **Against [such and such](#such-and-such)** — a category to extend against a single blank to fill. **The writing caution** — a list closed with *and the like* is a list the writer has not finished defining, and in technical, legal, and contractual prose it is struck out for exactly that reason: the class stays open and nobody can say what is in it.
**Variants:** *suchlike*, one word (*pens, paper, and suchlike*) — dated in American English, still ordinary in British and Irish speech and heard in rural Southern American speech; it is also a determiner (*suchlike nonsense*), catalogued in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#suchlike). Regional American speech also has *and the like of that*.

Closes a list by standing in for the rest of its kind — "and other things like these."

1. Bring rope, tape, zip ties, and *the like*.
2. The store carries feed, fencing, hand tools, and *the like*.
3. The policy covers floods, hurricanes, or *the like*.
4. He collects stamps, coins, and *suchlike*. (dated)
5. A crowd *the like of which* the town had never seen. (formal)
6. Did you ever see *the like*? (dated exclamation)
7. A different idiom: that club was never meant for *the likes of us*.

**See also:** [such](#such), [such and such](#such-and-such), [so](#so), [the same](#the-same), [this and that](#this-and-that), [suchlike (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#suchlike)

### the same

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈseɪm/ &middot; *thuh-SAYM* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/ before a consonant sound; *same* carries the stress
**Русский:** то же самое — тождество с уже названным: I'll have the same «мне то же самое»
**Type:** demonstrative pro-form of identity &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral (*I'll have the same*); *Same here* is informal; bare *same* without *the* is commercial and legal, and dated
**Person / number:** invariant; the referent may be singular or plural
**Refers to:** a thing, an action, or a state of affairs already named, marked as **identical to it** rather than merely like it: *She ordered the salmon and I'll have the same.* It also points back at whole propositions — *The same is true of the second quarter* — and at actions, after *do*: *He left at five and expected everyone else to do the same.*
**Agreement:** it takes the number of what it stands for, and with an abstract or clause-sized referent it is singular: *The same **is** true of the other branches. The same **goes** for you.* With a plural referent a plural verb is grammatical but stiff, and American English prefers *the same ones*: *The same ones **are** still on backorder* rather than ?*The same are still on backorder*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *the same* (*I'll have the same*, *Do the same*) &middot; *the same as* [noun phrase or clause] &middot; *The same goes for* [noun phrase] &middot; *The same is true of* [noun phrase] &middot; *Same here* / *Same to you* (bare, informal reply) &middot; *remit / ship same* (commercialese)
**Contrast:** **Identity against resemblance** — *the same* means one thing met twice; *similar* means two things that are alike. **The comparison word is *as*,** never *like* and never *than*: ✓ *the same as last year*, ✗ *the same like last year*, ✗ *the same than last year*. **Bare *same*** without *the* belongs to two registers only — casual replies (*Same here*, *Same to you*) and old commercial and legal writing (*Enclosed is our invoice; kindly remit same*), where every modern American style guide tells you to write *it*. **Against the determiner [same](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#same):** the determiner needs a noun (*the same problem*); the pronoun stands alone.

Points back at something already named and marks the new instance as identical to it rather than merely similar.

1. She ordered the salmon, and I'll have *the same*.
2. *The same* is true of every office we surveyed.
3. He left at five and expected everyone else to do *the same*.
4. *The same* goes for anyone who shows up after the doors close.
5. My rent is *the same as* it was three years ago.
6. Happy holidays! — *Same* to you. (informal)
7. We have received your order and will ship *same* on Monday. (commercial, dated)

**See also:** [such](#such), [so](#so), [one and the same](#one-and-the-same), [ditto](#ditto), [the like](#the-like), [that of](#that-of), [same (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#same)

### this much / that much

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ˈmʌtʃ/, /ˌðæt ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *this-MUCH, that-MUCH* &middot; no weak form — *much* takes the stress and the demonstrative keeps its full vowel; in *that much* the /t/ is normally unreleased or a glottal stop before the /m/, [ˌðæʔˈmʌtʃ], so the two words sound welded together
**Русский:** столько — о количестве; и в обороте this much is clear «одно ясно» — об одной мысли
**Type:** demonstrative + the degree quantifier *much* — a noun phrase standing for an amount, or for one measured piece of what is known &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — ordinary in speech and in writing alike
**Person / number:** third person singular, always; *much* is a mass quantifier and the phrase has no plural, the countable counterpart being *this many / that many*
**Refers to:** two things that look alike and are not. **(1) A quantity** just named, shown, or gestured at: *We need a gallon. — I don't have that much.* **(2) A measured piece of what is known**, in the frame *this much is clear* / *I'll say this much*, where the pronoun points forward at the one fact the speaker is willing to vouch for and quietly concedes that the rest is unsettled. Sense 2 is the one worth learning: it points at a proposition rather than at a substance, and it is a hedge as much as a pointer.
**Agreement:** singular verb, always — *This much **is** certain. That much **was** obvious to everybody.* Never ✗ *this much are*, even when the fact it stands for involves several things at once. With a countable referent the phrase changes rather than the verb: *That **many** were left over*, not ✗ *that much were left over*.
**Pattern:** *this much is clear / certain / true* &middot; *I'll say this much* (+ *for* [noun phrase]) &middot; *I know this much* &middot; [verb] *that much* (*I don't have that much*, *we didn't drink that much*) &middot; *not that much* &middot; *that much I do know* / *this much I can tell you* (fronted and contrastive) &middot; *so much for* [noun phrase] (a separate idiom — see Contrast) &middot; **not this construction** — *this much money*, *that much trouble*, determiner phrases sitting on a noun
**Contrast:** **Sense 1 against sense 2** — the quantity use is literal and points at a heap or a volume (*I don't have that much*), while the pro-clause use points at a single fact (*This much is clear*), and only the second takes the frames *I'll say this much* and *this much I know*. **Against [as much](#as-much)** — the closest relative in this file, and the line between them is worth keeping: *as much* stands for the whole proposition just stated and is a subject only in the one formal fronted frame *As much was clear from the start*, while *this much* stands for a **portion** of what is known and is a subject freely (*This much is clear*). **Against *so much*** — a different phrase doing three other jobs, none of them this one: *so much for that* dismisses something as finished, *so much the better* is a set comparative, and *not so much* opens a comparison. None of the three can be swapped in — ✗ *So much is clear* — which is why *so much* has no entry of its own here. **Against *this many / that many*** — mass against countable, and the line is hard: *that much flour*, *that many eggs*. **Against the determiner** — *this much rain*, *that much trouble* lean on a noun and belong with the determiners ([much](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#much)); only the standalone phrase is a pronoun.
**Variants:** *that much I do know* and *this much I can tell you*, the fronted emphatic order, idiomatic and common in speech; *this little / that little* at the opposite end (*I didn't expect that little*), which is rarer and always contrastive.

Points at an amount — or, in the frame *this much is clear*, at the one piece of a matter the speaker is prepared to vouch for, leaving the rest of it open.

1. *This much* is clear: nobody read the contract before signing it.
2. I'll say *this much* for him — he showed up every single day.
3. We need a gallon of primer and I don't have *that much*.
4. *That much* I do know, whatever else is in dispute. (fronted, emphatic)
5. Don't worry about it *that much*; the deadline moved to the fifteenth.
6. *This much* I can tell you: the check was cashed on the fourth.
7. Countable referents take *that many*: ✗ *that much were left over* → *that many* were left over.
8. A different idiom altogether: *so much for* the quiet weekend I had planned.

**See also:** [as much](#as-much), [so](#so), [not](#not), [the same](#the-same), [this](#this), [that](#that), [much](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#much), [much (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#much)

## Fixed expressions built on a demonstrative pronoun

Frozen phrases in which *this* or *that* is doing genuine pronoun work — pointing back at what was just said — inside an idiom that has to be learned whole. The parts do not predict the meaning, and the word order is not negotiable.

### at that

**Pronunciation:** /æt ˈðæt/ &middot; *at-THAT* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/ and its /t/ goes unreleased against the following /ð/ — [əˈðæt]
**Русский:** да ещё и, притом — в конце фразы; leave it at that «на том и остановиться»
**Type:** fixed phrase — preposition plus demonstrative pronoun, working as a sentence-final connective &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; chiefly spoken and in conversational writing
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** the thing or the fact just stated. Two idioms share the shape: sentence-final *at that*, meaning "and what's more," and *leave it at that*, meaning "stop there."
**Agreement:** none of its own — it modifies a clause and governs no verb.
**Pattern:** [clause], *and* [phrase] *at that* &middot; [clause] — *and a* [adjective] *one at that* &middot; *leave it at that* &middot; *let it go at that*
**Contrast:** Sentence-final *at that* is not *at that point* or *at that time*, where *that* is a determiner sitting on a following noun. It always adds something: the phrase in front of it must be a further detail (*and a cheap one at that*), never a repetition of what was already said. Against *besides* and *moreover*, which do the same work in writing, *at that* is spoken, tail-end, and faintly wry. And *leave it at that* is a separate idiom that happens to share the words — it closes a subject rather than adding to one, and there it is closest to [that's that](#thats-that).

Tacks a further, often surprising, detail onto what was just said — "and what is more."

1. He fixed it himself, and in ten minutes *at that*.
2. She found a buyer, and a cash buyer *at that*.
3. It's a long drive, and an ugly one *at that*.
4. He apologized once, and grudgingly *at that*.
5. We agreed to disagree and left it *at that*.
6. I'd rather not argue about it — let's leave it *at that*.

**See also:** [that](#that), [that's that](#thats-that), [with that](#with-that), [that said](#that-said)

### that is

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈɪz/ &middot; *that-IZ* &middot; the /t/ flaps between the vowels — [ˌðæɾˈɪz]; the phrase is never contracted to ✗ *that's* in this use
**Русский:** то есть — связка, вводящая более точную формулировку сказанного
**Type:** fixed phrase — demonstrative pronoun plus *be*, working as a discourse connective &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in edited prose
**Person / number:** frozen in the third person singular; *is* never varies
**Refers to:** the word, phrase, or clause immediately before it, which the material after it then restates more precisely.
**Agreement:** always singular *is* — the phrase is frozen, and ✗ *those are* is not a plural version of it.
**Pattern:** [statement], *that is*, [restatement] &middot; [statement] — *that is* — [restatement] &middot; *that is to say* &middot; sentence-final *that is* (*He's coming Tuesday. If the flight's on time, that is.*)
**Contrast:** *that is* is the English of Latin **i.e.** — "in other words, precisely" — while *for example* is **e.g.**, "here is one instance among several." Interchanging the two is the commonest abbreviation error in American writing. Punctuation: American style puts commas on both sides (*, that is,*), and *i.e.* likewise takes a comma after it (*i.e., the second draft*). Against the neighbors built on the same pronoun: [that said](#that-said) concedes and then pushes back, [that's that](#thats-that) closes a matter, and [*that's it*](#thats-it) either confirms a guess (*Yes, that's it*) or announces that something has run out.

Introduces a restatement that narrows, corrects, or specifies what was just said.

1. We'll meet on the first — *that is*, a week from Thursday.
2. He's her stepson, *that is*, her husband's boy from his first marriage.
3. The rule covers full-time employees, *that is to say*, anyone scheduled for thirty hours or more.
4. She'll be there Tuesday. If the flight lands, *that is*.
5. I'd like the small one — *that is*, if you can spare it.
6. The deadline is firm, *that is*, unless the client asks for an extension.

**See also:** [that](#that), [that said](#that-said), [that's that](#thats-that), [that's it](#thats-it)

### that said

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈsɛd/ &middot; *that-SED* &middot; no reduction; both words keep full vowels and the phrase takes its own intonation contour, set off by a comma
**Русский:** тем не менее, при этом — вводный оборот, отсылающий ко всей предыдущей уступке
**Type:** fixed phrase — a nominative absolute in which the demonstrative pronoun is the participle's own subject, working as a connective &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral; thoroughly established in business, academic, and journalistic prose
**Person / number:** invariant and frozen
**Refers to:** everything the writer has just conceded — the whole preceding sentence or paragraph, not a noun in it.
**Agreement:** none — the participle is non-finite, and the phrase governs no verb. The longer variants are *that being said* and *having said that*.
**Pattern:** [concession]. *That said*, [contrasting point] &middot; [concession] — *that said*, [contrasting point] &middot; *That being said*, … &middot; *Having said that*, …
**Contrast:** Against *however* and *nevertheless*: *that said* first grants the previous point and then turns against it, so it needs a real concession in front of it — *The design is expensive. That said, it will outlast three cheap ones.* Its variants are simply longer, and editors cut *that being said* back to *that said*. One objection worth answering: *that said* is sometimes called a dangling modifier, but it is not — the pronoun *that* is the participle's own subject, which is what makes it an absolute construction. The form that really can dangle is *having said that*, which attaches itself to the subject of the main clause. Against [at that](#at-that) and [with that](#with-that), which sit on the same pronoun: one adds a detail, one moves the story forward, and *that said* argues.

Concedes the point just made and then turns against it — "even so."

1. The car has 140,000 miles on it. *That said*, it's never once broken down.
2. I don't love the color. *That said*, it's a great price.
3. Attendance is down. *That said*, revenue is up nine percent.
4. He's inexperienced. *That said*, nobody prepares harder.
5. The evidence is thin. *That said*, the jury believed her.
6. We can't fund all of it — *that said*, we can cover the first phase.

**See also:** [that](#that), [with that](#with-that), [that is](#that-is), [at that](#at-that)

### that's it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæts ˈɪt/ &middot; *thats-IT* &middot; the /s/ links straight onto the vowel of *it* — [ˌðæˈtsɪt] — and a final /t/ before a pause is usually unreleased or glottal, [ˈɪʔ]; the stress falls on *it* when the phrase confirms or protests, and on *that's* when it reports that something has ended
**Русский:** вот именно; всё, кончилось; ну всё, с меня хватит — разг., три разных употребления
**Type:** fixed clause — demonstrative pronoun as subject, the personal pronoun *it* as complement &middot; **Case:** none — the demonstrative has no case forms, and *it* is the same shape in every slot &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; spoken far more often than written
**Person / number:** third person singular, frozen; the only other form is the narrative past *that was it*
**Refers to:** the thing, the guess, or the supply just mentioned or just pointed at. Three idioms share the shape: confirming that the hearer has hit on the right thing (*Yes, that's it*), reporting that a supply or a process has ended (*That's it, we're out of forms*), and, heavily stressed and usually with an exclamation point, announcing that the speaker's patience has run out (*That's it — I'm leaving*).
**Agreement:** singular *is* or *was* only — *that's it*, *that was it*. The idiom has no plural and no other tense; ✗ *those are it* is not a plural version of it.
**Pattern:** *That's it* (confirming) &middot; *That's it* (supply or process ended) &middot; *That's it!* (exasperation) &middot; *that's it for* [noun phrase] &middot; *That was it* (narrative past) &middot; *Is that it?* (question)
**Contrast:** Three neighbors built on the same pronoun divide the work between them. [that's that](#thats-that) declares a matter closed against further argument; *that's it* confirms, or reports that something has run out; *that's all* is the mildest of the three, reporting only that nothing remains. There is a grammatical difference under the surface as well: in *that's that* both words are the demonstrative, while here the demonstrative is the subject and *it* is an ordinary personal pronoun — which is why this phrase turns into a question (*Is that it?*) and *that's that* does not (✗ *Is that that?*). And the exasperated *That's it!* is not the confirming one said louder: the intonation, not the words, carries that difference.

Confirms that something is exactly the thing meant — or announces that a supply, a process, or the speaker's patience has run out.

1. Is the blue folder the one you wanted? — *That's it*.
2. Turn left after the church — no, wait, *that's it*, the brick one.
3. *That's it* for the questions; we're out of time.
4. We sold the last two this morning, so *that was it*.
5. *That's it* — I'm not driving back out there again. (exasperation)
6. Is *that it*, or is there another box still in the truck?
7. Sign at the bottom, and *that's it* — you're done.

**See also:** [that's that](#thats-that), [that is](#that-is), [that](#that), [at that](#at-that), [with that](#with-that)

### that's that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæts ˈðæt/ &middot; *thats-THAT* &middot; no reduction; the second *that* takes the stress, which is what makes the phrase sound final
**Русский:** вот и всё, и точка — вопрос закрыт, разг.
**Type:** fixed clause — the demonstrative pronoun as both subject and complement &middot; **Case:** none — English gives the demonstrative no case, which is why the same word fills both slots &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; spoken
**Person / number:** third person singular, frozen
**Refers to:** the matter just settled: the first *that* points back at it, and the second closes it off.
**Agreement:** singular *is* or *was* only — *that's that*, *that was that*. There is no plural (✗ *those are those*) and no other tense in the idiom.
**Pattern:** *and that's that* &middot; *that was that* (narrative past) &middot; *if* [clause], *then that's that* &middot; [statement]. *That's that.*
**Contrast:** *That's that* declares a matter closed and not open to discussion. [*That's it*](#thats-it) does two other jobs — confirming that a guess is right (*Yes, that's it*) and announcing that a supply or a process has ended (*That's it, we're out of forms*). *That's all* is weaker still: it merely reports that nothing remains. And *leave it at that* asks for the same closure politely, where *that's that* imposes it.

Declares a matter finished and not open to further discussion.

1. The decision is final, and *that's that*.
2. She said no twice, so *that was that*.
3. If the bank won't lend, *that's that* — we don't buy the building.
4. He hung up, and *that was that* for the partnership.
5. We're not going. *That's that*.
6. One more late payment and *that's that*.

**See also:** [that](#that), [at that](#at-that), [that is](#that-is), [that's it](#thats-it), [with that](#with-that)

### this and that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ən ˈðæt/ &middot; *this-un-THAT* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n/ — [ˌðɪsn̩ˈðæt]
**Русский:** то да сё — разг., о разных мелочах, которые говорящий не хочет перечислять
**Type:** fixed coordinate phrase of two demonstrative pronouns &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal
**Person / number:** invariant; notionally plural but grammatically frozen
**Refers to:** unspecified miscellaneous things, tasks, or topics — deliberately vague, because the speaker is declining to itemize.
**Agreement:** it almost always appears as the object of a verb or a preposition rather than as a subject, so the question rarely arises; where a subject is needed, speakers reach for *a few things* or *odds and ends* instead.
**Pattern:** *talk about this and that* &middot; *do this and that* &middot; *a bit of this and that* &middot; *this, that, and the other (thing)* (the three-part extension)
**Contrast:** This is the demonstrative version of *et cetera*, vague on purpose. *This and that* covers topics and small tasks; *odds and ends* covers physical objects; *this, that, and the other thing* stretches the same joke to three. The word order is fixed — ✗ *that and this* — and so is the *and*: *this or that* is not a variant of the idiom but a different phrase altogether, one that offers a genuine choice between two.

Various unspecified things — the polite way of not saying what.

1. We talked about *this and that* for the better part of an hour.
2. I spent the weekend doing *this and that* around the house.
3. She's been busy with *this and that* since she retired.
4. It's a bit of *this and that* — nothing you'd call a system.
5. He kept asking about *this, that, and the other thing*.
6. What did you buy? — Oh, *this and that*.

**See also:** [this](#this), [that](#that), [such](#such)

### this is he / this is she

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ɪz ˈhi/, /ˌðɪs ɪz ˈʃi/ &middot; *this-iz-HEE, this-iz-SHEE* &middot; in the everyday version the personal pronoun takes its weak form — *this is him* [ˈðɪsɪzɪm], *this is her* [ˈðɪsɪzɚ] — while the formal *he* and *she* stay strong, which is part of why they sound formal
**Русский:** это я, я у телефона — офиц. ответ на просьбу позвать к телефону; разг. this is him / her
**Type:** fixed telephone formula — demonstrative pronoun + *be* + personal pronoun as subject complement &middot; **Case:** **subject case** in the formal version (*he*, *she*); object case in the ordinary spoken one (*him*, *her*) &middot; **Register:** formal and business-standard (*this is he / this is she*); neutral (*this is him / this is her*, *speaking*)
**Person / number:** third person singular in form, first person in meaning — the speaker refers to himself or herself in the third person, a quirk unique to this formula
**Refers to:** the speaker, identified as the person the caller asked for.
**Agreement:** singular *is*. The pronoun that follows agrees with the speaker's own gender, which is the only place gender enters the demonstrative system.
**Pattern:** *May I speak to Ms. Ruiz? — This is she.* &middot; *Is Mr. Okafor available? — This is he.* &middot; *Speaking.* &middot; *This is* [name] (self-identification) &middot; *Is this Karen? — This is Karen.*
**Contrast:** The traditional rule says that the complement of *be* takes the subject case, which gives *This is he* and *This is she* — the same rule behind *It is I*. American speech went the other way long ago: *It's me* is standard, and *This is him* / *This is her* is what most Americans actually say. What keeps *This is he/she* alive is register rather than the rule. It is the receptionist's and the customer-service answer, and in that one setting it sounds crisp rather than stiff, where *It is I* would sound absurd anywhere at all. Both are safe on the phone; the dodge that avoids the question entirely is *Speaking* or *This is Karen*. Note also that this is close to the only everyday context in which Americans use a subject-case pronoun after *be* without irony — *It was he who signed* still turns up in formal writing, but nowhere in speech — and that British speakers, who normally answer *Speaking*, find *This is she* distinctly American.

The American telephone answer meaning "I am the person you are asking for."

1. May I speak with Diane Palmer? — *This is she*.
2. Is Mr. Okafor available? — *This is he*.
3. Am I speaking to the account holder? — You are; *this is he*.
4. Is this Dr. Ruiz? — *This is her*, but I'm with a patient. (ordinary speech)
5. Can I talk to whoever handles billing? — *This is she*. What can I do for you?
6. Hi, is this Karen? — *This is Karen*, yes.

**See also:** [this](#this), [that](#that), [that's that](#thats-that), [the grammar of pronouns: *It is I* vs *It's me*](../README.md)

### with that

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ˈðæt/ &middot; *with-THAT* &middot; the two /ð/ sounds merge into a single long one — [wɪˈðæt]; American *with* is voiced /wɪð/ before another voiced sound
**Русский:** с этими словами; на этом — сказав или сделав только что описанное
**Type:** fixed phrase — preposition plus demonstrative pronoun, working as a narrative connective &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary; a device of narrative and reportage
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** the words or the action just reported. *With that* means "having said that" or "having done that."
**Agreement:** none of its own; it modifies the clause that follows.
**Pattern:** *With that*, [subject] [verb] &middot; *And with that*, … &middot; [clause]*, and with that* [clause] &middot; *with that said* (a blend of *with that* and *that said* — common in speech, redundant in writing)
**Contrast:** Three connectives in this file are built on the same pronoun and none of them is interchangeable. *With that* is temporal and dramatic: the next thing happens immediately, and because of what just happened. [that said](#that-said) is concessive: it grants a point and then argues against it. [at that](#at-that) adds a further detail to what was just said. The blend *with that being said* is wordy on both ends and gets cut to *that said*.

Marks the immediate next action after something just said or done — "and on that note."

1. *With that*, she picked up her coat and left.
2. He signed the form, and *with that* the sale was final.
3. *With that*, the meeting broke up.
4. She thanked the jury, and *with that* the trial was over.
5. He set the box on the counter and, *with that*, walked out.
6. *With that*, the lights went down and the show began.

**See also:** [that](#that), [that said](#that-said), [at that](#at-that), [that's that](#thats-that)

## Archaic, regional, and nonstandard forms

Listed for recognition, not for production. Every form below will either date a text or mark a speaker, and a learner meets all of them in fiction, film, country music, and everyday speech across large parts of the United States. Each entry names the region where there is one. Two patterns recur: *here* and *there* pulled in front of the pronoun instead of trailing after it, and the object pronoun *them* standing in for *those*.

The quoted sentences carry other features of the same varieties along with them — *ain't*, nonstandard verb agreement (*This here **don't** look right*, *Them **was** good times*), and negative concord, the so-called **double negative** (*Them ain't **none** of mine*, *That there won't do **nobody no** good*). Negative concord is the one item on this page with no region to name: it is ordinary in Southern white speech, in African American English, and in working-class speech in every city in the country, it was standard English until the eighteenth century, and it is nonstandard in edited American writing everywhere today — *Them ain't none of mine* → *Those aren't any of mine* / *None of those are mine*. In that last rewrite the verb is a choice and not an error: *none* takes a singular or a plural verb, ✓ *None of those **is** mine* and ✓ *None of those **are** mine* are both standard, and the plural is the commoner of the two. Each of these features is nonstandard on its own account, and none of them is part of the demonstrative system; they appear here because that is how these forms actually occur in speech. The determiner counterparts — *them apples*, *them there hills*, *this here dog*, all of them rural, Southern, and working-class — are nonstandard in exactly the same way and are catalogued in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md). For the nonstandard personal and reflexive forms that travel in the same company, see [archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns](10-archaic-dialectal.md): **hisself** and **theirselves** (Southern, Appalachian, and African American English — regular formations, *his* + *self* on the model of *myself*, but nonstandard in writing), **youse** (New York, New Jersey, and Philadelphia, with Irish English behind it), **y'all** (Southern, and now spreading well beyond the South), and ✗ *Me and him went*, which belongs to no region at all — it is casual speech all over the United States and an error in writing all over it too, an object form standing in a subject slot.

### that there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈðɛr/ &middot; *that-THAIR* &middot; in fast speech the whole phrase collapses to [ˈðæɾɚ], written *that'ere* in dialect fiction
**Русский:** вон тот — прост., диал. (юж. и сельский амер.); усиленное указание вдаль
**Type:** **nonstandard** emphatic demonstrative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — rural and Southern American speech, and the standard shorthand for it in fiction and film
**Person / number:** third person singular
**Refers to:** one thing at a distance, with the pointing reinforced. Standard English puts the *there* after the noun phrase (*that one there*); this dialect pulls it inside.
**Agreement:** singular verb — *That there **is** my truck.*
**Pattern:** *that there* + [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *that there* &middot; *that there's* (contracted) &middot; the determiner use *that there truck*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#that-there)
**Contrast:** Standard equivalents are *that*, *that one*, *that one there*, and *that one over there*. The plural is [them there](#them-there) or [those there](#those-there). It is not an intensifier a learner can borrow: writing it outside dialogue marks the writer, not the speaker. Compare the parallel near forms [this here](#this-here) and [these here](#these-here).

*That*, with the distance emphasized. Nonstandard.

1. *That there* is my truck. (nonstandard)
2. Hand me *that there*, would you? (nonstandard)
3. *That there's* the one that's been leaking. (nonstandard)
4. I'll take *that there* and two of the little ones. (nonstandard)
5. *That there* ain't going to hold. (nonstandard)
6. Standard: *That one* is my truck.

**See also:** [this here](#this-here), [these here](#these-here), [those there](#those-there), [them there](#them-there), [them](#them), [that](#that)

### them

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛm/ &middot; *THEM* &middot; weak /ðəm/, and after a consonant frequently just /əm/ — *give me 'em*; the reduction is ordinary and standard, and it is the demonstrative **use** that is not
**Русский:** те, вон те — прост., диал. вместо those (не личное местоимение «их»): Them are the ones
**Type:** **nonstandard** demonstrative pronoun — the object pronoun *them* standing in for *those* &middot; **Case:** historically the object case, but in this use it appears as a subject as well (*Them are the ones*) &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — widespread in rural, Southern, and working-class American speech, and frozen inside a few idioms everyone knows
**Person / number:** third person plural
**Refers to:** plural things or people at a distance — the dialect equivalent of *those*.
**Agreement:** plural verb in most dialects — *Them **are** the ones I want* — though the same varieties often use a singular (*Them **was** good times*), which is a separate dialect feature riding along with it.
**Pattern:** *them* + plural verb (*Them are the ones*) &middot; [verb] *them* (indistinguishable from the ordinary personal pronoun) &middot; [them there](#them-there) &middot; the determiner use *them apples*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them) &middot; frozen: *Them's the breaks*, *Them's fightin' words*
**Contrast:** In standard English *them* is only the object form of *they* (*I saw them*) and can never be a subject or a demonstrative: ✗ *Them are mine* → *Those are mine*; ✗ *I'll take them apples* → *those apples*. The tell is position — a *them* in subject position, or a *them* sitting directly in front of a noun, is dialect. Do not confuse the **use** with the **pronunciation**: the reduced /əm/ of *I saw 'em* is ordinary standard American speech and carries no dialect marking at all. The fixed idioms *Them's the breaks* and *Them's fightin' words* are quoted by standard speakers as jokes, complete with the dialect verb.

*Those*. Nonstandard outside a handful of fixed idioms.

1. *Them* are the ones I want. (nonstandard)
2. *Them's* the breaks. (fixed idiom, jocular even in standard speech)
3. *Them's* fightin' words. (fixed idiom)
4. I'll take *them* in the back there. (nonstandard)
5. *Them* was good times. (nonstandard, with the dialect singular verb)
6. *Them* ain't *none* of mine. (nonstandard; negative concord — the double negative — riding along with it)
7. Standard: *Those* are the ones I want.

**See also:** [those](#those), [them there](#them-there), [those there](#those-there), [that there](#that-there), [them (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them)

### them there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛm ˈðɛr/ &middot; *them-THAIR* &middot; no reduction; the two words run together as one stressed unit, and the eye-dialect spelling of it is *them thar*
**Русский:** вон те — прост., диал.; сильно маркированная форма, мн. число к that there
**Type:** **nonstandard** emphatic demonstrative pronoun (plural, far) — nonstandard *them* with the reinforcing *there* pulled in front of it &middot; **Case:** none as a demonstrative; the *them* is historically the object form of *they*, and in this use it stands as a subject as well (*Them there are the ones*) &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — rural and Southern American speech, heavily marked, and the stock phrase of dialect comedy
**Person / number:** third person plural
**Refers to:** more than one thing at a distance, with the pointing doubly reinforced. It is the plural partner of [that there](#that-there), and within the dialects that have these forms it is commoner than [those there](#those-there).
**Agreement:** plural verb — *Them there **are** the ones I meant.* The same varieties often use a singular instead (*Them there **was** the good ones*), which is a separate dialect feature riding along and not a property of the phrase.
**Pattern:** *them there* + plural verb &middot; [verb] *them there* &middot; [preposition] *them there* &middot; *them there's* (contracted) &middot; *them thar* (eye-dialect spelling; the famous *There's gold in them thar hills* is the determiner use) &middot; the determiner use *them there hills*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them-there)
**Contrast:** It doubles two nonstandard features at once — *them* standing in for *those*, and *there* wedged in front of the pronoun instead of trailing after it. Standard equivalents are *those*, *those over there*, and *the ones over there*: standard English reinforces the pointing too, but always after the phrase (*those right there*), never inside it. The near counterpart is [these here](#these-here), the singular is [that there](#that-there), and the *them*-less variant is [those there](#those-there). In print it signals dialect; from a standard speaker it is nearly always deliberate humor.

*Those*, with the distance emphasized twice over. Nonstandard.

1. *Them there* are the ones we cut last fall. (nonstandard)
2. Load up *them there* and let's get going. (nonstandard)
3. *Them there's* the ones I told you about. (nonstandard, contracted)
4. I wouldn't give a nickel for *them there*. (nonstandard)
5. Are *them there* for sale? (nonstandard)
6. *Them there* ain't ripe yet. (nonstandard)
7. Standard: *Those* are the ones we cut last fall.

### these here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðiz ˈhɪr/ &middot; *theez-HEER* &middot; no reduction; the /h/ is often dropped in fast speech — [ˌðizˈɪr]
**Русский:** вот эти — прост., диал.; усиленное указание на близкое
**Type:** **nonstandard** emphatic demonstrative pronoun (plural, near) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — rural and Southern American speech; dialect writing
**Person / number:** third person plural
**Refers to:** more than one thing close to the speaker, with the nearness underlined.
**Agreement:** plural verb — *These here **are** the good ones.*
**Pattern:** *these here* + plural verb &middot; [verb] *these here* &middot; the determiner use *these here boots*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#these-here)
**Contrast:** Standard equivalents: *these*, *these ones* (speech only), *the ones here*. The distal counterpart is [those there](#those-there), and the singular is [this here](#this-here). *These*, with the nearness emphasized. Nonstandard.

### this here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ˈhɪr/ &middot; *this-HEER* &middot; no reduction; the /h/ is often dropped — [ˌðɪsˈɪr]
**Русский:** вот этот — прост., диал.; усиленное указание на близкое, мн. число these here
**Type:** **nonstandard** emphatic demonstrative pronoun (singular, near) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — rural and Southern American speech; dialect writing
**Person / number:** third person singular
**Refers to:** one thing close to the speaker, with the nearness underlined. It can introduce a person, exactly as standard *this* does: *This here is my brother.*
**Agreement:** singular verb — *This here **is** my brother.*

### those there

**Русский:** вон те — прост., диал.; усиленное указание вдаль
**Type:** **nonstandard** emphatic demonstrative pronoun (plural, far) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — rural and Southern American speech; dialect writing

**Agreement:** plural verb — *Those there **are** the ones we cut last fall.*

**Contrast:** Standard equivalents: *those*, *those ones* (speech only), *the ones over there*. Within the dialect itself, [them there](#them-there) is more usual than *those there* — the *them* form and the *there* reinforcement travel together. The near counterpart is [these here](#these-here).

### yon

**Pronunciation:** /jɑn/ &middot; *YAHN* &middot; no weak form; one syllable, always stressed
**Русский:** вон тот — устар., диал.; третья степень дальности: this — that — yon
**Type:** **archaic** demonstrative pronoun of the third, far degree &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **archaic** and literary; regional in Appalachian and Ozark speech, and alive in Scots and Northern English
**Person / number:** invariant — no singular/plural distinction at all, which is part of what makes it sound old
**Refers to:** something visible but far from speaker and hearer alike — the third term of a system English has otherwise lost (*this* by me, *that* by you, *yon* over there), the one Spanish still keeps as *este / ese / aquel*.
**Agreement:** the verb follows the referent, since the form itself never changes: *Yon **is** the house* / *Yon **are** the hills we crossed*. In practice the pronoun use is rare even in the dialects that keep the word; the determiner (*yon ridge*) and the adverb *yonder* carry nearly all of it.
**Pattern:** *yon* as subject (*Yon is the house*) &middot; [preposition] *yon* &middot; *hither and yon* (fixed adverbial, "here and there" — the one fully current use) &middot; the determiner use *yon ridge*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#yon)
**Contrast:** Modern standard English has collapsed the old three-way system into two, so *that* now covers everything *yon* once did. Of the three surviving forms, only [yonder](#yonder) is still said by Americans, and mostly as an adverb (*over yonder*); *yon* survives in verse, in hymns, and in the fixed *hither and yon*; *yond* is a Shakespeare quotation and nothing more. None of the three belongs in ordinary writing.

*That one over there* — archaic, and pointing at what is far from everyone present.

1. *Yon* is the house where he was born. (archaic)
2. Whose land is *yon*, beyond the creek? (archaic, regional)
3. He looked from this to *yon* and said nothing. (literary)
4. *Yon* was the ridge we crossed at dawn. (archaic)
5. The papers were scattered hither and *yon*. (fixed phrase, current)
6. Modern: *That* is the house where he was born.

**See also:** [yonder](#yonder), [that](#that), [those](#those), [yon (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#yon), [yond (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#yond)

### yonder

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjɑndɚ/ &middot; *YAHN-der* &middot; no weak form; stressed on the first syllable, with the r-colored /ɚ/ of General American
**Русский:** вон тот, вон там — устар. как местоимение; как наречие живо в юж. амер. говоре
**Type:** **archaic** demonstrative pronoun; far more commonly an adverb of place, which is the living use &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **archaic** as a pronoun; regional but thoroughly alive as an adverb — Southern, Appalachian, Ozark, and rural Midwestern
**Person / number:** invariant
**Refers to:** something at middle-to-far distance and in view. As a pronoun (*Yonder is the schoolhouse*) it is archaic and biblical-sounding; as an adverb (*He's out yonder*) it is ordinary regional American speech.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the noun phrase that follows, which is the real subject: *Yonder **is** the schoolhouse*, *Yonder **are** the hills*. That is itself the clue that most apparent pronoun uses are really a fronted adverb with the subject behind the verb.
**Pattern:** *Yonder is / are* + [noun phrase] &middot; *over yonder*, *out yonder*, *up yonder*, *down yonder* (adverb — the living use) &middot; *the wild blue yonder* (fixed noun phrase) &middot; the determiner use *yonder field*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#yonder)
**Contrast:** Against *that*: *that* means simply "not here," while *yonder* adds "and you can see it from where we stand" — a distance judged by eye. Against [yon](#yon) and *yond*: three forms of one word, of which *yonder* is the only one an American still says. And the part-of-speech caution matters most — nearly every modern *yonder* is an adverb (*He's out yonder*) or a determiner (*yonder field*), not a pronoun, so a learner needs it for listening rather than for writing.

That thing over there, in sight — archaic as a pronoun, current as a regional adverb of place.

1. *Yonder* is the schoolhouse my grandmother taught in. (archaic, regional)
2. *Yonder* are the hills we crossed. (archaic)
3. *Yonder* was the mill, before the fire took it. (archaic)
4. The cabin is just over *yonder*. (adverb — the ordinary use)
5. He's out *yonder* mending fence. (regional adverb)
6. They lit out for the wild blue *yonder*. (fixed noun phrase)
7. Modern standard: *That* is the schoolhouse. / It's over there.
