# Quantifying and distributive pronouns

> The pronouns of amount — the words that answer *how much?* and *how many?* without naming what they count, and the distributives that take a set apart one member at a time.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Almost every word in this group leads a double life: a **determiner** in front of a noun (*each student*, *most people*, *another chance*) and a **pronoun** standing alone or with *of* (*each was checked*, *most of them left*, *I'll have another*). Only the pronoun use belongs here — the determiner counterparts live in the [determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/README.md), and each entry links across. Two rules run through the whole group. First, **the *of* is obligatory in front of a personal pronoun** — *all of them*, *most of them*, *neither of us*, never ✗*all them*, ✗*most them* — and for most of the group it is obligatory in front of a determiner as well: *most of the students*, *each of the rooms*, ✗*most the students*. The exceptions are the three **predeterminers** *all*, *both*, and *half*, which may drop it before a determiner, so that *all the seats*, *both the books*, and *half the time* are as standard as *all of the seats*, *both of the books*, and *half of the time*. Whatever follows *of* stands in the **object case**: *all of us*, *both of them*, *neither of us*, never ✗*all of we*.

Second, and this is where the errors actually live, **verb agreement is not uniform across the group and cannot be guessed from the meaning**. *Each*, *either*, *neither*, *every one*, *any one*, *one or the other*, *another*, *the other*, *much*, *a great deal*, *little*, *a little*, *a bit*, *least*, *less*, *one*, *the one*, *the whole*, *the usual*, *the residue*, *every last one*, *everyone and their mother*, *not one*, *not a one*, and the regional *nary a one* take a **singular** verb no matter how plural the *of*-phrase behind them looks — *Each of the rooms **has** its own thermostat*. *Both*, *a couple*, *many*, *few*, *a few*, *a handful*, *fewer*, *fewest*, *several*, *certain*, *others*, *ones*, *a good many*, *quite a few*, *a slew*, *hundreds*, *thousands*, *dozens*, the bare cardinals from *two* up, *et al.*, *one and all*, and *all and sundry* take a **plural** verb, always. And *all*, *half*, *any*, *some*, *most*, *more*, *none*, *enough*, *plenty*, *a lot*, *lots*, *a bunch*, *a ton*, *tons*, *loads*, *heaps*, *oodles*, *scads*, *gobs*, *a boatload*, *a shitload*, *a third*, *the rest*, *the remainder*, *the balance*, *the majority*, *the minority*, *all of the above*, *none of the above*, *any of the above*, and *such* agree with **whatever they refer to** — plural for countables (*Some of the chairs **are** broken*), singular for a mass (*Some of the paint **is** dry*). One more point of current usage: when a singular pronoun in this group needs to be referred back to, **singular *they* is standard American English**, including in edited prose — *Each of them signed their own copy*. *Chicago*, the *MLA Handbook*, the APA *Publication Manual*, and the *AP Stylebook* all accept it; *his or her* is the conservative alternative, and generic *he* now reads as an error.

**Case.** Not one word in this group inflects for it. The **Case:** line on every entry below reads *none*, with one partial exception — the generic [one](#one), which has a possessive *one's* and a reflexive *oneself* but still uses a single form as subject and as object (*One never knows* / *It gives one pause*). Otherwise the label means exactly what it says: *all* is *all* in a subject slot and in an object slot alike (*All were counted* / *We counted all of them*), and there is no *whom*-style object form anywhere in the family. Case surfaces in two places around these words. The first is right after *of*, where the **object form is required**: *all of **us***, *both of **them***, *each of **us***, *half of **them***, never ✗*all of we*. The second is when one of them **floats** onto a coordinate subject, or sits inside a coordinate object — and there the ordinary personal-pronoun rules apply, with the ordinary errors in both directions. ***She** and **I** both went* is correct and ✗*Me and him both went* is **nonstandard**, an object form doing a subject's job; *He gave a copy to Bob and **me***, *between you and **me***, and *All of it went to my wife and **me*** are correct, while ✗*between you and I* and ✗*They invited my wife and I* are **hypercorrections** — object slots filled with a subject form by speakers reaching for something that sounds careful. **One test settles every one of them: delete the other conjunct and listen.** *He gave a copy to **me*** (so *to Bob and me*), *They invited **me*** (so *my wife and me*), ***I** went* (so *She and I went*). The full paradigm is in [personal pronouns](01-personal.md), and two facts there are worth carrying over: *It's me* is **standard** American English and *It is I* is formal to the point of stiffness, and *whom* now survives mainly in the one position where it is still the default for most writers, immediately after a preposition — *to whom*, *for whom*, *neither of whom*, *both of whom* — while everywhere else [who](05-relative.md#who) is the ordinary modern choice.

**What follows them.** These pronouns take relative clauses constantly — *all that remains*, *the few who stayed*, *the ones we saw* — and the relative is chosen by the **antecedent**, not by the quantifier: ***who*** for people (*the few **who** stayed*), ***which*** for things (*the crates, **which** arrived intact*), ***that*** for both (*all **that** remains*, *the ones **that** sold*, *the several **that** were damaged*), and ***whose*** for both as well (*the one **whose** name was missing*, *the crate **whose** lid was cracked*). Two honest notes. After *all*, *much*, *little*, *the only*, and a superlative, *that* is the idiomatic relative and *which* sounds wrong: *all **that** remains*, not ✗*all which remains*. And the familiar restrictive-*that* / nonrestrictive-*which* split is an **American editing convention**, enforced by *Chicago* and the AP, not a law of the language — restrictive *which* has been written by good writers for six hundred years and is unremarkable in British English. What is not optional is the punctuation: a nonrestrictive clause takes commas, a restrictive one does not. See [relative pronouns](05-relative.md) for the full account.

**Pronunciation.** The *of* in these partitives is almost never said in full. It reduces to /əv/ and then to /ə/, and the pronoun after it reduces too, so the whole phrase comes out as a single word: *all of them* /ˈɔl əvəm/ &middot; *AW-luh-vum*, *some of them* /ˈsʌməvəm/, *most of us* /ˈmoʊstəvəs/, *each of you* /ˈitʃ əvjə/. The weak forms doing that work belong to the personal pronouns — *them* /əm/ (spelled *'em*), *him* /ɪm/, *her* /ɚ/, *you* /jə/, *he* /i/ — and they are tabled in full at [personal pronouns](01-personal.md). The quantifiers themselves keep their own vowels and have no weak forms; only the articles inside *a lot*, *a couple*, *a few*, and *a little* reduce, always to /ə/ and never stressed.

**Nonstandard forms are labeled here, not hidden.** Three of them touch this group. ***Them* used as a determiner** — *them apples*, *all them boxes* — is the object pronoun doing a determiner's job; it is regular and systematic in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, in African American English, and in working-class speech generally, and it is nonstandard in every register of writing (dictionaried at [them (dialectal demonstrative)](10-archaic-dialectal.md#them-dialectal-demonstrative)). **Negative concord** — *I don't want neither*, *We didn't get none* — is likewise rule-governed in those same varieties rather than a failure of logic, and likewise stigmatized in edited prose; standard American English allows one negative per clause, so the repairs are *I don't want either* / *I want neither*. And ***a couple things*** without *of* is widespread informal American speech that editors still change to *a couple of things*. Three further nonstandard forms that readers look for here belong to neighboring families and are dictionaried there, and all three are **nonstandard in every register of writing** however systematic they are in speech. **[Hisself](10-archaic-dialectal.md#hisself)** and **[theirselves](10-archaic-dialectal.md#theirselves)** are reflexives built regularly on the possessive — exactly the way standard *myself*, *yourself*, and *ourselves* are built — and they are current in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, in African American English, and in working-class speech nationally; the standard forms are *himself* and *themselves*. **[Youse](10-archaic-dialectal.md#youse)** is a second-person plural of the Northeastern cities — New York, Philadelphia, and northern New Jersey above all, with Irish-immigrant roots — filling the same gap that [y'all](10-archaic-dialectal.md#yall) fills in the South, [yinz](10-archaic-dialectal.md#yinz) in Pittsburgh, and informal [you guys](10-archaic-dialectal.md#you-guys) nearly everywhere; standard written English has only *you* for both numbers.

## Totals and fractions: the whole set, half of it, and the empty set

*All*, *both*, and *none* take a set in one piece, *half* cuts it in two, and *a third* stands in for every other fraction that cuts it finer. *All*, *half*, *a third*, and *none* bend their number to what they point at; *both* is fixed at two and always plural, and *the whole*, which names a thing entire rather than the members of a set, is fixed at one and always singular. Around them sit the fixed phrases that do the same work with more feeling — *one and all*, *all and sundry*, and the exasperated *everyone and their mother* for the whole crowd, *not one*, *not a one*, and the Southern and Appalachian *nary a one* for the empty set — and the two list-pointing phrases *all of the above* and *none of the above*, whose antecedent is a set of lines on a page rather than a set of things in the world.

### a third

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈθɚd/ &middot; *uh-THURD* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed, and before *of* the phrase runs together — *a third of it* /əˈθɚdəvɪt/, *uh-THUR-duh-vit*. The compound fractions keep two beats and a hyphen in print: *two-thirds* /ˌtu ˈθɚdz/, *too-THURDZ*; *three-quarters* /ˌθri ˈkwɔrtɚz/, *three-KWOR-turz*
**Русский:** треть — одна часть из трёх; так же устроены все дроби: a quarter «четверть»
**Type:** fractional quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a third of the budget*, an ordinary count noun, *cut it into thirds*, and, in *the third to arrive*, the ordinal) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass or a sum
**Refers to:** one part in three of a set or a quantity — the members of a set of countables (plural) or an amount of a mass (singular). Every other fraction works the same way: *a quarter*, *two-thirds*, *three-quarters*, *a fifth*, *seven-eighths*.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *A third of the seats **were** empty* (countable → plural); *A third of the budget **is** gone* (mass → singular). The numerator does not decide it either: *Two-thirds of the members **have** signed* is plural because *members* is, and *Two-thirds of the roof **was** torn off* is singular because *roof* is. The *of*-phrase decides, exactly as with *half*, *all*, and *most*.
**Pattern:** *a third* [verb] &middot; *a third of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *a third of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a third* &middot; *about a third*, *nearly two-thirds*, *a good third*, *the bottom third* &middot; *by a third*, *by two-thirds* (adverbial)
**Variants:** *a quarter* and *a fourth* (both standard American, *a quarter* the commoner outside arithmetic class), *two-thirds*, *three-quarters*, *a fifth*, *three-eighths*, and every other fraction built on the same frame
**Contrast:** Against *half*: *half* is the one fraction that may drop the *of* in front of a determiner — *half the time* is standard and ✗*a third the time* is not, so the phrase has to be *a third of the time*. Against *some* and *most*: those are vague on purpose, while a fraction reads as a claim about proportion and a reader will treat it as one — write *about a third* when the number is an estimate. On the page a two-word fraction standing alone takes a hyphen (*two-thirds of them*, *three-quarters were sold*), while *a third* and *a quarter* need none. And keep the fraction apart from the ordinal: *the third of the applicants* is a portion, *the third applicant* is a position in line.

One part in three of something, and the model for every fraction used the same way.

1. *A third* of the seats were still empty when the lights went down.
2. *Two-thirds* of the roof came off in the first hour of the storm.
3. She paid *a third* at signing and the balance on delivery.
4. Of the forty applications, roughly *a third* were incomplete.
5. *Three-quarters* of them had never heard of the company before that week.
6. He read *a quarter* of it, decided it was hopeless, and gave the book away.

### all

**Pronunciation:** /ɔl/, in cot-caught merged American speech /ɑl/ &middot; *AWL* &middot; no reduced vowel of its own, but it runs into what follows: *all of them* /ˈɔl əvəm/ &middot; *AW-luh-vum*
**Русский:** всё; все — вся совокупность счётного множества или вся масса целиком
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *all children*, and a predeterminer, *all the children*) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariable form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant in form; construes as **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass or an abstraction
**Refers to:** the entirety of a set of countable things or people (plural), the whole of a mass or abstract quantity (singular), or everything covered by a following relative clause (*all that remains*, *all I know*). Bare *all* meaning "everybody" survives only in formal or ceremonial writing — *All are welcome*.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to.** Countable referent → plural verb: *All of the seats **are** taken.* Mass or abstract referent → singular verb: *All of the money **is** gone*; *All I need **is** a break.* The *of*-phrase decides, not the word *all*.
**Pattern:** *all* [verb] &middot; *all of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *all of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *all* &middot; *all* [relative clause] (*all that remains*) &middot; *that's all* &middot; floated after the subject: *they all left*, *we have all seen it*
**Contrast:** Against *each* and *every one*: *all* takes the members together and goes plural (*All of them are here*), while *each* and *every one* take them one at a time and go singular (*Each of them is here*) — same fact, different packaging. Against *half* and *none*: the three mark the whole of an *of*-phrase, half of it, and none of it, and all three take their number from it. Against *whole*: *all of the cake* is every piece of it; *the whole cake* is the cake as an undivided thing. **Where the *of* is required and where it is not:** before a **pronoun** it is obligatory — *all of them*, ✗*all them* — but before a **determiner** *all* is a predeterminer and may drop it, so *all the seats* and *all of the seats* are equally standard, exactly as with *both* and *half*. In *we all left* and *he thanked us all*, *all* has floated out of the noun phrase and needs no *of* either. The dialect *all them apples* is a different thing again — the object pronoun *them* doing a determiner's job, systematic in Southern and Appalachian vernacular and in African American English, and nonstandard in every register of writing.

Names the complete quantity or the complete membership of something, leaving nothing out.

**1. The entire number of a set of countables.** Plural verb.

1. *All* of the tickets were sold before noon.
2. Of the forty crates we shipped, *all* arrived intact.
3. She read the file and sent copies to *all* of us before the meeting.

**2. The entire amount of a mass or abstraction.** Singular verb.

4. She spent *all* of it on rent.
5. *All* of the paint had dried in the can.

**3. Everything, with a relative clause.** The verb stays **singular** even when the complement after it is plural — *All I want **is** answers* — because the subject is the whole clause *all I want*. The plural by attraction, *All I want **are** answers*, is common and turns up in edited prose; the singular is the safer choice and the one an editor will leave alone.

6. That's *all* I have to say about it.
7. *All* I want is a straight answer.

**4. Everybody — formal and ceremonial only.**

8. *All* are welcome at the Sunday service.

### all and sundry

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ən ˈsʌndri/ &middot; *awl-uhn-SUN-dree* &middot; the *and* is always reduced to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/ and is never said in full; the main beat falls on *sundry*
**Русский:** все кому не лень, все без разбора — книжн.-шутл., преим. брит.; только о людях
**Type:** fixed quantifying pronoun phrase meaning "everyone, without distinction" (the adjective *sundry* survives almost nowhere else outside the determiner of *sundry expenses*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal-jocular, and chiefly British; an American meets it in British writing, in legal boilerplate, and in deliberately arch journalism
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** every person in sight, taken as an undifferentiated crowd — invited or not. People only: ✗*all and sundry of the boxes*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *All and sundry **were** invited*; *All and sundry **have** had their say.* ✗*All and sundry is* is wrong even though the phrase names a lump.
**Pattern:** *all and sundry* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *all and sundry* (*He told all and sundry*) &middot; *to all and sundry* &middot; no *of*-phrase at all: ✗*all and sundry of them*
**Contrast:** Against *one and all*: the same crowd with the opposite attitude — *one and all* welcomes them, *all and sundry* complains about them. Against plain *everyone*: that is the neutral word, singular in agreement, and the one to use unless the archness is wanted. The legal doublet *all and singular* is a third relative and is pure boilerplate. Note that the phrase takes no *of*: for a specific set the pronoun is *all of them*.

Everybody in sight, named as a crowd rather than as people.

1. He announced the engagement to *all and sundry* before telling his mother.
2. *All and sundry* were welcome at the open house, which turned out to be the problem.
3. She has explained the plan to *all and sundry*, and nobody is any the wiser.
4. The gate was left open and *all and sundry* wandered in off the street.
5. Terms of the settlement were emailed to *all and sundry* by mistake.
6. It stops being a secret the moment you tell *all and sundry*.

**See also:** [one and all](#one-and-all), [all](#all), [every one](#every-one), [everyone](07-indefinite-compound.md#everyone), [everybody](07-indefinite-compound.md#everybody), [all and singular (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all-and-singular), [sundry (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#sundry)

### all of the above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl əv ði əˈbʌv/ &middot; *awl-uv-thee-uh-BUV* &middot; the *of* reduces to /əv/ and then to /ə/, and *the* before the vowel of *above* is /ði/
**Русский:** всё вышеперечисленное — отсылка к списку выше, язык анкет и тестов
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase, **deictic** — it points at a list on the page rather than at anything in the world &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral; the native language of forms, tests, and slides, and a stock joke in speech
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with the listed items and **singular** when the list is treated as one answer
**Refers to:** everything in a list just given — the lines above it on a page, a form, or a screen. Its antecedent is **textual**, which is why the phrase is nearly useless in conversation and indispensable on paper.
**Agreement:** **plural with the items, singular with the option** — *All of the above **are** covered by the warranty* (the things listed); *All of the above **is** correct* (the whole list as a single choice). Both are standard, and the plural is the safer default whenever the list is a list of things.
**Pattern:** *all of the above* [verb] &middot; [verb] *all of the above* &middot; standing alone as a multiple-choice option (*(d) all of the above*) &middot; the parallel forms *none of the above*, *any of the above*, *some of the above*, *both of the above*
**Contrast:** Against *none of the above* and *any of the above*: one list, and the three phrases answer *how much of it* exactly as *all*, *none*, and *any* do for a set of things. *The above* is functioning as a noun here, so the article cannot be dropped: ✗*all of above*. And because the antecedent is on the page, a reader who has scrolled past the list has lost it — in running prose, name the items instead.

Every item in the list just given.

1. Payment may be made by cash, check, credit card, or *all of the above*.
2. *All of the above* are covered under the standard warranty.
3. Was it the wiring, the roof, or the foundation? — *All of the above*.
4. If *all of the above* is correct, mark option (d).
5. She was asked whether she was tired, hungry, or annoyed, and answered *all of the above*.
6. Bring your license, your registration, and proof of insurance; *all of the above* must be current.

**See also:** [none of the above](#none-of-the-above), [any of the above](#any-of-the-above), [all](#all), [every one](#every-one), [none](#none), [all of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#all-of)

### both

**Pronunciation:** /boʊθ/ &middot; *BOHTH* &middot; no weak form; before a consonant the final /θ/ often drops in casual speech — *both times* /boʊ ˈtaɪmz/
**Русский:** оба, обе — ровно двое уже названных; не о массе и не о трёх и более
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *both books*, a predeterminer, *both the books*, and half of the correlative *both … and*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; **always plural** — it refers to exactly two
**Refers to:** exactly two countable things or people, already identified. Never a mass (✗*both of the water*) and never three or more (✗*both of the triplets* — use *all three*).
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Both **are** ready*; *Both of the applications **were** rejected.* There is no context in which ✗*both is* is correct.
**Pattern:** *both* [plural verb] &middot; *both of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *both of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *both* &middot; floated after the subject: *they both agreed*, *they have both agreed*, *he told us both*
**Contrast:** *Both* = the two together (plural verb); *each* = the two taken one at a time (singular verb); *either* = one or the other; *neither* = not one and not the other. **The negation trap:** *Both of them didn't pass* is genuinely ambiguous — it can mean neither passed or only one did. Write *Neither of them passed* or *Only one of them passed* and the ambiguity disappears. Before a pronoun, *of* is required when *both* heads the phrase (*both of them*, ✗*both them*); *them both* and *they both* are the floated forms and are equally standard.

Points at two things at once and insists on the fact that there are two — plain *the two* reports the pair, *both* emphasizes that neither one is left out.

1. *Both* were accepted, which nobody had expected.
2. I've read *both* of them twice.
3. She offered me coffee and tea, and I took *both*.
4. The lawyers agreed, but *both* wanted it in writing.
5. *Both* of my sisters live in Chicago now.
6. She sent the signed contract to *both* of them by noon.
7. Paperback or hardcover? — *Both*, if that's an option.

**See also:** [all](#all), [half](#half), [each](#each), [either](#either), [neither](#neither), [each other](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-other), [both (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#both)

### everyone and their mother

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvriwʌn ən ðɛr ˈmʌðɚ/ &middot; *ev-ree-wun-un-thair-MUTH-ur* &middot; the *and* is always reduced to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/ and is never said in full, *their* weakens to /ðɚ/ in fast speech, and the main beat falls on *mother*
**Русский:** все кому не лень, полгорода — разг.-шутл., о нелепо большой толпе людей
**Type:** hyperbolic quantifying pronoun phrase meaning "absolutely everybody" &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal and jocular — speech, social media, and casual writing, almost always with a complaint or a joke attached; never in edited prose, where the word is *everyone*
**Person / number:** third person; **singular** in agreement in ordinary use, because the phrase is felt as one emphatic *everyone*
**Refers to:** every person imaginable, presented as an absurdly large crowd. **People only** — there is no ✗*everything and its mother*.
**Agreement:** **singular is the ordinary choice** — *Everyone and their mother **was** at the lake*; *Everyone and their mother **has** a podcast now.* The plural (*were*, *have*) also occurs and is what an ordinary *and*-coordination would predict; since the phrase is barred from formal writing anyway, no editor is going to arbitrate between them. The *their* inside the phrase is **singular *they*** pointing back at *everyone*, which is standard American English.
**Pattern:** *everyone and their mother* [verb] &middot; [verb] *everyone and their mother* &middot; *everyone and their mother's* + noun (*everyone and their mother's opinion*) &middot; no *of*-phrase at all: ✗*everyone and their mother of them*
**Variants:** *everybody and their mother*, *everyone and their brother*, *everybody and his brother* (the older form, and one that generic *he* has been retreating from for forty years), *everyone and their dog*, *everyone and their uncle*; British and Australian English says *every man and his dog*
**Contrast:** Against *all and sundry*: the same crowd in a formal-jocular register instead of a casual American one, and both of them faintly disgusted. Against *one and all*: warm and ceremonial where this is exasperated. Against plain *everyone*: the neutral word, singular in agreement, and the one to write when nothing is being exaggerated. Note that the exaggeration is the entire content of the phrase — it adds no members to *everyone*, which already has all of them, and that logical impossibility is exactly the joke.

Absolutely everybody, named as a crowd nobody invited.

1. *Everyone and their mother* was at the lake that weekend.
2. By Tuesday *everyone and their mother* had an opinion about the new logo.
3. She posted it once and *everyone and their brother* shared it.
4. I wanted a quiet table, and what we got was *everyone and their mother*.
5. *Everyone and their mother* has a podcast now.
6. He told *everyone and their mother* before he told his own department.

### half

**Pronunciation:** /hæf/ &middot; *HAF* — the *l* is silent. The broad vowel /hɑf/ &middot; *HAHF* survives in Eastern New England and is the ordinary British form; the noun's plural is *halves* /hævz/. Before *of* the phrase runs together: *half of them* /ˈhæfəvəm/ &middot; *HAF-uh-vum*
**Русский:** половина — одна из двух равных частей; в речи и просто «добрая половина»
**Type:** fractional quantifying pronoun (also a determiner and a **predeterminer**, *half the time*, a count noun, *two halves*, and an adverb, *half finished*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** one of the two equal parts of a set or a quantity — countable things or people (plural), or a mass (singular). In ordinary speech it also does duty for "roughly half" and even "a large part" (*Half of them never showed up*), which is idiomatic English and not an arithmetic claim.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *Half of the seats **were** empty* (countable → plural); *Half of the cake **was** gone* (mass → singular). Bare *Half **is** enough* is singular on a mass reading and *Half **are** missing* plural on a count reading. The *of*-phrase decides, exactly as with *all*, *some*, and *most*.
**Pattern:** *half* [verb] &middot; *half of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *half of* [object pronoun] &middot; *half* [determiner + noun] with no *of* (predeterminer: *half the time*, *half my life*) &middot; [verb] *half* &middot; *half and half* &middot; *in half*, *by half*, *the better half*
**Contrast:** *Half* is one of the three words in this dictionary — with *all* and *both* — that can drop the *of* in front of a determiner: *half the time* and *half of the time* are both standard, and the shorter one is the more idiomatic in speech. Before a **pronoun** the *of* comes straight back and is obligatory: *half of them*, ✗*half them*. Against *all* and *none*: the three of them mark the whole of a set, half of it, and none of it, and all three take their number from the referent. Keep the pronoun apart from the count noun *half*, which has a real plural (*cut it into halves*, *the first half*). And note that *half an hour* and *a half hour* are both standard American English, while ✗*a half an hour* is a blend of the two that editors delete on sight.

One of the two equal parts of something — or, in loose everyday use, roughly that much.

1. *Half* of the seats were still empty at kickoff.
2. She read *half* of it on the plane and finished the rest at home.
3. *Half* of the cake was gone before the candles came out.
4. Of the forty applications, *half* were incomplete.
5. He split the fee with her and kept *half* for himself.
6. She had already sent a copy to *half* of them before anyone objected.

**See also:** [all](#all), [both](#both), [none](#none), [most](#most), [some](#some), [less](#less), [half (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#half)

### nary a one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɛri ə ˈwʌn/ &middot; *NAIR-ee-uh-WUN* &middot; *nary* is a worn-down *ne'er a*, "never a," so the article is already inside the word and the second *a* is always the reduced /ə/
**Русский:** ни один, ни единого — диал. и устар., южноамериканская сельская речь
**Type:** negative quantifying pronoun phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **regional and dated** — Southern, Appalachian, and older rural American speech; in print it appears as dialect writing, as quoted speech, or as a deliberate folksy flourish, and it is not standard edited English
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** not a single member of a set of countable things or people. Never a mass: ✗*nary a one of the water*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Nary a one **was** left*; *Nary a one of them **has** called.*
**Pattern:** *nary a one* [singular verb] &middot; *nary a one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *nary a one of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *nary a one* &middot; the determiner *nary a* + singular noun (*nary a soul*, *nary a dime*), which is where most readers meet the word at all
**Variants:** *ary a one* (its positive twin, since *ary* means "any" — *Have you got ary a one?*); *nary one* and the eye-dialect *narra one*
**Contrast:** Against *not a one*: the same phrase in national colloquial American English, with no regional marking on it. Against *none* and *not one*: those are the neutral words, usable in every register. **Where an American meets it.** In the South and in Appalachia it is ordinary speech, commoner from older speakers than from younger ones; everywhere else it survives in fiction, in country lyrics, and in journalism quoting either. Inside those varieties it carries no stigma at all; outside them it reads as a costume, so in edited prose the word is *none*.

Not a single one — the old rural negative, still alive in Southern and Appalachian speech.

1. We hunted morels all afternoon and found *nary a one*.
2. He asked six neighbors for help, and *nary a one* would come.
3. Of all the promises made that spring, *nary a one* was kept.
4. There were supposed to be eggs in the coop; there was *nary a one*.
5. *Nary a one* of them had ever set foot in the county.
6. She counted the tools twice, and *nary a one* was missing.

### none

**Pronunciation:** /nʌn/ &middot; *NUN* &middot; no weak form; a homophone of *nun*
**Русский:** ни один; нисколько — ничто из множества или массы под отрицанием
**Type:** negative quantifying pronoun — and **pronoun only**: unlike *all*, *some*, and *most*, it has no determiner use at all, because in front of a noun the word is *no* (*no money*, ✗*none money*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** — see below
**Refers to:** the members of a set of countables (people or things) or the amount of a mass, inside the scope of negation. It handles both, which is what separates it from *no one* and *nobody*.
**Agreement:** **either, and this is not a mistake.** With a plural countable referent, both *None of them **is*** and *None of them **are*** are standard, and the plural is the more common choice in American usage — plural *none* has been in continuous use since Old English and is not a modern slackening. Use the **singular** when the sense you want is emphatically "not a single one" (*None of the three candidates **is** acceptable*), and the **plural** as the ordinary default (*None of the windows **were** locked*). With a mass referent it is **singular** only: *None of the water **was** drinkable.* The rule that *none* must always be singular because it comes from *not one* is a **zombie rule** — it was invented in the eighteenth century and never described real usage. A conservative editor may still prefer the singular in formal writing; neither form will be marked wrong.
**Pattern:** *none of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *none of* [object pronoun] &middot; *none* [verb] (*None survived*) &middot; [verb] *none* &middot; *none but* [noun phrase] (formal) &middot; *none other than* [name] &middot; *none the wiser*, *none too pleased* (adverbial idioms, a different word class)
**Contrast:** Against *no one* and *nobody*: those two refer to people only and are always singular; *none* covers things and mass quantities as well and takes either number. Against *neither*: use *neither* when the set has exactly two (*Neither of the twins was hurt*), *none* for three or more. Against *no*: *no* is the determiner (*no money*), *none* the pronoun (*I have none*) — the two are in complementary distribution, so ✗*I have no* and ✗*none money* are both impossible.

Says that not one member of a set qualifies, or that not any of a quantity is left.

1. *None* of the applicants had the certification.
2. *None* of the milk was left by Sunday.
3. I looked for a charger, but there was *none* in the drawer.
4. *None* of the three explanations is convincing.
5. Of the forty seats, *none* were empty.
6. She read all three offers and accepted *none* of them.
7. She asked for volunteers, and *none* came forward.

**See also:** [all](#all), [half](#half), [any](#any), [neither](#neither), [some](#some), [no one](07-indefinite-compound.md#no-one), [nobody](07-indefinite-compound.md#nobody), [nothing](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing), [no (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no)

### none of the above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnʌn əv ði əˈbʌv/ &middot; *nun-uv-thee-uh-BUV* &middot; the *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/, and *the* before the vowel of *above* is /ði/
**Русский:** ничего из вышеперечисленного — последний вариант в тестах и бюллетенях
**Type:** negative quantifying pronoun phrase, **deictic** &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — the last option on a multiple-choice question, a line on some ballots, and a refusal in speech
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural**, exactly as [none](#none) does
**Refers to:** not one of the items in a list just given. The antecedent is **textual** — lines on a page rather than things in the world.
**Agreement:** **either.** *None of the above **applies** to me* and *None of the above **apply** to me* are both standard, the same split that runs through *none*: the singular is the emphatic "not a single one," the plural the ordinary default.
**Pattern:** standing alone as an option (*(e) none of the above*) &middot; *none of the above* [verb] &middot; [verb] *none of the above* &middot; on a ballot, *a vote for none of the above*
**Contrast:** Against *all of the above* and *any of the above*: one list, three quantities. Against bare *none*: *none* takes a set of things in the world, *none of the above* a set of lines on a page. Nevada prints a literal *None of These Candidates* line on its statewide ballots, which is where most Americans have seen the phrase used as a real choice rather than as a test answer; in ordinary speech it is a joke or a refusal, not a quantity.

Not one of the items in the list just given.

1. (e) *None of the above.*
2. Which of these describes your symptoms? — *None of the above*.
3. *None of the above* applies to part-time employees.
4. He read the four explanations the report offered and believed *none of the above*.
5. Given the three finalists, most of the committee would have voted for *none of the above*.
6. If *none of the above* is correct, write your own answer on the line.

**See also:** [all of the above](#all-of-the-above), [any of the above](#any-of-the-above), [none](#none), [not one](#not-one), [no one](07-indefinite-compound.md#no-one), [nothing](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing), [none of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#none-of)

### not a one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ə ˈwʌn/ &middot; *not-uh-WUN* &middot; the article reduces to /ə/, and in ordinary speech the /t/ of *not* is a flap or a glottal stop, *nah-duh-WUN*
**Русский:** ни один, ни единого — разг. амер.; в строгой прозе лучше not one или none
**Type:** emphatic negative quantifying pronoun phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** colloquial American — unremarkable in speech and in quoted speech, a shade too casual for formal prose, where *not one* or *none* does the same work
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** not a single member of a set of countable things or people. Never a mass: ✗*not a one of the water*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Not a one **was** broken*; *Not a one of them **has** answered.* The plural noun inside the *of*-phrase never pulls the verb over.
**Pattern:** *not a one* [singular verb] &middot; *not a one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *not a one of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *not a one* &middot; as a bare answer (*How many came? — Not a one.*)
**Contrast:** Against *not one*: the same meaning with the article added for emphasis, and that article is exactly what drops the register — *Not one of them called* is edited English, *Not a one of them called* is speech. Against *none*: the neutral, register-free word, and the only one of the four that also takes a mass. Against *nary a one*: that is the Southern and Appalachian version; *not a one* is the form used nationally.

Not a single one, said with feeling.

1. I asked around for a spare key and got *not a one*.
2. *Not a one* of those windows will open.
3. Sixty people were invited and *not a one* wrote back.
4. He has plenty of opinions and *not a one* worth hearing.
5. How many of the seedlings came up? — *Not a one*.
6. She went through the whole drawer, and there was *not a one* left.

### not one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈwʌn/ &middot; *not-WUN* &middot; both words carry stress, the heavier beat on *one*; the /t/ is commonly a glottal stop, *nah(t)-WUN*
**Русский:** ни один — эмфатический вариант none, уместен в любом стиле
**Type:** emphatic negative quantifying pronoun phrase — the negation of the pronoun *one* (also a determiner, *not one drop*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — usable in every register, and the standard way to make *none* emphatic
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** not a single member of a set of countable things or people. Never a mass: ✗*not one of the sand*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Not one **was** returned*; *Not one of the machines **is** working.* The plural noun inside the *of*-phrase cannot pull the verb over, and ✗*Not one of the machines are working* — common in speech — is the error to watch for.
**Pattern:** *not one* [singular verb] &middot; *not one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *not one of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *not one* &middot; fronted with inversion for emphasis (*Not one of them did he trust*) &middot; *not one but two*, which corrects a count rather than denying it
**Contrast:** Against *none*: identical in meaning and different in force — *None of the applicants were qualified* reports the fact, *Not one of the applicants was qualified* insists on it, and that insistence is why the singular verb is obligatory here while *none* takes either number. Against *not a one*: the same emphasis one register lower. Against *no one*: that is people-only and does not take an *of*-phrase in current English — ✗*no one of them*; the form is *not one of them*.

Not a single one, with the count made an issue.

1. *Not one* of the smoke detectors had a battery in it.
2. Twelve letters went out and *not one* came back.
3. *Not one* of us knew what the meeting was for.
4. She checked every seal, and *not one* had been broken.
5. He made four promises that spring and kept *not one*.
6. *Not one* of the three explanations survives a second reading.

### one and all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ən ˈɔl/ &middot; *wun-uhn-AWL* &middot; the *and* reduces to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/; the main beat falls on *all*
**Русский:** все до одного, все и каждый — торжеств.-шутл., в тостах и приветствиях
**Type:** fixed quantifying pronoun phrase meaning "everyone" &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** ceremonial and often jocular — toasts, welcomes, announcements, greeting cards; not ordinary conversational English
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** every person present or addressed, taken individually (*one*) and collectively (*all*) at the same time — the doubling is the whole point of the idiom. People only.
**Agreement:** **plural** — *One and all **are** invited*; *One and all **were** on their feet.* Standing in apposition to a subject, it takes that subject's number: *They rose, one and all.*
**Pattern:** *one and all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *one and all* (*Welcome, one and all*) &middot; in apposition after the subject (*The staff, one and all, signed the card*) &middot; *to one and all* &middot; as a vocative (*Good evening, one and all*) &middot; no *of*-phrase: ✗*one and all of them*
**Contrast:** Against *all and sundry*: the same crowd from the other side — *one and all* is warm, *all and sundry* faintly disgusted. Against *everyone*: the neutral word, and singular in agreement (*Everyone is invited*), where this phrase is marked and plural (*One and all are invited*). Against *each and every one*: that is an emphatic distributive, singular, and it takes *of* (*each and every one of them*), where *one and all* takes none.

Everybody, greeted as a group and as individuals at once.

1. Happy New Year to *one and all*.
2. *One and all* were invited to the reopening.
3. The mayor thanked *one and all* for turning out in the rain.
4. They stood and applauded, *one and all*.
5. Welcome, *one and all*, to the fortieth season.
6. The verdict pleased *one and all* except the defendant.

### the whole

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈhoʊl/ &middot; *thuh HOHL* &middot; the *w* of *whole* is silent, so *the* takes its ordinary weak form /ðə/ before the consonant; *the whole of it* runs together as /ðə ˈhoʊləvɪt/, *thuh HOH-luh-vit*
**Русский:** целое, всё целиком — книжн., в противопоставлении частям; о людях не говорят
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the adjective *whole* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *the whole of the morning*, an adjective, *the whole loaf*, and a count noun, *a whole made of parts*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; the bare pronoun belongs to writing about parts and wholes, and the everyday phrase for the same thing is *all of it*
**Person / number:** third person; **singular, always** — it names one undivided thing, however many parts that thing has
**Refers to:** a thing taken entire and undivided, as against its parts — an object, a body of work, an estate, a stretch of time, an argument. Never a set of countables taken severally: ✗*The whole of the guests were late*; for that the word is *all*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *The whole **is** greater than the sum of its parts*; *The whole of the argument **rests** on one footnote.* The verb stays singular even with a plural noun after *of*, as long as the sense is genuinely "the thing entire": *The whole of the proceedings **was** recorded.* Where a plural verb is what you want, the pronoun you want is *all*.
**Pattern:** *the whole* [singular verb] &middot; *the whole of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *the whole of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *the whole* &middot; *as a whole*, *on the whole*, *taken as a whole* (fixed adverbials, not this pronoun) &middot; *the whole nine yards*, *the whole shebang* (idioms built on the adjective)
**Contrast:** Against *all*: *all* takes the members of a set together and goes plural (*All of the guests were late*), while *the whole* takes one thing entire and stays singular — *all of the cake* is every piece of it, *the whole cake* is the cake as an undivided thing, which is the distinction the [all](#all) entry points at from the other side. Against *the rest* and *the remainder*: those name what is left over, this names what was never divided. Against *half* and *a third*: the same axis with nothing cut off. And two frames are not this pronoun at all — *on the whole* and *as a whole* are fixed adverbials, and *the whole day* and *the whole truth* are the ordinary adjective in front of a noun.

A thing taken entire, set against the parts it is made of.

1. *The whole* is greater than the sum of its parts.
2. *The whole* of the estate went to a cousin nobody had met.
3. She spent *the whole* of the morning on hold with the county.
4. Two of the chapters are strong, but *the whole* never quite holds together.
5. *The whole* of the argument rests on a single mistranslated footnote.
6. He took the machine apart and could not put *the whole* back together.
7. *On the whole*, the repairs went better than anyone expected. (fixed adverbial, not the pronoun)

## The distributives: one at a time

*Each*, *either*, *neither*, *every one*, its emphatic twin *every last one*, and *one or the other* reach into a set and pull out its members individually. All six take a **singular verb**, and that singular survives no matter how plural the *of*-phrase looks. This is also where *every* has to be dealt with, because *every* alone is never a pronoun.

### each

**Pronunciation:** /itʃ/ &middot; *EECH* &middot; no weak form
**Русский:** каждый в отдельности — о членах определённого множества от двух и более
**Type:** distributive pronoun (also a determiner, *each student*, a floating quantifier, *they each got one*, and an adverb of distribution, *they cost five dollars each*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **grammatically singular**, always, however many members the set has
**Refers to:** the individual members of a definite set of two or more, considered separately — people or things, countables only. Never a mass: ✗*each of the sand*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Each **is** inspected before shipping*; *Each of the rooms **has** its own thermostat*, never ✗*have*. The head of the phrase is *each*, not the plural noun inside the *of*-phrase. **One systematic exception:** when *each* has floated out of the subject and sits after it, the subject is the plural pronoun and the verb is plural — *They each **have** a key.* For the pronoun referring back, singular *they* is standard American English: *Each of them submitted **their** own draft.*
**Pattern:** *each of* [determiner + plural noun] + singular verb &middot; *each of* [object pronoun] &middot; *each* [singular verb] &middot; [plural subject] *each* [plural verb] (floated) &middot; [plural subject] [auxiliary] *each* [verb] &middot; [verb] [object] *each* (adverbial: *cost a dollar each*) &middot; *each other* is a separate reciprocal pronoun
**Contrast:** **Each vs. every — and why *every* is not in this dictionary.** *Every* is a **determiner only**; it has no pronoun use whatsoever, so ✗*Every was checked* is ungrammatical and you must write *Every one was checked* in two words. *Each* is both determiner and pronoun. In meaning, *each* looks at the members one by one while *every* sweeps the whole set, which is why *every* accepts *almost* and *nearly* (*almost every one*) and *each* does not (✗*almost each*). *Each* also works for a set of exactly two (*a ring on each hand*), where *every* is odd. **Each vs. both:** *Each of them is* (singular) and *Both of them are* (plural) report the same fact with different grammar.

Picks the members of a known group out one at a time, so that whatever is said applies to every member separately.

1. *Each* of the samples was tested twice.
2. She handed out the folders, and *each* contained a different contract.
3. *Each* of us has a copy of the key.
4. They *each* brought something to the potluck.
5. She handed a numbered folder to *each* of us as we came in.
6. *Each* was asked to sign before leaving the building.
7. The tickets are forty dollars *each*. (adverb of distribution, not the pronoun)

**See also:** [every one](#every-one), [either](#either), [neither](#neither), [both](#both), [all](#all), [one](#one), [each other](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-other), [each (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [every (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every)

### either

**Pronunciation:** /ˈiðɚ/, also /ˈaɪðɚ/ &middot; *EE-thur*, also *EYE-thur* — /i/ is the dominant American vowel and /aɪ/ a recognized variant, neither one wrong
**Русский:** любой из двух — один из ровно двух, безразлично какой
**Type:** distributive pronoun (also a determiner, *either side*, half of the correlative *either … or*, and an adverb of negative agreement, *I don't either*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** one of exactly **two**, with no commitment as to which — people or things, countables only. With three or more it is wrong: ✗*either of the three*; use *any*.
**Agreement:** **singular in careful use** — *Either of the routes **is** fine*; ***Is** either of them available?* Plural verbs after *either of* are common in speech and turn up in print, but a conservative editor will change them, so the singular is the safe choice everywhere. (Separately: when the correlative *either … or* joins two subjects, the verb agrees with the **nearer** one — *Either the manager or the assistants **are** handling it.* That is the conjunction, not this pronoun.)
**Pattern:** *either of* [determiner + plural noun] + singular verb &middot; *either of* [object pronoun] &middot; *either* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *either* &middot; in questions: *Is either of them …?* &middot; under negation: *I don't want either*
**Contrast:** *Either* = one of the two, choice open; *neither* = not one and not the other; *both* = the two together, plural verb. Under negation *not … either* is equivalent to *neither*: *I don't want either* = *I want neither*. Do not stretch it past two — with three or more the word is *any*. The *either* of *a fence on either side* means "each of two" and is the determiner, not this entry; the *either* of *I don't like it either* is an adverb.

Names one of two possibilities without saying which, usually because either one would do.

1. *Either* of those dates works for me.
2. I asked both lawyers, and *either* would take the case.
3. Is *either* of them still open on Sundays?
4. You can take the bus or the train — *either* gets you there by six.
5. He didn't like *either* of the proposals.
6. If *either* of you hears from her, call me right away.

**See also:** [neither](#neither), [each](#each), [both](#both), [any](#any), [none](#none), [either (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#either)

### every last one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˌlæst ˈwʌn/ &middot; *ev-ree-last-WUN* &middot; three beats with the main stress on *one*; the /t/ of *last* is regularly lost in the cluster in front of it, *ev-ree-lass-WUN*
**Русский:** все до единого — разг., подчёркивает отсутствие исключений; и о вещах тоже
**Type:** emphatic quantifying pronoun phrase — the two-word *every one* with an intensifier wedged into it &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — ordinary in speech and in journalism, a shade too emphatic for a report
**Person / number:** third person; **singular, always**
**Refers to:** every member of a definite set, with the absence of exceptions made the point — **things as well as people**, countables only. Never a mass: ✗*every last one of the water*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Every last one **was** accounted for*; *Every last one of the bulbs **has** to be replaced.* The plural noun inside the *of*-phrase never pulls the verb over, exactly as under [every one](#every-one). Referring back, singular *they* is standard: *Every last one of the drivers renewed **their** license.*
**Pattern:** *every last one* [singular verb] &middot; *every last one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *every last one of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *every last one* (*She read every last one*) &middot; as a bare answer (*How many were broken? — Every last one.*)
**Variants:** *every single one* (the commonest of the family and the most neutral of them), *each and every one* (the most formulaic, and the one editors cut), *every blessed one* and *every damn one* (stronger, and marked accordingly), and the fuller *every last one of them*
**Contrast:** Against plain *every one*: identical in meaning and different in force — *Every one of them was returned* reports the fact, *Every last one of them was returned* insists on it, usually with exasperation or triumph attached. Against *all*: *all* takes the set together and goes plural (*All of them were returned*), this takes it one member at a time and stays singular. Against *not one* and *not a one*: the same emphatic gesture on the other side of the negative. And note where the intensifier can go: only inside the two-word *every one*, since the solid compound *everyone* admits nothing between its halves — ✗*every last everyone*, ✗*everylast one* — which is one more reminder that *everyone* and *every one* are different words.

Every single member of a group, with the completeness made an issue.

1. *Every last one* of the smoke detectors was dead.
2. She read the complaints, *every last one*, before she answered any of them.
3. They promised forty chairs and delivered *every last one* of them broken.
4. *Every last one* of us had been told a different story.
5. How many of the seedlings came up? — *Every last one*.
6. He kept the receipts for eleven years, *every single one* of them filed by month.

### every one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈwʌn/ &middot; *ev-ree-WUN* — two words, with the main stress on *one*. Compare the solid compound *everyone* /ˈɛvriwʌn/ &middot; *EV-ree-wun*, stressed on the first syllable.
**Русский:** каждый; все до одного — раздельно, также и о вещах, в отличие от everyone
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the pronoun that stands in for the determiner *every*, which has no pronoun form of its own &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** every member of a definite set — **things as well as people**, which is the point of it, since the one-word *everyone* refers to people only. Countables only.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Every one of the bulbs **was** burned out*; *Every one of them **has** to be signed.* Referring back, singular *they* is standard: *Every one of the drivers renewed **their** license.*
**Pattern:** *every one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *every one of* [object pronoun] &middot; *every one* standing alone (*I've read every one*) &middot; *every single one* (emphatic) &middot; *not every one* (partial negation) &middot; *almost / nearly every one*
**Contrast:** **This entry exists because *every* has no pronoun use at all.** ✗*Every was accounted for* is ungrammatical; the pronoun is *every one*, two words. Written solid, *everyone* is a compound indefinite pronoun that refers only to people and never takes *of*: ✗*everyone of them* is a spelling error for *every one of them*. Both take a singular verb, as every compound indefinite does — *everyone **is***, *somebody **was***, *nobody **has*** — while the pronoun referring back to them is normally singular *they*. **The test:** if you can insert *single* — *every single one* — it is two words. Against *each*: *every one* stresses that the sweep is complete, *each* that the members are separate; both take singular verbs, and *every one* accepts *almost* and *nearly* while *each* does not.

Covers every member of a group without exception, and unlike *everyone* it can point at objects.

1. *Every one* of the smoke detectors was dead.
2. She has read *every one* of his novels.
3. We ordered a dozen, and *every one* arrived broken.
4. *Every one* of them has to be signed in blue ink.
5. I checked the receipts, and *every single one* was from the same store.
6. Not *every one* of the complaints was justified.

**See also:** [each](#each), [all](#all), [one](#one), [none](#none), [everyone](07-indefinite-compound.md#everyone), [everything](07-indefinite-compound.md#everything), [every (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every)

### neither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniðɚ/, also /ˈnaɪðɚ/ &middot; *NEE-thur*, also *NY-thur* — /i/ dominant in American English
**Русский:** ни тот ни другой — ни один из ровно двух; при трёх и более — none
**Type:** negative distributive pronoun (also a determiner, *neither side*, half of the correlative *neither … nor*, and an adverb of negative agreement, *Neither do I*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** not one and not the other of exactly **two** — people or things, countables only. With three or more it is wrong: ✗*neither of the three*; use *none*.
**Agreement:** **singular** — *Neither of the answers **is** correct*; *Neither **was** available.* Plural verbs (*Neither of them were*) are frequent in speech and have a long history, but this is the one place in the group where a conservative editor reliably objects, and the singular is correct in every register. Referring back, singular *they* is standard: *Neither of the drivers admitted **they** were at fault.*
**Pattern:** *neither of* [determiner + plural noun] + singular verb &middot; *neither of* [object pronoun] &middot; *neither* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *neither* &middot; standing alone as an answer (*Neither, thanks*) &middot; *neither … nor* (correlative conjunction, a different use) &middot; *Neither do I*, *Neither am I* (adverb, with inversion)
**Contrast:** *Neither* is the negative of *either* and, like it, is limited to two. **One negative to a clause in edited writing:** ✗*I don't want neither* is nonstandard in standard American English. It is not carelessness — negative concord is regular and systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, where it is how negation is built — but it is stigmatized in every kind of formal writing, so write *I don't want either* or *I want neither*. Against *none*: *neither* for a set of two, *none* for three or more or for a mass. Against *nor*: *nor* is the conjunction (*neither fish nor fowl*) and cannot stand in for this pronoun.

Rejects both members of a pair at once — the negative counterpart of *both*.

1. *Neither* of the candidates was willing to debate.
2. I tried both keys, and *neither* worked.
3. *Neither* of us had eaten since breakfast.
4. She looked at the two offers; *neither* was serious.
5. *Neither* of those explanations accounts for the noise.
6. He apologized to *neither* of them.
7. Which one do you want? — *Neither*, thanks.

**See also:** [either](#either), [both](#both), [none](#none), [each](#each), [nobody](07-indefinite-compound.md#nobody), [neither (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#neither)

### one or the other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ɚ ði ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *wun-er-thee-UTH-er* &middot; *or* reduces to /ɚ/ and *the* before the vowel of *other* is /ði/, so the four words run together with the stress on *other*
**Русский:** тот или другой, одно из двух — из ровно двух известных вариантов
**Type:** distributive pronoun phrase — the positive counterpart of *neither*, over a set of exactly two &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** one of exactly **two** already-identified alternatives, with a commitment that it is one of them and none as to which. People or things, countables only. With three or more it is wrong: ✗*one or the other of the five*; the phrase is *one of them* or *any of them*.
**Agreement:** **singular** — *One or the other **is** going to have to give*; *One or the other of the tenants **has** been feeding it.*
**Pattern:** *one or the other* [singular verb] &middot; *one or the other of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *one or the other of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *one or the other* &middot; *it has to be one or the other* &middot; *one way or the other* (an adverbial, not this pronoun)
**Contrast:** Against *either*: *either* leaves the choice genuinely open and often means that both would do (*Either works for me*), while *one or the other* insists that a choice must be made and that there is no third option (*It has to be one or the other*). Against *neither*: the negative over the same pair. Against *another*: that adds a further item to an open set, where this phrase closes the set at two — which is what the definite *the other* inside it is doing, and why the phrase cannot be stretched past two.

One of two, when the two are all there is.

1. You can't have both — it's *one or the other*.
2. *One or the other* of them has been leaving the gate open.
3. Either he apologizes or she does; *one or the other* has to move first.
4. The noise is the compressor or the fan, *one or the other*.
5. We can afford the roof or the furnace, and I would take *one or the other* over neither.
6. *One or the other* of those two dates is going to work.

**See also:** [either](#either), [neither](#neither), [both](#both), [other](#other), [another](#another), [each](#each), [one](#one), [one or two (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two)

## Some, any, and enough: indefinite amounts

The assertive/non-assertive pair plus the sufficiency word, with three relatives of *any* and *some* alongside them. *Any*, *enough*, and *some* bend their number to what they refer to, and all three are pronouns only when they stand alone or take *of* — in front of a bare noun they are determiners. *Any one* picks out a single member and is always singular, *any of the above* points at a printed list instead of at the world, and *certain* is the formal partitive for *some* that can never stand alone at all. With them sits *one or more*, the drafting phrase written to keep a rule true whether the count turns out to be one or twenty.

### any

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛni/ &middot; *EN-ee* &middot; regionally /ˈɪni/ &middot; *IN-ee* in Southern and South Midland speech; the word resists reduction even when unstressed
**Русский:** какой-нибудь, сколько-нибудь; никакой — форма вместо some в отрицаниях, вопросах, условиях
**Type:** non-assertive quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *any milk*, and an adverb of degree, *any better*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** with the referent
**Refers to:** an unspecified member, members, or amount of a set — countables (people or things) or a mass. It is the word that appears where *some* cannot: in negatives, questions, conditionals, and after words carrying negative force (*hardly*, *without*, *refuse*, *doubt*).
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to.** Plural countable referent → plural verb, and this is the default: ***Are** any of the seats still free?* Mass referent → singular: ***Is** any of the soup left?* The singular also appears with a plural *of*-phrase when the sense is "any single one" — *If any of these excuses **is** true, I'll be amazed* — and both numbers are standard there.
**Pattern:** *any of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *any of* [object pronoun] &middot; *any* [verb] &middot; [negative] + [verb] + *any* (*I don't have any*) &middot; [question] + *any* &middot; *if* + *any* &middot; *hardly any*, *scarcely any*, *not any*
**Contrast:** **Any vs. some.** *Some* is assertive and belongs in plain statements; *any* is non-assertive and belongs in negatives, questions, and conditionals: *I have some* / *I don't have any* / *Do you have any?* The two exceptions are worth knowing: *some* appears in a question when you expect or are inviting a yes (*Would you like some?*), and *any* appears in a plain statement when it means free choice, "no matter which" (*Any of them will do*). *Not any* is equivalent to *none*: *I didn't buy any* = *I bought none*. Before a bare noun *any* is a determiner (*any milk*); alone or with *of*, a pronoun.

Points at an unspecified part of a group or quantity — in the non-assertive contexts, at a part whose existence is in question.

**1. Non-assertive: in negatives, questions, and conditionals.**

1. I looked for eggs, but there weren't *any*.
2. Have *any* of them replied yet?
3. If *any* of this sounds familiar, say so now.
4. She wanted advice, and I didn't have *any* to give.

**2. Free choice: no matter which one.** Assertive, and usually singular.

5. Pick a card — *any* will do.
6. *Any* of these routes gets you downtown by seven.

**See also:** [some](#some), [none](#none), [either](#either), [enough](#enough), [anyone](07-indefinite-compound.md#anyone), [anything](07-indefinite-compound.md#anything), [any (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#any)

### any of the above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni əv ði əˈbʌv/ &middot; *EN-ee-uv-thee-uh-BUV* &middot; the *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/, and *the* before the vowel of *above* is /ði/
**Русский:** любое из вышеперечисленного — язык бланков, инструкций, гарантий, спецификаций
**Type:** non-assertive quantifying pronoun phrase, **deictic** &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — the language of forms, instructions, warranties, and specifications
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** with the referent
**Refers to:** any item at all from a list just given. Both the non-assertive *any* of negatives and questions and the free-choice *any* of "no matter which" apply to it, and its antecedent is **textual** — lines on a page rather than things in the world.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *If any of the above **apply**, call the office* (the listed conditions, plural, and the ordinary case) and *If any of the above **is** true, stop here* (any single one, singular) are both standard, exactly as under [any](#any).
**Pattern:** *if any of the above* [verb] &middot; [negative] + [verb] + *any of the above* &middot; [verb] *any of the above* &middot; *any of the above* + relative clause &middot; standing alone on a checklist
**Contrast:** Against *all of the above* and *none of the above*: one list, three quantities. Against bare *any*: the antecedent is the whole difference — *any of them* points at things already in the conversation, *any of the above* at lines on the page, which makes the phrase almost unusable in speech and hard to do without on a form. *The above* is a noun here, so ✗*any of above* is not English.

Any item at all from the list just given.

1. Check the box if *any of the above* apply to you.
2. Damage caused by *any of the above* is excluded from coverage.
3. He denied that *any of the above* had ever been discussed.
4. If *any of the above* is unclear, ask before you sign.
5. The grant is void if the applicant fails *any of the above*.
6. Symptoms may include fever, rash, or joint pain; call your doctor if you develop *any of the above*.

**See also:** [all of the above](#all-of-the-above), [none of the above](#none-of-the-above), [any](#any), [any one](#any-one), [some](#some), [either](#either), [any (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#any)

### any one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈwʌn/ &middot; *en-ee-WUN* &middot; two words, with the main stress on *one*; compare the solid compound *anyone* /ˈɛniwʌn/ &middot; *EN-ee-wun*, stressed on the first syllable
**Русский:** любой один, какой-нибудь один — раздельно, также и о вещах, в отличие от anyone
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the singular-picking counterpart of the determiner *any*, and the partner of *every one* &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** a single unspecified member of a set — **things as well as people**, which is the point of the two-word spelling, since the one-word *anyone* covers people only. Countables only: ✗*any one of the water*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Any one of the fuses **is** enough to trip it*; *Any one of them **was** capable of it.* Referring back, singular *they* is standard: *Any one of the drivers could have left **their** lights on.*
**Pattern:** *any one of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *any one of* [object pronoun] &middot; *any one* standing alone (*Take any one*) &middot; *any single one* (emphatic) &middot; *at any one time*, *no more than any one* (determiner uses, not this pronoun)
**Contrast:** **The spelling is the meaning.** Written solid, *anyone* refers to people only and never takes *of* — ✗*anyone of them* is a misspelling of *any one of them*. Written as two words, *any one* takes *of* freely and points at objects as readily as at people. The test is the one that separates *everyone* from *every one*: if you can insert *single*, it is two words. Against *either*: *either* is limited to a set of two, *any one* wants three or more. Against *every one*: the same shape and the opposite quantity — one member against all of them.

Any single member of a set, people or things alike.

1. *Any one* of these keys should open the shed.
2. The chain will break at *any one* of a dozen weak points.
3. *Any one* of them could have made the call.
4. Losing *any one* of the three would sink the deal.
5. Take *any one* and leave the rest for the next shift.
6. The failure of *any one* of the pumps shuts down the whole line.

**See also:** [every one](#every-one), [any](#any), [any of the above](#any-of-the-above), [one](#one), [either](#either), [none](#none), [anyone](07-indefinite-compound.md#anyone), [anything](07-indefinite-compound.md#anything), [any (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#any)

### certain

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɚtn̩/ &middot; *SUR-tn* &middot; the second syllable is a syllabic /n/ with no vowel of its own and the /t/ is usually a glottal stop, so the word comes out as two beats, *SUR-'n*
**Русский:** некоторые, определённые — книжн., о намеренно не названных лицах или вещах
**Type:** quantifying pronoun, **partitive only** — it lives in the frame *certain of* + noun phrase and nowhere else (also a determiner, *certain people*, and the adjective of confidence, *I'm certain*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal and literary — reports, minutes, and legal prose; the everyday word for the same quantity is *some*
**Person / number:** third person; **plural**
**Refers to:** some particular but deliberately unnamed members of a set of countables — people or things — with the implication that the writer knows which ones and is declining to say. Never a mass: ✗*certain of the water*.
**Agreement:** **plural** — *Certain of the members **have** objected*; *Certain of them **were** never informed.*
**Pattern:** *certain of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *certain of* [object pronoun] &middot; and nothing else — bare ✗*Certain refused* and ✗*I spoke to certain* are impossible, so unlike *some* and *most* this pronoun can never stand alone
**Contrast:** Against *some*: the plain word for the same quantity, at home in every register and in every frame; *certain of* adds formality and a note of discretion. Against the determiner *certain*: in front of a noun the word is a determiner (*certain irregularities*, *a certain reluctance*) and belongs to the determiner dictionary — the pronoun exists only after *of*. And keep both apart from the adjective *certain* meaning sure (*I am certain of it*), which also takes *of*: the giveaway is that the adjective has a personal subject and can be graded (*more certain*, *quite certain*), while the pronoun can do neither.

Some particular members of a group, unnamed on purpose.

1. *Certain* of the trustees have asked to see the accounts.
2. *Certain* of them were never told the meeting had moved.
3. The report is sharply critical of *certain* of the practices it describes.
4. *Certain* of the older tenants remember when the building had a doorman.
5. She declined to name *certain* of the parties involved.
6. *Certain* of the documents had been removed before the audit began.

**See also:** [some](#some), [several](#several), [a few](#a-few), [others](#others), [most](#most), [certain (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain), [a certain (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/01-articles.md#a-certain)

### enough

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈnʌf/, weak /əˈnʌf/ &middot; *ih-NUFF*, *uh-NUFF* — the first vowel is a schwa in ordinary speech and can drop entirely in the fixed phrase *'nough said*
**Русский:** достаточно, довольно — достаточное количество или число
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *enough chairs*, and an adverb that **follows** what it modifies, *warm enough*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular** with a mass and **plural** with countables
**Refers to:** a sufficient amount of a mass or a sufficient number of countables; also, in the idiom *I've had enough*, an abstract quantity of a situation.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *Enough of the money **is** left to cover rent* (mass, singular); *Enough of the volunteers **have** shown up to hold the vote* (countable, plural). The fixed sentence *Enough is enough* is singular.
**Pattern:** *enough* [verb] &middot; *enough of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *enough of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *enough* (*I've had enough*) &middot; *enough to* [infinitive] &middot; *more than enough*, *not nearly enough*, *quite enough*
**Contrast:** Against *plenty*: *enough* is exactly the amount needed and claims nothing beyond it; *plenty* is comfortably more than needed. The *of* is obligatory before a determiner or pronoun (*enough of the cake*, *enough of them*, ✗*enough them*), and bare *enough* is used when the referent is already understood. **The position trap:** as a determiner *enough* goes before the noun (*enough chairs*), but as an adverb it goes **after** the adjective (*warm enough*, ✗*enough warm*) — this is the most common error with the word, and it is not a pronoun error at all.

Names the amount or number that is sufficient for a purpose — no more and no less.

1. There's coffee left, but not *enough* for four people.
2. I've had *enough* of the excuses.
3. Buy two boxes — one isn't *enough*.
4. *Enough* of them showed up to hold the vote.
5. *Enough* is *enough*.
6. She had saved *enough* to cover the deposit.

**See also:** [plenty](#plenty), [a lot](#a-lot), [much](#much), [many](#many), [more](#more), [enough (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#enough)

### one or more

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ɚ ˈmɔr/ &middot; *wun-er-MOR* &middot; *or* reduces to /ɚ/ and never carries stress, so the three words come out in two beats
**Русский:** один или несколько — юр. и тех.: «не менее одного»; в речи at least one
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — a disjunction of *one* and *more* used as a single indefinite quantity &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal and technical — statutes, regulations, contracts, standards, forms, and specifications; ordinary speech says *at least one* or *some*
**Person / number:** third person; construes **singular or plural**, with the plural the ordinary choice
**Refers to:** at least one member of a set of **countables**, with no upper bound — things or people. Never a mass: ✗*one or more of the water*. Its whole reason for existing is to keep a rule true whether the count turns out to be one or twenty.
**Agreement:** **either, and the verb follows the nearer conjunct.** *One or more of the following **apply*** is the ordinary form and the one standards and statutes print, because *more* is the conjunct next to the verb; *One or more of the following **applies***, agreeing with *one*, is also standard and is common in older drafting. American editors accept both, and the plural is the safer default.
**Pattern:** *one or more* [verb] &middot; *one or more of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *one or more of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *one or more* &middot; *one or more of the following* &middot; the parallel *two or more*, *three or more*, *one or two*
**Contrast:** Against *any*: *any* leaves the existence of the thing open (*if any of them apply*), while *one or more* asserts that at least one does. Against *some*: the everyday word, vaguer, and unusable in a rule that has to be exact. Against *at least one*: the same meaning in plainer English, and what a plain-language editor substitutes. Against bare *one*: that commits to exactly one, which is precisely the commitment this phrase is drafted to avoid. Because it is drafting language it belongs where drafting language belongs, and **never in** narrative or conversation — *one or more of us was hungry* is a joke, not a sentence.

At least one, with the count deliberately left open — the phrase a drafter writes to keep a rule true either way.

1. *One or more* of the following documents must be attached to the application.
2. The policy is void if *one or more* of the conditions are not met.
3. *One or more* of them had clearly read the file before the hearing.
4. Check the box if *one or more* of the above apply to you.
5. Applicants may be interviewed by *one or more* of the committee members.
6. The statute takes effect whenever *one or more* of the parties resides in the state.

**See also:** [any one](#any-one), [any](#any), [some](#some), [several](#several), [one](#one), [two](#two), [any of the above](#any-of-the-above), [one or more (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one-or-more), [one or two (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two)

### some

**Pronunciation:** /sʌm/ stressed, weak /səm/ and in fast speech syllabic /sm̩/ &middot; *SUM*, weak *suhm* — *some of them* runs together as /ˈsʌməvəm/ &middot; *SUM-uh-vum*
**Русский:** некоторые, часть, некоторое количество — в утвердительных предложениях
**Type:** assertive quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *some milk*, and an informal adverb of degree, *she slept some*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** an unspecified part of a set or quantity — countable things or people (plural), or a mass (singular). Bare *some* as a subject can also refer to people in general, as in *Some say it was never finished*.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *Some of the chairs **are** broken* (countable → plural); *Some of the paint **is** dry* (mass → singular). This is the same rule that governs *all*, *any*, *most*, *more*, and *none*: with these the *of*-phrase decides the number, not the pronoun.
**Pattern:** *some of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *some of* [object pronoun] &middot; *some* [verb] (*Some disagree*) &middot; [verb] *some* (*I'd like some*) &middot; *some … others …* &middot; *some more*, *and some*
**Contrast:** **Some vs. any** — *some* in statements and in offers or requests where you expect a yes (*Would you like some?*), *any* in negatives, questions, and conditionals. Against *a few* and *a little*: *some* is neutral about how much, *a few* commits to a small count, *a little* to a small amount. Stressed /sʌm/ *some* means "certain ones as opposed to others" (*Some of us disagree*); unstressed /səm/ it just means an unspecified part. Before a bare noun *some* is a determiner, not this pronoun.

Names an unspecified part of a larger set or quantity, without saying which part or how much.

**1. An unspecified part or number of something.**

1. *Some* of the windows were painted shut.
2. There's soup on the stove if you want *some*.
3. *Some* of the evidence was thrown out before trial.

**2. Contrasted with the rest.** Often paired with *others* or *the rest*.

4. He kept *some* and gave the rest away.
5. *Some* arrived early; *others* never showed up at all.

**3. People in general, as a bare subject.** Formal in tone, common in journalism.

6. *Some* say the road was never meant to be finished.

**See also:** [any](#any), [all](#all), [most](#most), [none](#none), [others](#others), [a few](#a-few), [someone](07-indefinite-compound.md#someone), [something](07-indefinite-compound.md#something), [some (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#some)

## Large quantity

The words for a lot of something. *Many* and *several* count and are always plural, and so do *a good many*, *quite a few*, *a slew*, and the order-of-magnitude plurals *hundreds*, *thousands*, *millions*, and *dozens*; *much* and *a great deal* measure and are always singular; *more*, *most*, *plenty*, *a lot*, and *lots* do both and take their number from the referent. So do the hyperbolic informal ones — *a bunch*, *a ton*, *tons*, *loads*, *heaps*, *oodles*, *scads*, *gobs*, *a boatload*, and the vulgar *a shitload* — which differ from one another in register, in nationality, and in how much offense they can give, rather than in grammar: *tons*, *a bunch*, *scads*, and *gobs* are American, *loads* is British and has crossed over, *heaps* is British and Australian and has not, *oodles* is an American joke word, and *a shitload* is the one that cannot go in a letter to a client, for which *a boatload* is the standard repair. Two idioms in the group say the opposite of what their parts say: *quite a few* is a fairly large number and *a good many* is more than the listener expected.

### a boatload

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈboʊtloʊd/ &middot; *uh-BOHT-lohd* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/, and the /t/ is unreleased or glottal before the /l/, so the word comes out in two beats with the stress on the first
**Русский:** куча, вагон — разг., но не бранное; о большом количестве
**Type:** hyperbolic quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a boatload of money*, and the literal count noun, *a boatload of gravel*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal but **not vulgar** — the printable member of the *-load* family, at home in speech, in journalism, and in casual writing
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** a large number of countables or a large amount of a mass, stated as open exaggeration — money, work, trouble, complaints, people.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *A boatload of them **were** returned* (countable → plural); *A boatload of the money **was** never accounted for* (mass → singular). The literal cargo behaves like any other count noun and stays singular: *A boatload of gravel **was** delivered Tuesday.*
**Pattern:** *a boatload* [verb] &middot; *a boatload of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *a boatload of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a boatload* (*They made a boatload*) &middot; *a whole boatload*, *boatloads of*
**Variants:** *a truckload*, *a busload*, *a carload*, *a trainload*, and the plural *boatloads*; the same frame with a rude noun in it gives [a shitload](#a-shitload) and its minced relatives
**Contrast:** Against *a shitload*: the identical construction with the vulgarity taken out, which is exactly what it is for — an American who wants that much force in a sentence a client, a professor, or a stranger will read writes *a boatload*. Against *a ton* and *tons*: interchangeable in sense, with *a boatload* the more visual of them. Against *a lot*: the plain word, and the one to use when no exaggeration is wanted. In formal writing all of them go to *a great deal*, *many*, or *a large number*. And note the adverbial gap: *thanks a ton* is fixed and ✗*thanks a boatload* is not idiomatic.

A large quantity, exaggerated out loud, and the one member of the *-load* family that is safe to print anywhere.

1. They spent *a boatload* on lawyers before the case ever reached trial.
2. *A boatload* of them arrived damaged and had to be sent back.
3. She knows *a boatload* about municipal bonds and says almost nothing about it.
4. We got *a boatload* of complaints the week the new machine went in.
5. *A boatload* of the paperwork turned out to be duplicates.
6. He promised *a boatload* and delivered a folder.

**See also:** [a shitload](#a-shitload), [a slew](#a-slew), [a ton](#a-ton), [tons](#tons), [a lot](#a-lot), [a bunch](#a-bunch), [gobs](#gobs), [scads](#scads), [a shitload of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-shitload-of), [tons of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#tons-of)

### a bunch

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbʌntʃ/ &middot; *uh-BUNCH* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed; before *of* the phrase runs together, *a bunch of them* /əˈbʌntʃəvəm/ &middot; *uh-BUN-chuh-vum*
**Русский:** куча, кучка, полно — разг., неопределённо большое количество
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a bunch of questions*, and an ordinary count noun, *a bunch of grapes*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal — casual speech and casual writing; an editor replaces it with *several*, *many*, or *a number of*
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** an indefinite largish number of countable things or people, or amount of a mass — work, money, trouble. The literal cluster tied or grown together (*a bunch of bananas*) is the count noun, not this pronoun.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *A bunch of them **were** late* (countable → plural); *A bunch of the paint **was** ruined* (mass → singular). The count noun goes the other way and stays singular: *That bunch of grapes **is** overripe.*
**Pattern:** *a bunch* [verb] &middot; *a bunch of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *a bunch of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a bunch* (*I bought a bunch*) &middot; *a whole bunch*, *a bunch more* &middot; adverbial *thanks a bunch*
**Contrast:** Against *a lot*: the same job one notch more casual, and still faintly conscious of its origin in things gathered together, so it sits more comfortably with countables than with abstractions. Against *several* and *a number of*: what a copy editor puts in its place. *Thanks a bunch* is an adverbial and is more often sarcastic than grateful; *bunches* is the count noun and not this pronoun. Note the difference the article makes: *a bunch of us* is several of us, *the bunch of us* is all of us.

The casual word for a fair number of things, or a fair amount of one.

1. We ordered too much and had *a bunch* left over.
2. *A bunch* of them showed up without tickets.
3. She asked me to bring chairs, so I brought *a bunch*.
4. *A bunch* of the paperwork was still sitting in his inbox.
5. I had *a bunch* to say and no chance to say it.
6. Of the twenty who signed up, *a bunch* never came back.

**See also:** [a lot](#a-lot), [lots](#lots), [a ton](#a-ton), [tons](#tons), [loads](#loads), [several](#several), [many](#many), [plenty](#plenty), [a bunch of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-bunch-of)

### a good many

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡʊd ˈmɛni/ &middot; *uh-good-MEN-ee* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/, *good* keeps a light secondary beat, and the main stress falls on *many*, whose vowel is /ɛ/ and not /æ/
**Русский:** немало, изрядное число — слегка книжн.; только о счётных, для массы a good deal
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — *many* with a degree word fused in front of it (also a determiner, *a good many years*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a faintly bookish and older-American flavor; the everyday equivalents are *a lot* and *quite a few*
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a large number of **countable** things or people — more than *several*, and offered as more than the listener would guess. Never a mass: ✗*a good many of the water*; the mass counterpart is *a good deal*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *A good many **were** turned away*; *A good many of the houses **have** been rebuilt.* The indefinite article at the front changes nothing, because the head of the phrase is *many*, and there is no singular use at all.
**Pattern:** *a good many* [plural verb] &middot; *a good many of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *a good many of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a good many* &middot; the determiner *a good many* + plural noun (*a good many years*) &middot; the stronger *a great many*
**Variants:** *a great many* (stronger and more formal), the regional *a fair many*, and the mass counterparts *a good deal* and *a great deal*
**Contrast:** Against bare *many*: *many* is neutral and slightly formal as a bare subject, while *a good many* reads as a considered estimate — it says "more than you would think," which is why it so often follows a concession. Against *quite a few*: nearly the same quantity and the same effect, one notch more casual. Against *a good deal*: the mass twin, and the count/mass line between them is absolute — *a good many of the years* but *a good deal of the time*. The article cannot be dropped: ✗*good many of them came*.

A large number of countable things, offered as more than the listener probably expects.

1. *A good many* of the original tenants are still in the building.
2. Of the promises made that spring, *a good many* were quietly dropped.
3. He has *a good many* of them stored out in the garage.
4. *A good many* would disagree with you about that.
5. She interviewed sixty people and found *a good many* who remembered the fire.
6. *A good many* of us had never flown before that morning.

**See also:** [many](#many), [quite a few](#quite-a-few), [a great deal](#a-great-deal), [a lot](#a-lot), [several](#several), [a few](#a-few), [most](#most), [a good many (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-good-many), [a great many (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-great-many)

### a great deal

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡreɪt ˈdil/ &middot; *uh-grayt-DEEL* — the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed, and the /t/ of *great* is normally unreleased or glottal before the /d/, so the phrase comes out as two beats, *uh-gray(t)-DEEL*
**Русский:** очень много, масса — только о неисчисляемом; нейтр.-книжн.
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a great deal of money*, and an adverb of degree, *she travels a great deal*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Person / number:** third person; **singular, always** — the mass counterpart of *a lot*, and no plural sense is open to it
**Refers to:** a large amount of a **mass** or an abstraction — work, time, money, trouble, attention, knowledge. Never countable things: ✗*a great deal of the chairs*, ✗*A great deal of them were returned*; for countables the words are *many*, *a lot*, and *a good many*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *A great deal **has** changed since then*; *A great deal of the money **is** still unaccounted for.* The head of the phrase is the noun *deal*, so no plural inside the *of*-phrase can pull the verb over: ✗*A great deal of the evidence **were** ruled inadmissible*.
**Pattern:** *a great deal* [singular verb] &middot; *a great deal of* [determiner + mass noun] &middot; *a great deal of* [it / this / that] &middot; [verb] *a great deal* (*He knows a great deal*) &middot; *a great deal more*, *a great deal less* (degree modifier) &middot; the variants *a good deal* and, informally, *quite a bit*
**Contrast:** Against *a lot* and *lots*: the three mean the same thing and differ in **register only** — *lots* is casual, *a lot* is everyday, *a great deal* is the formal one, and it is what the [a lot](#a-lot), [lots](#lots), and [much](#much) entries send you to when the writing has to be edited upward. Against *much*: the two overlap in the mass slot, but bare *much* is stilted in a plain positive statement (✗*We have much*) exactly where *a great deal* is ordinary (*We have a great deal to discuss*). Against *many*: the count/mass line is absolute — *a great deal of work* but *many jobs*, and ✗*a great deal of people* is an error, not a stylistic choice. Before a **pronoun** or a **determiner** the *of* is obligatory (*a great deal of it*, *a great deal of the money*, ✗*a great deal it*); bare *a great deal* is used only when the referent is already understood. Keep the phrase apart from the ordinary count noun *deal* (*a good deal on a car*, *the deal fell through*, *two deals*), which pluralizes and has nothing to do with quantity.

A large amount of something measured rather than counted, said a register above *a lot*.

1. *A great deal* has changed since the last inspection.
2. *A great deal* of the money is still unaccounted for.
3. She knows *a great deal* about eighteenth-century printing.
4. He promised *a great deal* and delivered almost none of it.
5. Of the evidence collected that night, *a great deal* was later ruled inadmissible.
6. They spent *a great deal* of it on lawyers before the case ever reached trial.

**See also:** [a lot](#a-lot), [lots](#lots), [much](#much), [plenty](#plenty), [more](#more), [little](#little), [a great deal of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-great-deal-of)

### a lot

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈlɑt/ &middot; *uh-LAHT* — the /t/ is usually flapped or cut off in speech. Always two words: ✗*alot* is a misspelling, never a variant.
**Русский:** много, куча — обычное разговорное «много», и о счётных, и о массе
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a lot of money*, and an adverb, *she reads a lot*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; avoid in formal writing
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** with the referent
**Refers to:** a large number of countables or a large amount of a mass — things, people, or abstractions. It is the word American English actually uses in positive statements where *much* and *many* would sound stiff.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *A lot of the students **are** commuters* (countable → plural); *A lot of the work **is** already done* (mass → singular). Bare *A lot **has** changed* is singular because the referent is an unspecified mass of things.
**Pattern:** *a lot* [verb] &middot; *a lot of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *a lot of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a lot* &middot; *quite a lot*, *an awful lot*, *a whole lot* &middot; *a lot more*, *a lot less*
**Contrast:** Against *much* and *many*: in a plain positive statement, *a lot* is the natural choice and *much* is nearly impossible (*I ate a lot*, ✗*I ate much*), while *much* and *many* are perfectly natural in negatives and questions (*I didn't eat much*, *How many are left?*). Against *lots*: same meaning, same agreement, one notch more casual. In formal writing replace all three with *many*, *much*, [a great deal](#a-great-deal), or *a large number*. Note the spelling once more: two words.

The everyday word for a large quantity, whether counted or measured.

1. We invited thirty people, and *a lot* showed up.
2. *A lot* of the paperwork is still sitting on his desk.
3. She had savings once, but not *a lot*.
4. *A lot* of them were returned unopened.
5. *A lot* has changed since you were here last.
6. Of the original crew, *a lot* had already quit by June.

**See also:** [lots](#lots), [a great deal](#a-great-deal), [many](#many), [much](#much), [plenty](#plenty), [most](#most), [enough](#enough), [a lot of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-lot-of)

### a shitload

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈʃɪtloʊd/ &middot; *uh-SHIT-lohd* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/, and the /t/ is unreleased or glottal before the /l/, so the word comes out in two beats with the stress on the first
**Русский:** груб.: дохрена, до хрена — количество то же, что у a lot, грубость лишь усиливает
**Type:** **vulgar** hyperbolic quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a shitload of money*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **vulgar slang** — casual speech among people who already swear in front of one another, and writing that is imitating it. **Never in** a cover letter, a client email, a court filing, a paper, a press release, or anything a stranger will read; the printable substitutes are *a boatload*, *a ton*, and *a lot*.
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** a large number of countables or a large amount of a mass — money, work, trouble, time, people. The vulgarity carries emphasis and nothing else: the quantity named is exactly the quantity *a lot* names.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *A shitload of them **were** returned* (countable → plural); *A shitload of the money **was** gone* (mass → singular). The plural *shitloads* behaves the same way.
**Pattern:** *a shitload* [verb] &middot; *a shitload of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *a shitload of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a shitload* (*They made a shitload*) &middot; *a whole shitload*, *shitloads of* &middot; adverbial *a shitload better*
**Variants:** the minced *a crapload* and *a buttload*, mild enough to print in more places; the intensified *a shit ton* and *a fuckton*, the second of which is stronger than this entry and not weaker; and the entirely clean *a boatload*, *a truckload*, and *a busload*, dictionaried at [a boatload](#a-boatload)
**Contrast:** Against the **vulgar minimizers** *squat*, *diddly-squat*, and *jack shit*: the same register aimed the other way, and grammatically a different animal — *He knows squat* already means "he knows nothing" with no negative word anywhere in the sentence, while *a shitload* has no such negative-polarity behavior and simply means "a large amount." Against *a boatload*: the same construction with the vulgarity removed, and the usual repair when the sentence has to be shown to somebody. Against *a lot* and *tons*: the neutral and the merely informal members of the family. Nothing but force is lost in the substitution, which is worth knowing, because nothing about the word makes it more precise than *a lot*.

A large quantity, said with a swear word in it for emphasis.

1. They spent *a shitload* on the renovation and still can't sell the place.
2. *A shitload* of them showed up without tickets.
3. She knows *a shitload* about early punk labels.
4. We had *a shitload* of trouble getting the permit signed.
5. *A shitload* of the wiring had to be pulled out and replaced.
6. He promised *a shitload* and delivered almost nothing.

**See also:** [a boatload](#a-boatload), [a slew](#a-slew), [gobs](#gobs), [scads](#scads), [a ton](#a-ton), [tons](#tons), [a lot](#a-lot), [diddly, diddly-squat, squat](07-indefinite-compound.md#diddly-diddly-squat-squat), [jack, jack shit](07-indefinite-compound.md#jack-jack-shit), [a shitload of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-shitload-of)

### a slew

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈslu/ &middot; *uh-SLOO* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/; the noun is a homophone of the past tense *slew* of *slay* and of the *slough* that means a swamp, and it is related to neither — it comes from Irish *sluagh*, a crowd
**Русский:** целая куча, вал — амер., обычно о неприятном потоке счётных вещей
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a slew of complaints*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral and distinctly **American** — ordinary in speech and in journalism, and mild enough that copy editors often leave it alone
**Person / number:** third person; **plural**
**Refers to:** a large and usually unwelcome number of **countable** things or people arriving together — complaints, lawsuits, injuries, amendments, applicants, errors. Never a mass: ✗*a slew of the money*.
**Agreement:** **plural** — *A slew of them **were** rejected*; *A slew of complaints **have** come in since Friday.* The singular *A slew of complaints **has** come in*, treating the batch as one arrival, also occurs and is unobjectionable; the plural is much the commoner.
**Pattern:** *a slew of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *a slew of* [object pronoun] &middot; *a slew* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *a slew* &middot; *a whole slew of*
**Contrast:** Against *a bunch* and *a lot*: the same size with a different attitude — a slew arrives all at once and is usually a problem, which is why journalists reach for it in front of *complaints*, *injuries*, and *lawsuits*. Against *several* and *a number of*: the neutral words, and what an editor substitutes when the tone has to come down. Against *a shitload*: the same job with no vulgarity attached. Bare *a slew* as a subject is rare and strained; the phrase wants its *of*-phrase.

A large batch of countable things arriving together, usually as a problem.

1. *A slew* of complaints came in the week the new hours took effect.
2. The bill drew *a slew* of amendments before it ever reached the floor.
3. Of the applications filed that month, *a slew* were missing signatures.
4. She has *a slew* of them stacked on the passenger seat.
5. *A slew* of injuries kept the team out of the playoffs.
6. He answered two questions and dodged *a slew* of others.

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhʌndrɪdz/, in ordinary speech /ˈhʌnɚdz/ &middot; *HUN-dridz*, also *HUN-nerdz* &middot; the /d/ inside the cluster is regularly lost in casual American speech, which is why the careless spelling *hunderds* keeps turning up
**Русский:** сотни — неопределённо большое число счётных; о массе не говорят

**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**

**Contrast:** Against *lots*, *loads*, and *tons*: all four are informal words for a large quantity, and *oodles* is the only one that is also **jocular** — using it is a small stylistic act, and readers hear the whimsy whether or not it was intended. Against *scads*, *gobs*, and *a slew*: the same American family of hyperbolic quantity words, none of which belongs in edited prose. Bare *oodles* as a subject is rare; the word wants an *of*-phrase or an object slot.

A large quantity, said cheerfully.

1. She has *oodles* of time and no idea what to do with it.
2. There's *oodles* left in the freezer.
3. *Oodles* of them were handed out along the parade route.
4. He inherited the house and *oodles* besides.
5. *Oodles* of the frosting ended up on the floor.
6. We asked for a little help and got *oodles*.

**See also:** [lots](#lots), [loads](#loads), [tons](#tons), [a ton](#a-ton), [heaps](#heaps), [a lot](#a-lot), [plenty](#plenty), [oodles of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#oodles-of)

### plenty

**Pronunciation:** /ˈplɛnti/, with the American dropped /t/ after /n/ also /ˈplɛni/ &middot; *PLEN-tee*, also *PLEN-ee*
**Русский:** полно, вдоволь — вполне достаточно, и о счётных, и о массе
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *plenty of room*, and an informal adverb, *plenty big*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** with the referent
**Refers to:** an amply sufficient amount of a mass or number of countables — things, people, time, money. It covers both types, which is part of why speakers reach for it instead of choosing between *much* and *many*.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *Plenty of the seats **are** still open* (countable → plural); *Plenty of time **remains*** (mass → singular). *There's plenty* is singular on a mass reading; *There are plenty left* is plural when the referent is countable.
**Pattern:** *plenty* [verb] &middot; *plenty of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *plenty of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *plenty* (*We have plenty*) &middot; *there's plenty* &middot; *plenty more*, *plenty else*
**Contrast:** Against *enough*: *enough* is the amount needed and no more; *plenty* is comfortably beyond it — *We have enough* settles the question, *We have plenty* closes it. Against *much* and *many*: *plenty* is type-neutral and does not force the count/mass choice. Note that *plenty of* takes no article (✗*a plenty of chairs*), and that the noun *a plenty* is archaic. The Southern and Appalachian *aplenty* (*food aplenty*) is a **regional postposed adverb**, not this pronoun, and should be labeled as such.

More than enough of something — a comfortable sufficiency.

1. Don't bring chairs — we have *plenty*.
2. *Plenty* of them had already heard the news.
3. There's *plenty* left if you're still hungry.
4. She had *plenty* to say once the meeting ended.
5. *Plenty* of the equipment was still under warranty.
6. Of the free tickets, *plenty* went unclaimed.

**See also:** [enough](#enough), [a lot](#a-lot), [lots](#lots), [a great deal](#a-great-deal), [much](#much), [many](#many), [plenty of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#plenty-of)

### quite a few

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈfju/ &middot; *kwyt-uh-FYOO* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/ and the /t/ of *quite* is normally a flap in front of it, *kwy-duh-FYOO*
**Русский:** немало, порядочно — о счётных, больше, чем ожидает собеседник
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — *a few* with *quite* in front of it, and **not** a small quantity in spite of its parts (also a determiner, *quite a few years*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — ordinary in speech and in any writing that is not stiff
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a fairly large number of **countable** things or people, presented as more than the listener would expect. Never a mass: ✗*quite a few of the water*; the mass counterpart is *quite a bit*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Quite a few **were** damaged*; *Quite a few of the seats **are** still open.*
**Pattern:** *quite a few* [plural verb] &middot; *quite a few of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *quite a few of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *quite a few* &middot; the determiner *quite a few* + plural noun (*quite a few years*) &middot; the mass twin *quite a bit*
**Contrast:** The idiom reverses its own parts. *A few* is a small number and *quite* is an intensifier, so the phrase ought to mean "a very small number," and it means the opposite — a fairly large one. *Quite a bit* does the same thing for a mass, while *quite a lot* means exactly what it looks like, which is what makes the pair a reliable trap for learners. Against *a good many*: the same quantity a notch more formal. Against *several*: smaller, and neutral about expectation. Against bare *few*: the opposite sign entirely — *few of them came* is a complaint that almost nobody did, *quite a few of them came* is a report that a lot did, and two words separate them.

A fairly large number — the idiom whose parts point the wrong way.

1. *Quite a few* of the seats were still open at showtime.
2. She asked for volunteers and got *quite a few*.
3. *Quite a few* of them had done this kind of work before.
4. Of the sixty who registered, *quite a few* never showed up.
5. He has written *quite a few* on the subject and been wrong about most of it.
6. There were complaints, *quite a few*, and not one of them was answered.

**See also:** [a few](#a-few), [few](#few), [a good many](#a-good-many), [several](#several), [many](#many), [a lot](#a-lot), [a bit](#a-bit), [quite a few (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#quite-a-few), [quite a bit of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#quite-a-bit-of)

### scads

**Pronunciation:** /skædz/ &middot; *SKADZ* &middot; no weak form; one syllable, and there is no singular — ✗*a scad* is not a word in this sense
**Русский:** уйма, куча — амер. разг., слегка старомодно
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *scads of money*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal and **American** — mid-nineteenth-century in origin, casual and a little old-fashioned now; not for formal prose
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass, in spite of the *-s*
**Refers to:** a large number of countables or a large amount of a mass — people, tickets, relatives, money, time. Plural in form only, and with no singular of any kind behind it.
**Agreement:** **agrees with the referent, not with the *-s*** — *Scads of them **were** printed* (countable → plural); *Scads of money **was** poured into it* (mass → singular).
**Pattern:** *scads of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *scads of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *scads* (*We have scads*) &middot; *there are scads left* &middot; *scads more*
**Contrast:** Against *gobs*: the nearest relative, and the two are interchangeable in practice. Against *oodles*: playful where *scads* is merely casual. Against *tons*, *loads*, and *lots*: the unmarked informal words, and the ones nobody notices. Bare *scads* as a subject is awkward — *Scads were left over* is possible and strained, and *There were scads left over* is what people actually say. And note that the word has no singular at all, which is what separates it from *a bunch*, *a ton*, and *a boatload*.

A large quantity, in a casual American plural with no singular behind it.

1. There were *scads* of them for sale at the flea market.
2. She has *scads* of relatives in the county and sees none of them.
3. *Scads* of the tickets went unsold.
4. He made *scads* on the patent and lost it all on the next one.
5. There's *scads* left if anybody is still hungry.
6. We expected a dozen replies and got *scads*.

**See also:** [gobs](#gobs), [oodles](#oodles), [tons](#tons), [loads](#loads), [heaps](#heaps), [lots](#lots), [a slew](#a-slew), [scores of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#scores-of), [oodles of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#oodles-of)

### several

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛvrəl/, more fully /ˈsɛvərəl/ &middot; *SEV-ruhl* — the middle vowel normally drops, leaving two syllables
**Русский:** несколько — больше двух-трёх, но меньше, чем many; только о счётных
**Type:** quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, *several people*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** an indefinite number of **countable** things or people — more than two or three, fewer than *many*. Never a mass: ✗*several of the water*, ✗*several of the furniture*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Several **were** damaged in transit*; *Several of the windows **are** cracked.*
**Pattern:** *several* [plural verb] &middot; *several of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *several of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *several* (*I bought several*) &middot; *several more*, *several others*
**Contrast:** *Several* sits between *a few* and *many* — more than *a few*, fewer than *many* — and unlike *few* it carries no complaint in it. It is strictly countable, so the mass equivalents are *some* and *a little*. Watch the false friend: in legal English *several* means "separate, individual" (*joint and several liability*, *the several states*), an adjective with nothing to do with quantity.

An indefinite but more than token number of things — a handful, but a decent one.

1. *Several* of the panels had come loose.
2. She called all morning, and *several* of the calls went straight to voicemail.
3. I only needed one, but I bought *several*.
4. *Several* were still waiting when the office closed.
5. Of the applicants, *several* had worked there before.
6. He made *several* of the same mistakes again.

**See also:** [a few](#a-few), [a couple](#a-couple), [few](#few), [many](#many), [some](#some), [others](#others), [several (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#several)

### tons

**Pronunciation:** /tʌnz/ &middot; *TUNZ* &middot; no weak form; the vowel is the one in *sun*
**Русский:** куча, море — обычное амер. разг. преувеличение
**Type:** hyperbolic quantifying pronoun (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *tons of work*, an informal adverb, *tons better*, and the plural of the unit of weight) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal — the ordinary American exaggeration in speech and casual writing
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass, in spite of the *-s*
**Refers to:** a large number of countables or a large amount of a mass — things, people, time, money, work.
**Agreement:** **agrees with the referent, not with the *-s*** — *Tons of people **were** there* (countable → plural); *Tons of the paint **was** wasted* (mass → singular). The literal weights keep a real plural, and there the *-s* does count: *Three tons of gravel **were** delivered.*
**Pattern:** *tons of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *tons of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *tons* (*We have tons*) &middot; *there are tons left* &middot; *tons more* &middot; adverbial *tons better*, and the fixed singular *thanks a ton*
**Contrast:** Against *a ton*: one word, one agreement rule, two shapes — the plural is the commoner before *of*, and the singular is what survives in *thanks a ton* and *it helped a ton*. Against *lots* and *loads*: interchangeable in register and meaning, with *tons* the most plainly American of the three. Against the unit of weight: only the context tells you whether anything was weighed, and if the number matters, write *two tons* and be done with it. In formal writing, *many*, *much*, or *a great deal*.

The plural of the American exaggeration, and the commoner of its two shapes.

1. Don't bring food — we have *tons*.
2. *Tons* of them were still sitting unopened in the garage.
3. There's *tons* left if you want more.
4. She has read *tons* about the case and still can't explain it.
5. *Tons* of the wiring had to be replaced.
6. I expected a handful of replies and got *tons*.

## Small quantity, and comparison downward

The small-amount words, plus the comparatives and superlatives that go down instead of up. The rule that organizes the whole section is the **count/mass split** — *a couple*, *a few*, *a handful*, *few*, *fewer*, *fewest* for things you count, *little*, *a little*, *a bit*, *less*, *least* for things you measure — and inside it, the fact that the indefinite article flips the sign: *a few* is positive, *few* is negative.

### a bit

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbɪt/ &middot; *uh-BIT* &middot; the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed; the final /t/ is usually unreleased or glottal at the end of a phrase, and flapped before a vowel — *a bit of it* /əˈbɪdəvɪt/
**Русский:** немного, чуть-чуть — о неисчисляемом; мало, но всё же есть
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a bit of luck*, an adverb of degree, *a bit tired*, and an ordinary count noun, *a bit of string*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — the everyday small-quantity word in speech
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** a small but **positive** amount of a mass or an abstraction — time, money, luck, trouble, information. Never countables: ✗*a bit of the chairs*; for a small number the phrase is *a few*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *A bit of it **is** left*; *A bit of the plaster **has** come away.*
**Pattern:** *a bit* [singular verb] &middot; *a bit of* [determiner + mass noun] &middot; *a bit of* [it / this / that] &middot; [verb] *a bit* (*I ate a bit*) &middot; *a bit more*, *a bit less*, *quite a bit*, *every bit* &middot; adverbial *a bit late*, *wait a bit*, *a bit of a mess*
**Contrast:** Against *a little*: the two are all but identical in the pronoun slot and differ only in tone, *a bit* being the more casual — *There's a little left* and *There's a bit left* say the same thing. **The idiom to watch is *quite a bit*, which means a fairly large amount** — the same reversal that turns *a few* into *quite a few*. Against *a little bit*: a common and unobjectionable doubling, one degree more informal again. And note that most of this word's daily work is adverbial (*a bit tired*, *a bit of a mess*), where it modifies something rather than standing in for it.

A small amount of something measured, offered as some rather than none.

1. There's *a bit* left in the bottom of the pot.
2. *A bit* of the plaster had come away above the window.
3. She knows *a bit* about wiring, enough to be dangerous.
4. He saved *a bit* every month for eleven years.
5. I've heard *a bit* of it, but not the whole story.
6. Quite *a bit* of the money never reached the school.

**See also:** [a little](#a-little), [little](#little), [a few](#a-few), [a handful](#a-handful), [less](#less), [some](#some), [a bit of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#a-bit-of)

### a couple

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈkʌpl̩/ &middot; *uh-KUP-ul* — the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed; before *of* the whole phrase runs together, *a couple of them* /əˈkʌpləvəm/ &middot; *uh-KUP-luh-vum*
**Русский:** пара, парочка — строго двое, в разг. речи двое-трое
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a couple of days*, and an ordinary count noun, *a married couple*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; in formal writing *two* or *a few* is the safer choice
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a small number of **countable** things or people — strictly two, and loosely two or three. Never a mass: ✗*a couple of the water*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *A couple **were** left over*; *A couple of the panels **are** loose.* (The count noun *a couple* meaning a pair of people takes either number depending on whether they are seen as a unit or as two individuals — *The couple **was** married in June*, *The couple **were** arguing* — but the quantifying pronoun is invariably plural.)
**Pattern:** *a couple* [plural verb] &middot; *a couple of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *a couple of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a couple* (*I'll take a couple*) &middot; *a couple more*, *a couple others*
**Contrast:** Against *two*: *two* is exact, *a couple* is approximate in ordinary American use, and *a couple of days* frequently means three. Against *a few*: *a few* is vaguer and runs higher; the two overlap and either is usually fine. **The *of* is not optional in edited writing:** before a pronoun it is required outright (*a couple of them*, ✗*a couple them*), and *a couple of days* is the edited form of the very common spoken ✗*a couple days* — widespread informal American usage that copy editors still change. Keep the pronoun apart from the count noun in *the couple next door*, which takes an ordinary plural (*two couples*).

A small number of countable things, normally two and loosely two or three.

1. There were sandwiches left, so I took *a couple*.
2. *A couple* of them had already gone home.
3. She asked for volunteers and got *a couple*.
4. *A couple* of the panels were loose.
5. I gave a copy to *a couple* of the neighbors.
6. Of the twelve applicants, only *a couple* had the certification.

**See also:** [a few](#a-few), [few](#few), [several](#several), [some](#some), [ones](#ones), [a couple of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of)

### a few

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈfju/ &middot; *uh-FYOO* — the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed
**Русский:** несколько — мало, но всё же есть; ср. few «мало, недостаточно»
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a determiner, *a few people*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a small but **positive** number of countable things or people — "some, and that counts for something." Never a mass; for a small amount of a mass the phrase is *a little*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *A few **were** left over*; *A few of the guests **are** still here.*
**Pattern:** *a few* [plural verb] &middot; *a few of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *a few of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a few* &middot; *quite a few* &middot; *a few more*, *a few others*
**Contrast:** **A few vs. few — the article changes the sign.** *A few of the guests stayed* means some stayed, and that is reported as a positive fact; *Few of the guests stayed* means almost nobody did, and it is a complaint. The same flip runs through *a little* and *little*. **The idiom to watch:** *quite a few* means a fairly **large** number, the opposite of what its parts suggest. Against *several*: *several* is a bit more, but the two overlap and either is usually fine.

A small number of countable things, offered as enough to matter.

1. *A few* of the parts were missing from the box.
2. I know *a few* who would take that job tomorrow.
3. There were sandwiches left, so I took *a few*.
4. *A few* of us stayed behind to clean up.
5. Quite *a few* of the seats were still available at showtime.
6. He asked for volunteers and got *a few*.

**See also:** [few](#few), [a couple](#a-couple), [a little](#a-little), [several](#several), [some](#some), [fewer](#fewer), [a few (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few)

### a handful

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhæn(d)fʊl/ &middot; *uh-HAND-fool* &middot; the /d/ normally drops between the /n/ and the /f/, *uh-HAN-fool*; the article is always reduced to /ə/
**Русский:** горстка, считаные единицы — небольшое число счётных, меньше ожидаемого
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *a handful of people*, an ordinary count noun, *a handful of sand*, and an informal predicate noun, *that child is a handful*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **plural** in the quantifying use
**Refers to:** a small number of **countable** things or people — five or six, loosely, and always presented as fewer than there might have been. Never a mass; for a small amount the words are *a little* and *a bit*.
**Agreement:** **plural in the quantifying use** — *A handful of them **were** still waiting*; *Only a handful **have** replied.* The literal count noun is singular: *A handful of sand **was** all that came up.* The split is the same one that separates *a couple* the quantifier from *a couple* the pair of people.
**Pattern:** *a handful* [plural verb] &middot; *a handful of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *a handful of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *a handful* &middot; *only a handful*, *no more than a handful*, *a mere handful*
**Contrast:** Against *a few* and *several*: *a few* is neutral about the count, *several* runs a little higher, and *a handful* is the one that says openly that the number is small and probably disappointing — it is *few* with a friendlier face. Against *a couple*: two or three against five or six. Keep the quantifier apart from the two nouns it grew out of — the literal fistful (*a handful of gravel*) and the person who is hard work (*He's a handful*) — neither of which is this pronoun.

A small number of countable things, named as smaller than expected.

1. Only *a handful* of the invitations came back.
2. *A handful* of us stayed to finish the count.
3. She expected a crowd and got *a handful*.
4. *A handful* of them had done the reading.
5. Of the sixty applicants, *a handful* had any experience at all.
6. He named *a handful* of the people involved and stopped there.

**See also:** [a few](#a-few), [a couple](#a-couple), [several](#several), [few](#few), [some](#some), [a bit](#a-bit), [a handful of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#a-handful-of)

### a little

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈlɪtl̩/ &middot; *uh-LIT-ul* — with the American flapped or glottalized /t/, closer to *uh-LIH-dul*
**Русский:** немного — о неисчисляемом: мало, но есть; ср. little «мало, недостаточно»
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase (also a determiner, *a little milk*, and an adverb of degree, *a little tired*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** a small but **positive** amount of a **mass** or abstraction. Never countables — ✗*a little of the cookies*; for a small number the phrase is *a few*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *A little of the paint **is** left*; *A little **goes** a long way.*
**Pattern:** *a little* [singular verb] &middot; *a little of* [determiner + mass noun] &middot; *a little of* [it / this / that] &middot; [verb] *a little* (*I ate a little*) &middot; *a little more*, *a little less*
**Contrast:** **A little vs. little** — the article flips the sign exactly as it does with *a few* and *few*: *There's a little left* is reassuring, *There's little left* is a warning. **A little vs. a few** — mass versus count. And note that in *a little tired* or *wait a little* the phrase is an adverb of degree or duration, not this pronoun.

A small amount of something measured, presented as some rather than none.

1. There's still *a little* left in the bottle.
2. *A little* of the mortar had crumbled away.
3. She speaks Portuguese — not fluently, but *a little*.
4. *A little* goes a long way with that spice.
5. He knew *a little* about engines and nothing about wiring.
6. I've saved *a little* of the money for later.

**See also:** [little](#little), [a few](#a-few), [less](#less), [much](#much), [some](#some), [a little (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#a-little)

### few

**Pronunciation:** /fju/ &middot; *FYOO*
**Русский:** мало, немногие — с оттенком «недостаточно»; ср. a few «несколько»
**Type:** quantifying pronoun, negative in force (also a determiner, *few people*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral in writing; formal-sounding in speech, where *not many* is the usual substitute
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a small number of countable things or people, presented as **insufficient** or disappointing. Never a mass; the mass counterpart is *little*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Few **were** willing to say so publicly*; *Few of the changes **have** been implemented.*
**Pattern:** *few* [plural verb] &middot; *few of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *few of* [object pronoun] &middot; *the few* (*the few who stayed*) &middot; *very few*, *so few*, *too few*, *how few* &middot; comparative *fewer*, superlative *fewest*
**Contrast:** **Few vs. a few** — dropping the article makes the word negative: *Few of them came* is a complaint; *A few of them came* is not. **Few vs. little** — count versus mass. **Few vs. the few** — *the few* names a specific small group (*the few who bothered to vote*) rather than reporting a shortage. In everyday speech, bare *few* as a subject sounds bookish; people say *not many* or *hardly any*.

A small number, presented as smaller than it should be.

1. *Few* of the original members are still on the board.
2. Many applied; *few* were hired.
3. *Few* of us had ever seen anything like it.
4. Of the promises made that spring, *few* survived the summer.
5. *Few* have read the report all the way through.
6. The *few* who stayed did most of the work.

### fewer

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfjuɚ/ &middot; *FYOO-ur*
**Русский:** меньше по числу — только о счётных; для неисчисляемого — less
**Type:** comparative quantifying pronoun — the comparative of *few* (also a determiner, *fewer mistakes*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** a smaller **number** of countable things or people than some benchmark. Never a mass — that is *less*.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Fewer of the seats **were** occupied this year*; *Fewer **are** applying than a decade ago.*
**Pattern:** *fewer* [plural verb] &middot; *fewer of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *fewer of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *fewer* &middot; *fewer than* [number] &middot; *far fewer*, *no fewer than*, *the fewest*
**Contrast:** **Fewer vs. less — stated honestly.** The rule taught in school is count with *fewer*, measure with *less*: *fewer bottles*, *less water*; in pronoun position, *Fewer of the bottles were returned* but *Less of the water was wasted*. The rule holds well in edited American prose and a conservative editor will enforce it, but it is a **usage convention, not a law of the language** — *less* has been used with countables since Old English, and it is fully standard before amounts treated as a single measure: *less than three miles*, *less than fifty dollars*, *less than two weeks*, where ✗*fewer than two weeks* is actually wrong. The supermarket sign *10 items or less* is idiomatic English that editors dislike. Practical rule: when the referent is a plural count noun, *fewer* is always safe.

A smaller number of countable things than before, or than something else.

1. *Fewer* of the students showed up than last semester.
2. There were forty complaints last quarter and *fewer* this one.
3. *Fewer* than half of them were returned.
4. Of the twelve original tenants, *fewer* remain each year.
5. She made mistakes, but *fewer* than anyone expected.
6. *Fewer* are willing to work weekends now.

**See also:** [fewest](#fewest), [less](#less), [few](#few), [more](#more), [least](#least), [many](#many), [fewer (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer)

### fewest

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfjuɪst/ &middot; *FYOO-ist* — three sounds in the second syllable, not two; ✗*FYOOST* is a reduction that reads as a mistake in careful speech
**Русский:** меньше всего по числу — превосх. от few, о счётных; для массы — least
**Type:** superlative quantifying pronoun, almost always with *the* — the superlative of *few* (also a determiner, *the fewest mistakes*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **plural**
**Refers to:** the smallest **number** of countable things or people, in a comparison across three or more. Never a mass — the mass superlative is *least*.
**Agreement:** **plural** — *The fewest **were** sold in the week after the recall.* When *the fewest* is the object, the verb belongs to some other subject and takes that subject's number: *Whichever branch reports the fewest **gets** the bonus.*
**Pattern:** *the fewest* [plural verb] &middot; *the fewest of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; [verb] *the fewest* (*she made the fewest*) &middot; *the fewest possible* &middot; *the second fewest*, *by far the fewest*
**Contrast:** *Fewest* is to *few* what *least* is to *little* — count against mass — and it completes the *few / fewer / fewest* series that the file's [fewer](#fewer) entry sets up. With exactly two the word is *fewer*, not *fewest*: *the fewer of the two*. In practice *fewest* is much commoner as a determiner (*the fewest mistakes*) than as a bare pronoun, and speakers routinely reach for *least* where the referent is countable — *the least mistakes* is ordinary in speech and is exactly what a copy editor changes to *the fewest mistakes*. The same honesty applies here as under [fewer](#fewer): the count/measure split is a well-entrenched editing convention rather than a law of the language, but with a plural count referent *fewest* is always the safe choice.

The smallest number of countable things in a comparison.

1. Of the four teams, ours had *the fewest*.
2. She made *the fewest* of anyone in the class.
3. *The fewest* were sold in the week after the recall.
4. He compared the three routes and took the one with *the fewest*.
5. Whoever files *the fewest* still has to file something.
6. Among the three branches, the downtown office reported *the fewest*.

**See also:** [few](#few), [fewer](#fewer), [least](#least), [less](#less), [most](#most), [fewest (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#fewest)

### least

**Pronunciation:** /list/ &middot; *LEEST*
**Русский:** меньше всего, наименьшее количество — о неисчисляемом или по рангу
**Type:** superlative quantifying pronoun, almost always with *the* — the superlative of *little* and *less* (also a determiner, *the least trouble*, and a degree adverb, *least likely*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** the smallest amount of a mass or abstraction, or the lowest-ranking item in a comparison. Not normally used for a number of countables — the count superlative is *fewest*.
**Agreement:** **singular** — *The least of my worries **is** the paperwork*; *Least said, soonest mended.*
**Pattern:** *the least of* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *the least* &middot; *the least* [subject] *could do* &middot; *at least*, *not in the least*, *least of all*, *last but not least* (fixed adverbial idioms, a different word class)
**Contrast:** *Least* is to *little* what *fewest* is to *few* — mass against count. Most of the word's daily life is spent inside fixed phrases where it is no longer a pronoun at all: *at least* and *not in the least* are adverbials. As a genuine pronoun it appears mainly in *the least of* + noun phrase (*the least of our problems*) and in comparisons of amount (*he does the least*).

The smallest amount, or the thing that matters or counts for the smallest amount.

1. The cost is *the least* of our problems right now.
2. He does *the least* and complains the most.
3. Buying dinner was *the least* I could do.
4. Whoever contributes *the least* still gets an equal share.
5. *The least* of the three estimates still came to nine thousand dollars.
6. That was *the least* of what he told the investigators.

**See also:** [less](#less), [little](#little), [fewer](#fewer), [fewest](#fewest), [most](#most), [least (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#least)

### less

**Pronunciation:** /lɛs/ &middot; *LESS*
**Русский:** меньше по количеству — о неисчисляемом, а также о мерах: less than three miles
**Type:** comparative quantifying pronoun — the comparative of *little* (also a determiner, *less noise*, an adverb of degree, *less often*, and a marginal preposition of subtraction, *five less two*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** a smaller amount of a **mass** or abstraction; also, in fully standard idiom, a smaller **measured** amount even when the noun behind it is countable — *less than three miles*, *less than two weeks*.
**Agreement:** **singular** — *Less of the money **was** recovered than expected*; *Less **is** known about the second case.*
**Pattern:** *less* [singular verb] &middot; *less of* [determiner + mass noun] &middot; *less of* [it / this / that] &middot; [verb] *less* &middot; *less than* [amount] &middot; *much less*, *no less*, *nothing less*, *far less* &middot; *less of a* [noun] (*less of a problem*)
**Contrast:** **Less vs. fewer** — the full account is under [fewer](#fewer); the short version is that countable things take *fewer* and measured amounts take *less*, with money, distance, weight, and time going to *less* even though their nouns are plural. *Less* also owns a construction *fewer* cannot enter at all: *less of a problem*, *less of a hassle*, where the noun is singular and the sense is degree rather than number. Do not confuse *less* with the adjective *lesser* (*the lesser of two evils*), which grades importance, not quantity.

A smaller amount of something measured, or a lower degree of something.

1. There's *less* of it left than I thought.
2. *Less* was recovered from the second site than from the first.
3. She wanted more responsibility and got *less*.
4. *Less* of the budget went to maintenance this year.
5. It costs *less* than the model we looked at first.
6. Traffic is *less* of a problem on this side of town.

**See also:** [fewer](#fewer), [little](#little), [a little](#a-little), [least](#least), [more](#more), [much](#much), [less (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### little

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪtl̩/ &middot; *LIT-ul* — with the American flap, *LIH-dul*
**Русский:** мало — о неисчисляемом, с оттенком «недостаточно»; ср. a little «немного»
**Type:** quantifying pronoun, negative in force (also a determiner, *little time*, an adjective of size, *a little dog*, and a fronting adverb, *little did he know*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral in writing; formal-sounding in speech, where *not much* is the usual substitute
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** a small amount of a mass or abstraction, presented as **insufficient** — the mass counterpart of *few*. Never countables.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Little **is** known about him*; *Little of the original building **remains**.*
**Pattern:** *little* [singular verb] &middot; *little of* [determiner + mass noun] &middot; *little of* [it / this / that] &middot; [verb] *little* (*He said little*) &middot; *very little*, *so little*, *too little*, *precious little* &middot; comparative *less*, superlative *least*
**Contrast:** **Little vs. a little** — the article flips the sign: *little* is hardly any and is a complaint; *a little* is some and is not. **Little vs. few** — mass against count. And keep this pronoun apart from the **adjective** *little* meaning small (*a little house*, *my little brother*), which is a different word entirely and has nothing to do with quantity — the giveaway is that the adjective sits in front of a noun and can be graded (*littler*, *littlest*), while the pronoun stands alone.

A small amount of something, presented as too small.

1. *Little* is known about what happened after midnight.
2. He said *little* and left early.
3. *Little* of the original wiring is still in place.
4. She gave a great deal and asked for *little*.
5. Too *little* was done, and far too late.
6. Of the money raised that year, *little* ever reached the school.

## The definite portions: *the rest*, *the majority*, and their kin

A short family of *the* + noun phrases that behave exactly like the quantifiers above them: they stand alone or take *of*, they put whatever follows *of* in the object case, and they take their number from what they refer to. What sets them apart is the definite article, which **closes the set** — *some of them* leaves the count open, *the rest of them* accounts for everyone still unaccounted for. Three of them are register variants of a fourth: *the rest* is the plain word, *the remainder* the formal one, *the balance* the commercial one, and *the residue* the one that belongs to wills and probate. *The majority* and *the minority* differ from the other four in one practical way — they need members you can count, which is why *most of the money* is right and ✗*the majority of the money* is not.

### the balance

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈbæləns/ &middot; *thuh BAL-uns* &middot; *the* is the weak /ðə/ here, as it is before any consonant
**Русский:** остаток, сальдо — фин. и коммерч., чаще о недоплаченной сумме
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *balance* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *the balance of the term*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal and commercial — accounting, contracts, invoices, and business letters; the plain-English word is *the rest*
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular** with a sum or a mass and **plural** with countables
**Refers to:** what is left of a quantity after part of it has been paid, taken, delivered, or used — most often a sum of money still owed, and by extension any remainder at all.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *The balance **is** due on the first* (a sum, singular, and by far the commonest case); *The balance of the crates **were** damaged in transit* (countables, plural). The word attaches to amounts far more readily than to countable things, so the singular dominates.
**Pattern:** *the balance* [verb] &middot; *the balance of* [determiner + noun] &middot; [verb] *the balance* &middot; *the balance due*, *pay the balance*, *the balance of the term*, *the balance of the evidence*
**Contrast:** Against *the rest* and *the remainder*: the three name the same leftover in three registers — *the rest* is the plain word, *the remainder* the formal one, and *the balance* the commercial one, which keeps a faint suggestion of an account to be settled even where no money is involved (*the balance of the season*). Against the ordinary noun *balance* meaning equilibrium (*he lost his balance*) or the weighing instrument: the same spelling and an unrelated sense.

The part of an amount that is still outstanding.

1. A deposit is due at signing and *the balance* on delivery.
2. He paid half in March and *the balance* the following spring.
3. *The balance* of the estate went to her nephew.
4. She served two years of the contract and forfeited *the balance*.
5. The insurer covered the first ten thousand dollars and the family paid *the balance*.
6. *The balance* of the term will be taught by a substitute.

**See also:** [the rest](#the-rest), [the remainder](#the-remainder), [the majority](#the-majority), [others](#others), [more](#more), [the rest of, the remainder of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of)

### the majority

**Pronunciation:** /ðə məˈdʒɔrəti/ &middot; *thuh muh-JOR-uh-tee* &middot; the medial /t/ is normally a flap in American speech, *muh-JOR-uh-dee*
**Русский:** большинство — только о счётных; о деньгах и массе — most, не the majority
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *majority* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *the majority of voters*, and an ordinary count noun, *a two-thirds majority*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the word of journalism, reports, and minutes; *most* is the everyday equivalent
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with the members and **singular** with the bloc or the margin
**Refers to:** more than half of a set of **countable** people or things. Never a mass: *most of the money* is right and ✗*the majority of the money* is not, and that count/mass line is the sharpest practical difference between this phrase and *most*.
**Agreement:** **plural with the members, singular with the bloc.** *The majority of the residents **were** opposed* (the people, and this is the ordinary case); *The majority **was** slim* (the margin); *A working majority **has** controlled the chamber since* (the group as one thing). American editors accept both and choose by sense.
**Pattern:** *the majority* [verb] &middot; *the majority of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *the majority of* [object pronoun] &middot; *the vast majority*, *the overwhelming majority*, *the bare majority* &middot; *in the majority*, *by a majority of* [number]
**Contrast:** **Majority vs. most.** *Most* covers mass and count alike and belongs in every register; *the majority* takes countables only and sounds official, which is why *most of the money* is right and the bureaucratic ✗*the majority of the money* is wrong. Where the members can be counted, either word works: *most of the voters*, *the majority of the voters*. Against *the minority*: the two halves of one division. Against *a plurality*: a plurality is the largest share **without** being more than half — the winner of a three-way race with forty percent has a plurality and not a majority, and the difference matters in reporting an election.

More than half of a group, taken as a quantity.

1. *The majority* of the tenants signed the petition.
2. Of the six proposals, *the majority* were sent back for revision.
3. She won a second term, but *the majority* was down to eleven votes.
4. *The majority* of them had never voted in a primary before.
5. The vast *majority* of the complaints concerned noise.
6. *The majority* were willing to wait; a loud few were not.

**See also:** [the minority](#the-minority), [most](#most), [more](#more), [all](#all), [the rest](#the-rest), [some](#some), [the majority of, the minority of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), [a plurality of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#a-plurality-of)

### the minority

**Pronunciation:** /ðə maɪˈnɔrəti/, also /ðə mɪˈnɔrəti/ &middot; *thuh my-NOR-uh-tee* &middot; the medial /t/ is normally a flap, *my-NOR-uh-dee*
**Русский:** меньшинство — менее половины счётного множества
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *minority* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, and a count noun with several unrelated senses) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with the members and **singular** with the bloc or the share
**Refers to:** fewer than half of a set of **countable** people or things. Never a mass: for a small amount the words are *less* and *a little*.
**Agreement:** **plural with the members, singular with the bloc** — *The minority **were** never consulted* (the people); *The minority **was** vocal but small* (the group as one thing). Both are standard and the sense decides, exactly as with *the majority*.
**Pattern:** *the minority* [verb] &middot; *the minority of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *the minority of* [object pronoun] &middot; *a small minority*, *a vocal minority*, *a tiny minority* &middot; *in the minority* &middot; *in the minority of cases*
**Contrast:** Against *the majority*: the two halves of the same division, and both are countable-only. Against *few* and *a few*: those report a small number without reference to any whole, while *the minority* is explicitly the smaller part of a set that has been divided. **The other sense of the word.** In American English *minority* far more often names an ethnic or racial group that is not the dominant one — *minority students*, *minority-owned businesses* — and that is a noun and a modifier rather than this pronoun. Because the two senses share a spelling, a sentence like *the minority were unhappy* can be read either way; naming the group, or writing *the smaller group*, removes the ambiguity.

Fewer than half of a group, taken as a quantity.

1. *The minority* on the committee filed a separate report.
2. *The minority* were never asked what they wanted.
3. She was in *the minority* on that vote and said so afterward.
4. Of the twelve jurors, *the minority* held out for three days.
5. In *the minority* of cases where the drug fails, the symptoms return within a week.
6. The majority wanted to wait; *the minority* wanted to sue.

**See also:** [the majority](#the-majority), [few](#few), [a few](#a-few), [less](#less), [least](#least), [some](#some), [the majority of, the minority of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of)

### the remainder

**Pronunciation:** /ðə rɪˈmeɪndɚ/ &middot; *thuh rih-MAIN-der* &middot; the first vowel of *remainder* is a reduced /ɪ/ or /ə/ and never carries stress
**Русский:** остаток, оставшаяся часть — книжн. и офиц.; в обиходе the rest
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *remainder* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, and a technical noun in arithmetic and in bookselling) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal — reports, contracts, and technical prose; in ordinary writing the phrase is *the rest*
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass
**Refers to:** what is left of a set or a quantity after part of it has been removed, used, or accounted for — things, people, time, or an amount.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *The remainder of the crates **were** shipped Tuesday* (countable → plural); *The remainder of the money **was** returned* (mass → singular). Bare *The remainder **is** yours* is singular on an amount reading and *The remainder **are** unaccounted for* plural on a count reading.
**Pattern:** *the remainder* [verb] &middot; *the remainder of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *the remainder of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *the remainder* &middot; *for the remainder of the year*
**Contrast:** Against *the rest*: the same meaning a register up, and *the rest* is what a plain-English editor substitutes nine times out of ten. Against *the balance*: the commercial version, tied to sums owed. Against *others* and *the others*: those are countable and usually personal — *the others left* is people, *the remainder was discarded* is stuff. Two technical senses are not this pronoun at all: the *remainder* left over from a division in arithmetic, and *remainders*, the unsold books a publisher clears at a discount.

What is left after part has been taken, said formally.

1. *The remainder* of the shipment arrived on Thursday.
2. He read the first two chapters carefully and skimmed *the remainder*.
3. *The remainder* of the money was returned to the donors.
4. She will teach the class for *the remainder* of the term.
5. Two of the panels were salvageable; *the remainder* had to be scrapped.
6. Of the original grant, *the remainder* is barely enough for one more season.

**See also:** [the rest](#the-rest), [the balance](#the-balance), [the majority](#the-majority), [others](#others), [other](#other), [more](#more), [the rest of, the remainder of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of)

### the residue

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈrɛzəˌdu/ &middot; *thuh REZ-uh-doo* &middot; American English drops the yod, so it is *REZ-uh-doo* and not the British *REZ-i-dyoo*; *the* is the weak /ðə/ before the consonant
**Русский:** юр.: остаток наследства после долгов, налогов и завещательных отказов
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *residue* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *the residue of the estate*, and an ordinary noun in chemistry and cooking) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **formal and legal** — the language of wills, trusts, and probate above all. An American meets it in a will, in a probate filing, or in a letter from an estate lawyer, and essentially nowhere else.
**Person / number:** third person; **singular** in the legal use, where it names an estate; elsewhere it takes its number from the referent
**Refers to:** what is left of an estate after debts, taxes, expenses, and specific gifts have been paid out — the technical sense behind *residuary estate*, *residuary clause*, and *residuary legatee*. By extension, and much more rarely, whatever is left of anything at all.
**Agreement:** **singular in the legal sense** — *The residue **passes** to the testator's sister*; *The residue of the estate **is** to be divided equally.* On the rare general use it agrees with what it refers to, exactly as [the remainder](#the-remainder) does.
**Pattern:** *the residue* [singular verb] &middot; *the residue of* [determiner + noun] &middot; [verb] *the residue* &middot; *I give the residue to* [beneficiary] &middot; *the residuary estate*, *the residuary clause*, *the residuary legatee* (the adjective, not this pronoun)
**Contrast:** Against *the remainder*: the ordinary formal word for the same idea, usable in any kind of writing, where *the residue* is narrow and outside a will sounds either technical or faintly chemical. Against *the rest*: the plain-English word, and the one a modern drafter now writes even in a will — the classic legal triplet *the rest, residue and remainder of my estate* is three words doing one word's work, and plain-language drafting guides cut it to *the rest*. Against *the balance*: the commercial version, tied to sums owed. And keep the pronoun apart from the everyday noun *residue* meaning a film or a deposit (*soap residue*, *pesticide residue*), which is far the commoner sense of the word.

What is left of an estate after everything else has been paid out — the fused head of a will.

1. *The residue* passes to the testator's sister in equal shares with her children.
2. After the specific bequests were satisfied, *the residue* came to less than anyone expected.
3. The will was drafted without a residuary clause, so *the residue* fell into intestacy.
4. She left the house to her nephew and *the residue* to a scholarship fund.
5. Debts and taxes are paid first, and *the residue* is divided only after that.
6. The old form gave "the rest, residue and remainder" where *the residue* alone would have done.

### the rest

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈrɛst/ &middot; *thuh REST* &middot; *the* is the weak /ðə/, and it stays /ðə/ in *the rest of it* because the vowel that follows belongs to the next word — *the rest of it* /ðə ˈrɛstəvɪt/ &middot; *thuh RES-tuh-vit*
**Русский:** остальное, остальные — обычное нейтральное слово, о людях, вещах, времени
**Type:** quantifying pronoun phrase — the noun *rest* used as a fused head (also a quantifier before *of* + noun, *the rest of the day*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral — the ordinary everyday word, at home in every register
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **plural** with countables and **singular** with a mass, a stretch of time, or an abstraction
**Refers to:** everything or everyone left over once part of a set or quantity has been dealt with — people, things, time, money. It takes all of them, which is why it is the default word and the other three in this section are the marked ones.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *The rest of the guests **were** late* (countable → plural); *The rest of the milk **has** gone off* (mass → singular); *The rest **is** history* (an abstraction, singular). The *of*-phrase decides, exactly as with *all*, *some*, and *most*.
**Pattern:** *the rest* [verb] &middot; *the rest of* [determiner + noun] &middot; *the rest of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *the rest* &middot; *and the rest*, *all the rest* &middot; *for the rest of* [time] &middot; *the rest is history*
**Contrast:** Against *the others*: *the others* is countable and plural only and normally means people or discrete things, while *the rest* takes a mass or a stretch of time just as easily — *the rest of the afternoon*, *the rest of the paint*, and ✗*the others of the paint* is impossible. Against *others* without the article: that is indefinite, some further ones, where *the rest* closes the set. Against *the remainder* and *the balance*: the same idea in a formal and in a commercial register. **The *of* is obligatory before a pronoun or a determiner** — *the rest of them*, *the rest of the cake*, ✗*the rest them* — and bare *the rest* is used only when the referent is already understood. Keep the pronoun apart from the noun *rest* meaning repose (*a day of rest*), which is a different word with a different history.

Everything or everyone that is left.

1. She ate two slices and left *the rest*.
2. *The rest* of them were still out in the parking lot.
3. *The rest* of the paint had dried solid in the can.
4. He spent *the rest* of the afternoon on hold.
5. Two of the letters were answered; *the rest* went into a drawer.
6. Keep what you need and give away *the rest*.
7. They met in a bookstore in 1974, and *the rest* is history.

## The prop-words and substitutes

*One*, *ones*, *the one*, *other*, *others*, *another*, and *such* do not measure anything — they stand in for a noun already named, so the noun does not have to be repeated. Three more fused heads fill the same slot in the same way: the bare cardinals (*two*, *three*, *a dozen*), which count exactly where the rest of this dictionary only estimates; *the usual*, whose antecedent is shared knowledge rather than anything written down; and the scholarly *et al.*, which stands in for every name on a list after the first. They are grouped here because they are the other half of what *of*-quantification does: *some … others …*, *one … another …*, *the red one*.

### another

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnʌðɚ/ &middot; *uh-NUTH-ur* — one word, always: ✗*an other* is not a spelling of it
**Русский:** ещё один; другой — только о счётных, не о массе
**Type:** quantifying and substitute pronoun (also a determiner, *another chance*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular, always** — it contains the article *an*, so it can be neither plural (✗*anothers*) nor definite (✗*the another*)
**Refers to:** one more, or one different, member of a class of **countables** — things or people. Never a mass: ✗*another of the water*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *Another **is** on the way*; *Another of the pipes **has** burst.*
**Pattern:** *another* [singular verb] &middot; *another of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; *another of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] *another* (*I'll have another*) &middot; *one … another …* &middot; *one after another* &middot; *another two / another three* (the one place it precedes a plural, because the numeral makes the phrase a single unit)
**Contrast:** **Another vs. other vs. others.** *Another* = one more or one different, singular and indefinite. *The other* = the remaining one of two. *Others* = some more, unspecified and plural. *The others* = all the rest of a definite set. Because *another* already contains *an*, the definite and plural jobs have to go to the *other* forms. Against *more*: *another* adds one item, *more* adds an unmeasured quantity — *another coffee* is a second cup, *more coffee* is a refill.

**1. One additional item of the same kind.**

1. That coffee's gone cold — let me get you *another*.
2. He finished the sandwich and immediately ordered *another*.
3. *Another* of the tenants complained about the noise.

**2. A different one of the same kind.**

4. One door closed and *another* opened.
5. If this pen doesn't work, take *another*.
6. She lost one job and found *another* within the week.

**See also:** [other](#other), [others](#others), [one](#one), [more](#more), [each other](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#each-other), [another (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#another)

### et al.

**Pronunciation:** /ɛt ˈɑl/, also /ɛt ˈæl/ and /ɛt ˈɔl/ &middot; *et-AHL*, also *et-AL* &middot; two words, and the period belongs on the second only, because *et* is a whole Latin word meaning "and" while *al.* is an abbreviation of *alii*, *aliae*, or *alia*
**Русский:** и др., и другие — книжн., о людях в ссылках и юр. документах; о вещах — etc.
**Type:** abbreviated pronominal phrase meaning "and others" — a fused coordinate standing in a noun-phrase slot beside a name &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **formal and scholarly** — citations, footnotes, reference lists, case captions, and the address block of a legal notice. An American meets it in a bibliography, on a court docket (*Fenwick v. Alameda County et al.*), and in the sentence *Rivera et al. found no such effect*.
**Person / number:** third person; **plural** in what it names
**Refers to:** the remaining, unnamed members of a list of **people** — the co-authors after the first, the additional defendants after the first, the other signatories on a notice. For a list of things the word is *etc.*, and *et al.* used for things is an error editors correct.
**Agreement:** **plural** — *Rivera et al. **find** no such effect*; *Smith et al. **have** since retracted the paper.* The subject means "Rivera and the others," so the verb is plural, and a singular verb (*Rivera et al. **finds***) reads as treating the citation as the name of a paper rather than of its authors. Referring back, the pronoun is *they*.
**Pattern:** [surname] *et al.* &middot; [surname] *et al.* [plural verb] &middot; *(Okonkwo et al., 2019)* &middot; *v.* [name] *et al.* in a case caption &middot; in a reference list, after however many authors the style manual names
**Variants:** the unabbreviated *et alii* (masculine or mixed), *et aliae* (feminine), and *et alia* (neuter), none of which is ever written out in practice; the related scholarly abbreviations *et seq.* ("and following," for pages and sections), *ibid.*, and *id.*; the plain-English equivalent *and others*
**Contrast:** *Et al.* is for people and *etc.* is for things — *Rivera et al.* names co-authors, *pencils, erasers, etc.* names further items, and swapping the two is the commonest error with either. The period is not optional and belongs where it belongs: *et al.*, never ✗*et. al.*, because *et* is not an abbreviation, and never ✗*et al* bare in American style. Against *others* and *the others*: the ordinary English pronouns that mean the same thing, and the ones to write in running prose where no citation is being made — *Rivera and others have argued* is better English in a sentence than *Rivera et al. have argued*. Whether to italicize is a house matter: *Chicago* and the APA set it roman, and *The Bluebook* italicizes it inside a case name.

"And others" — the abbreviation that stands in for every name on a list after the first.

1. Rivera *et al.* found no such effect in a sample twice the size.
2. The case is docketed as *Fenwick v. Alameda County et al.*
3. Cite it as (Okonkwo *et al.*, 2019) on first mention and after.
4. The notice was served on the landlord *et al.* by certified mail.
5. Smith *et al.* have since published a correction.
6. The grant lists Chen *et al.* as co-investigators without naming any of them.

### one

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn/ &middot; *WUN* &middot; weak /wən/ when it props up a modifier — *the red one* /ðə ˈrɛd wən/ &middot; *thuh RED wun*
**Русский:** слово-заместитель существительного (the red one — красный); тж. обобщённо «человек» — книжн.
**Type:** prop-word (substitute) pronoun, and separately the generic or impersonal pronoun (also the numeral and the determiner *one day*) &middot; **Case:** none for the prop-word; the generic *one* has the possessive *one's* and the reflexive *oneself* &middot; **Register:** the prop-word is neutral; the generic *one* is formal and noticeably marked in American English
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**, with the plural *ones*
**Refers to:** (1) a countable thing or person already named or understood, so the noun need not be repeated — *the red one*; (2) people in general including the speaker — *one does what one can*. Never a mass: there is no ✗*a smooth one of the paint*.
**Agreement:** **singular, always** — *The blue one **is** mine*; *One never **knows**.* The construction to watch is *one of* + plural noun, which is still singular: *One of the windows **is** broken*, never ✗*are broken*, because the head of the phrase is *one*.
**Pattern:** [determiner / adjective] *one* (*this one*, *a big one*, *the last one*) &middot; *the one that / who* [clause] &middot; *which one* &middot; *one of* [determiner + plural noun] + singular verb &middot; generic *one* [singular verb] … *one's* … *oneself* &middot; *one another* (a separate reciprocal pronoun)
**Contrast:** **The agreement trap:** *one of* + plural takes a **singular** verb (*One of my friends is a nurse*), but a relative clause hanging off the plural noun goes **plural**, because *who* points at the noun and not at *one* — *He is one of those people **who never apologize***. **One vs. you:** generic *one* is formal, and American English overwhelmingly prefers generic *you* (*You never know*); what is genuinely an error is mixing the two in a single sentence — ✗*One should always watch your step*. **One vs. it:** *one* substitutes for the noun and takes a new referent (*I need a pen — do you have one?*); *it* points back at the specific thing already identified (*I lost my pen — have you seen it?*). And *one* cannot prop up a mass noun: ✗*I'll take the strong one* of coffee is not English unless *coffee* is being counted as cups.

**1. The prop-word.** Stands in for a countable noun already named, and always carries a determiner or a modifier.

1. I need a screwdriver — do you have *one*?
2. The blue *one* costs twice as much.
3. She's the *one* who called the inspector.
4. Of all the excuses he gave, that was the worst *one*.

**2. *One of* + a plural set.** Singular verb.

5. *One* of the tires was completely flat.
6. *One* of my closest friends teaches at the same school.

**3. The generic or impersonal *one*.** Formal; American speech prefers generic *you*.

7. *One* does what *one* can.
8. *One* cannot simply walk out of a contract like that.

### ones

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnz/ &middot; *WUNZ* &middot; weak /wənz/ — *the red ones* /ðə ˈrɛd wənz/
**Русский:** мн. ч. слова-заместителя one — вместо уже названных предметов; без определителя невозможно
**Type:** prop-word (substitute) pronoun — the plural of the prop-word *one* &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** countable things or people already named, in the plural. It **requires** a determiner, an adjective, or a relative clause to lean on — bare *ones* is impossible: ✗*I bought ones*, ✗*Do you have ones?*; say *some* or *any* instead.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *The red ones **are** cheaper*; *The ones we saw **have** already sold.*
**Pattern:** [determiner / adjective] *ones* (*the small ones*, *these ones*) &middot; *the ones that / who* [clause] &middot; *which ones* &middot; *the little ones*, *the loved ones* (semi-lexicalized)
**Contrast:** **Ones cannot stand alone**, where *one* can — *Do you have one?* is fine and ✗*Do you have ones?* is not; the plural slot is filled by *some* or *any*. **These ones / those ones** are standard in British and Canadian speech but are marked as redundant in American usage, where *these* and *those* stand alone: *I'll take these*, not *I'll take these ones*. Keep it apart from *one's*, the possessive of the generic *one* (*one's own business*), and from the plural of the numeral (*the ones column*).

1. The red *ones* are on sale this week.
2. Those are the *ones* we saw at the fair.
3. I like the *ones* with the wooden handles.
4. Which *ones* did you end up keeping?
5. The *ones* who complained loudest never showed up to help.
6. Take the small *ones* and leave the rest.

**See also:** [one](#one), [others](#others), [these](04-demonstrative.md#these), [those](04-demonstrative.md#those), [one (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one)

### other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *UTH-ur*
**Русский:** другой — оставшийся член множества, обычно второй из двух
**Type:** substitute pronoun, in practice almost always *the other* (also a determiner, *other people*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular** in the pronoun form *the other*; for a plural sense the form must be *others* or *the others*
**Refers to:** the remaining member of a set — typically the second of exactly two, when the first has already been named. People or things, countables only.
**Agreement:** **singular** as *the other* — *The other **is** broken*; *The other of the two **was** never found.* If the sense is plural, the word changes shape: *the others **are***.
**Pattern:** *the other* [singular verb] &middot; *one … the other …* &middot; [verb] *the other* &middot; *the other of the two* &middot; *some … other(s) …* &middot; *each other* (a separate reciprocal pronoun)
**Contrast:** **The -s is not optional.** Standing alone in a plural sense, the word must be *others*: ✗*Some like it hot; other don't* — write *others*. Standing in front of a noun it must never take the *-s*: ✗*others people*. And bare singular *other* without *the* is not a modern pronoun: ✗*I'll take other* — write *another* or *the other*. **The set-size rule:** with two, *one … the other*; with more than two, *one … another … another …*, and *the others* for all the rest.

The second of two, or the one that is left when the first has been accounted for.

1. One twin went to Boston; *the other* stayed home.
2. I have two keys — this one works, and *the other* doesn't.
3. She looked at both offers and took *the other*.
4. Of the two roads, *the other* is much faster this time of day.
5. He kept one copy and mailed *the other* to his lawyer.
6. Each brother blamed *the other* for the mess.

### others

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðɚz/ &middot; *UTH-urz*
**Русский:** другие; остальные — others «ещё какие-то», the others «все остальные»
**Type:** substitute pronoun, plural &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **always plural**
**Refers to:** additional or remaining people or things. Bare *others* is indefinite — some more, unspecified; *the others* is definite — all the rest of a known set.
**Agreement:** **plural, always** — *Others **were** waiting outside*; *The others **have** already left.*
**Pattern:** *others* [plural verb] &middot; *the others* [plural verb] &middot; *some … others …* &middot; [verb] *the others* &middot; *others of* [noun phrase] (formal) &middot; *and others* (the English of *et al.*)
**Contrast:** **Others vs. other:** the *-s* appears precisely because no noun follows — *other people* (determiner, no *-s*) but *others* (pronoun, *-s* required). **Others vs. the others:** without *the*, some unspecified additional ones; with *the*, every remaining member of a definite set — *Some left early; others stayed* leaves the number open, while *Two stayed; the others left* closes it. Against *another*: plural against singular.

The rest of a group, or some further members of it.

1. Some of the guests danced; *others* just watched.
2. *The others* had already gone home by the time we arrived.
3. She finished her section and helped *the others* with theirs.
4. A few complained, but *others* said nothing at all.
5. Two of the engines were repaired; *the others* were scrapped.
6. *Others* have tried this and failed.

### such

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ/ &middot; *SUCH*
**Русский:** таковой, такие — книжн. и юр., о только что описанном, часто о целой ситуации
**Type:** substitute pronoun (also a determiner, *such nonsense*, and a predeterminer, *such a mess*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and literary — the pronoun use is not ordinary conversational English
**Person / number:** third person; invariant; construes **singular or plural** with the referent
**Refers to:** things or people of the kind just described, or a whole preceding situation taken as a lump (*Such was the state of the roof*). Its antecedent is often an entire clause rather than a noun phrase.
**Agreement:** **agrees with what it refers to** — *Such **are** the risks* (plural referent); *Such **is** life* (singular). The verb typically inverts with it, since the pronoun is fronted.
**Pattern:** *such* [verb] [subject complement] (*Such is the case*) &middot; *such as* [clause] (formal: *such as remain*) &middot; *as such* (fixed adverbial) &middot; *and such* (informal: *tools and such*) &middot; *no such thing* (determiner) &middot; legal *such* + noun (*such defects*, a determiner)
**Contrast:** As a bare pronoun *such* has narrowed to a handful of frames — *such is life*, *such was the case*, *such are the risks*, *such as remain*. Everywhere else American English fills the slot with [that](04-demonstrative.md#that), [this](04-demonstrative.md#this), or [those](04-demonstrative.md#those): *Such was his answer* → *That was his answer*. The legal *such* of *such agreement* and *such party* is a **determiner** and belongs to the determiner dictionary. And *as such* traditionally means "in that capacity" or "strictly speaking," and it wants a noun antecedent to point back at: *He is the treasurer and as such signs the checks*. The causal *as such* meaning "therefore" — *It rained; as such, we stayed in* — has spread widely through business and academic prose, but there is no antecedent there for *such* to refer to, and the usage is still objected to often enough that *therefore*, *so*, or *for that reason* is the safer word.

Points back at a kind of thing just described, or at the situation as a whole.

1. *Such* is life.
2. *Such* were the terms he insisted on.
3. He gave no reason, and none was expected; *such* was his way.
4. Delays, floods, and the like — *such* are the risks the policy covers.
5. *Such* as remain unsold will be auctioned in the spring.
6. *Such* was the state of the roof that two tenants moved out that winter.
7. She was hired as an interpreter and *as such* attended every meeting. (fixed adverbial, not the pronoun)

**See also:** [so](04-demonstrative.md#so), [that](04-demonstrative.md#that), [those](04-demonstrative.md#those), [others](#others), [such (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such)

### the one

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈwʌn/ &middot; *thuh WUN* &middot; *one* begins with the consonant /w/, so *the* takes its ordinary weak form /ðə/ and not the pre-vowel /ði/; the /ði/ form appears only under contrastive stress, which is exactly what *he's THE one* is doing
**Русский:** тот самый — тот член множества, который выделяет следующее уточнение
**Type:** definite prop-word (substitute) pronoun phrase — the prop-word *one* with the definite article, used as a fused head &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**, with the plural *the ones*
**Refers to:** the particular member of a set that a following modifier, a relative clause, or the situation identifies — a thing or a person. It **needs** that identification: bare *the one* with nothing to pin it down works only where the context has already done the pinning (*That's the one*).
**Agreement:** **singular** — *The one on the left **is** cracked*; *The one who called **was** her brother.* The plural *the ones* takes a plural verb: *The ones we saw **have** already sold.* As a subject complement it does not control the verb at all — *You **are** the one who decides* — and the relative clause inside *one of those people who…* goes plural, a trap dealt with at [one](#one).
**Pattern:** *the one* + prepositional phrase (*the one on the left*) &middot; *the one* + relative clause (*the one that broke*, *the one who called*) &middot; *the one with / in / about* &middot; *that's the one*, *he's the one*, *she's not the one* &middot; *the one about* + a joke &middot; plural *the ones*
**Variants:** the plural *the ones*, which behaves the same way and is dictionaried at [ones](#ones); the demonstrative alternatives *this one* and *that one*
**Contrast:** Against bare prop-word [one](#one): *one* is indefinite and introduces a fresh instance (*I need a pen — do you have one?*), while *the one* is definite and picks a known instance out of a set (*the one in the drawer*). Against [ones](#ones): a difference of number only, with the caution that bare *ones* cannot stand alone where *one* can. Against *this one* and *that one*: the demonstratives point, while *the one* identifies by description, which is why something almost always follows it. Two idioms are worth naming, and both live entirely off the definite article — *the one* meaning the destined person (*He thinks she's the one*) and *the one* introducing a joke (*the one about the mule*).

The particular member that the words around it identify — the definite half of the prop-word *one*.

1. *The one* on the left has a chip in it.
2. She's *the one* who called the inspector.
3. Of the four keys he tried, *the one* that worked was the oldest.
4. That's *the one* — the blue folder on top of the pile.
5. He's convinced she's *the one*, and they have known each other three weeks.
6. Tell them *the one* about the mule.
7. *The ones* we saw at the fair were half the price. (plural)

**See also:** [one](#one), [ones](#ones), [another](#another), [other](#other), [the usual](#the-usual), [this one / that one](04-demonstrative.md#this-one--that-one), [those who](04-demonstrative.md#those-who), [he who, he that, she who, they that](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#he-who-he-that-she-who-they-that), [one (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one)

### the usual

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈjuʒuəl/, in ordinary speech /ðə ˈjuʒwəl/ and /ðə ˈjuʒəl/ &middot; *thuh YOO-zhoo-ul*, casually *thuh YOO-zhul* &middot; three syllables said carefully and two said quickly; *the* is the weak /ðə/ before the consonantal /j/
**Русский:** как обычно, обычное — разг., то, что собеседник и так знает по привычке
**Type:** fused-head pronoun phrase — the adjective *usual* standing where its noun would be (also the ordinary adjective, *the usual suspects*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — the language of bars, diners, and standing arrangements, and of dry understatement in writing
**Person / number:** third person; **singular**
**Refers to:** whatever the hearer already knows to be habitual — the drink you always order, the excuse he always gives, the trouble that always happens there. Its antecedent is **shared knowledge** rather than anything on the page, which is why the phrase works across a bar and fails in a document.
**Agreement:** **singular** — *The usual **is** waiting for you at the end of the bar*; *The usual **has** been happening at that intersection all year.* Where the referent is plainly plural the fused head turns unnatural and the noun comes back: *the usual complaints were filed*, not ✗*the usual were filed*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *the usual* (*I'll have the usual*) &middot; *the usual* [singular verb] &middot; *the usual from* [somewhere] &middot; as a bare answer (*How was your week? — The usual.*) &middot; *as usual*, *business as usual* (fixed adverbials, not this pronoun) &middot; *the usual suspects*, *the usual channels* (the adjective in front of a noun)
**Contrast:** Against *the same*: *the same* points back at something just named (*I'll have the same*), while *the usual* points at a standing habit that may never have been mentioned — the two are close, and the difference is where the antecedent lives. Against *such* and *the like*: those generalize about a kind of thing, this identifies one habitual instance. Against *as usual*: a fixed adverbial and a different word class, since *as usual, he was late* modifies the clause while *I'll have the usual* fills an object slot. Because the phrase runs on shared knowledge, **never in** writing for a reader who does not already have it: in a report, name the thing.

Whatever both of you already know is habitual — the fused head that runs entirely on shared knowledge.

1. *The usual*, please, and put it on my tab.
2. He asked how the week had gone and she said, *the usual*.
3. *The usual* was waiting for him at the end of the bar before he sat down.
4. They wanted an explanation and got *the usual*.
5. Breakfast was *the usual* — eggs, toast, and the crossword.
6. *The usual* has been happening at that intersection since they changed the light.

### two

**Pronunciation:** /tu/ &middot; *TOO* &middot; a homophone of *to* and *too*, and unlike them it never reduces — the numeral keeps its full vowel and normally carries stress, which is the audible difference between *I have two* and *I have to*
**Русский:** двое, две штуки — числительное-заместитель уже названных счётных предметов
**Type:** cardinal numeral used as a **fused head** — the numeral standing where its noun would be (also a determiner, *two chairs*, and an ordinary noun, *the number two*) &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person; **plural** for every cardinal from *two* upward; *one* is the singular member of the series and is dictionaried at [one](#one)
**Refers to:** the stated number of **countable** things or people already named or understood. Never a mass: ✗*two of the water*. The numeral counts exactly, which is the one thing that separates it from every other pronoun in this dictionary.
**Agreement:** **plural, always, from *two* up** — *Two **were** missing*; *Two of the panels **have** come loose*; *A dozen **were** broken in shipping.* Note that *a dozen* is plural in the same way in spite of its singular article, because the head of the phrase is a number and not a noun.
**Pattern:** [numeral] [plural verb] (*Three arrived*) &middot; [numeral] *of* [determiner + plural noun] &middot; [numeral] *of* [object pronoun] &middot; [verb] [numeral] (*I'll take three*) &middot; *the first two*, *all three*, *any two*, *another two* &middot; *a dozen*, *half a dozen*, *a hundred*, *a thousand* on the same pattern
**Variants:** every cardinal above *one* — *three*, *four*, *ten*, *forty-one* — and the countable group nouns used the same way: *a dozen*, *half a dozen*, *a pair*, *a score*, *a hundred*, *a thousand*. The indefinite plurals *dozens*, *hundreds*, and *thousands* are a different construction and are at [hundreds](#hundreds).
**Contrast:** The numeral is a pronoun only when the noun is gone. *Two chairs are missing* is a determiner in front of a noun and belongs to the determiner dictionary; *Two are missing* is this pronoun. Against *a couple*: *two* is exact and *a couple* approximate in ordinary American use, which is why an invoice says *two* and a story says *a couple*. Against *both*: *both* also names two, but it insists that they are the two already known and that neither is left out — *Two of them agreed* comes out of an open set, *Both of them agreed* out of a set of exactly two. Against *hundreds* and *dozens*: the *-s* turns an exact count into an estimate. And the *of* is obligatory before a determiner or a pronoun: *two of the panels*, *two of them*, ✗*two them*.

A bare number standing where its noun would be — the plainest fused head in the language.

1. We ordered six chairs and *two* arrived broken.
2. *Three* of them had never been opened.
3. She read the first chapter twice and skipped the next *two*.
4. Of the twelve applications, only *four* were complete.
5. I'll take *a dozen*, and make half of them plain.
6. *Two* of us stayed behind to lock up.
7. He asked for volunteers and *a dozen* put their hands up.
