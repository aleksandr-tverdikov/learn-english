# Reflexive, intensive, and reciprocal pronouns

> The *-self* words and the two reciprocals — the pronouns that bend a sentence back onto its own subject, or pass the action around a group.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every pronoun in this file needs something else in the sentence to lean on. A reflexive (*she taught **herself***) is used when the object of a verb or preposition names the same person as the subject of **that same clause**; an intensive (*I fixed it **myself***) is the identical word doing a different job — pure emphasis, deletable without changing the meaning. The reciprocals *each other* and *one another* need a plural or coordinated antecedent and distribute the action across it, so that *they blamed **each other*** and *they blamed **themselves*** describe two entirely different arguments. The single trap that runs through the whole file is the **untriggered reflexive** — *please contact **myself***, *Bob and **myself** attended* — where a speaker reaches for an *-self* word as a politeness upgrade in a clause that has no *I* in it to license it, and where American style guides and editors mark it wrong.

**The licensing test, applied to every entry here.** Look inside the clause for the matching antecedent: *myself* needs an *I*, *yourself* needs a *you*, *themselves* needs a plural third person. No antecedent in the clause, no *-self* word — use the plain object pronoun ([me](01-personal.md#me), [him](01-personal.md#him), [her](01-personal.md#her), [them](01-personal.md#them)) or the subject pronoun ([I](01-personal.md#i), [he](01-personal.md#he), [she](01-personal.md#she), [they](01-personal.md#they)), whichever the slot calls for. Two consequences follow. First, **no reflexive can be a subject**: ✗*Myself and Diane wrote the grant* is *Diane and **I** wrote the grant*. Second, a preposition that merely **locates** something takes the plain pronoun, not the reflexive — *she had the map beside **her***, *he shut the door behind **him***, *I have no cash on **me*** — because those phrases place an object in space rather than naming a second participant. *Beside herself* is not a location; it means overcome with emotion.

**Case: what the label means here, and the one place it goes wrong.** The **Case:** line on every reflexive and intensive pronoun entry below reads *reflexive*, and what that means concretely is that the word fills an **object** slot — direct object (*she cut **herself***), indirect object (*I poured **myself** one*), object of a preposition (*he kept it to **himself***) — or stands as an appositive beside a noun already in the sentence (*the mayor **himself***). There is no reflexive subject form, because there is no reflexive subject. The reciprocals [each other](#each-other) and [one another](#one-another) are labeled **object** for the same reason. When the slot wants an ordinary pronoun instead, the choice is between subject case (*I, he, she, we, they*) and object case (*me, him, her, us, them*), and **case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause**. That rule is broken in exactly one environment — a coordination, where the extra conjunct hides the job from the ear — and it is broken in both directions:

- **Subject slot takes the subject form.** ✓*She and **I** went to the closing.* ✓*My brother and **he** drove down together.* The nonstandard version — ✗*Me and him went*, object forms doing a subject's job — is repaired as *He and **I** went*.
- **Object slot takes the object form.** ✓*He gave it to Bob and **me**.* ✓*They invited my wife and **me**.* The hypercorrection ✗*They invited my wife and I* is the mirror image — an error manufactured by trying to avoid the first one.
- **After a preposition, the object form, always.** ✓*between you and **me***, ✓*for Karen and **me***, ✓*with him and **her***. ✗*Between you and I* is the most-repeated hypercorrection in American English. *Between* is a preposition, and no preposition in the language takes *I*.

**The deletion test settles all of it in one second.** Take the other person out of the sentence and read what is left. *Bob and I signed* leaves ***I** signed*, never *me signed*, so the coordination is *Bob and **I** signed*. *He gave it to Bob and me* leaves *he gave it to **me***, never *he gave it to I*, so the coordination is *to Bob and **me***. The one coordination the test cannot reach is *between* plus its two conjuncts, since removing either one leaves *between* with nothing to govern — there, fall back on the flat rule that a preposition takes the object form. Two footnotes. The order convention puts the first-person conjunct last (*my wife and I*, not *I and my wife*); that is courtesy, not grammar, and it changes no case. And *myself* is not an escape hatch from the choice: ✗*Bob and myself signed*, ✗*please contact myself* do not sidestep the case question, they add a second error on top of it — see [myself](#myself).

**Agreement, when the antecedent is an indefinite.** A reflexive carries no verb agreement of its own; the verb agrees with the **subject of its clause**, and when that subject is an indefinite pronoun the two halves of the sentence can legitimately disagree in shape. The rows worth memorizing, because half of them are taught wrong:

- ***everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody*** take a **singular verb**: *Everyone **is** here*, *Somebody **has** left a coat*, *Nobody **was** hurt*. Their reflexive is nevertheless normally *themselves*, or *themself* for one person: *Everyone **is** expected to sign in **themselves***. Singular verb, plural-shaped reflexive — both halves standard, in the same sentence.
- ***each*** takes a **singular verb**: *Each of the applicants **signs** in.* So do ***either*** and ***neither***: *Either **is** acceptable*, *Neither of them **is** ready*. (Plural verbs after *neither of them* are common in speech; edited American prose keeps the singular.)
- ***both, several, few, many, others*** take a **plural verb**, and their reflexive is *themselves*: *Both **were** at fault, and both blamed **themselves***.
- ***none*** takes **either**, and this is the row where the schoolroom rule is simply false. *None of the crew **was** injured* and *None of the crew **were** injured* are both standard; plural *none* is as old as English, and "*none* means *not one*, so it must be singular" is a nineteenth-century invention.
- ***all, some, most, any*** agree with **what they refer to**: *All of the water **is** gone* but *All of the guests **have** seated **themselves***; *Most of the report **is** finished* but *Most of the pages **are** done*.

The full entries for these words are in [indefinite and quantifying pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md); what matters here is which *-self* word they license, and the answer is almost always *themselves* or *themself*.

**Antecedents inside relative clauses.** A relative clause has a subject of its own, so it licenses a reflexive of its own: *the woman **who** hurt **herself***, *the machine **that** shut **itself** off*, *the account **which** balances **itself** monthly*. Which relative word to use is a separate question with a short answer — ***who*** for people, ***which*** for things, ***that*** for both, ***whose*** for both people and things (*the writer **whose** book sold **itself***, *the statute **whose** wording contradicts **itself***) — and the full treatment, including how little of *whom* is left, is in [relative pronouns](05-relative.md).

**Pronunciation, in two parts.** These are among the very few pronouns that carry stress, and it always lands on *-self* / *-selves*, never on the first syllable: *muh-SELF*, *er-SELF*, *thuhm-SELVZ*, *yer-SELVZ*. The *-self* half therefore never reduces. The first half does, to the same **weak forms** the plain pronouns use when unstressed — *him* /ɪm/, *her* /ɚ/, *them* /əm/, *you* /jə/, and *he* /i/ (*did 'e hurt 'imself?* /dɪd i hɚt ɪmˈsɛlf/) — which gives *himself* /ɪmˈsɛlf/, *herself* /ɚˈsɛlf/, *themselves* /əmˈsɛlvz/, *yourself* /jəˈsɛlf/, and *myself* /məˈsɛlf/ in ordinary connected speech. The /h/ of *him-* and *her-* drops routinely inside a clause and is pronounced at the start of one. Hearing these reduced forms is most of what it takes to follow fast American speech.

**Two smaller things worth noticing.** The reflexives are the only corner of modern English where the second person still marks number — *you* covers both, but *yourself* and *yourselves* do not. And the possessive determiners *my, your, his, her, its, our, their* are not pronouns: they live in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md), while the standalone possessive pronouns *mine, yours, hers, ours, theirs* are in [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md). **No determiner gets an entry in this file.** The one entry below built on a determiner, [one's self](#ones-self), is entered as what it is — the determiner *one's* in front of the ordinary noun *self* — because it is the form writers reach for when they want the pronoun [oneself](#oneself); the determiner itself lives at [one's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#ones). Demonstrative *them* (*them apples*) is cross-referenced to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them) rather than entered here, and the only genitives entered below — [each other's](#each-others) and [one another's](#one-anothers) — are inflected forms of the reciprocal **pronouns**, the one place this class inflects at all; for the determiner slot they fill in front of a noun, see [each other's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#each-others).

**Where this file stands on disputed usage.** Four things stated plainly, so that nothing below reads as a rule it is not. **Singular *they* is standard American English, edited prose included** — it is entered in Merriam-Webster, accepted by the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style, attested since the fourteenth century, and its reflexive is [themself](#themself) or [themselves](#themselves); neither is an error. It is not a concession to informality and not a workaround: it is the ordinary American form for one person of unknown, unstated, or nonbinary gender, and it appears as such in newspapers, court opinions, and university presses. ***It's me* is standard**, not a mistake to be repaired into *It is I*, which is defensible grammar and stilted English outside the telephone formula *This is she* (see [me](01-personal.md#me)). ***Whom* survives mainly after a preposition** — *to whom*, *for whom*, *several of whom* — and elsewhere American English has largely handed the object slot to *who*; *the agent who we hired* is a register choice, not an error (see [whom](05-relative.md#whom)). ***That* versus restrictive *which* is an editing convention**, not grammar: the only hard rule in that neighborhood is that *that* cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause (see [which](05-relative.md#which)). What this file does **not** soften: the untriggered *myself*, ✗*between you and I*, ✗*Me and him went*, and vernacular *hisself* / *theirself* / *theirselves* and the rare *theyself* are all still marked wrong in edited American English. Being honest about what has changed is not permission to pretend that nothing is an error. **Neopronoun reflexives** — *aerself*, *coself*, *eirself*, *emself*, *faerself*, *hirself*, *nemself*, *perself*, *verself*, *xemself*, *zirself* — get their own section at the end of the file, described rather than ruled on: they are the *-self* forms of pronoun sets used by a small number of speakers, most of them nonbinary, they take singular verb agreement where singular *they* takes plural, and the way to get one right is to use the set the person names.

## Reflexive and intensive pronouns

Ten forms, one paradigm. Each is built from a possessive or object base plus *-self* / *-selves*, and each does double duty: **reflexive** when it fills an object slot already occupied in meaning by the subject, **intensive** when it is an appositive tacked on for emphasis and could be deleted. The removal test separates them — delete the word, and if the sentence survives unchanged it was intensive. Two neighbors are entered alongside the ten: the coordinate phrase [himself or herself](#himself-or-herself), a reflexive assembled out of two of them, and [one's self](#ones-self), which is **not** a pronoun at all but the noun *self* behind a possessive determiner — entered because it is what writers produce when they mean [oneself](#oneself).

### herself

**Pronunciation:** /hɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *her-SELF* &middot; weak /ɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *er-SELF* — the /h/ drops routinely inside a clause (*she hurt 'erself*); the stress is on *-self* in both the reflexive and the intensive use
**Русский:** себя, сама — возвратное и усилительное 3 л. ед. ч. ж. р.; рус. «себя»/«-ся» рода не различает
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, feminine
**Refers to:** a female human antecedent, and in older or affectionate usage a ship, a country, or a named storm. In the reflexive use the antecedent must sit in the **same clause**: *Karen said that Beth cut **herself*** can only mean Beth cut Beth. For a person of unknown or nonbinary gender use [themself](#themself) or [themselves](#themselves).
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own — it is an object, and the verb agrees with the clause's subject. As an intensive it is an appositive and never changes the verb: *the senator herself **is** here*, *the senators themselves **are** here*.
**Pattern:** [she / female NP] [verb] *herself* &middot; [she / female NP] [verb] [preposition] *herself* &middot; [female NP] *herself* [verb] &middot; [female NP] [verb] [object] *herself* &middot; *by herself* &middot; *beside herself*
**Contrast:** *Herself* against [her](01-personal.md#her): reflexive when the object is the same person as the subject (*she cut **herself***), plain *her* when it is someone else (*he cut **her***) and when the preposition merely locates something (*she pulled the blanket over **her***, *she had the map beside **her***). *Beside herself* is the idiom, not the location — it means distraught. *Herself* can never be a subject: ✗*Herself and Diane wrote the grant* → *Diane and **she** wrote the grant*.

Points back at a female subject, or leans on a feminine noun phrase to emphasize it. English drops the reflexive after grooming verbs where many languages keep it — *she washed*, *she dressed*, *she shaved her legs* — so *she washed herself* is grammatical but sounds like a report from a nursing home rather than ordinary description.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *She taught herself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on a feminine noun phrase, deletable.** *The governor herself signed it.*
**3. In fixed expressions.** *by herself* (alone, or unaided), *beside herself* (overcome), *not herself* / *herself again* (out of, or restored to, normal condition).

1. She taught *herself* to weld from library books. *(1)*
2. My mother poured *herself* a second cup and sat down. *(1)*
3. She wasn't thinking about *herself* when she said that. *(1)*
4. Did she hurt *herself* on the ladder? *(1)*
5. The governor *herself* signed the pardon. *(2)*
6. She rebuilt the transmission *herself*. *(2)*
7. Nobody rode with her; she drove all eleven hours by *herself*. *(3)*
8. She was *beside herself* when the adoption came through. *(3)*
9. She hasn't been *herself* since the layoffs. *(3)*

**See also:** [himself](#himself), [themself](#themself), [oneself](#oneself), [her](01-personal.md#her), [hers](02-possessive.md#hers), [her (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#her)

### himself

**Pronunciation:** /hɪmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *him-SELF* &middot; weak /ɪmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *im-SELF*, and in casual speech /ɪmˈsɛf/ — the /h/ drops inside a clause (*he cut 'imself*)
**Русский:** себя, сам — возвратное и усилительное 3 л. ед. ч. м. р.; рус. «себя»/«-ся» рода не различает
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine
**Refers to:** a male human antecedent, and occasionally a male animal. In the reflexive use the antecedent must be in the same clause. **Generic *himself* for a person of unspecified sex is dated** — *every applicant should identify himself* now reads as either sexist or archaic; the current choices are *themself* / *themselves*, the plural rewrite (*applicants should identify themselves*), or the heavier *himself or herself*.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. The intensive appositive leaves the verb alone: *the author himself **admits** it*.
**Pattern:** [he / male NP] [verb] *himself* &middot; [he / male NP] [verb] [preposition] *himself* &middot; [male NP] *himself* [verb] &middot; [male NP] [verb] [object] *himself* &middot; *by himself* &middot; *to himself*
**Contrast:** *Himself* against [him](01-personal.md#him): reflexive when subject and object are the same man (*he blamed **himself***), plain *him* when they are not (*she blamed **him***) and after prepositions of place (*he closed the door behind **him***, *he had a hundred dollars on **him***). Against [hisself](#hisself): *hisself* is the regular formation and the nonstandard one; *himself* is the irregular form that standard English happens to have kept. And *himself* is not a subject: ✗*John and himself signed* → *John and **he** signed*.

Points back at a male subject, or emphasizes a masculine noun phrase. The intensive use is where *himself* earns its keep in journalism and testimony — *the mayor himself* signals that the action reached the top of the chain and did not stop at a deputy.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *He hurt himself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on a masculine noun phrase, deletable.** *The mayor himself called.*
**3. In fixed expressions.** *by himself* (alone, unaided), *to himself* (privately, or exclusively), *full of himself*, *come to himself*, *not himself*.

1. He cut *himself* shaving and bled onto his collar. *(1)*
2. He never forgave *himself* for missing the flight. *(1)*
3. He talks to *himself* when he's reading contracts. *(1)*
4. Why would he do that to *himself*? *(1)*
5. The mayor *himself* showed up at the ribbon cutting. *(2)*
6. He wired the whole garage *himself*. *(2)*
7. He lives by *himself* out past the reservoir. *(3)*
8. He kept the diagnosis to *himself* for six weeks. *(3)*
9. He hasn't been *himself* since the divorce. *(3)*

**See also:** [herself](#herself), [themself](#themself), [hisself](#hisself), [him](01-personal.md#him), [his](02-possessive.md#his), [his (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his)

### himself or herself

**Pronunciation:** /hɪmˈsɛlf ɔr hɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *him-SELF or her-SELF* &middot; weak /ɪmˈsɛlf ɚ ɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *im-SELF er er-SELF* — both /h/s drop inside a clause and *or* reduces to /ɚ/, so the phrase runs as a string of unstressed syllables around two stressed *-selfs*
**Русский:** сам или сама, себя — юр./книжн. родонейтральная формула, ныне вытесняемая themself
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun, coordinate phrase &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, contractual, regulatory, and institutional prose. It was the standard American editorial repair for the generic *himself* from roughly the mid-1970s to the mid-2010s, and in ordinary writing it now reads as heavy or legalistic.
**Person / number:** third person singular, sex-inclusive by coordination — it covers a male referent or a female one, one at a time, and stays singular throughout
**Refers to:** one person of unstated sex, licensed by a singular antecedent in the same clause: a generic noun phrase (*each employee*, *any applicant*, *the taxpayer*) or a singular indefinite (*everyone*, *anybody*, *whoever*). Because the phrase names two sexes and stops, it does not cover a nonbinary referent; for that, and for ordinary modern prose generally, use [themself](#themself) or [themselves](#themselves).
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own, and it does not pluralize its antecedent: *each applicant **is** expected to identify **himself or herself***, never ✗*each applicant are*. What usually breaks it is repetition — *each employee must submit his or her timesheet himself or herself* is grammatical and unreadable, which is the practical argument against the phrase rather than any grammatical one.
**Pattern:** [singular generic NP] [verb] *himself or herself* &middot; [singular indefinite] [verb] [preposition] *himself or herself* &middot; [singular NP] *himself or herself* [verb] &middot; *by himself or herself*
**Variants:** *herself or himself*, the same phrase in the other order, common in prose that alternates deliberately &middot; the suspended written form *him- or herself*, standard in legal drafting &middot; the slashed *himself/herself*, ordinary on forms and avoided in edited prose &middot; ✗*hisself or herself*, which drags the vernacular base into the formula
**Contrast:** Against [themself](#themself) and [themselves](#themselves) — the same job, one editorial generation apart. *Himself or herself* is what American style guides recommended once generic *himself* fell out of favor; after the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style both accepted singular *they* in 2017, the reflexive of choice became *themself* or *themselves*, and the coordinate phrase now survives mainly where a document has to track older statutory wording. Against generic [himself](#himself), which it was built to replace and which reads as dated or sexist today. Against the determiner *his or her* (*each employee must bring **his or her** badge*), which sits in front of a noun and is therefore not a pronoun at all — see [his or her (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her). Three repairs beat the phrase in most sentences: go plural (*employees must identify **themselves***), use singular *they* (*each employee must identify **themself***), or drop the reflexive altogether (*each employee must sign in*).

The two-sex coordinate reflexive. It is grammatical, unambiguous, and long, which is why it lives on in contracts, statutes, and compliance manuals — where precision outranks rhythm — and why editors of ordinary prose reach for *themself* or a plural rewrite instead.

**1. Reflexive with a generic or indefinite singular antecedent.** *Each employee must conduct himself or herself professionally.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on a generic singular noun phrase, deletable.** *The homeowner himself or herself must sign.*
**3. In drafted and fixed phrases.** *by himself or herself* (unaided), *on behalf of himself or herself*, *avail himself or herself of*.

1. No employee may approve a reimbursement to *himself or herself*. *(1)*
2. A defendant has the right to represent *himself or herself* at trial. *(1)*
3. Every applicant should ask *himself or herself* whether the commute is realistic. *(1)*
4. Anyone who is locked out can let *himself or herself* in with the keypad code. *(1)*
5. The policyholder *himself or herself* must sign the release; a spouse cannot. *(2)*
6. A tenant who makes the repair *himself or herself* may deduct the cost from the rent. *(2)*
7. A member may avail *himself or herself* of the grievance procedure at any time. *(3)*
8. A voter who cannot mark the ballot *by himself or herself* may request assistance at the polling place. *(3)*

**See also:** [himself](#himself), [herself](#herself), [themself](#themself), [themselves](#themselves), [oneself](#oneself), [his or her (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her)

### itself

**Pronunciation:** /ɪtˈsɛlf/ &middot; *it-SELF* — the /t/ before /s/ is usually unreleased or glottalized, /ɪʔˈsɛlf/, never flapped; there is no weak form, since *-self* always takes the stress
**Русский:** себя, сам, само — возвратное для предметов и понятий; by itself — сам по себе, автоматически
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, neuter; invariant
**Refers to:** an inanimate object, a substance, an abstraction, a process, an animal of unstated sex, an organization or country treated as a singular unit, or a whole preceding situation. It does **not** refer to a person, except when the person is being deliberately depersonalized. For a group treated as plural use [themselves](#themselves).
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. As an intensive it keeps the singular verb of its noun: *the building itself **is** sound*; contrast *the beams themselves **are** sound*.
**Pattern:** [inanimate subject] [verb] *itself* &middot; [inanimate subject] [verb] [preposition] *itself* &middot; [noun] *itself* [verb] &middot; *in itself* &middot; *in and of itself* &middot; *by itself* &middot; *of itself*
**Contrast:** *Itself* against [it](01-personal.md#it): reflexive when the thing acts on the thing (*the system reset **itself***), plain *it* otherwise (*she reset **it***). The three fixed phrases are not synonyms — ***by itself*** means alone or automatically (*the pilot light went out by itself*), ***in itself*** means intrinsically, considered apart from anything else (*the delay isn't fatal in itself*), and ***in and of itself*** is the emphatic version of *in itself*, common in American argument and redundant to some editors. ***Of itself*** is the older and now bookish variant of *in itself* (*the delay is not, of itself, unreasonable*). Against *it's*: *itself* has no apostrophe and never contracts.

Turns an inanimate or abstract subject back on itself, or emphasizes the thing as opposed to its parts, its copy, or its surroundings. The intensive use is doing real work in technical and legal writing, where *the statute itself* means the text as enacted and not the commentary around it.

**1. Reflexive: a thing acts on itself.** *The door locks itself.*
**2. Intensive: the thing as opposed to its parts or context.** *The engine itself is fine.*
**3. Fixed adverbial phrases.** *by itself*, *in itself*, *in and of itself*, *of itself*.

1. The software updates *itself* overnight. *(1)*
2. History repeats *itself*, usually with worse actors. *(1)*
3. The vine had wrapped *itself* around the downspout. *(1)*
4. The wound won't close *itself* — it needs stitches. *(1)*
5. The house *itself* is sound; the foundation is not. *(2)*
6. The word *itself* comes from Old Norse. *(2)*
7. The lamp switches on by *itself* every night around nine. *(3)*
8. A missed deadline is not, *in itself*, grounds for termination. *(3)*
9. That fact *in and of itself* proves nothing. *(3)*

**See also:** [themselves](#themselves), [oneself](#oneself), [it](01-personal.md#it), [its (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its)

### myself

**Pronunciation:** /maɪˈsɛlf/ &middot; *my-SELF* &middot; weak /məˈsɛlf/ &middot; *muh-SELF*, and in rapid speech /mˈsɛlf/, written informally *m'self*
**Русский:** себя, сам — возвратное 1 л. ед. ч.; без «I» в том же предложении это ошибка вместо me
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral in its licensed uses; **the untriggered use is a marked error in edited American English**
**Person / number:** first person singular
**Refers to:** the speaker, and only when the same clause already contains an *I* — or an understood first-person subject in a clause that has no overt one, as in *having taught **myself** the software, I …* or *to find **myself** back here*. No first person in the clause means no *myself*. An imperative is **not** such a clause: its understood subject is *you*, which licenses *yourself*, so ✗*Let myself in* is not a command anyone can give, while *I let **myself** in* is ordinary.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. The intensive appositive does not change the verb: *I myself **have** doubts*, not ✗*I myself **has** doubts*.
**Pattern:** *I* [verb] *myself* &middot; *I* [verb] [preposition] *myself* &middot; *I myself* [verb] &middot; *I* [verb] [object] *myself* &middot; *by myself* &middot; *to myself*
**Contrast:** **This is the most common register error in American business English.** ✗*Please contact myself with any questions* → *contact **me***. ✗*Bob and myself attended the review* → *Bob and **I** attended*. ✗*The report was prepared by Susan and myself* → *by Susan and **me***. **Two tests, in order.** First, the licensing test: is there an *I* earlier in the same clause for *myself* to point back at? If not, *myself* is wrong. Second, once you know it has to be *I* or *me*, take the other person out and listen: *Bob and I attended* leaves *I attended*, so *Bob and **I***; *contact Bob or me* leaves *contact **me***, so *Bob or **me***. The same nerve produces the same two errors with *myself* nowhere in sight: ✗*between you and I* and ✗*they invited my wife and I* are hypercorrections of *between you and **me*** and *they invited my wife and **me***, because a preposition or a verb takes the object form however many people are listed. After *but*, *than*, *as*, and *as for*, *myself* is common and widely tolerated (*no one but myself*, *as for myself*), though conservative editors still change it to *me*. One honest note on the main error: usage historians point out that *and myself* coordinations are centuries old and turn up in respectable writers. That has not moved the style guides, and in edited American prose the correction is still made.

Points the verb or preposition back at the speaker, or leans on an *I* already present to emphasize it. *Myself* is not more polite than *me* and not more formal than *I*; the instinct that it is, is exactly what produces the error above.

**1. Reflexive: I act on me.** *I cut myself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on an *I* already in the clause, deletable.** *I wrote it myself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by myself* (alone, or unaided), *to myself* (privately), *beside myself* (overcome), *not myself*.

1. I burned *myself* on the oven rack. *(1)*
2. I gave *myself* an extra day to finish the draft. *(1)*
3. I don't consider *myself* an expert on any of this. *(1)*
4. I caught *myself* checking my phone during her toast. *(1)*
5. I *myself* have never voted in a primary. *(2)*
6. I hung the drywall *myself*, badly. *(2)*
7. I ate the whole thing by *myself* and regretted it. *(3)*
8. I kept my opinion to *myself* through the entire meeting. *(3)*
9. I haven't felt like *myself* all week. *(3)*

### one's self

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnz ˈsɛlf/ &middot; *wunz SELF* &middot; two words, two stresses, with the phrase stress on the noun *self*; there is no weak form, because *self* here is a full noun and keeps its vowel — contrast the pronoun [oneself](#oneself), one word, stressed *wun-SELF*
**Русский:** своё «я», своя личность — не местоимение: притяж. one's + существительное self (книжн.)
**Type:** **not a pronoun** — the possessive determiner *one's* plus the count noun *self* &middot; **Case:** genitive determiner + noun; the phrase as a whole fills a noun slot &middot; **Register:** formal — philosophy, psychology, therapy, and religious writing; it is also the ordinary nineteenth-century printed spelling of the pronoun now written *oneself*
**Person / number:** not applicable — *self* is an ordinary count noun with the plural *selves* (*one's earlier selves*), and the determiner *one's* belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#ones). Nothing in the phrase inflects for person.
**Refers to:** a person's identity, character, or psyche — the self as a **thing**, not a slot pointing back at a subject. It therefore needs no antecedent in its clause, which is the deepest difference between it and [oneself](#oneself): *a threat to one's self* names something a person has, while *one should behave oneself* points back at *one*.
**Agreement:** as a noun phrase it can head a subject and takes a **singular** verb — *one's self **is** not fixed at twenty* — and the plural takes a plural verb: *one's earlier selves **are** embarrassing*. The pronoun *oneself* can do neither, because no reflexive can be a subject.
**Pattern:** *one's* [adjective] *self* — *one's true self*, *one's better self* &middot; [preposition] *one's self* &middot; *one's selves* (plural) &middot; ✗*one should behave one's self*, where the reflexive slot takes *oneself*
**Variants:** the same two-word shape runs through the whole paradigm as an ordinary noun phrase — *my self*, *your self*, *his self*, *their selves* — and none of them is a reflexive. Historically the two-word spelling was also the normal printing of the pronoun itself: through the nineteenth century American books set *one's self* where a modern editor sets *oneself*, so in older texts it is a period spelling rather than an error, though reproducing it today looks like one. The vernacular emphatic [my own self](#my-own-self) is the same construction with the emphasizer *own* wedged in.
**Contrast:** **Three tests separate the noun phrase from the pronoun, and all three take one second.** First, an adjective can slip in — *one's **true** self*, *one's **better** self* — and nothing can be inserted into *oneself*. Second, it pluralizes: *one's earlier **selves***. Third, it can be a subject: *one's self **changes***. When none of the three works, the word wanted is [oneself](#oneself): ✗*one should behave one's self*, ✗*one owes it to one's self*. The same split runs one person over between [myself](#myself) and the noun phrase *my self*, and it is why the emphatic [my own self](#my-own-self) is a phrase rather than a pronoun.

The two-word noun phrase that shadows the pronoun *oneself* — the determiner *one's* plus the noun *self*, naming the identity a person has rather than the person a verb points back at. It is entered here for the same reason [it's](02-possessive.md#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun) is entered among the possessives: the confusion runs in one direction only, and knowing which word is which settles it.

**1. The self as a thing: identity, character, psyche.** *a threat to one's self*
**2. With an adjective between the determiner and the noun.** *one's better self*, *one's true self*
**3. In the plural.** *one's earlier selves*
**4. As a nineteenth-century spelling of the pronoun.** *One should behave one's self.* (older printing)
**5. Wrong in the modern reflexive slot.** ✗*one owes it to one's self* → *oneself*

1. Solitude can be a threat to *one's self* as easily as a refuge for it. *(1)*
2. The lecture treated *one's self* as something built rather than something found. *(1)*
3. Grief rearranges *one's self* along with everything else. *(1)*
4. A room of one's own turns out to be easier to find than *one's self*. *(1)*
5. The essay distinguishes *one's true self* from the version one performs at work. *(2)*
6. It takes years to forgive *one's earlier selves*. *(3)*
7. Victorian editions print *one's self* where we now print *oneself*. *(4)*
8. ✗ One should always behave *one's self* at the table. → ✓ behave *oneself*. *(5)*

### oneself

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnˈsɛlf/ &middot; *wun-SELF* &middot; occasionally /ˌwʌnˈsɛlf/ with a secondary stress on the first syllable in careful speech; no weak form
**Русский:** себя, сам — возвратное к обобщённому one; книжн., в формулировках правил «для всех вообще»
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** formal; it belongs to the register that uses generic *one* at all, and it is rare in American speech
**Person / number:** third person singular, generic and gender-neutral; refers to people in general, including the speaker and hearer
**Refers to:** the generic person named by [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one) in the same clause — *one must take care of **oneself***. It cannot point at a specific individual, and it cannot be licensed by *you*, *he*, or *they*: ✗*you should pace oneself* → *pace **yourself***.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. Its antecedent *one* takes a singular verb (*one **is** expected to introduce oneself*), and American usage keeps the whole chain in *one* rather than switching to *he* or *they* mid-sentence.
**Pattern:** *one* [verb] *oneself* &middot; *one* [verb] [preposition] *oneself* &middot; [infinitive] *oneself* (*to help oneself*) &middot; *by oneself* &middot; *to oneself*
**Contrast:** *Oneself* against *one's self*: the solid spelling is the pronoun (*one should behave **oneself***); the two-word *one's self* is a possessive plus the noun *self*, used only when *self* means identity or psyche (*a threat to one's self*). Against [yourself](#yourself): American English overwhelmingly prefers generic *you* and *yourself* in speech (*you have to pace **yourself***), reserving *one* / *oneself* for formal prose. Two errors to name: ✗*one should behave one's self*, which puts the noun where the pronoun belongs, and ✗*the applicant should ask oneself*, which attaches the generic reflexive to a specific subject — repaired as *the applicant should ask **themself*** or *…**himself or herself***.

Serves as the reflexive of generic *one*. It is the form that lets a writer state a rule about people in general without naming a gender, which is why it survives in ethics, medicine, etiquette, and legal prose while nearly vanishing from conversation.

**1. Reflexive with generic *one*.** *One should not overtax oneself.*
**2. In infinitival and gerundive phrases with no subject.** *to make oneself understood*, *keeping oneself busy*.
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by oneself*, *to oneself*, *be oneself*, *keep to oneself*, *help oneself to*.

1. One cannot expect to change *oneself* in a weekend. *(1)*
2. One owes it to *oneself* to read the fine print. *(1)*
3. It is difficult to make *oneself* understood over that connection. *(2)*
4. Learning to forgive *oneself* takes longer than forgiving anyone else. *(2)*
5. Keeping *oneself* out of the argument was the wiser course. *(2)*
6. There is no shame in dining by *oneself*. *(3)*
7. The trick at these events is simply to be *oneself*. *(3)*
8. One is expected to help *oneself* to the coffee. *(3)*

### ourselves

**Pronunciation:** /ɑrˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *ar-SELVZ*; also /aʊɚˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *ow-er-SELVZ* in careful speech; weak /ɚˈsɛlvz/ in fast speech, which is why *ourselves* and *yourselves* can sound alike
**Русский:** себя, сами — возвратное 1 л. мн. ч.: «мы сами», «сами себя», «сами по себе»
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** first person plural
**Refers to:** the speaker together with at least one other person, licensed by a *we* or *us* in the same clause. Both the inclusive *we* (speaker plus hearer) and the exclusive *we* (speaker plus others) take *ourselves*.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. The intensive keeps the plural verb of *we*: *we ourselves **were** surprised*, never ✗*we ourselves **was***.
**Pattern:** *we* [verb] *ourselves* &middot; *we* [verb] [preposition] *ourselves* &middot; *we ourselves* [verb] &middot; *by ourselves* &middot; *among ourselves* &middot; *to ourselves*
**Contrast:** *Ourselves* against [us](01-personal.md#us): reflexive when subject and object are the same group (*we let **ourselves** in*), plain *us* otherwise (*she let **us** in*) and after prepositions of place (*we had the whole beach around **us***). Against [ourself](#ourself): the plural is *ourselves* in every ordinary use; *ourself* survives only with the royal or editorial *we*. And *ourselves* is never a subject: ✗*My wife and ourselves went* → *my wife and **I** went*, subject case, with the first-person conjunct last. Against [each other](#each-other): *we blamed **ourselves*** means the group blamed the group; *we blamed **each other*** means the blame went crosswise inside it.

The first person plural reflexive. It carries a small extra load in American English through *among ourselves* and *keep it to ourselves*, both of which mark information as staying inside a group.

**1. Reflexive: the group acts on the group.** *We let ourselves in.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on a *we* or *us* already present.** *We built it ourselves.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by ourselves*, *among ourselves*, *to ourselves*, *beside ourselves*.

1. We taught *ourselves* enough Spanish to order dinner. *(1)*
2. We found *ourselves* stuck behind a parade. *(1)*
3. We shouldn't kid *ourselves* about the numbers. *(1)*
4. We *ourselves* had no idea the lease was expiring. *(2)*
5. We poured the slab *ourselves* over two weekends. *(2)*
6. We had the campground to *ourselves* until Friday. *(3)*
7. We'll sort it out among *ourselves*. *(3)*

### themself

**Pronunciation:** /ðəmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thuhm-SELF* &middot; weak /əmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *uhm-SELF* (*somebody hurt 'emself*)
**Русский:** себя, сам(а) — возвратное к singular they: об одном человеке неизвестного или небинарного пола
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** standard and neutral — old in the language but only recently re-established in this use, and some editors still change it to *themselves*
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, gender-neutral — the reflexive of singular *they*
**Refers to:** one person whose gender is unknown, unstated, irrelevant, or nonbinary, licensed by a singular *they* or by a singular indefinite (*someone*, *anyone*, *each applicant*) in the same clause. Not used for plurals: a plural antecedent takes [themselves](#themselves).
**Agreement:** **the verb stays plural even though the reference is singular** — *they consider **themself** lucky*, never ✗*they considers*. When the antecedent is an indefinite, the indefinite keeps its own singular verb while the reflexive goes *-self*: *everyone **is** expected to introduce **themself***. Both halves of that sentence are correct as written.
**Pattern:** *they* [verb] *themself* &middot; [singular indefinite] [verb] *themself* &middot; [singular NP] *themself* [verb] &middot; *by themself*
**Contrast:** *Themself* against [themselves](#themselves) is a live split. Both are used for singular *they*, and both are defensible: Merriam-Webster enters *themself* as standard, the major American style guides now accept singular *they* and several allow *themself* alongside it, and *themself* is in fact the older of the two — it was the ordinary reflexive of singular *they* in Middle English, before *themselves* pushed it aside. Even so, *themselves* remains more frequent in edited American prose, including with singular reference. **The practical rule: match the number of the reference, not the shape of the pronoun — one person, *themself*; more than one, *themselves* — and if your editor objects, *themselves* is the safe fallback.** Against [himself](#himself) / *himself or herself*: *themself* is now the ordinary American solution to the epicene problem, and *himself* used generically reads as dated. ✗*Themself* for a known plural is simply wrong.

The singular reflexive of singular *they*. It fills a gap English had left open for centuries — a reflexive for one unnamed person — and it has moved from disputed to standard within a single generation, faster than almost any other change in the pronoun system.

**1. Reflexive with singular *they* referring to a known individual.** *Robin let themself in.*
**2. Reflexive with a singular indefinite antecedent.** *Nobody should have to defend themself twice.*
**3. Intensive with a singular gender-neutral antecedent, deletable.** *The candidate themself confirmed it.*

1. Robin poured *themself* a glass and sat on the porch. *(1)*
2. My cousin taught *themself* to solder from videos. *(1)*
3. They don't think of *themself* as an activist. *(1)*
4. Whoever left this note should identify *themself*. *(2)*
5. Anyone can register *themself* at the front desk. *(2)*
6. Someone locked *themself* out of the server room again. *(2)*
7. The witness *themself* asked to be recalled. *(3)*
8. No one who signs up should have to pay for the kit *themself*. *(3)*

### themselves

**Pronunciation:** /ðəmˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *thuhm-SELVZ* &middot; weak /əmˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *uhm-SELVZ* (*they hurt 'emselves*); a careful citation form is /ðɛmˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *them-SELVZ*
**Русский:** себя, сами — возвратное 3 л. мн. ч.; также об одном человеке при singular they
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person plural; **also used as a singular with singular *they* and with singular indefinites**
**Refers to:** two or more people, animals, or things, licensed by a plural third-person antecedent in the same clause — and also one person, when the antecedent is singular *they* or an indefinite like *everyone*, *someone*, *each applicant*. That singular use is standard and is the majority form in edited American prose, though [themself](#themself) is gaining.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. The intensive keeps the verb of its noun, so the number of the noun decides: *the engineers themselves **are** unsure*, *the committee itself **is** unsure*. With a singular indefinite antecedent the verb stays singular while the reflexive is plural in form: *everyone **is** expected to feed **themselves***.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *themselves* &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *themselves* &middot; [plural NP] *themselves* [verb] &middot; *by themselves* &middot; *among themselves*
**Contrast:** **The critical contrast is with [each other](#each-other).** *They blamed **themselves*** distributes the blame inward — each person blamed that same person; *they blamed **each other*** sends it crosswise. *The senators contradicted themselves* means each was inconsistent; *the senators contradicted each other* means they disagreed. Against [them](01-personal.md#them): reflexive when subject and object are the same group (*they helped **themselves***), plain *them* otherwise (*she helped **them***) and after prepositions of place (*they had the mountains behind **them***). Against [theirselves](#theirselves), which is nonstandard.

The plural reflexive, and the workhorse of the whole class — it covers people, animals, objects, institutions, and a single unnamed person all at once. **1. Reflexive, plural: the group acts on its members individually.** *They hurt themselves.*
**2. Reflexive, singular: with singular *they* or a singular indefinite.** *Everyone helped themselves.*
**3. Intensive: emphasis on a plural noun phrase, deletable.** *The doctors themselves disagreed.*

### yourself

**Pronunciation:** /jɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *yer-SELF* &middot; weak /jəˈsɛlf/ &middot; *yuh-SELF*; a careful citation form is /jʊrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *yoor-SELF*
**Русский:** себя, сам — возвратное 2 л. ед. ч.; также обобщённо «сам себя», о людях вообще
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral; the untriggered use (*could yourself confirm the booking?*) is nonstandard
**Person / number:** second person **singular** — one addressee. For more than one, [yourselves](#yourselves).
**Refers to:** the person being addressed, licensed by an overt *you* or by the understood *you* of an imperative (*help yourself*, *behave yourself*). Also used for the **generic** *you* meaning "anyone" — *you have to pace **yourself*** — which in American English does the work that [oneself](#oneself) does in formal prose.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. Second-person *you* takes a plural verb regardless of number, so both *you yourself **are** responsible* (one person) and *you yourselves **are** responsible* (several) use *are*.
**Pattern:** *you* [verb] *yourself* &middot; *you* [verb] [preposition] *yourself* &middot; *you yourself* [verb] &middot; [imperative] *yourself* &middot; *by yourself* &middot; *and yourself?*
**Contrast:** *Yourself* against [yourselves](#yourselves) is the last surviving number distinction in the English second person: *you* is both, *yourself* is one, *yourselves* is more than one. Against [you](01-personal.md#you): reflexive when the addressee acts on the addressee (*you'll hurt **yourself***), plain *you* otherwise (*I'll call **you***) and after prepositions of place (*keep the folder next to **you***). **The untriggered *yourself*** — ✗*Could yourself sign here?*, ✗*The package will be sent to yourself* — is the second-person twin of the *myself* error and just as wrong; the repair is *you*. The one genuine exception is the elliptical conversational reply *and yourself?* (*"How've you been?" "Good — and yourself?"*), which is established American idiom in speech. It does not extend to writing: ✗*The package will be sent to yourself* is wrong in a letter no matter how polite it feels.

The second person singular reflexive, and the one that lives inside more fixed expressions than any other pronoun in English: *help yourself*, *behave yourself*, *make yourself at home*, *enjoy yourself*, *suit yourself*, *be yourself*, *watch yourself*, *see for yourself*, *speak for yourself*, *pull yourself together*, *let yourself go*, *keep it to yourself*.

**2. Reflexive with an imperative's understood *you*.** *Help yourself.*
**3. Intensive: emphasis on a *you* already present, deletable.** *You said so yourself.*
**4. Generic, referring to people in general.** *You can't rush yourself through this.*
**5. Fixed expressions.** *by yourself*, *to yourself*, *and yourself?*, *not yourself*.

### yourselves

**Pronunciation:** /jɚˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *yer-SELVZ* &middot; weak /jəˈsɛlvz/; careful /jʊrˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *yoor-SELVZ*
**Русский:** себя, сами — возвратное 2 л. мн. ч.: показывает множественность, которую «you» уже не различает
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** second person **plural** — two or more addressees
**Refers to:** the group being addressed, licensed by a *you* (plural) or by the understood *you* of an imperative directed at several people. The regional and colloquial plural *you* forms license it too, and all of them take *yourselves*, never *yourself*: *y'all can seat **yourselves*** ([y'all](01-personal.md#yall), Southern), *you guys can seat **yourselves*** ([you guys](01-personal.md#you-guys), general and informal), and — nonstandard — [youse](01-personal.md#youse) (New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, Chicago), *yinz* (Pittsburgh), [you'uns](01-personal.md#youuns) (Appalachian).
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own, and *you* takes a plural verb either way: *you yourselves **are** responsible*.
**Pattern:** *you* [verb] *yourselves* &middot; *you* [verb] [preposition] *yourselves* &middot; *you yourselves* [verb] &middot; [imperative] *yourselves* &middot; *by yourselves* &middot; *among yourselves*
**Contrast:** *Yourselves* against [yourself](#yourself): number, and nothing else. Because *you* is ambiguous, the reflexive is often the only word in the sentence telling you how many people are being addressed — *did you make this yourself?* is one cook, *did you make this yourselves?* is a kitchen. Against [each other](#each-other): *introduce **yourselves*** means each person gives their own name to the room; *introduce **yourselves to each other*** means the introductions go crosswise. *Y'all's selves* (Southern vernacular) and *yourselfs* (a regularized plural, general nonstandard) are both nonstandard; the plural of *yourself* is *yourselves*, and it stays *yourselves* whatever plural *you* form the speaker uses.

The second person plural reflexive. Its main practical value is disambiguation — in a language whose *you* lost its number marking four hundred years ago, *yourselves* is one of the few places the plural still shows.

**1. Reflexive, plural addressees.** *You'll tire yourselves out.*
**2. Reflexive with a plural imperative.** *Seat yourselves anywhere.*
**3. Intensive: emphasis on a plural *you*, deletable.** *You built this yourselves.*
**4. Fixed expressions.** *by yourselves*, *among yourselves*, *make yourselves at home*.

1. You two are going to exhaust *yourselves* before the hike even starts. *(1)*
2. Grab a plate and help *yourselves*. *(2)*
3. Please seat *yourselves* wherever there's room. *(2)*
4. You *yourselves* voted for this schedule. *(3)*
5. Did you frame the whole addition *yourselves*? *(3)*
6. Make *yourselves* at home; we'll eat around seven. *(4)*
7. You'll have to settle this among *yourselves*. *(4)*
8. Were you kids out there by *yourselves* all afternoon? *(4)*

## Reciprocal pronouns

Two forms and their possessives. A reciprocal takes a plural or coordinated antecedent and sends the action **across** the group rather than back into each member, which is the whole difference between *they helped each other* and *they helped themselves*. Neither form can be a subject — both are **object**-case forms — both require an antecedent in the same clause, and the traditional two-versus-more-than-two rule that is supposed to separate them has never matched what good writers do and is rejected by modern American usage guides.

### each other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈitʃ ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *EECH UTH-er*; in connected speech the two words run together as /iˈtʃʌðɚ/ &middot; *ee-CHUTH-er*, and the final *-er* is the reduced /ɚ/
**Русский:** друг друга — взаимное местоимение: действие идёт крест-накрест по группе, а не на себя
**Type:** reciprocal pronoun (two words, always) &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral; the more common of the two reciprocals in American English by a wide margin
**Person / number:** invariant in form; requires a **plural or coordinated antecedent** — *they*, *we*, *you*, *the twins*, *Tom and Sue*. It has no singular and no plural of its own: ✗*each others*.
**Refers to:** the members of a plural antecedent, taken crosswise — every member is both actor and target. Works for people, animals, and things (*the two gears mesh with **each other***). The antecedent must be in the same clause: *they said the twins argue with **each other*** can only mean the twins argue.
**Agreement:** it is never a subject, so it triggers no agreement — ✗*Each other were late*. When *each other's* + noun does head a phrase, the verb agrees with that noun, not with *each other*: *each other's **schedules conflict***. Its plural antecedent keeps its own plural verb: *the sisters **call** each other daily*.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *each other* &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *each other* &middot; *each other's* [noun] &middot; ✗*each other* [verb]
**Contrast:** **Against [themselves](#themselves) — the contrast that changes the facts of a sentence.** *The candidates attacked **themselves*** means each was self-critical; *the candidates attacked **each other*** means it was a debate. Against [one another](#one-another): the school rule that *each other* is for exactly two and *one another* for three or more was invented in the eighteenth century, has never matched usage, and is not enforced by the Chicago Manual, AP, or Merriam-Webster — **treat them as interchangeable**, with *each other* the plainer and far more frequent choice. The possessive is ***each other's***, apostrophe before the *s*: ✗*each others*, ✗*each others'*. Using *each other* as a subject (*they know what each other wants*) is common in speech and still edited out of formal prose.

Distributes an action or relation across the members of a plural group so that each member is on both ends of it. The reciprocal is the reason English does not need to say *Tom looked at Sue and Sue looked at Tom* — *Tom and Sue looked at **each other*** carries both directions at once.

1. The two of them have known *each other* since kindergarten.
2. The panelists interrupted *each other* for a solid hour.
3. My brother and I text *each other* about once a week.
4. They don't speak to *each other* anymore.
5. The magnets snapped toward *each other* the moment I let go.
6. Do the twins still dress like *each other*?
7. Four of the five witnesses contradicted *each other*.
8. We promised not to lie to *each other* about money.

**See also:** [each other's](#each-others), [one another](#one-another), [themselves](#themselves), [each](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#each), [other](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#other)

### each other's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈitʃ ˈʌðɚz/ &middot; *EECH UTH-erz*; connected /iˈtʃʌðɚz/
**Русский:** друг друга (чей) — притяжательная форма взаимного местоимения, всегда перед существительным
**Type:** reciprocal pronoun, possessive form &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant; requires a plural or coordinated antecedent, exactly as [each other](#each-other) does
**Refers to:** the members of a plural antecedent, crosswise, marking what each one possesses relative to the others. It is always followed by a noun — it cannot stand alone the way *theirs* or *hers* can: ✗*that jacket is each other's*.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **noun that follows**, never with *each other's*: *each other's **names were** called*; *each other's **hair was** braided*. In practice the phrase almost always sits in object position or inside a prepositional phrase, because the reciprocal needs a plural antecedent earlier in the clause to lean on, and a subject has nothing earlier to lean on.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *each other's* [noun] &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *each other's* [noun]
**Contrast:** **The apostrophe goes on *other*, before the *s*, and the *s* never moves.** Write *each other's*, never ✗*each others* and never ✗*each others'* — *other* is singular here no matter how many people are involved. The noun after it may be singular or plural and both are standard: *they finished each other's **sentences*** and *they held each other's **hand*** are equally acceptable — the singular is the natural choice when each person has only one of the thing, the plural when each has several, and neither is an error. Against [their](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#their): *they wrecked **their** cars* leaves open whose car each wrecked; *they wrecked **each other's** cars* says the damage went crosswise. Against [one another's](#one-anothers), which is the same thing in a more formal register.

Marks reciprocal possession — what each member of a group has, seen from the others' side. It is one of only two possessives in English built from a two-word pronoun — [one another's](#one-anothers) is the other — which is why the apostrophe placement trips up even careful writers.

1. The twins finish *each other's* sentences.
2. They've been reading *each other's* email for years, apparently.
3. We stayed at *each other's* houses every weekend that summer.
4. The two firms poached *each other's* engineers all spring.
5. The horses stood nose to tail, swishing flies off *each other's* backs.
6. They memorized *each other's* coffee orders within a week.
7. Neighbors here still watch *each other's* kids.
8. The candidates spent the debate quoting *each other's* worst lines.

**See also:** [each other](#each-other), [one another's](#one-anothers), [theirs](02-possessive.md#theirs), [each other's (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#each-others)

### one another

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn əˈnʌðɚ/ &middot; *wun uh-NUTH-er* — the middle syllable is the reduced /ə/ and the stress falls on *NUTH*; connected speech gives /wʌ nəˈnʌðɚ/
**Русский:** друг друга — то же, что each other, но книжн., в возвышенном или официальном регистре
**Type:** reciprocal pronoun (two words, always) &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral to slightly formal; more common in writing, sermons, ceremony, and legal or diplomatic prose than in conversation
**Person / number:** invariant in form; requires a plural or coordinated antecedent
**Refers to:** the members of a plural antecedent, taken crosswise, exactly as [each other](#each-other) does. Used for people far more often than for things — *the gears mesh with each other* is ordinary, *the gears mesh with one another* sounds like a manual.
**Agreement:** never a subject, so it triggers no agreement of its own. The plural antecedent keeps its plural verb: *the members **support** one another*.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *one another* &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *one another* &middot; *one another's* [noun]
**Contrast:** **Against [each other](#each-other): register, not number.** The rule taught in schools — *each other* for two, *one another* for three or more — was proposed by eighteenth-century grammarians, was never followed by good writers, and is rejected today by Merriam-Webster, the Chicago Manual of Style, and most modern American usage guides. What actually separates them is tone: *one another* is the more formal and the rarer, and it dominates in fixed elevated phrases (*love one another*, *bear with one another*, *members of one another*). Use either for any number. The possessive is *one another's*, never ✗*one anothers'*.

The formal reciprocal. It means precisely what *each other* means and differs only in the height of the register, which is why it survives in wedding vows, scripture, bylaws, and treaties while *each other* does the daily work.

1. The volunteers helped *one another* load the truck.
2. The three agencies routinely blame *one another* for the delay.
3. We have promised to care for *one another* in sickness and in health.
4. Members of the cohort are expected to review *one another's* drafts.
5. The delegations spoke past *one another* for most of the afternoon.
6. Neighbors on that street have looked after *one another* for forty years.
7. They rarely see *one another* outside of work.
8. The two proposals contradict *one another* on funding.

**See also:** [each other](#each-other), [one another's](#one-anothers), [themselves](#themselves), [another](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#another), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one)

### one another's

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn əˈnʌðɚz/ &middot; *wun uh-NUTH-erz*
**Русский:** друг друга (чей) — притяжательная форма one another; книжн., всегда перед существительным
**Type:** reciprocal pronoun, possessive form &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** formal; the least frequent of the four reciprocal forms
**Person / number:** invariant; requires a plural or coordinated antecedent
**Refers to:** the members of a plural antecedent, marking crosswise possession. Always followed by a noun; it cannot stand alone.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the following noun, not with *one another's*: *one another's **arguments were** dismantled*. Like [each other's](#each-others), it almost never heads a subject, because the reciprocal needs a plural antecedent earlier in the clause.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *one another's* [noun] &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *one another's* [noun]
**Contrast:** **The apostrophe goes on *another*, before the *s*** — *one another's*, never ✗*one anothers* (no apostrophe) and never ✗*one anothers'* (apostrophe after the *s*); *another* stays singular however many people are involved. Against [each other's](#each-others): interchangeable in meaning, with *one another's* the more formal; in American prose *each other's* outnumbers it several times over. The following noun may be singular or plural: *they respect one another's **work*** and *they read one another's **drafts*** are both standard.

Marks reciprocal possession in a formal register. Everything true of [each other's](#each-others) is true here, one notch up the register scale.

1. The residents look after *one another's* gardens while they travel.
2. Committee members are asked to read *one another's* reports in advance.
3. The two firms have quietly copied *one another's* pricing for years.
4. They learned to respect *one another's* silences.
5. Congregants are encouraged to carry *one another's* burdens.
6. The delegations rejected *one another's* opening terms.
7. The lab groups check *one another's* calculations before publication.
8. Old friends forgive *one another's* worst habits.

**See also:** [one another](#one-another), [each other's](#each-others), [each other](#each-other), [theirs](02-possessive.md#theirs)

## Nonstandard, archaic, and disputed forms

Eight forms a learner will meet and should be able to recognize. Three of them — [hisself](#hisself), [theirself](#theirself), [theirselves](#theirselves) — are vernacular formations that are, ironically, more regular than the standard words they replace: English builds *myself*, *yourself*, and *ourselves* from possessives, so *his* + *self* and *their* + *selves* are exactly what the pattern predicts, and standard *himself* and *themselves*, built from the object forms, are the exceptions. All three are **nonstandard** and do not belong in school, business, or published writing. A fourth, [theyself](#theyself), is nonstandard for a different reason: it is built on the subject form *they*, a base English uses for no other reflexive, and it is a recent and rare formation rather than an inherited dialect one. The other two are different cases and are labeled differently below: [ourself](#ourself) is legitimate in the narrow royal and editorial uses and nonstandard everywhere else, and [thyself](#thyself) is **archaic** rather than wrong — the right word four hundred years ago, a costume today. Two more are phrases rather than single words: [his own self](#his-own-self) and [my own self](#my-own-self), possessive determiner plus the emphasizer *own* plus the noun *self*, the vernacular way of leaning on an intensive, and the source of the solid dialect spelling *hisownself*.

**Three neighbors, labeled.** The vernacular reflexives keep company with three other forms a learner will hear constantly and should be able to place. **Demonstrative *them*** — ✗ *them apples*, ✗ *them boys* — is **nonstandard** for *those*, and is widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech; note the line, though, because *them* after a verb or preposition (*I ate **them***) is the ordinary object pronoun and completely standard. Demonstrative *them* is a determiner, not a pronoun, so its entry is in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them) rather than here. **Plural *youse*** — **nonstandard**, and regional: New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration — is a personal pronoun and lives at [youse](01-personal.md#youse), next to [y'all](01-personal.md#yall) (Southern), [you'uns](01-personal.md#youuns) and *yinz* (Appalachian and Pittsburgh), and [you guys](01-personal.md#you-guys) (general, informal rather than nonstandard); every one of them takes *yourselves*. **The double negative** — *he didn't hurt hisself none*, *they never told nobody* — is **nonstandard in edited American English** and is marked wrong in school and business writing, but it is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernaculars, and it was ordinary English in Chaucer and Shakespeare before eighteenth-century grammarians ruled it out on a false analogy with arithmetic. The standard repair is one negative per clause: *he didn't hurt **himself***, *they never told **anybody***. One archaic neighbor while we are here: English once used plain object pronouns reflexively — *I laid **me** down*, *he sat **him** down* — a construction now confined to poetry, hymns, and set phrases.

### his own self

**Pronunciation:** /hɪz oʊn ˈsɛlf/ &middot; *hiz ohn SELF* &middot; weak /ɪz oʊn ˈsɛf/ &middot; *iz-ohn-SEF* — the /h/ drops inside a clause and the final /l/ often goes with it; written solid, *hisownself* spells the same three syllables run together, /ˌhɪzoʊnˈsɛlf/
**Русский:** сам, самолично — диал./прост. усиленное himself (юг США, Аппалачи, ААВЕ)
**Type:** intensive pronoun phrase — possessive determiner *his* + the emphasizer *own* + the noun *self* &middot; **Case:** reflexive; it fills the intensive appositive slot and the object slot after a preposition &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard and regional** — Southern, South Midland and Appalachian, and African American English, and older rural speech across the country. In print an American meets it almost only in dialogue and literary dialect, where the solid spelling *hisownself* is a standard way of marking a Southern voice. Not used in edited prose.
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine
**Refers to:** a male antecedent in the same clause — the same licensing that governs [himself](#himself), for which this is the emphatic vernacular substitute. The construction is fully productive across the paradigm: *my own self*, *your own self*, *her own self*, *our own selves*, *their own selves*, with *selves* in the plural.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own; the verb belongs to the clause's subject, exactly as with *himself*
**Pattern:** [he / male NP] [verb] [object] *his own self* &middot; [male NP] *his own self* [verb] &middot; *by his own self* &middot; *all by his own self*
**Variants:** the solid spellings *hisownself* and *his ownself*, both used in dialect writing to show the phrase said as one word &middot; *her own self*, *your own self*, *their own selves* for the other persons, and [my own self](#my-own-self), which has its own entry &middot; the Early Modern *mine own self*, the shape that also gives Shakespeare *to thine own self be true*
**Contrast:** Against [himself](#himself): the same slot with the emphasis turned up, and *own* is what turns it up. Against [hisself](#hisself): *hisself* substitutes for *himself* in the plain reflexive slot (*he cut **hisself***), while *his own self* is overwhelmingly **intensive** (*he shingled it **his own self***) — two different vernacular moves, usually made by the same speakers. Standard English gets the same emphasis from a bare intensive (*he did it **himself***) or from *own* in front of a noun (*his **own** truck*; see [own (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#own)). **Recognize it, do not write it: in edited prose the intensive is [himself](#himself).**

The emphatic vernacular intensive. It says what *himself* says and leans on it — the spoken equivalent of underlining the word — and its solid spelling is one of the oldest signals a writer uses to put a Southern voice on the page.

**1. Intensive: emphasis on a male subject, deletable.** *He shingled the roof his own self.*
**2. Appositive beside a masculine noun phrase.** *The preacher his own self came out.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by his own self*, *all by his own self* (alone, unaided).

1. He hauled every one of those bales *his own self*. *(1; standard: **himself**)*
2. He built that smokehouse *hisownself*, back before the war. *(1; solid spelling, Southern dialect writing)*
3. The sheriff *his own self* came out to look at the fence. *(2; standard: **himself**)*
4. She said the governor *his own self* signed the letter. *(2; standard: **himself**)*
5. He wouldn't hear it from anybody but the old man *his own self*. *(2; standard: **himself**)*
6. He was sitting out there all by *his own self* till dark. *(3; standard: **by himself**)*

**See also:** [himself](#himself), [hisself](#hisself), [my own self](#my-own-self), [theirself](#theirself), [theirselves](#theirselves), [own (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#own), [his (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his)

### hisself

**Pronunciation:** /hɪzˈsɛlf/ &middot; *hiz-SELF*, frequently reduced to /ɪzˈsɛlf/ &middot; *iz-SELF* or /ɪzˈsɛf/
**Русский:** себя, сам — прост./диал. вместо himself (юг США, Аппалачи, ААВЕ); на письме считается ошибкой
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard**; Southern, Appalachian, and African American English, and widespread in older rural speech across the country. Marked wrong in any school or workplace writing.
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine
**Refers to:** a male antecedent in the same clause — the same conditions that govern [himself](#himself), which is the standard equivalent
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own, exactly as *himself* does
**Pattern:** [he / male NP] [verb] *hisself* &middot; [male NP] [verb] [object] *hisself* &middot; *by hisself*
**Contrast:** *Hisself* is built the regular way — possessive *his* + *self* — matching *my**self***, *your**self***, *our**selves***. Standard English instead built the third person from the object forms, giving the irregular *himself* and *themselves*, so the nonstandard forms are the ones following the rule. It was ordinary in Middle and Early Modern English and only became a class marker after eighteenth-century grammarians standardized against it. **Recognize it, do not write it: the standard form is always [himself](#himself).**

The vernacular masculine reflexive. It is a reliable dialect marker in American speech and in dialogue written to represent it, and it carries strong social stigma in print, which is precisely why a learner needs to be able to identify it and avoid producing it.

1. He built that whole porch *hisself*, they say. *(vernacular; standard: **himself**)*
2. He was setting out there by *hisself* all evening. *(vernacular; standard: **by himself**)*
3. He'd never forgive *hisself* if that dog got loose. *(vernacular; standard: **himself**)*
4. The old man cut *hisself* on the fence wire. *(vernacular; standard: **himself**)*
5. He talks to *hisself* when he's working. *(vernacular; standard: **himself**)*
6. He fixed the tractor *hisself*, twice. *(vernacular intensive; standard: **himself**)*

**See also:** [himself](#himself), [theirself](#theirself), [theirselves](#theirselves), [him](01-personal.md#him), [his](02-possessive.md#his)

### my own self

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ oʊn ˈsɛlf/ &middot; *my ohn SELF* &middot; weak /mə oʊn ˈsɛlf/ &middot; *muh-ohn-SELF* — *my* reduces to /mə/ exactly as it does in *myself* /məˈsɛlf/, and the dialect spelling *myownself* shows the same run-together pronunciation
**Русский:** сам, самолично — разг./диал. усиленное myself: «я сам(а) это видел(а)»
**Type:** intensive pronoun phrase — possessive determiner *my* + the emphasizer *own* + the noun *self* &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **regional and colloquial** — Southern, Appalachian, and African American English, and informal speech well beyond them. Ordinary in conversation and in written dialogue anywhere in the country, out of place in edited prose, where the intensive is *myself*.
**Person / number:** first person singular
**Refers to:** the speaker, licensed by an *I* in the same clause exactly as [myself](#myself) is. Being emphatic, it lands almost always in the intensive slot rather than the plain reflexive one: *I saw it **my own self*** is ordinary in speech, while *I cut **my own self*** — grammatical enough, but flat where the phrase wants emphasis — is not how speakers actually use it.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own; the verb agrees with the *I* that licenses it — *I **have** been out there my own self*
**Pattern:** *I* [verb] [object] *my own self* &middot; *I my own self* [verb] (rare, heavily emphatic) &middot; *by my own self* &middot; *all by my own self*
**Variants:** the solid spelling *myownself* in dialect writing &middot; the archaic *mine own self*, the Early Modern shape behind *to thine own self be true* &middot; the rest of the paradigm, [his own self](#his-own-self), *her own self*, *your own self*, *our own selves*, *their own selves*
**Contrast:** Against [myself](#myself): the same intensive job with the volume raised, and dialect-marked where *myself* is not — *I built it **myself*** in writing, *I built it **my own self*** in speech. Against the noun phrase *my self* (no *own*): that is not this construction at all but an ordinary noun about identity, the first-person twin of [one's self](#ones-self). And the phrase is **no escape from the untriggered-*myself* error**: ✗*please forward the invoice to my own self* is exactly as wrong as ✗*to myself*, because the clause has no *I* in it — the word is *me*. **Recognize it, do not write it: in edited prose the intensive is [myself](#myself).**

The first-person emphatic. It is the most widely used member of the *own self* family, common in casual speech far outside the South, and the one an American is likeliest to have said out loud without noticing that it is not the written form.

**1. Intensive: emphasis on a first-person subject already in the clause.** *I saw it my own self.*
**2. Fixed expressions.** *by my own self*, *all by my own self* (alone, unaided).
**3. Wrong in the untriggered slot, where the plain object pronoun belongs.** ✗*contact my own self* → *contact me*.

1. I saw the whole thing *my own self*, standing right there. *(1; standard: **myself**)*
2. I wouldn't have believed it if I hadn't heard him say it *my own self*. *(1)*
3. I cooked every bit of that dinner *my own self*. *(1)*
4. I'll drive it up there *my own self* if nobody else will. *(1)*
5. I grew up on that road *my own self*. *(1)*
6. I got the truck out of the ditch all by *my own self*. *(2; standard: **by myself**)*
7. ✗ Please forward the invoice to *my own self*. → ✓ Please forward the invoice to *me*. *(3)*

**See also:** [myself](#myself), [his own self](#his-own-self), [one's self](#ones-self), [hisself](#hisself), [me](01-personal.md#me), [own (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#own), [my (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#my)

### ourself

**Pronunciation:** /ɑrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *ar-SELF*; also /aʊɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *ow-er-SELF*
**Русский:** себя, сами — устар./книжн. при «королевском» или редакционном we; иначе прост. вместо ourselves
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **split** — archaic and formal with the royal or editorial *we*; **nonstandard** as a substitute for *ourselves*
**Person / number:** first person, grammatically plural *we* referring to a **single** entity — a monarch, an editorial board speaking as one voice, or a collective treated as one body
**Refers to:** the single person or single corporate entity denoted by a plural *we*. It is licensed only when *we* means one: *we have asked **ourself** whether the paper went too far* (an editorial board). With an ordinary plural *we* it is an error: ✗*my wife and I let ourself in* → *ourselves*.
**Agreement:** *we* keeps its plural verb even in the royal and editorial senses — *we **are** not amused*, never ✗*we is*
**Pattern:** *we* [verb] *ourself* (with singular reference only) &middot; [imperative or infinitive with collective *we*] *ourself*
**Contrast:** ***Ourselves* is the form in every ordinary use.** *Ourself* is legitimate only where *we* is a plural of majesty or a corporate singular, and even there some usage panels reject it. Note the mirror-image logic with [themself](#themself): both are plural-shaped pronouns given a *-self* singular ending to match singular reference, and *themself* has won broad acceptance while *ourself* has not — because singular *they* is a living construction and the royal *we* is not. Outside those narrow contexts, *ourself* for *ourselves* is nonstandard.

A singular *-self* form attached to a plural *we*. It survives in three places: royal proclamation, editorial and institutional prose, and dialect where it simply replaces *ourselves*. Only the first two are defensible in writing, and both are rare enough that most American readers will read it as a typo.

1. We have asked *ourself* whether the paper served its readers well. *(editorial we)*
2. We shall reserve the decision to *ourself*. *(royal we)*
3. The board must ask *ourself* what obligation we owe the members. *(corporate we; many editors would write **ourselves**)*
4. We find *ourself* in an unfamiliar position. *(editorial we)*
5. We let *ourself* in with the key under the mat. *(nonstandard for **ourselves**)*
6. We had the cabin to *ourself* all weekend. *(nonstandard for **ourselves**)*

**See also:** [ourselves](#ourselves), [themself](#themself), [theirself](#theirself), [we](01-personal.md#we), [us](01-personal.md#us)

### theirself

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thair-SELF*; in fast speech the first syllable reduces to /ðɚ/ &middot; *thur-SELF*
**Русский:** себя, сам(а) — прост./диал. вместо themself, об одном человеке
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard**; Southern, Appalachian, and African American English, and older rural speech generally — the singular partner of [theirselves](#theirselves)
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, gender-neutral, where standard English uses [themself](#themself) or [themselves](#themselves)
**Refers to:** one person, named in the same clause by a singular indefinite (*somebody*, *anybody*, *everybody*, *nobody*, *whoever*) or by singular *they*. The licensing conditions are exactly those of the standard forms; only the base of the word is different.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. The indefinite antecedent keeps its own singular verb — *somebody **was** locked out* — precisely as it does with standard *themself*, and singular *they* keeps its plural verb — *they **were** locked out*.
**Pattern:** [singular indefinite] [verb] *theirself* &middot; *they* [verb] *theirself* &middot; [singular indefinite] [verb] [preposition] *theirself* &middot; *by theirself*
**Contrast:** ***Theirself* and standard [themself](#themself) are two different words solving the same problem from two different bases** — the vernacular builds on the possessive *their*, standard English on the object form *them* — and the vernacular one is the more regular of the two, matching *myself*, *yourself*, *ourselves*. It is still the one that gets marked wrong. Against [theirselves](#theirselves): number and nothing else, one person against a group; vernacular speakers hold that line more consistently than standard English holds the *themself* / *themselves* line. Against [hisself](#hisself): same nonstandard pattern, different person and gender. **Recognize it, do not write it: the standard forms are [themself](#themself) for one person and [themselves](#themselves) for a group — and *themselves* is also standard for one person.**

The vernacular singular reflexive. It deserves its own entry rather than a footnote, because it is the word standard English spent a century hunting for — a *-self* form for one unnamed person — reached from the other direction and stigmatized for its base rather than for its logic.

1. Somebody locked *theirself* out of the truck again. *(vernacular; standard: **themself** or **themselves**)*
2. Anybody could hurt *theirself* on that bottom step. *(vernacular; standard: **themself**)*
3. Nobody around here has to explain *theirself* twice. *(vernacular; standard: **themself**)*
4. He said whoever took it can keep it to *theirself*. *(vernacular; standard: **themself**)*
5. The new hire drove all the way out here by *theirself*. *(vernacular; standard: **by themself**)*
6. They wired that whole shed *theirself*, they told me. *(vernacular intensive; standard: **themself**)*

**See also:** [theirselves](#theirselves), [themself](#themself), [themselves](#themselves), [hisself](#hisself), [they](01-personal.md#they), [them](01-personal.md#them)

### theirselves

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *thair-SELVZ*; the singular variant *theirself* is /ðɛrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thair-SELF*
**Русский:** себя, сами — прост./диал. вместо themselves
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard**; Southern, Appalachian, and African American English, and older rural speech generally
**Person / number:** third person plural; the singular partner is [theirself](#theirself), which has its own entry
**Refers to:** a plural third-person antecedent in the same clause — the same conditions that govern [themselves](#themselves), which is the standard equivalent
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own, exactly as *themselves* does
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *theirselves* &middot; [plural subject] [verb] [preposition] *theirselves* &middot; *by theirselves*
**Contrast:** Like [hisself](#hisself), *theirselves* is the regular formation — possessive *their* + *selves*, matching *ourselves* and *yourselves* — while standard *themselves* is built from the object form *them*. The nonstandard form is the consistent one; the standard form is the exception. **Recognize it, do not write it: the standard forms are [themselves](#themselves) for a plural antecedent and [themself](#themself) or [themselves](#themselves) for a singular one.** Note that standard [themself](#themself) and nonstandard [theirself](#theirself) are different words solving the same problem from different bases.

The vernacular plural reflexive. It is common in American speech and in written dialogue representing it, and stigmatized everywhere else.

1. They painted the whole house *theirselves*. *(vernacular; standard: **themselves**)*
2. The kids got *theirselves* into trouble at the creek. *(vernacular; standard: **themselves**)*
3. They were out there by *theirselves* half the night. *(vernacular; standard: **by themselves**)*
4. Folks around here look after *theirselves*. *(vernacular; standard: **themselves**)*
5. They helped *theirselves* to the last of the pie. *(vernacular; standard: **themselves**)*
6. The girls sewed every one of those costumes *theirselves*. *(vernacular intensive; standard: **themselves**)*

**See also:** [themselves](#themselves), [themself](#themself), [theirself](#theirself), [hisself](#hisself), [them](01-personal.md#them), [theirs](02-possessive.md#theirs)

### theyself

**Pronunciation:** /ðeɪˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thay-SELF* &middot; weak /ðəˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thuh-SELF* — reduced, it stands one nasal away from the everyday fast-speech *themself* /ðəmˈsɛlf/, close enough that the two blur in running speech and are separated far more reliably on the page than in the ear
**Русский:** себя, сам(а) — неологизм, нестанд. вариант themself; в словари не входит
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard and rare** — met chiefly in informal online writing and in speech since the 2010s. No major American dictionary enters it, and editors treat it as an error; the standard form it reaches for is [themself](#themself).
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, gender-neutral — a reflexive of singular *they*, built on the subject form
**Refers to:** one person whose gender is unknown, unstated, irrelevant, or nonbinary, licensed by a singular *they* or by a singular indefinite (*someone*, *anyone*, *whoever*, *each applicant*) in the same clause — the same conditions that govern [themself](#themself). A plural antecedent takes [themselves](#themselves); ✗*theyselves* is not a form anyone uses.
**Agreement:** carries no verb agreement of its own. Singular *they* keeps its plural verb — *they **were** locked out*, never ✗*they was* — and a singular indefinite antecedent keeps its own singular verb: *somebody **is** going to hurt theyself*.
**Pattern:** *they* [verb] *theyself* &middot; [singular indefinite] [verb] *theyself* &middot; [singular NP] *theyself* [verb] &middot; *by theyself*
**Contrast:** **Three bases, one job.** Standard [themself](#themself) is built on the object form *them*, vernacular [theirself](#theirself) on the possessive *their*, and *theyself* on the subject form *they* — and the subject form is the one base English uses for no reflexive at all: there is no ✗*heself*, ✗*sheself*, ✗*weself*, ✗*Iself*. That is why *theyself* reads as an error even to readers who accept *themself* without blinking, and it is the reason this form has stayed rare while *themself* went standard inside a single generation. It is also a recent formation rather than an inherited one — unlike *hisself* and *theirselves*, which are centuries old and carry a regional history, *theyself* is what a writer produces by attaching *-self* to the pronoun actually spoken. **Recognize it, do not write it: the standard forms are [themself](#themself) for one person and [themselves](#themselves) for a group — and *themselves* is standard for one person too.**

The subject-base reflexive of singular *they*, and the newest and rarest of the three *they*-family *-self* forms. It has no dictionary, no style guide, and no dialect tradition behind it, which sets it apart from the vernacular forms above: those are stigmatized for their base, this one is simply not established.

1. Somebody locked *theyself* out of the studio again. *(nonstandard; standard: **themself** or **themselves**)*
2. Anyone can register *theyself* at the front desk. *(nonstandard; standard: **themself**)*
3. They don't think of *theyself* as a beginner anymore. *(nonstandard; standard: **themself**)*
4. Whoever wrote this note should identify *theyself*. *(nonstandard; standard: **themself**)*
5. My roommate taught *theyself* to knit over the winter. *(nonstandard; standard: **themself**)*
6. They drove all the way to Tucson by *theyself*. *(nonstandard; standard: **by themself**)*
7. They put up that whole fence *theyself*. *(nonstandard intensive; standard: **themself**)*

**See also:** [themself](#themself), [themselves](#themselves), [theirself](#theirself), [theirselves](#theirselves), [hisself](#hisself), [they](01-personal.md#they), [them](01-personal.md#them)

### thyself

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thy-SELF* — the *th* is voiced /ð/, as in *this*, not the voiceless *th* of *thin*
**Русский:** себя, сам — устар. возвратное к thou; ныне лишь в высоком стиле: «познай самого себя»
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **archaic**; found now only in scripture, hymns, Shakespeare, oaths, mottoes, and deliberate period style
**Person / number:** second person singular, familiar — the reflexive of *thou* / *thee* / *thy*
**Refers to:** a single addressee, in the intimate or familiar register that *thou* marked before English collapsed the second person into *you*. The modern equivalent is [yourself](#yourself).
**Agreement:** takes no verb agreement of its own; its antecedent *thou* took the old *-est* ending on the verb (*thou knowest thyself*)
**Pattern:** *thou* [verb-est] *thyself* &middot; [archaic imperative] *thyself* (*know thyself*) &middot; *to thine own self* (the *thine* + noun variant)
**Contrast:** *Thyself* against [yourself](#yourself): the same slot, four hundred years apart. Against the Shakespearean *thine own self* (*to thine own self be true*), which is not the pronoun at all but the possessive *thine* plus the noun *self* — an important distinction, because ✗*thineself* as one word is a modern invention with no historical basis. *Thyself* goes with *thou*; pairing it with *you* (✗*you should know thyself*) is a common mistake in mock-archaic writing.

The archaic second person singular reflexive. It is worth knowing because it appears in the most quoted sentences in English — *know thyself*, *love thy neighbor as thyself*, *physician, heal thyself* — and because those quotations are still used in ordinary modern American speech.

1. Know *thyself*. *(inscribed at Delphi, and quoted ever since)*
2. Thou shalt love thy neighbor as *thyself*.
3. Physician, heal *thyself*.
4. Thou knowest *thyself* better than any counselor could.
5. Trouble not *thyself* over what cannot be mended.
6. Gird *thyself*, for the road is long.

## Neopronoun reflexives

Eleven *-self* forms belonging to ten pronoun sets outside the inherited paradigm — ten object-base or single-base forms plus [eirself](#eirself), the possessive-base alternative inside the Spivak set. **Neopronouns** are third-person singular sets — *ze/hir*, *ze/zir*, *xe/xem/xyr*, *e/em/eir* (the Spivak set), *ae/aer*, *ne/nem/nir*, *per/pers*, *co/cos*, *ve/ver/vis*, *fae/faer* — used by a small number of English speakers, most of them nonbinary people, to refer to themselves; among speakers who use a gender-neutral pronoun at all, singular *they* is by a wide margin the most common choice. Each set builds its reflexive the way the rest of English does, by adding *-self* to the object or the possessive base — *hir* + *self*, *xem* + *self* — and each takes the singular *-self*, never *-selves*, because each names one person. Some sets are cut down from ordinary English words rather than coined outright — *per* from *person*, *co* from the prefix meaning *together*, *fae* from the noun for a fairy — and the noun-derived ones are often grouped as **nounself** pronouns.

**The one systematic difference from singular *they* is the verb.** A neopronoun subject takes a **singular** verb — *ze **is** here*, *xe **was** early*, *per **has** arrived* — while singular *they* keeps the plural verb however many people it names (*they **are** here*). Three practical points beyond that. The sets are **not interchangeable**: someone who uses *xe/xem* is not covered by *ze/hir*, and reaching for the wrong set is the same order of mistake as using the wrong name. General American dictionaries enter few of these forms, and the major style guides do not rule them out — AP and Chicago both tell writers to use the pronouns a person uses and to explain an unfamiliar form once for readers who will not recognize it. And spellings and pronunciations vary between speakers: the ones given below are the most frequently cited, and a person's own pronunciation of their own pronoun settles the question.

### aerself

**Pronunciation:** /ɛrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *air-SELF* &middot; also /eɪrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *ayr-SELF* for speakers who say *aer* like *ayr*; weak /ɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *er-SELF*, which is exactly the reduced *herself*, so in fast speech the two can be indistinguishable and the spelling carries the difference
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратная форма неоместоимения ae/aer
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *ae* / *aer* / *aers* set, one of the sets that spread through pronoun-listing sites and online profiles from the early 2010s on. Used by a small number of speakers; American dictionaries do not enter it, and a reader met with it in print will usually have seen it introduced first.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *ae* set, named in the same clause. *Aer* serves as both the object form and the possessive determiner (*I called **aer***, ***aer** brother*), so the reflexive has a single base and no competing spelling of the kind [xemself](#xemself) and [nemself](#nemself) show.
**Agreement:** *ae* takes a **singular** verb — *ae **is** on the schedule*, *ae **has** already answered*, *ae **was** the first one here*. The reflexive carries no agreement of its own and is always *-self*, never *-selves*.
**Pattern:** *ae* [verb] *aerself* &middot; *ae* [verb] [preposition] *aerself* &middot; [NP] *aerself* [verb] &middot; *by aerself*
**Contrast:** Against the Spivak variant [eirself](#eirself): the two are homophones for most speakers, /ɛrˈsɛlf/ both, and only the spelling and the subject pronoun — *ae* against *e* or *ey* — tell them apart. Against [herself](#herself): the same collision one step further along, since unstressed *herself* is /ɚˈsɛlf/ and *aerself* reduces to the same thing. Against [emself](#emself), [coself](#coself), [faerself](#faerself), [hirself](#hirself), [nemself](#nemself), [perself](#perself), [verself](#verself), [xemself](#xemself), and [zirself](#zirself): separate sets, not substitutes for one another. Against [themself](#themself): singular both, but *ae* takes a singular verb where singular *they* takes a plural one.

The reflexive of the *ae* set. It is the neopronoun likeliest to slip past an American ear rather than an American eye, because every pronunciation it has lands on or beside a word the language already uses.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Ae taught aerself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Ae rewired the kiln aerself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by aerself*, *to aerself*.

1. Ae taught *aerself* to throw pots out of a library book. *(1)*
2. Ae poured *aerself* the last of the coffee and kept reading. *(1)*
3. Ae doesn't count *aerself* among the regulars yet. *(1)*
4. Ae rewired the whole kiln shed *aerself*. *(2)*
5. The treasurer *aerself* asked for the audit. *(2)*
6. Ae drove out to the coast by *aerself* in February. *(3)*
7. Ae kept the news to *aerself* until the offer was signed. *(3)*

**See also:** [coself](#coself), [eirself](#eirself), [emself](#emself), [faerself](#faerself), [nemself](#nemself), [hirself](#hirself), [themself](#themself), [ae / aer](01-personal.md#ae--aer)

### coself

**Pronunciation:** /koʊˈsɛlf/ &middot; *koh-SELF* &middot; weak /kəˈsɛlf/ &middot; *kuh-SELF* — the /oʊ/ reduces to schwa in fast speech, the way the first syllable of *cooperate* does; the stress is on *-self*, as on every reflexive in the language
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратная форма набора co/cos (М. Орован, 1970)
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *co* / *co* / *cos* set, coined by Mary Orovan in her 1970 pamphlet *Humanizing English* and taken up by American intentional communities — Twin Oaks, founded in 1967 in Louisa County, Virginia, uses *co* in its bylaws and its everyday speech, and neighboring communities have followed. Rare outside that world and outside writing about it.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *co* set, named in the same clause. *Co* is the same word in the subject and the object slot (*co called*, *I called co*), the way *you* is, so the possessive *cos* and the reflexive *coself* are the only separate forms in the set — the shape [perself](#perself) has too.
**Agreement:** *co* takes a **singular** verb — *co **is** on kitchen shift*, *co **has** signed the sheet*, *co **was** at the meeting*. The reflexive is *-self*, never *-selves*.
**Pattern:** *co* [verb] *coself* &middot; *co* [verb] [preposition] *coself* &middot; [NP] *coself* [verb] &middot; *by coself*
**Contrast:** Against the prefix *co-* (*co-worker*, *co-signer*) and the abbreviation *Co.* — a spelling collision that, like [perself](#perself)'s collision with the preposition *per*, makes the set hard to search for and easy to misread at the head of a sentence. Against [perself](#perself) structurally: both sets are cut down from ordinary English material — *co-* from the prefix meaning *together*, *per* from *person* — and both use one form for subject and object, which is why the reflexive and the possessive are the only places either set inflects. Against [aerself](#aerself), [emself](#emself), [faerself](#faerself), [hirself](#hirself), [nemself](#nemself), [verself](#verself), [xemself](#xemself), and [zirself](#zirself): separate sets, not substitutes. Against [themself](#themself): singular both, but *co* takes a singular verb where singular *they* takes a plural one.

The reflexive of the *co* set, the oldest of these coinages — it predates the *z-* and *x-* forms by two decades — and the only one with a documented institutional home, in the bylaws and daily speech of a Virginia commune.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Co taught coself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Co framed the loft coself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by coself*, *to coself*.

1. Co taught *coself* to keep bees the first summer here. *(1)*
2. Co signed *coself* up for two extra kitchen shifts. *(1)*
3. Co doesn't think of *coself* as anybody's supervisor. *(1)*
4. Co framed the whole sleeping loft *coself*. *(2)*
5. The membership clerk *coself* read the bylaw aloud. *(2)*
6. Co walked back from the garden by *coself* in the dark. *(3)*
7. Co kept the decision to *coself* until the meeting. *(3)*

**See also:** [aerself](#aerself), [eirself](#eirself), [emself](#emself), [faerself](#faerself), [nemself](#nemself), [perself](#perself), [themself](#themself), [co](01-personal.md#co)

### eirself

**Pronunciation:** /ɛrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *air-SELF* &middot; weak /ɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *er-SELF* — the possessive *eir* is /ɛr/, *their* with the front clipped off, so the word opens on the sound of the ordinary noun *air*; reduced, it falls together with unstressed *herself* and with [aerself](#aerself) from the *ae* set, and the spelling carries a difference the ear does not
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратное набора Спивака на базе eir (ср. emself)
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun; the possessive-base form of the Spivak set, beside the object-base [emself](#emself) &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the Spivak set *e* (or *ey*) / *em* / *eir* / *eirs*, named for the mathematician Michael Spivak, whose typesetting writing used the forms, and spread through LambdaMOO and other text-based online communities in the 1990s. It is not part of the inherited English paradigm and is not used by most American speakers; among speakers who use a gender-neutral pronoun at all, singular *they* is by a wide margin the commoner choice. No general American dictionary enters it, and the major style guides neither endorse nor rule it out — AP and Chicago both tell a writer to use the forms a person uses and to gloss an unfamiliar one once for readers who will not recognize it. In practice the set is introduced by the person who uses it, in a profile line, an email signature, or an introduction round, and that is what settles which form a writer should use.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the Spivak set, named in the same clause by *e* / *ey* or by a noun phrase. The set inflects in full: subject ***e*** or ***ey***, object ***em***, possessive determiner ***eir***, possessive pronoun ***eirs***, reflexive ***emself*** or ***eirself***. *Eirself* is the member built on the possessive, the way *myself* and *yourself* are; [emself](#emself) is built on the object, the way *himself* and *themself* are. Licensing is ordinary reflexive licensing — the antecedent must sit in the same clause, exactly as *himself* needs its *he*.
**Agreement:** the subject takes a **singular** verb — *ey **is** on the list*, *e **has** already voted*, *ey **was** hired in March* — where singular *they* keeps the plural (*they **are** here*). The reflexive carries no agreement of its own, and it is always *-self*, never ✗*eirselves*, because it names one person.
**Pattern:** *ey / e* [verb] *eirself* &middot; *ey* [verb] [preposition] *eirself* &middot; [NP] *eirself* [verb] &middot; *by eirself* &middot; *to eirself*
**Variants:** [emself](#emself), the object-base form, which has its own entry below and is the more frequently listed of the two; speakers differ on which they take, and a person's own choice settles it. The split is structurally the same one standard [themself](#themself) (object base *them*) makes against vernacular [theirself](#theirself) (possessive base *their*), and the same one [xemself](#xemself) makes against *xyrself* and [nemself](#nemself) against *nirself* — with the difference that neither Spivak form carries stigma.
**Contrast:** Against [emself](#emself): the same set and the same person, two bases and two pronunciations — /ɛrˈsɛlf/ against /ɛmˈsɛlf/ — and neither is a correction of the other. Against [aerself](#aerself): a **homophone from a different set**, /ɛrˈsɛlf/ both, so only the spelling and the subject pronoun — *e* / *ey* against *ae* — separate them on the page, and nothing separates them in speech. Against [herself](#herself): unstressed *herself* is /ɚˈsɛlf/, which is exactly where reduced *eirself* lands. Against [themself](#themself): both name one person and neither marks gender, but the verb splits them — *ey **is** here*, *they **are** here*. Against [xemself](#xemself), [zirself](#zirself), [hirself](#hirself), [coself](#coself), [faerself](#faerself), [nemself](#nemself), [perself](#perself), and [verself](#verself): separate sets, not substitutes for one another, and reaching for the wrong set is the same order of mistake as using the wrong name.

The possessive-base reflexive of the Spivak pronouns. It is the form in this section an American reader is likeliest to take for a typo and an American listener cannot hear at all — *eir* is *their* with the front clipped off, and reduced *eirself* is indistinguishable from reduced *herself*.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Ey taught eirself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Ey wired the booth eirself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by eirself*, *to eirself*.

1. Ey taught *eirself* to read a wiring diagram over one winter. *(1)*
2. Ey gave *eirself* the weekend off after the launch. *(1)*
3. Ey doesn't count *eirself* among the founders, whatever the filing says. *(1)*
4. Ey introduced *eirself* to the room and wrote the pronouns on the whiteboard. *(1)*
5. E wired the entire booth *eirself* the night before the show opened. *(2)*
6. The archivist *eirself* pulled the box out of storage for us. *(2)*
7. Ey rode out to the trailhead by *eirself* and camped two nights. *(3)*
8. Ey kept the offer to *eirself* until the paperwork cleared. *(3)*

### emself

**Pronunciation:** /ɛmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *em-SELF* &middot; weak /əmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *uhm-SELF* — the same reduced syllable *themself* has in fast speech (*somebody hurt 'emself*), so in conversation the two can be indistinguishable
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратное набора Спивака e/ey — em — eir
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the Spivak set *e* (or *ey*) / *em* / *eir* / *eirs*, named for the mathematician Michael Spivak, whose typesetting writing used the forms, and spread through LambdaMOO and other text-based online communities in the 1990s. Used today by a small number of speakers, and met in print mainly in writing by or about them.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the Spivak set, named by *e* / *ey* or by a noun phrase in the same clause. The licensing is ordinary reflexive licensing — no different from *himself* needing its *he*.
**Agreement:** the subject takes a **singular** verb — *ey **is** on the list*, *e **has** already voted* — unlike singular *they*, which keeps the plural. The reflexive carries no agreement of its own and is always *-self*, never *-selves*.
**Pattern:** *ey / e* [verb] *emself* &middot; *ey* [verb] [preposition] *emself* &middot; [NP] *emself* [verb] &middot; *by emself*
**Variants:** [eirself](#eirself) /ɛrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *air-SELF*, built on the possessive *eir* rather than the object *em* and entered separately above; both are attested and speakers differ on which they use, and *eirself* is a homophone of [aerself](#aerself) from the *ae* set. The split has the same shape as standard *themself* (object base *them*) against vernacular *theirself* (possessive base *their*), with the difference that neither Spivak form carries stigma.
**Contrast:** Against [themself](#themself): both name one person and neither marks gender, but the verb tells them apart — *ey **is** here* against *they **are** here* — and in reduced speech the two reflexives converge on /əmˈsɛlf/. Against [xemself](#xemself), [zirself](#zirself), [hirself](#hirself), [perself](#perself), and [verself](#verself): separate sets, not substitutes for one another. In print the Spivak forms are the ones most often taken for typos, because *em* and *eir* look like *them* and *their* with the front clipped off — which is exactly how they were built.

The reflexive of the Spivak pronouns, the oldest of the sets still in ordinary use, and the one an American reader is likeliest to have passed over without noticing, since it hides inside the everyday reduced pronunciation of *themself*.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Ey taught emself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Ey filed the appeal emself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by emself*, *to emself*.

1. Ey taught *emself* enough Python to automate the whole report. *(1)*
2. Ey poured *emself* a coffee and took the seat by the window. *(1)*
3. Ey doesn't think of *emself* as the founder, whatever the paperwork says. *(1)*
4. E wired the studio *emself* over one long weekend. *(2)*
5. The chair *emself* asked that the item be tabled. *(2)*
6. Ey drove out to the site by *emself*. *(3)*
7. Ey kept the diagnosis to *emself* until the spring. *(3)*
8. Ey gave *eirself* the weekend off after the launch. *(1; the possessive-base variant)*

### faerself

**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратное набора fae/faer (от fae «фея»)
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *fae* / *faer* / *faers* set, built on the noun *fae* (a fairy) and circulated on Tumblr and other social platforms from the mid-2010s on. It is the most frequently cited of the **nounself** pronouns — sets drawn from an existing noun rather than from an invented syllable — and is used by a small number of speakers.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *fae* set, named in the same clause. *Faer* is both the object form and the possessive determiner (*I met **faer***, ***faer** roommate*), so there is one base and one spelling of the reflexive.
**Agreement:** *fae* takes a **singular** verb — *fae **is** presenting second*, *fae **has** the keys*, *fae **was** hired in June*. The reflexive is *-self*, never *-selves*.

Against [emself](#emself), [coself](#coself), [hirself](#hirself), [nemself](#nemself), [perself](#perself), [verself](#verself), [xemself](#xemself), and [zirself](#zirself): separate sets, not substitutes for one another. Against [themself](#themself): singular both, and again the verb splits them — *fae **is** here*, *they **are** here*.

The reflexive of the *fae* set, the best known of the nounself pronouns. Its base is an existing English noun rather than a coined syllable, and that is the feature the whole nounself group is named for.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Fae taught faerself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Fae hung the show faerself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by faerself*, *to faerself*.

1. Fae taught *faerself* to screen-print in a friend's garage. *(1)*

### hirself

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *heer-SELF* &middot; also /hɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *her-SELF* for speakers who say *hir* like *her*, which makes it a homophone of *herself*; weak /ɪrˈsɛlf/ — the /h/ drops inside a clause exactly as it does in *himself* and *herself*
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратное набора ze/hir
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *ze* (also spelled *zie* or *sie*) / *hir* / *hirs* set, which circulated in queer, academic, and Usenet writing from the early 1990s. Still in use, though less than *ze/zir* and far less than singular *they*.
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *ze/hir* set, named in the same clause. *Hir* serves as both the object form and the possessive determiner (*I called **hir***, ***hir** car*), so the reflexive has only one possible base and there is no competing spelling of the kind [xemself](#xemself) shows.
**Agreement:** *ze* takes a **singular** verb — *ze **is** waiting*, *ze **was** the first to sign*, *ze **has** two kids*. The reflexive is *-self*, never *-selves*.
**Pattern:** *ze* [verb] *hirself* &middot; *ze* [verb] [preposition] *hirself* &middot; [NP] *hirself* [verb] &middot; *by hirself*
**Contrast:** Against [herself](#herself): for speakers who pronounce *hir* as /hɚ/ the two words are homophones and only the spelling separates them, which is one reason the *z-* spellings have gained ground. Against [zirself](#zirself): the same subject pronoun *ze* heads both sets, and what differs is the object and possessive form (*hir* against *zir*), so the reflexive is often the clearest signal of which set a writer is using. Against [themself](#themself): singular either way, but *ze* takes a singular verb and singular *they* a plural one.

The reflexive of the *ze/hir* set, the oldest of the *z-* pronouns and the one most often met in writing from the 1990s and 2000s.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Ze taught hirself.*
**2. Intensive: emphasis on the antecedent, deletable.** *Ze wired the studio hirself.*
**3. Fixed expressions.** *by hirself*, *to hirself*.

1. Ze taught *hirself* to read music at forty. *(1)*
2. Ze poured *hirself* a cup and went back to the reading. *(1)*
3. Ze doesn't describe *hirself* as an activist. *(1)*
4. Ze built every one of those shelves *hirself*. *(2)*
5. The editor *hirself* called to ask about the footnote. *(2)*
6. Ze drove the whole way up to the cabin by *hirself*. *(3)*
7. Ze kept the news to *hirself* for a month. *(3)*

### nemself

**Pronunciation:** /nɛmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *nem-SELF* &middot; weak /nəmˈsɛlf/ &middot; *nuhm-SELF* — one consonant from the reduced *themself* and *emself*, both /əmˈsɛlf/, so in connected speech the three sit very close together

**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *ne* / *nem* / *nir* / *nirs* set, listed on pronoun-reference sites since the early 2010s and used by a small number of speakers. **Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *ne* set, named in the same clause. The licensing is ordinary reflexive licensing — no different from *herself* needing its *she*.
**Agreement:** *ne* takes a **singular** verb — *ne **is** in the lab*, *ne **was** hired in March*, *ne **has** two kids*. The reflexive is *-self*, never *-selves*.
**Pattern:** *ne* [verb] *nemself* &middot; *ne* [verb] [preposition] *nemself* &middot; [NP] *nemself* [verb] &middot; *by nemself*
**Variants:** *nirself* /nɪrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *neer-SELF*, built on the possessive *nir* rather than the object *nem*. **Contrast:** Against [emself](#emself): the two sets build the reflexive the same way, object form plus *-self*, and the results differ by an initial /n/ that fast speech does little to protect. 5. The reviewer *nemself* asked to see the raw data. *(2)*
6. Ne flew out to the conference by *nemself*. *(3)*

### perself

**Pronunciation:** /pɚˈsɛlf/ &middot; *per-SELF* &middot; the first syllable is already the reduced /pɚ/, so there is no separate weak form; the stress falls on *-self*, as on every reflexive in the language
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — возвратное набора per/pers (от person; М. Пирси)
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** neutral among the speakers who use the set, and rare in general American writing — the reflexive of the *per* / *per* / *pers* set, clipped from *person* — coined by Marge Piercy in the 1976 novel *Woman on the Edge of Time* and used since by a small number of speakers, chiefly people who prefer a form built out of an ordinary English word
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral
**Refers to:** one person who uses the *per* set, named in the same clause
**Agreement:** *per* takes a **singular** verb — *per **is** here*, *per **has** signed*. Note that *per* is the same word in the subject and object slots (*per called*, *I called per*), the way *you* is, so the reflexive *perself* and the possessive *pers* are the only separate forms in the set.
**Pattern:** *per* [verb] *perself* &middot; *per* [verb] [preposition] *perself* &middot; [NP] *perself* [verb] &middot; *by perself*

The reflexive of the *per* set, the one neopronoun built by cutting down an existing English noun rather than by inventing a syllable — which is why it reads as plain, and why it turns up in fiction earlier and more often than in style guides.

**1. Reflexive: the object is the subject.** *Per taught perself.*

**3. Fixed expressions.** *by perself*, *to perself*.

1. Per taught *perself* to weld the summer after graduation. *(1)*
