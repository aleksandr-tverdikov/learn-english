# Archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns

> The pronouns no American writes in an email but every American runs into — *thou* and *ye* in scripture and Shakespeare, *thereof* and *same* in a contract, *y'all* and *yinz* and *hisself* in the speech of half the country.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Everything in this file sits outside the standard modern paradigm — behind it in time (*thou*, *aught*, *whosoever*, *thereof*), beside it in region and register (*y'all*, *youse*, *yinz*, *hisself*, *theirselves*), or off to one side of it entirely, deliberately invented to patch a hole the standard paradigm leaves open ([thon](#thon), [e](#e)). One thread runs through the whole group: English used to distinguish a singular *you* from a plural one, lost the distinction around 1600 when the polite plural swallowed the intimate singular, and has been patching the hole ever since. The archaic half of this file is the system before it collapsed; the dialectal half is a dozen independent American repairs to the same gap. The trap at the center is that **thou was the intimate form and ye/you the formal one** — the exact opposite of what nearly every English speaker assumes when they hear *thou* in a prayer.

**A note on labels.** Every entry below is marked, and the marks are not decoration. *Archaic* means no living American produces it except in quotation, pastiche, or ritual. *Regional* means real speakers use it as their ordinary form in a nameable place. *Coined* means somebody made it up on purpose and it never belonged to any older paradigm or any region — it is neither archaic nor an error, and the entries say who coined it and who uses it. *Nonstandard* means it will be marked wrong by an editor, a teacher, or a standardized test, however common it is in speech — and *hisself*, *theirselves*, and *Me and him went* are extremely common in speech. A learner needs to recognize all of them and produce almost none of them. The one exception is *y'all*, which is now so widespread and so useful that it is spreading well beyond the South into ordinary informal American English nationwide.

**A note on what is not an error.** Because so much of this file is marked, it is worth being equally plain about what is not. ***It's me*** is standard American English, in speech and in edited writing alike; *It is I* is not wrong either, but it is stiff enough now to read as a costume, and nobody should be corrected into it. **Singular *they*** is standard American English — recognized by Chicago, the AP stylebook, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster — both for an unknown or unspecified person (*someone left their coat*) and for a person who uses it; nothing in the [theirselves](#theirselves) or [ourself](#ourself) entries below bears on that. ***Whom*** has not vanished, but it has retreated to one position: it survives mainly right after a preposition (*to whom it may concern*, *for whom the bell tolls*, *the tenant with whom we spoke*), and at the head of a clause or a question most careful American writers now use *who*. And the ***that* / *which*** split — *that* for restrictive clauses, *which* for nonrestrictive — is an **American editing convention, not a rule of grammar**: British editors ignore it, four centuries of good writers ignored it, and the part that actually carries meaning is the comma, not the word. See [that](05-relative.md#that) and [which](05-relative.md#which). What antecedents each relative may take is a separate matter and is not a convention at all: ***who*** takes people, ***which*** takes things, ***that*** takes **both**, and ***whose*** takes **both** — *the book whose cover is torn* is correct English and has been for six hundred years, whatever you were told about *of which*. In this file, errors are marked ✗ and conventions are called conventions.

**A note on pronunciation.** Archaic pronouns are recited rather than spoken, so they keep full stressed vowels: *thou* /ðaʊ/, *thee* /ði/, *ye* /ji/ — no weak forms, because nobody uses them at conversational speed. The dialectal forms are the opposite. They are the most frequent words in their speakers' mouths and they reduce hard: *y'all* /jɔl/ flattens to /jl̩/ in *y'all come back*, *you guys* runs to /jəˈɡaɪz/, *them* is /ðəm/ → /əm/ even when it is doing demonstrative work. Where a weak form exists, it is given.

The standard pronouns that these forms compete with reduce just as hard, and five of those weak forms are worth having in your ear before you read any example in this file out loud — they are what the coordinated phrases in the last section actually sound like, and they are why *me and him* and *between you and me* are so easy to mishear:

| Pronoun | Strong | Weak | Heard in |
|---|---|---|---|
| *him* | /hɪm/ | **/ɪm/** | *tell him* /ˈtɛlɪm/ &middot; *me and him* /ˈmi ən ɪm/ |
| *her* | /hɚ/ | **/ɚ/** | *give her* /ˈɡɪvɚ/ &middot; *ask her* /ˈæskɚ/ |
| *them* | /ðɛm/ | **/əm/** | *give me them apples* /ˈɡɪmi əm ˈæpəlz/ |
| *you* | /ju/ | **/jə/** | *you guys* /jəˈɡaɪz/ &middot; *did you* /ˈdɪdʒə/ |
| *he* | /hi/ | **/i/** | *did he* /ˈdɪdi/ &middot; *is he* /ˈɪzi/ |

The *h* drops in every one of the *h*-forms, which is ordinary connected speech and not sloppiness: *hisself* is /ɪzˈsɛlf/ as often as /hɪzˈsɛlf/, and *tell her* is /ˈtɛlɚ/ for everyone.

## The archaic second person: *thou* and *ye*

Middle and Early Modern English had a full two-way second person, borrowed in structure from French *tu/vous*: **thou** for one person you were intimate with, close to, or socially above, and **ye/you** for several people, or for one person you owed deference to. Modern speakers get this backwards because the only place they meet *thou* is in the King James Bible and in hymns, where it addresses God — so it sounds elevated. It is not elevated. Addressing God as *thou* was the point: the KJV translators chose the **intimate** singular deliberately, exactly as a child addresses a parent. Using *thou* to a stranger or a superior was an insult, and Renaissance drama turns on it; Shakespeare's characters switch between *thou* and *you* to mark affection, contempt, and rank, sometimes inside a single speech.

The full paradigm:

| | Subject | Object | Possessive determiner | Possessive pronoun | Reflexive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Second person singular** (intimate, familiar, or to an inferior) | thou | thee | thy (*thy name*) / thine before a vowel or *h* (*thine eyes*) | thine (*the kingdom is thine*) | thyself |
| **Second person plural** (also the polite singular) | ye | you | your | yours | yourselves (one person: yourself) |

And the verb agreement, which is the part learners skip and then get wrong. **Thou** takes a special ending, `-(e)st` or `-t`, on every finite verb:

| Verb | with *thou* | with *ye* |
|---|---|---|
| be | thou **art** (past: thou **wast** / **wert**) | ye **are** (past: ye **were**) |
| have | thou **hast** (past: thou **hadst**) | ye **have** (past: ye **had**) |
| do | thou **dost** / **doest** (past: thou **didst**) | ye **do** (past: ye **did**) |
| know | thou **knowest** | ye **know** |
| shall / will | thou **shalt** / thou **wilt** | ye **shall** / ye **will** |
| can / may | thou **canst** / thou **mayst** | ye **can** / ye **may** |

Nothing else in the sentence changes. *Thou shalt not steal* is an ordinary imperative-flavored future with an ordinary object; only the pronoun and the verb ending are archaic.

**Where an American actually meets these forms:** the King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer, hymns and Christmas carols (*O come, O come, Emmanuel… and ransom captive Israel*), Shakespeare and Marlowe, the Declaration-era and Romantic poets, Quaker meetings and older Quaker families (in a distinctive reduced form — see [thee](#thee)), the Mormon practice of addressing God as *thee/thou* in prayer, Renaissance fairs, tattoo and monument inscriptions, fantasy novels, heavy-metal album titles, and jokey elevated register (*What sayest thou?*).

### thee

**Pronunciation:** /ði/ &middot; *THEE* &middot; no weak form in modern recitation; in Quaker plain speech, unstressed /ði/ or /ðɪ/  
**Русский:** тебя, тебе — устар. объектная форма thou, 2 л. ед. ч.
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** object (but the **subject** in Quaker plain speech) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, and religious; still living inside some Quaker families  
**Person / number:** second person **singular only**. There is no plural *thee* — the plural object form is *you*.  
**Refers to:** one addressee: a person, God, or a personified thing (Donne's *Death, be not proud* addresses Death itself throughout as *thou*). Never two or more people. Never a thing you are talking *about* rather than *to*.  
**Agreement:** as an object it triggers no verb agreement at all. In **Quaker plain speech**, where *thee* took over the subject slot from *thou*, it takes the ordinary third-person singular verb — *thee is*, *thee has*, *thee knows*, *thee was* — and never ✗*thee art* or ✗*thee knowest*. That mismatch is the single most reliable way to tell genuine Quaker usage from Renaissance-fair pastiche.  
**Pattern:** [verb] *thee* &middot; [preposition] *thee* (*unto thee*, *with thee*, *of thee*) &middot; [verb] *thee* [object] (*I give thee my word*) &middot; *get thee* [directional] &middot; (Quaker) *thee* [third-person singular verb]  
**Contrast:** *thou* and *thee* divide exactly as *I/me* and *he/him* do — *thou* is the subject, *thee* the object: *Thou lovest me* / *I love thee*. The test here is **substitution**, not deletion: drop *he* or *him* into the slot and see which one fits — *he loves me* → *thou lovest me*; *I love him* → *I love thee*. (The **deletion test** is the tool for coordinated phrases — *between you and me*, *Bob and I went* — and it is set out under [Other nonstandard forms](#other-nonstandard-forms).) Against *ye/you*, the difference is not case but number and politeness: *thou/thee* to one intimate or inferior, *ye/you* to several people or to one superior. And *thee* is not a fancier *you* — it is a **smaller**, warmer, more familiar one.

The object form of the archaic singular *you*: the person spoken to, when that person is the object of a verb or a preposition.

1. "How do I love *thee*? Let me count the ways." (Elizabeth Barrett Browning)
2. "Get *thee* to a nunnery." (*Hamlet*)
3. "The Lord bless *thee*, and keep *thee*." (Numbers 6:24, King James Version)
4. I shall not tell *thee* twice, knave.
5. Fare *thee* well — we sail at dawn.
6. Friend, has *thee* signed the minute yet? (Quaker plain speech, with *thee* as subject and a third-person verb)

### thine

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪn/ &middot; *THYNE* (rhymes with *mine*)  
**Русский:** твоё, твой — устар. самостоятельное притяжательное местоимение (ср. mine)
**Type:** possessive pronoun, archaic — this entry covers the standalone **pronoun** only; the determiner *thine* that stands before a vowel (*thine eyes*) is a separate entry in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thine) &middot; **Case:** possessive, independent &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, religious  
**Person / number:** second person singular only, invariant; it does not change for the number of the thing possessed (*the kingdom is thine*, *these are thine*).  
**Refers to:** what belongs to the single person addressed. The possessor is always that one addressee; the thing possessed may be anything at all, count or mass, singular or plural, human or not.  
**Agreement:** as a subject complement it takes no agreement of its own; as a subject, the verb agrees with what *thine* stands for — *thine is the kingdom* (singular), *thine are the greater sins* (plural).  
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *is thine* &middot; *thine is* [noun phrase] &middot; *of thine* (*a friend of thine*) &middot; *what is thine* &middot; [verb] *thine* (*take thine and go*) &middot; [preposition] *thine* (*with thine*)  
**Contrast:** *thy* and *thine* split the way *my* and *mine* do — with one extra wrinkle. *Thy* is the determiner before a consonant (*thy name*), *thine* the independent pronoun (*it is thine*), **and** *thine* is also the determiner before a vowel or *h*, exactly as older English wrote *mine eyes* and *mine host*. So *thine* covers two of the three jobs, and which one it is doing depends on whether a noun follows it. A noun follows → determiner, and that is a determiner entry, not a pronoun one: *thine eyes*, *thine heart*, and *To thine own self be true* are all covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thine). No noun follows → the pronoun entry you are reading, and every example below is that. Modern *yours* replaces the pronoun, and never takes an apostrophe — ✗*your's* is as wrong as ✗*thine's*.

Stands alone for "the thing or things belonging to thee" — the archaic equivalent of *yours*.

1. "For *thine* is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory." (the Lord's Prayer)
2. A friend of *thine* waits at the gate. (after a preposition, the double genitive)
3. My sword is *thine*, my lord.
4. Take what is *thine* and go.
5. Is this purse *thine* or the innkeeper's?
6. What is mine is *thine*.
7. The fault was *thine*, and thou knowest it.

**See also:** [thou](#thou), [thee](#thee), [thyself](#thyself), [yourn](#yourn), [thine (possessive)](02-possessive.md#thine), [yours](02-possessive.md#yours), [thy](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#thy)

### thou

**Pronunciation:** /ðaʊ/ &middot; *THOW* (rhymes with *now*, not with *toe*)  
**Русский:** ты — устар. 2 л. ед. ч., подлежащее; форма близости или к низшему, а не почтения
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, religious  
**Person / number:** second person **singular only** — one addressee, never more. Marked for intimacy or for the addressee's lower rank, not for reverence.  
**Refers to:** the single person, deity, or personified thing being spoken to. It cannot refer to a group, and it cannot refer to anyone not present in the discourse as an addressee.  
**Agreement:** takes the special second-person singular verb ending `-(e)st` or `-t`: *thou art*, *thou hast*, *thou dost*, *thou wilt*, *thou shalt*, *thou canst*, *thou knowest*, *thou goest*, *thou lovest*; past *thou wast/wert*, *thou hadst*, *thou didst*. Pairing *thou* with a modern verb (✗*thou know*, ✗*thou are*) is the commonest mistake in imitation-archaic English.  
**Pattern:** *thou* [verb+*est*] &middot; *thou art* [complement] &middot; [verb] *thou* (inversion in questions: *knowest thou?*, *art thou?*) &middot; *thou* [modal+*t*] [verb] (*thou shalt not steal*)  
**Contrast:** *thou* is the subject, [thee](#thee) the object — *thou seest me*, *I see thee*. Against *ye/you*, *thou* is the **intimate singular** and *ye/you* the **plural or polite singular**, which is the reverse of what the King James Bible leads Americans to assume. Because the KJV addresses God as *thou*, the form now sounds solemn; historically, addressing a stranger as *thou* was rude enough to be actionable, and Shakespeare's characters use the switch as a weapon. Modern *you* descends from the polite plural, which is why the language keeps growing new plurals ([y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse), [yinz](#yinz)) to fill the gap *thou* left.

The subject form of the archaic singular *you*: the one person being addressed, as the subject of a verb.

1. "*Thou* shalt not steal." (Exodus 20:15, King James Version)
2. "*Thou* art the man." (2 Samuel 12:7)
3. "*Thou* art more lovely and more temperate." (Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 — *thou* to a beloved, the intimate use)
4. What sayest *thou* to that, sir?
5. Where art *thou* going with that lantern?
6. *Thou* knowest well enough what I meant.

**See also:** [thee](#thee), [thine](#thine), [thyself](#thyself), [ye](#ye), [y'all](#yall), [thou (personal)](01-personal.md#thou), [you](01-personal.md#you), [the personal pronoun paradigm](../README.md#the-full-personal-pronoun-paradigm)

### thyself

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thy-SELF* (stress on the second syllable)  
**Русский:** себя, сам — устар. возвратное и усилительное к thou: know thyself «познай себя»
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, religious; survives in two frozen sayings  
**Person / number:** second person singular only. The plural is *yourselves*; the archaic singular has no plural of its own.  
**Refers to:** the single addressee, and only when that addressee is already the subject of the same clause. Like every reflexive, it needs an antecedent inside its own clause — *know thyself* works because the understood subject of *know* is *thou*.  
**Agreement:** none of its own. The clause's verb agrees with its subject, which for an imperative is the understood *thou* (*heal thyself*), and for a stated subject takes the `-est` ending (*thou deceivest thyself*).  
**Pattern:** [verb] *thyself* (reflexive object) &middot; [preposition] *thyself* (*to thyself*, *of thyself*) &middot; *thou* [verb] *thyself* (intensive: *thou thyself hast said it*) &middot; imperative + *thyself* (*know thyself*, *help thyself*)  
**Contrast:** *thyself* is to *thou/thee* what *yourself* is to *you*. It is built on the possessive *thy* + *self*, following the first- and second-person pattern (*myself*, *thyself*, *ourselves*, *yourselves*), while the third person builds on the object form (*himself*, *themselves*) — which is exactly the inconsistency that produces nonstandard [hisself](#hisself) and [theirselves](#theirselves) by analogy. Do not use *thyself* for more than one person; that is *yourselves*.

The archaic singular reflexive: the addressee, when the addressee is both the subject and the object of the same clause. Also intensive, adding emphasis to *thou*.

1. "Physician, heal *thyself*." (Luke 4:23, King James Version)
2. Know *thyself*. (the Delphic maxim, in its standard English form)
3. Thou hast said it *thyself*, and in front of witnesses.
4. Trouble not *thyself* over so small a matter.
5. Help *thyself* to the bread; there is plenty.
6. Thou wilt only injure *thyself* by arguing further.

**See also:** [thou](#thou), [thee](#thee), [thine](#thine), [ourself](#ourself), [hisself](#hisself), [yourself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#yourself), [yourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#yourselves), [reflexive formation](../README.md#reflexive-formation)

### ye

**Pronunciation:** /ji/ &middot; *YEE* &middot; unstressed /jɪ/. **Not** the *ye* of *Ye Olde Shoppe* — see the contrast below, where the spelling is a printer's accident and the pronunciation is /ðə/, "the."  
**Русский:** вы — устар. подлежащная форма 2 л. мн. ч.; также вежливое «вы» к одному
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** subject (the matching object form is *you*) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary, religious; also mock-archaic and jocular  
**Person / number:** second person **plural** — and, in polite address, second person singular to one superior. Historically the *vous* of English.  
**Refers to:** two or more addressees taken together (*O ye of little faith*, addressed to a crowd), or one addressee treated with deference. It is always addressed, never third person.  
**Agreement:** plural verb throughout: *ye are*, *ye have*, *ye do*, *ye were*, *ye know* — never the `-est` ending, which belongs to *thou* alone. ✗*Ye art* and ✗*ye knowest* are the standard giveaway of fake archaism.  
**Pattern:** *ye* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *ye* (imperative with the pronoun expressed: *hear ye*, *gather ye*) &middot; *O ye* [noun phrase] (vocative: *O ye of little faith*) &middot; *ye* [modal] (*ye shall know*)  
**Contrast:** *ye* is the subject and *you* the object of the same plural pronoun — *ye see them* / *they see you* — though by Shakespeare's day the two were already being swapped freely, and the KJV itself is inconsistent. Against *thou/thee*, the split is number and politeness, not case. Now the trap: the *ye* of **Ye Olde Curiosity Shop** is not this word at all. Early printers had no letter for the Old English thorn `þ` (the "th" sound) and substituted the visually similar `y`, so `þe` came out as `ye` — it was always the word *the*, and the sign should be read *the old curiosity shop*. Pronouncing it /ji/ is a modern misreading so entrenched that it has become the joke.

The subject form of the archaic plural *you*: the group being addressed.

1. "Gather *ye* rosebuds while *ye* may." (Robert Herrick)
2. "O *ye* of little faith." (Matthew 8:26, King James Version)
3. "Judge not, that *ye* be not judged." (Matthew 7:1)
4. Hear *ye*, hear *ye* — this court is now in session.
5. Come, all *ye* faithful, and bring the rest with you.
6. *Ye* have heard the charges; how say *ye*?

**See also:** [thou](#thou), [thee](#thee), [y'all](#yall), [you all](#you-all), [youse](#youse), [yinz](#yinz), [ye (personal)](01-personal.md#ye), [you](01-personal.md#you)

## Archaic indefinites: *aught*, *naught*, and *somewhat*

Three old indefinite pronouns — "anything," "nothing," and "something." All three have been driven out of ordinary use by their compound replacements (*anything*, *nothing*, *something*), and all three survive only in fixed phrases an American still hears: *for aught I know*, *all for naught*, *somewhat of a*. Two of them, *aught* and *naught*, also lead second lives as names for the digit zero, which is why the decade from 2000 to 2009 is *the aughts*. Four quantifying leftovers sit with them: ***divers***, the older word for *several*; ***many another*** and ***many a one***, which both say "a great many" while keeping a singular verb; and ***twain***, the old form of *two* that survives in *never the twain shall meet*. Every one of them is a pronoun only when nothing follows it — put a noun after any of them and you have a determiner, which is a different dictionary.

### aught

**Pronunciation:** /ɔt/ &middot; *AWT* &middot; /ɑt/ for the majority of Americans, whose *cot* and *caught* have merged. Homophone of *ought* — which is a different word entirely.  
**Русский:** что-либо, что-нибудь — устар.; живёт в обороте for aught I know
**Type:** indefinite pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form in every position &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives only in the fixed phrase *for aught I know*  
**Person / number:** third person, singular, invariant. No plural, no possessive, no gender.  
**Refers to:** anything at all — a thing, an event, a fact, or a whole state of affairs. Non-human and inanimate by default; for people, older English used *any* or *any man*. Like modern *anything*, it lives in negatives, questions, and conditionals rather than in plain statements: *if aught should happen*, not ✗*aught happened*.  
**Agreement:** singular verb: *if aught remains*, *should aught go wrong*, *aught that is worth saving is already gone*.  
**Pattern:** *for aught* [subject] [verb] (*for aught I know*, *for aught I care*) &middot; *if aught* [verb] &middot; [verb] *aught* (*know aught of it*) &middot; *aught of* [noun] (*aught of value*) &middot; *aught but* [noun] (= *anything but*)  
**Contrast:** *aught* is "anything," *naught* is "nothing" — one letter and a complete reversal of polarity, which is why the two are worth learning as a pair. Do not confuse *aught* with its homophone *ought* (the modal of obligation: *you ought to go*); they sound identical and are unrelated. And keep the pronoun apart from the **numeral** *aught* meaning zero — *nineteen aught six* for 1906, *a thirty-aught-six rifle*, *the aughts* for 2000–2009 — which arose when *a naught* was misheard as *an aught*. That numeral is a noun, not a pronoun, and it is the only *aught* most Americans under forty have ever used.

Anything at all — the archaic ancestor of *anything*, now confined to a handful of set phrases.

**1. Anything whatever (pronoun, archaic).** In negatives, questions, and conditionals: *if aught should happen*, *he would not say aught against her*. This is the pronoun proper, and it is dead outside quotation and the phrase *for aught I know*.

**2. Zero (numeral noun, not a pronoun).** From *a naught* misheard as *an aught*: *nineteen aught six* for 1906, *a thirty-aught-six*, *the aughts* for the decade 2000–2009. Listed here only so the two are not confused; a numeral is not a pronoun.

1. For *aught* I know, she left the country years ago.
2. For *aught* I care, they can settle it in court.
3. If *aught* should befall me, the letter explains everything.
4. He would not say *aught* against his brother.
5. Is there *aught* here worth carrying home?
6. She never took *aught* from anyone without paying for it.

**See also:** [naught](#naught), [nought](#nought), [somewhat](#somewhat), [whatsoever](#whatsoever)

### divers

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdaɪvɚz/ &middot; *DY-verz* &middot; stress on the **first** syllable, which is the whole difference between this word and the modern adjective *diverse* /dɪˈvɚs/ &middot; *dih-VURSS*  
**Русский:** некоторые, несколько — устар. и юр.; неопределённое множество; не путать с diverse
**Type:** indefinite quantifying pronoun, archaic and legal — this entry covers the standalone **pronoun** only; *divers* standing in front of a noun (*divers reasons*, *divers persons*) is a determiner and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#divers) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form in every position &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; still faintly productive in old-fashioned legal drafting and in deliberate period flavor  
**Person / number:** third person **plural**, invariant. No singular, no possessive, no gender.  
**Refers to:** an unspecified handful of the members of a group already named or understood — people or things alike (*divers of the witnesses*, *divers of these instruments*). Its vagueness is the point: it says "more than two or three, and I am not going to count them."  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb throughout: *divers were present*, *divers of them have complained*, *divers have since died*. ✗*Divers was called* is an error, not a period touch.  
**Pattern:** *divers of* [noun phrase] (*divers of them*, *divers of the company*) &middot; *divers* [plural verb] (*divers were called*) &middot; *and divers others* &middot; *divers and sundry* (the frozen legal doublet, where the second word carries the same meaning)  
**Contrast:** the living word is **several**, and that is what a modern writer should put on the page: *several of the witnesses said the same thing*. Two confusions are worth heading off. *Divers* is **not** a spelling of *diverse*: they are the same Latin word split into two English ones, and they now mean different things — *divers* is "several, sundry," a matter of **number**, while *diverse* is "varied, of many kinds," a matter of **variety**, and they are not even stressed alike. And *divers* here is not the plural of *diver*; the identical spelling of the swimmers is an accident that has helped kill the word. In front of a noun it is a determiner, not a pronoun ([divers reasons](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#divers)), and the frozen doublet *divers and sundry* is the last place most Americans meet it.

Several; sundry ones; an unspecified few of a larger number. The archaic plural indefinite that *several* replaced.

1. "And *divers* of them came from far." (Mark 8:3, King James Version)
2. *Divers* of the witnesses gave the same account, though none of them had spoken to the others.
3. The estate was charged with *divers* of the debts recited in the schedule annexed hereto. (legal boilerplate; *divers debts*, with the noun following, would be the determiner)
4. He had tried *divers* of the remedies advertised in the back of the paper, and not one had helped.
5. *Divers* were called before the committee that spring, and *divers* declined to answer.
6. Standard: **Several** of the witnesses gave the same account.

**See also:** [many another](#many-another), [aught](#aught), [several](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#several), [some](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#some), [divers (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#divers), [sundry](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#sundry)

### many a one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛni ə ˈwʌn/ &middot; *men-ee-uh-WUN* &middot; the main stress falls on *one*; *many* reduces to /mɛni/ and the article to /ə/, so at speed the whole phrase runs together as /mɛniəˈwʌn/ and the *a* disappears entirely  
**Русский:** многие, не один — книжн., устар.; глагол при нём в ед. ч.
**Type:** indefinite pronoun phrase, archaic and literary — this entry covers the standalone **pronoun** only; *many a* in front of a singular noun (*many a man*, *many a summer*) is a determiner phrase and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a) &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; ballads, hymns, sermons, and prose written for a formal cadence  
**Person / number:** third person **singular** in form and in agreement, plural in sense — the same split that makes *many a man was killed* singular.  
**Refers to:** a great many individual **people**, counted one at a time rather than gathered into a group; things are possible (*many a one of these old clocks still runs*) but people are the ordinary case. It never refers to a clause or a stretch of text.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb, always: *many a one **has** tried*, *many a one **was** lost*, ✗ *many a one have tried*. The singular is not a quirk but the point of the construction — it lets a writer say "a great many" one at a time, which is why the phrase belongs to elegy and sermon.  
**Pattern:** *many a one* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *many a one* (object: *she buried many a one*) &middot; *many a one of* [plural noun phrase] (*many a one of them*) &middot; *there is many a one* [relative clause] (the existential, with the singular *is*) &middot; *many a one and many another*  
**Contrast:** its nearest neighbor is [many another](#many-another), and the two are not interchangeable: *many another* means "one more of the same kind" and needs the kind already named (*the recipe calls for saffron; many another would do*), while *many a one* is free-standing and simply means "a great many people." Both keep the singular verb that [many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a) forces. Modern American English says **a lot of people**, **plenty of them**, or **many**; reaching for *many a one* in ordinary prose reads as pulpit costume. And keep the pronoun apart from the determiner: put a noun after *many a* and the dictionary line has been crossed.

A great many people, taken one at a time. The archaic indefinite that keeps a singular verb while meaning a crowd.

1. *Many a one* has gone up that mountain and never come down again.
2. *Many a one* of them would have given a year's wages for the chance.
3. She had buried *many a one* she loved before that winter was out. (object of the verb)
4. There is *many a one* in this town who remembers your father. (the existential, with the singular *is*)
5. *Many a one* was lost that September whose name is on no stone.
6. Modern English: *A lot of people* have gone up that mountain and never come down.

**See also:** [many another](#many-another), [such a one](#such-a-one), [a body](#a-body), [divers](#divers), [twain](#twain), [many](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#many), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one), [many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a)

### many another

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛni əˈnʌðɚ/ &middot; *men-ee-uh-NUTH-er* &middot; the stress sits on *-noth-*; *many* reduces to /mɛni/ and never carries the main beat  
**Русский:** многие другие, не один другой — книжн., устар.; глагол в ед. ч.
**Type:** indefinite pronoun, literary and archaic — this entry covers the standalone **pronoun** only; *many another* in front of a singular noun (*many another summer*, *many another man*) is a determiner phrase and belongs with [many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a) in the determiner dictionary &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** literary and old-fashioned; alive in edited prose only where the writer wants a formal cadence  
**Person / number:** third person **singular** in form and in agreement, plural in sense — the same split that makes *many a man was killed* singular.  
**Refers to:** one further unnamed member of a large class, offered as a stand-in for all the rest of them. People or things; whatever noun has just been named is what it stands for (*the recipe calls for saffron; many another would do*).  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb, always: *many another has tried*, *many another was lost*, ✗*many another have tried*. This is the same rule as [many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a), and it is the whole reason the construction survives: it lets a writer say "a great many" while keeping the singular's weight.  
**Pattern:** *many another* [singular verb] &middot; *as many another has* [past participle] &middot; *and many another* [after a named example] &middot; *many another* standing for a noun just mentioned  
**Contrast:** the ordinary modern equivalent is **many others**, and it is plural: *many others have tried*. *Many another* keeps the singular verb and the older rhythm, and it is one of the last living members of the *many a* family, alongside [full many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#full-many-a) and [many's the](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#manys-the). Do not mix the two patterns: ✗*many another have* takes the plural verb of *many others* and glues it to the singular phrase. And note the split that decides which dictionary you want — a noun after it (*many another winter*) makes it a **determiner**, while nothing after it (*many another has said so*) makes it the pronoun described here.

One more of many; many others, counted one at a time. The singular-shaped literary pronoun that means a plural thing.

1. He gave up in the second year, as *many another* has before him.
2. The recipe calls for saffron, but *many another* would do at a tenth the price.
3. She lived through the flood of '27, and *many another* after it.
4. *Many another* would have quit on the spot, and would have been right to.
5. The store closed the way *many another* has closed — quietly, over a holiday weekend.
6. Standard and far commoner: *Many **others** have tried*; or, with the noun expressed, *many another **man** has tried*.

**See also:** [divers](#divers), [a body](#a-body), [another](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#another), [others](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#others), [many](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#many), [many a](../../09-determiners/catalog/04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a)

### naught

**Pronunciation:** /nɔt/ &middot; *NAWT* &middot; /nɑt/ in cot–caught merged accents, making it a homophone of *not* and *knot*  
**Русский:** ничто, ноль — устар.; all for naught «всё впустую», come to naught «сойти на нет»
**Type:** indefinite pronoun, archaic (also a noun meaning zero) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in *all for naught*, *come to naught*, *set at naught*  
**Person / number:** third person, singular, invariant.  
**Refers to:** nothing at all — no thing, no amount, no result. Non-human. It can stand for an outcome or a whole effort (*the whole campaign came to naught*), which is its commonest modern job.  
**Agreement:** singular verb: *naught remains*, *naught was said*, *naught but trouble comes of it*.  
**Pattern:** *all for naught* &middot; *come to naught* / *bring to naught* &middot; *set at naught* (= treat as worthless) &middot; *naught but* [noun] (= *nothing but*) &middot; *naught* [verb] (*naught remains of the house*)  
**Contrast:** *naught* is "nothing," [aught](#aught) is "anything." *Naught* also names the digit zero — *noughts and crosses* is the British name for tic-tac-toe, and *naught* survives in American speech in *a thirty-aught-six* and *nineteen aught nine*, where the same zero has drifted into the *aught* spelling. The two spellings *naught* and [nought](#nought) are the same word: American English keeps *naught* for the pronoun and leaves *nought* to the British for the digit. Do not confuse it with the adjective *naughty*, which descends from it (originally "having nothing," then "worthless," then "badly behaved").

Nothing at all — the archaic ancestor of *nothing*, surviving mainly in the phrase *for naught*.

1. Three years of work, and it was all for *naught*.
2. Their whole scheme came to *naught* within a month.
3. *Naught* remained of the barn but the foundation.
4. He gained *naught* but a reputation for stubbornness.
5. The court set his objections at *naught*.
6. She said *naught* to anyone about the letter.

**See also:** [aught](#aught), [nought](#nought), [somewhat](#somewhat)

### nought

**Pronunciation:** /nɔt/ &middot; *NAWT* &middot; identical to [naught](#naught) — the two spellings are one word  
**Русский:** ничто, ноль — устар.; вариант написания naught, в брит. языке — цифра «ноль»
**Type:** indefinite pronoun, archaic (chiefly a noun meaning zero in British usage) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary in American English; standard British spelling for the digit zero  
**Person / number:** third person, singular, invariant.  
**Refers to:** nothing; no amount; zero. Non-human, inanimate.  
**Agreement:** singular verb: *nought remains*, *nought was gained*.  
**Pattern:** *all for nought* &middot; *come to nought* &middot; *nought but* [noun] &middot; *noughts and crosses* (British, the game)  
**Contrast:** *nought* and *naught* are the same word in two spellings, and the split is national rather than semantic: American editors write **naught** for the pronoun (*all for naught*) and reserve *nought* for quotation, poetry, and British contexts. A conservative American copy editor will change *nought* to *naught* on sight. Neither should be confused with *not*, with which both are homophones for most Americans, nor with [aught](#aught), which means the opposite.

Nothing; zero. A spelling variant of *naught*, and the one an American is most likely to meet in British writing or in older mathematics.

1. All their careful planning went for *nought*.
2. *Nought* but the wind answered him.
3. The experiment yielded *nought* of any value.
4. Write *nought* point five, not one half. (British arithmetic teaching)
5. He reduced my objections to *nought* in a single sentence.
6. The children were playing *noughts* and crosses on the back of the menu. (British; American *tic-tac-toe*)

**See also:** [naught](#naught), [aught](#aught)

### somewhat

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmˌwʌt/ &middot; *SUM-wut* &middot; also /ˈsʌmˌhwʌt/ for speakers who keep the *wh*-  
**Русский:** нечто, кое-что — устар. как местоимение; современное somewhat — наречие «отчасти»
**Type:** indefinite pronoun, archaic (the modern word is an **adverb** of degree, which this entry does not cover) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic as a pronoun; the surviving *somewhat of a* is informal-to-neutral American English and mildly contested  
**Person / number:** third person, singular, invariant.  
**Refers to:** some thing, some amount, or some quality — non-human and typically abstract (*somewhat to say*, *somewhat of the truth*). Never a person.  
**Agreement:** singular verb: *there is somewhat in what he says*.  
**Pattern:** *have somewhat to* [verb] (*I have somewhat to say*) &middot; *somewhat of* [noun phrase] (*somewhat of the truth*) &middot; *there is somewhat in* [noun phrase] &middot; the modern survival, *somewhat of a* [noun] (*somewhat of a legend*)  
**Contrast:** the pronoun *somewhat* has been fully replaced by *something*: *I have something to tell you*, not ✗*I have somewhat to tell you*. What survives in American English is the quantifying idiom *somewhat of a*, which competes with *something of a* — *He's somewhat of an expert* vs *He's something of an expert*. Both are current in the United States; *something of a* is the older and safer choice, and conservative editors prefer it, but *somewhat of a* is common enough in edited American prose that calling it an error overstates the case. Keep all of this apart from the ordinary modern **adverb** *somewhat* (*somewhat tired*, *somewhat better*), which is standard, neutral, and not a pronoun at all.

Something; some amount or degree of a thing. The archaic pronoun, now alive only inside the phrase *somewhat of a*.

1. "Simon, I have *somewhat* to say unto thee." (Luke 7:40, King James Version)
2. There is *somewhat* in his account that does not add up.
3. He has become *somewhat* of a legend in the department.
4. The delay was *somewhat* of a blessing, given the weather.
5. She told him *somewhat* of what had passed, but not all.
6. I found it *somewhat* of a relief when the offer fell through.

**See also:** [aught](#aught), [naught](#naught), [whatsoever](#whatsoever)

### twain

**Pronunciation:** /tweɪn/ &middot; *TWAYN* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *rain*; **no weak form at all** — *twain* is always fully stressed, which is one reason it survived only inside set phrases. The article in *the twain* reduces normally to /ðə/  
**Русский:** двое — устар. форма two; жива в обороте never the twain shall meet
**Type:** fused-head numeral pronoun, archaic — the old form of *two* standing alone &middot; **Variants:** ***the twain***, the definite form, which is how nearly every American meets the word (*never the twain shall meet*); ***they twain***, the biblical form, where *twain* sits in apposition to a pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive for Americans in two quotations, one frozen phrase, and one pen name  
**Person / number:** third person **plural** — two of something, always exactly two. There is no singular and no possessive.  
**Refers to:** the two people or things already named, taken as a pair — East and West in Kipling, husband and wife in Matthew. **People or things**, never a clause. In the adverbial *in twain* it refers instead to the two pieces something is broken into, which is a different job and is noted in the contrast below.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb: *never the twain **shall** meet*, *the twain **were** never reconciled*, ✗ *the twain is*. This is worth stating because the phrase sounds singular to a modern ear — *twain* has no *-s* — and *the twain is* turns up in imitation-archaic writing.  
**Pattern:** *the twain* [plural verb] &middot; *never the twain shall meet* (the frozen Kipling line) &middot; *they twain* (biblical apposition: *they twain shall be one flesh*) &middot; *in twain* (adverbial: *rent in twain*, *cut in twain*) &middot; *a mile or twain* (the old postpositive numeral, in verse)  
**Contrast:** the living word is **two**, and the living pronoun for exactly two is **both** or *the two of them*. Three boundaries. *In twain* is an **adverb**, not a pronoun — it means "into two pieces," and *the veil was rent in twain* has no pronoun in it at all; it is listed here only so the two uses are not confused. *Twain* standing after a noun (*days twain*, *brethren twain*) is the archaic postpositive **numeral** and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#twain). And *Mark Twain* is neither: it is the Mississippi leadsman's call for a sounding of two fathoms, which Samuel Clemens took as a pen name.

The two of them. The archaic pronoun for a pair, kept alive by one line of Kipling and one verse of Matthew.

1. "Oh, East is East, and West is West, and never the *twain* shall meet." (Kipling, *The Ballad of East and West*)
2. "…and they *twain* shall be one flesh." (Matthew 19:5, King James Version)
3. The brothers quarreled in 1868, and the *twain* were never reconciled.
4. He would walk a mile or *twain* before breakfast, whatever the weather. (the old postpositive numeral)
5. "Mark *twain*!" — the leadsman's cry for two fathoms, and the pen name Samuel Clemens took from it.
6. "And the veil of the temple was rent *in twain* from the top to the bottom." (Mark 15:38 — the adverb, not the pronoun)
7. Modern English: *the two of them* never spoke again; the curtain was torn *in two*.

## The *-so* and *-soever* forms

Old English and Middle English built emphatic indefinite relatives by hanging *-so* and then *-soever* onto a *wh-* word: *whoso*, *whomso*, *whatso*, *whosoever*, *whomsoever*, *whatsoever*, *whichsoever*, *whencesoever*, *wheresoever*, *whithersoever*, *whensoever*, *howsoever*. They mean exactly what the modern *-ever* forms mean — *whosoever* is *whoever*, no more and no less — with the extra length doing the work of emphasis. An American meets them in the King James Bible, in hymns, in wills and deeds, and in the deliberately grand register of oaths and proclamations. **The case trap in this section is the one that catches educated writers:** the case of *whosoever/whomsoever* is fixed by the pronoun's job **inside its own clause**, never by the preposition sitting in front of it.

### whatso

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtsoʊ/ &middot; *WUT-so* &middot; also /ˈhwʌtsoʊ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; stress on the first syllable, unlike *whatsoever*, and there is no weak form — the word is only ever recited  
**Русский:** что бы ни, всё что — устар. (среднеангл.) краткая форма whatsoever
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic; Middle English and Early Modern English only, and dead in American English outside quotation and pastiche  
**Person / number:** third person, **singular** in agreement however broad the reference.  
**Refers to:** things, actions, and states of affairs — anything at all that satisfies the clause. Never people; the person word is [whoso](#whoso).  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb inside its own clause: *whatso remains is thine*, *whatso he asks shall be given*. ✗*Whatso remain* is wrong even in imitation.  
**Pattern:** *whatso* [singular verb] (subject of its own clause) &middot; [verb] *whatso* [subject] [verb] (object: *take whatso thou wilt*) &middot; *whatso* [subject] [verb], [main clause]  
**Contrast:** *whatso*, [whatsoever](#whatsoever), and modern *whatever* are one word at three lengths, exactly as [whoso](#whoso), [whosoever](#whosoever), and *whoever* are. *Whatso* is the oldest and shortest, and it is a full step deader than *whatsoever*: even Shakespeare preferred the longer form. Like every fused relative it swallows its own antecedent, so nothing may precede it (✗*the thing whatso he asks*) — that is what "fused" means. If you want the meaning today, write *whatever*; if you want the sound of 1400, this is the word, and the elided verse form is [whatsoe'er](#whoeer-whateer-whosoeer-whatsoeer).

Whatever; anything at all that. The oldest and shortest member of the *what-* family, alive now only in quotation and deliberate pastiche.

1. *Whatso* thou askest of me, that will I give thee. (Middle English pattern, with the *thou* verb endings intact)
2. *Whatso* remains after the debts are paid falls to the younger son.
3. Take *whatso* pleases thee and leave the rest upon the table.
4. *Whatso* is done in haste is commonly done twice.
5. He swore to obey, *whatso* the order might be.
6. Modern English: *Take **whatever** you like* — or, with the emphatic weight, *whatsoever you like*.

**See also:** [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whomso](#whomso), [whoso](#whoso), [whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er](#whoeer-whateer-whosoeer-whatsoeer), [whatever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever), [what (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative)

### whatsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌtsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wut-so-EV-er* &middot; the *-so-* reduces to /sə/ in speech: /ˌwʌtsəˈɛvɚ/  
**Русский:** что бы ни — устар. относительное; в совр. языке усилитель отрицания «вообще никакой»
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic (also a modern emphatic postmodifier) &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic as a pronoun; the negative-emphasis use is ordinary modern English at every level  
**Variants:** *whatsoe'er*, the metrically elided verse form (see [whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er](#whoeer-whateer-whosoeer-whatsoeer)); the shorter and older *whatso* has its own entry  
**Person / number:** third person, singular in agreement even when the reference is broad.  
**Refers to:** things, actions, and states of affairs — never people. For people the corresponding form is [whosoever](#whosoever). It can also take a whole clause as its reference (*whatsoever befalls us*).  
**Agreement:** singular verb inside its own clause: *whatsoever remains is yours*, *whatsoever thy hand findeth to do*.  
**Pattern:** *whatsoever* [verb] (subject of its clause) &middot; [verb] *whatsoever* (object) &middot; *whatsoever* [subject] [verb] (*whatsoever ye do*) &middot; the modern use, [negative noun phrase] + *whatsoever* (*no evidence whatsoever*, *none whatsoever*, *nothing whatsoever*)  
**Contrast:** as a pronoun, *whatsoever* is simply an emphatic *whatever*, and modern English uses *whatever*: *Take whatever you need*, not ✗*Take whatsoever you need*. What lives on is the **postposed emphatic**, which attaches to a negative and intensifies it — *no reason whatsoever*, *none whatsoever*, *nothing whatsoever to do with me*. That use is neither archaic nor formal; it is everyday American English, and it must follow the negative rather than precede it (✗*whatsoever no reason*). *Whatsoever* before a noun (*whatsoever reason*) is the **determiner**, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever); *whichsoever* is the same construction on *which* and is rarer still.

Whatever; anything at all that. The archaic fused relative, and — in negative contexts — a fully modern intensifier.

**1. Fused relative pronoun (archaic).** "Anything at all that," heading its own clause and needing no antecedent: *whatsoever ye do*, *whatsoever remains*. Modern English writes *whatever*.

**2. Emphatic postmodifier after a negative (standard modern English).** Attaches behind a negated noun phrase and intensifies the negation: *no evidence whatsoever*, *nothing whatsoever*, *none whatsoever*. This is ordinary American English at every level of formality, and it is the only living use of the word.

1. "*Whatsoever* thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might." (Ecclesiastes 9:10, King James Version)
2. "*Whatsoever* ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them." (Matthew 7:12)
3. There is no evidence *whatsoever* that the file was ever opened.
4. She had nothing *whatsoever* to do with the decision.
5. I have no objection *whatsoever* to the schedule as written.
6. The tenant shall make no alterations *whatsoever* without written consent.

**See also:** [whosoever](#whosoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whoso](#whoso), [aught](#aught), [whatever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever), [whichsoever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whichsoever), [whatsoever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)

### whencesoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛnsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wen-so-EV-er* &middot; also /ˌhwɛnsoʊˈɛvɚ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; five syllables with the main stress on *ev*, and the doubled *s* of *whence* + *soever* degeminates to one. No weak form — the word is only ever read aloud  
**Русский:** откуда бы ни — устар., юр.; «из какого бы места, источника или причины»
**Type:** fused relative pro-adverb, archaic — *whence* with *-soever* welded on, "from whatever place, source, or cause" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives only in legal-ceremonial drafting and in deliberately grand English  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the **origin** of something, left deliberately open — a place, a source of money, a cause. It refers to a thing or a source, never to a person, and never to a whole clause.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb inside its clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*whencesoever it **comes***, *whencesoever they **came***). In the participial legal formula there is no verb at all (*income, whencesoever derived*).  
**Pattern:** *whencesoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *whencesoever derived* / *whencesoever arising* (the drafting formula, on the model of *from whatever source derived*) &middot; *from whencesoever* (the redundant *from*, exactly as in *from whence*) &middot; [noun] *whencesoever* [clause]  
**Contrast:** it is [whence](#whence) with the emphatic tail added, and it means no more than *whence* does — *-soever* buys emphasis and length, nothing else, exactly as [whosoever](#whosoever) buys nothing over *whoever*. Its mates are [wheresoever, whithersoever, whensoever](#wheresoever-whithersoever-whensoever): *whencesoever* is "from wherever," *whithersoever* "to wherever," *wheresoever* "at wherever." Like *whence* it already contains *from*, so *from whencesoever* is redundant; like *from whence*, it is four hundred years old and nobody serious calls it an error. Modern American English writes **from wherever** or **from whatever source**, and the tax code's *from whatever source derived* is the living descendant of the formula.

From wherever; from whatever source or cause. The emphatic archaic form of *whence*.

1. The trustee shall pay over the income, *whencesoever* derived, to the beneficiary for life.
2. *Whencesoever* the rumor came, it was all over the county by Sunday.
3. He would take good advice *whencesoever* it came, and from whosoever offered it. (*whosoever*, not ✗*whomsoever*: it is the subject of *offered*)
4. The claim, *whencesoever* arising, is barred six years after the breach.
5. *Whencesoever* that money reached him, it did not come out of this office.
6. Modern English: *from wherever* it came; income *from whatever source* derived.

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [wheresoever, whithersoever, whensoever](#wheresoever-whithersoever-whensoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [thence](#thence), [therefrom](#therefrom)

### wheresoever, whithersoever, whensoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrsoʊˈɛvɚ/, /ˌwɪðɚsoʊˈɛvɚ/, /ˌwɛnsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wair-so-EV-er*, *with-er-so-EV-er*, *wen-so-EV-er* &middot; the *-so-* reduces to /sə/ at speed (/ˌwɛrsəˈɛvɚ/), and speakers who keep the *wh-* say /ˌhwɛr-/, /ˌhwɛn-/; the main stress is on *-ev-* in all three  
**Русский:** где бы ни, куда бы ни, когда бы ни — устар., библ. и юр.
**Type:** fused relative **pro-adverbs**, archaic — they stand in for a whole prepositional phrase of place, direction, or time rather than for a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, and legal; *wheresoever* still turns up in insurance policies and jurisdiction clauses  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** *wheresoever* → any place at all **in** which (position); *whithersoever* → any place at all **to** which (direction); *whensoever* → any time at all **at** which. Places and times, never people or things.  
**Agreement:** none of their own; the verb inside the clause agrees with that clause's own subject — *wheresoever the goods **are** stored*, *whensoever the tenant **fails** to pay*.  
**Pattern:** *wheresoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *whithersoever* [subject] [verb] (*whithersoever thou goest*) &middot; *whensoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *and wheresoever situate* (the frozen conveyancing tag, with *situate* as an adjective)  
**Contrast:** these are the *-soever* forms of the old place-and-time trio, and they line up exactly with it: *wheresoever* is emphatic [whence](#whence)'s stay-put cousin *where*, *whithersoever* is emphatic [whither](#whither) ("to where"), *whensoever* is emphatic *when*. The distinction they preserve is the one modern English threw away — *where* for position, *whither* for motion toward — so ✗*whithersoever it is kept* is a misuse for *wheresoever*, and *wheresoever thou goest* loses the motion the King James translators were marking. Modern English writes *wherever*, *wherever … to*, and *whenever*; the only one still earning its keep is *wheresoever* in legal drafting, where *goods wheresoever located* is doing real work by refusing to let a place be argued about.

Wherever; to wherever; whenever. The *-soever* forms of *where*, *whither*, and *when*, emphatic in the same way [whosoever](#whosoever) is emphatic.

1. "Whithersoever thou goest, I will go." (the pattern of Ruth 1:16, in the emphatic form)
2. The policy covers the insured property *wheresoever* located and howsoever stored.
3. *Whensoever* the note falls due, payment shall be made in lawful money.
4. He vowed to follow her *whithersoever* fortune might carry her.
5. *Wheresoever* two or three are gathered, there is a committee waiting to be formed. (jocular)
6. Modern English: *wherever it is stored*, *whenever the note comes due*, *wherever she goes*.

**See also:** [whosoever](#whosoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whence](#whence), [whither](#whither), [wherein](#wherein), [whatever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever), [anywhere](07-indefinite-compound.md#anywhere)

### whomso

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhumsoʊ/ &middot; *HOOM-so* &middot; stress on the first syllable, unlike *whomsoever*; the *wh-* is /h/ for every speaker, as in *who*  
**Русский:** кого бы ни — устар., книжн.; объектная форма whoso, только о людях
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** **object** &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; even deader than [whoso](#whoso), and essentially confined to Middle English and to poetry imitating it  
**Person / number:** third person; people only, open in number.  
**Refers to:** human beings only — whatever person the clause picks out. For things, [whatso](#whatso).  
**Agreement:** as an object it triggers no agreement of its own; the verb in its clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*whomso the king favors* — *king* is the subject, so *favors*).  
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomso* [subject] [verb] (*serve whomso thou wilt*) &middot; [preposition] *whomso* [subject] [verb] (*to whomso he names*) &middot; *whomso* [subject] [verb], [main clause]  
**Contrast:** [whoso](#whoso) is the subject form and *whomso* the object form, and — as with [whosoever](#whosoever) and [whomsoever](#whomsoever) — the choice is decided **inside the pronoun's own clause**, never by a preposition sitting in front of it. *Give it to whomso the steward names* is right because *whomso* is the object of *names*; *give it to whoso comes first* is right because *whoso* is the subject of *comes*. Substitute *he* or *him* to test it. Modern English has retired the whole set: write *whoever* and *whomever*, and note that even *whomever* is receding in American usage, exactly as plain *whom* has receded to the one position after a preposition.

Whomever; whatever person, as the object of a verb or a preposition. The object mate of *whoso*.

1. He would serve *whomso* the abbot appointed over him.
2. Let the ring pass to *whomso* she chooses, and let none dispute it.
3. *Whomso* the council summons must appear within the fortnight.
4. She would speak of it to *whomso* she trusted, and there were few enough of those.
5. Reward *whomso* thou findest faithful in a little thing.
6. Modern English: *the ring passes to **whomever** she chooses* — and in ordinary American writing, *to **whoever** she chooses*.

**See also:** [whoso](#whoso), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whatso](#whatso), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whom](05-relative.md#whom), [whomever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whomever)

### whomsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhumsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoom-so-EV-er* &middot; reduced /ˌhumsəˈɛvɚ/  
**Русский:** кого бы ни, кому бы ни — устар. и юр.; объектная форма whosoever
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic and legal &middot; **Case:** **object** &middot; **Register:** archaic; still productive in legal drafting  
**Person / number:** any person, singular or plural in reference; people only.  
**Refers to:** human beings only — an unnamed or not-yet-determined person or persons. For things, use [whatsoever](#whatsoever).  
**Agreement:** as an object it triggers no agreement of its own; the verb in its clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*whomsoever the board appoints* — *board* is the subject, so *appoints*).  
**Pattern:** [preposition] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] (*to whomsoever the court appoints*) &middot; [verb] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] (*hire whomsoever you please*) &middot; *whomsoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]  
**Contrast:** this is the entry where case actually matters, and where careful writers still go wrong. **The case of *whosoever/whomsoever* is set by its job inside its own clause, not by anything before it.** Compare: *The estate passes to **whomsoever** the testator names* — *whomsoever* is the **object** of *names*, so the object form is right. But *The estate passes to **whosoever** survives him* — here the pronoun is the **subject** of *survives*, so the subject form is right, even though a preposition sits immediately in front of it. The test: replace the clause with *he* or *him*. *The testator names **him*** → *whomsoever*. ***He** survives him* → *whosoever*. Writing ✗*to whomsoever survives him* is a hypercorrection, the same error as ✗*whom shall I say is calling*. Modern English writes *whoever/whomever*, and outside legal boilerplate even *whomever* is receding. Plain *whom* has made the same retreat: in present-day American English it **survives mainly right after a preposition** — *to whom it may concern*, *for whom the bell tolls*, *the tenant with whom we spoke* — while at the head of a clause or a question almost everyone now writes *who* (*Who did you invite?*, *Who were you talking to?*), and no American editor will stop them. Reaching for *whom* outside its one safe position is where the hypercorrections come from.

Whomever; whatever person, as the object of a verb or preposition.

1. The proceeds shall be paid to *whomsoever* the trustee designates.
2. Deliver the summons to *whomsoever* you find at the residence.
3. He would defend *whomsoever* the court assigned him.
4. Give the seat to *whomsoever* you please; it makes no difference to me.
5. To *whomsoever* these presents shall come, greeting. (the opening formula of an old deed)
6. She invited *whomsoever* she had met at the conference.

**See also:** [whosoever](#whosoever), [whoso](#whoso), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whom](05-relative.md#whom), [whomever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whomever)

### whoso

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhusoʊ/ &middot; *HOO-so* &middot; stress on the first syllable, unlike *whosoever*  
**Русский:** кто бы ни — устар., книжн.; «всякий, кто», глагол в ед. ч.
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; effectively confined to scripture and older poetry  
**Person / number:** third person; singular in agreement, though its reference is open-ended ("anyone who").  
**Refers to:** people only — any person who satisfies the condition in the clause. Never things.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb in both clauses: *whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing*, *whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein*. Treating it as plural (✗*whoso believe*) is wrong even in imitation.  
**Pattern:** *whoso* [singular verb] [rest of clause], [main clause with singular verb] &middot; *whoso is* [complement]  
**Contrast:** *whoso*, *whosoever*, and modern *whoever* are the same word at three degrees of length and archaism, and all three are **subject** forms that fuse the head and the relative — *whoso finds it* means "the person who finds it," so no separate antecedent is needed or possible (✗*the man whoso finds it*). *Whoso* is a full step older than *whosoever* and is essentially dead outside quotation; if you need the meaning, write *whoever*.

Whoever; anyone who. The oldest and shortest of the three, alive now only in quotation.

1. "*Whoso* diggeth a pit shall fall therein." (Proverbs 26:27, King James Version)
2. "*Whoso* findeth a wife findeth a good thing." (Proverbs 18:22)
3. *Whoso* enters here does so at his own peril.
4. *Whoso* keeps this covenant shall not be forsaken.
5. *Whoso* has ears to hear, let him hear.
6. *Whoso* would be a man must be a nonconformist. (Emerson, *Self-Reliance*)

**See also:** [whosoever](#whosoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whoever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever)

### whosoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhusoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-so-EV-er* &middot; reduced /ˌhusəˈɛvɚ/  
**Русский:** кто бы ни — устар., книжн., юр.; «всякий, кто», подлежащная форма
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, archaic &middot; **Case:** **subject** &middot; **Register:** archaic and religious; also legal, and jokingly grand  
**Variants:** *whosoe'er*, the metrically elided verse form (see [whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er](#whoeer-whateer-whosoeer-whatsoeer)); the object form is [whomsoever](#whomsoever)  
**Person / number:** third person; **singular** in agreement, however many people it may end up covering.  
**Refers to:** people only — any person at all who meets the description. For things, [whatsoever](#whatsoever).  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb in its own clause and in the main clause: *whosoever believeth in him should not perish*, *whosoever is found guilty shall be fined*. This is the same rule that gives *everyone is*, *somebody was*, *nobody knows*, and *anybody who wants a ticket has to line up*, and it runs through the neighboring families too: *each of them is*, *either of them is*, and *neither of them is* are all **singular**, while *both*, *several*, and *a few* are **plural**, and *all*, *some*, and *most* take their number from what they refer to (*all of the water **is** gone*, *all of the seats **are** taken*). Only [none](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#none) goes either way. A wrong plural here (✗*whosoever believe*) is a real error, not a stylistic slip.  
**Pattern:** *whosoever* [singular verb] [rest of clause] + [main clause] &middot; [preposition] *whosoever* [verb] (*to whosoever asks*) &middot; *whosoever will* (elliptical: "anyone who is willing")  
**Contrast:** *whosoever* is the **subject** form and [whomsoever](#whomsoever) the object form, and the choice depends on the pronoun's role in its own clause, not on a preceding preposition — *give it to whosoever asks* is correct because *whosoever* is the subject of *asks*. Against the modern forms: *whosoever* : *whoever* :: *whomsoever* : *whomever*, with the long forms adding weight and age and nothing else. In present-day American writing, use *whoever*; reserve *whosoever* for quotation, liturgy, and deliberate grandeur.

Whoever; any person who. The emphatic archaic form, familiar to most Americans from a single famous verse.

1. "That *whosoever* believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16, King James Version)
2. "*Whosoever* shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:39)
3. *Whosoever* enters this hall shall leave his weapons at the door.
4. Let *whosoever* is without sin cast the first stone.
5. The reward goes to *whosoever* brings him in alive.
6. *Whosoever* will may come.

**See also:** [whoso](#whoso), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whoever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever)

## Verse contractions: *'tis*, *'twas*, and the *-e'er* forms

English verse has always been allowed to squeeze a syllable out of a word to make a line scan, and two families of squeezed pronoun forms are common enough that an American meets them every December. The first fuses the pronoun *it* to the verb that follows — ***'tis***, ***'twas***, ***'twere***, ***'twill***, ***'twould*** — running the two words together from the front instead of the back, which is the reverse of the modern habit that gives *it's* and *it'll*. The second knocks the *v* out of *-ever*, giving ***whoe'er***, ***whate'er***, ***whosoe'er***, ***whatsoe'er***, and the same elision produces the non-pronouns *e'er*, *ne'er*, and *o'er* that fill out a metrical foot in hymns.

Both families are **archaic and metrical**, not dialectal: nobody has ever said them at conversational speed in ordinary American English. They are read aloud, sung, and quoted. The apostrophe in each of them marks a letter that is genuinely gone, which is why these forms take one and the *-n* possessives ([hisn](#hisn-hern-ourn-theirn), [yourn](#yourn)) do not.

### 'tis, 'twas, 'twere

**Pronunciation:** /tɪz/, /twʌz/, /twɚ/ &middot; *TIZ*, *TWUZ*, *TWER* &middot; weak forms /twəz/ and /twɚ/ in the middle of a line, where the whole point of the contraction is that the syllable is light; *'twill* is /twɪl/ and *'twould* /twʊd/  
**Русский:** это есть, это было — устар., поэт. стяжения it is, it was, it were
**Type:** frozen contractions of the dummy pronoun *it* with a following verb — pronoun plus verb welded into one word &middot; **Case:** subject (the *it* is the subject of its clause) &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic, and liturgical; in living American English, quotation, carols, and jokey elevated tone  
**Person / number:** third person singular throughout, invariant. *'Tis* is present, *'twas* past, and *'twere* the past subjunctive (*'twere well* = "it would be well").  
**Refers to:** whatever dummy *it* refers to — usually nothing at all. This is the empty *it* of weather, time, and general states of affairs (*'tis cold*, *'tis a pity*, *'twas the night before Christmas*), not a pointing pronoun with an antecedent.  
**Agreement:** the verb is already fused in and cannot change: *'tis* carries *is*, *'twas* carries *was*, *'twere* carries the subjunctive *were*. ✗*'Tis were* and ✗*'twas be* are impossible. Nothing can be inserted between the two halves, and the contraction cannot be negated inside itself — the negative is *'tis not*, never ✗*'tisn't* in careful verse, though *'tisn't* does turn up in nineteenth-century dialect writing.  
**Pattern:** *'tis* [complement] (*'tis a pity*, *'tis true*) &middot; *'twas* [noun phrase] (*'twas the night before Christmas*) &middot; *'twere* [complement] (subjunctive: *'twere well it were done quickly*) &middot; *'tis* [comparative] *to* [verb] (*'tis better to have loved*)  
**Contrast:** modern English contracts from the **back** — *it's*, *it'll*, *it'd* — and these contract from the **front**, which is the single fastest way to date a line of English. They are not the same words spelled differently: ✗*it's the night before Christmas* is grammatical but flattens the meter Moore wrote, and *'tis* in a business email is a costume, not a register. Keep *'twere* apart from *'twas*: *'twas* is a plain past (*'twas cold that night*), while *'twere* is the **past subjunctive** used for hypotheticals (*'twere better said in private*), the same *were* that survives in *if I were you*. And keep the whole family apart from the possessive: there is no ✗*'ts*, and *its* never takes an apostrophe.

It is; it was; it were. The old front-contracted forms of dummy *it* plus its verb, kept alive by poetry, carols, and one famous Christmas poem.

1. "*'Twas* the night before Christmas, when all through the house…" (Clement Clarke Moore, *A Visit from St. Nicholas*)
2. "*'Tis* better to have loved and lost than never to have loved at all." (Tennyson, *In Memoriam*)
3. "If it were done when *'tis* done, then *'twere* well it were done quickly." (*Macbeth* — all three forms in one sentence, and *'twere* the subjunctive)
4. "*'Tis* but thy name that is my enemy." (*Romeo and Juliet*)
5. *'Tis* a pity the letter arrived a day too late.
6. *'Twere* folly to sail with the glass falling like that. (subjunctive: "it would be folly")
7. Modern English: *It's a pity the letter came late*; *it would be foolish to sail*.

**See also:** [whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er](#whoeer-whateer-whosoeer-whatsoeer), [methinks](#methinks), [thou](#thou), [it](01-personal.md#it), [it (weather, time, and distance)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#it-weather-time-and-distance), [it's (not a possessive pronoun)](02-possessive.md#its-not-a-possessive-pronoun)

### whoe'er, whate'er, whosoe'er, whatsoe'er

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛr/, /wʌtˈɛr/, /ˌhusoʊˈɛr/, /ˌwʌtsoʊˈɛr/ &middot; *hoo-AIR*, *wut-AIR*, *hoo-so-AIR*, *wut-so-AIR* &middot; each is exactly one syllable shorter than the word it replaces, which is the entire reason it exists; no weak forms, since they occur only in recited verse  
**Русский:** кто бы ни, что бы ни — устар., поэт. стяжённые формы ради стихотворного размера
**Type:** fused relative pronouns, archaic — metrically elided forms of *whoever*, *whatever*, *whosoever*, and *whatsoever* &middot; **Case:** *whoe'er* and *whosoe'er* are **subject** forms, matched by the rare objects *whome'er* and *whomsoe'er*; *whate'er* and *whatsoe'er* have no case at all &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic; hymns, eighteenth- and nineteenth-century verse, and pastiche  
**Person / number:** third person, **singular** in agreement. *Whoe'er* and *whosoe'er* are people only; *whate'er* and *whatsoe'er* are things only.  
**Refers to:** whatever person (*whoe'er*, *whosoe'er*) or whatever thing, act, or state of affairs (*whate'er*, *whatsoe'er*) satisfies the clause. Like all fused relatives they carry their own antecedent inside them, so nothing may precede them.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb in the pronoun's own clause: *whoe'er believes*, *whate'er befalls*, ✗*whoe'er believe*. The elision changes the meter and nothing else — agreement, case, and meaning are identical to the full forms.  
**Pattern:** *whoe'er* [singular verb] [rest of clause] + [main clause] &middot; *whate'er* [subject] [verb] (*whate'er thou dost*) &middot; [verb] *whate'er* (object) &middot; *whatsoe'er* after a negative, exactly as *whatsoever* is used  
**Contrast:** these are not separate words and they carry no separate meaning: *whoe'er* is *whoever* with a syllable removed, and swapping one for the other changes only the scansion. The apostrophe marks the missing *v* of *-ever*, and it belongs where that *v* stood — ✗*whoe're* and ✗*who'er* misplace it. The same elision gives the adverbs *e'er* ("ever"), *ne'er* ("never"), and *o'er* ("over"), which are **not** pronouns and are not covered here. In present-day American writing, use the full forms; the elided ones are for singing and quoting, and reaching for them in prose reads as costume in exactly the way [methinks](#methinks) does.

Whoever, whatever, whosoever, whatsoever — each with the *v* of *-ever* knocked out to save a syllable of meter.

1. "*Whate'er* is best administer'd is best." (Pope, *An Essay on Man*)
2. *Whoe'er* thou art that readest this, remember me.
3. *Whatsoe'er* befall, we shall be found at our posts.
4. *Whosoe'er* shall keep this trust shall want for nothing.
5. She sang of a love that would follow him *whate'er* the cost.
6. Full forms: *whatever is best administered is best*; *whoever you are*; *whatever befalls*.

**See also:** ['tis, 'twas, 'twere](#tis-twas-twere), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whatso](#whatso), [whoso](#whoso), [whoever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever), [whatever](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever)

## Frozen forms and formal survivals

Ten leftovers that are not part of any living paradigm. ***A body*** is the generic pronoun that *one* and generic *you* pushed out, and it is still sitting inside *anybody*, *everybody*, *nobody*, and *somebody*. *Methinks* preserves a construction English abandoned six hundred years ago — a verb with a dative experiencer and no subject at all. *Oneself* is fully standard, formal but not archaic. *The same*, used as a pronoun, is the fossil that lawyers and shipping clerks kept alive after everyone else buried it. Around it stands the rest of the paperwork family — ***the aforesaid***, ***the foregoing***, ***the premises***, and ***the undersigned*** — four noun phrases that have hardened into pro-forms and now do the work of *it*, *that*, *the text above*, and *I*. And ***such a one*** is the archaic way to say *someone like that*, while ***et seq.*** is the citation abbreviation that stands in for "and the sections that follow." Every one of them is a phrase rather than a word, and every one of them is a pronoun only when nothing follows it: *the aforesaid vehicle* is a determiner and lives in another dictionary.

### a body

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbɑdi/ &middot; *uh-BOD-ee* &middot; the *d* taps to [ɾ], so it comes out /ə ˈbɑɾi/; in the Scots song it is /ə ˈbʌdɪ/ &middot; unstressed *a* is /ə/ and never /eɪ/  
**Русский:** человек, всякий — устар., диал. обобщённое «любой, и я в том числе» (ср. one)
**Type:** generic indefinite pronoun, archaic and dialectal &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object; the possessive is *a body's* &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; still heard in older rural American speech, and preserved for everyone in one Burns song  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, invariant. It has no plural — for more than one, English says *folks* or *people*.  
**Refers to:** any person at all, with the speaker quietly included — the same generic human that modern English calls *one* or generic *you*. **People only**, never a thing; and never a specific, identified person.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb, exactly like generic *one* and like *everyone*, *somebody*, and *nobody*: *a body gets tired*, *a body never knows*, *a body has to eat somewhere*. ✗*A body get tired* is an error, not a dialect feature.  
**Pattern:** *a body* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *a body* (*it would tire a body out*) &middot; [preposition] *a body* (*hard on a body*) &middot; *a body's* [noun] (possessive: *a body's own kin*) &middot; *if a body* [verb] &middot; *what's a body to do?*  
**Contrast:** English has four ways to be generic and this is the oldest one still recognizable. Formal writing uses [one](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#one-generic) (*one never knows*); ordinary speech uses generic *you* (*you never know*); the indefinite compounds cover the rest (*everybody knows*); and *a body* is the dialect member of the set. It is also the parent of the others: the *-body* in *anybody*, *everybody*, *nobody*, and *somebody* is this same noun *body* meaning "person," with a quantifier welded onto the front. Its reflexive is the phrasal *a body's self*, never ✗*abodyself*. Do not confuse the pronoun with the ordinary count noun *a body* meaning a corpse or a torso (*they found a body in the creek*) — that is not a pronoun at all, and it is the only sense most Americans under sixty still have.

One; a person; anyone at all. The archaic and dialectal generic pronoun that modern English replaced with *one* and with generic *you*.

1. "If *a body* meet *a body*, comin' thro' the rye." (Robert Burns — the line behind the title of *The Catcher in the Rye*)
2. *A body* can't get a moment's peace around here. (subject)
3. What's *a body* to do with a boy like that? (subject of an infinitive)
4. It would try *a body's* patience to sit through that twice. (possessive)
5. That road is hard on *a body* and harder on a truck. (object of a preposition)
6. They worked him till there was nothing left of *a body* at all. (object of a preposition, with the noun sense playing against the pronoun)
7. Standard: *One* never knows — or, far more naturally in American English, *you* never know.

### et seq.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛt ˈsɛk/ &middot; *et SEK* &middot; two syllables, stress on *seq*; lawyers who expand it say /ɛt sɪˈkwɛnʃə/ &middot; *et si-KWEN-sha* for *et sequentia*. There is no weak form — the phrase belongs to the page, and even in court it is read out at full value  
**Русский:** и след. — юр. и науч., лат. et sequentes «и последующие» (статьи, страницы)
**Type:** abbreviated Latin anaphoric phrase — *et sequentes* / *et sequentia*, "and the following ones"; a pro-form that stands in for the unnamed run of sections, pages, or verses after the one just cited &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** legal and scholarly; standard and unremarkable inside a citation, and out of place in any running sentence  
**Person / number:** third person **plural** — "and the ones after it." There is no singular; a single following item is *et seq.* only by courtesy, and older style wrote *et sequens* for it.  
**Refers to:** the units of **text** that follow the one cited — sections of a statute, pages of a report, paragraphs of a treatise, verses of scripture. Things, never people, and never a whole clause. It is strictly anaphoric on the citation immediately in front of it and cannot stand without one.  
**Agreement:** it heads no clause and takes no verb of its own. When the whole citation is the subject, the verb agrees with what the writer means by it — *29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. **governs** overtime* (the statute, singular), *sections 201 et seq. **are** the operative provisions* (the sections, plural). Both are correct, and the choice is a choice of referent.  
**Pattern:** [citation] *et seq.* (*§ 201 et seq.*, *pp. 45 et seq.*) &middot; *§§* [number] *et seq.* (the doubled section mark for a run) &middot; *et seqq.* (the older doubled *q*, for more than one following item) &middot; *ff.* (the humanities equivalent: *pp. 45ff.*)  
**Contrast:** the everyday equivalent is **and following**, or a stated range — and a stated range is what a careful modern drafter writes, because *et seq.* has no endpoint and a reader cannot tell where the cited run stops. Keep it apart from the other Latin abbreviations of the footnote: *et al.* is "and others" and points at **people** (co-authors, co-defendants); *ibid.* points at the work cited immediately above; *id.* at the same authority again; *supra* and *infra* at a place earlier or later in the text, and those two are adverbs with no pronoun in them at all. Note the period, which is not optional — *et* is a whole Latin word but *seq.* is an abbreviation — and note that American style now sets the phrase in roman rather than italic type.

And the sections following. The citation abbreviation that stands in for the run of text after the one named.

1. The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, sets the federal minimum wage.
2. Plaintiff's claims arise under Chapter 93A, §§ 2 *et seq.*, of the Massachusetts General Laws.
3. The committee's discussion of the amendment begins at page 45 *et seq.* of the report.
4. See Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 152 *et seq.* on mutual mistake.
5. Rule 26 *et seq.* — the discovery rules and everything that follows them — was rewritten in 2015.
6. Humanities style prefers *ff.*: *see pp. 45ff.*, meaning the same thing.
7. Careful modern drafting names the endpoint instead: *29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219*.

### methinks

**Pronunciation:** /miˈθɪŋks/ &middot; *me-THINKS* &middot; stress on the verb; the *me* is unstressed /mi/ and never reduces to /mɪ/ the way an ordinary object *me* can  
**Русский:** мне думается, сдаётся мне — устар.; в совр. языке только шутливо-иронически
**Type:** frozen impersonal construction — an object pronoun plus a fixed verb, functioning as a whole parenthetical &middot; **Case:** **object** (the *me* is an old dative, not a subject) &middot; **Register:** archaic; in living American English, purely jocular or ironic  
**Person / number:** first person singular, frozen. There is no ✗*theethinks*, ✗*usthinks*, or ✗*himthinks*; the only inflected relative is the past tense *methought*.  
**Refers to:** the speaker, as the person to whom something seems the case. What follows is a whole clause — the thing that seems true.  
**Agreement:** the verb is permanently third-person singular *thinks*, agreeing with an unexpressed impersonal "it," not with *me*. ✗*Methink* is impossible. The construction takes no auxiliary and cannot be negated internally: not ✗*me doesn't think*, but *methinks not*.  
**Pattern:** *methinks* [clause] (*methinks he protests too much*) &middot; [clause], *methinks* (parenthetical, at the end) &middot; *methinks not* &middot; past: *methought* [clause]  
**Contrast:** *methinks* is **not** a compressed *I think*. The verb inside it descends from Old English *þyncan* "to seem," a different word from *þencan* "to think," so the literal sense is "it seems to me" — and the *me* is the object of that seeming, which is why the pronoun is *me* and not *I* (✗*Ithinks*). The same dative survives in *woe is me* and in *if it please you*. Related frozen forms: *methought* (past), *meseems* (rarer and fully dead), *mehearty* (not related — that is *me* for *my* in stage-pirate speech). Every modern use is a costume: a writer reaching for mock-Elizabethan tone, usually attached to the misquotation below.

It seems to me. A frozen Middle English impersonal, now used only for comic or literary effect.

1. "The lady doth protest too much, *methinks*." (*Hamlet* — the line is Gertrude's, and it is regularly misquoted as *methinks the lady doth protest too much*)
2. *Methinks* someone has been reading the minutes rather closely.
3. *Methinks* not, sir.
4. This, *methinks*, is the third excuse we have heard this week.
5. *Methought* I heard the gate latch at midnight.
6. *Methinks* the committee has already decided and is merely being polite.

### oneself

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnˈsɛlf/ &middot; *wun-SELF* &middot; stress on *self*; the *one* does not reduce  
**Русский:** себя, сам — обобщённо-возвратное к one; книжн., но вполне литературное
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** formal but fully **standard** — one of the handful of entries in this file a careful writer can put in edited prose without hedging (the others being [whereby](#whereby), which is ordinary formal English, the modern postposed *whatsoever*, the quantifier *you all*, and, inside legal drafting, *herein*, *thereof*, and their relatives)  
**Person / number:** generic third person, singular, gender-neutral. It has no plural: the generic *one* has no plural of its own.  
**Refers to:** the generic person — anyone at all, people in general — and only when that generic *one* is already the subject of the same clause. It cannot refer to a specific named person; for that you need *himself*, *herself*, or *themselves*.  
**Agreement:** no agreement of its own. The clause's verb agrees with generic *one*, which is **singular**: *one does oneself no favors*, *one finds oneself wondering* — never ✗*one do*.  
**Pattern:** *one* [verb] *oneself* &middot; [preposition] *oneself* (*by oneself*, *to oneself*, *for oneself*) &middot; *to* [verb] *oneself* in an infinitive with generic reference (*to see oneself as others do*) &middot; intensive: *one must do it oneself*  
**Contrast:** the rule is **consistency**: once a sentence starts with generic *one*, it stays with *one*, *one's*, and *oneself* — *One should watch one's step*, not the mixed ✗*One should watch your step*. Older American style permitted *One should watch his step*, and you will meet it in mid-century prose, but it now reads as both dated and needlessly gendered; the modern American solution is usually to abandon *one* altogether and write *you* (*You should watch your step*), which is why *oneself* sounds formal. Spelled as two words, *one's self* means something different — the self as a thing, in psychological or philosophical writing (*the discovery of one's self*). And do not use *oneself* for a specific person: ✗*Karen finished the report oneself* → *herself*.

The reflexive of the generic pronoun *one*: any person at all, when that person is both subject and object of the same clause.

1. One should never send an angry email without giving *oneself* a night to think it over.
2. It is difficult to see *oneself* as others do.
3. One can easily talk *oneself* into a bad decision.
4. Living by *oneself* is not the same as being lonely.
5. One does *oneself* no favors by arguing with the referee.
6. To dress well is to present *oneself* honestly, not expensively.

### such a one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ə ˈwʌn/ &middot; *such-uh-WUN* &middot; the article reduces to /ə/ and the main stress lands on *one*; at speed the whole phrase runs to /sʌtʃəˈwʌn/  
**Русский:** такой человек, таковой — устар., книжн. «человек подобного рода»
**Type:** anaphoric indefinite pronoun phrase, archaic and formal &middot; **Variants:** ***such an one***, the older spelling, standard in the King James Bible and in prose down to about 1800 — see the contrast below, where the *an* turns out not to be an error &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; survives in formal, sermonic, and legal English, and in the fixed *such a one as*  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. The plural is *such ones* or, far more idiomatically, plain [such](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#such) (*such are the risks*).  
**Refers to:** a person or thing of the kind just described — the reference is backward, to a description already on the page, and the phrase substitutes for the whole of it. Most often human (*such a one is not to be trusted*), but things qualify too (*a knife, and such a one as would frighten a butcher*).  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb: *such a one is rare*, *such a one was found*, ✗*such a one are*. When it heads a comparison the verb belongs to the following clause instead (*such a one as **has** never been seen*).  
**Pattern:** *such a one* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *such a one* (object) &middot; *such a one as* [clause] (the fixed comparative: *such a one as I had never met*) &middot; *no such a one* &middot; *some such a one*  
**Contrast:** modern English says **someone like that**, **anyone of that kind**, or simply **such**: *such are the risks*, *no such thing*. Two points repay attention. The archaic spelling ***such an one*** is not an error and not a typo: *one* was once pronounced without the /w/ that begins it today, so it took *an* the way *an hour* still does, and the King James translators wrote *restore such an one in the spirit of meekness* (Galatians 6:1) on ordinary phonetic grounds. And note where the dictionary line falls — *such a* standing **in front of a noun** (*such a fool*, *such an idea*) is a determiner, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an); the entry you are reading is the phrase standing alone with *one* as its head, which makes it a pronoun.

Someone or something of that kind. The archaic pronoun phrase that modern English replaces with *someone like that*.

1. "I knew a man in Christ… *such an one* caught up to the third heaven." (2 Corinthians 12:2, King James Version)
2. "Restore *such an one* in the spirit of meekness." (Galatians 6:1)
3. *Such a one* is not to be trusted with money, whatever his references say.
4. He wanted a horse, and *such a one* as would carry him forty miles before noon.
5. If any man be idle, let *such a one* be set to work or sent away.
6. She had never met *such a one* before, and never met his like again.
7. Modern English: *Someone like that* is not to be trusted with money.

### the aforesaid, the aforementioned

**Pronunciation:** /ði əˈfɔrsɛd/, /ði əˌfɔrˈmɛnʃənd/ &middot; *thee uh-FOR-sed*, *thee uh-for-MEN-shund* &middot; the article is /ði/ before both, since both begin with a vowel; *aforementioned* also has a first-syllable-stressed variant /əˈfɔrˌmɛnʃənd/ &middot; *uh-FOR-men-shund*  
**Русский:** вышеупомянутый, вышеназванный — юр., канц.; отсылка к уже названному
**Type:** anaphoric pronoun phrases of legal and bureaucratic style — *the* plus a participle, standing in for a full noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** legal, official, and bureaucratic; outside those documents they read as parody, and plain-language drafting deletes them  
**Person / number:** third person; **singular or plural** according to what has been named (*the aforesaid is liable*, *the aforesaid are jointly liable*).  
**Refers to:** whatever was named earlier **in the same document** — a person, a company, a vehicle, a parcel, a sum, a date. Unlike [the same](#the-same), these can and routinely do refer to **people** (*the aforesaid shall appear at the hearing*), which is the one job they do that *same* cannot do without sounding grotesque.  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **referent**, never with the phrase: *the aforesaid (Mr. Alvarez) **is** hereby notified*; *the aforementioned (the three tenants) **are** in default*.  
**Pattern:** *the aforesaid* [verb] (as subject) &middot; [verb] *the aforesaid* (as object) &middot; [preposition] *the aforementioned* (*with respect to the aforementioned*) &middot; *all of the aforesaid* &middot; *the aforesaid, together with* [noun phrase]  
**Contrast:** the everyday equivalents are **it**, **they**, **that**, and **those** — and in a well-drafted document, the repeated noun (*the Tenant*, *the Vehicle*), which is what a modern drafting manual will tell you to write. Keep the standalone pronoun apart from the **determiner**: *the aforesaid vehicle* and *the aforementioned tenants* put a noun after the word, and those are determiner entries, covered as [aforesaid](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforesaid) and [aforementioned](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#aforementioned) alongside [said](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#said). The two words are near-perfect synonyms; *aforesaid* is the older and more purely legal of them, *aforementioned* the one that leaks into ordinary officialese. And distinguish them from [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), which points at a **passage of text** rather than at a thing the text named.

The person or thing already named in this document. The legal anaphors that stand where an ordinary writer would put *it*, *they*, or the noun again.

1. *The aforesaid* shall appear before the magistrate on the first Tuesday of the month.
2. The buyer agrees to indemnify *the aforementioned* against any claim arising from the sale.
3. Rent, utilities, and insurance: *the aforesaid* are payable on the first of each month.
4. Nothing in this section limits the obligations of *the aforementioned* under Article IV.
5. *The aforesaid* having been duly notified, the hearing proceeded in his absence.
6. Plain-language rewrite: *The tenant must appear before the magistrate*; *the buyer will indemnify the seller*.

### the foregoing

**Pronunciation:** /ðə fɔrˈɡoʊɪŋ/ &middot; *thuh for-GO-ing* &middot; stress on *go*; a first-syllable-stressed /ˈfɔrˌɡoʊɪŋ/ &middot; *FOR-go-ing* is also heard, and the article reduces to /ðə/  
**Русский:** вышеизложенное, вышесказанное — книжн., юр.; отсылка к предыдущему тексту
**Type:** anaphoric pronoun phrase of formal and legal style — *the* plus a participle, standing in for a stretch of text &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and academic; standard within those registers and stiff outside them  
**Person / number:** third person; **singular or plural** depending on what it gathers up — one clause or a list of twelve.  
**Refers to:** **the text immediately above** — the preceding sentence, paragraph, list, or section, taken as a lump. This is what separates it from every other entry in this group: it points at words on the page rather than at a thing those words named. It cannot point forward (for that, *the following*), and it cannot point outside the document.  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with what the phrase covers: *the foregoing **is** a summary* (one passage), *the foregoing **are** the only exceptions* (a list of them). Both are correct, and the choice is a matter of what you mean.  
**Pattern:** *notwithstanding the foregoing* (by far the commonest, and a full sentence-opener in contracts) &middot; *the foregoing* [verb] &middot; *subject to the foregoing* &middot; *in view of the foregoing* &middot; *all of the foregoing* &middot; *the foregoing shall not apply to* [noun phrase]  
**Contrast:** ***Notwithstanding the foregoing*** is the phrase to learn, because it does real work: it announces that the sentence about to be written **overrides** everything just said, and in a contract that reversal can be the most important thing on the page. The everyday equivalents are *the above*, *this*, and *all of that* — and in American editing, *the above* is perfectly respectable while *the foregoing* is a register choice, not an error. Its opposite is *the following*, which points down the page. Keep it apart from [the aforesaid](#the-aforesaid-the-aforementioned), which points at a **thing already named** rather than at the naming; from [the premises](#the-premises), which is the legal noun for the recitals of an instrument; and from the verb *forgo* ("to do without"), spelled without the *e* and unrelated.

Everything said above. The formal pro-form that gathers up a stretch of preceding text and refers to it as one thing.

1. Notwithstanding *the foregoing*, the Landlord may enter the premises without notice in an emergency.
2. *The foregoing* is a summary only and does not modify the terms of the policy.
3. Subject to *the foregoing*, the parties agree to bear their own costs.
4. *The foregoing* are the only exceptions the committee is prepared to allow.
5. In view of *the foregoing*, the court denies the motion.
6. Plain-English rewrite: *Even so, the landlord may enter without notice in an emergency.*

### the premises

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈprɛmɪsɪz/ &middot; *thuh PREM-iss-iz* &middot; stress on the first syllable; the singular *premise* /ˈprɛmɪs/ belongs to logic and to storytelling, and the legal word is plural only  
**Русский:** вышеизложенное — устар., юр. «упомянутые обстоятельства»; отсюда совр. «помещение»
**Type:** anaphoric pronoun phrase, legal and archaic — *the premises* meaning "the matters aforesaid" &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** legal and archaic; frozen in a handful of drafting formulas, and the source of the ordinary modern word for a building  
**Person / number:** third person **plural**, invariant. There is no singular in this sense.  
**Refers to:** the matters already set out in the same instrument — the recitals, the facts stated, the things previously described. It refers to **statements**, not to people. In a deed it originally covered the opening portion that described the property, which is exactly how the word came to mean the property itself.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb when it is the subject: *the premises are true and correct*, *the premises being considered*. ✗*The premises is* is wrong in either sense of the word — the building sense is plural too (*the premises **are** closed*).  
**Pattern:** *in consideration of the premises* (the old recital formula) &middot; *the premises considered* / *the premises being considered* &middot; *upon the premises aforesaid* &middot; *may it please the court, upon the premises* &middot; the modern survival: *the premises* = a building or grounds (*vacate the premises*, *no smoking on the premises*)  
**Contrast:** this is the entry where an ordinary American word turns out to be a fossil pronoun. A deed's opening section — the part that recited the parties, the consideration, and the description of the land — was called *the premises*, "the things set out before"; lawyers then used *the premises* to point back at all of it, and finally the phrase slid from the description onto the thing described, which is why *the premises* now means a building. All three senses are alive: the **pronominal** one (*in consideration of the premises*, archaic and formulaic), the **property** one (*vacate the premises*, ordinary modern English), and the **logic** one (*the premises of the argument*, where a single *premise* is a proposition an argument starts from). Only the first is a pronoun. Do not confuse the legal plural with the logical plural, and note that both are plural: *the premises are*, never ✗*the premises is*.

The matters aforesaid; the things already set out. The legal pronoun that turned into the ordinary word for a building.

1. Now therefore, in consideration of *the premises* and of the mutual covenants set forth herein, the parties agree as follows. (the standard recital)
2. *The premises* considered, the court finds for the plaintiff.
3. The affiant states that *the premises* set out above are true to the best of his knowledge.
4. Upon *the premises* aforesaid, the petitioner prays for relief.
5. The modern sense, and the only one most Americans use: the tenant agreed to vacate *the premises* by the first of the month.
6. Also modern: there is no smoking anywhere on *the premises*.

### the same

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈseɪm/ &middot; *thuh SAME* &middot; the article reduces to /ðə/; bare *same* /seɪm/ in the legal idiom  
**Русский:** оное, таковое — юр. и дел.; отсылка к уже названному (sign and return same)
**Type:** anaphoric pronoun of legal and commercial style &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** legal, commercial, and bureaucratic; widely criticized as jargon  
**Variants:** bare ***same***, with no article — the form that actually turns up in invoices and police reports (*please sign and return same*, *payment for same*). It is the same pronoun; dropping *the* is what marks the register as commercial rather than merely formal  
**Person / number:** third person; singular or plural depending entirely on what it stands for.  
**Refers to:** a noun phrase already named in the document — goods, a sum, a vehicle, a document, a right. Things and abstractions, not people (✗*we interviewed the witness and dismissed same* is grotesque even by the standards of the register).  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **referent**, not with the word *same*: *the goods were delivered and same **were** inspected on arrival*; *the payment was late and same **was** returned*.  
**Pattern:** [verb] *same* (*please sign and return same*) &middot; [preposition] *same* (*payment for same*) &middot; *the same* as subject (*the same shall be forfeited*) &middot; *the same being* [complement] (*the same being due and payable on the first*)  
**Contrast:** ordinary English uses *it* or *them* here, and any editor outside a law office will change *same* to one of those: *please sign and return **it***. The pronominal *same* is a genuine survival, not an error — it is standard inside contracts, invoices, insurance claims, and police reports — but it is exactly the kind of writing that plain-language statutes were passed to stamp out. Keep it apart from ordinary *the same* meaning "identical" (*we ordered the same*), which is everyday English and takes a following *as* (*the same as yours*). And keep it apart from the determiner [said](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#said) (*said vehicle*, *said defendant*), which does the same anaphoric job in front of a noun instead of standing alone.

It; them. The referring pronoun of legal and commercial boilerplate, standing for whatever was last named.

1. Please sign the enclosed agreement and return *same* to our office by Friday.
2. The invoice was received on the third, and payment for *same* was issued the following week.
3. The vehicle was recovered, and *same* was released to its registered owner.
4. Any deposit shall be held in escrow until the closing, at which time *the same* shall be applied to the purchase price.
5. We acknowledge receipt of your letter and will respond to *same* in due course.
6. The tenant shall keep the premises in good repair and shall surrender *the same* at the end of the term.

**See also:** [thereof](#thereof), [herein](#herein), [the same (demonstrative)](04-demonstrative.md#the-same), [said](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#said), [selfsame](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame)

### the undersigned

**Pronunciation:** /ði ˌʌndɚˈsaɪnd/ &middot; *thee un-der-SYND* &middot; the article is /ði/ before the vowel of *un-*; the main stress falls on *signed*, and the whole phrase has no reduced form because it appears in writing far more often than in speech  
**Русский:** нижеподписавшийся — юр., офиц.; автор документа о самом себе
**Type:** anaphoric pronoun phrase, legal and official — *the* plus a participle, used by writers to refer to **themselves** &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** legal, official, and military; standard in affidavits, certifications, and formal letters, and pompous anywhere else  
**Person / number:** grammatically **third** person, but referentially **first** — it means *I* or *we*. Singular or plural according to how many people sign (*the undersigned certifies* / *the undersigned parties agree*).  
**Refers to:** the person or persons who have signed, or are about to sign, the document in which the phrase appears — that is, the writer. It is self-reference in the third person, and it is the only entry in this file that lets a writer avoid saying *I* while still meaning it.  
**Agreement:** the verb follows the number of the signers, and the document has to tell you which: *the undersigned **hereby certifies** that…* (one signer), *the undersigned **have** reviewed the report* (several). Getting this wrong is the standard slip in form letters that were drafted for one signature and then signed by three.  
**Pattern:** *the undersigned hereby* [verb] (*hereby certifies*, *hereby acknowledges*, *hereby resigns*) &middot; *the undersigned, being duly sworn, deposes and says* (the affidavit formula) &middot; *the undersigned* [verb] (as subject) &middot; [preposition] *the undersigned* (*to the undersigned*, *by the undersigned*)  
**Contrast:** the word it replaces is **I** — or **we** — and in almost all present-day American writing that is what to put. *The undersigned hereby certifies* is *I certify*, three words shorter and no less binding; federal plain-language guidance says so outright, and no court has ever held that a signature meant less because the signer wrote *I*. Two related forms are worth knowing: **the below-signed** (rarer, and the same thing), and the older **your obedient servant**, which did the same self-effacing job in letters. Distinguish it from [the aforesaid](#the-aforesaid-the-aforementioned), which points at somebody else already named, and from [the same](#the-same), which points at a thing.

I; we — the writer, referred to in the third person as the person who signs below.

1. *The undersigned* hereby certifies that the foregoing statement is true and correct.
2. *The undersigned*, being duly sworn, deposes and says that he is over eighteen years of age. (the affidavit formula)
3. *The undersigned* have reviewed the audit and accept its findings. (several signers, plural verb)
4. Please direct any further correspondence to *the undersigned* at the address above.
5. *The undersigned* respectfully requests an extension of thirty days.
6. Plain-language rewrite: *I certify that the statement above is true*; *please write to me at the address above*.

**See also:** [the aforesaid, the aforementioned](#the-aforesaid-the-aforementioned), [the same](#the-same), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [hereby](#hereby), [hereunto](#hereunto), [herein](#herein), [I](01-personal.md#i), [we](01-personal.md#we)

## The legal pronominals: *hereof*, *thereof*, *whereof*

These are prepositions and pronouns welded into single words: *thereof* is "of it," *herein* is "in this document," *whereof* is "of which." Every American meets them, whether or not they notice — in a lease, an insurance policy, a mortgage, a warranty, a will, and in the Constitution. They are worth a section because they are perfectly regular once you see the pattern, and because the three prefixes are not interchangeable:

| Prefix | Stands for | Points at | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **here-** | *this* | the document you are holding — the contract, deed, or statute itself | *the terms set forth **herein*** = in this agreement |
| **there-** | *it*, *that* | the noun phrase most recently named in the text | *the roof and the shingles **thereon*** = on that roof |
| **where-** | *which*, *what* | a relative antecedent, or a question | *In witness **whereof*** = in witness of which |

Combine each prefix with a preposition — *of*, *in*, *to*, *on*, *under*, *by*, *after*, *with* — and you can generate the whole family: *hereof, herein, hereto, hereunder, hereby, hereinafter; thereof, therein, thereto, thereon, thereunder, thereafter, therewith; whereof, wherein, whereto, whereby, whereupon*. Stress falls on the preposition in every one of them: *there-OF*, *here-IN*, *where-OF*, *where-BY*. The whole family is given below, because a reader meeting one of them needs to know which kind it is, and they are not all the same kind. Most of them genuinely stand in for a noun phrase — *thereof* is *of it*, [wherewith](#wherewith) is *with which* — and those are pronominal in the strict sense. A few point at an **act** rather than a thing ([hereby](#hereby), which performs the sentence it sits in), and a few point at a **time** ([heretofore](#heretofore), [thereupon](#thereupon), [whereupon](#whereupon)); those are adverbs wearing the family uniform, and each entry says so in its first line. Two members are ordinary formal American English rather than legalese and can be written without apology: [whereby](#whereby) (*by which*) and [thereby](#thereby) (*by that means*). The rarest members are in, because a reader who meets one has to be able to look it up: [hereat](#hereat) and [thereat](#thereat), [hereinbelow](#hereinbelow), [thereinafter and thereinbefore](#thereinafter-thereinbefore), and the patent-drafting pair [therebetween and therethrough](#therebetween-therethrough). *Thereafter* and *hereabouts* are left out; they are adverbs of time and place with no pronoun inside them at all. So are the citation abbreviations ***supra*** and ***infra*** — "above" and "below" in a brief or a footnote (*Smith, supra, at 415*; *see infra note 12*). They look as though they belong with this family and they do not: they are the plain Latin adverbs, with no pronoun fused into them, and they never fill a noun-phrase slot the way [thereof](#thereof) and [wherewith](#wherewith) do. For those, and for *ibid.*, *id.*, and *et al.*, see the note under [et seq.](#et-seq).

### hereat

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈæt/ &middot; *heer-AT* &middot; stress on *at*, which keeps its full /æ/; the *here-* never takes the beat, and there is no weak form — the whole family is read rather than spoken  
**Русский:** при сём, тут же — устар.; «в ответ на это, вслед за этим» (о только что сказанном)
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *at* + *this*, where "this" is the thing just said or done, taken as the occasion of what follows &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused preposition *at*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary in the narrative sense, formal-legal in the drafting sense; the rarest member of the *here-* family and the one a drafter is least likely to have seen  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** the **statement or event immediately preceding** — what has just been said, read, or done — treated as the point at which the next thing happens. It points at an event or an utterance, not at a person and not at a physical place.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; it is adverbial, and the clause it opens takes its own subject and verb (*hereat the company **fell** silent*).  
**Pattern:** *hereat* [subject] [verb] (the narrative opener: *hereat the fiddler stopped*) &middot; [clause], *and hereat* [clause] &middot; *hereat* [subject] *must* [verb] (the essayist's aside: *hereat I must beg the reader's patience*)  
**Contrast:** the family splits by deixis and *hereat* is the *this* member: *hereat* is "at this," [thereat](#thereat) is "at that," and [whereat](#whereat) is "at which," the only one of the three with any real life left in it. Against *hereupon* — which English barely built at all, preferring [whereupon](#whereupon) and [thereupon](#thereupon) — the difference is that *upon* carries a sense of immediate sequence and *at* carries only occasion. Against [hereby](#hereby), the difference is the whole distinction that organizes this section: *hereby* performs the act the sentence names, while *hereat* merely says when something happened. Modern American English writes **at this**, **at this point**, or **at that**, and there is no reason to write anything else.

At this; at this point. The occasion-marking member of the *here-* family, and the rarest of them.

1. *Hereat* the whole company fell silent, and the fiddler put down his bow.
2. She named her price, and *hereat* the buyer rose and left the room without another word.
3. The bill was read a second time; *hereat* the Speaker adjourned the House until Tuesday.
4. He confessed the whole business, and *hereat* his lawyer asked the court for a recess.
5. *Hereat* I must beg the reader's patience for a short digression. (the essayist's aside)
6. Modern English: *At this*, the whole room fell silent.

**See also:** [thereat](#thereat), [whereat](#whereat), [hereby](#hereby), [herein](#herein), [thereupon](#thereupon), [whereupon](#whereupon)

### hereby

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *heer-BY* &middot; stress on *by*, which keeps its full diphthong; the *here-* never takes the beat  
**Русский:** настоящим — юр., офиц.; «этим самым документом или действием»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *by* + *this*, where "this" is the present document or utterance taken as an **act**; performative rather than referential &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused preposition *by*) &middot; **Register:** legal, official, and ceremonial; standard within those registers  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** the very sentence it stands in, understood as the deed that sentence performs — *I hereby resign* means "by these words, I resign." It is the only member of the *here-* family that points at the speech act rather than at a text.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; it is adverbial and the clause's own subject controls the verb (*the Company **hereby grants*** — singular; *the parties **hereby agree*** — plural).  
**Pattern:** [subject] *hereby* [performative verb] (*hereby certify*, *hereby resign*, *hereby grant*, *hereby declare*) &middot; [subject] *is hereby* [past participle] (*you are hereby notified*, *the motion is hereby denied*) &middot; *I hereby swear / affirm*  
**Contrast:** the family splits three ways and *hereby* is the odd member. [Whereby](#whereby) is *by which* and takes an antecedent; [thereby](#thereby) is *by that means* and points back at something already done; *hereby* points at **nothing** — it announces that the sentence you are reading is itself the act. That is why it only attaches to **performative** verbs, the ones a person can do by saying them: *resign*, *certify*, *grant*, *declare*, *notify*, *appoint*, *agree*, *waive*. Bolt it to any other verb and the sentence collapses (✗*I hereby believe the report*, ✗*I hereby went to the meeting*). It is also the one member you can almost always delete without changing the legal effect — *I resign* ends the job just as surely — which is why plain-language drafting cuts it, and why *hereby* survives mostly as a signal of formality. Do not confuse it with *hereby* in the sense "near here," which is not English; that is *hereabouts*.

By this act; by these words. The performative member of the *here-* family — it does not point at anything, it does the thing.

1. I *hereby* resign my position on the board, effective at the close of business Friday.
2. You are *hereby* notified that the lease will not be renewed.
3. The Company *hereby* grants the Employee an option to purchase one thousand shares.
4. The parties *hereby* agree to submit any dispute to binding arbitration.
5. The motion is *hereby* denied, and the hearing is adjourned.
6. Not performative, and therefore impossible: ✗ *I hereby believe the report is finished.*
7. Plain-language rewrite: *I resign from the board effective Friday.*

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereof](#hereof), [hereto](#hereto), [hereunto](#hereunto), [thereby](#thereby), [whereby](#whereby), [the undersigned](#the-undersigned)

### herein

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈɪn/ &middot; *heer-IN* &middot; stress on *in*  
**Русский:** в настоящем документе — юр.; «здесь, в этом тексте», не вне его
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *in* + *this*, a pro-form for "in this document" &middot; **Case:** object (the pronoun element is governed by the fused preposition *in*) &middot; **Register:** legal and formal; standard within that register  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** the present document or the present passage of it — the contract, the statute, the policy, the letter in which the word appears. It is self-referential: it never points at anything outside the text.  
**Agreement:** none — it is adverbial and takes no verb of its own.  
**Pattern:** [noun] *defined herein* &middot; *set forth herein* &middot; *contained herein* &middot; *except as otherwise provided herein* &middot; *nothing herein shall* [verb]  
**Contrast:** *herein* is "in **this** document"; [therein](#therein) is "in **that** thing just mentioned." Swapping them is the standard drafting error: *the Premises and all fixtures **therein*** means inside the premises, while *all fixtures **herein*** would absurdly mean fixtures inside the lease. Also distinguish *herein* from *hereinafter* ("later in this document," as in *hereinafter referred to as the Buyer*) and from *hereby*, which performs an act rather than pointing anywhere (*I hereby resign*).

In this document; in this agreement. The self-referential pro-form of legal drafting.

1. The terms defined *herein* apply to every schedule and exhibit.
2. Nothing *herein* shall be construed as a waiver of either party's rights.
3. Except as otherwise provided *herein*, notice must be given in writing.
4. The parties agree to the covenants contained *herein*.
5. The rates set forth *herein* are effective as of January 1.
6. Capitalized terms not defined *herein* have the meanings given in the Master Agreement.

**See also:** [hereof](#hereof), [hereto](#hereto), [therein](#therein), [wherein](#wherein), [the same](#the-same)

### hereinafter

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrɪnˈæftɚ/ &middot; *heer-in-AF-ter* &middot; four syllables, stress on *af*; the older /ˌhɪrɪnˈɑftɚ/ turns up in British-influenced drafting  
**Русский:** далее по тексту, в дальнейшем — юр.; «ниже в настоящем документе»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *after* + *herein*, a pro-form for "later in this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal; standard within legal drafting and out of place anywhere else  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the remainder of the present document — the text that follows the point where the word appears. It points at **text**, never at time, and never outside the document.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *hereinafter referred to as* [defined term] &middot; *hereinafter called* [name] &middot; *(hereinafter, the "Buyer")* &middot; *as hereinafter provided* &middot; *the terms hereinafter set forth*  
**Contrast:** the trap here is one letter of difference in meaning and a lawsuit's worth of difference in effect: ***hereinafter*** means "later **in this document**," while ***hereafter*** means "after now, from this time forward." A will that says *the trustee shall hereafter distribute the income* is talking about the future; one that says *hereinafter* is pointing at a later clause. Its mirror is [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore) ("earlier in this document"), and its time-facing mirror is [heretofore](#heretofore) ("until now"). The single commonest use is the defined-term parenthesis — *Acme Industries, Inc. (hereinafter "Acme")* — which modern drafting shortens to *(the "Buyer")* or just *("Acme")*, since the word adds nothing a reader could misunderstand.

Later in this document; below. The forward-pointing member of the *here-* family.

1. Acme Industries, Inc., *hereinafter* referred to as the Seller, warrants clear title to the goods.
2. The rates set out *hereinafter* supersede those in the previous schedule.
3. Except as *hereinafter* provided, no notice is required.
4. The property, *hereinafter* called the Premises, is described in Exhibit A.
5. This obligation is subject to the exceptions *hereinafter* enumerated in Section 12.
6. Modern drafting: *Acme Industries, Inc. (the "Seller") warrants clear title.*

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore), [heretofore](#heretofore), [hereof](#hereof), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing)

### hereinbefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrɪnbɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *heer-in-bee-FOR* &middot; four syllables, stress on *for*  
**Русский:** выше по тексту — юр.; «ранее в настоящем документе» (вариант hereinabove)
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *before* + *herein*, a pro-form for "earlier in this document" &middot; **Variants:** ***hereinabove*** /ˌhɪrɪnəˈbʌv/ &middot; *heer-in-uh-BUV*, the same word with *above* in place of *before*, and the commoner of the two in American drafting; *hereinbefore* is older and slightly more British &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the part of the present document that comes **before** the point where the word appears — a definition, a recital, a numbered section already passed.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *as hereinbefore provided* &middot; *the term hereinbefore defined* &middot; *the party hereinbefore named* &middot; *except as hereinabove set forth* &middot; *hereinabove described*  
**Contrast:** the four-way grid is worth memorizing, because two of the four are about **text** and two are about **time**: [hereinafter](#hereinafter) = later in this document; *hereinbefore* / *hereinabove* = earlier in this document; *hereafter* = after now; [heretofore](#heretofore) = before now. Drafters slip between the pairs constantly, and in an instrument the slip is substantive. Against [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), the difference is grammatical rather than semantic: *the foregoing* is a **noun phrase** that can be the subject of a sentence (*the foregoing is a summary*), while *hereinbefore* is an **adverb** that can only modify (*as hereinbefore stated*). Plain-language drafting replaces both with *above* — *as stated above*, *the term defined above* — which no reader has ever misunderstood.

Earlier in this document; above. The backward-pointing member of the *here-* family.

1. Except as *hereinbefore* provided, all notices must be sent by certified mail.
2. The party *hereinbefore* named as Trustee shall serve without bond.
3. The capitalized terms used in this Article have the meanings *hereinabove* assigned to them.
4. The property *hereinabove* described is conveyed subject to the easement of record.
5. Nothing *hereinbefore* stated limits the Buyer's remedies at law.
6. Plain-language rewrite: *Except as stated above, all notices must be sent by certified mail.*

**See also:** [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [heretofore](#heretofore), [herein](#herein), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [hereof](#hereof)

### hereinbelow

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrɪnbɪˈloʊ/ &middot; *heer-in-bee-LOW* &middot; four syllables, stress on *low*; the middle two syllables reduce to /ɪn/ and /bɪ/ and carry no beat at all  
**Русский:** ниже по тексту — юр., амер. драфтинг; «далее в настоящем документе»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *below* + *herein*, a pro-form for "later in this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal; American drafting almost exclusively, and a comparative newcomer, built on the pattern of *hereinabove* because drafters like matched pairs  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the part of the present document that comes **after** the point where the word appears — a schedule, an exhibit, a numbered section further down. It points at **text**, never at time, and never at anything outside the instrument.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *as hereinbelow provided* &middot; *except as hereinbelow set forth* &middot; *the terms hereinbelow defined* &middot; *hereinbelow described* &middot; *the schedule hereinbelow*  
**Contrast:** it means exactly what [hereinafter](#hereinafter) means, and *hereinafter* is older, commoner, and the safer choice in any document that will be read by a court. *Hereinbelow* exists because [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore) has the variant *hereinabove*, and once a drafter has written *hereinabove* the pull toward a matching *hereinbelow* is hard to resist. Fit it into the four-way grid that organizes this family, two members about **text** and two about **time**: *hereinafter* and *hereinbelow* = later in this document; *hereinbefore* and *hereinabove* = earlier in this document; *hereafter* = after now; [heretofore](#heretofore) = before now. Its *there-* counterpart, pointing at some **other** document, is [thereinafter](#thereinafter-thereinbefore). Plain-language drafting replaces all of them with *below* and *above*.

Later in this document; below. The younger twin of *hereinafter*, built to match *hereinabove*.

1. Except as *hereinbelow* provided, the Tenant shall pay all utilities charged to the premises.
2. The parcels *hereinbelow* described are conveyed subject to the easement of record.
3. The schedule of payments is set out *hereinbelow* in Section 9.
4. Nothing *hereinabove* or *hereinbelow* limits the Buyer's remedies at law. (the matched pair, which is why the word exists)
5. The warranties *hereinbelow* stated are the only warranties the Seller gives.
6. Plain-language rewrite: *Except as stated below, the tenant pays all utilities.*

**See also:** [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore), [herein](#herein), [heretofore](#heretofore), [thereinafter, thereinbefore](#thereinafter-thereinbefore), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing)

### hereof

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈʌv/ &middot; *heer-UV* &middot; stress on *of*, which keeps its full /ʌv/ rather than reducing to /əv/  
**Русский:** настоящего документа — юр., род. п.: Section 5 hereof, the date hereof
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *of* + *this*, a pro-form for "of this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the present document, or a numbered part of it. Almost always used to cross-reference a section or to date the instrument itself.  
**Agreement:** none.  
**Pattern:** *Section* [number] *hereof* &middot; *the date hereof* &middot; *as of the date hereof* &middot; *Article* [number] *hereof*  
**Contrast:** *hereof* points at this document, [thereof](#thereof) at the thing just named: *Section 4 **hereof*** is a section of this contract, while *the term **thereof*** is the term of whatever was last mentioned. The commonest live phrase is *the date hereof*, meaning the date of this agreement — which is not the same as *the date thereof*, the date of some other document already named. Modern plain-language drafting replaces both with a repeated noun: *the date of this Agreement*.

Of this document; of this agreement.

1. All representations are true and correct as of the date *hereof*.
2. Section 9 *hereof* governs the resolution of disputes.
3. The obligations described in Article III *hereof* survive termination.
4. This exhibit forms an integral part *hereof*.
5. The schedule attached to and made a part *hereof* lists every covered device.
6. The term *hereof* begins on the first day of the month following execution.

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereto](#hereto), [thereof](#thereof), [whereof](#whereof)

### hereto

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈtu/ &middot; *heer-TOO* &middot; stress on *to*  
**Русский:** к настоящему документу — юр.; the parties hereto «стороны настоящего договора»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *to* + *this*, a pro-form for "to this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the present document, most often in naming its signatories or its attachments.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; when it modifies a subject, that subject controls the verb — *the parties hereto **agree*** (plural *parties*), *the exhibit attached hereto **is** incorporated* (singular *exhibit*).  
**Pattern:** *the parties hereto* &middot; *attached hereto as Exhibit* [letter] &middot; *annexed hereto* &middot; *the signatories hereto*  
**Contrast:** *hereto* attaches things to **this** document; [thereto](#thereto) attaches them to something already named — *the lease and all amendments **thereto***. The single most common phrase in American contract drafting, *the parties hereto*, means simply "the parties to this agreement," and plain-language drafters delete *hereto* as adding nothing. Do not confuse it with the adverb *heretofore*, which means "until now" and points at time rather than at the document.

To this document; to this agreement.

1. The parties *hereto* agree to submit any dispute to binding arbitration.
2. A copy of the policy is attached *hereto* as Exhibit B.
3. The signatories *hereto* warrant that they are authorized to sign.
4. The schedules annexed *hereto* are incorporated by reference.
5. Neither party *hereto* may assign its rights without written consent.
6. The floor plan attached *hereto* controls in the event of any inconsistency.

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereof](#hereof), [thereto](#thereto), [the same](#the-same)

### heretofore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrtəˈfɔr/ &middot; *heer-tuh-FOR* &middot; stress on *for*; also /ˌhɪrtuˈfɔr/ with the middle syllable unreduced in careful reading  
**Русский:** до сих пор, ранее — книжн., юр.; о времени до настоящего момента
**Type:** pronominal adverb of **time** — *before* + *this*, where "this" is the present moment rather than the present document &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** formal and legal; the least jargon-ish member of the family, and usable in ordinary formal prose  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** all the time up to now. Unlike every other *here-* form, it points at a **period**, not at a text, which is why it is at home in a history book as well as in a contract.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; it sits happily with a perfect tense, which is the tense its meaning implies (*the parties have heretofore operated without a written agreement*).  
**Pattern:** [subject] *has/have heretofore* [past participle] &middot; *heretofore unknown / unavailable / unpublished* (before an adjective) &middot; *as heretofore* &middot; *the practice heretofore followed*  
**Contrast:** *heretofore* is "**until now**"; [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore) is "**earlier in this document**." The pair is easy to keep straight once you see that *-tofore* contains *before* in its temporal sense while *-inbefore* contains *herein*, the document. Its forward mate is *hereafter* ("from now on"), not [hereinafter](#hereinafter). The everyday equivalents are *until now*, *up to now*, and *previously*, and unlike most of this family *heretofore* survives in respectable non-legal prose — *a species heretofore unknown to science* is the kind of sentence a magazine will still print. Do not stretch it to mean "therefore"; the two are unrelated, and ✗*heretofore, the motion is denied* is a malapropism.

Until now; up to this time. The time-pointing member of the *here-* family, and the one an ordinary writer can still use.

1. The parties have *heretofore* operated without a written agreement.
2. The dig turned up a burial practice *heretofore* unknown in the region.
3. Nothing in this policy affects any claim *heretofore* filed and accepted.
4. *Heretofore* the committee has met twice a year; beginning in January it will meet quarterly.
5. The manuscript contains three letters *heretofore* unpublished.
6. Plain English: *Until now, the parties have worked without a written agreement.*

**See also:** [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore), [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [herein](#herein), [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### hereunder

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈʌndɚ/ &middot; *heer-UN-der* &middot; stress on *un*  
**Русский:** по настоящему договору — юр.; «в силу этого документа»; также «ниже по тексту»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *under* + *this*, a pro-form for "under this agreement" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal; one of the workhorses of American contract drafting  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the present agreement, statute, or instrument, treated as the **authority** that creates a right or duty — *rights hereunder* are the rights this contract gives you. A secondary and rarer sense points at position on the page ("below," as in *the schedule set out hereunder*), which American drafters usually write as *below* instead.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the noun it modifies keeps its own agreement (*the obligations hereunder **are** several*, *any payment due hereunder **is** payable in dollars*).  
**Pattern:** *rights and obligations hereunder* &middot; *any amount due hereunder* &middot; *performance hereunder* &middot; *a default hereunder* &middot; *notices given hereunder* &middot; *nothing hereunder shall* [verb]  
**Contrast:** *hereunder* means "under **this** instrument"; [thereunder](#thereunder) means "under **that** one," the agreement or statute just named. In a document that incorporates another one by reference — a master agreement with work orders under it — the difference decides which contract a right comes from, and swapping them is a real drafting error rather than a stylistic one. Distinguish it also from [hereto](#hereto) ("to this document," used of parties and attachments) and from *thereunder* in its physical sense (*the deck and the crawlspace thereunder*), which is ordinary spatial English. The plain rewrite is *under this Agreement*, which costs two words and removes all doubt.

Under this agreement; under this instrument.

1. The Contractor's obligations *hereunder* survive the termination of this Agreement.
2. Any amount due *hereunder* and not paid within thirty days bears interest at nine percent.
3. Notices given *hereunder* must be in writing and sent to the addresses in Section 14.
4. A failure to perform *hereunder* is not excused by a shortage of labor.
5. Nothing *hereunder* requires either party to violate applicable law.
6. Plain-language rewrite: *The Contractor's obligations under this Agreement survive termination.*

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereof](#hereof), [hereto](#hereto), [thereunder](#thereunder), [thereof](#thereof)

### hereunto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrʌnˈtu/ &middot; *heer-un-TOO* &middot; stress on *to*; the middle syllable is full /ʌn/, not reduced, because the word survives only in a formula that is read slowly  
**Русский:** к сему — устар., юр.; в формуле подписания hereunto set my hand
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *unto* + *this*, a ceremonial pro-form for "to this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** archaic and legal; effectively confined to the attestation clause at the foot of a signed instrument  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the document being signed, as the thing a signature is applied **to**.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the subject of the attestation clause controls the verb (*the parties **have** hereunto set their hands*).  
**Pattern:** *IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the parties have hereunto set their hands and seals* (the whole living use of the word) &middot; *hereunto affixed* &middot; *hereunto annexed* &middot; *the schedule hereunto attached*  
**Contrast:** [hereto](#hereto) is the living form and does all the ordinary work — *the parties hereto*, *attached hereto as Exhibit B*. *Hereunto* is the same meaning in ceremonial dress, built on *unto* rather than *to*, and it appears in modern American documents almost exclusively inside the closing formula that pairs it with [whereof](#whereof): *In witness whereof, the parties have hereunto set their hands.* Outside that sentence it is dead, and writing *attached hereunto* instead of *attached hereto* marks a drafter as reaching for an antique. Note that *hereunto* is not [hereunder](#hereunder): *unto* is direction, *under* is authority.

To this document; to this instrument. The ceremonial twin of *hereto*, alive inside one formula.

1. In witness whereof, the parties have *hereunto* set their hands and seals the day and year first above written.
2. I have *hereunto* subscribed my name and affixed the seal of the Court.
3. The exhibits *hereunto* annexed form part of this deed.
4. The plan *hereunto* attached shows the boundary as agreed by the surveyors.
5. In testimony whereof, the Secretary of State has *hereunto* caused the Great Seal to be affixed.
6. Ordinary modern drafting: *the parties have signed this Agreement as of the date first written above.*

**See also:** [hereto](#hereto), [hereunder](#hereunder), [whereof](#whereof), [hereby](#hereby), [thereunto](#thereunto), [the undersigned](#the-undersigned)

### herewith

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈwɪθ/ &middot; *heer-WITH* &middot; also /hɪrˈwɪð/ with the voiced *th*, exactly as ordinary *with* varies; stress on *with*  
**Русский:** при сём, настоящим направляется — офиц., дел.; «вместе с этим письмом»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *with* + *this*, a pro-form for "with this letter" or "with this document" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** formal, commercial, and legal; the most letter-flavored member of the family and the one most often criticized as padding  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the present letter, email, or filing, as the thing something is sent **along with**. Its natural home is a transmittal sentence — the sentence that says what is in the envelope.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *enclosed herewith* &middot; *transmitted herewith* &middot; *we submit herewith* &middot; *please find herewith* &middot; *herewith the report you requested* (verbless, in a cover note)  
**Contrast:** *herewith* means "with this letter"; [therewith](#therewith) means "with that thing already named." The reason *herewith* has a bad reputation is redundancy: in ***enclosed herewith please find***, every one of the four words is doing the work of the other three, and it is the standard example in every guide to plain business writing. *Enclosed is the report* says it, and *I've attached the report* says it in an email, where nothing is "enclosed" at all. Keep the pro-form apart from the adverb *herewith* meaning "hereby, by this act" (*I herewith resign*), which is an old and now stilted alternative to [hereby](#hereby).

With this letter; with this document.

1. Enclosed *herewith* is the executed counterpart of the lease.
2. We submit *herewith* the annual report of the trustees for the year ended June 30.
3. Transmitted *herewith* are the six exhibits identified in our letter of the fourth.
4. Please find *herewith* a check in the amount of four hundred dollars.
5. *Herewith* the draft you asked for; I have marked the two clauses that still worry me.
6. Plain English: *I've attached the signed lease* — and never ✗*enclosed herewith please find*.

**See also:** [herein](#herein), [hereto](#hereto), [hereby](#hereby), [therewith](#therewith), [hereunto](#hereunto)

### thereat

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈæt/ &middot; *thair-AT* &middot; stress on *at*; the *there-* is unstressed /ðɛr/ and never reduces to the /ðɚ/ of *there* in *there's a man here*, because the compound is a written word before it is a spoken one  
**Русский:** там; при этом — устар.; «в том месте» (юр.) или «после чего» (книжн. повеств.)
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *at* + *that*, with two distinct jobs: **locative** ("at that place or thing," in a deed) and **narrative** ("at that; upon that," in older prose) &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused preposition *at*) &middot; **Register:** archaic in the narrative sense and legal in the locative one; rare in both, and rare even in drafting  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** in the locative sense, the **place or thing** most recently named — a parcel, a mill, a building; in the narrative sense, the **event or statement** just reported, taken as the occasion of what follows. Never a person.  
**Agreement:** none of its own. In the narrative use it opens a clause whose own subject controls the verb (*thereat she **left** the house*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] + *thereat* (the locative postmodifier: *the mill and the machinery thereat*) &middot; *the premises and all improvements thereat* &middot; *thereat* [subject] [verb] (the narrative opener: *thereat he rose*) &middot; [clause]; *thereat* [clause]  
**Contrast:** the neighbors are close and the differences are prepositional: [therein](#therein) is "in that," [thereon](#thereon) "on that," [thereto](#thereto) "to that," and *thereat* "at that" — vaguer than any of them, which is why drafters reach for the others and why *thereat* has all but disappeared from modern instruments. In the narrative sense [thereupon](#thereupon) took the word's place entirely, and *thereupon* is what an American actually meets. Its deictic mates are [hereat](#hereat) ("at this") and [whereat](#whereat) ("at which"). Modern English writes **at it**, **at that point**, or names the place again.

At that; at that place. The vaguest of the *there-* compounds, displaced by *thereon* in deeds and by *thereupon* in narrative.

1. The Landlord shall insure the premises and all fixtures *thereat*.
2. The mill, together with the machinery *thereat*, passed to the younger son.
3. The parcel and the improvements *thereat* are assessed as a single unit.
4. He accused her of forging the letter, and *thereat* she left the house. (the narrative sense)
5. The clerk read the verdict; *thereat* a shout went up in the gallery.
6. Modern English: *at it*, *at that point*, or simply *on the property*.

**See also:** [hereat](#hereat), [whereat](#whereat), [thereon](#thereon), [therein](#therein), [thereto](#thereto), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### therebetween, therethrough

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrbɪˈtwin/, /ˌðɛrˈθru/ &middot; *thair-bee-TWEEN*, *thair-THROO* &middot; stress on the preposition in both, as everywhere in this family; *therethrough* keeps the voiceless *th* of *thin* in its second half and the voiced *th* of *this* in its first, which is why it is awkward to say and almost never said  
**Русский:** между ними; сквозь него — юр. и патент.; «между тем» и «через то»
**Type:** pronominal adverbs — *between* + *those*, *through* + *that*; a small **productive** set rather than two learned words &middot; **Variants:** the same rule builds *thereacross*, *therealong*, *therearound*, *thereabove*, and *therebeneath* on demand; a drafter who needs one simply makes it &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and technical, and effectively confined to **patent claims** and older engineering specifications; outside a patent an American will never meet either word  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. *Therebetween* requires **two or more** antecedents; *therethrough* takes one.  
**Refers to:** the **components** named earlier in the same claim — the two plates a gap sits between, the housing a bore runs through. Things only, never people, and never a clause. In a patent claim the antecedent must be an element already recited, which is the whole reason the compound is preferred to *between them*: the pronoun *them* could point anywhere, while *therebetween* points at the last-recited pair.  
**Agreement:** none of their own; both are adverbial and attach to a participle or a verb (*a gap formed therebetween*, *a shaft extending therethrough*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *formed therebetween* &middot; *the space therebetween* &middot; *a bore extending therethrough* &middot; *a conduit passing therethrough* &middot; [two elements recited] + participle + *therebetween*  
**Contrast:** plain English says **between them** and **through it**, and outside patent practice that is what to write. Inside it, the compound survives for a reason worth knowing: a claim must have unambiguous **antecedent basis** for every reference, and *therebetween* is read as pointing at the elements just recited, while a bare *them* invites an examiner's rejection for indefiniteness. That is a drafting convention rather than a rule of grammar, and modern claim-drafting guides increasingly recommend the plain forms anyway. Note that *therebetween* needs a pair: ✗ *a bore formed therebetween* with one element recited is meaningless. Its relatives in this file are the ordinary compounds [therein](#therein), [thereon](#thereon), and [thereunder](#thereunder), which are built by the same rule and are simply commoner.

Between them; through it. The patent-claim compounds, built on demand from *there-* and any preposition that is needed.

1. The two plates are spaced apart, defining a channel *therebetween*.
2. The housing includes a bore extending *therethrough* to receive the drive shaft.
3. A gasket is seated *therebetween* to prevent leakage at the joint.
4. The frame carries a lens, and light passing *therethrough* is focused on the sensor.
5. The first and second rollers are journaled in the frame, with the web traveling *therebetween*.
6. The same rule on demand: a rail *therealong*, a bracket *thereacross*.
7. Plain English: *a channel between them*; *a bore through it*.

**See also:** [therein](#therein), [thereon](#thereon), [thereto](#thereto), [thereunder](#thereunder), [thereof](#thereof), [thereat](#thereat), [wherein](#wherein)

### thereby

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *thair-BY* &middot; stress on *by*, which keeps its full diphthong; unlike *therefore* it is never stressed on the first syllable  
**Русский:** тем самым, посредством этого — книжн., юр.; вполне живое в деловом языке
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *by* + *that*, a pro-form for "by that means" &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused preposition *by*) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and academic — and, alongside [whereby](#whereby), one of the two members of this family that ordinary careful American writing still uses without irony  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the **action or means** just described, not a thing — what precedes it is normally a whole clause (*she paid the filing fee, thereby preserving the claim*). It cannot point at a person, and it cannot point forward.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [clause], *thereby* [present participle] (*thereby avoiding the penalty*) &middot; [subject] *thereby* [verb] &middot; *and thereby* [verb] &middot; *thereby hangs a tale* (frozen)  
**Contrast:** the pair to keep straight is *thereby* against ***therefore***. *Thereby* is **by that means** — it names the mechanism (*he signed the waiver, thereby giving up the appeal*). *Therefore* is **for that reason** — it names the consequence (*the waiver was signed; therefore no appeal lies*). Substitute the long forms to test which you want. Against the rest of its family, *thereby* points back at an act, [hereby](#hereby) performs one, and [whereby](#whereby) introduces a relative clause about a means. One caution of style: because *thereby* so often attaches to a participle, it collects dangling modifiers — *the fee was paid, thereby preserving the claim* leaves the participle with nothing to hang on, and a careful writer recasts it as *paying the fee preserved the claim*. Finally, the fixed *thereby hangs a tale*, Shakespeare's phrase, means "there is a story attached to that," and is the only place most Americans use the word in speech.

By that means; as a result of that.

1. "And *thereby* hangs a tale." (*The Taming of the Shrew*; Shakespeare uses the phrase more than once)
2. She filed the amended complaint within the window and *thereby* preserved the claim.
3. The statute exempts small estates from probate, *thereby* saving families several months.
4. He signed the release without reading it and *thereby* gave up the right to sue.
5. The company moved the servers offshore, *thereby* placing them outside the court's reach.
6. Compare *therefore*: the release was signed; **therefore** no suit lies. (reason, not means)

**See also:** [whereby](#whereby), [hereby](#hereby), [thereof](#thereof), [therewith](#therewith), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### therefor

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈfɔr/ &middot; *thair-FOR* &middot; stress on the **second** syllable — which is the entire difference in speech between this word and *therefore* /ˈðɛrfɔr/ &middot; *THAIR-for*  
**Русский:** за это, взамен этого — юр.; не путать с therefore «поэтому» (ударение на 2-м слоге)
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *for* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "for it" or "in exchange for that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal; outside legal drafting it is read as a typo for *therefore*, and copy editors "correct" it constantly  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; stands for singular or plural alike.  
**Refers to:** a thing, sum, service, or right already named, as the thing something is given **for** — most often in exchange (*the goods and the price therefor*) or in purpose (*the application and the fee therefor*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the noun it modifies keeps its own (*the consideration therefor **is** ten dollars*).  
**Pattern:** *the price therefor* &middot; *the consideration therefor* &middot; *the fee therefor* &middot; *in payment therefor* &middot; *and shall be liable therefor* &middot; *the reasons therefor*  
**Contrast:** this is the one entry in the family where a missing letter changes the word. ***Therefor*** (no final *e*, stressed on the second syllable) means "**for it**" or "**for that**." ***Therefore*** (with the *e*, stressed on the first) means "**consequently**." *The Buyer shall pay the price therefor* is the amount owed for the goods; *the Buyer shall therefore pay* is a conclusion drawn from something above. American legal drafting keeps both, ordinary writing keeps only *therefore*, and because the shorter spelling looks like an error, a drafter who wants *therefor* should expect to defend it — which is a good reason to write *for them* instead. The parallel members are [thereof](#thereof) ("of it") and [therewith](#therewith) ("with it").

For it; for that; in exchange for that.

1. The Seller shall deliver the goods, and the Buyer shall pay the price *therefor* within thirty days.
2. The application must be filed with the fee *therefor* in the amount of ninety dollars.
3. Any officer who exceeds this authority is personally liable *therefor*.
4. The board denied the request and stated its reasons *therefor* in writing.
5. The lot was conveyed for ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration, receipt *therefor* acknowledged.
6. Not the same word: the fee was not paid; ***therefore*** the application was rejected.

**See also:** [thereof](#thereof), [therewith](#therewith), [therefrom](#therefrom), [thereby](#thereby), [whereof](#whereof)

### therefrom

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈfrʌm/ &middot; *thair-FRUM* &middot; stress on *from*, which keeps its full vowel instead of reducing to /frəm/  
**Русский:** из этого, оттуда — юр., книжн.; «от того, из названного источника»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *from* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "from it" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal; heavy even by the standards of this family  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; stands for singular or plural.  
**Refers to:** the source-like noun phrase most recently named — a fund, a property, a sale, a trust, a document — as the thing something comes **out of**. Things and abstractions, not people.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the noun it modifies keeps its own (*the proceeds therefrom **are** taxable*, *the income therefrom **is** payable quarterly*).  
**Pattern:** *the income therefrom* &middot; *the proceeds derived therefrom* &middot; *any rents and profits therefrom* &middot; *excepting therefrom* (the conveyancing formula that carves a piece out of a described parcel) &middot; *arising therefrom*  
**Contrast:** the everyday equivalent is *from it* or *from them*, and *therefrom* is worth knowing mainly because of one formula: ***excepting therefrom***, which in a deed removes a described strip from a described parcel and is therefore the sentence that decides where your property line is. Distinguish it from [thereof](#thereof) ("of it"), which marks possession or portion rather than origin — *the proceeds **thereof*** and *the proceeds **therefrom*** are both used, but *thereof* attaches the proceeds to the thing while *therefrom* traces them out of it. And do not use it for people: ✗*the witness and the statement therefrom* should be *her statement*.

From it; from that; out of that.

1. The trustee shall hold the fund and pay the income *therefrom* to the beneficiary quarterly.
2. Excepting *therefrom* the westerly twenty feet conveyed to the county for road purposes. (deed language)
3. Any claim arising *therefrom* must be brought within one year.
4. The company sold the division and applied the proceeds *therefrom* to its debt.
5. The report and the conclusions drawn *therefrom* were published in the same week.
6. Plain English: *the trustee pays the income **from it** to the beneficiary.*

**See also:** [thereof](#thereof), [thereto](#thereto), [therein](#therein), [therefor](#therefor), [whence](#whence)

### therein

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈɪn/ &middot; *thair-IN* &middot; stress on *in*  
**Русский:** в нём, там — юр., книжн.; «внутри того, что названо» (о вещах, не о людях)
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *in* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "in that thing" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal, formal, and literary  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; the thing it stands for may be singular or plural.  
**Refers to:** the container-like noun phrase most recently named — a place, a building, a document, a pit, a fund. Things, not people.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [noun] *and everything contained therein* &middot; *the property and the improvements therein* &middot; *therein lies* [noun phrase] &middot; *described therein*  
**Contrast:** *therein* is "in that," [herein](#herein) is "in this document," [wherein](#wherein) is "in which." The idiom *therein lies the problem* is the one place the word survives in ordinary American speech, and it is fixed in that inverted order — *therein lies*, not ✗*therein the problem lies*. In a lease, watch the pair carefully: *the premises and all fixtures **therein*** means the fixtures inside the building; *all fixtures **herein*** would mean fixtures inside the lease document.

In it; in that place or thing.

1. "Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall *therein*." (Proverbs 26:27, King James Version)
2. The house and everything contained *therein* passed to his daughter.
3. *Therein* lies the difficulty: nobody wants to pay for the survey.
4. The report is attached, and the figures cited *therein* have been audited.
5. The tenant may use the parking structure and the spaces designated *therein*.
6. She bought the old bookshop and every volume *therein*.

**See also:** [thereof](#thereof), [thereon](#thereon), [thereto](#thereto), [herein](#herein), [wherein](#wherein)

### thereinafter, thereinbefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrɪnˈæftɚ/, /ˌðɛrɪnbɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *thair-in-AF-ter*, *thair-in-bee-FOR* &middot; four syllables each, stress on *af* and on *for*; the older /ˌðɛrɪnˈɑftɚ/ turns up in British-influenced drafting, exactly as it does in *hereinafter*  
**Русский:** далее / ранее в том документе — юр.; о тексте другого, уже названного документа
**Type:** pronominal adverbs — *after* and *before* welded onto *therein*, pro-forms for "later in" and "earlier in **that** document" &middot; **Variants:** ***thereinabove*** and ***thereinbelow*** are built on the same pattern and are rarer still; they mean what *thereinbefore* and *thereinafter* mean &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal; rare, and correct only where the writer is pointing at a document other than the one in hand — most often a judge construing an instrument quoted in the opinion  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** a part of a **document already named** — a will, a deed, a policy, a statute under construction — before or after the point already discussed. It points at **text** in another instrument, never at time, and never at the document the writer is drafting. That last restriction is the entire content of the entry.  
**Agreement:** none of their own.  
**Pattern:** *the beneficiary thereinbefore named* &middot; *as thereinafter provided* &middot; *the covenants thereinbefore recited* &middot; *the schedule thereinafter set out* &middot; *the trust deed and the amendments thereinafter described*  
**Contrast:** the *here-* / *there-* split is the whole point and it is substantive, not stylistic: [hereinafter](#hereinafter) means "later in **this** instrument," *thereinafter* means "later in **that** one." A drafter who writes *thereinafter* about the contract being drafted has pointed at nothing, and a court reading the clause has to guess. The word an American is far likelier to meet is *thereafter*, which is about **time** ("after that moment") and is ordinary English; the one-syllable difference between *thereafter* and *thereinafter* is the same trap that separates *hereafter* from *hereinafter*, and in an instrument the slip changes the meaning. Against [the foregoing](#the-foregoing), the difference is grammatical: *the foregoing* is a noun phrase that can be a subject, while these are adverbs that can only modify. Plain-language drafting writes *earlier in the deed* and *later in the policy*.

Later in that document; earlier in that document. The *there-* mates of *hereinafter* and *hereinbefore*, pointing at some other instrument.

1. The 1974 trust deed, and the amendments *thereinafter* set out, govern the distribution.
2. The court quoted the policy and the exclusions *thereinbefore* recited.
3. The lease refers to a schedule of fixtures, described *thereinafter* in Part III.
4. The residuary clause revoked the gift to the beneficiary *thereinbefore* named.
5. The statute defines "vehicle" in section 2 and uses the term *thereinafter* without qualification.
6. The error to avoid: a contract that says *as thereinafter provided* about **itself**, where it means [hereinafter](#hereinafter).
7. Plain-language rewrite: *later in that deed*; *earlier in the policy*.

### thereof

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈʌv/ &middot; *thair-UV* &middot; stress on *of*, with a full vowel  
**Русский:** этого, его, их — юр.; род. п. к названному: the terms thereof «его условия»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *of* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "of it," "of them," "of that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal; the commonest member of the family  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; stands for singular or plural alike (*the parts thereof* = of it or of them).  
**Refers to:** the noun phrase most recently named — a right, a term, a property, a law, a sum. Things and abstractions; not people.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb agrees with whatever noun *thereof* modifies (*any portion **thereof** **is** forfeited*, *all copies **thereof** **are** destroyed*).  
**Pattern:** *any part thereof* &middot; *the whole thereof* &middot; *the term thereof* &middot; *the exercise thereof* &middot; *or any portion thereof* &middot; *and the like thereof*  
**Contrast:** *thereof* replaces *of it* or *of them* and points **backward** to a noun already in the text, while [hereof](#hereof) points at this document. The everyday equivalent is simply *its* or *of it*: *the free exercise **thereof*** = *its free exercise*. It is not archaic in the way *thou* is — it is fully productive in legal English today — but outside that register it is pure jargon, and the fixed joke *or lack thereof* is the only form most Americans use in speech.

Of it; of them; of that.

1. "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise *thereof*." (First Amendment)
2. The lease may be terminated upon breach of any provision *thereof*.
3. The lot and any structures erected on any portion *thereof* are covered.
4. We discussed her qualifications, or the lack *thereof*, for most of the meeting.
5. The committee reviewed the proposal and rejected the greater part *thereof*.
6. Each shareholder shall be notified of the meeting and of the purpose *thereof*.

### thereon

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈɑn/ &middot; *thair-AWN* &middot; also /ðɛrˈɔn/; stress on *on*  
**Русский:** на нём, на это — юр.; о земле, участке, основной сумме, поверхности
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *on* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "on it" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal, chiefly in real-estate and lending documents; the variant *thereupon* is more literary  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** a surface-like or base-like noun phrase already named — land, a parcel, a principal sum, a structure. Things only.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *the land and all improvements thereon* &middot; *the principal and interest thereon* &middot; *the building erected thereon* &middot; *the note and all sums due thereon*  
**Contrast:** *thereon* is "on it," [therein](#therein) is "in it," [thereto](#thereto) is "to it" — the family differs only in which preposition got fused, and choosing the wrong one produces nonsense rather than mere clumsiness. *Thereupon* is the same word with the heavier preposition, and it has drifted: as a pro-form it still means "on that" (*the table and the lamp thereupon*), but its ordinary modern sense is temporal, "immediately after that" (*he read the letter and thereupon resigned*), which is an adverb of time, not a pronominal.

On it; on that.

1. The deed conveys the land and all improvements *thereon*.
2. Interest accrues on the principal balance and on any late charges assessed *thereon*.
3. The parcel and the barn standing *thereon* were sold as one lot.
4. The buyer accepts the lot and any encumbrances recorded *thereon*.
5. He set the tray down and arranged the glasses *thereon*.
6. The note is due in full, together with all interest earned *thereon*.

### thereto

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈtu/ &middot; *thair-TOO* &middot; stress on *to*  
**Русский:** к нему, к этому — юр.; о приложениях, дополнениях, сторонах договора
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *to* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "to it" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** a document, agreement, or thing already named, as the target of attaching, amending, adding, or being a party.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; a subject it modifies keeps its own agreement (*the amendments thereto **are** attached*).  
**Pattern:** *and all amendments thereto* &middot; *the parties thereto* &middot; *exhibits thereto* &middot; *any supplement thereto* &middot; *related thereto*  
**Contrast:** [hereto](#hereto) attaches to **this** document, *thereto* to one already mentioned: *the Master Agreement and all amendments **thereto***. Confusing the two reverses the reference and, in a contract, changes what is actually being incorporated. In everyday English the phrase is simply *to it*, and the plain-language rewrite of *the parties thereto* is *the parties to that agreement*.

To it; to that.

1. The Master Agreement and all amendments *thereto* remain in force.
2. The policy, together with any endorsements *thereto*, must be produced on request.
3. The parties *thereto* have already performed most of their obligations.
4. Any exhibits *thereto* are incorporated by this reference.
5. She reviewed the complaint and every document related *thereto*.
6. The proposal, and any objections *thereto*, will be heard on Tuesday.

**See also:** [hereto](#hereto), [thereof](#thereof), [therein](#therein), [thereon](#thereon)

### thereunder

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈʌndɚ/ &middot; *thair-UN-der* &middot; stress on *un*  
**Русский:** по нему, на основании его — юр.; о договоре или законе как источнике прав
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *under* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "under that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal and formal, chiefly where one document or statute sits beneath another  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** in its main sense, the **agreement, statute, or regulation just named**, treated as the authority under which a right or duty arises (*the Act and the regulations promulgated thereunder*). In a secondary and much rarer sense it is spatial — the thing physically beneath what was just mentioned (*the deck and the crawlspace thereunder*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the noun it modifies keeps its own (*the rights thereunder **are** assignable*).  
**Pattern:** *the Act and the rules thereunder* &middot; *rights and obligations thereunder* &middot; *any default thereunder* &middot; *licensed thereunder* &middot; *the Master Agreement and each order thereunder*  
**Contrast:** [hereunder](#hereunder) is "under **this** agreement," *thereunder* is "under **that** one," and in a layered deal — a master agreement with work orders beneath it, a statute with regulations beneath it — the choice decides which instrument a right comes from. That is a substantive error, not a stylistic one. Distinguish the authority sense from the spatial sense, which is ordinary English and which [thereon](#thereon) mirrors from above (*the land and the barn thereon* / *the deck and the crawlspace thereunder*). The plain rewrite names the instrument: *under the Master Agreement*.

Under that agreement; under that statute; underneath that thing.

1. The Clean Air Act and the regulations promulgated *thereunder* govern the permit.
2. The Master Agreement remains in force, and each work order issued *thereunder* is a separate contract.
3. A default *thereunder* is a default under this Agreement as well.
4. The patent and every license granted *thereunder* passed to the acquiring company.
5. They inspected the deck and the crawlspace *thereunder*. (the spatial sense)
6. Plain-language rewrite: *the regulations issued under that Act govern the permit.*

### thereunto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrʌnˈtu/ &middot; *thair-un-TOO* &middot; stress on *to*; the middle syllable keeps its full /ʌn/, since the word survives only inside slowly read formulas  
**Русский:** к тому, к сему — устар., юр.; в формулах о принадлежностях имущества
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *unto* + *that*, a ceremonial pro-form for "to that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** archaic and legal; effectively confined to conveyancing formulas and to scriptural style  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the property, right, or office just named, as the thing something **belongs to** or is attached to.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the noun it modifies keeps its own (*the rights thereunto belonging **are** conveyed*).  
**Pattern:** *together with the appurtenances thereunto belonging* (the deed formula) &middot; *the rights and privileges thereunto appertaining* &middot; *called thereunto* &middot; *thereunto authorized* (in a signature block: *by its officer thereunto duly authorized*)  
**Contrast:** [thereto](#thereto) is the ordinary working form — *the lease and all amendments thereto* — and *thereunto* is the same meaning in ceremonial dress, built on *unto*. It survives in two places an American may actually encounter: the deed phrase *together with all and singular the tenements, hereditaments, and appurtenances thereunto belonging*, and the corporate signature block *by its officer thereunto duly authorized*, which means nothing more than "authorized to do this." Its *here-* twin is [hereunto](#hereunto), and the two are just as easy to swap as [hereto](#hereto) and [thereto](#thereto): *hereunto* attaches to **this** document, *thereunto* to something already named.

To that; belonging to that. The ceremonial twin of *thereto*, surviving in two formulas.

1. Together with all the tenements, hereditaments, and appurtenances *thereunto* belonging.
2. The office, and the salary *thereunto* attached, passed to his successor in January.
3. Executed by the Company by its officer *thereunto* duly authorized.
4. He was called *thereunto* and did not refuse.
5. The easement, and the rights of way *thereunto* appertaining, run with the land.
6. Ordinary modern drafting: *together with everything that belongs to the property.*

### thereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; *thair-uh-PAWN* &middot; also /ˌðɛrəˈpɔn/; stress on the last syllable, and the middle vowel reduces to /ə/  
**Русский:** тотчас после этого; на том — книжн.; чаще временное «вслед за тем»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *upon* + *that*, with two jobs: a pro-form for "on that" and, far more commonly, an adverb of **time** meaning "immediately after that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and narrative; the temporal sense is at home in ordinary formal prose  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** in the spatial sense, a surface-like noun phrase already named (*the table and the lamp thereupon*); in the temporal sense, the **event** just narrated — what it points at is a moment, not a thing.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [clause]; *thereupon* [clause] (temporal: *he read the letter and thereupon resigned*) &middot; [noun] *thereupon* (spatial, rare) &middot; *whereupon* is its relative counterpart — see [whereupon](#whereupon)  
**Contrast:** [thereon](#thereon) is the everyday legal form for "on it" — *the land and all improvements thereon* — and *thereupon* is the heavier word that has drifted away from that job into **time**. In present-day American writing, *thereupon* nearly always means "at once, right after that," and reading it as "on that" in a modern text will usually be wrong. Its relative counterpart is [whereupon](#whereupon) ("immediately after which"), which does the same work inside a relative clause, and its *here-* mate would be *hereupon*, which is dead. Do not confuse the temporal *thereupon* with [thereby](#thereby): *thereupon* says **when**, *thereby* says **how**.

Immediately after that; on that. The *there-* compound that drifted out of place and into time, and now nearly always says *when* rather than *where*.

**1. Immediately after that; at once (adverb of time — the living sense).** *He read the letter and thereupon resigned.* Ordinary formal English, common in narrative and in judicial opinions.

**2. On that; upon that thing (pronominal, archaic).** *The table and the lamp thereupon.* The job [thereon](#thereon) took over, and rare in modern text.

1. He read the letter through twice and *thereupon* resigned.
2. The court found the notice defective and *thereupon* dismissed the petition.
3. The clerk called the case; the parties *thereupon* came forward.
4. She learned that the buyer had withdrawn and *thereupon* relisted the house.
5. The altar, and the offering laid *thereupon*, were consumed. (the older spatial sense)
6. Compare: *the land and the buildings **thereon*** — the everyday legal form for "on it."

### therewith

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈwɪθ/ &middot; *thair-WITH* &middot; also /ðɛrˈwɪð/ with the voiced *th*; stress on *with*  
**Русский:** с этим, посредством этого — юр., книжн.; «вместе с тем, что названо»
**Type:** pronominal adverb — *with* + *it*/*that*, a pro-form for "with it" or "with that" &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal, formal, and literary  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; stands for singular or plural.  
**Refers to:** the thing just named, as the thing something goes **along with** or is done **by means of** — a document, a payment, an instrument, a tool. Things and abstractions, not people.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *and all rights connected therewith* &middot; *in connection therewith* &middot; *together therewith* &middot; *dealt therewith* &middot; *therewithal* (the reinforced literary form)  
**Contrast:** [herewith](#herewith) is "with **this** letter," *therewith* is "with **that** thing already named," and the swap turns a transmittal sentence into a reference to something else entirely. The commonest living phrase is *in connection therewith*, which every plain-language guide rewrites as *in connection with it* or, better, by naming the thing. The literary form *therewithal* adds nothing but weight, and *therewith* in narrative can also carry a faint temporal sense — "with that, and at that moment" (*therewith he turned and left*), where it shades into [thereupon](#thereupon). Keep all of it apart from *thereof* ("of it"), which marks portion or possession rather than accompaniment.

With it; with that; along with that.

1. The Company shall pay all filing fees and any expenses incurred in connection *therewith*.
2. The deed, and the survey delivered *therewith*, were both recorded on Tuesday.
3. He handed her the key and *therewith* the deed to the cabin.
4. The approved site plan, and the permit issued *therewith*, must both be posted on the fence.
5. *Therewith* he turned on his heel and left the room. (the narrative use, shading into *thereupon*)
6. Plain English: *the Company pays the filing fees and any related expenses.*

### whereat

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæt/ &middot; *wair-AT* &middot; also /hwɛrˈæt/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; stress on *at*  
**Русский:** на что, после чего — устар., книжн.; вводит реакцию на сказанное или увиденное
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronominal adverb — *at* + *which*/*what* &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused *at*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; the rarest of the surviving *where-* forms  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent naming an **event, sight, or statement** that provokes a reaction — *whereat* introduces the reaction (*he named the price, whereat she laughed*). Things and happenings, never people (for people, *at whom*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb in the relative clause agrees with that clause's own subject.  
**Pattern:** [clause], *whereat* [subject] [verb] (*whereat the crowd cheered*) &middot; [noun] *whereat* [clause] &middot; *whereat* as a question word (*whereat do you marvel?*, thoroughly bookish)  
**Contrast:** *whereat* and [whereupon](#whereupon) both hang a reaction onto what came before, and they are not quite the same. *Whereupon* is about **sequence** — "immediately after which," and it reports what happened next. *Whereat* is about **cause or provocation** — "at which," and it reports a response to a stimulus, which is why it so often precedes verbs of emotion: *whereat she laughed*, *whereat the crowd jeered*, *whereat he took offense*. In present-day American English both are formal and *whereat* is genuinely archaic; the plain versions are *at which point*, *and then*, and simply *and*. Do not confuse it with the dialectal question *where at?* (*where's he at?*), two separate words, which is a different construction entirely.

At which; at what. The reaction-marking member of the *where-* family.

1. He named a price three times the estimate, *whereat* she laughed out loud.
2. The judge read the verdict, *whereat* the gallery erupted.
3. She let slip that the money had already been spent, *whereat* the meeting fell silent.
4. He was passed over a second time, *whereat* he began to look for another job.
5. *Whereat* do you marvel — that the train was late, or that it came at all? (the bookish question use)
6. Modern English: *He named a price three times the estimate, **at which point** she laughed.*

### whereby

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *wair-BY* &middot; also /hwɛrˈbaɪ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; stress on *by*, which keeps its full diphthong  
**Русский:** посредством чего, согласно которому — книжн. и офиц.; вполне живое
**Type:** relative pronominal adverb — *by* + *which* &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused preposition *by*) &middot; **Register:** formal and legal — and, alone in this family, **fully standard** in ordinary careful American writing  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. No plural, no possessive.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent noun phrase naming a **means or mechanism** — a system, an arrangement, a procedure, a method, a rule, a device. Things and abstractions only; for people the phrase is *by whom* (*the clerk **by whom** the form was filed*), never ✗*the clerk whereby*.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb inside the relative clause agrees with that clause's own subject — *an arrangement whereby the tenant **pays** the taxes* (singular *tenant*), *rules whereby claims **are** settled in thirty days* (plural *claims*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *whereby* [clause] &middot; *a system whereby* &middot; *an arrangement whereby* &middot; *a procedure whereby* &middot; *a means whereby* &middot; *no mechanism whereby* [clause]  
**Contrast:** *whereby* = *by which*, the relative counterpart of *thereby* ("by that means") and *hereby* ("by this act"). It is the one member of the *where-* family a present-day American writer can use without sounding like a deed: *a system whereby every claim is reviewed twice* is ordinary formal English and no editor will touch it, while [wherein](#wherein) and [whereof](#whereof) in the same sentence would read as costume. Two limits are worth holding onto. It takes **inanimate** antecedents only, as above. And it means *by which* and nothing else — ✗*the year whereby the company failed* is a misuse for *in which* ([wherein](#wherein)) or plain *when*, and ✗*the fund whereby the money was taken* should be *from which*. The plainest rewrite is usually to drop the pro-form altogether: *a system that reviews every claim twice*.

By which; by means of which.

1. The contract sets up an arrangement *whereby* the tenant pays the property taxes directly.
2. They proposed a system *whereby* every claim is reviewed by two adjusters before payment.
3. There is no mechanism *whereby* a member can be expelled without a hearing. (after a negated noun phrase)
4. The statute created a procedure *whereby* small estates skip probate entirely.
5. She explained the method *whereby* the samples are kept sterile in transit.
6. Standard plain-English rewrite: a system **in which** every claim is reviewed twice — or simply *a system that reviews every claim twice*.

### wherein

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɪn/ &middot; *wair-IN* &middot; also /hwɛrˈɪn/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; stress on *in*  
**Русский:** в чём, в котором — книжн., юр.; о месте, документе, положении дел, не о людях
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronominal adverb — *in* + *which*/*what* &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused *in*) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, and literary  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent noun phrase that can contain something — a place, a document, a situation, a state of affairs. Things and abstractions, not people (for people, *in whom*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb inside the relative clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*a system wherein every vote **counts***).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *wherein* [clause] (relative) &middot; *wherein lies* [noun phrase] &middot; *Wherein* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (interrogative: *wherein does the difference lie?*)  
**Contrast:** *wherein* = *in which*, so it is the relative counterpart of [therein](#therein) (*in that*) and [herein](#herein) (*in this*). Modern English writes *in which*, and the plainest version often drops the preposition entirely: *a system **in which** every vote counts*, or *a system where every vote counts*. As a question word, *wherein* means "in what respect" and is now thoroughly bookish — *wherein does the difference lie?* for *what exactly is the difference?*

In which; in what.

1. He described a system *wherein* every vote is counted twice before certification.
2. The paragraph *wherein* the warranty is disclaimed appears in capital letters.
3. *Wherein* lies the difference between the two proposals?
4. That was the year *wherein* the company nearly failed.
5. She wrote a memoir *wherein* almost nobody is named directly.
6. The court identified the respect *wherein* the statute is unconstitutional.

### whereof

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈʌv/ &middot; *wair-UV* &middot; also /hwɛrˈʌv/; stress on *of*  
**Русский:** о чём, чего — юр., книжн.; in witness whereof «в удостоверение чего»
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronominal adverb — *of* + *which*/*what* &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** legal, literary, and archaic  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent noun phrase — a fact, a matter, a document, a subject of knowledge. Things and abstractions.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *In witness whereof* [clause] &middot; *know whereof* [subject] [verb] (*he knows whereof he speaks*) &middot; [noun] *whereof* [clause] &middot; *the matter whereof he complains*  
**Contrast:** *whereof* = *of which*, the relative counterpart of [thereof](#thereof) (*of it*) and [hereof](#hereof) (*of this*). Two idioms keep it alive in American English: **In witness whereof**, the closing formula of nearly every executed contract, and **to know whereof one speaks**, meaning to speak from real knowledge — which is fixed in that order and cannot be modernized inside the idiom (✗*he knows of which he speaks*). Everywhere else, write *of which*, or recast: *a matter he knows nothing about*.

Of which; of what.

1. *In witness whereof*, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
2. He worked on the assembly line for thirty years, so he knows *whereof* he speaks.
3. The charges *whereof* the defendant stands accused are set out in the indictment.
4. She spoke of a shortage *whereof* the rest of us had heard nothing.
5. *Whereof* do you accuse him, exactly?
6. This is the treaty *whereof* the ambassador made so much in his speech.

### whereto

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈtu/ &middot; *wair-TOO* &middot; also /hwɛrˈtu/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; stress on *to*  
**Русский:** к чему, куда — устар., юр.; о цели или назначении
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronominal adverb — *to* + *which*/*what* &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused *to*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and legal; rare even in contracts, where *to which* has almost entirely replaced it  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent noun phrase treated as a **destination or an end** — a purpose, a fund, an office, a place. Things and abstractions; for people, *to whom*.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb inside the relative clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*the purposes whereto the fund **is** dedicated*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *whereto* [clause] &middot; *the purposes whereto* &middot; *the office whereto he was appointed* &middot; *Whereto?* (archaic question: "to what place?")  
**Contrast:** *whereto* = *to which*, and it is the relative counterpart of [hereto](#hereto) ("to this") and [thereto](#thereto) ("to that"). Two neighbors are easy to confuse with it. [Whither](#whither) also means "to where," but it is about **physical motion** — *whither goest thou* — while *whereto* is about attachment, purpose, and destination in the abstract. And *whereto* is not *whereat*: one is direction, the other is reaction. In present-day American English write *to which*, or recast to lose the pro-form entirely: *the fund's purposes*, *the office he was appointed to*.

To which; to what.

1. The trust names the charitable purposes *whereto* the income is dedicated.
2. The office *whereto* she was appointed carries no salary.
3. This is the agreement *whereto* the amendments of last spring were attached.
4. He never disclosed the account *whereto* the funds were transferred.
5. *Whereto* does this road lead, if not to the river? (the archaic question use)
6. Modern English: *the purposes **to which** the income is dedicated.*

**See also:** [hereto](#hereto), [thereto](#thereto), [whereof](#whereof), [wherein](#wherein), [whither](#whither), [to whom, for whom, with whom](05-relative.md#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom)

### whereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; *wair-uh-PAWN* &middot; also /ˌwɛrəˈpɔn/ and /ˌhwɛr-/; stress on the last syllable, with the middle vowel reduced to /ə/  
**Русский:** после чего, вслед за чем — книжн., повеств.; вполне живое
**Type:** relative pronominal adverb with two jobs — an adverb of **sequence** meaning "immediately after which," and, archaically, a pro-form for "on which" &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused *upon*) &middot; **Register:** formal and narrative; the sequence use is ordinary formal American English and needs no apology  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the **event or statement just narrated**, taken as the moment the next thing follows. It never points at a person, and in the modern sense it does not point at a surface either — its antecedent is a whole clause.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the clause it introduces keeps its own subject and verb (*whereupon the crowd **dispersed***).  
**Pattern:** [clause], *whereupon* [subject] [verb] &middot; [clause], *whereupon* [clause] (always second, never first in its sentence) &middot; the archaic spatial use, [noun] *whereupon* (*the stone whereupon he sat*)  
**Contrast:** *whereupon* says **what happened next**; [whereat](#whereat) says **what it provoked**; [whereby](#whereby) says **by what means**. All three attach a clause to what precedes, and only the first is common today. Its *there-* counterpart is [thereupon](#thereupon), which does the same job without a relative clause — *he read the letter and thereupon resigned* / *he read the letter, whereupon he resigned* — and the choice between them is the ordinary choice between an adverb and a relative. The plainest rewrites are *and then*, *at which point*, and simply *and*. Note that *whereupon* must follow the clause it attaches to; it cannot open a sentence on its own, and it takes a comma before it rather than after.

Immediately after which; at which point. Also, archaically, *on which*.

1. The clerk read out the verdict, *whereupon* the gallery emptied in about a minute.
2. She asked whether anyone objected, *whereupon* three hands went up.
3. The engine coughed twice, *whereupon* the pilot turned back toward the field.
4. He was told the shop had closed at six, *whereupon* he sat down on the curb to wait for morning.
5. This is the stone *whereupon* the first oath was sworn. (the archaic spatial use)
6. Plain English: *the clerk read the verdict, **and then** the gallery emptied.*

**See also:** [whereat](#whereat), [whereby](#whereby), [thereupon](#thereupon), [thereon](#thereon), [wherein](#wherein)

### wherewith

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈwɪθ/ &middot; *wair-WITH* &middot; also /wɛrˈwɪð/ and /hwɛr-/; stress on *with*  
**Русский:** чем, посредством чего — устар., библ.; о средстве (ср. живое wherewithal)
**Type:** relative and interrogative pronominal adverb — *with* + *which*/*what* &middot; **Case:** object (governed by the fused *with*) &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, and literary; dead in modern American prose, but its derived noun *wherewithal* is fully alive  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** an antecedent naming a **means or instrument** — money, a tool, a substance, an argument. Things only; for people, *with whom*.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb inside the clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*the salt wherewith the meat **is** cured*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *wherewith* [subject] [verb] (*the means wherewith he lived*) &middot; *wherewith* as a question word (*wherewith shall I come before the Lord?*) &middot; *wherewith to* [verb] (*nothing wherewith to pay*) &middot; the derived noun, *the wherewithal*  
**Contrast:** *wherewith* = *with which*, the relative mate of [herewith](#herewith) ("with this") and [therewith](#therewith) ("with that"), and it overlaps heavily with [whereby](#whereby) — the difference is that *whereby* names a **method** (*a system whereby claims are reviewed*) while *wherewith* names an **instrument or resource** (*the money wherewith the claims are paid*). Modern English writes *with which*, or recasts. The word that survives is the noun ***wherewithal***, meaning the means — usually the money — to do something: *he lacked the wherewithal to fight it in court*. That noun is ordinary American English at every level, and it is the only reason most Americans recognize the root at all.

With which; with what. The instrument-naming member of the *where-* family, alive today mainly through the noun *wherewithal*.

1. "*Wherewith* shall I come before the Lord?" (Micah 6:6, King James Version)
2. "If the salt have lost his savour, *wherewith* shall it be salted?" (Matthew 5:13)
3. The estate left him a name and a title, but nothing *wherewith* to keep either up.
4. She had neither the tools *wherewith* to repair it nor anyone to ask.
5. The fund out of which the claims are paid, and *wherewith* the reserve is maintained, is described in Article VI.
6. The living descendant: he simply did not have the ***wherewithal*** to fight it in court.

**See also:** [herewith](#herewith), [therewith](#therewith), [whereby](#whereby), [whereof](#whereof), [wherein](#wherein)

## The place pro-forms: *hither*, *thither*, *whither*

The *here-* / *there-* / *where-* deixis of the last section runs through one more set of words, and this one is older and simpler than the legal compounds. English used to mark **direction** on its place words, three ways: a form for being somewhere, a form for going toward it, and a form for coming from it. The whole system fits in a box:

| | Position — *in* or *at* | Motion **toward** — *to* | Motion **from** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **near the speaker** | here | *hither* | *hence* |
| **away from the speaker** | there | *thither* | *thence* |
| **which / what place** | where | *whither* | *whence* |

Modern English collapsed the whole right-hand side into the left: we say *come here*, *go there*, *where are you going*, and let the verb carry the direction. Nothing was lost that a preposition cannot supply, which is why the six words died — but they died slowly and unevenly, and an American still meets every one of them. *Hence* is alive in a wholly different job, meaning "therefore." *Thence* is alive in surveying and in itineraries. *Whence* is alive in one psalm and in the phrase *whence it came*. *Hither* is alive inside *come-hither*, and *hither and yon*; *thither* inside *hither and thither*; *whither* inside a headline formula (*Whither the euro?*) and in the wedding reading from Ruth.

They are **pro-adverbs**, not pronouns in the strict sense — they stand in for a prepositional phrase (*to that place*) rather than for a noun phrase — and they are grouped here because the deixis, the register, and the *wh-* / *th-* alternation are the same as in the rest of the file. Each is **archaic** unless the entry says otherwise.

### hence

**Pronunciation:** /hɛns/ &middot; *HENSS* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *fence*; no weak form  
**Русский:** отсюда; отныне; следовательно — «поэтому» живо, «отсюда» устар., «через (год)» книжн.
**Type:** pro-adverb with three jobs — motion **from here** (archaic), **from now** (formal), and **therefore** (a conjunctive adverb, and the only living use) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** the inferential sense is ordinary formal American English; the place sense is archaic; the time sense is formal and slightly literary  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** in the place sense, the location of the speaker ("from this place"); in the time sense, the moment of speaking ("from now"); in the inferential sense, the **statement just made**, which is the sense in which it still stands in for something — *hence the delay* means "because of what I have just told you, the delay."  
**Agreement:** none of its own. In the inferential use it very often heads a **verbless** phrase — *hence the confusion*, *hence the new forms* — which is standard and not an error, though a following clause needs its own verb (*hence we were late*).  
**Pattern:** *get thee hence* (archaic, place) &middot; [number] [time noun] *hence* (*three years hence*) &middot; *hence* [noun phrase] (verbless inference: *hence the delay*) &middot; *hence* [clause] (*hence the committee met twice*) &middot; *henceforth*, *henceforward* (from now on)  
**Contrast:** the three senses are one word only historically, and only the third is live: **hence** = *therefore* is what an American writes today, and it is fully standard. Against its neighbors, *hence* is "from **here**," [thence](#thence) is "from **there**," and [whence](#whence) is "from **where**" — and all three already contain *from*, which is why *from hence* is strictly redundant, even though the King James Bible and Shakespeare both use it. Do not confuse *hence* with *whence*: ✗*the country hence he came* should be *whence he came*. And keep the inferential *hence* apart from *thus* and *therefore*, which are its ordinary synonyms; *hence* is the shortest and the most comfortable in front of a bare noun phrase.

From this place; from this time; therefore. The three-way pro-adverb whose only living American sense is the third, *hence the delay*.

**1. From this place (archaic).** *Get thee hence.* Dead outside quotation, though *henceforth* keeps the shape alive.

**2. From this time; from now (formal).** *Three years hence*, *a week hence*. Alive in formal and literary writing, always after the time expression.

**3. Therefore; for that reason (standard modern English).** *Hence the delay.* The living sense, and the only one an American writes on purpose.

1. "Get thee *hence*, Satan." (Matthew 4:10, King James Version — the place sense)
2. The option expires three years *hence*, on the anniversary of closing. (time)
3. The parts were held at customs; *hence* the delay. (inference, verbless — the ordinary modern use)
4. The rules changed in March, and *hence* the forms you were given last year are worthless.
5. He was ordered *hence* and did not set foot in the city again. (archaic place sense)
6. *Henceforth* the office will close at four on Fridays. (the surviving compound)

**See also:** [thence](#thence), [whence](#whence), [hither](#hither), [thereby](#thereby), [heretofore](#heretofore), [herein](#herein)

### hither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɪðɚ/ &middot; *HITH-er* &middot; stress on the first syllable, voiced *th* as in *this*; no weak form  
**Русский:** сюда — устар., книжн.; движение к говорящему; hither and yon «туда-сюда»
**Type:** pro-adverb of **motion toward the speaker** — "to this place" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; survives for everyone inside two fixed phrases, *come-hither* and *hither and yon*  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the place where the speaker is, as a **destination**. It is the directional mate of *here*, and it can only be used with verbs of motion — *come hither*, *bring it hither*, never ✗*stand hither*.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *come hither* &middot; [verb of motion] *hither* (*bring hither*, *send hither*, *drew hither*) &middot; *hither and yon* / *hither and thither* (the fixed pairs, meaning "in every direction") &middot; *come-hither* as a compound adjective (*a come-hither look*)  
**Contrast:** *hither* is "**to** here" and *here* is "**at** here," and modern English uses *here* for both — *come here* does the whole job, because *come* already supplies the direction. That redundancy is what killed the word. Its mates are [thither](#thither) ("to there") and [whither](#whither) ("to where"); its opposite in direction is [hence](#hence) ("from here"). The one place it is genuinely alive is the compound adjective ***come-hither***, describing a look or a manner that invites — a phrase most Americans use without ever noticing that they are speaking Middle English. In *hither and yon* and *hither and thither*, the pair has stopped meaning direction at all and simply means "all over the place."

To this place; to here. The archaic directional mate of *here*, alive inside *come-hither*.

1. Come *hither*, boy, and let me look at you.
2. Bring the lamp *hither* — I can't read a word of this.
3. They were driven *hither* by the storm and stayed the winter.
4. She sent messengers *hither* and thither, and none of them came back with anything.
5. He gave her a *come-hither* look over the top of the menu. (the living compound)
6. Modern English: *Come here*; *bring the lamp over here.*

**See also:** [thither](#thither), [whither](#whither), [hence](#hence), [yonder](04-demonstrative.md#yonder), [yon](04-demonstrative.md#yon)

### thence

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛns/ &middot; *THENSS* &middot; one syllable, voiced *th* as in *this*, rhyming with *fence*  
**Русский:** оттуда — книжн., юр.; движение от названного места (описания границ, маршруты)
**Type:** pro-adverb of **motion from a place already named** — "from there" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** formal and legal; genuinely alive in two places, land surveying and itineraries  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the place, point, or state most recently named, as the point something moves **away from**. In a metes-and-bounds description each *thence* means "from the point I just described," and the chain of them walks the boundary all the way around.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** *and thence to* [place] (*to Reykjavik and thence to Oslo*) &middot; *thence* [compass bearing] [distance] (the surveyor's chain: *thence north 40 degrees east, 100 feet*) &middot; *thence forward* &middot; *thenceforth*, *thenceforward* (from that time on)  
**Contrast:** [hence](#hence) is "from here," *thence* is "from there," [whence](#whence) is "from where" — and, like its two mates, *thence* already contains *from*, so *from thence* is redundant, however often the King James Bible writes it. The word an American is most likely to meet it beside is *thenceforth*, which shifts the meaning from place to **time** ("from that moment on"), exactly as *henceforth* does. In ordinary writing the phrase is *and from there*: *we flew to Reykjavik and from there to Oslo*. Where *thence* still earns its keep is a legal description of land, where the whole point is that each call starts precisely where the last one ended, and no plainer word does that job as compactly.

From that place; from there. The one member of the trio that ordinary documents still use.

1. *Thence* north forty degrees east, one hundred feet, to an iron pin. (metes and bounds, the live legal use)
2. We flew to Reykjavik and *thence* to Oslo on the morning flight.
3. The river rises in the hills above the town and runs *thence* to the sea.
4. He went first to Paris, and *thence*, after two hard years, to New York.
5. The evidence went to the sheriff's office and *thence* to the state laboratory.
6. *Thenceforth* the family never spoke of it again. (the time compound)

**See also:** [hence](#hence), [whence](#whence), [thither](#thither), [therefrom](#therefrom), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### thither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθɪðɚ/ &middot; *THITH-er* &middot; with the **voiceless** *th* of *thin* in most American mouths, though /ˈðɪðɚ/ with the voiced *th* of *this* is also standard; stress on the first syllable  
**Русский:** туда — устар., книжн.; движение к названному месту; hither and thither «туда-сюда»
**Type:** pro-adverb of **motion toward a place already named** — "to there" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive for everyone only inside *hither and thither*  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the place already named or pointed at, as a **destination**. Like *hither*, it goes only with verbs of motion.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [verb of motion] *thither* (*go thither*, *sent thither*, *carried thither*) &middot; *hither and thither* (the fixed pair: "in all directions") &middot; *thitherward* (rarer still)  
**Contrast:** *thither* is "**to** there" and *there* is "**at** there," and modern English uses *there* for both: *go there* is the whole of it. Its mates are [hither](#hither) ("to here") and [whither](#whither) ("to where"); its directional opposite is [thence](#thence) ("from there"). The pronunciation is worth a note of its own, because *thither* is one of a small set of English words — with *thence* and *thistle* on one side and *this* and *those* on the other — where American speakers genuinely differ on whether the *th* is voiced; both are correct, and dictionaries list both. In *hither and thither* the pair has lost its direction entirely and just means "scattered all over," which is how nearly every living American use of the word arrives.

To that place; to there. The archaic directional mate of *there*.

1. The road runs *thither*, past the mill and over the ridge.
2. He sent men *hither* and *thither* with messages, and heard nothing back for a week.
3. She looked *thither*, toward the smoke rising over the trees.
4. The whole household was carried *thither* in two wagons.
5. Papers blew *hither* and *thither* across the parking lot. (the fixed phrase, with no direction left in it)
6. Modern English: *the road goes **there***; *he sent men **in every direction***.

**See also:** [hither](#hither), [whither](#whither), [thence](#thence), [thereto](#thereto), [yonder](04-demonstrative.md#yonder)

### whence

**Pronunciation:** /wɛns/ &middot; *WENSS* &middot; also /hwɛns/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; one syllable, rhyming with *fence*  
**Русский:** откуда — устар., книжн.; о месте, источнике или причине происхождения
**Type:** relative and interrogative pro-adverb — "from what place," "from which" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in one famous psalm, in the phrase *return whence you came*, and in bookish questions  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the **origin** — the place, source, or condition something came out of. It can point at a physical place (*the town whence her family emigrated*), at an abstract source (*whence the money came*), and, in the question use, at a cause (*whence this sudden enthusiasm?*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb in its clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*the hills, whence **cometh** my help*).  
**Pattern:** [noun] *whence* [clause] (relative: *the country whence he came*) &middot; *return whence* [subject] [verb] (*return whence you came*) &middot; *Whence* [verb] [subject]? (question: *whence comes this rumor?*) &middot; *whence* [noun phrase]? (verbless: *whence this enthusiasm?*) &middot; *from whence* (redundant but four hundred years old)  
**Contrast:** *whence* is "from where," [whither](#whither) is "to where," and *where* is neither — it is position only. The distinction English lost is exactly the one *whence* preserves, which is why the word survives at all. On ***from whence***: the *from* is redundant, since *whence* already contains it, and generations of usage guides have called it an error — but the King James translators wrote *from whence cometh my help*, Shakespeare wrote it, and no modern authority treats it as wrong. Use it if you like the sound; nobody who knows the history will object. Do not confuse *whence* with *hence*: they point in the same direction from different places (*from where* / *from here*), and swapping them produces nonsense. And *whence* is not *when*; the resemblance is an accident of spelling.

From where; from which place, source, or cause.

1. "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from *whence* cometh my help." (Psalm 121:1, King James Version)
2. Return *whence* you came, and do not come back through this valley.
3. He never would say *whence* the money had come.
4. She showed us the village *whence* her grandparents emigrated in 1892.
5. *Whence* this sudden enthusiasm for committee work? (the verbless bookish question)
6. Modern English: *the village **her grandparents came from***; *where did the money come from?*

**See also:** [whither](#whither), [hence](#hence), [thence](#thence), [wherein](#wherein), [therefrom](#therefrom), [of which](05-relative.md#of-which)

### whither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪðɚ/ &middot; *WITH-er* &middot; also /ˈhwɪðɚ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*; for everyone else it is an exact homophone of *wither*, "to dry up," which is the trap below  
**Русский:** куда — устар., книжн.; о цели движения; омофон wither «вянуть»
**Type:** relative and interrogative pro-adverb — "to what place," "to which" &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in one biblical passage, and in a headline formula that news magazines still use  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant.  
**Refers to:** the **destination** — the place, state, or future something is moving toward. In the headline use (*Whither the euro?*) the destination is figurative: the question is what will become of the thing.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb in the clause agrees with that clause's own subject (*whither thou **goest***, with the archaic *thou* ending).  
**Pattern:** *whither* [subject] [verb] (*whither thou goest*) &middot; *Whither* [verb] [subject]? (question with inversion: *whither goest thou?*) &middot; *Whither* [noun phrase]? (the verbless headline: *Whither the party?*) &middot; [noun] *whither* [clause] (relative: *the country whither he was sent*)  
**Contrast:** *whither* is "**to** where," [whence](#whence) is "**from** where," and *where* alone is position — the same three-way split as [hither](#hither) / [thence](#thence) / *here*. Modern English says *where … to*, or just *where*: *where are you going?* does the job that *whither goest thou?* once did. Two traps. First, the homophone: for the great majority of Americans *whither* and ***wither*** sound exactly alike, and the spelling is the only thing keeping *Whither the republic?* from reading as a prediction of its collapse — which headline writers have exploited more than once. Second, do not use *whither* for position: ✗*the town whither he lives* should be *where he lives*, or [wherein](#wherein) in formal prose.

To where; to which place. The direction-marking question word English replaced with plain *where*.

1. "*Whither* thou goest, I will go." (Ruth 1:16, King James Version — the wedding reading)
2. "None of you asketh me, *Whither* goest thou?" (John 16:5)
3. *Whither* the euro? (the magazine headline formula: "what is going to become of it?")
4. He asked *whither* the road led, and nobody could tell him.
5. The country *whither* she was posted had no embassy of its own.
6. Modern English: *Where are you going?*; *where does this road go?*

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [hither](#hither), [thither](#thither), [whereto](#whereto), [wheresoever, whithersoever, whensoever](#wheresoever-whithersoever-whensoever), [wherein](#wherein)

## Dialectal second-person plurals

When *you* swallowed *thou*, English was left with one word doing four jobs — singular and plural, subject and object — and no way to make clear that you are addressing the whole table rather than the person across from you. Every variety of American English has independently invented a repair, and the repairs are geographic enough to serve as a map: **y'all** in the South and in African American English, **you guys** almost everywhere else, **youse** and **yous** in the working-class Northeast, **you'uns** and **y'uns** in Appalachia and the Ohio Valley, and **yinz** in Pittsburgh alone. None of these is an error in the sense that *hisself* is an error; they are filling a real hole in the standard paradigm, which is exactly why every region built one. What they are is **informal and regionally marked**: they belong in speech and in writing that sounds like speech, and a job application is not the place for any of them. *Y'all* is the one that is genuinely moving upward and outward — it is now common in national informal writing as a gender-neutral alternative to *you guys*.

All of them take **plural verbs**: *y'all are*, *youse were*, *yinz have*. And all of them are invariant for case — the same form serves as subject and object (*y'all should call me* / *I'll call y'all*).

### all y'all

**Pronunciation:** /ɔl ˈjɔl/ &middot; *all-YALL* &middot; /ɑl ˈjɑl/ in merged accents; the first word reduces to /əl/ in fast speech  
**Русский:** вы все до одного — диал. (Юг США), разг.; усиленное y'all, весь адресат без исключения
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun, emphatic; regional &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken; Southern American English and African American English  
**Person / number:** second person plural, always plural, and specifically **exhaustive** — the entire group without exception.  
**Refers to:** every member of the group addressed, human. It cannot address one person, and that is the whole point of it.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *all y'all are late*, *all y'all were there*, *have all y'all eaten?* Never ✗*all y'all is*.  
**Pattern:** *all y'all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *all y'all* &middot; *all y'all's* [noun] (possessive) &middot; *every one of all y'all* (heavier still)  
**Contrast:** this form is the answer to the objection outsiders raise about [y'all](#yall) — that it has lost the ability to be emphatic because it is used so freely. *Y'all* addresses a group; *all y'all* insists on the whole of it, usually when part of the group has been assuming the remark is aimed at somebody else: *I'm not talking to some of you, I'm talking to **all y'all***. Structurally it is *all* + *y'all*, the same predeterminer stacking that gives *all of you* and *all of us*, so it is not a doubled *all* despite the *y'all* < *you all* etymology — no speaker parses *y'all* as containing *all* any more.

Every last one of the group being addressed — the emphatic, exhaustive plural.

1. I need *all y'all* in the van by six, not just the drivers.
2. *All y'all* are going to hear me out before anybody says a word.
3. This applies to *all y'all*, including the folks in the back.
4. Coach said he was tired of *all y'all* showing up late.
5. Happy Thanksgiving to *all y'all* and *all y'all's* families.
6. If *all y'all* would quiet down, I could read the list.

**See also:** [y'all](#yall), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [y'all's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#yalls)

### y'all

**Pronunciation:** /jɔl/ &middot; *YAWL* &middot; /jɑl/ for the cot–caught merged majority; weak form /jl̩/ or /jəl/, so *y'all come back now* runs together as /jl̩ˈkʌm/  
**Русский:** вы (мн. ч.) — диал., разг.; южноамериканское «вы» к нескольким, стяжение you all
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none — the same form is subject and object &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; ordinary, unstigmatized standard usage within the American South and in African American English, and spreading nationally in informal writing  
**Person / number:** second person plural. Contracted from *you all*, but a single word to its speakers — no one hears an *all* inside it.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only. In its **associative** use it can be spoken to one person while referring to that person's household, business, or group: asking a lone waiter *Do y'all have sweet tea?* means the restaurant, not the waiter.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb, always: *y'all are*, *y'all were*, *y'all have*, *do y'all know*. ✗*Y'all is* is not a Southern form; it is a caricature. The possessive is *y'all's* (*y'all's car*), and the reflexive is *y'all's selves* or plain *yourselves*.  
**Pattern:** *y'all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *y'all* (*I'll see y'all Sunday*) &middot; [preposition] *y'all* (*with y'all*) &middot; *y'all's* [noun] &middot; *all y'all* (emphatic) &middot; *y'all come back now* (the leave-taking formula)  
**Contrast:** *y'all* against *you guys* is the sharpest regional line in American pronouns: *y'all* south of roughly the Ohio River and throughout African American English, *you guys* nearly everywhere else. The persistent Northern claim that Southerners use *y'all* for one person is mostly a misreading of the associative use above; most Southern speakers reject a truly singular *y'all*, though genuinely singular uses are attested in some communities and the point is honestly contested. Spelling matters more than it should: the apostrophe stands for the missing *ou* of *you*, so it goes after the *y* — *y'all*, never ✗*ya'll*, which is a very common misspelling and is treated as an error even by people who use the word constantly. Against modern *you*, *y'all* is simply the plural that *you* stopped being when it absorbed [thou](#thou).

You, plural — the Southern and African American second-person plural, and the most successful of all the repairs to the missing plural *you*.

1. *Y'all* are welcome to stay for dinner.
2. I'll see *y'all* at the game on Saturday.
3. Do *y'all* have any sweet tea?
4. *Y'all's* dog got out again last night.
5. Where are *y'all* parked?
6. *Y'all* come back now, you hear?
7. I didn't mean any of *y'all* in particular.

**See also:** [all y'all](#all-yall), [you all](#you-all), [you guys](#you-guys), [youse](#youse), [yinz](#yinz), [who all, what all](#who-all-what-all), [ye](#ye), [y'all (personal)](01-personal.md#yall), [y'all's](02-possessive.md#yalls)

### y'uns

**Pronunciation:** /junz/ &middot; *YOONZ* &middot; also /jɪnz/, at which point it has become [yinz](#yinz)  
**Русский:** вы (мн. ч.) — диал., разг.; Аппалачи и Питтсбург, из you ones; вариант yinz
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; nonstandard outside its region  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only. Like *y'all*, it can be used associatively of a household (*Are y'uns coming to the reunion?*).  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *y'uns are*, *y'uns were*, *do y'uns want*.  
**Pattern:** *y'uns* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *y'uns* &middot; *y'uns's* [noun] (possessive, rare in writing) &middot; *y'uns guys* (reinforced)  
**Contrast:** *y'uns* is a contracted spelling of [you'uns](#youuns) — "you ones" — and the three-way set *you'uns / y'uns / yinz* is one word at three stages of erosion, moving west and north from Appalachia into the Ohio Valley and finally into Pittsburgh, where it lands as [yinz](#yinz). Region separates it from its rivals: *y'uns* belongs to Appalachia, western Pennsylvania, eastern Ohio, and West Virginia, while [y'all](#yall) belongs to the South proper. Speakers whose grandparents said *you'uns* often say *yinz*, and the spelling in print is unsettled — *y'uns*, *yuns*, *younz*, and *yinz* all occur.

You, plural — the Appalachian and Ohio Valley form, from *you ones*.

1. Are *y'uns* going up to the cabin this weekend?
2. *Y'uns* better bring a jacket; it gets cold up there.
3. I told *y'uns* the road was washed out.
4. Do *y'uns* need help carrying that in?
5. *Y'uns* can park behind the church.
6. Which one of *y'uns* left the gate open?

**See also:** [you'uns](#youuns), [yinz](#yinz), [y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse)

### yinz

**Pronunciation:** /jɪnz/ &middot; *YINZ* &middot; also spelled *yunz*, *yins*  
**Русский:** вы (мн. ч.) — диал., разг.; примета питтсбургской речи, вариант y'uns
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; a local identity marker as much as a pronoun  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *yinz are*, *yinz were*, *did yinz see*.  
**Pattern:** *yinz* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *yinz* &middot; *yinz guys* (reinforced) &middot; *yinz'll* (contracted with *will*)  
**Contrast:** *yinz* is the Pittsburgh form, the tightest geographic marker of any pronoun in American English — western Pennsylvania and essentially nowhere else. It is the end of the erosion chain *you ones* → [you'uns](#youuns) → [y'uns](#yuns) → *yinz*, and it is so strongly identified with the city that a native speaker of the local dialect is called a **yinzer**, a label worn with pride, irony, or contempt depending on who is using it. Against [y'all](#yall) and [you guys](#you-guys) the difference is purely regional; the grammar is identical.

You, plural — the Pittsburgh form.

1. Are *yinz* going dahntahn for the game?
2. *Yinz* want to split a pizza?
3. I'll meet *yinz* at the bridge around seven.
4. Did *yinz* hear about the water main on Liberty?
5. *Yinz* guys need to move that car before the street sweeper comes.
6. *Yinz* better redd up before your mother gets home.

**See also:** [y'uns](#yuns), [you'uns](#youuns), [youse](#youse), [y'all](#yall)

### you all

**Pronunciation:** /juˈɔl/ &middot; *yoo-AWL* &middot; reduces toward /jəˈɔl/ and, in the South, all the way to [y'all](#yall)  
**Русский:** вы все — разг. и нейтр.; полная форма y'all и обычное «вы все» повсеместно
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun (regional), or ordinary *you* plus the quantifier *all* &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; the uncontracted Southern form, and also plain English everywhere  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *you all are*, *you all were*, *have you all decided*.  
**Pattern:** *you all* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you all* (*thank you all*) &middot; *you all's* [noun] (Southern possessive) &middot; quantifier float, *you* [verb] *all* (*you have all seen the memo*)  
**Contrast:** this entry is two things wearing one spelling, and stress separates them. As a **pronoun**, *you all* is the uncontracted Southern plural, stressed on *all* as a unit with *you* (*You all are invited*), and it is the ancestor of [y'all](#yall) — many Southern speakers use both, with *you all* the more careful or emphatic choice. As ordinary **quantification**, *you all* is just the pronoun *you* with the floating quantifier *all* attached, and it means "all of you" in the arithmetic sense: *You all failed the quiz* (every one of you), *I thank you all*, *You have all seen the memo* — where *all* has floated away from *you* entirely, proving it is a separate word. That second use is standard everywhere in the country and is not regional at all. The determiner *all* is covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all).

You, plural — the full form behind *y'all*, and the neutral way to address a group without picking a regional side.

**1. Second-person plural pronoun (Southern, informal).** *You all* as a single unit addressing a group, the uncontracted parent of *y'all*: *You all are welcome*, *Is this you all's first time?*

**2. *You* plus the floating quantifier *all* (standard everywhere).** Two words doing two jobs, meaning "all of you": *I thank you all*, *You all failed the quiz*. The proof that it is two words is that *all* can float away from *you* entirely — *You have all seen the memo* — which the Southern pronoun cannot do.

1. *You all* are welcome to bring a dish.
2. Thank *you all* for coming out in this weather.
3. Is this *you all's* first time at the fair?
4. I hope *you all* had a good drive down.
5. *You all* have already seen the numbers, so I'll be brief.
6. Would *you all* like coffee before we start?

**See also:** [y'all](#yall), [all y'all](#all-yall), [you guys](#you-guys), [you all (personal)](01-personal.md#you-all), [all](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#all)

### you guys

**Pronunciation:** /juˈɡaɪz/ &middot; *yoo-GUYZ* &middot; weak form /jəˈɡaɪz/, and in fast speech the whole phrase can run to /jəɡaɪz/  
**Русский:** вы, ребята — разг.; общеамериканское «вы» к группе, независимо от пола
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; general American informal &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken; the default plural *you* for most of the United States outside the South  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more, human — and, for most speakers, **regardless of gender**: a group of women is routinely addressed as *you guys*. That mismatch between the literal *guys* and the actual reference is exactly why many speakers and institutions are now retreating from it.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *you guys are*, *you guys were*, *do you guys want*. The possessive is *you guys's* /ˈɡaɪzɪz/, which is awkward enough that speakers usually rephrase (*your car* rather than *you guys's car*).  
**Pattern:** *you guys* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you guys* &middot; *you guys's* [noun] (avoided in writing) &middot; *hey, you guys* (vocative)  
**Contrast:** *you guys* is to the North, West, and Midwest what [y'all](#yall) is to the South, and the two are in open competition. *You guys* is the more widespread; *y'all* is the one gaining ground, partly because it carries no gender. Many American workplaces and schools now discourage *you guys* for mixed or female groups and suggest *y'all*, *everyone*, *folks*, or *all of you* instead. Note that in this use *guys* is not the plural noun *guys* — it has bleached into part of the pronoun, which is why *you guys* can address a group of women while *those guys* cannot describe them. Regional relatives reinforce their pronoun the same way: *youse guys*, *yinz guys*, *y'uns guys*.

You, plural — the informal plural *you* of most of the country, gender-neutral in use if not in form.

1. Are *you guys* coming to the show tonight?
2. I told *you guys* the train leaves at 7:04.
3. Do *you guys* want to split an appetizer?
4. *You guys* were right about the shortcut.
5. Hey, *you guys* — the meeting moved to the third floor.
6. It was good seeing *you guys* again.

**See also:** [y'all](#yall), [you all](#you-all), [youse](#youse), [yinz](#yinz), [you lot](#you-lot), [you guys (personal)](01-personal.md#you-guys)

### you lot

**Pronunciation:** /juˈlɑt/ &middot; *yoo-LOT* &middot; British /juːˈlɒt/  
**Русский:** вы там, вы все — разг., брит. и ирл. (не амер.), с оттенком досады
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; **British and Irish, not American** &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken, and mildly brusque or exasperated  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the group addressed, human, usually with a faint edge of impatience or affectionate disparagement — a teacher to a class, a parent to children, a friend to a rowdy table.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *you lot are*, *you lot were*, *have you lot finished?*  
**Pattern:** *you lot* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you lot* &middot; *oi, you lot* (vocative, British)  
**Contrast:** **flagged as not American.** An American may read or hear *you lot* constantly in British television, novels, and football commentary, but producing it in American speech sounds like costume. Its American equivalents are [you guys](#you-guys) and [y'all](#yall), neither of which carries the built-in note of exasperation that *you lot* does: *What have you lot done now?* is a complaint in a way that *What have you guys done now?* is not. British English also uses *you two*, *you three*, and *this lot* the same way.

You, plural — the British and Irish informal plural, with a built-in note of impatience. Listed here so an American recognizes it; not for American use.

1. What have *you lot* done to the kitchen? (British)
2. *You lot* had better be ready when the coach arrives.
3. I'm not driving *you lot* anywhere until this car is cleaned out.
4. Are *you lot* coming to the pub or not?
5. *You lot* never listen to a word I say.
6. Right, *you lot* — books away.

**See also:** [you guys](#you-guys), [y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse)

### you'uns

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjunz/ &middot; *YOONZ* &middot; also /ˈjuənz/ when said slowly; the apostrophe marks the swallowed *o* of *ones*  
**Русский:** вы (мн. ч.) — диал., разг.; сельское, из you ones; за пределами региона просторечно
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; strongly rural and regional, and stigmatized outside its home ground  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only. Associative use of a household is common (*Are you'uns coming for Christmas?*).  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *you'uns are*, *you'uns were*, *do you'uns want*.  
**Pattern:** *you'uns* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *you'uns* &middot; *you'uns's* [noun] (possessive, rare in writing)  
**Contrast:** *you'uns* is transparently *you ones* — the same strategy as the Scots-Irish *you ones* that the settlers of Appalachia brought with them, and the same strategy that produced [yinz](#yinz) at the far end of the erosion. Its territory is Appalachia, the Southern Midland, West Virginia, eastern Ohio, and western Pennsylvania, overlapping with but distinct from the [y'all](#yall) country to the south. It is the most rural-sounding of the American plurals and the one most likely to draw a reaction outside its region, which is worth knowing before imitating it.

You, plural — the Appalachian *you ones*, in its fullest spelling.

1. Are *you'uns* headed to town this morning?
2. *You'uns* are more than welcome to stay for supper.
3. I reckon *you'uns* know the way from here.
4. Did *you'uns* get any rain over on your side of the ridge?
5. *You'uns* left the porch light on all night.
6. I'll ride down with *you'uns* if there's room.

### yous

**Русский:** вы (мн. ч.) — прост., диал.; рабочие кварталы Северо-Востока США
**Type:** second-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none — subject and object alike &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; working-class urban Northeast, and nonstandard everywhere  
**Person / number:** second person plural.  
**Refers to:** the people addressed, two or more; human only.  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *yous are*, *yous were*, *did yous hear*.  
**Pattern:** *yous* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *yous* &middot; *yous guys* (reinforced) &middot; *yous two*  
**Contrast:** *yous* and [youse](#youse) are one word in two spellings, and nothing but the spelling separates them — *yous* is the more transparent (*you* + the regular plural *-s*), *youse* the more common in print. Its territory is New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, Chicago's older neighborhoods, and Irish-American communities generally, with parallel forms in Irish, Scouse, and Australian English. Unlike [y'all](#yall), it has not spread and has not shed its class marking: it reads as working-class urban wherever it appears, and no editor will let it through outside quoted speech.

## Dialectal interrogatives: *who all* and *what all*

The same *all* that built [y'all](#yall) out of *you* also attaches to the question words, and for the same reason: it makes the question **exhaustive**. *Who came?* invites one name; *Who all came?* asks for the whole list, and a Southern speaker who gets one name back will ask the question again. Standard English has no single word for this and has to reach for *who else*, *which people*, or *everyone who*, which is why the construction is worth knowing even where it is not spoken. It is **regional, not erroneous** — Southern, South Midland, and Appalachian, and drifting into casual speech elsewhere — and like every regionalism in this file it belongs in speech rather than in a brief or a cover letter.

### who all, what all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhu ˈɔl/, /ˌwʌt ˈɔl/ &middot; *hoo-ALL*, *wut-ALL* &middot; /ɑl/ for the cot–caught merged majority; the stress falls on *all*, which is what marks the two words as a single pronoun rather than a question word with a stray quantifier after it  
**Русский:** кто все, что всё — диал. (Юг США); вопрос об исчерпывающем составе
**Type:** interrogative and relative pronouns, **exhaustive**; regional &middot; **Case:** *who all* takes the case of *who* — subject in *who all came?*, object in *who all did you invite?* and *to who all did he send it?*; the formal object *whom all* exists but is vanishingly rare, and *whom* is retreating from this position exactly as it is retreating everywhere else. *What all* is invariant, with no case at all &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; Southern American English, the South Midland, and Appalachia  
**Person / number:** *who all* is human and open in number — grammatically it can take either verb, but it always expects a list. *What all* is third person, non-human, invariant.  
**Refers to:** *who all* — the complete set of **people** who did, will do, or were affected by something. *what all* — the complete set of **things**, actions, or facts. Neither refers to a single known individual; asking *who all came?* when one guest was expected is odd.  
**Agreement:** either number is standard within the dialect, and the **singular** is the more common: *who all is coming?* alongside *who all are coming?*; *what all was in the box?* alongside *what all were in the box?* Neither is an error. This is the same latitude that [none](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#none) has, and it is not shared by the compounds — *everyone*, *somebody*, and *nobody* take a **singular** verb only.  
**Pattern:** *who all* [singular verb] (*Who all is going?*) &middot; *who all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] (*Who all did you tell?*) &middot; *what all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] (*What all did you buy?*) &middot; embedded: [verb] *who all* / *what all* [clause] (*I don't know who all he told*) &middot; *who all's* [noun] (possessive: *whose all* is not a form)  
**Contrast:** the two words are a **unit** and cannot be pulled apart — ✗*Who did all you tell?* is not the same construction and is not English. Keep the pronoun apart from the ordinary floating quantifier, which is standard everywhere and looks similar on the page: in *They all came*, *all* has floated off *they* and can be moved (*All of them came*), while the *all* of *who all* cannot move at all. The nearest standard equivalents are clumsier by design: *Who came — all of them?*, *Which people came?*, *What else did you buy?* The relative use (*I don't know who all he told*) is the one that most often escapes the dialect, since it fills a real gap in the standard language. The set also runs to *where all*, *when all*, and *how all* in the same regions, though those are adverbs rather than pronouns.

Who, exhaustively; what, exhaustively. The Southern question words that ask for the whole list rather than one answer.

1. *Who all* is coming to the reunion? (Southern; standard *Who is coming?*)
2. *Who all* did you invite to the wedding? (object of *invite*)
3. *What all* did she say about the offer? (object)
4. I don't know *who all* he told about the layoffs. (embedded relative)
5. *Who all's* truck is parked behind the barn? (possessive)
6. To *who all* did the letter go, exactly? (after a preposition)
7. Standard: *Who* came, and *what* did she tell them? — with *all of them* or *everything* added if the whole list is what you want.

**See also:** [y'all](#yall), [all y'all](#all-yall), [you all](#you-all), [who](06-interrogative.md#who), [what](06-interrogative.md#what), [whom](05-relative.md#whom), [all](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#all)

## Invented pronouns: *thon* and the Spivak set

English has no gender-neutral third-person singular that everybody agrees on, and for about a hundred and seventy years people have been proposing one. Dozens of sets have been coined — *thon*, *e*, *ey*, *ze*, *xe*, *hir*, *ne*, *ve*, *per* — and the ones in this section are here because an American is likelier to run into them than into the rest: **thon** because it spent sixty-six years in a major American dictionary, **e** because it was adopted by early online communities and is still in use, and **heesh** and **hesh** because they are the two blends that turn up in every published account of the attempts that failed. One entry here is not a coinage at all: **ou** is a genuine eighteenth-century English dialect pronoun that did this job by inheritance rather than by design, and it is included because it is the standing proof that the gap can be filled without anybody inventing anything.

Three factual notes frame them. First, the gap they were built to fill is real: English has no singular pronoun that is neutral as to gender and that everyone accepts, and writers have felt the lack since at least the 1850s. Second, the solution that actually spread is not on this list — it is **singular *they***, which is a native form six hundred years old, is now recognized as standard by Chicago, AP, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster, and is what the overwhelming majority of American writers use both for an unknown person and for a person who asks for it. Third, the invented sets are nevertheless used: some people use them for themselves, some publications and online platforms support them, and a reader may meet them in fiction, in academic writing, and in email signatures. This section describes what the forms are and how they inflect. Whether to use any of them for a given person is settled the same way any other name is settled — by asking, and then by doing what you were told.

The coined entries below are marked **coined** rather than *archaic* or *nonstandard*, because neither label fits: they were deliberately made, they were never part of an older paradigm, and they are not errors in any dialect. None of them belongs to any traditional English paradigm and none is used by more than a very small number of speakers; each entry gives the forms, the inflection, and the history, and stops there. [ou](#ou), the one inherited form in the group, is marked **dialectal** instead.

### e

**Pronunciation:** /i/ &middot; *EE* &middot; the object *em* is /ɛm/, the possessive *eir* /ɛr/ (rhyming with *air*), *eirs* /ɛrz/, and the reflexive *emself* /ɛmˈsɛlf/ &middot; in connected speech *em* is easily confused with the ordinary weak *'em* of *them*, which is one practical reason some users prefer the spelling *ey*  
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — гендерно-нейтральное 3 л. ед. ч.: e/em/eir/emself
**Type:** personal pronoun, **coined** (gender-neutral third person singular) &middot; **Case:** subject *e*; object *em*; possessive determiner *eir*; possessive pronoun *eirs*; reflexive *emself* &middot; **Register:** neutral where it is used; unfamiliar to most American readers, so writing usually introduces it or glosses it  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. There is no separate plural — for more than one person, ordinary *they* and *them*.  
**Refers to:** one person, without specifying gender. It is used both generically (for an unknown or unspecified person, in the slot where a writer might otherwise use *he or she*) and specifically (for a particular person who uses these forms).  
**Agreement:** takes the **same third-person singular verb forms as *he* and *she***: *e is*, *e has*, *e goes*, *e was*. This is the practical difference from singular *they*, which takes *are* and *were* — *e was late* against *they were late* for the same one person.  
**Pattern:** *e* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *em* &middot; *eir* [noun] (determiner: *eir coat*) &middot; *eirs* standing alone (*the coat is eirs*) &middot; *emself* as reflexive (*e taught emself*)  
**Contrast:** two closely related sets circulate under the name **Spivak pronouns**, and they differ in one letter. The set in this entry — ***e / em / eir / eirs / emself*** — is the one usually called "the original Spivak," from the mathematician Michael Spivak, who used it in his TeX manuals in the 1980s to write about a reader whose gender he did not know. The other set, ***ey / em / eir***, was proposed by Christine Elverson in 1975 and is formed by knocking the *th-* off *they / them / their*; it is now the commoner of the two, largely because a written *e* is easy to miss and a spoken /i/ is easy to lose. Both were adopted in early text-based online worlds — LambdaMOO offered "Spivak" as a selectable option in the early 1990s — and both are still used. Against singular *they*, the practical differences are verb agreement (*e is* / *they are*) and familiarity: *they* needs no explanation to any reader, and *e* usually does. Against *ze/hir*, another widely used set, the difference is only which forms were coined.

A coined gender-neutral third-person singular pronoun: *e*, *em*, *eir*, *eirs*, *emself*.

1. If a reader finds an error, *e* should write to the publisher at the address on the copyright page. (the generic use Spivak's manuals were written for)
2. Each student brings *eir* own laptop, and *e* is responsible for charging it. (determiner and subject in one sentence)
3. I asked Robin whether the package had reached *em* yet.
4. The blue notebook on the table is *eirs*, not mine.
5. E taught *emself* to solder from a library book. (reflexive)
6. The same sentences with singular *they*, and the agreement that comes with it: *if a reader finds an error, **they** should write to the publisher*; *each student brings **their** own laptop*.

### heesh

**Pronunciation:** /hiʃ/ &middot; *HEESH* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *leash*; no weak form, because the word is written far more often than it is said, and most of the people who have written it have never heard it spoken  
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — «он-она», слияние he и she, без полной парадигмы
**Type:** personal pronoun, **coined** (gender-neutral third person singular) — a **blend** of *he* and *she* &middot; **Case:** no settled paradigm. *Heesh* is a subject form; the object and possessive slots were never agreed on, and different proposers left the word invariant, paired it with *hisher* and *himer*, or simply fell back on *his or her*. Nothing standardized, because the word never got enough use to standardize anything &middot; **Register:** **coined and effectively dead.** It belongs to the long list of failed epicene pronouns, and an American meets it only in an article or a dictionary note about that list  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. There is no plural — for more than one person, ordinary *they* and *them*.  
**Refers to:** one person, without specifying gender. In every recorded use it is **generic** — an unknown or unspecified person, in the slot where a writer might otherwise put *he or she* — rather than a form any particular person has asked to be called by.  
**Agreement:** takes the **same third-person singular verb as *he* and *she***: *heesh is*, *heesh was*, *heesh has*, *heesh goes*. This is the practical difference from singular *they*, which takes *are* and *were* for the same one person.  
**Pattern:** *heesh* [singular verb] &middot; *heesh is* / *heesh was* / *heesh has* &middot; the other slots are unsettled, so a writer using it either leaves *heesh* invariant (*I told heesh*) or falls back on *him or her* and *his or her*  
**Contrast:** *heesh* is a **blend** — the front of *he* welded to the back of *she* — so both source words stay visible in it. That transparency is the argument its proposers made for it and the objection its critics made against it, and the word got no measurable use either way. Compare [hesh](#hesh), the same idea worked out into a full three-case paradigm; [thon](#thon), a blend of *that one*; and [e](#e), which is a **subtraction** from an existing word rather than a fusion of two. The written **s/he** does the same blending on the page and is the only member of this family with real currency, and even it is confined to writing. **None of this is part of any traditional English paradigm, and no measurable number of American speakers uses it.** The form that actually filled the gap is singular *they*, which is native, six hundred years old, and now recognized as standard by Chicago, AP, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster.

A coined gender-neutral third-person singular pronoun, blended out of *he* and *she*, proposed repeatedly and never adopted.

1. If a driver runs a red light, *heesh* will be photographed and mailed a citation. (the generic sentence the coinage was built for)
2. Whoever wrote the note did not sign it, and *heesh* has not come forward since.
3. The proposal was that a teacher should be free to write *heesh* rather than *he or she* on a form.
4. Each candidate is told the result as soon as *heesh* has finished the last section.
5. A subscriber may cancel at any time, and *heesh* will be refunded the unused balance.
6. What Americans write instead: *if a driver runs a red light, **they** will be photographed*.

### hesh

**Pronunciation:** /hɛʃ/ &middot; *HESH* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *mesh*; the object *himer* is /ˈhɪmɚ/ &middot; *HIM-er* and the possessive *hizer* /ˈhɪzɚ/ &middot; *HIZ-er*, both stressed on the first syllable. The *h* drops in connected speech exactly as it does in *him* and *his*, so *tell himer* is /ˈtɛlɪmɚ/  
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — гендерно-нейтральный набор hesh/himer/hizer
**Type:** personal pronoun set, **coined** (gender-neutral third person singular) — three blends built by one rule &middot; **Case:** a **full three-way paradigm**, which is what separates this set from [heesh](#heesh): subject ***hesh***; object ***himer***; possessive determiner and possessive pronoun ***hizer*** (*hizer coat*, *the coat is hizer*); reflexive ***himerself*** where a proposer supplied one &middot; **Register:** **coined**; circulated in American discussions of sexist language in the 1970s, adopted by nobody, and obsolete in practice  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. No plural of its own.  
**Refers to:** one person, gender unspecified — used generically, for an unknown or unspecified person, in the slot a writer might otherwise fill with *he or she*.  
**Agreement:** third-person **singular** verb, like *he* and *she*: *hesh is*, *hesh has*, *hesh was*, *hesh goes*. Not ✗ *hesh are*, which is singular *they*'s pattern, not this one's.  
**Pattern:** *hesh* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *himer* (object: *tell himer*) &middot; *hizer* [noun] (determiner: *hizer résumé*) &middot; *hizer* standing alone (*the coat is hizer*) &middot; *himerself* (reflexive)  
**Contrast:** every form in the set is a **blend of the corresponding masculine and feminine pair** — *he* + *she* → *hesh*, *him* + *her* → *himer*, *his* + *her* → *hizer* — so the whole paradigm is generated by one rule, which is more than most coinages manage and is the reason it is worth a separate entry from [heesh](#heesh). Against [e](#e), the difference is method: the Spivak set is made by **subtraction** (*they* minus *th-*), this one by **fusion**. Against [thon](#thon), the difference is age and standing: *thon* spent sixty-six years in American dictionaries and *hesh* never entered one. Against singular *they*, the practical difference is agreement (*hesh is* against *they are*) and familiarity. **These forms are not part of any traditional English paradigm and are not used by any appreciable number of speakers**; whether to use any coined set for a particular person is settled the way a name is settled — by asking.

A coined gender-neutral set with a full case paradigm: *hesh*, *himer*, *hizer*, *himerself*.

1. If a patient misses an appointment, *hesh* will be charged for the visit.
2. The committee asked each nominee to send *hizer* résumé by Friday. (the possessive determiner)
3. Nobody answered, so I left the message and hoped somebody would pass it to *himer*. (the object)
4. Whoever left that truck running should move it before *hesh* gets a ticket.
5. Every juror must decide the case for *himerself*, on the evidence and nothing else. (the reflexive)
6. The blue notebook is *hizer*, not mine. (the possessive standing alone)
7. What American writers use instead: *if a patient misses an appointment, **they** will be charged.*

### ou

**Pronunciation:** /u/ &middot; *OO* &middot; recorded as an unstressed /ə/ in running speech — *ou will* came out roughly /ə ˈwɪl/ — and it has **no stressed form at all**, which is exactly how a pronoun this small gets lost  
**Русский:** он, она — диал., устар.; унаследованное бесполое местоимение 3 л. ед. ч., подлежащее
**Type:** personal pronoun, **dialectal** and epicene (gender-neutral third person singular) — an inherited form, not a coinage &middot; **Case:** **subject** only. It is recorded in the subject slot and nowhere else; the ordinary *him*, *her*, and *it* did the object work in the same dialect, and *his* and *her* the possessive &middot; **Register:** **dialectal and obsolete.** An eighteenth-century English West Country form, written down by an observer rather than by its speakers, and never carried to America  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. It covered *he*, *she*, and *it* alike — one form for all three genders.  
**Refers to:** one person **or thing**, without specifying gender. It is the only English pronoun on record that did this job as an ordinary inherited word rather than as somebody's proposal, and that is why it is in this section.  
**Agreement:** ordinary third-person **singular** verb: *ou is*, *ou has*, *ou will*, *ou was*. Never the plural agreement that singular *they* takes.  
**Pattern:** *ou* [singular verb] &middot; *ou will* / *ou is* / *ou has* &middot; subject position only — there is no recorded ✗ *give it to ou* and no recorded possessive  
**Contrast:** everything else in this section was **invented**; *ou* was **inherited**. It is a worn-down descendant of Middle English *a*, itself a reduced form of *he*, *heo*, and *hit* — so the three genders collapsed into one unstressed syllable by ordinary sound change, with nobody deciding anything. That is why the word matters far out of proportion to its size: it is the standing evidence that English can grow a gender-neutral singular on its own, which is also what singular *they* shows on a much larger scale. It was reported in 1789 by the agricultural writer **William Marshall**, describing the speech of the Vale of Gloucester, and it is cited in nearly every modern discussion of epicene pronouns for that reason. Do not confuse it with the French *où*, with the digraph *ou*, or with the interjection *ow*.

He, she, or it — one form for all three. The eighteenth-century English dialect pronoun that filled the gender-neutral gap by inheritance rather than by design.

1. *Ou* will — the Gloucestershire form its recorder glossed as "he will, she will, or it will."
2. *Ou* is gone to market and will not be back before dark.
3. If anybody calls for me, tell them *ou* has left for the day.
4. *Ou* said nothing at all, and nobody could tell whether *ou* was pleased or not.
5. The dialect that kept *ou* had one third-person singular where the standard language had three.
6. Modern English: *they* — the native form that now does the same work, and the only one in general use.

### thon

**Pronunciation:** /ðɑn/ &middot; *THON* &middot; voiced *th* as in *this*, rhyming with *John*; also /ðɔn/ where *cot* and *caught* are kept apart. The possessive is *thon's* /ðɑnz/ and the reflexive [thonself](#thonself) /ðɑnˈsɛlf/  
**Русский:** неологизм, эквивалента в русском нет — гендерно-нейтральное «он/она»; ныне не употребляется
**Type:** personal pronoun, **coined** (gender-neutral third person singular) &middot; **Case:** *thon* serves as both subject and object; the possessive is *thon's*; the reflexive is *thonself* &middot; **Register:** coined and now obsolete in practice; historically it carried no informality of its own, and it was proposed for ordinary edited prose  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, gender-neutral. No plural.  
**Refers to:** one person whose gender is unknown, unstated, or irrelevant — the slot filled today by singular *they* and formerly by generic *he*.  
**Agreement:** third-person **singular** verb, like *he* and *she*: *thon is*, *thon has*, *thon was*, *thon knows*.  
**Pattern:** *thon* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *thon* (object: *tell thon*) &middot; *thon's* [noun] (possessive: *thon's coat*) &middot; *thonself* (reflexive)  
**Contrast:** *thon* is the most successful failure in the history of English pronoun coinage. It was proposed in 1858 by **Charles Crozat Converse**, an American lawyer and hymn composer, as a contraction of *that one*; it was entered in **Funk & Wagnalls' Standard Dictionary** in 1898 and stayed there until 1964, and it appeared in **Webster's Second New International** in 1934 — sixty-six years of dictionary standing without ever entering ordinary speech. Its case system is simpler than any other English pronoun's: one form for subject and object, and the ordinary apostrophe-*s* for the possessive, which no other personal pronoun takes (*his*, *her*, *their*, and *its* are all apostrophe-free). Against the modern coinages, *thon* is a **blend** (*that* + *one*), while [e](#e) and *ey* are **subtractions** from existing words, which is part of why the newer sets are easier to learn. Against singular *they*, *thon* was designed to answer the objection that *they* is plural — an objection that has since lost most of its force. Do not confuse *thon* with the Scots and Ulster demonstrative *thon*, a live regional word meaning "that one over there," a relative of *yon* and unrelated to Converse's coinage.

A gender-neutral third-person singular pronoun coined in 1858 and carried by American dictionaries until 1964.

1. If a subscriber wishes to cancel, *thon* must give thirty days' notice. (the kind of sentence Converse coined the word for)
2. Each candidate will be told the result as soon as *thon* has finished the last section.
3. I left a message, but nobody could tell me whether *thon* had received it.
4. Every driver is responsible for *thon's* own tolls. (the possessive, with its unusual apostrophe)
5. The applicant should sign the form *thonself* rather than send an agent. (the reflexive)
6. The same sentences today: *if a subscriber wishes to cancel, **they** must give thirty days' notice.*

### thonself

**Pronunciation:** /ðɑnˈsɛlf/ &middot; *thon-SELF* &middot; stress on *self*, exactly as in *himself* and *herself*; the plural *thonselves* was proposed but is not attested in use  
**Русский:** неологизм, эквивалента в русском нет — возвратное к thon, «сам себя» без указания пола
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun, **coined** — the reflexive of [thon](#thon) &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** coined and now obsolete; it appeared in the same dictionaries that carried *thon*  
**Person / number:** third person singular, gender-neutral. No plural in practice.  
**Refers to:** the same person *thon* refers to, and only when that person is already the subject of the same clause — the ordinary requirement of every English reflexive.  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the clause's verb agrees with *thon*, which is singular (*thon washes thonself*, never ✗*thon wash*).  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *thonself* (reflexive object) &middot; [preposition] *thonself* (*by thonself*, *to thonself*) &middot; [subject] *thonself* [verb] (intensive: *the author thonself said so*)  
**Contrast:** *thonself* is built on the **object** form *thon*, following the third-person pattern of *himself* and *themselves* rather than the first- and second-person pattern of *myself* and *yourself* — which is a small piece of evidence that Converse was thinking carefully, since *thon* is both subject and object and either analogy was available. The modern counterparts are *themself* (the singular reflexive of singular *they*, now recognized by Merriam-Webster and used widely) and [emself](#e), the reflexive of the Spivak set. All three do the same job: give a gender-neutral singular a reflexive of its own, which English otherwise lacks.

The reflexive of *thon*: the same person, when that person is both subject and object of the clause.

1. Each applicant must sign the form *thonself*.
2. Thon poured *thonself* a cup and sat down by the window.
3. The witness contradicted *thonself* twice in the space of a page.
4. Anyone living by *thonself* out there needs a generator.
5. The author *thonself* has said the ending was an accident. (intensive)
6. The modern equivalents: *each applicant must sign the form **themself***; *e poured **emself** a cup*.

## Other nonstandard forms

The rest of the file covers forms that are not filling any gap in the paradigm — they are alternatives to a standard form that already exists, and using them will be noticed. Two of them, *hisself* and *theirselves*, are more logical than the standard forms they replace. The *-n* possessives (*hisn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *theirn*, *yourn*) are a whole rival paradigm, as regular in its own terms as the standard one, and [mines](#mines) finishes the *-s* paradigm that standard English left one word short. The doubled negative (*I didn't do nothing*) is the majority pattern across the world's Englishes and the minority pattern in this one. And two of them, the coordinated *Me and him went* and the hypercorrected *between you and I*, are opposite errors that come from the same anxiety about case. A handful are exceptions to the opening sentence and do fill a gap: the associative plural [and them](#and-them-nem) says "that person and their people" in three syllables, which standard English needs a whole phrase for, and [them'uns](#themuns), [this'n and that'n](#thisn-thatn), and [t'other](#tother) are the surviving singular and third-person members of the Appalachian *-uns* paradigm whose second-person member, [you'uns](#youuns), fills the biggest gap in the language. Three more are older than the standard forms they compete with rather than newer: [hit](#hit) is the Old English form of *it* with its *h* still attached, [them that](#them-that) is the King James Bible's ordinary way of saying *those who*, and [meself](#meself) is nothing but the everyday weak pronunciation of *myself* written down. All of them are labeled honestly below, with the region named wherever there is one. **Learn to recognize every one, and to produce none of them in writing.**

The tool for the coordination cases is the **deletion test**: take the other person out of the coordinated phrase and see which pronoun survives. Run it on all three of the sentences people actually get wrong.

| Slot | The sentence | Delete the other person | What survives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Subject of a verb** — subject case | ✗*Me and him went* | ✗*Me went*, ✗*Him went* | ***He and I went*** — and by the same move ✗*Her and me went* → ***She and I went*** |
| **Object of a verb** — object case | ✗*They invited my wife and I* | ✗*They invited I* | ***They invited my wife and me*** |
| **Object of a preposition** — object case | ✗*He gave it to Bob and I* &middot; ✗*Between you and I* | ✗*to I* &middot; ✗*between I* | ***He gave it to Bob and me*** &middot; ***between you and me*** |

Inside a coordinated phrase the test never fails, and it works in **both** directions at once: the same single move catches the error (✗*Me and him went*) and the hypercorrection (✗*between you and I*, ✗*They invited my wife and I*). Two notes on its limits. It settles **case**, not politeness — *I and Jim went* is grammatical but nobody writes it, so put yourself second: *Jim and I went*. And it has nothing to say about the comparatives, where there is no coordination and no error to catch: *He's taller than me* and *He's taller than I am* are **both standard American English**.

### and them, 'nem

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈðɛm/ &middot; *un-THEM* &middot; the reduced form is /nɛm/ &middot; *NEM*, written *'nem* or *'n'em*, and it fuses onto the name in front of it: *Mama and them* → *Mama'n'em* /ˈmɑmənɛm/, *Bob and them* → *Bob'n'em* /ˈbɑbnɛm/. The *th* of *them* drops entirely, which is the ordinary weak form /əm/ carried one step further  
**Русский:** и его родня, и все свои — диал. (Юг США), разг.: Mama'n'em «мама и её домашние»
**Type:** **associative plural** — a postposed phrase that attaches to a personal name or kin term and turns it into "that person and the people who go with them" &middot; **Case:** none of its own; the whole phrase takes whatever case its position calls for (*Mama'n'em are coming* / *we visited Mama'n'em*) &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; Southern American English, African American English, and the South Midland, with a parallel *and dem* in Hawaiian Creole English and *and them* in Cajun and Gulf Coast speech  
**Person / number:** third person **plural**, always — even though the named person is singular, the phrase as a whole is plural.  
**Refers to:** the person named **plus their usual people** — family, household, in-laws, the friends they arrive with. Human only. The crucial point is that *them* here has **no antecedent**: nobody has said who "they" are, and nobody needs to, because the construction means "and the rest of that person's crowd." Guessing the exact membership is the listener's job and rarely matters.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb: *Mama'n'em **are** coming*, *Bob and them **were** already there*. ✗*Mama'n'em is coming* is wrong even in the dialect that produces the phrase.  
**Pattern:** [personal name or kin term] *and them* (*Ruthie and them*, *Aunt Della and them*) &middot; [name]*'n'em* (the reduced spelling) &middot; *my cousin and them* &middot; as object of a preposition (*over at Grandma'n'em's* — with the possessive on the end of the whole phrase)  
**Contrast:** ordinary English coordination — *Bob and them* meaning "Bob and those particular people over there" — needs *them* to point at somebody already identified. The associative *and them* does not, and that is the whole difference: *I ran into Ruthie and them* is perfectly informative to a Southern listener who has never met anyone in Ruthie's family. The standard equivalents are all longer: *Ruthie and her family*, *Ruthie and her people*, *Ruthie's crowd*, *Ruthie and the rest of them*. English is unusual in **not** having a grammatical associative plural; many languages mark one with an affix, and Southern American English has simply built its own out of the material at hand — the same instinct that produced [y'all](#yall) and [you'uns](#youuns). Note the possessive, which goes on the very end: *we stayed at Mama'n'em's*, never ✗*Mama's'n'em*.

That person and their family or usual companions. The Southern associative plural, built out of an ordinary coordination that has stopped needing an antecedent.

1. *Mama'n'em* are coming over after church. (regional; standard *Mama and the rest of the family*)
2. We ran into Ruthie *and them* at the county fair.
3. Tell Bob*'n'em* to bring a cooler and some chairs.
4. Aunt Della *and them* have had that place on the lake since the sixties.
5. I stayed over at my cousin *and them's* for most of a week. (the possessive on the whole phrase)
6. Standard: *We ran into Ruthie **and her family** at the fair.*

### hisn, hern, ourn, theirn

**Pronunciation:** /hɪzn/, /hɚn/, /aʊrn/, /ðɛrn/ &middot; *HIZN*, *HERN*, *OWRN*, *THAIRN* &middot; *ourn* is also /ɑrn/; one syllable each; the *-n* is a plain consonant, never a separate *-en* syllable, and the *h* of *hisn* and *hern* drops in connected speech (/ɪzn/, /ɚn/)  
**Русский:** его, её, наше, их — прост., диал. (Аппалачи); нелитературные his, hers, ours, theirs
**Type:** possessive pronouns; **nonstandard** and regional &middot; **Case:** possessive, independent — they stand alone and never precede a noun (✗*hisn truck*; the determiner is *his*) &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; Appalachian, the Southern Midland, the Ozarks, and older rural speech generally, and a stock marker of that speech in fiction from Twain onward  
**Person / number:** *hisn* third person singular masculine, *hern* third person singular feminine, *ourn* first person plural, *theirn* third person plural. [yourn](#yourn) is the second-person member of the same set, and *mine* — which already ended in *-n* — is shared with standard English.  
**Refers to:** whatever belongs to the person or people already named. The possessor is human in ordinary use; the thing possessed may be anything at all, singular or plural, count or mass.  
**Agreement:** as a subject complement, none of its own; as a subject, the verb agrees with **what the pronoun stands for**, not with the pronoun's form — *hisn is the red one* (one truck), *theirn are in the barn* (several).  
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *is hisn* &middot; *hern* as subject (*hern's the one with the dent*) &middot; [verb] *theirn* (*they keep theirn inside*) &middot; *of ourn* (*a neighbor of ourn*, the double genitive) &middot; [preposition] *hisn* (*next to hisn*)  
**Contrast:** the standard forms are **his**, **hers**, **ours**, and **theirs**, and those are the ones to write. What is worth seeing is that these are not sloppy versions of the standard words — they are a rival paradigm, and a regular one. Standard English generalized the *-s* of *his* across the whole set (*yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*); these dialects generalized the *-n* of *mine* and [thine](#thine) instead (*yourn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *theirn*), a change that happened in the dialects of northern and western England and crossed to Appalachia with their speakers. Two systems, opposite starting points, each internally consistent — which is why *hisn* is instantly parsable to people who would never say it. None of them takes an apostrophe, because nothing has been contracted away: ✗*his'n*, ✗*her'n*, ✗*their'n* add a mark for a letter that was never there, and ✗*hisn's* doubles the possessive. And do not mix the systems: ✗*hisn book* is wrong twice, since these forms cannot stand in front of a noun at all.

His, hers, ours, theirs. The rest of the Appalachian *-n* possessives, whose second-person member is [yourn](#yourn).

1. Is this coat *hisn* or *yourn*? (nonstandard; standard *his* or *yours*)
2. *Hern* is the one with the dent in the fender. (nonstandard; standard *hers*)
3. We keep ourn in the shed and they keep *theirn* in the barn. (nonstandard; standard *ours*, *theirs*)
4. That old dog of *theirn* has been in my garden again. (nonstandard, double genitive)
5. He parked right next to *hisn* and never said a word. (nonstandard, after a preposition)
6. Standard: Is this coat *his* or *yours*? — *Hers* is the one with the dent.

### hisownself, herownself, theirownselves

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪz oʊn ˈsɛlf/, /ˌhɚ oʊn ˈsɛlf/, /ˌðɛr oʊn ˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *hiz-own-SELF*, *her-own-SELF*, *thair-own-SELVZ* &middot; main stress on *self*, secondary on *own*; the *h* drops in connected speech, giving /ɪzoʊnˈsɛlf/ and /ɚoʊnˈsɛlf/, exactly as it does in [hisself](#hisself)  
**Русский:** он сам лично, она сама лично — прост., диал.; усиленное himself, herself
**Type:** emphatic reflexive and intensive pronouns; **nonstandard** and regional &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; Southern, South Midland, Appalachian, and older rural speech, and African American English; a stock marker of that speech in fiction from Twain and Harris through Faulkner  
**Person / number:** *hisownself* third person singular masculine, *herownself* third person singular feminine, *theirownselves* third person plural. The first- and second-person members are usually written as separate words (*my own self*, *your own self*) and are much less marked.  
**Refers to:** the same antecedent *himself*, *herself*, and *themselves* refer to — a person already the subject of the same clause — with **added emphasis**: not merely "he did it" but "he did it and nobody helped."  
**Agreement:** none of their own; the clause's verb agrees with its subject.  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *hisownself* (reflexive object) &middot; *by hisownself* / *all by herownself* (the commonest slot, meaning "alone, unaided") &middot; [subject] *hisownself* [verb] (intensive: *the preacher hisownself said it*) &middot; *theirownselves* after a plural subject  
**Contrast:** the standard forms are **himself**, **herself**, and **themselves**, and for the extra emphasis standard English says *his very own self*, *all by himself*, or *he himself*. What makes these forms interesting is that they are **doubly** regularized: [hisself](#hisself) already rebuilds the reflexive on the possessive (*his* + *self*) instead of the object form, and *hisownself* then wedges in the intensifier *own*, which standard English allows in the first and second person (*my own self*, *your own self* are both ordinary English) but blocks in the third. *Theirownselves* goes further still and marks the plural twice, on *their* and on *selves*. All of them are logical, all are common in speech across a wide swath of the country, and all will be marked wrong in writing.

Himself, herself, themselves — with *own* wedged in for emphasis. Nonstandard, and heard across the South and the Midland.

1. He built the whole porch *hisownself*, in one August. (nonstandard; standard *himself*)
2. She drove all the way to Memphis by *herownself*. (nonstandard; standard *by herself*)
3. The preacher *hisownself* came out to the house. (nonstandard, intensive)
4. They painted the barn *theirownselves* rather than wait on the co-op. (nonstandard)
5. He never could look after *hisownself*, not even as a grown man. (nonstandard)
6. Standard: *He built the whole porch **himself***; *she drove to Memphis **all by herself***.

### hisself

**Pronunciation:** /hɪzˈsɛlf/ &middot; *hiz-SELF* &middot; commonly /ɪzˈsɛlf/ with the *h* dropped in connected speech  
**Русский:** себя, сам — прост., диал.; нелитературная форма himself
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun; **nonstandard** &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; regionally ordinary in Southern, Appalachian, and rural vernacular English and in African American English, but marked as an error in all writing and in standardized testing  
**Person / number:** third person singular masculine.  
**Refers to:** a single male antecedent that is already the subject of the same clause — exactly what *himself* refers to.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *hisself* &middot; [preposition] *hisself* (*by hisself*, *to hisself*) &middot; [subject] *hisself* [verb] (intensive: *he hisself said it*)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **himself**, and that is the one to use. What is worth understanding is that *hisself* is the **regular** form and *himself* the irregular one: English builds first- and second-person reflexives on the possessive (*my*self, *your*self, *our*selves, *thy*self) and third-person reflexives on the object pronoun (*him*self, *them*selves) — with *herself* sitting on the fence, since *her* is both the possessive and the object form of *she*, which is part of why a paradigm this mixed keeps getting regularized. *Hisself* and [theirselves](#theirselves) simply extend the majority pattern across the whole paradigm, which is why children produce them without ever hearing them. Logic does not help here: they have been nonstandard for three centuries and will be marked wrong every time.

Himself. The regularized reflexive — logical, widespread in speech, and nonstandard in writing.

1. He fixed the transmission *hisself*. (nonstandard; standard *himself*)
2. He was setting out there by *hisself* all afternoon. (nonstandard)
3. The man never gave *hisself* any credit. (nonstandard)
4. He *hisself* admitted he'd been wrong. (nonstandard, intensive)
5. Don't worry about him — he can look after *hisself*. (nonstandard)
6. He talked *hisself* right out of the job. (nonstandard)

### hit

**Pronunciation:** /hɪt/ &middot; *HIT* &middot; **stressed only** — and the weak form is modern *it* /ɪt/, which is the whole history of the word in one line: the *h* survives under stress and drops everywhere else, so the same speaker says *Hit's a long way* and *I don't like it* in the same breath  
**Русский:** оно, это — устар. и диал.; старая форма it, где h сохранилось под ударением
**Type:** personal pronoun, archaic and dialectal — the older form of *it* &middot; **Case:** none in the dialect: the same form serves as subject and object (*hit is cold*, *give hit here*), and the possessive is ordinary *its* — Old English used *his* for it, which is why the King James Bible has *his* where a modern text has *its* &middot; **Register:** **archaic** (standard English down to about 1600, and normal in Chaucer and in the King James Bible) and **regional** (Appalachian, the Ozarks, and older Southern Highland speech, where it survives in stressed position); heavily stigmatized outside those regions  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, neuter. There is no plural — that is *them* or standard *they*.  
**Refers to:** everything modern *it* refers to: a **thing**, an animal, a situation, the weather, and the empty dummy subject (*hit's raining*, *hit's a shame*). It can also carry a whole **clause** as its referent (*hit don't matter what he said*). Never a person, except as *it* can be (*hit's the baby*).  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb: *hit is*, *hit was*, *hit has*. Where the dialect that keeps *hit* also has its own verb concord — *hit don't matter*, *hit ain't* — that is a property of the dialect's verbs, not of the pronoun, and the two should not be filed together.  
**Pattern:** *hit* [singular verb] (stressed subject at the head of a clause, where the *h* survives) &middot; *hit's* [complement] (*hit's a shame*, *hit's cold*) &middot; *hit don't* / *hit ain't* &middot; [verb] *hit* (object, though the unstressed *it* is commoner here) &middot; *hit* as dummy subject of weather and time  
**Contrast:** standard *it* **is** *hit* with the *h* worn off. Old English had *hit*, Middle English kept it, and standard English lost the *h* in unstressed position and then generalized the *h*-less form to every position. Appalachian and Ozark speech kept the **older distribution** instead: *h* under stress, no *h* elsewhere. That makes *hit* a **conservative** feature and not a corruption — the same relationship the *h*-dropping of ordinary connected speech has to *him* and *her*, where every American says /ˈtɛlɚ/ for *tell her* without noticing. Compare [hisself](#hisself), which is likewise more regular than the standard form it replaces. Do not confuse the pronoun with the verb *hit*, and do not write it in any register where you would not write [them (dialectal demonstrative)](#them-dialectal-demonstrative).

It. The Old English form of *it* with its *h* still attached, kept in Appalachian and Ozark speech under stress.

1. *Hit* was the coldest winter anybody in the county could remember. (regional; standard *it*)
2. *Hit's* a long way to the head of that hollow, and *hit's* farther coming back.
3. Give *hit* here before you drop it. (the stressed and unstressed forms in one sentence, which is the dialect's actual rule)
4. *Hit* don't make a particle of difference now.
5. *Hit* ain't the years, it's the miles.
6. *Hit* rained every day of that trip. (the dummy subject of weather)
7. Standard: *It* was the coldest winter anybody could remember.

### I (hypercorrect in object position)

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ/ &middot; *EYE* &middot; the subject pronoun *I* has no weak form — which is part of why speakers reach for it when they want to sound careful  
**Русский:** «я» вместо «меня» — гиперкоррекция: between you and I вместо me; ошибка
**Type:** personal pronoun, subject form, used **wrongly** in an object slot &middot; **Case:** subject (in a position that requires the **object** case) &middot; **Register:** nonstandard, and specifically a **hypercorrection** — an error produced by trying to avoid an error  
**Person / number:** first person singular.  
**Refers to:** the speaker. The problem is never the reference; it is the case.  
**Agreement:** none — as an object, it triggers no agreement at all, which is exactly why nothing in the sentence flags the mistake.  
**Pattern:** the error appears only inside coordination: [preposition] [noun/pronoun] *and I* (✗*between you and I*, ✗*for my wife and I*) &middot; [verb] [noun] *and I* (✗*he invited Bob and I*) &middot; it essentially never occurs alone (nobody says ✗*he invited I*)  
**Contrast:** the object form is **me**, and the fix is the **deletion test**: remove the other person and listen. ✗*Between you and I* → ✗*between I* → *between you and **me***. ✗*He gave it to my wife and I* → ✗*to I* → *to my wife and **me***. ✗*Thanks for inviting my husband and I* → *inviting **me***. This error is now more common in educated American speech than the [me](#me-nonstandard-as-subject) error it is overcorrecting for, precisely because so many speakers were taught in childhood that *me and him went* was wrong and drew the wrong conclusion — that *me* is always the vulgar choice. It is not: *me* is simply the object form, and after a preposition or a verb it is the **only** correct form. Honesty requires two footnotes. Shakespeare wrote *all debts are cleared between you and I* (*The Merchant of Venice*), so the construction is old; and *between you and I* is now so frequent that some linguists treat coordinated pronouns as a special case. Neither will save you: every American editor, teacher, and standardized test marks it wrong.

The subject pronoun *I* pushed into an object slot inside a coordinated phrase — the classic hypercorrection.

1. ✗ Between you and *I*, the numbers don't add up. → *between you and **me***
2. ✗ He gave the tickets to my brother and *I*. → *to my brother and **me***
3. ✗ Thanks for inviting my wife and *I* to the ceremony. → *my wife and **me***
4. ✗ This is just for Marcus and *I* to sort out. → *for Marcus and **me***
5. ✗ There has always been an understanding between she and *I*. → *between her and **me***
6. Correct: The manager spoke with Priya and *me* for an hour. (deletion test: *spoke with **me***)

**See also:** [me (nonstandard as subject)](#me-nonstandard-as-subject), [I](01-personal.md#i), [me](01-personal.md#me), [after a preposition, always the object case](../README.md#rule-2-after-a-preposition-always-the-object-case)

### me (nonstandard as subject)

**Pronunciation:** /mi/ &middot; *MEE* &middot; unstressed /mi/; in fast speech *me and him* runs to /mi ən ɪm/  
**Русский:** «меня» вместо «я» — прост.; me and him went, обычно в устной речи
**Type:** personal pronoun, object form, used in a **subject** slot &middot; **Case:** object (in a position that requires the **subject** case) &middot; **Register:** nonstandard in writing; extremely common in ordinary American speech at every social level, especially in children's speech  
**Person / number:** first person singular.  
**Refers to:** the speaker.  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the coordinated subject as a whole, which is plural: *Me and him **were** late* — the agreement is not the problem; the case is.  
**Pattern:** the error appears only in coordination and only at the front of a clause: *me and* [noun/pronoun] [verb] (✗*me and Jim went*) &middot; [noun] *and me* [verb] (✗*Jim and me went*) &middot; it never happens alone (nobody says ✗*me went*)  
**Contrast:** the subject form is **I**, and the **deletion test** settles it instantly: ✗*Me and Jim went to the game* → ✗*Me went* → ***I** and Jim went* → politely reordered, ***Jim and I** went to the game*. Two things are worth being honest about. First, this is genuinely nonstandard and will be corrected by teachers and editors, so it is worth training out of your writing. Second, several near neighbors that sound similar are **perfectly standard** and should not be overcorrected: ***It's me*** — standard American English in speech and in edited writing, and the normal form after *be*; *It is I* is not an error either, but it is stiff enough to sound like a costume, and correcting anyone into it is worse than leaving them alone. Likewise *Me too*, *Who wants coffee? — Me*, *Me neither*, and *He's taller than me* (standard in American English alongside the more formal *than I am*). The genuine error is narrow: the object form standing as the subject of a finite verb. Overcorrecting past it produces the opposite mistake, [I in object position](#i-hypercorrect-in-object-position).

The object pronoun *me* used as a subject inside a coordinated phrase — the most common nonstandard pronoun form in American speech.

1. ✗ *Me* and Jim went down to the lake. → ***Jim and I** went down to the lake*
2. ✗ *Me* and my sister split the cost. → ***My sister and I** split the cost*
3. ✗ Him and *me* were the last two out of the building. → ***He and I** were the last two out*
4. ✗ *Me* and her have known each other since third grade. → ***She and I** have known each other*
5. Correct and standard: Who's there? — It's *me*.
6. Correct and standard: She got there before *me*, and she's faster than *me* anyway.

**See also:** [I (hypercorrect in object position)](#i-hypercorrect-in-object-position), [me](01-personal.md#me), [I](01-personal.md#i), [methinks](#methinks), [case follows function](../README.md#rule-1-case-follows-function-not-proximity)

### meself

**Pronunciation:** /mɪˈsɛlf/ &middot; *mih-SELF* &middot; stress on *self*; also /məˈsɛlf/ with a plain schwa. The first syllable is the **weak** *my* /mɪ/ or /mə/, never the full /maɪ/ — which is the entire point of the spelling: *meself* writes down the ordinary unstressed pronunciation of *myself*  
**Русский:** себя, сам — диал. (ирл. и др.); запись безударного произношения myself
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun — a dialectal and eye-dialect spelling of *myself* &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** **dialectal**; Irish English above all, and Newfoundland, older Northern English, and Scots. In American writing it is almost always a novelist's marker for an Irish or Irish-American voice rather than a feature of any American dialect  
**Person / number:** first person **singular**. The plural is *ourselves* — there is no ✗ *usselves* in the same dialects.  
**Refers to:** the **speaker**, and, as a reflexive, only when the speaker is already the subject of the same clause (*I cut meself*). As an intensive it refers to the speaker for emphasis (*I saw it meself*), and in the Irish English cleft it stands where standard English wants a plain *I* (*it was meself that told him*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the clause's verb agrees with its subject, which for the reflexive use is *I* (*I **was** sitting by meself*).  
**Pattern:** [verb] *meself* (reflexive object: *I cut meself*) &middot; [preposition] *meself* (*by meself*, *to meself*) &middot; *I* [verb] *meself* (intensive: *I'll do it meself*) &middot; *be meself* (*I wasn't meself that day*) &middot; *it was meself that* [verb] (the Irish English cleft)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **myself**, and the two are the same word: *myself* is *my* + *self*, and *meself* is what that becomes when the possessive reduces to /mə/ — which happens in every variety of English, standard American included. Nearly every American who says *myself* at speed is saying something close to *meself*; what makes it dialectal is **writing it down**. So do not read it as a case error on the pattern of ✗ *me and him went*: it is a spelling of the weak form, not an object pronoun doing a subject's job, and the deletion test has nothing to catch here. It is also unlike [hisself](#hisself) and [theirselves](#theirselves), which really are different formations — those replace an object form with a possessive one, while *meself* was built on the possessive all along. What is genuinely syntactic in Irish English is the **cleft** — *it was meself that told him*, where standard English wants *it was I who told him* or *I told him* — and that is a difference in grammar, not in spelling.

Myself. The ordinary weak pronunciation of *myself*, written down — an Irish English marker rather than an American form.

1. I'll see to it *meself* and be done with it. (intensive)
2. I cut *meself* on the tin, and it bled for an hour. (reflexive)
3. I was sitting by *meself* at the back of the hall the whole evening.
4. Sure, I wasn't *meself* at all that week.
5. It was *meself* that told him, and I'd tell him again tomorrow. (the Irish English cleft, where standard English wants *I*)
6. I kept the news to *meself* until the papers were signed.
7. Standard American: *I'll see to it **myself***; *I cut **myself** on the tin.*

### mines

**Pronunciation:** /maɪnz/ &middot; *MYNZ* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *lines*; identical in sound to the plural noun *mines* and to the contraction *mine's* (= *mine is*)  
**Русский:** мой, моё — прост.; нелитературная форма mine: that's mines
**Type:** possessive pronoun; **nonstandard** &middot; **Case:** possessive, independent — it stands alone and never precedes a noun (✗*mines car*; the determiner is *my*) &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; a regular feature of African American English and of Chicano English, common in working-class speech generally, and produced by children of every background before school trains it out  
**Person / number:** first person singular. Invariant for the number of the thing possessed: *that book is mines*, *those books are mines*.  
**Refers to:** whatever belongs to the speaker. The possessor is always the speaker; the thing possessed may be anything at all, count or mass, singular or plural.  
**Agreement:** as a subject complement, none of its own; as a **subject**, the verb agrees with what *mines* stands for, not with the *-s* on the pronoun — *mines **is** the blue one* (one car), *mines **are** on the table* (several keys). The *-s* is a possessive marker, not a plural, and reading it as one is the commonest way people mis-hear the form.  
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *is mines* &middot; *mines* as subject (*mines is over there*) &middot; *of mines* (*a friend of mines*, the double genitive — the commonest context of all) &middot; [preposition] *mines* (*next to mines*) &middot; [verb] *mines* (*they took mines*)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **mine**, and that is the one to write. What makes *mines* interesting is that it is a regularization running the **opposite** way from [hisn, hern, ourn, theirn](#hisn-hern-ourn-theirn). Standard English generalized the *-s* of *his* across nearly the whole independent paradigm — *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs* — and then left *mine* behind as the one holdout; *mines* simply finishes the job, which is why it is so easy to produce and so hard to argue with on logical grounds. The *-n* dialects went the other way and generalized *mine*'s own *-n* instead (*yourn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *theirn*). Three systems, one paradigm, and only the middle one gets past an editor. Like every possessive pronoun, it never takes an apostrophe: ✗*mine's* is not a spelling of this word but the contraction of *mine is* (*Mine's the blue one*), and that contraction is perfectly standard — which is part of why the spoken forms are so easily confused.

Mine. The regularized first-person possessive pronoun — logical, widespread in speech, and nonstandard in writing.

1. That jacket on the chair is *mines*. (nonstandard; standard *mine*)
2. *Mines* is the one with the cracked screen. (nonstandard, as subject)
3. A friend of *mines* works over at the plant. (nonstandard, double genitive — the commonest context)
4. She parked right next to *mines*. (nonstandard, after a preposition)
5. They took hers and they took *mines* too. (nonstandard, as the object of a verb)
6. Standard: That jacket is *mine*, and a friend of *mine* has the other one. — and, with the contraction, *Mine's the blue one*.

### nothing, nobody (in double negatives)

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnʌθɪŋ/, /ˈnoʊbədi/ &middot; *NUTH-ing*, *NOH-buh-dee* &middot; the *d* of *nobody* taps to [ɾ]; in negative concord the sentence keeps one falling contour with the main stress on the pronoun — *I didn't do **noth**ing*  
**Русский:** ничего, никто — прост.; двойное отрицание: I didn't do nothing «я ничего не делал»
**Type:** indefinite pronouns used inside **negative concord**, the construction usually called the double negative &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** in Standard American English, marked wrong in all edited writing and on every standardized test — and at the same time completely **systematic and rule-governed** in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class urban vernacular English nationwide. There is no single region to name because there is no single region: negative concord is the majority pattern across the world's Englishes, it was ordinary in Chaucer and Shakespeare, and Standard English is the outlier that dropped it  
**Person / number:** third person singular, both of them. *Nobody* and *no one* are human; *nothing* is non-human.  
**Refers to:** no person (*nobody*, *no one*) and no thing (*nothing*). The reference does not change when the negative is doubled: *I didn't see nothing* means *I saw nothing*, never *I saw something*.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, with or without the second negative: *nobody **knows***, *nothing **works***, *no one **is** coming*, *nobody never **said** a word*. Doubling the negation does nothing to agreement, so ✗*nobody know* is a separate error, not part of this pattern. Compare [none](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#none), which is the one negative pronoun that takes **either** number — *none of them is* and *none of them are* are both standard, with the plural the ordinary default — while *nobody* and *nothing* are singular, always.  
**Pattern:** [negated verb] + *nothing/nobody* (*I didn't say nothing*, *he don't know nobody*) &middot; *nobody/nothing* + [negated verb] (*nobody never called*) &middot; *ain't nobody* / *ain't nothing* + [predicate], with the auxiliary fronted (*Ain't nobody home*) &middot; three or more markings in one clause (*I ain't never told nobody nothing*)  
**Contrast:** Standard English marks the negative **once**, and it offers two ways to do it: negate the verb and use the nonassertive form (*I didn't see **anything***), or leave the verb positive and let the pronoun carry the negation (*I saw **nothing***). Choose one; using both is what gets marked. The schoolroom argument that "two negatives make a positive" is imported from logic and has never described English — *I didn't see nothing* has never once been understood as *I saw something* by any listener. The second negative is **emphasis**, and what it signals socially is which variety the speaker is using, which is exactly why it is worth recognizing, worth respecting in the varieties that own it, and worth keeping out of a cover letter. One lookalike is genuinely standard and does cancel: *not uncommon*, *not unlike*, *not without merit*, where a negative prefix under a negated verb yields a deliberately hedged positive. Another is not a double negative at all: *I don't know nothing about it* is concord, but *I don't know **anything** about it* is the standard equivalent, and *I know nothing about it* is its more formal twin.

Nothing; nobody — negated twice. The negative pronouns as they appear in negative concord, where the second negative reinforces the first instead of canceling it.

1. I didn't do *nothing* wrong. (nonstandard; standard *I didn't do anything wrong* or *I did nothing wrong*)
2. He don't know *nobody* on that side of town. (nonstandard; standard *doesn't know anybody*)
3. *Nobody* never said a word about the money. (nonstandard as subject; standard *Nobody ever said*)
4. Ain't *nobody* home. (vernacular, with the auxiliary fronted; standard *Nobody is home*)
5. She ain't got *nothing* to prove to *nobody*. (nonstandard, marked three times over)
6. Standard: I didn't see *anything*, and I told *nobody* about it. (each negative marked once)

**See also:** [nothing (negative concord)](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord), [ain't nobody](07-indefinite-compound.md#aint-nobody), [nobody](07-indefinite-compound.md#nobody), [nothing](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing), [none](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#none), [no](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no)

### ourself

**Pronunciation:** /ɑrˈsɛlf/ &middot; *are-SELF* &middot; also /aʊrˈsɛlf/; stress on *self*  
**Русский:** мы сами (об одном лице) — устар., «королевское мы»; в совр. речи нелитературно
**Type:** reflexive pronoun; **archaic** in the royal use, **nonstandard** in the modern one &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** archaic and ceremonial (the royal or editorial *we*); nonstandard in present-day general use  
**Person / number:** first person; **plural in form, singular in reference** — that mismatch is the whole point of the word.  
**Refers to:** a *we* that names one person: a monarch using the royal plural, an editor using the editorial *we*, or, in the modern nonstandard use, a *we* that names a collective treated as one body (*as a nation, we must ask ourself*).  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the clause takes the plural verb that *we* requires (*we ourself **have** decided*, never ✗*we ourself has*).  
**Pattern:** *we* [verb] *ourself* &middot; *we ourself* [verb] (intensive) &middot; [preposition] *ourself* (*by ourself*)  
**Contrast:** the standard modern reflexive for *we* is **ourselves**, full stop: *we gave ourselves an extra day*. *Ourself* is right only where *we* refers to one person by convention — a king, a pope, an editorial page — and even there it is a period effect. The modern collective use (*we need to ask ourself what kind of company we want to be*) is a real and spreading pattern, but it is not yet standard and an editor will change it to *ourselves*. The exact parallel is *themself*, the singular reflexive of singular *they* (*someone locked themself out*), which unlike *ourself* **is** now accepted in much edited American prose alongside *themselves* — the two words are at very different points on the same road.

The singular reflexive of a plural-form *we*: the royal and editorial reflexive, and a modern nonstandard collective one.

**1. The royal or editorial reflexive (archaic, ceremonial).** With a *we* that names one person by convention — a monarch, a pope, a judge speaking for the bench, an editorial page. Correct within that convention and nowhere else.

**2. The collective reflexive (modern, nonstandard).** With a *we* that names a group treated as a single body: *as a nation, we must ask ourself what we have become*. Spreading, but an editor will change it to *ourselves*.

1. "*Ourself* will mingle with society, and play the humble host." (*Macbeth* — the royal *we*)
2. We have considered the petition and will rule upon it *ourself*. (ceremonial)
3. In this column we have never allowed *ourself* the luxury of a prediction. (editorial *we*)
4. ✗ As a company, we should ask *ourself* whether this is who we want to be. → *ourselves*
5. ✗ We have to be honest with *ourself* about the numbers. → *ourselves*
6. Standard: We gave *ourselves* an extra day to finish the audit.

**See also:** [theirselves](#theirselves), [hisself](#hisself), [oneself](#oneself), [ourselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#ourselves), [themself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themself), [we (generic)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#we-generic)

### theirselves

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈsɛlvz/ &middot; *thair-SELVZ* &middot; the singular variant *theirself* is /ðɛrˈsɛlf/  
**Русский:** себя, сами — прост., диал.; нелитературная форма themselves
**Type:** reflexive and intensive pronoun; **nonstandard** &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; regionally ordinary in Southern, Appalachian, and rural vernacular English and in African American English; marked wrong in all writing  
**Person / number:** third person plural (the variant *theirself* is third person singular, used with singular *they*).  
**Refers to:** a plural antecedent already serving as the subject of the same clause — exactly what *themselves* refers to.  
**Agreement:** none of its own.  
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [verb] *theirselves* &middot; [preposition] *theirselves* (*by theirselves*) &middot; [subject] *theirselves* [verb] (intensive)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **themselves**. Like [hisself](#hisself), *theirselves* is built on the possessive (*their* + *selves*), which is the pattern of *myself*, *yourself*, *ourselves*, and *yourselves* — so it is the regular form, and it is still nonstandard. Keep it apart from **themself**, which is a different matter entirely: *themself* is the singular reflexive of singular *they* (*whoever left this should claim themself*), it is built on the standard object base *them*, and it is increasingly accepted in edited American prose. Note what is and is not in dispute there: **singular *they* is standard American English** — recognized by Chicago, the AP stylebook, APA, MLA, and Merriam-Webster, for an unknown or unspecified person (*someone left their coat*) and for a person who uses it — and the only live question is which reflexive to build on it, *themself* or *themselves*. *Theirself* — possessive base, singular — is not a candidate in either camp, and is nonstandard like its plural.

Themselves. The regularized plural reflexive: common in speech, nonstandard in writing.

1. They built the whole deck *theirselves*. (nonstandard; standard *themselves*)
2. The kids were out there playing by *theirselves* all afternoon. (nonstandard)
3. They got *theirselves* into this mess. (nonstandard)
4. They *theirselves* never said a word about it. (nonstandard, intensive)
5. Folks around here mostly keep to *theirselves*. (nonstandard)
6. Standard: They talked *themselves* into buying the bigger truck.

**See also:** [hisself](#hisself), [ourself](#ourself), [themselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themselves), [themself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themself), [reflexive formation](../README.md#reflexive-formation)

### them (dialectal demonstrative)

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛm/ &middot; *THEM* &middot; weak /ðəm/ → /əm/, so *give me them apples* comes out /ˈɡɪmi əm ˈæpəlz/. The weak form is a clue to the word's origin: this is the ordinary object pronoun doing a new job, weak form and all.  
**Русский:** те, эти — прост., диал.; them apples вместо those apples
**Type:** demonstrative pronoun; **nonstandard** &middot; **Case:** the object form, used in both subject and object slots &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and older working-class speech nationwide, and in African American English  
**Person / number:** third person plural. There is no singular counterpart — *them* covers only what standard English calls *those*.  
**Refers to:** plural things or people already identified or physically present — the same job standard English gives to *those*. It points; it does not merely refer back.  
**Agreement:** plural verb in ordinary use — *them are the ones I want*, *them were good years*. The one systematic exception is the frozen phrase ***them's***, with a singular verb, in sayings like *them's the breaks* and *them's fighting words*.  
**Pattern:** *them* [plural verb] (*them are mine*) &middot; [verb] *them* (*I'll take them over there*) &middot; *them's* [noun phrase] (frozen: *them's the breaks*) &middot; *them there* (double-marked emphatic)  
**Contrast:** the standard demonstrative pronoun is **those**: *those are mine*, *those were good years*. The nonstandard *them* is the personal object pronoun recruited into demonstrative work, which is why it keeps the weak form /əm/ that no true demonstrative has — *this*, *that*, *these*, and *those* are pointing words and carry stress. Two frozen phrases have carried it into general American English, where speakers who would never say *them apples* still say ***them's the breaks*** and ***them's fighting words*** without a second thought; those are quotations of the dialect, not the dialect itself. Note the split with the determiner: *them apples*, with a noun following, is the **determiner** use and is covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them); the entry you are reading is the pronoun standing alone. The emphatic *them there* doubles the pointing and is covered there as well.

Those. The object pronoun *them* used as a demonstrative pronoun — nonstandard, but preserved for everyone in two famous sayings.

**1. Demonstrative pronoun (nonstandard, regional).** Standing alone for *those*, in subject or object position: *Them are the ones I want*, *I'll take them right there*. Rural, Southern, Appalachian, and older working-class speech.

**2. Frozen *them's* (general American).** In *them's the breaks* and *them's fighting words*, with a singular verb, used by millions of speakers who would never produce sense 1. These are quotations of the dialect rather than the dialect itself.

1. *Them's* the breaks. (frozen; general American, and the way most Americans meet this form)
2. *Them's* fighting words, mister. (frozen)
3. *Them* are the ones I was telling you about. (nonstandard; standard *those*)
4. I'll take *them* right there, the ones on the bottom shelf. (nonstandard as a pointing pronoun)
5. *Them* were good years, before the mill closed. (nonstandard)
6. Standard: *Those* are the ones I was telling you about.

### them that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛm ˈðæt/ &middot; *them-THAT* &middot; the first word takes the ordinary weak form /ðəm/ → /əm/, so the phrase comes out /əm ˈðæt/ and, at conversational speed, close to /mˈðæt/ — the *th* of *them* disappears entirely, exactly as it does in *give 'em*  
**Русский:** те, кто — прост., диал.; сращение «те» и «которые»: them that knows
**Type:** fused relative pronoun phrase — the demonstrative *them* as head plus the relative *that*, together meaning "those who" &middot; **Variants:** ***them as*** (*them as knows*), the same construction with the older relative *as*, Northern English and its American descendants; ***them what*** (*them what has, gets*), the third variant and the most heavily stigmatized of the three &middot; **Case:** none — the phrase serves as subject and as object alike &middot; **Register:** **archaic** in general English — it is the King James Bible's ordinary phrasing — and **nonstandard and regional** in modern American speech: Southern, Appalachian, African American English, and older rural speech nationwide  
**Person / number:** third person **plural**. Older English had the matching singulars *he that* and *him that* (*him that overcometh*), and those are archaic rather than dialectal; modern dialect keeps only the plural.  
**Refers to:** the **people** who do or are whatever the relative clause says — human, plural, and always characterized by the clause rather than named. It never refers to a thing (for things, *them that* gives way to *the ones that*), and never to a clause.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb after the phrase as a whole: *them that wait **are** blessed*, ✗ *them that wait is*. The verb **inside** the relative clause follows the dialect's own concord instead — standard English gives it a plural (*them that wait*), and much regional speech gives it the *-s* (*them that waits*, *them that has*). Both are recorded, and the *-s* is a feature of the dialect's verb system rather than a slip.  
**Pattern:** *them that* [verb] (subject: *them that work get paid*) &middot; [verb] *them that* [clause] (object: *give it to them that need it*) &middot; [preposition] *them that* &middot; *them that has, gets* (the proverb) &middot; *them as* / *them what* (the variants)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **those who** — or, for things, *those that* and *the ones that*. Three things are worth keeping straight. The *them* here is the **demonstrative** *them* of [them (dialectal demonstrative)](#them-dialectal-demonstrative), not the object pronoun, which is why it can head a subject at all; that is the same *them* as in *them apples*. The construction is **archaic before it is dialectal** — the King James Bible uses *them that* freely (*for them that honour me I will honour*), and readers who would call it an error in speech read it every Sunday without blinking. And put a **noun** after *them* and the dictionary line has been crossed: *them people that came* has *them* modifying *people*, which is a determiner and belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them).

Those who. The fused relative of the King James Bible and of Southern and Appalachian speech.

1. "For *them that* honour me I will honour." (1 Samuel 2:30, King James Version)
2. *Them that* work get paid; *them that* don't, don't. (regional; standard *those who*)
3. Give the seed to *them that* will plant it, not to *them that* will sell it. (object of a preposition)
4. *Them that* has, gets — that has always been the way of it around here. (with the dialect's own *-s* inside the clause)
5. She had no patience with *them as* talked and never did a thing about it. (the *as* variant)
6. *Them what* built the place never got paid for it. (the *what* variant)
7. Standard: *Those who* work get paid; give the seed to *those who* will plant it.

### them'uns

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɛmənz/ &middot; *THEM-unz* &middot; often /ˈðɛmz/ at speed in some communities, and the *th* weakens to /ð/ or drops as it does in every form of *them*; also spelled *them uns*, *themuns*, and *them'ns*  
**Русский:** они, те люди — диал., разг.; Аппалачи, из them ones
**Type:** third-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none for most speakers — the same form serves as subject and object (*them'uns are late* / *go with them'uns*) &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; Appalachian, the South Midland, and the Ozarks; old-fashioned and receding, and stigmatized outside the region  
**Person / number:** third person **plural**.  
**Refers to:** the **other** group — the people who are not the speaker's people. Human in ordinary use, and it very often carries a faint edge of distance or opposition, which is why it so naturally answers [we'uns](#weuns) in the same breath: *we'uns* draws the line and *them'uns* names who is on the far side of it.  
**Agreement:** **plural** verb: *them'uns are*, *them'uns were*, *do them'uns know*. Never ✗*them'uns is*.  
**Pattern:** *them'uns* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *them'uns* &middot; [preposition] *them'uns* (*with them'uns*, *for them'uns*) &middot; *them'uns over yonder* (with a pointing adverb) &middot; *we'uns and them'uns* (the paired contrast)  
**Contrast:** the standard forms are **they** and **them**, or, when the pointing is the point, *those people*. *Them'uns* is *them ones*, built exactly as [we'uns](#weuns) is *we ones* and [you'uns](#youuns) is *you ones*, and the three are one Scots-Irish-descended paradigm that came into Appalachia together. The singular members of that paradigm are [this'n and that'n](#thisn-thatn), where *one* has worn down to a syllabic *-n* rather than *-uns*. Note where *them'uns* sits relative to [them (dialectal demonstrative)](#them-dialectal-demonstrative): plain *them* for *those* is far more widespread and can be heard nationwide, while *them'uns* is narrowly regional and much rarer. And note that *them'uns* takes an apostrophe because a vowel really has been lost from *ones* — unlike [hisn](#hisn-hern-ourn-theirn) and [yourn](#yourn), where nothing was contracted and the apostrophe is an error.

They; them; those people. The Appalachian *them ones*, the far-side mate of *we'uns*.

1. *Them'uns* are coming up the road right now. (nonstandard; standard *they* or *those people*)
2. We'uns will take the near field and *them'uns* can have the far one. (the matched pair)
3. He never had any use for *them'uns* up at the mine office. (object of a preposition)
4. *Them'uns* over yonder have been fishing that hole since daylight.
5. Ask *them'uns* whether they mean to stay for supper.
6. Standard: *They're* coming up the road; *we'll* take the near field and *they* can have the far one.

### this'n, that'n

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɪsn̩/, /ˈðætn̩/ &middot; *THISS-n*, *THAT-n* &middot; two syllables each, with a syllabic /n̩/ carrying the second — there is no vowel left in it at all; the *t* of *that'n* taps to [ɾ] between vowels in *that'n over there*; the plurals are *these'ns* /ˈðizn̩z/ and [them'uns](#themuns)  
**Русский:** этот, тот — диал., разг.; стяжения this one и that one, мн. ч. these'ns
**Type:** demonstrative pronouns; regional and **nonstandard** — reduced forms of *this one* and *that one* &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; Appalachian, Southern, South Midland, and Ozark speech, and a standard marker of it in fiction and country songwriting  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**. The plurals are *these'ns* and *them'uns*, and there is no possessive.  
**Refers to:** one thing or person being pointed at or picked out of a set — *this'n* for the near one, *that'n* for the far one, on exactly the same axis as standard *this one* and *that one*. Things far more often than people, and always with something else available to contrast it with.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb: *this'n is broke*, *that'n was the good one*, ✗*that'n are*.  
**Pattern:** *this'n* / *that'n* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *that'n* (*I'll take that'n*) &middot; *this'n here* / *that'n yonder* (double-marked with a pointing adverb) &middot; *this'n or that'n* &middot; *that'n of yourn*  
**Contrast:** the standard forms are **this one** and **that one**, and the reduction is regular: *one* loses everything but its *n*, which then becomes the second syllable on its own. That makes the apostrophe honest — a vowel really has been lost — unlike the *-n* possessives [hisn and yourn](#hisn-hern-ourn-theirn), where nothing was ever contracted and the apostrophe is a mistake. Two boundaries are worth marking. Put a noun after the demonstrative and you have a **determiner**, not a pronoun: *that there truck* and *them apples* belong to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#that-there). And keep *that'n* apart from [t'other](#tother), which also means "the other one" but specifically picks the **second of two**, where *that'n* simply points.

This one; that one. The Appalachian and Southern reductions of the demonstrative pronouns.

1. I'll take *that'n* right there, the one with the red handle. (nonstandard; standard *that one*)
2. *This'n* here is broke, but *that'n* still runs. (nonstandard, the pair in contrast)
3. *That'n* was the good year; every one since has been dry.
4. Which of the pups do you want — *this'n* or the one asleep by the stove?
5. She kept *this'n* and gave *that'n* to her sister. (both as objects)
6. Standard: *I'll take **that one** right there*; *this one is broken, but that one still runs*.

### t'other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtʌðɚ/ &middot; *TUH-ther* &middot; two syllables, stress on the first, voiced *th*; also spelled *tother* and *t'othern*, and the older spelling without the apostrophe is the historically correct one, since nothing was ever cut out of it  
**Русский:** другой, второй — устар. и диал.; «тот другой», из the other
**Type:** indefinite and demonstrative pronoun, archaic and dialectal — "the other one" &middot; **Case:** none — the same form serves as subject and object &middot; **Register:** archaic in general English and regional in American speech; Appalachian, Southern, and older rural usage, and common in British dialect writing and in Twain  
**Person / number:** third person **singular**; the plural is *t'others*. No possessive.  
**Refers to:** **the second of two** already identified — the one that is not the one just mentioned. It needs a set of exactly two to work in; with three or more, English wants *the others* or *the rest*.  
**Agreement:** **singular** verb: *t'other is broke*, *t'other was late*. The plural *t'others* takes a plural verb.  
**Pattern:** *one … and t'other* (the frozen pairing: *one or t'other of them*) &middot; *this one or t'other* &middot; [verb] *t'other* &middot; *t'other of the two* &middot; the determiner use, *t'other day* / *t'other night*, which belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#tother)  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **the other**, and the history is the best part of this entry. Middle English wrote *that other*; the final *t* of *that* got re-analyzed as the start of the next word, giving *the tother*, and *t'other* is what that left behind. The same misdivision — a **metanalysis** — gave English *a newt* from *an ewt*, *a nickname* from *an ekename*, and *an apron* from *a napron*, and it explains why the apostrophe is misleading: the *t* is not a contracted *the*, it is the tail of *that*. Two limits. *T'other* means the second of **two**; for one of many, write *another* or *the others*. And *t'other* standing in front of a noun (*t'other day*, *t'other night*) is a determiner, covered as [t'other](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#tother) in the determiner dictionary; the entry you are reading is the pronoun standing alone.

The other one — the second of two. An archaic pronoun kept alive in Southern and Appalachian speech, and a fossil of a four-hundred-year-old misdivision.

1. One of them wanted to sell and *t'other* wanted to hold on another year. (nonstandard; standard *the other*)
2. Take this rope and give me *t'other*.
3. It was one or *t'other* — there was no third choice on the ballot.
4. This'n runs and *t'other* has been up on blocks since the spring.
5. The two brothers never agreed: what one built, *t'other* tore down.
6. Standard: *One wanted to sell and **the other** wanted to hold on.*

### we'uns

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwiənz/ &middot; *WEE-unz* &middot; often /winz/ at conversational speed; the object variant *us'uns* is /ˈʌsənz/ &middot; *USS-unz*  
**Русский:** мы — диал., разг.; Аппалачи, из we ones; объектный вариант us'uns
**Type:** first-person plural pronoun; regional &middot; **Case:** none for most speakers — the same form serves as subject and object (*we'uns are going*, *come with we'uns*) — though some speakers keep *us'uns* for the object and *we'uns* for the subject, preserving the standard split &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; Appalachian and Southern Midland, old-fashioned and receding even at home; stigmatized everywhere else, and the single most reliable dialect marker a novelist can hang on a mountain character  
**Variants:** ***us'uns*** (the object form) and its reduced spelling ***us'ns*** /ˈʌsn̩z/ &middot; *USS-nz*, along with *we'ns* and the apostrophe-free *weuns* and *usuns*. They are one word in several spellings, not separate pronouns; the plural mate is [them'uns](#themuns)  
**Person / number:** first person plural.  
**Refers to:** the speaker together with the speaker's own group — family, household, neighbors, side of the ridge. Human only. Its work is to draw a line between my people and yours, which is why it so often turns up in the same breath as [you'uns](#youuns).  
**Agreement:** plural verb: *we'uns are*, *we'uns were*, *do we'uns need*, *we'uns have*. Never ✗*we'uns is*.  
**Pattern:** *we'uns* [plural verb] &middot; [verb] *we'uns* &middot; [preposition] *we'uns* / *us'uns* (*with we'uns*, *for us'uns*) &middot; *we'uns'* [noun] (possessive, rare in writing) &middot; *we'uns and you'uns* (the paired contrast)  
**Contrast:** *we'uns* is *we ones*, built exactly as [you'uns](#youuns) is built from *you ones*, and the two are matched halves of one Scots-Irish-descended system that the settlers of Appalachia brought with them. But they are not doing the same job, and that is the interesting part. *You'uns* fills a genuine hole — standard English has no plural *you* — which is why it and its relatives ([y'all](#yall), [youse](#youse), [yinz](#yinz)) have thrived and spread. *We'uns* fills nothing: *we* is already unambiguously plural, so the form adds no distinction the standard pronoun lacks, and it has faded faster and further as a result. Nobody is spreading *we'uns* nationally the way *y'all* has spread. The standard forms are **we** (subject) and **us** (object).

We; us — the Appalachian *we ones*, the first-person mate of *you'uns*.

1. *We'uns* are going down to the church supper. (nonstandard; standard *we*)
2. *You'uns* take the truck and *we'uns* will walk. (nonstandard; the matched pair, one clause each)
3. He never had much use for *we'uns*. (nonstandard as the object of a preposition; standard *us*)
4. *We'uns* didn't get a drop of rain all summer. (nonstandard)
5. Come on down and eat with *us'uns*. (the object variant)
6. Standard: We're going down to the church supper, and you can walk with us.

### yourn

**Pronunciation:** /jʊrn/ &middot; *YOORN* &middot; also /jɔrn/, rhyming with *born*  
**Русский:** твой, ваш — прост., диал.; нелитературная форма yours
**Type:** possessive pronoun; **nonstandard** and regional &middot; **Case:** possessive, independent &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; Appalachian, Southern Midland, and older rural speech; a literary marker of that speech in fiction  
**Person / number:** second person, singular or plural alike — it does not distinguish number, any more than standard *yours* does.  
**Refers to:** what belongs to the person or people addressed. The possessor is the addressee; the thing possessed may be anything.  
**Agreement:** as a subject complement, none; as a subject, the verb agrees with what *yourn* stands for (*yourn is the blue one*, *yourn are in the truck*).  
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *is yourn* &middot; *yourn* as subject (*yourn's the one on the left*) &middot; *of yourn* (*a cousin of yourn*) &middot; parallel forms: *hisn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *theirn*  
**Contrast:** the standard form is **yours**, and the whole family is nonstandard: [*hisn* for *his*, *hern* for *hers*, *ourn* for *ours*, *theirn* for *theirs*](#hisn-hern-ourn-theirn). The pattern is an old and perfectly regular one — these forms were built by analogy with *mine* and [thine](#thine), the two possessive pronouns that already ended in *-n*, and the analogy simply spread to the rest of the paradigm in the dialects of northern and western England before crossing to Appalachia. Standard English went the other way and generalized *-s* instead (*yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*), so the two systems are mirror images, each regular in its own terms. Note that *mine* is shared by both — and that a third set of speakers finishes the standard pattern instead, saying [mines](#mines). None of these forms ever takes an apostrophe: ✗*your'n* and ✗*yourn's* are wrong twice over.

Yours. The Appalachian and Southern Midland possessive pronoun, built on the old *-n* pattern of *mine* and *thine*.

1. That truck out front is *yourn*, ain't it? (nonstandard; standard *yours*)
2. Is this coat *yourn* or hisn? (nonstandard)
3. *Yourn* is the one with the blue handle. (nonstandard)
4. A cousin of *yourn* stopped by the store this morning. (nonstandard)
5. We keep ourn in the shed and they keep *theirn* in the barn. (nonstandard)
6. Standard: That truck out front is *yours*, isn't it?
