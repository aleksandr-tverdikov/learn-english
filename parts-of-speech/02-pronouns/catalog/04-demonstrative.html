Demonstrative pronouns#

The pointing pronouns — the words that stand in for a noun and at the same time say where it is: near the speaker or away from the speaker, in the room, in time, or in the conversation itself.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

English runs a two-way demonstrative system — near this/these against far that/those — crossed with number, and apart from the reflexives (yourself → yourselves) these are the only pronouns whose plural is the same word reshaped rather than a different word: this → these and that → those, against I → we and he → they. Case, the difficulty that runs through the rest of the pronoun class, does not exist here: this, that, these, and those have no subject or object forms, and one shape does every job. What sets the class apart is what it is allowed to point at. A demonstrative pronoun can stand not only for a thing but for a whole clause, a whole paragraph, or something the speaker has not said yet — They cancelled the trip. That surprised me. and Listen to this: — which no other pronoun except it does as freely. That power is also the trap: a demonstrative whose antecedent is an entire idea rather than a noun is the commonest source of vagueness in American academic and business prose, and the repair is always the same, which is to hang a noun on it (That decision surprised me). The boundary this file sits on is the second trap — every word here is also a determiner, and only the standalone use is a pronoun.

A note on case. Demonstratives are the one pronoun class with no case at all. Those is those as a subject (Those are mine), as an object (I'll take those), and after a preposition (with those), so the I/me, he/him, who/whom choices never come up. Case enters this file at two points, and at neither one is it the demonstrative that inflects. The first is the personal pronoun after be in the telephone formula this is he / this is she, where the traditional subject-case rule and ordinary American speech disagree, and where It's me is standard English rather than an error. The second is the nonstandard them that replaces those (Them are the ones), where the object form of they has been pushed into a subject slot — the same shape of mistake as ✗ Me and him went, and nonstandard for the same reason. The coordination problems the subject/object rule creates belong to the personal pronouns, and they run in both directions. The object form is required after a preposition — ✓ between you and me, ✓ He gave it to Bob and me (both mes are objects of to and between) — and in a plain direct object: ✓ They invited my wife and me. That makes ✗ between you and I and ✗ They invited my wife and I hypercorrections: a subject form dropped into an object slot by a speaker trying to sound careful. ✗ Me and him went is the opposite error, an object form standing in a subject slot; the standard sentence is ✓ He and I went, and ✓ She and I went. One test settles every one of them — delete the other person and listen: He gave it to Bob and me ✓ (not ✗ to I), They invited my wife and me ✓ (not ✗ invited I), She and I went ✓ (not ✗ Me went). See also It is I vs. It's me.

A note on stress and weak forms. Nearly every pronoun in English reduces, and hearing the weak form is a learner's real problem: him /ɪm/ (tell 'im), her /ɚ/ (give 'er the keys), them /ðəm/ and, after a consonant, /əm/ (take 'em), you /jə/ (did ya see it), he /i/ (where'd 'e go). Demonstratives are the exception. They are pointing words, they carry the stress in their clause, and they keep their full vowels — /ðɪs/, /ðæt/, /ðiz/, /ðoʊz/ — even in fast speech. The reductions that belong in this file are on the words around them: the in the same is the ordinary weak /ðə/; such drops to /sətʃ/; of in that of drops to /əv/ and the /t/ in front of it flaps, [ˈðæɾəv]; and the dialect them of them's the breaks reduces to /ðəm/ or /əm/ precisely because it is the object pronoun doing a demonstrative's job.

A note on the three thats. Three different words are spelled that, and telling them apart is worth more than any other single fact in this file. The demonstrative pronoun is stressed, keeps its full vowel /ðæt/, and accepts this in its place: That surprised me → This surprised me. The relative pronoun (the car that we sold) and the conjunction (I know that she left) both reduce to /ðət/ and can usually be dropped outright — the car we sold, I know she left — which the demonstrative never can (✗ surprised me). Two tests, then: swap in this, and try deleting. The demonstrative is the only one of the three that takes this, and the only one that cannot be dropped. The deletion test has a limit worth knowing: a relative that can go only when it is the object of its own clause, so the subject relative in the car that broke down has to stay.

A note on pointing at people. A demonstrative pronoun refers to a person only while identifying or introducing one — This is my sister, Who's that?, That's Dan in the blue jacket, and on the telephone This is she. Once the person is established, English switches to a personal pronoun — he, she, or, for one person of unknown or unstated gender, the singular they that is now standard American English, in edited prose as much as in speech, and is accepted by the major American style guides, the AP Stylebook, Chicago, MLA, and APA among them, though AP and Chicago still prefer a recast where one comes easily: ✗ That went home early → He went home early, and Somebody left a coat. They must be coming back. (see singular they). The single place a demonstrative names people freely is in front of a relative clause, where those who means "the people who" — and there the antecedent rules of the relative pronouns take over: who for people, which for things, that for both, whose for both as well (those whose names are called, a house whose roof leaks), English never having built a separate possessive for things.

The near set: this and these#

The proximal pair: what is near the speaker in space, current in time, or still open in the conversation. Only number separates them, and the line is hard — this is singular, these is plural, and there is no third option.

these#

Pronunciation
/ðiz/ · THEEZ · no weak form — the vowel stays long even in fast speech, because the demonstrative carries the stress in its clause
Русский
эти — указательное местоимение мн. числа о близком; ставится только с мн. числом глагола
Type
demonstrative pronoun (plural, proximal)
Case
none — one form serves as subject, object, and object of a preposition
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person plural; no gender distinction, no case forms
Refers to
more than one thing near the speaker — near in the room, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the conversation currently open. It points at objects freely; it points at people only in the introducing frame These are my parents, never as an ordinary subject for people already known (✗ These are exhausted → They are exhausted). Unlike singular this, it cannot take a single clause as its antecedent — one clause counts as one thing, so this or that handles it — though it can gather up two or more statements at once (They cut the budget and froze hiring. These are the reasons we can't hire.).
Agreement
plural verb, always — These are the ones. These were on sale. These don't fit. Never ✗ these is. If what you are pointing at is singular or uncountable, the pronoun changes rather than the verb: This is the last of it.
Pattern
these + [plural verb] · [verb] these (I'll take these) · [preposition] these (with these, out of these) · these are [plural noun phrase] (identifying) · these … those (contrastive pair) · these + reduced relative (these left over from Friday)
Contrast
Two axes cross. Number — these is the plural of this and takes plural countable referents only. Distance — these is near (in space, in the current stretch of time, or in what is still live in the talk), where those is away, past, or finished. Pronoun against determiner — the pronoun is the whole noun phrase (these are taken, I'll take these), the determiner leans on a noun (these seats are taken). Two smaller traps: the partitive idiom is those of us / those of you whatever the distance, never ✗ these of us; and these ones, ordinary in speech and in Canadian and British usage, is struck out by most American copy editors — write these, or these two, or the red ones.

Points at more than one thing near the speaker — in the room, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the discussion currently in hand.

  2. Are these yours, or did somebody leave them behind?
  3. I'll take these and leave the rest.
  4. These were selling for twice as much last month.
  5. These don't fit anymore.
  6. These are my parents, Ray and Dolores.
  7. Everything in the box is cracked except these.

this#

Pronunciation
/ðɪs/ · THIS · keeps its full vowel as a pronoun; the casual /ðəs/ heard in this morning belongs to the unstressed determiner, and a standalone this is normally the stressed word in its clause
Русский
это, этот — указывает на близкое, а также на целую высказанную или предстоящую мысль
Type
demonstrative pronoun (singular, proximal)
Case
none — one form for every position
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular; no gender distinction, no case forms
Refers to
one thing near the speaker, or a mass of something (This is the last of the coffee); a stretch of time containing now; a whole preceding clause or idea (They cut the budget again. This is why we can't hire.); and, pointing forward, something the speaker is about to say or show (Listen to this:). Agreement
singular verb, always — This is mine. This was expensive. This doesn't work. The verb agrees with this and with nothing downstream of it: This is the reason our costs went up, This is what the neighbors complained about. Pattern
this + [singular verb] · [verb] this (Read this) · [preposition] this (after this, because of this) · this is [noun phrase] (identifying or introducing) · this pointing forward, followed by a colon and the thing itself (It comes down to this:) · this is + [personal pronoun] on the telephone (This is he) · this … that (contrastive pair)
Contrast
Against that. Proximity in space, but in discourse something subtler: this keeps a topic open and that closes it. Pointing back at the same fact, That settles it means the matter is done, while This changes everything means we now have to deal with it. Pointing forward, only this works — ✓ Listen to this:, ✗ Listen to that: about something not yet said. Against these. Number, and the boundary is absolute. Pronoun against determiner. The pronoun stands alone (this is late); the determiner leans on a noun (this report is late). The writing trap. A this whose antecedent is an entire clause is not ungrammatical — it is everywhere in speech and in good journalism — but in formal prose it makes the reader guess, and the standard repair is to attach a summary noun: not This means we're behind, but This delay means we're behind.

Points at a single thing — or a mass, a moment, or an idea — that is near the speaker: near in the room, near in time, or near in the flow of the conversation.

1. In space. The thing near me, or nearer than the alternative: This is the one I meant, not that.

2. In time. The stretch of time containing now, when it is the subject of comment: This is the busiest week of the year.

3. Pointing back at a whole clause. They cut the budget again. This is why we can't hire. Standard, and the source of the vagueness problem above.

4. Pointing forward. Listen to this: / It comes down to this: — the demonstrative announces something the hearer has not heard yet. That cannot do this.

5. Identifying and introducing people. Face to face, This is my sister, Nadia; on the telephone, This is Karen or, answering for oneself, This is she — see this is he / this is she.

  1. This is the last of the coffee.
  2. Take this and put it in the mail.
  3. They cut the budget again. This is why we can't hire.
  4. Listen to this: they want the whole report by Friday.
  5. Is this yours?
  6. This is my sister, Nadia.
  7. This doesn't feel right to me.
  8. I've had about enough of this.

The far set: that and those#

The distal pair, split by number on exactly the same line. Beyond distance they take on two jobs the near pair does not: closing off what has already been said, and standing in for a noun rather than repeating it.

that#

Pronunciation
/ðæt/ · THAT · never reduced — the demonstrative pronoun always keeps the full vowel /ðæt/; the weak /ðət/ belongs to the two other words spelled that, the relative pronoun and the conjunction, and that difference is the fastest way to tell them apart by ear
Русский
то, тот — указывает на далёкое или прошедшее, а также на всё только что сказанное
Type
demonstrative pronoun (singular, distal)
Case
none — one form for every position
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular; no gender distinction, no case forms
Refers to
one thing away from the speaker, or a mass, or a finished stretch of time; a whole preceding clause, sentence, or run of discourse (They cancelled the trip. That surprised me.), which is its most characteristic job and one no other pronoun but it does as cleanly; and, in that of, a noun already named. With people it is confined to identifying — Who's that? — That's my brother — and once the person is established English switches to he, she, or they: ✗ That went home early.
Agreement
singular verb, always — That is enough. That was a mistake. That doesn't help. Plural referents take those. A singular complement, and an amount, both keep the singular: That is the reason the numbers moved, That is twenty dollars. A plural complement is the one soft spot: That was my parents at the door is ordinary speech, but edited prose changes the pronoun along with the verb — Those were my parents.
Pattern
that + [singular verb] · [verb] that (Don't say that) · [preposition] that (after that, besides that, with that) · that's [noun phrase] (identifying) · that of [noun phrase] (formal substitution) · that which [clause] (formal) · frozen idioms: at that, that is, that said, that's that
Contrast
Three separate contrasts share this headword. Against this: distance and discourse status — that is away, past, finished, or the hearer's; this is near, current, mine, or still to come. Responding to something another person has just said, Americans overwhelmingly reach for that (That's a good point, That's not what I heard). Against those: number. Against the other two thats: the demonstrative pronoun is stressed /ðæt/ and can be replaced by this; the relative (the car that we sold) and the conjunction (I know that she left) reduce to /ðət/ and can usually be dropped. Apply both tests — swap in this, then try deleting — and the demonstrative is the only one that accepts this and the only one that cannot go. The deletion test has a limit: a relative that can be dropped only when it is the object of its own clause, so the car that broke down keeps it. And the same writing caution as this: pointing at a whole clause is standard, but in formal prose give it a summary noun (That decision surprised everyone).

Points at a single thing, a mass, a finished time, or a whole idea that is away from the speaker — across the room, back in the past, or already dealt with in the conversation.

1. In space. The thing farther off, or nearer the hearer: Hand me that, would you? A gesture or a glance usually goes with it.

2. In time. A stretch of time cut off from now: That was the summer we lived in Denver.

3. Pointing back at a whole clause or idea. They cancelled the trip a week out. That surprised everyone. The demonstrative treats what was said as finished and comments on it.

4. Identifying. Who's that at the door? / That's my brother in the blue jacket. This is the one frame in which a demonstrative pronoun may refer to a person.

5. Substituting for a repeated noun. In formal writing, that of replaces a singular or uncountable noun already named: a climate like that of Spain. See that of.

  1. That is the best offer you're going to get.
  2. They cancelled the trip a week out. That surprised everyone.
  3. Who's that at the door?
  4. Don't ever say that to your mother again.
  5. After that, nobody brought it up again.
  6. That wasn't what I meant.
  7. Is that all you're taking?
  8. That's my brother in the blue jacket.

those#

Pronunciation
/ðoʊz/ · THOHZ · no weak form — full diphthong, and normally the stressed word in its clause
Русский
те — указательное местоимение мн. числа о далёком; также в оборотах those who, those of
Type
demonstrative pronoun (plural, distal)
Case
none — one form for every position
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person plural; no gender distinction, no case forms
Refers to
more than one thing away from the speaker; plural items already mentioned; and, in the frames those who, those that/which, and those of, a class of people or things defined by whatever follows. Like these, it cannot take a single clause as its antecedent — that job belongs to singular this and that — though it can sum up two or more statements at once (The plant closed and the road washed out. Those are the two facts that matter.). It reaches people in two frames: identifying, exactly as these does (Those are my cousins, over by the window), and in front of a relative clause, where those who apply early means the people who apply early. Agreement
plural verb — Those are mine. Those were the days. Those don't match. Inside the those who frame both verbs stay plural: Those who arrive late are seated at intermission. Two traps follow from that. Pattern
those + [plural verb] · [verb] those · [preposition] those · those who [clause] · those that / those which [clause] · those of [noun phrase] · those of us / you / them who [clause] · those + reduced relative or participle (those left behind, those reported last year, those responsible)
Contrast
Against these: distance. Against that: number. Pronoun against determiner — the determiner sits on a noun (those people), the pronoun replaces it (those are the ones). Against a bare comparison — when a plural noun is being compared, the substitution is obligatory in formal writing: ✗ Our returns are higher than our competitors (returns against people) → than those of our competitors, or in plain American English than our competitors'. Against nonstandard them: Those are the ones is standard, Them are the ones is dialect. And those ones, like these ones, is speech-only in American English; edited prose writes those or the ones.

Points at more than one thing away from the speaker, or picks out a class of people or things that the following clause or phrase then defines.

1. In space and in plural pointing. Those are the files you requested. The plural counterpart of pointing that.

2. "The ones" — substituting for a repeated plural noun. Followed by a participle or a phrase: results consistent with those reported last year. Formal, and it saves repeating the noun.

3. "Those who" — the people who. Names a group by what is true of them: those who registered early. See those who.

4. "Those of us" and "those of you". A partitive that includes the speaker or the hearer: those of us who commute. The idiom takes those no matter how near the group is — never ✗ these of us.

5. Nostalgic and idiomatic. Those were the days. Fixed, always plural, always past.

  1. Those are the files you requested.
  2. I'd rather have those than these.
  3. The results are consistent with those reported last year.
  4. Those who registered early get priority seating.
  5. Those of us who commute know the 8:10 is never on time.
  6. Are those still under warranty?
  7. Those weren't the instructions we were given.
  8. Those were the days.

The demonstrative with the prop-word one: this one, these ones#

Two entries for the construction that lets a demonstrative pick one item out of a set without naming the noun again: the demonstrative plus one or ones, which stands in for the noun and carries the stress. The singular pair is standard everywhere in English. The plural pair is the one place in this file where ordinary American speech and American editing disagree flatly, and where Canadian and British usage side with the speech.

these ones / those ones#

Pronunciation
/ˌðiz ˈwʌnz/, /ˌðoʊz ˈwʌnz/ · theez-WUNZ, thohz-WUNZ · no weak form — ones takes the stress and keeps its full vowel, and the /z/ of these runs straight into the /w/, so the phrase is one unit, [ˌðiˈzwʌnz]
Русский

Type
demonstrative + the prop-word ones — a plural noun phrase with the noun left out
Case
none
Register
ordinary in American speech; struck out of American edited prose; unremarkable in Canadian and British usage, spoken and written

third person plural

The prop-word needs something countable behind it: there is no mass-noun version (✗ these ones of coffee), and no singular ✗ this ones. plural verb — These ones are cracked. Those ones were on sale. Never ✗ these ones is.

Contrast
Against bare these and those — the ones adds no meaning an American editor will pay for: These are cracked says everything these ones are cracked says, and this is one of the handful of things American copy editors delete on sight. It is worth being clear about what kind of objection that is: it is not a grammatical error and it is not dialect — it is normal in American speech, normal in Canada, normal in Britain, and simply not printed in American edited prose. Against the ones — the ones is standard everywhere and is the form to write when a relative clause follows (the ones that came in Tuesday), which bare those handles less comfortably. Against this one / that one — the singular pair draws no objection at all, in any variety, which is the clearest evidence that the complaint is about the redundant plural and not about the prop-word. A note on what is a determiner here — inside the phrase, these is a determiner sitting on the noun-like ones; it is the whole phrase, not the demonstrative alone, that does the pronoun's work, which is why it is catalogued here and these seats is catalogued with the determiners.
Variants

  1. These ones are cracked; put them in the other box. (speech — edited prose: These are cracked)
  2. Not the big ones — these ones, right here in front. (speech, contrastive)
  3. Do you want these ones or the plain ones?
  4. Those ones were on sale last week, and now they aren't. (speech)
  5. Canadian and British writing takes it without comment: Those ones were sold at the fair.
  6. American edited prose: Those were sold at the fair. / The ones on the left were sold at the fair.
  7. Nonstandard: Them ones ain't ripe yet.

Demonstratives that carry an of-phrase or a relative clause#

Five formal constructions in which the demonstrative stops pointing and starts substituting. In each one that or those heads a noun phrase and something following it — an of-phrase or a relative clause — does the describing. This is the register where a repeated noun would be clumsy and a possessive would be too plain.

that of#

Pronunciation
/ˈðæt əv/ · THAT-uv · the /t/ flaps between vowels and of reduces — the phrase runs to [ˈðæɾəv], and in rapid speech to [ˈðæɾə]
Русский
у, чей — заменяет повторяемое сущ. ед. числа в сравнении: than that of X = чем у X
Type
demonstrative pro-form (substitution for a repeated noun)
Case
none
Register
formal to neutral; a written construction, rare in speech
Person / number
third person singular; stands only for singular and uncountable nouns — the plural is those of
Refers to
a singular or uncountable noun already named in the same sentence, repeated without repeating the word: The climate of Portland is much like that of northern Spain (= the climate of northern Spain). It stands for things and abstractions. For a person, ordinary American English uses the possessive instead — than John's, not than that of John.
Agreement
the phrase heads a singular noun phrase, so any verb it governs is singular: That of the control group was unchanged. It does not affect the verb of the clause it sits in, which agrees with that clause's own subject.
Pattern
[noun] of X … than that of Y · like that of [noun phrase] · similar to that of · comparable to that of · that of the [noun] · a fraction of that of [noun phrase]
Contrast
The whole purpose of that of is to keep a comparison between like things. Leave it out and the sentence compares the wrong pair: ✗ Her salary is higher than a surgeon sets a salary against a person; ✓ higher than that of a surgeon, or, in plain American English, higher than a surgeon's. The other demonstrative pro-forms: so, such, the same, the like#

Ten words and phrases that point the way a demonstrative points but stand for a proposition, its negation, a kind, an identity, an amount, a category, or a deliberate blank rather than for a thing. None of them inflects. Each attaches to its own short list of verbs and frames, and several of them — as much, ditto, not, one and the same, such and such — exist only inside those frames and cannot be moved out of them.

Archaic, regional, and nonstandard forms#

Listed for recognition, not for production. Every form below will either date a text or mark a speaker, and a learner meets all of them in fiction, film, country music, and everyday speech across large parts of the United States. Each entry names the region where there is one. Two patterns recur: here and there pulled in front of the pronoun instead of trailing after it, and the object pronoun them standing in for those.

The quoted sentences carry other features of the same varieties along with them — ain't, nonstandard verb agreement (This here don't look right, Them was good times), and negative concord, the so-called double negative (Them ain't none of mine, That there won't do nobody no good). Negative concord is the one item on this page with no region to name: it is ordinary in Southern white speech, in African American English, and in working-class speech in every city in the country, it was standard English until the eighteenth century, and it is nonstandard in edited American writing everywhere today — Them ain't none of mine → Those aren't any of mine / None of those are mine. In that last rewrite the verb is a choice and not an error: none takes a singular or a plural verb, ✓ None of those is mine and ✓ None of those are mine are both standard, and the plural is the commoner of the two. Each of these features is nonstandard on its own account, and none of them is part of the demonstrative system; they appear here because that is how these forms actually occur in speech. The determiner counterparts — them apples, them there hills, this here dog, all of them rural, Southern, and working-class — are nonstandard in exactly the same way and are catalogued in the determiner dictionary. For the nonstandard personal and reflexive forms that travel in the same company, see archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns: hisself and theirselves (Southern, Appalachian, and African American English — regular formations, his + self on the model of myself, but nonstandard in writing), youse (New York, New Jersey, and Philadelphia, with Irish English behind it), y'all (Southern, and now spreading well beyond the South), and ✗ Me and him went, which belongs to no region at all — it is casual speech all over the United States and an error in writing all over it too, an object form standing in a subject slot.
