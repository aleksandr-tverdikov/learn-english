# Compound indefinite pronouns

> The pronouns built by welding *some-*, *any-*, *every-*, or *no-* onto *-body*, *-one*, or *-thing* — the words that name a person or a thing without saying which one.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every member of the core group is a two-part compound: a quantifier stem that fixes the **polarity** — *some-* asserts, *any-* questions and denies, *every-* universalizes, *no-* negates — welded to a head that fixes the **referent**, with *-body* and *-one* for people and *-thing* for things. Two rules run through the entire set and they pull in opposite directions. **The verb is always singular** — *everyone **is***, *nobody **was***, *something **smells*** — no matter how plural the meaning feels; but **the pronoun that refers back is normally *they***, because the meaning is plural or the gender unknown. Singular *they* is standard American English, in edited prose as well as in speech, and the *Chicago Manual of Style*, the *MLA Handbook*, the APA *Publication Manual*, and the *AP Stylebook* all accept it, AP the most cautiously; *his or her* is the conservative alternative, and generic *he* for a person of unknown gender is now rejected by every major American style guide and reads as dated to most American readers.

The singular-verb rule is absolute for the twelve compounds, and it is worth fixing against the neighboring group, where it is not. Among the **simple** indefinite pronouns, *each*, *either*, and *neither* take a **singular** verb however plural the *of*-phrase behind them looks — *Each of the rooms **has** its own thermostat*, *Either **is** fine*, *Neither of the plans **works*** (*neither of them are* is common in speech and still changed in edited prose). *Both*, *several*, and *few* take a **plural** verb, always — *Both **were** late*, *Several **have** already shipped*, *Few **know** the whole story*. And *all*, *some*, *most*, and *none* take the number of **whatever they refer to**: *All of the seats **were** taken* beside *All of the paint **was** dry*, *Some of the chairs **are** broken* beside *Some of the paint **is** dry*, *Most of the files **were** copied* beside *Most of the evidence **was** destroyed*. *None* is catalogued in this file because it is historically one of these compounds, and it is the one member here whose number is genuinely open; the rest of that group is at [quantifying indefinite pronouns](08-indefinite-quantifying.md).

Three more rules recur in every entry. **Modifiers follow the pronoun** rather than preceding it — *something strange*, *someone important*, *nothing new*, *anywhere quiet* — which is the reverse of ordinary English word order and the single easiest way to spot a nonnative sentence. ***Else* also follows**, and its possessive takes the apostrophe: *someone else's coat*, never ✗ *someone's else coat*. And **standard American English allows one negative per clause**, so *I didn't see anybody* and *I saw nobody* are the two repairs for ✗ *I didn't see nobody* — a construction that is rule-governed and systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, and stigmatized in edited writing. Every nonstandard form in this file is labeled as such.

One thing this family does **not** have is **case**. All twelve compounds are invariant — *everyone* is *everyone* whether it is subject or object, and none of them has a *whom*-style object form — so the coordination errors that plague the personal pronouns cannot arise on the compound itself. What these compounds do inflect for is the **genitive**, and only the *-body* and *-one* forms do it comfortably — *somebody's*, *everyone's*, *nobody else's* — while *nothing's* in running text is almost always *nothing is* or *nothing has*.

Case surfaces instead in the words standing next to them, and the rule is worth stating in full here because these compounds are one of the commonest places to lose track of it. **A pronoun takes the case of the job it does in its own clause**, and putting a second person in the slot beside it changes that job not at all. *But* and *except* are prepositions in modern American usage, so they take the **object** form: *nobody but **me***, *everyone except **him***. (The older prescription that a *but*-phrase attached to a subject keeps the subject form — *no one but I knew* — has literary backing and is not an error, but it now sounds stilted; *but me* is the modern standard.) The same rule governs the coordinations Americans actually break, and they break in both directions. The **hypercorrection** puts the subject form into an object slot: ✗ *between you and **I***, ✗ *They invited my wife and **I***. The first is the object of a preposition and the second is a plain direct object — no preposition anywhere in it — and both require *me*: ✓ *between you and **me***, ✓ *They invited my wife and **me***, ✓ *He gave it to Bob and **me***. In the other direction the **nonstandard** error puts an object form in a subject slot: ✗ *Me and him went*. **One test settles every one of them: delete the other person and read what is left aloud.** *Between you and \_\_\_* → *between **me***. *He gave it to Bob and \_\_\_* → *He gave it to **me***, never ✗ *to **I***. *They invited my wife and \_\_\_* → *They invited **me***. *\_\_\_ and I went* → ***I** went*, which is why ***She** and **I** went* is the right form. Note that the objection to *Me and him went* is to the case and not to the order, so the repair is *He and I went*, not *Him and me* rearranged. And one thing to unlearn rather than learn: ***It's me* is standard English**, not an error — *It is I* is grammatically defensible and sounds stiff outside the telephone formula *This is she*. The whole business is worked out at [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it).

These compounds are constantly the antecedent of a relative clause, so the **antecedent rules** matter here too. Use ***who* for people, *which* for things, *that* for either, and *whose* for both** — *someone **who** can drive*, *something **which** still works*, *a policy **whose** wording nobody checked*. After a *-body* or *-one* compound both *who* and *that* are standard (*anyone **who** wants a copy*, *anybody **that** asks*); after a *-thing* compound *that* is the overwhelming choice (*everything **that** matters*), and *what* is never a relative pronoun in standard English (✗ *everything what I own*). Two honest qualifications. The famous *that*/*which* rule is half grammar and half **house style**: *that* genuinely cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause, but restrictive *which* is perfectly grammatical, and an American copy editor changing *everything which matters* to *everything that matters* is applying an **editing convention**, not repairing an error. And ***whom* is nearly gone except after a preposition**: *someone **who** we hired* is unremarkable in American speech and increasingly in print, while *someone **to whom** we owe money* keeps the object form because it stands directly after *to*. Both are worked out at [relative pronouns](05-relative.md).

Finally, **listen for the reductions**, because the citation forms printed in this file are not what a learner actually hears. Inside the compounds the stressed first syllable never weakens — that is what carries the polarity — but everything after it does, and the /d/ of *-body* taps: *somebody* [ˈsʌmbəɾi], *anybody* [ˈɛnibəɾi], *everybody* [ˈɛvribəɾi], *something* [ˈsʌmpm̩], *nothing* [ˈnʌʔn̩]. And because these compounds so often sit right beside a personal pronoun, the personal-pronoun **weak forms** arrive with them: *him* is /ɪm/, *her* is /ɚ/, *them* is /əm/, *you* is /jə/, and *he* is /i/ — so *somebody told them* comes out [ˈsʌmbəɾi ˈtoʊld əm], *nobody saw him* comes out [ˈnoʊbəɾi ˈsɔ ɪm], and *did you tell her* comes out [ˈdɪdʒə ˈtɛl ɚ]. None of that is sloppy speech; it is the ordinary rhythm of the language. See [weak forms](01-personal.md#weak-forms-what-you-actually-hear).

The last recurring trap is the space bar. *Everyone*, *anyone*, and *someone* written solid are pronouns meaning *all the people*, *a person*, *a person*; written as two words, *every one*, *any one*, and *some one* are a determiner plus the pronoun *one*, they can refer to things, and they take *of*. The test is mechanical: **if an *of*-phrase follows, use two words** — *every one of the windows*, never ✗ *everyone of the windows*. The determiner halves of these pairs live in the determiner dictionary: [every](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [any](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [no](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no), [some](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#some), and [none of](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#none-of).

## The *some-* series

The assertive stem. A *some-* compound claims that a person or thing actually exists, which is exactly what its *any-* partner declines to do: *somebody called* reports a call, *did anybody call* asks whether there was one. Use these in plain affirmative statements — and in one class of question, the **offer or request**, where *some-* survives because the speaker is not really asking whether anyone exists (*Could somebody hold the door?*, *Would you like something to drink?*).

### somebody

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmbɑdi/ &middot; *SUM-bod-ee* &middot; stress is fixed on *some-* and never moves; the second element is unstressed, so the vowel centers and the /d/ becomes a tap between vowels, giving the everyday [ˈsʌmbəɾi] — roughly *SUM-buh-dee*. Many Americans have [ˈsʌmbʌdi]. The full [ˈsʌmˌbɑdi] is citation form and sounds deliberate in running speech
**Русский:** кто-то, кто-нибудь — неназванное лицо; разговорнее, чем someone, глагол всегда в ед. ч.
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (assertive) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *somebody's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more conversational than *someone*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender. The homograph in *he's a somebody* is a countable **noun**, not this pronoun, and it does pluralize (*nobodies and somebodies*).
**Refers to:** an unidentified or unspecified **person** — human referents only, one at a time. For a thing use [something](#something). Animals go with *something* unless the speaker is treating the animal as a person, which is why *somebody chewed my shoe* is perfectly natural about a dog.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Somebody **is** at the door*, *Somebody **has** been in here*. The pronoun that refers back is normally singular *they/them/their* (*Somebody left **their** badge on the table*), which is standard American English in edited prose as well as speech; the formal alternative is *his or her*, and generic *he* now reads as an error to most American editors.
**Pattern:** *somebody* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *somebody* &middot; [preposition] *somebody* &middot; *somebody's* [noun] &middot; *somebody* [adjective] — the adjective **follows** (*somebody new*) &middot; *somebody else* / *somebody else's* [noun] &middot; *somebody who/that* [clause] &middot; *somebody to* [verb]
**Contrast:** *Somebody* and [someone](#someone) mean exactly the same thing and swap freely; the only difference is texture, with the *-one* forms dominant in edited American prose and the *-body* forms holding their own in speech. Against [anybody](#anybody) the difference is **polarity**: *some-* in plain affirmative statements, *any-* under a negative, a question, or an *if*. The standing exception is the **offer or request**, which keeps *some-* inside a question — *Could **somebody** hold the door?* invites help, while *Could **anybody** hold the door?* sounds like a genuine survey of who is able. Keep the pronoun apart from the noun: *he's a somebody now* has an article and a plural, which the pronoun never takes.

Points at one person whose identity is unknown to the speaker, irrelevant to the point, or deliberately withheld. It **asserts that such a person exists**, which is precisely what its polarity partner *anybody* does not do: *somebody called* reports a call, *did anybody call* asks whether one happened at all.

1. *Somebody* is knocking at the back door.
2. We need *somebody* to sign for the delivery before five.
3. She's seeing *somebody* from her old firm.
4. *Somebody's* car is blocking the driveway again.
5. Could *somebody* please turn the thermostat down? — a request, so *some-* survives inside a question
6. There's *somebody* new in accounting, and I've already forgotten *their* name. — singular verb, plural-form pronoun, both standard
7. He said *somebody* had complained, but he wouldn't say who.

**See also:** [someone](#someone), [something](#something), [anybody](#anybody), [nobody](#nobody), [somebody else](#somebody-else), [somebody else's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses)

### someone

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmwʌn/ &middot; *SUM-wun* &middot; stress on *some-*; the *-one* is unstressed and its vowel routinely centers to /ə/, giving [ˈsʌmwən]. Never stress the second element — a stressed *some ONE* is the two-word phrase *some one (of them)*, which is a different structure with a different meaning
**Русский:** кто-то, кто-нибудь — неназванное лицо; нейтральный вариант somebody для письменной речи
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (assertive) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *someone's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the unmarked choice in edited writing
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** an unidentified or unspecified **person** — human referents only. For a thing use [something](#something); for a place use [somewhere](#somewhere).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Someone **is** waiting*, *Someone **was** here before us*. Back-reference is singular *they/them/their* in ordinary American usage (*Someone forgot **their** umbrella*); *his or her* is the conservative formal option and is clumsy when repeated.
**Pattern:** *someone* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *someone* &middot; [preposition] *someone* &middot; *someone's* [noun] &middot; *someone* [adjective] (*someone important*) &middot; *someone else* / *someone else's* [noun] &middot; *someone who/that* [clause] &middot; *someone to* [verb]
**Contrast:** The trap is the space bar. Welded into one word, *someone* is a pronoun meaning *a person*; split into two, *some one* is the determiner *some* plus the pronoun [one](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), it means *one particular member of a set already named*, and it takes *of* — *some one of the trustees must countersign*. That two-word form is stiff and rare now, but its siblings are alive and are the real hazard: *every one of the windows* and *any one of the applicants* are two words, and writing ✗ *everyone of the windows was cracked* is one of the most common errors in this whole family. Against [somebody](#somebody) it is free choice, *someone* being slightly more formal. Against [anyone](#anyone) it is polarity: *some-* asserts, *any-* questions or denies.

Names one person without identifying them. *Someone* is the written default of the four *some-* people-words and carries no implication about how many such people exist — only that at least one does.

1. *Someone* has been reading my mail.
2. I'd like *someone* with actual field experience on the call.
3. The letter was addressed to *someone* named A. Reyes.
4. *Someone's* phone kept buzzing all through the ceremony.
5. Is there *someone* who can cover the front desk until noon? — an appeal for help, so *some-* is natural in the question
6. We hired *someone* new, and *they* start Monday.
7. It has to be *someone* on the inside.

**See also:** [somebody](#somebody), [something](#something), [anyone](#anyone), [no one](#no-one), [someone else](#someone-else), [someone else's](#someone-elses)

### something

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmθɪŋ/ &middot; *SUM-thing* &middot; stress on *some-*. An epenthetic /p/ appears between the /m/ and the /θ/ in ordinary speech, giving [ˈsʌmpθɪŋ], and casual American English reduces the whole word to [ˈsʌmpm̩] or [ˈsʌmθɪn] — the *sump'm* and *somethin'* a learner actually hears. The final /ŋ/ is not /ŋɡ/
**Русский:** что-то, что-нибудь — неназванный предмет, мысль или событие; о людях не говорят
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (assertive) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; a genitive *something's* is possible but rare and usually rewritten &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural, no gender.
**Refers to:** **things, ideas, events, situations, substances, and actions** — non-human referents. It reaches a person only in the fixed predicates *he's really something* and *she's something else*, where it means *remarkable*. Back-reference is *it*, not *they*: *Something arrived; **it** was in a crate.*
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Something **is** burning*, *Something **has** to give*, *Something **smells** wrong in here*. A following adjective does not change this: *Something odd **is** going on*.
**Pattern:** *something* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *something* &middot; [preposition] *something* &middot; *something* [adjective] — the adjective **follows** the pronoun &middot; *something to* [verb] &middot; *something that* [clause] &middot; *something else* &middot; *something like* [number] (approximation)
**Contrast:** **Modifiers follow, they do not precede**: ✓ *something strange*, ✗ *strange something*. This is the one syntactic quirk shared by all twelve compounds, and it holds for adjectives, prepositional phrases, relative clauses, and infinitives alike (*something cheap from the deli that I can microwave*). Against [anything](#anything) the rule is polarity, with a scope wrinkle worth knowing: *I didn't hear anything* denies the whole set, while *I didn't hear something* is only usable when *something* sits outside the negation (*There's something I didn't hear*). Against *some thing* written as two words, which is the determiner *some* plus a literal noun and needs a context that makes *thing* mean an object. Against [nothing](#nothing), its negative counterpart. The hedge *something of a genius* is a different construction entirely; see [something of a](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#something-of-a).

Points at an unidentified or unspecified thing. It has three uses that are worth separating.

**1. An unspecified object, idea, event, or substance.** The core sense, and the one that takes postmodifiers: *something wrong*, *something to eat*, *something I've been meaning to ask*.

**2. An unspecified quality or worth.** *There's something about that room.* Predicatively it becomes a compliment: *That performance was really something.*

**3. An approximator.** Before a number it hedges the count — *something like two hundred people* — and hyphenated after one it makes an age bracket: *a thirty-something in a very expensive coat*.

1. *Something* is wrong with the transmission. — sense 1
2. Grab *something* to eat before the meeting starts. — sense 1, an infinitive postmodifier
3. She said *something* strange and then changed the subject. — sense 1, the adjective following
4. There's *something* about the way he tells that story. — sense 2
5. That last set was really *something*. — sense 2, predicative
6. *Something* like four hundred people showed up. — sense 3
7. We ordered *something* off the specials board and regretted it.

**See also:** [somebody](#somebody), [someone](#someone), [anything](#anything), [nothing](#nothing), [everything](#everything), [something else](#something-else), [something or other](#something-or-other)

### something or other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmθɪŋ ɚ ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *sum-thing-er-UTH-er* &middot; *or* is always weak, reduced to a syllabic /ɚ/ with no vowel of its own, so the phrase runs as four beats with main stress on *oth-*; in fast speech the whole thing compresses to [ˈsʌmpm̩ ɚ ˈʌðɚ]
**Русский:** что-то там, что-то такое — разг., когда говорящий не помнит или не хочет уточнять
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun with a vagueness tag &middot; **Case:** none — invariant; a genitive is not formed on the phrase &middot; **Register:** informal, conversational; a copy editor deletes it or replaces it with a fact
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** a **thing** whose identity the speaker cannot recall or does not care to supply. The parallel forms *somebody or other* and *someone or other* refer to people, and *somewhere or other* to places.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Something or other **has** gone wrong with the billing again.*
**Pattern:** *something or other* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *something or other* &middot; [noun] *or something or other* (tacked onto a list) &middot; *somebody / someone / somewhere or other* (the parallel forms)
**Contrast:** The tag attaches only to the ***some-*** forms. ✗ *anything or other*, ✗ *nothing or other*, ✗ *everything or other* are all impossible, because the tag's job is to assert that a particular thing exists while confessing ignorance of which one — and only *some-* makes that assertion. Compare the looser clause-final tag *or something* (*He's a consultant or something*), which does the same work with less commitment, and *[what have you](#whatnot)*, which closes a list rather than replacing a single item.
**Variants:** *somebody or other*, *someone or other*, *somewhere or other*, *someplace or other*, *somehow or other*, *sometime or other*, *some way or other* — the tag attaches to any *some-* form, the adverbs included, and to no other stem

Marks a referent as real but unrecoverable: the speaker knows a specific thing is involved and admits to having lost its identity. It is a confession of vagueness rather than genuine indefiniteness, which is why it sounds dismissive when used about someone's work.

1. He's writing a dissertation on medieval land tenure or *something or other*.
2. She left a message about a permit or *something or other*.
3. *Somebody or other* from the county called about the fence line.
4. I signed *something or other* at the front desk and never saw it again.
5. They moved to *somewhere or other* in Arizona and stopped answering email.
6. *Something or other* keeps tripping the breaker in the basement.

**See also:** [something](#something), [somebody](#somebody), [somewhere](#somewhere), [whatnot](#whatnot), [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), [God knows what, God knows who](#god-knows-what-god-knows-who)

## The *any-* series

The nonassertive stem, with a second life as free choice. Under a negative, in a question, in an *if*-clause, after *hardly*, *rarely*, *never*, *without*, and *before*, and after a comparative, *any-* sweeps the whole class without claiming a member exists. In affirmative clauses it flips to **free choice** — *anyone can learn to solder* — takes its own stress, and means *it does not matter which*. The rule learners miss most is one of **order**: an *any-* compound has to follow the negative that licenses it, so ✗ *anybody didn't call* is impossible and *nobody called* is the only way to say it at the front of a clause.

### anybody

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌbɑdi/ &middot; *EN-ee-bod-ee* &middot; *any* is /ˈɛni/ in General American — the vowel is that of *pen*, never that of *pan*. The /d/ taps between vowels, so the everyday form is [ˈɛnibəɾi]. In the free-choice sense the stress shifts forward and strengthens: *ANYbody can do it*
**Русский:** кто-нибудь (в вопросе и отрицании); любой, кто угодно (в утверждении)
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (nonassertive and free choice) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *anybody's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more conversational than *anyone*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural, no gender.
**Refers to:** a **person**, human referents only, with no commitment as to which one or whether one exists at all. For things use [anything](#anything).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always**, including in the free-choice sense where the meaning is emphatically universal — *Anybody **is** welcome to try*, *Has anybody **seen** my keys?*, *If anybody **calls**, take a message*. Back-reference is singular *they*: *If anybody objects, ask **them** to put it in writing.*
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anybody* (*I didn't tell anybody*) &middot; *Did / has / can* [subject] [verb] *anybody* (question) &middot; *if / unless* [subject] [verb] *anybody* (conditional) &middot; *hardly / rarely / never / without* … *anybody* &middot; [comparative] *than anybody* &middot; *anybody* [singular verb] (free choice: *anybody can join*) &middot; *anybody's* [noun] &middot; *anybody else* / *anybody else's*
**Contrast:** Two rules, and the second is the one learners miss. **Polarity**: *any-* belongs under a negative, in a question, in an *if*-clause, after *hardly / rarely / never / without / before*, and after a comparative; *some-* belongs in plain statements. **Order**: an *any-* compound must come **after** the negative that licenses it, never before it. ✓ *I didn't tell anybody*, ✗ *Anybody didn't tell me* — the subject slot in front of a negated verb takes *nobody* instead (*Nobody told me*). Against [nobody](#nobody), the pair *not … anybody* and *nobody* mean the same thing, with *nobody* the more emphatic and the only one available at the front of a clause. Against [somebody](#somebody), remember the offer exception runs the other way: a question that is really an invitation keeps *some-*.

Refers to a person without asserting that any such person exists. It has two clearly separate lives.

**1. Nonassertive.** Under a negative, a question, or a condition, it simply widens the search to the whole class: *I didn't see anybody* denies every candidate, one by one.

**2. Free choice.** In an affirmative clause, usually with a modal, it means *it does not matter which* and carries its own stress: *Anybody can learn to solder.* This is the sense that lets *any-* appear where the polarity rule would otherwise forbid it.

1. I didn't tell *anybody* about the offer. — sense 1, under a negative
2. Has *anybody* seen my keys? — sense 1, in a question
3. If *anybody* asks, we left at nine. — sense 1, in a conditional
4. *Anybody* can learn to solder with an afternoon of practice. — sense 2, free choice
5. Hardly *anybody* stayed for the second half.
6. She works harder than *anybody else* in that department. — the comparative frame; *else* because she works there too
7. *Nobody* told me — not ✗ *anybody didn't tell me*.

### anyone

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniwʌn/ &middot; *EN-ee-wun* &middot; stress on *an-*; the *-one* is unstressed and centers to [ˈɛniwən]. A stressed second element signals the two-word phrase *any ONE (of them)*, which is a different structure. Free-choice *anyone* takes extra force on the first syllable
**Русский:** кто-нибудь (в вопросе и отрицании); любой (в утверждении) — книжнее, чем anybody
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (nonassertive and free choice) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *anyone's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the usual choice in edited prose
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural, no gender.
**Refers to:** a **person**, human referents only. For things use [anything](#anything), for places [anywhere](#anywhere).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Is anyone **using** this chair?*, *Anyone who **wants** a copy should email me*. Back-reference is singular *they*: *Anyone who wants a copy should send me **their** address.*
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anyone* &middot; [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *anyone* (question) &middot; *if / unless* … *anyone* &middot; *hardly / scarcely / rarely / without* … *anyone* &middot; [comparative] *than anyone* &middot; *anyone* [singular verb] (free choice) &middot; *anyone who / that* [clause] &middot; *anyone's* [noun] &middot; *anyone else*
**Contrast:** *Anyone* against *any one* is the sharpest spelling trap in this family. One word is a pronoun meaning *a person*; two words is the determiner [any](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#any) plus the pronoun [one](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), it means *a single member of a stated set*, it can refer to things as well as people, and it takes *of*: *any one of the three routes will work*. The test is *of* — if an *of*-phrase follows, it is two words. Against [anybody](#anybody), free choice of style. Against [someone](#someone), the polarity rule, plus the ordering rule that keeps *any-* words out of subject position in front of a negated verb: ✗ *Anyone didn't come* → ✓ *No one came*.

Refers to a person while leaving open both who and whether. Like *anybody* it splits into a nonassertive use and a free-choice use.

**1. Nonassertive.** In negatives, questions, and conditionals it sweeps the whole class without claiming a member exists: *Did anyone call?*

**2. Free choice.** In affirmative clauses, usually with *can*, *may*, or a generic present, it means *no matter who*: *Anyone is welcome to sit in.*

1. Did *anyone* call while I was out? — sense 1
2. I don't know *anyone* who has actually read the whole report. — sense 1
3. If *anyone* has a conflict, say so now. — sense 1
4. *Anyone* is welcome to sit in on the rehearsal. — sense 2
5. She is faster over ten kilometers than *anyone else* on the team. — the comparative frame; *else* because she is on the team herself
6. *Anyone* who wants a copy should send me *their* address. — singular verb, singular *they*
7. It is *anyone's* guess how long the funding lasts.

**See also:** [anybody](#anybody), [anything](#anything), [anyone else](#anyone-else), [someone](#someone), [no one](#no-one), [everyone](#everyone), [someone else](#someone-else)

### anything

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniθɪŋ/ &middot; *EN-ee-thing* &middot; stress on *an-*; the final /ŋ/ is not /ŋɡ/, and casual speech gives [ˈɛniθɪn]. Free-choice *anything* takes a heavier first syllable and often a lengthened one: *I'd eat AAAnything right now*
**Русский:** что-нибудь (в вопросе и отрицании); что угодно (в утверждении)
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (nonassertive and free choice) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **things, ideas, events, and actions** — non-human referents. Back-reference is *it*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Is anything **wrong**?*, *If anything **goes** wrong, call me*, *Nothing here **is** worth anything*.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anything* &middot; [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *anything* (question) &middot; *if / unless* … *anything* &middot; *anything* [adjective] — the adjective follows (*anything cheap*) &middot; *anything to* [verb] &middot; *anything that* [clause] &middot; *anything but* [adjective/noun] &middot; *anything like* [noun] &middot; *anything* [singular verb] (free choice)
**Contrast:** Polarity as with the rest of the *any-* words, plus two fixed phrases that reverse the meaning and are worth learning whole. **Anything but** means *emphatically not*: *the exam was anything but easy* says it was hard. **Nothing but** means *only*: *nothing but trouble*. Getting those two backwards inverts the sentence. Against [something](#something), the polarity rule and the scope wrinkle — *I didn't buy anything* denies the whole set, while *I didn't buy something* only works when *something* escapes the negation. Against [everything](#everything), free-choice *anything* ranges over the set one member at a time (*eat anything you like* — pick one), while *everything* takes the set whole (*eat everything* — all of it).

Refers to a thing without asserting that one exists. Two uses, plus a set of idioms built on it.

**1. Nonassertive.** Under negation, in questions, and in conditionals: *I didn't hear anything.*

**2. Free choice.** In affirmative clauses, meaning *no matter what*: *She'll eat anything.*

The idioms: *anything but* (= far from), *anything like* (= at all resembling, almost always under a negative), *if anything* (= if there is any difference at all), and the clause-final tag *or anything* (*He didn't apologize or anything*).

1. I didn't hear *anything* unusual that night. — sense 1
2. Is there *anything* cheap on the menu? — sense 1, with the adjective following
3. If *anything* goes wrong, call the service line. — sense 1
4. That dog will eat *anything*. — sense 2, free choice
5. The second draft was *anything* but an improvement. — the idiom: it was worse
6. It doesn't taste *anything* like coffee.
7. *If anything*, the traffic was lighter than usual.

**See also:** [anybody](#anybody), [anyone](#anyone), [something](#something), [nothing](#nothing), [everything](#everything), [anything else](#anything-else)

## The *every-* series

The universal stem, and the place where the singular-verb rule is tested hardest. *Everyone*, *everybody*, and *everything* take a **singular verb** without exception, even with a plural noun sitting between the subject and the verb (*everyone in the two seminars **needs** a badge*), and even though the meaning is plainly plural. Everything downstream then goes plural: the pronoun (*everyone brought **their** own lunch*), the reflexive (*everybody helped **themselves***), and the tag question (*everyone's coming, **aren't they?***).

### everybody

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriˌbɑdi/ &middot; *EV-ree-bod-ee* &middot; *every* is two syllables in General American, /ˈɛvri/ — the medial vowel is normally dropped, and a careful three-syllable [ˈɛvəri] sounds like elocution. The /d/ taps between vowels, so the ordinary form is [ˈɛvribəɾi]. Stress never leaves the first syllable
**Русский:** все — но по-английски глагол в ед. ч.; разговорнее, чем everyone
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (universal) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *everybody's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; commoner than *everyone* in speech, less common in edited prose
**Person / number:** third person, **grammatically singular** though the meaning is plural; invariant, no gender.
**Refers to:** **every person in the relevant group** — human referents only. The group is whatever the context supplies: everyone in the room, in the company, in the world. For things use [everything](#everything).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Everybody **is** here*, *Everybody **was** given a badge*, *Everybody **knows** the story*. ✗ *Everybody are here*. The pronoun that refers back is plural in form: *Everybody brought **their** own lunch* — standard American English, in edited prose as well as speech. So is the plural tag question on a singular verb: *Everybody's coming,* **aren't they?** The reflexive is *themselves*: *Everybody helped **themselves** to the coffee.*
**Pattern:** *everybody* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *everybody* &middot; [preposition] *everybody* &middot; *everybody's* [noun] &middot; *everybody* [adjective/prepositional phrase] (*everybody present*, *everybody in the room*) &middot; *everybody else* / *everybody else's* &middot; *everybody who / that* [clause]
**Contrast:** *Everybody* and [everyone](#everyone) are interchangeable, *-one* being the safer bet in formal writing. The important contrast is with [all](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all): *everybody* is invariably singular and takes a singular verb (*Everybody **is** welcome*), while *all* takes its number from whatever it refers to — plural for a set of people or countable things (*All **are** welcome*, *All of the seats **were** taken*), singular for a mass or an abstract totality (*All **is** well*, *All of the paint **was** dry*). The second contrast is the notorious one-word/two-word split, which *everybody* is mercifully spared — there is no ✗ *every body* in this sense, though *every body* does exist literally in a coroner's report. Its sibling *everyone* is not so lucky; see the note there.

Takes in every member of a group of people, without exception, and treats them as a single grammatical unit. The mismatch between its singular grammar and its plural meaning is the source of nearly every error in this file: the **verb** follows the grammar and stays singular, while the **pronoun** and the **tag question** follow the meaning and go plural.

1. *Everybody* is already seated.
2. *Everybody* was handed a numbered ticket at the door.
3. The email went out to *everybody* on the distribution list.
4. *Everybody* brought *their* own folding chair. — singular verb, plural-form pronoun, both standard
5. *Everybody's* schedule shifted an hour when the flight changed.
6. *Everybody's* coming, *aren't they*? — plural tag on a singular verb
7. *Everybody* helped *themselves* to the leftovers.

**See also:** [everyone](#everyone), [everything](#everything), [everybody else](#everybody-else), [everyone else](#everyone-else), [nobody](#nobody), [anybody](#anybody), [every](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every)

### everyone

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriwʌn/ &middot; *EV-ree-wun* &middot; /ˈɛvri/ with the medial vowel dropped, plus an unstressed *-one* that centers to [-wən]. Stress stays on *ev-*. If you hear a stress on the last syllable — *every ONE* — the speaker means the two-word phrase, and the grammar is different
**Русский:** все — по смыслу мн. ч., но глагол в ед. ч.; обычный вариант в редактируемой прозе
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (universal) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *everyone's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard choice in edited American prose
**Person / number:** third person, **grammatically singular** with plural meaning; invariant, no gender.
**Refers to:** **every person in the relevant group** — human referents only. It cannot refer to things: for those use [everything](#everything).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Everyone **is** accounted for*, *Everyone **has** signed in*, *Everyone in the seminars **needs** a badge* (the plural noun in between changes nothing). ✗ *Everyone are*. Back-reference is singular *they/them/their*, which the *Chicago Manual of Style*, the *MLA Handbook*, the APA *Publication Manual*, and the *AP Stylebook* all now accept — AP being the most cautious, still asking writers to reword where a rewrite is easy: *Everyone should bring **their** own copy.* Singular *they* still takes plural verb forms — *they **were***, never ✗ *they was*.
**Pattern:** *everyone* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *everyone* &middot; [preposition] *everyone* &middot; *everyone's* [noun] &middot; *everyone* [adjective/prepositional phrase] (*everyone present*, *everyone here*) &middot; *everyone else* / *everyone else's* &middot; *everyone who / that* [clause]
**Contrast:** **One word or two —** *everyone* against *every one* — is the single most tested spelling point in this family. One word is a pronoun meaning *all the people*: *everyone laughed*. Two words is the determiner [every](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every) plus the pronoun [one](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), it means *each individual member of a stated set*, it can refer to things, and it ✗ *takes *of***: *every one of the windows was cracked*, *I read every one of them*. The test is mechanical — ✗ *if an *of*-phrase follows, use two words**; ✗ *everyone of the windows* is an error, and so is ✗ *every one laughed* for *everyone laughed*. Against [all](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all), the verb splits them: *Everyone is welcome* (singular) beside *All are welcome* (plural). Against [everybody](#everybody), free choice of register. One live dispute: *Everyone knows each other* is normal in speech and increasingly in print, but a strict reading objects that a singular subject cannot be reciprocal; *They all know each other* dodges it.

Sweeps in every person in the group under discussion. It is the universal member of the *-one* set and stands opposite [no one](#no-one), which sweeps none of them in. Its grammar is singular and its meaning is plural, and English resolves the conflict by keeping the verb singular and letting everything downstream go plural.

1. *Everyone* is accounted for.
2. *Everyone* in the two seminars needs a photo badge. — the plural noun in between does not make the verb plural
3. She thanked *everyone* who had stayed late.
4. *Everyone* should bring *their* own copy of the agenda. — singular verb, singular *they*
5. It was *everyone's* first time on a boat.
6. *Every one* of the applicants was interviewed twice. — two words, because *of* follows
7. *Everyone* here has read the report, *haven't they*?

**See also:** [everybody](#everybody), [everything](#everything), [everyone else](#everyone-else), [no one](#no-one), [anyone](#anyone), [someone](#someone), [every one of](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#every-one-of)

### everything

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriθɪŋ/ &middot; *EV-ree-thing* &middot; /ˈɛvri/ with the medial vowel dropped, stress on the first syllable; the final /ŋ/ is not /ŋɡ/, and casual speech gives [ˈɛvriθɪn]. In fast speech the /θ/ can weaken almost to nothing, leaving [ˈɛvriɪŋ]
**Русский:** всё — о вещах, фактах и обстоятельствах; глагол в ед. ч.
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (universal) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, **grammatically singular** with plural or mass meaning; invariant.
**Refers to:** **all the things, facts, or circumstances in view** — non-human referents, count or mass. Back-reference is *it*: *Everything is packed; **it** all fits.* It reaches people only in loose idiom (*she's everything to him*), where the meaning is *all that matters*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Everything **is** ready*, *Everything **has** been moved*, *Everything in those boxes **needs** labeling*. ✗ *Everything are ready*. Note that *everything* pairs with the singular *all* pattern rather than the plural one: *Everything **is** fine* beside *All **is** well* but *All the lights **are** on*.
**Pattern:** *everything* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *everything* &middot; [preposition] *everything* &middot; *everything* [adjective] — the adjective follows (*everything valuable*) &middot; *everything that* [clause] &middot; *everything* [reduced relative] &middot; *everything else* &middot; *everything but* [noun]
**Contrast:** Against [all](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all): *all* is a determiner when a noun follows (*all the boxes*) and takes the number of that noun, while *everything* stands alone and is always singular. Bare pronoun *all* meaning *everything* survives mainly in fixed and elevated phrasing (*All is well*, *That's all*), and in ordinary American prose *everything* is the natural choice. Against [anything](#anything): *everything* takes the set whole (*eat everything on the plate*), free-choice *anything* takes any one member of it (*eat anything on the plate*). In relative clauses after *everything*, **that** is the overwhelming choice — *everything that matters* — but be clear about what kind of rule that is: restrictive *which* is perfectly grammatical (*everything which matters* appears in British and in American legal prose), and changing it to *that* is an American **editing convention**, not a repair. What is genuinely barred is *what* as a relative: ✗ *everything what I own* is nonstandard, and *that* is impossible in a nonrestrictive clause. And *everything but the roof held* means the roof failed; *but* here excludes.

Takes in the whole of the relevant set of things, with nothing left out. Its negative counterpart is [nothing](#nothing) and its people counterparts are [everyone](#everyone) and [everybody](#everybody). Like the rest of the compounds it is postmodified rather than premodified — *everything valuable*, never ✗ *valuable everything*.

1. *Everything* is ready for the walkthrough.
2. They packed *everything* into a single trailer.
3. *Everything* valuable had already been moved to the safe.
4. *Everything* that could go wrong that morning did.
5. She lost *everything* but the photographs.
6. *Everything* in those crates needs a label before Friday.
7. Is that *everything*, or is there another box in the hall?

**See also:** [everybody](#everybody), [everyone](#everyone), [everything else](#everything-else), [nothing](#nothing), [anything](#anything), [something](#something)

## The *no-* series

The negative stem, plus *none*, which is the same word historically — Old English *ne* + *ān*, *not one* — worn down to one syllable. All of these carry their own negation, so **the verb after them stays positive**: *nobody came*, not ✗ *nobody didn't come*. Three of the four are rigidly singular. *None* is the exception and the reason this section needs care: with a mass noun it takes a singular verb, with a plural count set it takes either, and the plural is older, commoner, and usually better. The emphatic *not a soul* is catalogued here too: it is built on *not a* rather than on the *no-* stem, but it denies a person exactly as *nobody* does, it carries its own negation in the same way, and it is the form an American reaches for when *nobody* is not emphatic enough.

### no one

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊ ˌwʌn/ &middot; *NOH-wun* &middot; written as two words but spoken as one unit, with primary stress on *no* and secondary stress on *one*; the linking /w/ ties them together, [ˈnoʊwʌn]. Unlike the *-one* of *someone* and *anyone*, this *one* keeps a fairly full vowel, because the phrase is not written solid
**Русский:** никто — только о людях; глагол в ед. ч. и без второго отрицания, в отличие от русского
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (negative) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *no one's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the usual choice in edited American prose
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no gender.
**Refers to:** **no person at all** — human referents only. For things use [nothing](#nothing).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always, and the verb stays positive** — *No one **is** answering*, *No one **has** signed the form*, *No one in those departments **was** notified*. Back-reference is singular *they*: *No one admitted **they** had seen the memo.*
**Pattern:** *no one* [positive singular verb] &middot; [verb] *no one* &middot; [preposition] *no one* &middot; *no one's* [noun] &middot; *no one* [adjective/prepositional phrase] (*no one new*, *no one in the building*) &middot; *no one else* / *no one else's* &middot; *no one who / that* [clause]
**Contrast:** **Spelling first**: in American English this is **two words**. *No-one* with a hyphen is a British variant that American editors open out to two words, and *noone* solid is always an error — it would be read as rhyming with *moon*, which is exactly why English never welded this compound the way it welded *someone* and *anyone*. Against [nobody](#nobody), identical meaning; *no one* is somewhat commoner in print. Against *not … anyone*, the same meaning with different emphasis and a different available position (*I told no one* / *I didn't tell anyone*, but only *No one told me* at the front of a clause). Do not confuse *no one* with *none*: *none* can be plural and can refer to things. And keep it separate from the two-word quantifier phrase in *no one answer will satisfy everybody*, where *no* is a determiner modifying the noun *answer* and *one* is a numeral.

Denies that any person satisfies the description. It is the written-out counterpart of [nobody](#nobody) and the negative mirror of [everyone](#everyone), and like all the *no-* compounds it carries its own negative, so the verb after it is positive.

1. *No one* is answering the service line today.
2. *No one* has signed the acknowledgment form yet.
3. She told *no one* where she was going.
4. *No one* admitted *they* had seen the memo. — singular verb, singular *they*
5. It is *no one's* job to check the backups, which is the problem.
6. *No one* in either department was notified.
7. *No one* else knew the combination.

**See also:** [nobody](#nobody), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [no one else](#no-one-else), [everyone](#everyone), [anyone](#anyone), [nothing else](#nothing-else), [not a soul](#not-a-soul)

### nobody

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊˌbɑdi/ &middot; *NOH-bod-ee* &middot; stress on *no-*, whose vowel stays the full diphthong /oʊ/; the second element reduces and its /d/ taps, giving the everyday [ˈnoʊbəɾi] or [ˈnoʊbʌdi]. The word does not reduce further — a negative carries too much information to lose its first syllable
**Русский:** никто — то же, что no one, но чуть разговорнее; второго отрицания при глаголе не бывает
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (negative) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; genitive *nobody's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; slightly more conversational than *no one*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no gender. The homograph in *he's a nobody* is a countable **noun** and pluralizes (*a room full of nobodies*).
**Refers to:** **no person at all** — human referents only, denying the whole class. For things use [nothing](#nothing); for a mixed or non-human set use [none](#none).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always, and the verb stays positive** — *Nobody **is** home*, *Nobody **was** hurt*, *Nobody **knows***. The negation lives in the pronoun, so the verb must not be negated as well: ✗ *Nobody didn't come* (unless you truly mean everybody came). Back-reference is singular *they*: *Nobody wanted to give **their** name.*
**Pattern:** *nobody* [positive singular verb] &middot; [verb] *nobody* (*I saw nobody*) &middot; [preposition] *nobody* &middot; *nobody's* [noun] &middot; *nobody* [adjective/prepositional phrase] (*nobody important*, *nobody from the office*) &middot; *nobody else* / *nobody else's* &middot; *nobody who / that* [clause]
**Contrast:** Against *not … anybody*, which means the same thing: *I didn't tell anybody* and *I told nobody* are both standard, the *nobody* version being more emphatic and more formal. Only *nobody* can stand in front of the verb — ✗ *Anybody didn't call* is not English; *Nobody called* is. The hard rule is that **standard American English allows one negative per clause**: ✗ *I didn't see nobody* is negative concord, systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular but marked as nonstandard in edited prose; see [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord). Against [no one](#no-one), pure style — identical meaning, *no one* commoner in writing. Against [none](#none), coverage: *nobody* is people-only and always singular, *none* covers people and things and can take a plural verb.

Denies that any person at all satisfies the description. It is the negative member of the *-body* set, the mirror image of [everybody](#everybody), and unlike [anybody](#anybody) it carries its own negation and therefore works perfectly well as the subject of an ordinary positive verb.

1. *Nobody* is home on Thursdays.
2. *Nobody* was hurt in the collision.
3. I called twice and reached *nobody*.
4. *Nobody* wanted to give *their* name to the reporter.
5. It was *nobody's* fault, and the report said so.
6. *Nobody* from the county has returned my calls.
7. *Nobody* important is going to read this memo — the adjective follows the pronoun.

**See also:** [no one](#no-one), [nobody else](#nobody-else), [no one else](#no-one-else), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [anybody](#anybody), [everybody](#everybody), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord), [no](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no), [not a soul](#not-a-soul)

### none

**Pronunciation:** /nʌn/ &middot; *NUN* &middot; **rhymes with *fun*, not with *bone*** — it is a perfect homophone of *nun*. The spelling misleads because the word is a worn-down compound of Old English *ne* + *ān*, *not one*, and the vowel followed *one* rather than the spelling. There is no weak form; a one-syllable negative does not reduce
**Русский:** ни один, никто; ничто — из уже названного множества; глагол в ед. или мн. ч.
**Type:** indefinite pronoun (negative); historically the compound *ne* + *one* &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; no genitive (*✗ none's*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the emphatic singular is faintly formal
**Person / number:** third person; **number is variable**, singular or plural depending on what it refers to — the whole point of the entry.
**Refers to:** **people or things**, unlike the rest of the *no-* words, which are split between the two. It can range over a **count set** (*none of the applicants*) or a **mass** (*none of the milk*). Used alone, it needs a set already established in the context: *I looked for chairs and found none.*
**Agreement:** **The most misreported agreement fact in English.** With a **mass** noun the verb is **singular** — *None of the milk **was** spoiled*, *None of the evidence **is** admissible*. With a **plural count** set the verb may be **either**, and the **plural is older, commoner, and usually the better choice** — *None of the applicants **were** qualified*. Plural agreement runs unbroken from Old English through Shakespeare to the present, and Merriam-Webster, the *American Heritage Dictionary*, Fowler, and Garner all endorse it; the claim that *none* must be singular because it means *not one* was invented by eighteenth-century commentators and has no basis in the history of the word. Choose the **singular** when you want to press the *not one* reading (*None of the three has ever missed a deadline*) or when the *of*-phrase is a mass noun. Be aware that the *AP Stylebook* is the conservative holdout, defaulting to the singular, so a newsroom copy desk may change your plural.
**Pattern:** *none of* [the/my/these + plural noun] + [plural or singular verb] &middot; *none of* [the + mass noun] + [singular verb] &middot; *none of* [them/us/you] &middot; *none* [verb] (bare, with the set understood: *None were injured*) &middot; [verb] *none* (*I wanted coffee and there was none*) &middot; *none the* [comparative] (adverb: *none the wiser*) &middot; *none too* [adjective] (adverb: *none too pleased*)
**Contrast:** Against [no one](#no-one) and [nobody](#nobody): those are **people only** and **always singular**; *none* covers people and things and can go plural. Against [neither](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#neither): *neither* is for a set of exactly **two** and takes a **singular** verb in edited prose (*Neither of the two plans **works***; *neither of them are* is common in speech and still changed by editors), while *none* is for three or more and may go either way (*None of the five plans **work***). Against the determiner: ***none* is not one.** In modern English it never stands in front of a noun — ✗ *none applicants* — because that slot belongs to [no](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no) (*no applicants*); the determiner-flavored partitive *none of* is treated at [none of](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#none-of), and everything catalogued in this entry is the pronoun. Two adverbial idioms are a separate word class and never take a verb of their own: *none the wiser*, *none the worse for it*, *none too happy*.

Denies every member of a set already in view. It is the only member of this family whose number is genuinely open, which is why it needs a rule of its own rather than the flat *always singular* that governs the twelve compounds.

**1. Not any of a countable set.** *None of the smoke detectors were working.* Plural verb, and the default in modern American usage.

**2. Not any of a mass.** *None of the water was safe to drink.* Singular verb, obligatory.

**3. Emphatic not one.** *None of the four candidates has held elected office.* Singular verb, chosen deliberately for the one-by-one reading.

**4. Adverbial, in fixed phrases.** *None the wiser*, *none too pleased* — a different word class, modifying a comparative or an adjective.

1. *None* of the smoke detectors were working. — sense 1, plural, the ordinary modern choice
2. *None* of the water was safe to drink. — sense 2, mass noun, singular obligatory
3. *None* of the four finalists has ever held office. — sense 3, singular for the *not one* reading
4. We looked for a spare charger and found *none*. — bare, with the set understood
5. *None* were injured, according to the fire marshal.
6. He left an hour later, *none* the wiser. — sense 4, adverbial
7. She was *none* too pleased about the schedule change. — sense 4, adverbial

### not a soul

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ə ˈsoʊl/ &middot; *not-uh-SOHL* &middot; main stress on *soul*, whose vowel is the full diphthong /oʊ/ and never reduces. **The weak form is on the article**: *a* is /ə/ and never /eɪ/, and the /t/ of *not* taps between the two vowels, so the everyday form is [ˌnɑɾ ə ˈsoʊl]. Unmerged speakers keep /ɑ/ in *not*; the phrase carries one sweeping contour with the peak on *soul*
**Русский:** ни души — эмфатическое «совсем никого», с литературным оттенком
**Type:** emphatic negative indefinite phrase, filling a pronoun slot &middot; **Case:** none — invariant, one form for subject and object; no genitive is formed on it (rewrite as *nobody's*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a literary edge. An American meets it in narrative, in complaint, and in weather-and-emptiness description, and it is perfectly printable in edited prose
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular — and the singular is the whole point, since the phrase counts down to one person and then denies that one.
**Refers to:** **no person at all** — human referents only, and normally people who might have been present, seen, or heard from. *Soul* is a **synecdoche** for a person here and has no religious sense left in it. For things use [nothing](#nothing); for a set already in view, [none](#none).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, and the verb stays positive** — *Not a soul **was** there*, *Not a soul **has** called all week*. The negation lives inside the phrase, so a second one makes a double negative: ✗ *Not a soul didn't come*. Back-reference is singular *they*: *Not a soul admitted **they** had seen it.*
**Pattern:** *not a soul* [positive singular verb] &middot; *there wasn't a soul* [prepositional phrase] &middot; *not a soul in* [place] &middot; *not a soul for miles* &middot; *not a living soul* (the intensified form) &middot; [verb] *not a soul* (literary; ordinary American English puts *nobody* in the object slot)
**Contrast:** Against [nobody](#nobody) and [no one](#no-one), which state the same fact plainly: what *not a soul* adds is **emphasis** — *not even one* — which is why it is at home in front of the verb and in a short flat sentence (*Not a soul came*). It belongs to a family of emphatic negatives built on *not a* plus a minimal noun — *not a one*, *not a thing*, *not a word*, *not a drop*, and the dialectal *nary a one* — and it keeps their company in the negative-polarity frames *not a soul in sight* and *not a soul for miles*. Against [none](#none), which needs a set already established and can take a plural verb, where this phrase needs no set and is always singular. The intensifier *not a living soul* adds volume and nothing else. And keep the pronoun apart from the ordinary noun *soul*, which is alive and well in *a kind soul*, *the poor soul*, and *the soul of the record* — only inside this negative frame does the word mean simply *person*.
**Variants:** *not a living soul*, *never a soul* (literary), *not a soul in sight*, *not one soul*, *nary a soul* (Southern and Appalachian, and dialectal), *not a one* (of a set already named)

Denies that even one person satisfies the description, and denies it harder than *nobody* can. The phrase names the smallest possible number and then takes it away, which is the same move the [minimizers](#dick-shit-fuck-all) make with quantities, and it is why *not a soul* so often ends a sentence about an empty place.

1. *Not a soul* was waiting on the platform at that hour.
2. We knocked for ten minutes and *not a soul* came to the door.
3. *Not a soul* has called about the ad all week.
4. There wasn't *a soul* in the lot but the security guard. — the *there*-frame, where the negation sits on the verb and the phrase splits
5. *Not a living soul* outside the family knew about the loan. — the intensified form
6. He drove ninety miles down that road and saw *not a soul*. — the object slot, which is literary; ordinary speech says *saw nobody*
7. *Not a soul* would admit *they* had touched the thermostat. — singular verb, singular *they*

**See also:** [nobody](#nobody), [no one](#no-one), [none](#none), [nothing](#nothing), [not a one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#not-a-one), [not one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#not-one), [nary a one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#nary-a-one)

### nothing

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnʌθɪŋ/ &middot; *NUTH-ing* &middot; **the vowel is /ʌ/, not /oʊ/** — *nothing* does not rhyme with *no thing*, and mispronouncing it [ˈnoʊθɪŋ] is the commonest learner error on this word. Casual American English gives [ˈnʌθɪn] and, faster still, [ˈnʌʔn̩] — the *nuthin'* of ordinary speech. Stress on the first syllable
**Русский:** ничто, ничего — о вещах; глагол остаётся утвердительным, второго отрицания нет
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun (negative) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; *nothing's* in running text is almost always *nothing is* or *nothing has*, not a genitive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **no thing at all** — non-human referents: objects, ideas, events, amounts. Applied to a person it is an insult of the *he's nothing to me* kind, where the sense is *of no importance*. Back-reference is *it*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always, and the verb stays positive** — *Nothing **is** working*, *Nothing **has** changed*, *Nothing in these files **matches** the invoice*. ✗ *Nothing doesn't work* is a double negative unless you mean everything works.
**Pattern:** *nothing* [positive singular verb] &middot; [verb] *nothing* &middot; [preposition] *nothing* &middot; *nothing* [adjective] — the adjective follows (*nothing new*) &middot; *nothing to* [verb] &middot; *nothing that* [clause] &middot; *nothing but* [noun] &middot; *nothing like* [noun] &middot; *for nothing* &middot; *nothing else*
**Contrast:** **Nothing but** means *only* — *nothing but trouble* is all trouble. **Anything but** means *far from* — *anything but trouble* is no trouble at all. They look parallel and mean opposite things, and swapping them inverts the sentence. Against *not … anything*, same meaning with different weight: *I said nothing* is heavier than *I didn't say anything*, and only *nothing* works as a subject (*Nothing was said*). The one-negative rule applies: ✗ *I didn't say nothing* is negative concord, nonstandard in edited American English; see [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord). Two double negatives that are perfectly standard, though: **litotes**, where the second negative is deliberate and the meaning turns positive (*That's not nothing*, *the response was not uncommon*), and negation across a clause boundary (*I didn't say nothing was wrong* — two clauses, two independent negatives, entirely grammatical).

Denies the existence of any relevant thing. It has four uses in ordinary American English.

**1. Not a thing.** The core sense: *Nothing arrived in the mail.*

**2. Zero, in scores and arithmetic.** *We lost three to nothing.* Informal, alongside *zip*, *zilch*, and the tennis *love*.

**3. Something or someone of no value or importance.** *The award means nothing to her.* *He's nothing without that team.*

**4. Fixed phrases.** *Nothing but* (= only), *nothing like* (= not at all resembling), *nothing if not* (= emphatically), *for nothing* (= free, or in vain), *nothing doing* (= a refusal), *think nothing of it*.

1. *Nothing* is working in this building today. — sense 1
2. There's *nothing* new in the second report. — sense 1, adjective following
3. They lost the opener four to *nothing*. — sense 2
4. The apology meant *nothing* to the people who were laid off. — sense 3
5. That contract has brought us *nothing* but headaches. — sense 4, *nothing but* = only
6. We drove four hours for *nothing*.
7. *Nothing* that came out of that meeting surprised me.

## Compounds modified by *else*

*Else* subtracts. Attached to a compound it means *other than the one already in view*, and it **follows** the pronoun in every case: ✓ *someone else*, **else someone*. It combines with all twelve compounds, with the *-where* adverbs (*somewhere else*), and with the *wh-* words (*who else*, *what else*), but never with an ordinary noun — ✗ *the man else* has to become *the other man*. The possessive is the field's classic apostrophe question, and the answer is that ✗ *the *-s* goes on *else***: *someone else's coat*, *nobody else's business*. The same *else* attaches to the **quantifiers** as well, and there the quantifier keeps its own polarity — *all else*, *much else*, *little else* — which is why those three are catalogued together at the head of this section.

### all else, much else, little else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈɛls/, /ˌmʌtʃ ˈɛls/, /ˌlɪtl̩ ˈɛls/ &middot; *awl-ELS, much-ELS, LIT-l-ELS* &middot; main stress lands on *else* in all three, exactly as it does after the twelve compounds; the /l/ of *all* runs straight into the vowel of *else* with no break, [ˌɔl ˈɛls], and the /t/ of *little* is a tap or a glottal stop before its syllabic /l/, [ˈlɪɾl̩ ˈɛls]. Americans who merge *cot* and *caught* have /ɑl/ in *all*. **No weak forms** — *else* takes the stress and the quantifier in front of it keeps its full vowel
**Русский:** всё остальное; многое другое; мало что ещё — об оставшемся, только о вещах
**Type:** quantifying indefinite pronouns postmodified by *else* &middot; **Case:** none — invariant, one form for subject and object, and no genitive (✗ *all else's*, ✗ *little else's*) &middot; **Register:** neutral. *Much else* and *little else* are ordinary written and spoken English; *all else* is faintly formal and lives almost entirely inside three fixed phrases
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** **the remainder of a set of things, facts, or considerations** — whatever is left over once the thing just named is set aside. Non-human referents: for people the language uses [everybody else](#everybody-else) and [nobody else](#nobody-else), and there is no ✗ *much else* about persons. Back-reference is *it*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *All else **is** detail*, *Little else **matters***, *Not much else **was** said that evening*. *Little else* carries its own negation, so the verb after it stays positive: ✓ *Little else survived the fire*, ✗ *Little else didn't survive*.
**Pattern:** *all else being equal* &middot; *when all else fails* &middot; *all else* [singular verb] (*all else follows*) &middot; [negated verb] *much else* (*I don't remember much else*) &middot; *not much else to* [verb] &middot; *little else* [positive singular verb] &middot; *little else but / than* [noun] &middot; *there is little else* [infinitive]
**Contrast:** *Else* subtracts here exactly as it does after the compounds, but the words in front of it are **quantifiers**, so each one keeps its own quantifier behavior — and polarity is the practical difference. *Much else* is **nonassertive**: in ordinary usage it wants a negative, a question, or a conditional in front of it (✓ *I don't remember much else*, ✓ *Was there much else?*, and ✗ *I remember much else* is not idiomatic), which is the same restriction bare [much](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#much) lives under; the affirmative *there is much else to admire* does survive in formal writing, just as *much has been written* does. *Little else* supplies the negation itself and takes a positive verb — *Little else was salvageable* — and it is not the same as *a little else*, which is not English at all. *All else* is the universal member and is nearly frozen: *all else being equal*, *when all else fails*, *all else follows*, and elsewhere an American writes [everything else](#everything-else) instead — *everything else was fine* is ordinary, *all else was fine* reads like a translation. Keep the determiners out of it: *all* and *little* in front of a noun (*all the rest*, *little hope*) belong to the determiner dictionary, at [all](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#all) and [little](../../09-determiners/catalog/05-quantifiers-small.md#little).
**Variants:** *nothing else* and *everything else* (the compound equivalents, and the commoner words for the same facts), *not much else*, *precious little else*, *all the rest*

Three quantifiers wearing the same *else* the compounds wear, and meaning the remainder of the set. They are worth an entry of their own because the quantifier in front of *else* keeps its polarity: *much else* leans on a negative, *little else* is already negative, and *all else* has retired into a handful of set phrases.

1. *All else* being equal, take the earlier flight. — the fixed phrase, which is where *all else* mostly lives
2. When *all else* fails, call the manufacturer and ask for the service department.
3. Get the foundation right and *all else* follows.
4. There wasn't *much else* to do but wait for the tide. — nonassertive, licensed by the negative in front of it
5. I don't remember *much else* about that summer.
6. *Little else* in the report is worth the time it takes to read it. — positive verb; the negation sits inside *little*
7. The fire took the barn, and *little else* on the property was touched.

**See also:** [everything else](#everything-else), [nothing else](#nothing-else), [something else](#something-else), [anything else](#anything-else), [all](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#all), [much](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#much), [little](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#little)

### anybody else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛnibɑdi ˈɛls/ &middot; *en-ee-bod-ee-ELS* &middot; main stress moves onto *else*, which is what welds the phrase into one unit; *anybody* keeps a secondary stress on *an-*, its /d/ taps between vowels, and its final vowel runs straight into *else* with no break, giving the everyday [ˌɛnibəɾi ˈɛls]. The genitive *anybody else's* adds a syllable, [ˌɛnibəɾi ˈɛlsəz]
**Русский:** кто-нибудь ещё, кто-то другой — кроме уже названного; разговорнее, чем anyone else
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun postmodified by *else* (nonassertive and free choice) &middot; **Case:** none for the base form — one invariant shape for subject and object; genitive *anybody else's* &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more conversational than *anyone else*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural, no gender.
**Refers to:** **any additional or different person** — human referents only, with someone already named and now excluded, and with no commitment that such a further person exists. For a thing use [anything else](#anything-else).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *Is anybody else **coming**?*, *If anybody else **objects**, say so now*, *Hardly anybody else **was** there*. Back-reference is singular *they*, which is standard American English in edited prose as well as speech: *If anybody else wants a ride, tell **them** to wait out front.*
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anybody else* (*I didn't ask anybody else*) &middot; [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *anybody else* (question) &middot; *if / unless* … *anybody else* &middot; *hardly / scarcely / rarely / without* … *anybody else* &middot; [comparative] *than anybody else* &middot; *anybody else* [singular verb] (free choice) &middot; *anybody else who / that* [clause] &middot; *anybody else's* [noun] &middot; *anybody else* [adjective] (*anybody else new*)
**Contrast:** Identical in meaning to [anyone else](#anyone-else) and one notch more conversational, exactly as [anybody](#anybody) is to [anyone](#anyone); edited prose leans to the *-one* form. Polarity runs as it does for bare *anybody*: *anybody else* under a negative, in a question, or in a conditional, and [somebody else](#somebody-else) in a plain statement or in the question that is really an offer (*Would somebody else like to try?*). The ordering rule holds too — an *any-* compound cannot sit in front of the negative that licenses it, so only [nobody else](#nobody-else) can head a clause: ✓ *Nobody else knew*, ✗ *Anybody else didn't know*. In the **comparative** frame the *else* is doing real logical work: *she is faster than anybody else on the team* keeps her out of the very set she is being measured against, where the *else*-less version quietly puts her in it. The possessive puts the *-s* on *else*: ✓ *anybody else's guess*, ✗ *anybody's else guess*; see [someone else's](#someone-elses).

Refers to a further or different person without asserting that one exists. It carries both of bare *anybody*'s lives — nonassertive under a negative, a question, or a condition, and free choice in affirmatives — and adds the subtraction that *else* contributes: whoever is already in view is taken out of the set.

1. Did *anybody else* hear that? — nonassertive, in a question
2. I didn't tell *anybody else* about the transfer. — nonassertive, under a negative
3. If *anybody else* wants a ride, tell *them* to wait out front. — conditional, with singular *they*
4. *Anybody else* would have walked out an hour ago. — free choice
5. He finished ahead of *anybody else* in his age group. — the comparative, where *else* is doing real work
6. Hardly *anybody else* stayed for the second session.
7. It is *anybody else's* guess where the money went. — the genitive, with the *-s* on *else*

### anything else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛniθɪŋ ˈɛls/ &middot; *en-ee-thing-ELS* &middot; the main stress moves onto *else*, which is what welds the phrase into a single unit; *anything* keeps a secondary stress on its first syllable. The /ŋ/ links straight into the following vowel with no break between the words
**Русский:** что-нибудь ещё, что-то другое — кроме уже названного; «Anything else?» — «Ещё что-нибудь?»
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun postmodified by *else* (nonassertive and free choice) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant; the genitive *anything else's* is theoretically possible and practically never used &middot; **Register:** neutral; the counter-service question *Anything else?* is one of the most-heard sentences in American commercial life
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **any additional or different thing** — non-human referents, in a context where some thing has already been named or supplied.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Is there anything else that **needs** signing?*, *Nothing else **is** open at this hour*.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anything else* &middot; *Is / was there anything else* …? (question) &middot; *if* … *anything else* &middot; *anything else* [adjective] (*anything else useful*) &middot; *anything else to* [verb] &middot; *anything else that* [clause]
**Contrast:** Polarity works exactly as it does for bare [anything](#anything), which means the service-counter question splits two ways. ***Anything else?*** is neutral and open — it asks whether there is more. ***Something else?*** is an offer, and it presumes there is. A waiter who says *Anything else for you?* is closing out; one who says *Can I get you something else?* is selling. Under a negative, *anything else* is the only option: ✗ *I don't want something else* is odd unless *something else* escapes the negation. Against [nothing else](#nothing-else), the same relationship *anything* has with *nothing*: *I don't need anything else* equals *I need nothing else*, the second being heavier.

Asks after or denies any further thing beyond what has already been mentioned. *Else* here means *other than the one already in view*, and it follows the pronoun — this is the fixed order for the whole family.

1. Is there *anything else* you need before I lock up?
2. I couldn't find *anything else* in the file cabinet.
3. If there's *anything else* on the invoice, flag it now.
4. *Anything else* would have been an improvement on that ending.
5. She never mentioned *anything else* about the phone call.
6. Do you want fries, a drink, *anything else*?

### everybody else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌmwʌn ˈɛlsəz/ &middot; *sum-wun-EL-siz* &middot; the genitive *-s* attaches to *else* and is pronounced /əz/ after the /s/, adding a syllable: [ˌsʌmwʌn ˈɛlsəz]. Main stress stays on *else*

Things do not take this genitive comfortably: *nothing else's* and *everything else's* are avoided in favor of *of* (*the cost of everything else*). Compare the determiner treatment at [somebody else's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses).

Marks something as belonging to a person other than the one in view. Grammatically it behaves exactly like *John's* or *the neighbor's*: it fills the determiner slot, so it cannot combine with *the*, *a*, or *this* — ✓ *someone else's problem*, ✗ *the someone else's problem* — and it can equally stand alone as a full noun phrase with no noun after it, *that coat is someone else's*.

1. That's *someone else's* problem now.
2. I picked up *someone else's* umbrella by mistake.
3. *Someone else's* keys are sitting on the break-room counter.
4. You can't sign *someone else's* name on a federal form.
5. It was *nobody else's* business, and she said so.
6. *Everyone else's* flight got canceled that night.
7. This jacket isn't mine — it must be *someone else's*. — standing alone as a full noun phrase, with no noun after it

**See also:** [someone else](#someone-else), [someone](#someone), [everyone else](#everyone-else), [no one else](#no-one-else), [anyone else](#anyone-else), [somebody else's](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#somebody-elses)

### something else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌmθɪŋ ˈɛls/ &middot; *sum-thing-ELS* &middot; stress on *else*, with the reductions of *something* still available in the first half: [ˌsʌmpm̩ ˈɛls]. In the *remarkable* sense the phrase is often drawn out with a fall on *else* — *she's something ELSE*
**Русский:** что-то другое, нечто иное; разг. «нечто особенное, нечто» (she's something else)
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun postmodified by *else* (assertive) &middot; **Case:** none — invariant; no genitive in ordinary use &middot; **Register:** neutral in sense 1; informal and admiring in sense 2
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular; invariant.
**Refers to:** **a different thing** in sense 1 — non-human. In sense 2 it can be predicated of a **person** (*that kid is something else*), which is the one place in this family where a *-thing* compound reaches a human referent.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Something else **is** going on here*, *Something else **has** to give*.
**Pattern:** *something else* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *something else* &middot; *something else* [adjective] (*something else cheap*) &middot; *something else to* [verb] &middot; *something else that* [clause] &middot; [subject] *is something else* (predicative, the admiring sense)
**Contrast:** Against [anything else](#anything-else), the ordinary polarity split, which shows up sharply in offers: *Would you like something else?* offers a replacement, *Do you want anything else?* asks whether the list is finished. Against [nothing else](#nothing-else), its negative. The idiomatic sense 2 has no *any-* or *no-* counterpart at all — ✗ *he's anything else* is not English in that meaning — which is a reminder that fixed phrases sit outside the polarity system. Distinguish also from *some other thing*, which is the fully compositional paraphrase and sounds stilted.

Names a thing other than the one in view. Two uses, and the second is idiomatic.

**1. A different or additional thing.** *Let's talk about something else.* This is the ordinary sense and takes the usual postmodifiers.

**2. Remarkable — good or bad.** *That storm was something else.* Informal, predicative only, and applicable to people as well as things. The tone is admiring by default and can turn sardonic with the right delivery.

1. Let's talk about *something else*. — sense 1
2. *Something else* is going on here that nobody's saying out loud. — sense 1
3. If the sea bass is gone, I'll order *something else*. — sense 1
4. That thunderstorm last night was *something else*. — sense 2
5. Her second album is *something else* entirely. — sense 2
6. He asked for *something else* to read on the flight.

**See also:** [something](#something), [anything else](#anything-else), [nothing else](#nothing-else), [everything else](#everything-else), [someone else](#someone-else)

## The *-where* forms — adverbs, not pronouns

These four are **adverbs**, not pronouns: they fill an adverbial slot, so *I went somewhere* patterns with *I went home* rather than with *I saw something*, and none of them can be a direct object (✗ *I bought somewhere*). They are catalogued here because they take the same four stems, obey the same polarity rule, take the same postmodifiers (*somewhere quiet*, *nowhere to go*), and take *else* the same way. The honest wrinkle is that after a preposition they do head something that looks like a noun phrase — *out of nowhere*, *from somewhere in Ohio* — which is why grammars disagree about their category. The colloquial American *-place* series is included alongside them.

### anywhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌwɛr/ &middot; *EN-ee-wair* &middot; *any* is /ˈɛni/, the vowel of *pen*; *-where* keeps a full /ɛr/ and a secondary stress and never reduces to a schwa. Free-choice *anywhere* takes a heavier and often lengthened first syllable
**Русский:** где-нибудь, куда-нибудь; где угодно, куда угодно — наречие места, а не местоимение
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverb** (nonassertive and free choice) — not a pronoun, though it completes the pronoun system &middot; **Case:** none — adverbs take no case &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant; no person or number of its own.
**Refers to:** **a place, location, or destination**, unspecified and with no commitment that one exists. In the quantity idiom *anywhere from … to …* it refers to a point on a numerical range instead.
**Agreement:** as an adverb it triggers no verb agreement of its own; the verb agrees with the clause's own subject — *The keys **are** not anywhere in this house*. Under a negative it must follow the negation: ✓ *I didn't go anywhere*, ✗ *Anywhere I didn't go*.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *anywhere* (*I can't find it anywhere*) &middot; [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *anywhere* (question) &middot; *if* … *anywhere* &middot; *anywhere* [adjective] (*anywhere quiet*) &middot; *anywhere near* [noun] &middot; *anywhere from* [number] *to* [number] &middot; [preposition] *anywhere* (*from anywhere*, *to anywhere with a runway*)
**Contrast:** These four *-where* words are **adverbs, not pronouns** — they fill an adverbial slot, not a noun-phrase slot, so *I went somewhere* patterns with *I went home*, not with *I saw something*. They are catalogued here because they take the same four stems, obey the same polarity rule, take the same postmodifiers (*anywhere quiet*, *nowhere to go*), and take *else* the same way (*somewhere else*). The honest wrinkle is that they do occasionally head a genuine noun phrase after a preposition — *from nowhere*, *in somewhere warm* — which is why grammars disagree about their category. Against [somewhere](#somewhere), polarity. *Anywhere near* is a fixed intensifier under a negative: *not anywhere near enough* is far short.

Refers to a place without committing to which one, or to whether such a place exists. Two uses plus a quantity idiom.

**1. Nonassertive.** Under a negative, in a question, or in a conditional: *I can't find it anywhere.*

**2. Free choice.** In affirmatives, meaning *no matter where*: *Sit anywhere you like.*

**3. Quantity range.** *Anywhere from twenty to fifty people* — an approximator, and the one use where *anywhere* has nothing to do with place.

1. I can't find my other glove *anywhere*. — sense 1
2. Did you go *anywhere* interesting over the break? — sense 1, adjective following
3. Sit *anywhere* you like; the seats aren't assigned. — sense 2
4. That truck will get you *anywhere* with a road. — sense 2
5. We're expecting *anywhere* from twenty to fifty people. — sense 3
6. The bid wasn't *anywhere* near what the house was worth.

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [everywhere](#everywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere), [anything](#anything), [someplace, anyplace, everyplace, noplace](#someplace-anyplace-everyplace-noplace)

### everywhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriˌwɛr/ &middot; *EV-ree-wair* &middot; /ˈɛvri/ with the medial vowel dropped, as in *everyone*; *-where* keeps its full /ɛr/ and a secondary stress. Americans outside the Northeast pronounce the vowel of *-where* the same as in *air* and *bear*
**Русский:** везде, повсюду — наречие места
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverb** (universal) — not a pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — adverbs take no case &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant.
**Refers to:** **all the places in view**, or metaphorically all the parts of something (*there's dust everywhere*).
**Agreement:** no agreement of its own as an adverb. In the colloquial subject-like use it takes a **singular** verb — *Everywhere **was** closed by nine* — and in fronted emphatic position the clause inverts: *Everywhere I looked **there was** another form to sign.*
**Pattern:** [verb] *everywhere* &middot; *everywhere* [clause] (*everywhere I go*) &middot; *everywhere* [adjective/participle] (*everywhere visible*) &middot; *from / to everywhere* &middot; *everywhere else*
**Contrast:** Against *all over* and *all around*, which are its ordinary spoken equivalents (*dust all over the place*). Against [anywhere](#anywhere), the same universal-versus-free-choice split that separates [everything](#everything) from *anything*: *go everywhere* covers the whole map, *go anywhere* picks one point at will. The construction *everywhere I go* is a **free relative adverb** — *everywhere* is doing the work of *in every place where* — and it needs no *that*: *everywhere that I go* is not wrong, merely redundant, and editors cut the *that*. The colloquial *Everywhere was closed* treats the adverb as a subject; it is idiomatic and widespread but still flagged by some editors, and *Everything was closed* or *All the stores were closed* avoids the argument.

Covers the whole set of relevant places, with none left out. It is the universal member of the *-where* group and the mirror of [nowhere](#nowhere).

1. There was construction dust *everywhere*.
2. *Everywhere* I go, somebody asks about the merger.
3. The recall notice went *everywhere* except to the people who owned the car.
4. By ten o'clock *everywhere* downtown was closed. — the colloquial subject use, singular verb
5. She has looked *everywhere* else already.
6. Reports came in from *everywhere* between Denver and Omaha.

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [anywhere](#anywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere), [everything](#everything), [everything else](#everything-else)

### nowhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊˌwɛr/ &middot; *NOH-wair* &middot; stress on *no-*, whose vowel stays the full /oʊ/; *-where* takes a clear secondary stress. Unlike *nothing*, this compound keeps the vowel the spelling promises
**Русский:** нигде, никуда — наречие; вынесенное вперёд требует инверсии, чего в русском нет
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverb** (negative) — not a pronoun, though it takes noun-phrase position after prepositions &middot; **Case:** none — adverbs take no case &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant.
**Refers to:** **no place at all**. After a preposition it behaves like a noun (*out of nowhere*, *the middle of nowhere*), which is the clearest evidence that the *-where* words straddle two categories.
**Agreement:** no agreement of its own, but **fronting it forces subject-auxiliary inversion** — *Nowhere **is** this clearer than in the third chapter*, ✗ *Nowhere this is clearer*. This is a genuine rule, not a stylistic option, and it applies to every fronted negative adverb.
**Pattern:** [positive verb] *nowhere* (*The path led nowhere*) &middot; *Nowhere* + [auxiliary] + [subject] + [verb] (fronted, with inversion) &middot; *nowhere to* [verb] (*nowhere to sit*) &middot; *nowhere near* [noun/adjective] &middot; *out of nowhere* &middot; *the middle of nowhere* &middot; *get nowhere* &middot; *nowhere else*
**Contrast:** Against *not … anywhere*, the same meaning with different weight and different available positions: *We got nowhere* and *We didn't get anywhere* are both standard, but only *nowhere* can be fronted, and fronting it triggers inversion. The one-negative rule applies as it does across this whole file: ✗ *We didn't get nowhere* is negative concord, nonstandard in edited American English. ***Nowhere near*** is a fixed degree phrase meaning *far from* — *nowhere near enough money* — and is not about location at all. And *nowheres* with an *-s* is regional and nonstandard; see [nowheres, anywheres, somewheres](#nowheres-anywheres-somewheres).

Denies every place. Its life outside literal location is unusually rich for a negative adverb.

**1. No place.** *The trail led nowhere.*

**2. No progress.** *This conversation is getting us nowhere.*

**3. Fixed phrases.** *Out of nowhere* (suddenly and without warning), *the middle of nowhere* (remote), *nowhere near* (far from), *nowhere to be found*, *nowhere to turn*, and the attributive *a nowhere job*.

1. The service road led *nowhere*. — sense 1
2. This argument is getting us *nowhere*. — sense 2
3. A pickup came out of *nowhere* and took the mirror off. — sense 3
4. They bought forty acres in the middle of *nowhere*. — sense 3
5. It was *nowhere* near enough money to cover the repairs. — sense 3, degree
6. *Nowhere* is that clearer than in the last chapter. — fronted, with inversion
7. There was *nowhere* to sit and *nowhere* to put the bags.

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [anywhere](#anywhere), [everywhere](#everywhere), [nothing](#nothing), [nowheres, anywheres, somewheres](#nowheres-anywheres-somewheres)

### someplace, anyplace, everyplace, noplace

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmˌpleɪs/, /ˈɛniˌpleɪs/, /ˈɛvriˌpleɪs/, /ˈnoʊˌpleɪs/ &middot; *SUM-playss, EN-ee-playss, EV-ree-playss, NOH-playss* &middot; each keeps the stress pattern of its *-where* twin, with a full secondary stress on *-place*; the /s/ is voiceless, never /z/. All four are written solid in American English, though *some place* as two words is also seen and means the same thing
**Русский:** разг. амер.: где-то, где-нибудь, везде, нигде — просторечные замены форм на -where
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverbs**, American colloquial variants of the *-where* forms &middot; **Case:** none — adverbs take no case &middot; **Register:** **informal American English.** *Someplace* and *anyplace* are common in speech and in relaxed writing; *everyplace* and *noplace* are rarer and read as more markedly casual. All four sound distinctly American to British ears, and a formal American editor will usually restore the *-where* form
**Person / number:** invariant.
**Refers to:** **a place**, exactly as the corresponding *-where* word does, with the same polarity behavior: *someplace* asserts, *anyplace* questions and denies, *everyplace* universalizes, *noplace* negates.
**Agreement:** no agreement of their own. *Noplace* fronted takes inversion just as *nowhere* does, though the fronted construction is stiff enough that it is rarely paired with these informal forms.
**Pattern:** [verb] *someplace / anyplace / everyplace / noplace* &middot; *someplace* [adjective] (*someplace warm*) &middot; *someplace / anyplace else* &middot; [negated verb] *anyplace* &middot; *anyplace* [clause] (*anyplace they'll take a check*)
**Contrast:** These are stylistic variants, not different words: *let's go someplace quiet* and *let's go somewhere quiet* are identical in meaning, and only register separates them. The *-place* forms are one notch more casual, and *noplace* and *everyplace* are two. They do not take the fixed idioms that *nowhere* owns — ✗ *out of noplace*, ✗ *the middle of noplace*, ✗ *noplace near enough* are all wrong, because those phrases are frozen with *nowhere*. That is the practical rule: use the *-place* forms for ordinary reference to location, and keep the *-where* forms for every idiom.

The American colloquial parallel series to *somewhere*, *anywhere*, *everywhere*, and *nowhere*. They arose in nineteenth-century American English and remain a recognizable marker of it; their meanings and their polarity are identical to the *-where* forms, and only the idioms fail to transfer.

1. Let's go *someplace* quiet and talk it through.
2. I couldn't find parking *anyplace* near the arena.
3. She's got relatives *everyplace* between Tulsa and Amarillo.
4. There was *noplace* to sit, so we ate standing up.
5. Take it *someplace else* — this counter is closed.
6. Is there *anyplace* around here that's open past nine?

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [anywhere](#anywhere), [everywhere](#everywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere)

### somewhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmˌwɛr/ &middot; *SUM-wair* &middot; stress on *some-*; *-where* keeps a full /ɛr/ and a secondary stress and never reduces. Many Americans have a slight /hw/ at the start of *-where*, chiefly in the South and among older speakers; most say plain /w/
**Русский:** где-то, куда-то — неназванное место; также «примерно» (somewhere around fifty)
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverb** (assertive) — not a pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — adverbs take no case &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** invariant.
**Refers to:** **a place, location, or destination** that exists but is unidentified. In the approximator use (*somewhere around fifty*) it refers to a point on a numerical range.
**Agreement:** no agreement of its own. After a preposition it can head what looks like a noun phrase — *from somewhere in Ohio*, *in somewhere warm* — which is why its category is genuinely mixed.
**Pattern:** [verb] *somewhere* &middot; *somewhere* [adjective] — the adjective follows (*somewhere quiet*) &middot; *somewhere to* [verb] (*somewhere to park*) &middot; [preposition] *somewhere* (*from somewhere*, *in somewhere warm*) &middot; *somewhere else* &middot; *somewhere around / between* [number]
**Contrast:** Polarity as elsewhere in this file: *somewhere* in plain statements, [anywhere](#anywhere) under a negative, in a question, or after *if* — with the offer exception intact, so *Shall we go somewhere for lunch?* is an invitation while *Shall we go anywhere for lunch?* is not English at all in that reading. Against the pronoun [something](#something), the difference is what slot they fill: *something quiet* is a thing, *somewhere quiet* is a place, and only *something* can be a direct object (*I bought something*, ✗ *I bought somewhere*). The idiom *getting somewhere* means *making progress* and is the positive counterpart of *getting nowhere*.

Points at a place that exists but is unspecified. Two uses.

**1. An unidentified place.** *I left my keys somewhere in this house.* Takes the family's usual postmodifiers: *somewhere quiet*, *somewhere to park*, *somewhere I've never been*.

**2. An approximator.** Before a number or a range it hedges: *somewhere around forty dollars*, *somewhere between March and May*.

1. I left my keys *somewhere* in this house. — sense 1
2. Let's find *somewhere* quiet to sit. — sense 1, adjective following
3. We need *somewhere* to park before the game lets out. — sense 1
4. The estimate came in *somewhere* around eight thousand. — sense 2
5. It happened *somewhere* between Flagstaff and the state line. — sense 2
6. Now we're getting *somewhere*.

**See also:** [anywhere](#anywhere), [everywhere](#everywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere), [something](#something), [someplace, anyplace, everyplace, noplace](#someplace-anyplace-everyplace-noplace)

## Informal negatives and placeholders

Three small families sit at the edge of the indefinite system. All of them are thoroughly American, most of them are informal, and none of them behaves quite like the twelve compounds. The **minimizers** — *squat*, *diddly*, *diddly-squat*, *jack*, the vulgar *dick*, *shit*, and *fuck all*, and the printable *zilch*, *nada*, *bupkis*, and *zip* — all mean *nothing*, and they split on one grammatical point worth learning as a pair: *squat* and *jack* take a negated verb or a positive one with no change of meaning (*I didn't get squat* = *I got squat*), while *zilch* and its group carry the negation themselves and refuse a second one (✓ *I got zilch*, ✗ *I didn't get zilch*). The **placeholders** — *so-and-so*, *what's-his-name*, *you-know-who*, *whatchamacallit* and its synonyms — all stand where a name should be, and they divide by **why** the name is missing: nobody has picked one yet (*so-and-so*), the speaker had it and lost it (*what's-his-name*), the speaker has it and will not say it aloud (*you-know-who*), or the object's name simply will not come (*whatchamacallit*). *God knows what* is the fifth answer to that question and the bleakest: nobody has it and nobody is going to get it. The third family is the **list-closers**, which stand at the end of a coordination in place of the items the speaker declines to name — *you name it* here, and *whatnot* below at [whatnot](#whatnot). Every entry below is labeled for register, because register is the fact a learner most needs about this group: these are words American speech reaches for constantly and American editing removes without discussion. Two boundary notes. The placeholder that takes a noun after it — *such-and-such a date* — is a **determiner**, not a pronoun, and is catalogued at [such-and-such (a)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a), with its bare pronoun use at [such and such, so-and-so](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#such-and-such-so-and-so). And the **general extenders** that close a list — *and so on*, *and so forth*, *et cetera*, *and all that*, *and stuff*, and the hedging tags *or something*, *or anything*, and *or whatever* — are not pronouns at all: each is a conjunction plus a placeholder, filling a coordinator slot rather than a noun-phrase slot, and they are catalogued in the conjunction dictionary at [and so forth](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#and-so-forth), [et cetera / etc.](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#et-cetera--etc), [and stuff / and everything / and all that](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#and-stuff--and-everything--and-all-that), and [or something](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#or-something).

### dick, shit, fuck all

**Pronunciation:** /dɪk/, /ʃɪt/, /ˌfʌk ˈɔl/ &middot; *DICK, SHIT, fuck-AWL* &middot; one syllable each except *fuck all*, which takes its main stress on *all* and runs the /k/ straight into the vowel with no release between them, [ˌfʌk ˈɔl]; merged speakers have /ɑl/. The final /t/ of *shit* is an unreleased or glottal stop before a pause, [ʃɪʔ], and a tap before a vowel. **These words have no weak form.** They exist to carry the nuclear stress of the clause, so everything around them reduces instead: *he doesn't know dick about it* runs [i ˈdʌzn̩ ˈnoʊ ˈdɪk əˈbaʊɾ ɪt], with *he* at its weak /i/ and the /t/ of *about* tapped
**Русский:** груб.: ни хера, ни хрена; fuck all — ни хуя — «совсем ничего»
**Type:** minimizer indefinite pronouns (negative) — **vulgar** &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object, and no genitive (✗ *dick's*) &middot; **Register:** **vulgar.** *Dick* and *shit* in this use are obscene in most American settings and are bleeped on broadcast television; *fuck all* is the strongest of the three, is chiefly British and Irish, and reaches American speech mainly through imported film and television and in the fixed *sweet fuck all*. **Never in** a classroom, a job interview, a customer call, a cover letter, or any edited prose that is not quoting somebody — the printable substitutes are *squat* and *diddly-squat* one notch up, *zilch* and *zip* two notches up, and plain *nothing* in writing
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** **nothing at all** — knowledge, effort, money, help, or results, and most often somebody else's competence. Non-human referents only; for a person use [nobody](#nobody). The pronoun *shit* is a mass quantity word and has nothing to do with the countable noun of *a shit* or the plural of *the shits*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** in the uncommon subject use — *Dick **is** what we got for two weeks of overtime*. Their ordinary home is the object slot, where they trigger no agreement at all. As with every minimizer, **the verb may be negative or positive with no change in meaning**: *he knows dick about engines* and *he doesn't know dick about engines* both say he knows nothing.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *dick / shit / fuck all* (*You don't know dick*) &middot; [positive verb] *dick* (*He knows dick about it*) &middot; *not worth shit* &middot; *know dick about* [noun] &middot; *do fuck all* &middot; *sweet fuck all* (the intensified British form) &middot; *dick* standing alone as an answer (*What did they find? — Dick.*)
**Contrast:** These are [jack](#jack-jack-shit) and [squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat) with the euphemism stripped off, and they behave identically. All of them are **negative-polarity minimizers** whose bare form already means *nothing*, so the negative on the verb is optional and changes nothing: *I got shit for it* and *I didn't get shit for it* are the same complaint, and neither is a double negative. Against [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), which run the other way and refuse a negated verb (✓ *I got zilch*, ✗ *I didn't get zilch*). Against *jack*, register is the only difference: *jack* is the clipped euphemism that passes in ordinary conversation, and these three are the words the euphemism was invented to avoid. Two homograph traps, both live. The ordinary noun *shit* is everywhere in idiom (*give a shit*, *the shit hit the fan*, *a load of shit*) and is not this pronoun; and **the article reverses the meaning** — *that record is the shit* is high praise in American slang, where *that record is shit* is the opposite. *Dick* is also the man's name, the noun for a detective in older writing, and an insult for a person (*he's a dick*), none of which is the pronoun.
**Variants:** *jack shit*, *jack squat*, *jack diddly* (the blends), *sweet fuck all*, *bugger all*, *sod all* (all three chiefly British and Irish), *not a damn thing*, *squat*, *diddly-squat*, *zilch* (the printable equivalents)

The unprintable end of the minimizer family: three ways of saying *nothing at all* as rudely as American English says it. They earn an entry because a learner meets them constantly in film, in sports talk, and in argument, and needs to know two things at once — that they are ordinary in that register, and that they are unusable in every other.

1. He's been on the job a month and knows *dick* about the hydraulics. — positive verb, negative meaning
2. Two weeks of overtime and I got *shit* for it.
3. That warranty isn't worth *shit* once you break the seal. — under a negative, and the meaning is unchanged
4. — What did the inspector turn up? — *Dick*. — standing alone as an answer, which is where these words are strongest
5. They did *fuck all* about the leak until the ceiling came down. — the British-flavored member of the set
6. Never in: a classroom, an interview, a customer call, or anything with an editor in front of it.
7. Formal rewrite: *The inspection produced no findings.* — what goes in the report where the spoken version had *dick*

**See also:** [jack, jack shit](#jack-jack-shit), [diddly, diddly-squat, squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat), [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [nobody](#nobody), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord)

### diddly, diddly-squat, squat

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪdli/, /ˌdɪdli ˈskwɑt/, /skwɑt/ &middot; *DID-lee, did-lee-SKWOT, SKWOT* &middot; main stress falls on *squat*, whose vowel is that of *cot*; the final /t/ is regularly an unreleased stop or a glottal stop before a pause, [ˈskwɑʔ], and a tap before a vowel, so *squat about it* runs [ˈskwɑɾ əˈbaʊɾ ɪt]. **These words have no weak form.** They exist to carry the nuclear stress of the clause, so they never reduce; what reduces is everything around them, which is why *he doesn't know squat about it* comes out [i ˈdʌzn̩ ˈnoʊ ˈskwɑɾ əˈbaʊɾ ɪt], with *he* at its weak /i/. The older *doodly-squat* /ˈdudli ˌskwɑt/ is still heard in the South
**Русский:** разг., грубовато: ни фига, ни шиша, ноль — «совсем ничего»; мягче, чем dick и jack shit
**Type:** minimizer indefinite pronoun (negative) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object, and no genitive (✗ *squat's*) &middot; **Register:** **informal.** *Diddly* is the mildest and the most dated, *diddly-squat* is the everyday full form, and bare *squat* is the commonest and the bluntest. All three are generally traced to *doodly-squat*, where *squat* is a euphemism for excrement, so they are mildly coarse rather than clean; none of them survives an editor, and formal prose uses *nothing*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** **nothing at all**, or the smallest imaginable amount of something — knowledge, effort, money, or results. Non-human referents only; for a person use [nobody](#nobody). Back-reference is *it*, though in practice these words sit at the end of the clause and are rarely referred back to at all.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** in the uncommon subject use — *Diddly-squat **has** changed since the audit*. Their ordinary home is the object slot, where they trigger no agreement. The one fact to memorize is that **the verb may be negative or positive with no change in meaning**: *he knows squat about engines* and *he doesn't know squat about engines* both say he knows nothing.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *squat / diddly / diddly-squat* (*I didn't get squat*) &middot; [positive verb] *squat* (*I got squat*) &middot; *not worth diddly-squat* &middot; *know squat about* [noun] &middot; *do squat* &middot; *squat* standing alone as an answer (*How much did they pay? — Squat.*)
**Contrast:** The polarity of this little family is genuinely odd and is the whole reason it needs an entry. *Squat* patterns like a negative-polarity item, loving a negated verb exactly as [anything](#anything) does — but unlike *anything* it also works after a positive verb and stays negative. ✗ *I know anything about it* is not English in that meaning, while ✓ *I know squat about it* means *I know nothing about it*. So *I didn't get squat* and *I got squat* both say the speaker got nothing, and neither is a double negative. That behavior belongs to **minimizers** generally — *I didn't sleep a wink*, *he didn't lift a finger* — and it is why this group sits at the edge of the *no-* series rather than inside it. Against [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), which mean the same thing and go the other way: ✓ *I got zilch*, ✗ *I didn't get zilch*. Against [nothing](#nothing), identical in meaning and nowhere near it in register. And keep the pronoun apart from its homographs: *squat* is also a verb (*squat down*) and a countable noun (*a deep squat*, *a squat in an empty building*), and only the pronoun means *nothing*.
**Variants:** *doodly-squat* (the older Southern form), *diddly-poo*, *jack squat*, *jack diddly* — all interchangeable, all informal, and all products of the same euphemism treadmill

Says *nothing* with force and an American accent. All three are **minimizers**: they name the smallest conceivable quantity in order to deny even that much, which is why they live in complaints — about how much somebody knows, how much they did, and how much they were paid. *Diddly-squat* is the full form, *squat* the clipping, and *diddly* the mildest survivor of a chain of euphemisms that began well below the belt.

1. He's been on the job three weeks and knows *squat* about the wiring. — positive verb, negative meaning
2. We sat in that meeting for two hours and got *diddly-squat* out of it.
3. That extended warranty isn't worth *diddly*. — under a negative, and the meaning is unchanged
4. — How much did the settlement come to? — *Squat*. — standing alone as an answer, which is where these words are strongest
5. The new filter didn't do *diddly-squat* for the noise in the ductwork.
6. She knows *squat* about football and picked the bracket anyway.
7. Formal rewrite: *The revised policy produced no measurable change.* — what an editor puts where *didn't do squat* was

**See also:** [jack, jack shit](#jack-jack-shit), [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [nobody](#nobody), [anything](#anything), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord), [dick, shit, fuck all](#dick-shit-fuck-all)

### God knows what, God knows who

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɑd noʊz ˈwʌt/, /ˌɡɑd noʊz ˈhu/ &middot; *god-nohz-WUT, god-nohz-HOO* &middot; spoken as one unit, with the main stress on the *wh-* word, a secondary stress on *God*, and *knows* sitting in the trough between them with no stress of its own. **The reduction is on the middle**: the /d/ of *God* is regularly unreleased or lost before the /n/ of *knows*, giving the everyday [ˌɡɑ noʊz ˈwʌt]. Southern and older speakers may keep an initial /hw/ on *what*. Say it with a pause and a fall after *knows* and it stops being this pronoun
**Русский:** бог знает что, бог знает кто — с оттенком раздражения или подозрения
**Type:** fused indefinite pro-form — a frozen clause standing in a noun-phrase slot &middot; **Case:** none — invariant; no genitive &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, and **mildly profane** for a good many American speakers, which is exactly why the minced variants exist. *Heaven knows* and *goodness knows* are the mild ones, *Lord knows* is the warm and chiefly Southern one, and *God knows* is the one that will not be said in front of some grandmothers. An editor replaces the whole phrase with a fact
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant. Inside the phrase, *knows* is frozen in the third-person singular present: ✗ *God knew what*, ✗ *God know what*.
**Refers to:** **a thing (*what*) or a person (*who*) that the speaker cannot identify and assumes nobody else can either** — with parallel forms for place, time, reason, and manner (*God knows where*, *God knows when*, *God knows why*, *God knows how*). What separates it from the plain indefinites is attitude: it does not merely decline to name the referent, it declares the naming hopeless, usually with exasperation or suspicion attached.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** when the phrase is the subject — *God knows what **is** living under that porch*, *God knows who **has** a key to this door*. In the object slot it triggers no agreement at all, which is where it spends most of its life.
**Pattern:** [verb] *God knows what* (*He keeps God knows what in there*) &middot; [preposition] *God knows who* &middot; *God knows what* [singular verb] &middot; *and God knows what else* (closing a list) &middot; *God knows where / when / why / how* (the parallel forms) &middot; *God only knows* (the intensified bare form) &middot; *God knows* standing alone as an answer
**Contrast:** The trap is that the same words are also an ordinary clause with an ordinary meaning, and **intonation** is what separates them: *God knows what he was thinking* run together with the peak on *what* is the pro-form and means *nobody has any idea*, while *God knows* with its own falling contour and a pause after it is a claim about God's knowledge and the speaker's ignorance (*God knows, I don't*). Against [something](#something) and [something or other](#something-or-other): those leave the referent unidentified, where this one insists it is unidentifiable and adds a shrug of disapproval. Against [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), which is a name the speaker has mislaid, and [you-know-what](#you-know-who-you-know-what), which is a name the speaker has and will not say. One boundary worth marking: put a noun straight after it — *God knows what address*, *God knows how many forms* — and the phrase is working as a **determiner** on that noun rather than filling the slot by itself; the pronoun use is the one that stands alone.
**Variants:** *heaven knows what*, *Lord knows what*, *goodness knows what*, *God only knows what*, *who knows what*, *God knows who / where / when / why / how*, *God knows what else*

A frozen clause that stopped being about God and became a pro-form: it fills the slot where the name of a thing or a person would go and then declares the slot unfillable. What it adds to the plain indefinites is an attitude — exasperation, suspicion, or a flat refusal to guess.

1. He keeps *God knows what* in that storage unit.
2. She was on the phone with *God knows who* until two in the morning.
3. *God knows what* is living under that back porch. — the subject slot, with a singular verb
4. The box had screws, dead batteries, and *God knows what* else in it. — closing a list
5. *Heaven knows what* the neighbors made of all that. — the minced variant, no less common in America than the original
6. — Who signed for the delivery? — *God knows*. — the bare phrase, standing alone as an answer
7. Compare the ordinary clause: *God knows what he was thinking, and He isn't telling.* — a pause and a falling tone after *knows*, and a genuine claim about who knows what

**See also:** [something](#something), [something or other](#something-or-other), [whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), [you-know-who, you-know-what](#you-know-who-you-know-what), [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [whatnot](#whatnot), [who cares, who knows](06-interrogative.md#who-cares-who-knows)

### jack, jack shit

**Pronunciation:** /dʒæk/, /ˌdʒæk ˈʃɪt/ &middot; *JACK, jack-SHIT* &middot; main stress on *shit* in the two-word form, with the /k/ running straight into the /ʃ/ and no release between them, [ˌdʒæk ˈʃɪt]. **No weak form** — like the rest of the minimizers these words take the nuclear stress of the clause and never reduce, while the frame around them does, so *he doesn't know jack about it* runs [i ˈdʌzn̩ ˈnoʊ ˈdʒæk əˈbaʊɾ ɪt] with *he* at /i/ and the /t/ of *about* tapped
**Русский:** разг. ни фига (jack); груб. ни хера (jack shit) — «совсем ничего»
**Type:** minimizer indefinite pronoun (negative) &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; no genitive &middot; **Register:** **informal, and vulgar in the full form.** *Jack shit* is bleeped on broadcast television and cut from any published prose that is not quoting speech. Bare *jack* is the clipped euphemism and passes in ordinary conversation, in sports talk, and in workplace complaint, though not in writing that has an editor. *Jack shit* is recorded in American English from the mid-twentieth century and *jack* is the clipping of it, which is why *jack* still carries a whiff of the longer phrase for many listeners
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** **nothing at all** — knowledge, effort, money, or help. Non-human referents only. It has nothing to do with the personal name *Jack*, and nothing to do with the *jack* of *every man jack of them*, which means the opposite.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** in the rare subject use; normally the object of a verb, where it triggers no agreement at all. As with the rest of the minimizers, **a negated verb and a positive verb give the same reading**: *he knows jack* and *he doesn't know jack* both say he knows nothing.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *jack / jack shit* (*You don't know jack*) &middot; [positive verb] *jack* (*He knows jack about it*) &middot; *not worth jack* &middot; *know jack about* [noun] &middot; *do jack shit* &middot; *jack squat* / *jack diddly* (the blended euphemisms) &middot; *jack* standing alone as an answer
**Contrast:** Grammatically identical to [diddly, diddly-squat, squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat) — same minimizer polarity, so the negated verb is optional and changes nothing — and one clear notch cruder in the full form. Against [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), which refuse the negated verb entirely. Against [nothing](#nothing), which is what all of them mean and what all of them are replaced by in edited prose. The homographs are the real hazard for a learner, because *jack* is one of the busiest words in American English: the tool (*a car jack*), the card (*the jack of spades*), the verb (*jack up the price*, and *jacked* for *stolen*), the socket (*a headphone jack*), the man's name, and the fixed *every man jack of them* meaning *every single one*. Only the pronoun means *nothing*, and it is identifiable by its position — object of a verb, usually with *know*, *do*, *get*, or *be worth*.
**Variants:** *jack squat*, *jack diddly*, *jack all* (chiefly British and Irish; American English uses bare *jack*), *bugger all* (British), *squat* and *diddly-squat* (the clean-mouthed equivalents)

Denies that any knowledge, effort, or benefit exists, in the bluntest terms the language keeps in ordinary circulation. Its normal subject is somebody else's competence, and its normal home is an argument.

1. You don't know *jack* about running a kitchen at capacity.
2. Two years of physical therapy and it did *jack shit* for the pain. — vulgar, and exactly the register the speaker intends
3. They promised us overtime and delivered *jack*.
4. — What did the inspector find? — *Jack*. — standing alone as an answer
5. That coupon is worth *jack* on a sale item.
6. He hasn't done *jack squat* since the transfer went through. — the blended euphemism, printable where the original is not
7. Formal rewrite: *The course of therapy produced no improvement.*

**See also:** [diddly, diddly-squat, squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat), [zilch, nada, bupkis, zip](#zilch-nada-bupkis-zip), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [nobody](#nobody), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord), [dick, shit, fuck all](#dick-shit-fuck-all)

### so-and-so

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ ən ˌsoʊ/ &middot; *SOH-un-soh* &middot; main stress on the first *so*, secondary on the second, and the middle word is *and* in its **weak form only** — /ən/, or a syllabic /n̩/ with no vowel of its own, never the full /ænd/. That is why the phrase is three words on the page and about two and a half beats in the mouth: [ˈsoʊ ən ˌsoʊ], often [ˈsoʊn̩ˌsoʊ]. The same obligatory weak *and* runs through *such-and-such*, *cat-and-mouse*, and *bread-and-butter*
**Русский:** такой-то, имярек — намеренно не названное лицо; бранное — «такой-сякой»
**Type:** placeholder indefinite pronoun (person); also a countable **noun** in the insult sense &middot; **Case:** none for the pronoun — one invariant form for subject and object; the genitive *so-and-so's* is formed freely &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal in the placeholder sense. An American meets it wherever somebody is describing a procedure rather than an event — a supervisor explaining a workflow, a lawyer reading a form aloud. The insult sense is a **minced oath**, a polite stand-in for a word the speaker declines to use, and it reads as mild and a little old-fashioned
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular. The pronoun is invariant; the insult **noun** takes an article and pluralizes (*a nosy so-and-so*, *a pair of lazy so-and-sos*), which the pronoun never does.
**Refers to:** **a person the speaker deliberately leaves unnamed** — either because the name is beside the point (*call so-and-so in payroll and get the code*) or because the speaker is reciting a form rather than a fact (*Mr. So-and-so hereby agrees to pay*). It never means a person the speaker is unable to identify: that is [somebody](#somebody). For a **thing**, a date, or an amount left blank in the same way, English uses *such-and-such*, which takes a noun after it and is therefore a determiner: see [such-and-such (a)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a).
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *So-and-so **signs** at the bottom and the clerk **files** it*, *So-and-so from the county **was** supposed to call*. Back-reference is singular *they* when no gender is in view (*so-and-so turns in **their** form*), and *he* or *she* when the speaker has a particular person in mind and is withholding only the name.
**Pattern:** *so-and-so* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *so-and-so* &middot; [preposition] *so-and-so* &middot; *Mr. / Mrs. / Ms. So-and-so* (capitalized, in mock-formal citation) &middot; *so-and-so in* [department] &middot; *so-and-so's* [noun] &middot; *a* [adjective] *so-and-so* (the insult noun) &middot; *such-and-such* (the thing-and-date equivalent, a determiner)
**Contrast:** Four words divide this territory and they are not interchangeable. *So-and-so* is a **blank nobody has filled in** — no particular person has been chosen, or the speaker is deliberately not naming one. [what's-his-name](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face) is a **name the speaker once had and has lost**, which is why it always points at a particular person both parties know. [you-know-who](#you-know-who-you-know-what) is a **name the speaker has and refuses to say out loud**. And [somebody](#somebody) is the genuinely indefinite one: it asserts that a person exists and claims no knowledge of who. Against *such-and-such*, the same construction in a different slot — *such-and-such* leans on a following noun through the article (*such-and-such a date*), so it is a determiner, while *so-and-so* stands alone and never modifies a noun: ✗ *so-and-so a clerk*. The insult use is a different word class entirely, with an article in front of it and a plural available.
**Variants:** *so and so* (open spelling, common and not an error), *Mr. So-and-so*, *thus and so* (dated), *John Doe* and *Jane Doe* (the legal and hospital placeholders), *such-and-such* (the determiner counterpart)

Names the slot where a person's name goes and then leaves the slot empty, on purpose. It is the spoken equivalent of a blank line on a form, and it belongs to explanations rather than to narratives: nothing actually happened to *so-and-so*, because *so-and-so* is not anybody yet.

1. Call *so-and-so* in payroll and ask for the reimbursement code.
2. The letter always reads the same: Mr. *So-and-so* agrees to pay by the first of the month.
3. He kept telling us *so-and-so* said this and *so-and-so* said that, and never once gave a name.
4. It is always *so-and-so's* turn to make the coffee, and it is never mine. — the genitive
5. You sign here, *so-and-so* countersigns there, and the clerk stamps it. — walking a listener through a procedure
6. That nosy old *so-and-so* has been reading our mail. — the insult noun, with an article in front of it
7. Compare *such-and-such a date*, where the blank is a date instead of a person. — a determiner, catalogued in the determiner dictionary

**See also:** [somebody](#somebody), [someone](#someone), [something or other](#something-or-other), [what's-his-name, what's-her-name, what's-his-face](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face), [you-know-who, you-know-what](#you-know-who-you-know-what), [whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), [such-and-such (a)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a)

### whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtʃəməˌkɔlɪt/, /ˈθɪŋəməˌdʒɪɡ/, /ˈwʌtsɪt/ &middot; *WUCH-uh-muh-kaw-lit, THING-uh-muh-jig, WUT-sit* &middot; stress on the first syllable of each, and every syllable in the middle is a schwa, which is the whole point of the words: **they are frozen weak forms.** *Whatchamacallit* is the sentence *what you may call it* with *you* at its weak /jə/ — that is the *-cha-* — and *may* at /mə/; *whatsit* is *what is it* given the same treatment. Because the reduction is already baked into the spelling there is no further weak form; the words sound the same fast or slow. Cot-caught merged Americans have [ˈwʌtʃəməˌkɑlɪt], and Southern and older speakers may keep an initial /hw/ on the *what-* forms
**Русский:** разг.: штуковина, штука, как её там — предмет, название которого забылось
**Type:** placeholder pro-forms for a thing — informal; grammatically they behave as **countable nouns** at least as much as pronouns &middot; **Case:** none in the pronoun use; as nouns they take the ordinary genitive (*the whatsit's cover*) &middot; **Register:** **informal.** Ordinary in speech and in relaxed writing everywhere in the country; an editor replaces them with the object's real name, or with *the fitting*, *the part*, *the device*
**Person / number:** third person. Unlike every true pronoun in this file, **they inflect for number**: *a whatchamacallit*, *two thingamajigs*, *those whatsits*.
**Refers to:** **a physical object whose name the speaker cannot retrieve at that moment** — non-human, and nearly always something concrete enough to point at. For a **person** whose name has gone the same way, use [what's-his-name](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face); for a thing the speaker never meant to name in the first place, [something or other](#something-or-other).
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with **the number of the word itself**, not with anything else: *The whatchamacallit **is** stripped*, *Both thingamajigs **are** missing*, *Those whatsits **were** in the drawer*. That is a real break from the twelve compounds, which are singular no matter what the meaning is.
**Pattern:** *a / the / that* + *whatchamacallit* (the article is normal, which no true pronoun allows) &middot; *whatchamacallit* [verb agreeing in number] &middot; [verb] *the whatsit* &middot; *the whatsit's* [noun] &middot; *that whatchamacallit on the* [noun] &middot; *thingamajigs* (plural)
**Contrast:** Against [something](#something), the true pronoun: *something* takes no article and no plural (✓ *something is stripped*, ✗ *a something is stripped*), while these take both, which is the clearest evidence that they are placeholder **nouns** rather than pronouns proper. Against [whatnot](#whatnot), which closes a list and can never be a subject or take an article. Against [what's-his-name](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face), which does this same job for people. The synonyms are worth sorting by flavor rather than by meaning, since the meaning is identical: *doohickey* and *doodad* are general American and read as folksy; *thingamabob* is jocular; *thingummy* and *whatsit* have a British ring, though *whatsit* is thoroughly at home in America; *dingus* is chiefly Upper Midwestern, from Dutch; *gizmo* began as mid-century military slang and has drifted toward meaning *gadget* rather than *forgotten name*; and *widget* has been captured by economics and software and no longer works as a placeholder in speech at all.
**Variants:** *whatchacallit*, *whatsis*, *whatsitsname*, *thingamabob*, *thingummy*, *thingy*, *doohickey*, *doodad*, *dingus*, *gizmo*

Stands in for the name of an object while the speaker's memory is looking for it. The three headwords are interchangeable, and the choice among them and among the long list of synonyms is regional and generational rather than semantic — the word means *that thing, you know the one*, and it depends on the listener being able to see or infer which thing.

1. Hand me the *whatchamacallit* — the little brass fitting on the end of the hose.
2. The *thingamajig* that holds the pump to the bracket has rusted through.
3. I've lost the *whatsit* that came with the blender and now it won't lock.
4. There are two *thingamajigs* in that drawer and I don't know what either one does. — the plural, which no true pronoun in this file can form
5. Somewhere in the toolbox there's a *doohickey* for exactly this. — the folksier synonym
6. She called it a *whatchamacallit* for a week before somebody told her it was a grommet.
7. Formal rewrite: *the coupling at the end of the hose*.

**See also:** [something](#something), [something or other](#something-or-other), [whatnot](#whatnot), [what's-his-name, what's-her-name, what's-his-face](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face), [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [what have you](06-interrogative.md#what-have-you), [God knows what, God knows who](#god-knows-what-god-knows-who)

### what's-his-name, what's-her-name, what's-his-face

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ɪz ˈneɪm/, /ˌwʌts ɚ ˈneɪm/, /ˌwʌts ɪz ˈfeɪs/ &middot; *wuts-iz-NAME, wuts-er-NAME, wuts-iz-FAYSS* &middot; main stress on the last element, and the whole phrase is spoken as a single word. **The weak forms are the entire story here.** *His* appears only as /ɪz/ with the /h/ dropped, and *her* only as /ɚ/, which is why the running-together spellings *whatsisname* and *whatsername* look nothing like the words they are made of. Both weak forms are compulsory: put a full /hɪz/ or /hɚ/ back in and the phrase turns into the ordinary question *What is his name?* Southern and older speakers may keep an initial /hw/ on *what's*
**Русский:** разг.: как его там, как её там — знакомый человек, чьё имя забыто; -face пренебрежительно
**Type:** placeholder pro-form for a person whose name the speaker has forgotten &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; the genitive *what's-his-name's* is clumsy on the page and perfectly ordinary in speech &middot; **Register:** **informal**, with a dismissive edge that grows across the set. *What's-his-name* is plain forgetfulness; *what's-his-face* is openly dismissive and mildly rude about the person it names. Both are cut from edited prose, which supplies the name or writes *a colleague*, *the contractor*, *the man from the county*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular. **This is the one corner of the indefinite system that marks gender**, and it marks it on the pronoun buried inside the phrase: *what's-his-name* for a man, *what's-her-name* for a woman. A gender-neutral *what's-their-name* is built on the same model as singular *they* and is used when the speaker does not know the person's gender or does not wish to specify it; it is considerably less common than the two gendered forms, is not yet recorded in most American dictionaries, and inflects exactly as they do (*what's-their-name's car*).
**Refers to:** **a particular person, known to both speaker and listener, whose name the speaker cannot retrieve.** That is what separates this phrase from everything else in the file: it is definite in meaning and indefinite only in form. It cannot introduce a person the listener has never met, and it cannot refer to a thing — for a thing use [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit).
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *What's-his-name **is** on the phone again*, *What's-her-name **has** already signed off on it*. Back-reference follows the gender the form encodes (*what's-her-name left **her** badge*); with *what's-their-name* it is *they*.
**Pattern:** *what's-his-name* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *what's-his-name* &middot; [preposition] *what's-his-name* &middot; *that guy — what's-his-name — from the county* (parenthetical, its commonest home) &middot; *what's-his-name's* [noun] &middot; *old what's-his-face*
**Contrast:** Against [so-and-so](#so-and-so): *so-and-so* is a blank nobody has filled in, this is a blank that was filled in and has gone missing. Against [you-know-who](#you-know-who-you-know-what): that is a name the speaker has and declines to say. Against [somebody](#somebody): genuinely indefinite, with no particular person in view — *somebody called* reports an unknown caller, *what's-his-name called* reports a known one whose name will not come. Against the plain question *What's his name?*, which is a different structure altogether: the question stresses *name* and keeps a full /h/, the placeholder is one flat unit with /ɪz/ and no question intonation. And *what's-his-face* is about a face only historically — it means the same thing with more attitude.
**Variants:** *whatshisname*, *whatsisname*, *whatsername* (the run-together spellings), *what's-her-face*, *what's-their-name* (gender-neutral, newer and less common), *whozit*, *whatshername*

Points at a specific person and admits that the name has gone. It presumes shared knowledge, which is why it is nearly always followed by a repair clause — *what's-his-name, the one who used to run the front desk* — supplying by description what the speaker cannot supply by name.

1. *What's-his-name* from the insurance office called again this morning.
2. I ran into *what's-her-name* at the hardware store — the one who used to run the front desk.
3. Ask *what's-his-name* in shipping; he'll know where the pallet went.
4. That's *what's-his-name's* truck blocking the loading ramp. — the genitive, ordinary in speech
5. Old *what's-his-face* still hasn't returned my extension ladder. — the dismissive member of the set
6. I introduced them to *what's-their-name* from the co-op, since I wasn't sure either way. — the gender-neutral form
7. Compare the ordinary question: *What's his name?* — full /h/, stress on *name*, and a genuine request for information

**See also:** [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [you-know-who, you-know-what](#you-know-who-you-know-what), [whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), [somebody](#somebody), [someone](#someone), [they (generic)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#they-generic)

### you-know-who, you-know-what

**Pronunciation:** /ˌju noʊ ˈhu/, /ˌju noʊ ˈwʌt/ &middot; *yoo-noh-HOO, yoo-noh-WUT* &middot; main stress on the last element, with the whole phrase spoken as one unit and no pause inside it. The **weak form** sits on *you*, which reduces to /jə/ exactly as it does everywhere else in the language, giving [jə noʊ ˈhu]; *know* keeps its full /oʊ/ because it is carrying the middle of the phrase. An evenly stressed *you know who* with a rising tone is not this pronoun at all — it is the question *You know who?*
**Русский:** сам знаешь кто, сам знаешь что — то, что оба знают, но говорящий не называет вслух
**Type:** placeholder pro-form for a referent the speaker declines to name &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; the genitive *you-know-who's* is ordinary in speech &middot; **Register:** **informal and conspiratorial.** It presumes shared knowledge and a reason for discretion — somebody in earshot, a child in the room, a subject the family does not discuss. Capitalized *You-Know-Who* is a proper-noun use, most famously the epithet for the villain of the Harry Potter books, which is where a good many young Americans meet the construction first
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural. *You-know-who* takes human referents; *you-know-what* takes non-human ones.
**Refers to:** **a person, or a thing, whose identity both speaker and listener already know and which the speaker is deliberately not saying out loud.** Like [what's-his-name](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face) it is definite in meaning; unlike it, the name has not been forgotten but withheld.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *You-know-who **is** asking about the schedule again*, *You-know-what **has** turned up in the garage*. Back-reference follows the real referent's gender when both parties know it (*you-know-who forgot **her** keys again*), and *it* for *you-know-what*.
**Pattern:** *you-know-who* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *you-know-who* &middot; [preposition] *you-know-who* &middot; *you-know-who's* [noun] &middot; *a little you-know-what* (the euphemism, standing in for a noun the speaker will not use) &middot; *You-Know-Who* (capitalized, as a name)
**Contrast:** The trap is the plain clause *you know what* / *you know who*, which is a completely different structure and far commoner: *You know what? I'm leaving* is a discourse opener and *You know who I saw?* is a question, and neither is a pronoun. Intonation separates them in speech and the hyphens separate them in writing. Against [what's-his-name](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face), the difference is forgotten against withheld; against [so-and-so](#so-and-so), never-named against not-said-aloud; against [somebody](#somebody), genuinely unidentified against perfectly well identified. The euphemistic *you-know-what* also stands in for a word the speaker will not utter — *he can take that memo and file it you-know-where* — which is the same minced-oath move that gives [so-and-so](#so-and-so) its insult sense, and *you-know-where* exists in that use only.
**Variants:** *you-know-where* (euphemistic only), *you-know-when* (rare), *You-Know-Who* (capitalized as a name), *he-who-shall-not-be-named* (jocular), *a certain someone*

Names a referent by pointing at the listener's knowledge of it instead of at the referent itself. The whole construction is a piece of social machinery: it says *we both know who I mean, and I am not going to say it here*, and the reason for the discretion is left to the listener.

1. *You-know-who* has been asking about the schedule again. — said in front of the person's children
2. Don't leave *you-know-what* on the kitchen table where she'll see it.
3. I ran it past *you-know-who* before I sent it out. — discretion, not forgetfulness
4. That's *you-know-who's* car in the driveway, so keep your voice down. — the genitive
5. In the books he is never named; he is *You-Know-Who*. — the capitalized proper-noun use
6. He can take that memo and file it *you-know-where*. — the euphemism, standing in for a word the speaker will not say
7. Compare the ordinary clause: *You know what? Let's just go.* — a discourse opener, not a pronoun

**See also:** [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [what's-his-name, what's-her-name, what's-his-face](#whats-his-name-whats-her-name-whats-his-face), [whatchamacallit, thingamajig, whatsit](#whatchamacallit-thingamajig-whatsit), [somebody](#somebody), [something](#something), [what's what](06-interrogative.md#whats-what)

### you name it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌju ˈneɪm ɪt/ &middot; *yoo-NAYM-it* &middot; main stress on *name*, with the three words run together as a single unit and no pause anywhere inside it. **The weak forms carry it**: *you* reduces to /jə/ exactly as it does everywhere else in the language, and *it* to /ɪt/ with its /t/ tapped or glottalized into whatever follows, giving the everyday [jə ˈneɪm ɪʔ]. A full /ju/ with its own stress is not this pronoun at all — it is the live imperative *You name it*, meaning *you choose*
**Русский:** и что угодно, и всё такое прочее — закрывает открытый список: «чего там только нет»
**Type:** frozen clause used as an indefinite pro-form; a list-closer &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **informal.** Ordinary in speech, in sales talk, and in advertising copy of every kind; edited prose replaces it with *and so on* or with a finished list, and technical, legal, and contractual writing needs *including but not limited to* instead
**Person / number:** second person in form and third person in reference; it has no number of its own, because it never governs a verb. Nothing inside it can move: ✗ *you named it*, ✗ *he names it*, ✗ *you name them*.
**Refers to:** **any further item of the same kind, without limit** — the open remainder of a list, things far more often than people. Its distinctive move is to hand the extending over to the listener: whatever you can think of is in the set.
**Agreement:** it takes no agreement of its own. When it closes a list that is the subject, the verb agrees with **the list**, not with the phrase: *Hammers, saws, drills — you name it — **are** all on that wall*. It is essentially never a subject by itself: ✗ *You name it is in the drawer*.
**Pattern:** [list] — *you name it* &middot; [list], *you name it* &middot; *you name it,* [clause] (*You name it, we carry it*) &middot; it **closes** a list and never opens one, and it takes no question mark
**Contrast:** It belongs with [whatnot](#whatnot) and [what have you](06-interrogative.md#what-have-you), and it does one thing neither of them does: it **hands the job to the listener**. *Whatnot* says *and more of the same*; *you name it* says *and anything else you care to think of*, which is why it is the list-closer of the hardware store and the auto shop. It is also the only one of the three that can be **fronted**, in the fixed frame *you name it, we've got it*. Grammatically it is a fossil — an imperative with no imperative left in it — so it is invariant in every part. Against the live imperative it came from, which is a different sentence with a full /ju/, its own contour, and a real invitation: *We can eat anywhere. You name it.* And against the formal general extenders *and so forth*, *and so on*, and *et cetera*, which close a list without inviting anybody to extend it; those are **quasi-coordinators** rather than pronouns and are catalogued in the conjunction dictionary at [and so forth](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#and-so-forth) and [et cetera / etc.](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#et-cetera--etc).
**Variants:** *you name it, we've got it* (the fixed frame), *whatever you can think of*, *and what have you*, *and whatnot*, *and everything*

Closes an open-ended list by inviting the listener to extend it: whatever you can name belongs in the set. It is the most generous of the American list-closers and the only one that comes with a challenge attached, which is why it has been living on shop signs for a century.

1. Sockets, wrenches, torque bars — *you name it*, it's in that chest.
2. She's done every job in the building: front desk, billing, scheduling, *you name it*.
3. They stock mufflers, brakes, alignment kits, *you name it*.
4. Rain, hail, a downed line — *you name it* — that trip went wrong every way a trip can.
5. *You name it*, and somebody in that family has broken it. — the fronted frame
6. Compare the live imperative: — Where do you want to eat? — *You name it.* — a full /ju/, its own contour, and a real invitation
7. Formal rewrite: *tools of every description*, or, in a contract, *including but not limited to* the items listed.

**See also:** [whatnot](#whatnot), [what have you](06-interrogative.md#what-have-you), [anything](#anything), [everything](#everything), [something or other](#something-or-other), [and so forth](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/01-coordinating.md#and-so-forth)

### zilch, nada, bupkis, zip

**Pronunciation:** /zɪltʃ/, /ˈnɑdə/, /ˈbʌpkəs/, /zɪp/ &middot; *ZILCH, NAH-duh, BUP-kiss, ZIP* &middot; *nada* keeps the Spanish stress on the first syllable and the /ɑ/ of *father*, and its /d/ is a tap for most Americans, [ˈnɑɾə]; *bupkis* has first-syllable stress and a second vowel that wavers between /ə/ and /ɪ/. **None of the four has a weak form** — each is the word its sentence is built to land on, so it takes the nuclear stress and keeps a full vowel however fast the rest of the clause runs
**Русский:** разг., но не грубо: ноль, ничего, шиш, кот наплакал — «ничего совсем»
**Type:** informal indefinite pronouns (negative), meaning *nothing* or *zero* &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object; no genitive &middot; **Register:** **informal but not coarse**, which is what separates them from [jack](#jack-jack-shit) and [squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat): all four are printable, and *zilch* and *zip* turn up in American sports writing and in headlines. *Zilch* is generally traced to a character named Mr. Zilch in a humor magazine of the 1930s; *zip* for a score of zero is American and about a century old; *nada* is Spanish for *nothing* and has been in general American use since the middle of the twentieth century; *bupkis* is from Yiddish, literally *beans* by way of a Slavic root and often glossed as *goat droppings*, and it reached general American English through New York and through comedy writing, keeping a wry flavor the others do not have
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant — no plural and no gender.
**Refers to:** **nothing at all, zero** — an amount, a result, a response, or a score. Non-human referents only. *Zip* and *zilch* are the two that serve as **scores** (*we lost four to zip*); *nada* and *bupkis* do not (✗ *four to bupkis*).
**Agreement:** **singular verb** when one of them is the subject, which is uncommon but perfectly grammatical — *Zilch **has** come back from the lab*, *Nada **is** what we got for our trouble*. Their usual place is the object or the complement, where they trigger no agreement at all.
**Pattern:** [positive verb] *zilch / nada / bupkis / zip* (*We got zilch*) &middot; *worth zilch* &middot; [number] *to zip* (a score) &middot; *zilch / zip* standing alone as an answer &middot; *and I got bupkis for it* &middot; *nada* as a flat one-word reply
**Contrast:** All four mean what [nothing](#nothing) means, with the register lowered and the volume raised, and the one grammatical fact to learn is that **they carry the negation themselves and refuse a second one**: ✓ *I got zilch*, while ✗ *I didn't get zilch* is a double negative that either reverses the sense or collapses into nonsense. That is exactly the opposite of [squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat) and [jack](#jack-jack-shit), which take a negated verb happily and mean the same with it or without it. Against [nothing](#nothing) itself, interchangeable in meaning and never in register: a condolence note says *nothing*, a group chat says *zilch*. Against [none](#none), which needs a set already in view where these need none. And watch the homographs — *zip* is also the fastener, the verb (*zip through*), and the *zip code*; *nada* is a live Spanish word and stays Spanish inside a Spanish sentence.
**Variants:** *bupkes*, *bubkes*, *bopkes* (competing spellings of the Yiddish word), *zippo*, *nix*, *goose egg* (a zero, especially a score), *nil* (chiefly British, and in soccer scores), *nada* and *zilch* used adverbially in casual speech (*it helped zilch*)

Say *nothing* with emphasis, in the register of complaint, sports, and shop talk. All four are single-word negatives that need no negative verb to lean on, and their differences are entirely about flavor: *zilch* is neutral American slang, *zip* is the sportscaster's word, *nada* carries a shrug, and *bupkis* carries a punchline.

1. We searched the whole attic and turned up *zilch*.
2. Three interviews and *nada* — not even a rejection email.
3. Twenty years at that company and he got *bupkis* when it folded.
4. They were up four to *zip* by the third inning. — the score use
5. — Any word back from the lab? — *Zip*. — standing alone as an answer
6. Her share of the settlement came to *nada* once the fees were taken out.
7. Formal rewrite: *The search produced no results.*

**See also:** [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none), [diddly, diddly-squat, squat](#diddly-diddly-squat-squat), [jack, jack shit](#jack-jack-shit), [naught](#naught), [zero](../../09-determiners/catalog/07-numerals-multipliers.md#zero), [dick, shit, fuck all](#dick-shit-fuck-all)

## Nonstandard, dialectal, and archaic

Forms a learner will meet in dialogue, in song lyrics, in Twain, and in older legal prose, and should be able to recognize without imitating. Everything here is labeled: **negative concord** (*I didn't see nothing*) is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular and stigmatized in edited writing; the adverbial *-s* of *nowheres* is regional; *naught* and *aught* are archaic, opposite in meaning, and one letter apart. None of these are errors in the varieties that use them — they are simply not the variety an American cover letter is written in.

The nonstandard forms that belong to the **other** pronoun categories are labeled in the same way and catalogued in their own files; a learner meeting them alongside the forms below should know what they are and where they live. **Double negatives** — *I didn't see nothing*, *we didn't get nowhere*, *ain't nobody home* — are the in-family case and are treated below at [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord) and [ain't nobody](#aint-nobody); they are systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular English, and nonstandard in edited American prose. Outside this family: the reflexives ***hisself*** and ***theirselves*** are regular formations on the possessive stem (*his* + *self*, on the model of *myself*, *yourself*), heard in Southern, Appalachian, and African American vernacular English and nonstandard in edited prose — see [hisself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself) and [theirselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#theirselves). The demonstrative ***them*** before a noun — ***them** apples*, ***them** boots* — is general vernacular American, strongest in Southern, Appalachian, and Midland speech, and standard English uses *those*; see [them (dialectal demonstrative)](04-demonstrative.md#them). The plural *you* forms — ***youse*** in New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, beside *y'all* in the South, *yinz* in Pittsburgh, and *you'uns* in Appalachia — fill a real gap in standard English, which has only one *you*; see [youse](10-archaic-dialectal.md#youse). And the two case errors are at [personal pronouns](01-personal.md#nonstandard-pronoun-forms-recognize-them-label-them-dont-write-them): ***Me and him went***, general vernacular American and the most-corrected error in American classrooms, and ***between you and I*** / ***They invited my wife and I***, which are not vernacular at all but **hypercorrections** running upward through the social scale.

### ain't nobody

**Pronunciation:** /ˌeɪnt ˈnoʊbədi/ &middot; *aynt-NOH-buh-dee* &middot; *ain't* is /eɪnt/; the /d/ of *nobody* taps, giving [ˈnoʊbəɾi]. In the fronted construction the whole phrase carries a single sweeping contour with the peak on *no-*
**Русский:** прост.: «никого нет», «никто и не...» — двойное отрицание с инверсией, вне нормы
**Type:** negative-concord idiom with negative inversion — **nonstandard in Standard American English** &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **vernacular.** Rule-governed and completely systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular English; stigmatized in edited American writing. It is also quoted constantly by speakers who do not otherwise use it, which is a separate fact about it
**Person / number:** third person, singular in form; the following verb takes the bare or *-ing* form.
**Refers to:** **no person at all** — human referents only, exactly as [nobody](#nobody) does.
**Agreement:** the negated auxiliary *ain't* is invariant for person and number, and it precedes the subject. *Ain't nobody home*, *Ain't nobody got time for that*, *Ain't nobody gonna tell me different.*
**Pattern:** *ain't nobody* [adjective/locative] (*Ain't nobody home*) &middot; *ain't nobody* [past participle / bare verb] (*Ain't nobody got time*) &middot; *ain't nobody gonna* [verb] &middot; *ain't nothing* / *ain't no* [noun] (the parallel forms)
**Contrast:** Two things happen at once here, and they are worth separating. **Negative concord** marks the negative twice, on the auxiliary and on the pronoun. **Negative inversion** moves the negated auxiliary in front of the subject. Crucially, the meaning is **emphatic single negation** — *Ain't nobody home* means *nobody is home*, not *somebody is home*. The old schoolroom claim that two negatives make a positive is a borrowing from logic and has never described how English actually works; what the doubled negative signals is emphasis, and what it signals socially is the speaker's variety. Standard equivalents: *There isn't anybody home*, *Nobody is home*, *Nobody has time for that*. Recognize it, read it, quote it accurately — and keep it out of a cover letter.

A vernacular negative construction denying that any person satisfies the description, with the negation marked twice and the auxiliary fronted. It is the best-known example of English negative concord, and its wide circulation as a catchphrase has made it familiar to Americans who have never used it in their own speech.

1. *Ain't nobody* home — nonstandard for *Nobody is home*.
2. *Ain't nobody* got time for that. — the catchphrase, from a 2012 local news interview
3. *Ain't nobody* gonna tell me how to run my own shop. — vernacular
4. *Ain't nothing* wrong with that truck. — the parallel *-thing* form
5. Standard rewrite: *There isn't anybody home.*
6. Standard rewrite: *Nobody has time for that.*

**See also:** [nobody](#nobody), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord), [nothing](#nothing), [ain't no](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#aint-no), [no (negative concord)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no-negative-concord)

### aught

**Pronunciation:** /ɔt/ &middot; *AWT* &middot; one syllable, /ɔt/, a homophone of *ought* and — for the many Americans who merge *cot* and *caught* — of *ot* in *otter* without the *-er*. Merged speakers make *aught* and *not* rhyme exactly
**Русский:** устар., книжн.: что-либо, что-нибудь — живо лишь в обороте for aught I know «почём я знаю»
**Type:** archaic indefinite pronoun meaning *anything* &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** In living American English it survives in one fixed phrase, *for aught I know*, and in the decade name *the aughts*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **anything at all** — non-human referents. It is the historical ancestor of the nonassertive [anything](#anything) and appears in the same environments: negatives, questions, and conditionals.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *If aught **remains** of the estate, it goes to the college.*
**Pattern:** *for aught I know* (the surviving idiom) &middot; *if aught* [singular verb] &middot; [negated verb] *aught* &middot; *aught but* [noun] (= anything except)
**Contrast:** ***Aught* means *anything*; [naught](#naught) means *nothing*.** They differ by one letter, they are opposites, and confusing them reverses a sentence, which is the main reason both are now avoided. The zero sense muddies matters further: *the aughts* for the decade beginning in 2000, and *a nineteen-aught-six model*, come from misdividing *a naught* as *an aught*, so historically the zero belongs to *naught* and *aught* acquired it by accident. The spelling *ought* for this word exists in older texts and is now avoided because of the modal verb.

An archaic pronoun meaning *anything whatever*. A modern American meets it in older literature, in legal boilerplate, and in the frozen hedge *for aught I know*, which means *so far as I know* with an added shrug.

1. For *aught* I know, she never left the building that night. — the surviving idiom
2. If *aught* remains of the estate, it passes to the college. — archaic legal register
3. He would not take *aught* but what he had earned. — *aught but* = anything except
4. She was born in nineteen *aught* six. — the zero sense, from a misdivision of *a naught*
5. The album came out sometime in the *aughts*. — the decade, 2000 to 2009
6. Modern rewrite: *For all I know, she never left the building.*

**See also:** [naught](#naught), [anything](#anything), [nothing](#nothing)

### naught

**Pronunciation:** /nɔt/ &middot; *NAWT* &middot; one syllable; for the many Americans who merge *cot* and *caught* it is a perfect homophone of *not*, which is exactly what it descends from. Unmerged speakers keep /ɔ/ in *naught* against /ɑ/ in *not*
**Русский:** устар., книжн.: ничто, ничего; как существительное — ноль (цифра)
**Type:** archaic indefinite pronoun meaning *nothing*; also a noun meaning *zero* &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary**, surviving in a handful of set phrases that are still in ordinary use: *come to naught*, *all for naught*, *set at naught*
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **nothing at all** — non-human referents; and, as a noun, the digit **zero**.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *All his planning **came** to naught.*
**Pattern:** *come to naught* &middot; *all for naught* &middot; *for naught* &middot; *set at naught* [noun] (= treat as worthless) &middot; *naught but* [noun] (= nothing but) &middot; *avail naught* / *profit naught* (archaic, verb + object)
**Contrast:** *Naught* is *nothing*; [aught](#aught) is *anything*. **Spelling:** *naught* is the American form and *nought* the British, and British English additionally uses *nought* for the digit zero (*nought point five*), where Americans say *zero* or *oh*. Do not write ✗ *all for not* — the homophony with *not* makes this a common error, and it destroys the phrase. The living American descendant of the word is simply [nothing](#nothing), which is what a modern rewrite uses.

An archaic pronoun meaning *nothing*, worn down from Old English *nā wiht*, *no thing*. It survives chiefly in a few fixed phrases about wasted effort, and secondarily as a name for the digit zero — the *noughts and crosses* of the British game Americans call tic-tac-toe, spelled with the British *nought*.

1. Two years of negotiation came to *naught*. — the living idiom
2. All that overtime was for *naught* once the contract fell through.
3. He set at *naught* every warning the inspector gave him. — formal and archaic
4. They had *naught* but a tent and a week of rations. — *naught but* = nothing but
5. The British write *nought* and say it for zero; Americans write *naught* and say *zero*.
6. Common error: ✗ *all for not*. The word is *naught*.

**See also:** [aught](#aught), [nothing](#nothing), [none](#none)

### nothing (negative concord)

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnʌθɪŋ/ &middot; *NUTH-ing* &middot; identical to standard [nothing](#nothing) — /ˈnʌθɪŋ/, casually [ˈnʌθɪn] or [ˈnʌʔn̩]. Nothing about the pronunciation marks the construction; the syntax does
**Русский:** ничего при отрицательном глаголе — двойное отрицание: в русском норма, в англ. нет
**Type:** compound indefinite pronoun in a clause that is already negated — **nonstandard in Standard American English** &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard in edited American English; systematic and rule-governed in African American English, in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, and in many other varieties worldwide.** It is a reliable social marker, which is the whole reason to be able to recognize it
**Person / number:** third person, grammatically singular — the agreement is entirely regular; what is nonstandard is the second negative, not the number.
**Refers to:** **no thing at all** — the meaning is a single emphatic negative, not a positive. *I didn't see nothing* means *I saw nothing*, never *I saw something*.
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, as always. The construction changes nothing about agreement: *He don't know nothing* is nonstandard twice over — once for the concord and once for the third-person *-s* — but *He doesn't know nothing* is nonstandard only for the concord.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *nothing* (*I didn't see nothing*) &middot; [negated verb] *nobody / nowhere / no one* (the parallel forms) &middot; *can't* … *no* [noun] (*can't get no satisfaction*) &middot; *ain't nobody* / *ain't nothing* (with negative inversion)
**Contrast:** **Standard American English allows one negative marker per clause.** There are therefore exactly two repairs, and they are not stylistic variants of each other: move the negative onto the verb and switch the pronoun to *any-* — *I didn't see **anything*** — or leave the negative on the pronoun and un-negate the verb — *I saw **nothing***, which is heavier. Two superficially similar patterns are entirely standard and should not be confused with this one. **Litotes** doubles the negative on purpose to produce a positive: *That's not nothing*, *the reaction was not uncommon*. And **negation across a clause boundary** puts the two negatives in different clauses, where they never interact: *I didn't say nothing was wrong* is perfectly grammatical and means *I did not say that nothing was wrong*. The claim that two negatives make a positive is imported from logic; in English the second negative is agreement, not arithmetic, which is why *I can't get no satisfaction* has never once been misread.

A single emphatic negation marked twice — on the auxiliary and again on the pronoun. Chaucer used it, Shakespeare used it, and it remains standard in French and Spanish; English lost it from the prestige dialect during the eighteenth century and it survives vigorously below that line. Learn to read it and never to write it in edited prose.

1. *I didn't see nothing.* — nonstandard; standard: *I didn't see anything* or *I saw nothing*.
2. *He don't know nothing about engines.* — nonstandard twice: concord plus the verb form.
3. *We couldn't find nobody who'd take the job.* — nonstandard; standard: *couldn't find anybody*.
4. *I can't get no satisfaction.* — the best-known line in the construction, and unambiguous.
5. *That's not nothing.* — **standard** litotes: two negatives, deliberate, meaning it is significant.
6. *I didn't say nothing was wrong.* — **standard**: the negatives are in separate clauses.

**See also:** [nothing](#nothing), [nobody](#nobody), [ain't nobody](#aint-nobody), [anything](#anything), [no (negative concord)](../../09-determiners/catalog/06-distributives-negatives.md#no-negative-concord)

### nowheres, anywheres, somewheres

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊˌwɛrz/, /ˈɛniˌwɛrz/, /ˈsʌmˌwɛrz/ &middot; *NOH-wairz, EN-ee-wairz, SUM-wairz* &middot; identical to the standard forms with a final /z/ added; the /z/ is voiced because it follows a vowel-like /r/. Stress is unchanged
**Русский:** диал., прост.: нигде, где-нибудь, где-то — южные и сельские формы с лишним -s
**Type:** compound indefinite **adverbs** with an adverbial genitive *-s* — **nonstandard and regional** &middot; **Case:** none in the modern sense; the *-s* is a fossilized genitive ending, not a plural &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard and regional.** Heard in Southern and Midland American speech, in older rural New England, and in nineteenth-century literary dialect — Twain writes it constantly. Marked as an error by every American style guide
**Person / number:** invariant.
**Refers to:** **a place**, with exactly the meanings of the standard forms; the *-s* adds nothing but a regional flavor.
**Agreement:** no agreement of their own, as adverbs.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] *nowheres / anywheres* &middot; [verb] *somewheres* &middot; *nowheres near* [noun] &middot; standard rewrite: drop the *-s*
**Contrast:** The *-s* is not random. It is the old **adverbial genitive**, the same ending that survives perfectly standardly in *always*, *besides*, *nowadays*, and *unawares* — and, as an optional extra, in *afterwards* and *towards*, where American English generally prefers the *-s*-less *afterward* and *toward* and both forms are standard. English simply stopped extending it, and the varieties that kept extending it to *nowheres*, *anywheres*, and *somewheres* are now marked. The related *anyways* sits one notch higher — still informal and still cut by editors, but far more widely used than the *-where* forms, and *anyway* is the form to write. Recognize these in dialogue and in Twain; drop the *-s* everywhere else.

Regional and nonstandard variants of *nowhere*, *anywhere*, and *somewhere*, formed with a surviving adverbial *-s*. They are worth knowing because they are common in reported speech and in American literary dialect, and because a learner who meets them in Huckleberry Finn should not conclude that the standard forms take an *-s*.

1. *He wasn't nowheres to be found.* — nonstandard; standard: *nowhere to be found*.
2. *I ain't going nowheres tonight.* — nonstandard, with negative concord on top.
3. *She left it somewheres in the barn.* — nonstandard; standard: *somewhere*.
4. *It wasn't nowheres near enough.* — nonstandard; standard: *nowhere near*.
5. *Did you look anywheres besides the garage?* — nonstandard; standard: *anywhere*.
6. Compare the standard survivals of the same ending: *always*, *besides*, *nowadays*, *unawares*.

**See also:** [nowhere](#nowhere), [anywhere](#anywhere), [somewhere](#somewhere), [nothing (negative concord)](#nothing-negative-concord)

### whatnot

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtˌnɑt/ &middot; *WUT-not* &middot; stress on *what*; the /t/ of *what* is a tap or a glottal stop before the second element, giving [ˈwʌɾˌnɑt] or [ˈwʌʔˌnɑt]. Some Americans, chiefly Southern and older, keep an initial /hw/
**Русский:** разг.: и всякое такое, и тому подобное — только в конце перечисления
**Type:** indefinite pro-form used only at the end of a list; informal &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** **informal.** Fine in speech and in relaxed writing, cut by editors in anything formal, where *and so on* or a complete list replaces it
**Person / number:** third person; invariant. It has no number of its own because it never governs a verb.
**Refers to:** **further unspecified items of the same kind as those already listed** — usually things, occasionally activities. It cannot refer to a specific person.
**Agreement:** it takes no verb agreement, because it lives at the tail of a coordination and is essentially never a subject. ✗ *Whatnot is in the drawer* is not English; *the tape and the string and whatnot are in the drawer* takes its plural from the whole list.
**Pattern:** [item], [item], *and whatnot* &middot; [item] *and whatnot* &middot; *and what have you* (the near-synonym) &middot; *a whatnot* (the unrelated noun: a small display shelf)
**Contrast:** *And whatnot* closes a list; it cannot open one, stand alone as a subject, or replace a single named item — ✗ *Whatnot happened at the meeting* is impossible, where *something happened* is fine. Its close relatives are *and what have you*, *and all that*, *and stuff*, *and things like that*, and the formal *and so forth*, *et cetera*. Do not confuse it with the homograph *a whatnot*, a countable **noun** naming a small many-shelved stand for ornaments, which pluralizes (*two whatnots in the parlor*) — the pro-form never takes an article and never pluralizes. Compare the determiner [suchlike](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#suchlike).

Stands in for the unnamed remainder of a list, signaling that the speaker could go on and does not intend to. It is a coordination-closer rather than a true pronoun, which is why it is catalogued at the edge of this family rather than in its center.

1. The drawer's full of tape, twine, batteries, *and whatnot*.
2. She handles scheduling, invoicing, *and whatnot* for the whole practice.
3. We spent the weekend raking, hauling brush, *and whatnot*.
4. Bring a jacket, boots, *and whatnot* — it gets cold on the water.
5. He does contracts and licensing *and what have you*. — the near-synonym
6. There's an old *whatnot* in the front room with her grandmother's figurines. — the unrelated noun
