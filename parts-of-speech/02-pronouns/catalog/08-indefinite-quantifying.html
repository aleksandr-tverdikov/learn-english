Quantifying and distributive pronouns#

The pronouns of amount — the words that answer how much? and how many? without naming what they count, and the distributives that take a set apart one member at a time.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Almost every word in this group leads a double life: a determiner in front of a noun (each student, most people, another chance) and a pronoun standing alone or with of (each was checked, most of them left, I'll have another). Only the pronoun use belongs here — the determiner counterparts live in the determiner dictionary, and each entry links across. Two rules run through the whole group. First, the of is obligatory in front of a personal pronoun — all of them, most of them, neither of us, never ✗all them, ✗most them — and for most of the group it is obligatory in front of a determiner as well: most of the students, each of the rooms, ✗most the students. The exceptions are the three predeterminers all, both, and half, which may drop it before a determiner, so that all the seats, both the books, and half the time are as standard as all of the seats, both of the books, and half of the time. Whatever follows of stands in the object case: all of us, both of them, neither of us, never ✗all of we.

Second, and this is where the errors actually live, verb agreement is not uniform across the group and cannot be guessed from the meaning. Each, either, neither, every one, any one, one or the other, another, the other, much, a great deal, little, a little, a bit, least, less, one, the one, the whole, the usual, the residue, every last one, everyone and their mother, not one, not a one, and the regional nary a one take a singular verb no matter how plural the of-phrase behind them looks — Each of the rooms has its own thermostat. Both, a couple, many, few, a few, a handful, fewer, fewest, several, certain, others, ones, a good many, quite a few, a slew, hundreds, thousands, dozens, the bare cardinals from two up, et al., one and all, and all and sundry take a plural verb, always. And all, half, any, some, most, more, none, enough, plenty, a lot, lots, a bunch, a ton, tons, loads, heaps, oodles, scads, gobs, a boatload, a shitload, a third, the rest, the remainder, the balance, the majority, the minority, all of the above, none of the above, any of the above, and such agree with whatever they refer to — plural for countables (Some of the chairs are broken), singular for a mass (Some of the paint is dry). One more point of current usage: when a singular pronoun in this group needs to be referred back to, singular they is standard American English, including in edited prose — Each of them signed their own copy. Chicago, the MLA Handbook, the APA Publication Manual, and the AP Stylebook all accept it; his or her is the conservative alternative, and generic he now reads as an error.

Case. Not one word in this group inflects for it. The Case: line on every entry below reads none, with one partial exception — the generic one, which has a possessive one's and a reflexive oneself but still uses a single form as subject and as object (One never knows / It gives one pause). Otherwise the label means exactly what it says: all is all in a subject slot and in an object slot alike (All were counted / We counted all of them), and there is no whom-style object form anywhere in the family. Case surfaces in two places around these words. The first is right after of, where the object form is required: all of us, both of them, each of us, half of them, never ✗all of we. The second is when one of them floats onto a coordinate subject, or sits inside a coordinate object — and there the ordinary personal-pronoun rules apply, with the ordinary errors in both directions. She and I both went is correct and ✗Me and him both went is nonstandard, an object form doing a subject's job; He gave a copy to Bob and me, between you and me, and All of it went to my wife and me are correct, while ✗between you and I and ✗They invited my wife and I are hypercorrections — object slots filled with a subject form by speakers reaching for something that sounds careful. One test settles every one of them: delete the other conjunct and listen. He gave a copy to me (so to Bob and me), They invited me (so my wife and me), I went (so She and I went). The full paradigm is in personal pronouns, and two facts there are worth carrying over: It's me is standard American English and It is I is formal to the point of stiffness, and whom now survives mainly in the one position where it is still the default for most writers, immediately after a preposition — to whom, for whom, neither of whom, both of whom — while everywhere else who is the ordinary modern choice.

What follows them. These pronouns take relative clauses constantly — all that remains, the few who stayed, the ones we saw — and the relative is chosen by the antecedent, not by the quantifier: who for people (the few who stayed), which for things (the crates, which arrived intact), that for both (all that remains, the ones that sold, the several that were damaged), and whose for both as well (the one whose name was missing, the crate whose lid was cracked). Two honest notes. After all, much, little, the only, and a superlative, that is the idiomatic relative and which sounds wrong: all that remains, not ✗all which remains. And the familiar restrictive-that / nonrestrictive-which split is an American editing convention, enforced by Chicago and the AP, not a law of the language — restrictive which has been written by good writers for six hundred years and is unremarkable in British English. What is not optional is the punctuation: a nonrestrictive clause takes commas, a restrictive one does not. See relative pronouns for the full account.

Pronunciation. The of in these partitives is almost never said in full. It reduces to /əv/ and then to /ə/, and the pronoun after it reduces too, so the whole phrase comes out as a single word: all of them /ˈɔl əvəm/ · AW-luh-vum, some of them /ˈsʌməvəm/, most of us /ˈmoʊstəvəs/, each of you /ˈitʃ əvjə/. The weak forms doing that work belong to the personal pronouns — them /əm/ (spelled 'em), him /ɪm/, her /ɚ/, you /jə/, he /i/ — and they are tabled in full at personal pronouns. The quantifiers themselves keep their own vowels and have no weak forms; only the articles inside a lot, a couple, a few, and a little reduce, always to /ə/ and never stressed.

Nonstandard forms are labeled here, not hidden. Three of them touch this group. Them used as a determiner — them apples, all them boxes — is the object pronoun doing a determiner's job; it is regular and systematic in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, in African American English, and in working-class speech generally, and it is nonstandard in every register of writing (dictionaried at them (dialectal demonstrative)). Negative concord — I don't want neither, We didn't get none — is likewise rule-governed in those same varieties rather than a failure of logic, and likewise stigmatized in edited prose; standard American English allows one negative per clause, so the repairs are I don't want either / I want neither. And a couple things without of is widespread informal American speech that editors still change to a couple of things. Three further nonstandard forms that readers look for here belong to neighboring families and are dictionaried there, and all three are nonstandard in every register of writing however systematic they are in speech. Hisself and theirselves are reflexives built regularly on the possessive — exactly the way standard myself, yourself, and ourselves are built — and they are current in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, in African American English, and in working-class speech nationally; the standard forms are himself and themselves. Youse is a second-person plural of the Northeastern cities — New York, Philadelphia, and northern New Jersey above all, with Irish-immigrant roots — filling the same gap that y'all fills in the South, yinz in Pittsburgh, and informal you guys nearly everywhere; standard written English has only you for both numbers.

Totals and fractions: the whole set, half of it, and the empty set#

All, both, and none take a set in one piece, half cuts it in two, and a third stands in for every other fraction that cuts it finer. All, half, a third, and none bend their number to what they point at; both is fixed at two and always plural, and the whole, which names a thing entire rather than the members of a set, is fixed at one and always singular. Around them sit the fixed phrases that do the same work with more feeling — one and all, all and sundry, and the exasperated everyone and their mother for the whole crowd, not one, not a one, and the Southern and Appalachian nary a one for the empty set — and the two list-pointing phrases all of the above and none of the above, whose antecedent is a set of lines on a page rather than a set of things in the world.

a third#

Pronunciation
/ə ˈθɚd/ · uh-THURD · the article is always reduced to /ə/ and never stressed, and before of the phrase runs together — a third of it /əˈθɚdəvɪt/, uh-THUR-duh-vit. The compound fractions keep two beats and a hyphen in print: two-thirds /ˌtu ˈθɚdz/, too-THURDZ; three-quarters /ˌθri ˈkwɔrtɚz/, three-KWOR-turz
Русский
треть — одна часть из трёх; так же устроены все дроби: a quarter «четверть»
Type
fractional quantifying pronoun phrase (also a quantifier before of + noun, a third of the budget, an ordinary count noun, cut it into thirds, and, in the third to arrive, the ordinal)
Case
none
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person; invariant; construes plural with countables and singular with a mass or a sum
Refers to
one part in three of a set or a quantity — the members of a set of countables (plural) or an amount of a mass (singular). Every other fraction works the same way: a quarter, two-thirds, three-quarters, a fifth, seven-eighths.
Agreement
agrees with what it refers to — A third of the seats were empty (countable → plural); A third of the budget is gone (mass → singular). The numerator does not decide it either: Two-thirds of the members have signed is plural because members is, and Two-thirds of the roof was torn off is singular because roof is. The of-phrase decides, exactly as with half, all, and most.
Pattern
a third [verb] · a third of [determiner + noun] · a third of [object pronoun] · [verb] a third · about a third, nearly two-thirds, a good third, the bottom third · by a third, by two-thirds (adverbial)
Variants
a quarter and a fourth (both standard American, a quarter the commoner outside arithmetic class), two-thirds, three-quarters, a fifth, three-eighths, and every other fraction built on the same frame
Contrast
Against half: half is the one fraction that may drop the of in front of a determiner — half the time is standard and ✗a third the time is not, so the phrase has to be a third of the time. Against some and most: those are vague on purpose, while a fraction reads as a claim about proportion and a reader will treat it as one — write about a third when the number is an estimate. On the page a two-word fraction standing alone takes a hyphen (two-thirds of them, three-quarters were sold), while a third and a quarter need none. And keep the fraction apart from the ordinal: the third of the applicants is a portion, the third applicant is a position in line.

One part in three of something, and the model for every fraction used the same way.

  1. A third of the seats were still empty when the lights went down.
  2. Two-thirds of the roof came off in the first hour of the storm.
  3. She paid a third at signing and the balance on delivery.
  4. Of the forty applications, roughly a third were incomplete.
  5. Three-quarters of them had never heard of the company before that week.
  6. He read a quarter of it, decided it was hopeless, and gave the book away.

all#

Pronunciation
/ɔl/, in cot-caught merged American speech /ɑl/ · AWL · no reduced vowel of its own, but it runs into what follows: all of them /ˈɔl əvəm/ · AW-luh-vum
Русский
всё; все — вся совокупность счётного множества или вся масса целиком
Type
quantifying pronoun (also a determiner, all children, and a predeterminer, all the children)
Case
none — one invariable form for subject and object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person; invariant in form; construes as plural with countables and singular with a mass or an abstraction
Refers to
the entirety of a set of countable things or people (plural), the whole of a mass or abstract quantity (singular), or everything covered by a following relative clause (all that remains, all I know). Bare all meaning "everybody" survives only in formal or ceremonial writing — All are welcome.
Agreement
agrees with what it refers to. Countable referent → plural verb: All of the seats are taken. Mass or abstract referent → singular verb: All of the money is gone; All I need is a break. The of-phrase decides, not the word all.
Pattern
all [verb] · all of [determiner + noun] · all of [object pronoun] · [verb] all · all [relative clause] (all that remains) · that's all · floated after the subject: they all left, we have all seen it
Contrast
Against each and every one: all takes the members together and goes plural (All of them are here), while each and every one take them one at a time and go singular (Each of them is here) — same fact, different packaging. Against half and none: the three mark the whole of an of-phrase, half of it, and none of it, and all three take their number from it. Against whole: all of the cake is every piece of it; the whole cake is the cake as an undivided thing. Where the of is required and where it is not: before a pronoun it is obligatory — all of them, ✗all them — but before a determiner all is a predeterminer and may drop it, so all the seats and all of the seats are equally standard, exactly as with both and half. In we all left and he thanked us all, all has floated out of the noun phrase and needs no of either. The dialect all them apples is a different thing again — the object pronoun them doing a determiner's job, systematic in Southern and Appalachian vernacular and in African American English, and nonstandard in every register of writing.

Names the complete quantity or the complete membership of something, leaving nothing out.

1. The entire number of a set of countables. Plural verb.

  1. All of the tickets were sold before noon.
  2. Of the forty crates we shipped, all arrived intact.
  3. She read the file and sent copies to all of us before the meeting.

2. The entire amount of a mass or abstraction. Singular verb.

  1. She spent all of it on rent.
  2. All of the paint had dried in the can.

3. Everything, with a relative clause. The verb stays singular even when the complement after it is plural — All I want is answers — because the subject is the whole clause all I want. The plural by attraction, All I want are answers, is common and turns up in edited prose; the singular is the safer choice and the one an editor will leave alone.

  1. That's all I have to say about it.
  2. All I want is a straight answer.

4. Everybody — formal and ceremonial only.

  1. All are welcome at the Sunday service.

/ˌɔl ən ˈsʌndri/ · awl-uhn-SUN-dree · the and is always reduced to /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/ and is never said in full; the main beat falls on sundry

fixed quantifying pronoun phrase meaning "everyone, without distinction" (the adjective sundry survives almost nowhere else outside the determiner of sundry expenses)

none
Register
formal-jocular, and chiefly British; an American meets it in British writing, in legal boilerplate, and in deliberately arch journalism
Person / number
third person; always plural

every person in sight, taken as an undifferentiated crowd — invited or not. People only: ✗all and sundry of the boxes.

plural, always — All and sundry were invited; All and sundry have had their say. ✗All and sundry is is wrong even though the phrase names a lump.

Everybody in sight, named as a crowd rather than as people.

  1. He announced the engagement to all and sundry before telling his mother.
  2. All and sundry were welcome at the open house, which turned out to be the problem.
  3. She has explained the plan to all and sundry, and nobody is any the wiser.
  4. The gate was left open and all and sundry wandered in off the street.
  5. Terms of the settlement were emailed to all and sundry by mistake.
  6. It stops being a secret the moment you tell all and sundry.

Some, any, and enough: indefinite amounts#

The assertive/non-assertive pair plus the sufficiency word, with three relatives of any and some alongside them. Any, enough, and some bend their number to what they refer to, and all three are pronouns only when they stand alone or take of — in front of a bare noun they are determiners. Large quantity#

The words for a lot of something. Many and several count and are always plural, and so do a good many, quite a few, a slew, and the order-of-magnitude plurals hundreds, thousands, millions, and dozens; much and a great deal measure and are always singular; more, most, plenty, a lot, and lots do both and take their number from the referent. Two idioms in the group say the opposite of what their parts say: quite a few is a fairly large number and a good many is more than the listener expected.

The definite portions: the rest, the majority, and their kin#

A short family of the + noun phrases that behave exactly like the quantifiers above them: they stand alone or take of, they put whatever follows of in the object case, and they take their number from what they refer to. What sets them apart is the definite article, which closes the set — some of them leaves the count open, the rest of them accounts for everyone still unaccounted for. Three of them are register variants of a fourth: the rest is the plain word, the remainder the formal one, the balance the commercial one, and the residue the one that belongs to wills and probate. The majority and the minority differ from the other four in one practical way — they need members you can count, which is why most of the money is right and ✗the majority of the money is not.

third person; singular in the pronoun form the other; for a plural sense the form must be others or the others
Refers to
the remaining member of a set — typically the second of exactly two, when the first has already been named. People or things, countables only.
Agreement
singular as the other — The other is broken; The other of the two was never found. If the sense is plural, the word changes shape: the others are.
Pattern
the other [singular verb] · one … the other … · [verb] the other · the other of the two · some … other(s) … · each other (a separate reciprocal pronoun)
Contrast
The -s is not optional. Standing alone in a plural sense, the word must be others: ✗Some like it hot; other don't — write others. Standing in front of a noun it must never take the -s: ✗others people. And bare singular other without the is not a modern pronoun: ✗I'll take other — write another or the other.
The set-size rule
with two, one … the other; with more than two, one … another … another …, and the others for all the rest.

The second of two, or the one that is left when the first has been accounted for.

  1. One twin went to Boston; the other stayed home.
  2. I have two keys — this one works, and the other doesn't.
  3. She looked at both offers and took the other.
  4. Of the two roads, the other is much faster this time of day.
  5. He kept one copy and mailed the other to his lawyer.
  6. Each brother blamed the other for the mess.

Pronunciation
/ˈʌðɚz/ · UTH-urz
Русский
другие; остальные — others «ещё какие-то», the others «все остальные»
Type
substitute pronoun, plural
Case
none
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person; always plural
Refers to
additional or remaining people or things. Bare others is indefinite — some more, unspecified; the others is definite — all the rest of a known set.
Agreement
plural, always — Others were waiting outside; The others have already left.
Pattern
others [plural verb] · the others [plural verb] · some … others … · [verb] the others · others of [noun phrase] (formal) · and others (the English of et al.)
Contrast
Others vs. other: the -s appears precisely because no noun follows — other people (determiner, no -s) but others (pronoun, -s required). Others vs. the others: without the, some unspecified additional ones; with the, every remaining member of a definite set — Some left early; others stayed leaves the number open, while Two stayed; the others left closes it. Against another: plural against singular.

The rest of a group, or some further members of it.

  1. Some of the guests danced; others just watched.
  2. The others had already gone home by the time we arrived.
  3. She finished her section and helped the others with theirs.
  4. A few complained, but others said nothing at all.
  5. Two of the engines were repaired; the others were scrapped.
  6. Others have tried this and failed.

such#

Pronunciation
/sʌtʃ/ · SUCH
Русский
таковой, такие — книжн. и юр., о только что описанном, часто о целой ситуации
Type
substitute pronoun (also a determiner, such nonsense, and a predeterminer, such a mess)
Case
none
Register
formal, legal, and literary — the pronoun use is not ordinary conversational English
Person / number
third person; invariant; construes singular or plural with the referent
Refers to
things or people of the kind just described, or a whole preceding situation taken as a lump (Such was the state of the roof). Its antecedent is often an entire clause rather than a noun phrase.
Agreement
agrees with what it refers to — Such are the risks (plural referent); Such is life (singular). The verb typically inverts with it, since the pronoun is fronted.
Pattern
such [verb] [subject complement] (Such is the case) · such as [clause] (formal: such as remain) · as such (fixed adverbial) · and such (informal: tools and such) · no such thing (determiner) · legal such + noun (such defects, a determiner)
Contrast
As a bare pronoun such has narrowed to a handful of frames — such is life, such was the case, such are the risks, such as remain. Everywhere else American English fills the slot with that, this, or those: Such was his answer → That was his answer. The legal such of such agreement and such party is a determiner and belongs to the determiner dictionary. And as such traditionally means "in that capacity" or "strictly speaking," and it wants a noun antecedent to point back at: He is the treasurer and as such signs the checks. The causal as such meaning "therefore" — It rained; as such, we stayed in — has spread widely through business and academic prose, but there is no antecedent there for such to refer to, and the usage is still objected to often enough that therefore, so, or for that reason is the safer word.

Points back at a kind of thing just described, or at the situation as a whole.

  1. Such is life.
  2. Such were the terms he insisted on.
  3. He gave no reason, and none was expected; such was his way.
  4. Delays, floods, and the like — such are the risks the policy covers.
  5. Such as remain unsold will be auctioned in the spring.
  6. Such was the state of the roof that two tenants moved out that winter.
  7. She was hired as an interpreter and as such attended every meeting. (fixed adverbial, not the pronoun)

so, that, those, others, such (determiner)

the one#

Pronunciation
/ðə ˈwʌn/ · thuh WUN · one begins with the consonant /w/, so the takes its ordinary weak form /ðə/ and not the pre-vowel /ði/; the /ði/ form appears only under contrastive stress, which is exactly what he's THE one is doing
Русский
тот самый — тот член множества, который выделяет следующее уточнение
Type
definite prop-word (substitute) pronoun phrase — the prop-word one with the definite article, used as a fused head
Case
none
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person; singular, with the plural the ones
Refers to
the particular member of a set that a following modifier, a relative clause, or the situation identifies — a thing or a person. It needs that identification: bare the one with nothing to pin it down works only where the context has already done the pinning (That's the one).
Agreement
singular — The one on the left is cracked; The one who called was her brother. The plural the ones takes a plural verb: The ones we saw have already sold. As a subject complement it does not control the verb at all — You are the one who decides — and the relative clause inside one of those people who… goes plural, a trap dealt with at one.
Pattern
the one + prepositional phrase (the one on the left) · the one + relative clause (the one that broke, the one who called) · the one with / in / about · that's the one, he's the one, she's not the one · the one about + a joke · plural the ones
Variants
the plural the ones, which behaves the same way and is dictionaried at ones; the demonstrative alternatives this one and that one
Contrast
Against bare prop-word one: one is indefinite and introduces a fresh instance (I need a pen — do you have one?), while the one is definite and picks a known instance out of a set (the one in the drawer). Against ones: a difference of number only, with the caution that bare ones cannot stand alone where one can. Against this one and that one: the demonstratives point, while the one identifies by description, which is why something almost always follows it. Two idioms are worth naming, and both live entirely off the definite article — the one meaning the destined person (He thinks she's the one) and the one introducing a joke (the one about the mule).

The particular member that the words around it identify — the definite half of the prop-word one.

  1. The one on the left has a chip in it.
  2. She's the one who called the inspector.
  3. Of the four keys he tried, the one that worked was the oldest.
  4. That's the one — the blue folder on top of the pile.
  5. He's convinced she's the one, and they have known each other three weeks.
  6. Tell them the one about the mule.
  7. The ones we saw at the fair were half the price. (plural)

the usual#

Pronunciation
/ðə ˈjuʒuəl/, in ordinary speech /ðə ˈjuʒwəl/ and /ðə ˈjuʒəl/ · thuh YOO-zhoo-ul, casually thuh YOO-zhul · three syllables said carefully and two said quickly; the is the weak /ðə/ before the consonantal /j/
Русский
как обычно, обычное — разг., то, что собеседник и так знает по привычке
Type
fused-head pronoun phrase — the adjective usual standing where its noun would be (also the ordinary adjective, the usual suspects)
Case
none
Register
informal to neutral — the language of bars, diners, and standing arrangements, and of dry understatement in writing
Person / number
third person; singular
Refers to
whatever the hearer already knows to be habitual — the drink you always order, the excuse he always gives, the trouble that always happens there. Its antecedent is shared knowledge rather than anything on the page, which is why the phrase works across a bar and fails in a document.
Agreement
singular — The usual is waiting for you at the end of the bar; The usual has been happening at that intersection all year. Where the referent is plainly plural the fused head turns unnatural and the noun comes back: the usual complaints were filed, not ✗the usual were filed.
Pattern
[verb] the usual (I'll have the usual) · the usual [singular verb] · the usual from [somewhere] · as a bare answer (How was your week? — The usual.) · as usual, business as usual (fixed adverbials, not this pronoun) · the usual suspects, the usual channels (the adjective in front of a noun)
Contrast
Against the same: the same points back at something just named (I'll have the same), while the usual points at a standing habit that may never have been mentioned — the two are close, and the difference is where the antecedent lives. Against such and the like: those generalize about a kind of thing, this identifies one habitual instance. Against as usual: a fixed adverbial and a different word class, since as usual, he was late modifies the clause while I'll have the usual fills an object slot. Because the phrase runs on shared knowledge, never in writing for a reader who does not already have it: in a report, name the thing.

Whatever both of you already know is habitual — the fused head that runs entirely on shared knowledge.

  1. The usual, please, and put it on my tab.
  2. He asked how the week had gone and she said, the usual.
  3. The usual was waiting for him at the end of the bar before he sat down.
  4. They wanted an explanation and got the usual.
  5. Breakfast was the usual — eggs, toast, and the crossword.
  6. The usual has been happening at that intersection since they changed the light.

two#

Pronunciation
/tu/ · TOO · a homophone of to and too, and unlike them it never reduces — the numeral keeps its full vowel and normally carries stress, which is the audible difference between I have two and I have to
Русский
двое, две штуки — числительное-заместитель уже названных счётных предметов
Type
cardinal numeral used as a fused head — the numeral standing where its noun would be (also a determiner, two chairs, and an ordinary noun, the number two)
Case
none
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person; plural for every cardinal from two upward; one is the singular member of the series and is dictionaried at one
Refers to
the stated number of countable things or people already named or understood. Never a mass: ✗two of the water. The numeral counts exactly, which is the one thing that separates it from every other pronoun in this dictionary.
Agreement
plural, always, from two up — Two were missing; Two of the panels have come loose; A dozen were broken in shipping. Note that a dozen is plural in the same way in spite of its singular article, because the head of the phrase is a number and not a noun.
Pattern
[numeral] [plural verb] (Three arrived) · [numeral] of [determiner + plural noun] · [numeral] of [object pronoun] · [verb] [numeral] (I'll take three) · the first two, all three, any two, another two · a dozen, half a dozen, a hundred, a thousand on the same pattern
Variants
every cardinal above one — three, four, ten, forty-one — and the countable group nouns used the same way: a dozen, half a dozen, a pair, a score, a hundred, a thousand. The indefinite plurals dozens, hundreds, and thousands are a different construction and are at hundreds.
Contrast
The numeral is a pronoun only when the noun is gone. Two chairs are missing is a determiner in front of a noun and belongs to the determiner dictionary; Two are missing is this pronoun. Against a couple: two is exact and a couple approximate in ordinary American use, which is why an invoice says two and a story says a couple. Against both: both also names two, but it insists that they are the two already known and that neither is left out — Two of them agreed comes out of an open set, Both of them agreed out of a set of exactly two. Against hundreds and dozens: the -s turns an exact count into an estimate. And the of is obligatory before a determiner or a pronoun: two of the panels, two of them, ✗two them.

A bare number standing where its noun would be — the plainest fused head in the language.

  1. We ordered six chairs and two arrived broken.
  2. Three of them had never been opened.
  3. She read the first chapter twice and skipped the next two.
  4. Of the twelve applications, only four were complete.
  5. I'll take a dozen, and make half of them plain.
  6. Two of us stayed behind to lock up.
  7. He asked for volunteers and a dozen put their hands up.
