Personal pronouns#

The twelve forms that point at the speaker, the listener, and everyone else — with who/whom, the last words in English that still change shape depending on their job in the sentence.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Personal pronouns encode three things at once: person (who is speaking, who is spoken to, who is spoken about), number (one or more than one), and, in the third-person singular, gender. What sets them apart from every other word class is case — English nouns lost their case endings centuries ago, but I/me, he/him, she/her, we/us, and they/them still mark the difference between a subject and an object. The single rule running through the whole group is that case follows the pronoun's own job in its own clause, and the single place Americans most reliably get it wrong is inside a coordination. It goes wrong in both directions: between you and I, he gave it to Bob and I, and they invited my wife and I are hypercorrections — the object slot demands the object form — while me and him went is nonstandard in the opposite direction, an object form doing a subject's job. One one-second test fixes every case of it: delete the other person and listen. He gave it to Bob and me (because he gave it to me), They invited my wife and me (because they invited me), She and I went (because she went, I went). The second thing to know is that these words are almost never stressed, so the form a learner actually hears is the weak form: him is /ɪm/, her is /ɚ/, them is /əm/, he is /i/, and you is /jə/.

The possessive forms (my/mine, your/yours, his, her/hers, its, our/ours, their/theirs) and the reflexives (myself … themselves) belong to the same paradigm but are dictionaried separately — the possessive determiners in the determiner dictionary, the standalone possessives in possessive pronouns and the reflexives in reflexive and reciprocal pronouns.

The entries below cover those twelve in alphabetical order, plus the reduced forms that a listener actually hears in place of the full words in most casual American sentences — 'em, which is a separate word historically rather than a lazy spelling of them, ya, which is simply you said at speed, and 'im, 'er, 'is, which are him, her, and his with the /h/ gone — together with the coordinated pair he or she, him or her, s/he, the formal epicene that singular they has largely replaced, and the two counted phrases the two of us and you two, which are the only way English has of saying how many people a we or a you covers. None of the reduced forms is regional and none is nonstandard in speech; all are informal on the page. Three further sections follow the twelve: the regional, nonstandard, and archaic forms, the honorific address pronominals that address a person in the third person, and the neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets.

'em#

Pronunciation
/əm/ · uhm · also a syllabic /m̩/ after a consonant (tell 'em [ˈtɛl m̩]). It is never stressed and has no strong form; if the word carries stress it is them /ðɛm/.
Русский
их, им — редуцированное them в живой речи; на письме разг.
Type
personal pronoun, reduced (clitic)
Case
object
Register
neutral in speech, informal in writing — normal in dialogue, transcription, and advertising copy, out of place in a formal document
Person / number
third person plural, and also singular where it is the reduced object form of singular they. Invariant for gender.
Refers to
exactly what them refers to — any plural antecedent, people or things, and a single person of unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary gender. It carries no dialect marking of its own: 'em in I saw 'em is what educated General American speakers produce in ordinary conversation, not a regional feature.
Agreement
none of its own — an object controls no verb.
Pattern
[verb] 'em · [verb] 'em [direct object] · [preposition] 'em · all/both/some of 'em · fixed phrases: give 'em hell, let 'em have it, sock it to 'em, round 'em up
Contrast
Against them: same word in function, different register in writing only — you may always write them where you hear 'em, and in edited prose you generally should, outside quoted speech. The apostrophe is standard and points backward at the missing sound. And the etymology is the real surprise — 'em is not a clipping of them. It descends from Middle English hem (Old English heom), the native third-person plural object pronoun, which the Norse-borrowed them displaced in the written language while the old form survived unstressed in the mouth. So them and 'em are two different words that ended up sharing one job.

The form of them that ordinary speech actually produces. A learner who listens for /ðɛm/ and hears nothing is hearing /əm/ perfectly well and not recognizing it.

  1. Just tell 'em the shipment is delayed.
  2. I saw 'em leaving as I came in.
  3. Round 'em up and get 'em on the truck.
  4. Both of 'em said the same thing.
  5. There's nothing left in the box — somebody took all of 'em.
  6. Give 'em hell tomorrow.

he#

Pronunciation
/hi/ · HEE · weak /i/ — the /h/ drops whenever he is unstressed and not at the start of an utterance: is he /ˈɪzi/, did he /ˈdɪdi/, would he /ˈwʊdi/, has he /ˈhæzi/
Русский
он — о мужчине или о животном, чей пол известен; о предметах — it
Type
personal pronoun
Case
subject
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular, masculine
Refers to
a male human being, or an animal whose sex is known and matters (a family pet, a named racehorse). Not things — for objects, abstractions, plants, and animals whose sex is unknown or irrelevant, use it. Some religious writing capitalizes He for God. Generic he covering a person of unspecified gender (Every applicant must submit his fingerprints) was the traditional rule and is now outdated in American English; use singular they or recast in the plural.
Agreement
third-person singular verb forms — he is, he was, he has, he does, he goes, he doesn't. Never he are. The bare present without -s (he go, he don't) is systematic in African American English and in some Southern varieties, but it is not standard written English.
Pattern
He [verb] · He [auxiliary] [verb] · [auxiliary] he [verb]? · [name] and he [plural verb] · than he [auxiliary] · It was he who [verb] (formal)
Contrast
He is the subject form, him the object form. Test by putting the pronoun alone in the slot: He arrived / I saw him — whichever sounds right alone is right in a coordination too (Marcus and he arrived, I saw Marcus and him). Do not confuse he with the possessive his, which never stands as a subject. After than and as, both cases are current: faster than he [is] is the formal conjunction reading, faster than him the ordinary spoken one.

Points at a specific male person already identified, or one the listener can identify from context. It is subject-only: it can head a sentence, follow an auxiliary in a question, or sit in a coordinated subject, but it can never follow a verb or a preposition.

  1. He left the office before anyone could stop him.
  2. Did he say when the shipment would arrive?
  3. My brother and he were roommates for two years in Chicago.
  4. He hasn't answered a single email since Thursday.
  5. She types faster than he does, but he catches more mistakes.
  6. Neither the landlord nor he had a key to the back door.

he or she, him or her, s/he, himself or herself#

Pronunciation
/ˌhi ɚ ˈʃi/ · hee-er-SHEE · the or is a weak /ɚ/ in ordinary speech, and in him or her /ˌhɪm ɚ ˈhɚ/ both /h/s drop: [ˌhɪm ɚ ˈɚ]. S/he has no pronunciation of its own — a reader says he or she aloud; the slash exists only on the page, and so do the older he/she and (s)he.
Русский
он или она — книжн. формула для лица неизвестного пола; вытеснена единственным they
Type
coordinated pronoun pair, epicene — gender-inclusive by listing both
Case
he or she subject, him or her object, himself or herself reflexive
Register
formal and dated — the house solution of American statutes, contracts, employee handbooks, insurance policies, and academic prose from roughly 1970 to 2010
Person / number
third person singular, and grammatically singular in every slot, which is exactly what it was built to be.
Refers to
one unidentified person of unstated gender — the each applicant, every employee, a juror of institutional prose. Never a specific person already named, and never a group. Two things pushed it out. It names two genders, so it excludes anyone who is neither and cannot be used for a person who has stated they or a neopronoun; and it doubles the length of every pronoun slot, which collapses after two repetitions — Each applicant must sign his or her form and bring it with him or her when he or she reports.
Agreement
singular verb forms — he or she is responsible, he or she has thirty days. That is the cleanest difference from singular they, which takes a plural verb even for one person (they are responsible). The possessive determiner his or her precedes a noun and belongs to the determiner dictionary; the standalone possessive is his or hers.
Pattern
he or she [singular verb] · [verb] him or her · [preposition] him or her · [verb] himself or herself · s/he (writing only) · he or she, as the case may be (legal drafting) · he or she who [verb]
Variants
he/she, (s)he, s/he, she or he, her or him, herself or himself, he or she, as the case may be; the possessive halves his or her, his/her, and his or hers
Contrast
Against singular they, which is shorter, standard in edited American English, accepted by the major style guides, covers people of every gender, and takes a plural verb. Against generic he, which is dated in the other direction. In practice American editors now reach past both pronouns for a third repair — make the noun plural (All applicants must sign their forms) or cut the pronoun (Each applicant must submit a complete file) — because those remove the problem instead of managing it. The halves are not interchangeable across cases: ✗Give the form to he or she needs the object pair, him or her. And the reflexive still has to match a subject in its own clause, exactly as plain himself does: No employee may approve a payment to himself or herself.

The compromise American editing reached in the 1970s, when generic he stopped being acceptable and singular they had not yet been accepted back. It is grammatically unimpeachable and stylistically expensive, and it now marks a document as a generation old — which is where a learner meets it: leases, policies, bylaws, statutes, and older textbooks.

  1. Each applicant is responsible for making sure that he or she has submitted a complete file.
  2. Notify the account holder in writing before he or she is charged an overdraft fee.
  3. The plan shall pay the participant the amount due him or her within sixty days of the request.
  4. If a passenger's bag is lost, s/he should file a claim at the counter before leaving the airport.
  5. No employee may approve a reimbursement to himself or herself.
  6. A juror may not discuss the case with anyone, including his or her spouse. (the determiner half, which belongs with the determiners)
  7. The same sentence as an American editor would write it today: All applicants are responsible for submitting a complete file.

her#

Pronunciation
/hɚ/ · HUR · weak /ɚ/ — the /h/ drops when unstressed and non-initial: tell her /ˈtɛlɚ/, give her /ˈɡɪvɚ/, ask her /ˈæskɚ/, call her /ˈkɔlɚ/ (homophonous with caller)
Русский
её, ей — объектная форма she; притяжательное her (her car) сюда не входит
Type
personal pronoun
Case
object
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular, feminine
Refers to
a female human being, or an animal whose sex is known. The same word her is also the possessive determiner (her car) — that use belongs to the determiner dictionary and is not covered here. This entry is only the object pronoun: I called her.
Agreement
none of its own — an object controls no verb. When her follows be as a complement, the verb agrees with the subject, not with her: That's her, It was her who called (informal) / It was she who called (formal).
Pattern
[verb] her · [verb] her [direct object] · [preposition] her · [name] and her · That's her · than her
Contrast
Three words look alike and do different jobs. her = object pronoun (I saw her) or possessive determiner (her keys); hers = standalone possessive (The keys are hers); her's = not a word in any variety of English, ever. The test for object vs determiner is what follows: a noun after it makes it a determiner (her office), nothing after it makes it the object pronoun (I called her). Against she: she is the subject (She called), her the object (I called her).

The object counterpart of she — it takes the direct object, indirect object, and object-of-preposition slots. It is also what Americans actually say after be (That's her), where traditional grammar prescribes she.

  1. I called her twice from the airport and got voicemail both times.
  2. The committee offered her the position on Friday afternoon.
  3. This envelope is for her, not for me.
  4. Nobody had told her that the meeting was moved.
  5. Give the spare key to Dana and her before you leave.
  6. That's her in the second row, next to the window.

Type
personal pronoun
Case

Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular, masculine
Refers to
a male human being, or an animal whose sex is known and matters. Not things. Generic him for a person of unspecified gender (Ask each student to bring his own laptop) is outdated in American English — use singular them.
Agreement
none of its own — an object controls no verb. After be, the verb agrees with the subject: That's him, It was him (standard American) / It was he (formal, now stilted).

Contrast

Against his: his is possessive and never fills an object slot on its own (I called his is impossible). Against himself: use the reflexive only when the object is the same person as the subject of the same clause — He blamed himself vs. Marco said the manager would call him (different clauses, so the plain object form).

The object counterpart of he. It fills every non-subject slot, and in ordinary American speech it also fills the complement slot after be, where traditional grammar wants he.

  1. Tell him I'll be about ten minutes late.
  2. We haven't seen him since the reunion in June.
  3. The letter was addressed to him, but it came to my box.
  4. Is that him in the blue jacket, or am I thinking of his brother?
  5. She gave Marcus and him exactly the same instructions.
  6. I've worked with him longer than anyone else on this floor.

I#

Pronunciation
/aɪ/ · EYE · no true weak form — the vowel keeps its quality even unstressed, though the offglide shortens toward [ɑ] in fast speech (I dunno [ɑ dəˈnoʊ]) and across much of the South (I [aː]). The reductions happen in the contractions instead: I'm /aɪm/, I'll /aɪl/, I've /aɪv/, I'd /aɪd/.
Русский
я — только говорящий; в отличие от русского, опустить нельзя и пишется с заглавной
Type
personal pronoun
Case
subject
Register
neutral
Person / number
first person singular. Invariant for gender.
Refers to
the speaker or writer, and nobody else. In quoted or reported speech it shifts to whoever is doing the speaking, which is why "I'll pay," she said becomes She said she would pay.
Agreement
I is the only pronoun in English that takes am. Otherwise: I was, I have, I do, I go — the bare present, no -s. In the counterfactual subjunctive the form is were: If I were you.
Pattern
I [verb] · I [auxiliary] [verb] · [auxiliary] I [verb]? · [name] and I [plural verb] · than I [auxiliary] · It is I (formal, archaic-sounding)
Contrast
I is subject-only; me takes every other slot. The trap runs in both directions. The hypercorrection puts I into an object slot, and it is not confined to prepositions: ✗between you and I and ✗for my wife and I are objects of a preposition, but ✗They invited my wife and I and ✗She thanked Marcus and I are plain direct objects, and they are wrong for the same reason. This over-correction is now more common in educated American speech than the error it was invented to avoid. In the other direction, ✗Me and my friend are studying is nonstandard as a subject. The fix for both is the same: delete the other person. Send the invoice to Karen and ___ → Send the invoice to me. They invited my wife and ___ → They invited me. ___ and my friend are studying → I am studying. Orthographically, I is the only English word capitalized wherever it appears.

The speaker's own subject form. It heads a clause, inverts with an auxiliary in questions, and joins coordinated subjects — where politeness, not grammar, puts it last: My brother and I, not I and my brother.

  1. I disagree with the report's central conclusion.
  2. My sister and I drove up to Vermont on Saturday morning.
  3. Am I on the list, or did the registration never go through?
  4. She has been at the firm longer than I have.
  5. I have rewritten the opening paragraph three times and it still isn't right.
  6. Neither the auditors nor I could find the missing invoice.
  7. She and I went over the numbers twice before the meeting.

'im, 'er, 'is#

Pronunciation
/ɪm/ · im · 'er is /ɚ/ (ur) and 'is is /ɪz/ (iz). All three are weak forms and nothing else — they are never stressed, and under stress the words are him /hɪm/, her /hɚ/, his /hɪz/. Linking and flapping across the boundary are normal: get 'im /ˈɡɛɾɪm/, tell 'er /ˈtɛlɚ/, what's 'is name → whatsisname /ˈwʌtsɪzˌneɪm/.
Русский
его, её, его (притяж.) — слабые формы him/her/his с выпадением /h/; разг. написание
Type
personal pronoun, reduced (weak forms)
Case
'im and 'er object; 'is possessive
Register
neutral in speech, informal in writing — dialogue, song lyrics, transcription, and advertising copy
Person / number
third person singular — 'im masculine object, 'er feminine object, 'is masculine possessive.
Refers to
exactly what him, her, and his refer to. The h-dropping is not a dialect feature. In American English the /h/ of an unstressed, non-initial pronoun deletes for every speaker in every region: I told him is [aɪ ˈtoʊldɪm], Ask her is [ˈæskɚ], That's his car is [ˈðæts ɪz ˈkɑr]. It is the ordinary output of General American at conversational speed, and it is not the British h-dropping of 'ouse and 'ammer, which deletes /h/ from full lexical words and is socially marked in England. Only the apostrophe spelling is informal; the sound is universal.
Agreement
none of their own — 'im and 'er are objects, and an object controls no verb; 'is is a possessive.
Pattern
[verb] 'im · [preposition] 'er · fixed phrases: tell 'im, get 'im, let 'er rip, give 'er hell, whatsisname, whatshername · 'is [noun] (the determiner use)
Variants
written without the apostrophe as im, er, is in some novelists' eye dialect; 'er also spells the reduced possessive determiner her ('er car)
Contrast
Against 'em: the same reduction one cell over in the paradigm, with one difference — 'em is a separate word historically, the descendant of Old English heom, while 'im, 'er, and 'is are simply him, her, and his minus the /h/. Against the page: spelling these forms in one character's mouth while writing him and her in the narrator's tells a reader that the character talks differently, when the narrator would reduce them too — that is the eye-dialect trap, and it is why editors cut these spellings from everything but deliberate dialogue. And the determiner line runs straight through this entry: 'is before a noun is a determiner, not a pronoun — 'is car is the reduced possessive determiner his, dictionaried with the determiners; only the standalone use (That one's 'is) is the pronoun his.

What a listener actually receives in place of him, her, and his. A learner who cannot find the pronoun in Did you tell him? is not missing it — there is no /h/ there to hear.

  1. Tell 'im I'll call back after five.
  2. I gave 'er the keys and she drove off before I could explain the gate code.
  3. Get 'im out of the sun and bring 'im some water.
  4. What's 'is name — the guy who used to run the Denver office?
  5. Let 'er rip. (a fixed phrase, where the referent is a machine, not a woman)
  6. Somebody call 'im and find out whether the truck ever left the yard.
  7. Ask 'er whether she wants the last of the coffee.

it#

Pronunciation
/ɪt/ · IT · weak /ət/; frequently ends in an unreleased or glottal [ɪʔ] before a pause (Forget it [fɚˈɡɛɾ ɪʔ]). Flapping across the boundary is normal: get it /ˈɡɛɾɪt/, put it /ˈpʊɾɪt/. The contraction it's is /ɪts/.
Русский
он, она, оно — о неодушевлённом и о животных; также формальное подлежащее без перевода
Type
personal pronoun; also dummy (expletive) pronoun
Case
subject and object alike
Register
neutral
Person / number
third person singular, neuter. Identical in both cases; no plural of its own — the plural is they/them.
Refers to
things, places, abstractions, substances, and animals whose sex is unknown or irrelevant; also a whole preceding clause or situation (The vendor missed three deadlines, and it cost us the contract). It cannot refer to a specific human being except in identification questions before the person is known (Who is it? — Someone's at the door) — and except where a person has chosen it/its as their own pronoun, which is a separate entry: it (as a chosen personal pronoun). Using it for a baby was once ordinary and now reads as cold; use he, she, or they. Traditional English called ships, boats, cars, and countries she; American style has largely moved to it, and Lloyd's List switched its own house style to it in 2002. In its dummy uses it refers to nothing at all — it simply fills the subject slot that English syntax insists on.
Agreement
third-person singular verb forms — it is, it was, it has, it does, it seems. This holds even in the dummy uses, and even when what follows is plural: It is the deadlines that worry me, though It's the deadlines is what people say. A collective noun takes it in American English (The committee released its findings) where British English often takes they.
Pattern
It [verb] · [verb] it · [preposition] it · It is [adjective] that [clause] · It was [focused element] that/who [clause] · [verb] it [adjective] that [clause] · It's [weather / time / distance]
Contrast
it (possessive-free pronoun) vs. its (possessive determiner, no apostrophe, the dog and its bone) vs. it's (contraction of it is or it has). The test never fails: if you can expand the word to it is or it has, write it's; otherwise write its. Against dummy there: both fill an empty subject slot, but there introduces the existence of something and hands agreement to the noun that follows (There are two options), while it keeps singular agreement always (It is two miles). Against they: a plural antecedent takes they, and in American English a collective noun takes it.

It does more distinct jobs than any other pronoun in English, and only the first of them involves referring to anything.

1. Referential. Stands for a thing, animal, or abstraction already named. The package arrived. It was damaged.

2. Clausal / situational. Points back at an entire clause or an unnamed state of affairs. She quit without notice, and it put the whole project three weeks behind.

3. Dummy subject. Weather, time, date, distance, temperature, and ambient conditions, where there is no subject to be had. It is snowing. It is almost midnight. It is three miles to the trailhead.

4. Anticipatory subject. Holds the subject slot while the real subject, a clause, waits at the end. It is obvious that she was right (= That she was right is obvious).

5. Anticipatory object. The same trick in object position, after verbs like find, make, think, consider. I find it strange that nobody called.

6. Cleft focus. Splits a sentence to put the spotlight on one element. It was Daniel who signed the lease.

7. Empty idiomatic object. A fixed object with no referent at all: make it, cool it, wing it, beat it, knock it off, lord it over someone, rough it, hoof it.

  1. The printer jammed again this morning, and it took an hour to clear.
  2. It has been raining since Tuesday and the field is unplayable.
  3. It is unfortunate that the deadline fell on a holiday.
  4. I find it strange that nobody from the office has called.
  5. It was in Miami, not Atlanta, that they signed the agreement.
  6. Don't overthink the presentation — just wing it.
  7. Who is it? I wasn't expecting anyone.
  8. The committee published its findings, and it stands by them.

  5. Between you and me, that deal was dead a month ago.

The plural you that a document can use. English lost its second-person plural when thou died out, and every regional replacement in the next section is marked in some way; you plus a numeral is the repair that costs nothing, and it has the bonus of saying exactly how many.

  3. You two — over here, please. The rest can wait in the hall.

  5. This applies to you three as well, not just to the two who were in the room.

Regional, nonstandard, and archaic forms#

With one exception named below, nothing in this section is standard written American English, and none of it should appear in formal writing. The forms are here because a learner will meet every one of them — y'all and you guys daily, thou and ye in scripture, hymns, and Shakespeare — and needs to recognize what they mean and who says them. Almost every one exists for the same reason: standard English has no plural you, and speakers keep building one. Two are exceptions to that. We'uns fills no gap at all — we was never ambiguous — and is built by analogy with you'uns to mark a boundary rather than a number; and yo fills a different gap altogether, the missing gender-neutral third-person singular, which is the hole singular they fills for everybody else.

Two items here are odd in a different way, and neither is nonstandard. You all is the exception to the paragraph above: it is nationwide, unmarked, and — with the counted you two, which is filed with the standard forms — one of the only two plural-you forms that survive editing unchanged. It sits here because it is the phrase y'all contracted from and reads most naturally beside it, not because an editor would strike it. You people is likewise nationwide rather than regional, and perfectly grammatical — the ordinary appositive you plus a noun. It sits in this section because of what it does to a listener rather than because an editor would mark it wrong.

Read the labels literally. Archaic means no living American produces it outside quotation, ritual, or pastiche. Regional means real speakers use it as their ordinary form in a place that can be named, and the name is given. Nonstandard means an editor, a teacher, or a standardized test will mark it wrong however common it is in speech. The one form drifting out of this section is y'all, which is fully standard within Southern American English and African American English and is spreading nationally as ordinary informal speech. These forms are treated at greater length in archaic, literary, and dialectal pronouns.

Standard English has one second-person pronoun and no way to mark deference with it — no vous, no usted, no Sie. Neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets#

English has been proposing gender-neutral third-person singular pronouns for well over two centuries. Dennis Baron's survey of the record (What's Your Pronoun?, 2020) turns up more than two hundred coinages since the 1780s — thon, e, ne, hiser, ip, heer — and not one of them became a word of the general language. The form that filled the gap needed no coining at all: singular they, continuously attested since the 1300s and standard in edited American English today.

The entries below are the coinages still in live use, plus two related items — the chosen personal use of it, which is not a coinage but the ordinary English word, and the pronoun-set label that names any of these sets on a badge or in a signature line. One family inside the section is built differently from the rest: the nounself sets — bun, kit, star, and fae / faer — take an ordinary noun instead of an existing pronoun as their base, and live almost entirely online. Six things hold across the whole section, and they are the six a learner needs.

They belong to particular people, not to the language at large. A neopronoun is used for a specific person who has stated it. Nobody writes xe for a hypothetical applicant — that is singular they's job, and it is the job none of these forms does.

They take singular agreement. Ze is on the schedule, xe was late, ey has the file — unlike singular they, which keeps its plural verb (they are on the schedule) even when it refers to one person. A minority of speakers use plural agreement with these forms; the person's own practice settles it.

Each set has five slots, on the model of he / him / his / his / himself: subject, object, possessive determiner, independent possessive, reflexive. The determiner column is a determiner and belongs to the determiner dictionary; the independent possessives have their own entries in possessive pronouns.

Spelling and pronunciation vary by user, and the paradigms are not settled. Ze pairs with hir for some speakers and zir for others; xyr is also written xir; ve takes vis in one paradigm and vers in another. Where a set has more than one shape, the entry says so, and where a person states a pronunciation, that is the pronunciation.

How common they are. Far rarer than singular they. The Trevor Project's 2020 national survey of about 40,000 LGBTQ young people in the United States found roughly a quarter using they/them and about four percent using a neopronoun set such as ze/zir, xe/xem, or fae/faer. Americans meet them mainly in email signatures, profile fields, name badges, and class rosters rather than in print.

What the style guides say. The major American guides take up singular they and prescribe no particular coinage; their common instruction is to use the forms a person states, to gloss an unfamiliar one at first mention, and to recast the sentence where that reads more clearly than either.

Case: the rule, the test, and where Americans actually break it#

The rule. A pronoun takes the case its own job in its own clause requires. Subject of a verb → subject case. Direct object, indirect object, object of a preposition → object case.

FunctionFormExample
subject of a verbI, he, she, we, theyShe called.
direct objectme, him, her, us, themI called her.
indirect objectme, him, her, us, themGive him the receipt.
object of a prepositionme, him, her, us, themThis is for us.
complement of beme, him, her, us, them in practiceIt's me.

You and it have one form for every row above, which is why they never enter into any of this. Everything below concerns the ten forms that do change.

The test. Nearly every real-world case error happens inside a coordination, and one move fixes all of them: delete the other person and read the sentence aloud. Your ear has never once produced to I or me went, and it will not start now.

Note what the middle rows show: the hypercorrection is not only a preposition problem. They invited my wife and I has no preposition anywhere in it — the coordination is a plain direct object, and the object form is required there for exactly the same reason.

Where the breaks happen, and what they mean.

  • Between you and I is a hypercorrection — an error produced by over-applying a rule. Speakers are corrected so often for me and Bob that they come to hear and me as suspect anywhere, and start writing and I wherever a coordination appears, including the object slots where only the object form is possible. It is common in educated American speech, it appears in Shakespeare, and it is still marked as an error by every American style guide.
  • They invited my wife and I is the same hypercorrection in a direct object, and it is the version most likely to survive a proofread, because the writer is watching for prepositions. Delete my wife and the sentence answers itself.
  • Me and him went is nonstandard in the other direction. It is extremely common in casual American speech and will be corrected in any classroom or edited document. Note that the objection is to the case, not to the order: fixing it means He and I went, not Him and me went rearranged.
  • It's me is standard English, full stop. Traditional grammar prescribed the subject case after the linking verb be (It is I, That is he), on the model of Latin. Modern American usage overwhelmingly uses the object form, and It is I now sounds stiff outside the telephone formula This is she. Both are defensible; only one is what people say.
  • Appositive pronouns take the case of the slot, not of the noun beside them: We editors work late (subject) but They gave us editors a deadline (object). Delete the noun and the ear decides.
  • After but and except meaning "other than," the object case is normal: Everyone but me had read it. (Everyone but I is defended by some traditionalists on the grounds that but is a conjunction; almost nobody says it.)
  • Before a gerund, formal writing prefers the possessive and speech uses the object form: I appreciate your taking the time (formal) / I appreciate you taking the time (ordinary). Both are current; only the first is safe in a conservative document.

The same rule reaches who and whom. Who is the subject form and whom the object form, exactly as he is to him — and the substitution test is the same one: if he/she/they fits the slot, write who; if him/her/them fits, whom is the traditional form. What has changed is the honest description of where whom still lives. In American English it survives mainly right after a preposition — to whom it may concern, for whom the bell tolls, with whom I studied, the tenant to whom we sent the notice — and there it is genuinely required in edited prose. Everywhere else, and especially at the front of a question or clause where the preposition has drifted to the end, who is normal and unremarkable in standard American English: Who did you give it to? is what educated speakers say and write, and Whom did you give it to? is stiff at best and, at worst, sounds like a person who has just learned the rule. The one thing to avoid is hypercorrection in the other direction — ✗Whom shall I say is calling?, where the pronoun is the subject of is calling and must be who. Full treatment in relative pronouns and interrogative pronouns.

Case after than and as#

Both cases are established English, and which one is "correct" depends on whether you read than as a conjunction introducing a reduced clause or as a preposition.

She is taller than I [am]. — conjunction reading; formal, and the one traditionalists require She is taller than me. — preposition reading; normal in speech and accepted in most writing

The safest formal solution keeps the verb: taller than I am. That is unimpeachable in any register and never sounds stilted.

But there is one situation where the case is not a style choice, because the two forms mean different things:

He likes pizza more than I [do]. — I like pizza less than he does. He likes pizza more than me. — He likes pizza more than he likes me.

The same split applies to as: She works as hard as he [does] vs. as hard as him. When ambiguity is possible, supply the verb and the problem disappears.

The order of pronouns in a coordination#

Putting yourself last is a convention of politeness, not a rule of grammar — but it is expected in careful writing, and violating it reads as brusque.

My brother and I drove to Chicago. — preferred over I and my brother The award went to her and me. — preferred over me and her You, Marcus, and I are on the same team. — second person, then third, then first

The order carries no grammatical weight, and it does not change the case. In an object slot me and Bob is already correct and only impolite (He thanked me and Bob → He thanked Bob and me). In a subject slot it is wrong because of the case, not the order, so reordering it to Bob and me fixes nothing — the repair is Bob and I.

Three things this table is saying out loud:

  • I am is unique. No other pronoun in the language takes am, and I takes nothing else.
  • You are is correct for one person. We: inclusive, exclusive, editorial, royal, and the nurse's we#

English marks none of these grammatically. We is one word doing the seven jobs below, and only context separates them.

UseWho it actually meansExample
inclusivespeaker + addressee (+ others)Shall we get started?
exclusivespeaker + others, not the addresseeWe're going to lunch — see you at two.

The inclusive/exclusive ambiguity is the one that causes real trouble, since We're going to lunch is either an invitation or a dismissal. English has no fix built in — speakers disambiguate by adding we all, the two of us, you and I, or the rest of us. Languages that mark the distinction grammatically include Tagalog, Cherokee, and Mandarin (zánmen inclusive vs. wǒmen general).

The nurse's we deserves a warning label. Used by a clinician, teacher, or caregiver, it converts a you into a shared we the speaker is not actually part of. With rapport it lands as warmth; without it, it lands as condescension, and many patients specifically object to it. Recognize it; think twice before producing it.

On the double negative. I didn't see nobody does not mean "I saw somebody." In the varieties that use it, the second negative agrees with the first rather than canceling it — the technical name is negative concord — and the sentence means exactly what I didn't see anybody means in standard English. This is how negation works in French (ne … personne), in Spanish (no vi a nadie), in Russian, and in Old and Middle English, where Chaucer stacked four negatives across two lines of the General Prologue and no reader took him to have said something positive. The "two negatives make a positive" argument is imported arithmetic, invented by eighteenth-century grammarians, and it is not a fact about English.

None of which makes it standard. Negative concord is one of the most reliably stigmatized features of American English, and edited writing allows one negative per clause. There are two ways to get there: negate the verb and use the any- series (I didn't see anybody, He doesn't know anything), or leave the verb positive and let the pronoun carry the negation (I saw nobody, He knows nothing). Both are standard; what is not standard is using both at once. Recognize the double negative, understand that it is rule-governed rather than sloppy, and do not write it outside quoted speech.

The same disclaimer covers the whole table. These forms are systematic, ancient in several cases, and often more regular than the standard forms that displaced them. They are still nonstandard, and a learner needs to be able to hear them without producing them.
