Relative pronouns#

The words that hook a clause onto a noun already standing in the sentence, pointing back at it and filling a slot inside the new clause at the same time.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

A relative pronoun does two jobs in one word: it points back at a noun already in the sentence — its antecedent — and it fills a grammatical slot, subject or object or possessor, inside the clause it introduces. Which relative you reach for is settled by three questions, asked in this order: is the antecedent a person or a thing, does the clause narrow down which one you mean or merely comment on something already identified, and what job does the pronoun do inside its own clause. The second question carries the punctuation with it: a clause that identifies is restrictive and takes no commas (the car that needs repairs), while a clause that only adds is nonrestrictive and is fenced off by commas on both sides (my car, which needs repairs). Nearly every error in this category is a failure to answer one of those three questions before choosing the word.

The core relatives#

Six items do almost all the work: that, which, who, whom, whose, and the zero relative — the one you get by leaving the pronoun out. Four rules govern the whole set, and they are worth stating before the entries.

The comma rule is a meaning rule. No commas means the clause is doing identifying work and cannot be removed without changing what the sentence is about. Commas mean the clause is a parenthesis and could be lifted out. The passengers who had tickets were allowed to board says some passengers had tickets; The passengers, who had tickets, were allowed to board says all of them did. Same words, different fact.

Which vs. that, stated honestly. Half of the famous rule is real and half is house style. The real half: that can never introduce a nonrestrictive clause — ✗ my car, that needs repairs, is in the lot is simply wrong. The house-style half: restrictive which (the car which needs repairs) is perfectly grammatical, is the ordinary British choice, and appears throughout American legal and technical prose — but American copy editors following Fowler, Strunk, and the Chicago Manual will change it to that, so writing that in restrictive clauses is the low-friction choice, not a moral obligation.

Whom is nearly gone, except after a preposition. In American speech who has taken over the object slot almost entirely, and the man who I met is unremarkable. The one place whom is still obligatory is immediately after a preposition — to whom, for whom, with whom, several of whom — and there it is produced without effort even by people who never otherwise use it. Related to this: stranding a preposition at the end of a clause is normal English, not an error. The man I spoke to and the man to whom I spoke are both correct; the first is speech, the second is formal writing, and the prohibition on final prepositions was invented in the seventeenth century on a Latin analogy that does not apply.

Two case decisions, two different tests. A relative pronoun takes the case of the slot it fills in its own clause, and nothing outside that clause can change the answer; the tool for it is the he/him test (we hired him → the agent whom we hired). A personal pronoun sitting in a coordination inside that same clause is settled separately, by the deletion test: take the other person out and read what is left aloud. The clients whom my partner and I signed last year — delete my partner and, and I signed them is what your ear wants, so the subject form is right. The clients who signed with my partner and me — delete it and you get with me, an object after a preposition. The test catches two opposite errors, and both are common. On one side the nonstandard subject: ✗ Me and him went (✗ Me went, ✗ him went); the objection is to the case, not to the order, so the repair is He and I went, exactly as She and I went is right because I went is. On the other side the hypercorrections, produced by people over-applying the correction they were given as children: ✗ between you and I (✗ between … I) and ✗ They invited my wife and I (✗ They invited … I). The object form is required in both — between you and me, He gave it to Bob and me — because a preposition and a transitive verb each take an object, whatever else is standing in the slot with it. This is personal-pronoun business, worked out in full in personal pronouns; it is stated here because a relative clause is the commonest place to lose track, being the one structure where both decisions are running at once.

/ðət/ · thut · the weak form is the normal form here; the strong /ðæt/ THAT shows up only when the word is being quoted or contrasted. This is the reliable way to hear the difference between relative that and the demonstrative: the car that broke down /ðət/ versus that car /ðæt/.

Contrast
Against which: in edited American prose that takes the restrictive clauses and which the nonrestrictive ones, but only one direction of that rule is absolute — that is impossible in a nonrestrictive clause, while restrictive which is merely something a copy editor will change. Against who: that with a human antecedent (the man that hired me) is standard and has been since Old English; a minority of readers dislike it, so who is the safer choice when the antecedent is a named individual. Against the complementizer that (he said that he'd come): the relative fills a slot inside its clause — take it away and there is a hole (the report [that] arrived → …arrived has no subject); the complementizer fills no slot — take it away and the clause is still complete.

The workhorse of the English relative system, and the only relative that is at home with both people and things. It marks off the clause that tells you which one.

1. Subject of its own clause. The gap is the subject; that cannot be dropped here.

  1. The bill that passed last night wasn't the one anybody had read.
  2. He's one of the few engineers that have read the original specification.

2. Object of its own clause. The gap is the object; here that is optional and speakers usually leave it out.

  1. The apartment that we looked at on Tuesday is already rented.
  2. That's the folder that I pulled the numbers from.

3. After superlatives, quantifiers, and indefinite pronouns. The frame where that beats which and who even for writers who otherwise mix them freely.

  1. Everything that could go wrong on that drive did.
  2. She's the only reporter that got the mayor on the record.
  3. Nothing that he said afterward changed a single vote.

4. In it-clefts. The construction that pushes one element to the front for emphasis.

  1. It was my sister that first noticed the smell of gas.

which#

Pronunciation
/wɪtʃ/ · WICH · weak /wətʃ/. For most Americans which and witch are homophones; older speakers and many in the South and Appalachia keep the breathy /ʍɪtʃ/ that distinguishes them. The wh- here is the same Old English hw- that gives who its /h/.
Русский
который — относительное о вещах; в придаточных с запятыми или отсылка ко всему предложению
Type
relative pronoun; also interrogative, and a determiner when it precedes a noun
Case
none — one form for subject and object; the genitive is whose or of which
Register
neutral; the only option in a nonrestrictive clause about a thing, and the formal option after a preposition

invariant, third person; singular or plural entirely according to the antecedent

things, animals, institutions, and abstractions — never a person. ✗ the woman which called was ordinary English in 1611 (Our Father which art in heaven) and is a plain error now. as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent — the box, which is heavy; the boxes, which are heavy. Pattern

Contrast
Against that: see the honest version above — that is barred from nonrestrictive clauses absolutely, while restrictive which is grammatical but will be edited. Against who: things versus people, with no overlap. Against the fused relative what: which needs an antecedent sitting in the sentence, what contains its own (I heard what you said = the thing that you said).

The relative for things, and the everyday choice when the antecedent is a whole idea rather than a noun.

1. Nonrestrictive relative. Commas on both sides, and the clause could be lifted out without changing what the sentence is about.

  1. Her second novel, which took nine years, sold about four hundred copies.
  2. The engine, which had been rebuilt twice, finally seized on the interstate.

2. Restrictive relative. No commas. Grammatical, standard in British English, common in American legal and technical writing — and routinely changed to that by American editors.

  1. The provisions which survive termination are listed in Schedule C.

3. After a preposition. The pied-piping frame. Here that is impossible, the zero relative is impossible, and no editor objects to restrictive which.

  1. The file cabinet in which the originals were stored burned with the building.
  2. That's the argument to which I keep coming back.

4. Sentential relative. The antecedent is the whole preceding clause, not any noun in it. Always preceded by a comma. The trap: if the reader can find a noun that might be the antecedent, they will try it first, so keep the clause short and the reference obvious.

  1. He turned down the offer, which everyone else thought was insane.
  2. She never explained why she left, which is her right.

5. In a partitive phrase. With a quantifier or a number in front of it.

  1. There were forty-two proposals, six of which missed the deadline outright.

6. Object of the verb in its own clause. The gap is the object rather than the subject, and which keeps the same form either way — it has no whom.

  1. The bylaws, which nobody had read since 1974, turned out to settle the question.

who#

Pronunciation
/hu/ · HOO · weak /(h)ʊ/ — the vowel shortens in running speech (the man who called), but unlike him and her the /h/ normally survives. Who's /huz/ is who is or who has, and is pronounced exactly like whose.
Русский
который, кто — относительное о людях, обычно подлежащее своего придаточного
Type
relative and interrogative pronoun
Case
subject (nominative) in the paradigm — object form whom, genitive whose — but in present-day American English who also fills the object slot everywhere except directly after a preposition: the agent who we hired, the agent who I gave it to, but never ✗ the agent to who I spoke
Register
neutral as a subject; as an object it is the ordinary spoken form and increasingly the written one
Person / number
invariant in form; any person, either number. It transmits the person of its antecedent to the verb: I who am …, you who are …
Refers to
people, and animals granted personal status (our dog, who hates the mail carrier). Not things: ✗ the report who arrived. American English treats collective nouns as things and uses that or which for them (the committee that voted), though the band who turns up when the members rather than the body are meant.
Agreement
as subject, the verb agrees with the antecedent in number and person: the neighbor who lives upstairs, the neighbors who live upstairs, It is I who am to blame (formal). The classic trap: one of those people who do this takes the plural, because who points at those people; the only one of my friends who has a truck takes the singular, because who points at the only one. With an indefinite-pronoun antecedent the verb inside the relative clause is singular — everyone who wants a copy, somebody who knows the system, nobody who was on the call — even though the pronoun that picks that antecedent up later in the sentence is normally singular they: Everyone who wants a copy should raise their hand. Singular they is standard American English, accepted by the AP, Chicago, MLA, and APA manuals with differences only of scope (see they), and it leaves the relative clause's own verb singular. One honest footnote to It is I who am to blame: that is the formal pattern, and the reason person agreement is worth knowing at all, but the ordinary American sentence is It's me who's to blame or It's me that's to blame. It's me is standard English, not a lapse — the subject form after be is a Latin-modeled rule that American usage has left behind (see personal pronouns).
Pattern
[person noun] who [verb] … — subject · [person noun] who [subject] [verb] — object, standard in speech: the guy who I met · [person noun] who [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded: the guy who I gave it to · [person noun], who [verb] … , — nonrestrictive · never ✗ [preposition] who
Contrast
who vs. whom is decided by the he/him test: rebuild the relative clause as a sentence and put he or him in the gap. The agent who handled the sale → he handled the sale → who. The agent whom we hired → we hired him → whom. Do not let a parenthetical clause fool you: in the candidate who we thought would win, the gap belongs to would win (he would win), so it is who, and ✗ whom we thought would win is a hypercorrection — now a more common error than the one it was meant to prevent. who's vs. whose: expand it. If who is or who has fits, it is who's; otherwise whose. Against that: both are standard for people in restrictive clauses, but only who can appear in a nonrestrictive one. One case decision the relative does not make: a coordination sitting inside the relative clause is settled on its own by the deletion test — the clients who my partner and I signed (delete my partner and: ✗ me signed), the clients who signed with my partner and me (✗ with I). The two failures are the familiar pair, ✗ Me and him went on one side and the hypercorrections ✗ between you and I and ✗ They invited my wife and I on the other; the correct forms are between you and me and He gave it to Bob and me, and the whole subject belongs to personal pronouns.

The relative for people, filling the subject slot of its clause — and, in everything short of formal writing, the object slot as well.

1. Subject of its own clause, restrictive. No commas; the clause says which person.

  1. The nurse who took my blood pressure had been on shift for fourteen hours.
  2. Anyone who still has a paper ticket should come to the front.
  3. There's nobody in this office who knows how to reset the alarm.

2. Subject of its own clause, nonrestrictive. Commas; the person is already identified.

  1. My grandmother, who never learned to drive, walked everywhere until she was eighty.
  2. It was Ruiz who filed the original complaint, not his partner.

3. Subject across an intervening clause. We thought, they said, everyone assumed — these do not change the case.

  1. The candidate who we all assumed would win finished third.

4. Object of its own clause. Where formal writing wants whom, and where speech does not.

  1. He's the one who I gave the spare key to.
  2. The contractor who she hired last spring never finished the deck.

The object form, used where the person is on the receiving end of the verb or the preposition.

1. After a preposition. The pied-piped frame — formal, and the last stronghold of the word.

  1. The attorney to whom the case was reassigned had never tried a jury case.
  2. The people with whom we shared the cabin turned out to be cousins of my father's.
  3. He's the sort of man about whom nobody has a strong opinion.

2. Object of its own clause. Formal writing; ordinary speech uses who or nothing at all.

  1. The candidate whom everyone had written off won by nine points.
  2. She wrote to the senator, whom she had met exactly once, and got an answer in a week.

3. In a partitive phrase after a quantifier. Nonrestrictive, always after a comma, and the frame where no substitute exists.

  1. Several of the workers, three of whom had been there since the plant opened, refused to sign.
  2. He thanked the volunteers, most of whom had driven in from out of state.

The one relative that marks possession, and the one that does not care whether its antecedent is human.

1. With a human antecedent.

  1. The novelist whose letters were auctioned last year had asked that they be burned.
  2. Any student whose name appears on this list should see the registrar before Friday.
  3. She's the one whose car got towed on Tuesday.

2. With an inanimate antecedent. Standard, and lighter than any alternative.

  1. We finally bought a house whose roof doesn't leak.
  2. It's an argument whose logic falls apart the moment you check the numbers.
  3. There's a bill in committee whose effects nobody has bothered to model.

3. In a nonrestrictive clause.

  1. The company, whose founder resigned in March, has lost half its engineering staff.

4. After a preposition. Whose pied-pipes like which and whom, carrying both the preposition and the following noun to the front of the clause.

  1. They finally located the contractor in whose warehouse the crates had sat for a year.

none — the pronoun is not pronounced because it is not there. Relatives inside a preposition or a quantifier phrase#

When the relative has to carry a preposition or a quantifier along with it to the front of its clause, the field narrows sharply. That and the zero relative drop out entirely — neither can follow a preposition, and neither can appear after all of, several of, or the last of — and so does plain who, since ✗ to who is an error. What is left is which for things, whom for people, and whose for either when what the preposition governs is a possessor (a company in whose warehouse the crates sat for a year). These are the frames where whom is not a stylistic flourish but the only grammatical option, and where American writers who avoid it everywhere else still produce it.

Restricted, archaic, and nonstandard relatives#

Nine entries that are alive only inside a particular frame, in a particular century, or in a particular dialect — four single relatives, two frozen phrases, one cross-reference formula, one dialect frame that takes three different relatives inside it, and one family of six where- compounds. You will meet every one of them in reading. Only as belongs in ordinary writing, and only in the frames that license it — after such or the same, or as a comment on a whole clause; the one other survivor is whereupon, and it survives as a narrator's word for "and then." What is marked archaic below is dated rather than wrong, and what is marked nonstandard is marked that way on purpose and with the region named: these are regular, rule-governed forms in the varieties that use them, and they are still edited out of American prose.

The nonstandard neighbors, labeled. Dialect features do not arrive one at a time, so the relatives below turn up in sentences carrying other marked pronouns along with them. Each of those forms is regular and rule-governed in the variety that uses it, each is nonstandard in edited American prose, and none of them is a relative pronoun — so each is catalogued in the file that owns it, and named here only so a reader meeting it in one of the examples below knows what it is. Demonstrative *them — them** apples*, them boxes, and the subject use in Them as has, gets — stands in for those and is widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech; the pronoun use is at them, the determiner use (them apples) in the determiner dictionary, and the frame it makes with a relative hung on it at them that / them as / them what. Keep it apart from the reduced pronunciation /əm/ of I saw 'em, which is ordinary General American and carries no dialect marking at all. The vernacular reflexives hisself and theirselves — general in Southern, Appalachian, and rural white vernaculars and in African American English — are in fact more regular than the himself and themselves they replace, since they build on the possessive stem exactly as myself and yourself do; see hisself and theirselves. **Plural youse belongs to New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its American distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration, and it stands beside y'all (Southern, and standard within African American English), you'uns and yinz (Appalachia and Pittsburgh), and you guys (national, informal rather than nonstandard); see youse.   1. He gave such as he had and apologized for the rest.
  2. She wrote down such as she could remember of the conversation.

  1. The chair called on Dr. Whitfield, than whom nobody in the room had read the file more closely.

  1. The bridge, than which few structures in the state have aged better, reopened last spring.

  1. He signed off as "your obedient servant, than whom none is more obedient," and thought it was very funny.
  2. Readers usually meet the construction in Milton, where Beelzebub is the one than whom, Satan excepted, none higher sat.
