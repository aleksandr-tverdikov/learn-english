# Fused relatives, generic pronouns, and dummy pronouns

> The pronouns that point at nobody in particular — the ones that swallow their own antecedent, the ones that stand for people in general, and the ones that fill a subject slot English refuses to leave empty.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every other pronoun in this dictionary works by pointing back at a noun phrase already in the sentence. The three groups collected here do not. A **fused relative** — *what*, *whoever*, *whichever* — contains its own antecedent, so *what I need* already means "the thing that I need" and there is nothing left for it to point at. A **generic** pronoun — *one*, *you*, *they*, *we*, and the dated generic *he* — stands for people in general, with the speaker included or excluded as the occasion requires. A **dummy** pronoun — *it*, *there* — refers to nothing whatsoever and is present only because an English finite clause must have a subject.

Two rules cover most of the errors in the file. In a fused relative, **the case comes from the inside clause, not the outside one**: *Give it to whoever answers* is right because *whoever* is the subject of *answers*, no matter what preposition stands in front of the clause — ✗ *to whomever answers* is a hypercorrection, and it is now more common than the error it was meant to prevent. With a dummy, **the verb agrees with what follows, not with the dummy itself**: *there is a problem*, *there are problems* — except in the *it*-cleft, where the copula stays singular however plural the focus (*It was the neighbors who complained*).

The third theme is register. Generic *you* is what Americans actually say; generic *one* is formal and tips easily into stiffness; *whom*, *whomever*, and the whole *-soever* family survive mainly in contracts and scripture. This file says plainly which forms are alive, which are museum pieces, and which will get marked by a conservative editor even though everyone says them.

**Case, once, for the whole file.** A pronoun takes the case its own slot requires, and the thing that most often disguises the slot is a coordination. The fix is the **deletion test**: take the other person out and read what is left. *He gave it to Bob and **me*** → *He gave it to **me*** ✓ &middot; *between you and **me*** → *between … **me*** ✓ &middot; ***She** and **I** went* → ***She** went*, ***I** went* ✓. It catches the error in both directions. The plain error is ✗ *Me and him went* → ✗ *Me went*, ✗ *Him went*, so the repair is *He and I went* — and note that the **case** is what is wrong there, not the order. The hypercorrections run the other way: ✗ *between you and I* → ✗ *between … I*, and ✗ *They invited my wife and I* → ✗ *They invited … I*, which is the one that survives a proofread, because there is no preposition in the sentence to warn the writer — a coordinated **direct object** takes the object form for exactly the same reason a prepositional object does. Two things this file adds to that. After the copula, ***It's me*** is standard American English and is not an error to be repaired into *It is I* (see [it (cleft)](#it-cleft)). And in a fused relative the case is read off the **inner** clause and never off the preposition standing in front of it (see [whoever](#whoever)). The full table and the rest of the coordination cases are in [Case: the rule, the test, and where Americans actually break it](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it).

**Agreement, once, for the whole file.** Six facts, and everything below follows them. A subject *what*-clause and every *-ever* and *-soever* form take a **singular** verb — *What matters is the deadline*, *Whichever comes first is fine*, *Whoever wants seconds should say so*. The one fused relative that goes the other way is *as many as*, which is **plural** — *As many as want to come are welcome* (see [as many as](#as-many-as)). Generic *one* takes a **singular** verb (*one is*, *one has*), and so does the dated generic *he*; generic *you*, *we*, and *they* take the **plural** forms (*you are*, *we were*, *they say*). Dummy *it* is **always singular** in every one of its jobs, including the cleft, where the copula stays singular however plural the focus (*It was the neighbors who complained*). Existential *there* has no number of its own and takes it from **the noun that follows** (*There is a problem*, *There are problems*). The indefinite pronouns that raise the other agreement questions are not in this file, and the short version of each is: *everyone*, *somebody*, and *nobody* take a **singular** verb (with singular *they* standard for the follow-up pronoun); *each* is **singular**; *either* and *neither* are **singular** in careful use, though plural verbs after them are common in speech; *both*, *several*, and *a few* are **always plural**; *none* takes **either** number — singular with a mass referent, and with a plural count set the plural is older, commoner, and the ordinary default, the "*none* means *not one*" rule being an eighteenth-century invention; and *all*, *some*, and *most* **agree with whatever they refer to**. They are entered in [indefinite compounds](07-indefinite-compound.md) and [quantifying indefinites](08-indefinite-quantifying.md).

**Where this file stands on disputed usage.** Five things stated plainly, so that nothing below reads as a rule it is not. ***It's me* is standard** — the object form after *be* has been ordinary English for centuries; *It is I* is defensible grammar and elevated register, not a correction. ***Whom* and *whomever* survive mainly right after a preposition** — *to whom*, *for whom*, *to whomever it may concern* — and everywhere else American English has handed the object slot to *who* and *whoever*; *Invite whoever you want* is a register choice, not an error (see [whom](05-relative.md#whom)). **Singular *they* is standard American English** — entered in Merriam-Webster, accepted by the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style, and attested since the fourteenth century — so *Whoever left this should come get their coat* needs no repair. ***That* versus restrictive *which* is an editing convention**, not grammar: the one hard rule in that neighborhood is that *that* cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause (see [which](05-relative.md#which)). **The antecedent rules underneath all of that are settled and are not in dispute:** *who* is for **people**, *which* is for **things**, *that* is for **both** (*the woman that called*, *the report that arrived*), and *whose* is for **both** as well — *a book whose cover was torn* has been ordinary English since Middle English, and the objection to it is a superstition rather than a rule (see [who](05-relative.md#who), [which](05-relative.md#which), [that](05-relative.md#that), [whose](05-relative.md#whose)). What this file does **not** soften: ✗ *to whomever answers the phone*, ✗ *the thing what I need*, ✗ *Whatever are left*, ✗ *Is raining* with no subject, ✗ *There's two things* in edited prose, ✗ *between you and I*, and ✗ *Me and him went* are all still marked wrong in edited American English. Being honest about what has changed is not permission to pretend that nothing is an error.

**The nonstandard neighbors, labeled.** Five forms stand next to the pronouns in this file, and every one of them is labeled here rather than left to be guessed at. **Relative *what*** — *the man what fixed the roof* — is regular in London and other British vernaculars and in Southern, Appalachian, and rural American speech, where it is rule-governed; it is **nonstandard** in writing, and its entry is [what (nonstandard relative)](05-relative.md#what-nonstandard-relative). **The vernacular reflexives *hisself* and *theirselves*** are **nonstandard** — general in Southern, Appalachian, and rural white vernaculars and in African American English — and are in fact more regular than the standard *himself* and *themselves* they replace; see [hisself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself) and [theirselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#theirselves). **Demonstrative *them*** — *them apples*, *them boys* — is **nonstandard** for *those*, widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech, and it is a determiner rather than a pronoun, so it belongs to [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them); keep it separate from the reduced pronunciation /əm/ of *I saw 'em*, which is ordinary General American and carries no dialect marking at all. **Plural *youse*** is **nonstandard** — New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, its distribution tracking nineteenth-century Irish immigration ([youse](01-personal.md#youse)) — and it stands beside [y'all](01-personal.md#yall) (Southern and African American English, standard within those varieties), [you'uns](01-personal.md#youuns) and *yinz* (**nonstandard**; Appalachia and Pittsburgh), and [you guys](01-personal.md#you-guys) (national, informal rather than nonstandard); all of them are address forms, not generics. **The double negative** — *There ain't nobody home*, *they never told nobody* — is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernaculars, and was ordinary English in Chaucer and Shakespeare, but it is **nonstandard in edited American English**, where the repair is one negative per clause: *There isn't anybody home.* See [nothing (negative concord)](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord).

## Fused relatives

A fused relative — also called a free relative or a nominal relative — is a *wh-* word doing two jobs at once: it is the relative pronoun **and** its own antecedent. The whole clause it opens fills a noun slot, so it can be a subject, an object, or a complement. The test is substitution: if *the thing(s) that*, *anyone who*, or *any one that* can be dropped in, the word is a fused relative.

Because the antecedent is already inside the word, it must never be propped on a second one: ✗ *the thing what I need*, ✗ *anyone whoever wants it*. And because these clauses sit inside larger sentences, they raise the case question that gives the group its reputation — resolve it by looking **inward**, at the job the pronoun does in its own clause.

The *-ever* forms have a second life as concessives, meaning "no matter what / who / which." Those sit outside the main clause and are set off by a comma. The *-soever* forms are emphatic archaisms, alive today only in legal boilerplate, hymnody, and the King James Bible.

One older construction is entered with this group although nothing about it is fused: *he who*, *he that*, *she who*, *they that*, where the antecedent and the relative are still two separate words drawing their case from two different clauses — *give it to **him who** asks*. It survives in proverbs, scripture, and deliberate archaism, and *whoever* is the word that replaced it.

A second construction is entered with this group on the same footing: *as many as*, the old plural fused relative meaning "all those that" — *as many as want to come are welcome*. It is the one member of the family that takes a **plural** verb, and it has to be kept apart from the *as many as* that counts (*as many as forty flights were canceled*), which stands in front of a numeral and is not a pronoun at all.

### as many as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæz ˈmɛni əz/ &middot; *az-MEN-ee-uz* &middot; both instances of *as* are ordinarily weak /əz/, so the phrase runs to [əz ˈmɛni əz] with the only real accent on *man-*; the first *as* recovers its full /æz/ only under contrastive stress. *many* keeps its /n/ in every register — there is no reduction to ✗ [ˈmɛi]
**Русский:** столько, сколько; все те, кто — берёт множество целиком; глагол во мн. ч.
**Type:** fused relative pronoun — the plural member of the group &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** split. The "all those who" use is archaic, biblical, and formal, and an American meets it in scripture, in older prose, and in contract boilerplate; the everyday *take as many as you need* is neutral and unremarkable
**Person / number:** third person, invariant in form, and **plural** for agreement — the only fused relative in this file that is.
**Refers to:** the whole set that satisfies the clause — people (*as many as were willing*) or things (*as many as we could carry*). Unlike [whoever](#whoever) it is not restricted to people, and unlike [whatever](#whatever) it is not restricted to things; what it does insist on is that the set be **countable and plural**. For a mass quantity the phrase is *as much as*.
**Agreement:** **plural verb** — *As many as want to come are welcome*, ✗ *As many as wants to come is welcome*. This is the single agreement exception in the fused-relative group, and it comes from *many*, which is plural by nature. Compare the singular [whoever](#whoever), which says the same thing one person at a time: *Whoever wants to come is welcome.*
**Pattern:** *as many as* [plural verb] — as subject, *as many as were present signed it* &middot; [verb] *as many as* [subject] [verb] — as object, *take as many as you need* &middot; [preposition] *as many as* [clause] &middot; *as many as* [subject] *want / like / need / can carry* &middot; the counting frame *as many as* [numeral] [noun] — **not this pronoun**
**Contrast:** two constructions share the spelling and only one of them is here. As a **pronoun** it fills a noun slot and means "all those that": *As many as received the notice have already replied.* As a **degree modifier** it stands in front of a number and means "up to, and surprisingly so": *As many as forty people were turned away* — nothing there is filling a noun slot, the numeral is, and that use belongs with the quantifiers. The test is substitution: if *all those who* or *all the ones that* drops in cleanly, it is the pronoun. Against [whoever](#whoever), the difference is number and range — *whoever* is singular and people only, *as many as* is plural and takes things as well. Against *as much as*, the difference is count against mass: *as many as you need* of chairs, *as much as you need* of coffee. Against [such as (fused relative)](05-relative.md#such-as-fused-relative), which is the same archaic move made with *such* — *such as were present* is the older sibling of *as many as were present*. And put a noun straight behind it (*as many as fifty applicants*) and it has stopped being a pronoun altogether.
**Variants:** *as many* standing alone with the clause understood (*take as many*), the mass counterpart *as much as*, and the archaic sibling *such as*

'All those that' — the plural fused relative, and the one that takes a plural verb.

**1. All those that (fused relative).** The archaic core, alive in scripture and in formal drafting: *As many as were willing came forward.* **2. As many as you need.** The everyday use, where the *as*-clause sets a limit instead of naming a set: *Take as many as you want.* **3. Not this word at all.** *As many as forty people* is a degree modifier standing in front of a numeral, and no pronoun is involved.

1. *As many as* want to come are welcome to come.
2. Take *as many as* you need — there's a whole case of them in the back.
3. *As many as* received the notice have already written back.
4. We carried out *as many as* we could before the smoke got bad.
5. The clause binds *as many as* sign it, and nobody else. (contract boilerplate, and the archaic use)
6. *As many as* were present that evening still remember the noise.
7. The headline said *as many as* forty flights were canceled — a count in front of a numeral, not a pronoun.

### he who, he that, she who, they that

**Pronunciation:** /hi ˈhu/ &middot; *hee-HOO* &middot; the antecedent keeps its full /hi/ because it opens the phrase and carries a beat, and *who*, which takes the phrase accent, keeps its full /hu/; the word that reduces is *that*, so *he that* is ordinarily [hi ðət] with weak /ðət/ and *he that hath* runs to [hi ðət ˈhæθ]. *she who* /ʃi ˈhu/ *shee-HOO*, *he that* /hi ˈðæt/ *hee-THAT*, *they that* /ðeɪ ˈðæt/ *thay-THAT* with weak *they* [ðe]. The *w* of *who* has been silent for centuries, so the word begins with a plain /h/
**Русский:** тот, кто; та, кто; те, кто — устар., книжн.; в новой прозе заменено на whoever
**Type:** antecedent pronoun plus relative pronoun — the two-word ancestor of the fused relative, entered here beside the forms that replaced it &middot; **Case:** split — the antecedent takes the case of the **outer** clause (*he*, object *him*), the relative takes the case of the **inner** one (*who*, object *whom*) &middot; **Register:** archaic; alive in proverbs, scripture, hymns, and deliberate formality. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in maxims, and in nineteenth-century prose, and essentially never in new writing
**Person / number:** third person; *he who*, *she who*, and *he that* are singular, *they that* and *they who* plural.
**Refers to:** a person defined by the clause that follows — "anyone who," "the one who." *He who* was the traditional generic and now reads as generic masculine, which is why edited American prose has replaced it with *whoever*, *anyone who*, or *those who*; *she who* names a woman, or is used deliberately as a rhetorical counterweight.
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the antecedent pronoun: **singular** with *he who*, *she who*, and *he that* — *He who hesitates **is** lost* — and **plural** with *they that* and *they who* — *They that sow in tears **shall reap** in joy.* Older texts carry the archaic inflection inside the relative clause: *he that **hath** ears*, *she that **doeth** good*.
**Pattern:** *he who* [verb] … [main verb] &middot; *he that* [verb] &middot; [verb or preposition] *him who* [verb] — the split-case form, *give it to him who asks* &middot; *she who* [verb] &middot; *they that* [verb] &middot; the frozen proverb frames *He who hesitates…*, *He who laughs last…*
**Contrast:** this is the construction [whoever](#whoever) replaced, and setting them side by side shows what fusion bought. *He who* keeps the two case jobs in two separate words, so the outer clause can pull the antecedent into the object case while the relative stays a subject: *Give it to **him who** asks* — *him* is the object of *to*, *who* the subject of *asks*. *Whoever* has one word for both jobs and settles it on the **inner** clause alone: *Give it to **whoever** asks*, never ✗ *to whomever asks*. Against [those who](04-demonstrative.md#those-who): that is the modern plural replacement and what an American editor now reaches for, since it carries no masculine default. Against [that which](04-demonstrative.md#that-which): the same shape for things instead of people. *He which* was ordinary in 1611 (*Our Father which art in heaven*) and is a plain error today — for a person the relative is *who* or *that*. And the case inside the clause is still read off that clause: in *he who I met*, the relative is the **object** of *met*, so a frame this deliberately archaic wants *he **whom** I met* for consistency of register. Be exact about what that is — object *who* is standard American English everywhere else (see [whom](05-relative.md#whom)), so this is a register slip inside an antique construction, not a grammatical error. In ordinary English the whole thing is *the man I met*.
**Variants:** *he that*, *she that*, *they who*, *him who*, *him that*, and the single-word archaisms [whoso](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whoso) and [whosoever](#whosoever)

'Anyone who' — the antecedent and the relative kept as two separate words, the way English said it before *whoever* fused them.

**1. The proverb frame.** Quoted whole rather than built fresh: *He who hesitates is lost.* **2. Scripture and hymn.** *He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.* **3. Deliberate archaism.** A modern writer reaching for weight or for a joke: *she who must be obeyed.* **4. The split-case form.** *him who*, *him that* — antecedent in the object case, relative still a subject.

1. *He who* hesitates is lost. (a frozen proverb, quoted rather than produced)
2. *He that* hath ears to hear, let him hear. (King James Bible, with the archaic *-th* inside the clause)
3. Blessed is *he who* comes in the name of the Lord.
4. The prize goes to *him who* finishes first. (split case: object of *to*, subject of *finishes*; modern English *to whoever finishes first*)
5. *She who* must be obeyed has already decided where we are eating. (jocular archaism)
6. *They that* sow in tears shall reap in joy. (plural antecedent, plural verb)
7. In a new policy, write *those who* apply late, not *he who* applies late.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [whomever](#whomever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [he (generic)](#he-generic), [those who](04-demonstrative.md#those-who), [that which](04-demonstrative.md#that-which), [whoso](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whoso)

### what (fused relative)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/, also /wɑt/ &middot; *WUT* &middot; weak /wət/, and the *t* flaps into a following vowel, so *what I need* runs to [wəɾ aɪ ˈnid] and *what's* to [wəts]. Conservative and many Southern speakers keep an /hw-/ onset: /hwʌt/
**Русский:** то, что — содержит собственный антецедент; только о предметах, не о людях
**Type:** fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — one invariant form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person, invariant. The *what*-clause counts as singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** things, substances, ideas, events, and whole situations — never to a specific person. For people use [whoever](#whoever). The *complement* of a pseudo-cleft may still name people (*What we need is more nurses*), because *what* names the need, not the nurses.
**Agreement:** a subject *what*-clause takes a **singular** verb by default — *What matters is the deadline*, ✗ *What matter is the deadline*. In the pseudo-cleft the copula is singular by default too (*What I need is a vacation*); before a plural complement both *What we need is more volunteers* and *What we need are more volunteers* are current in educated American English and neither is worth correcting. The verb *inside* the clause goes plural only when the clause is itself plainly plural, which in practice means with a linking or raising verb in it: *What appear to be errors are actually rounding differences.*
**Pattern:** *what* [subject] [verb] — as clause subject &middot; [verb] *what* [subject] [verb] — as object &middot; [preposition] *what* [clause] &middot; *What* [clause] *is* [phrase] — the pseudo-cleft &middot; [phrase] *is what* [clause] — the reverse pseudo-cleft
**Contrast:** *what* means "the thing(s) that," so it must never sit on top of an antecedent: ✗ *the thing what I need* — write either *the thing that I need* or *what I need*, never both. That is exactly what separates it from relative [that](05-relative.md#that) and [which](05-relative.md#which), which require an antecedent already standing in the sentence. Against interrogative *what*, the test is whether an answer is being requested: *I know what he wants* reports a thing, *I asked what he wants* embeds a question. **Nonstandard:** *what* as a plain relative pronoun — *the man what fixed the roof* — is regular in London and other British vernaculars and in Southern, Appalachian, and rural American speech, and it is rule-governed there; it is not standard written English. Its entry is [what (nonstandard relative)](05-relative.md#what-nonstandard-relative).

'The thing that' — a noun phrase and its relative clause fused into a single word.

**1. The thing(s) that.** The core use: the clause names a thing without ever naming it. **2. The pseudo-cleft.** Fronting a *what*-clause and parking the news at the end (*What I need is coffee*) is one of the main ways English moves emphasis, and it is why so many spoken sentences begin with *What I'm saying is…* or *What happened was…*

1. *What* I need right now is about ten hours of sleep.
2. She got exactly *what* she paid for.
3. *What* happened at the meeting is still not clear to me.
4. Just tell them *what* you told me.
5. That is not *what* I meant, and you know it.
6. He never spends *what* he earns.
7. *What* worries the committee is the cost of the second phase.

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wut-EV-er* &middot; the *t* is a flap in ordinary speech, so it comes out [wəˈɾɛvɚ]; the accent sits on *-ev-*, never on *what-*
**Русский:** всё, что; что бы ни — о вещах; разг. отдельное Whatever. — «да пофиг»
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, and concessive pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standalone dismissive *Whatever.* is informal only
**Person / number:** third person, invariant, treated as singular.
**Refers to:** things and situations, with the range left open — "anything at all that." Not people (use [whoever](#whoever)). In the concessive use its scope can be a whole clause.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Whatever is left goes in the fridge*, ✗ *Whatever are left*. Even where the sense is plainly plural, the verb stays singular: *Whatever remains is yours.*
**Pattern:** *whatever* [verb] — as subject &middot; [verb] *whatever* [subject] [verb] — as object &middot; [preposition] *whatever* [clause] &middot; *Whatever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive, comma required
**Contrast:** two distinct jobs, and the comma tells them apart. As a fused relative it fills a noun slot: *I'll eat whatever you cook* (object of *eat*). As a concessive it is an adverbial sitting outside the main clause and paraphrasable as "no matter what": *Whatever you cook, I'll eat it.* Against [whichever](#whichever): *whatever* leaves the range open (*take whatever you want* — anything at all), *whichever* picks from a set already on the table (*take whichever you want* — one of these). Spaced *what ever* is a third thing entirely, an intensified question: *What ever were you thinking?* And the emphasizer in *no evidence whatsoever* is not this pronoun — nor, strictly, a determiner: it is a **postposed** negative-polarity emphasizer belonging to the noun phrase, which is why it stands **after** the noun and never in the determiner slot (see [whatsoever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)).

'Anything that,' with no limit put on the range.

**1. Anything that (fused relative).** Fills a noun slot in the sentence. **2. No matter what (concessive).** An adverbial clause, normally fronted and followed by a comma. **3. Dismissal.** Standalone *Whatever.* means "I decline to argue" — informal, and read as rude in most workplaces.

1. Take *whatever* you need from the supply closet.
2. *Whatever* she decides, the rest of us will go along with it.
3. He believes *whatever* the last person told him.
4. *Whatever* is left over goes home with you.
5. I'll pay *whatever* it costs to get the car back on the road.
6. They can call it *whatever* they like; it's still a tax.

**See also:** [what (fused relative)](#what-fused-relative), [whichever](#whichever), [whoever](#whoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whatever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever)

### whatsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌtsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wut-so-EV-er* &middot; main accent on *-ev-*, secondary on *what-*; the middle syllable is a full /soʊ/, not a reduced one
**Русский:** всё, что бы то ни было — устар., библ., юр. усиленное whatever
**Type:** emphatic fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, and legal
**Person / number:** third person, invariant, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** things and actions, with maximal scope — "anything at all that." Not people; the corresponding forms there are [whosoever](#whosoever) and [whomsoever](#whomsoever).
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Whatsoever is written here is binding.* Older texts keep the archaic inflection to match (*whatsoever he doeth*).
**Pattern:** *Whatsoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause] &middot; [verb] *whatsoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; [preposition] *whatsoever* [clause]
**Contrast:** identical in meaning to [whatever](#whatever) and differing only in emphasis and in age. The one place *whatsoever* is thoroughly alive in modern American English is after a negated noun — *no reason whatsoever*, *none whatsoever* — and there it is not a pronoun at all but a postposed emphasizer belonging to the noun phrase, covered in the determiner dictionary. Used as a pronoun in ordinary modern prose it reads as costume; use it only when the archaism is deliberate or the document is a contract that has always said it.

'Anything at all that' — the emphatic, archaic form of *whatever*.

1. *Whatsoever* ye do, do it heartily.
2. The trustee may sell, lease, or mortgage *whatsoever* belongs to the estate.
3. *Whatsoever* a man soweth, that shall he also reap.
4. He was authorized to do *whatsoever* the board directed.
5. The license covers *whatsoever* the artist produces during the term.
6. *Whatsoever* is not of faith is sin.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [whichsoever](#whichsoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whatsoever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wich-EV-er* &middot; accent on *-ev-*; conservative and Southern speakers have /hwɪtʃˈɛvɚ/
**Русский:** какой (который) угодно из; какой бы ни — выбор из известного ограниченного набора
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, and concessive pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in contracts and specifications
**Person / number:** third person, invariant, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** one member selected from a **known, limited set** — things, and people too when they are being selected from a set (*whichever of the twins answers*). Outside the *who-* forms, this is the one *-ever* pronoun that takes people comfortably, because the selection rather than the humanity is the point.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Whichever comes first is fine.* It stays singular even over a plural *of*-phrase: *Whichever of these routes is shortest works for me.*
**Pattern:** *whichever* [verb] &middot; *whichever of* [the/these/my + plural noun] &middot; *whichever of* [them/us] &middot; [verb] *whichever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *Whichever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive &middot; the fixed tie-breaker *whichever comes first / is greater / is less*
**Contrast:** against [whatever](#whatever), the difference is whether the range is closed. *Take whatever you want* opens the whole world; *take whichever you want* assumes a set already in view. That is why contracts say *three years or 36,000 miles, whichever comes first* and never ✗ *whatever comes first*. The ***of*** test separates pronoun from determiner exactly as it does elsewhere: *whichever of the two* is a pronoun, *whichever route* is a determiner and belongs to the determiner dictionary. Do not write it as two words: ✗ *which ever comes first*.

'Any one that' — picks a single member out of a set the sentence has already established.

**1. The one selected (fused relative).** *We'll take whichever is cheapest.* **2. No matter which (concessive).** *Whichever you pick, someone will complain.*

1. Take *whichever* you like — they're all the same price.
2. The warranty runs three years or 36,000 miles, *whichever* comes first.
3. *Whichever* of the two routes you pick, expect traffic.
4. She'll be happy with *whichever* they send.
5. *Whichever* is cheapest is the one we'll book.
6. We can meet Tuesday or Thursday, *whichever* works better for you.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [whoever](#whoever), [whichsoever](#whichsoever), [whichever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichever), [which](05-relative.md#which)

### whichsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪtʃsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wich-so-EV-er* &middot; main accent on *-ev-*, secondary on *which-*
**Русский:** какой бы то ни было из — устар., юр.; в современном языке практически не встречается
**Type:** emphatic fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — invariant &middot; **Register:** archaic and legal; effectively absent from ordinary modern English
**Person / number:** third person, invariant, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** one member of a stated set, things or people, with the choice emphatically unrestricted.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Whichsoever is greater shall govern.*
**Pattern:** *whichsoever* [verb] &middot; *whichsoever of* [the + plural noun] &middot; [verb] *whichsoever* [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** the emphatic twin of [whichever](#whichever), with the same closed-set meaning and none of the currency. In a modern contract, *whichever* does the identical work and reads as English rather than as pastiche; keep *whichsoever* for quoting instruments that already contain it. Like every member of the *-soever* family it is one word: ✗ *which so ever*.

'Any one at all that' — the archaic emphatic of *whichever*.

1. The buyer may select *whichsoever* of the parcels he prefers.
2. *Whichsoever* of the two conditions occurs first shall govern.
3. The court may impose a fine or imprisonment, *whichsoever* it deems appropriate.
4. He would take *whichsoever* of the roads was open to him that night.
5. *Whichsoever* is greater shall be paid within thirty days of demand.
6. They accepted *whichsoever* the arbitrator proposed.

**See also:** [whichever](#whichever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whichsoever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichsoever)

### whoever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-EV-er* &middot; accent on *-ev-*; the *w* has been silent for centuries, so the word begins with a plain /h/. In rapid speech a linking glide appears between the vowels: [hʊˈwɛvɚ]
**Русский:** тот, кто; кто бы ни — только о людях; падеж берётся из своего придаточного
**Type:** fused relative pronoun, and concessive pronoun &middot; **Case:** **subject** — and the case is taken from the inner clause &middot; **Register:** neutral; now the everyday form for both cases
**Person / number:** third person; singular for verb agreement, though the follow-up pronoun is normally singular *they*, which is standard American English and needs no repair — *Whoever left this should come get their coat.*
**Refers to:** people only — "anyone who," "the person who." For things use [whatever](#whatever) or [whichever](#whichever).
**Agreement:** **singular verb inside the clause** — *Whoever wants seconds should say so*, ✗ *Whoever want*. The outer verb is singular too, because the whole clause counts as one subject: *Whoever ate the last donut owes me one.*
**Pattern:** *whoever* [verb] — subject of its own clause &middot; [preposition] *whoever* [verb] — *to whoever answers* &middot; [verb] *whoever* [verb] &middot; *whoever* [subject] *is/are* — as complement, *whoever you are* &middot; *Whoever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive
**Contrast:** **the case comes from the inner clause, not from what stands in front of it.** In *Give the file to whoever answers the phone*, the preposition *to* governs the entire fused relative clause, not the pronoun; inside that clause *whoever* is the subject of *answers*, so the subject form is correct and ✗ *to whomever answers the phone* is a hypercorrection. The test: isolate the inner clause and substitute *he* or *him* — *he answers the phone* → *whoever*; *you choose him* → *[whomever](#whomever)*. Honest note: in speech *whoever* is now used in both roles (*Invite whoever you want*) and nobody blinks; a conservative editor will still change that one to *whomever* in formal prose. Two words is a different thing again: *Who ever told you that?* is an intensified question.

'Anyone who' — names a person by what they do rather than by who they are.

**1. Anyone who (fused relative).** *Whoever finds it can keep it.* **2. No matter who (concessive).** *Whoever she is, she has a lot of nerve.* **3. The unidentified agent.** With a mild note of exasperation or admiration: *Whoever designed this parking garage should be prosecuted.*

1. *Whoever* parked in my spot is going to hear about it.
2. Give the extra ticket to *whoever* wants it.
3. *Whoever* is in charge here needs to fix the thermostat.
4. I'll go with *whoever* is driving.
5. *Whoever* you are, please stop calling this number.
6. The job goes to *whoever* applies first.
7. *Whoever* said that clearly hadn't read the report.

**See also:** [whomever](#whomever), [whosever](#whosever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whatever](#whatever), [as many as](#as-many-as), [who](05-relative.md#who), [they (generic)](#they-generic)

### whomever

**Pronunciation:** /humˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoom-EV-er* &middot; accent on *-ev-*; silent *w*, so it opens with /h/
**Русский:** того, кого; кому бы ни — объектная форма whoever; книжн., в речи редка
**Type:** fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** **object** — taken from the inner clause &middot; **Register:** formal; rare in speech, and when it does surface there it is more often wrong than right
**Person / number:** third person, people only, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** people only, in the role of object of a verb or of a preposition **inside its own clause**.
**Agreement:** none of its own — it is an object, so the verb inside the clause agrees with that clause's own subject: *whomever **you** choose*, *whomever **the committee** selects*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomever* [subject] [verb] &middot; [preposition] *whomever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *whomever* [subject] [verb], [main clause] — fronted concessive &middot; the fixed formula *to whomever it may concern*
**Contrast:** *whomever* is correct only where it is the **object inside its own clause**: *Give it to whomever you choose* (you choose **him**) against *Give it to whoever answers* (**he** answers). A preposition in front of the clause is irrelevant, and reacting to it is precisely what produces the classic error ✗ *Give it to whomever answers the phone*. The he/him test settles every case in a second. Where *whomever* is genuinely alive is **right after a preposition**, in the fixed formulas — *to whomever it may concern*, *to whomever these presents shall come* — which is exactly where plain [whom](05-relative.md#whom) survives too. Be honest about the state of the word: outside that one construction *whomever* has essentially left the language, [whoever](#whoever) is standard in speech in both roles, and in writing a wrong *whomever* is far more conspicuous than a *whoever* a purist would have changed. If you cannot verify the object role, write *whoever* — or rewrite the sentence.

'Anyone whom' — names the person who is the object of the action in the inner clause.

1. Send the invoice to *whomever* you dealt with at the agency.
2. She will support *whomever* the party nominates.
3. He was ready to argue with *whomever* he met that evening.
4. Award the contract to *whomever* the committee selects.
5. *Whomever* they choose, the transition is going to be rough.
6. Address the letter to *whomever* it concerns.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whom](05-relative.md#whom), [who](05-relative.md#who)

### whomsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhumsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoom-so-EV-er* &middot; main accent on *-ev-*, secondary on *whom-*
**Русский:** кого бы то ни было — устар., юр. объектная форма whosoever
**Type:** emphatic fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** **object**, from the inner clause &middot; **Register:** archaic and legal
**Person / number:** third person, people only, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** people only, as object of a verb or preposition inside its own clause.
**Agreement:** none of its own; the inner verb agrees with the inner subject.
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; [preposition] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] &middot; *to whomsoever these presents shall come* (a fixed opening in deeds)
**Contrast:** the emphatic twin of [whomever](#whomever), and it carries exactly the same object-case requirement — which the legal writers who reach for it routinely get wrong, producing ✗ *to whomsoever shall apply* where the pronoun is the subject of *shall apply* and [whosoever](#whosoever) is the correct form. The he/him test applies unchanged. In anything that is not a quotation or a deed, use *whoever* or *whomever*.

'Anyone at all whom' — the archaic emphatic object form.

1. The estate passes to *whomsoever* the testator names in the codicil.
2. He would defend *whomsoever* the court appointed him to defend.
3. Notice may be served upon *whomsoever* the company designates as its agent.
4. *Whomsoever* the king favored rose quickly at court.
5. The reward was offered to *whomsoever* the sheriff should name.
6. She spoke freely to *whomsoever* she trusted.

### whosever

**Pronunciation:** /huzˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hooz-EV-er* &middot; accent on *-ev-*; silent *w*. The everyday spoken equivalent is *whoever's* /huˈɛvɚz/, and the legal *whosesoever* /ˌhuzəsoʊˈɛvɚ/ is vanishingly rare
**Русский:** тот, чей; чей бы ни — притяжательная форма; редк. и книжн.
**Type:** possessive fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** **possessive**, standing alone &middot; **Register:** rare and formal; most Americans avoid the word entirely
**Person / number:** third person, people (occasionally an organization), invariant.
**Refers to:** the possessor — "the person whose," "anyone whose." Human possessors in practice; a nonhuman possessor is vanishingly rare with this *-ever* form and is better rewritten with *of which*. Do not carry that limitation over to plain relative [whose](05-relative.md#whose), which covers **both people and things** and always has (*a book whose cover was torn*); the objection to it is a superstition, not a rule.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Whosever it is has left it here a week.*
**Pattern:** *Whosever* [clause], [main clause] &middot; [preposition] *whosever* [subject] [verb] &middot; everyday spoken form: *whoever's* [verb]
**Contrast:** the **pronoun** *whosever* stands alone (*Whosever this is, it needs a name on it*); *whosever* immediately followed by a noun (*whosever car is blocking the drive*) is a **determiner** and lives in the determiner dictionary. Everyday American English sidesteps both: *whoever's*, or a rewrite as *whoever owns this*. Two spelling traps: ✗ *whoevers* without the apostrophe is simply an error, and ✗ *who's* is *who is*, never a possessive. The **personal** possessive pronouns are the family that never takes an apostrophe — *its*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs*, *yours*, and *whose*/*whosever* with them. Do not over-apply that: the possessive of generic *one* and of the indefinites is written **with** an apostrophe — *one's*, *somebody's*, *each other's* — and the everyday *whoever's* belongs with those, since it is built with the genitive *'s* rather than being an inherited possessive form. What has no apostrophe is the fused word *whosever* itself.

'The one belonging to whoever' — a possessive that supplies its own antecedent.

1. *Whosever* this coat is, it has been on that hook for a week.
2. The wallet goes back to *whosever* it turns out to be.
3. *Whosever* the mistake was, the client is the one who paid for it.
4. *Whoever's* this is, they left the lights on. (the ordinary spoken form)
5. He said he would take *whoever's* was left over.
6. *Whosever* the responsibility was, nobody has admitted to it yet.

### whosoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhusoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-so-EV-er* &middot; main accent on *-ev-*, secondary on *who-*; silent *w*
**Русский:** всякий, кто; кто бы то ни был — устар., библ., юр. усиленное whoever
**Type:** emphatic fused relative pronoun &middot; **Case:** **subject**, from the inner clause &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, and legal
**Person / number:** third person, people only, singular for agreement.
**Refers to:** people, with the range emphatically unrestricted — "anyone at all who."
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, and the older texts carry the archaic inflection to match: *whosoever believeth*, *whosoever shall enter*.
**Pattern:** *Whosoever* [verb], [main clause] &middot; *Whosoever* [verb] *shall* [verb] &middot; [preposition] *whosoever* [verb]
**Contrast:** the emphatic form of [whoever](#whoever), and the **subject** member of the pair with [whomsoever](#whomsoever) — *whosoever asks* (he asks), *whomsoever you ask* (you ask him). 5. *Whosoever* will may come.

## Generic and impersonal pronouns

English has no dedicated impersonal pronoun the way French has *on* or German *man*. It improvises with ordinary pronouns pressed into generic service — *one*, *you*, *they*, *we*, and, for most of the twentieth century, *he* — plus one option that uses no pronoun at all: the passive.

They are not interchangeable, and the difference is mostly register and who is included. **Generic *you*** is the ordinary American choice and includes the hearer. **Generic *one*** is formal, includes the speaker, and tips into stiffness fast. **Generic *they*** is for what unnamed others do or say. **Generic *we*** claims a shared stake. **Generic *he*** is the one the older style guides prescribed and edited American English has now abandoned in favor of singular *they*; it is entered below so that it can be recognized in an old contract and not imitated in a new one. The **passive** removes the person entirely — *It is said that…*, *Mistakes were made* — which is why it is both the formal solution and the classic evasion.

Several more things do the same work without being pronouns in the strict sense, and they are entered here because this is where a reader goes looking for them. The **noun-phrase generics** *a person*, *a guy*, and *a fella* are ordinary nouns pressed into generic service — warmer than *one* and commoner than it in speech. The **placeholders** *such and such* and *so-and-so* name a slot instead of a value, one for a thing and one for a person. The **impersonal self-reference** *the present writer* puts the author of a page into the third person. And the **paired forms** *he or she*, its object *him or her*, and the written-only *s/he* are the repairs that were tried on generic *he* before singular *they* settled the question; each is entered with an honest label on its register and on whom it leaves out. Four families join them, on the same terms. The **placeholder names** *Joe Blow*, *Joe Schmo*, *John Q. Public*, and *the average Joe* stand for the ordinary person, and *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* for everybody indiscriminately — all of them male in form, and each carrying its own load of disparagement. The **sworn self-reference** *deponent* and *affiant* is *the present writer* in legal dress, and it is what an American meets at the head of an affidavit. The **citation pro-forms** *ibid.* and *idem* point back at a source rather than at a person, and live only in footnotes and briefs. And jocular **yours truly** is *me* with a wink.

Two rules hold across the whole group. First, **agreement never changes**: generic *one* and generic *he* take singular verbs, generic *you*, *we*, and *they* take plural forms, exactly as they do in their ordinary uses. Second, **do not switch generics mid-passage**: ✗ *When one is tired, you make mistakes.* Pick one and stay with it.

**One family that looks as though it belongs here and does not.** **Neopronouns** — *ze/hir*, *ze/zir*, *xe/xem/xyr*, *ey/em/eir* (the Spivak set), *ae/aer*, *ne/nem/nir*, *per/pers*, *ve/ver/vis*, *fae/faer*, *co/cos*, *hu/hum*, and the nounself sets such as *bun*, *kit*, and *star* — are third-person **singular** pronoun sets, each built on the ordinary five slots of *he / him / his / his / himself*: subject, object, possessive determiner, independent possessive, reflexive (*ze / hir / hir / hirs / hirself*; *xe / xem / xyr / xyrs / xemself*; *ey / em / eir / eirs / emself*). Two facts place them outside this section. They take a **singular** verb — *ze **is** on the schedule*, *xe **was** early* — where singular *they* keeps the plural (*they **are** on the schedule*). And each is used for a **particular person who has stated it**, never for people in general and never for a hypothetical one — those are the jobs generic *they* and singular *they* do, and no neopronoun does either. The paradigms and the individual entries are at [Neopronouns and chosen pronoun sets](01-personal.md#neopronouns-and-chosen-pronoun-sets), the independent possessives at [possessive pronouns](02-possessive.md#xyrs), and the reflexives at [neopronoun reflexives](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#neopronoun-reflexives).

### a person, a guy, a fella

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈpɚsən/ &middot; *uh-PER-sun* &middot; the article is the weak /ə/ and never /eɪ/ — a stressed *A person* is counting people, not being generic. *a guy* /ə ˈɡaɪ/ *uh-GY*; *a fella* /ə ˈfɛlə/ *uh-FEL-uh*, a worn-down *fellow*, also spelled *feller* /ˈfɛlɚ/ in dialect writing. The possessive adds /z/: *a person's* [ə ˈpɚsənz], *a guy's* [ə ˈɡaɪz]
**Русский:** человек; парень; разг. мужик — «любой на моём месте», часто о самом говорящем
**Type:** generic human noun phrase used as a pronoun — a quasi-pronoun, not a pronoun proper &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; possessive *a person's*, *a guy's*, *a fella's* &middot; **Register:** *a person* neutral and conversational; *a guy* informal; *a fella* informal and folksy, at home in older rural, Southern, and Midwestern speech and used in print to signal exactly that
**Person / number:** third person singular, with a singular verb. The follow-up pronoun in ordinary modern American English is singular *they* — *A person can't help how they feel* — which is standard and needs no repair.
**Refers to:** anybody at all in the situation described, with the speaker very often included, which is the whole point of the construction: *A person could get killed out there* is usually a complaint about the speaker's own morning.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *a person gets tired*, *a guy has to eat somewhere*. ✗ *a person get tired.* The possessive is written with an apostrophe (*a person's own money*), like every noun and unlike the personal possessive pronouns.
**Pattern:** *a person* [singular verb] &middot; [verb or preposition] *a person* — *it wears a person out*, *hard on a person* &middot; *a person's* [noun] &middot; *what's a person to do?* &middot; *a guy can't* [verb] &middot; *a fella* [singular verb]
**Contrast:** these are **nouns**, and that is what separates them from every true generic in this file: they need an article, they take the *'s* possessive, and they can be modified (*a reasonable person*, *a young guy*). What they buy is warmth. Formal writing takes [one](#one-generic), ordinary speech takes generic [you](#you-generic), and *a person* is what a speaker reaches for when *you* would land as an accusation: *A person could starve waiting for this kitchen* blames nobody in the room. Two labels, stated plainly. ***A guy*** began as and for many hearers remains **male**, so it is not a safe all-purpose generic in writing — *a guy can't win* said of a woman's situation will strike a fair number of readers as off. ***A fella*** is **regional and dated** — older rural, Southern, and Midwestern speech, with the eye-dialect spelling *feller* — and in print it is a costume, worn on purpose. The archaic ancestor of the whole pattern is [a body](10-archaic-dialectal.md#a-body), two centuries older and preserved for everyone in one Burns song. Note the determiner split as well: the article *a* is a determiner and is entered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/01-articles.md#a); what does the pronoun's work here is the whole phrase, not the article.
**Variants:** *a fella* and the eye-dialect *a feller* (both worn-down spellings of *a fellow*), *a body* (archaic and dialectal), *a person's* / *a guy's* (possessive)

'Anyone at all, and probably me' — a plain noun doing a pronoun's job.

**1. People in general.** The generic proper: *A person can only take so much.* **2. The veiled first person.** The speaker, at one remove and with a complaint attached: *A person could starve around here.* **3. The rhetorical question.** *What's a person supposed to do?*

1. *A person* can only take so much of that.
2. It's the kind of job that wears *a person* out.
3. What's *a person* supposed to do with a bill like this?
4. *A person's* word ought to count for something. (the possessive, spelled with an apostrophe)
5. *A guy* could get used to living like this. (informal, and still male to many hearers)
6. *A fella* can't get a decent cup of coffee anywhere in this town. (folksy; older rural, Southern, and Midwestern speech, and dialect writing)
7. The formal equivalent is *one* can only take so much; the ordinary spoken one is *you* can only take so much.

### deponent, affiant

**Pronunciation:** *deponent* /dɪˈpoʊnənt/ &middot; *di-POH-nunt* &middot; accent on the second syllable, the first vowel reduced to /ɪ/ or /ə/ and the last to /ə/; ✗ *DEE-po-nent* is a spelling pronunciation. *affiant* /əˈfaɪənt/ *uh-FY-unt* has three syllables with the accent on the second and a weak /ənt/ at the end, not ✗ *AF-fee-ant*. Neither word has a weak form in the sentence: used bare it opens its clause and carries the accent. The frozen closing keeps the archaic *sayeth* /ˈseɪəθ/ *SAY-uth* and *saith* /sɛθ/ *SETH*
**Русский:** юр. заявитель, дающий показания под присягой — о себе самом в третьем лице
**Type:** legal noun used bare as an impersonal self-reference — a third-person noun standing in for *I* &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; possessive *deponent's*, *affiant's* &middot; **Register:** legal only, and formulaic even there. An American meets it in an affidavit, a sworn declaration, a deposition transcript, or a notarized statement, and almost always in one of two frames — the opening *Deponent says that…* and the closing *Further affiant sayeth naught*
**Person / number:** grammatically **third person singular**, with a singular verb and third-person concord, although what it denotes is the first person — the man or woman swearing the statement.
**Refers to:** the individual making the sworn statement, and nobody else. The pronominal use is the **bare** one, with no article: *Deponent states…* Put the article back and it is an ordinary noun phrase pointing at a party from outside — *the deponent was cross-examined for six hours* is what the other side's brief says about him.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Deponent states that…*, *Affiant is over the age of eighteen*, *Further deponent saith not.* ✗ *Deponent state.* Because the grammar is third person, the reflexive is *himself* or *herself* and never ✗ *myself*: *Deponent examined the records himself.*
**Pattern:** *Deponent* [singular verb] *that* [clause] &middot; *Affiant* [singular verb] &middot; *Affiant further states that* [clause] &middot; the closings *Further affiant sayeth naught* and *Further deponent saith not* &middot; *deponent's* [noun] &middot; with the article, and no longer pronominal: *the deponent*, *the affiant*
**Contrast:** the whole pronominal effect sits in the missing article. *Deponent says* is the writer referring to himself in the third person; *the deponent says* is somebody else referring to him. Between the two words: **affiant** is the American term and goes with *affidavit* — the person who swears a written statement — while **deponent** covers that and also the witness giving oral testimony at a deposition, and it is the older word and the commoner one in British and older American practice. Against the neighbors that do the same job in other rooms: *the undersigned* is the contract's version and means whoever signed it, [the present writer](#the-present-writer-the-present-author) is the scholar's, and [yours truly](#yours-truly) is the joke. Say the modern practice plainly — federal court forms and the plain-language drafting movement write **I**: *I am over eighteen and competent to testify to the matters stated below.* Two traps. *Sayeth* and *saith* are frozen archaisms and take no half-modernizing: ✗ *further affiant says naught* is neither one thing nor the other. And *deponent* has an unrelated life in grammar books, where a **deponent verb** is a Latin or Greek verb passive in form and active in meaning — no connection to this word beyond the shared root.
**Variants:** *affiant* (the American affidavit term), *declarant* (the modern federal equivalent, in a declaration under penalty of perjury), *the deponent* and *the affiant* (with the article — ordinary noun phrases, not this use)

'I, under oath' — the person swearing a statement, named in the third person by the statement itself.

**1. The opening frame.** *Deponent says that he is the owner of the vehicle described above.* **2. The closing formula.** *Further affiant sayeth naught* — a fossil meaning "that is all I have to say," and the last line of countless affidavits. **3. With the article, and not this use.** *The deponent contradicted himself twice* is somebody else talking about him.

1. *Deponent* says that he is over the age of eighteen and competent to testify to the matters set out below.
2. *Affiant* further states that the signature on the second page is her own.
3. Further *affiant* sayeth naught. (the closing fossil; also written *Further deponent saith not*)
4. *Deponent* examined the payroll records himself before signing this statement. (third-person reflexive, because the grammar is third person)
5. The clerk would not accept the form because *affiant* had not initialed the correction on page three.
6. The modern federal version drops the device altogether: I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct.
7. Put the article back and the pronoun is gone: *the deponent* was cross-examined for six hours. (somebody else describing him)

### every Tom, Dick, and Harry

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈtɑm ˈdɪk ən ˈhɛri/ &middot; *ev-ree-TOM-DIK-un-HAIR-ee* &middot; each of the three names carries a beat and the heaviest falls on *Harry*; the *and* is **only ever weak** — /ən/ or a syllabic /n̩/, never the full /ænd/ — which makes the phrase a syllable shorter than it looks on the page. *every* is two syllables in ordinary American speech, [ˈɛvri], not three. In *any Tom, Dick, or Harry* the *or* is the weak /ɚ/
**Русский:** всякий встречный-поперечный — люди без разбора; всегда пренебрежительно
**Type:** fixed generic noun phrase used as a pronoun — a quasi-pronoun, not a pronoun proper &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; the possessive *every Tom, Dick, and Harry's* is grammatical and so ungainly that writers rewrite around it &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, and **always disparaging**. It says that the people in question are undistinguished and that there are far more of them than anybody wanted; there is no complimentary use of it and no neutral one
**Person / number:** third person **singular** for agreement, in spite of naming three people — *every* is a distributive singular and governs the whole phrase.
**Refers to:** people in general, taken indiscriminately — "anybody at all, and more of them than there ought to be." It never points at three particular men named Tom, Dick, and Harry, and it never points at one person.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Every Tom, Dick, and Harry has an opinion about it*, ✗ *Every Tom, Dick, and Harry have an opinion.* That is the ordinary behavior of *every*, which stays singular however long the list behind it — compare *every man, woman, and child was accounted for.* The *any* variant is singular too: *any Tom, Dick, or Harry who walks in off the street.*
**Pattern:** *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* [singular verb] &middot; [verb or preposition] *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* &middot; *any Tom, Dick, or Harry* [relative clause] &middot; the licensing frame *not just any Tom, Dick, or Harry* &middot; the bare *Tom, Dick, and Harry* after *all*
**Contrast:** against the other placeholder names the difference is one against many. [Joe Blow and John Q. Public](#joe-blow-joe-schmo-john-q-public) name **one** ordinary person; *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* names **the crowd**, and the crowd is the complaint. Against neutral [everybody](07-indefinite-compound.md#everybody): the meaning is the same and the attitude is not — *everybody has an opinion* reports, *every Tom, Dick, and Harry has an opinion* sneers. Against [all and sundry](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#all-and-sundry), which is the formal-jocular version of the same idea and manages it without naming anybody. Two labels stated plainly. The names are **male**, all three of them, which puts the phrase in the position generic *he* is in — it asks men's names to stand for people in general, and there is no established feminine counterpart. And the *Dick* of it is now why a fair number of American speakers steer around the phrase in public, the vulgar sense of the word being unavoidable to a modern ear even though it plays no part in the idiom. The neutral rewrites are *everybody*, *anybody at all*, and *all comers*. Mechanics: the names keep their capitals, the commas are the ordinary commas of a list, and the serial comma before *and* is the commoner spelling, though house styles that drop the serial comma elsewhere drop it here too — *every Tom, Dick and Harry* is not an error.
**Variants:** *any Tom, Dick, or Harry*, the bare *Tom, Dick, and Harry* after *all*, and the spellings without the serial comma — *every Tom, Dick and Harry*, *any Tom, Dick or Harry*

'Anybody at all, and far too many of them' — three men's names doing a pronoun's work, and doing it with contempt.

**1. The crowd complaint.** The core use: something once restricted is now open to anyone, and the speaker minds. *Every Tom, Dick, and Harry has a podcast now.* **2. The licensing frame.** *Not just any Tom, Dick, or Harry* — used to insist that a qualification is required after all. **3. Frozen, and not about anybody named Tom.** The three names cannot be swapped for others and leave the idiom standing.

1. *Every Tom, Dick, and Harry* has an opinion about the school board these days.
2. Once the gate code got out, *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* in the county was parking in that lot.
3. You can't hand the master key to *any Tom, Dick, or Harry* who fills out a form.
4. They are not going to hire just *any Tom, Dick, or Harry* for a job that needs a clearance.
5. He complains that the certification has been handed to *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* with four hundred dollars and a weekend free.
6. Note the verb: *every Tom, Dick, and Harry* **has** an opinion, never ✗ *have* — *every* keeps the phrase singular.
7. The neutral version is *everybody has an opinion*; this phrase is never the neutral one.

Public](#joe-blow-joe-schmo-john-q-public), [a person, a guy, a fella](#a-person-a-guy-a-fella), [such and such, so-and-so](#such-and-such-so-and-so), [they (generic)](#they-generic), [everybody](07-indefinite-compound.md#everybody), [anybody](07-indefinite-compound.md#anybody), [all and sundry](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#all-and-sundry), [one and all](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one-and-all)

### he (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /hi/ &middot; *HEE* &middot; weak /i/ — the *h* drops whenever *he* is unstressed and not utterance-initial: *is he* [ˈɪzi], *did he* [ˈdɪdi]. The object *him* is /hɪm/ with weak /ɪm/ (*tell him* [ˈtɛlɪm], *get him* [ˈɡɛɾɪm]), and *her*, in the paired *he or she*, is /hɚ/ with weak /ɚ/, so *call her* comes out like *caller*
**Русский:** он — обобщённо о человеке любого пола; устар., вне нормы совр. амер. прозы
**Type:** generic (epicene) pronoun — **dated**, and no longer standard in American edited prose &middot; **Case:** subject; object *him*; standalone possessive *his*; reflexive *himself* &middot; **Register:** the prescribed formal choice into the 1970s; now read as dated and as excluding, and current mainly in older statutes, deeds, and bylaws
**Person / number:** third person singular, masculine in form — and the whole difficulty is that the generic use asks a masculine form to stand for people of any gender.
**Refers to:** a person of unspecified or unknown sex — anyone who fits the description — normally licensed by a singular indefinite antecedent (*anyone*, *a driver*, *each applicant*). Pointing at one identified man is not this word at all but the ordinary personal pronoun [he](01-personal.md#he).
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *he is*, *he has*, *he does*. ✗ *he are*. The generic use changes nothing about agreement; what it changes is who the sentence sounds like it is about.
**Pattern:** [singular indefinite] … *he* [singular verb] — *If anyone calls, he can leave a message* &middot; [verb] *him* &middot; [preposition] *him* &middot; *himself* &middot; the paired *he or she* / *him or her* &middot; the drafting formula *words importing the masculine include the feminine*
**Contrast:** three replacements, in the order an American editor reaches for them. **Singular *they*** is the ordinary modern solution and is standard American English — *If anyone calls, they can leave a message.* **The plural rewrite** removes the problem instead of patching it: *Applicants must list their previous employers.* **The paired *he or she*** is grammatical and tolerable once, unreadable when repeated, and it leaves out people who are neither; see [his or her](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his-or-her). Be precise about what is wrong with generic *he*: it is not a grammatical error, and it is what American style guides prescribed for most of the twentieth century — the objection is a **usage** objection, supported by reading studies in which people picture a man, and the form is now out of step with edited American practice. In an old instrument, leave it and read it as that document's own interpretation clause directs. Against generic [one](#one-generic): the older American pattern switched to *he* after the first *one* (*One should do what he thinks best*), which is this same dated construction in a different frame. Note also the split with the determiner: *he* and *him* are pronouns and belong here, while generic *his* in *every applicant must bring his ID* is a possessive **determiner** and is covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#his).

'Anyone at all, referred to as though male' — the traditional generic, now replaced by singular *they*.

**1. Generic with an indefinite antecedent.** *If anyone calls, tell him I'll be back.* — dated; now *tell them*. **2. Generic after *one*.** The older American practice: *One should do what he thinks best.* **3. Legal and archaic survivals.** Statutes, deeds, and bylaws drafted before the 1980s, usually with an interpretation clause attached. **4. Frozen proverbs.** *He who hesitates is lost* — quoted, not produced.

1. If anyone calls, tell *him* I'll be back at two. (dated; now *tell them*)
2. *He* who hesitates is lost. (a frozen proverb, quoted rather than produced)
3. Every applicant is entitled to a hearing before *he* is dismissed. (dated; now *before they are dismissed*)
4. No employee may authorize a payment to *himself*. (the generic reflexive; the modern fix is the plural — *employees … themselves*)
5. *One* should do what *he* thinks best. (older American practice after generic *one*; now *what one thinks best*, or *what they think best*)
6. The reader may judge for *himself* whether the argument holds. (older essayistic style; now *readers can judge for themselves*)
7. A witness must answer unless *he* invokes the privilege. (older statutory drafting; modern drafting uses *they*)

### he or she

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhi ɔr ˈʃi/ &middot; *hee-or-SHEE* &middot; the *or* is weak /ɚ/ in ordinary speech, so the phrase runs to [hi ɚ ˈʃi]; the reversed *she or he* is /ˌʃi ɚ ˈhi/. The *h* of *he* survives here because the word carries a beat, unlike the weak /i/ of *is he* [ˈɪzi]. Written *he/she* is read aloud as this phrase, and *s/he* has no spoken form of its own at all
**Русский:** он или она — о человеке неизвестного пола; книжн., ныне звучит канцелярски
**Type:** paired (coordinate) generic pronoun — epicene &middot; **Case:** subject; object *him or her*; possessive determiner *his or her*, standalone possessive *his or hers*; reflexive *himself or herself* &middot; **Register:** formal and careful — the prescribed repair for generic *he* from roughly 1980 to 2010, still the house style in some legal and technical drafting, and now read as dated and bureaucratic beside singular *they*
**Person / number:** third person singular throughout — *he or she* is **one** person, not two, whatever the coordination looks like.
**Refers to:** a single person of unspecified or unknown sex, licensed by a singular indefinite or role-based antecedent (*each applicant*, *anyone*, *the tenant*). It cannot point at an identified person, and it names two sexes, so it does not cover people who are neither.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *If a customer calls, he or she is entitled to a refund.* ✗ *he or she are.* The reflexive is the paired *himself or herself*, and the possessive determiner is *his or her*, which must precede a noun and therefore belongs with the determiners.
**Pattern:** [singular indefinite antecedent] … *he or she* [singular verb] &middot; the reversed *she or he* &middot; written *he/she*, *(s)he*, *s/he* &middot; object *him or her* &middot; *his or her* [noun], *his or hers* standing alone &middot; reflexive *himself or herself* &middot; the drafting tag *he or she, as the case may be*
**Contrast:** grammatical, inclusive of two sexes, and unreadable in bulk — the objection is never to one use but to the fourth in a paragraph: *Each student must bring his or her laptop so that he or she can upload his or her work.* Three replacements, in the order an American editor reaches for them. **Singular *they*** is standard American English, entered in Merriam-Webster and accepted by AP and Chicago — *If a customer calls, they are entitled to a refund.* **The plural rewrite** is usually better than either — *Customers are entitled to a refund.* **The paired form** is what remains when a document must spell both out. Say the coverage limit plainly: *he or she* names two sexes and leaves out people who are neither, which is the second reason edited practice has moved on. Case works here as in every coordination — subject *he or she*, object [him or her](#him-or-her): ✗ *Give it to he or she*, ✗ *between he or she and the landlord*. And do not mix strategies inside one sentence: ✗ *If anyone calls, take their number and tell him or her I'll ring back.*
**Variants:** *she or he*, *he/she*, *she/he*, *s/he*, *(s)he*, and in older drafting *he or she, as the case may be*

'One person, sex unstated' — the paired form that replaced generic *he* and has itself been replaced by singular *they*.

**1. With an indefinite antecedent.** The core use: *Each applicant should bring proof that he or she is a resident.* **2. Legal and procedural drafting.** Where a document sets out to name both sexes explicitly, often with an interpretation clause behind it. **3. Reversed for balance.** *She or he* — used to keep the feminine form from always coming second, and no less correct.

1. If a customer is unhappy with the order, *he or she* can return it within thirty days. (now ordinarily *they can return it*)
2. Each participant will be told privately how *he or she* scored on the pretest.
3. The applicant must sign the form *himself or herself* in the presence of a notary. (the paired reflexive)
4. *She or he* will be assigned a mentor for the first year. (reversed order, used for balance)
5. Ask the patient whether *he or she* has taken anything for the pain. (older clinical style; current guidance prefers *they*)
6. Any employee who witnesses an injury must report it to *his or her* supervisor. (the possessive determiner — a determiner, not a pronoun)
7. Each student must bring *his or her* laptop so that *he or she* can upload *his or her* work. (perfectly grammatical, and exactly why the form fell out of favor)

### him or her

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪm ɔr ˈhɚ/ &middot; *him-or-HER* &middot; all three words have weak forms and all three are used at once in running speech — *him* → /ɪm/, *or* → /ɚ/, *her* → /ɚ/ — so *tell him or her* comes out [ˈtɛl ɪm ɚ ˈhɚ] and, with the last word unaccented too, [ˈtɛl əm ɚ ɚ]. The *h* of *her* returns whenever the word carries the phrase accent. The reversed *her or him* is /ˌhɚ ɔr ˈhɪm/
**Русский:** его или её, ему или ей — объектная форма he or she; книжн., канцелярское
**Type:** paired (coordinate) generic pronoun, object form &middot; **Case:** **object** — the subject form is *he or she*; possessive determiner *his or her*, standalone possessive *his or hers*; reflexive *himself or herself* &middot; **Register:** formal and careful, and dated in exactly the way its subject form is
**Person / number:** third person singular — one person, not two, and no verb agreement of its own.
**Refers to:** the same unspecified single person as [he or she](#he-or-she), standing after a verb or a preposition. Like the subject form it names two sexes and does not cover people who are neither.
**Agreement:** none of its own. It is an object, so the verb in the clause agrees with that clause's own subject: *The clerk **mails** it to him or her*, *The clerks **mail** it to him or her.*
**Pattern:** [verb] *him or her* &middot; [preposition] *him or her* &middot; *give* [something] *to him or her* &middot; reversed *her or him* &middot; written *him/her* &middot; the drafting chain *he or she … him or her … his or her*
**Contrast:** case is the whole entry. *Him or her* is the **object** form and can never be a subject: ✗ *Him or her should sign the form* → *He or she should sign it.* The deletion test settles it as it settles every coordination — take one member out and read what is left: *Give it to **him*** ✓ and *Give it to **her*** ✓, so *Give it to him or her* ✓, while ✗ *Him should sign* exposes the subject error instantly. The same test kills the hypercorrection that goes the other way: ✗ *between he or she and the landlord* → *between **him or her** and the landlord*, because *between* is a preposition and takes the object form, exactly as in *between you and me*. On register, everything at [he or she](#he-or-she) applies unchanged — singular *them* is the ordinary modern choice (*Give it to them*), and the plural rewrite is usually better than either. There is no written blend for this slot: *him/her* exists, ✗ *s/him* does not.
**Variants:** *her or him*, *him/her*, *her/him*

The object half of the paired generic — *he or she* after a verb or a preposition.

1. If a client asks for the file, give it to *him or her* the same day. (now ordinarily *give it to them*)
2. Notify *him or her* in writing at least ten days before the hearing.
3. The award will be presented to *him or her* at the June meeting.
4. Ask *him or her* to initial the second page and return it.
5. Any dispute between the tenant and *him or her* goes to arbitration. (object of a preposition)
6. Whoever is on call should keep the pager with *him or her* overnight. (modern drafting: *with them*)
7. ✗ *Him or her* should sign first → *He or she* should sign first. (the case error the pair invites)

### ibid.

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪbɪd/ &middot; *IB-id* &middot; two syllables, accent on the first, and the final *d* is fully released — the period on the page stops the spelling, not the word. The unabbreviated *ibidem* is /ɪˈbaɪdɛm/ or /ɪˈbɪdɛm/ *ib-EYE-dem*, accent on the second syllable, and is essentially never said aloud. There is no weak form: this is a written abbreviation, spoken only when somebody is reading a footnote out loud, and most Americans reading one out loud simply say *same source*
**Русский:** там же — в сноске: тот же источник (и та же страница), что в предыдущей ссылке
**Type:** scholarly citation pro-form — Latin *ibidem*, "in the same place," standing in the slot a citation would fill &middot; **Case:** none — invariant, and it never inflects &middot; **Register:** scholarly and formal, and narrower now than it once was. An American meets it in the footnotes of a humanities book, in a Chicago-style notes-and-bibliography paper, and in older scholarship of every kind; the Chicago Manual of Style now prefers a shortened citation in its place, and American legal writing uses *Id.* and never *ibid.*
**Person / number:** third person, with **no number of its own** — it inherits whatever the preceding citation had, one work or one volume out of many.
**Refers to:** **the source cited immediately before it**, and nothing else. It is strictly anaphoric and strictly adjacent: the note above it must name exactly one work, or *ibid.* has nothing to point at. Standing bare it means the same work **and** the same page; with a number after it, the same work at a new page.
**Agreement:** none — it governs no verb and enters into no concord. It is a pro-form for a noun phrase, not a subject.
**Pattern:** *Ibid.* — same work, same page &middot; *Ibid.*, [page number] — same work, new page &middot; capitalized at the head of a note, lowercase inside a running sentence &middot; the Bluebook equivalent *Id.* and *Id.* at [page] &middot; the abandoned neighbors *op. cit.* and *loc. cit.*
**Contrast:** three Latin shorteners, pointing in three different directions. ***Ibid.*** points at the **work just cited** — same source, immediately above. ***[Idem](#idem)*** points at the **author just cited**, with a different work about to follow. ***Op. cit.*** points at a work cited **somewhere earlier**, not necessarily just above, which is exactly why it failed: the reader has to go hunting, and Chicago has dropped it. Against [q.v.](05-relative.md#qv-quod-vide--which-see), which points **sideways** — at another entry in the book you are holding — rather than backward at a source. Two rules produce most of the errors. *Ibid.* cannot follow a note that cited **two or more** works, because there is then no single antecedent for it. And it does not survive editing: insert one note above a run of them and every one now cites the wrong book, which is the practical reason copyeditors replace them with short-form citations. Mechanics: the period is part of the abbreviation and is not optional (✗ *Ibid* bare), current Chicago sets the word in roman rather than italic, and the capital belongs only where a note begins.
**Variants:** *ibidem* (the unabbreviated Latin), *Id.* (the American legal equivalent), and the abandoned *op. cit.* and *loc. cit.*

'The same source, just above' — a footnote word that stands in for a citation instead of repeating it.

**1. Same work, same page.** Bare *Ibid.* repeats the whole of the note above it. **2. Same work, new page.** *Ibid.*, 214 — the commonest form by a wide margin. **3. Where an American actually meets it.** The notes of a humanities book, a Chicago-style paper, and older scholarship; a legal brief uses *Id.* instead, and a science paper uses neither, its citations being numbered.

1. Note 12 reads simply *Ibid.*, 47 — the same book as note 11, at a different page.
2. A bare *Ibid.* with no page number means the same work and the same page as the note above it.
3. If note 8 cites two different books, note 9 cannot say *Ibid.* — there is nothing single for it to point at.
4. The copyeditor replaced every *Ibid.* in the chapter with a short-form citation, and the notes stopped being unreadable.
5. Insert one new note above a run of *ibid.* references and every one of them now cites the wrong book.
6. American legal writing does the same job with *Id.*, which the Bluebook prescribes and which no brief goes three pages without.
7. Read aloud in a seminar, *ibid.* comes out as "same source" — nobody pronounces the Latin.

### idem

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaɪdɛm/, also /ˈɪdɛm/ &middot; *EYE-dem* &middot; accent on the first syllable, both vowels full, no weak form. The abbreviation *id.* is /ɪd/ *ID*, one syllable, and in American legal writing it is far commoner than the full word — a lawyer reading a citation aloud says the two letters, or simply *same*
**Русский:** он же, тот же автор — в библиографии; в амер. юр. сокр. Id. — «там же»
**Type:** scholarly and legal citation pro-form — Latin *idem*, "the same," standing in the slot an author's name would fill &middot; **Case:** none — invariant in English use. Latin inflected it (*eadem* feminine, *eidem* plural) and English scholarship abandoned the inflection, so ✗ *eadem* for a woman author now reads as an affectation rather than as precision &middot; **Register:** scholarly in its full form and legal in its abbreviation. An American meets *idem* spelled out in humanities bibliographies and older footnotes, and meets *Id.* in every American brief, memo, and judicial opinion, where the Bluebook makes it the standard short form
**Person / number:** third person, with no number of its own — it inherits whatever it points back at.
**Refers to:** **the same author** as the citation immediately before, in humanities practice, with a different work about to be named. In American legal practice the abbreviation *Id.* refers instead to the **whole preceding authority** — the source, and the same page unless a new one is given.
**Agreement:** none — it governs no verb.
**Pattern:** *Idem*, [title], [publication details] — same author, new work &middot; *Id.* — same authority, same page &middot; *Id.* at [page] — same authority, new page &middot; capitalized at the head of a note, lowercase inside a running sentence
**Contrast:** this is the entry where two traditions use one Latin word for two different jobs, and the difference is worth stating flatly. In **humanities** citation *idem* means the same **author**, and something new follows it: *Idem, The Later Essays, 1994.* In **American legal** citation *Id.* means the same **authority** altogether — which is the job [ibid.](#ibid) does in Chicago style. So a lawyer's *Id.* and a historian's *ibid.* are equivalents, while a historian's *idem* is a different word doing a different thing, and reading a brief with the humanities sense in mind will mislead you on every page. Against *ibid.* directly, the split is **work** against **author**. Chicago now discourages *idem* on the plain ground that writing the author's name again costs three words and confuses nobody. Mechanics: *idem* is a complete Latin word and takes **no** period (✗ *idem.*), while *id.* is an abbreviation and **must** have one (✗ *id* bare); neither is italicized in current Chicago style, though older scholarship italicized both.
**Variants:** *id.* (the American legal abbreviation), *eadem* and *ead.* (the Latin feminine, now rare and generally avoided), *the same* (the plain English equivalent)

'The same one, just above' — a footnote word standing in for an author's name, or, in a brief, for the whole citation before it.

**1. Same author, new work.** The humanities use: *Idem, The Later Essays* follows a note that has already named the author. **2. The abbreviation in a brief.** *Id.* means the same authority, and the same page unless a page follows — the commonest word in a brief's citations. **3. Not an abbreviation.** *Idem* is a whole Latin word and takes no period; *id.* is the abbreviation and takes one.

1. The bibliography gives the first title in full and the second as *Idem*, *The Later Essays*, 1994.
2. *Idem* points at the author and *ibid.* at the work — that one line settles most of the confusion.
3. Every other citation in the brief is *Id.* at 412, which is how American legal writing avoids repeating a case name.
4. Chicago now advises writing the author's name out again rather than reaching for *idem*.
5. *Idem* is a Latin word and takes no period; ✗ *idem.* is an error no spell-checker will flag.
6. A note that reads *Id.* with nothing after it means the same source and the same page as the note above.
7. Older scholarship wrote *eadem* for a woman author, and current practice uses *idem* for everybody.

(quod vide) / which see](05-relative.md#qv-quod-vide--which-see), [the present writer, the present author](#the-present-writer-the-present-author), [the aforesaid, the aforementioned](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-aforesaid-the-aforementioned), [the same](04-demonstrative.md#the-same), [the same](10-archaic-dialectal.md#the-same)

### Joe Blow, Joe Schmo, John Q. Public

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒoʊ ˈbloʊ/ &middot; *joe-BLOH* &middot; both words carry a beat and the heavier one falls on *Blow*. *Joe Schmo* /ˌdʒoʊ ˈʃmoʊ/ *joe-SHMOH*, spelled *Schmoe* and *Shmoe* as well, with the Yiddish *shm-* onset that no native English word has. *John Q. Public* /ˌdʒɑn kju ˈpʌblɪk/ *john-cue-PUB-lik* — the middle initial is said as the letter and is never expanded into a name. *the average Joe* /ði ˌævrɪdʒ ˈdʒoʊ/ *thee-AV-rij-JOH*, with *the* in its pre-vowel weak form /ði/ and *average* ordinarily two syllables, [ˈævrɪdʒ]. None of the names reduces: a placeholder name still has to be heard as a name
**Русский:** разг. рядовой обыватель, простой смертный — человек из толпы; все формы муж. рода
**Type:** placeholder proper name used as a generic — a quasi-pronoun, not a pronoun proper &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; possessive *Joe Blow's*, *John Q. Public's*, *the average Joe's* &middot; **Register:** informal throughout, and each member carries its own attitude. *The average Joe* is the mildest and the only one an American is likely to write in ordinary prose; *Joe Blow* is dismissive; *Joe Schmo* is the most dismissive of the set, its *shm-* being the pattern English borrowed from Yiddish for making a word contemptible; *John Q. Public* is civic and journalistic — the ordinary citizen as the government or the press addresses him — and now reads as mid-century
**Person / number:** third person **singular**, with a singular verb. *The average Joe* behaves like the ordinary noun phrase it is and pluralizes freely (*average Joes*); the bare names do not (✗ *Joe Blows*).
**Refers to:** **the ordinary, unremarkable person** — people in general, seen as one representative specimen. It is not a stand-in for a particular person whose name happens to be unknown, which is a different job done by a different set of names. All four forms are **male**.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *Joe Blow doesn't read the fine print*, *The average Joe has no idea what any of that costs.* ✗ *The average Joe have.* The follow-up pronoun is *he*, and because these are men's names singular *they* does not repair them the way it repairs generic *he* — the fix is to rewrite with *the average person* or *most people*.
**Pattern:** *Joe Blow* [singular verb] &middot; *some Joe Blow* / *any Joe Blow off the street* &middot; *John Q. Public* [singular verb] &middot; *the average Joe* [singular verb] &middot; *your average Joe* — with the informal ethical *your* &middot; possessive *the average Joe's* [noun]
**Contrast:** three kinds of placeholder name, and only one of them is a generic. **The generic** — *Joe Blow*, *Joe Schmo*, *John Q. Public*, *the average Joe* — stands for **people in general**, and is what this entry covers. **The unknown-identity placeholder** — *John Doe*, *Jane Doe*, *Richard Roe* — stands for **one particular person** whose name the court or the hospital does not have; it is a name sitting in a name's slot, not a generic, and this file says so already at [such and such, so-and-so](#such-and-such-so-and-so). **The forgotten name** — *what's-his-name*, *what's-her-name* — stands for a person the speaker knows and cannot retrieve. Against [a person, a guy, a fella](#a-person-a-guy-a-fella): identical work done with a common noun instead of a name, which is exactly what leaves *a person* usable of anybody while *Joe Blow* is not. Against [every Tom, Dick, and Harry](#every-tom-dick-and-harry): one ordinary person against a crowd of them. Label the gender plainly — all four are male in form, *Jane Q. Public* exists and is rare, and there is no established female counterpart in general use, so none of these is a safe all-purpose generic in writing; the neutral rewrites are *the average person*, *the ordinary reader*, *most people*, and *the general public*. Mechanics: the names keep their capitals, *the average Joe* keeps the capital on *Joe* alone, and ✗ *the average joe* is an error no spell-checker will catch.
**Variants:** *the average Joe*, *your average Joe*, *Joe Schmoe* and *Joe Shmoe* (spellings), *Joe Sixpack* (political and disparaging), *Jane Q. Public*, *the man on the street*, *the average American*, and the British *Joe Bloggs* and *Fred Bloggs*

'The ordinary person, given a name' — a proper name pressed into service as a generic.

**1. The ordinary person.** The core use: *the average Joe has no idea what a deductible is.* **2. Anybody at all, dismissively.** *Some Joe Blow off the street can walk in and sign for it.* **3. The citizen.** *John Q. Public* in journalism and civics — the public as the institution addresses it. **4. Not the unknown-identity placeholder.** *John Doe* is one particular person without a name; *Joe Blow* is nobody in particular.

1. *The average Joe* has no idea what any of this costs until the bill shows up.
2. Some *Joe Blow* off the street can walk in, sign one form, and take the whole file with him.
3. The rule was written for lawyers, not for *John Q. Public*.
4. Try explaining a deductible to *your average Joe* and see how far you get. (the informal ethical *your*)
5. He talks as though every customer were *Joe Schmo* who has never seen a contract before.
6. *The average Joe's* paycheck has not moved in real terms in twenty years. (the possessive)
7. Keep it apart from *John Doe*, which names one particular person nobody can identify — a placeholder for an identity, not a generic.

### one (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn/ &middot; *WUN* &middot; no weak form — generic *one* keeps at least a secondary accent, which is part of why it sounds formal. Homophone of *won*
**Русский:** обобщённое «человек», безличный оборот — книжн.; в амер. англ. звучит чопорно
**Type:** generic (impersonal) pronoun &middot; **Case:** one form for subject and object; possessive *one's*; reflexive *oneself* &middot; **Register:** formal, and in American English easily too formal — it can read as stuffy or affected
**Person / number:** grammatically third person singular; semantically it takes in the speaker and the hearer both — "people in general, myself among them."
**Refers to:** people in general, never a particular individual, and it can never take an antecedent: ✗ *Maria said one was tired.* It is also not a substitute for a noun already mentioned — that is the prop-word *one* (*the red one*), a different word.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *one is*, *one has*, *one does*, *one doesn't*. ✗ *one are*, ✗ *one have*.
**Pattern:** *one* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *one* &middot; [preposition] *one* &middot; *one's* [noun] &middot; *oneself* &middot; the fixed frames *one would think*, *one never knows*, *if one may say so*
**Contrast:** the transatlantic split is in the **follow-up pronoun**. Traditional American practice switched to *he/his/himself* after the first *one* — *One should do what he thinks best* — and that is what older American style guides prescribe; it now reads as both dated and pointedly gendered (see [he (generic)](#he-generic)). British practice repeats *one* (*One should do what one thinks best*), and current American editing has largely moved to that pattern or has dodged the construction altogether. What you must not do is mix the two inside a passage, or slide from *one* to *you* (✗ *One should read the fine print before you sign*). Against generic [you](#you-generic): same meaning, opposite register — *you* is what Americans say, *one* is what they write when the tone is elevated. Generic *one* is also distinct from the prop-word [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one) (*the ones on the left*) and from the numeral *one*, neither of which is generic.

'People in general, myself included' — states something as true of anybody in the situation.

**1. People in general.** The core use, and the formal counterpart of generic *you*: *One cannot be too careful.* **2. I, at a polite distance.** In the mouth of a public figure, *one* is a modest disguise for *I* (*One does what one can*), which is exactly why it can sound arch.

1. *One* cannot be too careful with contracts like this.
2. *One* does what *one* can.
3. It is the kind of book *one* reads twice.
4. *One* never knows how these things will turn out.
5. If *one* is going to complain, *one* should at least read the policy first.
6. The rules make it hard for *one* to plan more than a year ahead.
7. *One* should keep *one's* receipts until the warranty expires.

### oneself (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnˈsɛlf/ &middot; *wun-SELF* &middot; accent on *-self*; unstressed the first syllable reduces toward [wən]
**Русский:** себя, себе — возвратная форма обобщённого one; книжн.
**Type:** reflexive form of generic *one* &middot; **Case:** reflexive &middot; **Register:** formal
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant, gender-neutral. There is no plural: ✗ *oneselves*.
**Refers to:** generic [one](#one-generic), in the same clause. It requires that antecedent — a named person cannot have it (✗ *Maria hurt oneself*).
**Agreement:** no verb agreement of its own; the verb agrees with *one*, which is singular.
**Pattern:** *one* [verb] *oneself* &middot; [preposition] *oneself* &middot; *by oneself* &middot; *to find oneself* [verb-ing] &middot; *for oneself*
**Contrast:** *oneself* is to generic *one* what *yourself* is to generic *you* and *themselves* to generic *they*. Older American prose writes *himself* after generic *one* (see [he (generic)](#he-generic)); British and current American prose write *oneself*, and *oneself* is now the safer choice, since the generic masculine reads as excluding. **Nonstandard neighbors, labeled:** the vernacular reflexives *hisself* and *theirselves* are **nonstandard** — general in Southern, Appalachian, and rural white vernaculars and in African American English, and more regular than the standard [himself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#himself) and [themselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#themselves) they replace — and they are entered at [hisself](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#hisself) and [theirselves](03-reflexive-reciprocal.md#theirselves); there is no vernacular counterpart to *oneself*, because *one* is a written-register word to begin with. Mind the spelling split: ***oneself*** is the pronoun (*one should not take oneself too seriously*), while ***one's self*** is an ordinary noun phrase about identity (*a lifetime spent constructing one's self*). And like every reflexive, it needs its antecedent in the **same clause** — ✗ *One hoped that the committee would approve oneself.*

The reflexive that goes with generic *one* — the generic person acting on the generic person.

1. *One* should not take *oneself* too seriously.
2. It is difficult to explain *oneself* under that kind of pressure.
3. There is a real satisfaction in doing it *oneself*.
4. *One* can easily talk *oneself* out of a perfectly good idea.
5. Living by *oneself* takes some getting used to.
6. *One* finds *oneself* agreeing with him against *one's* better judgment.

### s/he

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhi ɔr ˈʃi/, also /ˌʃi ɔr ˈhi/ &middot; *hee-or-SHEE* &middot; a spelling rather than a word: it has no pronunciation of its own, and a reader who meets it aloud says *he or she*, *she or he*, or — reading around it — *they*. There is no weak form because there is nothing of its own to reduce; the *or* in whatever the reader substitutes is the usual weak /ɚ/
**Русский:** он/она — письменный гибрид she и he; только подлежащее, канцелярско-анкетное
**Type:** written epicene pronoun — an orthographic blend of *she* and *he* &middot; **Case:** **subject only.** No object or possessive form is built the same way: ✗ *s/him*, ✗ *s/his*. A writer who opens with *s/he* has to finish with *him or her* and *his or her*, and that asymmetry is one reason the form never settled &middot; **Register:** written only, and institutional — intake forms, HR and school policies, social-work and education prose, and textbooks of the 1970s through the 1990s. Never spoken, and now largely displaced by singular *they*
**Person / number:** third person singular, with a singular verb.
**Refers to:** a single person of unspecified sex, with an indefinite or role-based antecedent (*the applicant*, *the client*, *each student*). Like *he or she* it names two sexes and does not cover people who are neither.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *If the client cancels, s/he forfeits the deposit.* ✗ *s/he are.*
**Pattern:** *s/he* [singular verb] &middot; the variants *(s)he* and *he/she* in the same slot &middot; object *him/her*, possessive *his/her* &middot; the standard frame: [role noun] … *s/he* [modal] [verb]
**Contrast:** three written shortcuts that differ only in typography. ***S/he*** puts *she* first and can be read either way. ***(s)he*** treats the *s* as optional and so leaves *he* standing underneath, which is precisely the complaint against it. ***He/she*** spells both out and is the least ambiguous and the least readable. All three are **subject-only**, all three stop a reader who tries to say them, and the Chicago Manual of Style and most American editors advise against every one. The live replacements are the ones listed at [he (generic)](#he-generic): **singular *they***, which is standard American English; the **plural rewrite**, usually the best of the three; and the spelled-out [he or she](#he-or-she) where a document must name both sexes. Do not mix the shortcuts with anything else in a passage: ✗ *If a student misses the deadline, s/he must email their advisor.* And do not confuse the slash of *s/he* with the slash of *and/or* — this one joins two spellings of one word, not two options.
**Variants:** *(s)he*, *he/she*, *she/he*, with the object *him/her* and the possessive *his/her*

'He or she,' squeezed into one written form — a subject-only spelling that never learned how to be said aloud.

1. If the applicant is under eighteen, *s/he* must have a parent or guardian co-sign the form.
2. The tenant must give thirty days' notice before *s/he* vacates the unit. (1980s drafting; current drafting says *before they vacate*)
3. Each participant is asked at intake what *s/he* hopes to get out of the program.
4. The staff handbook uses *(s)he* throughout, which quietly leaves *he* standing under the parentheses.
5. The old clause read "he/she shall notify the department in writing"; the revision reads "they shall notify the department in writing."
6. Read aloud at orientation, *s/he* comes out as *he or she* every time — which is the argument for writing it that way to begin with.

### such and such, so-and-so

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *such-un-SUCH* &middot; the *and* is **only ever weak** — /ən/ or a syllabic /n̩/, never the full /ænd/ — which is why the phrase is shorter than it looks on the page. *so-and-so* is /ˈsoʊ ən ˌsoʊ/ *SOH-un-soh*, accent on the first *so*, with the same weak *and*; the possessive *so-and-so's* adds /z/, and the insult plural *so-and-sos* takes /z/ too
**Русский:** то-то и то-то, такой-то — намеренная замена имени или значения; so-and-so — о человеке
**Type:** placeholder pronouns — stand-ins for a name or a value the speaker declines to supply &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; possessive *so-and-so's* &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and in explanatory writing; *so-and-so* for an unnamed person is informal, and *so-and-so* as a mild insult (*the old so-and-so*) is informal, euphemistic, and dated — an American now meets that sense mostly from older speakers and in mid-century writing
**Person / number:** third person singular. *So-and-so* is the only one with a plural, and only in the insult sense: *those so-and-sos*.
**Refers to:** **nothing in particular, on purpose.** *Such and such* holds the place of a thing, a value, a date, or an action — "whatever it turns out to be." *So-and-so* holds the place of a **person**: one whose name is beside the point of the example, or one the speaker will not name.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *So-and-so calls, and you take a message*, *Such and such happens, and the claim is denied.* ✗ *So-and-so call.*
**Pattern:** *do such and such* &middot; *if you* [verb] *such and such, then* [clause] &middot; *talk to so-and-so in* [department] &middot; *Mr./Ms. So-and-so* &middot; *so-and-so's* [noun] &middot; the insult frames *the old so-and-so*, *you little so-and-so*
**Contrast:** the **slot test** separates the pronoun from the determiner here exactly as it does everywhere else in these two dictionaries. Standing alone it is a pronoun — *he claimed he had done such and such*; in front of a noun it is a **determiner** and belongs to [such-and-such (a)](../../09-determiners/catalog/09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a) — *such-and-such a date*, *such-and-such an amount*. Against the neighbors: *what's-his-name* and *what's-her-name* stand in for a name the speaker has genuinely forgotten, where *so-and-so* stands in for one that was never worth supplying; *thingamajig* and *whatchamacallit* fill in an **object's** name; [whatnot](07-indefinite-compound.md#whatnot) and *and such* close a list rather than filling a slot; and the legal placeholders *John Doe* and *Jane Roe* are proper names, not pronouns — as against the generic placeholder names [Joe Blow, Joe Schmo, and John Q. Public](#joe-blow-joe-schmo-john-q-public), which do stand for people in general. Against demonstrative [such](04-demonstrative.md#such): *Such was the outcome* points back at something real, while *such and such* points at a blank nobody has filled. Spelling: *so-and-so* is always hyphenated, and *such and such* is written open or hyphenated (*such-and-such*), neither being an error.
**Variants:** *such-and-such* (hyphenated spelling), *thus and so* (dated), *so and so* (rare open spelling)

A deliberate blank — the speaker names the slot instead of the value, for a thing (*such and such*) or for a person (*so-and-so*).

**1. The worked example.** Walking somebody through a procedure whose actual values do not matter yet: *You do such and such, and if it fails they do such and such.* **2. The unnamed person.** *Call so-and-so in accounting* — the name exists and is beside the point. **3. The name withheld.** The speaker knows it and will not say it. **4. The mild insult.** *The old so-and-so never paid me back* — a euphemism standing in for a stronger word.

1. They tell you to fill out the form, do *such and such*, and call back in a week.
2. If you do *such and such* on the site, the system flags the account automatically.
3. Talk to *so-and-so* in payroll — she's the one who actually fixes these.
4. He's forever dropping names: lunch with *so-and-so*, golf with *so-and-so's* brother. (the possessive)
5. The old *so-and-so* still hasn't paid me back. (the mild insult, standing in for a stronger word)
6. Every one of those letters opens "Dear Mr. *So-and-so*" and then asks for money.
7. Put a noun behind it and it stops being a pronoun: *such-and-such a date*, *such-and-such an amount* are determiners.

### the present writer, the present author

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈprɛzənt ˈraɪtɚ/ &middot; *thuh-PREZ-unt-RY-ter* &middot; *the* takes its weak /ðə/ before the consonant of *present* and its other weak form /ði/ before the vowel of *author*, so *the present author* is [ði ˈprɛzənt ˈɔθɚ]; *present* here is the adjective, accented on the first syllable /ˈprɛzənt/, never the verb /prɪˈzɛnt/
**Русский:** автор этих строк, пишущий эти строки — книжн., устар. вместо «я»
**Type:** impersonal self-reference — a third-person noun phrase standing in for *I* &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object; possessive *the present writer's* &middot; **Register:** formal, academic, and dated. An American meets it in older scholarly prose, book reviews, and mid-twentieth-century legal and scientific writing; in new writing it reads as arch or as false modesty, and plain *I* is now accepted by APA, Chicago, and most journals
**Person / number:** grammatically **third person singular** — singular verb, third-person follow-up pronouns — although it denotes the first person, the one doing the writing.
**Refers to:** the author of the text in your hands, and nobody else. The word *present* is carrying the load: it separates the writer of this page from "the author" being discussed on it.
**Agreement:** **singular verb** — *The present writer **has** argued elsewhere that…* ✗ *The present writer have.* Keep the follow-up pronouns in the third person as well; sliding back into the first breaks the device: ✗ *The present writer, in my view, would go further.*
**Pattern:** *the present writer* [singular verb] &middot; *in the present writer's view / opinion / experience* &middot; *it seems to the present writer that* [clause] &middot; the parallel forms *the present author*, *the present reviewer*, *this writer*, *the undersigned* (legal), *your correspondent* (journalism)
**Contrast:** four ways to keep *I* off the page, and they are not equals. **The present writer** is the stiffest, and it puts the writer into the third person outright. **Editorial [we (generic)](#we-generic)** is one writer wearing a plural pronoun (*we show in Section 3*) and is still ordinary in scientific papers. **Generic [one](#one-generic)** is a polite distance rather than a disguise, and in the mouth of a public figure it stands in for *I* (*one does what one can*). **The passive and the impersonal [it](#it-extraposition)** remove the person altogether (*it is argued here that…*) — the formal solution and the classic evasion in the same construction. Against plain *I*: American academic style has come around to it, APA recommends it outright, and *the present writer* now signals age rather than modesty. Three neighbors do adjacent jobs: *the undersigned* is legal boilerplate and means the person who signed, bare [deponent and affiant](#deponent-affiant) do the same office at the head of an affidavit, and jocular [yours truly](#yours-truly) means *me* with a wink. And do not switch frames inside a passage: pick *I*, or the editorial *we*, or the passive, and stay with it.
**Variants:** *the present author*, *the present reviewer*, *this writer*, *this reviewer*, *the undersigned* (legal), *your correspondent* (journalism)

'I, at arm's length' — the writer of the page referring to himself or herself in the third person.

**1. Scholarly self-reference.** *The present writer has argued elsewhere that…* **2. Disambiguation.** In a paper about somebody else's book, *the author* means that book's author and *the present writer* means you — which is the one job the phrase still does well. **3. Modesty and its opposite.** The device read as humble in 1930 and reads as pompous now.

1. *The present writer* has argued elsewhere that the statute was misread from the start.
2. In *the present writer's* view, the second experiment settles the question. (the possessive)
3. It seemed to *the present author* that nobody had gone back to the original source.
4. The author of the memoir was thirty when he wrote it; *the present writer* was not yet born. (the disambiguating use)
5. *The present reviewer* confesses to having enjoyed the first hundred pages and no more.
6. The modern version is simply *I*: I have argued elsewhere that the statute was misread. (accepted by APA, Chicago, and most journals)
7. The impersonal alternative drops the person altogether: it has been argued elsewhere that the statute was misread.

### they (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /ðeɪ/ &middot; *THAY* &middot; weak /ðe/ before a stressed verb, so *they say* runs to [ðe ˈseɪ] and *they're* to [ðɚ]. The object *them* has the weak form /ðəm/ → /əm/ — *make them* [ˈmeɪk əm], written *'em* — and that reduction is ordinary General American, carrying no dialect marking whatever
**Русский:** они — неназванные «люди вообще»; по-русски обычно безлично: «говорят», «пишут»
**Type:** generic (impersonal) pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject; object *them*; possessive determiner *their*, which is a determiner and is entered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#their); standalone possessive *theirs* &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; the formal alternative is the passive
**Person / number:** third person plural in form and in agreement, even though the group it names is entirely unspecified.
**Refers to:** **nobody in particular** — an unnamed, unidentifiable group: the people who know, the people in charge, people generally. Its defining property is that it has **no antecedent and cannot be given one**. If you can say who *they* are, it is not this pronoun.
**Agreement:** **plural verb, always** — *they say*, *they're building*, *they've decided*. ✗ *they says*.
**Pattern:** *they say (that)* [clause] &middot; *they* [plural verb] &middot; *they're* [verb-ing] &middot; formal equivalents: *it is said that*, *reportedly*, *the city says*
**Contrast:** keep three *theys* apart. **Generic *they*** has no antecedent (*They say it's going to rain*). **Singular *they*** does have one and is standard modern American English, including in edited prose (*Someone left their coat*) — it is entered in Merriam-Webster, accepted by the AP Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style, and attested since the fourteenth century, so say it plainly: it is not an error. **Ordinary plural *they*** points at a group already named. The ambiguity between the first and third is real: in *They raised the rates again*, *they* may be the utility company or nobody at all. In formal writing, replace generic *they* with the passive or with a named agent: *They say the bridge will close* → *The bridge is reported to close* / *The city says the bridge will close*. Notice too that an unnamed *they* is a rhetorical move — it asserts a source without supplying one. **Nonstandard:** *them* standing in front of a noun — *them apples*, *them boys* — is not this pronoun and not a pronoun at all; it is a **nonstandard** demonstrative **determiner**, widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech, and the standard form is *those*. Its entry is in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/02-demonstratives.md#them); the weak /əm/ of *I saw 'em* is a separate matter and is standard everywhere. Nonstandard too, and in the other direction: ✗ *they was* and ✗ *they says* are the vernacular levelings of *be* and of the present tense — general in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class white American speech, rule-governed there, and **nonstandard** in edited American English.

'People — unspecified, unnameable, and not present' — attributes an action or a claim to nobody in particular.

**1. Hearsay.** *They say…* introduces something everybody has heard and nobody sourced. **2. The authorities.** Whoever it is that makes the decisions: *They're closing the exit ramp.* **3. People in a place or trade.** *In France they eat much later.*

1. *They* say it's supposed to rain all weekend.
2. *They're* tearing down the old post office.
3. *They* don't make them the way they used to.
4. You can't fight city hall — *they* always win.
5. *They* raised the speed limit on that stretch last year.
6. In France *they* eat dinner much later than we do.
7. Apparently *they* found a cure for it years ago.

**See also:** [we (generic)](#we-generic), [you (generic)](#you-generic), [one (generic)](#one-generic), [he (generic)](#he-generic), [it (extraposition)](#it-extraposition), [they](01-personal.md#they), [them](01-personal.md#them)

### we (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /wi/ &middot; *WEE* &middot; unstressed it shortens toward [wɪ]; *we're* /wɪr/ reduces to [wɚ] and *we've* to [wɪv]. The object *us* has the weak form /əs/ — *tell us* [ˈtɛləs] — worn all the way down in *let's*
**Русский:** мы — обобщённо: люди, американцы, наша профессия; говорящий включён
**Type:** generic (inclusive) pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject; object *us*; possessive determiner *our*, which is a determiner and is entered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#our); standalone possessive *ours* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the editorial and royal uses are formal, and the institutional *we* is condescending
**Person / number:** first person plural in form and agreement — even when a single writer or a single monarch is meant.
**Refers to:** people in general with the speaker included and the hearer normally claimed as well — "humanity," "Americans," "this profession," "this journal," depending on context. English does not mark the inclusive/exclusive difference, so *We're going to lunch* may or may not be an invitation.
**Agreement:** **plural verb, always** — *we are*, *we have*, *we were*. ✗ *we is*, ✗ *we was* (**nonstandard** — the vernacular leveling of past *be*, general in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class white American speech). Editorial *we* standing for one author still takes the plural: *we show in Section 3 that…*
**Pattern:** *we* [plural verb] &middot; *we all* / *we as a society* &middot; *let us* / *let's* [verb] &middot; *us* [plural noun] in object position — *for us researchers* &middot; institutional: *how are we feeling?*
**Contrast:** generic *we* differs from generic [you](#you-generic) and [one](#one-generic) by **including the speaker explicitly and drafting the hearer** — *We need to talk about the deficit* asserts a shared stake that *One needs to* pointedly does not. Four flavors are worth telling apart: the **generic/inclusive** *we* (*we live in an age of…*), the **editorial or authorial** *we* standing for a single writer, the **royal** *we*, and the **institutional** *we* of *Are we ready for our shot?*, which means *you* and is best avoided with adults. Case still applies when a noun follows: *we researchers* as subject, *us researchers* as object (*for us researchers*, ✗ *for we researchers*) — delete the noun and the right form is obvious. That **deletion test** is the same one that settles every coordination, and it is worth stating in full here because this is the entry where case comes up. Take the other person out and read what is left: *He gave it to Bob and **me*** → *He gave it to **me*** ✓; *between you and **me*** → *between … **me*** ✓, the preposition taking the object form as it always does; ***She** and **I** went* → ***I** went* ✓. Run backwards, it catches the plain error ✗ *Me and him went* (✗ *Me went*, ✗ *Him went* — the repair is *He and I went*, since the case is what is wrong and not the order) and the two hypercorrections that come from over-fixing it, ✗ *between you and I* and ✗ *They invited my wife and I* (✗ *between … I*, ✗ *They invited … I*). The second of those has no preposition anywhere in it: a coordinated direct object takes the object form for the same reason. See [Case: the rule, the test, and where Americans actually break it](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it).

'People in general, and I am one of them' — states something as true of a group the speaker belongs to.

**1. Inclusive generic.** *We live in an age when nobody reads past the headline.* **2. Editorial or authorial.** One writer, plural pronoun: *We argue in this paper that…* **3. Royal.** A monarch speaking as the institution. **4. Institutional.** *How are we feeling today?* — meaning *you*.

1. *We* live in an age when nobody reads past the headline.
2. In this paper *we* argue that the second model fits the data better.
3. *We* don't say it that way in English.
4. How are *we* feeling this morning?
5. *We* have made our decision, and it is final.
6. *We* as a country have never really settled that question.
7. The results surprised *us* as much as they surprised anyone.

**See also:** [you (generic)](#you-generic), [one (generic)](#one-generic), [they (generic)](#they-generic), [he (generic)](#he-generic), [we](01-personal.md#we), [us](01-personal.md#us)

### you (generic)

**Pronunciation:** /ju/ &middot; *YOO* &middot; weak /jə/ and /jʊ/; after /d/ or /t/ it fuses — *did you* [dɪdʒə], *don't you* [doʊntʃə], *what do you think* [wʌɾəjə ˈθɪŋk]
**Русский:** обобщённое ты/вы — «любой в такой ситуации»; по-русски чаще безличный оборот
**Type:** generic (impersonal) pronoun &middot; **Case:** one form for subject and object; possessive determiner *your*, which is a determiner and is entered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#your); standalone possessive *yours*; reflexive *yourself* &middot; **Register:** neutral — the ordinary American choice in speech and in most writing; some academic style guides and conservative editors still object
**Person / number:** second person in form; number is not marked at all. It takes plural verb forms like every *you*, but the singular reflexive *yourself*.
**Refers to:** people in general — "anyone in this position." There is no antecedent, and the actual addressee is not the point, though a reader may take it personally anyway.
**Agreement:** **plural verb forms, always** — *you are*, *you have*, *you were* — even for one generic person. ✗ *you is*, ✗ *you was* (**nonstandard** — the vernacular leveling of *be*, general in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class white American speech, and *you was* was ordinary in eighteenth-century educated English before the prescriptivists drove it out). The reflexive is singular *yourself* (*you have to watch yourself*), because the reference is one person at a time.
**Pattern:** *you* [verb] &middot; *you can / have to / never* [verb] &middot; *your* [noun] &middot; *yourself* &middot; instructional: *first you brown the onions* &middot; *you know* / *you never know* as fixed frames
**Contrast:** generic *you* is what Americans say where the British write [one](#one-generic); *one* is formal, *you* is neutral, and generic [they](#they-generic) is for what unnamed others do. Two traps. **Ambiguity:** a reader can hear an accusation — *You can't trust anyone in that department* may land as a charge against the reader; the fixes are the passive, a plural noun (*drivers, patients, researchers*), or *one*. **Person shift:** choose one generic and hold it — ✗ *When one is tired, you make mistakes.* Note also that generic *you* is neither singular nor plural, so the regional plurals are **address forms, not generics**, and each carries its own label: [y'all](01-personal.md#yall) is Southern and African American English and standard within those varieties, and it is spreading nationally; [you guys](01-personal.md#you-guys) is national and informal rather than nonstandard, and is under pressure for being gendered; [youse](01-personal.md#youse) is **nonstandard**, and belongs to New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago; [you'uns](01-personal.md#youuns) and *yinz* are **nonstandard**, Appalachian and Pittsburgh. None of the marked forms belongs in formal writing — write *all of you*, *you all*, or name the group.

'Anyone in this position' — the everyday American way of saying something is true of people generally.

**1. People in general.** *You never know what a jury will do.* **2. Instructional.** Recipes, directions, and how-to prose: *You take two eggs and you beat them until…*

1. *You* can't get a decent apartment in that neighborhood anymore.
2. First *you* brown the onions, then *you* add the garlic.
3. It's the kind of noise *you* stop hearing after about a week.
4. *You* never know what a jury is going to do.
5. If *you* miss the last train, *you're* stuck downtown until morning.
6. *You* have to watch *yourself* on those stairs when they're icy.
7. They tax *you* on it whether *you* sell or not.

**See also:** [one (generic)](#one-generic), [we (generic)](#we-generic), [they (generic)](#they-generic), [he (generic)](#he-generic), [you](01-personal.md#you), [youse](01-personal.md#youse)

### yours truly

**Pronunciation:** /ˌjɔrz ˈtruli/ &middot; *yorz-TROO-lee* &middot; accent on *tru-*, with a secondary beat on *yours*; many Americans have /jʊrz/ for *yours*, and in fast speech the first word reduces to [jɚz], so the phrase runs to [jɚz ˈtruli]. The phrase as a whole has no weak form, because it almost always ends its sentence and takes the sentence accent — which is the whole point of reaching for it
**Русский:** шутл. ваш покорный слуга — только о самом говорящем; форма неизменяема
**Type:** jocular first-person pronominal — a frozen letter closing pressed into service as a noun phrase meaning *me* &middot; **Case:** **none, and that is the tell** — one invariant form does subject and object alike (*Yours truly got stuck with the bill*, *They gave it to yours truly*), which marks it as a fixed phrase rather than a pronoun with a paradigm; the possessive *yours truly's* exists as a further joke and is rare in print &middot; **Register:** informal and **always jocular**. It is a wink — the speaker steps into the third person to take credit, lodge a complaint, or land a punch line — and it has no neutral use and no place in formal writing, where the word is *I* or *me*
**Person / number:** **first person singular in meaning, third person singular in grammar**, and the mismatch is the joke.
**Refers to:** the **speaker or writer**, and nobody else, ever. It cannot be handed to a third party: ✗ *Ask yours truly's brother* said of somebody else's brother is not English.
**Agreement:** **third-person singular verb** — *Yours truly is the one who has to explain this to the board*, *Yours truly was volunteered again.* ✗ *Yours truly am*, which lets the meaning's person leak into the grammar. The follow-up pronouns stay in the third person as well, and sliding back into the first breaks the device: ✗ *Yours truly, in my opinion, would go further.*
**Pattern:** *Yours truly* [singular verb] &middot; [verb] *yours truly* &middot; [preposition] *yours truly* — *to yours truly*, *for yours truly*, *by yours truly* &middot; the answer frame *Who…? — Yours truly.* &middot; the credit line *photograph by yours truly* &middot; the letter closing *Yours truly,* — the frozen original, and **not** this pronoun
**Contrast:** the phrase has two lives and only one of them belongs in this file. The **letter closing** *Yours truly,* is a valediction: it fills no noun slot, takes no verb, and is where the pronoun came from. The **pronominal** *yours truly* stands exactly where *I* or *me* would — *Guess who got stuck cleaning up? Yours truly.* Against the other devices for keeping *I* off the page, all of them entered nearby: [the present writer](#the-present-writer-the-present-author) is the scholar's, stiff and dated; *the undersigned* is the contract's; [deponent](#deponent-affiant) is the affidavit's; the editorial [we](#we-generic) is one writer wearing a plural; and *yours truly* is the only one of the set that is meant to be funny. Two mechanics. No apostrophe, ever — *yours* is a possessive pronoun, and that family never takes one (✗ *your's truly*), a rule it shares with *its*, *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs*. And there is no plural: ✗ *ours truly* for a group is not available, and a speaker who wants the joke for two people has to build it some other way.

'Me, with a wink' — the speaker in the third person, claiming the credit or the blame.

**1. The punch line.** Answering a question the speaker has just set up: *And who do you suppose had to clean it all up? Yours truly.* **2. The complaint.** *They handed the whole thing to yours truly at five o'clock on a Friday.* **3. The credit line.** *Photograph by yours truly* — one of the few places the phrase turns up in print rather than in speech.

1. Guess who got stuck cleaning up after all of them? *Yours truly*.
2. The only person in the building who had read the whole contract was *yours truly*.
3. They handed the entire mess to *yours truly* about ten minutes before the deadline.
4. *Yours truly* has been saying that for three years, for whatever it is worth. (third-person verb, first-person meaning)
5. If anything goes wrong on Saturday, it is *yours truly* they will be calling at six in the morning.
6. Photograph by *yours truly*, taken from the roof of the parking garage. (the credit line)
7. The letter still closes *Yours truly*, but that is the valediction it started as, not the pronoun.

## Dummy and expletive pronouns

An English finite clause must have a subject, even when there is nothing for the subject to be. Nothing is raining in *It's raining*; *there* names no place in *There's a problem*. Both words are placeholders, and both are grammatically required: ✗ *Is raining*, ✗ *Has a problem with the invoice*. Speakers of Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, Russian, and Mandarin lose more ground to this rule than to almost anything else in the language, because their own grammars let the subject go.

Which dummy you need is decided by what the sentence is doing. Use ***it*** for the weather, the clock, the calendar, a distance, a temperature, an ambient condition — and for a clause you want to move to the end (*It is clear that…*, *I find it odd that…*) or an element you want to spotlight (*It was Maria who called*). Use ***there*** to assert that something exists (*There is a problem*). The pair *It's windy* and *There's a strong wind* shows the split cleanly: no noun takes *it*, a noun takes *there*.

Agreement is where the group bites. Dummy *it* is always singular. Existential *there* borrows its number from the noun that follows — and in speech *there's* runs in front of plurals constantly, which is the most-corrected honest fact in this file.

### it (anticipatory object)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; before a vowel the *t* flaps, so *find it odd* runs to [ˈfaɪn əɾ ˈɑd]
**Русский:** формальное дополнение it — держит место придаточного, вынесенного в конец; не переводится
**Type:** dummy (anticipatory) object pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant. No gender, no plural.
**Refers to:** **nothing** — it holds the object slot open for a *that*-clause, *when*-clause, *if*-clause, or infinitive that has been moved to the end of the sentence. What it anticipates is a clause, never a noun.
**Agreement:** none — it is an object. The main verb agrees with the sentence's own subject.
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] **it** [adjective] *that* [clause] — *I find it odd that…* &middot; [subject] [verb] **it** [noun phrase] *to* [verb] — *She considers it a waste to…* &middot; [subject] [verb] **it** *when/if* [clause] — *I hate it when…*, *I'd appreciate it if…* &middot; [subject] *owes/takes/leaves* **it** [phrase] — *He owes it to them to try*, *I take it that…*
**Contrast:** with the verbs that require it, **leaving the *it* out is an error**, and it is one of the most frequent in nonnative writing: ✗ *I find strange that he called*, ✗ *I'd appreciate if you would call*, ✗ *She considers a waste to argue about it.* The working rule: *it* is obligatory when an adjective or noun phrase stands between the verb and the clause (*find it odd that*, *make it clear that*, *think it best to*, *consider it a waste to*), and after *hate / like / love / appreciate / prefer* + *when* or *if*. One honest exception, common enough that it has to be named: ***make clear***, ***make plain***, and ***make known*** have fossilized enough to stand without the *it* in edited American English — *The court made clear that the rule applies* is as good as *made it clear that*, and neither is a correction of the other. That is a property of those *make* idioms and of nothing else in the group: the *find*, *consider*, and *appreciate* patterns above still require their *it*. It is **not** used when the clause follows the verb directly: *I know that he called*, ✗ *I know it that he called.* Distinguish it from referential *it*, which points at a thing — in *I found it odd* the *it* is the situation to come, in *I found it under the couch* it is the wallet.

Holds the object slot for a clause that has been pushed to the end of the sentence.

1. I find *it* strange that nobody called the police.
2. She made *it* clear that the offer expires on Friday.
3. I'd appreciate *it* if you'd let me know either way.
4. He owes *it* to his family to at least try.
5. I hate *it* when the coffee shop closes early.
6. The board thought *it* best to postpone the vote.
7. I take *it* you've already heard.

**See also:** [it (extraposition)](#it-extraposition), [it (cleft)](#it-cleft), [it (idiomatic object)](#it-idiomatic-object), [what (fused relative)](#what-fused-relative), [it](01-personal.md#it)

### it (cleft)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; *it was* runs to [əɾ wəz] and *it's* to [əts]
**Русский:** формальное it в обороте It was X who… — выделение, рус. «именно X»; не переводится
**Type:** dummy subject of the *it*-cleft &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral — the cleft is ordinary in both speech and writing
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant, and it stays singular even when the spotlighted element is plural.
**Refers to:** nothing. The cleft splits one clause in two so a single element can be spotlighted: *Maria called* becomes *It was Maria who called.*
**Agreement:** the copula agrees with the dummy, so it is **singular whatever the focus** — *It was the neighbors who complained*, ✗ *It were the neighbors.* The verb inside the cleft clause agrees with the focused element instead: *It is **I** who **am** responsible* (formal), *It's **the kids** who **are** the problem.*
**Pattern:** *It* + *be* + [focused noun phrase] + *who/that* [clause] &middot; *It* + *be* + [focused adverbial] + *that* [clause] — *It was on Tuesday that…* &middot; negative: *It wasn't the money that bothered him* &middot; question: *Was it Maria who called?* &middot; *It wasn't until* [time] *that* [clause]
**Contrast:** the cleft is also where the old case question gets settled. *It was I who broke it* is the formal nominative version; ***It's me*** is ordinary standard American English and has been for centuries — write *It's me*, not *It is I*, unless the register is deliberately elevated. That honesty has a limit, though: in a **coordination** the case is still decided by the slot the pronoun sits in, and the deletion test decides it — *between you and **me***, *They invited my wife and **me***, ***She** and **I** went*, against the error ✗ *Me and him went* and the hypercorrection ✗ *between you and I* (worked through at [we (generic)](#we-generic) and in full at [Case](01-personal.md#case-the-rule-the-test-and-where-americans-actually-break-it)). Inside the cleft clause, [who](05-relative.md#who) is for people, [which](05-relative.md#which) for things, and [that](05-relative.md#that) for both — *It was Maria who called* and *It was Maria that called* are equally good — while *whose* covers people and things alike. And remember that the American preference for restrictive *that* over restrictive *which* is an **editing convention**, not a rule of grammar; the one hard rule there is that *that* cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause. Against [it (extraposition)](#it-extraposition), the test is the **gap**: in a cleft the material after *who/that* is missing the focused element (*It was Maria who ___ called*), while in extraposition the *that*-clause is complete (*It is clear that she was right*).

Splits a sentence in two to put one element under the spotlight.

1. *It* was Maria who called, not her brother.
2. *It's* the humidity that gets to me, not the heat.
3. *It* wasn't until Thursday that anyone noticed the error.
4. *It* was the neighbors who complained about the noise.
5. Was *it* you who left the back door open?
6. *It's* not the falling that hurts; *it's* the landing.
7. *It* is in the second paragraph that the argument falls apart.

### it (extraposition)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; *it is* runs to [ɪɾ ɪz], *it's* to [əts]
**Русский:** формальное подлежащее it — место придаточного-подлежащего, вынесенного в конец
**Type:** dummy (anticipatory) subject &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral — the default way to build these sentences
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** nothing on its own. It is a placeholder for a subject clause — a *that*-clause, an infinitive, or an *-ing* clause — that has been moved to the end because English prefers heavy material last.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *It is clear that the numbers were wrong*, ✗ *It are clear.* The verb stays singular no matter how many plurals the extraposed clause contains: *It is obvious that the results were fabricated.*
**Pattern:** *It* [be] [adjective] *that* [clause] &middot; *It* [be] [noun phrase] *to* [verb] &middot; *It* [verb] [object] *that* [clause] — *It bothers me that…* &middot; *It seems / appears / turns out / happens that…* &middot; *It's no use* [verb-ing] &middot; *It is said / believed / thought that…* — the impersonal passive
**Contrast:** the unextraposed version is grammatical but almost never chosen: *That she was right is clear* against *It is clear that she was right.* What is **not** available is dropping the subject — ✗ *Is clear that she was right* — the standard interference error for speakers of Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, and Russian. Against [it (cleft)](#it-cleft): here the *that*-clause is **complete**, while a cleft leaves a gap where the focused element belongs. Against existential [there](#there-existential): *it* introduces a proposition, *there* introduces the existence of a thing — *It is a problem that nobody checked* versus *There is a problem.* This construction is also the polite formal substitute for generic [they](#they-generic): *They say the bridge will close* → *It is said that the bridge will close.*

Holds the subject slot for a clause that has been moved to the end of the sentence.

1. *It* is clear that nobody actually read the contract.
2. *It's* important to arrive at least an hour early.
3. *It* turns out the flight was canceled before we even left the house.
4. *It* bothers me that she never apologized.
5. *It's* no use complaining about it now.
6. *It* seems that the two departments never spoke to each other.
7. *It* has been decided that the office will close on Fridays.

**See also:** [it (cleft)](#it-cleft), [it (anticipatory object)](#it-anticipatory-object), [there (existential)](#there-existential), [they (generic)](#they-generic), [what (fused relative)](#what-fused-relative)

### it (idiomatic object)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; in these idioms *it* is unstressed and the verb or particle carries the accent — *knock it off* [ˈnɑk əɾ ˈɔf]
**Русский:** it в устойчивых оборотах (make it, hoof it) — часть идиомы, отдельно не переводится
**Type:** dummy object in fixed expressions &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, depending on the idiom
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant, and **frozen** — it cannot be pluralized, questioned, replaced by a noun, or promoted to subject by a passive.
**Refers to:** nothing at all. The *it* is part of the idiom's spelling, not a pronoun with a job.
**Agreement:** none.
**Pattern:** [verb] *it* — *make it, wing it, rough it, cool it, beat it* &middot; [verb] *it* [particle] — *knock it off, hash it out, tough it out, live it up* &middot; [verb] *it* [preposition] [object] — *lord it over someone, have it out with someone* &middot; [verb] *it* [noun] — *call it a day, call it quits*
**Contrast:** the test that separates this *it* from referential *it* is whether a *what*-question can reach it. *He fixed it* invites "Fixed what?" — referential. *He made it*, meaning he arrived or survived or succeeded, has no answer to "Made what?" — idiomatic. Because the *it* is frozen, the idiom will not passivize or pluralize: ✗ *It was made by him* in the "arrived" sense, ✗ *They lorded them over us.* And the spelling trap runs through every one of these: ***its*** is the possessive, ***it's*** is *it is* or *it has*. If you can expand it to *it is*, it takes the apostrophe.

A meaningless *it* welded into a fixed expression, where no noun could ever stand.

1. We barely made *it* to the gate before they closed the door.
2. Knock *it* off — people are trying to work.
3. He's been lording *it* over the rest of the team since the promotion.
4. Let's call *it* a day and pick this up tomorrow.
5. They roughed *it* for a week with no hot water.
6. I hadn't prepared anything, so I just winged *it*.
7. We're going to have to tough *it* out until payday.

**See also:** [it (weather, time, and distance)](#it-weather-time-and-distance), [it (anticipatory object)](#it-anticipatory-object), [it](01-personal.md#it), [its](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#its)

### it (weather, time, and distance)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt/ &middot; *IT* &middot; weak /ət/; *it's* is [əts], and in very casual speech the vowel drops out altogether — *it's raining* [ts ˈreɪnɪŋ]
**Русский:** безличное it: о погоде, времени, расстоянии — в русском подлежащего нет вовсе
**Type:** dummy (ambient) subject &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** third person singular, invariant.
**Refers to:** **nothing** — there is no answer to "what is raining?" This *it* covers the weather, the clock, the calendar, the temperature, a distance, a duration, and the general state of the surroundings, none of which the sentence has named.
**Agreement:** **singular verb, always** — *It is*, *It was*, *It gets*, *It has been*. ✗ *It are.*
**Pattern:** *It* [be] [weather adjective] — *It's cold* &middot; *It* [be] [verb-ing] — *It's snowing* &middot; *It* [be] [clock time / day / date] — *It's ten after four*, *It's Tuesday* &middot; *It* [be] [distance or duration] *to/from* [place] — *It's three miles to the airport* &middot; *It* [verb] — *It rained all night*, *It gets dark by five*
**Contrast:** this is the hardest dummy for speakers of languages that drop the subject or build these sentences with a possession verb. English demands the pronoun (✗ *Is raining*, ✗ *Rains a lot here*), and it demands *it* specifically — not *there* and not *have*: ✗ *There is raining*, ✗ *It has three miles to the airport*, ✗ *There are three miles to the airport* (compare Spanish *hay*, French *il y a*). Existential [there](#there-existential) asserts that a **thing** exists, so *There's a storm coming* is fine because *a storm* is a noun, while *It is storming* describes the ambient condition with no noun at all. The minimal pair to remember is *It's windy* against *There's a strong wind.*

Fills the subject slot for the weather, the clock, the calendar, and the space around you.

**1. Weather and temperature.** *It's snowing*, *It was ninety-four degrees.* **2. Clock and calendar.** *It's almost six*, *It's Tuesday*, *It's my birthday.* **3. Distance and duration.** *It's three miles to the airport*, *It's twenty minutes by train.* **4. Ambient conditions.** *It's noisy in here*, *It smells like smoke in the hallway.*

1. *It's* been raining since Thursday afternoon.
2. What time is *it*? — *It's* almost quarter to six.
3. *It's* only about twenty minutes from here if the traffic cooperates.
4. *It* gets dark around five o'clock in December.
5. *It* was ninety-four degrees on the tarmac when we landed.
6. *It's* freezing in this office.
7. *It's* Tuesday, so the trash goes out tonight.

### there (existential)

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛr/ &middot; *THAIR* &middot; existential *there* is nearly always **weak** — /ðɚ/, so *there are two* runs to [ðɚ ɚ ˈtu] and *there's* to [ðɚz]. The strong /ðɛr/ belongs to the locative adverb; if you hear a full vowel, the word means "in that place"
**Русский:** оборот there is/are — «есть, имеется, существует»; места не обозначает
**Type:** existential (expletive) subject &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Person / number:** none of its own — it borrows both from the noun phrase that follows the verb.
**Refers to:** nothing, and in particular **not to a place**. It exists so the sentence can announce that something exists or is present, with the real subject postponed until after the verb.
**Agreement:** **the verb agrees with the noun phrase that follows, not with *there*** — *There is a problem*, *There are problems*, *There was one applicant*, *There were three applicants.* Two honest qualifications: before a coordinated list the verb almost always matches the **first** noun (*There is a fork and two knives on the table*), and before an amount treated as a single block it is singular (*There is ten dollars in the drawer*).
**Pattern:** *There* [be] [indefinite noun phrase] ([place or time phrase]) &middot; *There* [be] [noun phrase] *to* [verb] — *There's a lot to do* &middot; *There* [modal] *be* [noun phrase] — *There must be a reason* &middot; formal, with other verbs: *There arose*, *There remains*, *There exists*, *There seems to be* &middot; question: *Is there…?*, *Are there any…?* &middot; tag: *There's a problem, isn't there?*
**Contrast:** existential *there* against locative *there* — both can appear in one sentence, which settles it: *There's a bus stop there.* The first is unstressed and means nothing; the second is stressed and means "at that spot." Against dummy [it](#it-weather-time-and-distance): *there* asserts existence, *it* describes an ambient condition or identifies something — *There is a problem* introduces the problem, *It is a problem* comments on something already in view. The noun after existential *there* is normally **indefinite**, which is why ✗ *There is my wallet on the table* is wrong as an existential (rewrite as *My wallet is on the table*) and only works if you are pointing, in which case *there* is the locative. The tag question is the proof that this *there* is a real grammatical subject — the tag copies it the way it would copy any subject: *There were no messages, were there?* **Nonstandard:** negative concord in the existential — *There ain't nobody home*, *There wasn't nothing we could do* — is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern, Appalachian, and working-class vernacular speech, and it was ordinary English before the eighteenth century; it is nevertheless **nonstandard in edited American English**, which allows one negative per clause: *There isn't anybody home*, *There was nothing we could do.* See [nothing (negative concord)](07-indefinite-compound.md#nothing-negative-concord).

Announces that something exists or is present, holding the subject slot while the real subject waits its turn.

1. *There's* a message for you on the machine.
2. *There* are three good reasons not to sign this.
3. *There* was nobody at the desk when I came in.
4. Is *there* any coffee left?
5. *There* must be a simpler way to do this.
6. *There* used to be a hardware store on that corner.
7. *There* were fewer complaints this quarter than last.
8. *There's* a lot to talk about, isn't *there*?

**See also:** [there's, there're](#theres-therere), [it (weather, time, and distance)](#it-weather-time-and-distance), [it (extraposition)](#it-extraposition), [their](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#their)

### there's, there're

**Pronunciation:** *there's* /ðɛrz/, weak [ðɚz] &middot; *THAIRZ* &middot; *there're* /ˈðɛrɚ/ collapses in ordinary speech to [ðɛɚ] or plain [ðɛr], barely distinguishable from *there* alone — one reason it is so rarely written. Also *there've* /ðɛrv/, *there'll* /ðɛrl/, *there'd* /ðɛrd/
**Русский:** стяжённые there is / there are — «есть, имеется»; разг.
**Type:** contracted existential subject plus verb &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral in speech; *there's* before a plural is informal in writing
**Person / number:** *there's* contracts a **singular** verb (*there is*, *there has*); *there're* contracts a plural one (*there are*).
**Refers to:** nothing — see [there (existential)](#there-existential).
**Agreement:** this is the whole point of the entry. In edited writing the verb must match the following noun: *There's a problem* / *There are problems.* In **speech**, ***there's* before a plural is near-universal** at every level of American English — *there's two things I want to say*, *there's a lot of people here* — and it is best understood as a fixed opener rather than an agreement decision made in real time. It is still marked as an error in edited prose, and a conservative editor will change every instance. *There're* is grammatically correct but awkward to say and rare on paper; most writers use the full *there are*.
**Pattern:** *There's* [singular noun phrase] &middot; *There're* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *There've been* [plural noun phrase] &middot; *There'll be* [noun phrase] &middot; *There'd been* [noun phrase] &middot; spoken only: *There's* [plural noun phrase]
**Contrast:** keep *there's* apart from *theirs* and from the homophone set. The apostrophe in *there's* marks the missing letter of *is* or *has*; ***theirs***, being a possessive pronoun, never takes one. In the three-way *there / their / they're*, *there* is the place or the dummy, *their* is the possessive determiner, *they're* is *they are* — and in the existential frame only *there* is possible: ✗ *Their's a problem*, ✗ *They're are two options.*

The contracted existential — the form the construction actually takes in speech, agreement problems and all.

1. *There's* a package for you by the front door.
2. *There've* been three complaints about that intersection this month.
3. *There'll* be plenty of time to talk about it after dinner.
4. *There're* a few things we should go over first.
5. *There's* two things I want to say before we start. (ordinary speech; edited to *there are*)
6. *There'd* been no warning at all before the layoffs.
