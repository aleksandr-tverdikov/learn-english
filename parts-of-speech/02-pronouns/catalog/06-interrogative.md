# Interrogative pronouns

> The five *wh-* words that stand in for the answer you are asking for — *who*, *whom*, *whose*, *what*, *which* — plus *how many* and *how much*, and the emphatic and elliptical forms built on them.

[← The grammar of pronouns](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

An interrogative pronoun stands where the answer will stand, and then moves to the front: *Someone called* becomes ***Who** called?*, and *You saw someone* becomes ***Who** did you see?* That single operation — put a *wh-* word in the gap, front it — generates every question in this file, and it explains the group's two signature rules. If the *wh-* word **is the subject**, nothing else moves and there is no *do*: *Who called?* — ✗*Who did call?* is wrong as a neutral question, though emphatic *did* comes back when a denial is being contradicted (*Somebody called, so who **did** call?*), and the negative takes *do* as usual (*Who didn't call?*); if the *wh-* word is anything else, the auxiliary inverts around the subject: *What did you say?* And when the question is **embedded** in a larger sentence, the inversion unwinds and statement order comes back — *I asked what he said*, never ✗*I asked what did he say*.

Two choices run the length of the file. **What** asks from an open set and **which** from a closed one the hearer can already see: *What do you want for dinner?* against *Which do you want, the soup or the salad?* And **who** asks for a person's identity where **what** asks for their role — *Who is she?* wants a name, *What is she?* wants an occupation.

## The five core forms

The whole class fits in five words, and only one pair of them marks case: *who* is the subject form and *whom* the object form, *whose* is the only possessive, and *what* and *which* never change shape at all. Pronunciation is uniform too. Most Americans have the **wine–whine merger**, so *what* and *which* begin with a plain /w/ and *which* is a homophone of *witch*; many Southern and older speakers keep an /hw-/ onset. *Who*, *whom*, and *whose* went the other way centuries ago — their *w* is silent and they begin with /h/. As the focus of the question these words carry the sentence accent and keep their full vowels; it is in **embedded** clauses that they weaken, which is why *I know what I want* comes out with a reduced [wət].

### what

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/, also /wɑt/ &middot; *WUT* / *WAHT* &middot; weak /wət/ whenever it is not the accented word (*I know what I want* → [wət]); the *t* flaps into a following vowel, so *what if* is [wʌˈɾɪf] and *what are you* runs together as *whaddaya*; plain /w/ for most Americans, /hwʌt/ for speakers who keep the older onset  
**Русский:** что — спрашивает о предмете, событии или действии из открытого, не заданного круга
**Type:** interrogative pronoun (also a fused relative; the exclamative *What a mess!* is a determiner — see the contrast) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; no plural form, and it is construed as singular unless something in the sentence forces otherwise  
**Refers to:** things, events, actions, ideas, amounts, and **whole clauses** — anything except a person's name. Pointed at a person it asks for the role or category rather than the identity (*What is she? — An anesthesiologist.*); for the identity, use [who](#who). It is **open-ended**: *what* assumes no list. For a choice out of a set both speakers can see, use [which](#which).  
**Agreement:** as subject it takes a **singular** verb by default — *What is in the box?*, *What happens next?* A plural verb appears only when the complement is plainly plural: *What are those?*, *What were the results?* Where the answer could go either way, edited American English prefers the plural with a plural complement: *What are the remaining options?*  
**Pattern:** *What* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; *What* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (object question) &middot; *What* [subject] [verb] [preposition]? (*What are you looking at?*) &middot; [clause] *what* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; [subject] [verb] *what*? (echo, in place) &middot; *What?* / *What about it?* (elliptical)  
**Contrast:** *what* against **[which](#which)** is the file's central choice: *which* selects from a set already on the table, *what* asks with no set assumed. *What did you order?* invites any answer; *Which did you order?* means one of the things in front of us. *What* against **[who](#who)** for a person turns on identity versus role: *Who is he?* gets *my brother-in-law*, *What is he?* gets *a structural engineer* — and asking *What is he?* about a person can sound cold, so *What does he do?* is the ordinary way to put it. Three other *what*s are not this one: the **fused relative** (*What you said was true* = "the thing that"), covered with [the fused relatives](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative); the **exclamative** (*What a mess!*), which is a determiner; and the **determiner** *what* in *what color, what time*, covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-interrogative). The test is the same one that runs through both dictionaries: a noun immediately after it makes it a determiner, standing alone makes it a pronoun.

Asks for the identity of a thing, an event, or an action, out of an open field of possibilities.

**1. Things, events, and actions.** The default use: *What happened?*, *What do you want?*, *What's in the bag?* The answer can be anything at all, which is what separates it from *which*.

**2. A person's role or category.** *What is she?* asks for an occupation, a nationality, a category — not a name. Beside it, *Who is she?* asks for the identity.

**3. Amount, price, and measure.** *What did it cost?*, *What does the whole thing weigh?* Here *what* is doing the work of *how much*.

**4. Standing for a whole clause.** *What happened next?* and *What's going on?* take an entire event as the answer, not a noun.

**5. As a request for repetition.** Bare *What?* means "say that again", and in American English it is blunt — *Sorry?*, *Excuse me?*, and *I'm sorry, what?* are the polite versions, *Huh?* the casual one. For the incredulous *You did what?*, see [what (echo)](#what-echo).

1. *What* happened to the second box of files?
2. *What* did she say when you told her the price?
3. *What* are you looking at?
4. He asked me *what* I wanted for my birthday.
5. *What* was your father before he retired, a machinist?
6. *What* does the whole thing cost with tax and delivery?
7. *What*'s in the trunk? Nothing that won't fit in the hall closet.
8. Nobody could tell us *what* the delay was about.

**See also:** [which](#which), [who](#who), [what (echo)](#what-echo), [what about](#what-about), [whatever](#whatever), [what (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative), [what (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-interrogative)

### which

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ &middot; *WICH* &middot; a homophone of *witch* for most Americans; /hwɪtʃ/ for Southern and older speakers who keep the *wh-* onset; weak /wətʃ/ in an embedded clause (*I forget which one it was*)  
**Русский:** который, какой из — просит выбрать из закрытого набора, известного обоим собеседникам
**Type:** interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun) &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; construed as singular or plural according to the answer expected  
**Refers to:** things **and** people alike, but only from a **known, bounded set** — one that is visible, has just been mentioned, or is listed in the question itself. With people it asks which member of a group (*Which of you is Dr. Reyes?*), and it never asks for a stranger's identity: ✗*Which is she?* → *Who is she?* The set need not be small, only bounded and mutually known (*Which state has the longest coastline?*).  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **number of the expected answer**, not with the *of*-phrase: *Which is yours?* (one expected) beside *Which are yours?* (more than one), and *Which of these boxes **is** yours?* beside *Which of these boxes **are** ready to ship?* With *which of us / you / them*, a singular verb is the default: *Which of us is going to tell him?*  
**Pattern:** *Which* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; *Which* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Which of* [determiner + noun]? &middot; *Which* [subject] [verb] [preposition]? &middot; [clause] *which* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *Which?* (elliptical, set understood)  
**Contrast:** **open set versus closed set** is the whole story. *What's your favorite movie?* leaves the field open to every movie ever made; *Which is your favorite?* means one of the three on the table. Choosing *what* when a list has already been given invites an answer from outside the list, and choosing *which* with no list leaves the hearer hunting for one. Against **[who](#who)** for people: *who* identifies, *which* selects — *Who called?* asks for a name, *Which of them called?* asks you to pick from a group already named. The **relative** *which* is a different job (*the report, which arrived late*) and lives with [the relative pronouns](05-relative.md#which) — and the famous *which/that* rule belongs to that entry, not this one: half of it is real (*that* cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause) and half is an **American editing convention** (restrictive *which* is perfectly grammatical, and a copy editor will still change it to *that*). The **determiner** *which* takes a noun directly (*which flight*) and lives in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative). Adding *of* always makes it the pronoun: *which flight* determiner, *which of the flights* pronoun.

Asks the hearer to pick one member — or several — out of a set both speakers already have in view.

1. *Which* do you want, the aisle or the window?
2. *Which* of these chargers is mine?
3. *Which* is faster this time of day, driving or the train?
4. I couldn't tell you *which* of the twins I spoke to.
5. *Which* of you has the parking pass?
6. *Which* are we taking, my car or yours?
7. *Which* did you end up ordering?
8. She never said *which* she had picked.

**See also:** [what](#what), [who](#who), [which of](#which-of), [whichever](#whichever), [which (relative)](05-relative.md#which), [which (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative)

### who

**Pronunciation:** /hu/ &middot; *HOO* &middot; the *w* is silent. No true weak form — as the question word it carries the accent — but it shortens in embedded position, and the contractions are ordinary: *who's* /huz/, *who'd* /hud/, *who'll* /hul/, *who're* /ˈhuɚ/. *Who's* is a homophone of *whose*.  
**Русский:** кто — спрашивает об имени, личности человека; форма подлежащего
**Type:** interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun) &middot; **Case:** subject — and, in everyday American English, **object as well** (*Who did you call?*) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** third person in agreement, whatever person the answer turns out to be; invariant in form, and singular by default no matter how many people the answer names  
**Refers to:** **people only** — including a pet spoken of as a person and a team, band, or company understood as the people in it (*Who won?*). For a thing use [what](#what) or [which](#which); to pick from a group already named use [which of](#which-of).  
**Agreement:** **singular by default**, even when several people are expected: *Who wants coffee?*, *Who is coming tonight?*, *Who's got the keys?* A plural verb appears only when a plural complement forces it (*Who are those men?*, *Who are the finalists?*). *Who* has no gender, so the pronoun that picks it up afterward is **singular *they*** — *Who left their coat on the porch?*, *Whoever called, they didn't leave a message*. That is standard American English and not a compromise; see [they](01-personal.md#they).  
**Pattern:** *Who* [verb]? (subject question — no *do* in a neutral question: ✗*Who did call?*) &middot; *Who* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (object question: *Who did you call?*) &middot; *Who* [subject] [verb] [preposition]? (stranded: *Who did you give it to?*) &middot; *Who* [auxiliary] *not* [verb]? (negative subject question, where *do* **does** appear: *Who didn't sign?*) &middot; [clause] *who* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *Who else*, *Who all* (regional), *Says who?*  
**Contrast:** **[whom](#whom)** is the object form, and the test is *he/him*: answer the question and see which fits — *Who called? — **He** called* → *who*; *Whom did you see? — I saw **him*** → *whom*. In real American usage *who* has taken over the fronted object slot, so *Who did you see?* is standard rather than an error, and *whom* survives mainly right after a preposition ([to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom)). Against **[what](#what)** for a person: *Who is he?* asks identity, *What is he?* asks occupation. Against **[which](#which)**: *who* identifies out of the blue, *which* selects from a set. And the two *do*-support facts that trip learners: a **positive subject question takes no *do*** (*Who called?*), but a **negative one does** (*Who didn't get paid?*), and emphatic *did* returns when a denial is being challenged (*Somebody called — so who did call?*). *Who's* with an apostrophe is *who is / who has*, never possessive: see [whose](#whose). **Answering** a *who* question is where case actually shows itself in speech, and the facts are these. The bare answer takes the **object** form — *Who wants coffee?* — ***Me***, not ✗*I* — and ***It's me* is standard English**, with *It is I* now stiff outside the telephone formula *This is she*. When the answer is built out into a full subject the subject form returns (*Who went?* — ***She and I** went*), and in an object slot the object form returns (*Who did they invite?* — *They invited **my wife and me***). The hypercorrections are the mirror image of the schoolroom error: ✗*between you and I*, ✗*They invited my wife and I*, ✗*He gave it to Bob and I* — every one of those is an object slot, **two of them after a preposition** (*between*, *to*) and **one after a verb** (*invited*), and the object form is what belongs in all three: *between you and **me***, *They invited my wife and **me***, *He gave it to Bob and **me***. **The deletion test** settles them: drop the other person and read what is left aloud — *He gave it to **me*** ✓ against ✗*He gave it to **I***, *They invited **me*** ✓ against ✗*They invited **I***, ***I** went* ✓ against ✗*Me went*, which is also what rules out ✗*Me and him went*. *Between* is the one place the test needs a second step, because deleting the partner leaves the phrase incomplete; put a plural in instead, and *between **us*** shows the case plainly — never ✗*between **we***. Full treatment with [me](01-personal.md#me) and [I](01-personal.md#i).

Asks for the identity of a person — the one who did it, or the one it happened to.

1. *Who* left the porch light on all night?
2. *Who* did you talk to at the bank?
3. *Who*'s bringing dessert on Thursday?
4. I still don't know *who* she voted for.
5. *Who* wants to go first?
6. *Who* are those people in the driveway?
7. *Who* didn't get their parking pass yet?
8. *Who* do you think is going to end up paying for it?

### whom

**Pronunciation:** /hum/ &middot; *HOOM* &middot; the *w* is silent. It keeps its full vowel almost everywhere, because it is usually being chosen deliberately; the one place it genuinely weakens is inside a fixed prepositional phrase in fast speech, where *to whom* can flatten to [tə ˈhəm]. The *w* stays silent even there — there is no /w/ sound in *whom* at any speed, so ✗[təˈwəm] is not a pronunciation of it.  
**Русский:** кого, кому — объектная форма who, при глаголе или предлоге; формальн., книжн.
**Type:** interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun) &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Person / number:** any person, singular or plural; people only, and invariant in form  
**Refers to:** **human referents only**, in an object slot — the object of a verb (*Whom did they hire?*) or of a preposition (*For whom is this?*). For things the object form is simply [what](#what) or [which](#which), neither of which marks case.  
**Agreement:** none of its own. Being an object, it never controls the verb; the verb agrees with the clause's own subject — *Whom **did** the committee **choose**?* has *committee* as subject.  
**Pattern:** *Whom* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; [preposition] *whom* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; [clause] *whom* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *To whom?* / *For whom?* (elliptical) &middot; the fixed salutation *To Whom It May Concern*  
**Contrast:** the *he/him* test decides it: if the answer takes *him*, the question takes *whom*. Beyond that, be honest about the state of the language. In ordinary American speech and in most published prose, **[who](#who) has replaced *whom* in fronted object position** — *Who did you see?* is standard American English, not a mistake, and *Whom did you see?* sounds careful or stiff depending on the room. Where *whom* is still genuinely required is **immediately after a preposition**: ✗*to who did you speak* will be corrected everywhere, while *Who did you speak to?* is fine because the preposition has been left at the end. The trap now runs the other way: writing *whom* where the word is the **subject** of an embedded clause is a hypercorrection and is more common than the error it was meant to prevent — ✗*Whom shall I say is calling?* (test: *he is calling*) and ✗*the candidate whom we thought would win*. Intervening words like *we thought* and *shall I say* never change the case.

Marks the person who is the object of the verb or of a preposition in the question.

1. *Whom* did the board finally appoint?
2. To *whom* should I address the complaint?
3. *Whom* are we meeting at four?
4. For *whom* was the second reservation made?
5. She wouldn't say *whom* she had consulted before signing.
6. *Whom* do you trust with a key to the building?
7. *Whom* did they notify first, the family or the press?

**See also:** [who](#who), [whose](#whose), [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom), [whomever](#whomever), [whom (relative)](05-relative.md#whom)

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ* &middot; the *w* is silent, and it is a perfect homophone of *who's* (= *who is* / *who has*). No weak form; as a question word it takes the accent.  
**Русский:** чей — спрашивает о владельце, авторе или ответственном за предмет
**Type:** interrogative pronoun (also a relative pronoun; and a determiner in *whose coat*) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant — it asks about a possessor of any person, number, or gender at once  
**Refers to:** **possessors**. As an interrogative the possessor asked about is almost always human (*Whose is this?*); the wider power of *whose* to take inanimate possessors (*a building whose roof leaks*) belongs to its [relative use](05-relative.md#whose). What it points at is the owner, never the thing owned.  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **thing possessed**, not with *whose*: *Whose is this?*, *Whose are these?*, *Whose was the idea?* Nothing about *whose* itself is singular or plural.  
**Pattern:** *Whose* [be] [noun phrase]? (*Whose is this jacket?*) &middot; *Whose* [be] [demonstrative]? (*Whose are those?*) &middot; *Whose* [be] *it*? &middot; [clause] *whose* [subject] [verb] (embedded: *I asked whose it was*) &middot; *Whose?* (elliptical)  
**Contrast:** **whose against who's** is the commonest spelling error in this whole class, and the test is expansion: if *who is* or *who has* fits, it takes the apostrophe (*Who's coming?*), and if the sentence is about ownership it does not (*Whose is this?*). The apostrophe form is never possessive — no possessive pronoun in English has one, which is the same fact behind *its/it's* and *your/you're*. **Determiner against pronoun** is the other line: a noun right after it makes it a determiner (*Whose coat is this?* — see [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whose)), and standing alone makes it the pronoun (*Whose is this coat?*, *Whose is this?*). Note also that *whose* has **no partitive**: ✗*whose of these* does not exist, and the job goes to [which of](#which-of) — *Which of these is yours?*

Asks who owns, wrote, made, or is otherwise responsible for the thing in question.

1. *Whose* is the truck blocking the driveway?
2. *Whose* are these sunglasses?
3. Somebody left a phone on the bench and nobody knows *whose* it is.
4. *Whose* was the idea to leave at four in the morning?
5. *Whose* is this — yours or your brother's?
6. She wouldn't tell me *whose* it was.
7. *Whose* are we taking, since mine is in the shop?

**See also:** [who](#who), [whom](#whom), [whosever](#whosever), [which of](#which-of), [whose (possessive pronoun)](02-possessive.md#whose), [whose (relative)](05-relative.md#whose), [whose (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whose), [who's (not a possessive pronoun)](02-possessive.md#whos-not-a-possessive-pronoun)

## Partitive, prepositional, and complex forms

The core five rarely stand completely bare. They take a preposition in front (*to whom*), a partitive *of*-phrase behind (*which of these*), or a modifier stuck on the end (*what else*, and regionally *who all*). Two rules govern the whole section. First, ***of* makes it a pronoun**: *which flight* is a determiner, *which of the flights* is a pronoun, and the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner, so ✗*which of flights* is impossible. Second, a **fronted preposition takes *whom*, a stranded one takes *who*** in ordinary American use — *To whom did you speak?* and *Who did you speak to?* are both correct, and the mixed ✗*To who did you speak?* is the only version that is not. (*Whom did you speak to?*, stranding the preposition but keeping the object form, is grammatical as well; it is simply the stiffest of the three and rare in speech.) Three entries here are not built on the core five and belong with them anyway: *how many* and *how much*, the quantifier pair that heads its own noun phrase and takes the same partitive *of*; *whether*, the archaic *which of two* that *which* long ago swallowed; and *wherefore*, the fossil that fuses a *wh-* word onto *for* and asks why. One more, *whomst*, is not a form of English at all but an internet joke made out of *whom*, and it is labelled as such where it stands.

### how many, how much

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *how-MEN-ee, how-MUCH* &middot; standing alone the accent sits on *many* or *much*, because the noun that would otherwise carry it has been left out; put a noun back and the accent shifts forward onto *how* (*HOW many PEOPLE came?*). *How* keeps its full vowel everywhere and has **no weak form**, but the second word reduces when the phrase is unaccented in an embedded clause — *I don't know how many are left* runs to [haʊ məni], *how much of it* to [haʊ mətʃ] — and *of* is the weak /əv/, often bare [ə], so *how many of them* is [ˈhaʊ ˌmɛni ə ðəm]  
**Русский:** сколько — how many о счётных предметах, how much о количестве, мере или цене
**Type:** interrogative pronoun — a quantifier standing as the head of its own noun phrase; the same two words **in front of a noun** are determiners &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant in form; *how many* is plural and *how much* singular, and the two split the field between them by countability  
**Refers to:** a **number** (*how many*) or an **amount, extent, or price** (*how much*) of something already named or plain from the situation — *How many are coming?* counts the people under discussion, *How much is it?* asks a price. What is counted in units takes *how many*, what is measured takes *how much*. Neither asks for identity; that is [what](#what) or [which](#which).  
**Agreement:** the **understood noun** controls the verb, not the phrase: *How many **are** left?* against *How much **is** left?* In the partitive the noun inside the *of*-phrase decides — *How many of the files **were** deleted?* beside *How much of the paint **was** wasted?* — and with *how many of us / you / them* the plural is the normal choice: *How many of us **are** actually going?*  
**Pattern:** *How many* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*: *How many showed up?*) &middot; *How much* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much is* [noun phrase]? (the price question) &middot; *How many of* / *How much of* [determiner + noun]? &middot; [clause] *how many* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *How many?* / *How much?* (elliptical) &middot; never directly in front of a noun in this use — a noun there makes it a determiner  
**Contrast:** the **noun test** settles the part of speech here exactly as it does for *what* and *which*: *how many chairs* has a noun after it and is a **determiner** (see [how many](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-many) and [how much](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-much) in the determiner dictionary), while *How many do we need?* stands alone and is the pronoun in this entry. Adding *of* makes it a pronoun as well — *how many of the chairs* — and the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner, so ✗*how many of chairs* is impossible; that is the same *of*-rule that governs [which of](#which-of) and [what of](#what-of). Countability is where learners actually go wrong: ✗*how much people* → *how many people*, ✗*how many money* → *how much money*, and a measure word brings the countable form back (*how many gallons of gas*). Against [what](#what), English asks a price with *how much* and not with *what* as the subject — ✗*What costs the jacket?* is a direct translation that never sounds native, though *What did it cost?* is ordinary. And a third *how much* is neither pronoun nor determiner but a **degree adverb**, with no noun anywhere in the sentence: *How much do you care?*, *How much longer?* Nothing counted or measured is expected as the answer there.

Asks for the number or the amount, leaving the thing counted or measured to be understood.

1. *How many* are coming to the walkthrough on Saturday?
2. *How much* is the gray one in the window?
3. "We'll take the shrimp." "*How many*?"
4. *How much* of the deposit do we get back if we cancel now?
5. Nobody could tell me *how many* had already shipped.
6. *How many* of you have actually read the lease?
7. She asked *how much* it would run us to have the tree taken down.

**See also:** [what](#what), [which of](#which-of), [what of](#what-of), [how many (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-many), [how much (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-much), [how many of, how much of (determiner dictionary)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-many-of-how-much-of)

### to whom, for whom, with whom

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈhum/, /fɚ ˈhum/, /wɪθ ˈhum/ &middot; *tuh-HOOM, fer-HOOM, with-HOOM* &middot; the preposition reduces to its weak form (*to* /tə/, *for* /fɚ/) and *whom* takes the accent; in rapid speech *to whom* flattens to [tə ˈhəm], the one context in which *whom* really does weaken — the /h/ survives and the *w* stays silent throughout  
**Русский:** кому, для кого, с кем — предлог вынесен вперёд вместе с whom; формальн.
**Type:** interrogative pronoun inside a fronted (pied-piped) prepositional phrase &middot; **Case:** object, governed by the preposition &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Person / number:** any person, singular or plural; invariant, and people only  
**Refers to:** **human referents only**. With a thing, the same frame takes *which* — *to which*, *for which*, *with which*, *in which* — and with a thing in ordinary speech the preposition simply goes to the end: *What did you open it with?*  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the verb agrees with the subject of the question (*For whom **were** the tickets **bought**?*)  
**Pattern:** [preposition] *whom* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; [preposition] *whom*? (elliptical: *To whom?*) &middot; [clause] [preposition] *whom* [subject] [verb] (embedded) &middot; the everyday alternative with the preposition stranded: *Who* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] [preposition]?  
**Contrast:** this is the last position in which *whom* is genuinely obligatory. When the preposition comes forward with it, only *whom* is possible; ✗*to who*, ✗*for who*, ✗*with who* are nonstandard for essentially every careful speaker and will be corrected. When the preposition is left at the end, everyday American English uses *who*: *Who did you speak to?* Both patterns are correct English and they differ in **register, not grammar** — the fronted version is formal and can sound stiff in conversation, the stranded version is standard everywhere except the most conservative prose. The one thing to avoid is mixing them. The fossil *To Whom It May Concern* keeps its capitals as a letter salutation.

Fronts the preposition along with its object, producing the formal version of a question about a person.

1. To *whom* should I send the signed copy?
2. For *whom* is the second ticket?
3. With *whom* did you discuss the settlement?
4. To *whom* am I speaking, please?
5. He asked to *whom* the account had been transferred.
6. "I need to hand this to somebody." "To *whom*?"
7. From *whom* did the tip actually come?

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [what for](#what-for), [whom (relative)](05-relative.md#whom)

### what about

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt əˈbaʊt/ &middot; *wut-uh-BOUT* &middot; the *t* flaps and the words run together, [wʌɾəˈbaʊt]; casual *whaddabout*  
**Русский:** а как же…?, а что насчёт…? — напоминает о забытом или предлагает вариант
**Type:** interrogative pronoun plus preposition, frozen into a frame &middot; **Case:** object of *about* &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal  
**Person / number:** invariant; the following noun phrase can be any person or number  
**Refers to:** whatever the following noun phrase names — a person, a thing, a date, a plan — or an *-ing* clause naming an action (*What about calling them first?*)  
**Agreement:** the elliptical frame is **verbless**, so nothing agrees: *What about the dog?* is a complete utterance with no verb in it at all.  
**Pattern:** *What about* [noun phrase]? &middot; *What about* [*-ing* clause]? &middot; *What about it?* &middot; *What about you?* (turning the question back) &middot; *What about* [noun phrase] — [tag question]?  
**Contrast:** two jobs, told apart by context and intonation. **Raising a neglected item**: *We've booked the flights.* — *What about the dog?* **Making a suggestion**: *What about Thursday?* In the second job *how about* is the more common American choice and it also accepts a full clause (*How about we meet Thursday?*), which *what about* resists. The confrontational *What about it?* is not a request for information but a challenge meaning "so what?" The literary equivalent of the whole frame is [what of](#what-of); the blunt one is [so what](#so-what).

Puts a person or a thing back on the table — either as something being overlooked or as a suggestion.

1. *What about* the deposit? Do we get any of it back?
2. We can take my car. *What about* the kids' seats?
3. *What about* Thursday afternoon for the walkthrough?
4. I'm fine, thanks. *What about* you?
5. "You were an hour late." "*What about* it?"
6. *What about* asking the neighbors before we call the city?
7. That covers the roof. *What about* the gutters?

**See also:** [what](#what), [what of](#what-of), [so what](#so-what), [what if](#what-if)

### what all, who all

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt ɔl/, /ˈhu ɔl/ &middot; *WUT-awl, HOO-awl* &middot; usually run together as one word, [ˈwʌɾɔl], [ˈhuɔl], with *all* unstressed and hanging on the pronoun like a suffix  
**Русский:** что именно (всё), кто именно (все) — диал., юж.-амер.; требует исчерпывающего ответа
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with the exhaustive marker *all* &middot; **Case:** as the bare pronoun — *who all* subject or object, *whose all* does not exist &middot; **Register:** **regional** — Southern and Midland American English, spoken; entirely standard inside those dialects, absent from formal writing, and unusual in the North and West  
**Person / number:** invariant; *who all* takes people and expects a list, *what all* takes things and events  
**Refers to:** the **full membership** of a set. *Who all is coming?* asks for the whole guest list, not for one name; *What all did she say?* asks for everything that was said.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, exactly as with plain *who*, even though the expected answer is a list: *Who all **is** coming?* The plural *Who all are coming?* occurs but is much less common.  
**Pattern:** *Who all* [verb]? &middot; *What all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; [clause] *what all* [subject] [verb] (embedded: *I don't know what all she told him*) &middot; also *who all's* (possessive, spoken)  
**Contrast:** the *all* here is **not** a quantifier attached to a noun — it is an exhaustiveness marker attached to the question word, and it changes what counts as a complete answer. Plain *Who came?* can be answered with a single name; *Who all came?* asks you to name everybody. Speakers outside the region reach for a periphrasis instead — *Who came, all told?*, *Who was there besides Ray?*, *Everything she said — what was it?* — none of which is as economical. Label it accurately: this is a genuine feature of Southern American English rather than an error, but an editor outside the South will delete it, and it does not belong in formal prose. The determiner counterpart takes a noun directly (*what all kinds of paperwork*) and is covered in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-all).

Asks for an exhaustive answer — every person, or everything, that fits.

1. *Who all* is coming to the cookout Saturday?
2. *What all* did the inspector write up?
3. I can't even remember *what all* we ordered.
4. *Who all* did you invite besides the Hendersons?
5. *What all* is included in the base price?
6. She told me *who all* had signed the letter.
7. *Who all* was at the courthouse that morning?

**See also:** [who](#who), [what](#what), [what else, who else](#what-else-who-else), [what all (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-all)

### what else, who else

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈɛls/, /hu ˈɛls/ &middot; *wut-ELS, hoo-ELS* &middot; the accent moves onto *else*, and the *t* of *what* flaps into it  
**Русский:** что ещё, кто ещё — спрашивает об оставшемся сверх уже названного
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with the postposed modifier *else* &middot; **Case:** as the bare pronoun; the possessive is *who else's* &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant; *who else* people, *what else* things  
**Refers to:** the **remainder** of a set after something has already been accounted for. Both forms presuppose that at least one item is already known, which is why *What else do you need?* is odd as the first thing you say to someone.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**: *What else is on the list?*, *Who else is coming?* — the same default as bare *who* and *what*, no matter how long the remaining list turns out to be.  
**Pattern:** *What else* [verb]? &middot; *Who else* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *who else's* [noun] (possessive) &middot; *What else?* (elliptical) &middot; [clause] *what else* [subject] [verb] (embedded)  
**Contrast:** *else* always follows the pronoun and never precedes it: ✗*else what*. The possessive is **who else's**, with the *-'s* on the end of the whole phrase exactly as in *somebody else's* — ✗*whose else* is not English. The object form ***whom else*** exists and behaves exactly like [whom](#whom): formal, and genuinely needed only after a fronted preposition (*To whom else did you show it?*), where ordinary speech says *Who else did you show it to?* And note the gap in the paradigm: ***which else* is not used**. For a remainder inside a defined set, English switches to a determiner — *Which other flight is there?*, *What other options do we have?* *What else* also has a rhetorical life meaning "obviously": *What else could I say?*

Asks for what remains after the obvious answers have been given.

1. *What else* do we need from the store?
2. *Who else* knew about the transfer before Monday?
3. *Who else's* car was in the lot at midnight?
4. *What else* did the doctor tell you?
5. I asked him *what else* had gone missing.
6. *Who else* is there to call at this hour?
7. "You apologized?" "*What else* could I do?"

**See also:** [what](#what), [who](#who), [what all, who all](#what-all-who-all), [somebody else's](02-possessive.md#somebody-elses), [what other, which other](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-other-which-other)

### what of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt əv/ &middot; *WUT-uv* &middot; *of* is the weak /əv/ and often reduces to a single [ə]; the *t* flaps, giving [ˈwʌɾəv]  
**Русский:** а как же…?, что же с…? — книжн., риторический вопрос об упущенном
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with an *of*-phrase &middot; **Case:** none — the *of*-phrase is its complement &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or rhetorical; rare in ordinary conversation  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** a topic that has been set aside — a person, a plan, or a thing already in the discussion — or, in the partitive sense, a portion of something  
**Agreement:** the frame is **verbless** in its main use: *What of the others?* has no verb to agree with anything. In the partitive sense the verb agrees with the *of*-phrase: *What of the original building **survives**?*  
**Pattern:** *What of* [noun phrase]? (topic shift) &middot; *What of it?* (fixed challenge) &middot; *What of* [noun phrase] [verb]? (partitive: *What of the estate is left?*) &middot; headline and rhetorical use with no verb at all  
**Contrast:** it does the same job as [what about](#what-about), one register higher: *What of the survivors?* is what a novelist writes, *What about the survivors?* is what a person says out loud. **What of it?** is fixed, confrontational, and means exactly [so what](#so-what). Keep it apart from the partitive [which of](#which-of): *which of the plans* asks you to pick one from a known list, *what of the plans* asks what is to be said about them. The determiner dictionary treats the same string from the determiner side under [what of](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-of).

Turns the conversation to something that has been left out, or asks how much of a thing remains.

**1. Topic shift (literary).** "And what is to be said about X?" — *What of the families still waiting?* Almost always rhetorical, and almost always in writing or in speech that is being performed.

**2. What of it? (fixed).** A challenge, equivalent to *So what?* and just as blunt.

**3. Partitive (formal).** "What part of" — *What of the original manuscript survives?* Here a verb follows and agrees with the *of*-phrase.

1. *What of* the families still waiting for news?
2. We have the funding. But *what of* the timeline?
3. "You left without telling anyone." "*What of it*?"
4. *What of* the promises made last spring?
5. And *what of* the man who signed the order?
6. *What of* the original building actually survives?

**See also:** [what about](#what-about), [so what](#so-what), [which of](#which-of), [what](#what), [what of (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-of)

### wherefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛrfɔr/ &middot; *WAIR-for* &middot; both syllables keep their full vowels when it opens a question and takes the accent; in the surviving legal and conjunctive use it is unaccented and the second syllable **weakens** to [-fɚ], [ˈwɛrfɚ], exactly the reduction *therefore* undergoes. Plain /w/ for most Americans, /hw-/ for speakers who keep the older onset  
**Русский:** почему, отчего, по какой причине — устар.; архаичный предок why и what for
**Type:** interrogative pro-form — *where-* (here meaning "what") fused with the preposition *for*, the same construction as *whereof* and *whereby*; in modern grammatical description an interrogative **adverb** rather than a pronoun, since it fills an adverbial slot and can be neither subject nor object &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **archaic** — an American meets it in Shakespeare, in the King James Bible and hymn texts that quote it, and, still alive and daily, in the closing paragraph of legal pleadings  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** a **reason or purpose** — "for what reason", "to what end". Never a place, and never a person or a thing: what it stands in for is an adverbial, which is why it sits at the edge of this dictionary rather than in the middle of it. For a thing the parallel fossil is *[whereof](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereof)* ("of which"), and for a person there is no *where-* form at all.  
**Agreement:** none of its own. As an adjunct it never controls the verb; in the archaic question the verb agrees with its own subject and inverts around it — *Wherefore **art thou** Romeo?*, *Wherefore **do the wicked live**?*  
**Pattern:** *Wherefore* [verb] [subject]? (archaic direct question, with inversion and no *do*-support) &middot; [clause] *wherefore* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; the living consequence marker, which is not a question at all: [clause]; *wherefore*, [clause] &middot; *WHEREFORE*, [party] prays … (the prayer for relief that closes an American complaint) &middot; the fixed plural noun *the whys and wherefores*  
**Contrast:** the misreading first, because it is nearly universal: Juliet's *Wherefore art thou Romeo?* asks **why he has to be a Montague**, not where he is standing, and the balcony makes the wrong reading almost irresistible. What replaced the word is *why* in general and *[what for](#what-for)* where the question is specifically about purpose — *What did you do that for?* is the living descendant of the construction, preposition stranded instead of fused. Be honest about the classification: unlike [whether](#whether), which genuinely was a pronoun meaning "which of two" before it died, *wherefore* never filled a noun-phrase slot at all; it is catalogued here because the *wh-* question words belong together and because its *where-* half is the same pro-form that shows up in *whereof*, *wherein*, and *whereby* — all of which are catalogued with [the archaic and dialectal pronouns](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereof). Its two living jobs are not interrogative and are covered elsewhere: the **conjunctive adverb** meaning "therefore," which takes a semicolon before it and a comma after, and the **legal** *WHEREFORE* clause, both in [the conjunction dictionary](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#wherefore). Add the fixed noun *the whys and wherefores*, and that is the whole of the word's modern life; nothing here belongs in a question an American would actually ask.

Asks for the reason — the archaic *why*, fused out of *where* and *for*.

1. "*Wherefore* art thou Romeo?" (Shakespeare, *Romeo and Juliet* — asking why he must be a Montague, not where he is)
2. "*Wherefore* do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?" (Job 21:7, King James Version)
3. "*Wherefore* criest thou unto me?" (Exodus 14:15)
4. "Friend, *wherefore* art thou come?" (Matthew 26:50)
5. The clerk asked *wherefore* the writ had issued, and nobody in the room could say. (embedded — costume English, statement order)
6. *WHEREFORE*, Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court enter judgment in her favor. (the living American use — a consequence marker meaning "therefore," not a question)
7. She wanted all the whys and *wherefores* before she would sign anything. (the fixed plural noun, likewise not a question)

**See also:** [what for](#what-for), [what](#what), [whether](#whether), [whereof](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereof), [whereby](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whereby), [whence](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whence), [wherefore (conjunctive adverb)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#wherefore), [wherefore (formal and archaic)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/12-formal-archaic.md#wherefore), [why](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/09-place-relative.md#why)

### whether

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðɚ/ &middot; *WETH-er* &middot; a perfect homophone of *weather* for most Americans; speakers who keep the older *wh-* onset say /ˈhwɛðɚ/, and theirs is the one accent in which the two words are told apart by sound. As a question word it takes the accent and keeps its full vowel; in the living conjunction use it is unaccented and weakens to [wəðɚ] (*I don't know whether it matters*), and in fast speech the medial /ð/ can drop altogether, [ˈwɛɚ]  
**Русский:** который из двух — устар. и вымершее местоимение; ныне эту роль целиком взяло which
**Type:** interrogative pronoun — **archaic**; the living word spelled this way is a subordinating conjunction &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** **archaic**, out of ordinary use since the seventeenth century; an American meets it today in the King James Bible and in older literature quoting it, and essentially nowhere else  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; construed as **singular**, since it picks one out of two  
**Refers to:** **one of exactly two** things or people already named — the two-member counterpart of [which](#which). The restriction is built into the word: *whether* carries the same comparative *-ther* that sits in *either*, *neither*, and *other*, and Old English *hwæþer* meant "which of two" where *hwilc* asked out of many. For three or more, older English used *which* — which is what English now uses for both.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, whichever of the two turns out to be the answer: *Whether **is** greater, the gold, or the temple?* (Matthew 23:17). With a partitive *of*-phrase it stayed singular as well — *Whether of them twain **did** the will of his father?* (Matthew 21:31).  
**Pattern:** *Whether* [singular verb] [option] *or* [option]? (direct question — archaic) &middot; *Whether of* [the two / them / them twain] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (partitive — archaic) &middot; the living patterns belong to the conjunction, not the pronoun: [clause] *whether* [clause] (*She asked whether the check had cleared*) &middot; *whether or not* &middot; *whether to* [verb] &middot; *whether* … *or* …  
**Contrast:** one spelling, two words, and only the dead one is a pronoun. As a **pronoun** *whether* meant "which of two", and it is gone: no American says ✗*Whether do you want, the soup or the salad?* — that sentence is *Which do you want …?* today, because [which](#which) absorbed the job and English gave up the two-versus-many distinction entirely, the same way it gave up *hwilc* against *hwæþer*. What survives is the **conjunction**, which introduces an embedded yes/no or alternative question (*I asked whether he was coming*) and is covered in [the conjunction dictionary](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#whether); it fills no noun-phrase slot, takes no case, and can never itself be the answer to anything, which is exactly what keeps it out of this dictionary. In that job it is *if* that has to be kept apart from it: only *whether* survives after a preposition (*the question of whether to appeal*), only *whether* goes straight in front of *to* (*whether to sell*), and only *whether* keeps the alternative reading unambiguous — *Tell me if you're going* can be read as "in the event that you go", while *Tell me whether you're going* cannot. Older English also put *whether* directly in front of a noun, and a word in that position is a **determiner** rather than a pronoun; that use died with the pronoun, and the living interrogative determiners are in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative).

Asks which one of two — the archaic ancestor of a job *which* now does alone.

1. "*Whether* of them twain did the will of his father?" (Matthew 21:31, King James Version)
2. "*Whether* is greater, the gold, or the temple that sanctifieth the gold?" (Matthew 23:17)
3. "*Whether* of the twain will ye that I release unto you?" (Matthew 27:21)
4. "Shew *whether* of these two thou hast chosen." (Acts 1:24)
5. "*Whether* is easier, to say, Thy sins be forgiven thee; or to say, Arise, and walk?" (Matthew 9:5)
6. *Whether* of the two roads is the shorter, the river road or the turnpike?
7. She asked *whether* the check had cleared. (The living use — a conjunction introducing an embedded question, not a pronoun standing in for the answer.)

**See also:** [which](#which), [which of](#which-of), [what](#what), [whether (conjunction)](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#whether), [whether or not](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/10-complementizers.md#whether-or-not)

### which of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪtʃ əv/ &middot; *WICH-uv* &middot; *of* weakens to /əv/ and in fast speech to [ə], so *which of us* runs to [wɪtʃ əˈvʌs], or with the /v/ dropped altogether, [wɪtʃ əˈʌs]  
**Русский:** который из, кто из — просит выбрать из группы, названной в самом вопросе
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with a partitive *of*-phrase &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant; singular or plural according to the answer expected  
**Refers to:** members of a set **spelled out in the *of*-phrase** — people and things equally: *which of the applicants*, *which of these keys*, *which of us*  
**Agreement:** the verb follows the **expected answer**, not the plural noun after *of*: *Which of these keys **opens** the shed?* when one is expected, *Which of these keys **open** the shed?* when more than one might. With *which of us / you / them* the default is singular: *Which of us **is** going to tell him?*  
**Pattern:** *Which of* [the / these / my + plural noun]? &middot; *Which of* [us / you / them]? &middot; *Which of* [the two]? &middot; *Which of* [noun phrase] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; never before a bare plural: ✗*which of applicants*  
**Contrast:** the ***of*-test** is what makes this a pronoun rather than a determiner: *which flight* takes a noun directly and is a determiner, *which of the flights* stands on its own and is a pronoun. Because the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner, *of* must be followed by *the*, a demonstrative, a possessive, a number word, or a pronoun. Against [who](#who) for people, *which of them* selects from a group already named while *who* asks for an identity out of nowhere. And note that [whose](#whose) has no partitive at all — ✗*whose of these is yours* → *Which of these is yours?*

Asks the hearer to pick one or more members out of a set that the question itself names.

1. *Which of* these chargers is yours?
2. *Which of* us is going to tell her?
3. *Which of* the two routes is faster this time of day?
4. *Which of* the applicants did the committee actually interview?
5. I couldn't say *which of* them called first.
6. *Which of* you has a car seat in the back?
7. *Which of* the boxes are ready to go out today?

**See also:** [which](#which), [who of, whom of](#who-of-whom-of), [whose](#whose), [whichever](#whichever), [which of (determiner dictionary)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-of)

### which one, which ones

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ ˈwʌn/, /wɪtʃ ˈwʌnz/ &middot; *wich-WUN, wich-WUNZ* &middot; the two words run together into one piece, [wɪtʃˈwʌn]. The accent lands on *one* only when the choice itself is being pressed (*Which **one**, the blue or the gray?*); in the ordinary question it stays on *which* and *one* drops to the **weak** [wən] — *Which one do you want?* is [ˈwɪtʃ wən …], and *which ones* weakens the same way to [wənz]. Plain /w/ for most Americans, /hwɪtʃ/ for Southern and older speakers who keep the *wh-* onset  
**Русский:** который именно, которые именно — разговорная развёрнутая форма which
**Type:** interrogative pronoun phrase — the interrogative determiner *which* sitting on the prop-word *one*, the two of them filling a single noun-phrase slot &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** neutral; this is the ordinary spoken form, and bare *which* is the clipped, slightly more formal one  
**Person / number:** third person; ***which one*** singular and ***which ones*** plural, and the form chosen announces the number of the expected answer before the verb arrives  
**Refers to:** members of a **known, bounded set** — things, animals, and people alike, exactly as bare [which](#which) does. With people it picks a member out of a group in view (*Which one is your brother?*) and never asks for a stranger's identity: ✗*Which one is she?* → *Who is she?*  
**Agreement:** the verb follows the **form chosen**: *Which one **is** yours?* against *Which ones **are** yours?* With a partitive *of*-phrase the singular form keeps a singular verb however plural the noun after *of* is — *Which one of these keys **opens** the shed?*, *Which one of us **is** going to tell him?* That is the practical advantage over bare *which*, where *Which is yours?* leaves the number to be guessed at.  
**Pattern:** *Which one* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; *Which one* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Which one of* [determiner + plural noun]? &middot; *Which ones* [verb]? &middot; [clause] *which one* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *Which one?* / *Which ones?* (elliptical) &middot; *which one* … *the other one* (contrastive pair)  
**Contrast:** against bare **[which](#which)** the meaning is identical and the weight is not: *one* spells the number out and is what Americans actually say, while bare *Which do you want?* is a shade more clipped and more formal. Against **[which of](#which-of)**, the set is named in the sentence (*which of these chargers*) instead of left understood, and the two combine freely: *which one of these chargers*. Against **[who](#who)** for people, *which one* selects from a set in view where *who* asks for an identity out of nowhere. **What is a determiner here** is worth stating plainly: inside the phrase, *which* is a determiner sitting on the noun-like *one*, and it is the whole phrase — not *which* alone — that does the pronoun's work, which is the same analysis given to [this one / that one](04-demonstrative.md#this-one--that-one) and the reason this entry sits in the pronoun dictionary while *which flight* sits in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative). Put a real noun in the slot and the whole thing is determiner plus noun; keep the prop-word *one* and it is this entry. One frequent worry does not apply here: the American objection to *these ones* (see [these ones / those ones](04-demonstrative.md#these-ones--those-ones)) is an objection to a demonstrative propped on *ones*, and no editor blinks at *which ones*. And there is no *what* counterpart — ✗*What one do you want?* is not American idiom, because an open-set question has no set to count members out of; it is simply *What do you want?*

Asks the hearer to pick a member out of a set in view, with the number of the answer stated by the pronoun itself.

1. *Which one* do you want, the blue or the gray?
2. *Which ones* are ready to go out today?
3. *Which one* of you has the parking pass?
4. I couldn't tell *which one* she was pointing at.
5. "Hand me the smaller wrench." "*Which one*?"
6. *Which one* is your brother — the one in the ball cap?
7. She never said *which ones* she had already paid for.

**See also:** [which](#which), [which of](#which-of), [what](#what), [who](#who), [whichever](#whichever), [this one / that one](04-demonstrative.md#this-one--that-one), [these ones / those ones](04-demonstrative.md#these-ones--those-ones), [one](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#one), [ones](08-indefinite-quantifying.md#ones), [which (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative)

### who of, whom of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhu əv/, /ˈhum əv/ &middot; *HOO-uv, HOOM-uv* &middot; *of* weak /əv/  
**Русский:** кто из — книжн., риторическое; о членах названной группы людей
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with a partitive *of*-phrase &middot; **Case:** *who of* subject, *whom of* object &middot; **Register:** literary and rhetorical; marginal in ordinary American English  
**Person / number:** invariant; people only  
**Refers to:** members of a group of people named in the *of*-phrase  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**: *Who of us **has** not made that mistake?*  
**Pattern:** *Who of* [us / you / them] [verb]? &middot; *Who of* [the + plural noun] [verb]? &middot; [preposition] *whom of* [noun phrase]? (rare) &middot; almost always in a rhetorical question expecting the answer "none of us"  
**Contrast:** the ordinary partitive for people is [which of](#which-of) — *Which of us is going?* — and that is what to use in plain prose. *Who of* survives mainly in the rhetorical "who among us" register, and even there *who among us* is the more idiomatic frame. *Whom of* is rarer still and reads as hypercorrect unless a preposition actually governs it; if a preposition does not, use *who of* or rewrite. Nothing is ungrammatical here, but the whole entry is a stylistic choice rather than a working part of the language.

Picks out members of a named group of people, usually in a rhetorical question.

1. *Who of* us has not sat through a meeting like that?
2. *Who of* the original crew is still with the company?
3. *Whom of* the three finalists would you trust with the account?
4. He asked *who of* the tenants had actually complained.
5. *Who of* you would have said no to an offer like that?
6. *Who of* them can honestly claim to be surprised?

**See also:** [which of](#which-of), [who](#who), [whom](#whom), [what of](#what-of)

### whomst, whomstve

**Pronunciation:** /humst/, /ˈhumstəv/ &middot; *HOOMST, HOOMST-uv* &middot; the *w* is silent, exactly as in *whom*; the final cluster is awkward enough that an intrusive [p] often turns up, [humpst]. There is **no weak form** — the word exists in order to be noticed, so it always carries the accent — and the longer forms scan as written: *whomst'd* /humstəd/ *HOOMST-ud*, *whomst'd've* /ˈhumstədəv/ *HOOMST-ud-uv*  
**Русский:** неологизм, русского эквивалента нет — шуточное интернет-передразнивание чопорного whom
**Type:** jocular pseudo-archaic variant of [whom](#whom) — an internet coinage, **not** a historical English form &middot; **Case:** object in shape; in practice it is written into subject slots as freely as object ones, because the joke does not track case &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard** — internet slang, humorous, current online among younger American writers since the later 2010s and out of place in any writing that is not itself joking  
**Person / number:** any person, singular or plural; people only, and invariant  
**Refers to:** **human referents**, exactly as *whom* does. The extra letters carry no meaning whatever: what they add is a performance of over-formality, usually aimed at the writer's own pretension rather than at anyone else's.  
**Agreement:** none of its own in the object use. Where the joke drops it into subject position — which real *whom* does not allow — the verb is **singular** by default: *Whomst **has** the aux cord?*  
**Pattern:** *Whomst* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Whomst* [verb]? (subject use, ungrammatical for real *whom* and part of the joke) &middot; [preposition] *whomst*? &middot; bare *Whomst?* / *Whomst've?* as an exclamation of mock astonishment &middot; the escalating ladder *whom* → *whomst* → *whomst'd* → *whomst'd've*, each rung longer than the last  
**Variants:** *whomst've*, *whomstve*, *whomst'd*, *whomst'd've* — the apostrophes are optional and inconsistent, since nothing is actually being contracted — plus open-ended nonce chains built by piling on more clitics (*whomst'd'st*, and strings written purely for the joke of their length); *whomst've* is the commonest of the extended spellings, and *whomstve* the commonest way of writing it without punctuation  
**Contrast:** there is no historical *whomst*, and saying so plainly is the whole entry. The *-st* is borrowed by ear from two real things — the *-st* of *whilst*, *amongst*, and *amidst*, and the old second-person verb ending of *thou dost* and *thou canst* — but it never attached to *whom* in Old, Middle, or Early Modern English, and no standard dictionary records the word. The genuinely old extended forms are [whomso](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whomso) and [whomsoever](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whomsoever), and neither is related to this. What the joke is about is [whom](#whom) itself: in American English *whom* already reads as a status marker, the word people reach for when they want to sound careful and the word they most often get wrong (the hypercorrect ✗*Whom shall I say is calling?*), so bolting a fake ending onto it mocks that impulse — commonly the writer's own. Practically, there is no context in which it is the right choice for a real question: where a true object form is wanted the word is *whom*, obligatory only after a fronted preposition, and everywhere else ordinary American English says [who](#who).

Mocks the formality of *whom* by piling on endings that never existed.

1. *Whomst* has the aux cord?
2. To *whomst* do I owe the pleasure?
3. "*Whomst*?" she typed back, three seconds after his text.
4. *Whomst've* among us has not hit reply-all by accident?
5. *Whomst* did they put in charge of the spreadsheet, exactly?
6. The group chat spent an hour escalating from *whom* to *whomst'd've*.
7. He signed off "regards, *whomstve*," and nobody in the thread acknowledged it.

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [whomever](#whomever), [whomso](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whomso), [whomsoever](10-archaic-dialectal.md#whomsoever), [whom (relative)](05-relative.md#whom)

## The emphatic *-ever* forms

Adding *-ever* to a question word turns it into an **emphatic question** expressing astonishment: *Whatever do you mean?*, *Whoever told you that?* Two things need saying up front. First, in American English this construction is dated and reads as literary or British — the everyday American intensifiers are *on earth*, *in the world*, *the heck*, and *the hell* (*What on earth do you mean?*), which get their own entry at the end of this section. Second, careful writers spell the emphatic question as **two words** (*What ever happened to Ted?*) to keep it apart from the fused relative *whatever* (*Take whatever you need*), which is a different word doing a different job and is covered with [the fused relatives](09-fused-generic-dummy.md). Nothing here is wrong; it is a register choice, and only *whoever* is still fully at home in ordinary American speech.

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wut-EV-er* &middot; the *t* flaps, giving [wʌˈɾɛvɚ], and casual speech reduces the first syllable to [wə-]; in the emphatic question a heavy accent lands on *-EV-*  
**Русский:** что же это такое?! — what с изумлением; в амер. англ. книжн., устар., «на британский лад»
**Type:** emphatic interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none — one form for subject and object &middot; **Register:** literary, dated, or British-flavored in American English  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; construed as singular  
**Refers to:** things, events, and actions, exactly as bare [what](#what) does — the *-ever* adds astonishment, not meaning. It never refers to a person's identity.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, as with *what*: *Whatever is going on?*, *Whatever has become of them?*  
**Pattern:** *Whatever* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; *Whatever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; two-word variant *What ever* …? &middot; American colloquial equivalents: *What on earth …?*, *What in the world …?*, *What the heck …?*  
**Contrast:** three different *whatever*s, and only the first is in this file. **(a) Emphatic interrogative** — *Whatever do you mean?* — the entry here. **(b) Fused relative** — *Take whatever you need*, meaning "anything that", covered with [the fused relatives](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever); it appears in statements, not questions. **(c) The dismissive interjection** — *Whatever.*, meaning "I don't care" — an interjection, not a pronoun, and rude in most company. Spelling follows the split: two words for the emphatic question, one word for the relative. The determiner *whatever* takes a noun directly (*whatever excuse he gives*) and lives in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever).

Asks *what* with astonishment, bewilderment, or exasperation built in.

1. *Whatever* do you mean by that?
2. *Whatever* possessed you to sign it without reading it?
3. *Whatever* is the matter with the dog tonight?
4. *Whatever* happened to the couple who lived upstairs?
5. *Whatever* will they think of next?
6. *Whatever* did she say to make him leave like that?

**See also:** [what](#what), [whoever](#whoever), [whichever](#whichever), [what (echo)](#what-echo), [whatever (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whatever), [whatever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever)

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wich-EV-er* &middot; the accent on *-EV-*; /hwɪtʃˈɛvɚ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-* onset  
**Русский:** который же из них?! — which с недоверием к самим вариантам; редк., книжн.
**Type:** emphatic interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** **rare** as a question word — literary at best; ordinary and frequent only in its fused-relative use  
**Person / number:** third person, invariant; singular or plural by the answer expected  
**Refers to:** members of a **known, bounded set**, like bare [which](#which); the *-ever* adds astonishment that any member of the set could be the answer  
**Agreement:** follows the expected answer, singular by default: *Whichever is he going to pick?*  
**Pattern:** *Whichever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Whichever of* [noun phrase] …? &middot; two-word variant *Which ever* …? &middot; the ordinary American alternative: *Which on earth …?* or simply *Which …?*  
**Contrast:** be honest about the frequency: as a genuine question word *whichever* is close to unattested in modern American speech, and a speaker wanting the emphatic version of *which* just says *Which one on earth …?* or leans on intonation. Overwhelmingly, *whichever* is a **fused relative** (*Take whichever you want* = "any one that") or a **determiner** (*whichever route is faster*), and neither of those is a question — see [the fused relatives](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whichever) and [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichever). If what you are writing is a question and the *-ever* form sounds strained, that is because it is; drop back to *which*.

Asks *which* with disbelief that any of the available options could be the answer.

1. *Whichever* of the two could he possibly have meant?
2. *Whichever* will she choose, after complaining about all of them?
3. *Whichever* did they end up sending, in the end?
4. *Whichever* of these routes could be the scenic one?
5. *Whichever* would you pick, if you had to pick one today?
6. *Whichever* of the applicants is he thinking of, after that interview?

**See also:** [which](#which), [which of](#which-of), [whatever](#whatever), [whichever (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whichever), [whichever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichever)

### whoever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-EV-er* &middot; the two words run together with no break, [huˈwɛvɚ]; the accent on *-EV-*  
**Русский:** кто же это?! — who с изумлением; разг., единственная живая -ever-форма вопроса
**Type:** emphatic interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** subject (and object in everyday use, exactly as with *who*) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — the one *-ever* question form still fully alive in American speech  
**Person / number:** third person in agreement; invariant, and singular by default  
**Refers to:** **people only**, like bare [who](#who); the *-ever* adds disbelief that any person could be the answer  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**: *Whoever is calling at this hour?*, *Whoever wants a job like that?*  
**Pattern:** *Whoever* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; *Whoever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; two-word variant *Who ever* …? &middot; American colloquial equivalents: *Who on earth …?*, *Who in the world …?*  
**Contrast:** against the **fused relative** *whoever* (*Whoever finishes first should start the next batch*), which is a statement subject meaning "the person who" and belongs with [the fused relatives](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever). One string, two grammars, and the question mark tells them apart: *Whoever told you that?* is a question about a person's identity, while *Whoever told you that was wrong* is a statement about whoever it was. Case: the emphatic question uses *whoever* even in object position in ordinary speech (*Whoever did they hire?*), and [whomever](#whomever) there is a formality most Americans skip. The classic error is the mirror image — ✗*give it to whomever wants it*, where the word is the **subject** of its own clause and must be *whoever*.

Asks *who* with astonishment that any person at all fits the description.

1. *Whoever* told you that?
2. *Whoever* could be calling at this hour?
3. *Whoever* would want to drive that far twice a week?
4. *Whoever* let the dog in the house?
5. *Whoever* thought putting the printer upstairs was a good idea?
6. *Whoever* does she think she is, rewriting the whole contract?

**See also:** [who](#who), [whomever](#whomever), [whatever](#whatever), [whosever](#whosever), [whoever (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whoever)

### whomever

**Pronunciation:** /humˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoom-EV-er* &middot; the accent on *-EV-*  
**Русский:** кого же?! — объектная пара к whoever; очень редк., подчёркнуто формальн.
**Type:** emphatic interrogative pronoun &middot; **Case:** object &middot; **Register:** **very rare and highly formal** as a question word; the ordinary spoken form in an object question is [whoever](#whoever)  
**Person / number:** any person, singular or plural; invariant, and people only  
**Refers to:** **human referents only**, in an object slot  
**Agreement:** none of its own — as an object it never controls the verb: *Whomever **did** the committee **appoint**?*  
**Pattern:** *Whomever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; [preposition] *whomever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; two-word variant *Whom ever* …?  
**Contrast:** everything true of [whom](#whom) is true here in a sharper form. As a question word *whomever* is vanishingly rare and, outside a fronted preposition, it reads as hypercorrect; even careful American writers use *Whoever did they hire?* or simply *Who did they hire?* Where the word earns its keep is the **fused relative in object position** — *Give it to whomever you choose* — and even there the test matters: the case is decided by the job the word does **inside its own clause**, so *give it to whoever wants it* is correct because *whoever* is the subject of *wants*, and ✗*whomever wants it* is the single most common *-ever* error in edited American prose. When in doubt, substitute *him*: if *him* fits the slot inside the clause, *whomever* is right.

Asks *whom* with astonishment — the object-case, formal counterpart of *whoever*.

1. *Whomever* did they appoint to run the committee?
2. To *whomever* could that letter have been addressed?
3. *Whomever* did the trustees have in mind for the position?
4. For *whomever* was such an enormous house built?
5. *Whomever* did he imagine he was fooling?
6. *Whomever* could they have been protecting all those years?

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [whoever](#whoever), [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom), [whomever (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whomever)

### whosever

**Pronunciation:** /huzˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *hooz-EV-er* &middot; the *w* is silent and the accent falls on *-EV-*; the spelling variants *whoseever* and the two-word *whose ever* are also found  
**Русский:** чей же?; чей бы он ни был — редк., книжн., юр.; о неизвестном владельце
**Type:** emphatic interrogative possessive pronoun (and a fused-relative possessive) &middot; **Case:** possessive &middot; **Register:** **rare** — formal, legal, or archaic  
**Person / number:** invariant; asks about a possessor of any person, number, or gender  
**Refers to:** **possessors**, effectively always human in this use  
**Agreement:** the verb agrees with the **thing possessed**, never with *whosever*: *Whosever these are, they don't belong here.*  
**Pattern:** *Whosever* [be] [noun phrase]? (question — archaic) &middot; *Whosever* [pronoun] [be], … (fused relative: *Whosever it is, they should claim it*) &middot; the determiner use takes a noun directly: *whosever coat*  
**Contrast:** it is the *-ever* form of [whose](#whose), and both of its uses are rare. As a **question** (*Whosever can this be?*) it is essentially archaic; an American says *Whose on earth is this?* or, far more often, just *Whose is this?* As a **fused relative** (*Whosever it is should come and get it*) it still turns up in writing, though many writers prefer *Whoever's* or rewrite the sentence entirely. One spelling point: the *-ever* attaches to the already-possessive *whose*, so there is no apostrophe anywhere — ✗*whose'ever*, ✗*whosever's*. The determiner counterpart is in [the determiner dictionary](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever).

Asks — or, more often, concedes without asking — who the owner is, when the owner is unknown.

1. *Whosever* can this umbrella be, left here all week?
2. *Whosever* it is, they should have claimed it by now.
3. The wallet on the counter — *whosever* it is, leave it where it is.
4. *Whosever* these are, they don't belong in the hallway.
5. *Whosever* it turns out to be, the boat still has to be moved.
6. "*Whosever* is this?" she asked, holding up a single glove.

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [whoever](#whoever), [whosever (possessive pronoun)](02-possessive.md#whosever), [whosever (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#whosever), [whosever (determiner)](../../09-determiners/catalog/03-possessives.md#whosever)

### what on earth, who on earth

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ɑn ˈɚθ/, /hu ɑn ˈɚθ/ &middot; *wut-on-URTH, hoo-on-URTH* &middot; the accent moves off the question word and onto the intensifier, and *on earth* links into one piece, [ɑˈnɚθ]; the variants scan the same way, with *the* reduced to its weak /ðə/ and the accent on the intensifier — *the heck* /ðə ˈhɛk/ *thuh-HEK*, *the hell* /ðə ˈhɛl/ *thuh-HEL*, *in the world* /ɪn ðə ˈwɚld/ *in-thuh-WERLD*, *in God's name* /ɪn ˈɡɑdz ˌneɪm/ *in-GODZ-name*  
**Русский:** что же (кто же), чёрт возьми — усиление вопроса; вариант the hell — груб.
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with an emphatic adjunct &middot; **Case:** as the bare pronoun — *who on earth* subject or object, *whom on earth* the formal object form, *whose on earth* the possessive &middot; **Register:** *on earth* and *in the world* neutral to informal; *the heck* and *the devil* mild; ***the hell* profane**, and out of place in writing that is not quoting speech  
**Person / number:** invariant; *who on earth* takes people, *what on earth* things and events  
**Refers to:** exactly what the bare pronoun refers to — the adjunct adds astonishment, exasperation, or impatience and changes nothing else. *Who on earth called at midnight?* asks the same question as *Who called at midnight?*, with feeling attached.  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, the same default as bare [who](#who) and [what](#what): *Who on earth **is** knocking?*, *What on earth **was** that noise?* The adjunct is invisible to agreement.  
**Pattern:** *What on earth* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Who on earth* [verb]? (subject question — no *do*) &middot; the adjunct sits **immediately after** the question word and never floats: ✗*What do you on earth mean?* &middot; also *Which on earth …?* and *Whose on earth …?* (both marginal) &middot; bare *What on earth?* / *What the heck?* (elliptical)  
**Variants:** *what in the world*, *who in the world*, and marginally *which in the world* (neutral to informal, and the commonest American alternative to *on earth*) &middot; *what the heck*, *who the heck*, *what the dickens*, *what the devil* (mild, and the printable ones) &middot; *what the hell*, *who the hell* (profane), and *what the fuck*, *who the fuck* (obscene) — speech and quoted dialogue only &middot; *what in God's name*, *who in God's name*, *what in heaven's name* (grave rather than casual) &middot; *what in blazes*, *what in tarnation* (dated and rural; an American now meets *tarnation* mainly in cartoons and old Westerns) &middot; every one of them sits immediately after the question word and changes nothing but the register  
**Contrast:** this is the **living American replacement for the [*-ever* forms](#whatever)**. Where an older novel or a British speaker has *Whatever do you mean?*, an American says *What on earth do you mean?* or *What the heck do you mean?* The family sorts by register and nothing else: *on earth* and *in the world* go anywhere, *the heck* and *the devil* are mild, *in God's name* is the emphatic-formal one, and *the hell* is profanity — accurate to label, easy to misjudge in print. Two spin-offs are not questions at all: *what the hell* as a shrug of consent (*Oh, what the hell — order the big one*), and the bare *What the …?* left unfinished as an exclamation of surprise. None of it belongs in edited prose except inside dialogue.

Asks the same question the bare pronoun asks, with astonishment or exasperation attached.

1. *What on earth* is that supposed to mean?
2. *Who on earth* would drive out there in this weather?
3. *What on earth* did you do to the printer?
4. *Who on earth* told him about the party?
5. *What in the world* are we going to tell the inspector?
6. "*What the heck* is that noise?" he said, getting up.
7. She looked at the bill and thought, *what on earth*.

## Echo questions, ellipsis, and fixed expressions

These are questions with most of the sentence taken away. An **echo question** leaves the *wh-* word exactly where the missing information was — *You did **what**?* — with no fronting, no *do*-support, and a hard rise at the end; it asks the speaker to repeat or confirm what they just said. The rest of the section is **ellipsis** that has frozen: *What for?*, *So what?*, *Who cares?*, *Now what?*, *…or what?* Each began as a full question, lost everything but the question word and one companion, and hardened into a fixed expression with its own meaning and its own intonation — several of them, like *So what?* and *Who cares?*, take a question mark by convention while expecting no answer at all. Three of them — *what's what*, *which is which*, and *who's who* — have gone further still and become **noun phrases**, and two more — *what have you* and *whatnot* — have stopped asking anything at all and become ways of ending a list. Where a full clause does follow one of these, it keeps **statement word order**, the same rule that governs every indirect question: *Who knows what she paid for it?*, never ✗*who knows what did she pay*. That rule is set out in full in [Reported questions](../../../grammar/01-reported-speech/05-questions.md).

### guess what, guess who

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɛs ˈwʌt/, /ɡɛs ˈhu/ &middot; *gess-WUT, gess-HOO* &middot; the accent on the question word, and the whole phrase usually falls rather than rises, because no answer is really wanted  
**Русский:** угадай что, угадай кто — разг., шутливое предисловие к новости
**Type:** imperative verb with an embedded interrogative pronoun, frozen as an opener &middot; **Case:** the *wh-* **clause** is the object of *guess*, and the *wh-* word itself takes whatever case its own clause gives it — subject in *Guess who's coming*, object in *Guess who I saw* &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** a piece of news the speaker is about to deliver (*guess what*), or a person (*guess who*)  
**Agreement:** no verb of its own beyond the imperative. When a clause follows, the embedded verb agrees normally and is singular by default: *Guess who's coming to dinner.*  
**Pattern:** *Guess what!* &middot; *Guess who!* &middot; *Guess what* [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order: *Guess what she said*) &middot; *Guess who's* [verb-*ing*]? &middot; *Guess who.* (as an answer, at a door or on the phone)  
**Contrast:** it is not a real question — it is an attention-getter announcing news, and the expected response is *What?*, not a guess. Punctuation follows function: *Guess what! I got the job.* takes an exclamation point or a period far more often than a question mark. *Guess who* has a second life as an **answer**: knocking on a door and saying *Guess who!* Word order inside a following clause stays statement order — *Guess who I saw*, never ✗*Guess who did I see*.

Announces news by pretending to ask for a guess.

1. *Guess what* — they approved the loan.
2. *Guess who* I ran into at the airport.
3. "Who's there?" "*Guess who*!"
4. *Guess what* happened on the way home.
5. *Guess who's* coming to dinner tomorrow night.
6. *Guess what* she said when I told her the price.

**See also:** [what](#what), [who](#who), [who's who](#whos-who), [what (echo)](#what-echo)

### now what, what now

**Pronunciation:** /naʊ ˈwʌt/, /wʌt ˈnaʊ/ &middot; *now-WUT, wut-NOW* &middot; the accent falls on the second word in both, and exasperation flattens the contour rather than raising it  
**Русский:** и что теперь? — разг., когда план исчерпан или случилась новая помеха
**Type:** elliptical interrogative &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the next step, or the next thing to go wrong  
**Agreement:** **verbless** — there is nothing in either phrase for a verb to agree with  
**Pattern:** *Now what?* &middot; *What now?* &middot; *So now what?* &middot; expanded: *Now what do we do?* / *What do we do now?*  
**Contrast:** *Now what?* usually carries exasperation — one thing has already gone wrong and here comes another. *What now?* can carry the same, but it is also a neutral request for the next step, which *Now what?* rarely is. Both are ellipses of *What do we do now?* Keep them apart from [what next, what then](#what-next-what-then): *now what* is about the present impasse, *what next* about the sequence still to come.

Asks what to do at a point where the plan has run out.

1. The car won't start. *Now what*?
2. Okay, the form is signed. *What now*?
3. *Now what* — we can't even get in without a badge.
4. "The flight's canceled." "*Now what*?"
5. *What now*, do we just wait for them to call us back?
6. Every time the phone rings I think, *now what*.

**See also:** [what next, what then](#what-next-what-then), [what](#what), [so what](#so-what), [what if](#what-if)

### or what

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr ˈwʌt/ &middot; *or-WUT* &middot; *or* reduces to [ɚ] and the two run together as [ɚˈwʌt]; the accent is on *what*, and the tone rises sharply at the end  
**Русский:** или как?, или что? — разг., закрывает вопрос, не называя прочих возможностей
**Type:** interrogative pronoun closing an elliptical alternative question &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the **unnamed alternatives** — *what* stands in for "any other possibility", left deliberately unspecified because the speaker does not care to list them  
**Agreement:** none of its own; the clause in front carries all of it (*Are we going or what?*, *Is he coming or what?*)  
**Pattern:** [yes/no question] *or what*? &middot; *Is that* [noun phrase] *or what*? (rhetorical admiration) &middot; *Are you going to* [verb] *or what*? (impatience) &middot; a main-clause tag only — it embeds badly (?*I asked whether we were going or what*)  
**Contrast:** one shape, two feelings that are nearly opposite. **Impatience**: *Are we leaving or what?* presses for a decision and can be rude. **Rhetorical admiration**: *Is that a view or what?* is not asking anything — it demands agreement, and the expected answer is *yes*. Both are ellipses of a genuine alternative question (*…or what are we doing?*), and the *what* is a real pronoun standing for the option nobody bothered to name. Keep it apart from a spelled-out alternative question (*Are we driving or flying?*), where the second option is given and *what* has no work to do. Spoken only: in writing it belongs in dialogue.

Closes a question by leaving every other possibility unnamed.

1. Are we ordering, *or what*?
2. Is that a sunset *or what*?
3. So is he coming to the meeting *or what*?
4. Do you want the job *or what*?
5. "Well? Are you going to tell me *or what*?"
6. Was that the strangest interview you have ever sat through *or what*?

**See also:** [what](#what), [what (echo)](#what-echo), [so what](#so-what), [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now)

### says who, who says

**Pronunciation:** /sɛz ˈhu/, /hu ˈsɛz/ &middot; *sez-HOO, hoo-SEZ* &middot; *says* is /sɛz/, never /seɪz/  
**Русский:** кто это сказал?, а кто сказал? — разг., вызов: требует назвать источник утверждения
**Type:** elliptical interrogative, frozen &middot; **Case:** subject — *who* is the subject of *says* in both orders &middot; **Register:** informal, often confrontational  
**Person / number:** invariant; third-person singular verb  
**Refers to:** the person whose authority is being challenged  
**Agreement:** **third-person singular** *says*, fixed: ✗*who say*. The past-tense *Who said?* and *Said who?* exist but are separate, milder questions asking who actually spoke.  
**Pattern:** *Says who?* &middot; *Who says?* &middot; *Who says* [clause]? (*Who says we have to be there at eight?*) &middot; the stock reply *Says me.*  
**Contrast:** the inverted *Says who?* and the plain *Who says?* mean the same thing — "on whose authority?" — and both are challenges rather than requests for a name. The stock reply *Says me* puts an object form where a subject belongs, and it is a deliberate idiom rather than an error to be corrected; it still has no place in writing, and the "corrected" ✗*Say I* is not English at all. *Who says* also takes a full clause, where it becomes a rhetorical denial: *Who says it has to cost that much?* means "it doesn't."

Challenges a claim by demanding to know who is behind it.

1. "You can't park there." "*Says who*?"
2. *Who says* we have to leave at six?
3. "That's not how it works." "*Says who*?" "*Says* the manual."
4. *Who says* you can't teach an old dog new tricks?
5. "You're too old to start over." "*Says who*?"
6. *Who says* it has to cost that much?

**See also:** [who](#who), [who cares, who knows](#who-cares-who-knows), [so what](#so-what)

### say what

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ ˈwʌt/ &middot; *say-WUT* &middot; a hard rise on *what*, which carries the accent and is often drawn out, [ˈwʌːt]; the two words run together and *say* takes the weak [sə] in fast speech, giving [səˈwʌt]. *What* has **no weak form here** — it is the questioned word, so it can never reduce to [wət] the way it does in *I know what I want*  
**Русский:** чего?, что-что? — разг., просьба повторить с недоверием; из афроамериканского англ.
**Type:** frozen imperative with the interrogative pronoun left in place — an echo question in shape &middot; **Case:** object — *what* is the object of *say*, and it does not front &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; it began in **African American English** and spread into general informal American speech over the second half of the twentieth century, and an editor will cut it from anything that is not dialogue  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the piece of what was just said that the hearer missed or cannot believe — the same thing the *wh-* word picks out in any other [echo question](#what-echo)  
**Agreement:** **none** — the imperative has no subject to agree with, and nothing inside the phrase inflects: ✗*said what?* and ✗*says what?* are not this idiom, and there is no past-tense version of it at all  
**Pattern:** *Say what?* &middot; *Say what now?* &middot; drawn out for comic disbelief, *Say whaaat?* &middot; it stands alone as a whole turn and takes no continuation: ✗*Say what did he tell you?* &middot; and it does not embed — ✗*I asked him say what* (say *I asked him to say it again*)  
**Contrast:** it belongs to the family of ways to ask for a repetition, and register is what sorts them: *Sorry?*, *Excuse me?*, and *I'm sorry, what?* are the polite ones, *Huh?* and *Come again?* the casual neutral ones, bare *[What?](#what-echo)* the blunt one, and *Say what?* the one that carries disbelief with it — far more often "I can't believe you just said that" than a genuine request to hear it again. Its history is worth stating plainly rather than hinting at: it is African American English in origin and is now general American slang, used across the board in speech and marked as casual rather than as dialect. Two things it is not. The ordinary imperative *Say what you mean* has a full clause after it and is not this idiom. And *You don't say* registers surprise at news without asking for anything, where *Say what?* asks. Structurally it is an echo question — the *wh-* word stays where it is, there is no *do*-support, and the tone rises hard.

Asks for a repetition, usually with disbelief attached.

1. "I sold the truck this morning." "*Say what*?"
2. *Say what*? You drove all the way to Reno for a sandwich?
3. "They want the whole thing by Friday." "*Say what* now?"
4. He looked up from the bill and said, "*Say what*?"
5. "Your cousin is moving in with us." "*Say what*?" "For a month, maybe two."
6. "Rent's going up two hundred dollars." "*Say what*?!"
7. "She quit on the spot." "*Say what*? She'd been there eleven years."

**See also:** [what (echo)](#what-echo), [what](#what), [guess what, guess who](#guess-what-guess-who), [what's up](#whats-up), [so what](#so-what)

### so what

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈwʌt/ &middot; *so-WUT* &middot; a heavy accent on *what* and a **falling** tone — it is punctuated as a question but does not sound like one  
**Русский:** ну и что? — разг., резко; отрицает важность только что сказанного
**Type:** elliptical interrogative used as a dismissal &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal; blunt, and rude in the wrong company  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the point just made, which the speaker refuses to treat as important  
**Agreement:** **verbless**  
**Pattern:** *So what?* &middot; *So what if* [clause]? &middot; *So what?* as a stand-alone reply &middot; noun use: *the so-what*, *the so-what test*  
**Contrast:** a rhetorical question with falling intonation: it takes a question mark by convention and expects no answer. Milder ways to make the same move are *Does it matter?*, *[What about it?](#what-about)*, and a bare *And?*; the formal and equally confrontational version is *[What of it?](#what-of)* The noun use — *every slide has to answer the so-what* — is business jargon for the point of a piece of information, and it is hyphenated.

Refuses to accept that what was just said matters.

1. "He's twenty years older than she is." "*So what*?"
2. *So what* if we're the last ones there?
3. She read the memo twice and thought, *so what*?
4. "You'll be the only one in a suit." "*So what*?"
5. *So what* — nobody is going to check the receipts.
6. Every slide has to answer the *so-what*.

**See also:** [what](#what), [what about](#what-about), [what of](#what-of), [who cares, who knows](#who-cares-who-knows)

### what (echo)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/ with a sharp rise &middot; *WUT↗* &middot; the echoed *wh-* word carries the nuclear accent and the whole utterance rises hard at the end; it is often lengthened, [wʌːt]  
**Русский:** что?, как ты сказал? — переспрос-эхо: недослышал или не может поверить
**Type:** interrogative pronoun in an echo question &middot; **Case:** whatever the slot requires — but see the note below on *who* &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; spoken  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the piece of the previous utterance that the hearer missed, or cannot believe  
**Agreement:** none of its own — the clause keeps the verb it already had, unchanged: *She said what?*, never ✗*She did say what?*  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *what*? (in place, no fronting, no *do*) &middot; [subject] *did what*? &middot; [subject] [verb] *who*? &middot; *You what?* &middot; bare *What?* &middot; the same rise on any other question word: *He's marrying who?*  
**Contrast:** an echo question breaks the two rules the rest of this file runs on. The *wh-* word does **not** move to the front, and there is **no *do*-support**: *You did **what**?* is an echo, while *What did you do?* is an ordinary question asking for information the speaker never had. Case relaxes too — in an echo, the object slot takes *who* even in careful speech (*You called who?*), and *whom* there sounds arch. A bare *What?* as a request for repetition is blunt in American English; *Sorry?*, *Excuse me?*, *Come again?* and *I'm sorry, what?* are the polite versions and *Huh?* the casual one. *Say what?* is a further casual variant — it began in **African American English** and is now general informal American speech, spoken only, and an editor will cut it from anything but dialogue. An echo can even target a *wh-* question itself: *Who did what?*

Repeats back the part of what was just said that the hearer missed or cannot believe.

1. "I sold the truck." "You did *what*?"
2. She's marrying *who*?
3. He said *what* to the officer?
4. You paid *what* for a used dishwasher?
5. "I quit this morning." "You *what*?"
6. Wait — you told *who* about the offer?
7. The company is doing *what* with our data?

**See also:** [what](#what), [who](#who), [say what](#say-what), [whatever](#whatever), [guess what, guess who](#guess-what-guess-who), [Reported questions](../../../grammar/01-reported-speech/05-questions.md)

### what for

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈfɔr/ &middot; *wut-FOR* &middot; the accent on *for*, which keeps its full vowel because it is stranded at the end; the *t* of *what* flaps, [wʌɾ ˈfɔr]  
**Русский:** зачем, для чего, с какой целью — вопрос о цели
**Type:** interrogative pronoun with a stranded preposition &middot; **Case:** object of *for* &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** a **purpose** — the goal an action was aimed at  
**Agreement:** the elliptical *What for?* is verbless; in the full pattern the clause keeps its own subject-verb agreement (*What **is** this bracket for?*)  
**Pattern:** *What for?* (elliptical) &middot; *What* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] [object] *for*? (*What did you do that for?*) &middot; *What's it for?* &middot; [clause] *what* [subject] [verb] *for* (embedded) &middot; the idiom *give someone what for*  
**Contrast:** *What for?* and *Why?* both ask for a reason, but *what for* asks specifically about **purpose**, while *why* covers cause as well. A pipe bursting has a cause and no purpose, so *Why did the pipe burst?* works while *What did the pipe burst for?* is odd — not ungrammatical, just asking after a purpose that a burst pipe does not have. In this idiom the preposition **stays at the end** and is not fronted: *What did you buy that for?* is ordinary, and the pied-piped ?*For what did you buy that?* is stilted rather than wrong — it is grammatical, which is why it gets a question mark here and not a cross, but nobody says it — though *For what purpose …?* is fine in formal writing. The frozen idiom *give someone what for* means to scold them sharply, and there *what for* is a noun phrase, not a question.

Asks what the purpose of something is.

1. "I'll need your license." "*What for*?"
2. *What* did you tell her that *for*?
3. *What*'s this bracket *for*?
4. *What* are we all standing in line *for*?
5. She asked *what* the second key was *for*.
6. He came home at two in the morning and his mother gave him *what for*.
7. "Bring your passport." "*What for*? It's a domestic flight."

**See also:** [what](#what), [what about](#what-about), [to whom, for whom, with whom](#to-whom-for-whom-with-whom), [what if](#what-if)

### what gives

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈɡɪvz/ &middot; *wut-GIVZ* &middot; the accent on *gives*  
**Русский:** в чём дело?, что происходит? — разг., амер. сленг середины XX в.
**Type:** fixed interrogative expression &middot; **Case:** *what* is the subject &middot; **Register:** informal; mid-century American slang that survives comfortably in speech  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** an unexplained situation — something that is not adding up  
**Agreement:** *gives* is **frozen** in the third-person singular present. There is no ✗*what gave* or ✗*what is giving* in this idiom; to put it in the past you have to rewrite (*I asked what was going on*).  
**Pattern:** *What gives?* &middot; *So what gives?* &middot; *What gives with* [noun phrase]? &middot; embedded: *I asked him what gives*  
**Contrast:** it means "what is going on?" or "what's the problem?", and it is an idiom rather than a live use of *give* — no ordinary sense of the verb is involved. The neutral equivalents are *What's going on?*, *What's the deal?*, and *What happened?* It is casual and slightly dated; nobody will misunderstand it, but it will read as a period detail in writing.

Asks for an explanation of something that does not make sense.

1. You've been quiet all night. *What gives*?
2. The store is closed on a Tuesday — *what gives*?
3. *What gives* with the new parking rules?
4. So *what gives*? Are we going or not?
5. I asked him *what gives* and he just shrugged.
6. Three emails and no answer. *What gives*?

**See also:** [what](#what), [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now), [so what](#so-what), [what's what](#whats-what)

### what have you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈhæv ju/ &middot; *wut-HAV-yoo* &middot; in running speech it flattens to [wʌɾəvjə] — the *t* flaps, *have* reduces to [əv], and *you* to its weak form [jə]; it is said quickly and low, as the tail end of a list  
**Русский:** и тому подобное, и всё такое прочее — завершает перечисление, не называя остального
**Type:** frozen interrogative clause used as a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** *what* is the **object** of *have* inside the frozen clause &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; ordinary in speech and in relaxed writing  
**Person / number:** invariant — the *you* is frozen and never changes person: ✗*what have they*, ✗*what has he*  
**Refers to:** the **open remainder of a list** — "and other things of that kind", with the speaker declining to name them  
**Agreement:** frozen solid. No tense, person, or number moves inside it: ✗*what had you*, ✗*what have we*. When the list is in the past, the surrounding sentence carries the tense — *the drawer **was** full of receipts, coupons, and what have you*.  
**Pattern:** [list], *and what have you* &middot; [list], *or what have you* &middot; [list], *what have you* (bare) &middot; it **closes** a list and never opens one  
**Contrast:** it does the job of *and so on*, *and so forth*, *et cetera*, and the more casual *and [whatnot](#whatnot)* or *and all that*, and register is what separates them: *et cetera* is the formal one, *and so on* the neutral written one, *what have you* and *whatnot* the conversational ones. Grammatically it is a fossil — a question in shape with no question left in it — which is why the *you* cannot change and why it never takes a question mark. Keep it out of technical, contractual, or legal prose, where an open-ended list needs *including but not limited to* rather than a shrug.

Closes a list by gesturing at everything else of the same kind.

1. Bring a sleeping bag, a flashlight, *what have you*.
2. The garage is full of paint cans, old tile, *and what have you*.
3. They'll want pay stubs, bank statements, *or what have you*.
4. He does the taxes, the payroll, *what have you* — the whole back office.
5. We can meet at the office, a coffee shop, *what have you*.
6. She kept every card, letter, and ticket stub, *and what have you*, in one shoebox.

**See also:** [whatnot](#whatnot), [what](#what), [what's what](#whats-what), [what gives](#what-gives), [what (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative)

### what if

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈɪf/ &middot; *wut-IF* &middot; the *t* flaps and links into the vowel, [wʌˈɾɪf]  
**Русский:** а что, если… — вводит предположение и спрашивает о его последствиях
**Type:** interrogative pronoun heading an elliptical conditional question &middot; **Case:** subject of the missing main clause &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the consequences of a hypothetical situation — *what* stands for the whole unstated outcome  
**Agreement:** the *if*-clause carries its own agreement and the main clause is simply left out: *What if it rains?* is *What (will happen) if it rains?*  
**Pattern:** *What if* [present clause]? (live possibility) &middot; *What if* [past clause]? (remote: *What if we missed it?*) &middot; *What if* [past perfect clause]? (counterfactual: *What if she hadn't called?*) &middot; noun use: *a what-if*, *the what-ifs*  
**Contrast:** the ellipsis is in the **main clause**, not the *if*-clause, so the *if*-clause keeps ordinary statement order and ordinary conditional tense marking. Three degrees of remoteness sit on the same frame: *What if it rains?* treats rain as a live possibility, *What if it rained?* holds it further off, *What if it had rained?* is counterfactual and about the past. It doubles as a **suggestion** — *What if we left at six instead?* — where *how about* would do the same work. As a noun it is hyphenated: *we ran through all the what-ifs*.

Asks what would follow if some supposed situation came true.

1. *What if* the flight is delayed again?
2. *What if* we just told them the truth?
3. *What if* she says no?
4. *What if* I had taken the other job?
5. He kept asking *what if* the tests came back positive.
6. *What if* we met halfway, somewhere around Bakersfield?
7. The whole proposal is a pile of *what-ifs*.

**See also:** [what](#what), [what about](#what-about), [what next, what then](#what-next-what-then), [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now)

### what next, what then

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈnɛkst/, /wʌt ˈðɛn/ &middot; *wut-NEKST, wut-THEN* &middot; the accent on the second word  
**Русский:** что дальше?, а потом что? — о следующем шаге или о следствии предположения
**Type:** elliptical interrogative &middot; **Case:** none &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the next item in a sequence (*what next*), or the consequence of something already supposed (*what then*)  
**Agreement:** **verbless** in the elliptical form  
**Pattern:** *What next?* &middot; *And what then?* &middot; *What then* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; the exclamatory *What next!* &middot; embedded: *she asked what next*  
**Contrast:** *What next?* asks what comes after in a series, and in its **exclamatory** use — *first the roof, now the furnace, what next!* — it registers dismay at an accumulation rather than asking anything, which is why it takes an exclamation point there. *What then?* asks about a **consequence** and needs a supposition in front of it to lean on: *Suppose the bank says no. What then?* Against [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now), which is about the present impasse rather than the sequence ahead.

Asks what comes next in a series, or what follows from a supposition.

1. We've painted the hallway. *What next*?
2. Suppose they refuse to sign. *What then*?
3. First the flood, then the furnace — *what next*!
4. If we sell the house, *what then*?
5. She asked me *what next* and I had no answer for her.
6. And *what then*, when the money runs out in March?

**See also:** [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now), [what if](#what-if), [what](#what), [so what](#so-what)

### whatnot

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtnɑt/ &middot; *WUT-not* &middot; one stress, on the first syllable; the *t* of *what* is unreleased or glottalized before the *n*, [ˈwʌʔnɑt], and the whole word is said fast and low at the tail of a list  
**Русский:** и всё такое, и прочее — разг., завершает перечисление взмахом руки
**Type:** frozen interrogative clause (*what not*) fused into a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none — the *what* inside it is frozen and neither takes nor assigns case &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; ordinary in speech and in relaxed writing  
**Person / number:** invariant; neither singular nor plural, and it never takes a verb of its own  
**Refers to:** the **open remainder of a list** — "and other things of that kind" — the same job [what have you](#what-have-you) does, with the speaker declining to name the rest. It closes lists of things, not of people: *rakes, hoses, and whatnot* is ordinary, and a list of people takes *and so on* or *and the rest* instead.  
**Agreement:** **none** — the phrase is a fossil with no verb inside it, so nothing in it agrees with anything. The surrounding sentence carries all the tense and number: *the drawer **was** full of stamps, rubber bands, and whatnot.*  
**Pattern:** [list], *and whatnot* &middot; [list], *or whatnot* &middot; [list], *whatnot* (bare) &middot; it **closes** a list and never opens one &middot; the unrelated count noun *a whatnot* (a small shelved stand)  
**Contrast:** it stands in the same slot as [what have you](#what-have-you), *and so on*, *and so forth*, *et cetera*, and *and all that*, and register is the only thing that separates them: *et cetera* is the formal one, *and so on* the neutral written one, *whatnot* and *what have you* the conversational ones, with *whatnot* a shade the more casual of the pair. Two mechanical points. It is written **solid, as one word** in this use: *and whatnot*. The two-word *and what not* is the older spelling and still surfaces, but American dictionaries and copy editors have settled on the solid form, and an editor will close it up. And there is no question left in it at all: it takes no question mark, and it cannot be fronted, inverted, or embedded as a real question — ✗*Whatnot did you buy?* The homograph *a whatnot*, a nineteenth-century piece of furniture with several small shelves, is an ordinary count noun with a plural (*two whatnots*) and is not a pronoun at all. Keep the list-closer out of contracts and specifications, where an open-ended list needs *including but not limited to* rather than a shrug.

Closes a list by waving at everything else of the same kind.

1. Bring chips, soda, *and whatnot* — whatever you'd want on the boat.
2. The shed is full of rakes, hoses, *and whatnot*.
3. She handles scheduling, payroll, *whatnot* — the whole front office.
4. They'll want pay stubs, tax returns, *or whatnot*.
5. There were flyers, coupons, *and whatnot* jammed into the screen door.
6. He collects stamps, coins, *and whatnot*, and none of it is worth much.

**See also:** [what have you](#what-have-you), [what](#what), [what's what](#whats-what), [what (fused relative)](09-fused-generic-dummy.md#what-fused-relative)

### what's it to you

**Pronunciation:** /wʌts ɪt tə ˈju/ &middot; *wuts-it-tuh-YOO* &middot; the accent lands on *you*, which keeps its full vowel because it is the point of the challenge; *to* is the weak /tə/ and the *t* of *what's it* flaps, so the whole phrase runs to [wʌtsɪɾəˈju]. Let the accent fall back onto *what* instead and the weak forms take over the end, giving the casual *what's it to ya* [ˈwʌtsɪɾəjə]  
**Русский:** тебе-то какое дело? — разг., грубовато; отказ отвечать через встречный вызов
**Type:** fixed interrogative expression — *what* as the complement of *be* &middot; **Case:** none — *what* is a predicate complement here, neither subject nor object &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; confrontational, and rude between people who are not close  
**Person / number:** invariant in practice; *what's it to him* and *what's it to them* occur, but they read as reports of the same challenge rather than as fresh ones  
**Refers to:** the **stake or the concern** the hearer has in the matter — and the expected answer is "none". It never asks for information; *what* here stands for an interest the speaker is denying the hearer has.  
**Agreement:** the contracted *is* is **fixed and singular**: ✗*what are it to you*. The past *What was it to you?* is grammatical but rarely said — the idiom lives in the present tense, and the older *What is that to thee?* (John 21:22, King James Version) is the same frame with an archaic pronoun in it.  
**Pattern:** *What's it to you?* (a whole turn on its own) &middot; *What's it to ya?* (casual spelling) &middot; *And what's it to you?* &middot; *What's it to you* [*wh-* clause]? (*What's it to you who I have lunch with?*) &middot; the older *What's that to you?* &middot; a *that*-clause after it is stiff — for that, say *What business is it of yours that …*  
**Contrast:** it is a **challenge**, not a question: it refuses the question just asked instead of answering it, and the expected reply is a retreat. The family sorts by heat — *Why do you ask?* is neutral, *What business is it of yours?* is sharper and can be said in full sentences, *What's it to you?* is blunter still and belongs to speech, and *Mind your own business* drops the question shape altogether. Against its neighbors in this section: *[So what?](#so-what)* dismisses the importance of what was said, *[What about it?](#what-about)* dares the hearer to make something of it, and *what's it to you* attacks the hearer's standing to have asked at all. The older wording *What's that to you?* is the King James phrasing and now reads as scriptural or archaic; *what's it to ya* is the same sentence with the weak forms written out, and belongs only in dialogue.

Refuses a question by challenging the asker's right to ask it.

1. "How much did you pay for it?" "*What's it to you*?"
2. "You're not going to the reunion?" "*What's it to you*?"
3. He asked where I'd been all afternoon, and I said, *what's it to you*.
4. "Is he still living out there by the lake?" "*What's it to ya*?"
5. *What's it to you* who I have lunch with on a Tuesday?
6. She has a way of asking *what's it to you* without saying a word.
7. "Just curious how the interview went." "*What's it to you*?" "Nothing. Forget I asked."

**See also:** [so what](#so-what), [what about](#what-about), [who's to say](#whos-to-say), [says who, who says](#says-who-who-says), [what (echo)](#what-echo)

### what's up

**Pronunciation:** /wʌts ˈʌp/ &middot; *wuts-UP* &middot; the /t/ and /s/ form a cluster that resyllabifies onto *up*, [wʌˈtsʌp]; casual speech drops the /t/ altogether, giving *whassup* [wəˈsʌp] and, reduced further, *'sup* [sʌp]  
**Русский:** что нового?, что случилось? — разг.; а также приветствие «привет, как дела?»
**Type:** fixed interrogative expression — *what* as subject of *be* &middot; **Case:** *what* is the subject &middot; **Register:** informal; ordinary in speech everywhere in American English, and *whassup* / *'sup* more casual still  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** whatever is new, happening, or wrong — and, in the greeting use, nothing at all  
**Agreement:** the contracted *is* is **singular and fixed**; the past *what was up* is ordinary (*I asked what was up*), but ✗*what are up* is not English  
**Pattern:** *What's up?* (greeting) &middot; *What's up?* (real question) &middot; *What's up with* [noun phrase]? &middot; [clause] *what's / what was up* (embedded, statement order) &middot; the stock reply *Not much.*  
**Contrast:** two jobs sharing one string. As a **greeting** it is phatic — the expected answer is *Not much* or *Not a lot*, and answering it in detail misreads it. As a **real question** it asks what is new or what is wrong, and it is usually marked either by a *with*-phrase (*What's up with the printer?*) or by heavy stress and a worried face. Its neighbors sort by assumption: *What's going on?* is neutral, *[What gives?](#what-gives)* asks for an explanation and is dated, *What's the matter?* assumes something is already wrong. The spellings *whassup* and *'sup* are speech transcribed and belong in dialogue only. Not to be confused with the adjective *up* in *something's up* ("something is going on"), which is a different construction with no question in it.

Asks what is new or what is wrong — or greets, and asks nothing.

1. "*What's up*?" "Not much — you?"
2. *What's up* with the front door? It sticks now.
3. Hey, *what's up* — you look upset.
4. She stopped by to see *what's up* with the schedule change.
5. I asked him *what was up* and he said the server had been down all morning.
6. Nobody would tell me *what was up* with the delay.
7. *What's up*, you two? Need a hand with that?

**See also:** [what](#what), [what gives](#what-gives), [now what, what now](#now-what-what-now), [what's what](#whats-what)

### what's what

**Pronunciation:** /wʌts ˈwʌt/ &middot; *wuts-WUT* &middot; the accent on the second *what*  
**Русский:** что к чему — истинное положение дел как нечто, что можно знать и объяснить
**Type:** frozen embedded interrogative clause, used as a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none — the first *what* is the subject, the second the complement &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the essential facts of a situation — which things are which, and what actually matters  
**Agreement:** the *is* inside the contraction is **fixed and singular**; the past-tense *what was what* is ordinary (*nobody could tell what was what*), but a plural is not: ✗*what were what*  
**Pattern:** *know what's what* &middot; *tell* [someone] *what's what* &middot; *figure out what's what* &middot; past tense *what was what*  
**Contrast:** an embedded question that has hardened into an idiom for "the true state of affairs." *Know what's what* means to be well informed and hard to fool; *tell someone what's what* means to set them straight, usually sharply. The people-version is [who's who](#whos-who). It is not used as a direct question in American English — ?*What's what?* as an opener is not idiomatic (odd, not ungrammatical), though *What's up?* and *What's going on?* are perfectly ordinary.

Names the real facts of a situation, considered as a thing you can know or explain.

1. Give her a week and she'll know *what's what*.
2. Somebody needs to sit that contractor down and tell him *what's what*.
3. Until the audit is finished, nobody knows *what's what*.
4. He talks a good game, but he doesn't really know *what's what*.
5. It took me a month on the job to figure out *what's what*.
6. She explained *what's what* about the lease in about four minutes.

**See also:** [who's who](#whos-who), [what](#what), [what gives](#what-gives), [Reported questions](../../../grammar/01-reported-speech/05-questions.md)

### which is which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪtʃ ɪz ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *wich-iz-WICH* &middot; the accent on the second *which*, the *is* unstressed to [ɪz] and often reduced to [z] after the first word in fast speech  
**Русский:** который из них который, где какой — задача различить похожие предметы
**Type:** frozen embedded interrogative clause, used as a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** none — the first *which* is the subject, the second the complement, and *which* has one form for both &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant; the set involved always has two or more members, but the clause itself stays singular  
**Refers to:** the **identities of the members of a known set** — which of the things in front of you is which of the ones you already have names for  
**Agreement:** **singular verb**, however many items are in the set: *which is which* in the present, *which was which* in the past. ✗*which are which* is not used, and the singular holds even past two (*I can never tell which is which among the three of them*).  
**Pattern:** *tell which is which* &middot; *know which is which* &middot; *figure out which is which* &middot; past tense *which was which* &middot; almost always after a verb of knowing or telling, and usually negated  
**Contrast:** the third member of the set with [what's what](#whats-what) and [who's who](#whos-who), and the one that stays closest to a live question. It presupposes a **bounded set of look-alikes**, exactly as bare [which](#which) does — *who's who* sorts people, *what's what* covers the state of affairs in general, and *which is which* tells apart two or more things you can label but not identify. It is not used as a bare direct question: ?*Which is which?* on its own is odd rather than ungrammatical, where *Which one is mine?* would be said. And unlike *who's who* it has **no noun use** — there is no ✗*a which is which of*.

Names the problem of telling apart the members of a set you can label but not identify.

1. The twins were in matching jackets and nobody could tell *which is which*.
2. Label the cables now or you'll never know *which is which*.
3. Both files are named "final" — good luck figuring out *which is which*.
4. She had two sets of keys and no idea *which was which*.
5. The plans look identical until you check the dates to see *which is which*.
6. I know one of them is the primary account. I just can't tell you *which is which*.

**See also:** [what's what](#whats-what), [who's who](#whos-who), [which](#which), [which of](#which-of)

### who cares, who knows

**Pronunciation:** /hu ˈkɛrz/, /hu ˈnoʊz/ &middot; *hoo-KAIRZ, hoo-NOHZ* &middot; the accent on the verb, and the tone falls — neither is really asking  
**Русский:** кому какое дело — пренебрежение; кто знает — признание, что ответа не знает никто
**Type:** rhetorical interrogative, frozen &middot; **Case:** *who* is the subject &middot; **Register:** informal; *who cares* is dismissive and can be rude, *who knows* is neither  
**Person / number:** invariant; third-person singular verb  
**Refers to:** nobody in particular — the implied answer is "nobody" in both  
**Agreement:** **third-person singular**, fixed: *who cares*, *who knows*, never ✗*who care*. In the past it inflects normally (*who knew*, which has its own idiomatic life meaning "who would have guessed").  
**Pattern:** *Who cares?* &middot; *Who cares if / whether* [clause]? &middot; *Who knows?* &middot; *Who knows* [*wh-* clause] (*Who knows what he'll say*) &middot; parenthetical *who knows* (*It'll take, who knows, another year*)  
**Contrast:** both are rhetorical questions whose answer is "nobody", but the force is different. *Who cares?* dismisses the importance of what was just said and is often rude; the softer moves are *Does it matter?* and *[So what?](#so-what)*, which is blunter still. *Who knows?* concedes ignorance and offends nobody, and it can float inside a sentence as a parenthetical hedge meaning "possibly" (*he's in Denver, or Boise, who knows*). When either takes a following clause, that clause keeps **statement word order**: *Who knows what she paid for it?*, never ✗*who knows what did she pay*.

Dismisses a point as unimportant, or concedes that the answer is unknown.

1. "The label says organic." "*Who cares*?"
2. *Who cares* if it rains — we're going anyway.
3. *Who knows* how long the permit will take.
4. "Is he coming back?" "*Who knows*."
5. He has, *who knows*, maybe two hundred of them out in the garage.
6. *Who cares* what the neighbors think about the fence?
7. *Who knows* what would have happened if we had left an hour earlier.

**See also:** [who](#who), [says who, who says](#says-who-who-says), [so what](#so-what), [Reported questions](../../../grammar/01-reported-speech/05-questions.md)

### who's to say

**Pronunciation:** /huz tə ˈseɪ/ &middot; *hooz-tuh-SAY* &middot; the *w* is silent, *to* is the weak /tə/ and often just [ɾə] after the /z/, and the accent falls on *say*; the tone **falls** rather than rises, because nothing is really being asked. *Who's* is /huz/ and a homophone of *whose*  
**Русский:** кто знает?, кому судить? — никто не может знать или не вправе выносить приговор
**Type:** rhetorical interrogative, frozen — *who* as subject of the *be to* construction &middot; **Case:** subject &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; at home in speech and in relaxed writing alike  
**Person / number:** invariant; third-person singular *is*, contracted to *'s* in nearly every use  
**Refers to:** **nobody in particular** — the implied answer is "no one", either because no one can know or because no one has the standing to judge  
**Agreement:** **third-person singular** *is*, fixed: ✗*who are to say*. The past *who was to say* is ordinary in narrative (*who was to say she was wrong?*), and the modal paraphrases *who can say* and *who could say* work the same way; ✗*who's saying* and ✗*who's to said* are not this idiom.  
**Pattern:** *Who's to say?* (stand-alone) &middot; *Who's to say* [*that*-clause]? (*Who's to say he's wrong?*) &middot; *Who's to say* [*wh-* clause]? (*Who's to say what would have happened?*) &middot; written with a period when the falling tone is being kept: *But who's to say.* &middot; past *who was to say* &middot; the following clause keeps statement order  
**Contrast:** two shades, both rhetorical, and the *be to* construction is what separates them. **Nobody can know**: *Who's to say what would have happened if we'd left earlier?* — the same concession [who knows](#who-cares-who-knows) makes, a degree more considered and a degree more formal. **Nobody has the standing to judge**: *Who's to say which of them is right?* — here *be to* is doing its "be in a position to" work, and the sentence is about authority rather than about knowledge, which is the line between this and *who knows*. Against [says who, who says](#says-who-who-says): that one challenges an authority that has actually been invoked, while *who's to say* denies that any authority exists. Two mechanical points. A clause after it keeps **statement word order**, the rule for every embedded question — *Who's to say what she meant?*, never ✗*who's to say what did she mean*. And the spelling is *who's*, the contraction of *who is*; ✗*whose to say* is a misspelling, and the test is at [whose](#whose).

Concedes that nobody can know the answer — or that nobody has the standing to judge.

1. "Would the surgery have helped?" "*Who's to say*?"
2. *Who's to say* what the market will look like in five years?
3. Maybe he'd have quit anyway. *Who's to say*.
4. *Who's to say* she was wrong to take the money and go?
5. *Who's to say* which of the two of them started it?
6. It might have turned out the same either way — *who's to say*.
7. *Who's to say* what counts as a fair price for a house on that road?

**See also:** [who cares, who knows](#who-cares-who-knows), [says who, who says](#says-who-who-says), [who](#who), [whose](#whose), [what's it to you](#whats-it-to-you), [Reported questions](../../../grammar/01-reported-speech/05-questions.md)

### who's who

**Pronunciation:** /huz ˈhu/ &middot; *hooz-HOO* &middot; the accent on the second *who*  
**Русский:** кто есть кто — различение людей в группе; тж. «список видных лиц» (a who's who of)
**Type:** frozen embedded interrogative clause, used as a noun phrase &middot; **Case:** *who* in both slots — the first is the subject of the contracted *is*, the second its complement, and ✗*who's whom* is not English &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Person / number:** invariant  
**Refers to:** the identities of the people in a group — which person is which  
**Agreement:** the verb is **always singular**, however many people are involved: *who's who* in the present, *who was who* in the past, never ✗*who are who*  
**Pattern:** *tell who's who* &middot; *know who's who* &middot; *figure out who's who* &middot; past tense *who was who* &middot; the noun *a who's who of* [field]  
**Contrast:** the people-version of [what's what](#whats-what), and it carries two senses worth keeping apart. As a **clause** it means "which person is which" — *with the masks on, you couldn't tell who's who*. As a **noun**, *a who's who of* is a roster of the notable people in a field — *the guest list was a who's who of Nashville songwriting*. Spelling: *who's* here is *who is* and takes the apostrophe, so ✗*whose who* is simply a misspelling; see [whose](#whose) for the test.

Names the question of which person is which, or a roster of the people who matter in a field.

1. With the twins in matching coats, nobody could tell *who's who*.
2. It took me a month to learn *who's who* in the department.
3. The reception was a *who's who* of state politics.
4. Print name tags so people know *who's who*.
5. By the third chapter I had lost track of *who's who*.
6. He can tell you *who's who* on that committee and who actually decides anything.

**See also:** [what's what](#whats-what), [who](#who), [whose](#whose), [guess what, guess who](#guess-what-guess-who)
