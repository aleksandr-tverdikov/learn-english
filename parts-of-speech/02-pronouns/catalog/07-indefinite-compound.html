Compound indefinite pronouns#

The pronouns built by welding some-, any-, every-, or no- onto -body, -one, or -thing — the words that name a person or a thing without saying which one.

← The grammar of pronouns · All categories

Every member of the core group is a two-part compound: a quantifier stem that fixes the polarity — some- asserts, any- questions and denies, every- universalizes, no- negates — welded to a head that fixes the referent, with -body and -one for people and -thing for things. Two rules run through the entire set and they pull in opposite directions. The verb is always singular — everyone is, nobody was, something smells — no matter how plural the meaning feels; but the pronoun that refers back is normally they, because the meaning is plural or the gender unknown. Singular they is standard American English, in edited prose as well as in speech, and the Chicago Manual of Style, the MLA Handbook, the APA Publication Manual, and the AP Stylebook all accept it, AP the most cautiously; his or her is the conservative alternative, and generic he for a person of unknown gender is now rejected by every major American style guide and reads as dated to most American readers.

The singular-verb rule is absolute for the twelve compounds, and it is worth fixing against the neighboring group, where it is not. Among the simple indefinite pronouns, each, either, and neither take a singular verb however plural the of-phrase behind them looks — Each of the rooms has its own thermostat, Either is fine, Neither of the plans works (neither of them are is common in speech and still changed in edited prose). Both, several, and few take a plural verb, always — Both were late, Several have already shipped, Few know the whole story. And all, some, most, and none take the number of whatever they refer to: All of the seats were taken beside All of the paint was dry, Some of the chairs are broken beside Some of the paint is dry, Most of the files were copied beside Most of the evidence was destroyed. None is catalogued in this file because it is historically one of these compounds, and it is the one member here whose number is genuinely open; the rest of that group is at quantifying indefinite pronouns.

Three more rules recur in every entry. Modifiers follow the pronoun rather than preceding it — something strange, someone important, nothing new, anywhere quiet — which is the reverse of ordinary English word order and the single easiest way to spot a nonnative sentence. Else also follows, and its possessive takes the apostrophe: someone else's coat, never ✗ someone's else coat. And standard American English allows one negative per clause, so I didn't see anybody and I saw nobody are the two repairs for ✗ I didn't see nobody — a construction that is rule-governed and systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, and stigmatized in edited writing. Every nonstandard form in this file is labeled as such.

One thing this family does not have is case. All twelve compounds are invariant — everyone is everyone whether it is subject or object, and none of them has a whom-style object form — so the coordination errors that plague the personal pronouns cannot arise on the compound itself. What these compounds do inflect for is the genitive, and only the -body and -one forms do it comfortably — somebody's, everyone's, nobody else's — while nothing's in running text is almost always nothing is or nothing has.

Case surfaces instead in the words standing next to them, and the rule is worth stating in full here because these compounds are one of the commonest places to lose track of it. A pronoun takes the case of the job it does in its own clause, and putting a second person in the slot beside it changes that job not at all. But and except are prepositions in modern American usage, so they take the object form: nobody but me, everyone except him. (The older prescription that a but-phrase attached to a subject keeps the subject form — no one but I knew — has literary backing and is not an error, but it now sounds stilted; but me is the modern standard.) The same rule governs the coordinations Americans actually break, and they break in both directions. The hypercorrection puts the subject form into an object slot: ✗ between you and I, ✗ They invited my wife and I. The first is the object of a preposition and the second is a plain direct object — no preposition anywhere in it — and both require me: ✓ between you and me, ✓ They invited my wife and me, ✓ He gave it to Bob and me. In the other direction the nonstandard error puts an object form in a subject slot: ✗ Me and him went. One test settles every one of them: delete the other person and read what is left aloud. Between you and \_\_\_ → between me. He gave it to Bob and \_\_\_ → He gave it to me, never ✗ to I. They invited my wife and \_\_\_ → They invited me. \_\_\_ and I went → I went, which is why She and I went is the right form. Note that the objection to Me and him went is to the case and not to the order, so the repair is He and I went, not Him and me rearranged. And one thing to unlearn rather than learn: It's me is standard English, not an error — It is I is grammatically defensible and sounds stiff outside the telephone formula This is she. The whole business is worked out at personal pronouns.

These compounds are constantly the antecedent of a relative clause, so the antecedent rules matter here too. Use who for people, which for things, that for either, and whose for both — someone who can drive, something which still works, a policy whose wording nobody checked. After a -body or -one compound both who and that are standard (anyone who wants a copy, anybody that asks); after a -thing compound that is the overwhelming choice (everything that matters), and what is never a relative pronoun in standard English (✗ everything what I own). Two honest qualifications. The famous that/which rule is half grammar and half house style: that genuinely cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause, but restrictive which is perfectly grammatical, and an American copy editor changing everything which matters to everything that matters is applying an editing convention, not repairing an error. And whom is nearly gone except after a preposition: someone who we hired is unremarkable in American speech and increasingly in print, while someone to whom we owe money keeps the object form because it stands directly after to. Both are worked out at relative pronouns.

Finally, listen for the reductions, because the citation forms printed in this file are not what a learner actually hears. Inside the compounds the stressed first syllable never weakens — that is what carries the polarity — but everything after it does, and the /d/ of -body taps: somebody [ˈsʌmbəɾi], anybody [ˈɛnibəɾi], everybody [ˈɛvribəɾi], something [ˈsʌmpm̩], nothing [ˈnʌʔn̩]. And because these compounds so often sit right beside a personal pronoun, the personal-pronoun weak forms arrive with them: him is /ɪm/, her is /ɚ/, them is /əm/, you is /jə/, and he is /i/ — so somebody told them comes out [ˈsʌmbəɾi ˈtoʊld əm], nobody saw him comes out [ˈnoʊbəɾi ˈsɔ ɪm], and did you tell her comes out [ˈdɪdʒə ˈtɛl ɚ]. None of that is sloppy speech; it is the ordinary rhythm of the language. See weak forms.

The last recurring trap is the space bar. Everyone, anyone, and someone written solid are pronouns meaning all the people, a person, a person; written as two words, every one, any one, and some one are a determiner plus the pronoun one, they can refer to things, and they take of. The test is mechanical: if an of-phrase follows, use two words — every one of the windows, never ✗ everyone of the windows. The determiner halves of these pairs live in the determiner dictionary: every, any, no, some, and none of.

The some- series#

The assertive stem. A some- compound claims that a person or thing actually exists, which is exactly what its any- partner declines to do: somebody called reports a call, did anybody call asks whether there was one. Use these in plain affirmative statements — and in one class of question, the offer or request, where some- survives because the speaker is not really asking whether anyone exists (Could somebody hold the door?, Would you like something to drink?).

The any- series#

The nonassertive stem, with a second life as free choice. Under a negative, in a question, in an if-clause, after hardly, rarely, never, without, and before, and after a comparative, any- sweeps the whole class without claiming a member exists. In affirmative clauses it flips to free choice — anyone can learn to solder — takes its own stress, and means it does not matter which. The rule learners miss most is one of order: an any- compound has to follow the negative that licenses it, so ✗ anybody didn't call is impossible and nobody called is the only way to say it at the front of a clause.

Refers to a person without asserting that any such person exists. It has two clearly separate lives.

1. Nonassertive. Under a negative, a question, or a condition, it simply widens the search to the whole class: I didn't see anybody denies every candidate, one by one.

2. Free choice. In an affirmative clause, usually with a modal, it means it does not matter which and carries its own stress: Anybody can learn to solder. This is the sense that lets any- appear where the polarity rule would otherwise forbid it.

  2. Has anybody seen my keys? — sense 1, in a question
  3. If anybody asks, we left at nine. — sense 1, in a conditional
  4. Anybody can learn to solder with an afternoon of practice. — sense 2, free choice
  5. Hardly anybody stayed for the second half.
  6. She works harder than anybody else in that department. — the comparative frame; else because she works there too
  7. Nobody told me — not ✗ anybody didn't tell me.

The every- series#

The universal stem, and the place where the singular-verb rule is tested hardest. Everyone, everybody, and everything take a singular verb without exception, even with a plural noun sitting between the subject and the verb (everyone in the two seminars needs a badge), and even though the meaning is plainly plural. Everything downstream then goes plural: the pronoun (everyone brought their own lunch), the reflexive (everybody helped themselves), and the tag question (everyone's coming, aren't they?).

The no- series#

The negative stem, plus none, which is the same word historically — Old English ne + ān, not one — worn down to one syllable. All of these carry their own negation, so the verb after them stays positive: nobody came, not ✗ nobody didn't come. Three of the four are rigidly singular. None is the exception and the reason this section needs care: with a mass noun it takes a singular verb, with a plural count set it takes either, and the plural is older, commoner, and usually better. The emphatic not a soul is catalogued here too: it is built on not a rather than on the no- stem, but it denies a person exactly as nobody does, it carries its own negation in the same way, and it is the form an American reaches for when nobody is not emphatic enough.

Compounds modified by else#

Else subtracts. Attached to a compound it means other than the one already in view, and it follows the pronoun in every case: ✓ someone else, else someone*. It combines with all twelve compounds, with the -where adverbs (somewhere else), and with the wh- words (who else, what else), but never with an ordinary noun — ✗ the man else has to become the other man. The possessive is the field's classic apostrophe question, and the answer is that ✗ the -s* goes on else: someone else's coat, nobody else's business. The same else attaches to the quantifiers as well, and there the quantifier keeps its own polarity — all else, much else, little else — which is why those three are catalogued together at the head of this section.

The -where forms — adverbs, not pronouns#

These four are adverbs, not pronouns: they fill an adverbial slot, so I went somewhere patterns with I went home rather than with I saw something, and none of them can be a direct object (✗ I bought somewhere). Informal negatives and placeholders#

Three small families sit at the edge of the indefinite system. All of them are thoroughly American, most of them are informal, and none of them behaves quite like the twelve compounds. The minimizers — squat, diddly, diddly-squat, jack, the vulgar dick, shit, and fuck all, and the printable zilch, nada, bupkis, and zip — all mean nothing, and they split on one grammatical point worth learning as a pair: squat and jack take a negated verb or a positive one with no change of meaning (I didn't get squat = I got squat), while zilch and its group carry the negation themselves and refuse a second one (✓ I got zilch, ✗ I didn't get zilch). The placeholders — so-and-so, what's-his-name, you-know-who, whatchamacallit and its synonyms — all stand where a name should be, and they divide by why the name is missing: nobody has picked one yet (so-and-so), the speaker had it and lost it (what's-his-name), the speaker has it and will not say it aloud (you-know-who), or the object's name simply will not come (whatchamacallit). God knows what is the fifth answer to that question and the bleakest: nobody has it and nobody is going to get it. The third family is the list-closers, which stand at the end of a coordination in place of the items the speaker declines to name — you name it here, and whatnot below at whatnot. Every entry below is labeled for register, because register is the fact a learner most needs about this group: these are words American speech reaches for constantly and American editing removes without discussion. Two boundary notes. The placeholder that takes a noun after it — such-and-such a date — is a determiner, not a pronoun, and is catalogued at such-and-such (a), with its bare pronoun use at such and such, so-and-so. And the general extenders that close a list — and so on, and so forth, et cetera, and all that, and stuff, and the hedging tags or something, or anything, and or whatever — are not pronouns at all: each is a conjunction plus a placeholder, filling a coordinator slot rather than a noun-phrase slot, and they are catalogued in the conjunction dictionary at and so forth, et cetera / etc., and stuff / and everything / and all that, and or something.

Names a referent by pointing at the listener's knowledge of it instead of at the referent itself. The whole construction is a piece of social machinery: it says we both know who I mean, and I am not going to say it here, and the reason for the discretion is left to the listener.

  4. That's you-know-who's car in the driveway, so keep your voice down. — the genitive
  5. In the books he is never named; he is You-Know-Who. — the capitalized proper-noun use
  6. He can take that memo and file it you-know-where. — the euphemism, standing in for a word the speaker will not say
  7. Compare the ordinary clause: You know what? Let's just go. — a discourse opener, not a pronoun

Nonstandard, dialectal, and archaic#

Forms a learner will meet in dialogue, in song lyrics, in Twain, and in older legal prose, and should be able to recognize without imitating. Everything here is labeled: negative concord (I didn't see nothing) is systematic and rule-governed in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular and stigmatized in edited writing; the adverbial -s of nowheres is regional; naught and aught are archaic, opposite in meaning, and one letter apart. None of these are errors in the varieties that use them — they are simply not the variety an American cover letter is written in.

The nonstandard forms that belong to the other pronoun categories are labeled in the same way and catalogued in their own files; a learner meeting them alongside the forms below should know what they are and where they live. Double negatives — I didn't see nothing, we didn't get nowhere, ain't nobody home — are the in-family case and are treated below at nothing (negative concord) and ain't nobody; they are systematic in African American English and in Southern and Appalachian vernacular English, and nonstandard in edited American prose. Outside this family: the reflexives hisself and theirselves are regular formations on the possessive stem (his + self, on the model of myself, yourself), heard in Southern, Appalachian, and African American vernacular English and nonstandard in edited prose — see hisself and theirselves. The demonstrative them before a noun — them apples, them boots — is general vernacular American, strongest in Southern, Appalachian, and Midland speech, and standard English uses those; see them (dialectal demonstrative). The plural you forms — youse in New York City, northern New Jersey, Philadelphia, Boston, and Chicago, beside y'all in the South, yinz in Pittsburgh, and you'uns in Appalachia — fill a real gap in standard English, which has only one you; see youse. And the two case errors are at personal pronouns: Me and him went, general vernacular American and the most-corrected error in American classrooms, and between you and I / They invited my wife and I, which are not vernacular at all but hypercorrections running upward through the social scale.

Standard American English allows one negative marker per clause.
