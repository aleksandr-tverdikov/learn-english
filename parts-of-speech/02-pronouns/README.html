Pronouns#

A pronoun is a word that stands in for a noun phrase, so you can refer to someone or something again without repeating its full name.

1. What it does#

A pronoun's job is substitution. Instead of naming the same person or thing over and over, you name it once and then point back at it. The full noun phrase that a pronoun points back to is called its antecedent.

Maria left the office at six. She had forgotten her keys, so she went back for them.

Here Maria is the antecedent. She replaces Maria, her signals possession, and them replaces her keys. Without pronouns the sentence would read: Maria left the office at six. Maria had forgotten Maria's keys, so Maria went back for the keys. Grammatical, but unbearable.

Because a pronoun replaces a whole noun phrase — not just a noun — it takes the same jobs in a sentence that a noun phrase takes: subject, direct object, indirect object, object of a preposition, subject complement, and possessor.

The tall man in the gray coat waved at my sister and me. He waved at us.

Notice that he replaces the entire phrase the tall man in the gray coat, and us replaces my sister and me. You cannot say the tall he in the gray coat — that is exactly what separates a pronoun from a noun.

Some pronouns do not point back at anything at all. In It is raining and There are three reasons, the words it and there fill the subject slot that English grammar requires but carry no meaning. These are called dummy or expletive pronouns, and they are covered in section 2.

Pronouns also connect clauses. In the woman who called me, the relative pronoun who simultaneously refers back to the woman and links the clause to it — doing two jobs at once.

2. Types#

Personal pronouns — subject case#

Personal pronouns refer to people or things by grammatical person (first, second, third), number, and — in the third-person singular — gender. The subject case (also called nominative) is used when the pronoun is the subject of a verb.

Members: I, you, he, she, it, we, they (and singular they)

I disagree with the report's conclusion. They arrive on Thursday morning. He and I grew up in the same neighborhood.

Personal pronouns — object case#

The object case (also called accusative or oblique) is used for direct objects, indirect objects, and objects of prepositions.

Members: me, you, him, her, it, us, them

The manager called me into her office. Give him the receipt. This package is for you and her.

Possessive determiners#

Possessive determiners (traditionally called possessive adjectives) mark ownership and sit before a noun. Strictly speaking they are determiners, not pronouns, but they belong to the same paradigm and are almost always taught with pronouns.

Members: my, your, his, her, its, our, their

My apartment is on the fourth floor. The company changed its logo last spring. Their flight was delayed six hours.

Possessive pronouns#

Possessive pronouns stand alone — they replace an entire possessive noun phrase and are never followed by a noun.

Members: mine, yours, his, hers, its (rare), ours, theirs

That coat is mine. Our schedule is tighter than theirs. Her explanation was clear; his was confusing.

Reflexive pronouns#

A reflexive pronoun is used when the object of a verb or preposition refers to the same entity as the subject of the same clause.

Members: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, oneself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves (and themself for singular they)

She taught herself Portuguese in eighteen months. Be careful — you'll hurt yourself. The system reset itself overnight.

Intensive (emphatic) pronouns#

Intensive pronouns have exactly the same forms as reflexives, but a different job: they emphasize a noun or pronoun already in the sentence. They can be deleted without breaking the sentence.

Members: same list as reflexives

The senator herself answered the phone. I fixed the leak myself. They built the deck themselves.

Remove the intensive pronoun and the sentence still works with the same meaning (The senator answered the phone). Remove a reflexive and the sentence either collapses (He cut shaving) or changes meaning entirely (She taught Portuguese — to a class, not to herself).

Demonstrative pronouns#

Demonstrative pronouns point to something in the physical world or in the surrounding discourse, distinguishing near from far and singular from plural.

Members: this, that, these, those

This is the best coffee in the neighborhood. Those are the files you requested. The budget was cut again. That explains the hiring freeze.

Those also functions as a substitute meaning "the ones":

The results are similar to those reported last year. Those who registered early get priority seating.

Note the difference between a demonstrative pronoun (stands alone) and a demonstrative determiner (precedes a noun): This is expensive (pronoun) vs. This laptop is expensive (determiner).

Relative pronouns#

Relative pronouns introduce a relative clause and refer back to a noun in the main clause. They do double duty: they link the clause and they fill a grammatical slot inside it.

Members: who, whom, whose, which, that; also the zero relative (omitted pronoun)

The engineer who designed the bridge retired last year. The report that you sent arrived too late. A city whose subway runs all night is a city that never sleeps. Her proposal, which the board rejected, is being revived.

Fused relative pronouns (nominal relatives)#

These contain their own antecedent — they mean roughly "the person who," "the thing that," "any amount that."

Members: what, whoever, whomever, whatever, whichever

What you said surprised everyone. (= the thing that you said) Whoever finishes first should start the next batch. Take whichever you prefer.

Interrogative pronouns#

Interrogative pronouns ask questions. They stand in for the unknown answer.

Members: who, whom, whose, what, which (plus emphatic whoever, whatever)

Who left this message? Whom did you consult? (formal) Whose is this jacket? What happened at the meeting? Which of these do you recommend?

They also introduce indirect questions, where normal statement word order returns and the auxiliary does not invert:

I asked what she said. (not I asked what did she say) Nobody knows which of the files he opened. (not which did he open)

Indefinite pronouns#

Indefinite pronouns refer to people or things without specifying exactly which ones. This is a large, partly open class.

Singular members: anybody, anyone, anything, another, each, either, everybody, everyone, everything, much, neither, nobody, no one, nothing, one, other, somebody, someone, something Plural members: both, few, fewer, many, others, several Variable (singular or plural depending on the referent): all, any, more, most, none, some, such

Everyone was told to bring identification. Several have already responded. Most of the milk is gone. / Most of the students are gone.

Distributive pronouns#

Distributive pronouns single out members of a group one at a time. Many grammars fold these into the indefinite pronouns; they are listed separately here because their agreement behavior is distinctive.

Members: each, either, neither, every one

They are consistently singular, which is what sets them apart from indefinites like all and none.

Each of the applicants was interviewed twice. Neither of the proposals addresses the cost. Either works for me.

Reciprocal pronouns#

Reciprocal pronouns express a mutual relationship among the members of a plural antecedent: each member both acts and is acted on.

Members: each other, one another (and the possessives each other's, one another's)

The twins finish each other's sentences. The volunteers helped one another load the truck. We haven't seen each other since college.

A traditional rule reserves each other for exactly two and one another for three or more. It has never matched actual usage, and no current American style guide enforces it — treat the two as interchangeable.

Dummy (expletive) pronouns#

Dummy pronouns occupy the subject position that English syntax requires, without referring to anything. English does not allow subjectless declarative sentences, so a placeholder is needed.

Members: it, there

Dummy it covers weather, time, distance, ambient conditions, extraposed clauses, and cleft sentences:

It is snowing in Denver. It is almost midnight. It is three miles to the trailhead. It is obvious that she was right. (extraposition) It was Daniel who signed the lease. (cleft)

Existential there introduces the existence of something; the real subject follows the verb:

There is a problem with the invoice. There were sixteen people in the elevator. There seems to be no record of the payment.

A note on classification: existential there began as the locative adverb there and some grammars still analyze it as an adverb rather than a pronoun. It is grouped here because it behaves like a subject — it inverts in questions (Is there a problem?) and appears in tag questions (There's a problem, isn't there?), which adverbs do not do.

Generic / impersonal pronouns#

These refer to people in general rather than to anyone specific.

Members: one, you (generic), they (generic), we (generic)

One should read the contract before signing it. (formal) You never know what the market will do. (informal, = anyone) They say the winter will be mild. (unspecified people) We still don't know what causes most migraines. (humanity in general)

Singular they#

They used with a singular referent — either a person of unknown or unspecified gender, or a specific person whose pronouns are they. Singular they is attested from the late 1300s and is now accepted by the major American style guides, including the AP Stylebook, the Chicago Manual of Style, the MLA Handbook, and the APA Publication Manual.

If a customer complains, ask them to fill out a form. Somebody left their umbrella in the lobby. Alex said they would email the draft tonight.

Singular they takes plural verb agreement (they were, never they was), and its reflexive is themselves or, increasingly, themself when the referent is clearly one person.

The prop-word one / ones#

One substitutes for a countable noun already established, so you can add a modifier without repeating the noun. It is a pro-noun rather than a pro-noun-phrase, and unlike other pronouns it can take a determiner and adjectives.

I don't want the blue shirt; I want the red one. These cookies are stale. Do we have fresh ones? Which one did she pick?

One cannot substitute for a mass (uncountable) noun: say I need some rice → I need some, not I need one.

Archaic and dialectal pronouns#

Learners encounter these in older texts, hymns, legal boilerplate, and regional American speech. They are not part of standard modern usage but are worth recognizing.

3. Forms and inflection#

Pronouns are the only word class in modern English that still inflects for case in a systematic way. Nouns have lost their case system except for the possessive 's, but pronouns preserve three distinct cases plus separate reflexive forms.

  • It is identical in subject and object.
  • His serves as both determiner and standalone pronoun: his car (determiner), That car is his (pronoun).
  • Her is a determiner (her car); the standalone pronoun is hers (That car is hers).
  • Its as a standalone possessive pronoun is grammatically possible but almost never used: The dog found its bone; the cat couldn't find its sounds odd. Rewrite instead.

Pronouns do not take plural -s the way nouns do — the plural forms are suppletive (I → we, not I-s). They do not take comparative or superlative endings. Demonstratives inflect only for number (this/these, that/those). Relative, interrogative, reciprocal, and most indefinite pronouns do not inflect at all, with these exceptions:

4. Position and word order#

Subject and object positions#

Pronouns occupy the same slots as the noun phrases they replace, and English word order is fixed: Subject – Verb – Object.

She emailed him the contract. subject — verb — indirect object — direct object

With a two-object verb you have two orderings. When the direct object is itself a pronoun, American English strongly prefers the prepositional pattern:

Give her the book. (indirect object + direct object) Give it to her. (direct object + prepositional phrase — the normal pattern when the direct object is a pronoun) Not: Give her it. (used in some British regional dialects, very unusual in American English)

Order inside coordinated pronouns#

Convention places the first-person pronoun last in a coordinated phrase. This is politeness, not grammar, but it is expected in careful writing:

My brother and I drove to Chicago. (preferred over I and my brother) The award went to her and me. (preferred over me and her)

Second person conventionally comes first, then third, then first:

You, Marcus, and I are on the same team.

Possessive determiner position#

A possessive determiner comes first in the noun phrase, before any adjectives, and cannot co-occur with articles or demonstratives:

my three old wooden chairs Not: the my chair, this my chair

To express both, use the "double genitive": a friend of mine, that book of hers.

Intensive pronoun position#

An intensive pronoun goes either directly after the noun it emphasizes, or at the end of the clause. Both are correct; the meaning shifts slightly.

The director himself approved it. (immediately after — emphasizes who) The director approved it himself. (clause-final — emphasizes that he did it personally)

Relative pronoun position#

A relative pronoun opens its clause and the clause follows the noun it modifies as closely as possible:

The email that I sent yesterday bounced. Not: The email bounced that I sent yesterday.

When the relative pronoun is the object of a preposition, the preposition either moves with it (pied-piping, formal) or stays behind (stranding, normal in American English):

The colleague with whom I traveled has since retired. (formal, pied-piped) The colleague who I traveled with has since retired. (normal, stranded) The colleague I traveled with has since retired. (zero relative, most natural in speech)

Interrogative pronoun position#

An interrogative pronoun moves to the front of the question. If it is the subject, no auxiliary inversion occurs; if it is anything else, inversion applies:

Who broke the window? (subject — no did) What did she say? (object — inversion with did) Which of these did you order? (object — inversion)

Dummy pronoun position#

Dummy it and existential there always occupy the subject slot and invert like any subject:

There is a delay. → Is there a delay? It is late. → Is it late?

Reflexive vs. intensive placement#

A reflexive object is locked into the ordinary object slot; an intensive can float:

She blamed herself. (reflexive — sits where any direct object sits) Not: She herself blamed.

I myself have never tried it. (intensive — after the subject) I have never tried it myself. (intensive — clause-final)

5. Rules and patterns#

Rule 1: Case follows function, not proximity#

Use the subject form for subjects and the object form for objects, even inside a compound. The trick is to test each part separately.

He and I finalized the budget. (test: He finalized, I finalized) The client thanked Priya and me. (test: thanked me, not thanked I)

Rule 2: After a preposition, always the object case#

between you and me for my wife and me among us according to them

One real exception: when the object of the preposition is a whole clause, the pronoun takes the case its job inside that clause requires, not the case the preposition would assign.

Give the file to whoever asks for it. (whoever is the subject of asks, so subject case wins) Give the file to whomever you trust. (whomever is the object of trust)

Rule 3: A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number, person, and gender#

The teachers submitted their grades. (plural → plural) Each employee must sign their form. (singular → singular they) The committee released its findings. (American English treats collectives as singular)

Rule 4: The antecedent must be unambiguous#

If a reader can reasonably attach the pronoun to two different nouns, rewrite.

Ambiguous: When Dana met Rachel, she was nervous. Clear: Dana was nervous when she met Rachel.

Rule 5: The antecedent must actually exist in the text#

Avoid pronouns whose referent is only implied.

Weak: In the article, it says the deadline moved. Better: The article says the deadline moved.

Rule 6: Use a reflexive when subject and object are the same in the same clause#

She poured herself a coffee. But: She poured her a coffee. (someone else)

Across clause boundaries, use the ordinary object pronoun:

Marco said the manager would call him. (not himself — different clauses)

Rule 7: Do not use a reflexive as a plain subject or object substitute#

Reflexives are not fancier versions of me and I.

✗ Please contact Sara or myself. → ✓ Please contact Sara or me.

Rule 8: Location prepositions take ordinary pronouns, not reflexives#

When the preposition expresses place and the coreference is obvious, English uses the plain object form:

He shut the door behind him. She had her passport with her. He looked around him.

Rule 9: Indefinite pronoun agreement#

Singular indefinites take singular verbs, even when a plural noun sits in between:

Each of the reports is overdue. Neither of the answers was correct. Everybody in the seminars needs a badge.

None may be singular or plural. Singular when it means "not one," plural when it means "not any of them":

None of the milk was spoiled. None of the applicants were qualified.

Variable indefinites (all, any, more, most, some) agree with the noun in the of-phrase:

Some of the water is gone. / Some of the bottles are gone.

Rule 10: Relative pronoun choice#

AntecedentRestrictive clauseNonrestrictive clause
personwho / that / whomwho / whom (never that)
thing or animalthat / whichwhich (never that)
possessorwhosewhose

The car that needs repairs is in the lot. (restrictive, no commas) My car, which needs repairs, is in the lot. (nonrestrictive, commas)

Rule 11: You may omit a relative pronoun only when it is the object of its clause#

The book [that] I read was excellent. (object — omission fine) The book that changed my mind was excellent. (subject — omission impossible) ✗ The book changed my mind was excellent.

Never omit the relative pronoun in a nonrestrictive clause.

Rule 12: Who vs. whom#

Substitute he/she and him/her into the clause. If he fits, use who; if him fits, use whom.

Who called? (He called → who) Whom did you call? (You called him → whom) The agent who handled the sale has retired. (He handled → who) The agent whom we hired last spring has retired. (We hired him → whom)

Do not let an intervening clause fool you:

The candidate who we thought would win lost. (He would win → who, despite we thought)

Rule 13: Existential there agrees with the following noun#

There is one exception. There are several exceptions.

In casual speech, there's is routinely used with plurals (there's two people waiting). This is very common but is still edited out of formal writing.

Rule 14: Reciprocals require a plural subject#

The two departments blamed each other. ✗ The department blamed each other.

Rule 15: Pronouns before gerunds#

Formal writing prefers the possessive; the object case is common and accepted in speech and informal writing.

Formal: I appreciate your taking the time. Informal: I appreciate you taking the time.

Rule 16: Consistency of person#

Do not drift between one, you, and we within a passage.

✗ If one wants to succeed, you must plan ahead. ✓ If you want to succeed, you must plan ahead.

Rule 17: Appositive pronouns take the case of the noun they attach to#

We editors work late. (subject → we) They gave us editors a deadline. (object → us)

Rule 18: Reflexive scope — a reflexive needs an antecedent in its own clause#

✓ The board defended itself. ✗ The board said the CEO would defend itself. → …would defend it.

Rule 19: Compound antecedents#

Two or more antecedents joined by and are plural. Antecedents joined by or or nor are not added together — the pronoun agrees with the nearer one.

The director and the producer brought their own notes. (and → plural) Either the interns or the manager will bring his or her badge. → more naturally: …will bring their badge. Neither the manager nor the interns brought their badges. (nearer antecedent is plural)

When the proximity rule produces something awkward, put the plural element second or rewrite the sentence.

Rule 20: Keep one antecedent-agreement strategy per passage#

Once you have chosen how to refer back to a singular indefinite, stay with it. Mixing strategies inside one passage reads as an error even when each choice is individually defensible.

✗ Every user sets his or her password, and then they confirm it. ✓ Every user sets their password, and then they confirm it. ✓ All users set their passwords, and then they confirm them. (plural rewrite — often the cleanest fix)

6. Common mistakes#

  • ✗ Between you and I, the deal is dead. → ✓ Between you and me, the deal is dead. — Between is a preposition, so it takes the object case; I is subject-only.
  • ✗ Please send the invoice to Karen and I. → ✓ Please send the invoice to Karen and me. — Drop the other name and the error is audible: send it to I is impossible.
  • ✗ Its a good idea to check the manual. → ✓ It's a good idea to check the manual. — It's is the contraction of it is; the possessive its never takes an apostrophe.
  • ✗ The dog wagged it's tail. → ✓ The dog wagged its tail. — Same rule in the other direction: possessive its is apostrophe-free.
  • ✗ Him and me went to the concert. → ✓ He and I went to the concert. — Subjects take the subject case; test each pronoun alone.
  • ✗ Contact myself if you have questions. → ✓ Contact me if you have questions. — A reflexive needs a matching subject in the same clause; myself is not a polite form of me.
  • ✗ He did it hisself. → ✓ He did it himself. — Third-person reflexives are built on the object pronoun (him, them), not the possessive.
  • ✗ They finished the job theirselves. → ✓ They finished the job themselves. — Same rule; theirselves is nonstandard everywhere in edited English.
  • ✗ Each of the students have a locker. → ✓ Each of the students has a locker. — Each is the singular subject; of the students is a modifier and cannot control the verb.
  • ✗ Who did you give the keys to? (formal writing) → ✓ Whom did you give the keys to? — In the clause you gave the keys to him, so the object form is required. Acceptable in speech, flagged in formal prose.
  • ✗ The company announced their new policy. → ✓ The company announced its new policy. — American English treats collective nouns as singular unless the members are acting individually.
  • ✗ Me and my friend are studying together. → ✓ My friend and I are studying together. — Subject case, and convention puts I last.
  • ✗ There's three messages waiting. → ✓ There are three messages waiting. — Existential there agrees with the noun that follows, which here is plural.
  • ✗ The report which I filed on Monday is missing. (American editing) → ✓ The report that I filed on Monday is missing. — American style reserves which for nonrestrictive clauses set off by commas.
  • ✗ The manager, that hired me, has left. → ✓ The manager, who hired me, has left. — That cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause, and who is preferred for people.
  • ✗ Sara told Rebecca that she had been promoted. → ✓ Sara told Rebecca, "You've been promoted." — The original is ambiguous; rewrite rather than leave two possible antecedents.
  • ✗ Everyone should bring his or her own laptop, and they should charge it. → ✓ Everyone should bring their own laptop, and they should charge it. — Keep one strategy; do not mix his or her with they for the same antecedent.
  • ✗ The three of them helped themself. → ✓ The three of them helped themselves. — Themself is reserved for a singular referent; a plural subject requires themselves.

7. American English notes#

Pronoun forms are essentially identical in American and British English. The genuine differences are in agreement conventions, style-guide preferences, and informal vocabulary.

Collective noun agreement — the biggest real difference. American English treats collective nouns as singular and refers back with it; British English commonly treats them as plural and uses they.

American: The team is playing well. It has won five straight. British: The team are playing well. They have won five straight.

This applies to team, band, government, staff, committee, family, company, jury, audience. American writers switch to plural only when the individual members are clearly acting separately: The jury were divided in their opinions.

Restrictive that vs. which. American style guides (Chicago, AP, Garner) enforce that for restrictive clauses and which — with commas — for nonrestrictive ones. British usage allows restrictive which far more freely and most British house styles do not object to it.

American: The proposal that passed will take effect in June. British (also acceptable there): The proposal which passed will take effect in June.

Plural you. Standard English has no distinct plural you, and American English has developed several informal fillers: you guys (nationwide, and now often gender-neutral), y'all (Southern, spreading, and standard in African American English), you all, and regionally yous/youse and you'uns/yinz (western Pennsylvania). British English uses you lot. None belong in formal writing.

Singular they. Both varieties accept it, but American style guides have moved fast and explicitly. Merriam-Webster entered the singular-person sense in 2019; the Chicago Manual of Style and the AP Stylebook both accept they for a person who uses it and for a person of unspecified gender, with AP still nudging writers toward a rewrite when one is easy. The older American workaround he or she now reads as clunky, and generic he is considered outdated in both varieties.

Generic one. In British English, once you start with one, you keep it: One must do one's best. American English tends to find sustained one stilted and either switches to he/she/they or avoids the construction: One must do his best is the traditional American pattern, though many American writers now simply rewrite with you.

Reflexives for me. Using myself in place of me (Please email John or myself) is a feature of business and bureaucratic register, not of a national variety. It is equally common in American offices and British ones, and editors on both sides flag it. This is not a transatlantic difference — do not learn it as one.

Spelling. No American/British spelling differences exist in the pronoun system — pronouns are all short, ancient, closed-class words. Any spelling variation you see (yall vs. y'all) is informal typing, not a transatlantic convention.

What is not different. Whom is declining in both varieties at roughly the same rate. It's me rather than It is I is the norm in both. Each other and one another are used interchangeably in both. Do not expect a British/American split where none exists.

8. Tricky cases#

Words that belong to more than one class#

Many pronoun forms are pronouns only in some sentences. The test is whether the word stands alone (pronoun) or modifies a noun (determiner).

WordAs pronounAs determiner
thisThis is broken.This chair is broken.
thatI saw that.I saw that movie.
whichWhich did you choose?Which color did you choose?
someSome disagreed.Some people disagreed.
allAll are welcome.All guests are welcome.
herI called her.I called her office.
oneI'll take the red one.One attempt is enough. (numeral)

That has a third job entirely — it is also a subordinating conjunction with no pronoun function at all: She said that she was tired. Here that replaces nothing and can usually be deleted.

Reflexive or intensive?#

Same form, different job. Delete the word: if the sentence still means the same thing, it was intensive; if the sentence breaks, it was reflexive.

He cut himself shaving. (delete → He cut shaving — broken, so reflexive) He himself cut the ribbon. (delete → He cut the ribbon — fine, so intensive)

It with four different jobs#

The package arrived. It was damaged. (referential — refers to the package) It is freezing outside. (dummy — weather) It is unfortunate that she resigned. (anticipatory — points forward to the that-clause) It was in Miami that they met. (cleft — focuses in Miami)

There: adverb or dummy?#

Put the box there. (adverb of place — stressed, replaceable with over there) There is a box in the hall. (dummy subject — unstressed, not replaceable)

One has three lives#

One should never assume. (impersonal pronoun) I want the blue one. (prop-word substituting for a noun) One ticket, please. (numeral determiner)

Subject complement case: It is I vs. It's me#

Traditional grammar requires the subject case after a linking verb (It is I, This is she). Modern American usage overwhelmingly prefers the object case (It's me, That's him). Both are defensible; It's me is normal, It is I is formal to the point of sounding stiff outside of set phrases like This is she when answering a phone.

Case after than and as#

The choice depends on whether you treat than as a conjunction (introducing a reduced clause) or a preposition.

She is taller than I [am]. (conjunction reading — formal) She is taller than me. (preposition reading — normal in speech)

Sometimes the two readings mean genuinely different things, and the case is not optional:

He likes pizza more than I [do]. (I like pizza less than he does.) He likes pizza more than me. (He likes pizza more than he likes me.)

Whose with inanimate antecedents#

Some learners are told whose only works for people. It does not — whose is the only possessive relative English has, and it works for anything.

A theory whose predictions fail is not a good theory. The alternative of which is heavier: A theory the predictions of which fail…

Each other's apostrophe placement#

The apostrophe goes on other, never on both words and never after the s:

✓ They wore each other's coats. ✗ each others' coats, ✗ each others coats

The same goes for one another's: They finished one another's sentences.

Vague this, that, which, and it#

A demonstrative or relative that points back at an entire clause rather than a noun is a common source of vagueness. Add a summary noun.

Vague: The vendor missed three deadlines, which upset everyone. Clear: The vendor missed three deadlines, a pattern that upset everyone.

Everyone is grammatically singular but notionally plural#

The verb stays singular; the pronoun that refers back can be they.

Everyone has their badge. (singular verb has, plural-form pronoun their)

This looks inconsistent but is standard modern English and appears in all major style guides.

Who after prepositions in speech#

Whom is required immediately after a preposition; if you strand the preposition, who becomes acceptable.

✓ To whom should I address this? ✓ Who should I address this to? ✗ To who should I address this?

Themself vs. themselves#

Themself is a newer form used with singular they. It appears in Merriam-Webster and is increasingly accepted, but themselves remains safe for both singular and plural. Never use themself with a plural referent.

Possessive antecedents#

A pronoun should not take its antecedent from inside a possessive, in careful writing.

Weak: In Melville's novel, he describes a whaling voyage. Better: In his novel, Melville describes a whaling voyage.

One of those who takes a plural verb#

The relative pronoun's antecedent is the plural noun, not one, so the verb inside the relative clause is plural.

✓ She is one of those managers who insist on a written agenda. (the managers insist) ✗ …one of those managers who insists…

Adding the only flips it, because then the antecedent really is one:

She is the only one of those managers who insists on a written agenda.

By + reflexive: two readings#

He fixed the sink by himself. (without help) He was sitting by himself in the back. (alone)

Both are standard and context disambiguates them. Either way the reflexive still has to match its subject: They went by themselves, never by themself with a plural subject.

10. Practice#

A. Fill in the blank with the correct pronoun.

  1. The invitation was addressed to my husband and ______ .
  2. Neither of the two candidates ______ (has / have) withdrawn from the race.
  3. The committee published ______ report in March.
  4. ______ are three unopened boxes in the storage room.
  5. The consultant ______ we hired last quarter has resigned.

B. Identify the type of the bolded pronoun.

  1. The architect herself reviewed the blueprints.
  2. It was clear that the flight would be canceled.
  3. The neighbors help one another with yard work.

C. Correct the error.

  1. Every applicant must submit their fingerprints, and they should do it hisself.
  2. The proposal, that the board approved last night, effects everyone — its going to change how we work.

D. Mixed review.

  1. My badge is still on my desk, but ______ (her / hers / her's) is already in the drawer.
  2. Correct the error: The team announced their decision, and each of the players were asked to sign it theirselves.
Answers
  1. me — to is a preposition, so the object case is required. Test by dropping the other person: addressed to me.
  1. has — neither is the subject and is grammatically singular, so it takes a singular verb. The intervening of the two candidates is a modifier and cannot control agreement. Neither of the two candidates have is common in speech but is corrected in edited American prose.
  1. its — American English treats committee as a singular collective, so the singular possessive determiner its is correct. No apostrophe.
  1. There — existential there fills the subject slot, and it agrees with the noun that follows: boxes is plural, so are is right.
  1. whom (formal) or who / that / omitted (informal) — the pronoun is the object of hired (we hired him), so whom is the strictly correct form. In everyday American writing, The consultant we hired is perfectly acceptable.
  1. Intensive pronoun — it emphasizes the architect and can be deleted without changing the core meaning (The architect reviewed the blueprints).
  1. Dummy (anticipatory) it — it does not refer back to anything; it holds the subject position for the extraposed clause that the flight would be canceled.
  1. Reciprocal pronoun — one another expresses a mutual relationship between the members of the plural subject the neighbors.
  1. Every applicant must submit their fingerprints, and they should do it themselves. — Singular they is fine after every applicant. The emphatic -self form must match that subject: hisself is nonstandard in any variety, and because the subject is they, the form is themselves (or themself, if you want to keep the singular reference explicit).
  1. The proposal, which the board approved last night, affects everyone — it's going to change how we work. — Three fixes: that cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause set off by commas, so use which; effects should be the verb affects; its should be the contraction it's (= it is). Nothing else in the sentence needs changing.
  1. hers — the blank stands alone with no noun after it, so it needs the independent possessive pronoun hers, not the determiner her. And no apostrophe: her's is never a word.
  1. The team announced its decision, and each of the players was asked to sign it. — Three fixes: American English treats team as a singular collective, so their → its; each is singular, so were → was; and theirselves is not a word in any variety. No -self form is needed here at all, since the players are not acting on themselves. If you want to keep the emphasis, …was asked to sign it themselves is fine — theirselves never is.
