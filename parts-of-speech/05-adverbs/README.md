# Adverbs

> An adverb is a word that modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause, telling us how, when, where, how often, how much, or with what attitude something happens.

## 1. What it does

An adverb adds circumstantial or evaluative information to something else in the sentence. Its most familiar job is modifying a verb — describing the manner, time, place, frequency, or degree of the action. But adverbs modify far more than verbs, and that breadth is what makes the class hard to pin down.

An adverb can modify an adjective (*deeply unhappy*), another adverb (*almost never*), a determiner or quantifier (*nearly all*), a prepositional phrase (*right in the middle*), a numeral (*roughly forty*), and even an entire clause (*Frankly, we lost*). This is the single most useful thing to remember: **the adverb is the class that modifies everything except nouns.** Adjectives modify nouns; adverbs handle the rest. (A short list of fixed patterns bends this rule — *quite a mess*, *the above statement* — and section 5 and section 8 cover them.)

Here are two annotated sentences:

> She **quietly** closed the door **yesterday** because the baby was **still** asleep.
>
> - **quietly** = manner (how she closed it) — modifies the verb *closed*
> - **yesterday** = time (when) — modifies the clause
> - **still** = continuation/aspect — sits in mid-position after *be* and modifies the predicate *asleep*

> **Honestly**, the movie was **almost** **unbearably** long.
>
> - **Honestly** = sentence adverb (the speaker's comment on the whole statement)
> - **almost** = degree — modifies the adverb *unbearably*
> - **unbearably** = degree — modifies the adjective *long*

A final point on terminology. An **adverb** is a single word; an **adverbial** is any element that does an adverb's job in the clause, including phrases and clauses: *He left [in a hurry]* (prepositional phrase), *He left [last night]* (noun phrase), *He left [because he was tired]* (subordinate clause). This document is about adverbs, but the position rules in section 4 apply to adverbials generally.

## 2. Types

Adverbs form an **open class** for manner and a mostly **closed class** everywhere else. The subcategories below overlap: *only* is a focusing adverb, *just* is both focusing and time, *so* is both degree and linking. Classify by the job the word does in the actual sentence, not by a fixed list.

### Adverbs of manner

Manner adverbs answer **how?** They describe the way an action is performed. This is the largest and most productive group; most are formed from adjectives with *-ly*.

Common members: *quickly, slowly, carefully, carelessly, badly, well, loudly, quietly, easily, hard, fast, beautifully, angrily, patiently, suddenly, automatically, deliberately, accidentally, honestly, politely, awkwardly, fluently, secretly, thoroughly*.

- She explained the rules **patiently** to the new hires.
- The engine started **automatically** when I unlocked the car.
- He plays the trumpet **badly**, but he plays it **loudly**.

### Adverbs of place and direction

Place adverbs answer **where?**; direction adverbs answer **where to?** or **from where?** Many are single words that look like prepositions but take no object.

Common members (place): *here, there, everywhere, nowhere, somewhere, anywhere, upstairs, downstairs, outside, inside, nearby, abroad, overhead, underground, downtown, uptown, ahead, behind, above, below*.

Common members (direction): *up, down, in, out, back, away, forward, backward, northward, homeward, sideways, clockwise, counterclockwise, left, right, there* (as in *go there*).

- The keys have to be **somewhere** in this apartment.
- We drove **downtown** and then walked **back**.
- Turn the dial **clockwise** and push the handle **down**.

### Adverbs of time (point in time)

These answer **when?** They locate an event on a timeline relative to now or to another event.

Common members: *now, then, today, tomorrow, yesterday, tonight, soon, later, early, late, recently, lately, already, yet, still, just, immediately, eventually, finally, afterward, beforehand, nowadays, currently, formerly, previously, once, someday, meanwhile*.

- I'll call the landlord **tomorrow**.
- She had **already** filed the paperwork by the time I got there.
- **Eventually** the noise stopped, but I was **still** awake.

### Adverbs of duration

Duration adverbs answer **how long?** They are fewer in number than time adverbs and often combine with perfect or continuous aspect.

Common members: *always* (in the "permanently" sense), *briefly, forever, long, indefinitely, momentarily, overnight, permanently, temporarily, still, since, yet*.

- The store closed **permanently** last March.
- He paused **briefly** before answering the question.
- The exhibit will be here **indefinitely**, so there's no rush.

### Adverbs of indefinite frequency

These answer **how often?** without giving a number. They sit on a scale from 100% to 0% and almost always take mid-position (see section 4).

Approximate scale: *always* (100%) → *almost always, usually, normally, generally, frequently, often, sometimes, occasionally, seldom, rarely, hardly ever, almost never* → *never* (0%).

- I **usually** take the train, but I **occasionally** drive.
- She has **never** missed a deadline.
- We **rarely** eat out on weeknights.

### Adverbs of definite frequency

These give a countable rate. Most are phrases, but several are single-word adverbs, and unlike indefinite frequency adverbs they normally go at the **end** of the clause.

Common members: *daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, annually, hourly, nightly, twice, once*.

- The newsletter goes out **weekly**.
- The team meets **twice** before every launch.
- Bills are reviewed **quarterly** by the finance committee.

### Adverbs of degree (intensifiers and downtoners)

Degree adverbs answer **how much?** or **to what extent?** They modify adjectives, other adverbs, and verbs. Split them into two directions.

**Intensifiers** (turn it up): *very, really, extremely, incredibly, terribly, awfully, so, too, quite* (AmE), *totally, completely, absolutely, utterly, entirely, thoroughly, highly, deeply, perfectly, remarkably, exceptionally, bitterly, dead* (informal).

**Downtoners** (turn it down): *fairly, rather, pretty, somewhat, slightly, a little, a bit, moderately, partly, partially, hardly, scarcely, barely, almost, nearly, practically, virtually, kind of, sort of, just, only*.

- The exam was **extremely** difficult but **not entirely** unfair.
- I'm **fairly** confident, though I'm **slightly** worried about the timing.
- She **barely** finished, and the room was **absolutely** silent.

### Focusing adverbs

Focusing adverbs point at one element of the sentence and either restrict attention to it or add it to something already mentioned. They are famously position-sensitive: moving them changes the meaning.

**Restrictive**: *only, just, merely, simply, purely, solely, exclusively, alone, particularly, especially, mainly, chiefly, primarily, largely*.
**Additive**: *also, too, as well, even, either, neither, again, further*.

- **Only** Marcus brought a laptop. (nobody else did)
- Marcus **only** brought a laptop. (he didn't bring anything else / didn't do anything else)
- She was tired, and **even** the short walk felt hard.

### Viewpoint and domain adverbs

These narrow the frame of reference: the claim is true *from a particular perspective* or *within a particular field*. They usually sit at the front of the clause, set off by a comma.

Common members: *financially, politically, technically, legally, medically, biologically, personally, theoretically, statistically, historically, culturally, economically, logistically, structurally*, plus the productive *-wise* forms (*weather-wise, budget-wise, time-wise*).

- **Financially**, the merger made sense; **culturally**, it was a disaster.
- **Technically**, the warranty expired last week.
- **Weather-wise**, we got lucky.

### Sentence adverbs (stance and comment adverbs)

Sentence adverbs comment on the *whole proposition* rather than on the verb. They express the speaker's judgment of how likely, how fortunate, or how honest the statement is. They are usually set off by commas in front position.

**Certainty / probability**: *certainly, definitely, surely, obviously, clearly, evidently, apparently, presumably, probably, possibly, maybe, perhaps, undoubtedly, arguably, allegedly, supposedly, reportedly, seemingly*.
**Evaluation**: *fortunately, unfortunately, luckily, sadly, regrettably, amazingly, surprisingly, thankfully, ironically, predictably, curiously, understandably, hopefully* (in modern use), *interestingly, naturally, remarkably*.
**Speech-act / manner-of-speaking**: *frankly, honestly, seriously, briefly, personally, admittedly, generally speaking, strictly speaking, confidentially*.

- **Unfortunately**, the flight was canceled.
- The building is **apparently** for sale.
- **Frankly**, I don't think the plan will work.

Note the two-way ambiguity: *Honestly, he answered* (= I'm being honest with you) versus *He answered honestly* (= his answer was honest). The same word is a sentence adverb in one position and a manner adverb in the other.

### Conjunctive (linking) adverbs

Conjunctive adverbs connect a clause to the preceding sentence or clause, showing a logical relationship. Grammatically they are adverbs, **not** conjunctions — which is why they cannot join two independent clauses with only a comma.

| Relationship | Members |
|---|---|
| Contrast | *however, nevertheless, nonetheless, conversely, instead, on the contrary, otherwise, still, yet, though* (final) |
| Addition | *moreover, furthermore, additionally, also, besides, likewise, similarly, in addition* |
| Result | *therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly, so* |
| Sequence | *first, next, then, finally, subsequently, meanwhile, afterward, previously* |
| Example / restatement | *namely, specifically, for example, for instance, that is, in other words, indeed* |
| Concession | *admittedly, granted, of course, certainly* |

- The proposal was late; **however**, the board reviewed it anyway.
- We missed the deadline. **Consequently**, the grant was withdrawn.
- The design is expensive. **Moreover**, it's hard to maintain.

### Interrogative adverbs

These introduce questions about circumstance. There are four core members plus compounds.

Members: *when, where, why, how* (and *how much, how often, how long, how far, how come*).

- **Where** did you leave the charger?
- **How often** does the bus run?
- **Why** wasn't I told?

### Relative adverbs

Relative adverbs introduce relative clauses that modify a noun of time, place, or reason. They replace a preposition + *which*.

Members: *where, when, why, whereby, wherever, whenever*.

- That's the diner **where** we first met. (= in which)
- I remember the summer **when** the power went out. (= in which)
- Nobody explained the reason **why** the policy changed. (= for which)

### Negative and restrictive adverbs

A small set of adverbs carries built-in negative or near-negative force. They matter because fronting them triggers subject–auxiliary inversion, and because they cannot combine with another negative.

Members: *never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, barely, little* (formal), *nowhere, no longer, not once, nowise* (archaic).

- **Never** had I seen such a mess. (inverted)
- **Seldom** does she leave before six.
- He **hardly** spoke all evening.

### Adverbial particles

Particles are the second half of phrasal verbs. They look like prepositions but take no object and belong to the verb's meaning.

Members: *up, down, in, out, on, off, over, away, back, through, around, along, apart, aside, forward*.

- Please turn the lights **off** before you leave.
- The meeting got called **off**.
- She looked the number **up** in the directory.

### Pro-form and substitute adverbs

A few adverbs stand in for a whole adverbial phrase already mentioned, the way pronouns stand in for nouns.

Members: *there, then, so, thus, likewise, otherwise, accordingly, hence*.

- I went to the clinic on Tuesday, and she was **there** too. (*there* = at the clinic)
- Finish the draft by Friday; **otherwise** we lose the slot. (*otherwise* = if you don't finish it)
- He asked me to sign, and I did **so**. (*so* = sign)

### Adverbs of reason and purpose

A small group states why something happened. Most overlap with the linking adverbs of result, but they can also appear clause-internally.

Members: *therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly, why* (in *the reason why*), *purposely, deliberately, intentionally, accidentally*.

- The road was closed; we **therefore** took the long way.
- She **deliberately** left her phone at home.
- The file was **accidentally** deleted during the migration.

## 3. Forms and inflection

Adverbs do not agree with anything. They have no number, no gender, no case, and no person. The only inflection they take is **comparison** (comparative and superlative), and only gradable adverbs take it. Everything else in this section is word **formation**, not inflection.

### Forming adverbs with *-ly*

| Adjective ending | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| most endings | add *-ly* | quick → quick**ly**, strange → strange**ly**, careful → careful**ly** |
| consonant + *-y* | *y* → *i* + *-ly* | happy → happ**ily**, easy → eas**ily**, angry → angr**ily** |
| *-ic* | add *-ally* | basic → basic**ally**, automatic → automatic**ally**, tragic → tragic**ally** |
| consonant + *-le* | drop *e*, add *-y* | simple → simp**ly**, gentle → gent**ly**, terrible → terrib**ly**, possible → possib**ly** |
| *-ll* | add *-y* only | full → ful**ly**, dull → dul**ly**, shrill → shril**ly** |
| *-ue* | drop *e*, add *-ly* | true → tru**ly**, due → du**ly** |

Exceptions worth memorizing: *public* → **publicly** (not *publically*); *whole* → **wholly** (two l's); *sole* → **solely** (keeps the *e*); *shy* → **shyly** (keeps the *y*); *dry* → **dryly** (AmE preferred) or *drily*; *good* → **well** (a suppletive form — there is no adverb *goodly* in modern English).

### Other adverb-forming suffixes and prefixes

| Pattern | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-ward(s)* | direction | forward, backward, upward, homeward, eastward |
| *-wise* | manner / with respect to | clockwise, likewise, otherwise, lengthwise, budget-wise |
| *-ways* | manner / direction | sideways, always, lengthways (AmE usually prefers the *-wise* forms: *lengthwise, edgewise*) |
| *-where* | place | somewhere, anywhere, nowhere, everywhere |
| *a-* prefix | state / direction | aloud, apart, ahead, aside, away, abroad, ashore |

### Flat adverbs (adverbs with no *-ly*)

A **flat adverb** has the same form as its adjective. These are not errors; they are older English forms that survived.

Common flat adverbs: *fast, hard, late, early, straight, long, high, low, deep, near, far, wrong, right, better, best, worse, worst, daily, weekly, monthly, much, little, enough, home, downtown*.

*Well* does not belong on that list: it is the suppletive adverb of *good*, and the identical-looking adjective *well* is a separate word meaning "healthy" (see section 8).

- The train runs **fast** on the express track.
- We got there **early** and stayed **late**.
- Go **straight** for two blocks, then turn **right**.

Informally, American English allows many more adjectives to serve as flat adverbs: *drive slow, come quick, hold tight, play fair, breathe deep, buy cheap, talk loud, sit tight, take it easy*. These are standard on road signs and in speech but generally avoided in formal writing, where *slowly, quickly, tightly* are expected.

### Pairs where the flat form and the *-ly* form mean different things

| Flat form | Meaning | *-ly* form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| hard | with effort | hardly | almost not |
| late | not on time | lately | recently |
| near | close (to) | nearly | almost |
| high | at a height | highly | very / to a high degree |
| deep | far down physically | deeply | intensely, emotionally |
| close | physically near | closely | attentively, in detail |
| free | without cost | freely | without restriction |
| wide | fully (open) | widely | over a broad area |
| most | to the greatest degree | mostly | mainly |
| short | abruptly (*stop short*) | shortly | soon |
| fair | honestly (*play fair*) | fairly | moderately / justly |
| right | directly (*right after*) | rightly | correctly, justifiably |
| just | exactly / only | justly | fairly, with justice |
| pretty | fairly (degree) | prettily | in a pretty way |
| sure | certainly (informal) | surely | I assume / certainly |
| scarce | rare (adj.) | scarcely | almost not |
| even | (adj./focus adv.) | evenly | in equal parts |
| last | most recently | lastly | finally, in a list |
| direct | straight, without stops | directly | immediately / straight |

- She worked **hard** but **hardly** finished. — *hard* = with effort; *hardly* = almost not.
- The plane flew **high** over the ridge; the price is **highly** unusual.
- He arrived **late**, and I hadn't seen him **lately**.

### Comparative and superlative

| Adverb type | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| One-syllable flat adverbs | *-er* / *-est* | fast → faster → fastest; hard → harder → hardest; late → later → latest |
| Two-syllable ending in *-y* | *-ier* / *-iest* | early → earlier → earliest |
| Adverbs in *-ly* | *more* / *most* | carefully → more carefully → most carefully; often → more often → most often |
| Both allowed (informal *-er*) | either | quickly / quicker; slowly / slower; loudly / louder; cheaply / cheaper |

Irregular adverbs:

| Base | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| well | better | best |
| badly | worse | worst |
| much | more | most |
| little | less | least |
| far | farther / further | farthest / furthest |
| late | later | last / latest |

- She finished **faster** than anyone else.
- Please explain it **more clearly**.
- Of everyone on the team, he handles pressure **best**.

**Equal and downward comparison.** Use *as + adverb + as* for equality, *not as / not so + adverb + as* for its negative, and *less / least* to compare downward.

- She types **as fast as** anyone in the office.
- He doesn't explain things **as clearly as** she does.
- The second draft reads **less awkwardly** than the first.

**The paired comparative.** The pattern *the + comparative … the + comparative* links two scales that rise or fall together.

- **The sooner** you file, **the faster** you get paid.
- **The more carefully** you read the contract, **the less** you like it.

**Intensifying a comparative adverb.** Use *much, far, a lot, a good deal, way* (informal), or the softeners *slightly* and *a little* — never *very*.

- ✓ She drives **much more carefully** than she used to. ✗ *very more carefully*
- ✓ The bus got us there **a lot faster**. ✓ He answered **slightly more politely** the second time.

American usage: *farther* is preferred for physical distance (*drive farther*) and *further* for degree or abstraction (*discuss it further*), though *further* is acceptable in both senses. Note also that *most* before an adverb can mean "very" rather than superlative in formal registers: *She spoke most eloquently* = very eloquently.

## 4. Position and word order

English has three adverb positions: **front** (before the subject), **mid** (with the verb), and **end** (after the verb and its objects).

### The three positions

| Position | Where exactly | Typical adverbs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Front | before the subject | sentence, linking, time, viewpoint | **Suddenly**, the lights went out. |
| Mid | after *be*; after the first auxiliary; otherwise before the main verb | frequency, degree, certainty, focus | She **always** arrives early. |
| End | after the verb (and object) | manner, place, definite time/frequency | He read the report **carefully**. |

### Mid-position: the exact rule

1. After the verb *be* when *be* is the only verb: *She is **often** late.*
2. After the first auxiliary when there is one: *He has **never** complained.* / *They will **probably** cancel.* / *It has been **completely** rebuilt.*
3. Before the main verb otherwise: *I **rarely** eat breakfast.*
4. Before *be* or the auxiliary for emphasis or contrast: *She **never** is on time.* / *He **certainly** has tried.*
5. In short answers and with emphatic *do*, the adverb precedes: *I **really** do think so.*

### Mid-position and negation

A frequency or certainty adverb can stand on either side of *not* / *n't*, and the side it takes changes the scope of the negation.

- *I **usually** don't check email at night.* (my usual practice is not checking)
- *I don't **usually** check email at night.* (checking is not my usual practice)
- *She **probably** isn't coming.* (it is probable that she is not coming) — the reverse order, *She isn't probably coming*, is not idiomatic.

Negative adverbs (*never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, barely*) carry the negation themselves and take no *do*-support.

- ✓ He **never** calls. ✗ *He doesn't never call.*
- ✓ We **rarely** hear from them. ✗ *We don't rarely hear from them.*

### Order of end-position adverbials

When several adverbials pile up at the end, the default English order is **Manner – Place – Time** (some sources add Frequency and Purpose: M–P–F–T–Purpose).

- She sang **beautifully** (manner) **at the recital** (place) **last night** (time).
- He works **hard** (manner) **at the lab** (place) **every weekend** (frequency).
- I drove **downtown** (place) **twice** (frequency) **yesterday** (time) **to pick up the permit** (purpose).

Two adjustments to that default:

- With verbs of movement (*go, come, arrive, travel, walk, drive*), place usually comes **before** manner: *She went **home quickly***, not *She went quickly home*.
- A long or heavy adverbial goes last regardless of category: *He explained the problem **calmly to a room full of skeptical investors***.

### Position rules that cause the most errors

**Never split a verb from its direct object with an adverb.**
- ✓ She speaks **English fluently**. (mid-position is also possible, though less common: *She **fluently** speaks four languages.*)
- ✗ She speaks fluently English.

**Degree adverbs go immediately before the word they modify.**
- *very tired*, *almost finished*, *quite clearly*, *rather expensive*, *nearly all the seats*, *right after lunch*, *just outside the door*.

**But *enough* follows the adjective or adverb it modifies** (while preceding a noun).
- The room isn't **big enough**. / She didn't run **fast enough**. / We have **enough time**.

**Focusing adverbs attach to whatever immediately follows them.** Place *only, just, even, also* right before the element you mean.
- I **only** lent him twenty dollars. (mid-position *only* is the ambiguous one — in speech stress picks the target; in writing it is usually read as "I lent it, I didn't give it")
- I lent **only** him twenty dollars. (no one else)
- I lent him **only** twenty dollars. (not more money)

**Fronted negative adverbs force inversion** (auxiliary before subject).
- **Never** *have I* been so embarrassed.
- **Rarely** *does she* miss a call.
- **Not until Monday** *did we* hear back.
- **Hardly** *had he* sat down when the phone rang.

**Mid-position is the neutral slot for frequency and certainty adverbs; front position is emphatic.** Fronting is not an error, but it foregrounds the adverb, so it reads as marked when nothing calls for the emphasis.
- ✓ She **often** forgets her keys. (neutral)
- △ **Often** she forgets her keys. (grammatical but marked)
- ✗ She forgets **often** her keys. (never between the verb and its object)

**Splitting an infinitive is fine in American English.** *To fully understand the risk*, *to really commit*, *to boldly go*. Moving the adverb out can change or blur the meaning: *to not fully understand* ≠ *not to understand fully*.

**Linking adverbs at the front need a comma; between clauses they need a semicolon or period.**
- ✓ It rained all day; **however**, we still went.
- ✓ It rained all day. **However**, we still went.
- ✗ It rained all day, however we still went. (comma splice)

## 5. Rules and patterns

**Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and clauses — never nouns.** If the word describes a noun, you need an adjective.
- *a **careful** driver* (adjective + noun) vs. *He drives **carefully*** (verb + adverb).

**After linking verbs, use an adjective, not an adverb.** The common linking verbs are *be, seem, appear, become, get, grow, turn, remain, stay, look, sound, smell, taste, feel*.
- The soup tastes **good**. (not *goodly*, not *well*)
- She seems **nervous**. / The plan sounds **reasonable**. / He got **angry**.
- Contrast with action uses: *He looked **carefully** at the invoice* (action) vs. *He looked **tired*** (linking).

**Gradable vs. non-gradable adjectives take different intensifiers.**

| Adjective type | Takes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Gradable (*cold, tired, expensive, interesting*) | very, extremely, fairly, rather, quite, a bit, slightly | **very** cold, **slightly** tired |
| Non-gradable / absolute (*freezing, exhausted, perfect, dead, impossible, unique*) | absolutely, completely, totally, utterly, quite (= completely), entirely | **absolutely** freezing, **completely** exhausted |

- ✓ It's **absolutely** freezing. ✗ It's very freezing.
- ✓ The design is **completely** unique. ✗ The design is very unique. (traditional rule; *very unique* is widely heard but criticized in formal writing)

**Use *much*, not *very*, to intensify a comparative or certain past participles.**
- **much** better, **far** better, **a lot** better, **way** better (informal) — not *very better*.
- **much** appreciated, **much** obliged, **much** improved, **greatly** exaggerated.
- But when a participle has become adjective-like, *very* is normal: *very tired, very interested, very confused, very surprised*.

**Only one negative per clause in standard English.** Negative adverbs (*never, hardly, scarcely, barely*) already count as the negative.
- ✓ I **hardly** ever see him. ✗ I don't hardly ever see him.
- ✓ We didn't find **anything**. ✗ We didn't find nothing.

**Adverbs of indefinite frequency answer *how often* and belong in mid-position; adverbs of definite frequency go at the end.**
- ✓ She **always** checks the logs. ✓ She checks the logs **daily**.
- ✗ She daily checks the logs. (marked/archaic)

**Standard intensifier collocations.** Intensifiers are not interchangeable; learn them as pairs.

| Collocation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| bitterly cold / disappointed | intensely |
| deeply concerned / grateful / sorry | intensely (emotion) |
| highly likely / unlikely / recommended / skilled | very (formal register) |
| fully aware / booked / automated | completely |
| painfully obvious / slow | uncomfortably |
| perfectly clear / reasonable / normal | entirely |
| strongly recommend / disagree / suggest | forcefully |
| badly damaged / injured / needed | severely |
| widely available / accepted / criticized | broadly |
| deeply / heavily discounted | substantially |

**Adverbs can modify quantifiers, numerals, and prepositional phrases.**
- **Nearly** all the applicants withdrew.
- We waited **roughly** twenty minutes.
- The store is **right** across the street. / He fell **just** short of the record.

**A few adverbs can modify noun phrases**, but only degree and focusing adverbs in fixed patterns: *quite a mess, rather a shock, only a suggestion, even a child, especially the interns*. (*Such a shame* looks like the same pattern, but *such* is a determiner, not an adverb — see section 8.)

**Adverbs cannot be the object of a preposition** — with the exception of a handful of place/time words that behave like nouns: *from here, until now, since then, by tomorrow, over there, for long*.

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *He drives very careful.* → ✓ *He drives very **carefully**.* — A verb takes an adverb, not an adjective.
- ✗ *She sings really good.* → ✓ *She sings really **well**.* — *Good* is an adjective; the adverb of *good* is *well*.
- ✗ *He speaks fluently English.* → ✓ *He speaks **English fluently**.* — Never place an adverb between a verb and its direct object.
- ✗ *I very like this song.* → ✓ *I **really** like this song.* / *I like this song **very much**.* — *Very* modifies adjectives and adverbs, not verbs.
- ✗ *Always she forgets her badge.* → ✓ *She **always** forgets her badge.* — Frequency adverbs go in mid-position, not before the subject.
- ✗ *The milk smells terribly.* → ✓ *The milk smells **terrible**.* — *Smell* here is a linking verb, so it takes an adjective.
- ✗ *That was real good.* → ✓ *That was **really** good.* — *Real* is an adjective; the adverb is *really*. (*Real good* is common in casual American speech but nonstandard in writing.)
- ✗ *He runs more fast than I do.* → ✓ *He runs **faster** than I do.* — Short flat adverbs take *-er*, not *more*.
- ✗ *She doesn't run enough fast.* → ✓ *She doesn't run **fast enough**.* — *Enough* follows the adjective or adverb it modifies, but precedes a noun (*enough time*).
- ✗ *I don't hardly know him.* → ✓ *I **hardly** know him.* — *Hardly* is already negative; a second negative is nonstandard.
- ✗ *We were late, however we still got seats.* → ✓ *We were late; **however**, we still got seats.* — *However* is an adverb, not a conjunction, so it can't join clauses with a comma.
- ✗ *I have seen him lately at the gym yesterday.* → ✓ *I saw him at the gym **yesterday**.* — *Lately* means "in the recent period" and clashes with a specific past time.
- ✗ *He worked hardly all summer.* → ✓ *He worked **hard** all summer.* — *Hardly* means "almost not," the opposite of the intended sense.
- ✗ *She spoke to me friendly.* → ✓ *She spoke to me **in a friendly way**.* — *Friendly* is an adjective that happens to end in *-ly*; the theoretical adverb *friendlily* is never used in practice, so English falls back on a phrase.
- ✗ *Never I have heard such nonsense.* → ✓ ***Never have I** heard such nonsense.* — A fronted negative adverb requires subject–auxiliary inversion.
- ✗ *He only has been here twice.* (when you mean the number) → ✓ *He has been here **only twice**.* — Put the focusing adverb next to the element it restricts.

## 7. American English notes

Adverbs are one of the areas where American and British English genuinely diverge, though the differences are mostly matters of preference rather than rule.

**Directional adverbs drop the final *-s*.** American English prefers *toward, forward, backward, upward, downward, afterward, anyway*; British English commonly uses *towards, forwards, backwards, upwards, downwards, afterwards*. Both are understood everywhere.
- AmE: *He stepped **backward** and looked **toward** the door.*

**Flat adverbs are more freely used in AmE speech and signage.** *Drive slow*, *Go slow*, *Come quick*, *Sit tight*, and the informal intensifiers *real* and *sure* (*It sure is hot*, *That's real nice*) are characteristically American. They are colloquial, not formal.

***Likely* as a bare adverb is standard in AmE.** Americans write *It will likely rain tonight*; British English generally requires a modifier — *It will very likely rain* / *most likely*.

**Perfect vs. simple past with *already, just, yet*.** American English readily uses the simple past where British English prefers the present perfect.
- AmE: *I **already** ate.* / *Did you finish **yet**?* / *He **just** left.*
- BrE: *I've already eaten.* / *Have you finished yet?* / *He's just left.*

***Quite* points in different directions.** In American English *quite* usually intensifies (*quite good* ≈ very good); in British English it often downtones (*quite good* ≈ fairly good). With non-gradable adjectives both varieties use it as "completely": *quite impossible*.

***Momentarily* and *presently*.** In AmE, *momentarily* commonly means "in a moment" (*We'll be landing momentarily*); the older sense "for a moment" is the British norm. *Presently* in AmE often means "currently" (*She is presently on leave*), whereas BrE keeps the sense "soon."

***Anymore*.** American English writes it as one word (*I don't go there anymore*); British English usually writes *any more*. In parts of the Midwest and Mid-Atlantic, *anymore* also appears in positive sentences meaning "nowadays" (*Gas is expensive anymore*) — regional, not general American.

**Place adverbs.** *Downtown, uptown, someplace, anyplace, everyplace* are American: *I'm heading **downtown***; *Let's go **someplace** quiet.* British English uses *into town*, *somewhere*, *anywhere*.

**Time expressions.** AmE *on the weekend* vs. BrE *at the weekend*; AmE *Monday through Friday* vs. BrE *Monday to Friday*.

**Bare plural time adverbials.** American English readily turns a plural day or time noun into a frequency adverbial with no preposition: *The office is closed **Sundays***, *We meet **Tuesdays***, *She works **nights***. British English more often keeps *on*: *closed on Sundays*.

***Anyways*.** A colloquial American variant of *anyway*, common in speech and casual writing. Keep *anyway* in edited prose.

**Spelling.** The *-ly* forms inherit the base word's spelling differences: AmE *skillfully, willfully, marvelously, fulfillment*-related forms vs. BrE *skilfully, wilfully, marvellously*. AmE also prefers *dryly* over *drily* and *counterclockwise* over *anticlockwise*.

**Mid-position with auxiliaries.** Both varieties allow the adverb on either side of the first auxiliary, and the reported difference is one of tendency, not of rule: *He probably has finished by now* is somewhat more common in American writing, while *He has probably finished by now* is the neutral order everywhere. Neither is an error in either variety.

**Not a difference:** the split infinitive. It is fully standard in both varieties, and the prohibition against it was never a real rule of English.

## 8. Tricky cases

**Words that are both adjective and adverb.** *Fast, hard, early, late, straight, daily, weekly, wrong, right, long, deep, high, low, far, near, only, just, well* work as either class depending on what they modify.
- *an **early** flight* (adjective) vs. *We left **early*** (adverb).
- *a **daily** report* (adjective) vs. *The report runs **daily*** (adverb).

***-ly* words that are adjectives, not adverbs.** *Friendly, lonely, lively, likely, lovely, silly, ugly, deadly, elderly, costly, orderly, timely, leisurely, cowardly, worldly, motherly, ghastly*. To express manner, use a paraphrase.
- ✓ *She greeted us **in a friendly manner**.* ✗ *She greeted us friendlily.*
- *Likely* is the exception: in American English it also functions as an adverb (*It will likely snow*).

***Good* vs. *well*.** *Good* is an adjective. *Well* is normally the adverb of *good*, **but** *well* is also an adjective meaning "healthy" or "not sick."
- *She plays **well**.* (adverb of manner)
- *I don't feel **well**.* (adjective = healthy) — and *I feel **good*** is also correct, meaning cheerful or comfortable.
- *The team is doing **well**.* (adverb) vs. *This is a **good** result.* (adjective)

***Bad* vs. *badly* after *feel*.** *I feel bad about it* is the standard form (adjective after a linking verb). *I feel badly* is widely used in American English for emotional regret; strictly, it describes a defective sense of touch. Use *bad* in writing.

***Hopefully*.** Traditionally a manner adverb (*She waited hopefully*). As a sentence adverb meaning "I hope" (*Hopefully, the package arrives today*), it is now standard and used by careful writers, but a minority of readers still object. In very formal writing, *I hope that* is safer.

***Only*, *even*, *just* create scope ambiguity.** In speech, stress resolves it; in writing, placement must.
- *Even Diego didn't call.* (Diego, of all people)
- *Diego didn't even call.* (calling was the minimum he could have done)

***Very* vs. *much* vs. *very much*.** *Very* modifies adjectives and adverbs; *much* modifies verbs, comparatives, and some participles; *very much* modifies verbs.
- *very tired*, *much better*, *I very much enjoyed it*, *Thank you very much*.
- ✗ *I very enjoyed it.*

**Adverb or preposition?** The same word is a **preposition** when it has an object and an **adverb** (or particle) when it does not.
- *He ran **up** the hill.* (preposition — *the hill* is its object)
- *He looked the number **up**.* (particle — no object of *up*)
- *She's **inside** the building* (preposition) vs. *She's **inside*** (adverb).

**Adverb or conjunction?** *However, therefore, thus, nevertheless, otherwise, still, then* are adverbs and require a semicolon or period between clauses. *But, so,* and *yet* are coordinating conjunctions and join two independent clauses with a comma; *although, because, while, since* are subordinating conjunctions and attach a dependent clause to a main one (with a comma when the dependent clause comes first).
- ✓ *I was tired, **but** I stayed.* ✓ *I was tired; **nevertheless**, I stayed.*

**Noun phrases doing adverb work.** *Last night, this morning, next week, every day, three times, a long way, home* are noun phrases functioning as adverbials. They are not adverbs, but they occupy adverb positions.
- *We arrived **last night** and drove **home**.*

***Ago* is unusual.** It follows its time expression instead of preceding it, and it requires the simple past, never the present perfect.
- ✓ *He left three hours **ago**.* ✗ *He has left three hours ago.* ✗ *ago three hours*.

***Still*, *yet*, *already* differ in polarity and position.**
- *still* = continuing, mid-position, works in positive and negative: *She's **still** waiting.* / *He **still** hasn't replied.*
- *yet* = up to now, end position, negatives and questions: *He hasn't replied **yet**.* / *Have you eaten **yet**?*
- *already* = sooner than expected, mid or end position, positives and surprised questions: *She's **already** left.* / *Are you leaving **already**?*

***So*, *such*, *too*, *enough*.** *So* is an adverb and modifies adjectives/adverbs; *such* is a determiner and modifies noun phrases.
- ✓ *It was **so** cold.* ✓ *It was **such** a cold day.* ✗ *It was so a cold day.*
- *too* = more than is acceptable (*too heavy to lift*); *very* = simply a high degree (*very heavy*). They are not interchangeable.
- *enough* is the mirror image of *too*, and it is the one degree word that **follows** what it modifies: *strong enough*, *soon enough*, *clearly enough* — but *enough chairs* before a noun. ✗ *enough strong*.

***Firstly* vs. *first*; *thusly* vs. *thus*.** *First, second, third* are perfectly good adverbs in lists and are preferred in American English. *Firstly/secondly* are acceptable but heavier. *Thusly* is a nonstandard back-formation; use *thus*.

***Comparative -er* on *-ly* adverbs.** *Quicker, slower, louder, cheaper* as adverbs are accepted informally (*Can you talk louder?*), but *more quickly*, *more slowly*, *more loudly* are safer in formal writing. *More carefully*, *more clearly* have no *-er* alternative.

**Adverbs that modify nouns in fixed phrases.** *The above statement, the then president, the meeting yesterday, our trip abroad, the people here.* These are established patterns, not a general license.

**Zero-derived time adverbs after prepositions.** *Until now, from here, since then, by tomorrow* — a small closed set where an adverb is licensed after a preposition.

## 9. Quick reference

| Subcategory | Question answered | Typical position | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manner | How? | End (or mid) | *carefully, quickly, well, badly, fluently* |
| Place / direction | Where? Where to? | End | *here, outside, downtown, upstairs, away, back* |
| Time (point) | When? | End or front | *now, yesterday, soon, later, already, still* |
| Duration | How long? | End | *briefly, forever, temporarily, overnight* |
| Frequency (indefinite) | How often? | Mid | *always, usually, often, rarely, never* |
| Frequency (definite) | How often? | End | *daily, weekly, twice, annually* |
| Degree (intensifier) | How much? | Before the modified word | *very, extremely, absolutely, so, really* |
| Degree (downtoner) | How much? | Before the modified word | *fairly, slightly, barely, almost, pretty* |
| Focusing | Which one exactly? | Directly before the focus | *only, just, even, also, especially* |
| Viewpoint / domain | From what angle? | Front + comma | *financially, technically, personally, budget-wise* |
| Sentence / stance | Speaker's comment | Front + comma, or mid | *probably, unfortunately, frankly, apparently* |
| Conjunctive / linking | What's the connection? | Front + comma, after `;` or `.` | *however, therefore, moreover, consequently* |
| Interrogative | Asks a question | Front of question | *when, where, why, how* |
| Relative | Introduces a clause | After the noun | *where, when, why, whereby* |
| Negative / restrictive | Negation | Mid; front triggers inversion | *never, rarely, hardly, scarcely, seldom* |
| Particle | Part of a phrasal verb | After verb or object | *up, off, out, back, away* |
| Pro-form | Replaces an adverbial | Varies | *there, then, so, thus, otherwise* |
| Reason / purpose | Why? | Front or mid | *therefore, hence, deliberately, accidentally* |

End-position order: **Manner → Place → Frequency → Time → Purpose.**
Mid-position rule: **after *be*, after the first auxiliary, otherwise before the main verb.**

## 10. Practice

**A. Fill in the blank with the correct form (adjective or adverb).**

1. He answered the question ______ (correct) even though he was nervous.
2. Your explanation sounds ______ (reasonable) to me.
3. She has been working ______ (hard) all semester, and she ______ (hard) ever takes a break.

**B. Put the adverb in the right position.**

4. (always) *My brother is complaining about the weather.*
5. (fluently) *She speaks three languages.*
6. (enough) *This suitcase isn't large for a two-week trip.*

**C. Correct the error.**

7. *The presentation went real good, however the client wasn't convinced.*
8. *Never I have waited so long for a refund.*
9. *He drives more careful than his sister.*

**D. Identify the type of the bolded adverb.**

10. ***Technically***, the contract expired in June; **fortunately**, nobody noticed, and we ***only*** lost one day of coverage.

**E. Choose the correct word.**

11. She sings ______ (good / well), though she isn't feeling ______ (good / well) today.
12. We drove ______ (farther / far) than we had planned.
13. I ______ (hard / hardly) recognized him after the haircut.

**F. Rewrite in standard written American English.**

14. *He walked towards the exit and afterwards drove home real slow.*

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **correctly** — The adverb modifies the verb *answered*, describing how he answered.
2. **reasonable** — *Sound* is a linking verb here, so it takes an adjective, not an adverb.
3. **hard** / **hardly** — *Hard* (flat adverb) = with effort; *hardly* = almost not. The two are not interchangeable.
4. *My brother is **always** complaining about the weather.* — Mid-position: after the verb *be* / the first auxiliary.
5. *She speaks three languages **fluently**.* — Manner adverbs go at the end; never between the verb and its direct object.
6. *This suitcase isn't **large enough** for a two-week trip.* — *Enough* follows the adjective it modifies (but precedes a noun: *enough room*).
7. *The presentation went **really well**; **however**, the client wasn't convinced.* — Three fixes: *good* → *well*, because *went* is an action verb and needs an adverb of manner; *real* → *really*, because only an adverb can modify the adverb *well*; and a semicolon (or period) before *however*, which is an adverb and cannot join two clauses with a comma.
8. ***Never have I** waited so long for a refund.* — A fronted negative adverb triggers subject–auxiliary inversion.
9. *He drives **more carefully** than his sister.* — Adverbs in *-ly* form the comparative with *more*, and the verb *drives* needs an adverb, not the adjective *careful*.
10. **Technically** = viewpoint/domain adverb (true within a particular frame); **fortunately** = sentence/evaluative adverb (the speaker's judgment of the whole proposition); **only** = focusing adverb, restricting the loss to one day.
11. **well** / **well** — The first is the adverb of *good*, modifying the verb *sings*. The second is the *adjective* *well*, meaning "healthy," after the linking verb *feel*. (*I feel good* is also correct, but it means cheerful or comfortable rather than not sick.)
12. **farther** — AmE prefers *farther* for physical distance and *further* for degree or abstraction, though *further* is acceptable in both senses. Bare *far* has no comparative force and cannot take *than* here.
13. **hardly** — *Hardly* = "almost not," which is the intended sense. *Hard* would mean "with effort," which does not fit *recognized*.
14. *He walked **toward** the exit and **afterward** drove home **really slowly**.* — AmE drops the *-s* on directional adverbs (*toward, afterward*); *real* → *really* and the flat adverb *slow* → *slowly* in written English. Note the order: with a verb of movement, place (*home*) precedes manner (*really slowly*).
