Question and relative adverbs#

when, where, why, how — asking about circumstances, and introducing clauses about time, place and reason.

← The grammar of adverbs · All groups

The same four words ask questions (Where did you park?) and introduce relative clauses (the lot where I parked). In indirect questions the word order returns to normal — Do you know where I parked?, not where did I park — which is the most persistent error with this group. The -ever compounds (wherever, whenever, however) mean no matter where/when/how.

when#

Pronunciation
/wɛn/ · WEN
Русский
когда
Type
interrogative; also relative
Position
front — first in a question, or first in its own clause after a time noun (the day when…)
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a whole clause — asks for or names a time
Contrast
Tense: a when question asks for a point in time, so it takes the past simple, not the present perfect — When did you arrive?, never When have you arrived? Word order: an indirect question keeps statement order — Do you know when the store opens?, not when does the store open. Will after when is fine in an indirect question (I don't know when she'll call) but wrong in a time clause (Call me when you land, not when you will land); that time-clause use, and when meaning considering that or although (Why buy it when you can borrow it?), are the conjunction when, covered in the conjunctions dictionary. Right after a time noun, a relative when can be replaced by that or dropped: the day (that) we met; after a comma (in 2019, when prices were low) it cannot.

When has 3 distinct senses.

1. At what time; on what occasion — in direct and indirect questions. (когда)

  1. When does the pharmacy close tonight?
  2. Do you know when the next bus comes?
  3. When did you move to Denver?

2. At or on which — after a time noun, or after a time already named, and in that's when. (когда (в который))

  1. I'll never forget the day when you were born.
  2. We bought the house in 2019, when prices were still low.
  3. That's when I realized I'd left my wallet at the gym.

3. In what circumstances; in what cases — asking about conditions rather than a clock time. (в каких случаях, когда)

  1. When is it OK to use a semicolon?
  2. When would I ever need to know calculus?
  3. When do you need a permit to build a fence?

where#

Pronunciation
/wɛr/ · WAIR
Русский
где, куда, откуда
Type
interrogative; also relative
Position
front — first in a question, or first in its own clause after a place or situation noun (the town where…)
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a whole clause — asks for or names a place or a situation
Contrast
Russian splits где, куда and откуда; English uses where for all three and lets a final preposition do the rest — Where are you from? (откуда), never From where are you?; Where are you going? needs no to, and Where are you at? is informal. As a relative, where already contains the preposition: the house where I grew up or the house I grew up in, never where I grew up in. A verb that takes a direct object needs that or which, not where: the city that we visited, not the city where we visited. Indirect questions keep statement order: Do you know where she lives?, not where does she live. A clause that asks nothing and follows no noun or that's (Sit where you like, Where there's smoke, there's fire) is the conjunction where. Spelling: where sounds like wear; were rhymes with her.

Where has 4 distinct senses.

1. In, at, to or from what place — in direct and indirect questions. (где, куда, откуда)

  1. Where did you park the car?
  2. Where are you from originally?
  3. Can you tell me where the restrooms are?

2. In what situation, at what stage or in what respect — in questions. (где, в чём, на каком этапе)

  1. Where do you stand on working from home?
  2. Where did we go wrong with this project?
  3. Where are we on the budget?

3. At, in or to which place — after a place noun or a place already named, and in that's where. (где, в котором, куда)

  1. This is the diner where we had our first date.
  2. They moved to Austin, where her sister lives.
  3. That's where I left my keys.

4. In which situation, case or stage — after nouns like point, case and situation. (когда, в котором, при котором)

  1. We've reached the point where nobody is listening anymore.
  2. There are cases where the rule doesn't apply.
  3. It's a situation where everybody loses.

why#

Pronunciation
/waɪ/ · WY
Русский
почему, зачем
Type
interrogative; also relative
Position
front — first in a question, or first in its own clause after reason or that's
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a whole clause — asks for or names a reason or purpose
Contrast
Why don't you…? and Why don't we…? are usually suggestions, not questions about a reason, so an answer starting Because… misses the point. Why not takes a bare verb: Why not ask her?, never Why not to ask her? Unlike how and where, why takes no to-infinitive: I don't know why I should go, not why to go. Why covers both почему and зачем; for purpose, speech also uses What for? (What did you buy that for?). Say the reason why or the reason that, and in careful writing The reason is that…, not The reason is because…. Indirect questions keep statement order: Tell me why you left, not why did you leave. Why, hello! is an old-fashioned interjection, not this adverb.

Why has 3 distinct senses.

1. For what reason or purpose — in direct and indirect questions. (почему, зачем)

  1. Why are you so late?
  2. I have no idea why she quit.
  3. Why would anyone pay that much for a parking spot?

2. For which — after reason, and in that's why and this is why. ((вот) почему, по которой)

  1. That's why I called you first.
  2. The reason why rents keep rising is simple: there aren't enough apartments.
  3. Nobody told us the reason why the bridge was closed.

3. In suggestions — in Why not…? and Why don't you/we…? questions. (почему бы не)

  1. Why not take the train instead?
  2. Why don't we just order a pizza?
  3. Why don't you ask your manager about it?

how#

Pronunciation
/haʊ/ · HOW
Русский
как
Type
interrogative; also relative (that's how) and degree (How nice!)
Position
front — first in a question, exclamation or clause; before its adjective or adverb in how old, how fast
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
verbs and whole clauses; adjectives, adverbs and much/many (how old, how often)
Contrast
Russian как often wants what in English: What do you call this in English? (not How do you call it), What is it called?, What do you think? (not How do you think?), What does she look like? (not How does she look like?). How is he? asks about health or mood; What is he like? asks about character. Exclamations use how with an adjective or adverb but what (a) with a noun — How beautiful!, but What a beautiful house! — and no inversion: How tall you've gotten!, not How tall have you gotten! How come keeps statement order: How come you're late? How do you do? is a formal greeting, not a question. Never the way how — say the way or how. Indirect questions keep statement order: I don't know how it works, not how does it work.

How has 7 distinct senses.

1. In what way; by what means. (как, каким образом)

  1. How did you get here so fast?
  2. Can you show me how this app works?
  3. I never learned how to swim.

2. In what state or condition; with what result — asking about health, mood or how something went. (как)

  1. How are you doing?
  2. How was your flight?
  3. How did the interview go?

3. To what degree, amount or extent — before an adjective, an adverb, or much/many. (насколько, сколько)

  1. How old is your son?
  2. How much did the tickets cost?
  3. How often do you work out?

4. The way in which — in that's how, this is how, and after verbs like love or remember. ((вот) как, то, как)

  1. That's how we met.
  2. I love how you decorated the living room.
  3. This is how you fold a fitted sheet.

5. In How about…? — making a suggestion or an offer, or turning the same question back to someone. (как насчёт, а (ты))

  1. How about a cup of coffee?
  2. How about we leave at seven?
  3. I'm free on Friday. How about you?

6. Why; for what reason — in how come, and in How could…? or How dare…? expressing surprise or protest. (как (же), почему, как так)

  1. How could you forget her birthday?
  2. How come you're still awake?
  3. How dare he talk to you like that?

7. To a very great degree — in exclamations. (как, какой)

  1. How nice of you to come!
  2. How quickly the summer went by!
  3. Look how big your puppy has gotten!

whereby#

Pronunciation
/wɛrˈbaɪ/ · wair-BY
Русский
посредством которого, согласно которому
Type
relative — formal
Position
front — first in its own clause, right after a noun of method or arrangement
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a clause that follows a noun such as system, process, agreement
Form
compound — where + by, meaning by which
Contrast
Formal — contracts, policy and news writing; in speech say in which, under which, or just that (a system that checks every claim twice). Despite the spelling it has nothing to do with place: the town where I live, never the town whereby I live. It always follows a noun naming a method or an arrangement, and in modern English it cannot open a question. Do not confuse it with the conjunction whereas (тогда как).

Whereby has 2 distinct senses.

1. By means of which — explaining how a method or process works. (посредством которого, при котором)

  1. The bank uses a system whereby every transfer is checked twice.
  2. They developed a process whereby old plastic is turned back into fuel.

2. Under which; according to which — stating the terms of an agreement or rule. (согласно которому, по которому)

  1. The two cities signed an agreement whereby they share one water supply.
  2. The company has a policy whereby employees can't accept gifts worth more than $50.

wherever#

Pronunciation
/wɛrˈɛvər/ · wair-EV-ur
Русский
где угодно, куда угодно
Type
place — bare or wherever; also interrogative (the emphatic Wherever did…?)
Position
front or end — bare wherever closes a list or a clause; Wherever did…? opens a question
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a verb, or stands at the end of a list of places
Form
where + -ever — no matter where, any place at all
Contrast
Its most common use takes a whole clause — Wherever you go, I'll follow (куда бы ни), Sit wherever you like (где хотите) — and that is the conjunction, covered in the conjunctions dictionary; as with when, no will inside it: wherever you go, not wherever you will go. Russian где бы ни has a ни, but English adds no negative: wherever you look, not wherever you don't look. Wherever is not everywhere (везде): I looked everywhere, not I looked wherever. The emphatic question sounds somewhat old-fashioned in American English and is often written as two words: Where ever did you find it?

Wherever has 2 distinct senses.

1. Any place at all; somewhere or other — used bare, often at the end of a list. (где угодно, куда угодно)

  1. We could go to Miami, San Diego, or wherever — I just need a beach.
  2. Just put your bags down wherever.
  3. You can buy it at Target, Walmart, wherever.

2. Where — in questions, stressing surprise or impatience. (где же, куда же)

  1. Wherever did you find that old photo?
  2. Wherever have you been? We were worried sick.

whenever#

Pronunciation
/wɛnˈɛvər/ · wen-EV-ur
Русский
когда угодно, когда-нибудь
Type
time — bare or whenever; also interrogative (the emphatic Whenever did…?)
Position
front or end — bare whenever closes a list or a reply; Whenever did…? opens a question
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a verb, or stands at the end of a list of times
Form
when + -ever — no matter when, any time at all
Contrast
Its most common use takes a whole clause — Whenever it rains, the basement floods (всякий раз, когда), Come whenever you want (когда угодно) — and that is the conjunction, covered in the conjunctions dictionary; no will inside it: whenever you're ready, not whenever you will be ready. Russian когда бы ни has a ни, but English adds no negative: whenever I call, not whenever I don't call. For one single occasion use when: when I was born, never whenever I was born. Bare Whenever. as a reply can sound offhand, as if the speaker doesn't care. The emphatic question is dated and often written when ever.

Whenever has 2 distinct senses.

1. At any time at all; at some time or other — used bare, often at the end of a list or as a reply. (когда угодно, когда-нибудь)

  1. Call me tomorrow, next week, or whenever.
  2. "When should I stop by?" "Whenever — I'm home all day."
  3. We'll repaint the deck this summer or whenever.

2. When — in questions, stressing surprise or impatience. (когда же)

  1. Whenever did you find the time to paint all these?
  2. Whenever is he going to grow up?

wherein#

Pronunciation
/wɛrˈɪn/ · wair-IN
Русский
в котором, в чём
Type
relative — formal; also interrogative (Wherein lies…?)
Position
front — first in its own clause after a noun, or first in a question
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
a clause that follows a noun, or a whole question
Form
compound — where + in, meaning in which
Contrast
Legal and literary; in ordinary English say in which, where, or in what way. Therein lies the problem (вот в чём проблема) points back at something already said, while wherein relates or asks — and neither is the two-word where in of Where in the world is my phone?

Wherein has 2 distinct senses.

1. In which — after a noun, usually in legal or formal writing. (в котором)

  1. The lease has a clause wherein the tenant agrees to pay for all repairs.
  2. She described a future wherein nobody owns a car.

2. In what; in what respect — in direct and indirect questions, mostly Wherein lies…?. (в чём)

  1. Wherein lies the appeal of a four-hour movie?
  2. The report never explains wherein the two proposals really differ.

whereupon#

Pronunciation
/ˌwɛrəˈpɑːn/ · wair-uh-PAHN
Русский
после чего, вслед за чем
Type
relative — formal, linking to the whole clause before it
Position
front — first in its own clause, after the main clause and a comma
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
the whole preceding clause
Form
compound — where + upon, meaning upon which
Contrast
Formal and narrative — police reports, court records, storytelling. It follows the main clause after a comma and means and right then, because of that; in conversation say and then or at which point. A new sentence opening with Whereupon belongs to meeting minutes and court transcripts (Whereupon, the meeting was adjourned); elsewhere keep it inside the sentence. Do not confuse it with whereas (тогда как), which contrasts two facts instead of putting events in order. Many learner's dictionaries label it a conjunction; it works like whereby and wherein, a relative word standing for upon which.

Immediately after which, and usually because of it.

  1. The reporter called him a liar, whereupon he stood up and walked out.
  2. She mentioned the word "bonus," whereupon everyone suddenly paid attention.
  3. The judge read the verdict, whereupon the courtroom erupted.

somehow#

Pronunciation
/ˈsʌmhaʊ/ · SUM-how
Русский
как-нибудь, каким-то образом, почему-то
Type
manner — the indefinite of how; also stance (for some reason)
Position
any position — Somehow, we…, we somehow…, …somehow
Comparison
not gradable
Modifies
verbs and whole clauses; sometimes an adjective (somehow different)
Form
some + how — the indefinite of how, like somewhere from where
Contrast
Russian как-то and как-нибудь cover more than somehow. Как-то раз is once or one day, not somehow; зайду как-нибудь is I'll drop by sometime, not somehow; как-то странно is usually kind of strange. Use somehow only for an unknown means (We'll manage somehow) or an unexplained feeling (Somehow I don't trust him). Somehow is not somewhat (немного) and not anyhow (в любом случае); somehow or other is an informal, stronger somehow.

Somehow has 2 distinct senses.

1. In some way that is not known or not explained. (как-нибудь, каким-то образом)

  1. Don't worry, we'll come up with the money somehow.
  2. Somehow the dog got out of the yard again.
  3. She somehow finished the marathon on a broken toe.

2. For a reason, or in a way, that you cannot explain. (почему-то, как-то)

  1. Somehow I just don't believe him.
  2. The house felt somehow different after the renovation.
  3. It somehow seems wrong to throw these letters away.
