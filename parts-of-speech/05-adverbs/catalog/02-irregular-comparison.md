# Adverbs with irregular comparison

> The handful of adverbs that do not take *-er* or *more*: *well*, *badly*, *much*, *little*, *far* and their forms.

[← The grammar of adverbs](../README.md) &middot; [All groups](README.md)

Almost every gradable adverb compares with *-er/-est* (*faster*) or *more/most* (*more carefully*). A few do neither and have forms that must simply be learned — *well*, *better*, *best*; *badly*, *worse*, *worst*. Each comparative and superlative has its own entry, because *better* and *further* do work of their own (*you had better go*, *discuss it further*) that the base word never does.

---
### well

**Pronunciation:** /wɛl/ &middot; *WEL*
**Русский:** хорошо
**Type:** manner; also degree
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*She speaks French well*); before its word as a degree adverb (*well over*, *well aware*); mid after *may*, *might*, *could* and *can* (*You may well be right*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *better*, *best*
**Modifies:** verbs; as a degree word, prepositions, adverbs, numbers and a few adjectives (*well after*, *well worth*)
**Form:** suppletive — the adverb of the adjective *good*
**Contrast:** *Good* is the adjective, *well* the adverb: *She's a good singer* / *She sings well* — *She sings good* is heard in casual American speech but is marked as wrong in careful English. *I'm well* (healthy) is the adjective, and *Well, I don't know* is the interjection; neither is this adverb. *As well* goes at the end of the clause (*I'll have a coffee as well*), not in the middle like Russian *тоже*. Before a past participle *well* makes a compound adjective that is hyphenated before a noun: *a well-known writer*, but *The writer is well known*.

Well has 11 distinct senses.

**1. In a good, skillful or successful way.** (хорошо)

1. She speaks Spanish really *well*.
2. The interview went *well*, I think.
3. He plays guitar *better* than anyone else in the band.

**2. as well: also; too.** (тоже, также)

4. I'd like a side salad *as well*.
5. She's a singer, and she writes her own songs *as well*.

**3. Thoroughly and carefully; completely.** (как следует, тщательно, хорошо)

6. Shake *well* before using.
7. I don't know him very *well*.
8. Make sure you dry your hair *well* before you go out.

**4. By a large amount; far — before prepositions, adverbs and numbers.** (намного, значительно, далеко)

9. The party went on *well* after midnight.
10. The repairs cost *well* over $2,000.
11. Stand *well* back from the grill.

**5. Fully; quite — with *aware* and *worth*, and after *can* or *could* with *imagine*, *believe* or *afford*.** (вполне, прекрасно)

12. I'm *well* aware of the risks.
13. The museum is *well* worth a visit.
14. I can *well* imagine how upset she was.

**6. Quite possibly; very likely — after *may*, *might* and *could*.** (вполне возможно, очень может быть)

15. You may *well* be right.
16. It could *well* rain tomorrow, so bring a jacket.

**7. might as well, may as well: there is no good reason not to; it would be just as good to.** (можно и, лучше уж)

17. There's nothing on TV, so we *might as well* go to bed.
18. You *may as well* take the umbrella — it's by the door anyway.

**8. Kindly or with approval.** (хорошо (отзываться, обращаться))

19. Everyone at the office speaks *well* of her.
20. They treated us very *well* at the hotel.

**9. With enough money; in comfort.** (в достатке, хорошо)

21. They live *well* on two teachers' salaries.
22. The job doesn't pay *well*, but I love it.

**10. Reasonably or properly — in *can't very well*, meaning that it would be wrong or awkward to do something.** (никак (не), не ... же)

23. I can't very *well* say no to my own sister.
24. We couldn't very *well* leave before the speeches were over.

**11. Wisely — in *would do well to*.** (стоит, разумно)

25. You would do *well* to listen to her advice.
26. New drivers would do *well* to stay off the highway at rush hour.

### badly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbædli/ &middot; *BAD-lee*
**Русский:** плохо; сильно, очень
**Type:** manner; also degree
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*He sang badly*); mid with *need*, *want* and before past participles (*I badly need*, *badly damaged*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *worse*, *worst*
**Modifies:** verbs and past participles
**Form:** -ly from *bad*
**Contrast:** *Bad* is the adjective: *I feel bad* (sorry, guilty or sick) is the standard form, and *I feel badly* is common in American speech but often corrected. In casual American English *bad* also stands in for *badly* in the 'very much' sense (*I want it so bad*) — normal to hear, but avoid it in writing. When the meaning is 'less well', the comparative is *worse*, never *more badly*. Russian *мне плохо* is *I feel sick* or *I don't feel well*, not *I feel badly*.

Badly has 4 distinct senses.

**1. In a poor, unskillful or unsuccessful way.** (плохо)

1. I did *badly* on the chemistry test.
2. The negotiations went *badly* from the start.
3. Our team played even *worse* in the second half.

**2. Seriously; causing a lot of harm or damage.** (сильно, тяжело, серьёзно)

4. Two people were *badly* hurt in the crash.
5. The roof was *badly* damaged by the storm.
6. The towns *worst* hit by the flooding are still without power.

**3. Very much — with *need*, *want* and *miss*.** (очень, крайне)

7. The house *badly* needs a coat of paint.
8. She wanted that job so *badly*.

**4. Unkindly, unfairly or in a way that is not acceptable.** (плохо, дурно, несправедливо)

9. The company treated its workers *badly*.
10. The kids behaved *badly* at the restaurant.
11. Please don't think *badly* of me.

### much

**Pronunciation:** /mʌtʃ/ &middot; *MUHCH*
**Русский:** очень, намного; часто
**Type:** degree; also frequency
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*I don't like it much*); before its word with comparatives and *too* (*much bigger*, *much too big*); mid with *prefer*, *appreciate* and *enjoy* (*I much prefer tea*)
**Comparison:** irregular — *more*, *most*
**Modifies:** verbs, comparatives, *too*, past participles and *the same*
**Contrast:** Bare *much* after a verb in an affirmative statement sounds wrong: say *I like it a lot* or *I like it very much*, not *I like it much* — but *much* is natural in questions and negatives (*Do you like it much?*, *I don't like it much*). *Much* goes before comparatives and *very* before plain adjectives: *much bigger*, *very big*, never *very bigger*. Russian *много* after a verb is *a lot* in speech (*He works a lot*), and *гораздо* or *намного* is *much* or *a lot* before a comparative. *Much* before a noun (*much money*) is the determiner and belongs to that dictionary.

Much has 6 distinct senses.

**1. To a great degree; a lot — mostly in questions and negatives, after *very* and *so*, and before past participles.** (очень, сильно)

1. I don't like horror movies *much*.
2. Thank you so *much* for coming.
3. Your help is very *much* appreciated.

**2. Far; a lot — before comparatives, *too* and *different*.** (намного, гораздо)

4. The new apartment is *much* bigger than the old one.
5. This coffee is *much* too sweet.
6. The new phone isn't *much* different from the old one.

**3. Often; a lot of the time — in questions and negatives.** (часто, много)

7. Do you get out *much*?
8. We don't eat out *much* anymore.
9. We travel *more* now that the kids are grown.

**4. Nearly; about — in *much the same* and *pretty much*.** (почти, примерно)

10. The town looks *much* the same as it did twenty years ago.
11. That's *pretty much* all I know about it.

**5. not so much ... as: not really one thing but rather another — correcting how something is described.** (не столько ..., сколько)

12. It's *not so much* the money — it's the principle.
13. He's *not so much* lazy as bored at school.
14. The problem wasn't *so much* the price as the delivery time.

**6. much less: and certainly not; let alone — after a negative.** (не говоря уже о, тем более не)

15. He can't boil an egg, *much less* cook a whole dinner.
16. I've never even met her, *much less* gone out with her.

### little

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪtəl/ &middot; *LIT-uhl*
**Русский:** немного; мало, почти не
**Type:** degree; also frequency
**Position:** before its word — in *a little* before adjectives, adverbs and comparatives (*a little tired*); end after the verb (*She slept little*); front with inversion in *Little did I know*
**Comparison:** irregular — *less*, *least*
**Modifies:** verbs, adjectives, adverbs and comparatives (*a little faster*)
**Contrast:** *A little* and bare *little* point in opposite directions: *I slept a little* (немного — I did get some sleep) vs *I slept little* (мало — not enough). In everyday American speech bare *little* sounds formal, and people say *not much* instead (*I didn't sleep much*). When *little* opens a clause it means 'not at all' and needs question word order: *Little did we know*, never *Little we knew*. The comparative is *less*, *least*; *littler* and *littlest* exist only for the adjective meaning small (*my littlest sister*), which belongs to the adjective dictionary.

Little has 4 distinct senses.

**1. a little: to a small degree; slightly.** (немного, слегка)

1. I'm *a little* tired tonight.
2. Could you turn the music down *a little*?
3. Drive *a little* slower through the school zone.

**2. Not much; hardly at all — without *a*, often after *very*.** (мало, почти не)

4. The neighborhood has changed very *little*.
5. He ate *little* and said even *less*.
6. We see them very *little* these days.

**3. Not at all — before *know*, *think*, *realize* and *suspect*, usually at the front with inversion.** (совсем не, и не (подозревать))

7. *Little* did I know that she was going to be my new boss.
8. *Little* did they realize how much the trip would cost.

**4. little by little: gradually; a bit at a time.** (постепенно, понемногу)

9. *Little by little*, the town recovered from the flood.
10. She paid off the loan *little by little*.

### better

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɛtər/ &middot; *BET-ur*
**Русский:** лучше; больше
**Type:** manner; also degree and linking
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*She sings better*); mid in *had better* (*You'd better go*) and before a past participle (*better known*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the comparative of *well*: *well*, *better*, *best*
**Modifies:** verbs and past participles
**Form:** suppletive — the comparative of *well*
**Contrast:** *Had better* is not a past tense and takes the bare verb: *You'd better go*, never *You'd better to go* or *You had better went*; the negative is *You'd better not*. It is stronger than *should* and can sound like a warning, so it is odd when advising a boss or a stranger. In casual American speech the *had* often drops out (*You better call her*), but keep it in writing. Russian *лучше* in a preference or suggestion is *I'd rather* or *let's*, not *better*: *Лучше я пойду пешком* is *I'd rather walk*. Americans often say *I like the blue one better* where British speakers tend to say *more*. *I feel better* (healthier) is the adjective.

Better has 6 distinct senses.

**1. In a more skillful, successful or satisfactory way — the comparative of *well*.** (лучше)

1. She speaks French *better* than I do.
2. The second interview went much *better*.
3. Things are going *better* at work now.

**2. More; to a greater degree — with *like*, *know* and *understand*, before past participles, and informally in *better than* before a number.** (больше, лучше; больше чем)

4. Which do you like *better*, the mountains or the beach?
5. He's *better* known as a painter than as a writer.
6. The drive took *better* than three hours.

**3. had better: ought to; should — often as a warning.** (лучше (бы), следует)

7. You'd *better* take an umbrella.
8. We *had better* leave before the traffic gets bad.
9. You *better* not tell Mom about this.

**4. know better: to be old enough or wise enough not to do something.** (понимать (что так нельзя), соображать)

10. You're twenty years old — you should *know better*.
11. She *knew better* than to argue with her father.

**5. think better of: to decide not to do something after thinking about it again.** (передумать, одуматься)

12. He opened his mouth to argue, then *thought better* of it.
13. I almost sent the angry email, but I *thought better* of it.

**6. better yet, better still: an even better idea is.** (а ещё лучше)

14. Call her tonight — or *better yet*, go see her.
15. Text me when you land, or *better still*, just come straight over.

### best

**Pronunciation:** /bɛst/ &middot; *BEST*
**Русский:** лучше всего; больше всего
**Type:** manner; also degree
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*She sings best*); before its word with past participles (*best known*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the superlative of *well*: *well*, *better*, *best*
**Modifies:** verbs and past participles
**Form:** suppletive — the superlative of *well*
**Contrast:** After a verb, American English uses *best* with or without *the*: *Which song do you like best?* and *Which song do you like the best?* are both normal. Before a noun, *best* is the adjective (*my best friend*) and belongs to that dictionary. *At best* (в лучшем случае) is not *at its best* (в лучшей форме): *The team was at its best in the final*. *Had best* (*You had best leave*) is an old-fashioned or regional form of *had better*.

Best has 4 distinct senses.

**1. In the most skillful, successful or satisfactory way — the superlative of *well*.** (лучше всего)

1. She works *best* under pressure.
2. Which of the three cars handled *best* in the snow?
3. We fixed the fence as *best* we could.

**2. Most; to the greatest degree — with *like* and *know*, and before past participles.** (больше всего)

4. What did you like *best* about Chicago?
5. He's *best* known for his early films.
6. You know her *best* — what should I get her for her birthday?

**3. In the most suitable or sensible way — before past participles such as *done*, *left* and *avoided*.** (лучше всего, разумнее всего)

7. Some things are *best* left unsaid.
8. Pie dough is *best* made the day before.
9. Downtown is *best* avoided during rush hour.

**4. at best: even in the most favorable view — used to show that something is small, poor or limited.** (в лучшем случае)

10. The plan will save us fifty dollars *at best*.
11. His Spanish is basic *at best*.
12. We'll finish in June *at best*, and more likely July.

### worse

**Pronunciation:** /wɜːrs/ &middot; *WURS*
**Русский:** хуже; сильнее
**Type:** manner; also degree and linking
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*He played worse*); front as a linker (*Worse, nobody told us*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the comparative of *badly*: *badly*, *worse*, *worst*
**Modifies:** verbs and whole clauses
**Form:** suppletive — the comparative of *badly* and of the adjective *bad*
**Contrast:** Never *badder* or *more bad*: *She did worse than last time*. Do not mix up *worse* (the comparative, used with *than*) and *worst* (the superlative, used with *the* or *of all*). *The weather is worse today* is the adjective. In casual American English *worse* also means 'more badly' with *want* and *need* (*I want it worse than you do*) — fine in speech, not in writing.

Worse has 3 distinct senses.

**1. In a poorer or less successful way; less well.** (хуже)

1. I did *worse* on the final than on the midterm.
2. The team played even *worse* after halftime.
3. Things turned out *worse* than we expected.

**2. More severely or seriously; to a greater degree.** (сильнее, хуже)

4. My knee hurts *worse* today.
5. The Gulf Coast was hit *worse* than anyone expected.
6. Nobody wants this job *worse* than I do.

**3. worse, worse still, even worse: and what is more serious — adding a further bad fact.** (хуже того, что ещё хуже)

7. We missed the train, and *worse still*, it was the last one that night.
8. The company lost money. *Worse*, it hid the losses from investors.

### worst

**Pronunciation:** /wɜːrst/ &middot; *WURST*
**Русский:** хуже всего; сильнее всего
**Type:** manner; also degree and linking
**Position:** end — after the verb (*Who played worst?*); before its word with past participles (*worst affected*); front in *worst of all* and *at worst*
**Comparison:** irregular — the superlative of *badly*: *badly*, *worse*, *worst*
**Modifies:** verbs, past participles and whole clauses
**Form:** suppletive — the superlative of *badly* and of the adjective *bad*
**Contrast:** American English says *if worse comes to worst* (на худой конец), and many Americans also say *if worst comes to worst*; British English says *if the worst comes to the worst*. *The worst* before a noun or after *be* (*the worst day*, *That was the worst*) is the adjective and belongs to that dictionary.

Worst has 4 distinct senses.

**1. In the poorest or least successful way.** (хуже всего)

1. Of all the kids in the class, I did *worst* in math.
2. The players who performed *worst* in camp were cut from the team.

**2. Most severely or seriously — often before past participles such as *hit* and *affected*.** (сильнее всего)

3. The coastal towns were *worst* hit by the hurricane.
4. Older patients were the ones *worst* affected by the virus.

**3. worst of all: and the most serious thing is.** (хуже всего (то, что))

5. The food was cold, the service was slow, and *worst of all*, they overcharged us.
6. *Worst of all*, I lost every photo from the trip.

**4. at worst: if the worst happens; in the least favorable view.** (в худшем случае)

7. *At worst*, we'll have to pay a small late fee.
8. The mistake was careless *at worst* — nobody thinks it was dishonest.

### more

**Pronunciation:** /mɔːr/ &middot; *MOR*
**Русский:** больше, более; ещё
**Type:** degree; also linking
**Position:** before its word — before adjectives and adverbs (*more careful*); end after the verb (*You should sleep more*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the comparative of *much*: *much*, *more*, *most*
**Modifies:** verbs, adjectives and adverbs
**Contrast:** Short adjectives and adverbs take *-er*, not *more*: *faster*, *harder*, never *more fast* — and never both at once (*more better*, *more easier*). Russian *больше не* is *not anymore* or *no longer*, never *more not*: *Он здесь больше не живёт* is *He doesn't live here anymore*; American English writes *anymore* as one word in that sense. Russian *ещё* is *more* after a number (*once more*, *two more times*), but *ещё не* is *not yet*, *всё ещё* is *still*, and *ещё* before a comparative is *even* (*even bigger*), never *more bigger*. *More* before a noun (*more time*) is the determiner and belongs to that dictionary.

More has 8 distinct senses.

**1. Used before adjectives and adverbs to form the comparative.** (более (или форма на -ее))

1. The second test was *more* difficult than the first.
2. Could you speak *more* slowly, please?
3. Flights are getting *more and more* expensive.

**2. To a greater degree or extent — with verbs.** (больше, сильнее)

4. You need to sleep *more*.
5. I like him *more* every time I see him.
6. The *more* you practice, the easier it gets.

**3. Again; in addition — in *once more*, *twice more* and similar phrases.** (ещё (раз))

7. Let's try it once *more*.
8. The phone rang twice *more* before anyone picked up.

**4. more or less: approximately; almost.** (более или менее, примерно)

9. The kitchen is *more or less* finished.
10. It'll cost about three hundred dollars, *more or less*.

**5. Rather; more accurately — in *more ... than*, comparing two descriptions of the same thing.** (скорее)

11. She was *more* surprised than angry.
12. He's *more* shy than rude — give him time.
13. For us, the trip was *more* about family than sightseeing.

**6. more than: very; extremely — before adjectives.** (более чем, очень)

14. I'd be *more than* happy to help.
15. The results were *more than* satisfactory.

**7. what's more: in addition, and this matters even more.** (более того, к тому же)

16. The apartment is cheap, and *what's more*, it's close to work.
17. He was late again. *What's more*, he didn't apologize.

**8. no more than: only; not over.** (не более, всего лишь)

18. The drive takes *no more than* twenty minutes.
19. She was *no more than* a child when she left home.

### most

**Pronunciation:** /moʊst/ &middot; *MOHST*
**Русский:** самый, наиболее; больше всего
**Type:** degree
**Position:** before its word — before adjectives and adverbs (*most useful*); end after the verb (*what I miss most*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the superlative of *much*: *much*, *more*, *most*
**Modifies:** verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and in informal American speech *every*, *all* and *everyone*
**Contrast:** *The most* + adjective is the superlative (*the most interesting book* — самая интересная); *a most* + adjective, or *most* with nothing being compared (*That's most kind of you*), is formal and means *very* (*a most interesting book* — весьма интересная). Do not confuse the adverb with *mostly* (mainly): *What I like most is the food* vs *The audience was mostly students*. *Most people* (большинство людей) is the determiner; in that meaning it is never *most of people* (only *most of the people*), and *the most people* means 'the largest number of people'. *Most* meaning *almost* (*most everyone*) is informal American — avoid it in writing. One-syllable words take *-est*: *fastest*, never *most fast*.

Most has 5 distinct senses.

**1. Used before adjectives and adverbs to form the superlative.** (самый, наиболее)

1. It's the *most* beautiful park in the city.
2. Of all the drivers, she drove *most* carefully.
3. Which of these plans is *most* likely to work?

**2. To the greatest degree; more than anything else — with verbs.** (больше всего)

4. What I miss *most* is my mom's cooking.
5. The people who need help the *most* are often the last to ask for it.
6. *Most of all*, I want to thank my parents.

**3. at most, at the most: not more than; at the maximum.** (самое большее, не более)

7. The drive takes an hour *at most*.
8. There were twenty people there, thirty *at the most*.
9. I can stay a week *at most*.

**4. Very; extremely — formal, often after *a*.** (весьма, чрезвычайно)

10. That's *most* kind of you.
11. It was a *most* unusual evening.

**5. Almost — before *every*, *all*, *everyone* and *anyone*; informal American.** (почти)

12. *Most* everyone in town came to the game.
13. We eat there *most* every Friday.

### less

**Pronunciation:** /lɛs/ &middot; *LES*
**Русский:** меньше, менее
**Type:** degree
**Position:** before its word — before adjectives and adverbs (*less expensive*); end after the verb (*I worry less now*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the comparative of *little*: *little*, *less*, *least*
**Modifies:** verbs, adjectives and adverbs
**Contrast:** Everyday speech prefers *not as ... as* to *less* with short adjectives: *He's not as tall as his brother* sounds natural, *He's less tall than his brother* does not — and Russian *менее* before an adjective is often best translated that way. *Less* before a plural noun (*less people*) is a determiner use that careful writing replaces with *fewer*. *Less than happy* is a polite way to say *unhappy*, not a comparison. *Lesser* is an adjective (*the lesser evil*), not a comparative adverb.

Less has 4 distinct senses.

**1. Used before adjectives and adverbs: not as; to a smaller degree.** (менее, не так)

1. The second hotel was *less* expensive.
2. I see her *less* often now that she's moved.
3. The trail is *less* crowded on weekdays.

**2. To a smaller degree; a smaller amount — with verbs.** (меньше)

4. Try to worry *less* and sleep more.
5. I've been driving *less and less* since I started biking to work.
6. My new job pays *less*, but I'm much happier.

**3. less than: not at all; far from — a polite understatement before an adjective.** (далеко не, не слишком)

7. She was *less than* thrilled with the news.
8. The service at the hotel was *less than* perfect.

**4. no less: used to stress that an amount is surprisingly large or a person surprisingly important.** (не менее, целых, не кто иной, как)

9. The house sold for *no less* than three million dollars.
10. The award was presented by the governor, *no less*.

### least

**Pronunciation:** /liːst/ &middot; *LEEST*
**Русский:** меньше всего, наименее
**Type:** degree; also focusing
**Position:** before its word — before adjectives and adverbs (*the least expensive*); mid or end with verbs (*when you least expect it*)
**Comparison:** irregular — the superlative of *little*: *little*, *less*, *least*
**Modifies:** verbs, adjectives, adverbs, numbers and whole clauses (*at least*)
**Contrast:** Do not mix up *at least* (по крайней мере, хотя бы) and *at last* (наконец): *At last you're here!* is relief after waiting. *At least* before a number means 'no fewer than', and after a complaint it means 'still, one good thing is': *At least nobody got hurt*. *Not in the least* (ничуть) is emphatic and a little formal. *The least* before a noun (*the least money*) is the determiner and belongs to that dictionary.

Least has 6 distinct senses.

**1. at least: not less than; at the minimum.** (по крайней мере, не менее)

1. The drive takes *at least* four hours.
2. You should brush your teeth *at least* twice a day.

**2. at least: anyway; even if nothing else is true or is done.** (хотя бы, по крайней мере)

3. The food was bad, but *at least* it was cheap.
4. You could *at least* call if you're going to be late.

**3. at least: or to put it more exactly — used to correct or limit what you have just said.** (во всяком случае, по крайней мере)

5. She's coming to the party — *at least*, she said she was.
6. Nobody saw anything, or *at least* nobody's saying so.

**4. Used before adjectives and adverbs: to the smallest degree.** (наименее)

7. This is the *least* expensive hotel in the area.
8. Math is the subject I'm *least* interested in.

**5. To the smallest degree; less than anything else — with verbs, and in *least of all*.** (меньше всего)

9. Trouble always comes when you *least* expect it.
10. Nobody wanted to move, *least of all* my kids.

**6. not in the least: not at all.** (ничуть, нисколько)

11. I'm not *in the least* surprised.
12. She didn't seem *in the least* worried about the exam.

### farther

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɑːrðər/ &middot; *FAHR-thur*
**Русский:** дальше
**Type:** place; also degree
**Position:** end — after the verb (*We walked farther*); before its word with *away*, *down*, *north* and similar words (*farther down the road*)
**Comparison:** irregular — a comparative of *far*: *far*, *farther*, *farthest*
**Modifies:** verbs, and adverbs and prepositions of place (*farther away*, *farther from*)
**Form:** suppletive — a comparative of *far*, alongside *further*
**Contrast:** Careful American usage keeps *farther* for physical distance and *further* for degree and addition: *We drove farther*, but *Let's discuss it further*. In speech *further* is used for distance too, but *farther* never means 'more' or 'in addition' — *Farther, the report found...* is wrong. Never *farer* or *more far*. *The farther shore* is the adjective.

Farther has 2 distinct senses.

**1. At or to a greater distance in space.** (дальше)

1. I can't walk any *farther*.
2. Their house is a little *farther* down the road.
3. The *farther* north you go, the colder it gets.

**2. At or to a more distant point in time, or to a greater extent.** (дальше, далее)

4. We can trace the family *farther* back than 1800.
5. Nothing could be *farther* from the truth.

### further

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɜːrðər/ &middot; *FUR-thur*
**Русский:** дальше; подробнее; кроме того
**Type:** degree; also place and linking
**Position:** end — after the verb and its object (*discuss it further*); front as a linker (*Further, the study found...*)
**Comparison:** irregular — a comparative of *far*: *far*, *further*, *furthest*
**Modifies:** verbs, whole clauses, and adverbs and prepositions of place (*further down*)
**Form:** suppletive — a comparative of *far*, alongside *farther*
**Contrast:** *Further* covers everything *farther* does and more: only *further* means 'to a greater degree' (*Prices fell further*) or 'in addition' (*Further, the report found...*), though careful American writing still gives physical distance to *farther*. *Further* is also an adjective (*further information* — дополнительная информация) and a verb (*to further your career* — продвигать), both in other dictionaries; the linker *furthermore* is its own word. Russian *и так далее* is *and so on*, not *and so further*.

Further has 3 distinct senses.

**1. To a greater degree or extent; more.** (дальше, ещё больше, подробнее)

1. We need to look into this *further*.
2. Prices dropped *further* in March.
3. I don't want to discuss it any *further*.

**2. At or to a greater distance in space or time, or later in a text.** (дальше, далее)

4. The gas station is a mile *further* down the road.
5. Let's walk a little *further* before we turn back.
6. See the chart *further* down the page.

**3. In addition; moreover — at the start of a sentence, formal.** (кроме того, более того)

7. *Further*, the study found no link between the two.
8. The plan is expensive. *Further*, it would take years to finish.

### furthest

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɜːrðəst/ &middot; *FUR-thuhst*
**Русский:** дальше всего; больше всего
**Type:** place; also degree
**Position:** end — after the verb (*Who traveled furthest?*)
**Comparison:** irregular — a superlative of *far*: *far*, *further*, *furthest*
**Modifies:** verbs, and adverbs and prepositions of place (*furthest away*, *furthest from*)
**Form:** suppletive — a superlative of *far*, alongside *farthest*
**Contrast:** Careful American writing uses *farthest* for physical distance, but *furthest* is common for distance in speech and is the only choice for degree: *the country that has gone furthest in cutting emissions*. *The furthest point* before a noun is the adjective.

Furthest has 2 distinct senses.

**1. At or to the greatest distance in space or time.** (дальше всего)

1. Of the three of us, I live *furthest* from the office.
2. The *furthest* back I can remember is our old house in Ohio.

**2. To the greatest degree or extent.** (больше всего, дальше всех)

3. Of all the candidates, she has gone *furthest* in promising tax cuts.
4. California has pushed *furthest* toward banning gas-powered cars.
