# Determiners

> A determiner is a word placed in front of a noun to show which thing you mean or how much/how many of it there is — *the*, *a*, *this*, *my*, *some*, *three*, *each*, *which*.

## 1. What it does

A determiner opens a noun phrase and answers one of two questions about the noun: **"Which one(s)?"** or **"How much / how many?"** It does not describe the noun the way an adjective does; it *anchors* the noun to the situation — pointing at it, claiming it, counting it, or leaving it vague.

Compare a bare noun with a determined one. *Book* by itself is just a concept. **The** *book* is a specific one you and I both know about. **A** *book* is one, any one. **My** *book* is the one I own. **That** *book* is the one over there. **Three** *books* is a count. Nothing about the noun changed — the determiner did all the work.

In standard English, singular countable nouns almost always **require** a determiner. You cannot normally say *"I bought car"* or *"Cat is sleeping."* Plural and uncountable nouns can appear with no determiner at all, and that absence is itself meaningful (it signals a general, non-specific reading): *Cars are expensive. Water freezes at 32°F.*

Annotated examples:

- **The** manager gave **each** employee **a** bonus. → *the* = the specific manager we know; *each* = distributed one by one; *a* = one unspecified bonus.
- **My** **two** older brothers borrowed **some** money from **those** neighbors. → *my* = possessor; *two* = quantity; *some* = indefinite amount; *those* = pointing away from the speaker.
- **Which** flight did you take — **the** 6 a.m. one or **the** red-eye? → *which* asks for a selection from a known set; *the* identifies each option.
- **All** **the** students in **that** class passed. → *all* is a predeterminer stacked in front of the central determiner *the*.

Determiners form a **closed class**: a small, fixed inventory that the language almost never adds to. That makes them learnable as a complete list — unlike nouns, verbs, or adjectives.

## 2. Types

### Articles

The three-way article system is the core of English determiner use. There are two spoken/written articles plus a meaningful **absence** of an article, traditionally called the *zero article*.

**Members:** `a`, `an` (indefinite), `the` (definite), and the zero article (written as nothing, often marked Ø in grammar books).

- **A** stranger knocked on the door. (indefinite — first mention, identity unknown)
- **The** stranger was holding **a** clipboard. (definite — now we know which stranger)
- Ø Strangers make me nervous. (zero — generic, all strangers in general)

The indefinite article has two forms chosen by **sound**, not spelling: *an* before a vowel *sound*, *a* before a consonant *sound*.

- She waited **an** hour for **a** university representative.
- He has **an** MBA and **a** one-bedroom apartment.

### Demonstrative determiners

Demonstratives point to something in physical space, in time, or in the surrounding discourse. They encode two contrasts: **near vs. far** and **singular vs. plural**.

**Members:** `this`, `that`, `these`, `those`. (Older/regional *yon*, *yonder* survive only dialectally.)

- **This** coffee is cold, but **that** pot over there looks fresh.
- **These** shoes hurt; **those** boots were worse.
- **That** summer in Chicago changed everything. (distance in time)

### Possessive determiners

Also called *possessive adjectives* in older textbooks, though they are determiners. They mark the noun as belonging to, associated with, or related to someone.

**Members:** `my`, `your`, `his`, `her`, `its`, `our`, `their`, plus archaic/poetic `thy`.

- **Our** flight was delayed, so **their** connection was missed.
- The company changed **its** logo again.
- **Her** apartment is bigger than **my** whole house.

### Genitive (possessive noun-phrase) determiners

A full noun phrase in the possessive case can function as a determiner. Once it is there, no article is possible — the genitive occupies the determiner slot itself.

**Members:** open-ended — any possessive noun phrase: `Sarah's`, `my brother's`, `the mayor of Boston's`, `somebody else's`.

- **Sarah's** proposal beat **the committee's** expectations.
- I borrowed **my roommate's** car.
- That is **somebody else's** problem now.

### Quantifiers of large quantity

These indicate a big amount or number.

**Members:** `much`, `many`, `a lot of`, `lots of`, `plenty of`, `a great deal of`, `a large/great amount of`, `a large number of`, `a good many`, `loads of` / `tons of` (informal).

- There isn't **much** time left before the deadline.
- **Many** Americans commute over an hour each way.
- We have **plenty of** parking and **a great deal of** patience.

### Quantifiers of small quantity

**Members:** `few`, `a few`, `little`, `a little`, `a bit of`, `a couple of`, `several` (moderate), `hardly any`.

- We have **a little** milk left — enough for coffee.
- **Few** people showed up, which was discouraging.
- I made **a couple of** calls and sent **several** emails.

### Quantifiers of whole, part, and comparison

**Members:** `all`, `both`, `half`, `most`, `some`, `any`, `enough`, `no`, and the comparison series `more`, `less`, `fewer`, `most`, `least`, `fewest`.

- **Most** applicants withdrew, but **all** the finalists stayed.
- We need **more** volunteers and **fewer** meetings.
- There is **enough** soup for everyone.

### Distributive determiners

Distributives treat the members of a group individually or divide a group in two.

**Members:** `each`, `every`, `either`, `neither`, and (in distributive uses) `both`, `all`, `half`.

- **Each** student receives **a** locker. (one by one)
- **Every** window on the block was broken. (the whole set, viewed collectively)
- **Either** route works; **neither** route is fast.

### Cardinal numerals

Cardinal numbers state exactly how many.

**Members:** `one`, `two`, `three` … `twenty-one` … `a/one hundred`, `a/one thousand`, `a/one million`, `a/one billion`.

- **Three** witnesses gave **two** conflicting accounts.
- She ran **twenty-six** miles in **four** hours.
- The city issued **two hundred fifty** permits last month.

### Ordinal and general-ordinal determiners

Ordinals rank items in a sequence. *General ordinals* express sequence without a specific number.

**Members (ordinal):** `first`, `second`, `third`, `fourth` … `twenty-first`, `hundredth`.
**Members (general ordinal):** `next`, `last`, `previous`, `following`, `subsequent`, `other`, `another`, `further`, `additional`, `same`.

- The **first** three chapters are free.
- I'll call you **next** Tuesday about the **last** invoice.
- Give me **another** minute, and I'll walk you through **one further** example.

### Multiplier and fraction determiners

These multiply or divide the quantity expressed by the rest of the noun phrase, and they sit in front of any other determiner.

**Members:** `double`, `twice`, `three/four/ten times`, `half`, `one-third`, `two-thirds`, `three-quarters`.

- They charged **double** the advertised price.
- The storm dropped **twice** the normal rainfall.
- **Half** the class failed the midterm.

### Interrogative determiners

These introduce a question about the identity or selection of the noun.

**Members:** `which`, `what`, `whose`; also the phrasal `how much`, `how many`.

- **Which** gate does the flight leave from?
- **What** kind of insurance do you carry?
- **Whose** jacket is on my chair?
- **How many** bedrooms does the house have? / **How much** notice do they need?

*Which* selects from a set the speaker treats as known and limited; *what* leaves the set open.

- **Which** size do you want — small, medium, or large? (closed set)
- **What** size do you want? (open set)

### Relative determiners

These introduce a relative or free-relative clause while still determining a noun.

**Members:** `whose`, `which` (in formal, non-restrictive use), `what` (free relative), `whatever`, `whichever`, and the rare `whosever`.

- The engineer **whose** design won the contract is only 29.
- He resigned in March, at **which** point the stock collapsed.
- Take **whichever** seat you like; I'll spend **what** money I have left.

### Exclamative determiners

These build exclamations.

**Members:** `what` (+ *a/an* with singular countables), `such`.

- **What** a mess the kitchen is!
- **What** nerve that guy has!
- I've never seen **such** enthusiasm from a jury.

### Negative determiners

**Members:** `no`, `neither`, and the fixed phrase `not a/one`.

- There is **no** excuse for that email.
- **Neither** candidate answered the question.
- **Not one** person raised a hand.

### Indefinite / general determiners

A grab bag of determiners expressing unspecified identity or unspecified quantity.

**Members:** `some`, `any`, `another`, `other`, `such`, `certain`, `various`, `whatever`, `whichever`.

- **Some** guy called while you were out. (singular *some* = unidentified)
- Do you have **any** questions?
- **Certain** members objected, for **various** reasons.

### Predeterminers (a positional class)

Not a meaning category but a **slot**: words that can appear *before* an article, demonstrative, or possessive.

**Members:** `all`, `both`, `half`, `double`, `twice`, `three times`, fractions, `such`, `what`, `quite`, `rather`, `many` (in *many a*).

- **All** my cousins came. / **Both** those windows leak.
- It was **quite** a performance and **rather** a shock.
- **Such** a delay would ruin the schedule.

## 3. Forms and inflection

Determiners are, with a few exceptions, **invariable**: they do not take plural `-s`, they do not take case endings, and they have no comparative `-er`/`-est` of their own. What varies instead is (a) *which member of a set* you select for the noun's number, and (b) a handful of suppletive comparison sets.

### Article forms

| Form | Written | Pronunciation | Used before |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indefinite | a | /ə/ (unstressed), /eɪ/ (stressed) | consonant **sound**: *a house, a university, a one-time fee* |
| Indefinite | an | /ən/, /æn/ (stressed) | vowel **sound**: *an hour, an heir, an FBI agent* |
| Definite | the | /ðə/ before consonant sound; /ði/ before vowel sound; /ðiː/ emphatic | *the car* /ðə/, *the apple* /ði/, *THE place to eat* /ðiː/ |
| Zero | (nothing) | — | plural and uncountable nouns, most proper nouns |

Sound, not letter, decides `a` vs. `an`:

| Looks like a vowel, takes *a* | Looks like a consonant, takes *an* |
|---|---|
| a **u**niversity, a **u**nion, a **u**seful tool (/ju/) | an **h**our, an **h**onor, an **h**eir (silent *h*) |
| a **E**uropean, a **e**ulogy (/ju/) | an **M**BA, an **H**MO, an **X**-ray (letter names) |
| a **o**ne-way street, a **o**nce-in-a-lifetime trip (/w/) | an **R**SVP, an **N**FL team, an **S**UV |

### Demonstratives — the only number-inflected determiners

| | Singular / uncountable | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Near** (proximal) | this | these |
| **Far** (distal) | that | those |

- **This** advice was useful. / **These** suggestions were useful.
- **That** furniture is old. / **Those** chairs are old.

### Possessive determiners vs. possessive pronouns

The determiner form precedes a noun; the pronoun form stands alone. Learners mix these constantly.

| Person | Determiner (+ noun) | Pronoun (alone) | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1sg | my | mine | This is **my** desk. This desk is **mine**. |
| 2sg/pl | your | yours | Is this **your** seat? Is this **yours**? |
| 3sg masc. | his | his | **His** car is new. This one is **his**. |
| 3sg fem. | her | hers | **Her** idea won. The idea was **hers**. |
| 3sg neut. | its | — (no ordinary pronoun form) | The dog wagged **its** tail. |
| 1pl | our | ours | **Our** turn. The turn is **ours**. |
| 3pl (incl. singular *they*) | their | theirs | **Their** offer. The offer is **theirs**. |
| Genitive NP | Ana's | Ana's | **Ana's** desk. That desk is **Ana's**. |

Note: `its` never has an apostrophe. `it's` = *it is / it has*.

**Possessive determiners agree with the possessor, not with the noun that follows.** English differs here from languages that inflect the possessive for the possessed noun.

- Maria lost **her** wallet, **her** keys, and **her** phone. (all *her* — Maria is the possessor)
- David called **his** mother. (*his*, though *mother* is feminine)
- The committee published **its** findings. (*its*, though *findings* is plural)

### Quantifier comparison sets (suppletive)

| Base (with uncountable) | Base (with plural count) | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|---|
| much | many | more | most |
| little | few | less / fewer | least / fewest |

- much salt → **more** salt → **the most** salt
- many errors → **more** errors → **the most** errors
- little patience → **less** patience → **the least** patience
- few options → **fewer** options → **the fewest** options

### Cardinal → ordinal derivation

Most ordinals add `-th`. The irregular ones must be memorized.

| Cardinal | Ordinal | Numeral | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| one | first | 1st | suppletive |
| two | second | 2nd | suppletive |
| three | third | 3rd | suppletive |
| four | fourth | 4th | regular |
| five | fifth | 5th | *ve* → *f* |
| six | sixth | 6th | regular |
| seven | seventh | 7th | regular |
| eight | eighth | 8th | one *t*, not *tt* |
| nine | ninth | 9th | drops the *e* |
| ten | tenth | 10th | regular |
| eleven | eleventh | 11th | regular |
| twelve | twelfth | 12th | *ve* → *f* |
| twenty | twentieth | 20th | *y* → *ie* |
| thirty | thirtieth | 30th | *y* → *ie* |
| twenty-one | twenty-first | 21st | only the last element changes |
| one hundred | one hundredth | 100th | regular |

### Number words: singular when counting, plural when vague

| Determiner use (no *-s*) | Vague-quantity noun use (with *-s* + *of*) |
|---|---|
| **two hundred** guests | **hundreds of** guests |
| **three thousand** dollars | **thousands of** dollars |
| **five million** people | **millions of** people |
| **a dozen** eggs | **dozens of** eggs |

### Determiner ↔ pronoun pairs

Several determiners have a distinct standalone form; others keep one form and simply switch roles:

| Determiner | Matching pronoun | Example pair |
|---|---|---|
| no | none | **No** tickets are left. / **None** are left. |
| every | every one / everyone | **Every** seat is taken. / **Every one** is taken. |
| my, your, her… | mine, yours, hers… | **Her** coat. / The coat is **hers**. |
| which (det.) | which (pron.) | **Which** book? / **Which** do you want? |

`this`, `that`, `these`, `those`, `each`, `either`, `neither`, `some`, `any`, `all`, `both`, `many`, `few`, `several`, `more`, `most`, `enough` use the **same form** as determiner and pronoun.

## 4. Position and word order

### The basic slot

A determiner comes **first** in the noun phrase, before any adjectives, and before the noun:

> determiner + (adjective(s)) + (noun modifier) + **NOUN**

- **the** three *shabby brown leather* **armchairs**
- **my** *favorite* **coffee** **shop**
- ✗ *big the house* → ✓ **the big house**

### Three determiner positions

English allows up to three determiner slots, in a fixed order:

| Slot | Contents | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Predeterminer** | all, both, half, double, twice, *n* times, fractions, such, what, quite, rather | **all** the… |
| **2. Central determiner** | articles, demonstratives, possessives, some/any/no/each/every/either/neither, which/what/whose | all **the**… |
| **3. Postdeterminer** | ordinals & general ordinals, cardinals, many/few/several/more/most | all the **first three**… |

Full stack (determiners bolded, noun plain): **All the first three** chapters were rewritten. / **Both her many** former clients complained. / **Twice that same** amount was refunded.

### Only one central determiner

Central determiners are **mutually exclusive**. You cannot stack two:

- ✗ *the my book* / ✗ *a your friend* / ✗ *this my car*

To combine definiteness with possession, use the **double genitive** (`of` + possessive pronoun) or a demonstrative + *of*-phrase:

- ✓ **a friend of mine**, ✓ **that car of yours**, ✓ **some cousins of my wife's**

### Order inside the postdeterminer slot

Ordinal before cardinal, always:

- ✓ the **first two** weeks — ✗ *the two first weeks*
- ✓ the **next three** flights — ✗ *the three next flights*
- ✓ her **last four** albums

### Predeterminers with and without *of*

`all`, `both`, `half` may attach directly to a following determiner, or take `of`. Before a **pronoun**, `of` is required.

| Pattern | Example | Grammatical? |
|---|---|---|
| all + determiner | **all the** money | ✓ |
| all of + determiner | **all of the** money | ✓ |
| all + pronoun | *all them* | ✗ |
| all of + pronoun | **all of them** | ✓ |
| all + bare plural | **all** children | ✓ (generic) |

Other quantifiers (`most`, `many`, `few`, `several`, `some`, `none`, `each`, `either`, `neither`) **require** `of` before another determiner:

- ✓ **most of the** students — ✗ *most the students*
- ✓ **each of my** cousins — ✗ *each my cousins*
- ✓ **none of these** answers — ✗ *no of these answers*

### Floating quantifiers

`all`, `both`, and `each` can detach from the subject and "float" next to the verb:

- **All** the guests have left. → The guests have **all** left.
- **Both** of my parents are retired. → My parents are **both** retired.
- **Each** of the winners got a check. → The winners **each** got a check.

### Predeterminers before *a/an*

`such`, `what`, `quite`, `rather`, `half`, and `many` sit in front of the indefinite article — not after it:

- ✓ **such a** long day — ✗ *a such long day*
- ✓ **What a** view! — ✗ *A what view!*
- ✓ **quite a** difference, ✓ **rather a** nuisance, ✓ **half an** hour, ✓ **many a** night

With `so` and `too`, the adjective moves ahead of the article: **so big a house**, **too heavy a load**.

### Determiners in questions

Interrogative determiners move to the front with their noun:

- You took **which** train? → **Which train** did you take?
- **How many** applications did you send?
- **Whose** signature is missing?

## 5. Rules and patterns

### Rule 1 — Singular countable nouns need a determiner

A singular countable noun cannot stand alone in normal prose.

- ✗ *I need pen.* → ✓ I need **a** pen. / ✓ I need **the** pen. / ✓ I need **my** pen.
- ✓ She's **an** engineer. (professions take *a/an* in the singular)

Exceptions: vocatives (*Waiter!*), certain fixed prepositional idioms (*by car*, *in bed*, *at school*, *on foot*), headlines (*Mayor Signs Bill*), and lists/labels (*Ingredients: flour, sugar*).

### Rule 2 — Choosing *a/an* vs. *the*

Use **the** when the hearer can identify exactly which one is meant. Use **a/an** when they cannot.

| Reason for *the* | Example |
|---|---|
| Second mention | I saw a dog. **The** dog was limping. |
| Unique in the world/context | **the** sun, **the** president, **the** kitchen (in this house) |
| Made specific by a modifier | **the** man *in the gray coat*, **the** book *that I ordered* |
| Superlatives and ordinals | **the** best price, **the** third attempt |
| Shared situational knowledge | Can you turn off **the** light? |
| Classes named by adjective | **the** rich, **the** unemployed, **the** French |
| Instruments (playing) | She plays **the** cello. |
| Ranges, seas, rivers, plural country names (Rule 13) | **the** Rockies, **the** Pacific, **the** Hudson, **the** Netherlands, **the** Philippines |
| Correlative comparatives | **The** sooner, **the** better. |

| Reason for *a/an* | Example |
|---|---|
| First mention / not identifiable | **A** package arrived for you. |
| Classifying (jobs, roles, types) | He's **a** paramedic. That's **a** sedan. |
| Meaning "one" | I'll be back in **an** hour. |
| Meaning "per" | It costs $12 **a** pound. Twice **a** week. |
| Generic in a definition | **A** thermometer measures temperature. |

### Rule 3 — When to use no article

| Zero-article context | Example |
|---|---|
| Plural generic | Ø **Elevators** make me nervous. |
| Uncountable generic | Ø **Honesty** matters. Ø **Rice** is cheap. |
| Most proper names | Ø **Denver**, Ø **Mexico**, Ø **Harvard**, Ø **Lake Erie** |
| Meals | We had Ø **breakfast** at seven. |
| Languages and school subjects | She teaches Ø **Spanish** and Ø **calculus**. |
| Transport with *by* | I came Ø **by train**. |
| Institutions used for their purpose | He's in Ø **prison** / at Ø **school** / in Ø **bed** / in Ø **class** |
| Most illnesses | She has Ø **diabetes** / Ø **pneumonia** (but: *a cold*, *a headache*, *the flu*) |
| Times and seasons after certain prepositions | at Ø **night**, in Ø **spring**, on Ø **Monday** |
| Streets, parks, and most airports | Ø **Fifth Avenue**, Ø **Central Park**, Ø **O'Hare** |
| Company names | Ø **Boeing** builds airplanes. |

### Rule 4 — Three ways to be generic

All three are correct but not identical:

- Ø **Computers** are getting cheaper. (most natural, everyday generic)
- **A computer** is only as good as its user. (any single representative member)
- **The computer** changed modern work. (the category/invention, formal)

### Rule 5 — Countability agreement

| Determiner | Singular count | Plural count | Uncountable |
|---|---|---|---|
| a/an | ✓ *a chair* | ✗ | ✗ |
| the | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| this/that | ✓ *this chair* | ✗ | ✓ *this water* |
| these/those | ✗ | ✓ *these chairs* | ✗ |
| much | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ *much water* |
| many | ✗ | ✓ *many chairs* | ✗ |
| (a) little | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ *a little water* |
| (a) few | ✗ | ✓ *a few chairs* | ✗ |
| each/every | ✓ *each chair* | ✗ | ✗ |
| no | ✓ *no answer* | ✓ *no answers* | ✓ *no time* |
| some/any | usually ✗ (but *some guy* = "unidentified"; *any student* = "it doesn't matter which") | ✓ | ✓ |
| all/most/enough/more | ✗ (but time spans: *all day*, *all week*) | ✓ | ✓ |
| several | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| both/either/neither | ✓ (either/neither) | ✓ (both) | ✗ |

### Rule 6 — Verb agreement driven by the determiner

- **Each** / **Every** / **Either** / **Neither** + singular noun → singular verb: **Every seat *is* taken.**
- **Each of** / **Neither of** + plural → singular verb in careful writing: **Neither of the plans *works*.** (Plural verbs occur in speech.)
- **None of** + plural → singular or plural, both accepted: **None of the files *was*/*were* saved.**
- **A number of** + plural → **plural** verb: **A number of issues *remain*.**
- **The number of** + plural → **singular** verb: **The number of issues *is* growing.**
- **All/Some/Most/Half of** + noun → verb agrees with the noun after *of*: **Half of the pizza *is* gone.** / **Half of the slices *are* gone.**

### Rule 7 — *some* vs. *any*

- **some** in affirmatives: I bought **some** apples.
- **any** in negatives and open questions: I didn't buy **any** apples. Did you buy **any** apples?
- **some** in offers, requests, and questions expecting *yes*: Would you like **some** coffee? Could you lend me **some** cash?
- **any** in affirmatives = "it doesn't matter which": **Any** mechanic can fix that.
- **any** after *if* and after negative-flavored words (*hardly*, *rarely*, *without*, *refuse*): He left without **any** explanation.

### Rule 8 — *a few / a little* vs. *few / little*

`a few` and `a little` are positive ("some, enough"). `few` and `little` are negative ("almost none").

- I have **a few** friends here. (positive — I'm fine)
- I have **few** friends here. (negative — I'm lonely)
- There's **a little** hope. / There's **little** hope.
- Informal reinforcement: **quite a few** = many; **only a few** = not many.

### Rule 9 — *each* vs. *every*

| | each | every |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | individuals, one at a time | the whole set |
| Minimum group size | 2 | 3 |
| Takes *of* | ✓ each of the… | ✗ (use *every one of the…*) |
| Can stand alone | ✓ They each paid. | ✗ |
| With *almost/nearly/practically* | ✗ | ✓ almost every day |

- **Each** twin got a separate room. / **Every** employee got the same memo.

### Rule 10 — The genitive blocks the article

If a possessive occupies the determiner slot, no article may appear:

- ✓ **my sister's** wedding — ✗ *the my sister's wedding*
- ✓ **the bride's** father (article belongs to *bride*, not to *father*)

### Rule 11 — *no* vs. *not a* vs. *none*

- **No** + noun: There were **no** survivors.
- **not a/any** + noun after a verb: There wasn't **a** survivor. / We didn't find **any** survivors.
- **none** stands alone: How many survived? **None.**
- Never combine two negatives in standard English: ✗ *We didn't see no one.*

### Rule 12 — Common determiner collocations

- quantity + *of*: **a piece of** advice, **a slice of** bread, **a bar of** soap, **a pair of** jeans, **a bunch of** grapes
- **the** + comparative … **the** + comparative: **The** more you practice, **the** easier it gets.
- **all** + time expression, no article: **all** day, **all** week, **all** year
- **last/next** + time expression, no article: **last** Friday, **next** month (but *the last week* = the final week)

### Rule 13 — *the* with proper nouns

Proper nouns usually take the zero article, but several well-defined groups take *the*. The pattern is roughly: plural names and names built on a common noun (*republic*, *river*, *gulf*) take *the*; simple singular names do not.

| Takes *the* | Takes Ø |
|---|---|
| Rivers, seas, oceans, gulfs, canals: **the** Hudson, **the** Pacific, **the** Gulf of Mexico | Lakes and bays: Ø Lake Michigan, Ø Hudson Bay |
| Mountain ranges: **the** Rockies, **the** Sierra Nevada | Single peaks: Ø Mount Rainier, Ø Pikes Peak |
| Island groups: **the** Florida Keys, **the** Bahamas | Single islands: Ø Manhattan, Ø Maui |
| Deserts and regions: **the** Mojave, **the** Midwest, **the** South | Continents, states, cities: Ø Asia, Ø Ohio, Ø Seattle |
| Plural or common-noun country names: **the** United States, **the** Netherlands, **the** Philippines, **the** Dominican Republic | Most country names: Ø Canada, Ø France, Ø Japan |
| *of*-phrase institutions: **the** University of Michigan, **the** Museum of Modern Art | Name-first institutions: Ø Michigan State University, Ø Stanford |
| Newspapers, hotels, theaters, ships: **the** Washington Post, **the** Plaza, **the** Titanic | Airports and stations: Ø LaGuardia, Ø Penn Station |
| Family names used as a group: **the** Sandovals are away | Individual people: Ø Dr. Sandoval is away |

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *She is teacher at a middle school.* → ✓ *She is **a** teacher at a middle school.* — Singular countable nouns, including job titles, need a determiner.
- ✗ *I need an information about the flight.* → ✓ *I need **some information** about the flight.* — *Information* is uncountable and cannot take *a/an* or a plural.
- ✗ *The life in New York is expensive.* → ✓ *Ø **Life** in New York is expensive.* — Abstract uncountables used generically take the zero article.
- ✗ *I like the dogs.* (meaning dogs in general) → ✓ *I like Ø **dogs**.* — *The* would mean specific, already-known dogs.
- ✗ *He has much friends in Texas.* → ✓ *He has **many** friends in Texas.* — *Much* goes with uncountables; *many* with plural countables.
- ✗ *Every students must sign in.* → ✓ *Every **student** must sign in.* — *Every* and *each* require a singular noun and a singular verb.
- ✗ *She's applying to an university in Ohio.* → ✓ *She's applying to **a** university in Ohio.* — *University* begins with the consonant sound /j/, so *a*, not *an*.
- ✗ *This shoes don't fit.* → ✓ ***These** shoes don't fit.* — Demonstratives must match the noun's number.
- ✗ *I visited most the museums downtown.* → ✓ *I visited **most of the** museums downtown.* — *Most* requires *of* before another determiner.
- ✗ *He showed me the my new office.* → ✓ *He showed me **my** new office.* — Only one central determiner per noun phrase.
- ✗ *I didn't have some cash on me.* → ✓ *I didn't have **any** cash on me.* — Use *any*, not *some*, in ordinary negatives.
- ✗ *The two first questions were easy.* → ✓ *The **first two** questions were easy.* — Ordinals precede cardinals.
- ✗ *We have few time before the meeting.* → ✓ *We have **little** time before the meeting.* — *Few* counts items; *little* measures uncountables.
- ✗ *He went to the home early.* → ✓ *He went Ø **home** early.* — *Home* as a destination takes no article.
- ✗ *The Japan exports a lot of cars.* → ✓ *Ø **Japan** exports a lot of cars.* — Singular country names take no article (exceptions: *the Netherlands*, *the Philippines*, *the United States*).
- ✗ *The dog hurt it's paw.* → ✓ *The dog hurt **its** paw.* — *Its* is the possessive determiner; *it's* means *it is/has*.
- ✗ *Neither of the answers are correct.* → ✓ *Neither of the answers **is** correct.* — *Neither of* takes a singular verb in careful writing.
- ✗ *It was a such long meeting.* → ✓ *It was **such a** long meeting.* — *Such* is a predeterminer and goes before *a/an*.
- ✗ *Both of brothers came to the wedding.* → ✓ ***Both** brothers came to the wedding.* / ✓ ***Both of the** brothers came to the wedding.* — *Of* must be followed by a determiner or a pronoun; before a bare noun, drop it.
- ✗ *Daniel forgot her passport.* (meaning Daniel's) → ✓ *Daniel forgot **his** passport.* — Possessive determiners agree with the possessor, never with the noun that follows.
- ✗ *There are less parking spots this year.* → ✓ *There are **fewer** parking spots this year.* — *Fewer* for countable plurals, *less* for uncountables.

## 7. American English notes

Determiners are one of the most uniform areas of English, so the transatlantic differences are few but real. The full inventory of determiners is identical in the two varieties; what differs is a handful of article conventions.

**1. Institutional nouns.** American English keeps *the* in several phrases where British English drops it.

| American | British |
|---|---|
| She's in **the** hospital. | She's in hospital. |
| He studied at **the** university. / He's in college. | He was at university. |
| In **the** future, be careful. | In future, be careful. |

Both varieties say *in school*, *in prison*, *at church*, *in bed* without an article. American English uses *in school* for K–12 and often for college too.

**2. *a historic*, not *an historic*.** American usage strongly prefers *a* before pronounced /h/: **a historic** decision, **a hotel**, **a heroic** effort. *An historic* survives in older British writing and sounds affected in the U.S.

**3. Numbers: American English often drops *and*.** *Two hundred fifty-three dollars* is the standard American reading; *two hundred and fifty-three* is standard British and also acceptable in the U.S.

**4. Dates.** Americans write *July 4* and say *July fourth* (or *the fourth of July* for the holiday); the British write and say *the 4th of July / 4 July*.

**5. Halves and fractions.** American English allows *a half hour* and colloquially *a half an hour*; British English prefers *half an hour*, which is also fully standard in the U.S.

**6. *couple* without *of*.** Informal American English drops the preposition: *a couple things*, *a couple days ago*. British English keeps *a couple of*. In American writing, keep *of*.

**7. Collective-noun agreement.** American English treats collectives as singular, which affects the surrounding determiners and pronouns: *The team **is** revising **its** playbook.* British English allows *The team are revising their playbook.*

**8. *the weekend*.** Americans say *on the weekend*; the British say *at the weekend* or *at weekends*.

Beyond these, article choice, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, and numerals behave identically. There are **no** American/British spelling differences in the determiner class itself.

## 8. Tricky cases

### *a little* vs. *little*, *a few* vs. *few*

Dropping the article flips the meaning from positive to negative. *He has a little money* (he's OK) vs. *He has little money* (he's broke). In speech, *little*/*few* alone sound formal; Americans usually say *not much* / *not many* / *hardly any*.

### *quite a few* means *many*

Despite containing *few*, the phrase is positive and large: *Quite a few people complained* = *a lot of people complained*.

### *most* + adjective without *the*

*The most* is a superlative: *the most expensive option* (of all options). *A most* is an intensifier meaning *very*: *a most unusual request*. And bare *most* is a quantifier: *most requests are routine*.

### *same* always needs a determiner in front of it

*Same* cannot open a noun phrase by itself: ✗ *We had same idea* → ✓ *We had **the same** idea.* The determiner is usually *the*, but a demonstrative or possessive works too: *that same afternoon*, *this same problem*, *her same excuse*. Bare *Same here* / *Same to you* are fixed idioms, not counterexamples.

### *another* vs. *other* vs. *the other* vs. *others*

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| another | + singular countable, "one more / a different one" | Have **another** cookie. |
| other | + plural or uncountable, unspecified | **Other** students disagreed. |
| the other | the remaining one(s) of a known set | Where's **the other** glove? |
| the others / others | pronoun, no noun follows | Two left; **the others** stayed. |
| another two weeks | *another* + plural is allowed when the plural is a single measured chunk | We waited **another two weeks**. |

### Double genitive

*A friend of mine* and *a friend of my father's* look redundant but are standard — they let you combine indefiniteness with possession, which the determiner slot forbids. Compare: *a photo of my father* (he's in the picture) vs. *a photo of my father's* (he owns it).

### *this* and *that* in narrative and discourse

Informal American storytelling uses *this* to introduce a new, vivid participant: *So this guy walks up to me…* Meanwhile *that* can refer back to a whole preceding idea: *He quit without notice. **That** was a mistake.*

### *last week* vs. *the last week*

*Last week* = the week before this one. *The last week* = the final week of a period. Same for *next month* vs. *the next month* ("the month that follows," often the coming 30 days). *Next Friday* is genuinely ambiguous in American speech — it may mean the upcoming Friday or the one after; speakers disambiguate with *this Friday* / *a week from Friday*.

### *many a* + singular

A formal, slightly literary pattern with singular noun and singular verb: *Many a young lawyer **has** made that mistake.*

### *no* vs. *none of* vs. *not any*

*No* is a determiner, *none* is a pronoun, and there is no *no of*: ✗ *no of them* → ✓ *none of them*. Similarly *every* has no *of* form: ✗ *every of the players* → ✓ *every one of the players*.

### *enough* can follow the noun

Unusually for a determiner, *enough* also occurs postnominally in a formal/literary register: *We have **time enough**.* The normal order is *enough time*.

### *half* is unusually flexible

*Half the cake*, *half of the cake*, *a half*, *one-half*, *half a pound*, *a half pound* — all standard American options. Only ✗ *the half cake* is wrong.

### Homophone traps

| Determiner | Confusable word | Test |
|---|---|---|
| its | it's (= it is/has) | Can you expand to *it is*? If not, use *its*. |
| your | you're (= you are) | Expand to *you are*. |
| their | there / they're | *They're* = they are; *there* = place. |
| whose | who's (= who is/has) | Expand to *who is*. |

### Words that look like determiners but are not

- **Adjectives with quantity meaning** — *numerous*, *countless*, *plentiful*, *myriad* look quantifying but are adjectives: they can follow a determiner (*his numerous excuses*), which no central determiner can. *Various* and *certain* are borderline: they act as determiners when they open the phrase (*various reasons*, *certain members*) and as adjectives when something else opens it (*the various reasons*, *a certain member*).
- **Adverbs modifying determiners** — in *not many people*, *almost every day*, *nearly all applicants*, the words *not*, *almost*, *nearly* are adverbs, not determiners.
- **Nouns used attributively** — in *chicken soup* or *summer job*, the first word is a noun modifier, not a determiner; it does not answer "which one / how many."
- **Possessive pronouns** — *mine*, *yours*, *hers*, *ours*, *theirs* never precede a noun.

### Determiner vs. pronoun vs. adjective

| Test | Determiner | Pronoun | Adjective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precedes a noun | ✓ **this** chair | ✗ (replaces the NP) | ✓ **red** chair |
| Stands alone as an NP | ✗ | ✓ **This** is mine. | ✗ |
| Can be graded (*very*, *-er*) | ✗ *very the*, *thiser* | ✗ | ✓ **very** red, **redder** |
| Can stack with others of its kind | ✗ *the my this book* | ✗ | ✓ a small **red wooden** box |
| Can be a predicate after *be* | ✗ *The book is my.* | ✓ *The book is mine.* | ✓ *The box is red.* |
| Position | first in the NP | anywhere an NP goes | after the determiner |

Worked contrasts:

- **That** movie was long. (determiner) / **That** was long. (pronoun)
- I need **more** coffee. (determiner) / I need **more**. (pronoun)
- **Which** exit? (determiner) / **Which** is faster? (pronoun)
- **Her** proposal. (determiner) / I called **her**. (object pronoun) / The proposal is **hers**. (possessive pronoun)

## 9. Quick reference

| Subcategory | Members (core) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Definite article | the | **The** report is late. |
| Indefinite article | a, an | I read **a** report. |
| Zero article | — | Ø Reports are boring. |
| Demonstratives | this, that, these, those | **These** files are corrupted. |
| Possessive determiners | my, your, his, her, its, our, their | **Their** office moved. |
| Genitive determiners | Maria's, my boss's, the CEO's | **My boss's** email arrived. |
| Large quantifiers | much, many, a lot of, plenty of, a great deal of | **Many** clients renewed. |
| Small quantifiers | (a) few, (a) little, several, a couple of | **A few** clients left. |
| Whole/part quantifiers | all, both, half, most, some, any, no, enough | **Most** clients stayed. |
| Comparative / superlative quantifiers | more, less, fewer; most, least, fewest | **Fewer** errors this quarter. |
| Distributives | each, every, either, neither | **Each** room has a fan. |
| Cardinal numerals | one, two, three, … , a hundred | **Twelve** jurors were seated. |
| Ordinal numerals | first, second, third, … | The **third** juror objected. |
| General ordinals | next, last, other, another, further, same | **Another** delay is likely. |
| Multipliers/fractions | double, twice, three times, half, two-thirds | **Twice** the cost. |
| Interrogatives | which, what, whose, how much/many | **Which** door? |
| Relatives | whose, which, what, whichever, whatever | the writer **whose** book won |
| Exclamatives | what (a/an), such (a/an) | **What a** week! |
| Negatives | no, neither | **No** refunds. |
| Indefinite/general | some, any, other, certain, various, such | **Certain** rules apply. |
| Predeterminers (slot) | all, both, half, such, quite, rather, what, multipliers | **All the** seats. |

**One-line reminders**

- Singular countable noun → it needs a determiner.
- `much`/`little` → uncountable; `many`/`few`/`fewer` → plural countable.
- Order: predeterminer → central determiner → ordinal → cardinal → adjective → noun.
- Only one central determiner: use *a friend of mine*, never *a my friend*.
- `most`, `each`, `either`, `neither`, `several`, `none` need `of` before another determiner.
- `no` → determiner; `none` → pronoun. `every` → determiner only; use `every one of`.

## 10. Practice

**A. Fill in the blank** with the correct determiner.

1. I've lived here for six years, but I still don't know ______ my neighbors. (most / most of / much)
2. She's ______ honest person, and ______ university she attended is well known.
3. ______ passenger must present a boarding pass. (Every / Everyone / All)
4. We have ______ time before the store closes, so hurry. (a few / little / few)
5. ______ of the two options is acceptable to me — pick whichever you like.
6. There were ______ people at the reception than we expected, so we had leftovers. (less / fewer / fewest)
7. I'd like ______ more coffee, but I don't want ______ sugar in it. (some / any)

**B. Identify the type** of the bolded determiner (article, demonstrative, possessive, quantifier, cardinal, ordinal, distributive, interrogative, exclamative, negative, predeterminer).

8. **Whose** laptop is charging in the hallway?
9. **All** the first three witnesses contradicted each other. (identify the type of *all*)
10. **What** a ridiculous excuse that was!
11. We toured **three** museums on the **second** day. (identify both)

**C. Correct the error.**

12. *He gave me an advice about the two first chapters of his book.*
13. *Neither of the proposals were ready, and I didn't receive some feedback from the my supervisor.*
14. *She's studying at an university in the Japan, and it was a such big change for her.*

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **most of** — *most* requires *of* before another determiner (*my*). ✗ *most my neighbors*. (*Much* is impossible: *neighbors* is a plural countable noun.)
2. **an** honest person / **the** university — *honest* begins with a silent *h*, so the vowel sound takes *an*; *university* begins with the consonant sound /j/, so it takes *a/the* normally, and *the* is right here because the university is identified by the relative clause *she attended*.
3. **Every** — *every* is a determiner and can precede the singular noun *passenger*. *Everyone* is a pronoun and takes no noun; *All* would require the plural *passengers*.
4. **little** — *time* is uncountable, so *few* is impossible; the sense is negative ("not much"), so *little*, not *a little*.
5. **Either** — with a two-member set, *either* means "one or the other," which matches *pick whichever you like*. *Neither* would contradict *acceptable to me*, and *both* would require a plural verb (*are*).
6. **fewer** — *people* is a plural countable noun, so *fewer*, not *less*; *fewest* is the superlative and cannot take *than*.
7. **some** more coffee / **any** sugar — *some* is normal in an affirmative statement (and in requests), while *any* is the form used after the negative *don't want*.
8. **Interrogative determiner** — *whose* asks about possession and precedes the noun *laptop*.
9. **Predeterminer** (a quantifier in the predeterminer slot) — *all* appears before the central determiner *the*, which is followed by the postdeterminers *first* (ordinal) and *three* (cardinal).
10. **Exclamative determiner** — *what* + *a* introduces an exclamation about a singular countable noun (*excuse*).
11. **Cardinal numeral** (*three*, how many) and **ordinal numeral** (*second*, rank in a sequence). Both sit in the postdeterminer slot; *second* follows the central determiner *the*.
12. ✓ *He gave me **some advice** about the **first two** chapters of his book.* — *Advice* is uncountable (no *an*; use *some advice* or *a piece of advice*), and ordinals precede cardinals.
13. ✓ *Neither of the proposals **was** ready, and I didn't receive **any** feedback from **my** supervisor.* — *Neither of* takes a singular verb in careful writing; *any* replaces *some* in a negative clause; and *the* cannot stack with the central determiner *my*.
14. ✓ *She's studying at **a** university in Ø **Japan**, and it was **such a** big change for her.* — *University* starts with the consonant sound /j/; singular country names take the zero article; and the predeterminer *such* goes before *a/an*.
