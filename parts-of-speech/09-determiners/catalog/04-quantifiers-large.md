# Quantifiers of large quantity

> The determiners that answer *how much?* and *how many?* with a big number — and the file where the *much/many* and *amount/number* lines get drawn.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What unites this group is one question, *how much or how many*, and one fault line running through every answer to it. **Countability decides the word**: *much*, *a great deal of*, and *a large amount of* take nouns you measure; *many*, *a great many*, and *a large number of* take nouns you count; and the phrases built on *a lot* — *a lot of*, *lots of*, *plenty of*, *tons of* — take either, which is exactly why they took over the spoken language. Cross the line and you produce the errors this file exists to prevent: ✗ *much books*, ✗ *many information*, ✗ *a large amount of people*, ✗ *a great deal of reasons*. Read the **Goes with** line first in every entry below, because it is the only line you cannot guess.

The second thing to learn here is **register**, which American English splits in a way textbooks rarely mention. *Much* and *many* are the neutral written forms and are at home everywhere in questions and negatives — *How much time do we have?*, *There aren't many left* — but bare *much* in a plain affirmative statement is stilted, and nobody says *We have much time*. In affirmative speech the defaults are *a lot of* and *lots of*, with *a bunch of* and *tons of* a rung further down; a rung up, in formal prose, sit *a great deal of*, *a large number of*, and *numerous*. Picking the wrong rung is not ungrammatical, merely audible.

Below those neutral rungs the file keeps four groups an American will meet without ever having been taught them, and each is labeled rather than smoothed over. Two words **follow** their noun instead of preceding it (*bargains galore*, *reasons aplenty*). A block of **regional and slang** forms belongs to particular places and particular people — *a passel of* and *a right smart of* to Appalachia, *a mess of* and *a sight of* and *a power of* to the South, *hella* to Northern California, *mad* to New York, *beaucoup* to the military — and one of them, *a shitload of*, is obscene and carries a line saying where it must never go. A block of **archaic and literary** forms lives only inside quotation marks (*enow*, *full many a*, *many's the*). And a block of **legal and technical** terms names a threshold fixed by a document rather than a quantity judged by a speaker (*a plurality of*, *a quorum of*, *a supermajority of*). Read the **Register** line in any of those entries as a boundary and not as flavor: not one of them is a neutral synonym for *a lot of*.

Two mechanical rules then apply almost everywhere in the file. ***Of***: the partitive phrases (*a lot of*, *plenty of*, *tons of*) carry *of* always and cannot drop it, while the bare determiners (*much*, *many*, *more*, *most*, *enough*) take it only in front of another determiner or a pronoun — *much of the report*, *many of them*, but never ✗ *much the report* and never ✗ *many of students*. That *of* is never stressed: it reduces to /əv/ and in ordinary speech to a bare /ə/ — *a lotta time*, *a buncha people*, *tonsa work* — and the *a* that opens these phrases is the weak article /ə/, never /eɪ/. **Agreement**: after an *of*-phrase the verb agrees with the noun after *of*, not with the quantity word in front of it — *A lot of people **are** waiting* beside *A lot of time **was** wasted*. And watch three phrases that mean the opposite of what they look like: *quite a few* means **many**, *quite a bit of* means **much**, and *no end of* means **a great deal of** rather than none.

One note on scope, since every determiner has exactly one home in this catalog. Several words that mean "a lot" are quantified elsewhere and are only linked from here — the table at the [end of this page](#large-quantity-words-homed-in-other-files) lists them all. This file links to every one of them and duplicates none.

## The core pair and the comparison series

*Much* and *many* are the same quantifier split down the countability line, and *more* and *most* are the comparative and superlative that serve both of them at once. Learn these five and you have the skeleton of the whole category; everything else in this file is a phrase built to do one of these jobs in a different register.

### many

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɛni/ &middot; *MEN-ee*
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*many people*); **postdeterminer** after a central determiner (*the many problems*, *her many friends*, *these many years*) &middot; **Register:** neutral — the standard written form; in plain affirmative speech Americans usually reach for *a lot of*
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *many people*, *many reasons*, *many times*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *many information*, ✗ *many money*, ✗ *many advice* — use *much* or *a lot of*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *many reason*). The single exception is the fixed frame *many a* + singular noun, which has its own entry below.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *many of the students*, *many of my friends*, *many of them*. Never ✗ *many the students*. No *of* before a bare plural: *many students*.
**Pattern:** *many* [plural countable] &middot; *many of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; *many of* [them/us] &middot; [the/her/these] *many* [plural] &middot; *how many* [plural] &middot; *too / so / as many* [plural] &middot; *many more* [plural]
**Contrast:** *Many* counts, *much* measures: *many books* but *much reading*. Both are perfectly natural in questions and negatives (*Do you have many?*, *There aren't many*), and both sound stiff in a plain affirmative statement in American English, where *a lot of* is the spoken default — *A lot of people came* rather than *Many people came*, unless you are writing. *Many* stays comfortable in affirmatives when it is modified (*so many*, *too many*, *as many*, *how many*, *many more*) and throughout formal prose. Determiner against pronoun: *many people left* has a noun after it, *many left* does not.
**Variants:** *so many*, *too many*, *as many*, *how many*, *many more*, *the many*, *a great many*, *a good many*

Marks a large but unspecified number of countable things.

**1.** Before a bare plural noun it names a big number drawn from an open set — *many Americans*, *many companies*. **2.** After *the*, a possessive, or a demonstrative it is a postdeterminer and simply describes an already-identified set as a large one — *the many problems we inherited*. **3.** As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun: *Many were called, few were chosen.*

1. *Many* Americans commute more than an hour each way.
2. How *many* people are coming to dinner?
3. There aren't *many* seats left for the early show.
4. She has too *many* projects going at once.
5. *Many* of the applicants had never written a line of code.
6. The *many* delays finally cost the contractor the job.

**See also:** [much](#much), [more](#more), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a great many](#a-great-many), [quite a few](#quite-a-few), [few](05-quantifiers-small.md#few), [how many](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-many)

### many a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɛni ə/ &middot; *MEN-ee uh* &middot; the *a* keeps its weak /ə/ and never takes stress here
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** predeterminer — the article *a* is locked inside the phrase, so no other determiner can appear: ✗ *many a the student* &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, or deliberately old-fashioned; alive in proverbs, sermons, and sports commentary
**Goes with:** **singular** countable nouns only — *many a time*, *many a young writer*, *many a night*. Never a plural noun (✗ *many a times*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *many a money*). This is the one corner of English where *many* takes a singular, and the singular runs through the whole clause: *Many a student **has** asked that question*, never ✗ *have asked*.
**With *of*:** never takes an *of*-phrase — ✗ *many a of the students*. Before a vowel sound the article changes as usual: *many an evening*. The only *of*-adjacent form is the archaic pronoun *many a one*.
**Pattern:** *many a* [singular countable] + **singular** verb &middot; *many an* [vowel-initial singular noun] &middot; *many's the* [singular noun] (fixed idiom)
**Contrast:** *Many a student was late* and *many students were late* mean the same thing with opposite grammar: *many* takes a plural noun and a plural verb, *many a* takes a singular of each. Do not confuse it with *a good many* and *a great many*, which are ordinary intensified *many* and take the plural — *a good many students were late*. The singular agreement is obligatory, not a stylistic option: ✗ *many a parent have asked* is an error. Modern American prose almost always prefers plain *many*; reach for *many a* only when the sentence wants that older cadence.
**Variants:** *many an*, *many's the*, *many a one (archaic pronoun)*

Names a large number of countable things while treating them grammatically one at a time. The singular is the whole point: it makes the reader picture the cases individually rather than as a crowd, which is why the phrase sounds weighty and old-fashioned.

1. *Many a* student has asked that same question.
2. He had made that drive *many a* time.
3. *Many an* evening ended with the two of them arguing about the thermostat.
4. *Many a* good idea has died in a committee meeting.
5. *Many a* parent learns that lesson the hard way.
6. *Many's* the night I fell asleep at that desk.

**See also:** [many](#many), [a great many](#a-great-many), [a good many](#a-good-many), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [many's the](#manys-the), [full many a](#full-many-a)

### more

**Pronunciation:** /mɔr/ &middot; *MOR*
**Type:** quantifier, comparative &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*more time*); **postdeterminer** after a determiner or a numeral (*some more coffee*, *no more excuses*, *two more days*, *many more people*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*more chairs*, *more people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*more time*, *more money*). This is what makes it easy: *more* is the comparative of both *many* and *much*, so it is one of the few quantifiers here that never asks you to decide countability. It does not take a bare singular countable noun (✗ *more chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *more of the cake*, *more of my time*, *more of them*. Never ✗ *more the cake*. No *of* before a bare noun: *more cake*.
**Pattern:** *more* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *more* [noun] *than* [phrase/clause] &middot; *more of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *more of* [them/it] &middot; [some / no / any / two / many / much / a lot] *more* [noun] &middot; *the more* … *the more* …
**Contrast:** *More* is the comparative of both *many* and *much*, and its two opposites split along the countability line: *fewer* for things you count, *less* for things you measure. Determiner against pronoun: *more volunteers* has a noun after it, *we need more* does not. Keep the determiner apart from the degree adverb — *more chairs* puts *more* in front of a noun, while *more careful* and *more slowly* put it in front of an adjective or adverb, where it is not a determiner at all. Watch the negatives too: *no more* means "none from here on" (*No more excuses*), *not … anymore* is about time (*I don't work there anymore*), and before a noun the two-word spelling is the right one (*any more questions*).
**Variants:** *some more*, *no more*, *any more*, *a little more*, *a lot more*, *many more*, *much more*, *two more*, *the more … the more*

Names a larger number or amount than something else — than a stated comparison, than before, or than what is already on hand. As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun after it: *We need more*, *Would you like some more?*

1. We need *more* volunteers for Saturday.
2. Is there any *more* coffee in the pot?
3. She has *more* patience than I do.
4. Give me two *more* days and I'll have the draft.
5. *More* of the money went to overhead than to programs.
6. *No more* excuses — the report is due Friday.
7. There's soup on the stove if anyone wants *more*.

**See also:** [most](#most), [many](#many), [much](#much), [fewer](05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less), [how many more](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-many-more)

### most

**Pronunciation:** /moʊst/ &middot; *MOHST*
**Type:** quantifier, superlative &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*most people*); after *the* it forms the superlative of quantity (*the most points*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*most people*, *most cities*) **and** uncountable nouns (*most information*, *most traffic*). It does not take a bare singular countable noun (✗ *most city*), though *most of the city* is fine, because *of* reattaches it to an already-determined phrase.
**With *of*:** **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *most of the students*, *most of my time*, *most of them*. ✗ *most the students* is not standard written English. No *of* before a bare noun: *most people*.
**Pattern:** *most* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *most of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *most of* [them/it] &middot; *the most* [noun] (superlative) &middot; *a most* [adjective + singular noun] (formal intensifier)
**Contrast:** The bare/*of* split is a meaning split, not a style choice. *Most people* is generic — people at large. *Most of the people* is specific — the majority of one identified group. Against *more*, *most* is the superlative to its comparative, and both cover countables and uncountables alike, unlike their small-quantity opposites *fewest* and *least*, which do not. Three lookalikes to keep separate: the degree adverb (*the most interesting talk*), the formal intensifier meaning "very" (*a most unusual request*), and the colloquial regional *most* meaning "almost" (*most everybody*, *most all of them*), which is at home in American conversation and out of place in writing.
**Variants:** *most of*, *the most*, *a most (intensifier)*, *most all / most everybody (informal, "almost")*

Names the majority — more than half, and usually a good deal more than half. Bare *most* generalizes about a whole class; *most of* picks the larger part out of a specific set. As a pronoun it stands alone: *Most had already gone home.*

1. *Most* people would rather not fly on a Sunday night.
2. *Most* of the money is already spent.
3. She spends *most* of her time on the road.
4. *Most* information on that site is out of date.
5. Which team scored the *most* points?
6. *Most* of them had never seen snow before.

**See also:** [more](#more), [many](#many), [much](#much), [a majority of](#a-majority-of), [the majority of, the minority of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), [least](05-quantifiers-small.md#least), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all)

### much

**Pronunciation:** /mʌtʃ/ &middot; *MUCH*
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central — it opens the noun phrase (*much time*); the identical degree adverb is a different animal (*much better*, *thanks very much*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in questions and negatives; in a plain affirmative statement it is formal enough to sound stilted in American speech
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *much time*, *much money*, *much information*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *much books*, ✗ *much people* — use *many*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *much book*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *much of the report*, *much of my time*, *much of it*. Never ✗ *much the report*. Bare *much* takes no *of* before a bare noun: *much time*.
**Pattern:** *much* [uncountable noun] &middot; *much of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *much of* [it/that] &middot; *how much* [uncountable] &middot; *too / so / as / very / that much* [uncountable] &middot; *much more* [uncountable]
**Contrast:** *Much* is to uncountables what *many* is to plural countables. In affirmative statements Americans avoid bare *much* — *We have much time* is grammatical but nobody says it — and reach for *a lot of* or *plenty of* instead. *Much* survives in full health in questions (*How much time do we have?*), in negatives (*not much time*), and after *too*, *so*, *as*, *how*, *very*, and *that*. It is also entirely natural in affirmative writing the moment it is followed by *of* or fronted as a subject: *Much of the report was wrong*, *Much remains to be done.*
**Variants:** *too much*, *so much*, *as much*, *how much*, *very much*, *that much*, *much of*, *much more*, *not much*

Marks a large but unspecified amount of something that cannot be counted. As a pronoun it stands alone: *There isn't much to say.*

1. We don't have *much* time.
2. How *much* money did the repair cost?
3. There isn't *much* to say about it.
4. She spends too *much* energy worrying about things she can't control.
5. *Much* of the report was out of date before it was printed.
6. I didn't get *much* sleep last night.

**See also:** [many](#many), [more](#more), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [quite a bit of](#quite-a-bit-of), [little](05-quantifiers-small.md#little), [not much](05-quantifiers-small.md#not-much), [how much](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-much)

## The spoken defaults: *a lot of* and its family

These are the phrases that actually carry large quantity in American conversation. Every one of them takes plural countables and uncountables alike — *a bunch of* only in casual speech, the rest without restriction — which is exactly why they won: a speaker who says *a lot of* never has to decide between *much* and *many*. All of them are built the same way — a noun of quantity plus obligatory *of* — and in all of them the verb agrees with the noun **after** *of*, not with the quantity word itself.

### a bunch of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbʌntʃ əv/ &middot; *uh BUNCH uhv* &middot; casually /ə ˈbʌntʃə/, "a buncha"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central — the whole phrase fills the determiner slot &middot; **Register:** informal American; everywhere in speech, out of place in formal writing
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*a bunch of people*, *a bunch of emails*) and, in casual American speech, uncountable nouns as well (*a bunch of money*, *a bunch of work*, *a bunch of time*). The literal use is a different thing: *a bunch of grapes*, *a bunch of keys*, *a bunch of flowers* name a physical cluster, and there *bunch* is an ordinary head noun — the giveaway is that it pluralizes and takes its own modifiers (*three big bunches of grapes*), which the quantifier never does.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory and welded to the phrase — ✗ *a bunch people*. Nothing changes before another determiner or a pronoun: *a bunch of the emails*, *a bunch of them*.
**Pattern:** *a bunch of* [plural countable] &middot; *a bunch of* [uncountable] &middot; *a bunch of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *a bunch of* [them/us] &middot; *a whole bunch of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *A bunch of* is the informal American twin of *a lot of*, and in speech the two swap freely. Agreement follows the noun after *of*, not *bunch*: *A bunch of people are waiting* (plural) beside *A bunch of money is missing* (singular). A prescriptive grammar would want *is* in the first, since *bunch* is singular, but notional plural agreement is what Americans actually say and write in informal registers. Against *a couple of* and *a handful of*, which name small numbers, *a bunch of* names a big one.
**Variants:** *a whole bunch of*, *bunches of*, *a bunch (pronoun: thanks a bunch)*

Names a large, uncounted quantity in casual speech — the conversational equivalent of *a lot of*, with no claim about how large.

1. *A bunch of* us are going to the game on Saturday.
2. He left *a bunch of* dishes in the sink again.
3. That's going to cost *a bunch of* money we don't have.
4. *A bunch of* the emails never went out.
5. I've got *a whole bunch of* questions about the lease.
6. She picked up *a bunch of* bananas on the way home.

**See also:** [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [lots of](#lots-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [a slew of](#a-slew-of), [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), [a handful of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-handful-of)

### a lot of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈlɑt əv/ &middot; *uh LOT uhv* &middot; in ordinary speech the *t* flaps and *of* reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈlɑɾə/, "a lotta"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — the default in American speech and in most everyday writing; formal prose prefers *much*, *many*, *a great deal of*, or *a large number of*
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*a lot of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*a lot of money*). That coverage is the whole reason it took over the spoken language: it spares the speaker the *much/many* decision. Not used with a bare singular countable noun (✗ *a lot of chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun and cannot be dropped — ✗ *a lot people*. It stays put before another determiner or a pronoun: *a lot of the money*, *a lot of them*. Bare *a lot* with no *of* is either a pronoun (*I have a lot*) or an adverb (*It helped a lot*, *a lot better*).
**Pattern:** *a lot of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *a lot of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *a lot of* [them/it] &middot; *quite a lot of* [noun] &middot; *a whole lot of* [noun] &middot; *an awful lot of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *A lot of* is what Americans say where writing uses *much* and *many*. Verb agreement is decided by the noun after *of* and never by *lot*: *A lot of people are waiting* but *A lot of time was wasted*. Two spelling traps come with it — it is always two words (✗ *alot*), and the plural variant *lots of* is a genuine alternative, not an error. Against *plenty of*, *a lot of* only says the quantity is big; *plenty of* adds the promise that it is enough.
**Variants:** *lots of*, *a whole lot of*, *quite a lot of*, *an awful lot of*, *a lotta (casual spelling)*

Names a large quantity of anything, countable or not, without measuring it. It is the workhorse of spoken American quantity and the safe answer whenever *much* and *many* feel risky.

1. *A lot of* people showed up for a Tuesday.
2. That job takes *a lot of* patience.
3. *A lot of* the paperwork was already done when we got there.
4. We wasted *a lot of* time on the wrong approach.
5. *A lot of* them had never voted in a primary before.
6. There's *an awful lot of* traffic on the bridge tonight.

**See also:** [lots of](#lots-of), [quite a lot of](#quite-a-lot-of), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of), [much](#much), [many](#many)

### lots of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɑts əv/ &middot; *LOTS uhv* &middot; casually /ˈlɑtsə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal and conversational — a step more casual than *a lot of*, and the form adults use talking to children
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*lots of friends*) **and** uncountable nouns (*lots of sugar*). Never a bare singular countable noun (✗ *lots of chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *lots people*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *lots of the candy*, *lots of them*. Bare *lots* is a pronoun (*We have lots*).
**Pattern:** *lots of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *lots of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *lots of* [them/it] &middot; *lots and lots of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Lots of* and *a lot of* mean exactly the same thing and differ only in register. The plural *-s* does not force a plural verb: agreement still follows the noun after *of*, so *Lots of people are here* but *Lots of sugar is bad for you*. Both are too casual for a research paper, where the equivalents are *many*, *much*, *a great deal of*, and *a large number of*.
**Variants:** *lots and lots of*, *a lot of*, *loads of*

A casual, warm-toned *a lot of*. It reports a big quantity and makes no attempt to be precise about it.

1. There were *lots of* kids at the pool this afternoon.
2. We still have *lots of* time before the movie starts.
3. She's got *lots of* ideas about how to fix the schedule.
4. *Lots of* the seats were still empty at kickoff.
5. He put *lots of* hot sauce on everything.
6. *Lots of* them come back a second year.

**See also:** [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [loads of](#loads-of), [plenty of](#plenty-of)

### plenty of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈplɛnti əv/ &middot; *PLEN-tee uhv* &middot; the *nt* commonly reduces in American speech: /ˈplɛni ə/, "plenny a"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; usable almost anywhere short of a legal brief
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*plenty of chairs*, *plenty of reasons*) **and** uncountable nouns (*plenty of room*, *plenty of time*). Never a bare singular countable noun (✗ *plenty of chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *plenty chairs*. Bare *plenty* is a pronoun (*We have plenty*) or, in casual American speech, an adverb meaning "quite" (*plenty big*, *plenty warm enough*).
**Pattern:** *plenty of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *plenty of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *plenty of* [them/it] &middot; *plenty more* [noun] &middot; *plenty* [adjective] (informal adverb)
**Contrast:** *Plenty of* is not a synonym for *a lot of*. It adds **sufficiency**: the quantity is large *and* it is enough for the purpose, which is why it is the natural answer to worry — *Don't rush, there's plenty of time.* *A lot of* makes no such promise, and *too much* overshoots. Against *enough*, which marks the line exactly, *plenty of* sits comfortably above it. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *Plenty of seats are left*, *Plenty of time remains*.
**Variants:** *plenty more*, *plenty (pronoun)*, *plenty (informal adverb)*, *in plenty*

Marks a quantity that is large and, more to the point, sufficient — as much as anyone needs and then some.

1. There's *plenty of* time before the train leaves.
2. We have *plenty of* parking behind the building.
3. He gave me *plenty of* reasons to be skeptical.
4. *Plenty of* people would take that job tomorrow.
5. There's *plenty of* room in the back seat for all three of you.
6. Don't worry about the food — there's *plenty* left.

**See also:** [enough](#enough), [ample](#ample), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [sufficient](#sufficient), [aplenty](#aplenty), [galore](#galore)

### quite a lot of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈlɑt əv/ &middot; *kwyt uh LOT uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; a shade more measured and more British-sounding than plain *a lot of*
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*quite a lot of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*quite a lot of money*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *quite a lot people*. Bare *quite a lot* is a pronoun (*She's read quite a lot*) or an adverb (*It helped quite a lot*).
**Pattern:** *quite a lot of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *quite a lot of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *quite a lot of* [them/it] &middot; *quite a lot* (pronoun or adverb)
**Contrast:** *Quite* in front of *a lot of*, *a few*, and *a bit of* is an **intensifier**, not a hedge: *quite a lot of trouble* is more trouble than *a lot of trouble*, not less. The same *quite* in front of a bare adjective can go the other way in American English (*quite good* often means only "fairly good"), and that split is what confuses learners. Treat *quite a lot of*, *quite a few*, and *quite a bit of* as three fixed phrases that all mean "a large amount or number."
**Variants:** *quite a bit of*, *quite a few*, *quite a number of*, *quite a lot (pronoun)*

Names a quantity the speaker considers notably large — a mild intensification of *a lot of*, often with a raised eyebrow attached.

1. That's *quite a lot of* money for a used dishwasher.
2. *Quite a lot of* people asked the same question afterward.
3. We got *quite a lot of* snow overnight.
4. He's put *quite a lot of* work into that yard.
5. *Quite a lot of* the data turned out to be duplicated.
6. She travels *quite a lot* for work.

**See also:** [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [quite a few](#quite-a-few), [quite a bit of](#quite-a-bit-of), [a fair amount of](#a-fair-amount-of), [a bit of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-bit-of)

## Large amounts of uncountable things: the *amount* and *deal* family

Almost everything in this section takes uncountable nouns, and this is where the classic red-pen error lives. **Amount** measures, **number** counts: *a large amount of water* but *a large number of bottles*, never ✗ *a large amount of bottles*. The *deal* phrases go one better — they are uncountable-only with no countable version at all, so ✗ *a great deal of people* has to be repaired as *a great many people* or *a large number of people*. Two entries stretch past the mass line and say so in their own **Goes with** line: *a body of* takes a plural that is being treated as a single collection (*a body of laws*), and *an excess of* takes a count measured against an expected figure (*an excess of deaths*). One more is here for its ancestry rather than its usefulness — bare *a deal of* is a nineteenth-century form, not a modern option.

### a body of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbɑdi əv/ &middot; *uh BOD-ee uhv* &middot; the article is the weak /ə/ and *of* reduces to /ə/ in ordinary speech: /ə ˈbɑdi ə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in academic, legal, and journalistic prose
**Goes with:** uncountable abstract nouns that name something accumulated — *a body of evidence*, *a body of work*, *a body of research*, *a body of law*, *a body of knowledge*, *a body of literature*. It reaches a plural only when the plural is itself being treated as one collection (*a body of laws*, *a body of texts*); ordinary countable plurals are out (✗ *a body of chairs*, ✗ *a body of emails*), and so are physical substances (✗ *a body of sugar*). The literal senses are a different word: *a body of water* is a geographic term and *a body of troops* an older military one, and in both *body* is a real head noun that pluralizes freely — *two bodies of water*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a body evidence*. It stays before another determiner: *a body of the case law that predates the statute*.
**Pattern:** *a body of* [mass abstract] &middot; *a body of* [the + mass abstract] &middot; *a growing / substantial / considerable / large body of* [noun] &middot; *the body of* [noun] (definite, naming the whole)
**Contrast:** *A body of* is not a plain *a lot of*: it claims the quantity is large **and coherent** — one accumulated mass that hangs together and can be argued about. That is why *a growing body of evidence* is the standard phrase and ✗ *a body of noise* is not. Against *a wealth of*, which approves of what it counts, and *a great deal of*, which measures and nothing more, *a body of* organizes. The verb is singular, since *body* is the head and the noun after *of* is a mass: *A body of research suggests otherwise.*
**Variants:** *a growing body of*, *a substantial body of*, *a large body of*, *the body of*, *bodies of*

Names a large, accumulated, coherent mass of evidence, work, or knowledge — quantity presented as one organized whole rather than as a heap.

1. A growing *body of* evidence links the two conditions.
2. Her *body of* work spans four decades and three countries.
3. There is a substantial *body of* research on the question and no consensus anywhere in it.
4. The ruling unsettles *a body of* case law going back to the 1970s.
5. *A body of* knowledge that takes a decade to acquire cannot be replaced in a summer.
6. Lake Michigan is the largest *body of* water inside a single state.

**See also:** [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a large amount of](#a-large-amount-of), [considerable](#considerable), [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of)

### a deal of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdil əv/ &middot; *uh DEEL uhv* &middot; the article is the weak /ə/, never /eɪ/, and *of* reduces to /əv/ or a bare /ə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic and dialectal**. The bare form dropped out of standard American English in the nineteenth century and survives now only in period prose, in reported country speech, and in British and Irish dialect. With an adjective wedged in, the same phrase is perfectly current: *a good deal of*, *a great deal of*
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *a deal of trouble*, *a deal of money*, *a deal of talk*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *a deal of people*, ✗ *a deal of reasons*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a deal trouble*. It stays before another determiner: *a deal of the money was gone*.
**Pattern:** *a deal of* [uncountable] &middot; *a deal of* [the/his + uncountable] &middot; *a deal* [comparative] (dated adverb: *a deal better*) &middot; compare current *a good deal of* / *a great deal of*
**Contrast:** This is the ancestor of *a good deal of* and *a great deal of*, and the only difference between them is the adjective the modern phrases keep. Do not write bare *a deal of* in present-day American English: it reads as costume rather than register. When you meet it in Dickens, in Twain, or in an 1880 deposition, read it as plain *much*. Keep it clear of the thoroughly current noun *a deal* meaning a bargain or an arrangement (*We made a deal*, *a good deal on the car*), which is unrelated to quantity.
**Variants:** *a good deal of*, *a great deal of*, *a deal more*, *a deal (dated adverb)*

The bare ancestor of *a good deal of* — a large amount of something uncountable. In American English today it is a period form, not an option.

1. It gave the old man *a deal of* trouble before he was done with it.
2. There was *a deal of* talk in the village that winter and very little sense in it.
3. He had spent *a deal of* money on the place and had nothing to show for it.
4. "I've heard *a deal of* nonsense in my time," the judge said, "but never so much in one afternoon."
5. Those letters cost her *a deal of* pains to write and were never answered.
6. A modern editor would change every one of them to *a good deal of*.

**See also:** [a good deal of](#a-good-deal-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [much](#much), [a power of](#a-power-of), [a sight of](#a-sight-of)

### a fair amount of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌfɛr əˈmaʊnt əv/ &middot; *uh fair uh-MOWNT uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; common in speech and in unfussy writing
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *a fair amount of money*, *a fair amount of work*, *a fair amount of rain*. For countable things the parallel phrase is *a fair number of* — *a fair number of people*, never ✗ *a fair amount of people* in careful writing.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a fair amount money*. It stays before another determiner: *a fair amount of the budget*, *a fair amount of it*.
**Pattern:** *a fair amount of* [uncountable] &middot; *a fair amount of* [the/my + uncountable] &middot; *a fair amount of* [it] &middot; compare *a fair number of* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** The *amount / number* split is the same countability line that separates *much* from *many*, and this pair is where it is easiest to hear. *A fair amount of* is a deliberate hedge: it claims a moderate-to-large quantity without committing to "a lot." Within the family it is the smallest — *a fair amount of* < *a good deal of* < *a great deal of*. Note that *fair* here means "considerable," not "just" or "average."
**Variants:** *a fair number of*, *a fair bit of*, *a fair amount*

Names a quantity that is respectably large without being remarkable — more than a little, less than a flood.

1. It takes *a fair amount of* practice before it feels natural.
2. We got *a fair amount of* rain over the weekend.
3. There's still *a fair amount of* work left on the second floor.
4. She's put *a fair amount of* her own money into the business.
5. *A fair amount of* the equipment was already obsolete.
6. I've spent *a fair amount of* time thinking about this.

**See also:** [a good deal of](#a-good-deal-of), [a large amount of](#a-large-amount-of), [quite a lot of](#quite-a-lot-of), [a number of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-number-of), [a bit of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-bit-of)

### a good deal of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡʊd ˈdil əv/ &middot; *uh good DEEL uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning slightly formal or writerly
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *a good deal of money*, *a good deal of trouble*, *a good deal of attention*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *a good deal of people*, ✗ *a good deal of problems*. The countable partner is *a good many*, which takes a plural noun and no *of*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a good deal money*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a good deal of the money*, *a good deal of it*.
**Pattern:** *a good deal of* [uncountable] &middot; *a good deal of* [the/my/this + uncountable] &middot; *a good deal of* [it] &middot; *a good deal* [comparative adjective] (adverb: *a good deal better*)
**Contrast:** *A good deal of* and *a great deal of* are the same construction with a different adjective; *great* is the stronger of the two. Both are uncountable-only, and that is the trap — their countable counterparts are *a good many* and *a great many*, which look nothing like them. Keep the quantifier apart from the ordinary noun phrase *a good deal* meaning a bargain (*We got a good deal on the car*) and from the adverb (*a good deal better*, *a good deal more expensive*).
**Variants:** *a great deal of*, *a good deal (adverb)*, *a good bit of*

Names a substantial amount of something uncountable, presented as worth noticing.

1. The project has taken *a good deal of* time and money.
2. There's *a good deal of* truth in what she said.
3. He put *a good deal of* thought into that letter.
4. *A good deal of* the criticism was fair.
5. It cost me *a good deal of* sleep before I decided.
6. The new engine is *a good deal* quieter.

**See also:** [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a good many](#a-good-many), [a fair amount of](#a-fair-amount-of), [much](#much), [a deal of](#a-deal-of)

### a great deal of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡreɪt ˈdil əv/ &middot; *uh grayt DEEL uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the standard written phrase where speech would say *a lot of*
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *a great deal of money*, *a great deal of evidence*, *a great deal of effort*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *a great deal of people*, ✗ *a great deal of studies*. The countable partner is *a great many*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a great deal money*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a great deal of the evidence*, *a great deal of it*.
**Pattern:** *a great deal of* [uncountable] &middot; *a great deal of* [the/my/this + uncountable] &middot; *a great deal of* [it] &middot; *a great deal* (pronoun: *That explains a great deal*) &middot; *a great deal* [comparative] (adverb)
**Contrast:** *A great deal of* is the formal register's *a lot of* for uncountables — the phrase that lets a paper avoid both the casual *a lot of* and the stilted bare *much*. Its countable twin is *a great many*, and the two are not interchangeable: ✗ *a great deal of reasons* and ✗ *a great many evidence* are both wrong. *A good deal of* says the same thing a notch quieter.
**Variants:** *a good deal of*, *a great deal (pronoun)*, *a great deal (adverb)*, *a very great deal of*

Names a large amount of something uncountable. In edited American prose it is the most common formal alternative to *a lot of*.

1. The study required *a great deal of* patience.
2. There is *a great deal of* evidence pointing the other way.
3. She has *a great deal of* experience with cases like this.
4. *A great deal of* the money never reached the schools.
5. It would save us *a great deal of* trouble later.
6. That one paragraph explains *a great deal*.

**See also:** [a good deal of](#a-good-deal-of), [a great many](#a-great-many), [a large amount of](#a-large-amount-of), [much](#much), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of)

### a large amount of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌlɑrdʒ əˈmaʊnt əv/ &middot; *uh larj uh-MOWNT uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in reports, science writing, and journalism
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *a large amount of money*, *a large amount of data*, *a large amount of water*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *a large amount of people*, ✗ *a large amount of errors*. The countable phrase is *a large number of*. Two nouns cause trouble here because they feel plural but are grammatically uncountable in English — *data* and *traffic* — so *a large amount of data* is correct.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a large amount money*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a large amount of the funding*, *a large amount of it*.
**Pattern:** *a large amount of* [uncountable] &middot; *large amounts of* [uncountable] &middot; *a large amount of* [the/this + uncountable] &middot; *a large amount of* [it]
**Contrast:** This entry and *a large number of* are the two halves of the single most-corrected error in the quantifier system. **Amount** goes with what you measure, **number** with what you count: *a large amount of sugar* but *a large number of sugar cubes*. When you cannot decide, the escape hatch is *a lot of*, which takes either. The *deal* phrases (*a great deal of*, *a good deal of*) behave identically to *amount* but have no countable version at all.
**Variants:** *a great amount of*, *a huge amount of*, *a vast amount of*, *an enormous amount of*, *large amounts of*

Names a large measured quantity of something uncountable. The adjective is freely swapped for emphasis — *huge*, *vast*, *enormous*, *tremendous* — without changing the grammar.

1. The process uses *a large amount of* water.
2. They collected *a large amount of* data over three winters.
3. *A large amount of* money went missing between the two accounts.
4. Storing *large amounts of* video is not cheap.
5. *A large amount of* the damage was invisible from the street.
6. That takes *a huge amount of* preparation.

**See also:** [a large number of](#a-large-number-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a fair amount of](#a-fair-amount-of), [much](#much), [a lot of](#a-lot-of)

### a wealth of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈwɛlθ əv/ &middot; *uh WELTH uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a favorite of book jackets and job applications
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*a wealth of experience*, *a wealth of detail*, *a wealth of information*) and plural countable nouns (*a wealth of options*, *a wealth of resources*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a wealth information*. It stays before another determiner: *a wealth of the material we collected*.
**Pattern:** *a wealth of* [uncountable] &middot; *a wealth of* [plural countable] &middot; *a wealth of* [the/his + noun]
**Contrast:** *A wealth of* carries a built-in **positive judgment**: the quantity is large and that is a good thing. This is why *a wealth of experience* is natural and *a wealth of problems* is not — for a large quantity of something unwelcome, use *a host of*, *a slew of*, or *no end of*. Compare *an abundance of*, which is positive but neutral about value, and *a plethora of*, which historically meant an unwelcome excess. Agreement follows the noun after *of*.
**Variants:** *a wealth of experience*, *an abundance of*, *a treasure trove of*, *a fund of*

Names a large and valuable supply of something — the size of the quantity and the speaker's approval of it come as one package.

1. She brings *a wealth of* experience to the job.
2. The archive holds *a wealth of* material on the 1930s.
3. There's *a wealth of* detail in the footnotes.
4. The new park gives the neighborhood *a wealth of* options for a Saturday.
5. He had *a wealth of* stories about the old newsroom.
6. *A wealth of* the detail in that book comes from letters found in an attic.

**See also:** [abundant](#abundant), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a host of](#a-host-of), [ample](#ample), [plenty of](#plenty-of)

### an excess of

**Pronunciation:** /ən ɪkˈsɛs əv/ &middot; *uhn ik-SESS uhv* &middot; the article is the weak /ən/; the noun keeps its stress on the second syllable, while the attributive adjective in *excess baggage* moves it to the first, /ˈɛksɛs/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in medicine, finance, statistics, and careful prose
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns above all — *an excess of caution*, *an excess of zeal*, *an excess of moisture*, *an excess of enthusiasm*. It takes a plural countable noun when the count is being measured against an expected figure, which is the statistical use — *an excess of deaths over the seasonal average*, *an excess of applications*. Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *an excess of chair*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *an excess caution*. Do not confuse it with the approximator *in excess of*, which attaches to a number and means "more than" (*in excess of $2 million*); that one is homed with the [numerals](07-numerals-multipliers.md#upwards-of-in-excess-of).
**Pattern:** *an excess of* [uncountable] &middot; *an excess of* [plural countable] (statistical) &middot; *in an excess of* [noun] (adverbial: *in an excess of caution*) &middot; *excess* [noun] (attributive adjective: *excess baggage*) &middot; compare *in excess of* [number]
**Contrast:** **This is not a neutral large quantity.** *An excess of* says the amount is past what is wanted, needed, or safe, which is why *an excess of caution* is a gentle apology and *an excess of fluid* is a diagnosis. *A lot of* and *a great deal of* pass no judgment at all; *a plethora of* and *a surfeit of* pass the same judgment this does. The near-twin *in excess of* is a different construction with a different meaning: *an excess of applicants* means too many, *in excess of 200 applicants* means more than 200.
**Variants:** *in an excess of*, *a surfeit of*, *an overabundance of*, *excess (attributive adjective)*, *excessive*

Names a quantity past the right one — large, and larger than it should be.

1. The refund was issued *in an excess of* caution rather than as any admission of fault.
2. *An excess of* rain in June cost the growers most of the crop.
3. He lost the account through *an excess of* enthusiasm and a shortage of preparation.
4. The study found *an excess of* respiratory cases in the three counties nearest the plant.
5. There is *an excess of* opinion on the subject and almost no data.
6. The winning bid came in *in excess of* four million dollars, which says how much and not too much.

**See also:** [a plethora of](#a-plethora-of), [a large amount of](#a-large-amount-of), [enough](#enough), [ample](#ample), [upwards of, in excess of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#upwards-of-in-excess-of)

### any amount of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni əˈmaʊnt əv/ &middot; *en-ee uh-MOWNT uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *any amount of money*, *any amount of time*, *any amount of trouble*. The countable partner is *any number of* — *any number of reasons* — and in American English that one is far the more common of the two.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *any amount money*. Before another determiner it holds: *any amount of the profit*.
**Pattern:** *any amount of* [uncountable] &middot; *any number of* [plural countable] &middot; *no amount of* [uncountable] (the negative counterpart)
**Contrast:** In an affirmative statement this is a **large-quantity idiom**, not ordinary free-choice *any*: *He'll spend any amount of time on a problem he likes* means a great deal of time, however much it takes. Under negation the phrase flips to its mirror image *no amount of*, which is emphatic and very common — *No amount of practice was going to fix that swing.* Keep it apart from plain [*any*](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), which is the free-choice determiner, and remember that *any number of* is the version you need for countable nouns.
**Variants:** *any number of*, *no amount of*, *any amount*

Names an unlimited or indefinitely large amount — "as much as it takes, and the speaker is not counting."

1. He'll spend *any amount of* time on a problem that interests him.
2. They would have paid *any amount of* money to get that tape back.
3. There are *any number of* reasons the deal could still fall apart.
4. You can throw *any amount of* money at the bridge and the traffic won't improve.
5. *No amount of* explaining was going to change her mind.
6. She has *any amount of* patience with beginners.

**See also:** [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a large number of](#a-large-number-of), [no end of](#no-end-of), [countless](#countless)

### quite a bit of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈbɪt əv/ &middot; *kwyt uh BIT uhv* &middot; both little words are weak — *a* /ə/ and *of* /əv/, and in ordinary speech the *t* flaps and *of* reduces: /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈbɪɾə/, "quite a bidda"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; very common in American speech and unremarkable in ordinary writing
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *quite a bit of money*, *quite a bit of time*, *quite a bit of work*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *quite a bit of people*, ✗ *quite a bit of reasons* — the countable phrase is *quite a few*) and never a bare singular countable noun (✗ *quite a bit of chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory and welded to the phrase — ✗ *quite a bit money*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *quite a bit of the money*, *quite a bit of it*. Bare *quite a bit* with no *of* is either a pronoun (*She knows quite a bit*) or an adverb (*It helped quite a bit*, *quite a bit better*).
**Pattern:** *quite a bit of* [uncountable] &middot; *quite a bit of* [the/my/this + uncountable] &middot; *quite a bit of* [it] &middot; *quite a bit* (pronoun or adverb) &middot; compare *quite a few* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** **It contains *a bit* and it means *much*.** *A bit of trouble* is a small amount; *quite a bit of trouble* is a large one — the same inversion that turns [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few) into *quite a few*, and there is no way to derive either from the parts. The pair splits on countability exactly as *much* and *many* do: *quite a bit of money* against *quite a few dollars*, never ✗ *quite a bit of dollars*. Distribution is the other half of the lesson: this is an **affirmative** phrase and one of the standard American repairs for stilted bare *much* — *We have quite a bit of time* where writing would say *We have much time* — while *much* keeps the questions and the negatives, where *quite a bit of* is at best awkward: you ask *How much time do we have?*, not *Do we have quite a bit of time?* Agreement follows the noun after *of*, which for an uncountable is always singular: *Quite a bit of the money **is** still unspent.*
**Variants:** *quite a bit*, *quite a little bit of*, *quite a few*, *quite a lot of*

Names a large amount of something uncountable, with the speaker mildly registering the size — "more than you would think."

1. That repair is going to take *quite a bit of* time.
2. We got *quite a bit of* rain out of Thursday's storm.
3. He has put *quite a bit of* work into the application.
4. *Quite a bit of* the money had already been spent by then.
5. There's *quite a bit of* interest in the second session.
6. She knows *quite a bit* about early jazz.

## Large numbers of countable things

Everything here takes a plural countable noun, and most of them take nothing else — feed *a large number of*, *a good many*, or *quite a few* an uncountable noun and the sentence breaks. A few entries carry a documented exception and say so in their own **Goes with** line: *a majority of* reaches a singular mass that names a share (*a majority of the vote*); *a multitude of* and *a plethora of* admit an uncountable in elevated or technical style; and *a wave of* takes an abstract mass naming a feeling that arrives all at once (*a wave of relief*). Three others restrict the **kind** of plural they will accept rather than admitting more — *a bevy of*, *droves of*, and *hordes of* count people and animals and sound wrong applied to objects. Two further rules run through the section. The *number* phrases mirror the *amount* phrases exactly (*a large number of errors* against *a large amount of data*), and the numeral phrases obey one fixed alternation: **after a number the word is singular with no *of*** (*two dozen eggs*, *three hundred people*), but **standing alone as a quantifier it is plural and requires *of*** (*dozens of eggs*, *hundreds of people*).

### a bevy of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbɛvi əv/ &middot; *uh BEV-ee uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal to journalistic and faintly jocular — a headline and society-page word. Applied to women (*a bevy of beauties*) it is dated and now reads as leering, which is worth knowing before anyone uses it that way
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns, and above all ones naming people or animals — *a bevy of aides*, *a bevy of celebrities*, *a bevy of quail*. Loose journalism extends it to things (*a bevy of new features*), which is standard enough but flattens the word. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a bevy of information*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a bevy lawyers*. It stays before another determiner: *a bevy of the usual consultants*.
**Pattern:** *a bevy of* [plural countable, usually animate] &middot; *a bevy of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a whole bevy of* [plural]
**Contrast:** *Bevy* began as a hunting collective for quail and larks, and the flock is still audible in it: the phrase pictures the group arriving together and slightly fluttering. That tone is what separates it from the neutral *a host of* and the brisk *a slew of*, neither of which pictures anything. Agreement is notional and normally plural: *A bevy of consultants were brought in.*
**Variants:** *a whole bevy of*, *a covey of*, *a flock of*, *a host of*

Names a sizable group of people or creatures arriving together, in a light journalistic register.

1. The governor arrived with *a bevy of* aides and no answers.
2. *A bevy of* startups turned up promising the same thing.
3. *A bevy of* quail broke out of the brush ahead of the dogs.
4. The premiere drew *a bevy of* second-tier celebrities and one genuine star.
5. *A bevy of* new regulations takes effect in January.
6. She fielded *a bevy of* questions about the budget before anyone asked about the schools.

**See also:** [a host of](#a-host-of), [a slew of](#a-slew-of), [hordes of](#hordes-of), [droves of](#droves-of), [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of)

### a good many

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡʊd ˈmɛni/ &middot; *uh good MEN-ee*
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a homespun or slightly old-fashioned flavor; common in Southern and Midwestern speech
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a good many people*, *a good many years*, *a good many of the houses*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a good many money*, ✗ *a good many information*. The uncountable partner is *a good deal of*.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *a good many of the applicants*, *a good many of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *a good many applicants*, never ✗ *a good many of applicants*.
**Pattern:** *a good many* [plural countable] &middot; *a good many of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; *a good many of* [them/us] &middot; *a good many more* [plural]
**Contrast:** Look at the syntax before you object to it: the singular article *a* stands in front of the plural quantifier *many*, and the phrase then takes a plural noun and a plural verb — *A good many people were unhappy.* It is fully standard. Its uncountable counterpart is *a good deal of*, and its stronger, more formal sibling is *a great many*. Against plain *many*, *a good many* is warmer and more conversational.
**Variants:** *a great many*, *a good many more*, *a good few*

Names a sizable number of countable things, with a note of the speaker having taken stock — "more than you would guess."

1. *A good many* people showed up in spite of the weather.
2. He's been doing this for *a good many* years.
3. *A good many* of the houses on that block have sold since spring.
4. There are *a good many* reasons not to sign that lease.
5. I've read *a good many* of them, but not all.
6. We'll need *a good many* more volunteers before Saturday.

**See also:** [a great many](#a-great-many), [many](#many), [a good deal of](#a-good-deal-of), [quite a few](#quite-a-few), [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few)

### a great many

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌɡreɪt ˈmɛni/ &middot; *uh grayt MEN-ee*
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; at home in written English and in careful speech
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a great many people*, *a great many books*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a great many evidence*, ✗ *a great many money*. The uncountable partner is *a great deal of*.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *a great many of the delegates*, *a great many of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *a great many delegates*.
**Pattern:** *a great many* [plural countable] &middot; *a great many of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a great many of* [them] &middot; *a great many more* [plural]
**Contrast:** *A great many* and *a great deal of* are a matched pair split by countability, and swapping them is a hard error: ✗ *a great deal of reasons*, ✗ *a great many evidence*. Against *a good many*, *a great many* is stronger and more formal. Against plain *many*, it is emphatic — it is the phrase a writer uses when *many* alone would undersell the number. Note again the singular *a* in front of a plural quantifier, and note that the verb still agrees plural.
**Variants:** *a good many*, *a great many more*, *a very great many*

Names an emphatically large number of countable things — *many*, with the volume turned up.

1. *A great many* people were affected by the recall.
2. She has written *a great many* essays on the subject.
3. *A great many* of the original documents were lost in the fire.
4. There are *a great many* things I would do differently.
5. *A great many* of them never finished the program.
6. It has been repeated by *a great many* otherwise careful writers.

**See also:** [a good many](#a-good-many), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [many](#many), [numerous](#numerous), [a large number of](#a-large-number-of)

### a host of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhoʊst əv/ &middot; *uh HOHST uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a staple of journalism and business writing
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a host of problems*, *a host of reasons*, *a host of new rules*. It does not take uncountable nouns in careful writing (✗ *a host of information*), and never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a host problems*. It stays before another determiner: *a host of the issues we raised*.
**Pattern:** *a host of* [plural countable] &middot; *a host of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a whole host of* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** *A host of* is neutral in form but skews toward the unwelcome in practice: its favorite companions are *problems*, *issues*, *questions*, *complications*, and *challenges*, where *a wealth of* takes the good things. Agreement is notional and normally plural — *A host of new rules are taking effect Monday.* Keep it separate from the ordinary noun *host* (the person giving the party, the host of a show) and from *a host of* in its older military sense, an army.
**Variants:** *a whole host of*, *a raft of*, *a battery of*, *an array of*

Names a large and varied number of countable things arriving together, most often problems or considerations.

1. The merger raises *a host of* regulatory questions.
2. She turned down the job for *a host of* reasons.
3. *A host of* small errors added up to a serious problem.
4. The bill would create *a whole host of* new exemptions.
5. *A host of* startups have tried the same idea and failed.
6. There are *a host of* reasons to be careful with that number.

**See also:** [a slew of](#a-slew-of), [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of), [numerous](#numerous), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of), [a large number of](#a-large-number-of)

### a large number of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌlɑrdʒ ˈnʌmbɚ əv/ &middot; *uh larj NUM-ber uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the standard phrase in academic, technical, and business writing
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *a large number of students*, *a large number of errors*, *a large number of the applicants*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a large number of money*, ✗ *a large number of information*. The uncountable phrase is *a large amount of*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a large number students*. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a large number of the applicants*, *a large number of them*.
**Pattern:** *a large number of* [plural countable] + **plural** verb &middot; *large numbers of* [plural countable] &middot; *a large number of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a large number of* [them]
**Contrast:** Two rules meet in this phrase. First, the countability rule: **number** counts, **amount** measures, so *a large number of bottles* but *a large amount of water*. Second, the agreement rule that catches native writers as often as learners: ***a* number of** takes a **plural** verb (*A large number of students were absent*), while ***the* number of** takes a **singular** one (*The number of students was surprising*). The indefinite article turns the phrase into a quantifier; the definite article leaves *number* as the real head noun.
**Variants:** *a great number of*, *a huge number of*, *a vast number of*, *large numbers of*, *a number of*

Names a large count of individual things, stated with enough formality to survive in a report or a paper.

1. *A large number of* students were absent that week.
2. The survey drew *a large number of* responses from rural counties.
3. *Large numbers of* birds pass through the refuge in October.
4. *A large number of* the files had never been opened.
5. The change affects *a large number of* small businesses.
6. *A large number of* them declined to give their names.

**See also:** [a large amount of](#a-large-amount-of), [a number of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-number-of), [many](#many), [numerous](#numerous), [a majority of](#a-majority-of)

### a majority of

**Pronunciation:** /ə məˈdʒɔrəɾi əv/ &middot; *uh muh-JOR-uh-tee uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in reporting, polling, and law
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a majority of voters*, *a majority of the states*, *a majority of them*. It also takes a singular mass that names a share rather than a count (*a majority of the vote*, *a majority of the budget*), and there the verb goes singular. It never takes an ordinary uncountable substance: ✗ *a majority of water*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a majority voters*. The noun after *of* is normally definite or generic: *a majority of the committee*, *a majority of Americans*.
**Pattern:** *a majority of* [plural countable] + **plural** verb &middot; *a majority of* [the + collective/mass] + **singular** verb &middot; *a vast / slim / overwhelming majority of* [plural]
**Contrast:** *A majority of* against *the majority of*: the definite version is the everyday one and points at an identified group, while the indefinite is a shade more statistical and is what you want after a vote count. Agreement is notional either way — plural when the phrase counts people or things (*A majority of voters were undecided*), singular when it names one body or one share (*A majority of the committee was in favor*). Against *most*, which says the same thing in one word and needs no *of* before a bare noun — *most voters* beside *a majority of voters*. A majority is anything past half; for a lopsided one you need *the vast majority*.
**Variants:** *the majority of*, *a vast majority of*, *an overwhelming majority of*, *a slim majority of*, *a bare majority of*

Names more than half of a set — the counted larger part, as opposed to the impressionistic "a lot."

1. *A majority of* voters said they had already made up their minds.
2. *A majority of* the states have passed some version of the law.
3. The measure needs *a majority of* the votes cast, not of the members present.
4. *A majority of* them had been with the company under five years.
5. *A vast majority of* the complaints came from one zip code.
6. *A majority of* the committee was in favor of postponing.

**See also:** [most](#most), [the majority of, the minority of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), [the bulk of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-bulk-of), [percent](07-numerals-multipliers.md#percent), [a plurality of](#a-plurality-of), [a supermajority of](#a-supermajority-of)

### a multiplicity of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌmʌltəˈplɪsəɾi əv/ &middot; *uh mul-tuh-PLISS-uh-tee uhv* &middot; the *t* of *-city* flaps to /ɾ/ in American speech, exactly as in *majority* and *variety*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal and technical — academic prose, engineering, and legal drafting. In ordinary writing it is heavy and faintly bureaucratic, and an editor will usually cut it to *many* or *a variety of*
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a multiplicity of factors*, *a multiplicity of causes*, *a multiplicity of forms*. Uncountable nouns read as an error in careful prose: ✗ *a multiplicity of information*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a multiplicity factors*. It stays before another determiner: *a multiplicity of the same objections*.
**Pattern:** *a multiplicity of* [plural countable] &middot; *a multiplicity of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a multiplicity of suits* (the legal set phrase)
**Contrast:** *A multiplicity of* stresses **kinds**, not headcount: it says the things are many and of many different sorts, which is why *a multiplicity of causes* is idiomatic and *a multiplicity of people* is not. Against *a multitude of*, which is about sheer number, and *a variety of*, which says the same thing in a register anyone can read. American legal writing gives it a near-technical sense — *a multiplicity of suits* names the problem that joinder rules exist to prevent, several lawsuits where one would do.
**Variants:** *a multitude of*, *a variety of*, *manifold*, *multiplicities of (rare)*

Names a large number of things of many different kinds — number and diversity together, in a formal register.

1. The failure had *a multiplicity of* causes, and the report names none of them.
2. Equity will intervene to avoid *a multiplicity of* suits arising from one transaction.
3. *A multiplicity of* standards has kept the industry from settling on a single connector.
4. The program is administered through *a multiplicity of* state agencies with overlapping jurisdiction.
5. She traced *a multiplicity of* small errors back to one bad assumption.
6. In plainer prose *a multiplicity of* factors is simply many different factors.

**See also:** [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of), [a variety of](#a-variety-of), [myriad](#myriad), [numerous](#numerous), [manifold](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#manifold)

### a multitude of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmʌltəˌtud əv/ &middot; *uh MUL-tuh-tood uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; slightly grand for everyday speech
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a multitude of reasons*, *a multitude of small decisions*. Uncountable nouns appear with it only in elevated or biblical style; the plural is the norm. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a multitude reasons*. It stays before another determiner: *a multitude of the smaller towns*.
**Pattern:** *a multitude of* [plural countable] &middot; *a multitude of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *multitudes of* [plural countable] &middot; *cover a multitude of sins* (fixed idiom)
**Contrast:** *A multitude of* is the elevated register's *a lot of*, and it shares its ground with *a host of* and *a myriad of*. Where *a host of* leans toward trouble and *a wealth of* toward good fortune, *a multitude* is neutral about value and emphatic about sheer number. Agreement is notional and usually plural (*A multitude of factors are involved*). The idiom *cover a multitude of sins* — a phrase from the King James Bible now used lightly of anything that hides a lot of flaws — is fixed and cannot be varied.
**Variants:** *multitudes of*, *a myriad of*, *a host of*, *cover a multitude of sins*

Names a very large number of countable things, viewed as a crowd rather than a list.

1. The delay had *a multitude of* causes, none of them decisive.
2. A good coat of paint covers *a multitude of* sins.
3. *A multitude of* small charges had piled up on the account.
4. *Multitudes of* travelers were stranded when the system went down.
5. The decision affects *a multitude of* people who had no say in it.
6. He offered *a multitude of* excuses, each less convincing than the last.

### a plethora of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈplɛθərə əv/ &middot; *uh PLETH-uh-ruh uhv* &middot; stress falls on the **first** syllable; the common /pləˈθɔrə/ is a mispronunciation
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal, often self-consciously so; heavily overused in business and marketing prose
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a plethora of options*, *a plethora of choices*. Uncountable nouns occur (*a plethora of information*) but are less established. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a plethora options*. Bare *plethora* without an article is nonstandard in American English: ✗ *plethora of options*.
**Pattern:** *a plethora of* [plural countable] &middot; *a plethora of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a veritable plethora of* [plural]
**Contrast:** *Plethora* came into English as a medical term for a dangerous **excess** — too much of something — and careful writers still use it that way, so *a plethora of options* implies more options than are useful. In current American usage it has drifted to a plain "a lot of," which is why editors often strike it: if you mean *many*, *many* is shorter and clearer. If you do want the note of excess, *a surfeit of* and *an overabundance of* keep it intact.
**Variants:** *a surfeit of*, *an overabundance of*, *a superfluity of*, *a veritable plethora of*

Names a large number of things, in careful use an unhelpfully large number — an oversupply rather than merely a lot.

1. The menu offers *a plethora of* options and no way to choose among them.
2. *A plethora of* new apps promise to do the same thing.
3. There is *a plethora of* research on the topic and very little agreement.
4. The form asks for *a plethora of* details nobody will ever read.
5. *A plethora of* competing standards slowed the rollout.
6. He answered with *a plethora of* statistics and no actual argument.

### a range of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈreɪndʒ əv/ &middot; *uh RAYNJ uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/ in ordinary speech: /ə ˈreɪndʒə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; a workhorse of business, policy, medical, and academic prose
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a range of options*, *a range of services*, *a range of temperatures*. It takes an uncountable noun only where that noun names something measurable along a scale (*a range of motion*, *a range of experience*); ✗ *a range of information* is odd. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a range options*. It stays before another determiner: *a range of the products we already carry*.
**Pattern:** *a range of* [plural countable] &middot; *a wide / broad / full / whole range of* [plural] &middot; *a range of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *the range of* [noun] (definite, naming the span itself)
**Contrast:** *A range of* claims **spread along a scale**: the things differ from one end to the other in price, size, difficulty, or temperature. *A variety of* claims assortment with no ordering at all — *a range of prices* runs from low to high, *a variety of flavors* runs from nowhere to nowhere. Both are far weaker quantity words than they look: *a range of options* can be four options, which is exactly why press releases like the phrase. Agreement is notional and usually plural (*A range of products are affected*), though a writer treating *range* as the head noun will use the singular.
**Variants:** *a wide range of*, *a broad range of*, *a full range of*, *a whole range of*, *ranges of*

Names a set of things spread out along some scale — several to many, differing from one another by degree.

1. The clinic offers *a range of* services on a sliding scale.
2. We tested the alloy across *a wide range of* temperatures.
3. *A range of* options was presented and none of them was cheap.
4. The bill drew objections from *a range of* groups that agree on nothing else.
5. She has worked on *a broad range of* cases, from zoning disputes to capital appeals.
6. The store stocks shoes in *a range of* widths, which is rarer than it sounds.

### a series of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsɪriz əv/ &middot; *uh SEER-eez uhv* &middot; the noun is spelled and pronounced the same in the singular and the plural — *one series*, *two series*; there is no ✗ *a serie*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; at home everywhere from news copy to a lab report
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns naming events, steps, or items that follow one another — *a series of meetings*, *a series of errors*, *a series of storms*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a series of information*, ✗ *a series of trouble*. Never a bare singular countable noun.

**Contrast:** *A series of* adds **succession** to number: the things came one after another, in time or in order, which is the whole difference from *a bunch of*, which says nothing about sequence. **Variants:** *a string of*, *a succession of*, *a run of*, *a chain of*, *a whole series of*

1. *A series of* small mistakes ended with the wrong address on four thousand envelopes.

4. He answered with *a string of* excuses, each thinner than the last.
5. The paper documents *a series of* failures in the inspection process.
6. After *a string of* one-run losses the team fired the hitting coach.

### a slew of

**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a slew of complaints*, *a slew of new hires*. Not used with uncountable nouns (✗ *a slew of money*) and never with a bare singular countable noun.

### a variety of

**Pronunciation:** /ə vəˈraɪəɾi əv/ &middot; *uh vuh-RY-uh-tee uhv* &middot; the first syllable of *variety* is the weak /və/, never /væ/, and the *t* flaps to /ɾ/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard in every register short of poetry, and one of the commonest hedges in American writing
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a variety of reasons*, *a variety of products*, *a variety of ways*. Uncountables only when the noun names a kind rather than a substance: *a variety of music* is fine, ✗ *a variety of water* is not. It does take a singular countable noun in the unrelated botanical sense, where *a variety of apple* names one cultivar and means the opposite of a large quantity.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a variety reasons*. It stays before another determiner: *a variety of the same excuses*.
**Pattern:** *a variety of* [plural countable] &middot; *a wide / great / rich / whole variety of* [plural] &middot; *a variety of* [the + plural] &middot; *for a variety of reasons* (fixed) &middot; compare *a variety of* [singular] = one cultivar
**Contrast:** The claim is **assortment**, not size: *a variety of reasons* is several reasons of different kinds and can be three of them. That makes it the polite way to be vague, and *for a variety of reasons* is the standard American refusal to give any. Against *a range of*, which spreads its items along a scale; against *a multiplicity of*, which says the same thing in a lab coat; against *a host of* and *a slew of*, which claim a big number and no diversity at all. Agreement is notional and usually plural: *A variety of factors are at work.* And watch the singular trap — *a variety of apple* is one apple, not many.
**Variants:** *a wide variety of*, *a great variety of*, *a rich variety of*, *a whole variety of*, *varieties of*

Names a number of things of different kinds — diversity first, quantity second.

1. The store carries *a wide variety of* flours, none of them cheap.
2. She left the job for *a variety of* reasons and named exactly one of them.
3. *A variety of* approaches have been tried and none has been evaluated.
4. The grant supports *a variety of* after-school programs across the district.
5. Those symptoms can be caused by *a variety of* things, most of them harmless.
6. Honeycrisp is *a variety of* apple, which is one apple and not a crowd.

**See also:** [a range of](#a-range-of), [a multiplicity of](#a-multiplicity-of), [myriad](#myriad), [a host of](#a-host-of), [all kinds of, all sorts of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of)

### a wave of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈweɪv əv/ &middot; *uh WAYV uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/ in speech: /ə ˈweɪvə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; a staple of news writing and completely at home in speech
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns naming events, people, or arrivals — *a wave of layoffs*, *a wave of immigrants*, *a wave of protests* — and, just as commonly, **abstract uncountable nouns naming a feeling that comes over someone**: *a wave of relief*, *a wave of nausea*, *a wave of nostalgia*. It does not quantify ordinary masses: ✗ *a wave of money* is odd, and *a wave of water* is literal rather than quantitative.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a wave layoffs*. It stays before another determiner: *a wave of the same complaints*.
**Pattern:** *a wave of* [plural countable] &middot; *a wave of* [abstract mass] &middot; *waves of* [noun] &middot; *the first / next / latest wave of* [noun] &middot; *a fresh wave of* [noun] &middot; *a flurry of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *A wave of* is a quantity **with a shape in time**: the things arrive together, crest, and pass, and another wave may follow. That is what separates it from *a lot of*, which is flat, and from *a series of*, which is a line rather than a surge. Its short-lived cousin *a flurry of* names a brief scattered burst — *a flurry of emails*, *a flurry of activity* — and cannot describe anything sustained; *a spate of* insists the burst was sudden and usually unwelcome; *a surge of* is the same shape without the falling half. Agreement follows the head in practice: *A wave of layoffs is coming* keeps the singular, while *Waves of protesters were arriving* is plural.
**Variants:** *a flurry of*, *waves of*, *a fresh wave of*, *a surge of*, *a spate of*, *a rash of*

Names a large number of things arriving together in a surge that rises and passes — and, with an abstract noun, a feeling that comes over someone the same way.

1. *A wave of* layoffs hit the industry in the second quarter.
2. She felt *a wave of* relief when the phone finally rang.
3. The bill set off *a flurry of* amendments in the last week of the session.
4. *A wave of* new arrivals doubled the town's population in a decade.
5. *Waves of* thunderstorms moved across the state all afternoon.
6. There was *a flurry of* activity at the front desk and then nothing for an hour.

**See also:** [a series of](#a-series-of), [a slew of](#a-slew-of), [a host of](#a-host-of), [droves of](#droves-of), [a bevy of](#a-bevy-of)

### dozens of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʌzənz əv/ &middot; *DUZ-uhnz uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (indefinite numeral phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *dozens of emails*, *dozens of people*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *dozens of information*, ✗ *dozens of furniture*, ✗ *dozens of money*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **obligatory** in this plural quantifier — ✗ *dozens emails*. The exact numeral behaves in the opposite way: after a number, *dozen* is **singular and takes no *of*** — *two dozen eggs*, *three dozen roses*. ✗ *two dozens of eggs* breaks both halves of the rule at once.
**Pattern:** *dozens of* [plural countable] &middot; *dozens of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *dozens upon dozens of* [plural] &middot; compare [number] *dozen* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** This is the governing rule for the whole *dozen / hundred / thousand / million* family: **after a number, singular and no *of*** (*two dozen*, *three hundred*, *five thousand*); **alone as a quantifier, plural and *of* required** (*dozens of*, *hundreds of*, *thousands of*). *Dozens of* claims an indefinite number in the tens — more than a handful, fewer than a hundred — and no one is expected to have counted. The one place the numeral and the quantifier meet is *a dozen*, which means exactly twelve except in the loose idiom *a dozen times*. Bare *dozens* is also a pronoun, with the *of*-phrase left understood: *Dozens were turned away at the door.*
**Variants:** *dozens upon dozens of*, *several dozen*, *a dozen*, *half a dozen*

Names an indefinite number in the tens, offered as a large one.

1. *Dozens of* people were waiting outside before the doors opened.
2. She sent *dozens of* emails and got two replies.
3. *Dozens of* flights were canceled up and down the coast.
4. We found *dozens of* the same typo scattered through the manuscript.
5. He must have watched that movie *dozens of* times.
6. I picked up two *dozen* eggs on the way home.

**See also:** [hundreds of](#hundreds-of), [thousands of](#thousands-of), [scores of](#scores-of), [dozen](07-numerals-multipliers.md#dozen), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](07-numerals-multipliers.md#hundred-thousand-million-billion)

### droves of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdroʊvz əv/ &middot; *DROHVZ uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal and mildly vivid; common in journalism, since the word still pictures livestock being driven along a road
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns naming people or animals only** — *droves of tourists*, *droves of voters*, *droves of cattle*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *droves of money*) and, in careful use, never inanimate things: ✗ *droves of emails* reads as a mixed metaphor.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory in the determiner use — ✗ *droves tourists*. The far commoner construction is the **adverbial** *in droves*, which takes no noun at all and modifies the verb — *They left in droves* — and is a prepositional phrase, not a determiner: it cannot be moved in front of a noun (✗ *in droves people left*). The singular *a drove of* survives mainly for livestock.
**Pattern:** *droves of* [plural animate] &middot; *droves of* [the + plural] &middot; [verb] + *in droves* (adverbial) &middot; *a drove of* [livestock]
**Contrast:** *Droves of* and *in droves* are one word doing two jobs, and only the first is a determiner. Both carry the same picture — a herd moving in one direction — and both are usually about people behaving as a mass: *subscribers canceled in droves*. Against *hordes of*, which adds disorder and a faint hostility, and against *masses of*, which is shapeless, *droves of* implies movement with a direction. Unlike *dozens*, *hundreds*, and *thousands*, bare *droves* is not used as a pronoun: ✗ *Droves were turned away*.
**Variants:** *in droves*, *a drove of*, *throngs of*, *hordes of*

Names a large number of people or animals moving together in one direction.

1. *Droves of* tourists come through the canyon between May and September.
2. Subscribers canceled *in droves* after the price increase.
3. *Droves of* young families have moved into the county since the plant opened.
4. Volunteers turned out *in droves* for the first cleanup and in twos for every one after it.
5. *Droves of* voters skipped the primary altogether.
6. The road was closed for *a drove of* cattle crossing to the summer pasture.

**See also:** [hordes of](#hordes-of), [masses of](#masses-of), [a bevy of](#a-bevy-of), [thousands of](#thousands-of), [scores of](#scores-of)

### hordes of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɔrdz əv/ &middot; *HORDZ uhv* &middot; a homophone of *hoards* (stockpiles); the two spellings are not interchangeable
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal to journalistic; vivid and mildly disapproving — the crowd it names is one the speaker would rather avoid
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns naming people or animals** — *hordes of shoppers*, *hordes of tourists*, *hordes of mosquitoes*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *hordes of traffic*) and not naturally used of inanimate objects (✗ *hordes of emails*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *hordes tourists*. The singular *a horde of* is equally common and means the same thing: *a horde of reporters*. Bare *hordes* works as a pronoun only in headline style: *Hordes descended on the outlet mall.*
**Pattern:** *hordes of* [plural animate] &middot; *a horde of* [plural animate] &middot; *hordes of* [the + plural] &middot; *a whole horde of* [plural]
**Contrast:** *Horde* came into English as the name of a Mongol army and never lost the connotation: the crowd is large, unruly, and unwelcome. That built-in judgment is the reason to choose it over neutral *crowds of* or *throngs of*, and the reason not to use it of the people you are writing for. Against *droves of*, which moves in a direction, and *masses of*, which is shapeless and British-flavored. One spelling warning: *horde* is a crowd, *hoard* is a stash, and ✗ *hoards of tourists* is a common and conspicuous error.
**Variants:** *a horde of*, *a whole horde of*, *throngs of*, *swarms of*, *droves of*

Names a large, disorderly crowd of people or animals, with the speaker's disapproval built into the word.

1. *Hordes of* shoppers were already in line when the doors opened.
2. *A horde of* reporters was waiting on the courthouse steps.
3. *Hordes of* mosquitoes came off the marsh at dusk.
4. The trail is quiet in April and overrun by *hordes of* day hikers in July.
5. *Hordes of* the summer people arrive the week after Memorial Day.
6. She had pictured a quiet museum morning and walked into *a horde of* fourth-graders.

**See also:** [droves of](#droves-of), [masses of](#masses-of), [a bevy of](#a-bevy-of), [thousands of](#thousands-of), [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of)

### hundreds of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhʌndrədz əv/ &middot; *HUN-dridz uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (indefinite numeral phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *hundreds of people*, *hundreds of dollars*, *hundreds of miles*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *hundreds of money*, ✗ *hundreds of water*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **obligatory** — ✗ *hundreds people*. The exact numeral takes the opposite treatment: after a number, *hundred* stays **singular with no *of*** — *three hundred people*, ✗ *three hundreds of people*.
**Pattern:** *hundreds of* [plural countable] &middot; *hundreds of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *hundreds and hundreds of* [plural] &middot; *hundreds of thousands of* [plural] &middot; compare [number] *hundred* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** Same alternation as *dozens of* and *thousands of*: plural plus *of* for the vague quantity, singular and bare after an exact number. *Hundreds of* is genuinely vague — it covers anything from about two hundred to just under a thousand, and speakers reach for it precisely because they have not counted. The stacked form *hundreds of thousands of* is standard and means what it says; each *of* is required. Bare *hundreds* is a pronoun in the same way as *dozens* and *thousands*: *Hundreds were still waiting at midnight.*
**Variants:** *hundreds and hundreds of*, *several hundred*, *hundreds of thousands of*, *a hundred*

Names an indefinite number in the hundreds, presented as a large one.

1. *Hundreds of* people lined up before the store opened.
2. The repair is going to cost *hundreds of* dollars.
3. *Hundreds of* the letters were returned undelivered.
4. She drove *hundreds of* miles out of her way to avoid the interstate.
5. *Hundreds of thousands of* households lost power in the storm.
6. There were three *hundred* seats and twice that many people.

### millions of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɪljənz əv/ &middot; *MIL-yuhnz uhv* &middot; *of* is unstressed and commonly reduces to /ə/
**Type:** quantifier (indefinite numeral phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral in the literal sense; informal and hyperbolic in the *millions of times* sense
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *millions of people*, *millions of dollars*, *millions of years*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *millions of money*, ✗ *millions of information*, ✗ *millions of traffic*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **obligatory** — ✗ *millions people*. After an exact number the word goes **singular and takes no *of***: *two million people*, ✗ *two millions of people*. *Billions of* behaves identically (*three billion dollars*, *billions of dollars*).
**Pattern:** *millions of* [plural countable] &middot; *millions of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *millions upon millions of* [plural] &middot; *tens of millions of* [plural] &middot; compare [number] *million* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** The top of the *dozen / hundred / thousand / million* series, obeying the same alternation as the rest of it: plural plus *of* for a vague quantity, singular and bare after an exact figure. Two of its uses are not counts at all — the plain hyperbole of *millions of times* and the singular *a million* in *I have a million things to do*, which reports exasperation rather than a number and belongs with *a zillion* and *umpteen*. Agreement is plural when the phrase counts things (*Millions of ballots **were** mailed*) but singular when a figure names one sum or one span (*Two million dollars **is** a lot to write off*). Bare *millions* is also a pronoun: *Millions were watching.*
**Variants:** *millions upon millions of*, *tens of millions of*, *billions of*, *several million*, *a million (hyperbolic)*

Names an indefinite number in the millions — and, loosely, any number the speaker wants to call enormous.

1. *Millions of* people watched the hearing live.
2. The cleanup has already cost *millions of* dollars.
3. These formations took *millions of* years to build up.
4. *Millions of* the old accounts were never closed out.
5. I've told him that *a million* times.
6. The company employs two *million* people worldwide.

### quite a few

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈfju/ &middot; *kwyt uh FYOO*
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; very common in American speech and unremarkable in writing
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *quite a few people*, *quite a few times*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *quite a few money*, ✗ *quite a few advice*. The uncountable partner is [quite a bit of](#quite-a-bit-of).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *quite a few of the guests*, *quite a few of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *quite a few guests*.
**Pattern:** *quite a few* [plural countable] &middot; *quite a few of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; *quite a few of* [them/us] &middot; *quite a few more* [plural] &middot; *quite a few* (pronoun: *Quite a few showed up*)
**Contrast:** **It contains *few* and it means *many*.** *A few people came* reports a small number; *quite a few people came* reports a large one. The same reversal runs through *quite a bit of*, which means a large amount despite containing *a bit*. There is no way to derive this from the parts, and the reliable fix is to file *quite a few* and *quite a bit of* as unanalyzable single words meaning "a lot." Its uncountable partner is *quite a bit of*; the merely-small versions are [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few) and [a bit of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-bit-of).
**Variants:** *quite a bit of*, *quite a number of*, *quite a lot of*, *quite a few more*

Names a large number of countable things — noticeably more than the listener might expect, with a hint that the speaker was mildly surprised too.

1. *Quite a few* people asked about that after the meeting.
2. I've been there *quite a few* times over the years.
3. *Quite a few* of the guests stayed past midnight.
4. There are *quite a few* things wrong with that argument.
5. *Quite a few* of them had driven in from out of state.
6. We still have *quite a few* more boxes to unpack.

### scores of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈskɔrz əv/ &middot; *SKORZ uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (indefinite numeral phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** literary and journalistic; slightly elevated and almost always about people or events
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *scores of protesters*, *scores of injuries*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *scores of damage*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **obligatory** — ✗ *scores protesters*. As an exact numeral, *score* means twenty and stays singular with no *of*, but that use is archaic and survives mainly in the biblical *three score and ten* and in Lincoln's *four score and seven years ago*.

**Contrast:** Same family rule as *dozens of* and *hundreds of*: plural plus *of* for a vague quantity. ### umpteen

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌmpˈtin/ &middot; *ump-TEEN*
**Type:** quantifier (indefinite numeral) &middot; **Position:** central, in the numeral slot; like a numeral it is also a **postdeterminer** after a central determiner — *the umpteen forms they made us sign*, *his umpteen cousins* &middot; **Register:** informal and exasperated; current in speech, out of place in formal writing
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *umpteen times*, *umpteen forms*, *umpteen reasons*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *umpteen money*, ✗ *umpteen advice*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *umpteen excuse*), because it behaves grammatically as a plural number word.
**With *of*:** no *of* before a bare noun — *umpteen times*, never ✗ *umpteen of times*. The partitive *umpteen of the forms* is possible on the numeral pattern but rare; speakers say *umpteen forms*.
**Pattern:** *umpteen* [plural countable] &middot; [the/his/my] *umpteen* [plural countable] &middot; *for the umpteenth time* (the ordinal, far commoner)
**Contrast:** *Umpteen* is a joke numeral, built from the *-teen* of *thirteen* and *nineteen* on a placeholder *ump-* out of old military slang. **Variants:** *umpteenth*, *for the umpteenth time*, *umpty-ump*, *a zillion*, *a gazillion*, *a bazillion*

4. We tried *umpteen* different passwords before the lock finally opened.
5. The *umpteen* meetings this quarter have eaten the whole schedule.

## Adjectives that quantify

These seven are **adjectives**, not determiners, and the difference is testable. A determiner cannot sit inside another determiner's phrase and cannot be graded — ✗ *the much time*, ✗ *a much money*, ✗ *more much* — while an adjective sits happily after a determiner (*the numerous errors*, *his abundant energy*), grades and compares (*more numerous*, *most abundant*), and can stand after the verb (*the errors were numerous*). *Many* is the one word that muddies the test, because it doubles as a **postdeterminer** and therefore does follow *the* (*the many problems*); it still refuses grading (✗ *more many*) and still heads the *of*-phrase (*many of them*) that none of these adjectives can. They are here because they do a large quantifier's semantic work and because the countability rules apply to them exactly as they do to the determiners: *countless* and *numerous* count and cannot take a mass noun, and *considerable* measures and cannot take an ordinary plural.

### abundant

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbʌndənt/ &middot; *uh-BUN-duhnt*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*abundant evidence*, *the abundant wildlife*) or predicative (*rainfall was abundant that spring*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in science and nature writing
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*abundant evidence*, *abundant sunshine*, *abundant rainfall*) and plural countable nouns (*abundant resources*, *abundant opportunities*). With a singular countable noun it needs an article and reads oddly unless the noun is mass-like: *an abundant harvest* works, ✗ *an abundant chair* does not.
**With *of*:** the adjective takes no *of*-phrase — ✗ *abundant of evidence*. The noun it is built on does: *an abundance of evidence*, *an abundance of options*, and that phrase is a full partitive quantifier.
**Pattern:** *abundant* [uncountable/plural countable] &middot; [the/his] *abundant* [noun] &middot; [noun] *is abundant* &middot; *an abundance of* [noun] &middot; *abundantly* [adjective/verb]
**Contrast:** Three neighbors, three different claims. *Abundant* says the supply is large and freely available; *ample* says it is large **enough for the purpose**; *plentiful* is the plain everyday word for what *abundant* says formally. Because it is an adjective it can be graded (*more abundant*, *most abundant*) and can follow a determiner (*the abundant evidence*) — two things no real determiner can do, and the quickest way to prove it is not one. The small-quantity opposites are *scarce* and [sparse](05-quantifiers-small.md#sparse).
**Variants:** *plentiful*, *an abundance of*, *abundantly*, *superabundant*, *in abundance*

Describes a supply that is large and easily had. Unlike the *of*-phrases in this file, it modifies the noun rather than heading its own phrase, which is why it can stack with a determiner: *their abundant natural resources*.

1. There is *abundant* evidence that the practice continued for years.
2. The region has *abundant* sunshine and almost no rain.
3. *Abundant* parking is the one thing that mall got right.
4. Wildflowers were *abundant* along the whole ridge.
5. The county's *abundant* water rights turned out to be worth more than the land.
6. The archive contains an *abundance of* material nobody has read.

**See also:** [ample](#ample), [copious](#copious), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [sparse](05-quantifiers-small.md#sparse)

### ample

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæmpəl/ &middot; *AM-pul*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive almost always (*ample time*, *ample parking*, *the ample evidence*); predicative use is possible but stiff &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*ample time*, *ample evidence*, *ample room*, *ample warning*) and plural countable nouns (*ample opportunities*, *ample supplies*). With a singular countable it takes an article and shifts toward the physical sense of "roomy": *an ample kitchen*.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase — ✗ *ample of time*. The idea is expressed instead by *plenty of* or *an abundance of*.
**Pattern:** *ample* [uncountable/plural countable] &middot; [the/his] *ample* [noun] &middot; *more than ample* [noun] &middot; *amply* [past participle] (*amply rewarded*)
**Contrast:** *Ample* is *plenty of* in one word: it does not merely report a large quantity, it certifies that the quantity is **enough and to spare**. That sufficiency is what separates it from *abundant* (large supply, no promise about need) and from *sufficient* (exactly enough, nothing spare). Note the second, physical sense — *an ample kitchen*, *ample proportions* — where the word means spacious rather than numerous, and be aware that applied to a person's body it is a dated euphemism.
**Variants:** *more than ample*, *amply*, *ampleness*, *plenty of*

Describes a quantity that is large enough for the purpose with room left over.

1. There's *ample* time to catch the connecting flight.
2. The lot has *ample* parking even on a game day.
3. They were given *ample* warning that the deadline would not move.
4. There is *ample* evidence in the record to support the ruling.
5. The grant gave her *ample* opportunities to travel.
6. His patience was *amply* rewarded.

**See also:** [plenty of](#plenty-of), [enough](#enough), [sufficient](#sufficient), [abundant](#abundant), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of)

### considerable

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɪdərəbəl/ &middot; *kuhn-SID-er-uh-bul* &middot; the first syllable is unstressed and reduced, /kən/, never /kɑn/
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*considerable damage*, *the considerable expense*) or predicative (*the damage was considerable*) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral — the written stand-in for *a lot of* in front of a mass noun; in speech Americans say *a lot of*
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns** above all — *considerable time*, *considerable damage*, *considerable expense*, *considerable evidence*, *considerable experience*. It reaches plural countable nouns only when the plural names an aggregate or an abstract quantity rather than a set of individual objects — *considerable resources*, *considerable sums*, *considerable delays*, *considerable difficulties* are all standard, while ordinary concrete plurals are not: ✗ *considerable chairs*, ✗ *considerable emails*, where the word you want is *numerous* or *a lot of*. With a singular countable noun it takes an article and shifts from "much" to "large": *a considerable sum*, *a considerable distance*.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase — ✗ *considerable of the damage*. To pick a part out of a determined phrase you must switch words: *much of the damage*, *a good deal of the damage*. The fixed phrase *of considerable* + noun exists as a formal predicate (*a matter of considerable importance*).
**Pattern:** *considerable* [uncountable] &middot; [the/his] *considerable* [uncountable] &middot; *a considerable* [singular noun of measure] &middot; *considerable numbers / amounts of* [noun] &middot; *considerably* [comparative] (*considerably worse*)
**Contrast:** *Considerable* is the mass half of the formal pair whose count half is [numerous](#numerous): *considerable damage* beside *numerous complaints*, and neither will take the other's noun. Against *substantial*, which suggests solidity and is the word for sums and meals; against *significant*, which in careful writing claims **importance** rather than size — *a significant delay* matters, *a considerable delay* is merely long. Being an adjective it follows a determiner (*the considerable expense*), stands after the verb (*the damage was considerable*), and has a degree adverb of its own (*considerably better*) — three things no determiner in this file can do.
**Variants:** *considerably*, *substantial*, *significant*, *appreciable*, *of considerable* [noun]

Describes a large amount of something uncountable, in the register a report or a ruling uses.

1. The storm did *considerable* damage to the pier.
2. She has *considerable* experience in labor arbitration.
3. It took *considerable* time to reconstruct the records.
4. The *considerable* expense of the repair was never in the budget.
5. They committed *considerable* resources to a project nobody wanted.
6. The second draft is *considerably* better than the first.

**See also:** [numerous](#numerous), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [much](#much), [ample](#ample), [abundant](#abundant)

### copious

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkoʊpiəs/ &middot; *KOH-pee-uhs*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*copious notes*, *her copious correspondence*); predicative is rare and bookish &middot; **Register:** formal and slightly literary; in speech Americans say *tons of* or *a lot of*
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*copious rainfall*, *copious bleeding*) and plural countable nouns (*copious notes*, *copious footnotes*). It is unusually collocation-bound: *copious notes*, *copious amounts of*, and *copious quantities of* account for most real uses.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase of its own — ✗ *copious of notes* — but it very often modifies one: *copious amounts of coffee*, *copious quantities of data*.
**Pattern:** *copious* [uncountable/plural countable] &middot; [her/the] *copious* [noun] &middot; *copious amounts of* [uncountable] &middot; *copiously* (adverb)
**Contrast:** Where *abundant* describes a supply that is there for the taking, *copious* describes **output** — what something produces or emits in volume: notes, tears, rainfall, bleeding, correspondence. That is why *copious notes* is idiomatic and *copious parking* is not. Its Latin root is the same as *copy* and *cornucopia*, and the register it carries is bookish enough that the phrase *copious amounts of* often reads as mild self-parody.
**Variants:** *copiously*, *copious amounts of*, *copious quantities of*, *voluminous*

Describes something produced or supplied in great volume.

1. She took *copious* notes throughout the deposition.
2. The season brought *copious* rainfall to the valley.
3. He left behind *copious* correspondence with three separate publishers.
4. The recipe calls for *copious* amounts of butter.
5. The manuscript is annotated *copiously* in two different hands.
6. *Copious* footnotes do not make an argument sound.

**See also:** [abundant](#abundant), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of), [ample](#ample), [masses of](#masses-of)

### countless

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaʊntləs/ &middot; *KOWNT-luhs*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive only (*countless times*, *the countless small decisions*); it has no predicative use — ✗ *the errors were countless* is at best strained &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a rhetorical lift; fine in speech and in writing
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *countless times*, *countless hours*, *countless people*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *countless information*, ✗ *countless money*, ✗ *countless damage*. The word literally means "too many to count," so it needs something countable to fail to count.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase — ✗ *countless of the applicants*. Before a determined phrase you need a different word: *many of the applicants*, *any number of the applicants*.
**Pattern:** *countless* [plural countable] &middot; [the/his] *countless* [plural noun] &middot; *countless times* / *countless hours* (the two commonest collocations)
**Contrast:** *Countless* is a rhetorical *many*: it does not report a number, it announces that the speaker declines to. *Innumerable* is its formal twin, and *untold* is the one to reach for with uncountables, since *untold damage* and *untold wealth* are idiomatic where ✗ *countless damage* is not. The informal equivalent is *umpteen*, and the hyperbolic ones are *a million* and *a zillion*.
**Variants:** *innumerable*, *numberless*, *untold (with uncountables)*, *uncounted*

Describes a number so large the speaker will not attempt it — an emphatic *many* rather than an estimate.

1. She has explained this *countless* times to *countless* people.
2. *Countless* hours went into cleaning up that database.
3. The bridge has been photographed by *countless* tourists.
4. *Countless* small decisions add up to a house that doesn't work.
5. He rewrote the opening paragraph *countless* times.
6. The storm caused *untold* damage along the coast.

**See also:** [numerous](#numerous), [myriad](#myriad), [umpteen](#umpteen), [thousands of](#thousands-of), [many](#many)

### myriad

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɪriəd/ &middot; *MIR-ee-uhd*
**Type:** adjective and noun with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive with no article at all (*myriad problems*) or as the head of a partitive phrase (*a myriad of problems*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary in both constructions
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns in both patterns — *myriad options*, *a myriad of options*. Uncountable use is rare and reads as a stretch (*myriad information* is odd). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** the two constructions split on exactly this point. Bare *myriad* takes **no** *of* — *myriad reasons*, ✗ *myriad of reasons* without an article. The noun construction **requires** both an article and *of* — *a myriad of reasons*. Both are standard, despite a long-lived complaint that the *of* version is an error; the noun use is in fact the older of the two by centuries.
**Pattern:** *myriad* [plural countable] &middot; *a myriad of* [plural countable] &middot; *myriads of* [plural countable] &middot; [the/her] *myriad* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** *Myriad* was Greek for exactly ten thousand and now means simply "very many," usually with a note of **variety** built in: *myriad options* suggests not just a lot of options but a lot of different kinds. Against *countless*, which stresses that counting is hopeless, *myriad* stresses that the set is diverse. The only live usage question is *of*, and the answer is that both forms are correct so long as the article and the *of* travel together.
**Variants:** *a myriad of*, *myriads of*, *manifold*, *multifarious*

Describes a great and varied number of countable things.

1. The city faces *myriad* problems and has money for about three of them.
2. There are *a myriad of* ways to configure the thing, all badly documented.
3. *Myriad* small streams feed into the reservoir.
4. Her essays touch on *a myriad of* subjects.
5. The reef supports *myriad* species found nowhere else.
6. *Myriads of* insects rose off the water at dusk.

**See also:** [countless](#countless), [numerous](#numerous), [a multitude of](#a-multitude-of), [a plethora of](#a-plethora-of), [many](#many)

### numerous

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnumərəs/ &middot; *NOO-mer-uhs*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*numerous errors*, *his numerous absences*) or predicative (*the errors were numerous*) &middot; **Register:** formal — the written stand-in for *many* in reports, legal writing, and journalism
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *numerous complaints*, *numerous occasions*, *numerous studies*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *numerous information*, ✗ *numerous evidence*, ✗ *numerous progress*. For uncountables the formal equivalents are *much*, [considerable](#considerable), and *a great deal of*.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase in standard American usage — ✗ *numerous of the students*. To pick a subset out of a determined phrase you must switch words: *many of the students*, *a large number of the students*.
**Pattern:** *numerous* [plural countable] &middot; [his/the] *numerous* [plural noun] &middot; [plural noun] *are numerous* &middot; *more numerous than* [phrase] &middot; *on numerous occasions*
**Contrast:** *Numerous* is *many* in a suit, and the differences are grammatical, not semantic. It is an adjective, so it follows a determiner (*his numerous absences*), grades and compares (*more numerous*, *most numerous*), and stands after the verb (*the objections were numerous*) — none of which *many* does in the same way, and *many* in turn heads an *of*-phrase that *numerous* cannot. It counts only, which rules out ✗ *numerous evidence*, and its register rules it out of casual speech: nobody says *I got numerous emails* at lunch.
**Variants:** *more numerous*, *on numerous occasions*, *numerously (rare)*, *innumerable*

Describes a large number of countable things, in a formal register.

1. The agency received *numerous* complaints about the same contractor.
2. He has been warned on *numerous* occasions.
3. *Numerous* studies have failed to replicate the original finding.
4. Her objections were *numerous* and, as it turned out, correct.
5. The bill contains *numerous* exemptions added late in the session.
6. Small farms are far more *numerous* there than the statistics suggest.

**See also:** [many](#many), [considerable](#considerable), [countless](#countless), [a large number of](#a-large-number-of), [a great many](#a-great-many), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several)

## Sufficiency: *enough* and *sufficient*

Sufficiency is a large quantity measured against a need rather than against zero. *Enough* marks the line exactly; *plenty of* and *ample* sit comfortably above it; *too much* and *too many* overshoot it; and below it lie the small quantifiers *not enough*, *too few*, and *too little*. *Enough* is also the strangest-behaving determiner in English — it is the only one that can follow its noun, and the only degree word that follows the adjective it modifies.

### enough

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnʌf/ &middot; *uh-NUF* &middot; carefully /ɪˈnʌf/; the first syllable is always reduced, never /iː/
**Type:** quantifier (sufficiency) &middot; **Position:** central determiner before the noun (*enough time*); in formal and fixed usage it may also **follow** the noun (*time enough*, *reason enough*, *money enough*), which no other determiner in English can do; as a degree adverb it follows its adjective (*good enough*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*enough chairs*, *enough volunteers*) **and** uncountable nouns (*enough time*, *enough money*). Not used with a bare singular countable noun (✗ *enough chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *enough of the food*, *enough of my time*, *enough of this*, *enough of them*. No *of* before a bare noun: *enough food*.
**Pattern:** *enough* [plural/uncountable] &middot; [noun] *enough* (formal/fixed) &middot; *enough of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *enough of* [them/it] &middot; [adjective/adverb] + *enough* &middot; *enough to* [verb] &middot; *not enough* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Enough* names the sufficiency line itself: below it are *not enough*, *too few*, and *too little*; above it are *too many* and *too much*; comfortably above it are *plenty of* and *ample*. Two structural oddities are worth memorizing. First, as a degree word it **follows** what it modifies — *warm enough*, *quickly enough*, never ✗ *enough warm* — which is the reverse of *very*, *too*, and *so*. Second, it is the only determiner that can trail its own noun in standard English (*There's time enough for that*), a survival of older word order now limited to a handful of nouns. As a pronoun it stands alone: *That's enough.*
**Variants:** *not enough*, *more than enough*, *enough to*, *enough of*, *sure enough*, *oddly enough*, *fair enough*, *enough is enough*

Marks a quantity that meets the need exactly — as much or as many as is required, and no claim beyond that. Under negation it becomes the standard way to report a shortfall: *not enough chairs*, *not enough time*.

1. Do we have *enough* chairs for everyone?
2. There isn't *enough* coffee left for a second round.
3. He's finally saved *enough* money for the deposit.
4. I've had *enough* of these delays.
5. Is it warm *enough* out there for the porch?
6. There's time *enough* to fix it before Monday.

**See also:** [sufficient](#sufficient), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [ample](#ample), [much](#much), [not much](05-quantifiers-small.md#not-much), [little](05-quantifiers-small.md#little), [enow](#enow)

### sufficient

**Pronunciation:** /səˈfɪʃənt/ &middot; *suh-FISH-uhnt*
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*sufficient evidence*, *sufficient funds*) or predicative (*the evidence was sufficient*) &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, medical, financial, and academic writing; in speech Americans say *enough*
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*sufficient evidence*, *sufficient time*, *sufficient cause*) and plural countable nouns (*sufficient funds*, *sufficient resources*, *sufficient grounds*). Not used with a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** takes no *of*-phrase — ✗ *sufficient of the evidence*; the repair is *enough of the evidence*. The abstract noun *a sufficiency of* exists but is rare and archaic.
**Pattern:** *sufficient* [uncountable/plural countable] &middot; [the] *sufficient* [noun] &middot; [noun] *is sufficient* &middot; *sufficient to* [verb] &middot; *sufficient for* [noun] &middot; *insufficient* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Sufficient* is the formal *enough*, and the grammar is where they part. *Sufficient* is an ordinary adjective: it goes **before** the noun only (✗ *evidence sufficient* outside legal set phrases), it cannot modify another adjective (✗ *sufficient warm* — *warm enough*), and it has a standard negative twin, *insufficient*, where *enough* has to borrow *not*. Note also the technical sense from logic and mathematics — *a sufficient condition* is one that guarantees a result, which has nothing to do with quantity. *Adequate* sits between the two: like *sufficient* it is formal, but it hints that the amount only barely meets the need.
**Variants:** *insufficient*, *adequate*, *sufficiently*, *a sufficiency of (archaic)*, *self-sufficient*

Describes an amount or number that meets a stated requirement — the formal register's *enough*.

1. The court found *sufficient* evidence to send the case to trial.
2. The account did not have *sufficient* funds to cover the check.
3. Allow *sufficient* time for the adhesive to cure.
4. Two witnesses were deemed *sufficient*.
5. There were *insufficient* grounds for a second search.
6. The lighting was *sufficient* for reading but not for close work.

**See also:** [enough](#enough), [ample](#ample), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [abundant](#abundant)

## The informal intensives

Hyperbole, all of it, and nearly all built on the same frame: a noun of bulk — usually plural — plus obligatory *of*. Every one takes plural countables and uncountables alike, none is literal, and the verb agrees with the noun after *of* — *tons of people are*, *tons of work is*. What separates them is geography and age. *Tons of* is the American default and *a heap of* its singular, Southern-flavored cousin; *loads of* and *masses of* are British-leaning though universally understood; *heaps of* is chiefly Australian and British; *oodles of* is dated and jocular; and *no end of* is the odd one out, because it contains a negative and means a great deal.

### a heap of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhip əv/ &middot; *uh HEEP uhv* &middot; in casual speech *of* reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈhipə/, "a heapa trouble"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal American with a Southern and rural flavor — ordinary in speech across the South and the lower Midwest, common in country lyrics and in reported dialect, and out of place in formal writing. It is the **domestic singular** and is not the same animal as the Australian and British plural [heaps of](#heaps-of)
**Goes with:** **both** uncountable nouns (*a heap of trouble*, *a heap of money*, *a heap of work*) **and** plural countable nouns (*a heap of problems*, *a heap of folks*). The literal sense is an ordinary noun and pluralizes freely: *a heap of laundry*, *two heaps of gravel*. Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *a heap of chair*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a heap trouble*. Bare *a heap* is an adverb in the same register (*I like it a heap*, *a heap better*), which is regional and dated and not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *a heap of* [uncountable] &middot; *a heap of* [plural countable] &middot; *a whole heap of* [noun] &middot; *a heap* (regional adverb: *a heap better*) &middot; compare *heaps of* (Australian and British)
**Contrast:** The singular *a heap of* is American and Southern-flavored; the plural *heaps of* is Australian and British and sounds imported in the United States — the same word split by an ocean. The intensified *a whole heap of* is the commoner of the two in speech and is almost always about trouble: *you are in a whole heap of trouble* is close to a fixed phrase. The neutral equivalents are *a lot of* and *a great deal of*; the vulgar one is *a shitload of*. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *A heap of people were waiting*, *A heap of work is left*.
**Variants:** *a whole heap of*, *heaps of*, *a heap (regional adverb)*, *a pile of*, *a mountain of*

Names a large quantity of anything, countable or not, in an informal and distinctly American register with a rural edge.

1. That boy is in *a whole heap of* trouble.
2. It'd cost *a heap of* money to put a new roof on that barn.
3. We've got *a heap of* work to do before the inspector comes.
4. *A heap of* folks around here still heat with wood.
5. She's forgotten *a heap of* things she used to know by heart.
6. There was *a heap of* laundry on the bed and nowhere to sit down.

**See also:** [heaps of](#heaps-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [a mess of](#a-mess-of), [a passel of](#a-passel-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of)

### heaps of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhips əv/ &middot; *HEEPS uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal; chiefly Australian, New Zealand, and British. Americans understand it and read it as foreign — the domestic equivalent is *tons of*
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*heaps of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*heaps of time*, *heaps of room*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *heaps people*. Bare *heaps* is a pronoun or adverb in the same dialects (*Thanks heaps*, *heaps better*), and that use does not travel to American English at all.
**Pattern:** *heaps of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *heaps of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *heaps* (pronoun/adverb, non-American)
**Contrast:** The literal *a heap of* — a physical pile — still exists (*a heap of laundry*), and the quantifier is its bleached descendant, exactly as *a pile of*, *a mountain of*, and *a stack of* are heading the same way. Within the informal group, *heaps of* and *loads of* are interchangeable in the dialects that use them; the American reflexes are *tons of* and *a bunch of*. Agreement follows the noun after *of*.
**Variants:** *a heap of*, *heaps (adverb)*, *loads of*, *tons of*

Names a large quantity of anything, countable or not, in casual non-American speech.

1. There's *heaps of* room in the back if you want to stretch out.
2. She's got *heaps of* experience with this kind of client.
3. *Heaps of* people turned up for the first session.
4. We've still got *heaps of* time before the ferry.
5. He brought *heaps of* food, most of which went home again.
6. There was a *heap of* laundry on the bed.

**See also:** [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [loads of](#loads-of), [masses of](#masses-of), [lots of](#lots-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of)

### loads of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈloʊdz əv/ &middot; *LOHDZ uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal; British-leaning but widely used and fully understood in the United States
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*loads of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*loads of money*, *loads of time*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *loads people*. Bare *loads* is a pronoun (*We've got loads*) and, in British usage, an adverb (*loads better*), which Americans generally avoid.
**Pattern:** *loads of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *loads of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *loads of* [them/it] &middot; *a load of* [noun] (often dismissive: *a load of nonsense*)
**Contrast:** *Loads of* and *lots of* differ only in bulk of imagery, and both are one step below *a lot of* in formality. The singular *a load of* has drifted somewhere else entirely: it is usually dismissive (*a load of nonsense*, *a load of trouble*) rather than merely quantitative. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *Loads of people are coming*, *Loads of work is left*.
**Variants:** *a load of*, *loads (adverb, British)*, *lots of*, *tons of*

Names a large casual quantity of anything, countable or not.

1. We've got *loads of* time before the show starts.
2. *Loads of* people said the same thing on the way out.
3. She has *loads of* patience with the new hires.
4. There were *loads of* the little green ones and none of the red.
5. That project ate *loads of* money and produced nothing.
6. He talked for an hour and it was *a load of* nonsense.

**See also:** [lots of](#lots-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [heaps of](#heaps-of), [masses of](#masses-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of)

### masses of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmæsəz əv/ &middot; *MASS-uhz uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal; chiefly British, understood but uncommon in American speech
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*masses of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*masses of paperwork*, *masses of data*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory before a noun — ✗ *masses people*. Bare *masses* is an ordinary plural noun (*the masses*, meaning ordinary people), not a quantifier.
**Pattern:** *masses of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *masses of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *a mass of* [uncountable/plural]
**Contrast:** *Masses of* stresses undifferentiated bulk — the quantity is large and shapeless, which is why it suits paperwork, data, and crowds. The singular *a mass of* is more common in American English and can be neutral or faintly negative (*a mass of contradictions*, *a mass of wires*). Do not read *the masses* as a quantifier: it is a plain noun phrase meaning ordinary people.
**Variants:** *a mass of*, *masses (pronoun)*, *loads of*, *heaps of*

Names a large, shapeless quantity of anything, countable or not.

1. There were *masses of* people on the platform by seven.
2. The move generated *masses of* paperwork.
3. She has *masses of* material she has never published.
4. *Masses of* the old records were destroyed in the flood.
5. He came home with *masses of* photographs and no notes.
6. The wall behind the rack was *a mass of* unlabeled wires.

**See also:** [loads of](#loads-of), [heaps of](#heaps-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [copious](#copious), [a lot of](#a-lot-of)

### no end of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈɛnd əv/ &middot; *noh END uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal to conversational; slightly old-fashioned but alive
**Goes with:** **both** uncountable nouns (*no end of trouble*, *no end of fun*) **and** plural countable nouns (*no end of problems*, *no end of excuses*). It strongly favors abstract nouns naming trouble or pleasure; it is odd with concrete objects (✗ *no end of chairs*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory and cannot be dropped — ✗ *no end trouble*. Note that the *no* attaches to *end*, not to the noun after *of*, which is why the phrase is not a negative.
**Pattern:** *no end of* [uncountable] &middot; *no end of* [plural countable] &middot; *cause/give/have no end of* [noun] &middot; *no end* (adverb: *it pleased her no end*)
**Contrast:** **It contains *no* and it means "a great deal of."** *That gave us no end of trouble* means a great deal of trouble, not none: the *no* negates *end*, so the phrase says the trouble was endless. Because it is positive in quantity it does not license negative polarity items — *no end of trouble at all* is odd where *no trouble at all* is fine. It is the mirror image of *no amount of*, which really is negative (*No amount of trouble would stop her*). The adverb *no end* is the same idiom (*It pleased him no end*).
**Variants:** *no end (adverb)*, *endless*, *any amount of*, *no shortage of*

Names a large and seemingly inexhaustible quantity — most often of trouble, and occasionally of pleasure.

1. That one clause caused us *no end of* trouble.
2. The kids had *no end of* fun with the box the couch came in.
3. He's given the department *no end of* headaches.
4. There is *no end of* advice available and very little of it useful.
5. *No end of* people have offered to help and none have shown up.
6. The promotion pleased her *no end*.

**See also:** [any amount of](#any-amount-of), [countless](#countless), [tons of](#tons-of), [no](06-distributives-negatives.md#no), [a host of](#a-host-of)

### oodles of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈudəlz əv/ &middot; *OO-dulz uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal and jocular; dated-cute, American in origin, used with a wink
**Goes with:** **both** uncountable nouns (*oodles of time*, *oodles of money*) **and** plural countable nouns (*oodles of cookies*). It gravitates to pleasant things — time, money, charm, fun — and sounds wrong with unpleasant ones (✗ *oodles of debt*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *oodles time*. There is no singular ✗ *an oodle*; the word exists only in this plural quantifier frame.
**Pattern:** *oodles of* [uncountable] &middot; *oodles of* [plural countable] &middot; *oodles and oodles of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Oodles of* is the most self-consciously playful member of the group. Its nearest relatives in tone are *scads of*, *gobs of*, and *bazillions of*, all American and all jocular; the neutral equivalents are *plenty of* and *a lot of*. The positive skew is real and is worth noticing: *oodles of charm* is idiomatic, *oodles of trouble* is not — for trouble the idiom is *no end of*.
**Variants:** *oodles and oodles of*, *scads of*, *gobs of*, *a boatload of*

Names a large and pleasing quantity of anything, in a deliberately light register.

1. The house has *oodles of* charm and no insulation.
2. They gave us *oodles of* time to get the paperwork in.
3. She baked *oodles of* cookies for the bake sale.
4. There's *oodles of* room in that back yard for a garden.
5. He's got *oodles of* stories about that summer.
6. *Oodles and oodles of* people signed up and about half showed.

**See also:** [tons of](#tons-of), [loads of](#loads-of), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of)

### tons of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtʌnz əv/ &middot; *TUNZ uhv*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal American — the default hyperbolic quantifier in US speech
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*tons of people*, *tons of emails*) **and** uncountable nouns (*tons of money*, *tons of work*, *tons of time*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *tons people*. The singular *a ton of* is equally common and equally figurative (*a ton of work*), and both stay before another determiner: *tons of the leftover paint*.
**Pattern:** *tons of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *a ton of* [plural/uncountable] &middot; *tons of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *tons of* [them/it] &middot; *a ton* (adverb: *it helped a ton*)
**Contrast:** This is the American member of the *heaps / loads / masses* set and by far the most frequent of them in US speech. Nothing about it is literal: *tons of time* weighs nothing, and the literal use (*two tons of gravel*) keeps a number in front and stays singular, exactly like the *dozen / hundred* alternation. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *Tons of people are coming* beside *Tons of work is left*. Register is the only thing keeping it out of formal writing, where the equivalents are *a great deal of* and *a large number of*.
**Variants:** *a ton of*, *a ton (adverb)*, *a metric ton of*, *a boatload of*, *a truckload of*

Names a large quantity of anything, countable or not, with no pretense of measurement.

1. We got *tons of* email about that segment.
2. There's *tons of* parking behind the building.
3. She's put *tons of* work into the garden this spring.
4. *Tons of* people showed up for the free clinic.
5. I have *a ton of* reading to do before Monday.
6. Thanks — that helped *a ton*.

**See also:** [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of), [loads of](#loads-of), [heaps of](#heaps-of), [lots of](#lots-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of)

## The postpositive pair: *galore* and *aplenty*

Two quantity words in American English go **after** the noun instead of in front of it. *Bargains galore*, *reasons aplenty* — the noun comes first and the quantifier trails it, which is why neither can open a noun phrase (✗ *galore bargains*, ✗ *aplenty reasons*) and why neither takes *of* (✗ *galore of bargains*). They attach to a bare plural or a bare mass noun and bring no article of their own. Both are marked: *galore* is jocular and skews to good news, *aplenty* is literary and takes trouble as happily as bargains. They are the last survivors of a word order English otherwise abandoned, and the only other determiner that can still do it is [enough](#enough) in fixed phrases like *time enough*.

### aplenty

**Pronunciation:** /əˈplɛnti/ &middot; *uh-PLEN-tee* &middot; the first syllable is always the weak /ə/, never /eɪ/; the *nt* commonly reduces in American speech to /əˈplɛni/, "a-plenny"
**Type:** quantifier, **postpositive** &middot; **Position:** **after** the noun it quantifies — *reasons aplenty*, *food aplenty* — and never before it: ✗ *aplenty reasons*. It attaches to a bare noun and brings no determiner slot of its own &middot; **Register:** literary and a little archaic; alive in journalism reaching for a flourish, in sports writing, and in travel and cookbook prose. Nobody says it at a kitchen table. The hyphenated *a-plenty* is the older spelling and now reads as period or country-music styling
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*reasons aplenty*, *bargains aplenty*) and uncountable nouns (*food aplenty*, *trouble aplenty*, *rain aplenty*). Never a singular countable noun: ✗ *a reason aplenty*.
**With *of*:** it never takes *of* — ✗ *aplenty of food*. The *of*-phrase equivalent is *plenty of*, which is the same word in the ordinary prenominal position: *plenty of food* = *food aplenty*.
**Pattern:** [plural or uncountable noun] + *aplenty* &middot; *there is / there are* [noun] *aplenty* &middot; compare prenominal *plenty of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Aplenty* is *plenty of* turned around, and the reversal is the whole difference: *plenty of food* is neutral reporting, *food aplenty* is a flourish. Its fellow postpositive is *galore*, which is louder, more jocular, and mostly reserved for welcome things, where *aplenty* is happy to quantify trouble. Neither may precede its noun and neither takes *of*. The old spelling *a-plenty* preserves the *a-* prefix the word is built on, the same one in *afire*, *asleep*, and *abed*.
**Variants:** *a-plenty*, *plenty of*, *galore*, *in plenty*

Marks a large and sufficient quantity, tacked onto the end of the noun for effect.

1. There were reasons *aplenty* to walk away from that deal.
2. The estate sale had bargains *aplenty* and nowhere to park.
3. That summer brought rain *aplenty* and not one good tomato.
4. He has stories *aplenty* about the years before the mill closed.
5. The season gave the team trouble *aplenty* and one memorable night in October.
6. In the older spelling the fiddler promised music *a-plenty* and dancing until dawn.

**See also:** [galore](#galore), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [enough](#enough), [ample](#ample), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of)

### galore

**Pronunciation:** /ɡəˈlɔr/ &middot; *guh-LOR* &middot; stress on the second syllable; the first is the weak /ɡə/, never /ɡeɪ/
**Type:** quantifier, **postpositive** &middot; **Position:** **after** the noun it quantifies — *bargains galore*, *whiskey galore* — and never before it: ✗ *galore bargains*. It attaches to a bare noun and does not combine with a determiner of its own: ✗ *the bargains galore* &middot; **Register:** informal and jocular; a headline, advertising, menu, and flyer word. Common in American English, but always with a wink — a store advertises *savings galore*, an annual report does not
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*bargains galore*, *prizes galore*, *options galore*) and uncountable nouns (*fun galore*, *whiskey galore*). Never a singular countable noun: ✗ *a prize galore*. It strongly prefers **welcome** things; *problems galore* is possible but lands as sarcasm.
**With *of*:** it never takes *of* — ✗ *galore of bargains*. The prenominal equivalents are *plenty of*, *lots of*, and *tons of*.
**Pattern:** [plural or uncountable noun] + *galore* &middot; *there are* [noun] *galore* &middot; compare prenominal *lots of* / *plenty of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Galore* is the loudest of the postpositives and the only common American quantifier borrowed from Irish — *go leor*, "enough" — which is also why the stress sits where it does. Against *aplenty*, which is literary and neutral about what it counts, *galore* is playful and skews to good news. The rule to memorize is positional: **it goes after the noun**, and a learner who writes ✗ *galore opportunities* has made the one error the word invites. Compton Mackenzie's *Whisky Galore* and the film made from it are where many readers meet the word first.
**Variants:** *aplenty*, *plenty of*, *lots of*, *in abundance*

Marks a large and pleasing quantity, tacked onto the end of the noun.

1. The flyer promised bargains *galore* and delivered a parking lot of folding tables.
2. There are opportunities *galore* for anyone willing to move.
3. The buffet had shrimp *galore* and nothing else worth the trip.
4. October brings tourists, traffic, and pumpkin bread *galore*.
5. The rewrite fixed the plot and left us with typos *galore*.
6. He came home from the fair with prizes *galore* and no money.

**See also:** [aplenty](#aplenty), [plenty of](#plenty-of), [tons of](#tons-of), [oodles of](#oodles-of), [a wealth of](#a-wealth-of)

## Regional, dialect, and slang quantifiers

Every word in this section means "a lot," and not one of them is neutral. Some belong to a place: *a passel of* and *a right smart of* to Appalachia and the Upper South, *a mess of* and *a sight of* and *a power of* to Southern and older country speech, *hella* to Northern California, *mad* to New York City. One belongs to a group — *beaucoup* came home from the wars with American servicemen and still travels with them. One, *a shitload of*, is obscene, and its entry says where it must never go. They are catalogued because Americans really do meet them, in conversation, in lyrics, in fiction, and in quoted speech, and because the **Register** line in each entry is the load-bearing one: using a regional form outside its home is not a style choice but a costume, and none of these belongs in writing that is not quoting somebody. Grammatically they are mostly ordinary partitive phrases with obligatory *of*; *beaucoup*, *hella*, and *mad* are the exceptions that sit bare in the determiner slot with no article and no *of* at all.

### a mess of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmɛs əv/ &middot; *uh MESS uhv* &middot; casually /ə ˈmɛsə/, "a messa"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **regional American** — Southern, Appalachian, and rural Midwestern. Ordinary in speech there and instantly marked as dialect anywhere else, though it is understood nationally from fiction, film, and food writing
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*a mess of catfish*, *a mess of kids*) **and** uncountable nouns (*a mess of trouble*, *a mess of money*). It has one specialized use that is not a quantifier at all but a food term: *a mess of greens*, *a mess of beans*, *a mess of fish* names as much as gets cooked and put on the table at one meal, and *mess* there is an ordinary countable noun (*two messes of collards*). Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *a mess of chair*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a mess trouble*. It stays before another determiner: *a mess of the paperwork*.
**Pattern:** *a mess of* [plural countable] &middot; *a mess of* [uncountable] &middot; *a whole mess of* [noun] &middot; *a mess of* [greens / beans / fish] (the food sense)
**Contrast:** Do not read the ordinary noun *mess* — disorder, a bad state — into the quantifier: *a mess of kids* means a lot of children, not badly behaved ones, and *a whole mess of money* is a compliment to the sum. The food sense descends from the same *mess* as *mess hall*, a portion served out, and is the older of the two. Within the region *a mess of*, *a passel of*, and *a heap of* are close neighbors; the national equivalents are *a bunch of* and *a lot of*.
**Variants:** *a whole mess of*, *a passel of*, *a heap of*, *messes of (food sense)*

A large quantity of anything, in Southern and Appalachian speech — and, in the kitchen, as much of something as feeds the table.

1. He caught *a mess of* crappie off the dock before breakfast.
2. Mama cooked *a mess of* greens and a pan of cornbread.
3. That truck is going to cost you *a whole mess of* money.
4. They've got *a mess of* grandkids running around that house.
5. You're fixing to buy yourself *a mess of* trouble.
6. There's *a mess of* paperwork to get through before any of that can happen.

**See also:** [a passel of](#a-passel-of), [a right smart of](#a-right-smart-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [a sight of](#a-sight-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of)

### a passel of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈpæsəl əv/ &middot; *uh PASS-ul uhv* &middot; casually /ə ˈpæsələ/, "a passela"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **regional American** — Appalachian, Southern, and Western. It is also a stock ingredient of folksy and frontier writing, where it tends to be laid on thick. Understood nationally, produced regionally, and never neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns above all, and especially people and animals — *a passel of kids*, *a passel of hound dogs*, *a passel of lawyers*. Uncountables occur (*a passel of trouble*) but the plural is the norm. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a passel kids*. It stays before another determiner: *a passel of them*.
**Pattern:** *a passel of* [plural countable] &middot; *a whole passel of* [plural] &middot; *a passel of* [the/them + plural]
**Contrast:** *Passel* is a worn-down pronunciation of *parcel*, and the two are one word with two histories: the standard spelling kept the contempt and lives on in [a parcel of](#a-parcel-of) *lies*, while the dialect spelling kept the quantity and went into American country speech. Its neighbors *a mess of* and *a heap of* cover the same ground; what marks *a passel of* off from them is that it nearly always counts people or animals. Use it in dialogue only, and know that laying it on is the standard tell of a writer imitating a region they did not grow up in.
**Variants:** *a whole passel of*, *a parcel of*, *a mess of*, *a slew of*

A large number of countable things — usually children, relatives, or animals — in Appalachian, Southern, and Western American speech.

1. They came into town with *a passel of* kids and a truck full of furniture.
2. There's *a whole passel of* cousins on her mother's side.
3. *A passel of* hound dogs came around the side of the house.
4. The company sent *a passel of* lawyers to a hearing that lasted twelve minutes.
5. He'd been through *a passel of* jobs before he settled on the mail route.
6. We've got *a passel of* work to do before dark.

**See also:** [a mess of](#a-mess-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [a right smart of](#a-right-smart-of), [a parcel of](#a-parcel-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of)

### a power of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈpaʊɚ əv/ &middot; *uh POW-er uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈpaʊɚə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **dialectal and old-fashioned** — Southern and Appalachian American, and the Irish and British country speech the American use descends from. In present-day American English it survives almost entirely inside the fixed phrase *a power of good*; anything past that reads as period writing or as dialect
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns above all — *a power of good*, *a power of money*, *a power of trouble*, *a power of work*. Plural countables are rare and strictly dialectal (*a power of people*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a power good*. Keep it clear of the ordinary noun *power* taking a complement, where *power* is the real head and means authority: *the power of the press*, *a power of attorney*, *the power of the purse*.
**Pattern:** *do* [someone] *a power of good* (the surviving fixed phrase) &middot; *a power of* [uncountable] &middot; *a power of* [the + uncountable]
**Contrast:** The living American equivalent is *a world of*: *it did him a world of good* says today what *a power of good* says in an older or more rural voice. Against its regional neighbors *a sight of* and *a heap of*, *a power of* is the one most likely to have been learned from a grandparent rather than from a neighbor. Do not confuse it with *a power of attorney*, which is a legal instrument and not a quantity, or with *the powers that be*.
**Variants:** *a power of good*, *a world of*, *a sight of*, *a heap of*

A great deal of something uncountable, in older rural American and Irish-descended speech — now chiefly alive in *a power of good*.

1. A week at the lake would do you *a power of* good.
2. That woman has forgotten *a power of* things most people never learned.
3. It took *a power of* work to get the bottom field cleared that first year.
4. "He's spent *a power of* money on that house," she said, "and it still leans."
5. The rain that spring did *a power of* good and a fair bit of damage besides.
6. In today's American English the living version of the phrase is *a world of* good.

**See also:** [a sight of](#a-sight-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [a mess of](#a-mess-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of), [a deal of](#a-deal-of)

### a right smart of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌraɪt ˈsmɑrt əv/ &middot; *uh ryt SMART uhv* &middot; *right* here is the intensifying adverb "very" and is unstressed; the phrase carries its main stress on *smart*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **regional American — Appalachian and Upper South**, chiefly Virginia, West Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, and the Carolinas. Ordinary in older speech there, unknown to most Americans elsewhere, and never written outside dialogue and dialect reporting
**Goes with:** **both** uncountable nouns (*a right smart of money*, *a right smart of work*, *a right smart of snow*) **and** plural countable nouns (*a right smart of people*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory in the quantifier — ✗ *a right smart money*. Bare *a right smart* with no *of* is the pronoun or the adverb in the same dialects: *We got a right smart done today*, *It rained right smart last night*.
**Pattern:** *a right smart of* [uncountable] &middot; *a right smart of* [plural countable] &middot; *a right smart* (adverb: *it hurt right smart*) &middot; *right smart* [adjective] (regional: *right smart cold*)
**Contrast:** *Smart* here has nothing to do with intelligence: it is the older sense "sharp, brisk, considerable," the one still preserved in *a smart pace* and in *smarting*. *Right* is the regional intensifier "very," the same one in *right nice*, *right quick*, and *right smart cold*. The phrase is one of the most reliable regional markers in American English and outside its home a listener will simply not follow it, which is reason enough to keep it inside quotation marks.
**Variants:** *right smart*, *a right smart bit of*, *a heap of*, *a sight of*

A considerable quantity or number, in Appalachian and Upper South speech.

1. They paid *a right smart of* money for that bottom land.
2. There was *a right smart of* snow on the ridge road.
3. "We've got *a right smart of* work left," he said, looking at the barn.
4. *A right smart of* people turned out for the funeral.
5. She's put up *a right smart of* beans and tomatoes this summer.
6. It rained *right smart* all through the night.

**See also:** [a mess of](#a-mess-of), [a passel of](#a-passel-of), [a sight of](#a-sight-of), [a power of](#a-power-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of)

### a shitload of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈʃɪtˌloʊd əv/ &middot; *uh SHIT-lohd uhv* &middot; casually /ə ˈʃɪtloʊdə/, "a shitloada"
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **vulgar slang** — obscene, and common in American casual speech among adults who know each other, in comedy, in fiction, and online. **Never in**: any classroom, any workplace where you are not certain of the room, customer-facing speech, a job interview, a court filing, an academic paper, a business email, or a first conversation with anybody. The softened *a crapload of* and *a buttload of* are mildly vulgar rather than obscene and clear some of those bars but none of the formal ones; *a boatload of* and *a truckload of* are not vulgar at all and are the safe way to say the same thing
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*a shitload of emails*, *a shitload of people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*a shitload of money*, *a shitload of work*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a shitload money*. The plural *shitloads of* exists and is equally obscene. It stays before another determiner: *a shitload of the paperwork*.
**Pattern:** *a shitload of* [plural countable / uncountable] &middot; *shitloads of* [noun] &middot; *a whole shitload of* [noun] &middot; non-vulgar substitutes: *a boatload of*, *a truckload of*, *a ton of*
**Contrast:** The whole family is built on *-load*, and nothing but register separates its members: *a boatload of* and *a truckload of* are neutral, *a crapload of* and *a buttload of* are mild, *a shitload of* is obscene, and all of them take countables and uncountables alike and all of them mean *a lot of*. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *A shitload of people were there*, *A shitload of work is left*. If there is any doubt about the room, the sentence works exactly as well with *a ton of*.
**Variants:** *a crapload of*, *a buttload of*, *shitloads of*, *a whole shitload of*, *a boatload of (not vulgar)*, *a truckload of (not vulgar)*

A very large quantity of anything, countable or not, in vulgar American slang.

1. We got *a shitload of* email about that episode.
2. There's still *a shitload of* work left on the second floor.
3. He spent *a crapload of* money on a car he drives twice a month.
4. *A buttload of* people showed up and we had thirty chairs.
5. That decision cost the company *a shitload of* money and two vice presidents.
6. In the memo version of the same sentence it becomes *a great deal of*, or at worst *a ton of*.

**See also:** [tons of](#tons-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [loads of](#loads-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a bunch of](#a-bunch-of)

### a sight of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsaɪt əv/ &middot; *uh SYT uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/ and the *t* flaps: /ə ˈsaɪɾə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **regional and old-fashioned American** — Southern, Appalachian, and older rural New England, and British dialect before that. Alive chiefly in the speech of older speakers; the piece of it most Americans still hear is the adverbial *a sight better*
**Goes with:** **both** uncountable nouns (*a sight of money*, *a sight of trouble*) **and** plural countable nouns (*a sight of people*). Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory in the quantifier — ✗ *a sight money*. Bare *a sight* is the commoner survival and is an **adverb** of degree in front of a comparative: *a sight better*, *a sight more sense*, *a durn sight worse*. That use is not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *a sight of* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; *a sight* [comparative] (adverb: *a sight better*) &middot; *a durn / dang sight* [comparative] &middot; compare the ordinary noun in *not a sight of him*
**Contrast:** Two different things wear this spelling. The quantifier means "a great deal of" and is regional; the ordinary noun means a view or a glimpse, so *we never got a sight of him* is that noun under a negative and means the opposite of a lot. The adverbial *a sight better* is the part of the idiom still heard nationally. Its regional neighbors are *a power of* and *a heap of*; the neutral equivalent is *a great deal of*.
**Variants:** *a sight better (adverb)*, *a durn sight*, *a power of*, *a heap of*

A great deal of something, countable or not, in older regional American speech.

1. That fence cost him *a sight of* money and half a summer.
2. There was *a sight of* people at the courthouse that morning.
3. "You'll save yourself *a sight of* trouble if you call first," she said.
4. He knows *a sight of* things about that river that nobody ever wrote down.
5. The second doctor was *a sight* better than the first.
6. We waited two hours and never got *a sight of* the parade.

**See also:** [a power of](#a-power-of), [a heap of](#a-heap-of), [a mess of](#a-mess-of), [a right smart of](#a-right-smart-of), [a great deal of](#a-great-deal-of)

### beaucoup

**Pronunciation:** /ˈboʊku/ &middot; *BOH-koo* &middot; also /ˌbuˈku/, *boo-KOO*, which is the pronunciation behind the eye-spellings *boocoo* and *boo-koo*; the French original is /boˈku/
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central and **bare** — it fills the determiner slot with no article in front of it and no *of* after it: *beaucoup bucks*, never ✗ *a beaucoup of bucks* &middot; **Register:** **slang, borrowed from French**. It entered American English through servicemen stationed in France and came home again from Vietnam, and it is still strongly associated with the military, with veterans, and with Southern speech. Jocular everywhere else, and not written outside dialogue and casual prose
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*beaucoup problems*, *beaucoup people*) **and** uncountable nouns (*beaucoup money*, *beaucoup trouble*). The commonest collocations are about money — *beaucoup bucks*, *beaucoup dollars*. Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *beaucoup problem*.
**With *of*:** it takes **no** *of* — ✗ *beaucoup of money* — and no article. This is where the French original and the American slang part company: French *beaucoup de* requires its preposition, and a speaker who carries it over produces ✗ *beaucoup of*, which is not the English idiom.
**Pattern:** *beaucoup* [plural countable] &middot; *beaucoup* [uncountable] &middot; *beaucoup bucks* (the stock phrase) &middot; compare French *beaucoup de* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Beaucoup* belongs to the small set of American quantifiers that sit bare in the determiner slot with no article and no *of* — the slang *hella* and *mad* and the standard *many* and *much*. Register is the thing to get right: in the mouth of a veteran it is unremarkable, in a marketing deck it is a joke, and in a report it is an error. The eye-spellings *boocoo* and *boo-koo* are common in American dialogue and are the same word.
**Variants:** *boocoo*, *boo-koo*, *beaucoup bucks*, *hella*, *mad*

A lot of anything, countable or not, in American slang borrowed from French and carried home by the military.

1. That house out on the point cost *beaucoup* bucks.
2. We had *beaucoup* problems with the first shipment.
3. He put in *beaucoup* hours that year and never saw a dime of overtime.
4. There were *beaucoup* mosquitoes and not a breath of wind.
5. "They gave us *beaucoup* paperwork and about ten minutes to fill it out."
6. English drops the preposition the French keeps: *beaucoup* money, never ✗ *beaucoup of* money.

**See also:** [hella](#hella), [mad](#mad), [tons of](#tons-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a shitload of](#a-shitload-of)

### hella

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɛlə/ &middot; *HELL-uh* &middot; the second syllable is the weak /ə/; there is no /r/ in it
**Type:** quantifier, and a degree adverb in its other job &middot; **Position:** as a quantifier, central and **bare** — no article, no *of*: *hella people*, *hella money*. As a degree adverb it precedes an adjective or another adverb (*hella good*, *hella fast*), which is not a determiner use at all &middot; **Register:** **regional American slang — Northern California**. It was born in the Bay Area and spread nationally through music and the internet without losing its accent: in the Bay Area it is ordinary casual speech, elsewhere it is either borrowed or performed, and older speakers everywhere hear it as teenage. Not used in writing outside dialogue and social media
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*hella people*, *hella cars*) **and** uncountable nouns (*hella money*, *hella traffic*, *hella work*). Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *hella car*.
**With *of*:** it takes **no** *of* — ✗ *hella of people* — and no article: ✗ *a hella people*.
**Pattern:** *hella* [plural countable] &middot; *hella* [uncountable] &middot; *hella* [adjective] (degree adverb: *hella tired*) &middot; *hecka* [noun / adjective] (the euphemistic form)
**Contrast:** *Hella* is a clipping of *hell of a*, and the clipping is what freed it from the article: *a hell of a lot of people* collapsed into *hella people*. It does two jobs and only one of them belongs in this file — before a noun it is a quantifier (*hella cars*), before an adjective it is an intensifier (*hella expensive*). The softened *hecka* is the same word with the profanity filed off and is what gets said in school. Its East Coast counterpart is *mad*; the neutral equivalent everywhere is *a lot of*.
**Variants:** *hecka*, *hella (intensifier: hella good)*, *hell of a*, *mad*

A lot of anything, countable or not, in Northern California slang.

1. There were *hella* people at Ocean Beach on Saturday.
2. That parking ticket cost me *hella* money.
3. We sat in *hella* traffic on the bridge for an hour.
4. She's got *hella* work due before finals.
5. My little brother says *hecka* because our mom won't let him say the other one.
6. The show was *hella* good, which is the same word doing the intensifier's job.

**See also:** [mad](#mad), [beaucoup](#beaucoup), [tons of](#tons-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [a hell of a, one hell of a, one heck of a](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#a-hell-of-a-one-hell-of-a-one-heck-of-a)

### mad

**Pronunciation:** /mæd/ &middot; *MAD* &middot; identical to the ordinary adjective *mad*; only the grammar tells them apart
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central and **bare** — no article, no *of*: *mad people*, *mad respect*. It cannot follow a determiner in this sense, and the moment one appears the meaning flips: *the mad people* is about insanity, not about numbers &middot; **Register:** **regional American slang — New York City**, out of hip-hop and Black and Latino youth speech of the 1980s and 1990s and spread from there. Still strongly New York-flavored. Casual speech only; nothing about it belongs in writing that is not quoting somebody
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*mad people*, *mad cars*) **and** uncountable nouns (*mad money*, *mad respect*, *mad love*, *mad traffic*). Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *mad car*.
**With *of*:** it takes **no** *of* — ✗ *mad of people* — and no article: ✗ *a mad people*.
**Pattern:** *mad* [plural countable] &middot; *mad* [uncountable] &middot; *mad respect / mad love / mad props* (the stock collocations) &middot; *mad* [adjective] (intensifier: *mad cool*)
**Contrast:** This is not the adjective. *He has mad skills* means a great many skills; *he has a mad look* means something else entirely, and the article is the giveaway, because the quantifier refuses one. Watch the collision with the older national idiom *mad money*, which means cash set aside for an emergency or a splurge and says nothing about quantity — only context separates the two. Its West Coast counterpart is *hella*, and like *hella* it also works as an intensifier before an adjective (*mad cool*), which is not a determiner use.
**Variants:** *mad props*, *mad respect*, *mad love*, *mad (intensifier: mad cool)*, *hella*

A lot of anything, countable or not, in New York City slang.

1. There were *mad* people outside the venue by eight o'clock.
2. I've got *mad* respect for what she pulled off with that budget.
3. He's been putting in *mad* hours since the promotion.
4. There's *mad* traffic on the expressway every afternoon.
5. The crowd gave the opening act *mad* love.
6. Mind the article: *mad* skills is a great deal of skill, and a *mad* scientist is something else.

**See also:** [hella](#hella), [beaucoup](#beaucoup), [tons of](#tons-of), [a lot of](#a-lot-of), [umpteen](#umpteen)

## Archaic and literary quantifiers

These are the large-quantity forms an American meets in reading rather than in conversation — in the hymnal and the King James Bible, in Gray and FitzGerald and Poe, in Scott and Dickens, in a nineteenth-century deed or deposition. Not one of them is current. *Enow* is dead outside quotation. *Full many a* and *many and many a* are verse intensifications of [many a](#many-a) and belong to poetry. *A parcel of* survives only as an insult. *Many's the* is the one item here that living American speakers still produce, in a deliberately old-fashioned and reminiscent voice. They are catalogued because reading requires them, and because a learner who meets *Paradise enow* deserves better than a guess — but write none of them in your own prose unless you are writing period dialogue.

### a parcel of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈpɑrsəl əv/ &middot; *uh PAR-sul uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈpɑrsələ/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic**, and wherever it survives, **contemptuous**. In present-day American English it is alive only in the dismissive frame *a parcel of lies*, *a parcel of fools*, *a parcel of nonsense*, and even there it sounds like a quotation. Its country descendant [a passel of](#a-passel-of) is the living form of the same word
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns naming people held in contempt (*a parcel of rogues*, *a parcel of fools*, *a parcel of idlers*) and uncountable nouns naming falsehood or foolishness (*a parcel of lies*, *a parcel of nonsense*). It is not used of neutral things: *a parcel of chairs* reads as the literal noun, a wrapped package of them.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a parcel lies*. The literal noun is a separate and thoroughly current word — *a parcel of land*, *a parcel from the post office* — and there *parcel* pluralizes freely and carries no scorn.
**Pattern:** *a parcel of* [rogues / fools / lies / nonsense] &middot; *a whole parcel of* [noun] &middot; compare literal *a parcel of land*
**Contrast:** *Parcel* and *passel* are one word split in two: the standard spelling kept the contempt, the dialect spelling kept the quantity. Burns fixed the phrase in English with "such a parcel of rogues in a nation," and nearly every modern use is echoing him or the register he wrote in. Where a present-day writer wants the same scorn, the living idiom is *a pack of lies* — which is exactly what *a parcel of lies* turned into.
**Variants:** *a passel of*, *a pack of (lies)*, *a whole parcel of*

A collection of people or claims the speaker holds in contempt — archaic, and never neutral.

1. "We're bought and sold for English gold — such *a parcel of* rogues in a nation."
2. The whole defense was *a parcel of* lies and the jury knew it within an hour.
3. He called the commissioners *a parcel of* fools and was removed from the room.
4. The pamphlet is *a parcel of* nonsense dressed up in footnotes.
5. An 1840s witness dismissed the whole claim as *a parcel of* idle talk.
6. Today the same insult comes out as *a pack of* lies.

**See also:** [a passel of](#a-passel-of), [a plethora of](#a-plethora-of), [a host of](#a-host-of), [a deal of](#a-deal-of), [a mess of](#a-mess-of)

### enow

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnaʊ/ &middot; *uh-NOW* &middot; it rhymes with *now*, not with *know*, and the first syllable is the weak /ə/ exactly as in *enough*
**Type:** quantifier (sufficiency) &middot; **Position:** before its noun (*enow provisions*) or, far more often in verse, **after** it — *Paradise enow*, *gold enow* — exactly as *enough* once could and still can in a few fixed phrases &middot; **Register:** **archaic**. It is the old northern English and Scots form of *enough*, dead in American English since the nineteenth century and alive now only in quotation — above all in FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*, which is where nearly every American who knows the word learned it
**Goes with:** **both** plural countable nouns (*friends enow*, *books enow*) **and** uncountable nouns (*gold enow*, *trouble enow*). Historically *enow* was the plural-countable form and *enough* the mass form — *books enow* beside *bread enough* — the same countability split that separates *many* from *much*; but the distinction had broken down before the word died, and the surviving quotations do not observe it.
**With *of*:** in the surviving quotations it takes no *of*-phrase. Its modern replacement does: *enough of the money*, *enough of them*.
**Pattern:** [noun] + *enow* (postpositive, the usual order in verse) &middot; *enow* [noun] &middot; compare current [enough](#enough)
**Contrast:** *Enow* is not a separate word to learn but the ancestor of one: read it as *enough* every time and the line will make sense. Its historical job — countable plural against mass *enough* — is the only place in English where sufficiency was ever divided the way *many* and *much* divide quantity, and nothing of it survives. Modern American English has no trace of the form at all: write *enough*.
**Variants:** *enough*, *anow*, *enow (Scots and northern English)*

The archaic form of *enough* — a sufficient or plentiful quantity, met today only inside quotation marks.

1. "A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou / Beside me singing in the Wilderness — / Oh, Wilderness were Paradise *enow*!"
2. The old ballad promises gold *enow* to any man who dares the crossing.
3. He had friends *enow* while the money lasted.
4. "There is trouble *enow* in this house without yours," the housekeeper said.
5. A nineteenth-century hymn asks whether grace is *enow* for the day.
6. Every one of those sentences takes *enough* in modern English.

**See also:** [enough](#enough), [sufficient](#sufficient), [aplenty](#aplenty), [ample](#ample), [plenty of](#plenty-of)

### full many a

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʊl ˈmɛni ə/ &middot; *fool MEN-ee uh* &middot; *full* here has the vowel of *pull*, /fʊl/, and is unstressed; the phrase carries its stress on *many*, and the article stays the weak /ə/
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** predeterminer — the article *a* is locked inside the phrase, so nothing may precede it: ✗ *the full many a gem* &middot; **Register:** **archaic and poetic**. It is a verse intensification of [many a](#many-a), at home in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English poetry, and the line most Americans have met it in is Gray's *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard*. No living register produces it
**Goes with:** **singular** countable nouns only, exactly as *many a* does — *full many a gem*, *full many a flower*, *full many a time*. Never a plural (✗ *full many a gems*) and never an uncountable noun. The singular runs through the whole clause: *Full many a flower **is** born to blush unseen.*
**With *of*:** it never takes *of* — ✗ *full many a of them*. Before a vowel sound the article changes as usual: *full many an hour*.
**Pattern:** *full many a* [singular countable] + **singular** verb &middot; *full many an* [vowel-initial singular noun]
**Contrast:** *Full* here is the old adverb meaning "very," the same one preserved in *full well* (*you know full well*) and in the archaic *full sore*; it is not the adjective, and it never agrees with anything. Strip it off and what remains is plain *many a*, which is formal but alive. Its companion intensification is *many and many a*, which repeats rather than amplifies. Both belong to verse, and in modern prose both read as pastiche.
**Variants:** *many a*, *many and many a*, *full well (the surviving adverb)*

A poetic intensification of *many a* — a great number of things, considered one at a time.

1. "*Full many a* gem of purest ray serene / The dark unfathom'd caves of ocean bear."
2. "*Full many a* flower is born to blush unseen, / And waste its sweetness on the desert air."
3. The hymn recalls *full many a* year of wandering before the promise was kept.
4. *Full many an* evening had ended the same way in that house.
5. *Full many a* traveler had turned back at the pass, and the guide could name them all.
6. Strip the old adverb off and the living phrase remains: *many a* traveler turned back.

**See also:** [many a](#many-a), [many and many a](#many-and-many-a), [many's the](#manys-the), [many](#many), [enow](#enow)

### many and many a

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛni ən ˈmɛni ə/ &middot; *MEN-ee uhn MEN-ee uh* &middot; the linking *and* is always the weak /ən/ or a bare /n/, never the full /ænd/; the second *many* carries the main stress
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** predeterminer, with the article *a* locked inside the phrase — nothing may precede it &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary**; a reduplication used in nineteenth-century verse and storytelling for emphasis and rhythm. Poe's "It was many and many a year ago, / In a kingdom by the sea" is the line that keeps it in circulation. Living speakers produce it only in a deliberately storybook voice
**Goes with:** **singular** countable nouns only — *many and many a year*, *many and many a time*, *many and many a night* — with a strong preference for nouns of time. Plural and uncountable nouns are both impossible: ✗ *many and many a years*, ✗ *many and many a money*. Singular agreement runs through the clause.
**With *of*:** it never takes *of* — ✗ *many and many a of them*. Before a vowel sound the article changes: *many and many an hour*.
**Pattern:** *many and many a* [singular countable, usually a time word] + **singular** verb &middot; *many and many an* [vowel-initial noun] &middot; compare plain *many a* and intensified *full many a*
**Contrast:** The three members of the family differ only in weight. *Many a night* is formal but current; *full many a night* is poetic and amplified; *many and many a night* is poetic and **repeated**, and the repetition does as much work for "again and again" as for "a great number." It is the most sing-song of the three and the least usable in prose. The doubling is the same pattern behind *again and again* and *round and round*.
**Variants:** *many a*, *full many a*, *many and many a time*, *many's the*

An emphatic, repeated *many a* — a great many occasions, one after another, in a storytelling voice.

1. "It was *many and many a* year ago, / In a kingdom by the sea."
2. *Many and many a* night he sat up with the lamp burning.
3. The old woman had told that story *many and many a* time.
4. *Many and many an* hour was lost at that crossing before the bridge went in.
5. *Many and many a* traveler passed that way and left nothing behind.
6. In plain modern English the sentence says simply *many* nights.

**See also:** [many a](#many-a), [full many a](#full-many-a), [many's the](#manys-the), [many](#many), [umpteen](#umpteen)

### many's the

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɛniz ðə/ &middot; *MEN-eez thuh* &middot; the contracted *'s* is /z/ and the article is the weak /ðə/, becoming /ði/ only before a vowel
**Type:** quantifier in a fixed clause frame &middot; **Position:** predeterminer inside a **fronted** noun phrase: the phrase opens the sentence and a relative clause completes it — *Many's the night I fell asleep at that desk*. It cannot appear anywhere else in the sentence &middot; **Register:** **old-fashioned but not dead** — still produced by living American speakers reaching for a folksy, reminiscent tone, and common in older fiction and in Irish and Irish-American speech. It is the one form in this section you might actually hear said out loud
**Goes with:** **singular** countable nouns only — *many's the night*, *many's the time*, *many's the man*, *many's the story* — with nouns of time dominating. Never a plural (✗ *many's the nights*) and never an uncountable noun.
**With *of*:** it never takes *of*. The frame instead requires a clause: *many's the* + singular noun + a relative clause, with the relative pronoun usually left out — *Many's the time I have wondered* rather than *Many's the time that I have wondered*.
**Pattern:** *many's the* [singular countable] + [relative clause] &middot; *many's the man who* … &middot; *many's the time* [subject + verb] &middot; compare *many a* [singular noun] in ordinary subject position
**Contrast:** The frame is an inversion. It means *there have been many nights when* …, with the quantity pushed to the front as a predicate, and that is why the sentence cannot continue as an ordinary clause: ✗ *Many's the night was cold* is wrong, because *many's the night* is not the subject of what follows. Plain *many a night* is an ordinary subject (*Many a night was cold*) and can sit anywhere in the sentence, where *many's the* can only open one.
**Variants:** *many a*, *many's the time*, *many's the man*, *many and many a*

There have been a great many occasions of the kind named — a fronted, reminiscent way of saying *many a*.

1. *Many's the* night I fell asleep at that desk.
2. *Many's the* time she warned him about that road in the winter.
3. *Many's the* man who has lost a boat in that channel.
4. *Many's the* story that came out of the mill and never made the paper.
5. *Many's the* dollar the two of them spent trying to keep it running.
6. The ordinary version is flatter and says the same thing: *many a* night I fell asleep there.

**See also:** [many a](#many-a), [many](#many), [full many a](#full-many-a), [many and many a](#many-and-many-a), [a good many](#a-good-many)

## Legal and technical quantity terms

Three of the four phrases here name a **threshold** rather than an impression. A plurality, a quorum, and a supermajority are counted, and what counts as one is fixed somewhere else — in a statute, a charter, a set of bylaws — so the word tells you that a line exists without telling you where it is. That is what makes them technical rather than merely formal, and it is why using one loosely, as a dressed-up *many*, misleads a reader who knows the field. The fourth, *so much of … as*, is not a threshold but a drafting frame: it is how legal English says "the part of X that." All four take *of*, all four live in statutes, bylaws, minutes, and the reporting that quotes them, and outside that world most Americans meet them on election night.

### a plurality of

**Pronunciation:** /ə plʊˈræləɾi əv/ &middot; *uh pluh-RAL-uh-tee uhv* &middot; the *t* flaps to /ɾ/ in American speech, as it does in *majority* and *variety*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **technical** — election law and political reporting in the first sense, patent drafting in the second. Outside those two worlds an American meets it on election night and nowhere else
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a plurality of voters*, *a plurality of the votes cast*, *a plurality of the members*. In patent claims it takes any countable noun at all: *a plurality of apertures*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a plurality of money*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory — ✗ *a plurality voters*. The noun after *of* is normally definite or generic: *a plurality of the votes*, *a plurality of Americans*. Bare *a plurality* is common with the set understood: *She won a plurality.*
**Pattern:** *a plurality of* [plural countable] &middot; *a plurality of* [the + plural] &middot; *win / take a plurality of the vote* &middot; patent claim: *comprising a plurality of* [noun]
**Contrast:** Two technical senses, and neither means what it looks like. **1.** In elections a plurality is the **largest share, not necessarily a majority**: a candidate who takes 42 percent of a three-way race has a plurality and not a majority, and that distinction is what decides whether a runoff is required. **2.** In United States patent practice *a plurality of* means simply **two or more** — *a plurality of vents* claims at least two vents and nothing more — which is why patent claims are full of a phrase that in ordinary English sounds like a crowd. Do not reach for it as a formal synonym for *many*: a reader in either field will read it as a number.
**Variants:** *a majority of*, *pluralities of*, *win by a plurality*, *a plurality (bare)*

Names the largest share of a vote or a group — and, in patent drafting, nothing grander than "more than one."

**1.** In elections and in any counted body, the largest share, whether or not it passes half — *a plurality of the vote*. **2.** In United States patent claims, exactly "two or more" and no more than that — *a plurality of vents*.

1. She won *a plurality of* the vote in a five-way primary and never came near fifty percent.
2. *A plurality of* the members favored postponement, but the bylaws require a majority.
3. Under state law the office goes to whoever takes *a plurality of* the votes cast.
4. The claim recites a housing comprising *a plurality of* vents.
5. *A plurality of* respondents named cost as the main obstacle; no single answer drew half.
6. A plurality is not a majority, and *a plurality of* forty-two percent is the usual reason for a runoff.

**See also:** [a majority of](#a-majority-of), [a supermajority of](#a-supermajority-of), [a quorum of](#a-quorum-of), [most](#most), [the majority of, the minority of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of)

### a quorum of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈkwɔrəm əv/ &middot; *uh KWOR-uhm uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈkwɔrəmə/
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **technical** — parliamentary procedure, corporate governance, statutes, and the minutes of any body that meets and votes. Neutral inside that world and out of place outside it
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns naming the members of a defined body — *a quorum of the board*, *a quorum of members*, *a quorum of the senate*. It cannot take an uncountable noun (✗ *a quorum of money*) and cannot take a set with no membership rule behind it (✗ *a quorum of customers*).
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory when the body is named — ✗ *a quorum the board*. Bare *a quorum* with the body understood is the commoner form in practice: *We have a quorum*, *A quorum was present*, *The meeting lacked a quorum*.
**Pattern:** *a quorum of* [the + defined body] &middot; *a quorum was present* &middot; *lack / constitute / establish a quorum* &middot; *in the absence of a quorum*
**Contrast:** A quorum is a **minimum, and the minimum is set elsewhere** — by statute, charter, or bylaws — so the phrase names a number without telling you what it is: a quorum may be a majority, two-thirds, or five people in a room. That is what separates it from *a majority of*, which is arithmetic and needs no governing document. Note what the threshold is for: a quorum makes a body able to act at all, which is a separate question from how many votes any particular motion then needs. The verb is singular, since *quorum* is the head noun: *A quorum of the board was present.* The word is Latin for "of whom," lifted out of the wording of old commissions.
**Variants:** *a quorum*, *quorum call*, *lack a quorum*, *quorate (chiefly British)*

The number of a body's members that must be present before it can transact business — a threshold fixed by rule, not by the speaker.

1. *A quorum of* the board was present, and the meeting was called to order.
2. Without *a quorum of* the members, the committee may debate but cannot vote.
3. The bylaws set *a quorum of* five, which the club has not managed since March.
4. *A quorum of* the commission failed to appear and the hearing was continued.
5. The clerk noted the absence of *a quorum* and the chamber adjourned.
6. State law requires *a quorum of* two-thirds for any vote to amend the charter.

**See also:** [a supermajority of](#a-supermajority-of), [a plurality of](#a-plurality-of), [a majority of](#a-majority-of), [enough](#enough), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all)

### a supermajority of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌsupɚməˈdʒɔrəɾi əv/ &middot; *uh soo-per-muh-JOR-uh-tee uhv* &middot; main stress on *-jor-*, secondary stress on *su-*; the *t* flaps to /ɾ/ as in *majority*
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **technical** — constitutional and corporate law, legislative reporting, and bylaws. It has become common enough in political journalism since the 1970s that most Americans meet it every budget season
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns naming members or votes — *a supermajority of the senate*, *a supermajority of shareholders*, *a supermajority of the votes cast*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *a supermajority of support*.
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory when the body is named — ✗ *a supermajority the senate*. Bare *a supermajority* is common with the body understood: *The amendment needs a supermajority.*
**Pattern:** *a supermajority of* [the + body] &middot; *a two-thirds / three-fifths supermajority of* [body] &middot; *require / reach / fall short of a supermajority* &middot; *supermajority requirement*
**Contrast:** A supermajority is any threshold **above a simple majority**, and like a quorum the actual fraction is set by the governing document — most often two-thirds, sometimes three-fifths or three-quarters. That puts the three threshold words in a series: a **plurality** is the largest share, a **majority** is more than half, a **supermajority** is more than a majority by a stated amount. The verb is singular, since *supermajority* is the head noun: *A supermajority of the members was required.* The word is American and modern; older documents simply say *a two-thirds vote*.
**Variants:** *a two-thirds supermajority*, *a three-fifths supermajority*, *supermajority requirement*, *a majority of*

A share of a body larger than a simple majority, at a level fixed by statute, charter, or bylaws.

1. Overriding the veto takes *a supermajority of* both chambers.
2. The bylaws require *a supermajority of* shareholders to approve any sale of assets.
3. *A supermajority of* the council voted to rezone the block over the mayor's objection.
4. The measure fell two votes short of *a supermajority*.
5. Several states require *a supermajority of* the legislature to raise any tax.
6. *A supermajority of* three-fifths is written into the district's charter.

**See also:** [a majority of](#a-majority-of), [a plurality of](#a-plurality-of), [a quorum of](#a-quorum-of), [most](#most), [the majority of, the minority of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-majority-of-the-minority-of)

### so much of … as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv … æz/ &middot; *soh MUCH uhv … AZ* &middot; read aloud, the *as* is normally weak, /əz/, and *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/
**Type:** quantifier in a correlative frame &middot; **Position:** central — *so much* fills the determiner slot and the *as*-clause that completes it follows the noun &middot; **Register:** **legal and formal** — the ordinary drafting idiom of statutes, wills, appropriations, and contracts, and stiff to the point of parody anywhere else. Plain-language drafting is steadily replacing it with *the part of … that*
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns and mass-like abstractions in the *much* version — *so much of the fund as remains*, *so much of the Act as relates to fees*. The countable version is *so many … as*: *so many of the directors as are present*. Never a bare singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **obligatory**, and so is the *as*-clause: both halves are required. ✗ *so much of the fund* alone is incomplete, and *so much as* by itself is a different construction entirely, an emphatic negative-polarity item meaning "even" (*he never so much as looked at it*).
**Pattern:** *so much of* [the + noun] *as* [verb phrase] &middot; *so many of* [the + plural] *as* [verb phrase] &middot; plain-language equivalent: *the part of* [noun] *that* [verb phrase]
**Contrast:** The frame is a **restrictive quantifier**: it does not report a quantity, it carves one out. *So much of the appropriation as is unexpended* means exactly the unexpended part and no more, which is why drafters reach for it — they must define a share without knowing its size. Keep it clear of two lookalikes. *So much as* on its own means "even" and needs a negative to lean on. *As much … as* is the ordinary comparative: *as much of the fund as we can spare* compares two amounts, where *so much of the fund as remains* defines one. In your own prose, write *the part of the fund that remains*.
**Variants:** *so many … as*, *so much as (= even)*, *as much … as*, *such … as*

The part of something, and only that part, which meets the stated condition — the legal drafter's way of quantifying by definition instead of by number.

1. *So much of* the appropriation *as* remains unexpended on June 30 shall revert to the general fund.
2. *So much of* the Act *as* relates to licensing takes effect immediately.
3. The trustee shall distribute *so much of* the income *as* is necessary for the beneficiary's support.
4. *So many of* the directors *as* are present may adjourn the meeting to a later date.
5. The court considered *so much of* the record *as* had been designated on appeal.
6. A plain-language redraft says the same thing: the part of the appropriation that is unspent reverts.

**See also:** [much](#much), [most](#most), [enough](#enough), [a quorum of](#a-quorum-of), [so, too, as](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#so-too-as)
