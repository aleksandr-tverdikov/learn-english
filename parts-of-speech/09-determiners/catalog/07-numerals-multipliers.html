Numerals, ordinals, fractions, and multipliers#

The determiners that count, rank, divide, and multiply a noun phrase — how many, which one in the sequence, what share of it, and how many times over.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

Everything in this file answers a question about quantity or sequence: how many (cardinals), which one in order (ordinals and general ordinals), what share (fractions and part-words), how many times over (multipliers), and roughly how many (approximators). What unites them grammatically is that most of them are not central determiners — they stack around one. Multipliers and fractions sit in the predeterminer slot, ahead of the, my, or this (double the price, half that amount); cardinals and ordinals sit in the postdeterminer slot, behind it (the first three chapters). That is why all the first three chapters is a legal noun phrase with four determiners in it — all, the, first, three — and only one of them, the, central. English allows exactly one central determiner per noun phrase, so the predeterminers and postdeterminers are the only way to pile more quantity onto the same noun.

Two ordering rules run through the whole group and account for most of the errors. Ordinal before cardinal: it is the first three questions, never ✗ the three first questions. And counting words stay singular: three hundred people, two dozen eggs, ten percent — the -s appears only when the number is indefinite and of follows (hundreds of people, dozens of eggs). The third trap is smaller but constant: a singular article can stand in front of a plural noun when a quantity word blocks the two apart, because the quantity counts as one unit — a good twenty miles, a mere three days, another two hundred dollars, a full two weeks.

One more thing runs through every entry below: these words are unstressed almost every time you say them. A is /ə/, an is /ən/, the is /ðə/ before a consonant and /ði/ before a vowel, of is /əv/, to is /tə/, and than is /ðən/. The only members of this class that keep a full stressed vowel are the numbers themselves — one is always /wʌn/, never ✗ /wən/ — and that stress difference is what separates one hour from an hour in speech.

Cardinal numerals#

Cardinals state how many. They are postdeterminers, they take plural countable nouns (except one, which takes a singular), and they never take an -s when a number governs them. Several members break the postdeterminer pattern and are labeled accordingly below: zero behaves like a central determiner, since nothing can stand in front of it (✗ the zero calories); a pair of and a gross of head partitive phrases of their own; and twain, the archaic form of two, follows its noun instead of preceding it. The inexact small numbers — a couple of, a few, one or two, several — behave like quantifiers rather than numerals and live with the small quantifiers.

a couple three#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈkʌpəl ˈθri/ · uh KUP-uhl THREE · a is weak, /ə/, and in running speech couple loses its /l/ into the number — a couple three days is usually /ə ˈkʌpə ˈθri ˈdeɪz/; the fuller a couple two three /ə ˈkʌpəl tu ˈθri/ runs all three numbers together with no pause Type: cardinal numeral (indefinite small count, = two or three) · Position: a quantity phrase filling the determiner slot of a bare plural noun; nothing central may join it · Register: regional and colloquial — Upper Midwestern and Great Lakes speech above all (Wisconsin, Minnesota, Michigan, Chicago), where it passes unnoticed in conversation, and casual speech more widely. It is not standard written English and appears in print only in dialogue. Goes with: plural countable nouns only — a couple three days, a couple three guys. Never a singular countable noun (✗ a couple three day) and never an uncountable noun (✗ a couple three water → a couple three glasses of water). With of: No of before a bare plural noun — a couple three beers — and speakers who use this construction drop of from plain a couple too (a couple beers). Of returns before a determiner or a pronoun: a couple three of the guys, a couple three of them. Pattern: a couple three [plural noun] · a couple two three [plural noun] · two-three [plural noun] · a couple three of [the/my + plural noun] · a couple three (standing alone) Contrast: This is two or three with the or worn away, and the stacked a couple two three is one of the clearest markers of Upper Midwestern speech. The count is genuinely approximate — nobody saying it is promising three. In writing, use two or three, or a couple of, which is the neutral form of the same idea. Against a few: a few is vaguer and can run to five or six, while a couple three stays pinned at two or three. Agreement is plain plural: A couple three guys were waiting. Variants: a couple two three, two-three, two or three, a couple of, a few

Names an indefinite small count of two or three, in Upper Midwestern and casual American speech.

  1. Give me a couple three days and I'll have the estimate.
  2. "There was a couple three guys out front waiting on you."
  3. We're two-three weeks out from opening, tops.
  4. He pulled a couple two three beers out of the cooler and sat down.
  5. It's a couple three miles up the county road, past the silo.
  6. In writing, make it two or three: ✗ a couple three days → ✓ two or three days.

a gross of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈɡroʊs əv/ · uh GROHSS uv · a /ə/ and of /əv/ are weak; the vowel is the /oʊ/ of grow, and the s is voiceless — /ɡroʊs/, never ✗ /ɡroʊz/ Type: cardinal numeral (collective, = 144, a dozen dozen) · Position: a is the central determiner of gross; the whole phrase heads a partitive · Register: trade and wholesale — purchase orders, invoices, and stationery and hardware catalogs. An American who has never worked a stockroom may never meet it, and in ordinary prose 144 is clearer. Goes with: plural countable nouns, and only the small manufactured things that are actually sold by the gross — a gross of pencils, two gross of screws, a gross of buttons. Never an uncountable noun (✗ a gross of water) and never a singular countable noun (✗ a gross of pencil). With of: Required in the ordinary a gross of pencils — ✗ a gross pencils. As with dozen, the order desk drops it when a number governs a bare plural (ten gross envelopes), but ten gross of envelopes is the safer written form. Of is required before a determiner or a pronoun: a gross of the small ones, a gross of them. Pattern: a gross of [plural noun] · [cardinal] gross of [plural noun] · half a gross of [plural noun] · a great gross of [plural noun] (= 1,728, effectively dead) · by the gross Contrast: The count word stays singular under a number, exactly like dozen and hundred: two gross of screws, never ✗ two grosses of screws. Unlike dozen, though, gross has no everyday life left outside the supply room, so write 144 unless you are quoting an invoice. Do not confuse the numeral with the far commoner adjective gross meaning "before deductions" (gross income, gross weight) or the slang gross meaning disgusting — same spelling, unrelated words. Agreement follows the plural noun: A gross of pencils were delivered. Variants: two gross, half a gross, a great gross (= 1,728), a dozen dozen, by the gross, 144

A hundred and forty-four of something, counted as one wholesale unit.

  1. The order sheet says a gross of number-two pencils.
  2. They shipped two gross of machine screws in the wrong size.
  3. We go through a gross of zip ties a month at the shop.
  4. Half a gross of envelopes will cover the whole mailing.
  5. The invoice reads 3 gross, which is four hundred thirty-two units.
  6. Outside a stockroom nobody says a gross of anything — they say 144.

a jillion#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈdʒɪljən/ · uh JIL-yuhn · a is weak, /ə/, exactly as in a million; the variant forty-eleven is /ˌfɔrti ɪˈlɛvən/, FOR-tee-ih-LEV-uhn, with the main beat on -lev- Type: cardinal numeral (hyperbolic indefinite — an uncountably large number) · Position: postdeterminer, in the same slot as million, after a · Register: slang and hyperbolic, never literal. A jillion, a zillion, and a bazillion are ordinary American informal speech and writing — texts, complaints, sports talk. Forty-eleven is narrower: Southern and Appalachian folk speech, often addressed to a child, and it sounds regional to everyone else. None of them belongs anywhere a real number is being reported. Goes with: plural countable nouns — a jillion reasons, forty-eleven forms. Never a singular countable noun (✗ a jillion reason) and never an uncountable noun (✗ a jillion homework → a jillion assignments). With of: No of before a bare plural noun: a jillion reasons. Of is required before a determiner or a pronoun (a jillion of those forms, a jillion of them), and the plural jillions of always takes it, exactly like millions of. Pattern: a jillion [plural noun] · jillions of [plural noun] · forty-eleven [plural noun] · a jillion times · a zillion / bazillion / gazillion [plural noun] Contrast: These are not numbers; they are complaints and boasts wearing a number's clothes, which is why nothing that measures can sit beside them: ✗ about a jillion, ✗ a jillion and six, ✗ at least a jillion. umpteen is the same idea one register up, and the matching ordinals live at nth, umpteenth. Forty-eleven is built the way a child builds a big number — a real ten stacked on a real teen — and that is its flavor in Southern speech: I've told you forty-eleven times. Agreement is plain plural: A jillion things are wrong with it. Variants: a zillion, a bazillion, a gazillion, a kajillion, jillions of, forty-eleven (Southern and Appalachian), umpteen, a hundred and one

Names a comically large, uncounted number — hyperbole rather than arithmetic.

  1. There are a jillion reasons not to refinance right now.
  2. I've got a zillion emails and not one that needs answering.
  3. Mama's told you forty-eleven times to stay off that porch rail.
  4. Jillions of mosquitoes came off the river after the rain.
  5. He's got forty-eleven cousins scattered between here and Knoxville.
  6. That's a bazillion dollars in equipment sitting in an unlocked shed.

a pair of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈpɛr əv/ · uh PAIR uv · both a /ə/ and of /əv/ are weak and unstressed; only pair carries a beat Type: cardinal numeral (unit counter, = two, and the counter for plural-only nouns) · Position: a is the central determiner of pair; the whole phrase heads a partitive · Register: neutral Goes with: plural countable nouns only — either a plural-only noun that has no singular at all (a pair of scissors, a pair of pants, a pair of glasses, a pair of tweezers) or an ordinary noun naming things that come in twos (a pair of shoes, a pair of gloves, a pair of twins). Never a singular countable noun (✗ a pair of shoe) and never an uncountable noun (✗ a pair of water, ✗ a pair of advice). With of: Required and fixed — ✗ a pair scissors. A second of builds a partitive on top: a pair of the good scissors, two of the pairs I bought. Pattern: a pair of [plural noun] · [cardinal] pairs of [plural noun] · this/that pair of [plural noun] · another pair of [plural noun] · a pair of [pronoun] (rare) Contrast: This is the only way to count a plural-only noun. Scissors, pants, jeans, shorts, glasses, pliers, tweezers, and binoculars have no singular form, so a cardinal cannot touch them directly: ✗ two scissors, ✗ one pant, ✗ three jeans. The number counts the pairs instead — two pairs of scissors, one pair of pants. Agreement follows pair, not the noun after of: One pair of scissors is missing, Two pairs of scissors are missing — but the bare noun by itself stays plural, The scissors are missing, never ✗ The scissors is missing. With ordinary countables, a pair of means exactly two where a couple of means roughly two. Variants: pairs of, a set of, a couple of (approximate two), a brace of (game birds, archaic), pant / scissor (attributive singulars only: a pant leg, a scissor kick)

Counts a noun that has no singular of its own, or one that comes in twos, by counting the units instead.

  1. I need a pair of scissors that actually cuts.
  2. She bought two pairs of jeans and returned both.
  3. Do you have a pair of pliers in the truck?
  4. A pair of glasses was sitting on the dashboard.
  5. There isn't a pair of clean socks left in the house.
  6. He owns exactly one pair of dress shoes.
  7. A pair of twins joined the class in October.

dozen#

Pronunciation: /ˈdʌzən/ · DUZ-uhn · the article in front is weak: a dozen /ə ˈdʌzən/, never ✗ /eɪ ˈdʌzən/ Type: cardinal numeral (collective, = twelve) · Position: postdeterminer, after a, the, a possessive, or another cardinal · Register: neutral Goes with: plural countable nouns — a dozen eggs, two dozen roses. Not uncountable nouns (✗ a dozen water → a dozen bottles of water) and not singular countable nouns (✗ a dozen egg). With of: No of before a bare plural noun: a dozen eggs, ✗ a dozen of eggs. Of is required before a determiner or a pronoun: a dozen of the eggs, a dozen of them. The plural dozens always takes of: dozens of complaints. Pattern: a/one dozen [plural noun] · [cardinal] dozen [plural noun] · a dozen of [the/my/these + plural noun] · dozens of [plural noun] · half a dozen [plural noun] Contrast: Like hundred and thousand, dozen stays singular when a number counts it — two dozen eggs, never ✗ two dozens eggs. It goes plural only when the count is vague and of follows: dozens of eggs. The same rule governs score (twenty), which survives mainly in four score and seven. Agreement follows the plural noun: A dozen eggs were on the counter. And dozen doubles as a pronoun — as a determiner it precedes a noun (a dozen eggs), as a pronoun it stands alone (I'll take a dozen, he sent two dozen). Variants: half a dozen, a baker's dozen (thirteen), dozens of, score (= twenty, archaic)

Twelve of something, counted as a unit.

  1. Pick up a dozen eggs on your way home.
  2. They ordered three dozen chairs for the reception.
  3. Half a dozen people had already complained.
  4. Dozens of flights were canceled before noon.
  5. I've read a dozen of these reports and they all say the same thing.
  6. She sent two dozen roses and no note.
  7. We don't have a dozen chairs, let alone three dozen.

hundred, thousand, million, billion#

Pronunciation: /ˈhʌndrəd/, /ˈθaʊzənd/, /ˈmɪljən/, /ˈbɪljən/ · HUN-druhd, THOW-zuhnd, MIL-yuhn, BIL-yuhn · the article in front is weak — a hundred /ə ˈhʌndrəd/, a million /ə ˈmɪljən/; the full one hundred /ˈwʌn ˈhʌndrəd/ is the stressed, precise alternative Type: cardinal numeral (large, multiplicative) · Position: postdeterminer · Register: neutral Goes with: plural countable nouns — a hundred reasons, two million dollars. Never an uncountable noun directly (✗ a thousand water → a thousand gallons of water) and never a singular countable noun (✗ a hundred reason). With of: No of before a bare plural noun: three hundred people, ✗ three hundred of people. Of is required before a determiner or a pronoun (three hundred of the applicants, a million of them) and after the plural forms (hundreds of people, thousands of dollars). Pattern: a/one hundred [plural noun] · [cardinal] hundred [plural noun] · [cardinal] hundred [cardinal] [plural noun] · hundreds of [plural noun] · [cardinal] hundred of [the/my + plural noun] Contrast: The singular rule is the whole entry. When a number governs them they never take -s: three hundred people, five thousand miles, two million dollars — ✗ three hundreds people. The -s form exists only for an indefinite, uncounted quantity, and it always requires of: hundreds of people, millions of dollars. Compare dozen, which behaves identically. Agreement splits the same way it does for the small cardinals: a plural count takes a plural verb (Two hundred permits were issued), but a sum of money, a distance, or a stretch of time treated as one amount takes a singular (Two million dollars is a lot to write off, Five thousand miles is a long way to drive). Variants: one hundred (precise, formal), a hundred (ordinary), hundreds of, a hundred and one (idiomatic "very many"), trillion, zillion (informal)

A round large number used to count a plural noun.

  1. The city issued two hundred fifty permits last month.
  2. A thousand things could go wrong between now and Friday.
  3. Hundreds of commuters were stranded at the station.
  4. The settlement came to four million dollars.
  5. Only three hundred of the tickets were ever sold.
  6. She has a hundred and one reasons not to go.

Note on and. The standard American reading of 253 is two hundred fifty-three; two hundred and fifty-three is standard British and perfectly acceptable in the United States. Keep and for the decimal point in dictation (two hundred and five tenths) and out of whole numbers in American technical writing.

one#

Pronunciation: /wʌn/ · WUN · it has no weak form. One keeps its full stressed vowel every time, which is exactly what separates it from the article: an hour /ən ˈaʊɚ/ against one hour /ˈwʌn ˈaʊɚ/. If the syllable reduces to /ə/ or /ən/, the word was a/an, not one. Type: cardinal numeral · Position: postdeterminer as a numeral (the one exception); functions alone as the determiner of a bare noun phrase (one reason) · Register: neutral Goes with: singular countable nouns only — one reason, one child. Never a plural countable noun (✗ one reasons) and never an uncountable noun (✗ one information → one piece of information). With of: Required before a determiner or a pronoun, and the following noun is plural while the verb stays singular: one of the students was late, one of them. Never ✗ one of student. Pattern: one [singular noun] · the/my/this one [singular noun] · one of [the/my/these + plural noun] · one of [pronoun] · one [singular noun] or another Contrast: One against a/an is the core contrast. Both precede a singular countable noun, but one counts and a merely introduces: I waited an hour reports duration, I waited one hour insists it was exactly one and not two. Use a unless the number is the point. Note also that one is a pronoun (I'll take the red one, One never knows) — as a determiner it precedes a noun, as a pronoun it stands alone. Variants: one and only, one or two, one another, the one (emphatic), a one (informal: that's a one)

Counts exactly one of something, as opposed to any other number.

  1. The number. Exactly one, in contrast to any other number.
  2. The emphatic singular. The one + noun singles out a unique member: the one thing I asked for.
  3. "A certain." Formal, slightly legal or literary: One Robert Hayes has filed a claim.
  1. One witness changed her story twice.
  2. I'll be there in one hour, two at the most.
  3. One of the elevators has been out since March.
  4. That's the one mistake we can't afford to make.
  5. Give me one good reason to stay.
  6. One Mr. Delgado called while you were out.

See also: a, two, three, four, first, another, once

one or more#

Pronunciation: /ˈwʌn ɚ ˈmɔr/ · WUN ur MOR · or is weak here, /ɚ/, and never carries a beat; one and more both keep their full vowels — the phrase is two stresses with a schwa between them Type: cardinal numeral (open-ended lower bound) · Position: fills the determiner slot of a bare plural noun; it admits no central determiner in front of it (✗ the one or more copies) · Register: formal, legal, and technical — statutes, standards, specifications, and software documentation, where it is the standard way to write "at least one." It is stiff in conversation, which prefers one or two or some. Goes with: plural countable nouns — one or more witnesses, one or more of the following conditions. Never a singular countable noun (✗ one or more witness) and never an uncountable noun (✗ one or more evidence → one or more pieces of evidence). With of: Required before a determiner or a pronoun, and the noun after of stays plural: one or more of the conditions, one or more of them, one or more of the following. Pattern: one or more [plural noun] · one or more of [the/these + plural noun] · one or more of the following · of one or more [plural noun] · one or more (standing alone) Contrast: The verb goes plural, which surprises everyone who sees one first: One or more of the panels are loose, If one or more conditions are met…. That is the reverse of one of, where the verb is singular (one of the panels is loose) — coordinating with more is what pluralizes it. Against at least one, which means the same thing in plainer words but takes a singular noun (at least one witness): drafters who want the plural noun reach for one or more. Against one or two, a casual estimate with a ceiling on it, this one has no ceiling at all. In plain-language editing, one or more of the following is usually just any of the following. Variants: at least one, one or two, any of, one or more of the following, ≥ 1 (technical)

Sets a floor of one with no ceiling above it: at least one, possibly many.

  1. The form must be signed by one or more witnesses.
  2. Coverage lapses if one or more of the conditions are not met.
  3. Each account may be linked to one or more devices.
  4. The statute applies wherever one or more parties reside in the state.
  5. Check one or more of the boxes below.
  6. One or more of the sensors were reporting bad data all week.

See also: one, at least, at most, one or two, any, two, three, four

score#

Pronunciation: /skɔr/ · SKOR · the article in front is weak: a score of years /ə ˈskɔr əv ˈjɪrz/; in the compounds the stress stays on the first element, threescore /ˈθriskɔr/, fourscore /ˈfɔrskɔr/ Type: cardinal numeral (collective, = twenty) · Position: postdeterminer, after a, the, or another cardinal · Register: archaic and literary. An American meets it in three places and almost nowhere else: the King James Bible (threescore and ten), the Gettysburg Address (Four score and seven years ago), and the living journalistic plural scores of, which no longer means twenty at all. Using it for a real count of twenty is a period costume. Goes with: plural countable nouns — fourscore years, a score of witnesses. Never an uncountable noun (✗ a score of water) and never a singular countable noun (✗ a score of year). With of: None in the old bare use: fourscore years, threescore and ten. Of is normal once the indefinite article appears — a score of years, a score of witnesses — and it is obligatory with the modern vague plural, scores of complaints. Pattern: [cardinal] score [plural noun] · a score of [plural noun] · threescore and ten · fourscore and seven · scores of [plural noun] (modern) Contrast: It stays singular under a number, like dozen and hundred: four score years, ✗ four scores years. The -s belongs only to the vague modern plural, which always takes of. Threescore (60) and fourscore (80) are single words frozen inside their famous phrases — threescore and ten is the biblical span of a life, seventy years, and fourscore and seven is eighty-seven. Note that score keeps a whole separate life as an ordinary noun — a tally, a grudge, a piece of written music — and none of that is a determiner. Variants: threescore (= 60), fourscore (= 80), a score of, scores of (modern, vague), twoscore, three score and ten

Twenty of something counted as a unit — an archaic collective numeral surviving in scripture, oratory, and quotation.

  1. Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent a new nation.
  2. The days of our years are threescore years and ten. (Psalm 90, King James Version)
  3. He had been dead a score of years before anyone opened the trunk.
  4. Fourscore militiamen answered the muster that morning.
  5. Scores of protesters gathered outside the statehouse — the only form still in ordinary use.
  6. Write twenty, not a score of, unless you are quoting somebody.

See also: dozen, a gross of, scores of, hundred, thousand, million, billion, twain

twain#

Pronunciation: /tweɪn/ · TWAYN · one syllable, rhyming with rain; there is no weak form — twain is always fully stressed, which is part of why it survives only inside set phrases Type: cardinal numeral (archaic, = two) · Position: postpositive or fused-head — it follows its noun (days twain) or stands alone after a determiner (the twain, in twain). It does not sit in front of a noun in any living construction. · Register: archaic and literary. An American meets it in the King James Bible (they twain shall be one flesh), in Kipling (never the twain shall meet), in the frozen cut in twain, and in Mark Twain's pen name, taken from the leadsman's river cry mark twain — two fathoms. Nowhere else. Goes with: plural countable nouns, and only in the archaic postpositive frame — brethren twain, days twain. It cannot precede a noun: ✗ twain days, ✗ twain reasons. As a fused head it takes the or they and stands alone: the twain, they twain. With of: Does not take of. The surviving phrases carry prepositions instead: in twain, between the twain. Pattern: [plural noun] twain (archaic postpositive) · the twain (fused head) · in twain (= into two pieces) · they twain (biblical) · mark twain (river sounding) Contrast: This is two in the shape two used to have. English once had more than one form of the numeral, and twain is the survivor of the one used when the number stood after its noun or alone — which is exactly why it never occupies a determiner slot the way two, three, four does. Days twain is a poetic inversion of two days, not a different determiner, and never the twain shall meet is a fused-head pronoun meaning "the two of them." Do not treat it as a fancy synonym you can drop into ordinary prose: outside quotation and deliberate pastiche it reads as costume. Variants: two (the living form), in twain, the twain, they twain, mark twain (two fathoms), both

The archaic form of two, surviving only after its noun, in fixed phrases, and in quotation.

  1. …and they twain shall be one flesh. (Matthew 19:5, King James Version)
  2. Oh, East is East, and West is West, and never the twain shall meet.
  3. The veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom.
  4. He took his soundings and called "mark twain" — two fathoms, safe water.
  5. In older verse the number follows the noun: days twain for two days.
  6. Nothing in modern American English lets it stand in front of a noun: ✗ twain days → ✓ two days.

two, three, four#

Pronunciation: /tu/, /θri/, /fɔr/ · TOO, THREE, FOR · the numbers are always stressed, but the function words around them are not: three of the witnesses is /ˈθri əv ðə ˈwɪtnəsəz/, with of /əv/ and the /ðə/ both reduced Type: cardinal numeral · Position: postdeterminer — it follows any central determiner and follows an ordinal · Register: neutral Goes with: plural countable nouns only — two witnesses, three days. Never a singular countable noun (✗ two witness) and never an uncountable noun (✗ three information, ✗ four advice → three pieces of information, four pieces of advice). The one systematic exception is the hyphenated premodifier, where the noun goes singular: a three-day weekend, a two-mile run, a six-foot fence. With of: No of before a bare plural noun: three witnesses. Of is required before a determiner or a pronoun: three of the witnesses, two of them, four of my clients. Pattern: [cardinal] [plural noun] · the/my/these [cardinal] [plural noun] · the [ordinal] [cardinal] [plural noun] · [cardinal] of [the/my/these + plural noun] · a [cardinal]-[singular measure noun] [noun] Contrast: Ordinal before cardinal, always: the first two questions, ✗ the two first questions; her last three albums, ✗ her three last albums. And note the agreement split: a plural count takes a plural verb (Three years have passed since then), but a measured quantity treated as one unit takes a singular (Three years is a long time to wait, Twenty miles is too far to walk). Nouns that are permanently plural — scissors, pants, glasses, tweezers — cannot be counted directly at all (✗ two scissors); they take a pair of, and the number counts the pairs: two pairs of scissors. Nonstandard: them two, them three (them two guys never showed) replaces the demonstrative those with object-form them; it is widespread in casual and regional American speech and is not standard written English — see them. Variants: twenty-one through ninety-nine (hyphenated), two-and-a-half, a pair of (with permanently plural nouns), nought/naught (rare), them two (nonstandard)

Any exact number above one, counting a plural noun.

  1. Three witnesses gave two conflicting accounts.
  2. The first two chapters are free; after that you pay.
  3. She ran twenty-six miles in four hours.
  4. Two of the panelists never showed up.

Ordinals#

Ordinals rank an item within a sequence. They are postdeterminers, they almost always follow the or a possessive, and they come before any cardinal in the same phrase.

These express sequence without a number — before, after, the one already mentioned, one more of the same. same#

Pronunciation: /seɪm/ · SAYM Type: general ordinal (identity determiner) · Position: postdeterminer — it requires a central determiner, nearly always the, this, or that · Register: neutral Goes with: all three noun types — singular countable (the same problem), plural countable (the same two people), and uncountable (the same information, the same advice). What it cannot do is stand bare before a noun in ordinary prose: ✗ same problem came up again → the same problem came up again. It stands bare only in fixed and commercial compounds (same-day shipping, same-sex marriage) and in elliptical replies (Same here, Same to you). With of: Does not take of. The comparison is made with as (the same as before) or with a relative clause (the same one that broke) — ✗ the same of. Fractions and part-words#

These name a share of something rather than a count of it. Half and all are the odd ones out: both are true predeterminers that can skip of entirely (half the cake, all the cake). Every other fraction and part-word needs of before a determined noun phrase, and all of them take notional agreement — the verb follows the noun after of, not the fraction: Half the cake is gone, Half the guests are gone.

Multipliers#

Multipliers state how many times over. Grammatically they are predeterminers: they stand in front of a complete, already-determined noun phrase (double the price, twice that amount) and they take no of — ✗ double of the price. Five entries here are not multiplier determiners at all and say so in their own lines: once is a frequency adverb, twofold and its family are adjectives and adverbs of change, a factor of and an order of magnitude are fixed phrases that multiply a verb or a comparison rather than a noun, and per is the preposition that states a rate.

Approximators#

These attach to a numeral rather than to the noun, and they hedge it — roughly this many, at least this many, no more than this many. Two of them sit here for contrast rather than for hedging: a total of attaches to a numeral in exactly the same way and hedges nothing at all, and -plus attaches after the number instead of in front of it, as -odd does. Watch the article: when the quantity is treated as a single block, a singular a can stand in front of a plural noun (a good twenty miles, a full three days).
