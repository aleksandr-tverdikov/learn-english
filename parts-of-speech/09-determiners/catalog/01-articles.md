# Articles: a, an, the, and the zero article

> The three-way system — definite, indefinite, and nothing at all — that every English noun phrase has to pass through before it can be spoken.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Articles are the most frequent words in English and the hardest for anyone whose first language has none: *the* is the commonest word in the language, *a* is in the top ten, and the third member of the system is a silence. What unites them is a single question — **can my listener already identify which one I mean?** *The* answers yes, *a/an* answers no, and the zero article says the question does not apply, because the noun names a substance, a class, a proper name, or a routine rather than an item you could point at. The rule running through the whole file is that **article choice is decided by the noun's countability and by the listener's knowledge, not by how important or how specific the thing feels** — which is why *I need Ø information* and *I need a pen* are both right and *an information* is not English. The second rule, smaller and just as unforgiving, is that **the choice between *a* and *an*, and between the two pronunciations of *the*, is made by sound and never by spelling.**

**A note on Ø.** The zero article has no written form; grammar books mark it Ø so that its absence can be talked about. In this file Ø appears only in the header lines and the tables, where it marks the empty determiner slot. It never appears in an example sentence: there the italics fall on the noun — or on the frozen phrase — that carries the empty slot, so every example reads as ordinary American English with nothing extra in it.

## The indefinite article

### a

**Pronunciation:** weak /ə/ &middot; *uh* &mdash; stressed /eɪ/ &middot; *AY*. The weak form is the real one: in running speech *a* is a single unstressed schwa leaning onto the next word, so *a car* is "uh-CAR." The stressed /eɪ/ survives only in quotation, correction, and dictation (*I said A dog, not THE dog*).
**Русский:** неопределённый артикль — русского эквивалента нет: один из класса, слушателю не известный
**Type:** article (indefinite) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only.** *a chair*, *a decision*, *a forty-dollar fine*. **Never a plural countable** (*a books*, *a two dogs*) and **never an uncountable** (*a information*, *a advice*, *a furniture*, *a homework*, *a research*, *a equipment*, *a luggage*, *a progress*, *a weather*). To say the same indefinite thing about a plural or an uncountable, use the [zero article](#zero-article-ø) or *some*: *a chair* → *Ø chairs / some chairs*; *a fact* → *Ø information / some information*. The one systematic escape is **re-cutting**: an uncountable noun that is reinterpreted as a portion, a serving, a type, or an instance genuinely becomes countable and then takes *a* — *a coffee* (a cup of it), *a wine* (a variety), *a paper* (an essay), *a light* (a lamp), *a kindness* (an act of it), *an experience* (an event, versus uncountable *experience* = know-how).
**With *of*:** *a* takes no *of*-phrase of its own. It appears as the head-marker inside partitives (*a slice of the pie*, *a couple of days*, *a lot of noise*) and it is obligatory in the **double genitive**, where both *a* and *of* must be present: *a friend of mine*, *a client of my father's* — *a my friend* and *a friend of me* are both impossible.
**Pattern:** *a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *a* [adjective] [noun] &middot; [so / too / as / how / that] [adjective] *a* [noun] (*too big a risk*, *as good a place as any*) &middot; [such / what / quite / rather / half] *a* [noun] &middot; *many a* [singular countable noun] + **singular verb** (*Many a contractor has walked off that job*) &middot; [amount or frequency] *a* [unit] (*twice a week* — see [a (per)](#a-per)) &middot; *a* [proper noun] (*a Mr. Kowalski*, *a Rembrandt*)
**Contrast:** Against [the](#the), the difference is not vague-versus-specific but **unidentifiable versus identifiable**: *I talked to a lawyer* leaves you unable to say which lawyer, *I talked to the lawyer* assumes you can. Against the [zero article](#zero-article-ø), the difference is only number and countability — *a* is what the zero article turns into when the noun is singular and countable. Against *one*, *a* is the unstressed default and *one* is a counted quantity or a contrast: *Give me a minute* is a request, *Give me one minute* is a limit; *a hundred* and *one hundred* mean the same number but *one* insists on it. Against [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), the singular *some* is dismissive rather than merely indefinite: *A guy called* is neutral, *Some guy called* says you neither know nor care who.
**Variants:** *an* before a vowel **sound**. The two are one word with two shapes, chosen by the sound that follows and never by the spelling — see [an](#an) and the [sound table](#a-or-an-the-test-is-sound-not-spelling).

Marks a singular countable noun as one of a class, without claiming the listener can pick out which one.

**1. First mention.** Introducing something the listener has not met yet. The next mention takes *the*.

1. *A* package came for you while you were out.
2. She's dating *a* guy from her old firm.

**2. Classifying.** Jobs, roles, memberships, and types. English requires the article here even where most other languages drop it: *She's engineer* is one of the commonest errors in the file.

3. He's *a* paramedic in Cuyahoga County.
4. That's *a* sedan, not *a* hatchback.

**3. Any representative member.** The generic of definitions and rules, true of each member one at a time. See [Three ways to be generic](#three-ways-to-be-generic).

5. *A* thermometer measures temperature.
6. *A* dog will eat until it's sick.

**4. Meaning "one."** Before *hundred, thousand, million, dozen, quarter, half* and in measurements of time and distance.

7. I'll be back in *an* hour and *a* half.
8. There were *a* hundred people crammed into that room.

**5. Meaning "per."** Rates and frequencies — treated separately at [a (per)](#a-per).

9. The garage charges eighteen dollars *a* day.

**6. A certain, unnamed one.** Before a personal name, *a* means the speaker does not know the person or expects the listener not to. The fuller frame *a certain Ms. Okonjo*, where the article is joined by a word that marks the person as known to the speaker and withheld from the listener, is a determiner in its own right and has an entry of its own at [a certain](#a-certain); the *certain* half is at [certain](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain).

10. *A* Ms. Okonjo called about the lease.

**7. A work by, or one of a family or type.** With a proper name reinterpreted as a common noun.

11. They have *a* Rothko in the lobby and nobody looks at it.
12. He's no Einstein, but he keeps the books straight.

**8. After a predeterminer or a shifted adjective.** *Such, what, quite, rather, half, many* stand in front of *a*, and after *so, too, as, how,* and *that* the adjective jumps in front of it as well. This construction is only possible with a singular countable noun.

13. It was *a* long meeting — no, *such* *a* long meeting that two people left. *(such a)*
14. That's too big *a* risk for the money involved.
15. I've never seen *quite* *a* mess like that kitchen.

**9. In fixed quantity phrases.** *A few, a little, a lot of, a couple of, a great deal of, a number of, a bit of* are frozen units in which *a* is no longer doing its own work.

16. Give it *a* little time and *a* lot of water.
17. *A* couple of days later the check cleared.
18. There were *a* number of complaints about the noise.

**10. In questions and negatives.** Neither one touches *a/an* — unlike the [zero article](#zero-article-ø), which trades places with *any*. What does happen is that a negated *a* often gives way to *no*: *There isn't a problem* and *There's no problem* say the same thing, the second more flatly.

19. Do you have *a* pen I can borrow for one second?
20. That isn't *a* reason, it's *an* excuse.

**See also:** [an](#an), [a (per)](#a-per), [a certain](#a-certain), [the](#the), [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [Three ways to be generic](#three-ways-to-be-generic), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), [one](07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [certain](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain)

### a (per)

**Pronunciation:** weak /ə/ &middot; *uh* &mdash; *an* /ən/ &middot; *uhn* before a vowel sound
**Русский:** за, в — распределительное «на единицу»: $6 a pound «за фунт», twice a day «дважды в день»
**Type:** article (indefinite) in distributive use &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral — the ordinary spoken and written form; *per* is the technical alternative
**Goes with:** **singular countable unit nouns** of time, weight, distance, volume, or head-count — *a day, a week, a month, an hour, a pound, a gallon, a mile, a person, a head, a serving, a night*. Never a plural (*six dollars a pounds*) and never an uncountable (*six dollars a produce*).
**With *of*:** takes none. The *of* in *four out of five* belongs to a different construction.
**Pattern:** [price or amount] *a* [unit] (*$6 a pound*) &middot; [number of times] *a* [time unit] (*twice a day*, *three times a week*) &middot; [quantity] *a* [time unit] (*two sick days a month*) &middot; [rate] *an* [hour] (*sixty miles an hour*)
**Contrast:** This is not the counting *a* of *a pound of flour*; it is a worn-down descendant of the preposition *on* (Middle English *twice on the day*), which is why it means **per** and why it can follow a number without sounding like a contradiction. Against *per*: same meaning, different register — *per* belongs to invoices, contracts, and science (*per unit*, *per capita*, *per diem*, *120 beats per minute*) and takes **no article** (*per an hour* is impossible). Against *by the*: *by the* names the unit a thing is **sold or paid in** (*sold by the pound*, *paid by the hour*, *rented by the week*), while *a* states a rate already computed (*$6 a pound*, *$22 an hour*). Against *each*, which comes after the noun and prices items rather than rates: *Tickets are $12 each*.

States a rate: so much of one thing for every one of another.

1. Ground chuck is six dollars *a* pound this week.
2. She takes it twice *a* day with food.
3. The limit drops to twenty-five miles *an* hour past the school.
4. Tickets are fifteen dollars *a* person, forty for a family.
5. He puts in fifty hours *a* week and still picks up Saturdays.
6. We each get two personal days *a* month.

**See also:** [a](#a), [an](#an), [the](#the), [one](07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every)

### a certain

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsɚtn̩/ &middot; *uh SUR-tn* &mdash; the article is **always** in its weak form here, a single schwa leaning onto the stressed first syllable of *certain*, whose final /n/ is syllabic: *a certain look* is /ə ˌsɚtn̩ ˈlʊk/. The stressed /eɪ/ of [a](#a) does not survive inside the frame, and neither does *an* — *certain* begins with /s/, so the pre-vocalic form has no work to do and *an certain* never occurs.
**Русский:** некий, один — говорящий знает, о ком или о чём речь, но намеренно не называет
**Type:** article (indefinite) inside a fixed determiner frame — *a* plus the unstressed determiner *certain* &middot; **Position:** central determiner, with *certain* riding behind it in the postdeterminer slot; the two are chosen and used as one unit &middot; **Register:** neutral and fully standard — an American meets it in conversation, in a news story, in a complaint letter, and in a novel, with no dialect or period flavor at all. Its only coloring is attitude: in speech it is often faintly coy, because it advertises that the speaker could name the thing and will not.
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns** (*a certain restaurant*, *a certain look*, *a certain tone of voice*), **personal names and titles** (*a certain Mr. Kowalski*, *a certain Dr. Reyes*), and — the exception worth memorizing — **abstract uncountable nouns** (*a certain charm*, *a certain menace*, *a certain restlessness*). That third case is the one place in the language where *a* stands in front of an uncountable noun **without** re-cutting it as a portion or a type: the article hedges the quality instead of counting it, which is why *a certain charm* is ordinary English where *a charm* means something else entirely. **Never a plural countable** (*a certain people*, *a certain questions*) and **never an ordinary concrete uncountable** (*a certain water*, *a certain furniture*, *a certain information*). For those two the article simply drops out and bare *certain* takes over the central slot: *certain people*, *certain questions*, *certain evidence* — see [certain](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain). The frame also refuses any other central determiner in front of it: *the certain restaurant* can only mean the sure one, and *my certain restaurant* is not English.
**With *of*:** the bare frame takes none — *a certain of the restaurants* is impossible, and the bookish partitive *certain of the members* belongs to bare [certain](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain) rather than to this one. But *a certain* heads two frozen quantity phrases in which the *of* is **obligatory**: *a certain amount of* before an uncountable noun (*a certain amount of delay*) and *a certain number of* before a plural (*a certain number of claims*). Drop the *of* and both collapse — *a certain amount delay* is not English. The frozen adverbial *to a certain extent* takes no *of* at all.
**Pattern:** *a certain* [singular countable noun] &middot; *a certain* [title] [personal name] (*a certain Ms. Okonjo*) &middot; *a certain* [abstract uncountable noun] (*a certain charm*) &middot; *a certain amount of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a certain number of* [plural countable noun] &middot; *a certain someone / a certain something* &middot; *to a certain extent / to a certain degree*
**Contrast:** Against plain [a](#a), the difference is whose knowledge is at stake. Both are indefinite **to the listener**, but *A lawyer called* leaves open that the speaker cannot identify the caller either, while *A certain lawyer called* says the speaker can and is not going to — this is the ordinary American way of marking a **specific** indefinite. Against [the](#the), which hands the identification over: *the restaurant she had in mind* assumes you can pick it out, *a certain restaurant* keeps it back. Against bare *certain*, the split is number and nothing else — singular takes the article (*a certain question*), plural and uncountable refuse it (*certain questions*, *certain evidence*). Against the **adjective** *certain* meaning "sure," which is stressed, follows *be*, and takes degree modifiers (*I'm quite certain*): the determiner does none of that, and *the restaurant is certain* cannot mean "it is a particular one." Against *a particular*, which singles one out with no suggestion that anything is being withheld, and which goes under *no* where this frame cannot: *no particular reason* is idiomatic and *no certain reason* is not English. Against dismissive singular [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some): *Some guy called* says the speaker neither knows nor cares who, and *A certain guy called* says the exact opposite. Against [such-and-such (a)](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#such-and-such-a): *such-and-such a date* means nobody has picked one yet, *a certain date* means somebody has and will not say.
**Variants:** *a certain amount of*, *a certain number of*, *a certain someone*, *a certain something*, *to a certain extent*. There is no *an* variant. The *certain* half has an entry of its own at [certain](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#certain), where the bare plural and uncountable uses live.

Marks a singular noun as one the speaker could name and deliberately does not — indefinite to the listener, definite to the speaker.

**1. One particular thing, withheld.** The core use. The speaker has a specific referent in mind and declines to identify it.

1. She had *a certain* restaurant in mind but wouldn't say which one.
2. There's *a certain* member of this committee who has never once read the packet.

**2. Before a personal name.** The fuller version of the plain *a* at [a](#a) sense 6: the name is supplied, and the frame says the listener is not expected to know the person. This is the shape a complaint, a report, or a piece of gossip reaches for.

3. *A certain* Mr. Kowalski has been calling the front desk since eight this morning.
4. The complaint was filed by *a certain* Dr. Reyes, who then declined to be interviewed.

**3. A quality that can be felt but not named.** With an abstract uncountable noun, where the article hedges instead of counting. This is the sense that keeps the frame alive in reviews, obituaries, and literary prose.

5. The old station has *a certain* charm, run down as it is.
6. There was *a certain* menace in how politely he said it.

**4. In the fixed quantity phrases.** *A certain amount of* before an uncountable noun and *a certain number of* before a plural, both with obligatory *of*, plus the frozen adverbial *to a certain extent*.

7. *A certain* amount of delay was built into the schedule from the start.
8. *A certain* number of claims come back rejected every month no matter who files them.
9. To *a certain* extent that's true, but it leaves the financing out of it.

**5. Coy and insinuating.** *A certain someone* and *a certain something* point at a person or a quality everyone listening can already guess at.

10. *A certain* someone forgot whose anniversary it was on Tuesday.
11. He has *a certain* something that never shows up on the stat sheet.

### an

**Pronunciation:** weak /ən/ &middot; *uhn* &mdash; stressed /æn/ &middot; *AN*. The weak form is a schwa plus /n/, identical to reduced *and*, which is why *fish and chips* and *fish an' chips* sound the same. The /n/ links forward onto the noun: *an apple* is "uh-NAPple," *an hour* is "uh-NOWer." Stressed /æn/ is heard only in correction and dictation.
**Русский:** тот же неопределённый артикль перед гласным звуком — выбор по звуку, а не по букве
**Type:** article (indefinite) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — the same restriction as [a](#a), of which *an* is the pre-vocalic form. Never a plural (*an apples*), never an uncountable (*an information*, *an advice*, *an equipment*, *an evidence*). Exactly as with *a*, an uncountable that has been re-cut as a portion or a type can take it: *an oil* (a variety of cooking oil), *an ice* (a serving), *an anger that surprised him* (a specific instance of it).
**With *of*:** none of its own; identical behavior to [a](#a) inside partitives (*an inch of rain*) and in the double genitive (*an uncle of mine*).
**Pattern:** *an* [vowel-initial singular countable noun] &middot; *an* [vowel-initial adjective] [noun] &middot; [so / too / as / how] [adjective] *an* [noun] (*too odd an answer*) &middot; [such / what / quite / half] *an* [noun] &middot; [amount] *an* [hour / ounce / inch]
**Contrast:** Against [a](#a) there is no difference of meaning whatsoever — the two are one word with two shapes, selected by the sound that follows. The traps run in both directions: ✗ *vowel letters that take *a*** (*a university, a union, a uniform, a used car, a European, a eulogy, a U-turn, a UFO, a one-time offer, a once-in-a-lifetime chance*) and ✗ *consonant letters that take *an*** (*an hour, an honest mistake, an MBA, an FBI agent, an X-ray, an SUV, an RSVP, an 18-wheeler*). Against British-influenced *an historic*: American English pronounces the *h* in *historic, hotel, heroic, humble*, so American English writes *a historic decision*.
**Variants:** *a* before a consonant sound. *An* stands before a vowel **sound**, not a vowel letter, and the choice is made by the very next word, adjective included: *an old truck* but *a used truck*, *an ugly sweater* but *a useful sweater*. Silent *h* takes *an* (*an hour*, *an honor*, *an heir*, and in American English *an herb*); *u* pronounced /ju/ takes *a* (*a university*, *a European*); initial *o* pronounced /w/ takes *a* (*a one-way street*).

Marks a singular countable noun as one of a class, before a word beginning with a vowel sound.

1. She waited *an* hour and forty minutes at the DMV.
2. He has *an* MBA and *an* opinion about everything.
3. That's *an* honest answer, even if it isn't the one you wanted.
4. *An* 18-wheeler jackknifed across two lanes of I-70.
5. They want *an* X-ray before they'll write anything.
6. Add *an* ounce of the syrup, not *a* whole cup.
7. I'd call it *an* unusual choice rather than *a* useless one.
8. *An* SUV with Michigan plates has been parked there all week.

## A or *an*: the test is sound, not spelling

Say the next word out loud and listen to its first sound. Vowel sound → *an*. Consonant sound → *a*. Every difficult case in English is a word whose spelling and sound disagree.

| The spelling suggests | The first sound is | Article | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| a vowel (*u-, eu-*) | the consonant /j/ | **a** | *a university, a union, a unit, a uniform, a utility bill, a used car, a useful tool, a European, a eulogy, a ewe, a U-turn, a UFO, a UN resolution* |
| a vowel (*o-*) | the consonant /w/ | **a** | *a one-way street, a one-time fee, a once-in-a-lifetime trip, a one-bedroom apartment* |
| a consonant (*h-*) | a vowel — the *h* is silent | **an** | *an hour, an honor, an honest mistake, an heir, an heirloom, an herb* (American), *an hourly wage, an honorarium* |
| a consonant, read as a **letter name** | the vowel in the letter's name | **an** | *an MBA* (em), *an FBI agent* (ef), *an X-ray* (ex), *an SUV* (es), *an RSVP* (ar), *an NFL team* (en), *an HMO* (aitch), *an L-shaped desk* (el), *an S-curve* (es) |
| a consonant, read as a **word** | the consonant | **a** | *a NASA engineer, a NATO summit, a FEMA trailer, a FIFA ruling, a PIN number* |
| a numeral | whatever the number is said as | either | *an 8-ounce cup, an 11th-hour deal, an 18-wheeler, an $80 fine* — but *a 40-hour week, a $50 ticket, a 1-in-4 chance* ("one in four") |
| *h-* that is actually pronounced | /h/ | **a** | *a historic decision, a hotel, a heroic effort, a humble request, a hospital* |

Two American notes. **Herb** has a silent *h* in the United States, so *an herb garden* is correct American and *a herb garden* is correct British. **Historic** has a pronounced *h* in both countries; *an historic* is a British literary survival that reads as affected in American writing.

## The definite article

### the

**Pronunciation:** /ðə/ &middot; *thuh* before a consonant sound &mdash; /ði/ &middot; *thee* before a vowel sound &mdash; stressed /ðiː/ &middot; *THEE* for emphasis. Same rule as *a/an*, run on sound: *the car*, *the university*, *the one-way street* are all /ðə/; *the apple*, *the hour*, *the FBI*, *the MBA* are all /ði/. See the [pronunciation table](#the-two-pronunciations-of-the).
**Русский:** определённый артикль — русского эквивалента нет: слушатель уже может понять, о чём речь
**Type:** article (definite) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **every noun type there is** — singular countable (*the chair*), plural countable (*the chairs*), uncountable (*the water*), and the subset of proper nouns listed in [The with proper nouns](#the-with-proper-nouns). This universality is what makes it dangerous: *the* is never blocked by countability, so a learner who reaches for it whenever the thing feels definite will overuse it. The block on *the* is not grammatical but informational — it is wrong whenever the listener could not say **which one**.
**With *of*:** *the* takes no *of*-phrase of its own but is the article the *of*-constructions are built around. It is required inside partitives (*most of the students*, *half of the pie*, *none of the money*), inside *of*-genitives (*the works of Melville*, *the top of the page*), and inside *of*-named institutions (*the University of Michigan*, *the Gulf of Mexico*). Predeterminers stack in front of it: *all (of) the students*, *both (of) the windows*, *half (of) the class* — *of* optional before *the*, **required** before a pronoun (*all of them*, never *all them*).
**Pattern:** *the* [noun] &middot; *the* [adjective] [noun] &middot; *the* [noun] [that / which / who clause] &middot; *the* [noun] *of* [noun] &middot; *the* [superlative or ordinal] [noun] &middot; *the* [adjective] as a plural class (*the unemployed*) &middot; [all / both / half / double / twice] *the* [noun] &middot; *by the* [unit] (*by the pound*)
**Contrast:** Against [a/an](#a), *the* claims the listener can identify the referent; *a* claims they cannot. Against the [zero article](#zero-article-ø), *the* narrows to a known instance where Ø leaves the noun general: *I like Ø dogs* is about dogs as a class, *I like the dogs* is about particular dogs already in play — and this is the single most damaging transfer error for speakers of Spanish, French, Italian, German, Greek, and Arabic, whose languages use the definite article for exactly the generic that English marks with nothing. Against [this / that](02-demonstratives.md#this), *the* identifies without pointing: *the report* assumes you know which, *this report* physically or mentally singles it out. Against a [possessive](03-possessives.md#my), only one central determiner is allowed, so *the my office* is impossible — you choose one. The only words that may stand **in front of** *the* are the [predeterminers](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#predeterminers-the-slot-in-front-of-the-article), which occupy a slot of their own ahead of the central slot: *all the students*, *both the windows*, *half the class*, *twice the price*, *double the fine*. *Such* is not one of them — it selects *a* or a bare noun (*such a mess*, *such problems*, *such nonsense*) and never *the*, so *such the mess* is impossible.
**Variants:** /ðə/, /ði/, and stressed /ðiː/ are one word in three shapes. In relaxed American speech /ðə/ creeps in before vowels with a small catch in the throat where the /i/ would be ("thuh ʔapple"); that is normal and unremarkable, but /ði/ is never wrong, so it is the safer habit. Mock-archaic [ye](#ye) and eye-dialect [th'](#th) are separate entries.

Marks a noun as one the listener can already pick out.

**1. Second mention.** The noun has been introduced; *the* now says "that one."

1. A dog followed us home. *The* dog had no collar.
2. She sent over a draft and a spreadsheet. *The* spreadsheet was the useful part.

**2. Inferable from what came before.** No previous mention is needed if the referent follows from one — a house has a kitchen, a car has an engine, a book has an author.

3. We rented a house in Ypsilanti and *the* kitchen was unusable.
4. I started the novel last night and gave up by *the* third chapter.

**3. Unique in the world, or unique here.** One of a kind either globally or in the situation you and the listener share.

5. *The* sun was already down by the time we got to *the* car.
6. Can you get *the* door? I'm on *the* phone.

**4. Made unique by what follows.** A relative clause, an *of*-phrase, a prepositional phrase, or a *that*-clause that narrows the noun to one.

7. *The* man in the gray coat has been standing there since noon.
8. Read *the* part of the contract that covers early termination.
9. *The* fact that nobody flagged it is the real problem.

**5. Superlatives, ordinals, and uniqueness adjectives.** *Best, worst, first, third, last, next, only, same, main, right, wrong, very* force *the* because each of them narrows to one.

10. That's *the* best price you're going to find in this zip code.
11. Get off at *the* third exit — it's *the* only one with a light.
12. She gave me *the* wrong number twice.

**6. Institutions and things everyone in the culture knows.** *The president, the mayor, the news, the DMV, the internet, the flu, the market, the interstate.*

13. It was on *the* news before *the* mayor's office confirmed anything.
14. Half his department is out with *the* flu.

**7. Generic — the class as a whole.** Formal, written, and restricted to singular countable nouns; also the *the* + adjective construction, which names a class of people or an abstraction and takes a plural verb for people.

15. *The* smartphone changed what an American commute looks like.
16. *The* unemployed are the last group anyone budgets for.

**8. Body parts after a preposition.** Where most languages use a possessive, English uses *the*.

17. He got hit on *the* head with a foul ball.
18. She grabbed me by *the* arm and pointed.

**9. Musical instruments.** In the playing sense, an instrument takes *the* where a sport takes nothing.

19. She's been playing *the* cello since she was seven.

**10. Periods, decades, and centuries.**

20. Most of that neighborhood went up in *the* 1920s.
21. Nothing about *the* Depression is in the curriculum anymore.

**11. Proper nouns of the *the*-taking groups.** Rivers, oceans, seas, gulfs, canals, deserts, mountain ranges, island groups, plural or common-noun country names, newspapers, hotels, theaters, ships, museums, and family names in the plural. Full list at [The with proper nouns](#the-with-proper-nouns).

22. We crossed *the* Mississippi twice before we hit *the* Ozarks.
23. *The* Sandovals are in *the* Bahamas until Sunday.

**12. Units of sale and measure after *by*.** *By the pound, by the hour, by the dozen, by the week.*

24. They pay by *the* hour and round down.
25. Crab legs are sold by *the* pound at the counter in back.

### the (adjective as noun)

**Pronunciation:** /ðə/ &middot; *thuh* before a consonant sound &mdash; /ði/ &middot; *thee* before a vowel sound (*the elderly*, *the unemployed*, *the injured*, *the accused*)
**Русский:** the + прилагательное — класс людей (the poor «бедные») или абстракция (the unknown «неведомое»)
**Type:** article (definite), in the substantivized-adjective construction &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the standard form in journalism, policy writing, and statistics
**Goes with:** **a bare adjective or participle standing where a noun would, with no noun after it** — *the rich, the poor, the young, the elderly, the homeless, the unemployed, the uninsured, the injured, the accused, the deceased, the wounded, the living, the dead* — and **nationality adjectives that have no plural noun form**, which is the ones ending in a sibilant — /s, z, ʃ, tʃ/: *the French, the Dutch, the Spanish, the British, the English, the Welsh, the Irish, the Swiss, the Chinese, the Japanese, the Portuguese*. There are exactly two readings and they take different verbs: **a plural class of people**, with a **plural verb** (*the poor are*, *the French take*), or **a singular abstraction** with an abstract adjective, taking a **singular verb** (*the impossible takes longer*, *the unknown is*). It **cannot take a plural *-s*** (*the unemployeds*, *the Frenches*) and **has no singular member form** — one of them is *a poor person*, *a French person* or *a Frenchman*, never *a poor* or *a French*.
**With *of*:** takes an *of*-phrase when the class is narrowed or ranked: *the poorest of the poor*, *the youngest of the wounded*. Nothing forces one.
**Pattern:** *the* [adjective] + **plural verb** (*The unemployed **are** counted monthly*) &middot; *the* [nationality adjective] + **plural verb** (*The Dutch **build** for water*) &middot; *the* [abstract adjective] + **singular verb** (*The impossible **takes** longer*) &middot; *the* [comparative or superlative] as a class (*the more experienced*, *the very poorest*)
**Contrast:** Against ordinary *the* + noun, there is no noun here at all — which is why *the unemployed* and *the unemployed people* mean the same thing while *the unemployeds* means nothing. Against the [zero article](#zero-article-ø) plural generic, *Ø poor people have fewer options* and *the poor have fewer options* both name the class, but the second treats it as a fixed social category, which is why policy prose prefers it. Against nationality words that really are **nouns** — *American, Canadian, German, Italian, Mexican, Israeli, Egyptian, Norwegian* — those pluralize normally and take Ø for the generic: *Ø Americans work too much*; *the Americans work too much* can only mean particular ones. The split is phonological, not political: a nationality word ending in a sibilant has no plural, so it must borrow this construction.
**Variants:** *the* + comparative or superlative works the same way (*the fittest survive*, *the less fortunate*). Note that the same adjective with the [zero article](#zero-article-ø) and a real noun after it is always available and often plainer: *Ø homeless veterans* rather than *the homeless*.

Turns an adjective into a noun naming the whole class of people who have that quality, or naming the quality itself as an abstraction.

**1. A class of people, with a plural verb.** The commonest use, and the one that trips up agreement.

1. *The* unemployed are the last group anyone budgets for.
2. *The* elderly in that county have no public transportation at all.
3. Emergency rooms are required to stabilize *the* uninsured before they ask anything else.

**2. Nationalities with no plural noun form.**

4. *The* Dutch build for water the way Americans build for cars.
5. *The* French take August seriously and nobody apologizes for it.

**3. An abstraction, with a singular verb.**

6. *The* impossible takes a little longer and costs a great deal more.
7. He has always been drawn to *the* unknown, which is why he is broke.

**4. Comparative and superlative classes.**

8. The pilot program was written for *the* very poorest and reaches almost none of them.

Every road to definiteness](#why-the-every-road-to-definiteness)

### the (degree)

**Pronunciation:** /ðə/ &middot; *thuh* &mdash; /ði/ &middot; *thee* before a vowel sound (*the earlier the better*)
**Русский:** чем… тем — не артикль, а наречие степени при сравнительной: the sooner, the better
**Type:** adverb of degree — historically **not** the article but the Old English instrumental *þȳ*, "by that much," which is why it can sit in front of a comparative where no noun is possible &middot; **Position:** not a determiner; it modifies a comparative &middot; **Register:** neutral, common in speech and proverbs
**Goes with:** **comparative adjectives and comparative adverbs only** — *the sooner, the more, the less, the better, the worse, the bigger, the harder*. Never a noun on its own, never a positive-degree adjective (*the soon*, *the good* in this sense are impossible), and never a superlative.
**With *of*:** takes none.
**Pattern:** *The* [comparative], *the* [comparative] (*The sooner, the better*) &middot; *The* [comparative] [clause], *the* [comparative] [clause] &middot; *all the* [comparative] &middot; *none the* [comparative] &middot; *so much the* [comparative]
**Contrast:** Identical in spelling and sound to the article [the](#the) and unrelated to it in origin and in job. The test is what follows: a noun phrase means you have the article (*the better offer*), a bare comparative means you have this word (*all the better*). Note that the correlative frame is a paired construction — both halves need their *the*, and the verbs are usually deleted (*The bigger the crowd, the longer the wait*).

Measures a change in one thing against a change in another, or against a cause just mentioned.

**1. The correlative pair.** Two clauses, each opened by *the* plus a comparative; the first states a condition, the second its consequence.

1. *The* sooner we leave, *the* better.
2. *The* more you practice, *the* easier it gets.
3. *The* bigger the crowd, *the* longer the wait at the counter.

**2. *All the* + comparative.** "By that much more."

4. If they cancel, *all the* better — I wasn't going anyway.
5. The delay made him *all the* angrier.

**3. *None the* + comparative.** "Not at all more," almost always with *wiser*, *worse*, *better*.

6. He explained it twice and I was *none the* wiser.
7. She rode out the whole thing *none the* worse for it.

**See also:** [the](#the), [the (stressed)](#the-stressed), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### the (stressed)

**Pronunciation:** /ðiː/ &middot; *THEE* — long, fully stressed, and used before consonants and vowels alike (*THEE place*, *THEE only option*)
**Русский:** тот самый — ударное the: не «какое-то место», а «то самое место»
**Type:** article (definite), emphatic &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** spoken and informal written; in print it is shown by italics, small caps, or capitals
**Goes with:** the same nouns as ordinary [the](#the) — singular countable, plural countable, uncountable — plus, distinctively, **personal names**, where unstressed *the* would be impossible: *Are you THE Sandra Cisneros?*
**With *of*:** same behavior as ordinary *the*; the stress does not change the syntax.
**Pattern:** *THE* [noun] (*the* place, *the* guy, *the* problem) &middot; *THE* [proper name] (*the* Sandra Cisneros) &middot; predicative: [X] *is* THE [noun]
**Contrast:** Against unstressed [the](#the), the meaning shifts from "the one you can identify" to "the one that counts — the definitive, the famous, the only one worth naming." Against [a](#a), stressed *the* is the direct rebuttal: *It's not a barbecue place, it's THE barbecue place*. Do not confuse the stressed vowel with the ordinary pre-vowel /ði/, which is short and unstressed; the emphatic form is long and carries the sentence's stress.

Singles a referent out as the definitive or best-known one of its kind.

1. It's not *a* taco place — it's *the* taco place.
2. Wait, you worked with *the* Bill Withers?
3. That was *the* moment the whole negotiation turned.
4. She isn't just an authority on water rights; she's *the* authority.
5. Cleveland in July is *the* reason people buy air conditioners.
6. This is *the* question the committee keeps refusing to answer.
7. Those aren't just good tires — they're *the* tires every shop in town puts on a truck like that. *(plural countable)*
8. It isn't evidence; it's *the* evidence, and the whole case rests on it. *(uncountable)*

**See also:** [the](#the), [a](#a), [the (degree)](#the-degree), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this)

## The two pronunciations of *the*

| Before | Pronunciation | Respelling | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| a **consonant sound** | /ðə/ | *thuh* | *the car, the house, the university* (/j/), *the one-way street* (/w/), *the European Union, the historic district, the NASA budget* |
| a **vowel sound** | /ði/ | *thee* | *the apple, the office, the hour* (silent *h*), *the FBI, the MBA, the X-ray, the 8:15 train, the only exit* |
| **any** word, for emphasis | /ðiː/ | *THEE* | *the* place to eat &middot; Are you *the* Sandra Cisneros? |

The test is the same one that decides *a* versus *an*, applied to whatever word comes next — noun or adjective. *The old truck* is /ði/; *the used truck* is /ðə/. In casual American speech /ðə/ creeps in before vowels with a glottal catch in place of the /i/; that is normal, but /ði/ is never wrong, and it is what listeners expect in careful speech, broadcasting, and anything read aloud.

## Why *the*? Every road to definiteness

*The* is licensed by the listener's ability to identify the referent. There are exactly this many ways to earn that.

| Road to *the* | Example |
|---|---|
| Second mention | A dog followed us home. **The** dog had no collar. |
| Inferable from a previous mention | We rented a house. **The** kitchen was unusable. |
| Unique in the world | **the** sun, **the** equator, **the** internet, **the** Constitution |
| Unique in the situation | Can you get **the** door? / She's in **the** kitchen. |
| Narrowed by a following modifier | **the** man in the gray coat, **the** file I sent, **the** top of the page, **the** fact that he lied |
| Superlative | **the** best price, **the** cheapest flight |
| Ordinal | **the** third try, **the** last stop |
| Uniqueness adjective | **the** only exit, **the** same answer, **the** next train, **the** wrong number, **the** main issue |
| Shared cultural knowledge | **the** president, **the** mayor, **the** DMV, **the** news, **the** flu |
| Class named by an adjective | **the** rich, **the** unemployed, **the** French, **the** impossible |
| Generic species or invention (formal) | **The** smartphone changed everything. |
| Body part after a preposition | hit him on **the** head, grabbed me by **the** arm, looked me in **the** eye |
| Musical instrument being played | plays **the** cello, took up **the** banjo |
| Decade, century, or historical period | **the** 1980s, **the** twentieth century, **the** Depression |
| Unit of sale or pay | sold by **the** pound, paid by **the** hour |
| Proper nouns of the *the*-taking groups | **the** Rockies, **the** Pacific, **the** Hudson, **the** Netherlands, **the** Washington Post |

Two notes on the edges. **Superlatives can lose *the*** when they are predicative or adverbial: *This route is (the) fastest*, *She's happiest on a job site*, *He ran fastest*. And ***a* + ordinal** is a real alternative with a different meaning: *the third try* is the one after the second; *a third try* is one more attempt, and *a second chance* is an additional chance rather than a numbered one.

## *The* with proper nouns

Most proper nouns take the [zero article](#zero-article-with-proper-nouns). The exceptions are systematic: **plural names, and names built on a common noun** — *river, gulf, republic, times, plaza* — take *the*.

| Takes *the* | Takes Ø |
|---|---|
| Rivers, seas, oceans, gulfs, canals, straits: **the** Hudson, **the** Pacific, **the** Gulf of Mexico, **the** Erie Canal | Lakes and bays: Ø Lake Michigan, Ø Hudson Bay |
| Mountain ranges: **the** Rockies, **the** Sierra Nevada, **the** Appalachians | Single peaks: Ø Mount Rainier, Ø Pikes Peak, Ø Denali |
| Island groups: **the** Florida Keys, **the** Bahamas, **the** Aleutians | Single islands: Ø Manhattan, Ø Maui, Ø Key West |
| Deserts and named regions: **the** Mojave, **the** Midwest, **the** South, **the** Bay Area | Continents, countries, states, cities: Ø Asia, Ø Japan, Ø Ohio, Ø Seattle |
| Plural or common-noun countries: **the** United States, **the** Netherlands, **the** Philippines, **the** Dominican Republic | Ordinary country names: Ø Canada, Ø France, Ø Mexico |
| *Of*-phrase institutions: **the** University of Michigan, **the** Museum of Modern Art, **the** Bank of America | Name-first institutions: Ø Michigan State University, Ø Stanford, Ø Chase Bank |
| Newspapers: **the** Washington Post, **the** Chicago Tribune, **the** New York Times — and the magazines whose titles carry it: **the** Atlantic, **the** New Yorker | Most magazines: Ø Time, Ø Newsweek, Ø Vogue, Ø Wired |
| Hotels, theaters, ships, bridges, buildings: **the** Plaza, **the** Fillmore, **the** Titanic, **the** Golden Gate Bridge, **the** Empire State Building | Airports, stations, parks, streets: Ø LaGuardia, Ø Penn Station, Ø Central Park, Ø Fifth Avenue |
| Family names as a group: **the** Sandovals, **the** Kennedys | Individual people: Ø Dr. Sandoval, Ø Robert Kennedy |
| Political and historical entities built on a noun: **the** Senate, **the** Supreme Court, **the** Renaissance, **the** Civil War | Holidays, months, days: Ø Thanksgiving, Ø July, Ø Tuesday |

A few names carry *the* as part of the name itself and capitalize it: **The** Hague, **The** Gambia. *The Bronx* keeps its article but normally lowercases it. And several older *the*-forms have been dropped in current American usage: write *Ukraine*, *Sudan*, *Lebanon* — *the Ukraine* is dated and, in the case of Ukraine, actively objected to.

## The zero article

### zero article (Ø)

**Pronunciation:** silent — Ø has no phonetic form of its own. What you hear is the noun starting with no unstressed syllable in front of it: *Ø dogs bark* /dɔɡz ˈbɑrk/ against *the dogs bark* /ðə ˈdɔɡz ˈbɑrk/. Learning to hear the missing schwa is most of learning to hear articles at all.
**Русский:** нулевой артикль — пустой слот при мн. числе и неисчисляемых: общее, неограниченное
**Type:** article (zero / null) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns.** *Ø chairs*, *Ø water*, *Ø information*, *Ø police cars*. **Never a singular countable noun in ordinary prose** — *I need pen*, *Cat is sleeping*, and *She is teacher* are all ungrammatical, and a singular countable must take *a/an*, *the*, or another central determiner. The exceptions are not free: they are the specific frames catalogued in the eleven entries that follow this one (proper nouns, institutions, meals, languages, sports, transport, times, illnesses, fixed phrases, block language, roles).
**With *of*:** a zero-marked noun takes an *of*-phrase freely (*Ø plenty of time*, *Ø millions of dollars*, *Ø half of what he said*). Predeterminers may stand directly in front of it with no *of* at all — *all Ø children*, *both Ø parents* — and adding *of* then requires a following determiner: *all of the children*, never *all of children*.
**Pattern:** Ø [plural countable noun] &middot; Ø [uncountable noun] &middot; Ø [adjective] [plural or uncountable noun] &middot; *there is / there are* Ø [noun]
**Contrast:** Against [the](#the): Ø is general or new, *the* is identifiable. *I like Ø dogs* is about the species; *I like the dogs* is about specific dogs you already know of. Against [a/an](#a): Ø is what *a* becomes when the noun goes plural or uncountable — *a chair* → *Ø chairs*, *a fact* → *Ø information*. Against [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some): unstressed *some* /səm/ names an indefinite but bounded quantity, while Ø leaves the noun unbounded — *We need Ø volunteers* (as a category) against *We need some volunteers* (a certain number of them). In questions and negatives the counterpart is *any*: *Is there Ø parking?* is about the general availability, *Is there any parking?* about whether even a little exists.

Leaves the determiner slot empty, which marks a plural or uncountable noun as general, unbounded, or newly introduced.

**1. Generic plural and uncountable.** The default way to talk about a whole class or substance — and the **only** generic available to an uncountable noun.

1. *Elevators* make me nervous.
2. *Rice* is cheaper by the bag.
3. *Honesty* costs you something now and saves you more later.

**2. Indefinite and non-generic.** The plural counterpart of *a/an*: something real but not yet identified.

4. There were *police cars* parked all down the block.
5. We bought *chairs*, *lamps*, and a rug in one afternoon.

**3. Abstract nouns used absolutely.** Abstractions take Ø unless something narrows them. *Life is hard* is general; *the life of a claims adjuster* is narrowed and takes *the*.

6. *Life* in Phoenix takes some getting used to.
7. She has *experience* with Medicaid billing and *patience* with the people who don't.

**4. In questions and negatives.** The slot stays empty in both, and this is where the choice against *any* is live: the bare noun asks about the category in general, *any* asks whether even a little of it exists.

8. Is there *parking* behind the building, or is it all permit spots?
9. We don't have *chairs* for that many people and I'm not renting any.

**See also:** [a](#a), [the](#the), [Three ways to be generic](#three-ways-to-be-generic), [The decision procedure](#the-decision-procedure), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### zero article in block language

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)* — Ø marks the empty slot in the patterns below and is never written or said
**Русский:** нулевой артикль в телеграфном стиле — заголовки, вывески, рецепты; в обычной прозе нельзя
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** **block language** — headlines, signs, labels, captions, notes, listings, recipes, instructions, telegraphic reports. Not available in running prose.
**Goes with:** **any noun at all, including singular countable nouns** — this is the one systematic place in English where *Ø mayor* and *Ø elevator* are correct. The license comes from the register, not from the noun.
**With *of*:** unaffected; *of*-phrases survive intact (*Ø Board of Education Cuts Ø Budget*).
**Pattern:** Ø [subject noun] [verb] Ø [object noun] (headline) &middot; Ø [noun] — Ø [instruction] (sign) &middot; [imperative verb] Ø [noun] (recipe) &middot; Ø [noun phrase], Ø [noun phrase], Ø [noun phrase] (listing)
**Contrast:** Block language is a register, not a permission slip. The article returns the moment the same content is written as a sentence: the headline reads *Mayor Signs Bill*, and the story under it reads *The mayor signed a bill*. Learners who generalize from headlines produce *Mayor signed bill yesterday*, which is wrong in prose. Note that block language also deletes forms of *be* and relative pronouns, so an article-free headline is usually missing more than its articles.

Strips the determiner slot for compression, in registers where space is short and the reader supplies the grammar.

1. Storm Knocks Out Power Across Three Counties. (newspaper headline)
2. Elevator out of service — please use stairs. (posted sign)
3. Add flour to butter and whisk until smooth. (recipe step)
4. Suspect fled on foot; canvass of area ongoing. (police report)
5. Meeting moved to Thursday — room TBD. (note left on a desk)
6. Studio apartment, second floor, laundry in building. (rental listing)
7. Insert card, then remove quickly. (card-reader instruction)

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [a](#a), [the](#the), [The decision procedure](#the-decision-procedure)

### zero article in fixed phrases and binomials

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль в застывших оборотах — hand in hand «рука об руку», by heart «наизусть»
**Type:** article (zero) in frozen idiom &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular nouns locked inside a fixed frame.** Most are **countable** nouns that outside the frame would be forced to take an article (*hand, face, side, word, cover, knife, purpose*); a handful are **uncountable** and are listed here only because the whole phrase is frozen (*war, peace, danger, charge, touch, sale*). Never a plural inside the frame — *hands in hands*, *from covers to covers*, *husbands and wives* (in the idiom sense) all break it. Four frames account for nearly all of them: the repeated-noun binomial (*hand in hand, face to face, side by side, day after day, word for word, back to back, step by step, arm in arm, year after year*), the *from* X *to* X frame (*from door to door, from cover to cover, from start to finish, from coast to coast, from head to toe*), the paired-noun conjunction (*husband and wife, mother and child, knife and fork, cause and effect, night and day*), and the bare prepositional idiom (*at war, at peace, at sea, in debt, in danger, in charge, in touch, in stock, on file, on sale, on purpose, on board, by heart, out of hand, under way*). The list is closed — you may recognize new members but you may not coin them.
**With *of*:** the frame decides. Some idioms contain *of* already (*out of hand*, *out of stock*, *out of order*); inserting *of* anywhere else breaks them.
**Pattern:** [noun] [preposition] [same noun] &middot; *from* Ø [noun] *to* Ø [noun] &middot; Ø [noun] *and* Ø [noun] &middot; [preposition] Ø [noun]
**Contrast:** The same nouns behave normally the moment they leave the frame: *hand in hand* but *He raised a hand*; *by heart* but *the heart of the problem*; *at sea* (afloat, or bewildered) but *at the sea* (at the shore). Compare also *in charge* (holding authority) with *in the charge of* (under someone's care), and *on sale* (discounted, American) with *for sale* (available to buy).

Keeps a singular countable noun bare inside a frozen expression, where the phrase has a meaning of its own and the article slot has been worn away.

1. They came down the aisle *hand in hand*.
2. I read the whole report *from cover to cover* and found nothing new.
3. The two of them have gone at it *day after day* since March.
4. Quote it *word for word* or don't quote it at all.
5. The meeting got *out of hand* about ten minutes in.
6. She learned the entire statute *by heart*.
7. We sat *face to face* for two hours and settled it.
8. The company has been *in debt* since the second store opened.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [zero article with institutions](#zero-article-with-institutions), [zero article with transport and communication](#zero-article-with-transport-and-communication), [the](#the)

### zero article with illnesses and conditions

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при названиях болезней — has diabetes «болен диабетом»; но a cold, the flu
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **uncountable disease names** — *diabetes, pneumonia, cancer, hepatitis, asthma, arthritis, anemia, COVID, tuberculosis, dementia, strep*. Not with the countable minor ailments, which take *a/an* (*a cold, a headache, a fever, a sore throat, a rash, an earache, a stomachache, a concussion, a heart attack, a stroke*), and not with the small *the*-group (*the flu, the measles, the mumps, the chickenpox, the hiccups, the chills*).
**With *of*:** takes none of its own; *a case of Ø pneumonia* and *a history of Ø diabetes* put the *of* on the measuring noun.
**Pattern:** [have / has / had] Ø [disease] &middot; [be diagnosed with / be treated for / die of] Ø [disease] &middot; Ø [disease] *runs in the family*
**Contrast:** Three groups, no logic connecting them, so they must be learned as lists. Ø for most named diseases, *a/an* for short countable episodes, *the* for a handful of older folk-medical names. American usage is drifting: *the measles* and *the mumps* are giving way to bare *measles* and *mumps*, but *the flu* is fixed and *a cold* never loses its article. When a disease name is modified, *the* comes back in the ordinary way: *the diabetes she was diagnosed with in 2019*.

Leaves a named disease bare, because the name of a disease is a mass term rather than a countable episode.

1. He was diagnosed with *diabetes* at thirty-one.
2. She's been out all week with *pneumonia*.
3. Both of his parents died of *cancer*.
4. *Asthma* runs in the family on her mother's side.
5. Half the office has *COVID* again.
6. The knee pain turned out to be *arthritis*, not a torn meniscus.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [a](#a), [the](#the)

### zero article with institutions

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при учреждении как занятии — in school «учится», in jail «сидит»
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a **small closed set of singular countable nouns** naming an institution or a routine, used for its purpose rather than as a building: *school, class, college, church, court, jail, prison, camp, bed, work, home, town, sea, campus*. The set does not extend by analogy — *at Ø library*, *at Ø office*, *at Ø store*, *at Ø gym*, *at Ø airport*, *at Ø bank* are all wrong and take *the*.
**With *of*:** takes none. Adding an *of*-phrase forces the ordinary article back: *the school of hard knocks*, *the court of appeals*.
**Pattern:** [preposition] Ø [institution noun] — *at Ø school*, *in Ø class*, *in Ø bed*, *to Ø work*, *in Ø jail*, *out of Ø town*, *at Ø sea*, *on Ø campus* &middot; *go / come / be* Ø *home* (with no preposition at all)
**Contrast:** Adding *the* switches from **purpose** to **place**: *He's in Ø jail* means he is serving time, *He's at the jail* means he is at the building, possibly visiting; *She's in Ø school* means enrolled, *She's at the school* means physically present. *Home* is the odd member — American English says *go Ø home* and *be Ø home*, with no article **and** no preposition, while *at home* keeps the preposition. American English also keeps *the* in three phrases where British English drops it: *in the hospital*, *at the university*, *in the future*. See [American and British article splits](#american-and-british-article-splits).

Leaves an institution noun bare when the phrase names an activity or a state rather than a location.

1. He's been in *bed* since Thursday with a fever.
2. Both kids are still in *school* — one in middle school, one in high school.
3. I'll head to *work* early and beat the beltway.
4. She spent eight months in *jail* before the charges were dropped.
5. They collect the phones during *class* and hand them back at the bell.
6. We're out of *town* until the ninth.
7. Nobody was *home*, so I left the box on the porch.
8. He was at *sea* for six weeks and came back with a beard.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [zero article in fixed phrases and binomials](#zero-article-in-fixed-phrases-and-binomials), [the](#the), [American and British article splits](#american-and-british-article-splits)

### zero article with languages and school subjects

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при языках и учебных предметах — «говорит по-испански», «изучает экономику»
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **language names** (*Spanish, Mandarin, Arabic, Navajo, American Sign Language*) and **names of academic subjects and fields** (*calculus, chemistry, economics, art history, computer science, nursing, constitutional law*). Both behave as uncountable proper-ish nouns. Note that the subject nouns ending in *-ics* are singular despite the *s*: *Ø economics is brutal*.
**With *of*:** takes none of its own, and an *of*-phrase pulls *the* onto the noun: *the history of Rome*, *the economics of the deal*, *the Spanish of northern New Mexico*.
**Pattern:** [speak / learn / teach / study / take / major in / minor in] Ø [language or subject] &middot; [in / into / from] Ø [language] &middot; Ø [subject] *is* [adjective]
**Contrast:** Add the word *language* and *the* becomes obligatory: *She speaks Ø Spanish* but *She studies the Spanish language*. Add any modifier and *the* returns in the ordinary way. And beware the same word with *the* plus no noun, which names **people, not a language**: *the Spanish* are Spaniards, *the French* are the French, *the Dutch* are the Dutch — a plural class, not a tongue.

Leaves a language or a field of study bare, because it names a body of knowledge rather than an object.

1. She teaches *Spanish* at a charter school in Tucson.
2. He's majoring in *economics* with a minor in *statistics*.
3. The contract was translated into *Korean* and then back into *English*.
4. *Calculus* wrecked my GPA sophomore year.
5. Everybody in that office speaks at least some *Portuguese*.
6. I took two years of *Latin* and remember none of it.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [zero article with proper nouns](#zero-article-with-proper-nouns), [the](#the)

### zero article with meals

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при названиях приёмов пищи — «завтракать», «за обедом», «после ужина»
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** the **meal nouns** *breakfast, brunch, lunch, dinner, supper*, and by extension *coffee, drinks,* and *dessert* when they name the occasion rather than the substance. These are singular countable nouns going bare in this use only.
**With *of*:** takes none. *A dinner of soup and bread* has re-countable *a* on it, not Ø.
**Pattern:** [have / eat / make / skip / serve] Ø [meal] &middot; [at / before / after / during / over / for] Ø [meal] &middot; Ø [meal] *is at* [time]
**Contrast:** An article comes straight back as soon as the meal is described or singled out as an event: *a big breakfast*, *a late lunch*, *a dinner in her honor*, *the dinner we had in Tulsa*, *The lunch was terrible*. Compare *We had Ø dinner at seven* (the routine meal) with *We had a dinner for the retirees* (an occasion). And note *for Ø lunch* versus *for the lunch*, which would mean a specific catered event.

Leaves a meal name bare when it refers to the daily meal as an institution rather than to a particular one.

1. We had *breakfast* standing at the counter.
2. Let's talk about it over *lunch* on Wednesday.
3. She skipped *dinner* and went straight to bed.
4. *Brunch* is at eleven if you want to come by.
5. Everybody stayed for *dessert* and nobody left before midnight.
6. Call me after *dinner* and we'll go through the numbers.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [a](#a), [the](#the), [zero article with times, seasons, and holidays](#zero-article-with-times-seasons-and-holidays)

### zero article with proper nouns

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при именах собственных — имя само называет, артикль ему не нужен
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular proper names** — people (*Ø Maria*, *Ø Dr. Ahmed*), cities, states, countries, continents (*Ø Denver*, *Ø Ohio*, *Ø Japan*, *Ø Asia*), lakes and single mountains and single islands (*Ø Lake Erie*, *Ø Mount Hood*, *Ø Maui*), streets, parks, airports and stations (*Ø Fifth Avenue*, *Ø Central Park*, *Ø O'Hare*, *Ø Penn Station*), companies and name-first universities (*Ø Boeing*, *Ø Ohio State*), months, days, and holidays (*Ø August*, *Ø Tuesday*, *Ø Thanksgiving*), and planets except *the earth*. **Not** with the *the*-taking groups — rivers, seas, oceans, gulfs, canals, deserts, mountain ranges, island groups, plural or common-noun countries, newspapers, hotels, ships, and *of*-phrase institutions — listed in full at [The with proper nouns](#the-with-proper-nouns).
**With *of*:** a proper name takes no *of*-phrase of its own, but when a common noun is named by *of* plus a place, the **common noun** takes *the* and the name stays bare: *the University of Ø Michigan*, *the Gulf of Ø Mexico*, *the mayor of Ø Cleveland*.
**Pattern:** Ø [proper noun] &middot; Ø [title] [personal name] &middot; Ø [name] [common noun] (*Ø Lake Erie*, *Ø Mount Hood*, *Ø Kennedy Airport*) &middot; [preposition] Ø [place name]
**Contrast:** Modify a proper name and *the* comes back, because the modifier makes the name behave like a common noun: *Ø Shakespeare* but *the Shakespeare of the late plays*; *Ø Paris* but *the Paris I remember*; *Ø Sinatra* but *the young Sinatra*. Pluralize a family name and it also takes *the*: *the Sandovals*. And a name preceded by *a* means "someone of that name, unknown to me" (*a Ms. Okonjo called*) or "a work by" (*a Rothko*).

Leaves a name bare, because a name already identifies its referent and needs no article to do it.

1. *Denver* gets more sun than people expect.
2. *Dr. Ahmed* is covering the clinic on Friday.
3. We drove along *Lake Michigan* most of the way into *Milwaukee*.
4. *Delta* cut half its *Cincinnati* flights.
5. She starts at *Ohio State* in *August*.
6. *Mount Rainier* was completely socked in the whole trip.
7. Take *Fifth Avenue* down to *Washington Square Park*.

**See also:** [The with proper nouns](#the-with-proper-nouns), [the](#the), [a](#a), [zero article with languages and school subjects](#zero-article-with-languages-and-school-subjects), [zero article with roles and titles](#zero-article-with-roles-and-titles)

### zero article with roles and titles

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при единственной должности — elected mayor «избран мэром», Judge Ramos
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns naming a unique office, role, or rank** in three frames: after *as* (*as Ø treasurer*), after the appointment verbs *become, be elected, be appointed, be named, be made, be sworn in as, be crowned* (*elected Ø mayor*), and directly before a personal name as a title (*Ø President Lincoln*, *Ø Judge Ramos*, *Ø Coach Sanders*, *Ø Senator Feinstein*). Not with ordinary predicate nouns naming a class of job, which require *a/an*: *She's a teacher*, *He's an engineer*.
**With *of*:** the role may carry one and stays bare: *appointed Ø chief of staff*, *served as Ø chair of the committee*.
**Pattern:** *as* Ø [role] &middot; [become / be elected / be appointed / be named / be sworn in as] Ø [role] &middot; Ø [title] [personal name] &middot; [vocative] Ø [title] (*Thank you, Ø Senator*)
**Contrast:** The line is **uniqueness**. There is one treasurer at a time, so *elected Ø treasurer* goes bare; there are many teachers, so *She's a teacher* takes the article. Compare *She was appointed Ø dean* with *She's a dean at a community college*. Turn the title into a description and *the* returns: *Ø President Lincoln* but *the president*, *the sixteenth president of the United States*; *Ø Judge Ramos* but *the judge*. A title used alone in direct address goes bare (*Thank you, Ø Senator*), and dropping the article before an ordinary job title is a common learner error in the other direction: *He is Ø engineer* is wrong.

Leaves a role name bare when only one person holds it at a time.

1. She was elected *treasurer* on the first ballot.
2. He served as *interim principal* for most of the year.
3. *Judge Ramos* denied the motion from the bench.
4. They named her *chief of staff* in April.
5. As *chair*, you set the agenda and you take the blame.
6. Thank you, *Senator* — I'll submit the rest in writing.

**See also:** [zero article with proper nouns](#zero-article-with-proper-nouns), [a](#a), [the](#the)

### zero article with sports and games

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при видах спорта и играх — play soccer «играть в футбол»; но play the piano
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **names of sports and games**, which behave as **uncountable activity nouns**, after *play, watch, coach, teach,* and after prepositions — *soccer, baseball, basketball, football, tennis, golf, chess, poker, tag, hide-and-seek*. Also the bare position nouns of American team sports (*play Ø quarterback*, *play Ø shortstop*, *play Ø defense*).
**With *of*:** takes none. *A game of Ø chess* puts the article on *game*, not on the sport.
**Pattern:** [play / watch / coach / teach] Ø [sport] &middot; [good at / a fan of / into] Ø [sport] &middot; [play] Ø [position]
**Contrast:** **Musical instruments take *the* in exactly this frame** — *play Ø soccer* against *play the piano* — and this single pair is the most useful article contrast in English for a learner. (Jazz and studio musicians say *play Ø piano*, *play Ø bass*; that is standard inside music but not the general rule.) A sport name with *the* means a particular contest or the coverage of it: *Did you watch the game?* Team names always take *the*: *the Bengals*, *the Reds*.

Leaves the name of a sport or game bare, because it names an activity rather than a countable thing.

1. They play *soccer* on Tuesday nights at the rec center.
2. He coached *basketball* at the middle school for eleven years.
3. My grandfather taught me *chess* when I was six and never let me win.
4. She's very good at *poker* and it has cost me actual money.
5. We watched *baseball* all afternoon with the sound off.
6. The kids played *tag* in the yard until it got dark.
7. He played *quarterback* for two seasons and then blew out a knee.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [the](#the), [zero article with roles and titles](#zero-article-with-roles-and-titles)

### zero article with times, seasons, and holidays

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль при времени, сезонах и праздниках — at night «ночью», in spring, on Tuesday
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** the **time-of-day nouns** *night, noon, midnight, dawn, dusk, daybreak, sunrise, sunset* after a preposition; **season names** (*spring, summer, fall, winter*); **month and day names**; **holiday names** (*Christmas, Thanksgiving, Passover, Labor Day, Halloween*); and the bare *last* / *next* frames (*last week*, *next month*, *last Tuesday*, *next summer*), where the noun phrase carries no determiner at all. **Do not put *this*, *that*, or *every* on that list:** those are central determiners in their own right, so *this week* and *every spring* **fill** the slot rather than leaving it empty. They are article-free for a different reason and belong to [demonstratives](02-demonstratives.md#this) and [distributives](06-distributives-negatives.md#every).
**With *of*:** takes none of its own — and an *of*-phrase forces *the*: *the summer of 2019*, *the night of the fire*.
**Pattern:** *at* Ø *night / noon / midnight / dawn* &middot; *in* Ø [season] &middot; *on* Ø [day] &middot; *in* Ø [month] &middot; *at / on* Ø [holiday] &middot; *last / next* Ø [time noun]
**Contrast:** The trap is that ***morning, afternoon,* and *evening* take *the*** in precisely the frame where *night* does not: *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening*, but *at Ø night*. Seasons take either — *in Ø spring* and *in the spring* are both standard American, the bare form slightly more general. And *last Ø week* is not *the last week*: the first means the week before this one, the second means the final week of some period. Same for *next Ø month* against *the next month*.

Leaves a time expression bare when it names a recurring point or period rather than a particular one.

1. The dogs bark at *night* and sleep all day.
2. We're closed on *Thanksgiving* and the Friday after it.
3. She's starting a new job in *January*.
4. That intersection floods in *spring* and again in *fall*.
5. *Last week* was a disaster and *next month* looks worse.
6. The store opens at *noon* on *Sunday*.
7. He gets up before *dawn* whether he has to or not.

**See also:** [zero article (Ø)](#zero-article-ø), [the](#the), [zero article with meals](#zero-article-with-meals), [zero article with proper nouns](#zero-article-with-proper-nouns)

### zero article with transport and communication

**Pronunciation:** silent &middot; *(nothing)*
**Русский:** нулевой артикль после by — by car «на машине», by mail «почтой», on foot «пешком»
**Type:** article (zero) &middot; **Position:** the central determiner slot, left empty &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** ✗ *singular countable vehicle nouns after *by*** (*by car, by bus, by train, by plane, by boat, by subway, by cab, by bike, by ferry*) and ✗ *medium nouns after *by*** (*by mail, by email, by phone, by text, by fax, by hand, by check, by credit card, by wire*). Also the irregular *on Ø foot*, which uses *on* rather than *by*. Never a plural (*by cars*) and never with a modifier — the moment you say *which* car, the frame collapses.
**With *of*:** takes none.
**Pattern:** [go / travel / get there / commute / send / pay] *by* Ø [vehicle or medium] &middot; *on* Ø *foot* &middot; *by* Ø [medium] *rather than by* Ø [medium]
**Contrast:** *By* + Ø names the **manner in the abstract**. The moment you mean one particular vehicle you change preposition and add a determiner: *by Ø car* → *in the car*, *in my car*; *by Ø bus / train / plane* → *on the bus*, *on the 6:40 train*, *on a flight to Newark*. Note also that the everyday American way to say it is *take the bus*, *take the train*, *take a cab* — with an article, not with *by*. *By foot* is heard but *on foot* is the standard form.

Leaves a vehicle or a medium bare after *by*, where the phrase names a method rather than an object.

1. We went by *car* because the flights that week were absurd.
2. Send it by *mail* if it has to be notarized.
3. She'd rather do it by *phone* than by *email*.
4. They came by *train* from Albany and got in after midnight.
5. Every one of those seams is stitched by *hand*.
6. It's twenty minutes by *bike* and close to an hour on *foot*.
7. The deposit has to go by *wire*, not by *check*.

## Informal, dialectal, and archaic article forms

Five forms a learner will meet in print and in speech and must be able to read without imitating. Each is labeled with the register that licenses it; none of them belongs in ordinary American writing. The short recognition list at [Forms you will see but should not produce](#forms-you-will-see-but-should-not-produce) covers the rest.

### a whole nother

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌhoʊl ˈnʌðɚ/ &middot; *uh hohl NUTH-er*
**Русский:** разг. совсем другой — «совсем другая история»; только в устной речи, не на письме
**Type:** article (indefinite) inside a fixed frame — *a* plus *whole* plus the stranded remainder of *another* &middot; **Position:** central determiner, with *whole* wedged in behind it &middot; **Register:** **informal spoken American English.** Very common in speech and in quoted speech; **not usable in edited prose**, where the form is *a whole other* or *an entirely different*.
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *a whole nother story, a whole nother level, a whole nother ballgame, a whole nother conversation, a whole nother animal, a whole nother thing*. Never a plural (*a whole nother stories*) and never an uncountable (*a whole nother information*). In practice the noun is almost always one of a dozen abstract "category" nouns; the frame does not extend freely.
**With *of*:** takes none.
**Pattern:** *a whole nother* [singular countable noun]
**Contrast:** This is **tmesis** — one word split open so another word can be dropped inside it. *Another* is historically *an* + *other*; speakers re-analyze the *an* as the article, which strands *nother* as a word that exists nowhere else in the language. Against [another](07-numerals-multipliers.md#another), which is the ordinary word this is built from, and against the written equivalents *a whole other* and *an entirely different*. **Flag: informal spoken only.** It is a genuine American idiom rather than an error, but it does not belong in writing unless you are quoting speech.

Wedges *whole* inside *another* for emphasis, meaning "an entirely different one."

1. Getting the permit was one thing; getting it inspected was *a whole nother* story. (informal speech)
2. Cooking for six is *a whole nother* level of planning. (informal speech)
3. Once the lawyers got involved it turned into *a whole nother* thing. (informal speech)
4. Triple-A ball and the majors are *a whole nother* ballgame. (informal speech)
5. That's *a whole nother* conversation and we don't have time for it tonight. (informal speech)
6. In writing, make it *a whole other* story or *an entirely different* story. (standard equivalents)

**See also:** [a](#a), [another](07-numerals-multipliers.md#another), [Forms you will see but should not produce](#forms-you-will-see-but-should-not-produce)

### an (before stressed h- in scriptural and pre-1900 texts)

**Pronunciation:** weak /ən/ &middot; *uhn* &mdash; stressed /æn/ &middot; *AN*. The weak form is the only one in play, and it is where the strangeness lives: the *h* after it is fully pronounced, so *an house* comes out /ən ˈhaʊs/ &middot; *uhn HOWSS* and *an hundred* comes out /ən ˈhʌndrəd/ &middot; *uhn HUN-druhd*, the /n/ running into a consonant instead of linking onto a vowel the way it does in *an hour*. Americans reading these texts aloud split three ways: some say it as printed, most quietly substitute *a*, and a few drop the *h* to make the article work (*uhn OWSS*) — a lectern habit, not an American pronunciation.
**Русский:** устар. an перед произносимым ударным h- — an house, an hundred; читать, но не писать
**Type:** article (indefinite), archaic distribution &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** **archaic.** Ordinary in printed English through the seventeenth century, thinning through the eighteenth and nineteenth, and gone from edited American prose by roughly 1900. It survives only inside quoted or reprinted text.
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns beginning with a stressed, fully pronounced *h*-** — *an house*, *an hundred*, *an hedge*, *an handful*, *an high priest*, *an holy nation*, *an hard saying*, *an help meet*. Otherwise the restrictions are exactly those of modern [a](#a) and [an](#an): singular countable only, never a plural (*an houses*), never an uncountable (*an hay*). It stands in front of an adjective as readily as a noun — *an hard saying*, *an holy nation* — because the choice was made by the very next word either way, the same mechanism modern English still uses.
**With *of*:** none of its own; identical to [a](#a) and [an](#an). *An handful of meal* and *an hundredfold* sit inside partitives and quantity phrases exactly as their modern counterparts do.
**Pattern:** *an* [stressed *h*- noun] &middot; *an* [stressed *h*- adjective] [noun] &middot; *an hundred* [noun] — far and away the commonest single case &middot; *an* [stressed *h*- noun] beside an ordinary *a* in the same breath (*a cake, but an handful of meal*)
**Contrast:** Against modern [an](#an), which goes before a **vowel sound**: this one goes before an audible /h/, which is a consonant, so it breaks the rule the rest of this file runs on. Against [an historic](#an-historic) — the confusion worth clearing up, because the two look identical and are not the same thing. *An historic* stands before an **unstressed** *h*- syllable that really was weak or dropped in the accents that produced it, so *an* was doing its ordinary pre-vowel job; this form stands before a **stressed** *h*- (*HOUSE*, *HUNdred*, *HARD*), where no amount of h-dropping accounts for it. The cause here is age rather than accent: *an* is the older and fuller shape of the article, worn down from *ān*, "one," and English shed that *n* in front of consonants gradually, finishing before /h/ last of all. Seventeenth-century printers had not finished the job, and the King James translators' English was already deliberately old-fashioned when it was set in 1611 — which is why one verse can hold *a chosen generation* and *an holy nation* four words apart. **Flag: archaic — read it, never write it.** Modern American English has *a house*, *a hundred*, *a hard saying*.
**Variants:** none of its own. This is not a separate word but [an](#an) in an older distribution. The exact parallel in the same texts is *thine* for *thy* in front of a vowel or an *h* (*thine eyes*, *thine heart*), which retreated on the same schedule and for the same reason — see [thy](03-possessives.md#thy).

Marks a singular countable noun as one of a class, in older English, where the article kept its *n* in front of an audible *h*.

1. "Then Isaac sowed in that land, and received in the same year *an* hundredfold." (Genesis 26:12, King James Bible — *an hundred* is the commonest instance by far and the one an American has most likely already heard)
2. "Shalt thou build me *an* house for me to dwell in?" (2 Samuel 7:5 — read aloud from a pulpit somewhere in the country every week)
3. "But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, *an* holy nation, a peculiar people." (1 Peter 2:9 — the old and the new distribution inside a single verse)
4. "I have not a cake, but *an* handful of meal in a barrel, and a little oil in a cruse." (1 Kings 17:12 — *a cake* and *an handful* three words apart)
5. "Hast not thou made *an* hedge about him, and about his house, and about all that he hath on every side?" (Job 1:10)
6. "Christ glorified not himself to be made *an* high priest." (Hebrews 5:5)
7. Shakespeare's compositors set the same forms — *an hundred*, *an heir* — but the Folger and Arden texts modernize them silently, so you meet them only in a facsimile or an old-spelling edition. (Shakespeare, unmodernized)
8. Nineteenth-century American deeds and probate records carry it as well: a title search can turn up *an hundred acres* where the modern instrument would say *a hundred acres*. (county records, where genealogists run into it)
9. It survives by ear rather than by rule in hymns and memorized verses — a congregation sings *an hundredfold* without hearing anything odd in it. (hymnody and quotation)
10. In your own prose it reads as an error and not as a flourish: write *a house*, *a hundred people*, *a hard saying*. (standard)

### an historic

**Pronunciation:** /ən ɪˈstɔrɪk/ &middot; *uhn iss-TOR-ik* — the form only makes sense if the *h* is dropped or very weak, as it was in the accents that produced it
**Русский:** брит. книжн. an перед безударным h- — an historic; по-американски пишут a historic
**Type:** article (indefinite), variant form &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** **British literary and older formal writing.** In American English it reads as affected or hypercorrect, and American style guides call for *a historic*.
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns and adjectives beginning with an unstressed *h*- syllable** — *an historic decision, an hotel, an hypothesis, an habitual offender, an heroic effort, an hysterical outburst*. The frame requires that unstressed first syllable, which is why this survival never reaches *an hand* or *an highway*: there the *h* carries full stress and is fully pronounced. Older English did write *an house* and *an hundred*, but from a different cause and in a different century, and that form has an entry of its own at [an (before stressed h- in scriptural and pre-1900 texts)](#an-before-stressed-h--in-scriptural-and-pre-1900-texts).
**With *of*:** none of its own; identical to [a](#a) and [an](#an).
**Pattern:** *an* [unstressed *h*- adjective] [noun] &middot; *an* [unstressed *h*- noun]
**Contrast:** The rule has not changed — *an* still goes before a **vowel sound**. What changed is the pronunciation underneath it: in older and more British accents the *h* of *historic* was weak or absent, so the word began with a vowel and *an* was simply correct. American English pronounces that *h*, so American English writes *a historic decision*. Do not extend the pattern to ***an herb***, which is the opposite case and **is** correct American English, because *herb* has a genuinely silent *h* in the United States. **Flag: not standard American.** Recognize it in older and British text; write *a historic*.

A variant *an* before a weakly aspirated *h*, standard in British literary usage and out of place in American writing.

1. It was *an* historic moment for the party. (British and older formal; American writing has *a* historic moment.)
2. He checked into *an* hotel just off the Strand. (British literary; American has *a* hotel.)
3. The paper advances *an* hypothesis nobody had bothered to test. (older formal; American has *a* hypothesis.)
4. She made *an* heroic effort to keep the deal together. (British; American has *a* heroic effort.)
5. American copy editors change every one of these to *a*: *a* historic decision, *a* hotel, *a* hypothesis, *a* heroic effort.
6. The genuine American exception runs the other way: *an* herb garden, because *herb* really does begin with a vowel sound here.

**See also:** [an](#an), [a](#a), [an (before stressed h- in scriptural and pre-1900 texts)](#an-before-stressed-h--in-scriptural-and-pre-1900-texts), [A or an: the test is sound, not spelling](#a-or-an-the-test-is-sound-not-spelling), [American and British article splits](#american-and-british-article-splits)

### th'

**Pronunciation:** /ð/ &middot; *th* — *the* with its schwa deleted, so the /ð/ leans straight onto the next word: *th' whole time* is one run of sound, and *th' other* comes out /ðˈʌðɚ/. What the apostrophe records is the loss of the vowel, not a different word.
**Русский:** the с проглоченным гласным — передача выговора на письме, только в репликах персонажей
**Type:** article (definite), **eye-dialect respelling** of a reduced *the* &middot; **Position:** central determiner — the same slot *the* fills, since it **is** *the* &middot; **Register:** **written dialogue only** — fiction, song lyrics, comics, transcribed speech. Never in expository prose, never in the narrator's own voice.
**Goes with:** whatever noun [the](#the) goes with, with no countability restriction of its own — **singular countable** (*th' truck*), **plural countable** (*th' kids*), **uncountable** (*th' money*), and the *the*-taking proper nouns (*th' Mississippi*). What licenses it is the register, not the noun.
**With *of*:** identical to [the](#the); the apostrophe changes nothing about the syntax. Predeterminers still stack in front of it (*all th' way*, *half th' town*), and *of* is still required before a pronoun (*all of 'em*).
**Pattern:** *th'* [noun] &middot; [preposition] *th'* [noun] &middot; frozen survivor: *t'other* (*one or t'other*)
**Contrast:** Against [the](#the) there is **no difference of word, meaning, or grammar** — only of spelling. The reduction it transcribes is completely ordinary American speech: everyone shortens /ðə/ and many speakers drop the vowel outright before another word, so *th'* is not a dialect feature so much as a novelist's way of pointing at one. Against [ye](#ye), which is a genuine old **printed** form using a letter that no longer exists, *th'* is a modern authorial device. Northern British *t'* (*t'mill*, *t'pub*) is the same trick applied to a different accent. **Flag: eye-dialect, nonstandard in writing.** Fine inside quotation marks in fiction; wrong everywhere else, and applied unevenly it reads as condescension toward the speaker being quoted.

Writes out a *the* whose vowel has been swallowed, as a way of putting an accent on the page.

1. "Shut *th'* gate behind you," he said without turning around. (written dialogue)
2. She'd been out since four working on *th'* truck. (written dialogue)
3. *Th'* other day I ran into him out by the grain elevator. (written dialogue)
4. He'd spent *th'* money before *th'* check even cleared. (written dialogue — uncountable, then countable)
5. A Yorkshire novel spells the same reduction *t'*: *He's down t' pub*. (British eye-dialect)
6. The one form most Americans ever write is the frozen *t'other*, as in *one or t'other*.
7. Outside quoted speech it is simply wrong: write *the gate*, *the truck*, *the other day*. (standard)

**See also:** [the](#the), [ye](#ye), [The two pronunciations of *the*](#the-two-pronunciations-of-the), [Forms you will see but should not produce](#forms-you-will-see-but-should-not-produce)

### ye

**Pronunciation:** /ðə/ &middot; *thuh* — **not** /ji/. The *y* is a printer's substitute for the lost Old English letter thorn (þ), which in blackletter type looked close enough to a *y* that early printers set it that way. *Ye Olde Tavern* is read "the old tavern."
**Русский:** устар. старое написание the — «Ye Olde Shoppe» читается «the», не /ji/; стилизация
**Type:** article (definite), archaic spelling &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** **mock-antique decoration only** — shop signs, pub names, festival banners, and jokes. Never ordinary writing.
**Goes with:** whatever noun [the](#the) goes with — singular countable, plural countable, uncountable — because it is not a separate word but an old **spelling** of *the*. In practice it survives almost exclusively in frozen sign phrases: *Ye Olde Curiosity Shoppe*, *Ye Olde Cheese Shoppe*, *Ye Olde Tavern*, *Ye Olde Book Barn*.
**With *of*:** identical to [the](#the); the spelling changes nothing about the syntax.
**Pattern:** *Ye Olde* [noun] &middot; *Ye* [noun]
**Contrast:** Do not confuse this *ye* with the genuine archaic **pronoun** *ye* /ji/, the old plural of *thou* (*Gather ye rosebuds while ye may*). They are unrelated words that happen to be spelled alike: the pronoun is a real second-person plural, this one is only *the* in old type. **Flag: decorative only.** Writing *ye* for *the* in running prose is a mistake, and pronouncing it /ji/ is a mistake even on a sign.

An old printed spelling of *the*, surviving today only as decoration.

1. *Ye* Olde Curiosity Shoppe has been sitting on that pier since 1899.
2. The sign out front said *Ye* Olde Tavern and everyone read it as "the old tavern."
3. Renaissance fairs put *ye* on everything, down to the parking signs.
4. The *y* is a thorn, so *Ye* Olde is pronounced /ðə ˈoʊld/ and never "yee."
5. Compare the real archaic pronoun: *Gather ye rosebuds while ye may* — that *ye* is /ji/ and means "you."
6. No American writes *ye* in running prose; it belongs on a sign, a menu, or in a joke.

## Three ways to be generic

English has three generics and they are not interchangeable. Getting this wrong is what makes otherwise fluent writing sound foreign.

| Form | Example | What it means | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ø + plural / uncountable** | Ø **Computers** are getting cheaper. Ø **Rice** is cheap. | The default, everyday generic — the class seen as its members. | None. It is the **only** generic available to uncountable nouns. |
| ***a/an* + singular countable** | **A computer** is only as good as its user. | Any representative member, taken one at a time. | Singular countables only, and it fails for statements true of the class but not of each member: ✗ *A dodo is extinct*, ✗ *A tiger is disappearing from Asia*. |
| ***the* + singular countable** | **The computer** changed modern work. | The class as an institution, a species, or an invention. Formal and written. | Singular countables only. Does not work with mass nouns — *The rice is cheap* is about specific rice — and can slide into a specific reading if the context allows one. |

The fourth possibility does not exist. ***The* + plural is not generic in English.** *The computers are getting cheaper* can only mean particular computers you and your listener already have in mind. Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, German, Greek, and Arabic all use the definite article for exactly this generic, which is why *I like the dogs* and *The Americans work too much* are the most persistent article errors in the language. When you mean a class, use nothing.

One extra generic exists for people: ***the* + adjective** names a whole class and takes a plural verb — *the rich*, *the poor*, *the unemployed*, *the homeless*, *the French*. It has no singular and cannot be pluralized: *the unemployeds* is impossible. Full entry at [the (adjective as noun)](#the-adjective-as-noun).

## American and British article splits

The inventory of articles is identical on both sides of the Atlantic. What differs is a short list of conventions.

| American | British | Note |
|---|---|---|
| She's in **the** hospital. | She's in hospital. | The most-cited split. Both say *in Ø school, in Ø prison, at Ø church, in Ø bed*. |
| He was at **the** university. / He's in Ø college. | He was at Ø university. | American English uses *in Ø school* for K–12 and often for college too. |
| In **the** future, call first. | In Ø future, call first. | American *in future* sounds clipped and British. |
| on **the** weekend | at **the** weekend / at Ø weekends | Americans also say *over the weekend*; *at the weekend* is unmistakably British. |
| **a** historic decision | **an** historic decision | Both pronounce the *h*; the American article follows the sound. |
| **an** herb, **an** herb garden | **a** herb | *Herb* has a silent *h* in the United States only. |
| **a** half hour, **a** half an hour (informal) | half **an** hour | *Half an hour* is fine in the United States too. |
| **a** couple things (informal speech) | **a** couple of things | Keep *of* in American writing. |
| two hundred fifty-three | two hundred **and** fifty-three | Not an article, but the same reading-aloud habit. |

## Forms you will see but should not produce

Recognize these; do not write them.

- ***an historic*, *an hotel*, *an hypothesis*.** A British literary survival from a time when the *h* was weaker. American English pronounces the *h*, so American English writes *a historic*, *a hotel*. **Not an error in British writing; affected in American writing.** Note that *an herb* is the opposite case and **is** correct American, because the *h* really is silent there. Full entry at [an historic](#an-historic).
- ***an hundred*, *an house*, *an hard saying*.** The article in front of a **stressed, fully pronounced** *h*- — a much older layer than *an historic*, and not the same thing. Standard through the seventeenth century and gone by about 1900; an American meets it in the King James Bible, in an unmodernized Shakespeare, and in nineteenth-century deeds. **Read it, never write it.** Full entry at [an (before stressed h- in scriptural and pre-1900 texts)](#an-before-stressed-h--in-scriptural-and-pre-1900-texts).
- ***Ye Olde Shoppe*.** The *y* is a printer's substitute for the lost letter thorn (þ). The word is *the*, pronounced /ðə/, never /ji/. **Mock-antique decoration only** — signage, pub names, and jokes. Full entry at [ye](#ye).
- ***th'*, *t'*.** Eye-dialect spellings of a reduced *the* in written dialogue. **Fine inside quoted speech in fiction, wrong everywhere else.** Full entry at [th'](#th).
- ***a apple*, *a hour*.** Heard in some American dialects and in very fast speech. **Nonstandard in writing without exception.**
- ***a whole nother*.** A real and widespread American idiom formed by wedging *whole* inside *another*. **Informal speech only; unusable in writing** except as reported speech. Full entry at [a whole nother](#a-whole-nother).
- ***a-huntin'*, *a-comin'*, *a-fixin' to*.** The *a-* here is not the article but a worn-down preposition. **Archaic and regional** — Appalachian and older folk registers.
- ***the Ukraine*, *the Sudan*, *the Lebanon*.** Older forms. **Current American usage drops the article**, and in the case of Ukraine the older form is actively objected to.
- ***the both of them*, *the one*.** Colloquial and common in speech. **Acceptable informally, avoidable in edited prose.**
- **Dropping articles entirely** — *I go to store*, *He is engineer*. Standard in many languages and in learner English; **never standard in any variety of English**.

## The decision procedure

Run these in order. The first rule that fires gives you the answer.

**1. Is the noun a proper name?** If yes, use Ø — unless it belongs to a *the*-taking group (river, ocean, gulf, desert, mountain range, island group, plural or common-noun country, newspaper, hotel, ship, *of*-phrase institution, plural family name). Check [The with proper nouns](#the-with-proper-nouns) and stop.

**2. Is the whole phrase one of the fixed frames?** Institution used for its purpose (*in Ø bed*, *at Ø school*), meal (*after Ø lunch*), language or school subject (*teaches Ø Spanish*), sport or game (*plays Ø chess*), *by* + transport or medium (*by Ø car*), time or season (*at Ø night*, *in Ø July*), disease (*has Ø diabetes*), frozen idiom (*by Ø heart*), role after *as* or an appointment verb (*elected Ø treasurer*), or block language (a headline, a sign, a label). If yes, use Ø and stop.

**3. Can your listener identify exactly which one you mean?** Second mention, inferable from something already said, unique in the world or in the room, narrowed by a following modifier, superlative, ordinal, *only / same / next / last*, or shared cultural knowledge. If yes, use *the* and stop. See [Why the? Every road to definiteness](#why-the-every-road-to-definiteness).

**4. They cannot identify it — so what kind of noun is it?**
- Singular countable → *a* or *an*.
- Plural countable → Ø, or *some* if you mean a bounded quantity, or *any* in a question or a negative.
- Uncountable → Ø, or *some* / *any* on the same terms.

**5. Are you speaking generically?** Then choose deliberately: Ø + plural for the everyday generic, *a* + singular for "any representative member," *the* + singular for the formal species-or-invention reading. Never *the* + plural. See [Three ways to be generic](#three-ways-to-be-generic).

**6. Now fix the form.** Say the next word out loud. Vowel sound → *an* and /ði/. Consonant sound → *a* and /ðə/. Spelling has no vote.

The whole system collapses into one grid:

| Noun type | Listener can identify it | Listener cannot | Generic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Singular countable** | *the* chair | *a* chair | *a* chair (each one) / *the* chair (the species) |
| **Plural countable** | *the* chairs | Ø chairs / *some* chairs | Ø chairs |
| **Uncountable** | *the* water | Ø water / *some* water | Ø water |
| **Proper noun** | Ø Chicago, *the* Hudson | — | — |

And the two rules that fail most often, restated: **a singular countable noun can never stand alone** (*I need pen* is not English, ever), and **the plural generic takes nothing** (*I like Ø dogs*, never *I like the dogs*).
