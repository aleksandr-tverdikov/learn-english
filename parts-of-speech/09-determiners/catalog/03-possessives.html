Possessive and genitive determiners#

The determiners that answer whose? — the seven personal possessives, the interrogative and relative whose, and any noun phrase at all with an apostrophe-s stuck on the end.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

Every member of this group marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or connected with somebody or something. Two facts unite them. First, they all occupy the central determiner slot, and that slot holds exactly one item — which is why ✗ the my book and ✗ a your friend are impossible in English, and why the double genitive (a friend of mine) exists as the workaround. Second, the possessive is written with an apostrophe, and four of these determiners have a contraction that sounds identical to them — its/it's, their/they're, whose/who's, your/you're — which together make up the most common error class in written American English.

The class is closed at one end and open at the other. The personal possessives are a fixed list of seven (my, your, his, her, its, our, their), plus whose, generic one's, and archaic thy. But the genitive is a productive construction: any noun phrase can take 's and drop straight into the same slot — John's, the company's, the children's, somebody else's, the King of Spain's. One last rule to carry through the whole file: possessives agree with the possessor, never with the noun that follows. David called his mother; the committee published its findings.

The personal possessive determiners#

Seven words, one for each person and number, and each has a matching standalone pronoun (mine, yours, his, hers, —, ours, theirs). The determiner precedes a noun; the pronoun replaces the whole noun phrase. Get this pair wrong and you get ✗ a my friend or ✗ the book is my.

her#

Pronunciation: /hɝ/, weak /hɚ/, /ɚ/ · HUR, weak her / er Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (her car), plural countable (her keys), uncountable (her patience) — and gerunds (her leaving). Never another central determiner: ✗ the her car, ✗ a her friend. Never stands alone with no noun after it — that is the pronoun hers. With of: Takes no of-phrase of its own. When of appears before her, it belongs to a preceding quantifier: all of her friends, most of her time, neither of her options. The double genitive requires the pronoun: a friend of hers, never ✗ a friend of her. Pattern: her [noun] · [predeterminer] her [noun] — all her cousins, half her paycheck · her [ordinal] [cardinal] [adjective] [noun] — her first two real jobs · her own [noun] · [quantifier] of her [noun] Contrast: English has three hers. The determiner precedes a noun (her coat); the object pronoun follows a verb or preposition (I called her, with her); the possessive pronoun hers stands alone (the coat is hers). The spelling ✗ her's is never correct. Against his: the choice tracks the possessor's gender, not the noun's — Maria called her brother, David called his mother.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a female person or animal already identified.

1. The ordinary possessive. 2. A dated and now largely poetic use for ships, countries, and machines (the ship and her crew). Modern American usage, including AP style, writes its here.

  1. Her apartment is two blocks from the office. (singular countable)
  2. Maria left her keys, her badge, and her laptop on the desk. (plural countable)
  3. I admire her patience with that class. (uncountable)
  4. All her cousins flew in for the wedding. (predeterminer all, no of)
  5. She started the company with her own savings.
  6. I appreciated her driving me to the airport. (gerund)
  7. Isn't that her car in the driveway? (question — the determiner does not change)
  8. She hasn't returned her library books. (negative)
  9. The ship and her crew reached port at dawn. (sense 2, dated — modern American writing uses its)

See also: his, their, my, of mine, own

his#

Pronunciation: /hɪz/, weak /ɪz/ · HIZ, weak iz Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (his phone), plural countable (his parents), uncountable (his luggage) — and gerunds (his quitting). Never with another central determiner: ✗ the his phone. With of: No of-phrase of its own; of before his belongs to a preceding quantifier: both of his sisters, none of his excuses. The double genitive uses the identical form: a cousin of his. Pattern: his [noun] · [predeterminer] his [noun] — all his friends, both his brothers · his [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — his first three albums · his own [noun] · a [noun] of his Contrast: his is the one possessive whose determiner and pronoun forms are identical: his car (determiner, noun follows) versus the car is his (pronoun, nothing follows). Compare her/hers, your/yours, our/ours, their/theirs. Against generic their: older writing used his for a person of unknown gender (Every student must bring his book); modern American writing uses singular their or, in formal prose, his or her.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a male person or animal already identified.

1. The ordinary possessive. 2. The generic masculine, covering a person of unspecified gender. Standard until the 1970s, now avoided in American editing — replace with singular their, with his or her, or by making the subject plural.

  1. His flight lands at six. (singular countable)
  2. David forgot his wallet, his phone, and his charger. (plural countable)
  3. The airline lost his luggage in Denver. (uncountable)
  4. Both his brothers work downtown. (predeterminer both, no of)
  5. He paid the deposit out of his own savings.
  6. Every applicant should submit his résumé by Friday. (dated generic — today, their résumé)
  7. Didn't he bring his laptop? (question)
  8. He never answers his phone after six. (negative)

See also: her, their, his or her, his (periphrastic genitive), of mine

its#

Pronunciation: /ɪts/ — no separate weak form. Alone among the personal possessives, its does not reduce: unstressed it stays /ɪts/, exactly like it's · ITS Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (its handle), plural countable (its wheels), uncountable (its funding) — and gerunds (its failing). The possessor is a thing, a place, an organization, an abstraction, or an animal whose sex is unstated; not a named person. Never another central determiner: ✗ the its logo. With of: None of its own. Of before its belongs to a preceding quantifier: most of its revenue, half of its board. There is no double-genitive form — ✗ a page of its is impossible; say one of its pages. Pattern: its [noun] · [predeterminer] its [noun] — all its employees, half its budget · its own [noun] · [quantifier] of its [noun] Contrast: its versus it's is the most-corrected error in written English. Its is the possessive determiner and never takes an apostrophe (the dog wagged its tail). It's is the contraction of it is or it has (it's raining, it's been a week). The test: if you can expand the word to it is or it has, write it's; otherwise write its. The form ✗ its' does not exist in English in any sense. Note also that the possessive-pronoun slot is empty here: there is no ✗ the tail is its — rewrite as the tail is the dog's.

Marks the following noun as a part, property, product, or possession of a thing, an animal, an institution, or an idea already mentioned.

  1. The dog chased its tail for ten minutes. (singular countable)
  2. The company changed its logo again.
  3. Portland is famous for its bridges. (plural countable)
  4. The startup ran through its funding in a year. (uncountable)
  5. Every state sets its own speed limits. (every + singular countable, singular verb sets)
  6. The bill passed on its third reading.
  7. Doesn't the museum publish its attendance numbers? (question)
  8. The city has never released its full report. (negative)

See also: it's (not a determiner), their, the company's, own

my#

Pronunciation: /maɪ/, weak /mə/ · MY, weak muh Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (my car), plural countable (my parents), uncountable (my luggage) — plus gerunds (my leaving) and vocative address terms (my friend, my dear). Never another central determiner: ✗ the my car, ✗ a my friend. With of: None of its own; of before my belongs to a preceding quantifier (all of my cousins, neither of my options, a couple of my coworkers). The double genitive requires the pronoun mine: a friend of mine, ✗ a friend of my. Pattern: my [noun] · [predeterminer] my [noun] — all my cousins, half my paycheck, both my sisters · my [ordinal] [cardinal] [adjective] [noun] — my first two real jobs · my own [noun] · my [gerund] — my leaving early Variants: mine /maɪn/ (MYNE) — not a separate word but the archaic prevocalic shape of this same determiner, standing before a vowel sound exactly as an does: mine eyes, mine own, mine host, mine enemy. It is the thy ~ thine alternation one person over, and it is dead outside quotation. Keep prenominal mine apart from standalone mine, which is a pronoun, not a determiner: mine eyes (noun follows — determiner) versus that seat is mine (nothing follows — pronoun). See mine for the register, the h- extension, and the examples. · dialectal me — me dad, me mum (British, Irish, and Australian speech; not standard American) Contrast: my precedes a noun, mine stands alone: my seat versus that seat is mine. Modern English does not use mine as a determiner except in the frozen archaisms mine eyes and mine host (see mine). Do not confuse the possessive with the object pronoun me: ✓ He gave me my ticket, ✗ He gave my me ticket.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the speaker.

1. The ordinary possessive. 2. In direct address, marking closeness or (often) condescension: my friend, my dear, my good man. 3. Before a gerund, naming the doer of the action: They didn't appreciate my leaving early.

  1. My keys are in my other jacket. (plural countable, then singular countable)
  2. All my cousins live in Ohio. (predeterminer all; all of my cousins is equally correct)
  3. She was my first real boss.
  4. I paid for it with my own money. (uncountable)
  5. They didn't appreciate my leaving early. (gerund)
  6. My friend, that is not how this works. (vocative)
  7. Have you seen my charger anywhere? (question)
  8. I can't find my passport. (negative)

See also: our, your, mine, of mine, own

our#

Pronunciation: /ˈaʊɚ/, /aʊr/, weak /ɑr/ · OW-er / OWR, weak ar Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (our house), plural countable (our kids), uncountable (our luggage) — and gerunds (our leaving). Never another central determiner: ✗ the our house. With of: None of its own; of before our belongs to a preceding quantifier: several of our clients, most of our time. The double genitive uses ours: a neighbor of ours. Pattern: our [noun] · [predeterminer] our [noun] — all our neighbors, half our budget · our [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — our first two attempts · our own [noun] · [quantifier] of our [noun] Contrast: our precedes a noun, ours stands alone: our table versus the table is ours. In relaxed speech our reduces to /ɑr/ and collides with are and with hour, which is why ✗ are house shows up in casual writing. English does not distinguish inclusive our (you and I) from exclusive our (my group, not you) — only context does.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a group that includes the speaker.

  1. Our flight was delayed three hours. (singular countable)
  2. Our neighbors repainted their porch. (plural countable)
  3. Half our budget goes to rent. (predeterminer half; half of our budget is equally correct)
  4. This one is our own fault.
  5. We lost our luggage on the way back. (uncountable)
  6. All our records are backed up offsite.
  7. Is this our gate or the next one over? (question)
  8. We haven't gotten our deposit back yet. (negative)

See also: my, their, of mine

their#

Pronunciation: /ðɛr/, weak /ðɚ/ · THAIR, weak ther Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (their house), plural countable (their kids), uncountable (their time) — and gerunds (their leaving). The possessor may be plural, or a single person whose gender is unknown, unspecified, irrelevant, or nonbinary (singular they). Never another central determiner: ✗ the their house. With of: None of its own; of before their belongs to a preceding quantifier: none of their arguments, two of their kids. The double genitive uses theirs: a client of theirs. Pattern: their [noun] · [predeterminer] their [noun] — all their friends, both their cars · their own [noun] · [quantifier] of their [noun] Contrast: A four-way trap: their (determiner, noun follows), theirs (pronoun, nothing follows), there (a place, or the dummy subject in there is), and they're (= they are). ✗ their's is not a word. Against his or her: singular their is the ordinary spoken form and is now accepted by AP and Chicago; his or her is the formal written alternative and turns clumsy the moment it repeats.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the possessor(s) already identified.

1. A plural possessor: the Wilsons sold their house. 2. Singular they — one person of unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary gender: Somebody left their umbrella. The noun stays singular if the referent has only one (Everyone brought their own lunch), and the verb still agrees with the subject, not with their. 3. An institution treated as a group of people: They raised their rates again. American editing normally prefers its for a named organization — the company raised its rates.

  1. Their flight lands before ours. (singular countable)
  2. Both their kids play soccer. (predeterminer both; both of their kids is equally correct)
  3. Somebody left their umbrella in the lobby. (singular they)
  4. If a patient misses their appointment, the clinic charges a fee.
  5. The Wilsons sold their house in March.
  6. Everyone brought their own lunch. (everyone takes a singular verb — brought is neutral, but Everyone is bringing their own lunch, never ✗ Everyone are)
  7. We never did get their luggage back. (uncountable, negative)
  8. Did the Hills sell their boat? (question)

See also: they're (not a determiner), his or her, its, our

your#

Pronunciation: /jʊr/, /jɔr/, weak /jɚ/ · YOOR / YOR, weak yer Type: possessive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (your seat), plural countable (your kids), uncountable (your luggage) — and gerunds (your coming). One form covers a singular and a plural addressee. Never another central determiner: ✗ the your seat. With of: None of its own; of before your belongs to a preceding quantifier: some of your questions, each of your options. The double genitive uses yours: a friend of yours. Pattern: your [noun] · [predeterminer] your [noun] — all your files, half your salary · your [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — your last two emails · your own [noun] · your [gerund] — your coming on short notice Variants: regional plural forms — y'all's and emphatic all y'all's (Southern), you guys' (general informal), yinz's (Pittsburgh), youse's (New York, Philadelphia, Chicago), you-uns' / you'uns' (Appalachian and the Ohio Valley). All are informal speech, and edited writing uses your for both numbers; see y'all's for the pronunciations and the regional detail. Contrast: your + noun, yours alone, you're = you are. The test is expansion: ✗ Your welcome fails as your are welcome, so it must be You're welcome. In fast speech your and you're both reduce to /jɚ/, which is exactly why the pair is confused so often.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the person or people addressed.

1. The ordinary possessive. 2. Generic or impersonal you, meaning "people in general": Your average commuter spends an hour in traffic. Informal; formal writing uses the average commuter or one's. 3. Before a gerund, naming the doer: I appreciate your coming.

  1. Is this your seat? (question, singular countable)
  2. Your passport and your boarding pass, please.
  3. I appreciate your coming on such short notice. (gerund)
  4. All your files were backed up overnight. (predeterminer all + plural countable)
  5. Do it your own way.
  6. Your average commuter spends an hour a day in traffic. (generic your)
  7. Don't forget your badge. (negative imperative)
  8. We couldn't find your baggage on the carousel. (uncountable)

See also: you're (not a determiner), y'all's, one's, thy

Generic, formal, archaic, and regional possessives#

The seven personal possessives leave two gaps: there is no dedicated form for a person of unknown gender, and no dedicated plural you. English has filled both gaps repeatedly — with the compound his or her, with generic one's, with singular their, and, regionally, with y'all's. The archaic thy is the singular your that the language lost; mine and thine are the prevocalic shapes of my and thy, the a ~ an rule applied to possessives, and the only reason mine eyes and thine own self are not mistakes. The periphrastic his-genitive (John his book) is a dead rival of the 's ending that survives on a few Renaissance title pages.

his (periphrastic genitive)#

Pronunciation: /hɪz/, weak /ɪz/ — and the weak form is the whole point: unstressed, John his book and John's book are the same string of sounds · HIZ, weak iz Type: genitive (periphrastic, archaic) · Position: central — the possessor noun and his together fill the one central slot · Register: obsolete Early Modern English; an American meets it only on Renaissance title pages, in the Book of Common Prayer, and in colonial deeds, wills, and inventories Goes with: the noun possessed may be singular countable (John his book), plural countable (the Duke his men), or uncountable (the Bishop his patience). The real restriction is on the possessor, which must be a full noun — normally a personal name or a title — sitting immediately in front of his: ✓ Thomas Hooker his farm, ✗ he his farm, ✗ somebody his farm. It cannot be stacked on a possessed noun that already has a determiner (✗ John his the book), and nothing may precede the phrase (✗ the John his book), because the whole thing is the central determiner. With of: No of-form of its own. The ordinary alternative in the same period was the plain of-genitive — the fall of Sejanus, the farm of Thomas Hooker. There is no double genitive built on it: ✗ a book of John his; Early Modern English wrote a book of John's. Pattern: [possessor noun] his [noun] — John his book · [feminine possessor] her [noun] — Mistress Sands her jointure · [plural possessor] their [noun] — the Commons their petition · frozen survival — for Jesus Christ his sake Variants: the pronoun matches the possessor, so the same construction also runs as her with a feminine possessor (Elizabeth her letters) and as their with a plural one (the Commons their petition). Grammar books call the whole set the his-genitive or the periphrastic genitive. Contrast: Not the same thing as the possessive determiner his, which needs no possessor spelled out in front of it: his book names an owner already known, while John his book names him twice over. Against 's (singular genitive): the two were homophones — unstressed his is /ɪz/, and so is the -es of older Johnes book — which is why Early Modern writers, and the King James translators after them, sometimes wrote the pronoun out in full. The folk etymology that grew out of the resemblance is false: the 's ending descends from the Old English genitive -es and is centuries older than this construction, and a feminine possessor took her, which no contraction of his could ever produce. The mistaken belief did leave one permanent mark, though — it helped fix the apostrophe in 's, on the theory that a letter had been dropped.

The dead rival of the 's ending: a genitive built by naming the possessor and then adding a matching possessive pronoun after it. Recognize it; never write it.

  1. Sejanus his Fall is the title Ben Jonson put on his tragedy when it was printed in 1605 — the shape an American is likeliest to meet. (singular countable)
  2. The Prayer Book collect ends …for Jesus Christ his sake. (the one frozen survival still read aloud)
  3. A 1639 deed records Thomas Hooker his farm on the east bank of the river. (colonial land record — the other place it turns up)
  4. The Duke his men were quartered in the town all winter. (plural countable)
  5. Mistress Sands her jointure was entered in the parish book. (feminine possessor takes her, never ✗ his)
  6. The Commons their petition was read aloud to the King. (plural possessor takes their)
  7. Is that Cotton Mather his diary in the case, or a facsimile? (question)
  8. The Bishop his patience was not inexhaustible. (uncountable, negative)
  9. No American clerk would draft ✗ the plaintiff his signature today; the construction died out in the eighteenth century. (negative — modern legal drafting uses the plaintiff's signature)

See also: his, 's (singular genitive), of, thy

his or her#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪz ɚ ˈhɝ/ · hiz-er-HUR Type: possessive (compound) · Position: central · Register: formal, written Goes with: any noun — singular countable (his or her locker), plural countable (his or her dependents), uncountable (his or her luggage). The restriction is on the possessor, not on the noun possessed: the possessor must be one person of unknown or unspecified gender (each employee … his or her contact information). A plural possessor takes their, never ✗ the employees … his or her lockers. Never another central determiner: ✗ the his or her locker. With of: None of its own; of before it belongs to a preceding quantifier (each of his or her dependents). The matching standalone pronoun is his or hers, awkward enough that writers rewrite the sentence instead. Pattern: his or her [singular noun] · his or her own [noun] · variants his/her, her or his, his, her, or their Contrast: The formal-register alternative to singular their. Their is shorter, accepted by AP and Chicago, and includes people who use they for themselves; his or her excludes them and becomes unreadable when repeated across a paragraph. In practice the best fix is usually neither one: make the noun plural — All employees must update their emergency contacts. Against generic his: his or her was the 1980s repair for generic his, and singular their has since largely replaced both. Agreement: the antecedent that licenses his or her is grammatically singular, so it takes a singular verb — ✓ Each employee is responsible for his or her own equipment, ✗ Each employee are; ✓ Every candidate submits his or her transcript, ✗ Every candidate submit. The same holds for either, neither, and anyone: ✓ Neither applicant has withdrawn his or her name. Choosing singular their instead does not change the verb — ✓ Each employee is responsible for their own equipment.

Names a possessor who is one unidentified person, without committing to a gender.

  1. Each employee must update his or her emergency contact. (each + singular countable)
  2. Every candidate submitted his or her transcript by the deadline. (every + singular countable)
  3. Each participant is responsible for his or her travel expenses. (plural countable noun, singular verb is)
  4. A juror may not discuss the case with his or her spouse. (negative)
  5. Anyone who parked in Lot C should move his or her car.
  6. No child may enter the pool without his or her parent.
  7. Every applicant must show that his or her experience is relevant. (uncountable)
  8. Has each of the finalists submitted his or her portfolio? (question; each of the requires of)

See also: their, his, her, one's

mine#

Pronunciation: /maɪn/ — no weak form. My reduces to /mə/ in ordinary speech, but this word survives only inside stressed, quoted phrases and never reduces · MYNE (rhymes with LINE) Type: possessive (archaic, prevocalic) · Position: central · Register: archaic — biblical, liturgical, hymn, and poetic English. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in hymnals, and in the Battle Hymn of the Republic, and essentially nowhere else. Never in ordinary speech or modern prose. Goes with: any noun beginning with a vowel sound — singular countable (mine host, mine enemy), plural countable (mine eyes, mine ears), uncountable (mine iniquity) — plus the emphasizer own (mine own self). The vowel is the whole condition, so before a consonant sound the determiner is my: ✗ mine car, ✗ mine father, ✗ mine book. Seventeenth-century printing extends it before h- as well (mine heart, mine house), where the h was weak or silent. Never another central determiner: ✗ the mine eyes, ✗ a mine friend. Never with nothing after it — that is the pronoun mine. With of: None of its own. The of-phrase a friend of mine looks like this word and is not: there mine is the possessive pronoun filling the double genitive, and it is ordinary modern English. See of mine. Pattern: mine [vowel-initial noun] — mine eyes, mine enemy · mine own [noun] — mine own self, mine own people · the alternation — my [consonant-initial noun] ~ mine [vowel-initial noun], as in my God but mine enemies · vocative — mine host Variants: my is the same determiner in its other shape, and the pair runs exactly parallel to thy ~ thine one person over. · Early Modern printing also spells it myne. · It has no modern or regional survival as a determiner: the -n forms still heard in Appalachian and Ozark speech (hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn) were built by analogy with mine and thine, but they stand alone and are pronouns, not determiners — see Notes and tricky cases. Contrast: Two different words are spelled mine, and only one of them is archaic. The determiner has a noun after it (mine eyes) and has been dead for four centuries; the possessive pronoun has nothing after it (that seat is mine, a friend of mine) and is perfectly ordinary today. That split is why mine looks alive while mine eyes looks like costume. Against my: one word, two shapes, chosen by the sound that begins the next word — the same machinery as a ~ an, and my hand / mine eyes is the pair to remember. Against thine: identical rule, one person over.

The prevocalic shape of my, frozen inside quotation: it stands before a vowel sound the way an does. Recognize it; never write it.

  1. Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord. (the Battle Hymn of the Republic — plural countable, and the line most Americans know the form from)
  2. Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace … for mine eyes have seen thy salvation. (Luke 2:29–30, King James Version)
  3. Deliver me from mine enemies, O my God. (Psalm 59:1, KJV — both shapes in one line: mine before a vowel, my before a consonant)
  4. Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee. (Psalm 119:11, KJV — the older extension of mine before h-)
  5. Thou mine inheritance, now and always. (the hymn Be Thou My Vision — uncountable, and the title itself shows my before a consonant)
  6. Mine host of the Garter kept the best cellar in Windsor. (Shakespeare's form of address to an innkeeper — singular countable)
  7. Is that mine host at the door, or a stranger? (question, in deliberate period pastiche — the only way a modern writer produces the form on purpose)
  8. ✗ Mine car is in the shop is wrong twice over: the alternation needs a vowel sound, and the determiner died out in the seventeenth century. (negative — the error, not the word)

See also: my, thine, thy, of mine, own

one's#

Pronunciation: /wʌnz/ · WUNZ Type: possessive (generic) · Position: central · Register: formal Goes with: any noun — singular countable (one's passport), plural countable (one's options), uncountable (one's temper). The possessor is the generic pronoun one, meaning "a person, anyone," and standard usage keeps one … one's consistent through the sentence. Never another central determiner: ✗ the one's temper. With of: Only in the fixed phrase of one's own — a room of one's own, a place of one's own. Pattern: One … one's [noun] · one's own [noun] · [noun] of one's own · [gerund phrase] — losing one's temper Contrast: Three meanings, two spellings. One's with an apostrophe is the possessive (one's own business); ones without one is the plural of the noun or pronoun one (the red ones); and one's — the same spelling as the possessive — is also a contraction of one is or one has (one's never sure). Against your: American English almost always switches generic one to generic you — You should mind your own business is the everyday version, while One should mind one's own business reads as British or stiff. Older American usage shifted the possessive to his (One should mind his own business); that is now dated.

Names a possessor who is anyone at all, in the impersonal generic construction.

  1. One should mind one's own business. (singular countable)
  2. It is easy to lose one's temper in traffic. (uncountable)
  3. One needs a room of one's own.
  4. Losing one's passport abroad is a nightmare.
  5. One rarely regrets holding one's tongue.
  6. Keeping one's options open is generally good advice. (plural countable)
  7. One cannot always trust one's first impression. (negative)
  8. Where does one file one's appeal? (question)

See also: your, his or her, own

thine#

Pronunciation: /ðaɪn/ — no weak form; the word only ever turns up stressed · THYNE (rhymes with MINE) Type: possessive (archaic, prevocalic) · Position: central · Register: archaic — the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, Shakespeare, hymns, and deliberate period writing. A few conservative Quaker meetings still keep the plain speech (thee, thy, thine) as a religious practice; outside that, an American reads this word and never says it. Interrogative and relative genitives#

Whose is the only wh-word that is a possessive determiner, and it does double duty: it asks a question (Whose car is that?) and it introduces a relative clause (the man whose car was stolen). Its formal competitor for inanimate antecedents is of which, and its free-relative cousin is whosever.

of which#

Pronunciation: /əv ˈwɪtʃ/ · uhv WICH Type: relative genitive · Position: postmodifier — not the central determiner slot · Register: formal, written Goes with: a head noun naming a part, property, or component of an inanimate antecedent — a house the roof of which leaks. That head noun may be singular countable (the roof of which), plural countable (the terms of which), or uncountable (the funding of which). Because of which is a prepositional phrase and not a determiner, the head noun keeps its own determiner: the roof of which, three floors of which. With of: Of is part of the item itself and is obligatory. Two legal orders: postposed (the roof of which) and fronted (of which the roof), the second more formal still. Pattern: [antecedent], [determiner] [noun] of which [verb] … · [antecedent], of which [determiner] [noun] [verb] … Contrast: The formal alternative to relative whose with a non-human antecedent. Whose is fully standard with things — a house whose roof leaks — and far more natural; of which belongs to legal, academic, and technical registers where some writers still avoid whose for inanimates. With a human antecedent only whose works: ✓ the man whose car was stolen, ✗ the man the car of which was stolen.

Links a relative clause to its antecedent by naming something the antecedent has or contains.

  1. She cited a study the conclusions of which have since been retracted. (plural countable head — verb have)
  2. It is an old contract, the terms of which nobody remembers.
  3. The building, the top three floors of which are vacant, sold last month.
  4. He owns a vineyard of which the yield has doubled in five years. (fronted order)
  5. They passed a rule the effect of which was to end the program. (singular countable head — verb was)
  6. This is the only clause the meaning of which is genuinely disputed.
  7. It was a grant the funding of which has never been accounted for. (uncountable head, negative)
  8. Is that the statute the wording of which is under review? (question)

See also: whose, of, whosever

whose#

Pronunciation: /huz/ · HOOZ Type: interrogative and relative (genitive) · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (whose car), plural countable (whose keys), uncountable (whose money). The possessor may be a person, an animal, or a thing (a novel whose ending nobody likes). It fills the central determiner slot, so ✗ the whose car and ✗ a whose idea are impossible. With of: None as a determiner. The partitive question uses which: Which of these is yours? When whose takes no following noun it is a pronoun, not a determiner: Whose is this? Pattern: Whose [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — Whose car is that? · [antecedent noun] whose [noun] [verb] … (relative) · I don't know whose [noun] it is. (embedded question) · Whose is this? (pronoun — no noun follows) Contrast: whose versus who's. Whose asks or states possession; who's is the contraction of who is or who has. Expand it to test: Who's calling? = who is calling, but Whose call is it? has no expansion, so it takes whose. Against of which: both build an inanimate genitive relative, and whose is the ordinary choice (a car whose brakes failed), of which the formal one. Against which: which selects an item from a set (Which car?), whose asks who owns it (Whose car?).

1. Interrogative — asks which person or thing the following noun belongs to. 2. Relative — introduces a relative clause and ties its noun to the antecedent, for human and non-human antecedents alike.

  1. Whose jacket is on my chair? (question, singular countable)
  2. I have no idea whose idea this was. (embedded question)
  3. The engineer whose design won the contract is only 29. (relative, human antecedent)
  4. She's the one whose flight got canceled.
  5. It's an old house whose wiring needs replacing. (relative, non-human antecedent, uncountable)
  6. Whose keys are these, and whose car is blocking the driveway? (plural countable, then singular)
  7. Nobody remembers whose money paid for it. (negative, uncountable)
  8. Whose is this? (pronoun — no noun follows, so not a determiner here)

See also: who's (not a determiner), of which, whosever, Interrogative determiners

whosever#

Pronunciation: /huˈzɛvɚ/ · hoo-ZEV-er Type: relative genitive (free relative) · Position: central · Register: formal, rare Goes with: any noun — singular countable (whosever car), plural countable (whosever keys), uncountable (whosever property) — inside a free relative clause meaning "the one whose." Unlike whose, it supplies its own antecedent, so no noun precedes it. Never another central determiner: ✗ the whosever car. With of: None. Pattern: Whosever [noun] [verb] …, [main clause] · [main clause] whosever [noun] [verb] … · everyday spoken equivalent: whoever's [noun] Variants: whoseever, whosesoever (archaic and legal); the ordinary spoken form is whoever's Contrast: In American speech people say whoever's: Whoever's car that is needs to move it. Whosever is the tidy written form and rare enough to sound stilted. Do not confuse it with relative whose, which requires an antecedent noun (the man whose car…); whosever contains its own (whosever car…). Introduces a clause that identifies a possessor without naming one — "the person whose."

  2. Whosever name is on the lease is responsible for the damage.
  3. She promised the prize to whosever entry arrived first.
  4. Whosever idea this was, it worked.
  5. The court will notify whosever property is affected.
  6. Whosever turn it is, the trash goes out tonight.
  7. Whosever keys these are needs to come get them. (plural countable noun, singular main verb)
  8. We will not release the car to whosever insurance pays first. (uncountable, negative)

The genitive endings#

Any noun phrase in English can be turned into a determiner by adding the genitive ending. The ending is a clitic, not a suffix: it attaches to the last word of the phrase regardless of what part of speech that word is, which is why the King of Spain's daughter and somebody else's problem work the way they do. Adding it makes the phrase definite, so no article can join it: ✓ John's book, ✗ the John's book.

's (singular genitive)#

Pronunciation: /s/ after a voiceless sound (Pat's /pæts/), /z/ after a voiced sound (Dan's /dænz/), /ɪz/ after a sibilant (Chris's /ˈkrɪsɪz/) · s / z / iz Type: genitive · Position: central — the whole genitive phrase fills the slot · Register: neutral Goes with: attaches to the end of a complete noun phrase, not to the head noun. The noun it then determines may be singular countable (Dan's car), plural countable (Dan's kids), or uncountable (Dan's patience). It cannot combine with an article or demonstrative on the same noun: ✗ the Dan's car, ✗ this Dan's car. With of: The rival construction is the of-genitive — the girl's name / the name of the girl. See of for which one to choose. Pattern: [noun phrase]'s [noun] · [noun phrase]'s own [noun] · [predeterminer] [noun phrase]'s [noun] — all Dan's friends · [noun phrase]'s [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — Dan's first two jobs Contrast: Three identical-sounding endings. 's marks possession (Dan's car); plain s marks a plural (the Smiths are away); and 's also contracts is and has (Dan's here = Dan is here). Since the three are indistinguishable in speech, the apostrophe carries the whole load in writing — hence the greengrocer's ✗ apple's for sale. Against the plural genitive: the student's essays is one student, the students' essays is several. See s' (plural genitive).

The productive genitive ending: it converts any noun phrase into a central determiner, and with a true possessor it makes the resulting phrase definite. (The measure genitive is the exception — two weeks' notice stays indefinite; see two weeks'.)

  1. That's my sister's car. (singular countable)
  2. The dog's leash is by the door.
  3. Chicago's winters are brutal. (plural countable)
  4. Dan's patience finally ran out. (uncountable)
  5. The senator's office issued a statement.
  6. I borrowed my roommate's charger.
  7. Isn't that Ana's apartment on the corner? (question)
  8. We never got the landlord's approval. (negative)

See also: s' (plural genitive), John's, the company's, of, the King of Spain's, his (periphrastic genitive)

s' (plural genitive)#

Pronunciation: silent — the students' is pronounced exactly like the students /ðə ˈstudn̩ts/ · (no added sound) Type: genitive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: a regular plural noun already ending in -s; the apostrophe goes after that s and nothing further is added. The noun it determines may be singular countable, plural countable, or uncountable: the teachers' lounge, the teachers' contracts, the teachers' patience. Irregular plurals that do not end in -s take a full 's instead — see the children's. With of: the of-genitive is the usual alternative for long or inanimate plural possessors: the conclusions of the reports. Pattern: [regular plural noun]' [noun] — the teachers' lounge, the Johnsons' driveway · measure phrases: two weeks' notice, ten years' experience Contrast: the student's essays = one student, several essays. The students' essays = several students. ✗ The students essays = a missing apostrophe. Placement is the only signal and it is completely inaudible, which is why plural possessives get checked so hard in editing. Family names pluralize first, then take the apostrophe: the Johnsons → the Johnsons' house; never ✗ the Johnson's house for a family. Against the descriptive plural, which takes no apostrophe at all: a farmers market, a teachers college, a veterans hospital, Publishers Weekly — here the plural noun names the kind of thing rather than an owner, and American style guides drop the apostrophe. Ask whether the sense is ownership (the teachers' contracts) or description (a teachers college).

  2. We repaved the Johnsons' driveway.
  3. All the players' contracts expire in June. (plural countable head — verb expire)
  4. The workers' patience ran out in the third week. (uncountable)
  5. That's the girls' locker room.
  6. Both parties' lawyers agreed to a delay. (predeterminer both + plural)
  7. Isn't that the neighbors' dog again? (question)
  8. The owners' association has not met since March. (negative)

Genitive noun phrases as determiners#

Eleven recurring shapes of the genitive phrase. They are all the same construction — noun phrase plus 's in the central determiner slot — but each raises its own question about where the apostrophe goes, or about whether the of-genitive would be better.

Anna and Ben's#

Pronunciation: /ˈænə ən ˈbɛnz/ · AN-uh un BENZ Type: genitive (coordinate) · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (Anna and Ben's cabin), plural countable (Anna and Ben's kids), uncountable (Anna and Ben's furniture). One 's, on the last name only, means joint possession (Anna and Ben's house — one house). An 's on each name means separate possession, and the following noun is then usually plural (Anna's and Ben's houses — two houses). With of: The of-genitive sidesteps the ambiguity but sounds stiff with people: the cabin of Anna and Ben. Pattern: [Name] and [Name]'s [noun] — joint · [Name]'s and [Name]'s [plural noun] — separate · [Name]'s and my [noun] — with a first-person partner Contrast: The single-'s pattern is another consequence of the ending attaching to the phrase: Anna and Ben is one noun phrase, so it takes one ending. When a pronoun joins the coordination the pattern breaks, because pronouns have their own possessive forms: ✓ Ben's and my apartment, ✗ Ben and my apartment, ✗ me and Ben's apartment (common in speech, nonstandard in writing).

Marks two or more possessors, with the apostrophe placement showing whether they own the thing together or separately.

  1. We spent the weekend at Anna and Ben's cabin. (joint, singular countable)
  2. Anna and Ben's wedding is in October.
  3. Anna's and Ben's families have never met. (separate, plural countable)
  4. Ben's and my apartment is on the third floor.
  5. The judge compared the plaintiff's and the defendant's accounts.
  6. Mom and Dad's anniversary is Sunday.
  7. All of Anna and Ben's furniture came from the same store. (uncountable)
  8. Isn't that Anna and Ben's dog? (question)

Chris's#

Pronunciation: /ˈkrɪsɪz/ · KRIS-iz Type: genitive · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (Chris's desk), plural countable (Chris's parents), uncountable (Chris's luggage). The possessor is a singular name or noun that already ends in -s or another sibilant — Chris, the boss, the witness, Texas, Dickens. With of: The of-genitive is an escape hatch when the pile-up is ugly: the policies of Congress rather than Congress's policies. Both are correct. Pattern: [singular name or noun ending in a sibilant]'s [noun] — Chris's desk, the boss's office, Texas's economy Variants: the two American style guides split, and they split differently for names than for common nouns.   1. Chris's desk is the one by the window. (singular countable)
  2. The boss's office is at the end of the hall.
  3. Chris's parents are flying in on Thursday. (plural countable)
  4. Texas's economy grew faster than the national average. (uncountable)
  5. The witness's testimony contradicted the report.
  6. She quoted Dickens's description of the fog.
  7. Isn't that James's car out front? (question)
  8. The hostess's name was not on the list. (negative)

each other's#

Pronunciation: /ˌitʃ ˈʌðɚz/ · eech UTH-erz Type: genitive (reciprocal) · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun — singular countable (each other's car), plural countable (each other's kids), uncountable (each other's mail). The possessors are two or more people or things in a crosswise relation, so the subject of the clause is always plural; the possessive phrase itself stays singular in shape. Against both: both their cars is two cars owned one apiece, while each other's cars is the swap. Agreement: the subject is plural, so the verb is plural — ✓ The twins wear each other's clothes, ✗ The twins wears. The noun after each other's can be singular or plural on its own terms: they finished each other's sentences, they parked in each other's spot.

  4. The roommates keep opening each other's mail. (uncountable)
  5. The partners countersigned one another's forms. (formal variant)
  6. The two candidates attacked each other's voting record. (singular countable)
  7. Are those two still parking in each other's spots? (question)
  8. The sisters never wear each other's clothes. (negative)

somebody else's#

The of-genitive and the double genitive#

English marks possession two ways. The s-genitive puts the possessor before the noun and inside the determiner slot; the of-genitive puts it after the noun in a prepositional phrase, leaving the head noun free to take its own determiner. The double genitive uses both at once, and exists precisely because the determiner slot cannot hold two things.

of#

Pronunciation: /ʌv/, weak /əv/, /ə/ · UHV, weak uhv / uh Type: genitive (of-genitive) — a preposition, not a determiner · Position: postmodifier; the head noun keeps its own determiner · Register: neutral Goes with: links a head noun to a following noun phrase that owns, contains, produces, or defines it — the roof of the house. Each half keeps its own determiner, one on the head noun and another on the noun after of, which is exactly what the s-genitive cannot do. Any noun type can head the phrase: singular countable (the size of the room), plural countable (the members of the board), uncountable (the smell of the paint). With of: Of is the item itself. It requires a full noun phrase or a possessive pronoun after it, never a bare possessive determiner: ✓ of the house, ✓ of mine, ✗ of my. The partitive frame runs on its own schedule: all (of) the students leaves of optional, each of the students and most of the students require it, all of them requires it before a pronoun, and every never takes it at all — ✗ every of the students, ✓ every one of the students. Pattern: [determiner] [head noun] of [noun phrase] — the title of the book · [determiner] [head noun] of [possessive pronoun] — a friend of mine (see of mine) · [quantifier] of [determiner + noun] — most of the students Contrast: The choice between the girl's name and the name of the girl runs on three factors. Animacy — people and animals prefer 's (the girl's name), plain objects prefer of (the leg of the table). Weight — long or heavily modified possessors go after of (the proposal of the committee that met last Thursday). Focus — whatever lands last carries the emphasis. Institutions, places, publications, vehicles, and time words break the animacy preference and take 's freely; see the company's and today's.

  4. The members of the board have not been notified. (plural countable head, negative)
  5. He's a friend of my brother's. (double genitive)
  6. The population of Chicago has been flat for a decade.
  7. I can't stand the smell of the paint. (uncountable head)
  8. Each of the applicants was interviewed twice. (each of the — of is obligatory, verb singular)

Notes and tricky cases#

Only one central determiner. A possessive fills the same slot as an article or a demonstrative, so it excludes them: ✗ the my book, ✗ a your friend, ✗ this her idea, ✗ the John's car. Predeterminers are fine in front (all my cousins, both her sisters, half his paycheck, twice their salary), and postdeterminers are fine behind (my first two jobs, her many objections). To combine indefiniteness with possession, use the double genitive: a friend of mine, that car of yours, some cousins of my wife's.

Of before a possessive. A quantifier in front of a possessive follows exactly the same of-rule it follows in front of the. Predeterminers make of optional: all (of) my cousins, both (of) her sisters, half (of) his paycheck. Everything else requires of: ✓ each of my options, ✗ each my options; ✓ neither of your choices, ✗ neither your choices; ✓ several of our clients, ✓ most of its revenue, ✓ none of their arguments, ✓ two of their kids. And every never takes of at all — ✗ every of my friends; the phrase has to be built with one: ✓ every one of my friends. Before a pronoun, of is compulsory even for the predeterminers: ✓ all of them, ✗ all them; ✓ both of us, ✗ both us.

Agreement. A verb agrees with the head noun, never with the possessor sitting in front of it. The teachers' lounge is on the second floor (singular lounge, though teachers is plural); The children's coats are in the closet (plural coats, though children's is one phrase); Two weeks' notice is required (singular notice, though weeks is plural). Where the possessor is itself a quantified antecedent, the ordinary quantifier concord applies: each, every, either, neither take a singular verb (Each applicant has submitted their résumé; Neither driver was insured), while both, all, several take a plural one (Both drivers were insured). Choosing singular their over his or her never changes the verb.

The possessive with a gerund. Careful writing uses the possessive before an -ing form used as a noun: I appreciate your coming, They objected to his leaving early, We were surprised at Maria's quitting. The objective form (I appreciate you coming) is ordinary in speech and increasingly accepted in print; the possessive is safer in formal writing. With a long or inanimate subject the possessive is genuinely awkward and gets dropped: ✓ the chance of the plan failing, stiff the chance of the plan's failing.

Singular they. Somebody left their umbrella is standard modern American English, endorsed by AP and Chicago, and is also the correct possessive for a person who uses they for themselves. The alternatives are his or her (formal and clumsy when repeated), generic his (dated), and, best of all, a plural rewrite: All the guests brought their own lunches.

Plurals that are not genitives. A plural noun in front of another noun is not automatically a possessive. When it merely describes the kind of thing, American style guides treat it as descriptive rather than possessive and drop the apostrophe: a farmers market, a teachers college, a veterans hospital, a writers workshop, Publishers Weekly, Veterans Affairs. The line is not always sharp, and dictionaries still record farmers' market alongside farmers market; where a proper name has frozen one way or the other (Lowe's, St. John's, Teachers Insurance), look it up rather than reason it out.

The dialectal -n possessives are pronouns, not determiners. His'n, her'n, our'n, your'n, their'n — spelled with or without the apostrophe (hisn, hern, ourn, yourn, theirn) and heard in Appalachian, Ozark, and older Southern and Midland American speech, as well as in the northern English dialects they came from — were built by analogy with the old absolute forms mine and thine. They behave like the pronoun mine, not like the determiner: they stand alone and never precede a noun. ✓ That truck is his'n, ✓ Ourn is the blue house, ✓ Is this yourn? — but ✗ his'n truck, ✗ yourn keys, ✗ their'n place. Apply the slot test and it settles at once: nothing can follow them, so they never fill the central determiner slot. The determiner in those same dialects is the ordinary his, her, our, your, their. Because they are possessive pronouns, they get no entry in this file; see Pronouns. Like the forms in y'all's, they are unremarkable in local speech and strongly marked in writing — recognize them, don't write them.

Possessives agree with the possessor. English possessive determiners take their form from who owns the thing, never from the thing owned — unlike French, Spanish, Italian, and many other languages. David called his mother (not ✗ her mother); Maria lost her wallet, her keys, and her phone; the committee published its findings, though findings is plural.
