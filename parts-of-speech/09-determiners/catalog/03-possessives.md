# Possessive and genitive determiners

> The determiners that answer *whose?* — the seven personal possessives, the interrogative and relative *whose*, and any noun phrase at all with an apostrophe-s stuck on the end.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every member of this group marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or connected with somebody or something. Two facts unite them. First, they all occupy the **central determiner** slot, and that slot holds exactly one item — which is why ✗ *the my book* and ✗ *a your friend* are impossible in English, and why the double genitive (*a friend of mine*) exists as the workaround. Second, the possessive is written with an apostrophe, and four of these determiners have a contraction that sounds identical to them — *its/it's*, *their/they're*, *whose/who's*, *your/you're* — which together make up the most common error class in written American English.

The class is closed at one end and open at the other. The personal possessives are a fixed list of seven (*my, your, his, her, its, our, their*), plus *whose*, generic *one's*, and archaic *thy*. But the genitive is a productive construction: **any** noun phrase can take *'s* and drop straight into the same slot — *John's*, *the company's*, *the children's*, *somebody else's*, *the King of Spain's*. One last rule to carry through the whole file: possessives agree with the **possessor**, never with the noun that follows. *David called his mother*; *the committee published its findings*.

## The personal possessive determiners

Seven words, one for each person and number, and each has a matching standalone **pronoun** (*mine, yours, his, hers, —, ours, theirs*). The determiner precedes a noun; the pronoun replaces the whole noun phrase. Get this pair wrong and you get ✗ *a my friend* or ✗ *the book is my*.

### her

**Pronunciation:** /hɝ/, weak /hɚ/, /ɚ/ &middot; *HUR, weak her / er*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*her car*), plural countable (*her keys*), uncountable (*her patience*) — and gerunds (*her leaving*). Never another central determiner: ✗ *the her car*, ✗ *a her friend*. Never stands alone with no noun after it — that is the pronoun *hers*.
**With *of*:** Takes no *of*-phrase of its own. When *of* appears before *her*, it belongs to a preceding quantifier: *all of her friends*, *most of her time*, *neither of her options*. The double genitive requires the pronoun: *a friend of hers*, never ✗ *a friend of her*.
**Pattern:** *her* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *her* [noun] — *all her cousins*, *half her paycheck* &middot; *her* [ordinal] [cardinal] [adjective] [noun] — *her first two real jobs* &middot; *her own* [noun] &middot; [quantifier] *of her* [noun]
**Contrast:** English has three *her*s. The **determiner** precedes a noun (*her coat*); the **object pronoun** follows a verb or preposition (*I called her*, *with her*); the **possessive pronoun** *hers* stands alone (*the coat is hers*). The spelling ✗ *her's* is never correct. Against *his*: the choice tracks the possessor's gender, not the noun's — *Maria called her brother*, *David called his mother*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a female person or animal already identified.

**1.** The ordinary possessive. **2.** A dated and now largely poetic use for ships, countries, and machines (*the ship and her crew*). Modern American usage, including AP style, writes *its* here.

1. *Her* apartment is two blocks from the office. (singular countable)
2. Maria left *her* keys, *her* badge, and *her* laptop on the desk. (plural countable)
3. I admire *her* patience with that class. (uncountable)
4. All *her* cousins flew in for the wedding. (predeterminer *all*, no *of*)
5. She started the company with *her* own savings.
6. I appreciated *her* driving me to the airport. (gerund)
7. Isn't that *her* car in the driveway? (question — the determiner does not change)
8. She hasn't returned *her* library books. (negative)
9. The ship and *her* crew reached port at dawn. (sense 2, dated — modern American writing uses *its*)

### his

**Pronunciation:** /hɪz/, weak /ɪz/ &middot; *HIZ, weak iz*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*his phone*), plural countable (*his parents*), uncountable (*his luggage*) — and gerunds (*his quitting*). Never with another central determiner: ✗ *the his phone*.
**With *of*:** No *of*-phrase of its own; *of* before *his* belongs to a preceding quantifier: *both of his sisters*, *none of his excuses*. The double genitive uses the identical form: *a cousin of his*.
**Pattern:** *his* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *his* [noun] — *all his friends*, *both his brothers* &middot; *his* [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — *his first three albums* &middot; *his own* [noun] &middot; *a* [noun] *of his*
**Contrast:** *his* is the one possessive whose determiner and pronoun forms are identical: *his car* (determiner, noun follows) versus *the car is his* (pronoun, nothing follows). Compare *her/hers*, *your/yours*, *our/ours*, *their/theirs*. Against generic *their*: older writing used *his* for a person of unknown gender (*Every student must bring his book*); modern American writing uses singular *their* or, in formal prose, *his or her*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a male person or animal already identified.

**1.** The ordinary possessive. **2.** The generic masculine, covering a person of unspecified gender. Standard until the 1970s, now avoided in American editing — replace with singular *their*, with *his or her*, or by making the subject plural.

1. *His* flight lands at six. (singular countable)
2. David forgot *his* wallet, *his* phone, and *his* charger. (plural countable)
3. The airline lost *his* luggage in Denver. (uncountable)
4. Both *his* brothers work downtown. (predeterminer *both*, no *of*)
5. He paid the deposit out of *his* own savings.
6. Every applicant should submit *his* résumé by Friday. (dated generic — today, *their résumé*)
7. Didn't he bring *his* laptop? (question)
8. He never answers *his* phone after six. (negative)

**See also:** [her](#her), [their](#their), [his or her](#his-or-her), [his (periphrastic genitive)](#his-periphrastic-genitive), [of mine](#of-mine)

### its

**Pronunciation:** /ɪts/ — no separate weak form. Alone among the personal possessives, *its* does not reduce: unstressed it stays /ɪts/, exactly like *it's* &middot; *ITS*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*its handle*), plural countable (*its wheels*), uncountable (*its funding*) — and gerunds (*its failing*). The possessor is a thing, a place, an organization, an abstraction, or an animal whose sex is unstated; not a named person. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the its logo*.
**With *of*:** None of its own. *Of* before *its* belongs to a preceding quantifier: *most of its revenue*, *half of its board*. There is no double-genitive form — ✗ *a page of its* is impossible; say *one of its pages*.
**Pattern:** *its* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *its* [noun] — *all its employees*, *half its budget* &middot; *its own* [noun] &middot; [quantifier] *of its* [noun]
**Contrast:** ***its* versus *it's* is the most-corrected error in written English.** *Its* is the possessive determiner and **never** takes an apostrophe (*the dog wagged its tail*). *It's* is the contraction of *it is* or *it has* (*it's raining*, *it's been a week*). The test: if you can expand the word to *it is* or *it has*, write *it's*; otherwise write *its*. The form ✗ *its'* does not exist in English in any sense. Note also that the possessive-pronoun slot is empty here: there is no ✗ *the tail is its* — rewrite as *the tail is the dog's*.

Marks the following noun as a part, property, product, or possession of a thing, an animal, an institution, or an idea already mentioned.

1. The dog chased *its* tail for ten minutes. (singular countable)
2. The company changed *its* logo again.
3. Portland is famous for *its* bridges. (plural countable)
4. The startup ran through *its* funding in a year. (uncountable)
5. Every state sets *its* own speed limits. (*every* + singular countable, singular verb *sets*)
6. The bill passed on *its* third reading.
7. Doesn't the museum publish *its* attendance numbers? (question)
8. The city has never released *its* full report. (negative)

**See also:** [it's (not a determiner)](#its-not-a-determiner), [their](#their), [the company's](#the-companys), [own](#own)

### my

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ/, weak /mə/ &middot; *MY, weak muh*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*my car*), plural countable (*my parents*), uncountable (*my luggage*) — plus gerunds (*my leaving*) and vocative address terms (*my friend*, *my dear*). Never another central determiner: ✗ *the my car*, ✗ *a my friend*.
**With *of*:** None of its own; *of* before *my* belongs to a preceding quantifier (*all of my cousins*, *neither of my options*, *a couple of my coworkers*). The double genitive requires the pronoun *mine*: *a friend of mine*, ✗ *a friend of my*.
**Pattern:** *my* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *my* [noun] — *all my cousins*, *half my paycheck*, *both my sisters* &middot; *my* [ordinal] [cardinal] [adjective] [noun] — *my first two real jobs* &middot; *my own* [noun] &middot; *my* [gerund] — *my leaving early*
**Variants:** ***mine*** /maɪn/ (*MYNE*) — not a separate word but the archaic **prevocalic** shape of this same determiner, standing before a vowel sound exactly as *an* does: *mine eyes*, *mine own*, *mine host*, *mine enemy*. It is the *thy* ~ *thine* alternation one person over, and it is dead outside quotation. Keep prenominal *mine* apart from standalone *mine*, which is a **pronoun**, not a determiner: *mine eyes* (noun follows — determiner) versus *that seat is mine* (nothing follows — pronoun). See [mine](#mine) for the register, the *h*- extension, and the examples. &middot; dialectal *me* — *me dad*, *me mum* (British, Irish, and Australian speech; not standard American)
**Contrast:** *my* precedes a noun, *mine* stands alone: *my seat* versus *that seat is mine*. Modern English does not use *mine* as a determiner except in the frozen archaisms *mine eyes* and *mine host* (see [mine](#mine)). Do not confuse the possessive with the object pronoun *me*: ✓ *He gave me my ticket*, ✗ *He gave my me ticket*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the speaker.

**1.** The ordinary possessive. **2.** In direct address, marking closeness or (often) condescension: *my friend*, *my dear*, *my good man*. **3.** Before a gerund, naming the doer of the action: *They didn't appreciate my leaving early.*

1. *My* keys are in *my* other jacket. (plural countable, then singular countable)
2. All *my* cousins live in Ohio. (predeterminer *all*; *all of my cousins* is equally correct)
3. She was *my* first real boss.
4. I paid for it with *my* own money. (uncountable)
5. They didn't appreciate *my* leaving early. (gerund)
6. *My* friend, that is not how this works. (vocative)
7. Have you seen *my* charger anywhere? (question)
8. I can't find *my* passport. (negative)

**See also:** [our](#our), [your](#your), [mine](#mine), [of mine](#of-mine), [own](#own)

### our

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊɚ/, /aʊr/, weak /ɑr/ &middot; *OW-er / OWR, weak ar*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*our house*), plural countable (*our kids*), uncountable (*our luggage*) — and gerunds (*our leaving*). Never another central determiner: ✗ *the our house*.
**With *of*:** None of its own; *of* before *our* belongs to a preceding quantifier: *several of our clients*, *most of our time*. The double genitive uses *ours*: *a neighbor of ours*.
**Pattern:** *our* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *our* [noun] — *all our neighbors*, *half our budget* &middot; *our* [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — *our first two attempts* &middot; *our own* [noun] &middot; [quantifier] *of our* [noun]
**Contrast:** *our* precedes a noun, *ours* stands alone: *our table* versus *the table is ours*. In relaxed speech *our* reduces to /ɑr/ and collides with *are* and with *hour*, which is why ✗ *are house* shows up in casual writing. English does not distinguish inclusive *our* (you and I) from exclusive *our* (my group, not you) — only context does.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with a group that includes the speaker.

1. *Our* flight was delayed three hours. (singular countable)
2. *Our* neighbors repainted their porch. (plural countable)
3. Half *our* budget goes to rent. (predeterminer *half*; *half of our budget* is equally correct)
4. This one is *our* own fault.
5. We lost *our* luggage on the way back. (uncountable)
6. All *our* records are backed up offsite.
7. Is this *our* gate or the next one over? (question)
8. We haven't gotten *our* deposit back yet. (negative)

**See also:** [my](#my), [their](#their), [of mine](#of-mine)

### their

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛr/, weak /ðɚ/ &middot; *THAIR, weak ther*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*their house*), plural countable (*their kids*), uncountable (*their time*) — and gerunds (*their leaving*). The possessor may be plural, **or a single person** whose gender is unknown, unspecified, irrelevant, or nonbinary (singular *they*). Never another central determiner: ✗ *the their house*.
**With *of*:** None of its own; *of* before *their* belongs to a preceding quantifier: *none of their arguments*, *two of their kids*. The double genitive uses *theirs*: *a client of theirs*.
**Pattern:** *their* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *their* [noun] — *all their friends*, *both their cars* &middot; *their own* [noun] &middot; [quantifier] *of their* [noun]
**Contrast:** A four-way trap: *their* (determiner, noun follows), *theirs* (pronoun, nothing follows), *there* (a place, or the dummy subject in *there is*), and *they're* (= *they are*). ✗ *their's* is not a word. Against *his or her*: singular *their* is the ordinary spoken form and is now accepted by AP and Chicago; *his or her* is the formal written alternative and turns clumsy the moment it repeats.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the possessor(s) already identified.

**1.** A plural possessor: *the Wilsons sold their house*. **2.** **Singular *they*** — one person of unknown, unspecified, or nonbinary gender: *Somebody left their umbrella*. The noun stays singular if the referent has only one (*Everyone brought their own lunch*), and the verb still agrees with the subject, not with *their*. **3.** An institution treated as a group of people: *They raised their rates again.* American editing normally prefers *its* for a named organization — *the company raised its rates*.

1. *Their* flight lands before ours. (singular countable)
2. Both *their* kids play soccer. (predeterminer *both*; *both of their kids* is equally correct)
3. Somebody left *their* umbrella in the lobby. (singular *they*)
4. If a patient misses *their* appointment, the clinic charges a fee.
5. The Wilsons sold *their* house in March.
6. Everyone brought *their* own lunch. (*everyone* takes a singular verb — *brought* is neutral, but *Everyone is bringing their own lunch*, never ✗ *Everyone are*)
7. We never did get *their* luggage back. (uncountable, negative)
8. Did the Hills sell *their* boat? (question)

**See also:** [they're (not a determiner)](#theyre-not-a-determiner), [his or her](#his-or-her), [its](#its), [our](#our)

### your

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr/, /jɔr/, weak /jɚ/ &middot; *YOOR / YOR, weak yer*
**Type:** possessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*your seat*), plural countable (*your kids*), uncountable (*your luggage*) — and gerunds (*your coming*). One form covers a singular and a plural addressee. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the your seat*.
**With *of*:** None of its own; *of* before *your* belongs to a preceding quantifier: *some of your questions*, *each of your options*. The double genitive uses *yours*: *a friend of yours*.
**Pattern:** *your* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] *your* [noun] — *all your files*, *half your salary* &middot; *your* [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — *your last two emails* &middot; *your own* [noun] &middot; *your* [gerund] — *your coming on short notice*
**Variants:** regional plural forms — *y'all's* and emphatic *all y'all's* (Southern), *you guys'* (general informal), *yinz's* (Pittsburgh), *youse's* (New York, Philadelphia, Chicago), *you-uns'* / *you'uns'* (Appalachian and the Ohio Valley). All are informal speech, and edited writing uses *your* for both numbers; see [y'all's](#yalls) for the pronunciations and the regional detail.
**Contrast:** *your* + noun, *yours* alone, *you're* = *you are*. The test is expansion: ✗ *Your welcome* fails as *your are welcome*, so it must be *You're welcome*. In fast speech *your* and *you're* both reduce to /jɚ/, which is exactly why the pair is confused so often.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or associated with the person or people addressed.

**1.** The ordinary possessive. **2.** Generic or impersonal *you*, meaning "people in general": *Your average commuter spends an hour in traffic.* Informal; formal writing uses *the average commuter* or *one's*. **3.** Before a gerund, naming the doer: *I appreciate your coming.*

1. Is this *your* seat? (question, singular countable)
2. *Your* passport and *your* boarding pass, please.
3. I appreciate *your* coming on such short notice. (gerund)
4. All *your* files were backed up overnight. (predeterminer *all* + plural countable)
5. Do it *your* own way.
6. *Your* average commuter spends an hour a day in traffic. (generic *your*)
7. Don't forget *your* badge. (negative imperative)
8. We couldn't find *your* baggage on the carousel. (uncountable)

**See also:** [you're (not a determiner)](#youre-not-a-determiner), [y'all's](#yalls), [one's](#ones), [thy](#thy)

## Generic, formal, archaic, and regional possessives

The seven personal possessives leave two gaps: there is no dedicated form for a person of unknown gender, and no dedicated plural *you*. English has filled both gaps repeatedly — with the compound *his or her*, with generic *one's*, with singular *their*, and, regionally, with *y'all's*. The archaic *thy* is the singular *your* that the language lost; *mine* and *thine* are the **prevocalic** shapes of *my* and *thy*, the *a* ~ *an* rule applied to possessives, and the only reason *mine eyes* and *thine own self* are not mistakes. The periphrastic *his*-genitive (*John his book*) is a dead rival of the *'s* ending that survives on a few Renaissance title pages.

### his (periphrastic genitive)

**Pronunciation:** /hɪz/, weak /ɪz/ — and the weak form is the whole point: unstressed, *John his book* and *John's book* are the same string of sounds &middot; *HIZ, weak iz*
**Type:** genitive (periphrastic, archaic) &middot; **Position:** central — the possessor noun and *his* together fill the one central slot &middot; **Register:** obsolete Early Modern English; an American meets it only on Renaissance title pages, in the Book of Common Prayer, and in colonial deeds, wills, and inventories
**Goes with:** the noun possessed may be singular countable (*John his book*), plural countable (*the Duke his men*), or uncountable (*the Bishop his patience*). The real restriction is on the **possessor**, which must be a full noun — normally a personal name or a title — sitting immediately in front of *his*: ✓ *Thomas Hooker his farm*, ✗ *he his farm*, ✗ *somebody his farm*. It cannot be stacked on a possessed noun that already has a determiner (✗ *John his the book*), and nothing may precede the phrase (✗ *the John his book*), because the whole thing is the central determiner.
**With *of*:** No *of*-form of its own. The ordinary alternative in the same period was the plain *of*-genitive — *the fall of Sejanus*, *the farm of Thomas Hooker*. There is no double genitive built on it: ✗ *a book of John his*; Early Modern English wrote *a book of John's*.
**Pattern:** [possessor noun] *his* [noun] — *John his book* &middot; [feminine possessor] *her* [noun] — *Mistress Sands her jointure* &middot; [plural possessor] *their* [noun] — *the Commons their petition* &middot; frozen survival — *for Jesus Christ his sake*
**Variants:** the pronoun matches the possessor, so the same construction also runs as *her* with a feminine possessor (*Elizabeth her letters*) and as *their* with a plural one (*the Commons their petition*). Grammar books call the whole set the *his*-genitive or the periphrastic genitive.
**Contrast:** Not the same thing as the possessive determiner [his](#his), which needs no possessor spelled out in front of it: *his book* names an owner already known, while *John his book* names him twice over. Against ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive): the two were homophones — unstressed *his* is /ɪz/, and so is the *-es* of older *Johnes book* — which is why Early Modern writers, and the King James translators after them, sometimes wrote the pronoun out in full. The folk etymology that grew out of the resemblance is **false**: the *'s* ending descends from the Old English genitive *-es* and is centuries older than this construction, and a feminine possessor took *her*, which no contraction of *his* could ever produce. The mistaken belief did leave one permanent mark, though — it helped fix the **apostrophe** in *'s*, on the theory that a letter had been dropped.

The dead rival of the *'s* ending: a genitive built by naming the possessor and then adding a matching possessive pronoun after it. Recognize it; never write it.

1. *Sejanus his Fall* is the title Ben Jonson put on his tragedy when it was printed in 1605 — the shape an American is likeliest to meet. (singular countable)
2. The Prayer Book collect ends *…for Jesus Christ his sake*. (the one frozen survival still read aloud)
3. A 1639 deed records *Thomas Hooker his farm* on the east bank of the river. (colonial land record — the other place it turns up)
4. *The Duke his men* were quartered in the town all winter. (plural countable)
5. *Mistress Sands her jointure* was entered in the parish book. (feminine possessor takes *her*, never ✗ *his*)
6. *The Commons their petition* was read aloud to the King. (plural possessor takes *their*)
7. Is that *Cotton Mather his diary* in the case, or a facsimile? (question)
8. *The Bishop his patience* was not inexhaustible. (uncountable, negative)
9. No American clerk would draft ✗ *the plaintiff his signature* today; the construction died out in the eighteenth century. (negative — modern legal drafting uses *the plaintiff's signature*)

**See also:** [his](#his), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [of](#of), [thy](#thy)

### his or her

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪz ɚ ˈhɝ/ &middot; *hiz-er-HUR*
**Type:** possessive (compound) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal, written
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*his or her locker*), plural countable (*his or her dependents*), uncountable (*his or her luggage*). The restriction is on the **possessor**, not on the noun possessed: the possessor must be **one** person of unknown or unspecified gender (*each employee … his or her contact information*). A plural possessor takes *their*, never ✗ *the employees … his or her lockers*. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the his or her locker*.
**With *of*:** None of its own; *of* before it belongs to a preceding quantifier (*each of his or her dependents*). The matching standalone pronoun is *his or hers*, awkward enough that writers rewrite the sentence instead.
**Pattern:** *his or her* [singular noun] &middot; *his or her own* [noun] &middot; variants *his/her*, *her or his*, *his, her, or their*
**Contrast:** The formal-register alternative to singular [their](#their). *Their* is shorter, accepted by AP and Chicago, and includes people who use *they* for themselves; *his or her* excludes them and becomes unreadable when repeated across a paragraph. In practice the best fix is usually neither one: make the noun plural — *All employees must update their emergency contacts.* Against generic *his*: *his or her* was the 1980s repair for generic *his*, and singular *their* has since largely replaced both. **Agreement:** the antecedent that licenses *his or her* is grammatically singular, so it takes a **singular verb** — ✓ *Each employee is responsible for his or her own equipment*, ✗ *Each employee are*; ✓ *Every candidate submits his or her transcript*, ✗ *Every candidate submit*. The same holds for *either*, *neither*, and *anyone*: ✓ *Neither applicant has withdrawn his or her name.* Choosing singular *their* instead does **not** change the verb — ✓ *Each employee is responsible for their own equipment*.

Names a possessor who is one unidentified person, without committing to a gender.

1. Each employee must update *his or her* emergency contact. (*each* + singular countable)
2. Every candidate submitted *his or her* transcript by the deadline. (*every* + singular countable)
3. Each participant is responsible for *his or her* travel expenses. (plural countable noun, singular verb *is*)
4. A juror may not discuss the case with *his or her* spouse. (negative)
5. Anyone who parked in Lot C should move *his or her* car.
6. No child may enter the pool without *his or her* parent.
7. Every applicant must show that *his or her* experience is relevant. (uncountable)
8. Has each of the finalists submitted *his or her* portfolio? (question; *each of the* requires *of*)

**See also:** [their](#their), [his](#his), [her](#her), [one's](#ones)

### mine

**Pronunciation:** /maɪn/ — no weak form. *My* reduces to /mə/ in ordinary speech, but this word survives only inside stressed, quoted phrases and never reduces &middot; *MYNE (rhymes with LINE)*
**Type:** possessive (archaic, prevocalic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic — biblical, liturgical, hymn, and poetic English. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in hymnals, and in the Battle Hymn of the Republic, and essentially nowhere else. **Never** in ordinary speech or modern prose.
**Goes with:** any noun **beginning with a vowel sound** — singular countable (*mine host*, *mine enemy*), plural countable (*mine eyes*, *mine ears*), uncountable (*mine iniquity*) — plus the emphasizer *own* (*mine own self*). The vowel is the whole condition, so before a consonant sound the determiner is *my*: ✗ *mine car*, ✗ *mine father*, ✗ *mine book*. Seventeenth-century printing extends it before *h*- as well (*mine heart*, *mine house*), where the *h* was weak or silent. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the mine eyes*, ✗ *a mine friend*. Never with nothing after it — that is the **pronoun** *mine*.
**With *of*:** None of its own. The *of*-phrase *a friend of mine* looks like this word and is not: there *mine* is the possessive **pronoun** filling the double genitive, and it is ordinary modern English. See [of mine](#of-mine).
**Pattern:** *mine* [vowel-initial noun] — *mine eyes*, *mine enemy* &middot; *mine own* [noun] — *mine own self*, *mine own people* &middot; the alternation — *my* [consonant-initial noun] ~ *mine* [vowel-initial noun], as in *my God* but *mine enemies* &middot; vocative — *mine host*
**Variants:** *my* is the same determiner in its other shape, and the pair runs exactly parallel to [thy](#thy) ~ [thine](#thine) one person over. &middot; Early Modern printing also spells it *myne*. &middot; It has no modern or regional survival as a determiner: the *-n* forms still heard in Appalachian and Ozark speech (*hisn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *yourn*, *theirn*) were built by analogy with *mine* and *thine*, but they stand alone and are **pronouns**, not determiners — see [Notes and tricky cases](#notes-and-tricky-cases).
**Contrast:** Two different words are spelled *mine*, and only one of them is archaic. The **determiner** has a noun after it (*mine eyes*) and has been dead for four centuries; the possessive **pronoun** has nothing after it (*that seat is mine*, *a friend of mine*) and is perfectly ordinary today. That split is why *mine* looks alive while *mine eyes* looks like costume. Against [my](#my): one word, two shapes, chosen by the sound that begins the next word — the same machinery as *a* ~ *an*, and *my hand* / *mine eyes* is the pair to remember. Against [thine](#thine): identical rule, one person over.

The prevocalic shape of *my*, frozen inside quotation: it stands before a vowel sound the way *an* does. Recognize it; never write it.

1. *Mine* eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord. (the Battle Hymn of the Republic — plural countable, and the line most Americans know the form from)
2. Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace … for *mine* eyes have seen thy salvation. (Luke 2:29–30, King James Version)
3. Deliver me from *mine* enemies, O my God. (Psalm 59:1, KJV — both shapes in one line: *mine* before a vowel, *my* before a consonant)
4. Thy word have I hid in *mine* heart, that I might not sin against thee. (Psalm 119:11, KJV — the older extension of *mine* before *h-*)
5. Thou *mine* inheritance, now and always. (the hymn *Be Thou My Vision* — uncountable, and the title itself shows *my* before a consonant)
6. *Mine* host of the Garter kept the best cellar in Windsor. (Shakespeare's form of address to an innkeeper — singular countable)
7. Is that *mine* host at the door, or a stranger? (question, in deliberate period pastiche — the only way a modern writer produces the form on purpose)
8. ✗ *Mine* car is in the shop is wrong twice over: the alternation needs a vowel sound, and the determiner died out in the seventeenth century. (negative — the error, not the word)

**See also:** [my](#my), [thine](#thine), [thy](#thy), [of mine](#of-mine), [own](#own)

### one's

**Pronunciation:** /wʌnz/ &middot; *WUNZ*
**Type:** possessive (generic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*one's passport*), plural countable (*one's options*), uncountable (*one's temper*). The possessor is the generic pronoun *one*, meaning "a person, anyone," and standard usage keeps *one … one's* consistent through the sentence. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the one's temper*.
**With *of*:** Only in the fixed phrase *of one's own* — *a room of one's own*, *a place of one's own*.
**Pattern:** *One* … *one's* [noun] &middot; *one's own* [noun] &middot; [noun] *of one's own* &middot; [gerund phrase] — *losing one's temper*
**Contrast:** Three meanings, two spellings. *One's* with an apostrophe is the possessive (*one's own business*); *ones* without one is the plural of the noun or pronoun *one* (*the red ones*); and *one's* — the same spelling as the possessive — is also a contraction of *one is* or *one has* (*one's never sure*). Against *your*: American English almost always switches generic *one* to generic *you* — *You should mind your own business* is the everyday version, while *One should mind one's own business* reads as British or stiff. Older American usage shifted the possessive to *his* (*One should mind his own business*); that is now dated.

Names a possessor who is anyone at all, in the impersonal generic construction.

1. One should mind *one's* own business. (singular countable)
2. It is easy to lose *one's* temper in traffic. (uncountable)
3. One needs a room of *one's* own.
4. Losing *one's* passport abroad is a nightmare.
5. One rarely regrets holding *one's* tongue.
6. Keeping *one's* options open is generally good advice. (plural countable)
7. One cannot always trust *one's* first impression. (negative)
8. Where does one file *one's* appeal? (question)

**See also:** [your](#your), [his or her](#his-or-her), [own](#own)

### thine

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪn/ — no weak form; the word only ever turns up stressed &middot; *THYNE (rhymes with MINE)*
**Type:** possessive (archaic, prevocalic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic — the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, Shakespeare, hymns, and deliberate period writing. A few conservative Quaker meetings still keep the plain speech (*thee*, *thy*, *thine*) as a religious practice; outside that, an American reads this word and never says it.
**Goes with:** any noun **beginning with a vowel sound**, with a **single, familiar** addressee — the *thou* of older English — so singular countable (*thine enemy*), plural countable (*thine eyes*, *thine ears*), uncountable (*thine anger*), plus the emphasizer *own* (*thine own self*). Before a consonant sound the determiner is *thy*: ✗ *thine name*, ✗ *thine sword*, ✗ *thine father*. Seventeenth-century printing extends it before *h*- as well (*thine heart*, *thine house*). A plural addressee never takes it — that is *your*. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the thine eyes*. Never with nothing after it — that is the **pronoun** *thine*.
**With *of*:** None. There is no double genitive built on the determiner: Early Modern English wrote *a friend of thine*, where *thine* is the **pronoun**.
**Pattern:** *thine* [vowel-initial noun] — *thine eyes* &middot; *thine own* [noun] — *thine own self* &middot; the alternation — *thy* [consonant-initial noun] ~ *thine* [vowel-initial noun], as in *thy whole body* but *thine eye* &middot; pronoun use, nothing following — *the kingdom is thine*
**Variants:** *thy* is the same determiner in its other shape; see [thy](#thy) for the pair treated together. &middot; Early Modern printing also spells it *thyne*. &middot; The first-person twin is [mine](#mine), and the machinery is identical.
**Contrast:** Two words are spelled *thine*, and the test is the usual one: a noun after it makes it a **determiner** (*thine eyes*), nothing after it makes it a **pronoun** (*the kingdom is thine*, *what's mine is thine*). Against [thy](#thy): one word, two shapes, chosen by the sound that begins the next word, exactly as *a* ~ *an* is. Against [your](#your): modern English lost the familiar singular altogether, so *your* now covers everything *thy* and *thine* used to. The standard beginner's mistake is sprinkling *thine* on any noun to sound old — ✗ *thine sword*, ✗ *thine kingdom*. The rule is mechanical, and it is about the next sound, not about solemnity.

The prevocalic shape of *thy*: the possessive of one familiar addressee, standing before a vowel sound the way *an* does. Recognize it; never write it outside quotation.

1. To *thine* own self be true. (Polonius in *Hamlet* — the line most Americans can quote, with *thine* before the emphasizer *own*)
2. *Thine* eyes shall see the king in his beauty. (Isaiah 33:17, King James Version — plural countable)
3. If *thine* eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light. (Matthew 6:22, KJV — both shapes in one verse: *thine* before a vowel, *thy* before a consonant)
4. Trust in the Lord with all *thine* heart, and lean not unto *thine* own understanding. (Proverbs 3:5, KJV — the older extension before *h-*, then *thine own*)
5. We are consumed by *thine* anger, and by thy wrath are we troubled. (Psalm 90:7, KJV — uncountable)
6. Cast out first the beam out of *thine* own eye. (Luke 6:42, KJV — singular countable)
7. Is *thine* heart right, as my heart is with thy heart? (2 Kings 10:15, KJV — question)
8. ✗ *Thine* sword is the beginner's error every time: *sword* begins with a consonant sound, so the phrase is *thy sword*. (negative — the error, not the word)

**See also:** [thy](#thy), [mine](#mine), [my](#my), [your](#your)

### thy

**Pronunciation:** /ðaɪ/; prevocalic *thine* /ðaɪn/ &middot; *THY (rhymes with MY); THYNE*
**Type:** possessive (archaic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic — liturgical, biblical, poetic, or deliberately old-fashioned
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*thy name*), plural countable (*thine eyes*), uncountable (*thy mercy*) — with a singular and familiar addressee (the *thou* of older English). The form *thy* comes before a consonant sound and *thine* before a vowel sound. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the thy hand*.
**With *of*:** None. The standalone pronoun is *thine* — *the kingdom is thine*.
**Pattern:** *thy* [consonant-initial noun] — *thy name* &middot; *thine* [vowel-initial noun] — *thine eyes* &middot; *thine own* [noun]
**Variants:** ***thine*** /ðaɪn/ (*THYNE*) — not a separate word but the **prevocalic** shape of this same determiner, used before a vowel sound exactly as *an* is: *thine eyes*, *thine enemy*, *thine own self*, *thine heart* (older texts extend it before *h*- as well). It is also the standalone possessive **pronoun** — *the kingdom is thine*, *what's mine is thine* — so the test is the usual one: a noun after it makes it a determiner, nothing after it makes it a pronoun. An American meets *thine* in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, and in hymns, and nowhere else; see [thine](#thine) for the full entry. &middot; *thy* is to *thou* what *your* is to *you*
**Contrast:** The *thy* ~ *thine* alternation copies *a* ~ *an* exactly, and for the same reason: it smooths the join between words. Modern English collapsed the singular/plural and familiar/formal distinctions, so *your* now does every job that *thy* and *your* once split. You will meet *thy* only in the King James Bible, Shakespeare, hymns, and deliberate archaism — never write it in ordinary prose.

Names a single, familiar addressee as the possessor; the ancestor of singular *your*.

1. Hallowed be *thy* name. (singular countable)
2. Honor *thy* father and *thy* mother.
3. To *thine* own self be true. (*thine* before a vowel sound)
4. *Thy* rod and *thy* staff, they comfort me.
5. Open *thine* eyes and look around you. (plural countable)
6. O death, where is *thy* sting? (question)
7. *Thy* mercy endureth forever. (uncountable)
8. Take not *thy* hand from mine. (negative, in the archaic word order that goes with it)

**See also:** [thine](#thine), [your](#your), [mine](#mine), [the](01-articles.md#the)

### y'all's

**Pronunciation:** /jɔlz/, /jɑlz/ &middot; *YAWLZ*
**Type:** possessive (regional) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal Southern and South Midland American speech; **nonstandard in edited writing**
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*y'all's car*), plural countable (*y'all's kids*), uncountable (*y'all's help*). The addressee is plural, or a singular addressee understood together with their household or group. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the y'all's car*.
**With *of*:** None. The same form doubles as the standalone pronoun: *that seat is y'all's*.
**Pattern:** *y'all's* [noun] &middot; *all y'all's* [noun] (emphatic, larger group)
**Variants:** *y'alls*, *your all's*; ***all y'all's*** /ˌɔl ˈjɔlz/ (*awl YAWLZ*) — the emphatic form, addressing every member of a larger group: *All y'all's cars are getting towed.* &middot; parallel regional plural-*you* possessives, every one built the same way (a regional plural *you* plus *'s*): ***you'uns'*** / ***you-uns'*** /ˈjunz/ (*YOONZ*) — Appalachia and the Ohio Valley; ***yinz's*** /ˈjɪnzɪz/ (*YINZ-iz*) — Pittsburgh, where *yinz* is the worn-down local descendant of *you'uns*; ***youse's*** /ˈjuzɪz/ (*YOO-ziz*) — New York, Philadelphia, Chicago, and Irish-American speech; ***you guys'*** /ju ˈɡaɪz/ (*yoo GYZE*) — general informal American, the one form with no regional flag on it. Each takes any noun (*you'uns' truck*, *yinz's kids*, *youse's help*) and, like *y'all's*, doubles as the standalone pronoun (*that one's yinz's*). All are ordinary in speech where they are native and out of place on the page, where *your* covers both numbers.
**Contrast:** Standard English uses one *your* for both singular and plural addressees, and regional Englishes have repeatedly built a plural to fill the gap. These forms are unremarkable in speech within their regions and out of place in formal writing, where *your* covers both numbers. Note the two apostrophes in *y'all's*: one for the contracted *you all*, one for the possessive. Flagged clearly here because a learner needs to **recognize** it, not to write it.

Marks the following noun as belonging to the group being addressed. One entry covers the whole regional set: *y'all's* is the commonest of them, and *all y'all's*, *you'uns'*, *yinz's*, *youse's*, and *you guys'* are the same determiner in the wording of a different region — they never contrast with one another, because no speaker has two of them.

1. Is that *y'all's* dog out front? (question, singular countable)
2. We loved *y'all's* new place.
3. *Y'all's* flight got canceled too?
4. I'll need *y'all's* signatures on this form. (plural countable)
5. All *y'all's* excuses sound the same. (emphatic *all y'all's* — the whole larger group, Southern)
6. Thanks for *y'all's* help with the move. (uncountable)
7. We didn't get *y'all's* message until this morning. (negative)
8. *Y'all's* car is blocking the driveway.
9. Did yinz leave *yinz's* lights on again? (Pittsburgh speech, where *yinz* is the worn-down local descendant of *you'uns*)
10. *You'uns'* truck is settin' out there blockin' the gate. (Appalachian and Ohio Valley speech)
11. *Youse's* order is up at the counter. (New York, Philadelphia, Chicago, and Irish-American speech)

**See also:** [your](#your), [their](#their), [Notes and tricky cases](#notes-and-tricky-cases)

## Interrogative and relative genitives

*Whose* is the only wh-word that is a possessive determiner, and it does double duty: it asks a question (*Whose car is that?*) and it introduces a relative clause (*the man whose car was stolen*). Its formal competitor for inanimate antecedents is *of which*, and its free-relative cousin is *whosever*.

### of which

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *uhv WICH*
**Type:** relative genitive &middot; **Position:** postmodifier — **not** the central determiner slot &middot; **Register:** formal, written
**Goes with:** a head noun naming a part, property, or component of an **inanimate** antecedent — *a house the roof of which leaks*. That head noun may be singular countable (*the roof of which*), plural countable (*the terms of which*), or uncountable (*the funding of which*). Because *of which* is a prepositional phrase and not a determiner, the head noun keeps its own determiner: *the roof of which*, *three floors of which*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is part of the item itself and is obligatory. Two legal orders: postposed (*the roof of which*) and fronted (*of which the roof*), the second more formal still.
**Pattern:** [antecedent], [determiner] [noun] *of which* [verb] … &middot; [antecedent], *of which* [determiner] [noun] [verb] …
**Contrast:** The formal alternative to relative [whose](#whose) with a non-human antecedent. *Whose* is fully standard with things — *a house whose roof leaks* — and far more natural; *of which* belongs to legal, academic, and technical registers where some writers still avoid *whose* for inanimates. With a **human** antecedent only *whose* works: ✓ *the man whose car was stolen*, ✗ *the man the car of which was stolen*.

Links a relative clause to its antecedent by naming something the antecedent has or contains.

1. She cited a study the conclusions *of which* have since been retracted. (plural countable head — verb *have*)
2. It is an old contract, the terms *of which* nobody remembers.
3. The building, the top three floors *of which* are vacant, sold last month.
4. He owns a vineyard *of which* the yield has doubled in five years. (fronted order)
5. They passed a rule the effect *of which* was to end the program. (singular countable head — verb *was*)
6. This is the only clause the meaning *of which* is genuinely disputed.
7. It was a grant the funding *of which* has never been accounted for. (uncountable head, negative)
8. Is that the statute the wording *of which* is under review? (question)

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [of](#of), [whosever](#whosever)

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ*
**Type:** interrogative and relative (genitive) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*whose car*), plural countable (*whose keys*), uncountable (*whose money*). The possessor may be a person, an animal, or a thing (*a novel whose ending nobody likes*). It fills the central determiner slot, so ✗ *the whose car* and ✗ *a whose idea* are impossible.
**With *of*:** None as a determiner. The partitive question uses *which*: *Which of these is yours?* When *whose* takes no following noun it is a pronoun, not a determiner: *Whose is this?*
**Pattern:** *Whose* [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — *Whose car is that?* &middot; [antecedent noun] *whose* [noun] [verb] … (relative) &middot; *I don't know whose* [noun] *it is.* (embedded question) &middot; *Whose is this?* (pronoun — no noun follows)
**Contrast:** ***whose* versus *who's*.** *Whose* asks or states possession; *who's* is the contraction of *who is* or *who has*. Expand it to test: *Who's calling?* = *who is calling*, but *Whose call is it?* has no expansion, so it takes *whose*. Against *of which*: both build an inanimate genitive relative, and *whose* is the ordinary choice (*a car whose brakes failed*), *of which* the formal one. Against *which*: *which* selects an item from a set (*Which car?*), *whose* asks who owns it (*Whose car?*).

**1.** **Interrogative** — asks which person or thing the following noun belongs to. **2.** **Relative** — introduces a relative clause and ties its noun to the antecedent, for human and non-human antecedents alike.

1. *Whose* jacket is on my chair? (question, singular countable)
2. I have no idea *whose* idea this was. (embedded question)
3. The engineer *whose* design won the contract is only 29. (relative, human antecedent)
4. She's the one *whose* flight got canceled.
5. It's an old house *whose* wiring needs replacing. (relative, non-human antecedent, uncountable)
6. *Whose* keys are these, and *whose* car is blocking the driveway? (plural countable, then singular)
7. Nobody remembers *whose* money paid for it. (negative, uncountable)
8. *Whose* is this? (pronoun — no noun follows, so not a determiner here)

**See also:** [who's (not a determiner)](#whos-not-a-determiner), [of which](#of-which), [whosever](#whosever), [Interrogative determiners](../README.md#interrogative-determiners)

### whosever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈzɛvɚ/ &middot; *hoo-ZEV-er*
**Type:** relative genitive (free relative) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal, rare
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*whosever car*), plural countable (*whosever keys*), uncountable (*whosever property*) — inside a free relative clause meaning "the one whose." Unlike *whose*, it supplies its own antecedent, so no noun precedes it. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the whosever car*.
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** *Whosever* [noun] [verb] …, [main clause] &middot; [main clause] *whosever* [noun] [verb] … &middot; everyday spoken equivalent: *whoever's* [noun]
**Variants:** *whoseever*, *whosesoever* (archaic and legal); the ordinary spoken form is *whoever's*
**Contrast:** In American speech people say *whoever's*: *Whoever's car that is needs to move it.* *Whosever* is the tidy written form and rare enough to sound stilted. Do not confuse it with relative [whose](#whose), which requires an antecedent noun (*the man whose car…*); *whosever* contains its own (*whosever car…*). **Agreement:** the free relative names **one** person, so a main verb takes the singular even when the noun inside the clause is plural — ✓ *Whosever keys these are needs to claim them*, ✗ *…need to claim them*.

Introduces a clause that identifies a possessor without naming one — "the person whose."

1. *Whosever* car is blocking the driveway needs to move it.
2. *Whosever* name is on the lease is responsible for the damage.
3. She promised the prize to *whosever* entry arrived first.
4. *Whosever* idea this was, it worked.
5. The court will notify *whosever* property is affected.
6. *Whosever* turn it is, the trash goes out tonight.
7. *Whosever* keys these are needs to come get them. (plural countable noun, singular main verb)
8. We will not release the car to *whosever* insurance pays first. (uncountable, negative)

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [of which](#of-which)

## The genitive endings

Any noun phrase in English can be turned into a determiner by adding the genitive ending. The ending is a **clitic**, not a suffix: it attaches to the last word of the phrase regardless of what part of speech that word is, which is why *the King of Spain's daughter* and *somebody else's problem* work the way they do. Adding it makes the phrase definite, so no article can join it: ✓ *John's book*, ✗ *the John's book*.

### 's (singular genitive)

**Pronunciation:** /s/ after a voiceless sound (*Pat's* /pæts/), /z/ after a voiced sound (*Dan's* /dænz/), /ɪz/ after a sibilant (*Chris's* /ˈkrɪsɪz/) &middot; *s / z / iz*
**Type:** genitive &middot; **Position:** central — the whole genitive phrase fills the slot &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** attaches to the **end of a complete noun phrase**, not to the head noun. The noun it then determines may be singular countable (*Dan's car*), plural countable (*Dan's kids*), or uncountable (*Dan's patience*). It cannot combine with an article or demonstrative on the same noun: ✗ *the Dan's car*, ✗ *this Dan's car*.
**With *of*:** The rival construction is the *of*-genitive — *the girl's name* / *the name of the girl*. See [of](#of) for which one to choose.
**Pattern:** [noun phrase]*'s* [noun] &middot; [noun phrase]*'s own* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] [noun phrase]*'s* [noun] — *all Dan's friends* &middot; [noun phrase]*'s* [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — *Dan's first two jobs*
**Contrast:** Three identical-sounding endings. *'s* marks possession (*Dan's car*); plain *s* marks a plural (*the Smiths are away*); and *'s* also contracts *is* and *has* (*Dan's here* = *Dan is here*). Since the three are indistinguishable in speech, the apostrophe carries the whole load in writing — hence the greengrocer's ✗ *apple's for sale*. Against the plural genitive: *the student's essays* is one student, *the students' essays* is several. See [s' (plural genitive)](#s-plural-genitive).

The productive genitive ending: it converts any noun phrase into a central determiner, and with a true possessor it makes the resulting phrase definite. (The measure genitive is the exception — *two weeks' notice* stays indefinite; see [two weeks'](#two-weeks).)

1. That's *my sister's* car. (singular countable)
2. *The dog's* leash is by the door.
3. *Chicago's* winters are brutal. (plural countable)
4. *Dan's* patience finally ran out. (uncountable)
5. *The senator's* office issued a statement.
6. I borrowed *my roommate's* charger.
7. Isn't that *Ana's* apartment on the corner? (question)
8. We never got *the landlord's* approval. (negative)

**See also:** [s' (plural genitive)](#s-plural-genitive), [John's](#johns), [the company's](#the-companys), [of](#of), [the King of Spain's](#the-king-of-spains), [his (periphrastic genitive)](#his-periphrastic-genitive)

### s' (plural genitive)

**Pronunciation:** silent — *the students'* is pronounced exactly like *the students* /ðə ˈstudn̩ts/ &middot; *(no added sound)*
**Type:** genitive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a **regular plural noun already ending in -s**; the apostrophe goes after that *s* and nothing further is added. The noun it determines may be singular countable, plural countable, or uncountable: *the teachers' lounge*, *the teachers' contracts*, *the teachers' patience*. Irregular plurals that do not end in *-s* take a full *'s* instead — see [the children's](#the-childrens).
**With *of*:** the *of*-genitive is the usual alternative for long or inanimate plural possessors: *the conclusions of the reports*.
**Pattern:** [regular plural noun]*'* [noun] — *the teachers' lounge*, *the Johnsons' driveway* &middot; measure phrases: *two weeks' notice*, *ten years' experience*
**Contrast:** *the student's essays* = one student, several essays. *The students' essays* = several students. ✗ *The students essays* = a missing apostrophe. Placement is the only signal and it is completely inaudible, which is why plural possessives get checked so hard in editing. Family names pluralize first, then take the apostrophe: *the Johnsons* → *the Johnsons' house*; never ✗ *the Johnson's house* for a family. Against the **descriptive plural**, which takes no apostrophe at all: *a farmers market*, *a teachers college*, *a veterans hospital*, *Publishers Weekly* — here the plural noun names the kind of thing rather than an owner, and American style guides drop the apostrophe. Ask whether the sense is ownership (*the teachers' contracts*) or description (*a teachers college*).

The plural counterpart of *'s*: same job, same silence, different apostrophe placement.

1. *The teachers'* lounge is on the second floor. (singular countable head — verb *is*, agreeing with *lounge*, not with *teachers*)
2. We repaved *the Johnsons'* driveway.
3. All *the players'* contracts expire in June. (plural countable head — verb *expire*)
4. *The workers'* patience ran out in the third week. (uncountable)
5. That's *the girls'* locker room.
6. Both *parties'* lawyers agreed to a delay. (predeterminer *both* + plural)
7. Isn't that *the neighbors'* dog again? (question)
8. *The owners'* association has not met since March. (negative)

**See also:** ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [the children's](#the-childrens), [two weeks'](#two-weeks), [Chris's](#chriss)

## Genitive noun phrases as determiners

Eleven recurring shapes of the genitive phrase. They are all the same construction — noun phrase plus *'s* in the central determiner slot — but each raises its own question about where the apostrophe goes, or about whether the *of*-genitive would be better.

### Anna and Ben's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈænə ən ˈbɛnz/ &middot; *AN-uh un BENZ*
**Type:** genitive (coordinate) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*Anna and Ben's cabin*), plural countable (*Anna and Ben's kids*), uncountable (*Anna and Ben's furniture*). One *'s*, on the last name only, means **joint** possession (*Anna and Ben's house* — one house). An *'s* on each name means **separate** possession, and the following noun is then usually plural (*Anna's and Ben's houses* — two houses).
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive sidesteps the ambiguity but sounds stiff with people: *the cabin of Anna and Ben*.
**Pattern:** [Name] *and* [Name]*'s* [noun] — joint &middot; [Name]*'s and* [Name]*'s* [plural noun] — separate &middot; [Name]*'s and my* [noun] — with a first-person partner
**Contrast:** The single-*'s* pattern is another consequence of the ending attaching to the **phrase**: *Anna and Ben* is one noun phrase, so it takes one ending. When a pronoun joins the coordination the pattern breaks, because pronouns have their own possessive forms: ✓ *Ben's and my apartment*, ✗ *Ben and my apartment*, ✗ *me and Ben's apartment* (common in speech, nonstandard in writing).

Marks two or more possessors, with the apostrophe placement showing whether they own the thing together or separately.

1. We spent the weekend at *Anna and Ben's* cabin. (joint, singular countable)
2. *Anna and Ben's* wedding is in October.
3. *Anna's and Ben's* families have never met. (separate, plural countable)
4. *Ben's and my* apartment is on the third floor.
5. The judge compared *the plaintiff's and the defendant's* accounts.
6. *Mom and Dad's* anniversary is Sunday.
7. All of *Anna and Ben's* furniture came from the same store. (uncountable)
8. Isn't that *Anna and Ben's* dog? (question)

**See also:** ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [the King of Spain's](#the-king-of-spains), [my](#my)

### Chris's

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkrɪsɪz/ &middot; *KRIS-iz*
**Type:** genitive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*Chris's desk*), plural countable (*Chris's parents*), uncountable (*Chris's luggage*). The possessor is a **singular** name or noun that already ends in *-s* or another sibilant — *Chris*, *the boss*, *the witness*, *Texas*, *Dickens*.
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive is an escape hatch when the pile-up is ugly: *the policies of Congress* rather than *Congress's policies*. Both are correct.
**Pattern:** [singular name or noun ending in a sibilant]*'s* [noun] — *Chris's desk*, *the boss's office*, *Texas's economy*
**Variants:** the two American style guides split, and they split differently for names than for common nouns. **AP** uses the bare apostrophe for a singular **proper name** ending in *s* (*Chris'*, *Dickens'*, *Texas'*) but a full *'s* for a singular **common noun** (*the boss's office*, *the witness's testimony*). **Chicago**, and book publishing generally, adds *'s* to both (*Chris's*, *the boss's*). Classical and biblical names traditionally take the bare apostrophe — *Jesus'*, *Moses'*, *Achilles'* — a convention AP keeps and Chicago has since folded back into its general *'s* rule. The fixed phrases *for goodness' sake* and *for conscience' sake* keep the bare apostrophe everywhere.
**Contrast:** This is a **spelling** choice, not a grammar one — *Chris's* and *Chris'* are the same word and most Americans pronounce both /ˈkrɪsɪz/. Pick one style guide and stay consistent. Keep it separate from the plural *the Chrises* and from the plural possessive *the Joneses'* (family of Joneses → *the Joneses' house*).

The singular genitive of a possessor that already ends in a hissing sound, where the extra syllable is heard but the extra *s* is optional in print.

1. *Chris's* desk is the one by the window. (singular countable)
2. *The boss's* office is at the end of the hall.
3. *Chris's* parents are flying in on Thursday. (plural countable)
4. *Texas's* economy grew faster than the national average. (uncountable)
5. *The witness's* testimony contradicted the report.
6. She quoted *Dickens's* description of the fog.
7. Isn't that *James's* car out front? (question)
8. *The hostess's* name was not on the list. (negative)

**See also:** ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [s' (plural genitive)](#s-plural-genitive), [John's](#johns), [each other's](#each-others)

### each other's

**Pronunciation:** /ˌitʃ ˈʌðɚz/ &middot; *eech UTH-erz*
**Type:** genitive (reciprocal) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*each other's car*), plural countable (*each other's kids*), uncountable (*each other's mail*). The **possessors** are two or more people or things in a crosswise relation, so the subject of the clause is always plural; the possessive phrase itself stays singular in shape. Never another central determiner: ✗ *the each other's sentences*.
**With *of*:** No *of*-form of its own; *of* before it belongs to a preceding quantifier (*several of each other's clients*). Keep the genitive apart from the plain *of*-phrase, which means something different: *photos of each other* are photos they appear in, while *each other's photos* are the photos they own.
**Pattern:** [plural subject] [plural verb] *each other's* [noun] &middot; [plural subject] [plural verb] *one another's* [noun] &middot; *in* / *on* / *at each other's* [noun] — *they slept at each other's houses*
**Variants:** *one another's* — the formal-register twin. The old schoolroom rule reserved *each other* for two and *one another* for three or more, but American usage treats them as interchangeable and no editor enforces the split.
**Contrast:** The apostrophe **never moves**, however many possessors there are: ✓ *each other's names*, ✗ *each others' names*, ✗ *each others names*. *Each other* is a fixed singular-shaped phrase, so the ending lands where it would on any singular. Against *their own*: *They graded their own papers* means each graded the paper he or she wrote; *They graded each other's papers* means the grading went crosswise. Against *both*: *both their cars* is two cars owned one apiece, while *each other's cars* is the swap. **Agreement:** the subject is plural, so the verb is plural — ✓ *The twins wear each other's clothes*, ✗ *The twins wears*. The **noun** after *each other's* can be singular or plural on its own terms: *they finished each other's sentences*, *they parked in each other's spot*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to the members of a group taken crosswise — each of them to the other.

1. They finished *each other's* sentences all evening. (plural countable)
2. The two teams studied *each other's* playbooks.
3. We borrowed *each other's* notes before the exam.
4. The roommates keep opening *each other's* mail. (uncountable)
5. The partners countersigned *one another's* forms. (formal variant)
6. The two candidates attacked *each other's* voting record. (singular countable)
7. Are those two still parking in *each other's* spots? (question)
8. The sisters never wear *each other's* clothes. (negative)

**See also:** [own](#own), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [somebody else's](#somebody-elses), [their](#their)

### John's

**Pronunciation:** /dʒɑnz/ &middot; *JAHNZ*
**Type:** genitive (proper name) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*John's car*), plural countable (*John's parents*), uncountable (*John's luggage*). The possessor is a personal name, which is the most natural possessor of all for the s-genitive.
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive is grammatical but marked with people — *the car of John* sounds foreign. Use the s-genitive with human possessors. The one everyday *of* pattern is the double genitive: *a book of John's*.
**Pattern:** [proper name]*'s* [noun] &middot; [proper name]*'s own* [noun] &middot; [predeterminer] [proper name]*'s* [noun] — *all John's friends* &middot; [proper name]*'s* [ordinal] [cardinal] [noun] — *John's first two novels*
**Contrast:** A genitive proper name **is** the determiner, so it blocks every other one: ✓ *John's book*, ✗ *the John's book*, ✗ *a John's book*. To keep the noun indefinite, use the double genitive — *a book of John's*. Plural family names take the plural genitive (*the Johnsons'*), and a name ending in *s* becomes *Chris's* or *Chris'*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to a named person; also the standard way to attach an author, inventor, or origin to a noun.

1. *John's* car is in the shop. (singular countable)
2. Have you met *John's* parents? (question, plural countable)
3. *Sarah's* proposal impressed the whole committee.
4. The airline lost *Miguel's* luggage. (uncountable)
5. All *John's* friends showed up. (predeterminer *all*; *all of John's friends* is equally correct)
6. *Lincoln's* second inaugural address is carved on the wall.
7. *Sarah's* replacement has not been named. (negative)
8. We're borrowing *Miguel's* truck this weekend.

**See also:** ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [Chris's](#chriss), [of mine](#of-mine), [the company's](#the-companys)

### somebody else's

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌmbɑdi ˈɛlsɪz/ &middot; *SUM-bah-dee EL-siz*
**Type:** genitive (group genitive) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*somebody else's problem*), plural countable (*somebody else's kids*), uncountable (*somebody else's money*). The ending lands on *else*, the last word of the phrase, never on the pronoun: ✗ *somebody's else problem*.
**With *of*:** No plain *of*-form; the indefinite version is the double genitive — *a car of somebody else's*.
**Pattern:** [indefinite pronoun] *else's* [noun] — *somebody else's*, *someone else's*, *anybody else's*, *everyone else's*, *nobody else's*, *who else's* &middot; bare indefinite genitive, no *else* — *anybody's guess*, *nobody's business*, *everyone's problem*
**Contrast:** The clearest small proof that *'s* attaches to a **phrase**, not a word: *else* is not the possessor, but it ends the phrase, so it carries the ending — the same mechanism as [the King of Spain's](#the-king-of-spains). Note also *who else's* (*Who else's number do you have?*) against the impossible ✗ *whose else*. Against [each other's](#each-others): *somebody else's* points outside the group, *each other's* points crosswise inside it. **Agreement:** the indefinite pronoun is singular, but when the genitive phrase heads the subject the verb follows the **head noun**, not the pronoun — *Somebody else's kids were in the pool* (plural *kids*), *Somebody else's kid was in the pool* (singular *kid*).

Marks the following noun as belonging to an unnamed person other than the one already mentioned.

1. That's *somebody else's* problem now. (singular countable)
2. Don't take *anybody else's* word for it. (negative imperative)
3. *Everyone else's* flights were on time. (plural countable)
4. It was *nobody else's* fault but mine.
5. She was wearing *someone else's* coat.
6. *Who else's* signature do we need on this? (question)
7. Nobody wants to clean up *somebody else's* mess. (uncountable)
8. *Anybody's* guess is as good as mine. (bare indefinite genitive, no *else*)

**See also:** [the King of Spain's](#the-king-of-spains), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [whose](#whose)

### the butcher's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈbʊtʃɚz/ &middot; *thuh BOOCH-erz*
**Type:** genitive (independent, elliptical) &middot; **Position:** fills the entire noun phrase — the head noun is deleted &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **no following noun at all.** The noun the genitive would have determined — *shop*, *house*, *office*, *church*, *place* — is understood and left out: *at the butcher's* = *at the butcher's shop*; *at Ana's* = *at Ana's place*.
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** [preposition] *the* [tradesperson]*'s* — *at the butcher's*, *to the dentist's* &middot; [preposition] [personal name]*'s* — *at Ana's*, *at my grandmother's* &middot; [business or church name]*'s* — *Macy's*, *St. Paul's*
**Contrast:** An ordinary genitive is a determiner **because a noun follows it**; the elliptical genitive is a complete noun phrase with the noun missing, so it determines nothing. American English keeps the head noun more often than British English — *at the doctor's office*, *the dry cleaner's* — and many American business names froze in this shape with no shop word ever following: *Macy's*, *Wendy's*, *Lowe's*, *Trader Joe's*.

Names a place by naming the person or business that occupies it, with the word for the place left unsaid.

1. She's at *the dentist's* until noon.
2. I picked up the shirts from *the dry cleaner's*.
3. We had dinner at *Ana's* last night.
4. They got married at *St. Paul's*.
5. Meet me outside *Macy's* on 34th Street.
6. He spent the afternoon at *his grandmother's*.
7. Aren't you going to *the butcher's* first? (question)
8. We didn't stop at *the pharmacist's* on the way home. (negative)

**See also:** ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [John's](#johns), [of mine](#of-mine)

### the children's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈtʃɪldrənz/ &middot; *thuh CHIL-drunz*
**Type:** genitive (irregular plural) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun. The possessor is a plural noun that does **not** end in *-s* — *children, men, women, people, mice, geese, feet, alumni*. The determined noun may be singular countable (*the children's table*), plural countable (*the children's coats*), or uncountable (*the children's luggage*).
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive is available and often better with long phrases: *the rights of the children who were separated at the border*.
**Pattern:** *the* [irregular plural]*'s* [noun] — *the children's books*, *the men's room*, *the women's team*, *the people's choice*
**Contrast:** Regular plurals put the apostrophe **after** the *s* and add no sound (*the students'*); irregular plurals take a full *'s* and add a /z/ or a whole syllable (*the children's*). ✗ *the childrens'* and ✗ *the childrens* are both wrong, because *children* is already plural — same for ✗ *the mens' room* and ✗ *the peoples' choice*.

The genitive of a plural that forms itself without *-s*, which therefore takes the ordinary singular-shaped ending.

1. *The children's* coats are in the hall closet. (plural countable head — verb *are*)
2. *The men's* room is around the corner. (singular countable head — verb *is*)
3. She coaches *the women's* soccer team.
4. It won *the people's* choice award three years running.
5. *The children's* luggage never made it onto the plane. (uncountable, negative)
6. *The mice's* nest was behind the water heater.
7. Where is *the children's* section in this library? (question)
8. *The alumni's* donations covered the whole renovation.

**See also:** [s' (plural genitive)](#s-plural-genitive), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [Nouns: irregular plurals](../../01-nouns/README.md)

### the company's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈkʌmpəniz/ &middot; *thuh KUM-puh-neez*
**Type:** genitive (inanimate, institutional) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*the company's headquarters*), plural countable (*the company's employees*), uncountable (*the company's revenue*). The possessor is an organization, a place, a publication, a vehicle, or another inanimate noun rather than a person.
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive is the direct competitor and is often better when the possessor is long or heavily modified: ✓ *the company's policy*, ✓ *the policy of the company that acquired us last spring*.
**Pattern:** *the* [organization]*'s* [noun] &middot; [place name]*'s* [noun] — *Chicago's winters* &middot; *the* [publication]*'s* [noun] — *the Times's editorial*
**Contrast:** The old schoolroom rule that only living things take the s-genitive is out of date. Organizations, cities, countries, publications, vehicles, and time expressions all take it freely in American English: *the city's budget*, *the report's conclusion*, *the car's warranty*. What still resists it are ordinary concrete objects and their physical parts — ✓ *the leg of the table* over awkward *the table's leg*, ✓ *the roof of the house* over *the house's roof*. See [of](#of) for the full decision.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, produced by, or characteristic of an institution or other non-human entity.

1. *The company's* stock dropped nine percent overnight. (uncountable)
2. *The city's* budget passed on a 7–4 vote. (singular countable)
3. *The report's* conclusion contradicts its own data.
4. *The company's* employees were not told until Friday. (plural countable, negative)
5. *The university's* enrollment is up for the third year.
6. *The car's* warranty expires in March.
7. Has *the Times's* editorial board endorsed anyone yet? (question)
8. *The city's* revenue has not recovered. (uncountable, negative)

**See also:** [of](#of), [its](#its), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [today's](#todays)

### the King of Spain's

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˌkɪŋ əv ˈspeɪnz/ &middot; *thuh king uv SPAYNZ*
**Type:** genitive (group genitive) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech; avoided in edited writing when long
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*the King of Spain's daughter*), plural countable (*the King of Spain's daughters*), uncountable (*the King of Spain's luggage*). The ending attaches to the **last word of the whole possessor phrase**, even when that word is not the possessor: in *the King of Spain's daughter*, the daughter belongs to the King, not to Spain.
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive is usually the cleaner choice when the possessor phrase is long: *the daughter of the King of Spain*.
**Pattern:** [determiner] [noun] [postmodifier]*'s* [noun] — *the King of Spain's daughter*, *the woman next door's cat*, *the guy I met yesterday's number*
**Contrast:** Proof that *'s* is a **phrase-level clitic**, not a noun suffix: it lands on the end of the phrase, never on the head noun. ✗ *the King's of Spain daughter* is the classic error. Long group genitives are ordinary in speech and clumsy on the page, where the *of*-genitive is preferred: *the number of the guy I met yesterday.*

Turns an entire modified noun phrase — article, head, and everything that follows it — into a single determiner.

1. *The King of Spain's* daughter studied in the United States. (singular countable)
2. *The woman next door's* cat keeps getting into our garage.
3. *The guy I met yesterday's* number is in my phone somewhere.
4. *The Secretary of State's* plane landed in Rome this morning.
5. *The head of marketing's* proposals were all rejected. (plural countable)
6. *Somebody I used to work with's* brother owns the place. (natural in speech; rewrite it in edited prose)
7. *The man in 4B's* mail keeps coming to us. (uncountable)
8. Is that *the woman next door's* car in our spot? (question)

**See also:** [somebody else's](#somebody-elses), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [of](#of)

### today's

**Pronunciation:** /təˈdeɪz/ &middot; *tuh-DAYZ*
**Type:** genitive (temporal) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*today's paper*), plural countable (*today's numbers*), uncountable (*today's mail*). The possessor is a time word: *today, yesterday, tomorrow, tonight, this morning, last year, next week*.
**With *of*:** The *of*-genitive exists but is stiffer and rarer: *the newspaper of today*.
**Pattern:** *today's* [noun] &middot; *yesterday's* / *tomorrow's* / *tonight's* [noun] &middot; *last year's* / *next week's* [noun] &middot; *this morning's* [noun]
**Contrast:** Time expressions take the s-genitive freely although they are not animate — one of the oldest exceptions to the animacy preference. Distinguish the genitive *today's meeting* (the meeting held today) from the measure genitive *a day's work* (work amounting to a day — see [two weeks'](#two-weeks)). Note too that *today*, *yesterday*, and *tomorrow* cannot modify a noun bare — ✓ *today's meeting*, ✗ *today meeting* — even though day and month names can: ✓ *the Monday meeting*, ✓ *a March deadline*.

Marks the following noun as belonging to, occurring on, or dated to a particular time.

1. Did you read *today's* paper? (question, singular countable)
2. *Yesterday's* storm knocked out power on the whole block.
3. *Tomorrow's* numbers will not be final until noon. (plural countable, negative)
4. *Last year's* model is on clearance.
5. *Tonight's* game starts at seven.
6. *This morning's* email explains the change.
7. *Today's* mail is still on the porch. (uncountable)
8. *Next week's* schedule has not gone out yet. (negative)

### two weeks'

**Pronunciation:** /tu ˈwiks/ — the apostrophe adds no sound &middot; *too WEEKS*
**Type:** genitive (measure) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a noun naming an amount, period, or entitlement. That noun is normally **uncountable** (*two weeks' notice*, *ten years' experience*, *three months' revenue*, *a dollar's worth*, *a day's work*, *two weeks' pay*) or **singular countable** (*an hour's drive*, *a moment's hesitation*, *a week's delay*). Plural countable heads are rare and get rephrased: ✓ *two weeks' worth of meals* rather than the odd *two weeks' meals*. The possessor is a quantity of time, money, or distance rather than an owner.
**With *of*:** Not used with *of* between the measure and its noun. The competing structure is a hyphenated attributive with **no apostrophe and a singular unit**: *a two-week notice*, *a ten-year contract*. *Of* does appear after the head noun in the partitive frame *two weeks' worth of* [noun].
**Pattern:** [number] [plural unit]*'* [noun] — *two weeks' notice*, *ten years' experience* &middot; *a* / *an* [singular unit]*'s* [noun] — *a day's work*, *an hour's drive*, *a dollar's worth*
**Contrast:** The apostrophe is compulsory here and is dropped constantly: ✗ *two weeks notice* (the movie title notwithstanding). The apostrophe-free alternative uses a **singular** unit with a hyphen — *a two-week vacation*, *a ten-dollar bill*. So *two weeks' pay* and *a two-week delay* are both right, while ✗ *a two-weeks delay* and ✗ *two week's pay* are both wrong. Note also what this genitive does **not** do: unlike an ordinary possessive, it leaves the phrase **indefinite**. *John's notice* is definite, but *two weeks' notice* is not — which is why *a day's work* can still carry its own *a* (the *a* belongs to *day*, not to *work*). **Agreement:** the verb follows the head noun, not the number in the measure — ✓ *Two weeks' notice **is** required*, ✗ *two weeks' notice are required*; ✓ *Ten years' experience **is** preferred*.

Expresses an amount as if it were a possessor: the notice that two weeks amounts to, the drive that an hour amounts to.

1. She gave *two weeks'* notice on Friday. (uncountable)
2. *Ten years'* experience is preferred for this role. (singular verb *is*)
3. It's *an hour's* drive from the airport. (singular countable)
4. That's *a full day's* work right there.
5. I bought *five dollars'* worth of quarters for the laundry.
6. The strike cost them *three months'* revenue.
7. Isn't *two weeks'* notice the standard here? (question)
8. He didn't give them *a moment's* warning. (negative)

## The of-genitive and the double genitive

English marks possession two ways. The s-genitive puts the possessor **before** the noun and inside the determiner slot; the of-genitive puts it **after** the noun in a prepositional phrase, leaving the head noun free to take its own determiner. The double genitive uses both at once, and exists precisely because the determiner slot cannot hold two things.

### of

**Pronunciation:** /ʌv/, weak /əv/, /ə/ &middot; *UHV, weak uhv / uh*
**Type:** genitive (of-genitive) — a **preposition**, not a determiner &middot; **Position:** postmodifier; the head noun keeps its own determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** links a head noun to a following noun phrase that owns, contains, produces, or defines it — *the roof of the house*. Each half keeps **its own** determiner, one on the head noun and another on the noun after *of*, which is exactly what the s-genitive cannot do. Any noun type can head the phrase: singular countable (*the size of the room*), plural countable (*the members of the board*), uncountable (*the smell of the paint*).
**With *of*:** *Of* is the item itself. It requires a full noun phrase or a possessive **pronoun** after it, never a bare possessive determiner: ✓ *of the house*, ✓ *of mine*, ✗ *of my*. The partitive frame runs on its own schedule: *all (of) the students* leaves *of* optional, *each of the students* and *most of the students* require it, *all of them* requires it before a pronoun, and *every* never takes it at all — ✗ *every of the students*, ✓ *every one of the students*.
**Pattern:** [determiner] [head noun] *of* [noun phrase] — *the title of the book* &middot; [determiner] [head noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] — *a friend of mine* (see [of mine](#of-mine)) &middot; [quantifier] *of* [determiner + noun] — *most of the students*
**Contrast:** The choice between *the girl's name* and *the name of the girl* runs on three factors. **Animacy** — people and animals prefer *'s* (*the girl's name*), plain objects prefer *of* (*the leg of the table*). **Weight** — long or heavily modified possessors go after *of* (*the proposal of the committee that met last Thursday*). **Focus** — whatever lands last carries the emphasis. Institutions, places, publications, vehicles, and time words break the animacy preference and take *'s* freely; see [the company's](#the-companys) and [today's](#todays).

Builds a possessive relationship after the noun instead of before it, and takes over wherever the s-genitive is awkward.

1. The roof *of* the house needs replacing. (singular countable head)
2. What's the title *of* the book you mentioned? (question)
3. The conclusion *of* the report that the auditors filed last spring was ignored.
4. The members *of* the board have not been notified. (plural countable head, negative)
5. He's a friend *of* my brother's. (double genitive)
6. The population *of* Chicago has been flat for a decade.
7. I can't stand the smell *of* the paint. (uncountable head)
8. Each *of* the applicants was interviewed twice. (*each of the* — *of* is obligatory, verb singular)

**See also:** [of mine](#of-mine), ['s (singular genitive)](#s-singular-genitive), [the company's](#the-companys), [of which](#of-which)

### of mine

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈmaɪn/ &middot; *uhv MYNE*
**Type:** genitive (double genitive) &middot; **Position:** postmodifier — it frees the central slot for a different determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a head noun already determined by *a/an*, a number, a demonstrative, or a quantifier — *a friend of mine*, *two cousins of hers*, *that car of yours*, *several clients of ours*. That head noun is normally **countable** — singular with *a/an* or a demonstrative (*a friend of mine*, *this book of theirs*), plural with a number or quantifier (*two cousins of hers*, *several clients of ours*). With an **uncountable** noun the construction is limited to demonstrative and evaluative uses: ✓ *that attitude of yours*, ✓ *this nonsense of theirs*, but ✗ *a money of mine*. The complement of *of* must be a possessive **pronoun** (*mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs*) or a genitive noun phrase (*of my father's*, *of John's*) — never a possessive determiner: ✗ *a friend of my*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is obligatory; the construction is nothing but *of* plus a possessive.
**Pattern:** [*a/an* / number / demonstrative / quantifier] [noun] *of* [mine / yours / his / hers / ours / theirs] &middot; [same] [noun] *of* [name]*'s* — *a friend of John's*
**Contrast:** This construction exists because the central determiner slot holds exactly one item. You cannot say ✗ *a my friend* or ✗ *that your car*, so English pushes the possessive to the end. Note the meaning split against a plain *of*-phrase: *a picture of my father* has him in it, while *a picture of my father's* means he owns it. With a demonstrative the construction usually carries an attitude — *that car of yours* is rarely neutral.

Combines possession with an indefinite, demonstrative, or quantified determiner, which the one-determiner rule otherwise forbids.

1. She's an old friend *of mine* from college. (*a/an* + singular countable)
2. That car *of yours* is going to break down on the highway. (demonstrative)
3. Two cousins *of his* flew in for the funeral. (number + plural countable)
4. Several clients *of ours* asked the same question. (quantifier + plural countable)
5. He borrowed a jacket *of my brother's* and never returned it. (genitive noun phrase after *of*)
6. Is this book *of theirs* worth reading? (question)
7. That attitude *of yours* is not helping. (uncountable, evaluative, negative)
8. I have not met a single neighbor *of theirs*. (negative)

**See also:** [of](#of), [my](#my), [mine](#mine), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this), [a](01-articles.md#a)

## Words that lean on a possessive

### own

**Pronunciation:** /oʊn/ &middot; *OHN*
**Type:** emphasizer — **not itself a determiner** &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, between the central determiner and the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** any noun — singular countable (*my own room*), plural countable (*her own reasons*), uncountable (*his own money*) — but **only** when a possessive determiner or a genitive phrase comes first: ✓ *my own room*, ✓ *John's own words*, ✗ *the own room*, ✗ *an own car*. It may also precede a numeral: *my own two hands*.
**With *of*:** Yes, in the fixed frame [noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] *own* — *a place of her own*, *a room of one's own*, *ideas of his own*.
**Pattern:** [possessive] *own* [noun] &middot; [possessive] *own* [number] [noun] — *my own two hands* &middot; *on* [possessive] *own* — *she did it on her own* &middot; [determiner] [noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] *own* — *a place of his own*
**Contrast:** *Own* is an intensifier, not a determiner: it cannot open a noun phrase and it never replaces the possessive. Keep it apart from the verb *own* (*she owns three rentals*) and from the frozen soccer term *an own goal*, which is the one place English tolerates *own* with no possessive in front of it. Against [each other's](#each-others): *their own rooms* gives each person the room he or she owns, *each other's rooms* swaps them.

Reinforces a possessive — "belonging to that person and to nobody else" — and blocks any reading in which the noun is shared.

1. She paid for the trip with her *own* money. (uncountable)
2. Every state sets its *own* speed limits. (plural countable)
3. I built the shelves with my *own* two hands. (before a numeral)
4. He'd like a place of his *own* someday. (singular countable, *of*-frame)
5. They finished the whole project on their *own*.
6. Those were the mayor's *own* words, not mine. (after a genitive phrase)
7. Doesn't she have an office of her *own* yet? (question)
8. He never had a room of his *own* growing up. (negative)

**See also:** [my](#my), [its](#its), [one's](#ones), [of mine](#of-mine), [each other's](#each-others)

## Apostrophe impostors — words that sound like possessives and are not

Four possessive determiners each have a homophone that is a contraction, and confusing the two is the single most common error class in written American English. None of the four contractions can precede a noun, because none of them is a determiner: each is a subject plus a verb. The test never changes — **expand the contraction**. If the expansion is grammatical, the apostrophe belongs; if it is nonsense, you want the possessive.

### it's (not a determiner)

**Pronunciation:** /ɪts/ &middot; *ITS*
**Type:** contraction of *it is* / *it has* &middot; **Position:** none — it is a subject plus a verb, not part of a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing; spelled out in the most formal prose
**Goes with:** **nothing.** It cannot precede a noun the way a determiner does. In *It's a problem*, the determiner is *a*, not *it's*.
**With *of*:** Not applicable.
**Pattern:** *It's* [adjective] — *It's cold.* &middot; *It's* [noun phrase] — *It's a shame.* &middot; *It's* [verb]*-ing* — *It's raining.* &middot; *It's been* [noun phrase] — *It's been a week.*
**Contrast:** ✗ *The dog hurt it's paw* → ✓ *its paw*. Expand it: *the dog hurt it is paw* is nonsense, so the possessive [its](#its) is required. In the other direction, ✗ *Its raining* → ✓ *It's raining*. And ✗ *its'* is not a word in any sense at all — there is no situation in English that calls for it.

The contraction of *it is* or *it has*; a determiner-shaped trap with no determiner function whatsoever.

1. *It's* going to rain all weekend.
2. *It's* been three years since we moved.
3. I think *it's* your turn.
4. *It's* not the money, it's the principle.
5. *It's* a shame nobody told us sooner.
6. If *it's* broken, its warranty should cover the repair.

**See also:** [its](#its), [who's (not a determiner)](#whos-not-a-determiner), [you're (not a determiner)](#youre-not-a-determiner)

### they're (not a determiner)

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛr/, weak /ðɚ/ &middot; *THAIR, weak ther*
**Type:** contraction of *they are* &middot; **Position:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral in speech and ordinary writing
**Goes with:** **nothing.** No noun can follow it the way one follows *their*.
**With *of*:** Not applicable.
**Pattern:** *They're* [adjective] &middot; *They're* [verb]*-ing* &middot; *They're* [noun phrase] &middot; *They're not* / *They aren't* …
**Contrast:** Three homophones, one sound. *Their* is the possessive determiner (*their house*); *there* is a place or the dummy subject (*over there*, *there is a problem*); *they're* is *they are*. Expand to test: *They're late* = *they are late*, while ✗ *Their late* fails. Watch for a fourth error alongside these: ✗ *their's* is not a word — the pronoun is *theirs*.

The contraction of *they are*; identical in sound to the possessive *their* and to the locative *there*.

1. *They're* landing in about twenty minutes.
2. *They're* the only ones who showed up.
3. I don't know whether *they're* coming.
4. *They're* not going to like this.
5. *They're* fixing their roof over there this week.
6. If *they're* late again, we'll start without them.

**See also:** [their](#their), [it's (not a determiner)](#its-not-a-determiner)

### who's (not a determiner)

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ*
**Type:** contraction of *who is* / *who has* &middot; **Position:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **nothing.** Compare *Whose keys are these?* (determiner plus noun) with *Who's driving?* (subject plus verb).
**With *of*:** Not applicable.
**Pattern:** *Who's* [verb]*-ing*? &middot; *Who's* [noun phrase]? &middot; *Who's been* [verb]*-ing*? &middot; [antecedent] *who's* … (relative *who* plus *is*)
**Contrast:** Expand to *who is* or *who has*. *Who's coming?* = *who is coming*; *Whose car is that?* has no expansion, so it takes the possessive [whose](#whose). In speech the two are identical — /huz/ both times — which is why this error survives every spell-check.

The contraction of *who is* or *who has*; the twin of the interrogative and relative determiner *whose*.

1. *Who's* driving tonight?
2. *Who's* been using my desk?
3. Do you know *who's* in charge here?
4. She's the one *who's* always early.
5. *Who's* going to tell him whose idea it was?
6. *Who's* got the tickets?

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [whosever](#whosever), [it's (not a determiner)](#its-not-a-determiner)

### you're (not a determiner)

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr/, weak /jɚ/ &middot; *YOOR, weak yer*
**Type:** contraction of *you are* &middot; **Position:** none &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **nothing.** It cannot precede a noun; *You're the third caller* has *the* as its determiner.
**With *of*:** Not applicable.
**Pattern:** *You're* [adjective] &middot; *You're* [verb]*-ing* &middot; *You're* [noun phrase] &middot; *You're not* / *You aren't* …
**Contrast:** ✗ *Your welcome* → ✓ *You're welcome*; ✗ *You're keys are on the table* → ✓ *Your keys*. Expand to *you are*. In fast speech both words reduce to /jɚ/, so the ear gives no help at all — this pair and *its/it's* are the two most frequently corrected apostrophe errors in English.

The contraction of *you are*; identical in sound to the possessive determiner *your*.

1. *You're* welcome.
2. *You're* going to love this place.
3. I think *you're* right about the schedule.
4. *You're* the third person to ask me that today.
5. If *you're* late, your seat goes to someone else.
6. *You're* not the one who has to explain it.

**See also:** [your](#your), [it's (not a determiner)](#its-not-a-determiner), [they're (not a determiner)](#theyre-not-a-determiner)

## Notes and tricky cases

**Only one central determiner.** A possessive fills the same slot as an article or a demonstrative, so it excludes them: ✗ *the my book*, ✗ *a your friend*, ✗ *this her idea*, ✗ *the John's car*. Predeterminers are fine in front (*all my cousins*, *both her sisters*, *half his paycheck*, *twice their salary*), and postdeterminers are fine behind (*my first two jobs*, *her many objections*). To combine indefiniteness with possession, use the double genitive: *a friend of mine*, *that car of yours*, *some cousins of my wife's*.

***Of* before a possessive.** A quantifier in front of a possessive follows exactly the same *of*-rule it follows in front of *the*. **Predeterminers** make *of* optional: *all (of) my cousins*, *both (of) her sisters*, *half (of) his paycheck*. **Everything else requires *of***: ✓ *each of my options*, ✗ *each my options*; ✓ *neither of your choices*, ✗ *neither your choices*; ✓ *several of our clients*, ✓ *most of its revenue*, ✓ *none of their arguments*, ✓ *two of their kids*. And *every* never takes *of* at all — ✗ *every of my friends*; the phrase has to be built with *one*: ✓ *every one of my friends*. Before a **pronoun**, *of* is compulsory even for the predeterminers: ✓ *all of them*, ✗ *all them*; ✓ *both of us*, ✗ *both us*.

**Agreement.** A verb agrees with the **head noun**, never with the possessor sitting in front of it. *The teachers' lounge **is** on the second floor* (singular *lounge*, though *teachers* is plural); *The children's coats **are** in the closet* (plural *coats*, though *children's* is one phrase); *Two weeks' notice **is** required* (singular *notice*, though *weeks* is plural). Where the possessor is itself a quantified antecedent, the ordinary quantifier concord applies: *each, every, either, neither* take a **singular** verb (*Each applicant **has** submitted their résumé*; *Neither driver **was** insured*), while *both, all, several* take a **plural** one (*Both drivers **were** insured*). Choosing singular *their* over *his or her* never changes the verb.

**The possessive with a gerund.** Careful writing uses the possessive before an *-ing* form used as a noun: *I appreciate your coming*, *They objected to his leaving early*, *We were surprised at Maria's quitting*. The objective form (*I appreciate you coming*) is ordinary in speech and increasingly accepted in print; the possessive is safer in formal writing. With a long or inanimate subject the possessive is genuinely awkward and gets dropped: ✓ *the chance of the plan failing*, stiff *the chance of the plan's failing*.

**Singular *they*.** *Somebody left their umbrella* is standard modern American English, endorsed by AP and Chicago, and is also the correct possessive for a person who uses *they* for themselves. The alternatives are *his or her* (formal and clumsy when repeated), generic *his* (dated), and, best of all, a plural rewrite: *All the guests brought their own lunches.*

**Choosing *'s* or *of*.**

| Prefer *'s* | Prefer *of* |
|---|---|
| People and animals: *the girl's name* | Concrete objects and their parts: *the leg of the table* |
| Organizations, places, publications: *the city's budget* | Long or heavily modified possessors: *the proposal of the committee that met Thursday* |
| Time and measure: *today's paper*, *two weeks' notice* | Abstract relations of quantity: *the size of the problem* |
| Short possessor, long possessed thing | Short possessed thing, long possessor |

**The apostrophe checklist.**

| Write | Not | Because |
|---|---|---|
| its paw | ✗ it's paw, ✗ its' paw | *it's* = *it is/has*; *its'* is not a word |
| their house | ✗ they're house, ✗ there house | *they're* = *they are*; *there* is a place |
| whose turn | ✗ who's turn | *who's* = *who is/has* |
| your seat | ✗ you're seat | *you're* = *you are* |
| the students' union | ✗ the student's union (for many students) | apostrophe after the plural *s* |
| the children's room | ✗ the childrens' room | *children* is already plural |
| two weeks' notice | ✗ two weeks notice | measure genitives keep the apostrophe |
| the Johnsons' house | ✗ the Johnson's house (for a family) | pluralize first, then add the apostrophe |
| each other's names | ✗ each others' names | *each other* is a fixed singular-shaped phrase |
| Veterans Affairs, a veterans hospital | ✗ Veterans' Affairs | descriptive plural, not a possessive |

**Plurals that are not genitives.** A plural noun in front of another noun is not automatically a possessive. When it merely describes the kind of thing, American style guides treat it as **descriptive rather than possessive** and drop the apostrophe: *a farmers market*, *a teachers college*, *a veterans hospital*, *a writers workshop*, *Publishers Weekly*, *Veterans Affairs*. Reserve the apostrophe for actual ownership — *the teachers' contracts*, *the veterans' benefits*. The line is not always sharp, and dictionaries still record *farmers' market* alongside *farmers market*; where a proper name has frozen one way or the other (*Lowe's*, *St. John's*, *Teachers Insurance*), look it up rather than reason it out.

**The dialectal *-n* possessives are pronouns, not determiners.** *His'n, her'n, our'n, your'n, their'n* — spelled with or without the apostrophe (*hisn*, *hern*, *ourn*, *yourn*, *theirn*) and heard in Appalachian, Ozark, and older Southern and Midland American speech, as well as in the northern English dialects they came from — were built by analogy with the old absolute forms [mine](#mine) and [thine](#thine). They behave like the **pronoun** *mine*, not like the determiner: they **stand alone** and never precede a noun. ✓ *That truck is his'n*, ✓ *Ourn is the blue house*, ✓ *Is this yourn?* — but ✗ *his'n truck*, ✗ *yourn keys*, ✗ *their'n place*. Apply the slot test and it settles at once: nothing can follow them, so they never fill the central determiner slot. The determiner in those same dialects is the ordinary *his*, *her*, *our*, *your*, *their*. Because they are possessive **pronouns**, they get no entry in this file; see [Pronouns](../../02-pronouns/README.md). Like the forms in [y'all's](#yalls), they are unremarkable in local speech and strongly marked in writing — recognize them, don't write them.

**Possessives agree with the possessor.** English possessive determiners take their form from who owns the thing, never from the thing owned — unlike French, Spanish, Italian, and many other languages. *David called his mother* (not ✗ *her mother*); *Maria lost her wallet, her keys, and her phone*; *the committee published its findings*, though *findings* is plural.
