# Quantifiers of small quantity and partial amount

> The determiners that answer *how much?* and *how many?* with a small number — and the ones where a single letter, the article *a*, flips a complaint into a reassurance.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Two things unite this group, and both are traps. The first is **countability**: English keeps two parallel sets here, one for things you count (*few, fewer, fewest, a couple of, several, one or two*) and one for things you measure (*little, less, least, a bit of, a touch of, a modicum of*). Crossing them produces the two most recognizable learner errors in the language — ✗ *few water*, ✗ *less people* — so the **Goes with** line is the line to read first in every entry below. The second trap is the **indefinite article**, which is not decoration here but a semantic switch: *few* and *little* are negative and mean "almost none, and that is a problem," while *a few* and *a little* are positive and mean "some, and that is enough." Drop the *a* and you have changed the message from reassurance to complaint.

One warning about scope. *Any*, *no*, *all*, *both*, *each*, and *every* are determiners of coverage rather than of amount and live with the [distributives and negatives](06-distributives-negatives.md); *half*, *a third*, and *a fraction of* state a proportion and live with the [numerals and fractions](07-numerals-multipliers.md); the large-quantity partners *much*, *many*, *a lot of*, and *plenty of* are one file back in [quantifiers of large quantity](04-quantifiers-large.md). *Some* is catalogued here, because its job is to name a partial, unspecified amount. And beware the two phrases in this file's own vocabulary that mean the opposite of what they look like: *quite a few* and *quite a bit of* are **large**.

A word on **weak forms**, because every entry below is affected by it. Determiners are almost never stressed, and the small ones reduce hard in ordinary American speech. The *a* of *a few*, *a little*, and *a bit of* is /ə/, never /eɪ/. *The* in *the odd* and *the occasional* is /ði/ before a vowel sound and /ðə/ everywhere else. *Some* is /səm/ as a quantifier of amount and only straightens out to /sʌm/ in the "a certain" and admiring senses. And the *of* of every partitive phrase in this file reduces to /ə/: *a bit of sugar* comes out *uh BIT uh SHUG-er*, *a couple of days* comes out *uh KUP-luh days*. The strong forms are not wrong, but a speaker who uses them everywhere sounds like someone reading aloud.

## The core four: *few*, *a few*, *little*, *a little*

These four carry most of the work of the category, and between them they cover the whole countability grid: *few* and *a few* take plural countable nouns, *little* and *a little* take uncountable ones. Within each pair, the article decides the attitude. Note also that in ordinary American speech all four sound somewhat formal on their own — a native speaker is far more likely to say *not many*, *not much*, or *hardly any*, and to save bare *few* and bare *little* for writing or for deliberate emphasis.

### a few

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈfju/ &middot; *uh FYOO*
**Русский:** несколько — небольшое, но достаточное число исчисляемых; в отличие от few звучит позитивно
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a few chairs*, *a few minutes*, *a few people*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *a few water*, ✗ *a few advice*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *a few chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *a few of the students*, *a few of my friends*, *a few of them*. Never ✗ *a few the students*. Before a bare plural noun there is no *of*: *a few students*.
**Pattern:** *a few* [plural countable] &middot; *a few of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *a few of* [them/us/you] &middot; *only a few* [plural countable] &middot; *a few more* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** *a few* is positive, *few* is negative. *A few people came* means some came and that was fine; *Few people came* means almost nobody came and that was a letdown. The countability partner is *a little*, which does the same positive job for uncountable nouns. And watch *quite a few*, which contains *few* but means **many** — see [quite a few](04-quantifiers-large.md#quite-a-few).
**Variants:** *only a few*, *just a few*, *a few more*, *the few*, *quite a few* (opposite meaning)

Marks a small but sufficient number — "some, and enough for the purpose." The speaker is reporting, not complaining. As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun: *A few had already left.*

1. I have *a few* questions before we sign anything.
2. We stayed *a few* days longer than we planned.
3. *A few* of the seats were still empty when the lights went down.
4. She knows *a few* of them from college.
5. Give it *a few* minutes and try the link again.
6. Only *a few* of the applicants made it past the first round.

**See also:** [few](#few), [a little](#a-little), [a couple of](#a-couple-of), [several](#several), [one or two](#one-or-two), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### a little

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈlɪɾl̩/, carefully /ə ˈlɪtl̩/ &middot; *uh LIT-l*
**Русский:** немного — небольшой, но достаточный объём неисчисляемого; позитивно, в отличие от little
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *a little sugar*, *a little patience*, *a little time*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *a little chairs*). It looks as if it takes singular countables in *a little dog*, but that is a different word: there *little* is the **adjective** meaning "small" and *a* is the ordinary article, which is why *a little dog* pluralizes to *two little dogs* while the quantifier cannot (✗ *two a little sugars*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *a little of the milk*, *a little of my time*, *a little of it*. No *of* before a bare noun: *a little milk*.
**Pattern:** *a little* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a little of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *a little of* [it/that] &middot; *just a little* [uncountable] &middot; *a little more* [uncountable]
**Contrast:** *a little* is positive, *little* is negative. *We have a little time* means we are all right; *We have little time* means we are in trouble. The countability partner is *a few*. Do not confuse the determiner with the identical **adverb** *a little*, which modifies adjectives and verbs rather than nouns (*a little tired*, *rest a little*), or with *quite a little*, which is rare in American English — the American intensifier is *quite a bit of*.
**Variants:** *just a little*, *only a little*, *a little more*, *a little bit of*, *the little* (definite)

Marks a small but sufficient amount of something uncountable — "some, and enough." As a pronoun it stands alone: *I know a little about it.*

1. There's still *a little* milk left, enough for coffee.
2. Add *a little* salt and taste it again.
3. She speaks *a little* Portuguese.
4. It took *a little* courage to say that out loud.
5. I've read *a little* of the report, but not the appendices.
6. Give me *a little* more time and I'll have the numbers.

**See also:** [little](#little), [a few](#a-few), [a bit of](#a-bit-of), [a little bit of](#a-little-bit-of), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much)

### few

**Pronunciation:** /fju/ &middot; *FYOO*
**Русский:** мало, немногие — только с исчисляемыми; отрицательный оттенок «меньше, чем хотелось бы»
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*few people*); postdeterminer after a central determiner (*the few people*, *her few friends*, *these few days*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — in speech Americans usually say *not many* or *hardly any*
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *few people*, *few options*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *few time*, ✗ *few money* — use *little* there) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *few option*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *few of the applicants*, *few of my colleagues*, *few of them*. Never ✗ *few the applicants*. No *of* before a bare plural: *few applicants*.
**Pattern:** *few* [plural countable] &middot; *few of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; [the/her/these] *few* [plural] &middot; *very/so/too few* [plural] &middot; *few, if any,* [plural]
**Contrast:** *few* vs *a few* is the central contrast of this file — *few* is negative ("not many, and that's disappointing"), *a few* is positive ("some, and that's enough"). The countability partner is *little*, which is negative in the same way for uncountables: *few options* but *little time*. As a determiner it precedes a noun; as a pronoun it stands alone (*Few knew what was coming*).
**Variants:** *very few*, *so few*, *too few*, *precious few*, *few if any*, *the few*, *one of the few*

**1.** Bare *few* is negative and near-empty: it says the number fell short of what was wanted or expected, and it behaves like a negative in the sentence, taking positive question tags (*Few people noticed, did they?*) and licensing *any* (*Few of them had any experience*). **2.** After *the*, a possessive, or a demonstrative, the negativity drops out and *few* simply names a small number that does exist — *the few options we have left* is neutral, even faintly appreciative.

1. *Few* people showed up, and the ones who did left early.
2. *Few* of the proposals survived the first round of cuts.
3. There are *few* things I enjoy less than a conference call at seven a.m.
4. She is one of the *few* engineers who can read the old firmware.
5. The *few* copies still in print sell for hundreds of dollars.
6. *Few*, if any, of these results will replicate.

**See also:** [a few](#a-few), [little](#little), [fewer](#fewer), [not many](#not-many), [hardly any](#hardly-any), [precious few / precious little](#precious-few--precious-little), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### little

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪɾl̩/, carefully /ˈlɪtl̩/ &middot; *LIT-l*
**Русский:** мало — только с неисчисляемыми; отрицательный оттенок «недостаточно»
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*little time*); postdeterminer after a central determiner (*the little time we had*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; in speech Americans usually say *not much* or *hardly any*
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *little time*, *little evidence*, *little hope*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *little options* — use *few*) and never a singular countable noun in the quantity sense. The adjective *little* meaning "small" is a different word and does take countable nouns (*a little house*, *those little birds*); the giveaway is that the adjective can be graded and compared (*littler*, *the littlest*) while the quantifier cannot.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *little of the report*, *little of my time*, *little of it*. No *of* before a bare noun: *little time*.
**Pattern:** *little* [uncountable noun] &middot; *little of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; [the/his] *little* [uncountable] &middot; *very/so/too little* [uncountable] &middot; *little, if any,* [uncountable]
**Contrast:** *little* vs *a little* — the article flips the meaning from negative to positive. *There is little hope* is grim; *There is a little hope* is not. The countability partner is *few*: *little patience* but *few options*. Also distinguish the adjective (*a little dog* = a small dog) and the adverb (*little did she know*, *I slept little*). As a pronoun *little* stands alone: *Little was said about the cost.*
**Variants:** *very little*, *so little*, *too little*, *precious little*, *little if any*, *little to no*, *the little*

**1.** Bare *little* is negative: it reports that the amount fell short, and like *few* it behaves as a negative in the clause, licensing *any* and taking positive tags (*There's little chance, is there?*). **2.** After *the* or a possessive it loses the negativity and names the small amount that does exist — *the little money he had left* is a statement of fact, not a complaint. **3.** In the fronted literary frame *little did I know*, *little* is an adverb and triggers subject–auxiliary inversion.

1. We have *little* time before the store closes.
2. There is *little* evidence that the policy changed anything.
3. He showed *little* interest in the offer.
4. *Little* of the original building survived the fire.
5. She spent the *little* money she had on a bus ticket.
6. *Little* did we know that the flight had already been canceled.

**See also:** [a little](#a-little), [few](#few), [less](#less), [not much](#not-much), [hardly any](#hardly-any), [precious few / precious little](#precious-few--precious-little), [scant](#scant), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much)

## The comparison series: *fewer*, *fewest*, *least*, *less*

The small quantifiers have **suppletive** comparatives: *few → fewer → fewest* for things you count, *little → less → least* for things you measure. The famous prescriptive rule — *fewer* with plural countables, *less* with uncountables — is worth learning, because American editors, style guides, and teachers enforce it. But an honest note is owed: it was not a rule of the language. Robert Baker floated it as a personal preference in 1770, and later handbooks hardened his preference into law; *less* with plural nouns has been in continuous use since Old English. Three things follow. First, *less* is fully standard even in careful writing with plural **measures** treated as a single amount — *less than three miles*, *less than $50*, *less than five years*, *less than ten pounds*, *less than 40 percent*. Second, *less* with ordinary plural nouns (*less people*, *less options*, *10 items or less*) is extremely common in American speech and on American signage and is still marked as an error in edited prose. Third, the safe advice for a learner is asymmetric: **write *fewer* with plural nouns, expect to hear *less*, and never use *fewer* with an uncountable noun** — ✗ *fewer money* is wrong for everyone, with no usage debate behind it.

### fewer

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfjuɚ/ &middot; *FYOO-er*
**Русский:** меньше (по числу) — сравнительная степень few, только с исчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier, comparative &middot; **Position:** central — *fewer* fills the central determiner slot itself and does not stack under another one: ✗ *the fewer errors*, ✗ *my fewer options*, ✗ *these fewer meetings*. The *the* of *the fewer options we have, the better* looks like a counterexample and is not: that *the* is the adverbial correlative *the*, not a determiner, which is why the frame needs its second clause to be grammatical at all. &middot; **Register:** neutral; the form careful writing requires
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *fewer errors*, *fewer cars*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *fewer money*, ✗ *fewer information*, ✗ *fewer traffic*) and never a singular countable noun. This is the one direction of the *fewer/less* question with no usage debate: *fewer* simply cannot take a mass noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *fewer of the errors*, *fewer of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *fewer errors*.
**Pattern:** *fewer* [plural countable] &middot; *fewer* [plural countable] *than* [phrase/clause] &middot; *fewer of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *no fewer than* [number] &middot; *far/many/considerably fewer* [plural]
**Contrast:** *fewer* counts, *less* measures: *fewer calories* but *less fat*, *fewer employees* but *less staff*. The superlative partner is *fewest*, and unlike *fewer* it never takes *than*. One idiom runs the other way and is fixed: *one less thing to worry about* is what Americans say, not *one fewer thing*. And do not confuse *fewer* with *less* in the sense "minus" — *ten less two* is arithmetic, not quantity.
**Variants:** *no fewer than*, *far fewer*, *fewer and fewer*, *one fewer*, *the fewer … the fewer*

Names a smaller number than some other number, stated or implied. As a pronoun it stands alone: *Fewer are applying this year.*

1. There were *fewer* students in the program than the year before.
2. We need *fewer* meetings and more time to actually build things.
3. *Fewer* of the returning players made the roster this season.
4. No *fewer* than twelve agencies had signed off on the plan.
5. Far *fewer* people commute downtown now than in 2019.
6. Each revision left us with *fewer* and *fewer* open questions.

**See also:** [less](#less), [fewest](#fewest), [few](#few), [not many](#not-many), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more)

### fewest

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfjuɪst/ &middot; *FYOO-ist*
**Русский:** наименьшее число — превосходная степень few, только с исчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier, superlative &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it normally follows *the* or a possessive (*the fewest complaints*, *our fewest injuries in years*); bare *fewest* occurs mainly in comparative frames (*whoever has fewest points*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly formal; *the least number of* and *the smallest number of* are commoner in speech
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *the fewest errors*, *the fewest votes*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *the fewest money*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is possible but heavy — *the fewest of the complaints* is grammatical and rarely said; the natural form attaches *fewest* directly to a bare plural. *Of* is required in the partitive pronoun use: *the fewest of them*.
**Pattern:** *the fewest* [plural countable] &middot; [possessive] *fewest* [plural countable] &middot; *the fewest of* [the/them] &middot; *the fewest possible* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** *fewest* counts, *least* measures: *the fewest calories* but *the least fat*. And *fewest* is a superlative, so it never takes *than* — that is *fewer*'s job: ✗ *fewest errors than last year* → ✓ *fewer errors than last year*. Careful writers who accept *less people* still tend to keep *fewest* for counted things, because *the least people* is odd in a way *less people* is not.
**Variants:** *the fewest possible*, *the fewest number of* (widely criticized as redundant — prefer *the smallest number of*)

Names the smallest number in a compared set — the superlative of *few* and *fewer*. As a pronoun it stands alone: *Ours had the fewest.*

1. This route has the *fewest* traffic lights.
2. The team with the *fewest* penalties usually wins the series.
3. She made the *fewest* mistakes of anyone in the group.
4. Pick the option with the *fewest* moving parts.
5. Of the three drafts, the last one raised the *fewest* objections.
6. We want the *fewest* possible interruptions during the recording.

**See also:** [fewer](#fewer), [least](#least), [few](#few), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

### least

**Pronunciation:** /list/ &middot; *LEEST*
**Русский:** наименьшее количество — превосходная степень little, с неисчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier, superlative &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it normally follows *the* (*the least trouble*); bare *least* survives in fixed phrases (*the path of least resistance*, *least said, soonest mended*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only in the quantity sense — *the least trouble*, *the least effort*, *the least resistance*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *the least errors* — use *the fewest*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *the least of the damage*, *the least of my worries*, *the least of them*. Most of these are pronoun uses with no following noun.
**Pattern:** *the least* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the least of* [my/the + noun] &middot; *the least possible* [uncountable] &middot; *not the least* [uncountable] &middot; *at least* [number/phrase] (adverbial, not a determiner)
**Contrast:** *least* measures, *fewest* counts: *the least effort* but *the fewest attempts*. Two other *least*s are not determiners and should not be confused with this one. *The least* before an adjective is a superlative degree adverb (*the least expensive option*, *the least likely outcome*), the mirror of *the most*. And *at least*, *not in the least*, *least of all*, and *to say the least* are fixed adverbial phrases in which *least* modifies nothing at all.
**Variants:** *the least possible*, *not the least*, *the very least*, *least of all*, *at least* (adverbial)

Names the smallest amount in a compared set — the superlative of *little* and *less*. As a pronoun it stands alone: *That's the least I can do.*

1. The plan that causes the *least* disruption is usually the right one.
2. Water follows the path of *least* resistance.
3. He showed not the *least* embarrassment about any of it.
4. Which of these gives us the *least* exposure to risk?
5. Losing the deposit was the *least* of our problems that week.
6. She wants the *least* possible attention from the press.

**See also:** [less](#less), [fewest](#fewest), [little](#little), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

### less

**Pronunciation:** /lɛs/ &middot; *LESS*
**Русский:** меньше (по количеству) — сравнительная степень little, с неисчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier, comparative &middot; **Position:** central — *less* fills the central determiner slot itself and does not stack under another one: ✗ *the less noise*, ✗ *my less time*. In *the less attention it gets, the better*, the *the* is the adverbial correlative *the*, not a determiner, so that frame is no exception either. &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *less water*, *less noise*, *less enthusiasm*. It also goes, in fully standard usage, with **plural measures of distance, time, money, weight, and percentage** treated as one amount: *less than three miles*, *less than five years*, *less than $50*, *less than ten pounds*. With ordinary plural countable nouns (*less people*, *less options*) it is common in American speech and signage but is corrected to *fewer* in edited prose. It never takes a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *less of the cake*, *less of my time*, *less of it*. No *of* before a bare noun: *less cake*.
**Pattern:** *less* [uncountable noun] &middot; *less* [uncountable] *than* [phrase/clause] &middot; *less of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *no less than* [amount] &middot; *far/much/considerably less* [uncountable] &middot; *the less* … *the less* …
**Contrast:** *less* measures, *fewer* counts — the rule is real in edited American English but younger than the language, and *less* has never actually left the plural (see the section note above). Do not confuse the quantifier with the adjective *lesser* ("smaller in importance": *the lesser evil*, *a lesser charge*), with the arithmetic *less* meaning "minus" (*ten less two*), or with the conjunctive *much less* / *still less* ("let alone": *He can't drive, much less fly a plane*).
**Variants:** *no less than*, *far less*, *much less*, *less and less*, *one less*, *nothing less than*, *couldn't care less*

Names a smaller amount than some other amount, stated or implied. As a pronoun it stands alone: *Less is more.*

1. We're using *less* electricity since the panels went in.
2. There was *less* traffic than I expected for a Friday.
3. I'd like *less* salt and more lemon in the dressing.
4. The trip took *less* than four hours each way.
5. She spent *less* of her own money on it than anyone realized.
6. That's one *less* thing to worry about tonight.

**See also:** [fewer](#fewer), [least](#least), [little](#little), [not much](#not-much), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more)

## Small and moderate quantities

Almost everything in this section counts: these take plural countable nouns and refuse uncountables. Several members sit outside that pattern, and it is worth knowing which way each of them breaks. *A handful of*, *a smattering of*, and *a sample of* take uncountables in their **literal** senses as well as plurals — *a handful of rice*, *a smattering of Spanish*, *a sample of the well water*. *A subset of* is the technical member and takes whatever the surrounding set happens to be made of. *Some* belongs to both grids at once, which is why it serves as the neutral baseline the rest are measured against. And *the odd* and *the occasional* are the two outliers that demand a **singular** countable noun, because what they count is occasions rather than things. Most of the rest are **partitive phrases** — an indefinite article, a measure noun, and *of* — that have hardened into quantifiers, which is why *of* is not optional in them the way it is in *all (of) the*.

### a couple of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈkʌpl̩ əv/ &middot; *uh KUP-l uhv*; in ordinary speech *of* reduces to /ə/ and the phrase comes out *uh KUP-luh*
**Русский:** пара, парочка — два, а нестрого два-три; самое обиходное малое число
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central — the phrase as a whole fills the central determiner slot &middot; **Register:** neutral; the *of*-less form is informal spoken American
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a couple of days*, *a couple of people*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *a couple of water*, ✗ *a couple of advice*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is part of the phrase and is **required** in writing — *a couple of days*, *a couple of the students*, *a couple of them*. In casual American speech *of* is routinely dropped before a bare noun (*a couple days*, *a couple times*, *a couple hundred bucks*); this is very common and still nonstandard in edited prose. It is never dropped before a pronoun: ✗ *a couple them*.
**Pattern:** *a couple of* [plural countable] &middot; *a couple of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; *a couple of* [them/us] &middot; *a couple more* [plural] &middot; *a couple* [plural] (informal, no *of*)
**Contrast:** *a couple of* is literally two; *a few* is roughly three to five. In practice Americans stretch *a couple of* to "two or three," so it is vaguer than the arithmetic suggests, and *a couple of days* rarely means exactly forty-eight hours. Against *one or two* it is slightly more definite; against *several* it is clearly smaller.
**Variants:** *a couple* (informal), *a couple more*, *a couple hundred/thousand*, *the couple of*

Names two, or loosely two or three, of something counted. It is the most everyday small number in American English and is neutral in attitude. As a pronoun it drops both *of* and the noun and stands alone: *I'll take a couple.*

1. Give me *a couple of* minutes and I'll be ready.
2. We tried *a couple of* different approaches before this one worked.
3. *A couple of* the reviewers asked for the raw data.
4. I ran into *a couple of* them at the airport.
5. It'll cost *a couple of* hundred dollars to fix.
6. Grab *a couple* chairs from the other room. (informal — *of* dropped)

### a handful of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhændfʊl əv/ &middot; *uh HAND-full uhv*
**Русский:** горстка — небольшое число, примерно пять
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns in the quantifying sense — *a handful of people*, *a handful of complaints*. It also takes uncountable nouns in the literal sense of what fits in a hand — *a handful of sand*, *a handful of rice*. Never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory — *of* is part of the phrase. Before another determiner or pronoun it stays: *a handful of the survivors*, *a handful of them*.
**Pattern:** *a handful of* [plural countable] &middot; *a handful of* [uncountable, literal] &middot; *a handful of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a handful of* [them] &middot; *only a handful of* [plural]
**Contrast:** *a handful of* is roughly five and always carries the flavor of "and that's all" — it leans negative in a way *a few* does not. *A few complaints* is neutral; *a handful of complaints* quietly says the number was small enough not to matter, or in the other direction, small enough to be conspicuous. Verb agreement is normally plural: *A handful of students were waiting.* Do not confuse the quantifier with the countable noun *a handful* meaning "someone hard to manage" (*that kid is a handful*), which takes no *of*-phrase.
**Variants:** *only a handful of*, *a mere handful of*, *a handful* (pronoun), *a fistful of*

Names a small number, around five, of counted things — or literally as much as a hand will hold, of an uncountable substance. As a pronoun it drops both *of* and the noun and stands alone: *Only a handful showed up.*

1. Only *a handful of* people showed up for the vote.
2. *A handful of* states have passed similar laws.
3. She grabbed *a handful of* rice and threw it at the couple.
4. *A handful of* the original team members are still here.
5. There were *a handful of* complaints, nothing serious.
6. I recognized *a handful of* them from the conference.

**See also:** [a couple of](#a-couple-of), [a few](#a-few), [several](#several), [a smattering of](#a-smattering-of), [a fraction of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#a-fraction-of)

### a matter of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmæɾɚ əv/ &middot; *uh MAT-er uhv*
**Русский:** всего каких-нибудь — о поразительно малом сроке, расстоянии или сумме
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns, and almost only **units of time, distance, or money** — *a matter of days*, *a matter of inches*, *a matter of dollars*, *a matter of degrees*. It does not take ordinary plural nouns (✗ *a matter of students*), does not take an uncountable noun in this sense, and takes a singular countable noun only in the unrelated topic sense (*a matter of principle*).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase.
**Pattern:** *a matter of* [plural unit noun] &middot; *(only/just) a matter of* [plural unit noun] &middot; *within a matter of* [plural unit noun] &middot; *in a matter of* [plural unit noun]
**Contrast:** *a matter of* means "no more than, and strikingly little" — it is *only* built into a noun phrase, which is why it usually appears after *within* or *in*, or with *just*. The identical-looking *a matter of* + abstract singular noun is a completely different construction meaning "a question of" (*a matter of opinion*, *a matter of taste*, *a matter of national security*), and *as a matter of fact* is a fixed adverbial. Against *a couple of* and *a few*, this one is the emphatic form: *a matter of days* dramatizes the shortness in a way *a few days* does not.
**Variants:** *within a matter of*, *in a matter of*, *just a matter of*, *a matter of time* (idiom: inevitability)

Names a strikingly small span of time, distance, or money — "only this much, and no more."

1. The building went up in *a matter of* months.
2. She missed the record by *a matter of* inches.
3. Within *a matter of* hours the story was everywhere.
4. It's *a matter of* dollars, not thousands — just approve it.
5. The whole outage lasted *a matter of* minutes.
6. They were separated by *a matter of* feet the entire time and never saw each other.

**See also:** [a couple of](#a-couple-of), [one or two](#one-or-two), [a few](#a-few), [a fraction of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#a-fraction-of)

### a number of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈnʌmbɚ əv/ &middot; *uh NUM-ber uhv*
**Русский:** ряд, некоторое количество — неопределённое умеренное число исчисляемых
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, faintly formal — common in reports and journalism
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a number of issues*, *a number of the delegates*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *a number of information* — use *an amount of*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase, and it stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a number of the students*, *a number of them*.
**Pattern:** *a number of* [plural countable] &middot; *a number of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *a number of* [them] &middot; *a small/large/growing number of* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** the agreement trap is the point of this entry. ***A number of* takes a plural verb; *the number of* takes a singular verb**: *A number of issues remain* but *The number of issues is growing*. The indefinite phrase is a quantifier and the plural noun controls agreement; the definite phrase is a real noun phrase headed by *number*. Note also the countability split with *amount*: *a number of* counts, *an amount of* measures — ✗ *a number of water*, ✗ *an amount of chairs*. Bare *a number of* means roughly "several"; add an adjective and it can go either way (*a large number of* is a large quantifier).
**Variants:** *a small number of*, *a growing number of*, *any number of* ("a great many"), *the number of* (different construction)

Names a vague, moderate quantity of counted things — more than a couple, not enough to specify. It buys the writer precision-free plurality.

1. *A number of* issues remain unresolved.
2. *A number of* the residents have already filed complaints.
3. We heard from *a number of* them after the article ran.
4. *A number of* factors contributed to the delay.
5. *A growing number of* companies have dropped the requirement.
6. There are *any number of* reasons this could have failed.

### a sample of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsæmpl̩ əv/ &middot; *uh SAM-pl uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/ in ordinary speech, giving *uh SAM-pluh*
**Русский:** выборка, образец — малая часть, взятая как представитель целого
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning technical — the ordinary word of survey research, market research, quality control, and the laboratory
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*a sample of voters*, *a sample of 400 households*) and uncountable nouns in the literal, physical sense (*a sample of blood*, *a sample of the soil*, *a sample of the fabric*). Never a singular countable noun in the quantity sense (✗ *a sample of respondent*). It wants a **specified** whole behind it: *a sample of the population* is natural, bare ✗ *a sample of population* is not.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase and stays before another determiner or a pronoun: *a sample of the students*, *a sample of them*. When the whole is already understood the phrase drops to the bare noun *a sample* (*we took a sample*), and that is a noun, not this quantifier.
**Pattern:** *a sample of* [plural countable] &middot; *a sample of* [uncountable substance] &middot; *a sample of* [the/this + noun] &middot; *a random/representative sample of* [plural countable] &middot; *a sample of* [number] [plural countable]
**Contrast:** *a sample of* names a part **taken to stand for the whole**, and that is what separates it from every other partitive in this section. *A handful of voters* is merely a few voters; *a sample of voters* is a few voters chosen to represent all of them. That is why this phrase takes *random*, *representative*, *stratified*, and *biased* and the others do not. Against [a subset of](#a-subset-of), which claims only membership, *a sample of* claims representativeness. Verb agreement follows the singular head in careful writing — *A sample of 400 voters was surveyed* — though plural agreement is common when the writer is thinking of the people rather than of the sample.
**Variants:** *a random sample of*, *a representative sample of*, *a cross-section of*, *a subset of*, *a sample* (bare noun)

Names a small part drawn from a larger whole and treated as standing for it — small in size, but chosen rather than merely found.

1. *A sample of* 1,200 registered voters was polled last weekend.
2. The lab ran *a sample of* the well water for nitrates.
3. We sent *a sample of* the fabric to the client before printing the whole run.
4. *A sample of* the returning students agreed to be interviewed again.
5. A random *sample of* them will be audited each quarter.
6. The study drew *a sample of* patients from four hospitals.

**See also:** [a subset of](#a-subset-of), [a handful of](#a-handful-of), [a number of](#a-number-of), [a fraction of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#a-fraction-of), [part of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#part-of)

### a smattering of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsmæɾərɪŋ əv/ &middot; *uh SMAT-er-ing uhv*
**Русский:** крупицы, разрозненные немногие — мало и вразброс, а не в куче
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Goes with:** both plural countable nouns (*a smattering of tourists*, *a smattering of gray hairs*, *a smattering of complaints*) and uncountable nouns — mass nouns such as *snow*, *rain*, and *applause*, and especially the names of languages and fields of knowledge (*a smattering of Spanish*, *a smattering of physics*). Never a singular countable noun. Note which side *applause* falls on: it is uncountable, so *a smattering of applause* is an uncountable use, and ✗ *a smattering of applauses* is impossible.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a smattering of* [plural countable] &middot; *a smattering of* [uncountable] &middot; *a smattering of* [language name] &middot; *only a smattering of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a smattering of* adds **scattering** to smallness — the items are few *and* spread thinly, which is why it suits applause, snow, gray hairs, and tourists. Against *a handful of*, which groups its items, *a smattering* disperses them. With a subject of knowledge it means superficial acquaintance and carries a mild put-down: *a smattering of French* means you cannot really speak it, where *a little French* is neutral.
**Variants:** *a sprinkling of*, *a scattering of*, *a smattering* (pronoun), *only a smattering of*

Names a small number or amount thinly distributed — few, and scattered rather than gathered.

1. There was *a smattering of* applause when he finished.
2. She has *a smattering of* Italian, enough to order dinner.
3. *A smattering of* gray had appeared at his temples.
4. Only *a smattering of* tourists come this far north in March.
5. The lecture assumed *a smattering of* statistics.
6. *A smattering of* early snow was still on the roofs.

### a subset of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈsʌbˌsɛt əv/ &middot; *uh SUB-set uhv* — main stress on *SUB*; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh SUB-set uh*
**Русский:** подмножество, часть — термин: группа внутри большей, названной группы
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** technical — mathematics, statistics, computing, and medicine, plus the corporate prose that borrowed the word from them. It is not a word of ordinary conversation, and *some of* usually says the same thing more plainly.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*a subset of users*, *a subset of the patients*) and the mass-like collective nouns of data work (*a subset of the data*, *a subset of the population*, *a subset of the code*). Never a singular countable noun (✗ *a subset of user*). Like *a sample of* it wants a **specified** whole behind it: *a subset of the records* rather than bare *a subset of records*.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural, and it takes a **definite** complement far more often than the other partitives in this file — *a subset of the responses*, *a subset of them*.
**Pattern:** *a subset of* [the + plural countable] &middot; *a subset of* [the + mass or collective noun] &middot; *a small/large subset of* [noun] &middot; *a subset of* [them] &middot; *some subset of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a subset of* says only that the members belong to the larger set; it makes no claim at all about how many there are. *A subset of the users* may be three people or nearly all of them, which is why the phrase so often needs *small* or *large* in front of it before it means anything, and why it sits in this file only loosely. Against [a sample of](#a-sample-of), which is drawn in order to represent the whole, a subset is simply a part of it; against *a fraction of*, which implies smallness, *a subset of* implies nothing. In mathematics *subset* is a strict technical term — every set is a subset of itself — and the office use is a loosened borrowing from it.
**Variants:** *a small subset of*, *some subset of*, *a proper subset of* (mathematics), *a segment of*, *a portion of*

Names a group whose members all belong to some larger, specified group — the technical register's word for a part of a set.

1. Only *a subset of* the users ever open the settings page.
2. We reran the model on *a subset of* the data from 2019.
3. *A subset of* patients responded to the second-line therapy.
4. The bug affects *a subset of* them, mostly on older hardware.
5. A small *subset of* the recommendations made it into the final plan.
6. Every finite set is *a subset of* itself. (mathematics)

**See also:** [a sample of](#a-sample-of), [a number of](#a-number-of), [a handful of](#a-handful-of), [some](#some), [a fraction of](07-numerals-multipliers.md#a-fraction-of)

### one or two

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ɚ ˈtu/ &middot; *wun er TOO*
**Русский:** один-два — очень малое число, намеренно неточное
**Type:** quantifier (coordinated numeral phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *one or two things*, *one or two people*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *one or two water*) and never a singular countable noun, despite the *one* — the noun is plural because the phrase as a whole is: *One or two things are missing*, not ✗ *is missing*.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *one or two of the chapters*, *one or two of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *one or two chapters*.
**Pattern:** *one or two* [plural countable] &middot; *one or two of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *one or two of* [them/us] &middot; *just one or two* [plural countable]
**Contrast:** *one or two* is not arithmetic. It is a deliberate understatement meaning "a small number, and I am not going to be precise about it," and it is regularly used of three or four. Against *a couple of* it sounds more offhand and more British-inflected but is fully current in American English. Note the reversed twin: [noun] + *or two* (*a day or two*, *a mile or two*, *a thing or two*), where *a thing or two* has drifted to mean "quite a lot" and is used sarcastically.
**Variants:** *just one or two*, *a day or two*, *a thing or two*, *two or three*, *a mile or two*

Names a very small number of counted things, with the vagueness built in on purpose. As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun and takes a plural verb: *One or two have already complained.*

1. I have *one or two* things to finish before I leave.
2. *One or two* of the chapters could probably be cut.
3. There were *one or two* complaints, but nothing serious.
4. *One or two* of them stayed to help clean up.
5. Just *one or two* more questions and we're done.
6. She's made *one or two* enemies in this building.

### several

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛvrəl/, also /ˈsɛvərəl/ &middot; *SEV-ruhl*
**Русский:** несколько — больше двух, но не много; нарочито неопределённо
**Type:** quantifier &middot; **Position:** central when it opens the noun phrase (*several people*); postdeterminer after a central determiner in formal use (*her several attempts*, *the several states*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *several emails*, *several weeks*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *several information*, ✗ *several furniture*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *several email*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *several of the students*, *several of my colleagues*, *several of them*. Never ✗ *several the students*. No *of* before a bare plural: *several students*.
**Pattern:** *several* [plural countable] &middot; *several of* [the/my/these + plural] &middot; *several of* [them/us] &middot; *several more* [plural countable] &middot; [possessive] *several* [plural] (formal)
**Contrast:** *several* sits above *a few* and below *many* — roughly three to seven, though the boundaries are soft and nobody counts. It is the most neutral member of the whole section: unlike *few* it is not disappointed, unlike *a handful of* it is not dismissive, and unlike *a couple of* it is not nearly exact. As a determiner it precedes a noun; as a pronoun it stands alone (*Several were damaged*). Watch the second, unrelated *several* of formal and legal English meaning "separate, individual" — *joint and several liability*, *the several states* — where it is an adjective, not a quantity word.
**Variants:** *several more*, *several other*, *several hundred/thousand*, *several times*

Names a number that is more than two and fewer than many, left deliberately imprecise.

1. I sent *several* emails and never heard back.
2. *Several* of the students had already dropped the course.
3. We've been over this *several* times.
4. *Several* hundred people were still without power.
5. She turned down *several* of them before accepting this one.
6. There are *several* more boxes in the garage.

### some

**Pronunciation:** /səm/, stressed /sʌm/ &middot; *suhm* (weak) / *SUM* (strong) &middot; the weak /səm/ is the ordinary determiner and is what learners actually hear — *some milk* comes out [sm̩ ˈmɪlk]; the strong /sʌm/ appears only in the "a certain" and admiring senses below
**Русский:** немного, несколько — неопределённая часть; под ударением перед ед. ч. «какой-то, некий»
**Type:** quantifier (general/indefinite) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*some chairs*) and uncountable nouns (*some water*) in the quantity sense — this is one of the few quantifiers that takes both. It also takes a **singular countable** noun, but only in the separate "a certain, unidentified" sense (*some guy called*); ✗ *some chair* is impossible as a statement of quantity.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *some of the milk*, *some of my friends*, *some of them*. Never ✗ *some the milk*, never ✗ *some them*. No *of* before a bare noun: *some milk*, not ✗ *some of milk*.
**Pattern:** *some* [plural countable / uncountable] &middot; *some of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *some of* [them/it/us] &middot; *some* [singular countable] ("a certain") &middot; *some* … *others* &middot; *some more* [noun]
**Contrast:** *some* vs *any* is the classic pair. *Some* is the default in affirmative statements (*I have some money*), *any* the default in negatives and questions (*I don't have any money*, *Do you have any money?*) — and they never combine: ✗ *I don't have some money*. The rule is a tendency, not a law: *some* returns in offers and requests, where the speaker expects a yes (*Would you like some coffee?*, *Could I have some water?*), and *any* appears in affirmatives meaning "it doesn't matter which" (*Take any seat*). Against the rest of this file, *some* is the neutral baseline — it names a partial amount without judging it, where *a few* and *a little* insist the amount is small and *few* and *little* complain that it is too small. Determiner *some* precedes a noun; pronoun *some* stands alone (*Some left early*).
**Variants:** *some more*, *some other*, *some … or other*, *some few* (formal, archaic), *somewhat*, *some* + numeral (approximative — a separate use)

**1.** The everyday indefinite quantifier: an unspecified, partial amount or number, neither large nor pointedly small. With an uncountable noun it names part of a mass (*some rice*); with a plural it names part of a set (*some of the seats*), and it implies the rest exist — *Some of the windows were painted shut* invites the question of the others. **2.** Before a **singular countable** noun, stressed /sʌm/, it means "a certain, unidentified, and I don't much care which" — *some guy*, *some excuse or other*, *some day next week*. It is often faintly dismissive. **3.** Also stressed, before a singular noun in an exclamation, it is an intensifier of admiration or sarcasm: *That was some party.* *Some help you turned out to be.* **4.** Before a numeral, *some* means "approximately" (*some twenty people*) — that use belongs with the [numerals](07-numerals-multipliers.md#some-approximative).

1. There's *some* coffee left if you want it.
2. *Some* of the windows were painted shut.
3. Would you like *some* help with those bags?
4. We spent *some* time going over the numbers before the call.
5. *Some* guy from the leasing office called about the deposit.
6. I gave *some* of it away and kept the rest.
7. *Some* people never learn.

**See also:** [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [several](#several), [a few](#a-few), [a little](#a-little), [a bit of](#a-bit-of), [some (approximative)](07-numerals-multipliers.md#some-approximative), [a lot of](04-quantifiers-large.md#a-lot-of)

### the occasional

**Pronunciation:** /ði əˈkeɪʒənl̩/ &middot; *thee uh-KAY-zhuh-nl* — *the* takes its pre-vowel form /ði/ here, because *occasional* begins with the vowel /ə/
**Русский:** редкий, изредка попадающийся — мало по частоте, а не по счёту
**Type:** quantifier (fixed frame: *the* + *occasional*) &middot; **Position:** *the* fills the central determiner slot and *occasional* behaves as a postdeterminer inside the frame &middot; **Register:** neutral — the plain American equivalent of British-flavored *the odd*, and the form to reach for in American writing and speech alike
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns in this frame — *the occasional mistake*, *the occasional email*, *the occasional late night*. Never an uncountable noun in the quantity sense (✗ *the occasional traffic*). It does combine with plural countable nouns (*occasional showers*, *occasional delays*), but that is the plain adjective of frequency doing ordinary adjective work, not this determiner frame, and there it usually appears without *the*.
**With *of*:** Not used — the frame takes no *of*-phrase. ✗ *the occasional of them* is impossible; the partitive is built on *one*: *the occasional one of them*.
**Pattern:** *the occasional* [singular countable noun] &middot; [verb] *the occasional* [singular countable noun] &middot; *apart from the occasional* [singular countable] &middot; *the occasional* [singular countable] *here and there* &middot; *occasional* [plural countable] (plain adjective, no *the*)
**Contrast:** *the occasional* and [the odd](#the-odd) mean the same thing and fill the same slot; the only difference is register, and *the occasional* is the one an American writes. Both measure **frequency** rather than total, which is what separates them from *a few*: *a few emails* counts them up, *the occasional email* spreads them out over time. Both are singular where every other small quantifier of number is plural — *the occasional mistake*, never ✗ *the occasional mistakes* in this sense — and that is where learners misfire. Keep the frame apart from the adverb *occasionally*, which modifies the verb instead (*she occasionally emails*).
**Variants:** *the odd* (British-flavored equivalent), *the occasional one*, *occasional* (bare adjective with plurals), *occasionally* (adverb), *every so often* (adverbial)

Names something that turns up now and then, at irregular intervals — small in frequency rather than in count.

1. She sends *the occasional* email, but we haven't really talked in years.
2. Apart from *the occasional* thunderstorm, it was a dry summer.
3. He still makes *the occasional* spelling mistake, but the writing is clean.
4. We get *the occasional* complaint about the noise.
5. There's *the occasional* good restaurant along that stretch of highway.
6. I take *the occasional* day off in the winter, nothing more than that.

**See also:** [the odd](#the-odd), [a few](#a-few), [one or two](#one-or-two), [a smattering of](#a-smattering-of), [a handful of](#a-handful-of)

### the odd

**Pronunciation:** /ði ˈɑd/ &middot; *thee AHD* — *the* takes its pre-vowel form /ði/ here
**Русский:** редкий, время от времени попадающийся — брит. оттенок; амер. эквивалент the occasional
**Type:** quantifier (fixed frame: *the* + *odd*) &middot; **Position:** *the* fills the central determiner slot and *odd* behaves as a postdeterminer inside the frame &middot; **Register:** neutral but distinctly British-flavored; understood everywhere in American English and used mainly in writing with a dry tone. The plain American equivalent is *the occasional*.
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns only — *the odd mistake*, *the odd typo*, *the odd rainy day*. Along with *the occasional* it is one of only two quantifiers in this file that requires a **singular** noun. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *the odd mistakes*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *the odd traffic*).
**With *of*:** Not used — this word takes no *of*-phrase. ✗ *the odd of them* is impossible; the partitive is built on *one*: *the odd one of them*.
**Pattern:** *the odd* [singular countable noun] &middot; [verb] *the odd* [singular countable noun] &middot; *the odd* [singular countable] *here and there*
**Contrast:** three different *odd*s sit next to each other and only one is this quantifier. *The odd mistake* means an occasional mistake, scattered over time. *An odd mistake* means a strange one. And *thirty-odd mistakes* means about thirty, where *-odd* is a suffix on a number. Against *a few*, this one is about **frequency** rather than total: *a few mistakes* counts them up, *the odd mistake* spreads them out. It is singular where nearly every other quantifier of small number is plural — its one companion in that is [the occasional](#the-occasional), which means the same thing in plainer American English — and that singular requirement is exactly why learners misfire on it.
**Variants:** *the occasional* (the everyday American equivalent), *the odd one*, *the odd … here and there*, *-odd* (numeral suffix, different word)

Names something that happens now and then, infrequently and irregularly — small in frequency rather than in count.

1. He still makes *the odd* spelling mistake, but the writing is clean.
2. We get *the odd* complaint about the noise.
3. She'll take *the odd* day off in the winter.
4. There's *the odd* good restaurant out that way.
5. Apart from *the odd* thunderstorm, it was a dry summer.
6. I see him at *the odd* conference, never anywhere else.

**See also:** [the occasional](#the-occasional), [a few](#a-few), [one or two](#one-or-two), [a smattering of](#a-smattering-of), [several](#several)

## Small amounts of uncountable things: the *bit* family

These are partitive phrases — [*a* + measure noun + *of*], or [*an* + measure noun + *of*] when the measure noun begins with a vowel (*an element of*, *an inkling of*) — that have hardened into quantifiers of small amount. Almost all take uncountable nouns, *of* is structural rather than optional in every one of them, and what separates them is register and what they measure: liquids, flavors, feelings, or abstractions. Several have adverbial twins that drop the noun entirely (*a bit tired*, *a touch salty*, *a shade too long*, *a tad early*, *a mite slow*), and those are not determiners — with *a tad*, *a skosh*, and *a mite* the adverb is in fact the commoner use, and the presence of *of* is what tells you a determiner is in front of you. Two of them invert when *quite* is added — *quite a bit of* means **a lot**, exactly as *quite a few* does.

### a bit of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈbɪt əv/ &middot; *uh BIT uhv*
**Русский:** немного, чуть-чуть — малое количество неисчисляемого
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *a bit of luck*, *a bit of trouble*, *a bit of advice*. It reaches singular countable nouns only through the doubled frame *a bit of a* + noun (*a bit of a problem*, *a bit of a mess*), where the second *a* is obligatory: ✗ *a bit of problem*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *a bit of chairs*).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase and cannot be dropped: *a bit of sugar*, *a bit of the cake*, *a bit of it*.
**Pattern:** *a bit of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a bit of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *a bit of a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *quite a bit of* [uncountable] (= a lot) &middot; *a bit* [adjective] (adverb, not a determiner)
**Contrast:** *a bit of* and *a little* are near-synonyms, and the structural difference is *of*: *a little sugar* attaches directly to the noun, *a bit of sugar* cannot. *A bit of* is the more colloquial of the two and is the one that can take the *of a* frame. Beware the inversion: *quite a bit of* means a large amount, the same trap as *quite a few*. And *not a bit* means "not at all," which is the negative pole, not a small quantity.
**Variants:** *a little bit of*, *quite a bit of* (= a lot), *not a bit of*, *a bit of a*, *every bit of*

Names a small amount of something uncountable, or with the *of a* frame, a mild or partial instance of something.

1. We had *a bit of* luck with the weather.
2. Can I give you *a bit of* advice?
3. There's still *a bit of* the cake left in the fridge.
4. Parking downtown is *a bit of* a nightmare.
5. It took *a bit of* persuading to get her on the plane.
6. He knows quite *a bit of* Japanese, actually. (= a lot)

**See also:** [a little](#a-little), [a little bit of](#a-little-bit-of), [a touch of](#a-touch-of), [a couple of](#a-couple-of)

### a dab of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdæb əv/ &middot; *uh DAB uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh DAB uh*
**Русский:** капелька, мазок — совсем чуть-чуть мягкого или намазывающегося
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning informal; a domestic, hands-on word at home in kitchens, bathrooms, and workshops
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns naming soft or spreadable substances — *a dab of butter*, *a dab of glue*, *a dab of paint*, *a dab of toothpaste*, *a dab of ointment*. Figurative use is rare and stays close to the literal (*a dab of color*). Never a plural countable noun (✗ *a dab of crumbs*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — ✗ *a dab butter*.
**Pattern:** *a dab of* [soft uncountable substance] &middot; *just a dab of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a dab of* [the/this + noun] &middot; *put/add a dab of* [noun] *on* [X]
**Contrast:** the small measures divide by the physics of what they measure, and *a dab of* is the one for something **soft that you apply to a surface** — butter, glue, cream, paint. [*A drop of*](#a-drop-of) is a liquid that falls, [*a pinch of*](#a-pinch-of) a dry powder held between two fingers, and [*a dollop of*](#a-dollop-of) a soft substance spooned out, which is distinctly more than a dab. Unlike *a bit of* and *a touch of*, this one barely goes figurative at all. Keep it apart from *a dab hand at* ("someone skilled at"), a British idiom that has nothing to do with quantity.
**Variants:** *just a dab of*, *a smear of*, *a dollop of* (larger), *a bit of*, *a touch of*

Names a very small quantity of something soft or spreadable — the amount you would apply with a fingertip or the tip of a knife.

1. Put *a dab of* butter on each ear of corn.
2. It only needs *a dab of* glue at the corner.
3. She used *a dab of* concealer under each eye.
4. Just *a dab of* the ointment, twice a day.
5. He touched up the scratch with *a dab of* white paint.
6. Add *a dab of* mustard and stir it in.

**See also:** [a dollop of](#a-dollop-of), [a drop of](#a-drop-of), [a pinch of](#a-pinch-of), [a bit of](#a-bit-of), [a touch of](#a-touch-of)

### a dash of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdæʃ əv/ &middot; *uh DASH uhv*
**Русский:** щепотка, капля — чуть-чуть добавленного, в кулинарии и переносно
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; at home in recipes and in descriptive writing
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — seasonings and liquids literally (*a dash of salt*, *a dash of bitters*), and by extension qualities and abstractions (*a dash of humor*, *a dash of color*, *a dash of skepticism*). Never a plural countable noun and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a dash of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *with a dash of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a dash of* [the + noun] &middot; [X] *with a dash of* [Y]
**Contrast:** the culinary smalls divide by what they measure: *a dash of* for liquids and loose seasonings, [*a pinch of*](#a-pinch-of) for dry powders you hold between two fingers, *a splash of* for a larger pour of liquid, *a sprinkling of* for something scattered over a surface. All four are freely used figuratively, and in the figurative use *a dash of* is the flattering one — it names the ingredient that makes the mixture interesting, where *a bit of* is neutral and *a trace of* is clinical.
**Variants:** *a pinch of* (dry), *a splash of* (liquid), *a sprinkling of*, *a drizzle of*, *a hint of*

Names a very small quantity added to something else — literally in cooking, figuratively of any quality mixed into a whole.

1. Add *a dash of* salt and a squeeze of lemon.
2. The recipe calls for *a dash of* bitters.
3. It's a serious book with *a dash of* humor.
4. She wanted *a dash of* color in an otherwise gray room.
5. Their pitch had ambition and *a dash of* delusion.
6. Finish it with *a dash of* olive oil.

**See also:** [a drop of](#a-drop-of), [a pinch of](#a-pinch-of), [a hint of](#a-hint-of), [a touch of](#a-touch-of), [a bit of](#a-bit-of)

### a dollop of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdɑləp əv/ &middot; *uh DAHL-up uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh DAHL-up uh*
**Русский:** ложка, шматок — мягкая округлая порция; самая щедрая из «малых» мер
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; culinary first, and mildly playful when it goes figurative
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns naming soft, semi-solid foods above all — *a dollop of sour cream*, *a dollop of whipped cream*, *a dollop of jam*, *a dollop of mashed potatoes* — and by extension abstract nouns, usually with a humorous tilt (*a dollop of nostalgia*, *a dollop of self-pity*). Never a plural countable noun and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a dollop of* [soft uncountable food] &middot; *a dollop of* [abstract noun] (figurative) &middot; *with a dollop of* [noun] &middot; *a generous dollop of* [noun] &middot; *a dollop of* [the + noun]
**Contrast:** *a dollop of* is a **spoonful** — noticeably more than [a dab of](#a-dab-of), more than a *dash* or a *pinch*, and unlike all of them it is not apologizing for being small. That is the difference worth learning: most of this section insists the quantity is tiny, while *a dollop of* names a comfortable, visible serving and readily takes *generous* or *good* in front of it. Figuratively it stays warm and slightly comic — *a dollop of sentimentality* is an affectionate complaint where *a trace of sentimentality* would be a clinical one.
**Variants:** *a generous dollop of*, *a good dollop of*, *a spoonful of*, *a scoop of*, *a dab of* (smaller)

Names a soft, rounded spoonful of something — small, but the most generous of the small measures.

1. Serve the pie warm with *a dollop of* whipped cream.
2. Add *a dollop of* sour cream to each bowl of chili.
3. She put *a dollop of* jam in the center of each cookie.
4. The speech had ambition and *a dollop of* self-congratulation.
5. A generous *dollop of* mashed potatoes went on the plate first.
6. Stir in *a dollop of* the yogurt just before serving.

**See also:** [a dab of](#a-dab-of), [a pinch of](#a-pinch-of), [a dash of](#a-dash-of), [a bit of](#a-bit-of)

### a drop of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdrɑp əv/ &middot; *uh DRAHP uhv*
**Русский:** капля — мельчайшее количество жидкости; при отрицании «ни капли»
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns, chiefly liquids (*a drop of water*, *a drop of blood*, *a drop of rain*) and, under negation, abstract qualities (*not a drop of sympathy*). Never a plural countable noun and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a drop of* [uncountable liquid] &middot; *not a drop of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *every drop of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a drop of the* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a drop of* is the smallest of the liquid measures — smaller than *a dash of*, far smaller than *a splash of*. Its figurative life is almost entirely **negative**: *not a drop of pity*, *not a drop of rain all summer*. Positively it stays literal, which is what separates it from *a hint of* and *a touch of*, both of which are perfectly at home with abstractions in positive sentences. Note the idiom *a drop in the bucket*, meaning a quantity too small to matter.
**Variants:** *not a drop of*, *every drop of*, *a drop in the bucket*, *a splash of*, *a dash of*

Names the smallest visible quantity of a liquid, and by extension, under negation, the complete absence of a quality.

1. Add *a drop of* vanilla to the batter.
2. There hasn't been *a drop of* rain in six weeks.
3. He showed not *a drop of* remorse in the interview.
4. She used every *drop of* fuel getting back to the airfield.
5. *A drop of* blood had dried on the sleeve.
6. What they're offering is *a drop of* what it would actually cost.

### a glimmer of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈglɪmɚ əv/ &middot; *uh GLIM-er uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh GLIM-er uh*
**Русский:** проблеск — слабый, но различимый след (надежды, интереса)
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary; common in journalism and in ordinary speech about difficult situations
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns, and a short list of them at that — *hope* above all, then *light*, *recognition*, *interest*, *understanding*, *doubt*, *humor*. *A glimmer of hope* accounts for most uses of the phrase in American English. It reaches a singular countable noun only where that noun names an expression or a quality (*a glimmer of a smile*), and the second article is then required. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *a glimmer of options*) and never a concrete substance (✗ *a glimmer of flour*).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural. The literal noun behind the quantifier is an ordinary countable one (*a glimmer on the water*, *the first glimmers of dawn*), and that noun takes no *of*-phrase.
**Pattern:** *a glimmer of* [abstract noun] &middot; *a glimmer of hope* (the dominant collocation) &middot; *not a glimmer of* [abstract noun] &middot; *the first glimmer of* [abstract noun] &middot; *a glimmer of a* [singular quality noun]
**Contrast:** *a glimmer of* names an amount that is **faint but visible, and pointed forward** — the metaphor is a light that has not gone out yet. That forward tilt separates it from [a hint of](#a-hint-of), which merely registers what is present, and from [a trace of](#a-trace-of), which looks backward at a residue. Unlike [a shred of](#a-shred-of), whose life is almost entirely negative, it works comfortably in positive sentences (*there is a glimmer of hope*) as well as negative ones (*not a glimmer of interest*).
**Variants:** *a glimmer of hope*, *not a glimmer of*, *the first glimmer of*, *a flicker of*, *a gleam of*, *a spark of*, *a ray of* (hope)

Names a small, faint, but unmistakable amount of an abstract quality — enough to be seen, and usually enough to hope on.

1. There is *a glimmer of* hope in this morning's numbers.
2. She showed not *a glimmer of* recognition when I said his name.
3. For the first time in months there was *a glimmer of* progress.
4. He answered with *a glimmer of* the old humor.
5. The proposal offers *a glimmer of* relief for renters.
6. I caught *a glimmer of* a smile before she turned away.

**See also:** [a hint of](#a-hint-of), [an inkling of](#an-inkling-of), [a trace of](#a-trace-of), [a shred of](#a-shred-of), [a touch of](#a-touch-of)

### a hint of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhɪnt əv/ &middot; *uh HINT uhv*
**Русский:** намёк, лёгкий оттенок — едва уловимое количество
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns, especially perceptions and qualities registered at the edge of noticing — flavors, smells, sounds, colors, accents, and emotions: *a hint of lemon*, *a hint of an accent*, *a hint of sarcasm*. Never a plural countable noun. It reaches a singular countable noun only where that noun names a quality or feature (*a hint of a smile*, *a hint of an accent*), and there the second article is required.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a hint of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a hint of a/an* [singular quality noun] &middot; *without a hint of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the merest hint of* [noun] &middot; *not a hint of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a hint of* names an amount so small that it is **detected rather than measured** — the word is about perception, which is why it collocates with senses and moods and sounds wrong with bulk substances (*a hint of gravel* is odd). Against *a touch of*, which adds something deliberately, *a hint of* merely registers it. Against *a trace of*, which is clinical and often forensic, *a hint of* is impressionistic. Do not confuse it with the countable noun *a hint* meaning a clue (*give me a hint*), which takes no *of*-phrase in that sense.
**Variants:** *the merest hint of*, *not a hint of*, *without a hint of*, *a suggestion of*, *a whisper of*

Names a barely perceptible amount — enough to notice, not enough to name.

1. There's *a hint of* lemon in the sauce.
2. She answered without *a hint of* hesitation.
3. He still has *a hint of* an accent from somewhere north of Boston.
4. There was *a hint of* sarcasm in the reply that everyone caught.
5. The sky had *a hint of* pink at the edges.
6. Not *a hint of* scandal has ever attached to her name.

**See also:** [a touch of](#a-touch-of), [a trace of](#a-trace-of), [a shade of](#a-shade-of), [a dash of](#a-dash-of)

### a little bit of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˌlɪɾl̩ ˈbɪt əv/ &middot; *uh LIT-l BIT uhv*
**Русский:** чуть-чуть, самая малость — разг., с подчёркнутой малостью
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; conversational
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — *a little bit of sugar*, *a little bit of help*, *a little bit of trouble*. Like *a bit of*, it reaches singular countable nouns only through *a little bit of a* + noun (*a little bit of a problem*). Never a plural countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* cannot be dropped.
**Pattern:** *a little bit of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a little bit of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *a little bit of a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *just a little bit of* [uncountable noun]
**Contrast:** this is the softened, spoken expansion of both *a little* and *a bit of*, and the extra words do rhetorical rather than grammatical work — they make the request smaller and the speaker gentler. In writing it is usually trimmed to *a little* or *a bit of*. Note that *a little bit* without *of* is an adverb, not a determiner: *a little bit tired*, *move over a little bit*.
**Variants:** *just a little bit of*, *a little bit* (adverb), *a wee bit of* (jocular), *a tiny bit of*

Names a small amount of something uncountable, with the smallness underlined — the most conversational member of the family.

1. I need *a little bit of* help with the last section.
2. Add *a little bit of* water if the dough feels dry.
3. There's *a little bit of* the old logo still showing through.
4. It'll take *a little bit of* time to get used to.
5. We ran into *a little bit of* a problem at customs.
6. Just *a little bit of* patience and it'll finish installing.

**See also:** [a bit of](#a-bit-of), [a little](#a-little), [a touch of](#a-touch-of)

### a mite of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmaɪt əv/ &middot; *uh MYTE uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh MYTE uh*
**Русский:** капелька, самая малость — устар. и диал. (южные штаты); наречное a mite живо
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **old-fashioned and rural-flavored.** Before a noun (*a mite of trouble*) it now belongs to older Southern and Midland speech and to fiction reproducing it. What survives nationally is the **adverbial** *a mite* — *a mite tired*, *a mite early* — which every American recognizes as folksy but current. Read this one; do not write it.
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns, usually abstract and usually naming mild trouble or mild comfort — *a mite of trouble*, *a mite of sense*, *a mite of comfort*, *a mite of luck*. Never a plural countable noun and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory in the determiner use — ✗ *a mite trouble*. The *of* is exactly what separates the determiner from the surviving adverb: *a mite of trouble* is a quantity, *a mite tired* is a degree.
**Pattern:** *a mite of* [abstract uncountable noun] &middot; *not a mite of* [noun] &middot; *a mite* [adjective] (adverb, not a determiner) &middot; *a mite* [comparative] (adverb) &middot; *the widow's mite* (biblical idiom, a different noun)
**Contrast:** semantically *a mite of* is simply [a bit of](#a-bit-of), and *a bit of* is what an American writes today. The word's living home is the adverb — *he's a mite slow*, *that's a mite steep* — which is dialect deployed for warmth, the same way *nary a* is. Keep the quantifier apart from the countable noun *mite* meaning a tiny arachnid (*dust mites*) and from *the widow's mite*, a small coin in the Gospel of Mark.
**Variants:** *a mite* (adverb: *a mite tired*), *not a mite of*, *a bit of* (the neutral modern equivalent), *a tad of*, *a smidgen of*

Names a very small amount of something uncountable — an older, countrified way of saying *a bit of*.

1. "We had *a mite of* trouble with the fence line last spring."
2. "If he had *a mite of* sense he'd have stayed home."
3. The letters gave her *a mite of* comfort and not much else.
4. "All we're asking for is *a mite of* patience, ma'am."
5. She allowed there had been *a mite of* talk about it in town.
6. "It's *a mite* colder than the radio said." (the surviving adverbial use)

### a modicum of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmɑdɪkəm əv/ &middot; *uh MAH-dih-kum uhv*
**Русский:** толика, хоть сколько-нибудь — книжн.: минимум приличного количества
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal, often faintly reproachful
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns only — *a modicum of respect*, *a modicum of decency*, *a modicum of success*, *a modicum of privacy*, *a modicum of common sense*. It does not take concrete substances (✗ *a modicum of water*, ✗ *a modicum of flour*), does not take a plural countable noun, and does not take a singular countable noun. **With *of*:** obligatory and structural.

**Contrast:** *a modicum of* means "at least a minimally acceptable amount," which is why it so often appears in reproach — *if he'd shown a modicum of decency* implies he showed none. Against *a bit of* and *a little*, which are neutral and colloquial, this one is Latinate, formal, and evaluative. Against *a shred of*, which lives under negation, *a modicum of* works in positive sentences too (*we had a modicum of success*).
**Variants:** *a measure of*, *a degree of*, *some measure of*, *even a modicum of*, *a scintilla of* (legal)

Names the minimum decent or acceptable amount of an abstract quality — small, but enough to count.

1. All we're asking for is *a modicum of* respect.
2. The project enjoyed *a modicum of* success before the funding dried up.
3. If she had shown even *a modicum of* remorse, the sentence would have been lighter.
4. He wants *a modicum of* privacy, which seems reasonable.
5. It requires *a modicum of* skill and a great deal of patience.

### a pinch of

**Русский:** щепотка — кулинарная мера сыпучего, и переносно совсем чуть-чуть
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard small measure of American recipes
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — dry, granular seasonings literally (*a pinch of salt*, *a pinch of saffron*, *a pinch of cinnamon*), and by extension abstract qualities (*a pinch of luck*, *a pinch of skepticism*). Never a plural countable noun (✗ *a pinch of raisins*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *a pinch of onion* in the quantity sense).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase and cannot be dropped: *a pinch of salt*, *a pinch of the spice mix*, *a pinch of it*.
**Pattern:** *a pinch of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *just/only a pinch of* [uncountable] &middot; *a pinch of* [the/this + noun] &middot; *add a pinch of* [uncountable] &middot; *a couple of pinches of* [uncountable]

[*A dash of*](#a-dash-of) is for liquids and loose seasonings, *a splash of* for a larger pour, *a sprinkling of* for what is scattered over a surface, and *a drop of* is smaller than any of them. Figuratively *a pinch of* is much rarer than *a dash of* and keeps the flavor of the kitchen when it is used. Two things to keep separate: the noun *a pinch* meaning a tight spot (*in a pinch*, *feel the pinch*), which takes no *of*-phrase, and the British idiom *take it with a pinch of salt* — the American form of that idiom is *a grain of salt*.
**Variants:** *just a pinch of*, *a smidgen of* (informal), *a dash of* (liquid), *a sprinkling of*, *in a pinch* (idiom, different sense)

Names the smallest cooking measure of a dry ingredient — as much as two fingers will hold — and by extension a very small added amount of anything.

1. Add *a pinch of* salt to the flour before you mix it.
2. The recipe calls for *a pinch of* saffron, no more than that.
3. Finish each bowl with *a pinch of* cinnamon.
4. It needs *a pinch of* cayenne, not a spoonful.
5. Their plan calls for hard work and *a pinch of* luck.
6. I used *a pinch of* the spice mix and saved the rest for later.

**Goes with:** uncountable abstract nouns naming degrees and qualities — *a shade of doubt*, *a shade of irony*, *not a shade of difference*. **With *of*:** obligatory and structural.

Names the very slightest degree of a quality, most naturally in a negative sentence denying that any exists at all.

1. There was not *a shade of* doubt in her voice.
2. He said it without *a shade of* irony.
3. Between the two drafts there isn't *a shade of* difference.
4. *A shade of* regret crossed his face and was gone.
5. The report contained not *a shade of* criticism.
6. It came in *a shade* under an hour. (adverbial use)

Names a very small amount of something uncountable — the slangiest of the casual American smalls.

1. Give me *a skosh of* cream in the coffee, not much.

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈslɪvɚ əv/ &middot; *uh SLIV-er uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh SLIV-er uh*
**Русский:** тонкий ломтик, щепка — узкий кусочек, отделённый от большего
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; at home in food writing, journalism, and physical description
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns for things that can be cut or split thin — *a sliver of cheese*, *a sliver of glass*, *a sliver of light*, *a sliver of moon* — and abstract nouns in the figurative sense (*a sliver of hope*, *a sliver of doubt*, *a sliver of the market*). **With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *a sliver of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a sliver of* [abstract noun] (figurative) &middot; *a sliver of a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *the thinnest sliver of* [noun] &middot; *only a sliver of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a sliver of* adds **shape** to smallness: the quantity is small because it is thin and cut from something larger, which is why it suits cake, glass, light, and land, and why it sounds wrong for a powder (✗ *a sliver of flour*). Names a thin, narrow piece cut or broken from something larger — small in a way you can picture.

1. She cut herself *a sliver of* cake and called it dessert.
2. *A sliver of* light came in under the door.
3. There is still *a sliver of* hope that the bill clears committee.
4. He pulled *a sliver of* glass out of his palm.
5. That program reaches only *a sliver of* the population that qualifies.
6. *A sliver of* moon hung over the parking lot.

### a speck of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈspɛk əv/ &middot; *uh SPEK uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh SPEK uh*
**Русский:** крупица, пылинка — мельчайшая частица; почти всегда при отрицании «ни крупицы»
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; the negative frame *not a speck of* is much the commonest use in speech
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns naming fine, granular, or particulate matter — *a speck of dust*, *a speck of dirt*, *a speck of blood*, *a speck of paint* — and, under negation, abstract nouns (*not a speck of evidence*, *not a speck of sympathy*). Never a plural countable noun and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural in the quantifier use. The bare countable noun *a speck* meaning a tiny dot is a different word and takes no *of*-phrase: *a speck on the horizon*.
**Pattern:** *a speck of* [particulate uncountable noun] &middot; *not a speck of* [noun] &middot; *without a speck of* [noun] &middot; *every speck of* [noun] &middot; *there isn't a speck of* [abstract noun]
**Contrast:** *a speck of* is the **dot** among the small measures: the smallest visible piece of something dry or granular, where [a drop of](#a-drop-of) is the smallest visible piece of a liquid and [a sliver of](#a-sliver-of) the smallest cut piece of a solid. Its figurative life is almost entirely negative and emphatic (*not a speck of proof*), which puts it beside [a shred of](#a-shred-of) rather than beside [a hint of](#a-hint-of). Note that *not a speck of dust* is a compliment about housekeeping, not a complaint.
**Variants:** *not a speck of*, *every speck of*, *a fleck of*, *a grain of*, *a particle of*, *a speck* (countable noun)

Names the smallest visible piece of a fine or granular substance — and by extension, nearly always under negation, the total absence of an abstract quality.

1. There wasn't *a speck of* dust anywhere in the house.
2. *A speck of* something had gotten into my eye.
3. They found *a speck of* blood on the doorframe.
4. He hasn't shown *a speck of* remorse about any of it.
5. She wiped up every *speck of* flour before the inspector came.
6. There isn't *a speck of* evidence that the treatment works.

**With *of*:** **obligatory whenever a noun follows** — ✗ *a tad salt*. Drop the *of* and the word stops being a determiner: *a tad of sugar* is a quantity, *a tad sweet* is a degree, and only the first belongs in this file.

Names a very small amount of something uncountable — the plainest of the casual American smalls, and frequently an understatement.

**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.

Names a small amount added to or present in something — enough to change the character of the whole without dominating it.

### a trace of

**Русский:** след, следы — мельчайшее обнаружимое количество; тех. и криминалистическое
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning technical and forensic
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns — substances detected in tiny quantities (*a trace of arsenic*, *a trace of moisture*) and abstract qualities (*a trace of an accent*, *a trace of bitterness*). Never a plural countable noun. It reaches a singular countable noun only where that noun names a quality or a residue (*a trace of a smile*, *a trace of an accent*), and the second article is then required.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural. Note the article-less attributive form *trace amounts of*, *trace elements*, *trace minerals*, where *trace* has become an adjective.

**Contrast:** *a trace of* is the vocabulary of detection: it means the smallest amount an instrument or an observer can find, which is why it dominates lab reports, toxicology, and police procedurals. The negative *no trace of* is far commoner than the positive, and *vanish without a trace* is fixed. Against *a hint of*, which is impressionistic, *a trace of* claims to be measured; against *a shred of*, which is only ever abstract and only ever negated, *a trace of* takes concrete substances and works in positive sentences.
**Variants:** *no trace of*, *without a trace*, *trace amounts of*, *trace elements*, *not a trace of*, *the faintest trace of*

Names the smallest detectable amount of something — the amount an instrument, or a careful observer, can just barely find.

1. The lab found *a trace of* lead in the water.

3. The plane vanished without *a trace of* wreckage.
4. Not *a trace of* the original paint has survived.
5. She answered with *a trace of* impatience.
6. The supplement contains *trace* amounts of iodine. (attributive)

**Русский:** тонкая струйка — мало по темпу поступления, а не по объёму
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral; common in journalism
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns literally (*a trickle of water*, *a trickle of blood*) and, figuratively, plural countable nouns regarded as a slow stream (*a trickle of applicants*, *a trickle of visitors*) and abstract mass nouns (*a trickle of information*, *a trickle of funding*). ### an element of

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈɛləmənt əv/ &middot; *uhn EL-uh-munt uhv* — the article is the weak /ən/, never /æn/, and the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uhn EL-uh-munt uh*
**Русский:** доля, элемент — некоторая, намеренно не уточняемая примесь качества
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — common in journalism, analysis, legal writing, and careful speech
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns — *an element of truth*, *an element of risk*, *an element of surprise*, *an element of doubt*, *an element of luck*, *an element of danger*. Never a plural countable noun, and never a concrete substance in the quantity sense (✗ *an element of water* — that is the chemistry noun). It reaches a singular countable noun only where the noun is itself abstract (*an element of a defense*), and the second article is then required.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural. Note that the article is *an* rather than *a* because *element* begins with a vowel — the same adjustment [an inkling of](#an-inkling-of) and [an iota of](#an-iota-of) make.
**Pattern:** *an element of* [abstract uncountable noun] &middot; *there is an element of* [noun] *in* [X] &middot; *not without an element of* [noun] &middot; *a strong/large element of* [noun] &middot; *the element of surprise* (fixed, definite)
**Contrast:** *an element of* concedes rather than measures. *There is an element of truth in that* grants that some truth is present while declining to say how much, and the concession is usually the front half of a sentence whose second half begins with *but*. That argumentative job is what separates it from [a hint of](#a-hint-of), which is about perception, and from [a modicum of](#a-modicum-of), which is about sufficiency. Keep the quantifier apart from the ordinary countable noun *element* meaning a component or a chemical substance (*the elements of a contract*, *seventeen elements*), which pluralizes freely where the quantifier cannot.
**Variants:** *the element of surprise*, *a large element of*, *some element of*, *an aspect of*, *a degree of*, *a measure of*

Names an unspecified but real amount of an abstract quality present in a situation — "some of this is in play, and I am not going to say how much."

1. There is *an element of* truth in what he said, but only an element.
2. Every one of these trades carries *an element of* risk.
3. The plan depended entirely on *an element of* surprise.
4. Her account leaves *an element of* doubt that the report never addresses.
5. There was *an element of* luck in the way the schedule fell.
6. The job has *an element of* danger that the posting never mentioned.

**Русский:** смутная догадка, малейшее подозрение — едва-едва осознавать, что что-то есть
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly literary; commonest in narrative and in reports of what somebody did or did not know
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns of knowledge and awareness, and a narrow set at that — *an inkling of trouble*, *an inkling of the truth*, *an inkling of suspicion*. Very often it takes a **clause** instead of a noun: *an inkling of what he meant*, *an inkling of how bad it was*. Never a plural countable noun and never a concrete substance.
**With *of*:** obligatory before a noun or a *wh*-clause. With a *that*-clause the *of* disappears: *I had an inkling that she'd say no*, never ✗ *an inkling of that she'd say no*. The bare noun also stands alone: *I hadn't the faintest inkling.*
**Pattern:** *an inkling of* [abstract noun] &middot; *an inkling of* [*what*- or *how*-clause] &middot; *an inkling that* [clause] &middot; *not the faintest inkling of* [noun] &middot; *have/get/give someone an inkling of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *an inkling of* measures **knowledge**, not substance — it is the smallest amount of awareness a person can have of something, which is why it collocates with *have*, *get*, and *give* and leans hard on negation (*no inkling*, *not the faintest inkling*). That is what separates it from [a hint of](#a-hint-of), which is a property of the thing rather than of the knower: a remark can carry *a hint of* sarcasm, but only a person can have *an inkling of* it. Against [a glimmer of](#a-glimmer-of), which looks forward hopefully, *an inkling* is neutral and often uneasy.
**Variants:** *not the faintest inkling of*, *no inkling of*, *an inkling that*, *an inkling* (bare noun), *a suspicion of*, *a hint of*

Names the faintest amount of awareness or suspicion — just enough knowledge to register that something is there.

1. She had *an inkling of* what the meeting was really about.
2. Nobody had *an inkling of* trouble until the second quarter.
3. He gave us *an inkling of* the direction the company was heading.
4. I hadn't the faintest *inkling of* how expensive it would be.
5. There was *an inkling of* suspicion in the way she asked.
6. If they'd had *an inkling of* the cost, they would never have started.

**See also:** [a glimmer of](#a-glimmer-of), [a hint of](#a-hint-of), [an element of](#an-element-of), [a trace of](#a-trace-of), [a shred of](#a-shred-of)

## Near-zero: the almost-nothing quantifiers

These push a small quantity down toward none without quite reaching it. Most are **negative-polarity** items: they behave like negatives, which means the verb stays positive. Write *There's hardly any left*, not ✗ *There isn't hardly any left*; *We have barely any time*, not ✗ *We don't have barely any time*. Doubling the negation is **negative concord**, which is systematic and rule-governed in African American English, Southern American English, and many other varieties and is not a sign of confusion — but it is nonstandard in edited written English, and *hardly no*, *scarcely no*, and *barely no* will be corrected in school and at work. Learners should recognize these forms and write the single-negative version.

This is also where the file's register spread is widest, and the labels matter more than the meanings, because most of these phrases mean roughly the same thing. *A lack of* is plain modern English that anyone says. *A dearth of* is formal and journalistic. *An iota of* is current and emphatic. *A scintilla of* is a legal term of art with a doctrine attached, met in briefs and opinions and essentially nowhere else. *A want of* survives mainly inside fixed phrases such as *for want of a better word*. And *a jot of* and *a whit of* are archaic — Bible-and-Dickens words that Americans read and do not say. Write *a lack of*, *hardly any*, or *a shred of*; recognize the rest.

### a dearth of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdɚθ əv/ &middot; *uh DURTH uhv* — the vowel is the one in *bird*, not the one in *dear*; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh DURTH uh*
**Русский:** нехватка, дефицит — книжн.: меньше, чем требуется, с оттенком осуждения
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative in sense &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal to journalistic — a leader-column word. Its close synonym *a paucity of* is a step more formal again and belongs to academic and technical prose (*a paucity of data*, *a paucity of evidence*), where *a dearth of* is what an editorial writer reaches for.
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*a dearth of information*, *a dearth of evidence*, *a dearth of talent*, *a dearth of funding*) and plural countable nouns (*a dearth of options*, *a dearth of qualified applicants*). It is one of the few phrases in this file that takes **both** noun types with no change of meaning. Never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural — *of* is part of the phrase and stays before another determiner (*a dearth of the skills we need*), though pronoun complements are rare and awkward.
**Pattern:** *a dearth of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a dearth of* [plural countable noun] &middot; *there is a dearth of* [noun] &middot; *a relative dearth of* [noun] &middot; *a paucity of* [noun] (the academic equivalent)
**Contrast:** *a dearth of* does not merely report that an amount is small; it says the amount is **too** small. It is a complaint dressed as a measurement, and it presupposes a standard that has not been met. That evaluative charge separates it from *a shortage of*, which is practical and temporary, and from [a lack of](#a-lack-of), which is blunter, far commoner, and states plain absence rather than insufficiency. *A dearth of* also carries a faint mustiness, and a plain writer is often better served by *not enough*.
**Variants:** *a paucity of* (academic), *a shortage of*, *a scarcity of*, *a relative dearth of*, *a lack of*, *a want of* (archaic)

Names an amount or number that falls short of what the situation requires — a scarcity, stated with disapproval.

1. There is *a dearth of* affordable housing in every county in the state.
2. The investigation was hampered by *a dearth of* physical evidence.
3. *A dearth of* qualified applicants has kept three positions open since May.
4. The literature shows *a paucity of* long-term follow-up data. (the academic equivalent)
5. What the report reveals is *a dearth of* imagination, not a dearth of money.
6. After a decade of *a dearth of* new construction, permits are finally rising.

**See also:** [a lack of](#a-lack-of), [a want of](#a-want-of), [scant](#scant), [precious few / precious little](#precious-few--precious-little), [little](#little)

### a jot of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdʒɑt əv/ &middot; *uh JAHT uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh JAHT uh*
**Русский:** ни на йоту — устар., библейск.: мельчайшая доля, только при отрицании
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** The word is *iota*, the smallest letter of the Greek alphabet, worn down in transmission, and it entered English on the King James rendering of Matthew 5:18 — "one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law." An American meets it in scripture, in nineteenth-century prose, and in writing deliberately reaching for that sound. Nobody says it at the lunch counter. The everyday verb *jot down* is a different word entirely in feel.
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns — *a jot of evidence*, *a jot of sense*, *a jot of difference*, *a jot of sympathy*. Never a plural countable noun and never a concrete substance. It is used at least as often with no *of*-phrase at all: *not a jot*, *not one jot*.
**With *of*:** required when a noun follows (✗ *a jot evidence*), but the phrase very often stops at the bare noun and works adverbially — *it does not matter a jot*.
**Pattern:** *not a jot of* [abstract noun] &middot; *without a jot of* [abstract noun] &middot; *not a jot* (bare adverbial) &middot; *not one jot* &middot; *jot or tittle* (the biblical doublet)
**Contrast:** *a jot of* and [a whit of](#a-whit-of) are the two archaic minimizers of English and they are interchangeable — *jot* comes from Greek *iota*, *whit* from Old English *wiht* "creature, thing." Both do the work that [a shred of](#a-shred-of) and [an iota of](#an-iota-of) do in living American English, and either of those is what a writer should use today. Like all of them it is **negative-polarity**: it needs a negation, and a plain affirmative ✗ *We have a jot of evidence* is impossible.
**Variants:** *not a jot*, *not one jot*, *jot or tittle*, *a whit of*, *an iota of* (the modern form), *a shred of*

Names the smallest imaginable amount of an abstract quality, used only to deny that even that much is present — the King James Bible's minimizer.

1. "Till heaven and earth pass, one *jot* or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law." (Matthew 5:18)
2. He would not yield *a jot of* the ground he had taken.
3. There is not *a jot of* evidence in the whole of the correspondence.
4. "It does not signify *a jot of* difference to me which of them wins."
5. She never abated *a jot of* her conviction that the letters were forged.
6. Nothing the witness said altered the case *a jot*. (bare adverbial use)

**See also:** [a whit of](#a-whit-of), [an iota of](#an-iota-of), [a shred of](#a-shred-of), [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of)

### a lack of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈlæk əv/ &middot; *uh LAK uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh LAK uh*
**Русский:** отсутствие, нехватка — обычный, нейтральный способ сказать «недостаточно»
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative in sense &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral — much the commonest of the shortage phrases, and equally at home in speech, journalism, and formal writing
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*a lack of evidence*, *a lack of sleep*, *a lack of interest*, *a lack of funding*) and plural countable nouns (*a lack of options*, *a lack of qualified teachers*). It also takes a singular countable noun where that noun is abstract enough to be treated as a quality — *a lack of a plan*, *a lack of a clear answer* — and there the second article is required.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural. Note the article-free frame *for lack of* (*for lack of a better word*, *dismissed for lack of evidence*), which is fixed and very common, and the article-free *lack of* of headlines and captions.
**Pattern:** *a lack of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *a lack of* [plural countable noun] &middot; *for lack of* [noun] (no article) &middot; *a complete/total lack of* [noun] &middot; *due to a lack of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a lack of* states plain **absence or insufficiency** and does not editorialize, which is exactly what separates it from [a dearth of](#a-dearth-of), which complains, and from *a shortage of*, which implies a supply that ought to be replenished. It is the phrase to reach for when the others sound overdressed. Two cautions. It heads a singular noun phrase and takes a singular verb — *A lack of resources was the problem*, not ✗ *were the problem*. And it is already negative, so a second negation reverses the sense: *a lack of progress* means no progress, and ✗ *a lack of no evidence* says the opposite of what is meant.
**Variants:** *for lack of*, *a complete lack of*, *a total lack of*, *lack of* (headline style), *an absence of*, *a shortage of*

Names the absence of something, or an amount of it too small to serve — the ordinary, unmarked way of saying there is not enough.

1. The case was thrown out for *a lack of* evidence.
2. *A lack of* sleep is showing in everybody by Thursday.
3. What killed the project was *a lack of* funding, not *a lack of* interest.
4. There is *a lack of* affordable options for families in this district.
5. For *lack of* a better word, the whole afternoon was a mess.
6. His answer showed a complete *lack of* interest in the details.

**See also:** [a dearth of](#a-dearth-of), [a want of](#a-want-of), [next to no](#next-to-no), [hardly any](#hardly-any), [little](#little)

### a scintilla of

**Pronunciation:** /ə sɪnˈtɪlə əv/ &middot; *uh sin-TIL-uh uhv* — stress on the second syllable; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh sin-TIL-uh uh*
**Русский:** юр.: мельчайшая доля, «искра» — о минимуме доказательств; вне суда не употребляется
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **legal.** Latin *scintilla* means "spark." In American law the phrase is a term of art: the **scintilla rule** and the "more than a scintilla of evidence" threshold recur constantly in summary-judgment and directed-verdict opinions, where a party opposing judgment must produce more than a scintilla of evidence to reach a jury. Outside a courtroom, a brief, or a law review an American meets it only when a writer is borrowing the courtroom's voice on purpose. It is not ordinary English and should not be written as though it were.
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns, and a very short list of them — *evidence* above all, then *proof*, *support*, *doubt*, *merit*. Never a plural countable noun, never a singular countable noun, and never a concrete substance (✗ *a scintilla of water*).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *not a scintilla of* [abstract noun] &middot; *more than a scintilla of* [evidence] (the legal threshold) &middot; *without a scintilla of* [noun] &middot; *there is not a scintilla of* [noun] &middot; *no scintilla of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a scintilla of* is the courtroom's [a shred of](#a-shred-of), and in bare meaning the two are interchangeable. What differs is that *scintilla* drags a **legal test** behind it: "more than a scintilla" is a bar a party has to clear, which is why this phrase — alone among the emphatic minimizers — appears freely in the affirmative (*the record contains more than a scintilla of evidence*) even though ordinary use is negative. Against [an iota of](#an-iota-of), the everyday emphatic minimizer, *a scintilla of* is the one that will sound like a lawyer talking.
**Variants:** *not a scintilla of*, *more than a scintilla of* (the legal threshold), *no scintilla of*, *a shred of*, *an iota of*, *a particle of*

Names the smallest conceivable amount of evidence or support — a spark of it — used to deny that even that much exists, or in law to state the minimum a party must show.

1. There is not *a scintilla of* evidence connecting my client to the vehicle.
2. The plaintiff must produce more than *a scintilla of* evidence to survive summary judgment.
3. The panel found not *a scintilla of* support for that reading of the statute.
4. He made the accusation without *a scintilla of* proof.
5. The record does not contain *a scintilla of* evidence of discriminatory intent.
6. Not *a scintilla of* doubt was raised at trial about the chain of custody.

**See also:** [a shred of](#a-shred-of), [an iota of](#an-iota-of), [a jot of](#a-jot-of), [a whit of](#a-whit-of), [de minimis](#de-minimis)

### a shred of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈʃrɛd əv/ &middot; *uh SHRED uhv*
**Русский:** ни капли, ни грамма — мельчайшая доля качества, почти всегда при отрицании
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; emphatic
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns only — *evidence*, *proof*, *doubt*, *dignity*, *decency*, *remorse*, *self-respect*, *credibility*. Never a plural countable noun, never a singular countable noun, and not a concrete substance in this sense (*a shred of paper* is the literal noun, not the quantifier).
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural.
**Pattern:** *not a shred of* [abstract noun] &middot; *without a shred of* [abstract noun] &middot; *there isn't a shred of* [abstract noun] &middot; *no shred of* [abstract noun]
**Contrast:** *a shred of* is almost exclusively **negative-polarity**: it needs a negative, and a bare positive *We have a shred of evidence* sounds wrong. This is what separates it from *a modicum of*, which works in positive sentences. It has a family of interchangeable emphatic partners — *an ounce of*, *an iota of*, *a scrap of*, *a particle of*, and in legal writing *a scintilla of* — plus two that are locked to a single noun each: *a lick of work* and *a stitch of clothing*. The older *a whit of* and *a jot of* are archaic and read as deliberately literary.
**Variants:** *an ounce of*, *an iota of*, *a scrap of*, *a particle of*, *a scintilla of* (legal), *a lick of* (work), *a stitch of* (clothing), *a whit of* (archaic), *a jot of* (archaic)

Names the smallest conceivable amount of an abstract quality, used almost always to deny that even that much exists.

1. There isn't *a shred of* evidence for any of this.
2. He left without *a shred of* dignity.
3. She hasn't shown *a shred of* remorse.
4. Not *a shred of* doubt remained after the second test.
5. The story has no *shred of* credibility.
6. They didn't have *an ounce of* proof, and they filed anyway.

**See also:** [an iota of](#an-iota-of), [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of), [a modicum of](#a-modicum-of), [a trace of](#a-trace-of), [not much](#not-much), [hardly any](#hardly-any)

### a want of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈwɑnt əv/, also /ə ˈwɔnt əv/ &middot; *uh WAHNT uhv* — the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh WAHNT uh*
**Русский:** нехватка, отсутствие — устар. и юр.; современный вариант — a lack of
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative in sense &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic and formal.** In current American English it survives almost entirely inside fixed frames — *for want of a better word*, *for want of anything better to do*, and the proverb *for want of a nail the shoe was lost* — plus legal boilerplate, where *dismissed for want of prosecution* and *for want of jurisdiction* are live phrases on court dockets today. Free use before an ordinary noun (*a want of evidence*) reads as nineteenth-century prose. The modern everyday form is [a lack of](#a-lack-of).
**Goes with:** uncountable abstract nouns (*a want of evidence*, *a want of sense*, *a want of charity*, *a want of prosecution*) and, in older prose, plural countable nouns (*a want of hands*, *a want of provisions*). Never a singular countable noun except inside the *for want of a* frame, where the second article is required: *for want of a nail*.
**With *of*:** obligatory and structural. The dominant surviving form drops the article altogether: *for want of* [noun], not ✗ *for a want of* [noun].
**Pattern:** *for want of* [noun] (the living frame) &middot; *for want of a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *a want of* [abstract uncountable noun] (archaic) &middot; *dismissed for want of* [prosecution/jurisdiction] (legal) &middot; *from want of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *a want of* and [a lack of](#a-lack-of) mean the same thing, and *a lack of* has taken over everywhere except the fixed phrases — *for want of a better word* and *for lack of a better word* are both idiomatic, but *a want of evidence* should become *a lack of evidence* in anything written today. Against [a dearth of](#a-dearth-of), which is formal but current, *a want of* is formal and **dated**. Keep it apart from the ordinary verb *want* and from the countable noun *a want* meaning a desire (*his wants are few*).
**Variants:** *for want of*, *for want of a better word*, *for want of anything better*, *for want of prosecution* (legal), *a lack of* (the modern equivalent)

Names an absence or insufficiency of something — the older word that [a lack of](#a-lack-of) has very nearly replaced.

1. For *want of* a nail the shoe was lost; for want of a shoe the horse was lost.
2. The appeal was dismissed for *want of* jurisdiction.
3. He agreed to it for *want of* anything better to do that evening.
4. "The house was cold and cheerless, for *want of* a fire and for want of company."
5. It failed for *want of* capital, not for want of ideas.
6. Call it a truce, for *want of* a better word.

**See also:** [a lack of](#a-lack-of), [a dearth of](#a-dearth-of), [a jot of](#a-jot-of), [a whit of](#a-whit-of), [next to no](#next-to-no)

### a whit of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈwɪt əv/, with the older *wh*- kept in some regional speech /ə ˈʍɪt əv/ &middot; *uh WIT uhv* — a homophone of *wit* for most Americans; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uh WIT uh*
**Русский:** ни на йоту, ни капли — устар., книжн., только при отрицании
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** From Old English *wiht*, "creature, thing." It belongs to older prose and to modern writing imitating it, and its commonest surviving shape is the bare adverbial *not a whit*, meaning "not at all" — *he cared not a whit*. Americans read this phrase; they do not say it.
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns — *a whit of evidence*, *a whit of sense*, *a whit of difference*, *a whit of sympathy*. Never a plural countable noun and never a concrete substance.
**With *of*:** required when a noun follows, but the phrase very often stops at the bare noun and works adverbially: *not a whit*, *never a whit*, *cared not a whit*.
**Pattern:** *not a whit of* [abstract noun] &middot; *without a whit of* [abstract noun] &middot; *not a whit* (bare adverbial) &middot; *care/matter not a whit* &middot; *never a whit*
**Contrast:** *a whit of* is the twin of [a jot of](#a-jot-of) — same job, same era, different ancestry — and both are archaic where [a shred of](#a-shred-of) and [an iota of](#an-iota-of) are current. Like them it is **negative-polarity** and cannot stand in a plain affirmative: ✗ *We found a whit of evidence*. Note how close *not a whit* is to *not a bit*: the second is ordinary modern English, the first is costume.
**Variants:** *not a whit*, *never a whit*, *a jot of*, *an iota of* (the modern form), *not a bit* (the modern adverbial)

Names the smallest conceivable amount of an abstract quality, used only to deny it — an old word kept alive by old books.

1. "He cared not *a whit* for what the neighbors might say."
2. There is not *a whit of* evidence in the record to support the charge.
3. The revision made not *a whit of* difference to the outcome.
4. "She abated not *a whit* of her demands."
5. Twenty years in office had not softened him *a whit*.
6. They showed not *a whit of* remorse when the verdict came down.

**See also:** [a jot of](#a-jot-of), [an iota of](#an-iota-of), [a shred of](#a-shred-of), [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of)

### an iota of

**Pronunciation:** /ən aɪˈoʊɾə əv/, carefully /ən aɪˈoʊtə əv/ &middot; *uhn eye-OH-tuh uhv* — the article is the weak /ən/; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *uhn eye-OH-tuh uh*
**Русский:** ни капли, ни на йоту — мельчайшая доля; живой эмфатический оборот
**Type:** quantifier (partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal, emphatic — a live American phrase. It is named for iota, the smallest letter of the Greek alphabet, but the etymology is invisible in use, and it should not be confused on that account with [a jot of](#a-jot-of), which descends from the same Greek word and is archaic. Its rarer partner *a particle of* does identical work with a bookish air.
**Goes with:** uncountable **abstract** nouns — *evidence*, *proof*, *doubt*, *sense*, *sympathy*, *credibility*, *difference*, *remorse*. Never a plural countable noun, never a singular countable noun, and never a concrete substance (✗ *an iota of sugar*).
**With *of*:** obligatory when a noun follows. The bare noun also stands alone adverbially: *it doesn't matter an iota*, *not one iota*.
**Pattern:** *not an iota of* [abstract noun] &middot; *without an iota of* [abstract noun] &middot; *there isn't an iota of* [abstract noun] &middot; *not one iota* (bare, emphatic) &middot; *not an iota* [comparative]
**Contrast:** *an iota of* is **negative-polarity**: it needs a negation, and a plain affirmative ✗ *We have an iota of evidence* is not English. It belongs to the emphatic family headed by [a shred of](#a-shred-of), alongside *an ounce of*, *a scrap of*, *a particle of*, and [a speck of](#a-speck-of); of these it is the most abstract and the most emphatic, and it is the one that pairs with *one* for extra force (*not one iota*). Against [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of), a legal term of art, *an iota of* is plain English; against [a jot of](#a-jot-of) and [a whit of](#a-whit-of), which are archaic, it is fully current.
**Variants:** *not one iota*, *not an iota*, *a particle of* (literary), *an ounce of*, *a scrap of*, *a shred of*, *a scintilla of* (legal)

Names the smallest conceivable amount of an abstract quality, used almost always to deny that any of it exists at all.

1. There isn't *an iota of* evidence that he knew about the transfer.
2. She hasn't shown *an iota of* remorse.
3. Not *one iota* of the plan has changed since March.
4. The apology contained not *an iota of* sincerity.
5. It won't make *an iota of* difference who signs it.
6. He answered without *a particle of* embarrassment. (the literary variant)

**See also:** [a shred of](#a-shred-of), [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of), [a jot of](#a-jot-of), [a whit of](#a-whit-of), [a speck of](#a-speck-of), [a modicum of](#a-modicum-of)

### barely any

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɛrli ˌɛni/ &middot; *BAIR-lee EN-ee*
**Русский:** почти нисколько — количество на грани нуля, но не совсем ноль (разг.)
**Type:** quantifier (adverb + determiner), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** *barely* modifies the central determiner *any*, which fills the central slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — the most colloquial of the three *any* phrases
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*barely any people*) and uncountable nouns (*barely any time*). Never a singular countable noun (✗ *barely any chair*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *barely any of the seats*, *barely any of it*. No *of* before a bare noun: *barely any seats*.
**Pattern:** *barely any* [plural countable / uncountable] &middot; *barely any of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *barely any of* [them/it] &middot; *there's barely any* [noun] &middot; *barely any* (pronoun, standing alone)
**Contrast:** *barely any*, *hardly any*, and *scarcely any* are near-interchangeable and differ mainly in register: *barely* is the most casual, *hardly* the everyday default, *scarcely* the most formal. All three are negative-polarity and keep the verb positive: ✓ *There's barely any left* / ✗ *There isn't barely any left*, which is negative concord and nonstandard in writing. Against bare *few* and *little*, these are what Americans actually say.
**Variants:** *hardly any*, *scarcely any*, *barely anything* (pronoun), *barely a* (+ singular noun)

Names a quantity so small it is on the edge of nothing — "almost none, but not quite none."

1. There's *barely any* milk left.
2. *Barely any* of the seats were taken.
3. We had *barely any* warning before the road closed.
4. *Barely any* of them showed up for the second session.

### few if any

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfju ɪf ˈɛni/ &middot; *FYOO if EN-ee*
**Русский:** мало, если вообще есть — оговорка, которая усиливает, а не смягчает
**Type:** quantifier (coordinated frame), emphatic &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; common in journalism, academic prose, and argument

**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *few if any of the applicants*, *few if any of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *few if any applicants*.
**Pattern:** *few, if any,* [plural countable] &middot; *few, if any, of* [the/these + plural] &middot; [noun phrase] *— few, if any —* [verb] &middot; *little, if any,* [uncountable] (the uncountable partner)

### hardly any

**Русский:** почти нисколько, почти никаких — обиходная разговорная замена few и little
**Type:** quantifier (adverb + determiner), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** *hardly* modifies the central determiner *any*, which fills the central slot &middot; **Register:** neutral — the everyday American default
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*hardly any people*) and uncountable nouns (*hardly any money*). Never a singular countable noun (✗ *hardly any chair*), except in the separate frame *hardly a* + singular noun (*hardly a day goes by*).

### next to no

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɛks tə ˈnoʊ/, carefully /ˌnɛkst tu ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *neks tuh NO*
**Русский:** почти никакого — практически ноль
**Type:** quantifier (degree phrase + negative determiner) &middot; **Position:** central — *next to* modifies the central determiner *no* &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*next to no time*, *next to no warning*) and plural countable nouns (*next to no options*, *next to no results*). Singular countable nouns are possible but awkward and rare (*next to no chance* works because *chance* is on the mass side of the line).
**With *of*:** it does not take an *of*-phrase; the pronoun form is *next to nothing* (*we got next to nothing out of them*), and *next to none* covers the countable pronoun (*next to none of them*).
**Pattern:** *next to no* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; *next to nothing* (pronoun) &middot; *next to none of* [them/the + plural] &middot; *for next to nothing* (idiom: very cheaply)
**Contrast:** *next to no* is stronger than *hardly any* — it puts the quantity adjacent to zero rather than merely near it — and it is the emphatic sibling of *little to no* and *virtually no*. The idiom *for next to nothing* means "very cheaply" and is fixed.
**Variants:** *little to no*, *little or no*, *virtually no*, *practically no*, *almost no*, *next to nothing*, *next to none*

Names a quantity that is as close to zero as it can be without being zero.

1. We had *next to no* warning before the layoffs.

### not many

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ˈmɛni/ &middot; *not MEN-ee*
**Русский:** немного, немногие — разг. замена few, с исчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier (negated central determiner) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the central determiner *many*, which fills the central slot &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *not many people*, *not many options*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *not many time* — use *not much*) and never a singular countable noun.
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *not many of the applicants*, *not many of them*. No *of* before a bare plural: *not many applicants*.
**Pattern:** *not many* [plural countable] &middot; *not many of* [the/these + plural] &middot; *not many of* [them] &middot; *not many* (pronoun, standing alone) &middot; *there weren't many* [plural] (negation on the verb instead)

### not much

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *not MUCH*
**Русский:** немного, мало — разг. замена little, с неисчисляемыми
**Type:** quantifier (negated central determiner) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the central determiner *much*, which fills the central slot &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *not much time*, *not much money*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *not much people* — use *not many*) and never a singular countable noun, except through the separate frame *not much of a* + singular noun (*not much of a cook*).
**With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *not much of the report*, *not much of it*. No *of* before a bare noun: *not much time*. The frame *not much of a* + singular countable noun is a distinct construction meaning "a poor example of" (*not much of a view*, *not much of a plan*).
**Pattern:** *not much* [uncountable] &middot; *not much of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *not much of* [it/that] &middot; *not much of a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *there isn't much* [uncountable] (negation on the verb instead)
**Contrast:** *not much* is the everyday spoken equivalent of bare *little*, and its countable partner is *not many*. Note where the negation goes: *Not much time is left* negates inside the noun phrase, *There isn't much time left* moves it to the verb, and speech prefers the second. The *of a* frame is a different thing entirely — *not much of a swimmer* is a judgment of quality, not of quantity. As a pronoun *not much* stands alone with no noun and takes a singular verb (*Not much has changed*). Do not stack a second negative on it: ✗ *We didn't get not much sleep*, ✗ *We didn't get no sleep*. That is **negative concord**, which is systematic and rule-governed in African American English, Southern American English, and other varieties, but is corrected in edited writing, where the single negative *We didn't get much sleep* is the form to write. It is the negated form of [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much), which lives in the large-quantity file.
**Variants:** *not too much*, *not that much*, *not very much*, *not much of a*, *there isn't much*

The ordinary conversational way of saying *little* — a small amount, stated as a denial of largeness.

1. There's *not much* time before the store closes.
2. *Not much* of the report was still accurate.
3. *Not much* money was left in the fund by December.
4. He's *not much* of a cook, but he tries.
5. *Not much* has changed since the last audit.

### precious few / precious little

**Русский:** до обидного мало — эмфатическое, с ноткой возмущения
**Type:** quantifier (intensifier + quantifier), emphatic &middot; **Position:** central — *precious* intensifies the quantifier, which fills the central slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to journalistic; rhetorical, faintly indignant
**Goes with:** *precious few* takes plural countable nouns only (*precious few options*); *precious little* takes uncountable nouns only (*precious little evidence*). **With *of*:** *of* is **required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *precious few of the survivors*, *precious little of the money*, *precious little of it*. No *of* before a bare noun.

### the least bit of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlist ˈbɪt əv/ &middot; *thuh LEEST BIT uhv* — *the* keeps its weak pre-consonant form /ðə/, never /ði/; the *of* reduces to /ə/, giving *thuh LEEST BIT uh*

**Type:** quantifier (superlative + partitive phrase), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** central — the whole phrase fills the central determiner slot &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational

**With *of*:** required when a noun follows — ✗ *the least bit trouble*. Drop the *of* and the phrase becomes the adverbial *not the least bit* + adjective, which is not a determiner at all.

## Adjectives that quantify

The last five entries open a noun phrase and feel like determiners, but they are **adjectives**, and one test settles it: a central determiner cannot follow another determiner, and these can — *a de minimis amount*, *the minimal effort required*, *the scant evidence we have*, *a slight delay*, *the sparse attendance*. Most of them also take degree modifiers (*fairly minimal*, *very slight*, *rather sparse*) and stand after a linking verb (*The evidence is sparse*, *the harm was de minimis*), neither of which any determiner in this file can do. They are catalogued here because learners reach for the same slot with them, and because the countability restrictions still matter.

The same test disqualifies a longer list of words that are often mistaken for quantifiers of small amount and are **not** catalogued in this file at all: *meager*, *paltry*, *negligible*, *nominal*, *insufficient*, *modest*. Each one describes a quantity instead of stating one, and each sits happily behind a determiner — *the meager rations*, *a paltry sum*, *the negligible difference* — which is exactly what a central determiner can never do. They are adjectives, and they belong with the adjectives.

### de minimis

**Pronunciation:** /deɪ ˈmɪnɪmɪs/, also /di ˈmɪnɪmɪs/ &middot; *day MIN-ih-miss*, also *dee MIN-ih-miss* — both are current in American courtrooms and neither is corrected; the stress falls on *MIN*
**Русский:** юр.: ничтожно малый — такой, которым закон пренебрегает
**Type:** Latin adjective phrase with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive. It stands where a determiner would (*de minimis risk*), but it can also follow one — *a de minimis amount*, *the de minimis exception*, *this de minimis error* — which is the proof that it is an adjective. It is also used predicatively: *the harm was de minimis*. &middot; **Register:** **legal and technical only.** It is the head of the maxim *de minimis non curat lex*, "the law does not concern itself with trifles." An American meets it in tax rules (the de minimis fringe benefit, the de minimis safe harbor), in wage-and-hour and employment cases, in copyright and environmental law, and in the compliance memos that quote them. It is never neutral English, and outside that world it reads as showing off.
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*de minimis risk*, *de minimis harm*, *de minimis use*) and plural countable nouns (*de minimis amounts*, *de minimis quantities*, *de minimis errors*). With a singular countable noun it needs an article, exactly as an adjective does: *a de minimis amount*, never ✗ *de minimis amount*.
**With *of*:** Not used — the phrase takes no *of*-phrase of its own. The partitive work is done by the noun it modifies: *a de minimis amount of lead*, *a de minimis quantity of the compound*.
**Pattern:** *de minimis* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; [a/the] *de minimis* [singular countable noun] &middot; *the de minimis exception/threshold/rule* &middot; [noun] *is de minimis* (predicative) &middot; *de minimis non curat lex* (the full maxim)
**Contrast:** *de minimis* does not merely mean "very small." It means **small enough that the law will disregard it**, which is a conclusion rather than a measurement — a court calling an exposure de minimis is declining to act on it. That is the whole distance between *de minimis harm* and *minimal harm*: the second is a quantity, the first is a ruling. Against [minimal](#minimal), which is technical but ordinary English, *de minimis* is a term of art with a doctrine behind it. Against [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of), the other Latin small of American law, note the grammar: *de minimis* modifies a noun, while *a scintilla of* takes one.
**Variants:** *de minimis non curat lex* (the full maxim), *the de minimis rule*, *the de minimis exception*, *the de minimis threshold*, *minimal*, *negligible*

Describes a quantity so small that the law or the rule declines to take notice of it — a legal threshold wearing the clothes of a measurement.

1. The court held that three minutes of unpaid setup time was *de minimis*.
2. The employer treats occasional personal use of the copier as a *de minimis* fringe benefit.
3. Concentrations at that level are *de minimis* under the current standard.
4. The sampled clip ran too long to be *de minimis* use.
5. Counsel argued that the error was *de minimis* and could not have affected the verdict.
6. The rule includes a *de minimis* exception for shipments under $800.

**See also:** [minimal](#minimal), [a scintilla of](#a-scintilla-of), [scant](#scant), [slight](#slight), [a modicum of](#a-modicum-of)

### minimal

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɪnəməl/ &middot; *MIN-uh-mul*
**Русский:** минимальный — сведённый к наименьшему рабочему количеству, намеренно
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** it stands where a determiner would, but it can also follow one (*the minimal disruption we caused*), which proves it is an adjective &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning technical and corporate
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*minimal effort*, *minimal risk*, *minimal damage*) and plural countable nouns (*minimal changes*, *minimal side effects*). With a singular countable noun it needs an article, exactly as an adjective does: *a minimal fee*, not ✗ *minimal fee*.
**With *of*:** Not used — this word takes no *of*-phrase. The nominal *a minimum of* does that work instead: *a minimum of three days*.
**Pattern:** *minimal* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; [the/a] *minimal* [noun] &middot; *fairly/relatively/absolutely minimal* &middot; [noun] *is minimal* (predicative)
**Contrast:** *minimal* is an adjective, so unlike the quantifiers above it can be modified by a degree adverb, used predicatively, and preceded by a determiner. Strictly it is an absolute — from Latin *minimus*, "the smallest possible" — so *fairly minimal* and *more minimal* are logically loose, though both are ordinary in American usage and only style guides object. Against the determiner *little*, it is more clinical and less negative: *minimal damage* is a measurement, *little damage* is a judgment.
**Variants:** *minimally* (adverb), *the bare minimum*, *a minimum of*, *negligible*, *nominal*

Describes a quantity reduced to the smallest workable amount — small by design or by measurement rather than by disappointment.

1. The upgrade required *minimal* effort from the users.
2. There was *minimal* damage to the roof.
3. We made *minimal* changes to the original draft.
4. The treatment has *minimal* side effects.
5. She charged us a *minimal* fee for the consultation.
6. The disruption was *minimal*, all things considered.

**See also:** [scant](#scant), [slight](#slight), [sparse](#sparse), [a modicum of](#a-modicum-of), [little](#little)

### scant

**Pronunciation:** /skænt/ &middot; *SKANT*
**Русский:** скудный, едва достаточный — книжн.; сочетается и с исчисляемыми, и с неисчисляемыми
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive. It stands where a determiner would (*scant evidence*), but it can also follow one — *the scant evidence we have*, *his scant regard for the rules* — which is the proof that it is an adjective and not a central determiner. Unlike *minimal* and *sparse* it resists the predicative slot: *The evidence is scant* occurs but sounds stiff next to *The evidence is thin*. &middot; **Register:** formal to journalistic
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*scant evidence*, *scant attention*, *scant comfort*, *scant regard*) and plural countable nouns (*scant resources*, *scant details*). With a singular countable noun it needs an article, exactly as an adjective does, and it shifts meaning to "barely a full one": *a scant teaspoon*, *a scant hour*, *a scant two miles*. Never ✗ *scant teaspoon* with no article.
**With *of*:** Not used — this word takes no *of*-phrase.
**Pattern:** *scant* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; [the/his/their] *scant* [noun] &middot; *a scant* [singular unit noun] ("barely a full") &middot; *pay scant attention to* [X] &middot; *show scant regard for* [X]
**Contrast:** *scant* is the formal written cousin of bare *little*, and the test that separates them is position: *little* cannot follow a determiner, *scant* can — ✓ *the scant evidence*, ✗ *the little evidence* in that same sense. It carries the same disappointment bare *little* does (*scant comfort* means no real comfort at all), and it covers **both** countability types, which neither *little* nor *few* can do alone. Against *minimal*, which is neutral and technical, *scant* is a complaint; against *sparse*, which measures how thinly a quantity is spread, *scant* measures only how little of it there is. In *a scant teaspoon* it means "just short of," the exact opposite of *a generous teaspoon*. Keep it apart from *scanty*, which describes clothing and coverage rather than quantity.
**Variants:** *scanty*, *scantily* (adverb), *a scant* (+ unit noun), *scant regard*, *scant attention*, *scant comfort*

Describes a quantity that falls short of what is needed or expected — the formal register's version of bare *little*, and one of the few quantifying words that takes countable and uncountable nouns alike.

1. There is *scant* evidence that the program worked.
2. The report paid *scant* attention to what any of it would cost.
3. That is *scant* comfort to the families still waiting for answers.
4. They did the whole job with *scant* resources and no permanent staff. (plural countable)
5. He has shown *scant* regard for the deadline since the day he took over.
6. Add *a scant* teaspoon of baking soda, not a heaping one. (singular — "barely a full")

**See also:** [minimal](#minimal), [slight](#slight), [sparse](#sparse), [little](#little), [precious few / precious little](#precious-few--precious-little)

### slight

**Pronunciation:** /slaɪt/ &middot; *SLYTE*
**Русский:** незначительный, лёгкий — малая степень или мера чего-либо
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive after a determiner (*a slight delay*, *the slightest chance*) or predicative (*the difference was slight*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns without an article (*slight improvement*, *slight discomfort*) and singular countable nouns **with** an article (*a slight delay*, *a slight chance*). With plural countable nouns it is possible but uncommon (*slight variations*). Because it is an adjective, it cannot replace the article the way a determiner can: ✗ *slight delay held us up* → ✓ *a slight delay held us up*.
**With *of*:** Not used — this word takes no *of*-phrase.
**Pattern:** *a slight* [singular countable noun] &middot; *slight* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the slightest* [noun] &middot; *not the slightest* [noun] &middot; [noun] *is slight* (predicative)
**Contrast:** *slight* measures **degree or intensity**, not bulk — a slight delay is short, a slight accent is faint, a slight chance is improbable. That is what separates it from *minimal*, which measures quantity, and from *little*, which is a determiner and takes no article. Its superlative *slightest* is a negative-polarity favorite: *not the slightest interest*, *without the slightest hesitation*, *hasn't the slightest idea*. Keep it apart from the unrelated noun and verb *slight* meaning "an insult" / "to snub."
**Variants:** *slightly* (adverb), *slighter*, *the slightest*, *not the slightest*, *the merest*

Describes a small degree or extent of something — faint, mild, or barely appreciable.

1. There's been *a slight* delay with the connecting flight.
2. She has *a slight* accent that's hard to place.
3. We saw *slight* improvement after the second week.
4. There's *a slight* chance of snow tonight.
5. He hasn't *the slightest* idea what he's agreeing to.
6. The difference between the two samples was *slight*.
7. There were *slight* variations between the two batches. (plural countable — possible, but uncommon)

**See also:** [minimal](#minimal), [scant](#scant), [sparse](#sparse), [a hint of](#a-hint-of), [a shade of](#a-shade-of), [a little](#a-little)

### sparse

**Pronunciation:** /spɑrs/ &middot; *SPARSS*
**Русский:** редкий, разреженный — малочисленный и разбросанный по площади
**Type:** adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive (*sparse vegetation*, *the sparse crowd*) or predicative (*the data are sparse*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning descriptive and technical
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*sparse vegetation*, *sparse data*, *sparse attendance*) and plural countable nouns (*sparse crowds*, *sparse trees*). With a singular countable noun it needs an article: *a sparse crowd*.
**With *of*:** Not used — this word takes no *of*-phrase.
**Pattern:** *sparse* [uncountable / plural countable] &middot; [a/the] *sparse* [noun] &middot; *sparsely populated* (adverb) &middot; [noun] *is sparse* (predicative) &middot; *sparser* / *more sparse*
**Contrast:** *sparse* adds **distribution** to smallness: the quantity is small *and* thinly spread over an area, which is why it fits vegetation, hair, population, attendance, and data points, and why it sounds wrong for a compact quantity (*sparse money* is odd). Among the adjectives here, *minimal* measures amount, *slight* measures degree, and *sparse* measures density. Its nearest determiner-side relative is [a smattering of](#a-smattering-of), which does the same scattering work as a partitive phrase. The opposite is *dense*.
**Variants:** *sparsely* (adverb), *sparser*, *sparsity* / *sparseness*, *thin*, *scattered*

Describes something small in quantity and thinly distributed over an area or a set.

1. The vegetation gets *sparse* above the tree line.
2. Attendance was *sparse* for a Tuesday matinee.
3. Data on the early years is *sparse* and unreliable.
4. He ran a hand through his *sparse* hair.
5. *Sparse* crowds gathered along the parade route.
6. The northern counties are *sparsely* populated.

**See also:** [minimal](#minimal), [scant](#scant), [slight](#slight), [a smattering of](#a-smattering-of), [a trickle of](#a-trickle-of)
