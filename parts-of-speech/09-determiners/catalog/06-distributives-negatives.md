# Distributive, universal, and negative determiners

> The determiners that quantify over a whole set — covering it one member at a time, all at once, two at a time, or not at all.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What unites this group is that none of its members states an amount. They state **coverage** — how much of a set the sentence reaches. *Each* walks the set one member at a time, *every* and *all* take the whole of it, *both*, *either*, and *neither* work over a set of exactly two, and *no*, *none of*, and *not any* reach none of it at all. The trap running through the group is **number**: *each*, *every*, *either*, and *neither* all mean something plural but take a **singular** noun and a **singular** verb — *each student has*, *every window was*, *neither plan works* — while *both* and *all* take the plural. Getting that backwards produces ✗ *each students* and ✗ *every windows*, the most common errors in the entire determiner system.

The second rule running through the group is ***of***, and it is worth memorizing as three lists. *All* and *both* make it **optional** before another determiner (*all (of) the students*, *both (of) my parents*) but **required** before a pronoun (*all of them*, never ✗ *all them*). *Each*, *either*, *neither*, and *any* **require** it before anything already determined (*each of the students*, ✗ *each my cousins*). And *every* and *no* **forbid** it outright — there is no ✗ *every of the students* and no ✗ *no of the students* — which is the entire reason *every one of*, *every bit of*, and *none of* exist as separate headwords.

Negation has rules of its own here. Standard American English allows **one** negative marker per clause, so *no* and *any* never combine: ✗ *I have no any money* is impossible in every variety of English, and the two grammatical repairs are *I have no money* and *I don't have any money*. Doubling the negative onto both the verb and the determiner (*I don't have no money*) is **negative concord** — rule-governed and completely systematic in African American English, Southern vernacular, and many other spoken varieties, but stigmatized in edited American writing. Recognize it, label it, and keep it out of a cover letter. Distinguish it, too, from **partial** negation: *not all* and *not every* leave some members standing, where *no* and *none of* leave none.

Finally, most of these words are also **pronouns**. The determiner precedes a noun; the pronoun stands alone. *All the tickets sold out* (determiner) against *All is well* (pronoun); *Each student paid* against *They each paid*. The pure pronouns of this family — *everyone*, *everything*, *no one*, *nobody*, *nothing*, and bare *none* — are never determiners and live with the [pronouns](../../02-pronouns/README.md). The correlative pairs *both … and*, *either … or*, and *neither … nor* are conjunctions and live with the [conjunctions](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/02-correlative.md). And several near neighbors are catalogued elsewhere in this dictionary: [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), the polarity partner of *any*, and [hardly any](05-quantifiers-small.md#hardly-any) sit with the [small quantifiers](05-quantifiers-small.md); [much, many, most](04-quantifiers-large.md) sit with the large ones; and the *wh-* free-choice words sit with the [interrogatives and relatives](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md).

## Universal and distributive

The words that take in a whole set — either sweeping it up at once or walking through it member by member. Watch the number agreement in every entry: *all* and *both* are plural, everything else here is singular. One near neighbor is deliberately absent. *Each additional* and *every additional* are not determiners in their own right but *each* or *every* plus the ordinary postdeterminer *additional*, which keeps its full descriptive meaning — "one more, beyond the base" — and is catalogued with the numerals at [further, additional](07-numerals-multipliers.md#further-additional). The test is that it can be swapped for another adjective without breaking anything (*each extra passenger*, *each remaining passenger*), which is exactly what the bleached intensifiers of *every single*, *every last*, and *every blessed* cannot do.

### all

**Pronunciation:** /ɔl/ &middot; *AWL* &middot; /ɑl/ for the many Americans who merge *cot* and *caught*; there is no weak form of *all* itself, but the /l/ vocalizes before a consonant and *all right* reduces casually to [ɔˈɹaɪt] or even [aɪt]; in the partitive the *of* is weak, /əv/ reducing to /ə/, so *all of the* runs to [ˈɔlə ðə] and *all of them* to [ˈɔlə ðəm]
**Type:** quantifier (universal) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer before another determiner (*all the money*); central determiner before a bare noun (*all children*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*all students*) and uncountable nouns (*all information*); a singular countable **only** when another determiner follows (*all my life*, *all this trouble*) or the noun names a span of time (*all day*, *all summer*). Never a bare singular countable: ✗ *all book*, ✗ *all student*.
**With *of*:** **Optional** before another determiner — *all (of) the students*, *all (of) my cousins*. **Required** before a personal pronoun — *all of them*, *all of us*; ✗ *all them*. **Impossible** before a bare noun — *all children*, ✗ *all of children*.
**Pattern:** *all* [plural/uncountable noun] &middot; *all* (*of*) [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *all of* [them/us/you] &middot; [plural subject] + auxiliary + *all* + verb (floated) &middot; *all* [day/week/year]
**Contrast:** *all* covers three or more, or an undivided mass; [both](#both) covers exactly two. Against [every](#every), the difference is grammatical rather than semantic: *all* takes a plural noun and a plural verb and sees the group as one body (*all the students were notified*), while *every* takes a singular noun and a singular verb and runs down the list (*every student was notified*). Against [not all](#not-all), watch the scope — *All the seats weren't taken* is ambiguous in speech and should be rewritten as *Not all the seats were taken* (some were empty) or *None of the seats were taken* (all were empty). As a determiner *all* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*All is well*, *That's all*).
**Variants:** *all of*, *all the*, *floated all*, *all day / all week / all year*, *all but*

Sweeps in the entire set or the entire quantity, with nothing left out. The verb agrees with the noun *all* is quantifying, not with *all* itself — *All the money **is** gone* but *All the coins **are** gone*. Like *both* and *each*, *all* can **float** away from its subject and land next to the verb: *All the guests have left* → *The guests have **all** left*.

1. *All* the students passed the exam.
2. She spent *all* her savings on the down payment.
3. *All* of them showed up an hour early.
4. *All* this trouble started with one missed email. — a singular countable, grammatical only because *this* follows
5. We waited *all* day for a callback from the insurance company.
6. *All* information on this form is confidential.
7. The guests have *all* gone home.
8. *All* children learn to read at their own pace.

**See also:** [both](#both), [every](#every), [each](#each), [not all](#not-all), [none of](#none-of), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

### all and singular

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ən ˈsɪŋɡjəlɚ/ &middot; *awl-uhn-SING-gyuh-lur* &middot; *and* takes its weak form /ən/ or a syllabic /n̩/, giving [ˌɔl n̩ ˈsɪŋɡjəlɚ]; *all* keeps /ɔl/, or /ɑl/ for the many Americans who merge *cot* and *caught*; the main stress lands on *singular*, whose /j/ is often dropped in fast speech to give [ˈsɪŋɡəlɚ]
**Type:** quantifier (universal, emphatic legal doublet) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer — it stands in front of the central determiner, exactly where *all* stands in *all the goods* &middot; **Register:** **archaic legal boilerplate.** An American meets it in deeds, wills, probate inventories, admiralty writs, and old insurance policies, and essentially nowhere else; plain-language drafting guides delete it on sight
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns**, almost always inside a legal doublet of their own — *all and singular the goods and chattels*, *all and singular the lands and tenements*. It takes an **uncountable** noun only in a few frozen formulas (*all and singular the personal property*). Never a bare singular countable: ✗ *all and singular the house*, ✗ *all and singular house*.
**With *of*:** **Never** — ✗ *all and singular of the goods*. The doublet sits directly on top of *the*, exactly as predeterminer [all](#all) does; the *of* form a modern reader expects, *all of the goods*, is the rewrite rather than a variant.
**Pattern:** *all and singular the* [plural noun] &middot; *all and singular the* [noun] *and* [noun] (the doublet doubled again) &middot; *to have and to hold all and singular the* [noun] &middot; *all and singular, the* [noun] (with the comma, as older deeds set it)
**Contrast:** *Singular* here does not mean "one" or "unusual." It is the Latin *singuli*, "one by one," arriving through Law French, so the phrase means "the whole set, and every item in it taken separately" — which makes it [all](#all) and [each](#each) welded into one predeterminer. It is the courtroom ancestor of the two doublets already in this file: [any and all](#any-and-all), which does the same belt-and-suspenders work in modern contracts, and [each and every](#each-and-every), which does it in speech. Against *all and sundry*, which looks parallel but is a **pronoun** meaning "everybody" and takes no noun at all (*the party was open to all and sundry*), *all and singular* always has a noun phrase after it; see [sundry](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#sundry).
**Variants:** *all and singular the*, *all and every (older)*, *any and all*, *each and every*, *all and sundry (a pronoun — a different word)*

An archaic legal predeterminer meaning "the entire set, and each member of it individually." It exists to defeat two opposite readings at once — that a grant covers the collection but not the pieces, or the pieces but not the collection — which is the same anxiety that produces *any and all*. The verb agrees with the plural noun, not with *singular*: *All and singular the parcels **are** conveyed*. Modern American drafters write *all* and stop.

1. The deed conveys *all and singular* the lands and tenements described in Schedule A. — a conveyance
2. I give and bequeath *all and singular* my goods and chattels to my sister. — a will
3. The executor filed an inventory of *all and singular* the personal property of the estate. — probate
4. The policy insures *all and singular* the vessels named in the endorsement. — marine insurance, where the phrase is oldest
5. To have and to hold *all and singular* the premises, with the appurtenances thereto belonging. — the habendum clause of an old deed
6. A modern contract says *all the property described above* and loses nothing. — the plain-language rewrite

**See also:** [all](#all), [each](#each), [any and all](#any-and-all), [each and every](#each-and-every), [sundry](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#sundry)

### both

**Pronunciation:** /boʊθ/ &middot; *BOHTH* &middot; the final /θ/ is often lost before a following consonant in fast speech: *both sides* → [boʊ ˈsaɪdz]; the *of* of the partitive takes its weak /əv/ and usually drops to /ə/, giving *both of them* [ˈboʊθə ðəm]
**Type:** quantifier (universal, dual) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer before another determiner (*both the twins*, *both my parents*); central determiner before a bare plural (*both hands*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only, and only when the set has exactly two** — *both hands*, *both candidates*. Never a singular countable (✗ *both hand*), never an uncountable (✗ *both furniture*, ✗ *both information*), and never a set of three or more (✗ *both of the five finalists*).
**With *of*:** **Optional** before another determiner — *both (of) the twins*, *both (of) these windows*. **Required** before a personal pronoun in this order — *both of them*, *both of us*; ✗ *both them*, though the reversed order *them both* is perfectly good (*I saw them both*). Not used before a bare plural — *both parents*, ✗ *both of parents*.
**Pattern:** *both* [plural countable noun] &middot; *both* (*of*) [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *both of* [them/us/you] &middot; [plural subject] + *are/have* + *both* + … (floated)
**Contrast:** *both* is the plural member of the dual set, and the three of them split the verb neatly: *both* takes the two together and a **plural** verb (*Both plans work*), [either](#either) takes one of the two and a **singular** verb (*Either plan works*), [neither](#neither) takes zero of the two and a singular verb (*Neither plan works*). Against [all](#all), *both* is capped at two — for three or more, *all* is the only choice. The correlative conjunction [both … and](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/02-correlative.md#both--and) joins two items and is a different word class entirely. As a determiner *both* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*Both are broken*).
**Variants:** *both of*, *both the*, *floated both*, *them both / us both*

Refers to the two members of a two-member set together, as a pair. The verb is always **plural**, even though the sense is "each of the two" — *Both of my parents **are** retired*. *Both* floats like *all* and *each*: *Both of my parents are retired* → *My parents are **both** retired*. It sits badly with a negative — *Both of them didn't come* is ambiguous and avoided; say *Neither of them came* for zero, or *Only one of them came* for one.

1. *Both* windows in the front room leak.
2. *Both* of my parents grew up in Cleveland.
3. She broke *both* her wrists in the fall.
4. *Both* candidates dodged the question.
5. I've read *both* of them, and neither is any good.
6. My sisters are *both* teachers.

**See also:** [all](#all), [either](#either), [neither](#neither), [each](#each), [both … and](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/02-correlative.md#both--and)

### each

**Pronunciation:** /itʃ/ &middot; *EECH* &middot; the /tʃ/ links straight into a following /j/, so *each year* runs together as [ˈitʃɪɹ]; the obligatory *of* is unstressed and reduces from /əv/ to /ə/, so *each of the* comes out [ˈitʃə ðə]
**Type:** distributive &middot; **Position:** central determiner; also floats to a position beside the verb, and postposes as an adverb (*ten dollars each*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *each student*, *each page*, *each day*. Never a plural countable (✗ *each students*), never an uncountable (✗ *each water*, ✗ *each advice*). To reach a plural noun, *each* must go through *of*: *each of the students*.
**With *of*:** **Required** before any noun phrase that already has a determiner, and before every pronoun — *each of the students*, *each of my cousins*, *each of them*. ✗ *each my cousins* and ✗ *each them* are ungrammatical. Bare *each* + singular noun takes no *of*: ✗ *each of student*.
**Pattern:** *each* [singular countable noun] &middot; *each of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *each of* [them/us/you] &middot; [plural subject] + *each* + [plural verb] (floated) &middot; [noun] + *cost/weigh* … *each* (postposed)
**Contrast:** *each* against [every](#every) is the central distinction of this file. *Each* looks at members **individually, one at a time**; *every* looks at the set **collectively**. *Each* works with a set of two, *every* needs three or more. *Each* takes *of* and can float away from its subject; *every* can do neither. *Almost*, *nearly*, and *practically* modify *every*, not *each* — ✓ *almost every day*, ✗ *almost each day*. As a determiner *each* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*Each was inspected*).
**Variants:** *each of*, *each one*, *each and every*, *floated each*, *postposed each (a dollar each)*

Picks out the members of a group one by one, treating them separately. The noun and the verb are both singular — *Each student **has** a locker* — but when *each* **floats** away from a plural subject and lands next to the verb, the verb goes back to plural: *The students **each have** a locker*, *We **each have** our own copy*. *Each* also survives at the end of a phrase as an adverb of price or weight: *The tickets are twenty dollars each*.

1. *Each* student receives a locker and a combination.
2. *Each* of the four applicants was interviewed twice.
3. She wrote a note to *each* of them.
4. The winners *each* got a check for five hundred dollars.
5. *Each* day brought a new complication.
6. Limes are seventy-nine cents *each* this week.

**See also:** [every](#every), [each and every](#each-and-every), [either](#either), [both](#both), [every one of](#every-one-of)

### each and every

**Pronunciation:** /ˌitʃ ən ˈɛvri/ &middot; *eech-uhn-EV-ree* &middot; *and* reduces to the weak form /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/; the main stress lands on *every*
**Type:** distributive (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** emphatic; natural in speech and rhetoric, and routinely cut by copy editors as redundant
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *each and every student*. Never a plural (✗ *each and every students*), never an uncountable (✗ *each and every water*), exactly like both of its parts.
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase — ✗ *each and every of the students*. The partitive is *each and every one of the students*, with *one* inserted to carry the *of*.
**Pattern:** *each and every* [singular countable noun] &middot; *each and every one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun]
**Contrast:** Semantically it adds nothing to [each](#each) or to [every](#every) — it is a rhetorical doubling that means "with no exceptions whatsoever, and I mean it." Grammatically it inherits *every*'s behavior rather than *each*'s: it cannot float (✗ *The students each and every paid*) and cannot take a bare *of*. Its counterpart at the opposite end of the register scale is the legal doublet [any and all](#any-and-all).
**Variants:** *each and every one of*, *every single*, *every last*

An emphatic fusion of *each* and *every*, used when the speaker wants to insist that nothing was skipped. The verb is singular. It is common in speeches, sales pitches, and coaching talk, and because it draws attention to itself it wears out fast in writing.

1. *Each and every* employee will get the same bonus this year.
2. I checked *each and every* box on that form twice.
3. *Each and every* one of you needs to sign in at the front desk.
4. She answers *each and every* email the same day.
5. *Each and every* seat in the theater was taken.
6. He thanked *each and every* volunteer by name.

**See also:** [each](#each), [every](#every), [every single](#every-single), [every last](#every-last), [any and all](#any-and-all)

### either

**Pronunciation:** /ˈiðɚ/ &middot; *EE-thur* &middot; /ˈaɪðɚ/ (*EYE-thur*) is fully standard, but /ˈiðɚ/ is the majority American pronunciation — the reverse of the British preference; whichever vowel a speaker uses, they normally use the same one in *neither*; the required *of* is weak, so *either of them* runs to [ˈiðɚə ðəm]
**Type:** distributive (dual) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only, and only from a set of exactly two** — *either route*, *either parent*. Never a plural (✗ *either routes*), never an uncountable (✗ *either milk*), and never a set of three or more — for three or more the word is [any](#any).
**With *of*:** **Required** before a determined noun phrase or a pronoun — *either of the routes*, *either of them*. ✗ *either the routes* and ✗ *either them* are ungrammatical. Note that the noun after *of* is **plural** while the verb stays singular: *Either of the plans works*.
**Pattern:** *either* [singular countable noun] &middot; *either of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *either of* [them/us/you] &middot; *on either side of* [noun]
**Contrast:** *either* is the positive dual and [neither](#neither) its negation: *Either answer is acceptable* against *Neither answer is acceptable*. Against [any](#any), the difference is set size — *either* for two, *any* for three or more (*Either day works* for a weekend, *Any day works* for a week). Against [both](#both), *either* takes one of the two where *both* takes them together. As a determiner *either* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*Either will do*), and *either* at the end of a negative sentence is an **adverb** (*I don't like it either*) — three different word classes for one spelling.
**Variants:** *either of*, *either one*, *either way*, *on either side*

Two distinct uses. **(1)** *One or the other of two, it doesn't matter which* — the ordinary sense: *Either route gets you there*. **(2)** *Each of two, both* — a fossil sense surviving in fixed spatial phrases: *trees on either side of the road* means both sides, not one of them. Because sense (2) is still live, *either* is occasionally ambiguous, and careful writers switch to *each* or *both* when they mean sense (2) anywhere other than *either side*, *either end*, *either hand*. The verb is singular in careful writing — *Either of the plans **works*** — though *work* is very common in speech.

1. *Either* route will get you to the airport by six.
2. *Either* of these applicants would do the job well.
3. There were cornfields on *either* side of the highway.
4. I'm happy with *either* answer.
5. You can pay in cash or by card — *either* one is fine.
6. Did *either* of them call you back?

**See also:** [neither](#neither), [both](#both), [any](#any), [each](#each), [either … or](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/02-correlative.md#either--or)

### every

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvri/ &middot; *EV-ree* &middot; two syllables in ordinary American speech; the careful three-syllable /ˈɛvəri/ exists but sounds deliberate
**Type:** distributive (universal) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every window*, *every chance*. Never a plural countable (✗ *every windows*), never an uncountable (✗ *every water*, ✗ *every advice* — for those, see [every bit of](#every-bit-of)). The one systematic exception is the **frequency pattern**, where *every* takes a numeral plus a plural: *every three days*, *every ten minutes*, *every few weeks*, *every two years*.
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *every of the students* does not exist in English. The repair is [every one of](#every-one-of) the students for plural countables and [every bit of](#every-bit-of) the paint for uncountables — which is precisely why those two phrases exist.
**Pattern:** *every* [singular countable noun] &middot; *every* [numeral] [plural time noun] (frequency) &middot; *every one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *almost/nearly/practically every* [singular noun]
**Contrast:** *every* against [each](#each) is the distinction to learn: *every* views the set **collectively** and wants at least three members; *each* views the members **individually** and works with two. *Every* takes no *of* and cannot float (✗ *The students every arrived*); *each* does both. *Every* accepts *almost*, *nearly*, and *practically*; *each* does not. Against [all](#all), *every* is singular where *all* is plural — *every student was notified* = *all the students were notified*. Against [not every](#not-every), note that negation is partial, not total.
**Variants:** *every single*, *every last*, *every other*, *each and every*, *every one of*, *every bit of*

Covers the entire membership of a set of three or more, viewed as a complete sweep rather than as separate individuals. Both the noun and the verb are **singular** — *Every seat **is** taken* — but the pronoun that refers back is normally singular *they* in modern American usage: *Every applicant should bring **their** own laptop*. *Every* is the base for a whole family of emphatic and alternating phrases: *every single*, *every last*, *every other*, *each and every*.

1. *Every* window on the block was broken.
2. She checks her email *every* ten minutes.
3. *Every* applicant has to submit two references.
4. Almost *every* restaurant on that street closed during the pandemic.
5. I've read *every* book he's written.
6. *Every* one of those excuses is a lie.

**See also:** [each](#each), [all](#all), [every one of](#every-one-of), [every other](#every-other), [every single](#every-single), [every blessed](#every-blessed), [every third / every fourth / every nth](#every-third--every-fourth--every-nth), [every which](#every-which), [not every](#not-every)

### every bit of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈbɪt əv/ &middot; *ev-ree-BIT-uv* &middot; *of* takes its weak form /əv/ and often reduces further to /ə/, giving [ˌɛvri ˈbɪɾə]; the stress sits on *bit*
**Type:** distributive (universal, partitive) &middot; **Position:** central determiner heading a partitive phrase — *every* determines *bit*, and the *of*-phrase supplies the real noun &middot; **Register:** neutral, mildly emphatic
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns, reached through *of*** — *every bit of evidence*, *every bit of the paint*, *every bit of energy*. It also takes a singular countable construed as a mass (*every bit of that old house*). Never a plural countable: ✗ *every bit of the students* — that is *every one of the students*.
**With *of*:** ***Of*** **is required**; carrying the *of* is the entire function of *bit*. ✗ *every bit evidence* is impossible, and so is ✗ *every evidence*, which is why the phrase exists.
**Pattern:** *every bit of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *every bit of* [the/my/this + uncountable noun] &middot; *every bit of* [it/this/that] &middot; *every last bit of* [uncountable noun]
**Contrast:** *every bit of* is to uncountable nouns what [every one of](#every-one-of) is to plural countables: both exist because [every](#every) can take neither *of* nor a non-singular noun. Against [all](#all) *of*, the difference is insistence — *all of the paint* reports the quantity, *every bit of the paint* insists that none was left. Do not confuse the determiner with the adverbial *every bit as* (*every bit as good as the original*), which modifies an adjective and quantifies nothing.
**Variants:** *every last bit of*, *every single bit of*, *every ounce of*, *every scrap of*, *every drop of*

The partitive that lets *every* quantify something uncountable, insisting that the whole of it was covered with nothing left over. The verb stays **singular**, agreeing with *bit*: *Every bit of the evidence **was** thrown out*.

1. *Every bit of* evidence pointed the same direction.
2. She used *every bit of* the fabric and had nothing left over.
3. He put *every bit of* energy he had into that last mile.
4. We scraped *every bit of* ice off the windshield.
5. *Every bit of* the money went to the shelter.
6. I meant *every bit of* it.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every one of](#every-one-of), [every last](#every-last), [all](#all), [none of](#none-of)

### every blessed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈblɛsɪd/ &middot; *ev-ree-BLESS-id* &middot; ***blessed* has two syllables here, not one** — the adjective /ˈblɛsɪd/, never the past-tense /blɛst/ of *he blessed the meal*. The stress moves onto *blessed*, which is where the exasperation lives; there is no weak form, because the whole point of the word is that it is leaned on
**Type:** distributive (emphatic, euphemistic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner — *every* fills the slot and *blessed* is a fixed intensifier with no religious content &middot; **Register:** **dated, folksy, and emphatic.** An American meets it in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century fiction, in older rural and Southern speech, and from grandparents; in current writing it reads as deliberately quaint rather than wrong
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every blessed day*, *every blessed one*. Never a plural (✗ *every blessed days*), never an uncountable (✗ *every blessed money*), following *every* exactly. In practice it attaches to a short list of nouns and does not spread much beyond it: *day*, *night*, *time*, *minute*, *one*, *thing*, *word*, *penny*.
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase — ✗ *every blessed of them*. The partitive is *every blessed one of*: *every blessed one of them*.
**Pattern:** *every blessed* [singular time noun] &middot; *every blessed one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *every blessed* [thing/word/penny] &middot; *the whole blessed* [noun] (the same intensifier in the other frame)
**Contrast:** *Blessed* here is a **minced oath** — a euphemism standing in for *damned*, *blasted*, or *cursed* — which is why the phrase carries irritation rather than reverence: *every blessed day* means "every last miserable day," not a day that was blessed. That bleaching puts it in the same family as *old* in [any old](#any-old), *single* in [not a single](#not-a-single), and *earthly* in [no earthly](#no-earthly), where an ordinary adjective has lost its meaning and kept only its force. Against [every single](#every-single), which counts members off, and [every last](#every-last), which exhausts a supply, *every blessed* adds **exasperation** and nothing else. Do not confuse it with the literal adjective in *every blessed sacrament*, where *blessed* means what it says and the phrase means what it looks like.
**Variants:** *every blessed one of*, *every blasted*, *every livelong (as in the livelong day)*, *every last*, *every single*

Emphatic *every* with a note of grievance in it — the speaker is complaining about the relentlessness of whatever the noun names. The noun and the verb are both **singular**, exactly as after plain *every*: *Every blessed one of them **was** late*. It has aged out of neutral American English and now works mainly as period flavor, as affectionate imitation of an older speaker, or as comic exaggeration.

1. He calls me at six in the morning *every blessed* day.
2. She sat by that window *every blessed* night for a year.
3. We spent *every blessed* penny we had on the roof.
4. *Every blessed* one of those forms had to be signed twice.
5. "I've told you *every blessed* thing I know," he said, and shut the door. — the phrase as fiction sets it down
6. The machine broke down *every blessed* time we ran it.
7. Current American writing says *every single day* here; *every blessed day* is the grandparent's version of the same sentence. — the register in one line

**See also:** [every](#every), [every single](#every-single), [every last](#every-last), [each and every](#each-and-every), [any old](#any-old), [no earthly](#no-earthly)

### every last

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈlæst/ &middot; *ev-ree-LAST* &middot; the stress moves onto *last*, and the /t/ is usually unreleased before a consonant: *every last dime* → [ˌɛvri ˈlæs daɪm]
**Type:** distributive (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** informal and emphatic — common in speech, rare in formal writing
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every last dime*, *every last crumb*. Never a plural (✗ *every last dimes*), never an uncountable (✗ *every last money*), following *every* exactly.
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase. Use *every last one of* for countables — *every last one of them* — and *every last bit of* for uncountables.
**Pattern:** *every last* [singular countable noun] &middot; *every last one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *every last bit of* [uncountable noun]
**Contrast:** *every last* and [every single](#every-single) are near-twins; *every last* leans toward the **exhaustion of a supply** (*every last dollar*, *every last drop*), while *every single* leans toward **counting off members** of a set (*every single person in the room*). To reach an uncountable noun, both need a partitive: *every last bit of the paint*.
**Variants:** *every last one of*, *every last bit of*, *every single*, *each and every*

Emphatic *every*, insisting that the set has been used up or accounted for down to the final member. It carries a note of effort or grievance — the speaker is usually pointing out that this took some doing, or that nothing was left over.

1. He spent *every last* dime of his savings on that truck.
2. We ate *every last* crumb of the cake.
3. She read *every last* page of the contract before signing it.
4. They fired *every last* one of us.
5. I want *every last* box out of this garage by Sunday.
6. The drought killed *every last* seedling in the field.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every single](#every-single), [every bit of](#every-bit-of), [each and every](#each-and-every), [all](#all)

### every one of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈwʌn əv/ &middot; *ev-ree-WUN-uv* &middot; *of* takes the weak form /əv/ and often /ə/; the stress on *one* is what separates it in speech from the pronoun *everyone*
**Type:** distributive (universal, partitive) &middot; **Position:** central determiner heading its own partitive phrase — the head of the phrase is *one*, not the plural noun after *of* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** ***of*** **plus a plural noun that already has a determiner, or a pronoun** — *every one of the students*, *every one of my cousins*, *every one of them*. Never a bare plural (✗ *every one of students*), never an uncountable (✗ *every one of the water* — that is *all of the water* or *every bit of the water*).
**With *of*:** ***Of*** **is required** — that is the whole point of the phrase. [Every](#every) itself cannot take *of*, so *one* is inserted to carry it.
**Pattern:** *every one of* [the/these/my + plural noun] &middot; *every one of* [them/us/you] &middot; *every single one of* … &middot; *every last one of* …
**Contrast:** The trap is spelling. ***Every one*** **(two words)** is this partitive determiner and refers to things or people already specified: *Every one of the cookies was gone*. ***Everyone*** **(one word)** is an indefinite **pronoun**, refers only to people, and never takes *of*: *Everyone laughed*. If you can insert *single* between the words, it is two words. Against [all](#all) *of*, this phrase counts the members one at a time and takes a singular verb, where *all of* takes them as a body and agrees with the noun.
**Variants:** *every single one of*, *every last one of*, *each and every one of*, *every one (of them)*

The repair phrase that lets *every* do what *every* alone cannot: attach to a noun phrase that already has a determiner. The verb stays **singular** — *Every one of the applicants **was** rejected* — which is the giveaway that the head of the phrase is *one*.

1. *Every one of* the applicants was rejected.
2. I've met *every one of* her brothers.
3. *Every one of* them denied knowing anything about it.
4. She corrected *every one of* my mistakes in red ink.
5. *Every one of* those windows needs to be replaced.
6. *Every single one of* the tickets sold in under an hour.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every bit of](#every-bit-of), [each](#each), [all](#all), [none of](#none-of)

### every other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *ev-ree-UTH-ur* &middot; the two words run together with no break, and a linking /j/ often appears between them: [ˌɛvriˈjʌðɚ]
**Type:** distributive &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every other week*, *every other student*. Never a plural (✗ *every other weeks*), never an uncountable, inheriting *every*'s restriction exactly.
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase. *Every other one of* exists but is clumsy and is usually rephrased as *every second one*.
**Pattern:** *every other* [singular time noun] (alternating) &middot; *every other* [singular countable noun] (the remainder) &middot; *every second* [time noun] (synonym of the alternating sense)
**Contrast:** Do not confuse it with ***each other***, which is a reciprocal **pronoun**, not a determiner: *They helped each other* means mutually, and *every other* never means that. Within *every other* itself, the noun tells the two senses apart: a **time** noun gives the alternating reading (*every other Tuesday*), and any other noun in a context of comparison gives the remainder reading (*every other candidate withdrew*). Its negative and free-choice twins are [no other](#no-other) and [any other](#any-other).
**Variants:** *every second*, *every other one*, *each other (different word — a pronoun)*

Two distinct uses. **(1) Alternating — every second one, skipping one in between:** *We meet every other Thursday* means twice a month, on alternate Thursdays. **(2) All the remaining ones, everything apart from the one just named:** *Every other option was worse* means all the rest were worse. Sense (2) is what makes comparative sentences work — *She's faster than every other runner on the team* correctly excludes her from the comparison class, where *than every runner* would absurdly include her.

1. The recycling truck comes *every other* Tuesday.
2. She calls her mother *every other* day.
3. *Every other* candidate had already withdrawn by March.
4. He's cheaper than *every other* contractor we called.
5. They repainted *every other* room and left the rest.
6. *Every other* seat was blocked off for distancing.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every third / every fourth / every nth](#every-third--every-fourth--every-nth), [any other](#any-other), [no other](#no-other), [each](#each)

### every single

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈsɪŋɡəl/ &middot; *ev-ree-SING-guhl* &middot; the stress shifts onto *single*, which is the whole point of the phrase
**Type:** distributive (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, emphatic
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every single person*, *every single time*. Never a plural (✗ *every single people*), never an uncountable (✗ *every single information*).
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase. Use *every single one of* — *every single one of them*.
**Pattern:** *every single* [singular countable noun] &middot; *every single one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *every single time* [clause]
**Contrast:** *Every single* counts off members and stresses that not one was missed; [every last](#every-last) stresses that a supply was used up; [each and every](#each-and-every) is the third member of the emphatic set and the most formal-sounding of the three, which is why it turns up in speeches. All three take a singular noun and a singular verb. The negative mirror image is [not a single](#not-a-single).
**Variants:** *every single one of*, *every single time*, *every last*, *each and every*

Emphatic *every*, adding "with no exceptions at all." It is the most common emphatic determiner in spoken American English, and *every single time* has become a near-idiom for expressing exasperation at something predictable.

1. *Every single* person in the room turned around.
2. He forgets his badge *every single* time.
3. I read *every single* review before I bought it.
4. *Every single* one of those files was corrupted.
5. She's been late *every single* day this month.
6. They checked *every single* seat for the missing ring.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every last](#every-last), [each and every](#each-and-every), [not a single](#not-a-single), [all](#all)

### every third / every fourth / every nth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈθɚd/, /ˌɛvri ˈfɔrθ/, /ˌɛvri ˈɛnθ/ &middot; *ev-ree-THURD*, *ev-ree-FORTH*, *ev-ree-ENTH* &middot; the stress moves onto the ordinal, which carries the whole meaning and therefore has **no weak form**; *every* keeps its ordinary two-syllable /ˈɛvri/ and does reduce, to [ˌɛvri] or even [ˌɛvi], since it is the unstressed half. The final clusters simplify in speech: *every fifth row* → [ˌɛvri ˈfɪf roʊ], *every sixth one* → [ˌɛvri ˈsɪks wʌn]
**Type:** distributive (interval) &middot; **Position:** central determiner *every* with an **ordinal postdeterminer** behind it — the ordinal fills the same slot it fills in *the third row* &middot; **Register:** neutral; the *nth* form is informal in speech and technical in writing
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *every third row*, *every fourth patient*, *every tenth customer*. Never a plural after the ordinal (✗ *every third rows*), never an uncountable (✗ *every third water*), inheriting *every*'s restriction exactly. **Do not confuse it with the frequency pattern**, which takes a **cardinal plus a plural**: *every three days*, *every ten minutes*. The two are the same interval expressed two ways, and each has its own number agreement.
**With *of*:** Not with the bare phrase — ✗ *every third of the rows*, inheriting *every*'s prohibition. *Every third one of the rows* is grammatical and clumsy, and Americans rewrite it as *one in three of the rows* or *a third of the rows*.
**Pattern:** *every* [ordinal] + [singular countable noun] &middot; *every* [cardinal] + [plural time noun] (the frequency twin: *every three days*) &middot; *every nth* [noun] (unspecified interval) &middot; *every third one of* … (grammatical, avoided)
**Contrast:** Two patterns sit side by side and mean the same thing with opposite grammar: *every third day* (ordinal + **singular**) and *every three days* (cardinal + **plural**). Both name an interval of three, and crossing them produces ✗ *every third days* and ✗ *every three day*. Against [every other](#every-other) in its alternating sense, this is the same construction with the gap widened — *every other week* is *every second week*, and from three up the ordinal series takes over: *every third week*, *every fourth week*. Watch how the interval is counted: *every third row* means rows one, four, and seven — **two** rows skipped, not three — which is the same inclusive counting that makes *every other* skip only one. *Every nth* is the algebraist's placeholder let loose in ordinary speech, where it usually means "at some regular interval I am not going to specify."
**Variants:** *every second (= every other)*, *every third one of*, *every three days (the cardinal twin)*, *every umpteenth (jocular)*, *one in three*

Names a repeating interval by counting positions in a series. The noun and the verb are both **singular** — *Every fourth seat **is** blocked off* — exactly as with plain *every*. It is the workhorse pattern of schedules, sampling plans, seating charts, and quality control, and it runs indefinitely up the ordinal series, which is why *nth* exists as an informal cover term for the whole family.

1. *Every third* row of seats was left empty.
2. The inspector pulls *every fourth* unit off the line and tests it. — quality control
3. She works *every third* weekend, so we plan around it.
4. *Every fifth* customer gets a free coffee. — a promotion
5. Plant them six inches apart and stake *every sixth* one. — an instruction
6. He tells that same story *every nth* time we go out, and I still laugh.
7. *Every third day* and *every three days* mean the same thing — ordinal with a singular, cardinal with a plural.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every other](#every-other), [every single](#every-single), [each](#each), [third, fourth, fifth](07-numerals-multipliers.md#third-fourth-fifth), [second](07-numerals-multipliers.md#second), [nth, umpteenth](07-numerals-multipliers.md#nth-umpteenth)

### every which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvri ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *ev-ree-WICH* &middot; speakers who keep the *wh-* distinction say /ˌɛvri ˈhwɪtʃ/; the phrase carries one main stress, on *which*
**Type:** distributive (fossilized idiom) &middot; **Position:** central determiner in form, but frozen — the phrase behaves as a single lexical item, not as a productive pattern &middot; **Register:** informal idiom; fine in speech and casual writing, out of place in formal prose
**Goes with:** **the singular countable noun *way*, and effectively nothing else** — *every which way*. *Every which direction* is occasionally heard; beyond that the phrase does not extend (✗ *every which chair*, ✗ *every which student*). Learn it as one word-sized chunk rather than as a rule.
**With *of*:** Never — ✗ *every which of the ways*.
**Pattern:** [verb of scattering or turning] + *every which way* &middot; *going/running/flying every which way* &middot; *every which way but loose*
**Contrast:** It looks like two central determiners stacked — *every* on top of *which* — and historically that is what it was, but modern English allows only **one** central determiner per noun phrase, which is exactly why the phrase cannot be extended to a new noun. Against plain *every way*, which means "by all available means" (*we tried every way we could*), *every which way* means "in all directions at once, with no order to it." The disorder is part of the meaning. Its free-choice twin is *any which way* ("in whatever direction, it doesn't matter which") — the same fossil with [any](#any) swapped in for *every*.
**Variants:** *every whichaway (dialectal)*, *every which direction*, *every which way but loose*, *any which way*

A fossilized emphatic meaning "in every direction, all over the place." The noun phrase functions adverbially, so it normally answers *where* or *how* rather than serving as a subject or object.

1. The wind sent the leaflets *every which way*.
2. Kids were running *every which way* across the parking lot.
3. His notes go *every which way* on the page.
4. When the alarm went off, people scattered *every which way*.
5. The old fence posts lean *every which way*.
6. She turned the map *every which way* and still got lost.

**See also:** [every](#every), [every other](#every-other), [all](#all), [which (interrogative)](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#which-interrogative)

## Free choice and open sets

Determiners that pick a member without specifying which one, and leave the choice genuinely open. The *wh-* members of this family are catalogued with the other *wh-* determiners in [interrogative, exclamative, and relative determiners](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md): [whatever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever) draws from an open range (*take whatever seat you like*), [whichever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichever) from a closed one (*whichever of the two you prefer*), [whichsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichsoever) is their archaic legal form, and [whatsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever) is the postposed emphasizer that sharpens a negative (*no doubt whatsoever*).

### any

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛni/ &middot; *EN-ee* &middot; the vowel is /ɛ/ as in *pen*, never /æ/ as in *pan* — the single most common mispronunciation of the word; through the American South and Midland it raises further to [ˈɪni]. Polarity *any* is unstressed and reduces toward [əni]; free-choice *any* is stressed and keeps its full vowel. In the partitive the *of* reduces as well: *any of the* → [ˈɛniə ðə]
**Type:** quantifier (general — negative-polarity and free-choice) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*any questions*) and uncountable nouns (*any money*) in questions, negatives, and conditionals; **singular countable** nouns in the free-choice sense (*any student can tell you*) and with a small set of abstract nouns (*any idea*, *any reason*, *any chance*, *any doubt*, *any way*). Not an ordinary singular countable in plain polarity use: ✗ *Did you buy any apple?* — say *any apples*.
**With *of*:** **Required** before a determined noun phrase or a pronoun — *any of the students*, *any of this money*, *any of them*. ✗ *any the students* is ungrammatical. Bare *any* + noun takes no *of*.
**Pattern:** *Do you have any* [plural/uncountable noun]? &middot; [negated verb] + *any* [plural/uncountable noun] &middot; *if* … *any* [noun] &middot; *any* [singular countable noun] (free choice) &middot; *any of* [the/my + noun or pronoun]
**Contrast:** *any* against *some* is a matter of **polarity**, not of meaning. [Some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some) is the default in affirmative statements (*I bought some apples*); *any* is the default in negatives and open questions (*I didn't buy any apples*, *Did you buy any apples?*). The exception that trips learners is the **polite offer**: a question that expects or invites a *yes* takes *some*, not *any* — *Would you like some coffee?*, *Could you lend me some cash?* *Would you like any coffee?* is grammatical but sounds grudging, as if you would rather not pour it. Against [no](#no), the two are alternative ways of negating and never co-occur: ✓ *I have no money*, ✓ *I don't have any money*, ✗ *I have no any money*. Against [either](#either), the difference is set size — *either* for exactly two, *any* for three or more. As a determiner *any* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*Is there any left?*).
**Variants:** *any of*, *any old*, *any other*, *any and all*, *not any*, *any at all*, *any one of*

Two distinct uses. **(1) Negative polarity** — in questions, negatives, conditionals, and after negative-flavored words (*hardly*, *rarely*, *without*, *refuse*, *doubt*, *before*), *any* marks a quantity whose existence is open or denied: *Do you have any questions?*, *He left without any explanation*. **(2) Free choice** — in **affirmative** statements, *any* takes a singular countable noun and means "it doesn't matter which one": *Any mechanic can fix that*. Sense (2) is not a negative at all, and it is the one that surprises learners taught that "*any* is for negatives." Stress separates them in speech: free-choice *any* is stressed, polarity *any* is not. After *of* + plural, the verb may be singular or plural — *If any of the files **is/are** missing, tell me*.

1. Do you have *any* questions before we start?
2. We didn't get *any* warning that the road was closed.
3. Did you bring *any* cash, or should I stop at an ATM?
4. *Any* student can tell you that class is a waste of time.
5. If *any* of these numbers look wrong, flag them.
6. He left without *any* explanation at all.
7. Is there *any* chance you could come in on Saturday?
8. Take *any* seat you like.

**See also:** [no](#no), [not any](#not-any), [any other](#any-other), [any old](#any-old), [any such](#any-such), [any manner of / any kind of / any sort of](#any-manner-of--any-kind-of--any-sort-of), [either](#either), [none of](#none-of), [ary / ary a](#ary--ary-a), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some)

### any and all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ən ˈɔl/ &middot; *en-ee-uhn-AWL* &middot; *and* takes its weak form /ən/ or a syllabic /n̩/; the stress lands on *all*
**Type:** quantifier (universal + free choice, emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** legal and contractual boilerplate; in ordinary prose it reads as padding
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*any and all claims*, *any and all documents*) and **uncountable nouns** (*any and all liability*, *any and all correspondence*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *any and all claim*.
**With *of*:** Possible but heavy — *any and all of the documents*. Contracts normally write *any and all documents* instead and keep the *of* out of it.
**Pattern:** *any and all* [plural countable noun] &middot; *any and all* [uncountable noun] &middot; *waive/deny any and all* [rights/claims/liability]
**Contrast:** Semantically it is just [all](#all), with *any* prefixed to close the loophole that a reader might argue *all* covers only the items already listed. Legal drafters keep it for that belt-and-suspenders reason; plain-language style guides delete it. It is the mirror image of [each and every](#each-and-every) — the same doubling instinct at the opposite end of the register scale.
**Variants:** *any and all of*, *each and every*, *all and sundry*

An emphatic legal doublet meaning "every one of these, without exception and however described." Its job is to defeat a narrow reading, which is why it clusters around *claims*, *rights*, *liability*, *damages*, and *documents*. Outside contracts and formal notices it sounds like someone imitating a lawyer.

1. The tenant waives *any and all* claims against the landlord.
2. Please produce *any and all* correspondence relating to the sale.
3. The company denies *any and all* liability for the delay.
4. *Any and all* unused funds revert to the general account.
5. She is authorized to sign *any and all* documents on his behalf.
6. The release covers *any and all* injuries arising from the event.

**See also:** [any](#any), [all](#all), [each and every](#each-and-every), [whatsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)

### any manner of / any kind of / any sort of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈmænɚ əv/, /ˌɛni ˈkaɪnd əv/, /ˌɛni ˈsɔrt əv/ &middot; *en-ee-MAN-ur-uv*, *en-ee-KYND-uv*, *en-ee-SORT-uv* &middot; the stress sits on *manner*, *kind*, or *sort*, and *of* takes its weak form /əv/ and regularly reduces to /ə/, giving [ˈmænɚə], [ˈkaɪndə], and — with the flapped /t/ — [ˈsɔrɾə]. ***Any* has two weak forms here, and they separate the two senses**: polarity *any* is unstressed and reduces toward [əni], so *not any kind of proof* runs to [nɑɾ əni ˈkaɪndə ˈpruf], while free-choice *any* is stressed and keeps its full [ˈɛni]. The casual reductions *kinda* and *sorta* belong to the hedging adverb (*kinda tired*), never here, where the *of* is structural and the phrase keeps its shape
**Type:** quantifier (negative-polarity and free choice, partitive, category-covering) &middot; **Position:** *any* is the **central determiner** of a partitive phrase whose head is *manner*, *kind*, or *sort*; the *of*-phrase supplies the real noun &middot; **Register:** *any kind of* and *any sort of* are neutral to informal and entirely ordinary in American speech; ***any manner of*** **is formal and archaic-flavored.** An American meets *any manner of* in contracts, leases, insurance policies, older statutes, and the King James Bible, and essentially never in conversation, where it would sound like someone doing an impression of a deed
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns** (*any manner of doubt*, *any kind of proof*) and **plural countable nouns** (*any manner of alterations*, *any sort of guarantees*), reached through *of*. A **singular countable** works only with its article left in place after *of* — *any kind of a plan* — and that pattern is informal and usually edited out. Note the fossilized singular inside the phrase: ✗ *any manners of work*, ✗ *any kinds of proof*, even though *manners* and *kinds* are perfectly good nouns elsewhere.
**With *of*:** ***Of*** **is required** and is part of the phrase — ✗ *any manner doubt*, ✗ *any kind proof*. **No determiner may follow it**: ✓ *any manner of excuses*, ✗ *any manner of the excuses*. To select from a set already identified, drop the frame and use plain *any of*: *any of those excuses*.
**Pattern:** [negated verb] + *any manner of* [uncountable/plural noun] &middot; *without any kind of* [noun] &middot; *if any sort of* [noun] &middot; *any kind of* [noun] *will do* (free choice) &middot; *any kind of a* [singular noun] (informal) &middot; *Is that any kind of* [noun]? (the dismissive question)
**Contrast:** This is the **polarity partner** of [no manner of / no kind of / no sort of](#no-manner-of--no-kind-of--no-sort-of), and the pair splits exactly as [any](#any) and [no](#no) do: *there was no manner of doubt* puts the negative on the determiner, *there wasn't any manner of doubt* puts it on the verb, and the two mean the same thing. They never combine: ✗ *no any manner of doubt*. Like plain *any*, it has **two senses** — negative-polarity in questions, negatives, and conditionals (*without any kind of warning*), and free choice in affirmatives (*any kind of wrench will do*) — and stress tells them apart. Against [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of), which is affirmative and **exhaustive** ("every variety there is"), this frame is **open** ("whichever variety, or none"): *all manner of excuses* says he produced many, *any kind of excuse* says one would do. Against [any such](#any-such), which is **anaphoric** and reaches back to the one thing just described, this covers a whole category and needs no antecedent. And keep the dismissive question apart from the quantifying use: *Is that any kind of apology?* judges quality, not quantity.
**Variants:** *no manner of*, *any type of*, *not any kind of*, *any manner of thing*, *all manner of*, *all kinds of / all sorts of*

Holds open a whole **category** rather than a quantity — "anything of that description, in any form at all." *Kind* and *sort* are the everyday words and *manner* the ceremonial one; the three are otherwise interchangeable, and only *manner* carries the archaic flavor. The verb agrees with the noun after *of* rather than with *manner* — *If any manner of records **survive**, the archive has not found them* — though the agreement is as unsettled here as in the negative twin, and careful writers rebuild the sentence rather than decide.

1. The lease does not permit *any manner of* alterations without written consent. — contract boilerplate, where *manner* still lives
2. Ye shall not do *any manner of* work therein. — the King James sabbath formula, and the reason the phrase still sounds biblical
3. There wasn't *any manner of* doubt that the signature was genuine. — the negation on the verb; *there was no manner of doubt* puts it on the determiner instead
4. They never offered *any kind of* explanation for the delay.
5. The used dryer came without *any sort of* guarantee.
6. Bring *any kind of* dish you like — it's a potluck. — free choice, stressed, in ordinary speech
7. ✗ *They didn't offer any manner of the explanations.* — impossible; no determiner may follow the *of*. Say *any of those explanations*.

**See also:** [no manner of / no kind of / no sort of](#no-manner-of--no-kind-of--no-sort-of), [any](#any), [any such](#any-such), [any old](#any-old), [not any](#not-any), [none of](#none-of), [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of), [all kinds of, all sorts of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of)

### any old

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈoʊld/ &middot; *en-ee-OHLD* &middot; the final /d/ is regularly dropped before a consonant, so *any old thing* comes out [ˌɛni oʊl ˈθɪŋ]; the stress is on *old*
**Type:** quantifier (free choice, dismissive) &middot; **Position:** central determiner — *any* fills the determiner slot and *old* is a fixed intensifier with no reference to age &middot; **Register:** informal and colloquial; ordinary in speech, out of place in formal writing
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*any old excuse*), plural countable nouns (*any old boxes*), and uncountable nouns (*any old junk*) — the same range as free-choice *any*, though it is most at home with a singular countable.
**With *of*:** Not used — ✗ *any old of them*. The partitive is plain *any of them*.
**Pattern:** *any old* [noun] &middot; *not just any old* [noun] &middot; *any old time / any old thing / any old how*
**Contrast:** *old* here is a **dismissive intensifier**, not an adjective of age: *any old chair* does not mean an aged chair, it means "whatever chair, I don't care which," and it works fine for a chair fresh out of the box. The negated frame *not just any old* flips the sense into praise — *That's not just any old guitar* means it is special. Against plain [any](#any), the difference is attitude rather than grammar: *any* is neutral free choice, *any old* adds a shrug.
**Variants:** *just any old*, *not just any old*, *any old how*, *any old time*, *any old thing*

Colloquial free choice with a shrug — the speaker is signaling that the choice does not matter and that the standard is low. Because *old* has bleached out of its literal meaning, it attaches happily to brand-new things without contradiction.

1. Grab *any old* chair; it doesn't matter which one.
2. He'll eat *any old* thing you put in front of him.
3. Come by *any old* time this weekend.
4. That's not just *any old* guitar — it belonged to her father.
5. You can't just throw *any old* numbers into the spreadsheet.
6. She keeps *any old* junk that might come in handy someday.

**See also:** [any](#any), [any other](#any-other), [whatever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some)

### any other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *en-ee-UTH-ur* &middot; the two words run together and the /i/ of *any* links into the following /ʌ/ with a /j/ glide: [ˌɛniˈjʌðɚ]
**Type:** quantifier (free choice, exclusive) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*any other question*), plural countable nouns (*any other questions*), and uncountable nouns (*any other evidence*) — it inherits the full range of *any*.
**With *of*:** *any other of the applicants* is grammatical but stiff; Americans reorder it to *any of the other applicants*, where *of* attaches to *any* and is required.
**Pattern:** *any other* [noun] &middot; [comparative] + *than any other* [singular countable noun] &middot; *before/without any other* [noun]
**Contrast:** The comparative trap is why this phrase earns its own entry. *She is faster than any other runner on the team* is correct because *other* subtracts her from the set she is compared against; *She is faster than any runner on the team* is illogical, since it claims she is faster than herself. The same repair applies with [no other](#no-other) — *No other city has this problem* excludes the city under discussion. Its universal twin is [every other](#every-other) in that word's remainder sense.
**Variants:** *any other one*, *any of the other*, *no other*, *every other*

Points to a member of the set outside the one already named. In comparisons it is doing real logical work rather than merely adding a word: *other* subtracts the subject from the comparison class.

1. Chicago has colder winters than *any other* big American city.
2. Do you have *any other* questions for the panel?
3. He wouldn't accept help from *any other* doctor.
4. There wasn't *any other* evidence to go on.
5. She'd rather live here than in *any other* state.
6. Call me if *any other* problems come up.

**See also:** [any](#any), [no other](#no-other), [every other](#every-other), [whichever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whichever), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

### any such

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *en-ee-SUCH* &middot; *such* keeps its full vowel /sʌtʃ/ and the stress, exactly as in *no such* and unlike the weak *such a* [sətʃə] of the exclamative. Polarity *any* is unstressed in front of it and reduces toward [əni], so the phrase lands as [əni ˈsʌtʃ]; free-choice *any such* keeps the fuller [ˈɛni ˈsʌtʃ]
**Type:** quantifier (negative-polarity and free choice, anaphoric) &middot; **Position:** *any* is the **central determiner**; *such* follows it as a pre-head modifier inside the same phrase. Because *any* has taken the one central slot English allows, no article can follow: ✗ *any such a claim* &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning formal — its natural home is legal, official, and academic prose
**Goes with:** **all three noun types**, exactly like *any* — singular countable (*any such claim*, *any such person*), plural countable (*any such records*, *any such payments*), and uncountable (*any such evidence*, *any such conduct*). The singular countable takes **no article**: ✓ *any such request*, ✗ *any such a request*.
**With *of*:** **Not with the bare phrase** — ✗ *any such of the claims*, ✗ *any such of them*, inheriting the block that *such* puts on the partitive. To reach an already-identified set, drop *such* and use plain *any of*: *any of those claims*. *Any such one* exists and is stilted.
**Pattern:** *if any such* [noun] *exists* &middot; [negated verb] + *any such* [noun] &middot; *without any such* [noun] &middot; *deny/dispute any such* [noun] &middot; *Any such* [noun] *shall be* … (the contract frame)
**Contrast:** *any such* is the polarity partner of [no such](#no-such), and the pair splits exactly as [any](#any) and [no](#no) do: *no such rule exists* puts the negative on the determiner, *there isn't any such rule* puts it on the verb, and the two mean the same thing. They never combine: ✗ *no any such rule*. What *such* adds to *any* is **anaphora** — *any rule* denies rules in general, *any such rule* denies the particular rule just described, so the phrase needs a description already on the table. And note the article rule it shares with *no such* and breaks with [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an): *such a mess* keeps the article, *any such mess* drops it, because *any* has already spent the slot.
**Variants:** *no such*, *any such thing*, *not … any such*, *if any such*, *any such other (legal)*

Denies or holds open the existence of anything of the kind just mentioned. It is **anaphoric** — *such* points back at a description already given — and it is the shape the negative takes when the negation lands on the verb instead of on the determiner. Contracts and statutes lean on it because it lets a clause reach back to a defined term without repeating it: *any such notice* means "any notice of the kind defined above." The verb agrees with the noun after it: *Any such record **is** confidential*, *Any such records **are** confidential*.

1. If *any such* records still exist, the archive has not found them.
2. The company denies that *any such* agreement was ever reached.
3. There is no evidence of *any such* payment anywhere in the ledger.
4. *Any such* request must be submitted in writing within thirty days. — the standard contract frame
5. He left without *any such* explanation, and nobody asked for one.
6. I've never heard of *any such* rule, and I've read the handbook twice.
7. ✗ *Do you have any such a form?* — nonstandard; *any* has already filled the determiner slot, so drop the *a*.

**See also:** [no such](#no-such), [any](#any), [any other](#any-other), [not any](#not-any), [such](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [whatsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)

## Negative

The determiners that reduce a set to zero — plus the two that reduce it only partway, and the rules that keep English from stacking two negatives in one clause. Related negatives of small quantity (*hardly any*, *scarcely any*, *barely any*, *next to no*, *a shred of*) are in [quantifiers of small quantity](05-quantifiers-small.md); the dialectal and archaic negatives — [ain't no](#aint-no), [ary / ary a](#ary--ary-a), [nary a](#nary-a), [never a](#never-a), and [negative concord](#no-negative-concord) — have their own section below.

One phrase that looks as though it belongs in this section does not. ***An absence of*** is not a determiner at all but a full lexical **noun** heading its own noun phrase: it takes *the* and a possessive (*the absence of evidence*, *in his absence*), it pluralizes (*three unexplained absences*), it accepts modifiers (*a complete absence of*, *a conspicuous absence of*), and — the decisive test — the verb agrees with *absence* rather than with the noun after *of*: ✓ *An absence of witnesses **was** noted*, ✗ *were noted*. In a genuine partitive determiner like [every bit of](#every-bit-of) or *a shred of*, the *of*-phrase supplies the real noun; here it does not. To deny a quantity, the determiners are [no](#no) and [none of](#none-of); to name the smallest imaginable amount and then deny even that, see [a shred of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-shred-of) and [not so much as a](#not-so-much-as-a).

### neither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniðɚ/ &middot; *NEE-thur* &middot; /ˈnaɪðɚ/ (*NYE-thur*) is fully standard in America; most speakers use the same vowel here that they use in *either*; the required *of* is weak, giving *neither of them* [ˈniðɚə ðəm]
**Type:** negative determiner (distributive, dual) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly formal — everyday speech often prefers *not either* or *neither one*
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only, and only from a set of exactly two** — *neither candidate*, *neither parent*. Never a plural (✗ *neither candidates*), never an uncountable (✗ *neither milk*), and never a set of three or more — for three or more the words are *no* and *none of*.
**With *of*:** **Required** before a determined phrase or a pronoun — *neither of the candidates*, *neither of them*. ✗ *neither the candidates* and ✗ *neither them* are ungrammatical. The noun after *of* is **plural** while the verb stays singular: *Neither of the plans works*.
**Pattern:** *neither* [singular countable noun] &middot; *neither of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *neither of* [them/us/you] &middot; *neither one of* [them]
**Contrast:** *neither* is the negation of [either](#either) and covers exactly two: *Neither answer is right*. For three or more, use [none of](#none-of) the answers or [no](#no) answer. Because *neither* is already negative it cannot sit in a clause that is negated again — ✗ *I don't like neither of them* is nonstandard; write *I don't like either of them* or *I like neither of them*. As a determiner *neither* precedes a noun; standing alone it is a **pronoun** (*Neither will do*), and *Neither do I* is an adverbial construction with subject–verb inversion.
**Variants:** *neither of*, *neither one*, *not either*, *neither … nor*

Denies both members of a two-member set at once: not the one and not the other. The noun and the verb are both **singular** — *Neither candidate **was** available* — and in careful writing the verb stays singular even after *of* + plural: *Neither of the plans **works***. Plural agreement (*Neither of the plans work*) is very common in speech and increasingly in print, but the singular remains the safe choice in edited prose.

1. *Neither* candidate answered the question.
2. *Neither* of my parents finished college.
3. She liked *neither* option, so we started over.
4. *Neither* of them has called back.
5. *Neither* road is plowed yet.
6. *Neither* one of those explanations makes sense.

**See also:** [either](#either), [no](#no), [none of](#none-of), [both](#both), [not any](#not-any), [neither … nor](../../07-conjunctions/catalog/02-correlative.md#neither--nor)

### no

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ/ &middot; *NOH* &middot; **no weak form** — *no* carries the clause's negation, so it keeps its full diphthong and some stress even in fast speech. Do not confuse it with the interjection *No.* or with *know*, its exact homophone
**Type:** negative determiner &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral; in subject position it is the only option, and elsewhere it is the more emphatic and more written choice
**Goes with:** **all three noun types** — singular countable (*no answer*), plural countable (*no answers*), and uncountable (*no time*). It is one of the very few determiners with no countability restriction at all. It cannot follow another central determiner: ✗ *the no answer*, ✗ *my no money*.
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *no of the students* does not exist. The partitive is [none of](#none-of) the students — which is the entire reason *none of* is a separate headword.
**Pattern:** *no* [singular countable noun] &middot; *no* [plural countable noun] &middot; *no* [uncountable noun] &middot; *There is/are no* [noun] &middot; *No* [noun] + verb (subject position)
**Contrast:** *no* against [not any](#not-any) is a choice of position and force, not of meaning: *We found no survivors* = *We didn't find any survivors*. *No* is stronger, and it is **required** when the negated phrase is the subject — ✓ *No students came*, ✗ *Not any students came*. Against *none*, the split is grammatical: *no* precedes a noun, *none* stands alone or takes *of* — ✓ *No tickets are left* / ✓ *None are left* / ✓ *None of the tickets are left* / ✗ *No of the tickets are left*. Against [neither](#neither), *no* has no limit on set size while *neither* is confined to two. Against [not all](#not-all), *no* is **total** negation where *not all* is partial.
**Variants:** *no other*, *no such*, *no one / no-one (pronoun)*, *no way / no doubt / no idea (fixed phrases)*

Reduces the noun to zero. The verb agrees with the noun that follows, not with *no* — *No student **was** notified*, *No students **were** notified*, *No news **is** good news*. Because *no* is itself the negative it never combines with another negative in the same clause: ✗ *I have no any money* is impossible in every variety of English, and *I don't have no money* is [negative concord](#no-negative-concord) — systematic in several American dialects but nonstandard in edited writing. *No* is also the base of a large set of fixed phrases: *no doubt*, *no way*, *no idea*, *no problem*, *no such thing*, *no wonder*.

1. There is *no* excuse for that email.
2. *No* students signed up for the eight o'clock section.
3. We have *no* time to argue about it.
4. *No* two witnesses told the same story.
5. She had *no* idea he was already gone.
6. *No* news is good news.
7. There's *no* way I'm driving in this.

**See also:** [none of](#none-of), [not any](#not-any), [not a](#not-a), [not one](#not-one), [not all](#not-all), [neither](#neither), [any](#any), [no other](#no-other), [no such](#no-such)

### no earthly

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈɚθli/ &middot; *noh-URTH-lee* &middot; the stress moves onto *earthly*. **Neither word has a weak form** — *no* carries the clause's negation and keeps its full diphthong, and *earthly* is the emphatic the phrase exists for; many Americans voice the /θ/ to [ð], giving [ˈɚðli], and the whole phrase runs to [noʊ ˈɚðli] with no break
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central determiner — *no* fills the slot and *earthly* is a fixed intensifier inside the phrase, with no reference to the planet &middot; **Register:** emphatic, informal to neutral; slightly old-fashioned but in current American use, and heard most in the fixed phrases *no earthly idea* and *no earthly reason*
**Goes with:** a short list of **singular countable abstract nouns** — *no earthly idea*, *no earthly reason*, *no earthly use*, *no earthly chance*, *no earthly way*, *no earthly clue*. It does not extend freely to concrete nouns: ✗ *no earthly chair*, ✗ *no earthly student*. A plural is possible but rare (*no earthly reasons*), and it reaches an **uncountable** only in the fossil *no earthly good* (*it did him no earthly good*).
**With *of*:** **Never** — ✗ *no earthly of them*, ✗ *no earthly of the reasons*, inheriting [no](#no)'s prohibition exactly. The partitive is [none of](#none-of): *none of it was any earthly use*.
**Pattern:** *have no earthly* [idea/clue/reason] &middot; *There is no earthly* [reason/way] *to* [verb] &middot; *do someone no earthly good* &middot; *of no earthly use* (*to* someone) &middot; *what earthly* [noun] …? (the same intensifier in a question)
**Contrast:** *Earthly* is a **bleached intensifier**, not an adjective of place: *no earthly reason* does not contrast a terrestrial reason with a heavenly one, it means "no reason whatsoever." The literal adjective is the one in *earthly possessions* and *earthly delights*, and it is a different word doing a different job. The bleaching puts this phrase with *old* in [any old](#any-old), *single* in [not a single](#not-a-single), and *blessed* in [every blessed](#every-blessed). Against plain [no](#no) the difference is force alone — *I have no idea* reports, *I have no earthly idea* insists — and against the postposed emphasizers *no idea whatsoever* and *no idea at all*, which sharpen the same negative from the other end of the phrase, the difference is only position; see [whatsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever).
**Variants:** *no earthly idea*, *no earthly reason*, *no earthly good*, *what earthly …?*, *no … whatsoever*, *not the faintest / not the foggiest*

An emphatic *no* meaning "none at all, none even conceivable." It comes from an older habit of swearing by heaven and earth, and the same idea survives with the phrase moved behind the noun — *no reason on earth* — which is the version most Americans now reach for. The verb agrees with the noun, exactly as after *no*: *No earthly reason **exists** for that clause*. Learn it as a short list of collocations rather than as a productive rule, because it does not spread past them.

1. I have *no earthly* idea where he put the keys.
2. There's *no earthly* reason to drive all the way out there tonight.
3. That contraption is of *no earthly* use to anybody.
4. She had *no earthly* clue the meeting had been moved. — informal speech, where the phrase is commonest
5. It did him *no earthly* good to argue with the inspector. — the uncountable fossil
6. What *earthly* reason could she have for lying about it? — the same intensifier in a question, with the negation carried by the rhetorical frame
7. *No reason on earth* says the same thing with the phrase behind the noun.

**See also:** [no](#no), [no such](#no-such), [none of](#none-of), [not a single](#not-a-single), [any old](#any-old), [every blessed](#every-blessed), [whatsoever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatsoever)

### no manner of / no kind of / no sort of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmænɚ əv/, /ˌnoʊ ˈkaɪnd əv/, /ˌnoʊ ˈsɔrt əv/ &middot; *noh-MAN-ur-uv*, *noh-KYND-uv*, *noh-SORT-uv* &middot; *of* takes its weak form /əv/ and regularly reduces to /ə/, giving [ˈmænɚə], [ˈkaɪndə], and — with the flapped /t/ — [ˈsɔrɾə]; *no* has **no weak form**, since it carries the clause's negation. The casual reductions *kinda* and *sorta* belong to the hedging adverb (*kinda tired*), not here, where the *of* is structural and the phrase keeps its shape
**Type:** negative determiner (partitive, category-denying) &middot; **Position:** *no* is the **central determiner** of a partitive phrase whose head is *manner*, *kind*, or *sort*; the *of*-phrase supplies the real noun &middot; **Register:** *no kind of* and *no sort of* are neutral to informal; ***no manner of*** **is formal and archaic-flavored.** An American meets *no manner of* in contracts and older statutes and, above all, in the King James Bible, where *no manner of work* is the sabbath formula — which is why the phrase still sounds biblical in anyone's mouth
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns** (*no manner of doubt*, *no kind of proof*) and **plural countable nouns** (*no manner of excuses*, *no sort of guarantees*), reached through *of*. A **singular countable** works only with its article left in place after *of* — *no sort of a plan* — and that pattern is informal and usually edited out. Note the fossilized singular inside the phrase: ✗ *no manners of work*, ✗ *no kinds of proof*, even though *manners* and *kinds* are perfectly good nouns elsewhere.
**With *of*:** ***Of*** **is required** and is part of the phrase — ✗ *no manner doubt*, ✗ *no kind proof*. **No determiner may follow it**: ✓ *no manner of excuses*, ✗ *no manner of the excuses*. To select from a set already identified, switch to the real partitive [none of](#none-of): *none of the excuses*.
**Pattern:** *no manner of* [uncountable/plural noun] &middot; *no kind of* [noun] &middot; *no sort of a* [singular noun] (informal) &middot; *That's no kind of* [noun] (predicative, dismissive) &middot; *not* … *any manner of* [noun] (the polarity twin)
**Contrast:** All three deny not just the thing but **every variety of** it: *no proof* denies proof, *no kind of proof* denies proof of any description. They are the negative counterpart of [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of) and [all kinds of, all sorts of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of), which sit with the predeterminers, and the polarity partner of ***any manner of*** — *there wasn't any manner of doubt* puts the negation on the verb, *there was no manner of doubt* puts it on the determiner, and the two mean the same thing. As always, they never combine: ✗ *no any manner of doubt*. Against [no such](#no-such), which is **anaphoric** and denies the one thing just described, these deny a whole category and need no antecedent. And keep the dismissive predicative use apart from the quantifying one: *That's no kind of apology* judges quality, not quantity.
**Variants:** *any manner of*, *no type of*, *not any kind of*, *no manner of thing*, *all manner of*, *all kinds of / all sorts of*

Denies a category outright — "nothing of that description, in any form at all." *Kind* and *sort* are the everyday words and *manner* the ceremonial one; the three are otherwise interchangeable, and only *manner* carries the archaic flavor. The verb agrees with the noun after *of* rather than with *manner* — *No manner of excuses **were** accepted* — though the agreement is genuinely unsettled here and careful writers tend to rebuild the sentence rather than decide. The dismissive predicative use (*that's no kind of answer*) has drifted from quantity into judgment and is by far the liveliest use of the frame in American speech.

1. There is *no manner of* doubt that the signature is genuine. — formal writing, where *manner* still lives
2. *No manner of* work shall be done on that day. — the King James formula, and the reason the phrase sounds biblical
3. They offered *no kind of* explanation for the delay.
4. *No sort of* guarantee came with the used dryer.
5. That's *no kind of* apology, and you know it. — the dismissive use, the commonest live one
6. The lease permits *no manner of* alterations without written consent. — contract boilerplate
7. ✗ *There was no any manner of doubt.* — impossible; *no* and *any* are two candidates for one slot. Say *no manner of doubt* or *not any manner of doubt*.

**See also:** [any manner of / any kind of / any sort of](#any-manner-of--any-kind-of--any-sort-of), [no](#no), [no such](#no-such), [none of](#none-of), [not any](#not-any), [any](#any), [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of), [all kinds of, all sorts of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of)

### no other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *noh-UTH-ur* &middot; the two words run together with a linking /w/: [noʊˈwʌðɚ]
**Type:** negative determiner (exclusive) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** all three noun types, like *no* — singular countable (*no other choice*), plural countable (*no other options*), and uncountable (*no other evidence*).
**With *of*:** **Never** — *no* takes no *of*-phrase of any kind. The partitive is *none of the other* — *none of the other applicants*.
**Pattern:** *no other* [noun] &middot; *There is no other* [noun] &middot; *No other* [noun] + verb (subject position) &middot; *have no other choice but to* [verb]
**Contrast:** It is the negative twin of [any other](#any-other) and does the same logical job in comparisons: *No other city has this problem* excludes the city being discussed. Written as *No city has this problem*, the sentence would contradict itself. The fixed phrase *no other choice* competes with *no choice* — *no other choice* implies one option remains, *no choice* implies none does.
**Variants:** *none of the other*, *no other choice*, *any other*, *every other*

Excludes every member of the set except the one already named. It shows up constantly in superlative claims and in advertising, where *no other* does the work of a superlative without having to prove one.

1. *No other* airline flies from here to Boise nonstop.
2. She had *no other* choice but to sell the house.
3. *No other* candidate came close in the primary.
4. There was *no other* evidence tying him to the scene.
5. *No other* product on the market does all three things.
6. We looked, but *no other* apartments in that range were available.

**See also:** [no](#no), [any other](#any-other), [every other](#every-other), [no such](#no-such), [none of](#none-of)

### no such

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *noh-SUCH* &middot; *such* keeps its full vowel /sʌtʃ/ here, unlike the unstressed *such a* [sətʃə] of the exclamative, because *no such* carries the clause's negation and the stress that goes with it
**Type:** negative determiner (anaphoric) &middot; **Position:** *no* is the **central determiner**; *such* follows it as a pre-head modifier inside the same phrase. Because *no* has already taken the one central-determiner slot English allows, there is no room left for an article: ✗ *no such a thing*, ✗ *the no such rule* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **all three noun types**, exactly like *no* — singular countable (*no such thing*, *no such person*), plural countable (*no such rules*, *no such records*), and uncountable (*no such evidence*, *no such luck*). The singular countable takes **no article**: ✓ *no such thing*, ✗ *no such a thing*.
**With *of*:** **Never** — ✗ *no such of the rules*, ✗ *no such of them*, inheriting *no*'s prohibition exactly. To reach an already-identified set, switch to [none of](#none-of) (*none of those rules*) or to the fixed phrase *nothing of the sort*.
**Pattern:** *There is no such* [singular countable noun] &middot; *no such* [plural countable / uncountable noun] &middot; *no such thing as* [noun phrase] &middot; *No such* [noun] + verb (subject position)
**Contrast:** The article rule is the whole trap. [Such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an) **requires** the article with a singular countable — *such a mess* — but *no such* **drops** it, because *no* and *a* are competing for one slot: ✓ *no such thing*, ✗ *no such a thing* (occasionally heard in speech and found in older writing, but nonstandard in edited American English). Against plain [no](#no), *such* adds **anaphora**: *no rule exists* denies rules in general, *no such rule exists* denies the particular rule just described. Against [no other](#no-other), the two point opposite ways — *no other* excludes everything **except** the one named, *no such* excludes the one named itself.
**Variants:** *no such thing*, *no such thing as*, *no such luck*, *no such place*, *such (the positive)*

Denies that anything of the kind just mentioned exists. It is **anaphoric** — it needs a description already on the table, and *such* points back at it. The verb agrees with the noun that follows, exactly as after *no*: *No such rule **exists***, *No such rules **exist***. Two of its fixed phrases have hardened into idiom: *no such thing as* introduces what is being denied, and *No such luck* is a stock reply meaning the hoped-for outcome did not arrive.

1. There is *no such* thing as a free lunch.
2. *No such* rule appears anywhere in the handbook.
3. We checked the roster twice and found *no such* person.
4. *No such* records were kept before 1974.
5. The report offered *no such* evidence.
6. I was hoping the meeting would be canceled — *no such* luck.
7. ✗ *There's no such a thing as a free lunch.* — nonstandard in American English; *no* has already filled the determiner slot, so drop the *a*.

**See also:** [any such](#any-such), [no](#no), [no other](#no-other), [no manner of / no kind of / no sort of](#no-manner-of--no-kind-of--no-sort-of), [none of](#none-of), [not any](#not-any), [such](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an)

### none of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnʌn əv/ &middot; *NUN-uv* &middot; the vowel is /ʌ/ as in *sun*, not the /oʊ/ of *no*; *of* takes its weak form /əv/ and often reduces to /ə/, giving [ˈnʌnə] before a consonant
**Type:** negative quantifier (partitive) &middot; **Position:** not a central determiner in the ordinary sense — *none* is a pronoun heading its own phrase and the *of*-phrase supplies the noun, which is exactly why ✗ *none students* fails &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** ***of*** **plus a noun phrase that is already definite, or a pronoun** — a plural countable (*none of the students*), an uncountable (*none of the money*), or a pronoun (*none of them*). **Never directly before a bare noun**: ✗ *none students*, ✗ *none money* — those are *no students* and *no money*.
**With *of*:** ***Of*** **is required.** Without it, *none* is a bare **pronoun** standing alone (*How many survived? None.*) and cannot take a noun at all.
**Pattern:** *none of* [the/these/my + plural noun] &middot; *none of* [the/this + uncountable noun] &middot; *none of* [them/us/it] &middot; *none of the above*
**Contrast:** [No](#no) and *none* split the work of one meaning: *no* attaches directly to a noun, *none* stands alone or takes *of*. ✓ *No tickets are left* / ✓ *None are left* / ✓ *None of the tickets are left* / ✗ *No of the tickets are left*. Against *neither of*, the difference is set size — *neither of* covers exactly two, *none of* covers three or more. Against *not any of*, the two are near-equivalents, but *none of* is the one that works comfortably as the subject of a sentence.
**Variants:** *none of the above*, *none other than*, *not any of*, *no*

Denies every member of an already-identified group. The verb may be **singular or plural** when the noun after *of* is plural, and both are fully standard — *None of the files **was** saved* and *None of the files **were** saved* are equally correct, with the plural far more common in American usage. The old rule that *none* must be singular because it means "not one" is a nineteenth-century invention with no basis in the history of the word. With an uncountable noun the verb is singular: *None of the money **is** missing*.

1. *None of* the students turned in the assignment.
2. *None of* the money was ever recovered.
3. I called three times and *none of* them picked up.
4. *None of* this makes any sense to me.
5. *None of* the above applies to part-time employees.
6. Honestly, *none of* your explanations add up.

**See also:** [no](#no), [neither](#neither), [not any](#not-any), [not one](#not-one), [all](#all), [every one of](#every-one-of)

### not a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɑt ə/ &middot; *NOT-uh* &middot; the /t/ flaps between vowels, giving [ˈnɑɾə]; before a vowel sound the form is *not an* /ˈnɑt ən/, and the article keeps its weak vowel /ə/ in both
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the phrase and sits in front of the central determiner *a/an* &middot; **Register:** emphatic; neutral inside fixed phrases like *not a chance*, *not a soul*
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, since *a/an* itself takes only a singular countable — *not a word*, *not an excuse*. For a plural or an uncountable the phrase is *not any* or *no*: ✗ *not a students*, ✗ *not a money*.
**With *of*:** Not directly — ✗ *not a of them*. The partitive routes are *not a single one of the students* and *not one of the students*.
**Pattern:** *Not a* [singular countable noun] + singular verb &middot; *not an* [noun beginning with a vowel sound] &middot; *There wasn't a* [noun] &middot; [subject] *didn't* [verb] *a* [noun]
**Contrast:** Against plain [no](#no), *not a* is more emphatic and more countable-flavored: *There was no chair in the room* states a fact, *There wasn't a chair in the room* insists on the count. Against [not one](#not-one) and [not a single](#not-a-single), *not a* is the mildest of the three. Note the position rule: fronted before a subject, *not a* stays intact and sounds literary (*Not a sound was heard*); after the verb, the negative normally migrates onto the auxiliary and the noun phrase keeps only the bare article (*There wasn't a sound*). Both are the same construction.
**Variants:** *not an*, *not a single*, *not a one*, *not a chance / not a soul (fixed phrases)*

Negates the indefinite article, insisting that not even one instance exists. Two positions are available and they are not equally common. Fronted, before the subject, the negative stays attached to the noun phrase. After the verb, the negative moves onto the auxiliary and the article is left behind — which is the ordinary spoken form, and still the same construction underneath.

1. *Not a* soul was out on the street at that hour.
2. *Not an* hour goes by that I don't think about it.
3. *Not a* dime of that money ever reached the charity.
4. *Not a* word of this leaves the room.
5. There wasn't *a* cloud in the sky all week. — the same construction with the negative moved onto the verb
6. He didn't say *a* word to me the entire drive home. — negative on the verb again

**See also:** [not one](#not-one), [not a single](#not-a-single), [not so much as a](#not-so-much-as-a), [not any](#not-any), [no](#no), [nary a](#nary-a), [never a](#never-a), [a](01-articles.md#a)

### not a single

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ə ˈsɪŋɡəl/ &middot; *not-uh-SING-guhl* &middot; the /t/ flaps into the weak article, giving [ˌnɑɾə]; the main stress lands on *single*, which is where the emphasis lives
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the phrase; *a* is the central determiner and *single* an emphatic adjective inside it &middot; **Register:** emphatic — never a plain statement of fact, always a point being made
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, because the phrase contains *a* — *not a single seat*, *not a single complaint*. Never a plural (✗ *not a single seats*), never an uncountable (✗ *not a single money* — say *not a single dollar*, or *no money*).
**With *of*:** Through *one* — *not a single one of the students*, *not a single one of them*. ✗ *not a single of them* is ungrammatical.
**Pattern:** *Not a single* [singular countable noun] + singular verb &middot; *There wasn't a single* [noun] &middot; *not a single one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun]
**Contrast:** This is the top of the zero-quantity scale, which runs [not any](#not-any) (plain) → [no](#no) (firmer) → [not a](#not-a) (emphatic) → [not one](#not-one) / *not a single* (most emphatic). *Not a single* and *not one* are interchangeable, with *not a single* a shade more colloquial. *Single* here is purely an intensifier — it has nothing to do with being unmarried or one-part — and it is the same intensifier that makes [every single](#every-single).
**Variants:** *not a single one of*, *not one single*, *not a solitary*, *not one*

Counts the set down to zero and dares the hearer to find an exception. The verb is **singular** even when the *of*-phrase after it is plural: *Not a single one of the machines **was** working*.

1. *Not a single* seat was left by the time we got there.
2. In four years she missed *not a single* practice.
3. *Not a single* one of the applicants had the certification.
4. *Not a single* person complained about the change.
5. He read the contract twice and found *not a single* error.
6. There wasn't *a single* light on in the whole building.

**See also:** [not one](#not-one), [not a](#not-a), [every single](#every-single), [no](#no), [none of](#none-of)

### not all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈɔl/ &middot; *not-AWL* &middot; the /t/ flaps before the vowel, giving [ˌnɑɾ ˈɔl]; the stress is on *all*
**Type:** negative determiner (partial negation) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the predeterminer *all*, and the whole phrase occupies the predeterminer slot (*not all the students*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*not all students*) and uncountable nouns (*not all information*), plus a singular countable when another determiner or a time span follows (*not all my life*, *not all day*) — the same range as *all*, since it is *all* with a negative bolted on the front.
**With *of*:** **Optional** before another determiner — *not all (of) the students*. **Required** before a pronoun — *not all of them*, ✗ *not all them*.
**Pattern:** *Not all* [plural/uncountable noun] + verb &middot; *not all* (*of*) *the* [noun] &middot; *not all of* [them/us/you] &middot; *Not all* [noun] *are* [adjective]
**Contrast:** This entry fixes the most damaging ambiguity in the file. *Not all* is **partial** negation — some yes, some no. [No](#no) and [none of](#none-of) are **total** negation — zero. *Not all the seats were taken* means some were empty; *None of the seats were taken* means every seat was empty. Keep *not* in front of *all*, because moving it onto the verb creates the ambiguity back again: *All the seats weren't taken* is read both ways and should be rewritten. The singular counterpart is [not every](#not-every).
**Variants:** *not all of*, *not quite all*, *not every*, *not everybody (pronoun)*

Denies that the whole set is covered, without denying any particular member — "some, but not the whole." The verb agrees with the noun, exactly as with *all*: *Not all the money **is** accounted for*, *Not all the checks **have** cleared*.

1. *Not all* heroes wear capes.
2. *Not all* of the applicants were interviewed.
3. *Not all* the money has been accounted for.
4. *Not all* insurance policies cover flood damage.
5. *Not all* of them agreed with the decision.
6. She works most weekends, though *not all* of them.

**See also:** [all](#all), [not every](#not-every), [no](#no), [none of](#none-of), [every](#every)

### not any

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈɛni/ &middot; *not-EN-ee* &middot; almost always contracted onto the auxiliary in speech — *didn't … any*, *isn't … any*, *haven't … any* — so the two words are rarely adjacent
**Type:** negative determiner (analytic) &middot; **Position:** *any* is the central determiner; *not* negates the verb or the clause, not the noun phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral — the ordinary spoken alternative to *no*
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*not any survivors*) and **uncountable nouns** (*not any money*); a singular countable only with the abstract nouns that take *any* at all (*not any reason to worry*, *not any chance of that*). ✗ *not any apple*.
**With *of*:** Follows *any* — *not any of the students*, *not any of them*; *of* is required before a determined phrase or a pronoun.
**Pattern:** [subject] + *didn't/doesn't/isn't/haven't* … *any* [noun] &middot; *There isn't any* [noun] &middot; *without any* [noun] &middot; *not any of* [the/my + noun]
**Contrast:** *not any* and [no](#no) mean the same thing and differ in position and force, and two rules decide which one you can use. **(1)** In **subject** position only *no* works: ✓ *No students came*, ✗ *Not any students came*. **(2)** After the verb both work, and *no* is the more emphatic and more written of the two: *We found no survivors* against *We didn't find any survivors*. The one absolute prohibition is stacking them: ✗ *I have no any money* is impossible in every variety of English — a clause gets one negative marker, and *no* and *any* are competing candidates for the job.
**Variants:** *not any of*, *hardly any*, *not … any at all*, *no*

The analytic way to negate a quantity: put the negative on the verb and let *any* carry the noun. This is the default in spoken American English, where *no* before a noun after the verb sounds formal or emphatic. The archaic pattern *I have not any money*, with *not* directly before *any* and no auxiliary, is dead in American English — modern usage requires *do*-support: *I don't have any money*.

1. We didn't find *any* survivors.
2. There's *not any* point in waiting around for him.
3. She hasn't given me *any* reason to worry.
4. There isn't *any* hot water in the building this morning.
5. He left without *any* explanation.
6. I don't want *any* of these leftovers.
7. They haven't had *any* complaints about the new system.

**See also:** [no](#no), [any](#any), [none of](#none-of), [not a](#not-a), [hardly any](05-quantifiers-small.md#hardly-any)

### not every

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈɛvri/ &middot; *not-EV-ree* &middot; the /t/ flaps before the vowel of *every*, giving [ˌnɑɾ ˈɛvri]
**Type:** negative determiner (partial negation) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the central determiner *every*, and the phrase fills the determiner slot &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *not every student*, *not every day*. Never a plural (✗ *not every students*), never an uncountable (✗ *not every water*), inheriting *every*'s restriction exactly.
**With *of*:** Never with bare *every* — ✗ *not every of them*. The partitive is *not every one of* — *not every one of them*.
**Pattern:** *Not every* [singular countable noun] + singular verb &middot; *not every one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *It is not every* [noun] *that* … (literary)
**Contrast:** *not every* is the singular partner of [not all](#not-all), and both are **partial**: *Not every student passed* and *Not all the students passed* both mean some did and some did not. Neither means *No student passed*, which is total. Word order carries the whole meaning — *Not every student passed* is unambiguous, while *Every student didn't pass* can be read either way and should be rewritten.
**Variants:** *not every one of*, *not all*, *not everyone / not everybody (pronouns)*

Denies that the coverage is complete, member by member, while leaving individual members untouched. The noun and the verb stay **singular**: *Not every window **opens***.

1. *Not every* student needs a lab section.
2. *Not every* window in the house opens.
3. *Not every* day is going to go this smoothly.
4. *Not every* one of them agreed to sign.
5. *Not every* problem has a technical fix.
6. *Not every* restaurant on that block takes reservations.

**See also:** [every](#every), [not all](#not-all), [no](#no), [each](#each), [none of](#none-of)

### not one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈwʌn/ &middot; *not-WUN* &middot; the /t/ is unreleased before /w/; the stress on *one* is what carries the emphasis
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** *not* negates the phrase headed by the numeral *one* &middot; **Register:** emphatic — used to make a point, not to state a plain fact
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *not one person*, *not one complaint*. Never a plural (✗ *not one people*), never an uncountable (✗ *not one water*).
**With *of*:** Yes, and it is common — *not one of the students*, *not one of them*. *Of* is required before a determined phrase or a pronoun.
**Pattern:** *Not one* [singular countable noun] + singular verb &middot; *not one of* [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] &middot; *not one single* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Not one* is the most emphatic of the zero-quantity phrases because it makes the hearer count. The scale runs [not any](#not-any) (plain) → [no](#no) (firmer) → [not a](#not-a) (emphatic) → *not one* / [not a single](#not-a-single) (most emphatic). Against [none of](#none-of), *not one of* insists on the counting: *none of the students came* reports, *not one of the students came* complains. The verb is **singular** even after *of* + plural: *Not one of them **was** on time*.
**Variants:** *not one of*, *not one single*, *not a single*, *not a one*

Denies a set down to its final unit, with the numeral doing the emphatic work. It appears most often in subject position, where fronting it puts the zero at the very head of the sentence.

1. *Not one* person raised a hand.
2. *Not one* of the applicants had the right certification.
3. *Not one* of them offered to help clean up.
4. In three years he has missed *not one* deadline.
5. *Not one* complaint has come in since the update.
6. She sent out forty resumes and got *not one* reply.

**See also:** [not a](#not-a), [not a single](#not-a-single), [no](#no), [none of](#none-of), [nary a](#nary-a)

### not so much as a

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt soʊ ˈmʌtʃ æz ə/ &middot; *not-soh-MUCH-az-uh* &middot; **the weak forms do most of the work here.** The /t/ of *not* flaps or goes unreleased, *so* reduces to /sə/, *as* to /əz/, and the article to /ə/, so the phrase runs together as [nɑt sə ˈmʌtʃ əzə] — and after *without*, where it lives most of the time, [wɪˈðaʊt sə ˈmʌtʃ əzə]. The single stress is on *much*
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic minimizer), negative-polarity &middot; **Position:** the negative — *not*, *without*, *never*, or another negative in the clause — licenses the minimizer *so much as*, which stands in front of the central determiner *a* &middot; **Register:** emphatic and faintly literary; entirely ordinary in speech after *without*, and used in writing when a point is being made rather than a fact reported
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, since the phrase contains *a* — *not so much as a word*, *without so much as a goodbye*. Never a plural (✗ *not so much as a words*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *not so much as a money*); to reach an uncountable you must put a unit noun in front of it — *not so much as a drop of rain*, *not so much as a scrap of evidence*, *not so much as a dime of the money*.
**With *of*:** Not directly — ✗ *not so much as a of them*. The partitive is *not so much as one of*: *not so much as one of them offered to help*.
**Pattern:** *without so much as a* [singular countable noun] &middot; [negated verb] + *so much as a* [noun] &middot; *There wasn't so much as a* [noun] &middot; *not so much as a* [unit noun] *of* [uncountable] &middot; [negative] + *so much as* + [verb] (the adverbial twin)
**Contrast:** This sits at the top of the zero-quantity scale running through this file — [not any](#not-any) (plain) → [no](#no) (firmer) → [not a](#not-a) (emphatic) → [not one](#not-one) and [not a single](#not-a-single) (most emphatic) → *not so much as a*, which adds the further claim that even the smallest imaginable instance is missing. It is a **negative-polarity item** and cannot stand on its own: a bare positive ✗ *He gave me so much as a dollar* is ungrammatical, and the phrase needs *not*, *without*, *never*, *hardly*, or a question to license it. Note the **adverbial twin**, which takes a verb where this takes a noun and is just as common: *He didn't so much as look at me*. And keep it clear of the connective *not so much … as* (*not stupid so much as careless*), which is a comparison and negates nothing at all.
**Variants:** *without so much as a*, *not even a*, *not so much as one*, *not so much as* + verb (adverbial), *nary a*, *not a single*

Denies a set down below its smallest member — "not even this much." *So much as* is a **minimizer**: it names the least the speaker can picture and then denies that too, which is why the construction almost always carries a grievance. Its commonest home by far is after *without*, where it reports a slight: *she left without so much as a goodbye*. The verb stays **singular**, agreeing with *a*: *Not so much as a light **was** on in the building*.

1. She walked out without *so much as a* goodbye.
2. Three months of drought, and *not so much as a* drop of rain.
3. He read the whole contract and didn't find *so much as a* typo.
4. They took the job and never sent *so much as a* thank-you note.
5. There wasn't *so much as a* chair left in the apartment.
6. He didn't *so much as* look up when I came in. — the adverbial twin, with a verb instead of a noun
7. ✗ *He gave me so much as a dollar.* — ungrammatical on its own; the phrase needs *not*, *without*, or *never* to license it.

**See also:** [not a](#not-a), [not a single](#not-a-single), [not one](#not-one), [no](#no), [nary a](#nary-a), [not any](#not-any), [a shred of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-shred-of)

## Dialectal, archaic, and nonstandard

A learner will meet all five of these constantly in speech, in song lyrics, in fiction, and in older writing, and needs to recognize them and know where they belong. Two of them — *ain't no* and concord *no* — are the same phenomenon seen from two angles, and both are stigmatized in edited writing. The other three carry no stigma at all; they are costumes. *Nary a* and its positive twin *ary a* are Appalachian and rural Southern dialect used on purpose, and *never a* — the ancestor of both, by way of the contracted *ne'er a* — is Early Modern English surviving in a handful of frozen phrases. None of those three doubles its negative, which is precisely what separates them from the two that do.

### ain't no

**Pronunciation:** /ˌeɪnt ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *aynt-NOH* &middot; the /t/ is usually unreleased before /n/, giving [eɪnˈnoʊ]
**Type:** negative determiner inside a negative-concord frame &middot; **Position:** *no* is the central determiner; *ain't* is the negated verb &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard in edited American English** — systematic and rule-governed in African American English, in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, and in casual speech everywhere, but out of place in academic writing, business correspondence, and any formal register
**Goes with:** whatever *no* goes with — singular countable (*ain't no way*), plural countable (*ain't no jobs*), and uncountable (*ain't no money*). The restriction on this phrase is social, not grammatical.
**With *of*:** Not directly; the partitive is *ain't none of*, which doubles the concord again (*There ain't none of them left*).
**Pattern:** *There ain't no* [noun] &middot; [subject] *ain't got no* [noun] &middot; *Ain't no* [noun] + verb
**Contrast:** This is **negative concord**: the negative is marked twice, once on the verb (*ain't*) and once on the determiner (*no*). Standard American English marks it once and fills the second slot with *any* — *There isn't any way*, *I don't have any money*. The dialectal versions are neither confused nor illogical; they follow a consistent rule that many languages, including Spanish and French, use as their standard. What makes them nonstandard is social convention, not logic. Standard equivalents: *ain't no way* → *isn't any way* / *is no way*; *ain't got no money* → *don't have any money* / *have no money*.
**Variants:** *ain't got no*, *ain't none of*, *ain't nothing / ain't nobody (pronoun forms)*

The most visible form of negative concord in American English, fixed enough in songs, titles, and catchphrases (*Ain't No Sunshine*, *ain't no thing*) that it functions almost as an idiom. Recognize it, understand the grammar behind it, and keep it out of your own writing unless you are quoting or writing dialogue.

1. There *ain't no* way I'm driving in this weather. — nonstandard; standard: *There isn't any way I'm driving in this weather.*
2. He *ain't* got *no* patience for that kind of thing. — nonstandard; standard: *He has no patience for that kind of thing.*
3. *Ain't no* point in arguing about it now. — nonstandard; standard: *There's no point in arguing about it now.*
4. There *ain't no* jobs left in that town. — nonstandard; standard: *There aren't any jobs left in that town.*
5. She *ain't* got *no* time for excuses. — nonstandard; standard: *She has no time for excuses.*
6. *Ain't no* mountain high enough to keep me from you. — the song title, and a fair sample of the idiom in the wild

**See also:** [no (negative concord)](#no-negative-concord), [no](#no), [not any](#not-any), [any](#any), [nary a](#nary-a)

### ary / ary a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛri/ &middot; *AIR-ee* &middot; with the article, *ary a* /ˈɛri ə/ &middot; *AIR-ee-uh* &middot; the vowel is the one in *hair*, matching [nary a](#nary-a) exactly; some speakers say /ˈæri/ (*ARR-ee*). The article keeps its **weak** /ə/, and in fast speech the whole phrase reduces to [ˈɛrə] or further to *arn* [ɑrn]. The word is a worn-down *e'er a*, "ever a" — the positive counterpart of *ne'er a*, "never a," which gave *nary*
**Type:** quantifier (free choice and negative-polarity), dialectal &middot; **Position:** with the article, *ary* stands in front of the central determiner *a* (*ary a match*); the older bare form *ary* fills the central slot itself (*ary man*) &middot; **Register:** **dialectal — Appalachian, Ozark, and rural Southern speech**, and now largely a marker of older or rural speakers. An American meets it in Appalachian fiction and oral history, in old-time and bluegrass lyrics, and from relatives in the mountain South. It belongs to no written standard, and outside dialogue a writer using it will be read as imitating an accent
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, since the usual form contains *a* — *ary a match*, *ary a soul*, *ary one*. Never a plural (✗ *ary a matches*), never an uncountable (✗ *ary a money*). The bare form takes a singular countable too: *ary man*, *ary bit*.
**With *of*:** Not directly — ✗ *ary a of them*. The idiom is *ary a one*: *ary a one of them*.
**Pattern:** *Have you got ary a* [singular countable noun]? &middot; [negated verb] + *ary a* [noun] &middot; *if ary* [noun] &middot; *ary a one* (*of* …) &middot; *ary* [noun] (bare, the oldest form)
**Contrast:** *ary* is the **positive** member of a pair and [nary a](#nary-a) the negative one: *ary a* means "any," *nary a* means "not a single." *Do you have ary a match?* asks; *I've got nary a match* denies. Standard English fills the same two slots with [any](#any) and [not a](#not-a), and the mapping is exact — *ary a* → *any*, *nary a* → *not a*. The two words have had very different careers: *nary a* escaped the mountains and became a national literary flourish that costs a writer nothing, while *ary* stayed home and is still heard as regional speech rather than as style. Note that it is **not** negative concord — *ary a* takes one negative per clause like any standard form, and in that it is unlike [ain't no](#aint-no).
**Variants:** *ary a one*, *arn / arn a (further reduced)*, *e'er a (the source phrase)*, *nary a (the negative twin)*, *any*

A dialectal free-choice determiner meaning "any, a single," descended from *e'er a* — "ever a." It survives almost entirely in questions, negatives, and conditionals, which is exactly the polarity profile of standard *any*, and it clusters around the same short list of nouns as *nary a*: *one*, *soul*, *word*, *bit*, *match*, *dime*. Learn to read it; do not write it outside dialogue or quotation.

1. Have you got *ary a* match on you? — a question, where the word lives
2. I asked around, but there wasn't *ary a* soul who'd seen him. — a negative
3. If *ary* one of you knows better, speak up now. — a conditional, and the bare form
4. He never had *ary a* dime to his name. — dialect speech; standard: *He never had a dime to his name.*
5. "Did *ary a* letter come today?" she asked from the porch. — the phrase as a novelist sets it down
6. Not *ary* bit of that story is true. — the bare form, now the rarest of the three
7. Standard American English says *any* in every one of these sentences. — the whole mapping in one line

**See also:** [nary a](#nary-a), [never a](#never-a), [any](#any), [not a](#not-a), [ain't no](#aint-no), [not any](#not-any)

### nary a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛri ə/ &middot; *NAIR-ee-uh* &middot; a worn-down *ne'er a*, "never a"; the vowel is the one in *hair*, not the one in *nasty*, and the article keeps its weak /ə/
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** *nary* negates the phrase headed by the central determiner *a* &middot; **Register:** **dialectal and literary** — Appalachian and rural Southern in origin, now used nationally as a deliberately folksy or old-fashioned flourish; never neutral, but not stigmatized either
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, since the phrase contains *a* — *nary a soul*, *nary a word*. Never a plural (✗ *nary a words*), never an uncountable (✗ *nary a money*).
**With *of*:** Not used — ✗ *nary a of them*. The idiom is *nary a one*: *nary a one of them showed up*.
**Pattern:** *nary a* [singular countable noun] &middot; *nary a one* (*of* …) &middot; *There was nary a* [noun] *in sight*
**Contrast:** It is a stylistic variant of [not a](#not-a) and [not one](#not-one), filling the same grammatical slot but carrying a strong regional and archaic flavor. Where *not a soul was there* is plain English, *nary a soul was there* is a costume. Unlike the negative-concord forms in this section, *nary a* is not heard as an error — it is dialect deployed on purpose, and it takes only one negative per clause like any standard form.
**Variants:** *nary a one*, *ne'er a (the source phrase)*, *not a*, *not one*

An old-fashioned emphatic negative meaning "not a single one." It survives in fiction, folk speech, and journalism reaching for color, and it almost always precedes a small set of nouns: *soul*, *word*, *sound*, *drop*, *penny*, *one*.

1. There was *nary a* soul on the road at that hour.
2. He worked all winter and got *nary a* word of thanks.
3. Three months of drought and *nary a* drop of rain.
4. She asked all six of them and *nary a* one would answer.
5. The old barn stood there with *nary a* nail out of place.
6. We waited an hour, and *nary a* bus came by.

**See also:** [not a](#not-a), [not one](#not-one), [not a single](#not-a-single), [no](#no), [ain't no](#aint-no), [ary / ary a](#ary--ary-a), [never a](#never-a)

### never a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛvɚ ə/ &middot; *NEV-ur-uh* &middot; the article keeps its **weak** /ə/ throughout, and *never* itself has no weak form, since it carries the negation. In fast and older speech the medial /v/ drops and the two words run to [ˈnɛɚ ə] or [ˈnɛrə] — which is the exact road that produced the written contraction ***ne'er a*** /nɛr ə/ &middot; *NAIR-uh*, and one step further, dialectal [nary a](#nary-a) /ˈnɛri ə/. The three spellings are one word at three stages of erosion
**Type:** negative determiner (emphatic), archaic and fossilized &middot; **Position:** *never* negates the phrase headed by the central determiner *a* &middot; **Register:** **archaic as a productive determiner; alive only inside fixed phrases.** An American meets the old determiner use in Shakespeare and his contemporaries and in older poetry, and nowhere in modern prose. What survives in ordinary speech is the frozen *never a dull moment* and the deliberately old-fashioned idiom *never a one*
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, since the phrase contains *a* — *never a word*, *never a dull moment*, *never a one*. Never a plural (✗ *never a words*), never an uncountable (✗ *never a money*). Do not mistake the live modern pattern for this one: in *He never wrote a word*, *never* is an ordinary **adverb** on the verb and *a* is just the article, and that pattern takes any noun the verb will allow.
**With *of*:** Not directly — ✗ *never a of them*. The idiom is *never a one*: *never a one of them answered*.
**Pattern:** *never a* [singular countable noun] (the archaic determiner) &middot; *ne'er a* [singular countable noun] (the same determiner in its contracted spelling, the form verse uses to save a syllable) &middot; *Never a dull moment* (frozen) &middot; *never a one* (*of* …) &middot; *never a whit* (archaic) &middot; [subject] + *never* + [verb] + *a* [noun] (the ordinary modern pattern, a different construction)
**Contrast:** The entry exists to separate two things that look identical on the page. **(1)** In Early Modern English *never a* was a genuine determiner meaning "not a single, no" — *never a man in the company would speak* meant "not one man." **(2)** In modern American English *never* is an **adverb of frequency** negating the verb, and *a* is simply the article: *She never sent a thank-you note*. The determiner is gone; the fossils are what is left of it. Against [not a](#not-a), which fills the same slot and is fully alive, *never a* adds "not even once, ever" — time rather than count. Against [nary a](#nary-a), the relation is genealogical rather than stylistic: *never a* → *ne'er a* → *nary a*, one worn-down step at a time, and of the three only *nary a* is still productive, in dialect. Its positive counterpart, by the same derivation, is [ary / ary a](#ary--ary-a), from *e'er a*.
**Variants:** *ne'er a (the contracted spelling)*, *never a one*, *never a dull moment*, *never a whit (archaic)*, *nary a (the dialectal descendant)*, *not a*

An archaic emphatic negative meaning "not a single one." Its life as a productive determiner ended centuries ago, and it survives in two places: the stock phrase *never a dull moment*, which every American says and nobody parses, and the idiom *never a one*, which reads as costume. Written ***ne'er a***, it is the form poets used to save a syllable, and it is the form from which dialectal *nary a* was worn down — so *never a*, *ne'er a*, and *nary a* are not three words but one word photographed at three ages. The verb is **singular**: *Never a word **was** spoken*.

1. *Never a* dull moment around here. — the one fully live use, frozen solid
2. She asked all six of them and *never a* one would answer. — the surviving idiom, deliberately old-fashioned
3. *Never a* man in the company would speak against him. — the Early Modern determiner, meaning "not one man"; this is the use you meet in Shakespeare and nowhere in modern prose
4. He worked ten years there and got *ne'er a* word of thanks. — the contracted spelling, as verse and older prose set it down
5. In three months of drought there was *never a* drop of rain. — the phrase reached for as period flavor
6. She never sent a thank-you note. — the ordinary modern pattern: *never* is an adverb on the verb and *a* is just the article
7. Modern American writing says *not a word of thanks*, dialect says *nary a word of thanks*, and *never a word of thanks* is the antique. — the three registers in one line

**See also:** [nary a](#nary-a), [ary / ary a](#ary--ary-a), [not a](#not-a), [not one](#not-one), [not a single](#not-a-single), [no](#no)

### no (negative concord)

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ/ &middot; *NOH* &middot; no weak form — the concord *no* keeps the same full diphthong and the same stress as standard *no*, which is what makes it audible
**Type:** negative determiner inside a negative-concord frame &middot; **Position:** central determiner, in a clause whose verb is already negated &middot; **Register:** **nonstandard in edited American English** — fully systematic in African American English, Southern and Appalachian vernacular, Chicano English, and casual speech across the country, but read as an error in academic, professional, and published prose
**Goes with:** the same three noun types as standard *no* — singular countable (*I don't have no car*), plural countable (*He didn't make no promises*), uncountable (*We don't have no time*). Nothing about the noun blocks it; the objection is entirely social.
**With *of*:** The partitive doubles the concord too — *I don't want none of that*, where standard English has *I don't want any of that*.
**Pattern:** [subject] + *don't/doesn't/didn't/won't* + [verb] + *no* [noun] &middot; *There isn't/ain't no* [noun] &middot; [subject] + *never* + [verb] + *no* [noun]
**Contrast:** Standard American English marks negation **once** per clause and fills the second slot with [any](#any): *I don't have any money*, not *I don't have no money*. Keep two facts apart. **(1)** *Don't … no* is **negative concord** — nonstandard but perfectly grammatical inside its own dialects, and it does not cancel into a positive. **(2)** ✗ *I have no any money* is **impossible in every variety**, standard or vernacular, because *no* and *any* are two candidates for one job; no English speaker anywhere produces it. Note also that standard English does have a genuine cancelling double negative, carried by contrastive stress: *I don't have NO money — I just don't have much* means the speaker has some.
**Variants:** *ain't no*, *don't … no*, *never … no*, *not … none*, *didn't … nothing*

**Negative concord**, popularly the "double negative": the single negative meaning of the clause is spelled out twice, once on the verb and once on the determiner. It does not reverse the meaning — *I don't have no money* means the speaker has none, exactly as *I don't have any money* does. English used concord as its standard through Chaucer's day, and Spanish, French, Russian, and Portuguese still do; it became stigmatized in English only after eighteenth-century grammarians applied an argument from arithmetic to it. Learn to hear it and understand it; do not write it outside dialogue or quotation.

1. I *don't* have *no* money. — nonstandard; standard: *I don't have any money.*
2. He *didn't* make *no* promises. — nonstandard; standard: *He didn't make any promises.*
3. We *ain't* got *no* room for that. — nonstandard; standard: *We don't have any room for that.*
4. She *never* gave me *no* trouble. — nonstandard; standard: *She never gave me any trouble.*
5. There *wasn't no* reason to call. — nonstandard; standard: *There wasn't any reason to call.*
6. ✗ *I have no any money.* — impossible in every variety of English; say *I have no money* or *I don't have any money*.
