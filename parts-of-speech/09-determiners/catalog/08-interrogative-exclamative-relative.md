# Interrogative, exclamative, and relative determiners

> The *wh-* determiners: the words that ask which noun you mean, exclaim about it, or fasten a whole clause onto it.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Almost every word in this file begins with *wh-*, and that is no accident: they descend from a single Old English question stem and they all still do the same underlying job — they **point at a noun without naming it**. What separates them is the sentence they land in. In a question the *wh-* determiner asks the hearer to identify the noun (*which gate?*); in an exclamation it registers a reaction to it (*what a gate!*); in a relative clause it hooks a clause onto a noun the sentence already has (*the gate whose sign was missing*).

Two traps run the length of the file. The first is **determiner or pronoun**. A determiner has a noun immediately after it — *which flight*, *whose coat*, *how many boxes*. A pronoun stands alone — *which is yours?*, *whose is that?*, *how many were left?* The reliable test is ***of***: the moment you add it, the *wh-* word has become a pronoun, and the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner. *Which books* is a determiner; *which of the books* is a pronoun. That one test settles the matter for *which*, *what*, *whatever*, *whichever*, *how much*, and *how many* alike. *Whose* is the single exception, and only because it has no *of*-partitive at all (✗ *whose of these is yours?*); for *whose* the noun test alone does the work.

The second trap is the **article rule in exclamations**. *What* and *such* are predeterminers, so they stand in front of *a/an*: *what a long day*, *such a long day*. *How* is not a determiner at all — it is a degree adverb that takes an adjective, and it leaves the article exactly where it always was: *how long the day was*. Nearly every exclamative error in English is some scramble of those two patterns.

One pronunciation note covers the whole file. Most Americans have the **wine–whine merger**, so *what*, *which*, and *whatever* begin with a plain /w/ and *which* is a homophone of *witch*; older speakers and many Southern speakers keep an /hw-/ onset. *Whose* and *whosever* are different — their *w* has been silent for centuries and they begin with /h/. And because determiners are almost never accented, the weak forms are the ones you will actually hear: *what* as /wət/, *of* as /əv/ or /ə/, and the *t* of *what* flapped into any following vowel, so *what a mess* comes out [wʌɾəˈmɛs].

## Interrogative determiners

These stand at the front of a question and ask the hearer to identify the noun or measure it. All of them front the **entire noun phrase**, not just the *wh-* word: *Which gate does it leave from?*, never *Gate which does it leave from?* In an indirect question the same phrase fronts inside the subordinate clause, but the clause keeps **statement word order**: *I asked which gate it left from*, not *I asked which gate did it leave from*. That single rule accounts for more errors than every countability rule in this file combined.

### how big a, how good a, how much of a

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˈbɪɡ ə/, /ˌhaʊ ˈɡʊd ə/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv ə/ &middot; *how-BIG-uh, how-GOOD-uh, how-MUCH-uhv-uh* &middot; the article is **always** the weak /ə/ here and never surfaces as /eɪ/; *of* reduces to /əv/ and in fast speech to a single syllabic [ə], so *how much of a problem* runs to [haʊ ˈmʌtʃ əvə ˈprɑbləm]. *How* itself has no weak form
**Русский:** насколько большой / хороший / серьёзный — вопрос о степени; прилагательное встаёт перед a/an
**Type:** interrogative (degree), and exclamative in the uninverted version &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** — the degree phrase stands in front of *a/an*, which then does its ordinary job on the noun; *how much of a* is the *of*-partitive member of the same slot &middot; **Register:** neutral in both speech and writing; *how much of a* is the more colloquial of the two frames
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, because the whole construction is built around *a/an* — *how big a house*, *how good a teacher*, *how much of a problem*. Never a plural (✗ *how big a houses*, ✗ *how big houses*) and never an uncountable (✗ *how heavy a traffic*); with those you drop back to the ordinary quantifiers, which take no article at all — *how many houses*, *how much traffic*. *How much of a* additionally wants a noun that can be **graded**: ✓ *how much of a risk*, ✓ *how much of a difference*, ✗ *how much of a chair*.
**With *of*:** *How big a* and *how good a* take **no** *of* in edited writing: ✗ *how big of a house*. That said, *how big of a* is thoroughly established in spoken American English — Midland, Western, and increasingly general — and it is a real spoken pattern rather than a slip of logic; editors still delete it. *How much of a* is the one member of the trio where *of* is **obligatory** and standard: ✗ *how much a problem is it?*
**Pattern:** *How* [adjective] *a/an* [singular countable noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much of a* [singular countable noun] [verb]? &middot; *How* [adjective] *a/an* [noun] [subject] [verb]! (exclamative, no inversion) &middot; [clause] *how* [adjective] *a/an* [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; spoken only: *how* [adjective] *of a* [noun]
**Variants:** *how big of a, how good of a (spoken American, nonstandard in edited writing)*, *that big a (informal)*, *so/too/as + adjective + a (the statement partners)*
**Contrast:** this is the one construction in English where **the adjective jumps in front of the article**: ordinary *a big house* inverts to *how big a house*, never ✗ *a how big house*. The statement partners are [so, too, as](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#so-too-as) — *too big a risk*, *as good a time as any* — and *how* is simply the question-and-exclamation member of that family. Against [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), the difference is whether a noun is present: *How big the house is!* has only an adjective and leaves the article behind, while *How big a house it is!* brings the noun along in front of it. Against [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an), where the *wh-* word takes the article directly and the adjective stays put: ✓ *What a big house!*, ✗ *What big a house!* And *how big a* against *how much of a* is a difference of scale type — *how big a problem* measures the problem, *how much of a problem* asks whether it counts as one at all.

Asks — or exclaims — how far along a scale a single countable thing sits: what size, what quality, or what degree of a thing it really is.

1. *How big a* house do you actually need for three people?
2. *How good a* lawyer do you have to be to argue that in front of a jury?
3. *How much of a* problem is the noise going to be at night?
4. I hadn't realized *how serious a* mistake the second signature was.
5. *How much of a* difference does the premium gas really make?
6. *How long a* drive is it from the airport to the hotel?
7. She asked *how big a* deposit the landlord was going to want.

**See also:** [so, too, as](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#so-too-as), [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an), [how much](#how-much), [a](01-articles.md#a)

### how few

**Pronunciation:** /haʊ ˈfju/ &middot; *how-FYOO* &middot; the stress falls on *few*, because the point of the phrase is the smallness
**Русский:** как мало (штук) — о малом числе счётных; чаще восклицание, чем настоящий вопрос
**Type:** interrogative (usually exclamative in force) &middot; **Position:** central — the two words together fill one central-determiner slot &middot; **Register:** neutral; more often rhetorical than a genuine request for a number
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *how few voters*, *how few options*. Never an uncountable (✗ *how few money* → *how little money*) and never a singular countable (✗ *how few voter*).
**With *of*:** Required before another determiner or a pronoun — *how few of the applicants*, *how few of them*. In that construction *how few* is a **pronoun**, not a determiner, and the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner. Never before a bare plural: ✗ *how few of applicants*.
**Pattern:** *how few* [plural countable] &middot; *how few of* [the/these/my + plural noun] &middot; *how few of* [them/us] &middot; *How few* [plural countable] [verb]! (exclamative) &middot; [clause] *how few* [plural countable] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order)
**Contrast:** *how few* is to plural countables what *how little* is to uncountables, and it is the loaded twin of *how many*. *How many students showed up?* is neutral and simply asks for a number; *How few students showed up!* presupposes that the number was small and usually registers dismay. The negative loading comes from bare *few*, which means 'not many' — and because the loading is the whole point, there is no ✗ *how a few*.

Asks — or, far more often, exclaims — about a small number, treating the smallness as the point.

1. *How few* people actually read the terms is the frightening part.
2. *How few* votes does it take to block the motion?
3. You would be amazed *how few* restaurants downtown open on Mondays.
4. *How few* of these applicants have any lab experience?
5. It is shocking *how few* students can name their own senator.
6. *How few* hours can I sleep and still function at work?

**See also:** [how little](#how-little), [how many](#how-many), [few](05-quantifiers-small.md#few), [fewer](05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer)

### how little

**Pronunciation:** /haʊ ˈlɪtəl/ &middot; *how-LIT-ul* &middot; the *t* is a flap in General American, so it comes out as *how-LID-ul*
**Русский:** как мало (о неисчисляемом) — о малом объёме; малость подразумевается заранее
**Type:** interrogative (usually exclamative in force) &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *how little time*, *how little sleep*, *how little evidence*. Never a plural countable (✗ *how little friends* → *how few friends*). It also runs as a **degree adverb** with no noun at all (*how little it matters*), and that use is not a determiner.
**With *of*:** Required before another determiner or a pronoun — *how little of the budget*, *how little of it*. *How little* is a pronoun there. Bare *how little* + bare uncountable takes no *of*.
**Pattern:** *how little* [uncountable] &middot; *how little of* [the/this/my + uncountable] &middot; *How little* [uncountable] [subject] [verb]! &middot; *how little* [verb] (degree adverb, no noun)
**Contrast:** the uncountable partner of *how few*, and the loaded twin of *how much*. The noun test separates the determiner from the adverb: *how little sleep do you need* has a noun after it and is a determiner; *how little he cares* has none and is an adverb of degree. As with *how few*, the bare negative-loading form is the only one available: ✗ *how a little*.

Asks or exclaims about a small amount, with the smallness taken for granted.

1. *How little* sleep can you get by on?
2. It is amazing *how little* money they spent on the whole trip.
3. *How little* notice do we actually have to give?
4. I had not realized *how little* time was left.
5. *How little* of the budget is left for marketing?
6. *How little* effort would it take just to answer the email?

**See also:** [how few](#how-few), [how much](#how-much), [little](05-quantifiers-small.md#little), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### how many

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhaʊ ˌmɛni/ &middot; *HOW-men-ee* &middot; *how* carries the accent when a noun follows, and the sentence nucleus lands on the noun: *HOW many PEOPLE came?*
**Русский:** сколько (штук) — вопрос о числе исчисляемых предметов
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *how many people*, *how many times*. Never an uncountable (✗ *how many money* → *how much money*), never a singular countable (✗ *how many person*). A measure word makes an uncountable countable and brings *how many* back: *how many gallons of gas*, *how many pieces of advice*.
**With *of*:** Required before another determiner or a pronoun — *how many of the seats*, *how many of them*. Never ✗ *how many them* and never ✗ *how many of seats*. In the *of*-construction *how many* is a pronoun.
**Pattern:** *How many* [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How many* [plural noun] [verb]? (subject question) &middot; *How many of* [the/these/my + plural noun]? &middot; [clause] *how many* [plural noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *How many?* (pronoun, noun understood)
**Contrast:** *how many* is to plural countables what *how much* is to uncountables — the single most common countability error learners make. Against *how few*, *how many* is neutral and expects a number of any size. Note the answer types as well: *how many* is answered with a number, *how much* with an amount or a price. In a subject question the verb agrees with the plural noun, not with *how many*: ✓ *How many people **are** coming?*, ✗ *How many people is coming?*

Asks for the number of a countable set.

1. *How many* people are coming to dinner?
2. I don't know *how many* times I've explained this.
3. *How many* of these boxes can I recycle?
4. *How many* hours a week do you actually work?
5. She asked *how many* copies we needed.
6. *How many* pieces of ID does the DMV want?

**See also:** [how much](#how-much), [how few](#how-few), [how many of, how much of](#how-many-of-how-much-of), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### how many fewer, how much less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˌmɛni ˈfjuɚ/, /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *how-men-ee-FYOO-er, how-much-LESS* &middot; *fewer* is two syllables, /ˈfju.ɚ/, not *fyoor*
**Русский:** на сколько меньше — вопрос о величине убыли: fewer со счётными, less с неисчисляемыми
**Type:** interrogative (comparative) &middot; **Position:** *how many* / *how much* fills the central slot; *fewer* and *less* are postdeterminers inside the phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral; common in analytical, business, and journalistic speech
**Goes with:** *how many fewer* + **plural countable** (*how many fewer flights*); *how much less* + **uncountable** (*how much less sugar*). The split is the ordinary *fewer/less* split, so ✗ *how much less flights* and ✗ *how many fewer sugar*. *How much less* also runs as a degree adverb with no noun (*how much less does it cost?*), which is not the determiner use.
**With *of*:** Required before another determiner or a pronoun — *how much less of the budget*, *how many fewer of them*. Pronoun there, not determiner.
**Pattern:** *How many fewer* [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much less* [uncountable] …? &middot; *How much less* [adjective/adverb]? (degree) &middot; … *than* [comparison]
**Variants:** *how many less (widespread in speech, nonstandard in edited writing)*
**Contrast:** the downward mirror of *how many more* and *how much more*. *Fewer* counts, *less* measures. In edited American English *less* with a plural countable (*how many less calories*, *less calories*) is a usage error — though it is extremely common in speech and is fully standard in the fixed expressions *less than five miles* and *one less thing*, where the plural is measured rather than counted.

Asks for the size of a decrease — how much smaller the number or the amount will be.

1. *How many fewer* flights will the airline run this winter?
2. *How much less* sugar does the new recipe use?
3. I want to know *how many fewer* people showed up than last year.
4. *How much less* of the budget goes to overhead now?
5. *How many fewer* hours would you have to work?
6. *How much less* rent are you paying in the new place?

**See also:** [how many more](#how-many-more), [how much more](#how-much-more), [fewer](05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### how many more

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˌmɛni ˈmɔɹ/ &middot; *how-men-ee-MOR* &middot; in an impatient question the accent jumps to *more*: *how many MORE times?*
**Русский:** сколько ещё (штук) — вопрос о числе сверх уже имеющегося; часто с досадой
**Type:** interrogative (comparative) &middot; **Position:** *how many* fills the central slot; *more* is a postdeterminer between it and the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral, frequently impatient or rhetorical
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *how many more chairs*, *how many more times*. Never an uncountable (✗ *how many more water* → *how much more water*).
**With *of*:** Partitive *how many more of the / of them* is possible and is **required** before a pronoun; *how many more* is a pronoun there. Never before a bare plural: ✗ *how many more of chairs*.
**Pattern:** *How many more* [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How many more* [plural noun] *are there*? &middot; *How many more of* [them/these]? &middot; [clause] *how many more* [plural noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded) &middot; *How many more?* (pronoun)
**Contrast:** pairs with *how much more* exactly as *many* pairs with *much*. Keep the determiner apart from the pronoun: a noun follows the determiner (*how many more chairs*), nothing follows the pronoun (*how many more do we need?*). Word order inside the phrase is fixed: ✓ *how many more chairs*, ✗ *how more many chairs*.

Asks for the number still to come, or the number in addition to a quantity already counted.

1. *How many more* chairs do we need for the reception?
2. *How many more* times do I have to say it?
3. She wanted to know *how many more* applications were still unread.
4. *How many more* days until the deadline?
5. *How many more* of these do you have in the back?
6. *How many more* people can the elevator hold?

**See also:** [how much more](#how-much-more), [how many](#how-many), [how many fewer, how much less](#how-many-fewer-how-much-less), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more)

### how many of, how much of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni əv/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv/ &middot; *how-MEN-ee-uhv, how-MUCH-uhv* &middot; *of* is nearly always the weak /əv/, and in fast speech reduces to /ə/ — *how many of them* [ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni ə ðəm]
**Русский:** сколько из, какая часть из — партитив; это уже местоимение, а не определитель
**Type:** interrogative (partitive — a **pronoun** construction, not a determiner) &middot; **Position:** not a determiner slot at all: *how many* / *how much* heads its own noun phrase and the *of*-phrase completes it, so the following noun keeps its own determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** *how many of* + a **definite plural** (*of the emails*, *of these people*, *of them*); *how much of* + a **definite uncountable** or a whole singular thing (*of the money*, *of this pie*, *of it*). The complement of *of* must already be determined: ✓ *how many of the emails*, ✗ *how many of emails*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is the construction itself and is obligatory. Drop it and you are back to the determiner: *how many emails*, not ✗ *how many the emails*.
**Pattern:** *How many of* [the/these/my + plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much of* [the/this/my + uncountable] …? &middot; *How many of* [them/us/you]? &middot; *How much of* [it/that]? &middot; embedded: [clause] *how many of* [noun phrase] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** this is the ***of* test** that runs through the whole file, in its clearest form. *How many emails did you read* is a **determiner** — the noun follows directly. *How many of the emails did you read* is a **pronoun** — an *of*-phrase follows and the noun inside it keeps its own determiner. The same split separates *which* from *which of*, and *what* from *what of*. Verb agreement follows the noun in the *of*-phrase: *How many of the files **were** deleted?*

Asks what part or what proportion of an already identified set or mass is involved.

1. *How many of* the emails did you actually read?
2. *How much of* the budget is already committed?
3. *How many of* them showed up on time?
4. *How much of* this is he paying for himself?
5. I wonder *how many of* those reviews are real.
6. *How much of* the movie did you sleep through?

**See also:** [how many](#how-many), [how much](#how-much), [which of](#which-of), [what of](#what-of)

### how much

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhaʊ ˌmʌtʃ/ &middot; *HOW-much* &middot; *how* takes the accent when a noun follows; standing alone as a price question the phrase is usually *how MUCH?*
**Русский:** сколько (о неисчисляемом), почём — вопрос об объёме, количестве или цене
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *how much money*, *how much time*, *how much sugar*. Never a plural countable (✗ *how much people* → *how many people*) and never a singular countable (✗ *how much chair*). With no noun at all it is a pronoun or an adverb asking about price or degree (*How much is it?*, *How much do you care?*), not a determiner.
**With *of*:** Required before another determiner or a pronoun — *how much of the money*, *how much of it*. Bare *how much* + bare uncountable takes no *of*: ✗ *how much of money*.
**Pattern:** *How much* [uncountable] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much* [uncountable] [verb]? (subject question) &middot; *How much of* [the/this/my + uncountable]? &middot; [clause] *how much* [uncountable] [subject] [verb] (embedded) &middot; *How much?* (pronoun)
**Contrast:** *how much* is to uncountables what *how many* is to plural countables. Note also that *how much* is the **price** question in English — *How much is that jacket?* — where many other languages use their word for *what*; ✗ *What costs the jacket?* is a direct-translation error that never sounds native. In a subject question the verb is singular, matching the uncountable noun: ✓ *How much money **is** left?*, ✗ *How much money are left?*

Asks for the amount, quantity, or price of something that is not counted in units.

1. *How much* money do you have on you?
2. *How much* time do we have before the train?
3. She asked *how much* experience I had with Python.
4. *How much* salt does the recipe call for?
5. *How much* rent are you paying now?
6. *How much* of the report did you finish?
7. *How much* is that jacket in the window?

**See also:** [how many](#how-many), [how little](#how-little), [how many of, how much of](#how-many-of-how-much-of), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much)

### how much more

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈmɔɹ/ &middot; *how-much-MOR*
**Русский:** сколько ещё (о неисчисляемом) — вопрос о добавке; часто риторически, с раздражением
**Type:** interrogative (comparative) &middot; **Position:** *how much* fills the central slot; *more* is a postdeterminer between it and the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral, often impatient or rhetorical
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *how much more time*, *how much more evidence*. Never a plural countable (✗ *how much more people* → *how many more people*). It is also common with no noun at all, as a degree adverb (*how much more expensive is it?*) or an endurance question (*how much more can I take?*) — neither of those is the determiner use.
**With *of*:** Partitive *how much more of the / of this / of it* is possible and is **required** before a pronoun, where *how much more* is a pronoun.
**Pattern:** *How much more* [uncountable] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *How much more of* [this/it]? &middot; *How much more* [adjective]? (degree — *how much more expensive*) &middot; *How much more* [verb]? &middot; embedded: [clause] *how much more* [uncountable] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** pairs with *how many more* as *much* pairs with *many*. Do not confuse the determiner with the degree adverb: *how much more expensive is it?* modifies an adjective and is an adverb; *how much more money do you need?* has a noun after it and is a determiner. The rhetorical use — *how much more evidence do you want?* — expects no answer and means 'that is already enough.'

Asks for the additional amount — what remains to be added, endured, or paid.

1. *How much more* time do you need?
2. *How much more* evidence does the jury want?
3. *How much more* money is this going to cost me?
4. I don't know *how much more* patience I have for this.
5. *How much more* work is left on the kitchen?
6. *How much more* of this do I have to sit through?

**See also:** [how many more](#how-many-more), [how much](#how-much), [how many fewer, how much less](#how-many-fewer-how-much-less), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more)

### what (interrogative)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/, /wɑt/ &middot; *WUHT, WAHT* &middot; weak form /wət/ when unstressed; the final /t/ **flaps** into a following vowel, so *what a* is [ˈwʌɾə] and *what is it* runs to [ˈwʌɾɪzɪt]. Most Americans have the wine–whine merger and begin the word with plain /w/; older and Southern speakers keep /hwʌt/
**Русский:** какой, что за — вопрос без опоры на закрытый список (в отличие от which)
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **any noun type** — singular countable (*what time*, *what color*), plural countable (*what books*, *what options*), uncountable (*what music*, *what evidence*). What it cannot do is share the slot with another central determiner: ✗ *the what book*, ✗ *what the book*, ✗ *what my size*. With no noun after it, it is a **pronoun**, not a determiner: *What happened?*
**With *of*:** *What of* + noun phrase exists, but it is a fixed formal idiom meaning 'and what about' — see [what of](#what-of) — not the determiner's partitive. The everyday partitive question uses *which of*: ✓ *Which of these is yours?*, ✗ *What of these is yours?*
**Pattern:** *What* [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *What* [noun] [verb]? (subject question — *What train leaves at six?*) &middot; [clause] *what* [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *What* [noun]! (exclamative — a separate entry)
**Contrast:** ***what* against *which*** is the central choice of this file. *Which* asks you to pick from a **closed set** — one both speakers can see, count, or already have in mind. *What* leaves the set **open**. On a plane: *What airline do you usually fly?* (any of hundreds) against *Which seat is mine?* (a fixed, printed list). Where the choice is spelled out as alternatives, only *which* is natural: ✓ *Which do you want, tea or coffee?* Where the field is wide open, *what* is natural: *What music do you like?* rather than *which music*. In a great many everyday cases both are grammatical and the difference is only how bounded the speaker takes the options to be. Remember too that embedded questions keep statement order: ✓ *I asked what time it was*, ✗ *I asked what time was it*.

Asks the hearer to identify or specify the noun, with no assumption that the answer comes from a limited list.

1. *What* time does the meeting start?
2. *What* books did you take with you?
3. *What* music do you listen to when you work?
4. *What* size shoe does she wear?
5. I never found out *what* excuse he gave.
6. *What* color should we paint the hallway?
7. *What* evidence do they actually have?

**See also:** [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [what (exclamative)](#what-exclamative), [what (relative)](#what-relative), [whose](03-possessives.md#whose)

### what all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈɔl/ &middot; *wuht-AWL* &middot; /ˌwʌt ˈɑl/ for the majority of Americans who merge *cot* and *caught*; the two words run together over a flap, [wʌˈɾɔl]
**Русский:** что именно, какие всё — просьба о полном перечне; диал. (юг и Мидленд США), разг.
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** **regional and spoken** — Southern and Midland American English. Rule-governed and completely natural in those varieties, but regionally marked elsewhere and out of place in edited writing
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns in the determiner use — *what all classes*, *what all paperwork*. Not used with a singular countable. It is far more often a **pronoun** standing alone (*What all did she say?*), and that is the use most non-Southern readers will meet first.
**With *of*:** Not used. ✗ *what all of the classes* — the ordinary partitive is *which of the classes*.
**Pattern:** *What all* [plural/uncountable noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *What all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (pronoun) &middot; parallel regional forms: *who all*, *which all*, *where all*, *whose all*
**Variants:** *who all*, *which all*, *whose all*, *what-all (hyphenated in dialect writing)*
**Contrast:** the *all* adds an **exhaustive-list** reading that plain *what* does not carry: *What did you buy?* invites one answer, *What all did you buy?* asks for the whole list. This is a genuine grammatical feature of Southern and Midland English, not an error of logic — but it is dialect, so flag it as such and use *what things*, *what sorts of things*, or *everything that* in standard writing.

Asks for a complete list rather than a single item. **Regional** — Southern and Midland American English.

1. *What all* classes are you taking this semester?
2. *What all* paperwork does the closing require?
3. *What all* questions did they ask you in the interview?
4. She wanted to know *what all* documents we needed.
5. *What all* did you buy at the hardware store?
6. *What all* has he told you about the job?

**See also:** [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [y'all's](03-possessives.md#yalls)

### what kind of, what sort of, what type of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈkaɪnd əv/ &middot; *wuht-KYND-uhv* &middot; *kind of* reduces heavily to [ˈkaɪnə], and *what* flaps into it, so the whole phrase is commonly [wəˈkaɪnə] — *what kinda car*
**Русский:** какого рода, какой (по типу) — вопрос о разновидности, а не об отдельном предмете
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central — the whole phrase fills one slot, and the head noun that follows normally takes no article of its own &middot; **Register:** neutral; *what sort of* leans British, *what type of* leans technical
**Goes with:** a following noun that is normally **bare** — singular countable (*what kind of car*), plural countable (*what kind of shoes*, *what kinds of shoes*), uncountable (*what kind of music*). Number agreement runs on *kind*, not on the head noun: *what kind of dog* for one category, *what kinds of dogs* for several. Do not insert an article: the neutral form is article-less, and *what kind of a car* is a marked variant that adds scorn (*What kind of a question is that?*).
**With *of*:** *Of* is part of the item and is obligatory: ✗ *what kind car*.
**Pattern:** *What kind of* [bare noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *What kinds of* [plural noun] …? &middot; *What kind of* [noun] *is it*? &middot; *What kind of a* [noun] …? (scornful) &middot; *What kind* [verb]? (pronoun, noun elided)
**Variants:** *what sort of*, *what type of*, *what brand of*, *what make of*, *what manner of (archaic, literary)*
**Contrast:** the demonstrative partners are [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of) and [these kinds of](02-demonstratives.md#these-kinds-of) — the same construction, pointing instead of asking. Against plain *what*: *what car do you drive* asks for the identity of one vehicle, *what kind of car do you drive* asks for the category. Against *which kind of*: *which* narrows to a set already on the table (*Which kind of icing, buttercream or fondant?*).

Asks for the category, class, or variety rather than for the individual item.

1. *What kind of* car does she drive?
2. *What kind of* music do you put on when you cook?
3. *What sort of* person leaves a note like that?
4. *What kinds of* documents do I need to bring?
5. *What type of* insulation did the contractor use?
6. *What kind of* dog is that?
7. *What kind of a* question is that?

**See also:** [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of), [these kinds of](02-demonstratives.md#these-kinds-of), [what manner of](#what-manner-of), [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative), [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative)

### what manner of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈmænɚ əv/ &middot; *wuht-MAN-er-uhv* &middot; *of* is the weak /əv/ and reduces further to /ə/, and the *t* of *what* flaps straight into *manner*, so the phrase runs to [wʌɾ ˈmænɚ ə]
**Русский:** что за, какого рода — устар., книжн. эквивалент what kind of
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central — the whole phrase fills one slot, and the noun that follows takes no article of its own &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary**. It is alive today only in deliberate period style, in the one fixed phrase *what manner of man*, and in mock-solemn or comic use
**Goes with:** a **bare** noun with no determiner of its own — singular countable (*what manner of man*), plural countable (*what manner of creatures*), uncountable (*what manner of mischief*). The noun is usually singular, because the fossilized phrase pulls it that way. *Manner* itself never pluralizes in this frame: ✗ *what manners of man*. And the noun cannot keep an article: ✗ *what manner of a man* in the neutral sense.
**With *of*:** *Of* is part of the item and is obligatory: ✗ *what manner man*. Nothing partitive can be built on top of it: ✗ *what manner of these*.
**Pattern:** *What manner of* [bare noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *What manner of* [noun] [verb]? (subject question) &middot; *What manner of* [noun] *is this*? (the fixed frame) &middot; [clause] *what manner of* [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order)
**Variants:** *what kind of*, *what sort of*, *what type of (the living forms)*, *all manner of (the statement partner)*, *no manner of (archaic negative)*
**Contrast:** grammatically identical to [what kind of, what sort of, what type of](#what-kind-of-what-sort-of-what-type-of) — same slot, same bare noun, same obligatory *of*. Only the register differs, and it differs enormously. **Where an American meets it:** the King James Bible, in the verse that keeps the whole phrase in circulation — *What manner of man is this, that even the winds and the sea obey him?* — plus Shakespeare, hymn texts, and fantasy novels reaching for an old-sounding voice. Its declarative partner [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of) fared better and is still ordinary formal English, but the question form did not survive. In modern American prose *what manner of* reads as either scriptural or sarcastic: *what manner of idiot* is a joke, not a register choice.

Asks for the kind, class, or nature of a thing in an elevated and archaic register — 'what kind of.'

1. *What manner of* man is this, that even the winds obey him?
2. *What manner of* creature could have left tracks like these?
3. He wondered *what manner of* welcome was waiting for him at the gate.
4. *What manner of* mischief have the two of you gotten into now?
5. The court asked *what manner of* proof the state intended to offer.
6. *What manner of* people do they take us for?

**See also:** [what kind of, what sort of, what type of](#what-kind-of-what-sort-of-what-type-of), [all manner of](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#all-manner-of), [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative), [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of)

### what of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt əv/ &middot; *WUHT-uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/, and the flapped *t* joins it: [ˈwʌɾəv]
**Русский:** а как же…?, что же с…? — книжн., риторически вводит новую, ещё не затронутую тему
**Type:** interrogative — a **pronoun plus preposition**, not a determiner &middot; **Position:** heads its own noun phrase; the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, rhetorical
**Goes with:** a following *of*-phrase containing an **already-determined** noun phrase — *what of the others*, *what of my proposal*, *what of it*. Never a bare noun: ✗ *what of children*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is the construction itself and is obligatory.
**Pattern:** *What of* [determined noun phrase]? &middot; *What of* [it/them/that]? &middot; *And what of* …? (paragraph-turning rhetorical question)
**Variants:** *what about (the ordinary spoken equivalent)*
**Contrast:** listed here because it is the *of*-partner of interrogative *what*, but the important point is that **it is not a partitive.** *Which of these is yours?* genuinely selects a member from a set; *What of the others?* selects nothing — it means 'and what about the others,' raising them as the next topic. The everyday partitive is [which of](#which-of), and ✗ *What of these is yours?* is simply wrong. Keep it apart from the fixed dismissal *What of it?*, which means 'so what?'

Raises a previously unaddressed item as the next topic — 'and what about …?'

1. *What of* the families who were never notified?
2. And *what of* the money the committee promised?
3. *What of* the second recording — has anyone listened to it?
4. The plan covers the city, but *what of* the outlying towns?
5. *What of* those who cannot afford the fee?
6. *What of* it? Nobody was hurt.

**See also:** [which of](#which-of), [how many of, how much of](#how-many-of-how-much-of), [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative)

### what other, which other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈʌðɚ/, /ˌwɪtʃ ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *wuht-UTH-er, wich-UTH-er* &middot; the flapped *t* of *what* links straight into *other*: [ˌwʌɾ ˈʌðɚ]
**Русский:** какие ещё, какие другие — о членах множества помимо уже названных
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** *what* / *which* is the **central determiner**; *other* is a **postdeterminer** sitting between it and the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*what other options*), uncountable nouns (*what other evidence*), and singular countable nouns when the sense is 'besides this one' (*what other choice do I have?*). Word order inside the phrase is fixed: ✗ *other what options*.
**With *of*:** Bare *what other* + noun takes no *of*. The partitive is built on the pronoun instead — *which of the others*, or the plural noun *which others*.
**Pattern:** *What other* [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Which other* [noun] …? &middot; *What other* [noun] *is there*? &middot; rhetorical: *What other* [noun] *could* [subject] *have* [past participle]?
**Contrast:** the same open/closed split as everywhere in this file: *which other* implies a fixed remaining set (*Which other doors did you try?* — the building has a countable number of them), *what other* leaves the field open (*What other jobs have you applied for?*). The same slot is filled by a quantifier in [any other](06-distributives-negatives.md#any-other) and [no other](06-distributives-negatives.md#no-other). In American English *what other* very often introduces a **rhetorical** question meaning 'none at all': *What other choice did I have?*

Asks about members of the set apart from the one or ones already named.

1. *What other* options do we have at this point?
2. *Which other* candidates made the short list?
3. *What other* evidence did the detective find?
4. *What other* choice did I have?
5. *Which other* branch is open on Sundays?
6. *What other* languages does she speak?

**See also:** [other](07-numerals-multipliers.md#other), [any other](06-distributives-negatives.md#any-other), [no other](06-distributives-negatives.md#no-other), [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative)

### which (interrogative)

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ &middot; *WICH* &middot; a homophone of *witch* for most Americans; older speakers and many Southern speakers keep the /hw-/ onset, /hwɪtʃ/. The word does not reduce — there is no weak form
**Русский:** который, какой из — выбор из известного, ограниченного набора
**Type:** interrogative &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **any noun type** — singular countable (*which gate*), plural countable (*which shoes*), uncountable (*which paint*, *which water*). It cannot combine with another central determiner: ✗ *which the gate*, ✗ *the which gate*, ✗ *which my keys*. With no noun after it, it is a **pronoun**: *Which is yours?*
**With *of*:** **Required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *which of the gates*, *which of them*. Not used before a bare noun: ✓ *which gates*, ✗ *which of gates*. In the *of*-construction *which* is a pronoun, not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *Which* [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Which* [noun] [verb]? (subject question) &middot; *Which of* [the/these/my + noun]? &middot; [clause] *which* [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) &middot; *Which* [noun], [option] *or* [option]?
**Contrast:** ***which* against *what*.** *Which* presupposes a **closed set** — one the speaker and hearer can enumerate, see, or point at. *What* leaves the set **open**. *Which flight are you on?* assumes a printed list; *What flight are you on?* does not. The cleanest test is a spelled-out alternative, where only *which* is natural: ✓ *Which do you want, tea or coffee?*, and *Which hand is it in?* rather than ✗ *what hand*. Against *whose*, the difference is identity versus ownership: *Which coat is it?* against [whose](03-possessives.md#whose) *coat is it?*

Asks the hearer to pick the intended item out of a set that is already known, visible, or listed.

1. *Which* gate does the flight leave from?
2. *Which* shoes should I pack, the black ones or the brown?
3. *Which* apartment is yours?
4. I can't remember *which* file I saved it in.
5. *Which* day works better for you, Tuesday or Thursday?
6. *Which* paint did you end up using on the trim?
7. *Which* of these keys opens the shed?

**See also:** [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative), [which of](#which-of), [which (relative)](#which-relative), [whichever](#whichever)

### which of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪtʃ əv/ &middot; *WICH-uhv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ and, in fast speech, to /ə/ — *which of them* [ˈwɪtʃ ə ðəm]
**Русский:** который из, кто из — партитив; местоимение, а не определитель
**Type:** interrogative (partitive — a **pronoun** construction, not a determiner) &middot; **Position:** heads its own noun phrase; the noun inside the *of*-phrase keeps its own determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a **definite, already-determined** plural or a pronoun — *which of the applicants*, *which of these*, *which of them*, *which of us*. A two-member set takes the same frame — *which of the two offers*, *which of the two of you*. Never a bare plural: ✗ *which of applicants*, and never an uncountable mass: ✗ *which of the money* → *how much of the money*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is the construction itself and is obligatory. Drop it and you are back to the determiner: *which applicants*.
**Pattern:** *Which of* [the/these/my + plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? &middot; *Which of* [them/us/you]? &middot; *Which of the two* …? &middot; embedded: [clause] *which of* [noun phrase] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** the ***of* test** in its purest form. *Which applicants did you interview?* is a **determiner** — the noun follows directly. *Which of the applicants did you interview?* is a **pronoun** — an *of*-phrase follows and the noun inside keeps its own determiner. Both are correct; they differ only in whether the set has already been made definite. Against [what of](#what-of): *which of* genuinely selects, *what of* only raises a topic. Verb agreement follows the noun inside the *of*-phrase: *Which of the files **were** deleted?*

Asks the hearer to select from a set that has just been identified.

1. *Which of* these keys opens the shed?
2. *Which of* the applicants did you actually interview?
3. *Which of* them do you trust?
4. *Which of* the two offers is better?
5. I couldn't tell you *which of* her books I like best.
6. *Which of* you left the door unlocked?

**See also:** [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [what of](#what-of), [how many of, how much of](#how-many-of-how-much-of), [whichever](#whichever)


## Whose, whosever, and of which: the genitive *wh-* words

*Whose* is the one *wh-* determiner that marks **possession**, and being a possessive it has its home in the [possessives file](03-possessives.md) rather than here. It is catalogued there once and covers both jobs it does in this file: [whose](03-possessives.md#whose) asks a question (*Whose car is that?*) and introduces a relative clause (*the engineer whose design won*), with the free relative [whosever](03-possessives.md#whosever) and the formal [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which) alongside it. The archaic legal intensives *whosesoever* and, in older printing, the two-word *whose soever* — the genitive members of the *-soever* family that gives this file [whatsoever](#whatsoever) and [whichsoever](#whichsoever) — are variants of *whosever* and are listed with it there rather than duplicated here. Four points from those entries are worth repeating here, because they are what sends readers to this file in the first place.

**1. *Whose* works with things, not only with people.** English has no separate inanimate genitive relative, so *whose* covers both: ✓ *a house whose roof leaks*, ✓ *a novel whose ending nobody likes*, ✓ *a car whose brakes failed*. Older handbooks called this an error and told writers to reserve *whose* for humans. That advice never matched real usage, and the alternative it recommends is clumsy.

**2. *Of which* is the formal alternative — and only for inanimates.** *A house the roof of which leaks* is grammatical and belongs to legal, academic, and technical registers; *a house whose roof leaks* is the ordinary English sentence. With a **human** antecedent only *whose* is possible: ✓ *the man whose car was stolen*, ✗ *the man the car of which was stolen*. Note also that *of which* is not a determiner at all — it is a postmodifying prepositional phrase, which is exactly why the head noun keeps its own article (***the*** *roof of which*).

**3. *Whose* against *who's*.** *Whose* asks or states possession; *who's* is the contraction of *who is* or *who has*. Expand it to test: *Who's calling?* = *who is calling*, but *Whose call is it?* has no expansion, so it takes *whose*. See [who's (not a determiner)](03-possessives.md#whos-not-a-determiner).

**4. The determiner-or-pronoun test applies here too.** *Whose coat is this?* has a noun after it and is a determiner; *Whose is this?* stands alone and is a pronoun. And *whose* has no *of*-partitive of its own — that question is built on *which*: ✓ *Which of these is yours?*, ✗ *Whose of these is yours?*

## Exclamative determiners

These stand in front of the article and mark the noun as extreme, remarkable, or hard to believe. Two determiners do the work — *what* and *such* — and both obey the same rule: **the predeterminer goes before *a/an*, never after it.** ✓ *what a long day*, ✓ *such a long day*, and ✗ *a such long day*, ✗ *a what long day*. In front of plurals and uncountables the article simply disappears (*what beautiful flowers*, *such nonsense*), because *a/an* was never going to be there. *How* heads the section even though it is not a determiner at all, because half of every exclamative error in English comes from crossing it with *what*. The degree predeterminers [quite a](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#quite-a) and [rather a](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#rather-a) fill the identical slot in a cooler register — *quite a storm*, *rather a nuisance* — and follow the same article rule, but they assess rather than exclaim.

### how (exclamative)

**Pronunciation:** /haʊ/ &middot; *HOW* &middot; carries the nuclear accent of the exclamation, and the following adjective is drawn out — *How LOOONG the day was!*
**Русский:** как! — восклицание о степени; ставится перед прилагательным или наречием, артикль не трогает
**Type:** degree adverb — **not a determiner**. It is entered here because the article rule that separates it from *what* is the commonest exclamative error in English &middot; **Position:** fronts the clause and takes an adjective or adverb; it never enters the determiner slot at all &middot; **Register:** neutral; the full-clause form (*How long the day was!*) reads as literary, and everyday speech clips it to *How long!* or switches to *what*
**Goes with:** an **adjective** or an **adverb**, never a noun. ✗ *How a day!*, ✗ *How day!* Because it never touches a noun, it never displaces the noun's own determiner — which is exactly why the article stays behind: *How long **the** day was!*
**With *of*:** No partitive — *how* is not a determiner, so there is no set for it to take a part of. The *of* in *How kind of you to remember* belongs to the adjective (*kind of you*), not to *how*.
**Pattern:** *How* [adjective]! &middot; *How* [adjective] [subject] [verb]! — *How long the day was!* &middot; *How* [adverb] [subject] [verb]! &middot; *How* [subject] [verb]! — *How she suffered!* &middot; *How* [adjective] *of* [person] *to* [verb]! — *How kind of you to remember*
**Contrast:** **the article rule.** *What* is a predeterminer, so it swallows the article and brings the noun along: ✓ *What a long day!* *How* is an adverb, so it takes only the adjective, and the noun phrase keeps its own article behind it: ✓ *How long the day was!* Cross the two and you get the classic errors **How a long day!* and ✗ *What long the day was!* The quick test: ✗ *if there is a noun in the exclamation, use *what*; if there is only an adjective, use *how*.**

Marks a high degree of the quality named by the following adjective or adverb.

1. *How* long the day was!
2. *How* quickly she picked it up!
3. *How* strange that nobody mentioned it.
4. *How* cold it got last night!
5. *How* kind of you to remember.
6. *How* beautifully the room turned out.

**See also:** [what (exclamative)](#what-exclamative), [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an), [such a, such an](#such-a-such-an), [how big a, how good a, how much of a](#how-big-a-how-good-a-how-much-of-a)

### such

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ/ &middot; *SUCH* &middot; weak form /sətʃ/ when unstressed, which is most of the time — *such a mess* runs to [sətʃəˈmɛs]
**Русский:** такой — усиление степени; «такого рода» (отсылка к названному); such … that — следствие
**Type:** exclamative and identifying determiner &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** before *a/an* (*such a mess*); **central determiner** when it attaches straight to a bare plural or uncountable (*such nonsense*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*such problems*) and **uncountable nouns** (*such nonsense*, *such enthusiasm*) directly. A **singular countable noun requires *a/an***: ✓ *such a mess*, ✗ *such mess* — see [such a, such an](#such-a-such-an). It can never follow an article: ✗ *a such mess*, ✗ *the such problems*.
**With *of*:** Not used in ordinary English. The partitive *such of the* + noun + *as* … survives only in legal drafting (*such of the funds as remain unspent*).
**Pattern:** *such* [plural noun] &middot; *such* [uncountable] &middot; *such a/an* [singular noun] &middot; *such* [noun] *that* [clause] (result) &middot; *such* [noun] *as* [noun/clause] (identifying, and the formal fused relative *such money as I had*) &middot; *no such* [noun] (anaphoric)
**Variants:** *such a, such an*, *no such*, *such as*, *suchlike (dialectal and dated)*
**Contrast:** ***such* against *what*.** Both are predeterminers taking the same slot and obeying the same article rule, but *what* is confined to exclamations and questions while *such* also runs in ordinary statements: ✓ *I've never seen such enthusiasm*, ✗ *I've never seen what enthusiasm*. ***Such* against *so*:** *so* takes an adjective with no noun (*so cold*), *such* takes a noun (*such cold weather*) — hence ✗ *so cold weather* and ✗ *such cold*. The formal fused relative *such money as I had* is the older twin of [what (relative)](#what-relative).

**1. Exclamative and intensifying** — marks a high degree of whatever the noun names. **2. Identifying and anaphoric** — 'of that kind,' pointing back to something already described (*no such rule exists*). **3. Result** — paired with *that*, it introduces the consequence of the degree.

1. I've never seen *such* enthusiasm from a jury.
2. *Such* problems are common in older houses.
3. There is no *such* rule in the handbook.
4. She spoke with *such* confidence that nobody questioned her.
5. It was done in *such* haste that half the forms came back blank.
6. *Such* people rarely apologize.
7. He gave me *such* good advice.

**See also:** [such a, such an](#such-a-such-an), [such other](#such-other), [what (exclamative)](#what-exclamative), [what (relative)](#what-relative), [no such](06-distributives-negatives.md#no-such)

### such a, such an

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ə/, /ˌsʌtʃ ən/ &middot; *such-UH, such-UHN* &middot; both words normally reduce: *such a* [sətʃə], *such an* [sətʃən]
**Русский:** такой — усиление перед исчисляемым ед. ч.; such стоит перед a/an: such a mess
**Type:** exclamative determiner (predeterminer plus article) &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** — *such* stands in front of the indefinite article, which then does its ordinary job on the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *such a day*, *such an idiot*. Never a plural (✗ *such a problems* → *such problems*), never an uncountable (✗ *such a nonsense* → *such nonsense*), and never with the definite article (✗ *such the day*).
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** *such a/an* [(adjective) singular countable noun] &middot; *such a/an* [noun] *that* [clause] &middot; *such a/an* [noun] *as* [noun/clause] &middot; exclamative: *Such a* [noun]!
**Contrast:** the article-order rule in its most-tested form: ✓ *such a long day*, ✗ *a such long day*. It matches *what a long day* exactly, and contrasts with *how long the day was*, where no noun is involved and the article therefore stays behind. Against *so*: ✓ *so long a day* (formal) and ✓ *such a long day*, but ✗ *so a long day* and ✗ *such long a day*. Choose *an* by **sound**, not spelling: *such an hour*, *such a union*.

Marks a single countable thing or person as extreme, remarkable, or exactly of that kind.

1. It was *such a* long day.
2. He's *such an* idiot about money.
3. *Such a* shame the weather turned.
4. She's *such a* good listener.
5. It was *such a* mess that we gave up and ordered takeout.
6. I've never met *such an* honest salesman.

**See also:** [such](#such), [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an), [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), [an](01-articles.md#an)

### such other

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *such-UTH-er* &middot; *such* takes its weak form /sətʃ/ here, because the accent runs forward to the noun — *such other TERMS*, *such other RELIEF*
**Русский:** прочие такие, иные подобные — юр., договорная формула, обычно с оборотом as …
**Type:** identifying determiner plus postdeterminer &middot; **Position:** *such* fills the **central** slot and *other* is a **postdeterminer** between it and the noun &middot; **Register:** **legal and contractual**. This is drafting boilerplate, not ordinary English; used outside a document it sounds like an impression of a lawyer
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*such other terms*, *such other documents*) and **uncountable nouns** (*such other relief*, *such other property*). A singular countable would need *a/an* and the frame does not take it — drafters write *any other case*, not ✗ *such other a case*. It can never follow an article: ✗ *the such other terms*, ✗ *a such other document*.
**With *of*:** Not used. The drafting partitive is a different frame, *such of the* + noun + *as* — *such of the funds as remain unspent* — and there *such* is a **pronoun**, not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *such other* [plural/uncountable noun] *as* [clause] — the *as*-tail is all but obligatory &middot; *such other and further* [noun] *as* … (the fixed doublet) &middot; *such other* [noun] *as the* [party] *may* [verb] &middot; *and for such other relief as the Court deems just and proper* (the fixed closing of an American pleading)
**Variants:** *such other and further*, *any other (the plain-English replacement)*, *such … as*
**Contrast:** against [what other, which other](#what-other-which-other), which **ask** about the remaining members of a set; *such other* asserts them and then defines them with an *as*-clause. Against bare [such](#such), which points back to a kind already described: *such other* points to the **rest** of that kind — everything answering the description that has not already been listed. Its whole value to a drafter is that the *as*-clause can leave the category open-ended without naming a single member, which is also why plain-language style guides tell drafters to write *any other* instead. **Where an American actually meets it:** three places, all of them documents — a contract's catch-all clause, the prayer for relief at the end of a complaint, and a will.

'The remaining ones of that kind, whichever they turn out to be' — a drafting formula that leaves a category open and then closes it with an *as*-clause.

1. The tenant shall pay rent, utilities, and *such other* charges as the lease may specify.
2. Plaintiff prays for damages, costs, and *such other* and further relief as the Court deems just.
3. The board may appoint a treasurer and *such other* officers as it considers necessary.
4. I leave my house, my car, and *such other* property as I may own at my death to my sister.
5. The contractor shall furnish the permits and *such other* documents as the architect reasonably requests.
6. Employees receive health coverage and *such other* benefits as the company provides from time to time.

**See also:** [such](#such), [what other, which other](#what-other-which-other), [other](07-numerals-multipliers.md#other), [no such](06-distributives-negatives.md#no-such)

### what (exclamative)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/ &middot; *WUHT* &middot; carries the accent of the exclamation; the /t/ flaps into a following vowel, so *what an idiot* is [wʌɾənˈaɪɾiət]
**Русский:** какой!, что за! — восклицание-реакция: восторг, ужас, презрение или удивление
**Type:** exclamative &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** in front of *a/an* (*what a mess!*); **central determiner** when it attaches straight to a bare plural or uncountable (*what nonsense!*) — the same two-slot behavior as *such* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*What beautiful flowers!*) and **uncountable nouns** (*What nonsense!*, *What luck!*) directly. A **singular countable requires *a/an*** and is treated at [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an): ✗ *What mess!* → ✓ *What a mess!* It never combines with *the*: ✗ *What the flowers!*
**With *of*:** Not used in exclamations. The formal idiom [what of](#what-of) is a question, not an exclamation.
**Pattern:** *What* [plural noun]! &middot; *What* [uncountable]! &middot; *What* [(adjective) noun] [subject] [verb]! — *What nerve that man has!* &middot; *What* [noun] *to* [verb]! &middot; embedded: [clause] *what* [noun] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** against [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), apply the **noun test**: a noun in the exclamation means *what*, an adjective alone means *how*. Against [such](#such), which fills the same slot in ordinary statements rather than exclamations. Against **interrogative *what***, which is identical in form and separated only by intonation and by word order — the question inverts (*What time is it?*), the exclamation does not (*What time we had!*).

Registers a strong reaction to the noun — admiration, dismay, scorn, or surprise.

1. *What* beautiful flowers!
2. *What* nonsense — none of that is in the contract.
3. *What* nerve that man has!
4. *What* luck, running into you here.
5. *What* terrible timing.
6. *What* lovely manners those children have.

**See also:** [what a, what an](#what-a-what-an), [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), [such](#such), [what (interrogative)](#what-interrogative)

### what a, what an

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ə/, /ˌwʌt ən/ &middot; *wuht-UH, wuht-UHN* &middot; almost always flapped in running speech: [ˈwʌɾə], [ˈwʌɾən] — *what a mess* is [wʌɾəˈmɛs]
**Русский:** какой!, что за! — восклицание об исчисляемом ед. ч.; what стоит перед a/an
**Type:** exclamative (predeterminer plus article) &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** — *what* stands in front of *a/an* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *what a day*, *what an honor*. ✗ *what a flowers*, ✗ *what a nonsense*. With plurals and uncountables the article simply disappears: see [what (exclamative)](#what-exclamative). It never takes the definite article: ✗ *what the day*.
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** *What a/an* [(adjective) singular noun]! &middot; *What a/an* [noun] [subject] [verb]! — *What a day I've had!* &middot; *What a/an* [noun] *to* [verb]! &middot; embedded: [clause] *what a* [noun] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** the article-placement rule at its clearest: ✓ *What a long day!*, ✗ *What long a day!*, ✗ *A what long day!* Against *how*, which never touches a noun and so leaves the article behind: ✓ *How long the day was!* Choose *an* by sound: *what an hour to call*, *what a European mess*. Note the sarcasm American English gets out of it — said flatly, *What a friend* is an insult, and *What a question!* usually means the question was foolish.

Registers a strong reaction to a single countable thing or person.

1. *What a* long day!
2. *What an* idiot I've been.
3. *What a* mess the kitchen is.
4. *What a* week we've had.
5. *What an* honor to be asked.
6. *What a* way to find out.

**See also:** [what (exclamative)](#what-exclamative), [such a, such an](#such-a-such-an), [how (exclamative)](#how-exclamative), [a](01-articles.md#a)


## Relative determiners

A relative determiner hooks a clause onto a noun while still determining that noun. Two kinds live here. The **bound** relative determiner needs an antecedent already in the sentence and repeats it with a noun of its own — *he arrived at noon, by which time we had left*. The **fused** relative determiner supplies its own antecedent and packages noun and clause into one phrase — *what money I had* means 'the money that I had,' with nothing standing in front of it. The *-ever* forms (*whatever*, *whichever*) are fused relatives that also do concessive duty: *whatever excuse he gives* means 'no matter what excuse.' As always, the test for determiner status is whether a noun follows immediately: *whatever seat you like* is a determiner, *take whatever you like* is a pronoun.

### however little, however few

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvɚ ˈlɪtəl/, /haʊˈɛvɚ ˈfju/ &middot; *how-EV-er-LIT-ul, how-EV-er-FYOO* &middot; written as one word in this use; the *t* of *little* is a flap in General American, so it comes out *how-EV-er-LID-ul*
**Русский:** как бы мало ни — уступка о малом количестве (little — неисчисл., few — счётные)
**Type:** relative (concessive fused relative) &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; less frequent than *however much* and *however many*
**Goes with:** *however little* + **uncountable** (*however little money*, *however little time*); *however few* + **plural countable** (*however few options*, *however few people*). The split is the ordinary *little/few* split, so ✗ *however little options* and ✗ *however few money*, and neither takes a singular countable (✗ *however few option*). With no noun following, *however little* is a degree adverb (*however little it matters*), not a determiner.
**With *of*:** Partitive *however little of the* / *however few of the* is possible, and *of* is **required** before a pronoun (*however few of them*, *however little of it*). The *wh-* word is a **pronoun** in that construction. Never before a bare plural: ✗ *however few of options*.
**Pattern:** *However little* [uncountable] [subject] [verb], [main clause] &middot; *However few* [plural noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause] &middot; [main clause], *however little* [uncountable] [subject] [verb] &middot; *however few of* [them/the + plural noun] &middot; *however little* [subject] [verb] (adverb, no noun)
**Variants:** *no matter how little*, *no matter how few*
**Contrast:** the negatively loaded twin of [however much, however many](#however-much-however-many). *However much money you have* concedes an amount of any size; *however little money you have* concedes that the amount is small and says it makes no difference. The quantifier is bare *little* and bare *few*, never the *a*-forms: ✗ *however a little money*, ✗ *however a few options*. Against interrogative [how little](#how-little) and [how few](#how-few), which **ask** about the quantity instead of conceding it, and against the fused relatives [what little](#what-little) and [what few](#what-few), which **assert** a small quantity rather than waiving it.

Concedes a small amount or number without settling on one — 'no matter how little.'

1. *However little* money you start with, the closing costs add up.
2. *However few* options are left, we still have to choose one.
3. He'll find something to complain about, *however little* work you give him.
4. *However few* people show up, the reading goes ahead.
5. She made do with *however little* counter space the kitchen had.
6. *However few* hours he sleeps, he's up at five.
7. *However little* of the budget survives, the audit still has to happen.

**See also:** [however much, however many](#however-much-however-many), [how little](#how-little), [how few](#how-few), [what little](#what-little), [what few](#what-few)

### however much, however many

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvɚ mʌtʃ/, /haʊˈɛvɚ ˈmɛni/ &middot; *how-EV-er-much, how-EV-er-MEN-ee* &middot; written as one word in this use; the two-word *how ever* is a different, emphatic interrogative and is stressed *how EV-er*
**Русский:** сколько бы ни — уступка о любом количестве (much — неисчисл., many — счётные)
**Type:** relative (concessive fused relative) &middot; **Position:** central — the whole phrase fills one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Goes with:** *however much* + **uncountable** (*however much money*); *however many* + **plural countable** (*however many times*). The countability split is the ordinary *much/many* split, so ✗ *however much times* and ✗ *however many money*. With no noun following, *however much* is a degree adverb (*however much it costs*), not a determiner.
**With *of*:** Partitive *however much of the* / *however many of the* is possible, and *of* is **required** before a pronoun (*however many of them*). The *wh-* word is a pronoun in that construction.
**Pattern:** *However much* [uncountable] [subject] [verb], [main clause] &middot; *However many* [plural noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause] &middot; [main clause] *however much* [uncountable] [subject] [verb] &middot; [verb] *however many* [plural noun] [subject] [verb] &middot; *however much* [subject] [verb] (adverb, no noun)
**Variants:** *no matter how much*, *no matter how many*, *how ever much (emphatic, two words)*
**Contrast:** concessive, not interrogative: it does not ask, it **concedes**. *However much money you throw at it* means 'no matter what amount of money.' Keep it apart from *however* the connective adverb, which is followed by a comma and no noun (*However, the flight was canceled*), and from the emphatic two-word *how ever* (*How ever did you manage?*). Against *whatever*, which concedes without measuring: *whatever excuses he gives* names the kind, *however many excuses he gives* counts them. The negatively loaded twins are [however little](#however-little-however-few) and *however few*.

Concedes any amount or number without settling on one — 'no matter what amount of.'

1. *However much* money you throw at it, the roof will still leak.
2. *However many* times I explain it, he asks again.
3. Take *however many* copies you need.
4. *However much* patience I start with, that meeting drains it.
5. They'll seat you, *however many* people you bring.
6. Spend *however much* time you want on the draft.

**See also:** [however little, however few](#however-little-however-few), [whatever](#whatever), [whichever](#whichever), [how much](#how-much), [how many](#how-many)

### in which case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˌwɪtʃ ˈkeɪs/ &middot; *in-wich-KAYSS* &middot; the noun takes the accent — *at which POINT*, *by which TIME*
**Русский:** в таком случае — цепляет следующую фразу к только что описанной ситуации
**Type:** relative &middot; **Position:** *which* is the central determiner of the noun, inside a fronted prepositional phrase; the phrase as a whole opens its clause &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; standard in careful writing, contracts, and business speech
**Goes with:** a **small closed set of abstract nouns** naming a situation, moment, or reason — *case*, *point*, *time*, *event*, *instance*, *circumstances*, *reason*, *occasion*, *juncture*, *stage*, *respect*. It cannot take an ordinary concrete noun in this relative use: ✗ *He sat down, in which chair we later found his keys*, ✗ *by which sandwich*. (The interrogative *In which chair did he sit?* is a different construction and is fine.)
**With *of*:** None — the preposition is already supplied by the phrase itself (*in*, *at*, *by*, *for*, *after*, *during*, *since*).
**Pattern:** [clause], *in which case* [clause] &middot; [clause], *at which point* [clause] &middot; [clause], *by which time* [clause] &middot; [clause], *for which reason* [clause] &middot; the same phrases also open a new sentence as a connector
**Variants:** *at which point*, *by which time*, *at which time*, *in which event*, *for which reason*, *on which occasion*, *during which time*, *since which date*, *after which*
**Contrast:** the antecedent here is not a noun but the **whole preceding clause** — *He arrived at noon, by which time we had left*: *which time* reaches back and names the moment the first clause established. This is the one place where relative *which* works comfortably as a determiner in everyday American English; elsewhere the determiner use (*He offered a settlement, which offer we declined*) is legal or archaic. It cannot introduce a **restrictive** clause: ✗ *the meeting in which case we voted*. And it takes a **comma** in front of it — running it on without one is the standard error.

Hooks a following clause onto the situation just described, naming that situation with a noun.

1. The flight may be canceled, *in which case* we'll drive.
2. He arrived at noon, *by which time* we had already left.
3. The tenant may refuse, *at which point* the matter goes to arbitration.
4. She could still appeal, *in which event* the hearing would be reopened.
5. The check might bounce, *in which case* you'll hear from me.
6. We waited until Friday, *by which time* the offer had expired.

**See also:** [which (relative)](#which-relative), [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which)

### the which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh-WICH* &middot; *the* is the ordinary weak /ðə/ in front of the consonant /w/, never /ði/; /hwɪtʃ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-* onset. In verse the article can be elided to *th'* for the meter, *th' which*
**Русский:** который, каковой — устар. (совр. which с определённым артиклем впереди)
**Type:** relative — **archaic**. A relative **pronoun** in most of its attested uses and a relative **determiner** in the minority of them; it is entered here for that minority &middot; **Position:** *the* and *which* together fill the central slot when a noun follows (*the which time*); with no noun after it the pair is a pronoun and holds no determiner slot at all &middot; **Register:** **obsolete** in every variety of modern English — Middle and Early Modern English only
**Goes with:** in the determiner use, chiefly the same **small set of abstract nouns** that modern relative *which* still takes — *the which time*, *the which place*, *the which cause*, *the which thing* — and, in Middle English, a wider range of ordinary nouns besides. In modern English no noun is possible at all: ✗ *the which gate*, ✗ *the which report*. Far more often nothing follows and the phrase is simply a relative pronoun meaning *which*.
**With *of*:** The construction is already built on an article, so *of* attaches outside it, in the older genitive frame *of the which* — the direct ancestor of today's [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which). There is no partitive: ✗ *the which of these*.
**Pattern:** [clause], *the which* [verb] … (pronoun — the commonest use by far) &middot; [clause], [preposition] *the which* [noun] [clause] — *in the which place*, *at the which time* (determiner) &middot; [clause], *the which* [noun] [verb] (determiner, subject) &middot; the modern equivalents are plain *which*, or [in which case](#in-which-case) and its siblings
**Variants:** *which (the living form)*, *the whiche (Middle English spelling)*, *of the which (the older of which)*
**Contrast:** this is the construction behind the ✗ mark in [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative): *the which gate* is impossible today, but *the which* was ordinary English for three centuries. Modern *which* carries its own definiteness; Middle English still spelled that definiteness out with an article in front, exactly as French does with *lequel*. The determiner reading survives in fossil form in [in which case](#in-which-case) and *at which point*, which are the same idea with the article dropped. **Where an American meets it:** Chaucer and other Middle English texts, Spenser, Shakespeare, and a scattering of places in the King James Bible, where it alternates freely with plain *which* for no reason a modern reader can detect. It is a period marker rather than a style option — recognize it, never write it.

Archaic *which*, with the definite article still standing in front of it — alone as a relative pronoun, or, less often, before a noun as a relative determiner.

1. He came at midnight, at *the which* hour the gates were already shut. (determiner)
2. They sailed to a far country, in *the which* land no man knew them. (determiner)
3. She made him a promise, *the which* she never kept. (pronoun — the ordinary older use)
4. The king granted him a charter, by *the which* grant the town was made free. (determiner)
5. He fell into a great sickness, from *the which* he never recovered. (pronoun)
6. There was a famine in the land, for *the which* cause they went down into Egypt. (determiner)

**See also:** [which (relative)](#which-relative), [in which case](#in-which-case), [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which)

### what (relative)

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/ &middot; *WUHT* &middot; weak form /wət/, since the fused relative is rarely accented — *what money I had* runs to [wətˈmʌni]
**Русский:** то (немногое), что — сращённое относительное: what money I had «те деньги, что у меня были»
**Type:** relative (fused relative — it contains its own antecedent) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral, with a slightly literary edge
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns** (*what money I had*) and **plural countable nouns** (*what photographs survived*). A bare singular countable is marginal and normally rephrased (✗? *what car I owned* → *the car I owned*). The noun is nearly always small in quantity — the construction carries an undertone of 'the little there was,' which is why the fixed expansions [what little](#what-little) and [what few](#what-few) exist.
**With *of*:** None in the determiner use. [what of](#what-of) is a separate, non-determiner idiom and means something else entirely.
**Pattern:** *what* [noun] [subject] [verb] — *what money I had* &middot; [verb] *what* [noun] [subject] [verb] — *He spent what savings he had* &middot; *what* [noun] [verb] — *What evidence remains is inconclusive* &middot; expansions: *what little* [uncountable], *what few* [plural]
**Variants:** *what little*, *what few*, *such … as (the formal older equivalent)*
**Contrast:** the fused relative has **no antecedent** — it supplies its own. *What money I had* is a complete noun phrase meaning 'the money that I had.' Relative *which* and relative *whose* both require an antecedent already standing in the sentence (*the money which I had*). Against interrogative *what*, the test is whether an answer is being requested: *I asked what money he had* embeds a question, *He spent what money he had* asks nothing. **Nonstandard warning:** the relative *pronoun* *what* in *the book what I read* is a well-established feature of several British and American vernaculars and is entirely systematic there, but it is not standard written English — use *that* or *which*.

'The (little) X that' — packages a noun together with its relative clause into a single phrase, with the quantity implied to be limited.

1. He spent *what* money he had on the repair.
2. She sold *what* furniture would fit in the truck.
3. *What* evidence remains is inconclusive.
4. Take *what* supplies you need from the closet.
5. They saved *what* photographs survived the flood.
6. I'll give you *what* help I can.

**See also:** [what little](#what-little), [what few](#what-few), [whatever](#whatever), [such](#such)

### what few

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈfju/ &middot; *wuht-FYOO* &middot; the accent falls on *few*
**Русский:** те немногие, что — о малом оставшемся числе счётных: what few friends he had
**Type:** relative (fused relative plus quantifier) &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *what few friends I have*, *what few options remain*. Never an uncountable (✗ *what few money* → *what little money*) and never a singular countable.
**With *of*:** None. ✗ *what few of the friends I have* — the partitive would be *the few of my friends who* …
**Pattern:** *what few* [plural noun] [subject] [verb] &middot; *what few* [plural noun] [verb] &middot; [verb] *what few* [plural noun] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** the plural-countable partner of [what little](#what-little), and a fixed expansion of fused-relative [what](#what-relative). The *few* keeps its usual negative loading — 'not many, and that is the point' — so *what few friends I have* asserts that the number is small. The *a*-form is impossible here: ✗ *what a few friends*. Do not confuse it with interrogative *how few*, which asks about the number instead of asserting it.

'The few X that' — names a small remaining number and attaches its clause in one phrase.

1. *What few* friends he had left stopped calling.
2. She used *what few* vacation days she had on the funeral.
3. *What few* records survived are in the county archive.
4. They spent *what few* dollars remained on gas.
5. *What few* options we have are all expensive.
6. He answered *what few* questions he could.

**See also:** [what little](#what-little), [what (relative)](#what-relative), [few](05-quantifiers-small.md#few), [how few](#how-few)

### what little

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈlɪtəl/ &middot; *wuht-LIT-ul* &middot; the *t* of *little* is a flap, so it comes out *wuht-LID-ul*
**Русский:** то немногое, что — о малом оставшемся объёме неисчисляемого: what little time we had
**Type:** relative (fused relative plus quantifier) &middot; **Position:** central — the two words fill one slot &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns only** — *what little time remains*, *what little money he had*. Never a plural countable (✗ *what little friends* → *what few friends*).
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** *what little* [uncountable] [subject] [verb] &middot; *what little* [uncountable] [verb] &middot; [verb] *what little* [uncountable] [subject] [verb]
**Contrast:** the uncountable partner of [what few](#what-few). The quantifier is bare *little*, the negative-loading form, never *a little*: ✗ *what a little money*. Against interrogative [how little](#how-little), which asks about the amount rather than asserting it. Against plain [what (relative)](#what-relative), *what little* simply makes the small quantity explicit.

'The little X that' — names a small remaining amount and attaches its clause in one phrase.

1. He gave *what little* money he had to the driver.
2. *What little* time remains should go to the conclusion.
3. She salvaged *what little* dignity she could.
4. *What little* evidence there is points the other way.
5. They shared *what little* food was left in the pack.
6. I'll offer *what little* advice I have.

**See also:** [what few](#what-few), [what (relative)](#what-relative), [little](05-quantifiers-small.md#little), [how little](#how-little)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wuht-EV-er* &middot; the /t/ flaps, so the ordinary American pronunciation is [wəˈɾɛvɚ]; the dismissive interjection *whatever* is stressed differently, *WHAT-ever*
**Русский:** любой какой угодно; какой бы ни — сращённое относительное и уступительное «неважно какой»
**Type:** relative (fused relative) and concessive; also emphatic interrogative &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **any noun type** — singular countable (*whatever excuse he gives*), plural countable (*whatever books you want*), uncountable (*whatever money is left*). It cannot share the slot with another central determiner: ✗ *the whatever excuse*.
**With *of*:** Not used in the determiner sense. *Whatever of* + noun phrase exists but is archaic; the living partitive is *whichever of the* …
**Pattern:** *whatever* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause] (concessive) &middot; [verb] *whatever* [noun] [subject] [verb] (fused relative, object) &middot; *whatever* [noun] [verb] (fused relative, subject) &middot; *Whatever* [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (emphatic question) &middot; [noun] *whatever* (postposed emphasizer after a negative)
**Variants:** *whatsoever (emphatic and archaic)*, *no matter what (the everyday spoken equivalent)*, *what ever (two words, in the emphatic question)*
**Contrast:** ***whatever* against *whichever*** repeats the *what/which* split exactly: *whatever* leaves the set **open** (*whatever books you want* — from anywhere at all), *whichever* picks from a set already **fixed** (*whichever of these two you want*). Determiner against pronoun: a noun follows the determiner (*whatever seat*), nothing follows the pronoun (*take whatever you like*). *Whatever* also runs as a **free-choice** quantifier meaning 'any at all, it makes no difference' (*call me whatever day suits you*), which puts it next to [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any) in meaning; the grammar is the same fused relative in every case, so the word is catalogued here.

**1. Fused relative** — 'any X that,' supplying its own antecedent: *Take whatever seat you like.* **2. Concessive** — 'no matter what X': *Whatever excuse he gives, I don't believe it.* **3. Emphatic interrogative** — adds exasperation to a question (*Whatever reason could she have had?*); chiefly British in this determiner use, where Americans say *what on earth*. **4. Postposed emphasizer** after a negative — *no reason whatever*.

1. Take *whatever* seat you like.
2. *Whatever* excuse he gives, I'm not covering for him again.
3. Spend *whatever* money is left in the account.
4. She reads *whatever* books the library has on the subject.
5. *Whatever* problems the merger creates, they'll be somebody else's.
6. Bring *whatever* tools you think you'll need.

**See also:** [whichever](#whichever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [what (relative)](#what-relative), [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any)

### whatsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌtsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wuht-soh-EV-er* &middot; four syllables, accent on *ev*; the *t* does not flap here because /s/ follows
**Русский:** вообще никакой, ни малейшего — усилитель после отрицания; устар. «какой бы ни»
**Type:** emphatic negative-polarity item; also, archaically, a fused relative &middot; **Position:** **postposed** in the modern use — it follows the noun it strengthens and therefore never occupies the determiner slot at all. In the archaic relative use it precedes the noun &middot; **Register:** formal and emphatic; the relative use is biblical or legal
**Goes with:** a noun already determined by *no*, *any*, *none*, or *little* — *no evidence whatsoever*, *any doubt whatsoever*, *nothing whatsoever*. It is a **negative-polarity item**: a negative or a question has to license it, so ✗ *He has some evidence whatsoever*. In the archaic fused-relative use it takes a bare plural or uncountable ahead of it (*whatsoever things are true*).
**With *of*:** None.
**Pattern:** *no* [noun] *whatsoever* &middot; *not* … *any* [noun] *whatsoever* &middot; *none whatsoever* &middot; *nothing/nobody whatsoever* &middot; archaic: *whatsoever* [noun] [subject] [verb]
**Variants:** *whatever (the same postposed emphasizer, slightly less formal: no reason whatever)*, *at all (the everyday spoken equivalent)*, *whatsoe'er (poetic)*
**Contrast:** against [whatever](#whatever): the postposed emphasizer is the same word in a different slot, and *whatsoever* is the more emphatic and more formal of the two. It is licensed by the same negatives catalogued in [no](06-distributives-negatives.md#no) and [not any](06-distributives-negatives.md#not-any), and it strengthens them rather than negating anything itself. The archaic relative — *whatsoever things are true* — is a fused relative exactly like *whatever*, and today survives only in scripture, hymnody, and legal boilerplate.

Strengthens a negation to 'not the smallest amount of' — or, archaically, 'any … at all that.'

1. There is no evidence *whatsoever* to support that claim.
2. She has no interest *whatsoever* in running for office.
3. I have no doubt *whatsoever* that he meant it.
4. Do you have any objection *whatsoever*?
5. The policy makes no exception *whatsoever* for late filings.
6. *Whatsoever* things are true, think on these things.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [whichsoever](#whichsoever), [not any](06-distributives-negatives.md#not-any), [no](06-distributives-negatives.md#no)

### which (relative)

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ &middot; *WICH* &middot; unreduced even though unstressed; /hwɪtʃ/ for speakers who keep the *wh-* onset
**Русский:** каковой, этот самый — книжн.: подхватывает сказанное (by which time, at which point)
**Type:** relative &middot; **Position:** central, normally inside a fronted prepositional phrase &middot; **Register:** formal; only *in which case* and its siblings are ordinary in everyday American English
**Goes with:** abstract nouns naming a time, point, case, event, or reason, in a **supplementary (nonrestrictive)** clause — *by which time*, *at which point*, *in which case*, *for which reason*. Beyond that closed set the determiner use survives only in legal and older formal prose, where it can take almost any noun that repeats or reclassifies the antecedent: *He made an offer, which offer we declined.*
**With *of*:** [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which) is a different construction — a relative **genitive** that names something the antecedent has, not this determiner. Compare *a house the roof of which leaks* (genitive) with *he left at noon, by which time we had gone* (determiner).
**Pattern:** [clause], [preposition] *which* [noun] [clause] &middot; [clause], *which* [noun] [verb] (legal or archaic) &middot; nonrestrictive only, and a comma is required in front
**Variants:** *in which case*, *at which point*, *by which time*, *for which reason*
**Contrast:** **determiner *which* against pronoun *which*.** The pronoun stands alone and is the everyday relative (*the report, which arrived late*); the determiner has a noun immediately after it (*he left at noon, by which time the deadline had passed*). The determiner cannot introduce a restrictive clause at all: ✗ *the moment which time we left*. Against *that*: American usage reserves *that* for restrictive clauses and comma-*which* for nonrestrictive ones, and since the determiner use is nonrestrictive by nature, *that* is never an option. Against relative *whose*, which marks **possession** rather than repeating the antecedent as a noun.

Carries the antecedent — often a whole clause — forward into a following clause by naming it with a noun.

1. He arrived at noon, by *which* time we had already left.
2. The tenant may object, at *which* point the matter goes to arbitration.
3. She refused the offer, for *which* reason the sale collapsed.
4. They proposed a settlement, *which* offer my client declined.
5. Payment may be late, in *which* event a fee applies.
6. He was in Denver until Friday, during *which* time the office stayed closed.

**See also:** [in which case](#in-which-case), [the which](#the-which), [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [of which](03-possessives.md#of-which), [whose](03-possessives.md#whose)

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈtʃɛvɚ/ &middot; *wich-EV-er* &middot; three syllables, accent on *ev*; the two-word *which ever* (emphatic interrogative) is chiefly British
**Русский:** какой угодно из; какой бы ни — свободный выбор внутри уже известного набора
**Type:** relative (fused relative) and concessive &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable** (*whichever route we take*), **plural countable** (*whichever seats are free*), and, less often, **uncountable** (*whichever paint you prefer*). Like every central determiner it cannot stack with another: ✗ *the whichever route*.
**With *of*:** **Required** before another determiner or a pronoun — *whichever of the two*, *whichever of them*. *Whichever* is a **pronoun** in that construction, not a determiner. Never before a bare plural: ✗ *whichever of routes*.
**Pattern:** *whichever* [noun] [subject] [verb] &middot; *whichever* [noun] [verb] &middot; *whichever of* [the/these + plural noun] &middot; [clause], *whichever* [subject] [verb] (concessive) &middot; *whichever comes first* (fixed contractual tag, pronoun)
**Variants:** *whichsoever (archaic and legal)*, *no matter which (spoken)*, *which ever (two words, emphatic interrogative)*
**Contrast:** ***whichever* against *whatever*** repeats the *which/what* split exactly: *whichever* selects inside a set already fixed (*whichever of these two rooms*), *whatever* leaves it open (*whatever room is free*). Determiner against pronoun: *whichever route* has a noun after it, *take whichever you like* does not. Note the fixed legal tag *whichever comes first*, where *whichever* stands alone as a pronoun. The free-choice use — *use whichever card you like* — sits alongside [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any) in meaning but keeps the fused-relative grammar described here.

'Any one of the known set that' — picks freely inside a set already established, or concedes the choice to someone else.

1. Take *whichever* seat you like.
2. *Whichever* route we take, we'll hit traffic.
3. Use *whichever* card has room on it.
4. *Whichever* candidate wins, the policy won't change.
5. Pick *whichever* two days work for you.
6. *Whichever* of the two offers you accept, get it in writing.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [whichsoever](#whichsoever), [which (interrogative)](#which-interrogative), [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any)

### whichsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪtʃsoʊˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wich-soh-EV-er* &middot; four syllables, accent on *ev*
**Русский:** какой бы ни (из) — устар., юр. усиленная форма whichever
**Type:** relative (fused relative) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** **archaic** — it survives only in legal boilerplate, scripture, and deliberate period style
**Goes with:** singular and plural countable nouns inside a free relative clause — *whichsoever party*, *whichsoever terms*. Uncountables are possible but vanishingly rare.
**With *of*:** *Whichsoever of the* + plural noun, as a pronoun; *of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun, exactly as with *whichever*.
**Pattern:** *whichsoever* [noun] [subject] [verb] &middot; *whichsoever of* [the + plural noun] &middot; [clause] *whichsoever* [noun] [verb]
**Variants:** *whichever (the living form)*, *whatsoever*, *whosoever*, *whosesoever* and the two-word *whose soever* (the genitive members — catalogued at [whosever](03-possessives.md#whosever)), *whensoever*
**Contrast:** an intensified *whichever*, built the same way *whatsoever* is built from *whatever*. In modern American English it is not a stylistic option, it is a **period marker** — use *whichever*. It is entered here so a reader can recognize it in a deed, an insurance policy, or the King James Bible.

Archaic emphatic form of *whichever* — 'any one at all of the set that.'

1. *Whichsoever* party breaches first shall bear the costs.
2. The estate passes to *whichsoever* heir survives the longer.
3. *Whichsoever* terms the arbitrator sets are binding on both.
4. He vowed to follow *whichsoever* road led home.
5. *Whichsoever* of the two dates is chosen, notice must be given.
6. *Whichsoever* course ye take, take it wholeheartedly.

**See also:** [whichever](#whichever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whosever](03-possessives.md#whosever)

## Notes and tricky cases

**The determiner-or-pronoun test.** Every word in this file can be placed by asking one question: does a noun follow it immediately?

| Word | Determiner (a noun follows) | Pronoun (nothing follows) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *which* | *Which* **gate** is it? | *Which* is it? |
| *what* | *What* **time** is it? | *What* is it? |
| *whose* | *Whose* **coat** is this? | *Whose* is this? |
| *how many* | *How many* **boxes** are left? | *How many* are left? |
| *how much* | *How much* **money** is left? | *How much* is left? |
| *whatever* | Take *whatever* **seat** you like. | Take *whatever* you like. |
| *whichever* | Use *whichever* **card** works. | Use *whichever* works. |

Adding *of* always tips the word into the pronoun column, and the noun inside the *of*-phrase then keeps its own determiner: *which of **the** books*, *how many of **my** cousins*.

**The exclamative slot, all four fillers at once.** They compete for the same position in front of the article and obey the same rule.

| Filler | Singular countable | Plural or uncountable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *what* | ✓ *What a mess!* &nbsp; ✗ *What mess!* | ✓ *What lies!* &nbsp; ✓ *What nonsense!* |
| *such* | ✓ *such a mess* &nbsp; ✗ *such mess* | ✓ *such lies* &nbsp; ✓ *such nonsense* |
| *quite a* | ✓ *quite a mess* | ✓ *quite a few lies* &nbsp; ✗ *quite lies* |
| *rather a* | ✓ *rather a mess* | ✓ *rather a lot of lies* |
| *how* (adverb, not a determiner) | ✗ *How a mess!* → ✓ *How messy it was!* | ✗ *How lies!* |

**Countability, at a glance.** The *much/many* split runs through the interrogatives and the fused relatives alike, and it is the same split every time.

| Uncountable | Plural countable |
| --- | --- |
| *how much* time | *how many* times |
| *how little* time | *how few* times |
| *how much less* time | *how many fewer* times |
| *how much more* time | *how many more* times |
| *what little* time I had | *what few* times I tried |
| *however much* time | *however many* times |
| *however little* time | *however few* times |

**Verb agreement follows the noun, not the *wh-* word.** *How many* and *how few* take a **plural** verb because the noun they determine is plural — *How many people **are** coming?*, *How few options **remain**?* — while *how much* and *how little* take a **singular** — *How much money **is** left?*, *How little evidence **was** there?* *What*, *which*, *whatever*, and *whichever* simply inherit their noun's number: *Which gate **is** ours?* against *Which gates **are** open?* In the *of*-partitives the controller is the noun **inside** the *of*-phrase, never the *wh-* word in front of it: *Which of the files **were** deleted?*, *How much of the report **is** finished?*, *How many of them **have** replied?* The fused relatives behave the same way — *What few records survive **are** in the archive*, *What little evidence remains **is** inconclusive.*

**Which or what — the practical rule.** Use *which* when the options are countable, visible, or already listed, and *what* when the field is open. Where the alternatives are spelled out in the question itself, *which* is effectively obligatory: ✓ *Which do you want, tea or coffee?* Where no set is presupposed at all, *what* is the natural choice: *What music do you like?* In the wide middle ground both are grammatical, and the choice only reflects how bounded the speaker takes the options to be.

**Embedded questions keep statement order.** This is the commonest error made with every interrogative determiner in the file: ✓ *I asked which gate it left from*, ✗ *I asked which gate did it leave from*; ✓ *She wants to know how much it costs*, ✗ *She wants to know how much does it cost*.

**Nonstandard and regional forms — recognize them, do not imitate them in writing.** *What all*, and its relatives *who all*, *which all*, and *whose all*, are systematic in Southern and Midland American English but regionally marked everywhere else. The relative pronoun *what* in *the book what I read* is standard in several vernaculars and not in edited American English; use *that* or *which*. *Whatsoever* and *whichsoever* in their relative senses are archaic rather than dialectal — legal and biblical, not wrong. So are [the which](#the-which) and [what manner of](#what-manner-of), which belong to Chaucer, Shakespeare, and the King James Bible rather than to any living American register, and [such other](#such-other), which is not archaic at all but is confined to drafted documents. One further archaism gets **no entry in this file**: *whether* in the old sense 'which of two,' as in *Whether of them twain did the will of his father?* and *Whether is greater, the gold, or the temple?* In every text a modern reader will actually meet, that *whether* either stands alone or takes an *of*-partitive — which, by the test above, makes it a **pronoun**, not a determiner. The attributive use died out in Middle English, and the living replacement is *which of the two*.
