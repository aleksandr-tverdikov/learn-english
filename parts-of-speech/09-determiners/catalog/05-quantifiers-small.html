Quantifiers of small quantity and partial amount#

The determiners that answer how much? and how many? with a small number — and the ones where a single letter, the article a, flips a complaint into a reassurance.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

Two things unite this group, and both are traps. The first is countability: English keeps two parallel sets here, one for things you count (few, fewer, fewest, a couple of, several, one or two) and one for things you measure (little, less, least, a bit of, a touch of, a modicum of). Crossing them produces the two most recognizable learner errors in the language — ✗ few water, ✗ less people — so the Goes with line is the line to read first in every entry below. The second trap is the indefinite article, which is not decoration here but a semantic switch: few and little are negative and mean "almost none, and that is a problem," while a few and a little are positive and mean "some, and that is enough." Drop the a and you have changed the message from reassurance to complaint.

One warning about scope. Any, no, all, both, each, and every are determiners of coverage rather than of amount and live with the distributives and negatives; half, a third, and a fraction of state a proportion and live with the numerals and fractions; the large-quantity partners much, many, a lot of, and plenty of are one file back in quantifiers of large quantity. Some is catalogued here, because its job is to name a partial, unspecified amount. And beware the two phrases in this file's own vocabulary that mean the opposite of what they look like: quite a few and quite a bit of are large.

A word on weak forms, because every entry below is affected by it. Determiners are almost never stressed, and the small ones reduce hard in ordinary American speech. The a of a few, a little, and a bit of is /ə/, never /eɪ/. The in the odd and the occasional is /ði/ before a vowel sound and /ðə/ everywhere else. Some is /səm/ as a quantifier of amount and only straightens out to /sʌm/ in the "a certain" and admiring senses. And the of of every partitive phrase in this file reduces to /ə/: a bit of sugar comes out uh BIT uh SHUG-er, a couple of days comes out uh KUP-luh days. The strong forms are not wrong, but a speaker who uses them everywhere sounds like someone reading aloud.

The core four: few, a few, little, a little#

These four carry most of the work of the category, and between them they cover the whole countability grid: few and a few take plural countable nouns, little and a little take uncountable ones. Within each pair, the article decides the attitude. Note also that in ordinary American speech all four sound somewhat formal on their own — a native speaker is far more likely to say not many, not much, or hardly any, and to save bare few and bare little for writing or for deliberate emphasis.

  3. A few of the seats were still empty when the lights went down.
  4. She knows a few of them from college.

  5. She spent the little money she had on a bus ticket.

The comparison series: fewer, fewest, least, less#

The small quantifiers have suppletive comparatives: few → fewer → fewest for things you count, little → less → least for things you measure. The famous prescriptive rule — fewer with plural countables, less with uncountables — is worth learning, because American editors, style guides, and teachers enforce it. But an honest note is owed: it was not a rule of the language. Robert Baker floated it as a personal preference in 1770, and later handbooks hardened his preference into law; less with plural nouns has been in continuous use since Old English. Three things follow. First, less is fully standard even in careful writing with plural measures treated as a single amount — less than three miles, less than $50, less than five years, less than ten pounds, less than 40 percent. Second, less with ordinary plural nouns (less people, less options, 10 items or less) is extremely common in American speech and on American signage and is still marked as an error in edited prose. Third, the safe advice for a learner is asymmetric: write fewer with plural nouns, expect to hear less, and never use fewer with an uncountable noun — ✗ fewer money is wrong for everyone, with no usage debate behind it.

fewer#

Pronunciation
/ˈfjuɚ/ · FYOO-er
Русский
меньше (по числу) — сравнительная степень few, только с исчисляемыми
Type
quantifier, comparative
Position
central — fewer fills the central determiner slot itself and does not stack under another one: ✗ the fewer errors, ✗ my fewer options, ✗ these fewer meetings. The the of the fewer options we have, the better looks like a counterexample and is not: that the is the adverbial correlative the, not a determiner, which is why the frame needs its second clause to be grammatical at all.
Register
neutral; the form careful writing requires
Goes with
plural countable nouns only — fewer errors, fewer cars. Never an uncountable noun (✗ fewer money, ✗ fewer information, ✗ fewer traffic) and never a singular countable noun. This is the one direction of the fewer/less question with no usage debate: fewer simply cannot take a mass noun.

With of: of is required before another determiner or a pronoun — fewer of the errors, fewer of them. No of before a bare plural: fewer errors.

Pattern
fewer [plural countable] · fewer [plural countable] than [phrase/clause] · fewer of [the/these + plural] · no fewer than [number] · far/many/considerably fewer [plural]
Contrast
fewer counts, less measures: fewer calories but less fat, fewer employees but less staff. The superlative partner is fewest, and unlike fewer it never takes than. One idiom runs the other way and is fixed: one less thing to worry about is what Americans say, not one fewer thing. And do not confuse fewer with less in the sense "minus" — ten less two is arithmetic, not quantity.
Variants
no fewer than, far fewer, fewer and fewer, one fewer, the fewer … the fewer

Names a smaller number than some other number, stated or implied. As a pronoun it stands alone: Fewer are applying this year.

  1. There were fewer students in the program than the year before.
  2. We need fewer meetings and more time to actually build things.
  3. Fewer of the returning players made the roster this season.
  4. No fewer than twelve agencies had signed off on the plan.
  5. Far fewer people commute downtown now than in 2019.
  6. Each revision left us with fewer and fewer open questions.

Small and moderate quantities#

Almost everything in this section counts: these take plural countable nouns and refuse uncountables. Several members sit outside that pattern, and it is worth knowing which way each of them breaks. A handful of, a smattering of, and a sample of take uncountables in their literal senses as well as plurals — a handful of rice, a smattering of Spanish, a sample of the well water. A subset of is the technical member and takes whatever the surrounding set happens to be made of. Some belongs to both grids at once, which is why it serves as the neutral baseline the rest are measured against. And the odd and the occasional are the two outliers that demand a singular countable noun, because what they count is occasions rather than things. Most of the rest are partitive phrases — an indefinite article, a measure noun, and of — that have hardened into quantifiers, which is why of is not optional in them the way it is in all (of) the.

Near-zero: the almost-nothing quantifiers#

These push a small quantity down toward none without quite reaching it. Most are negative-polarity items: they behave like negatives, which means the verb stays positive. Write There's hardly any left, not ✗ There isn't hardly any left; We have barely any time, not ✗ We don't have barely any time. Doubling the negation is negative concord, which is systematic and rule-governed in African American English, Southern American English, and many other varieties and is not a sign of confusion — but it is nonstandard in edited written English, and hardly no, scarcely no, and barely no will be corrected in school and at work. Learners should recognize these forms and write the single-negative version.

This is also where the file's register spread is widest, and the labels matter more than the meanings, because most of these phrases mean roughly the same thing. A lack of is plain modern English that anyone says. A dearth of is formal and journalistic. An iota of is current and emphatic. A scintilla of is a legal term of art with a doctrine attached, met in briefs and opinions and essentially nowhere else. A want of survives mainly inside fixed phrases such as for want of a better word. And a jot of and a whit of are archaic — Bible-and-Dickens words that Americans read and do not say. Write a lack of, hardly any, or a shred of; recognize the rest.

Adjectives that quantify#

The last five entries open a noun phrase and feel like determiners, but they are adjectives, and one test settles it: a central determiner cannot follow another determiner, and these can — a de minimis amount, the minimal effort required, the scant evidence we have, a slight delay, the sparse attendance. Most of them also take degree modifiers (fairly minimal, very slight, rather sparse) and stand after a linking verb (The evidence is sparse, the harm was de minimis), neither of which any determiner in this file can do. They are catalogued here because learners reach for the same slot with them, and because the countability restrictions still matter.

The same test disqualifies a longer list of words that are often mistaken for quantifiers of small amount and are not catalogued in this file at all: meager, paltry, negligible, nominal, insufficient, modest. Each one describes a quantity instead of stating one, and each sits happily behind a determiner — the meager rations, a paltry sum, the negligible difference — which is exactly what a central determiner can never do. They are adjectives, and they belong with the adjectives.

scanty, scantily (adverb), a scant (+ unit noun), scant regard, scant attention, scant comfort

Describes a quantity that falls short of what is needed or expected — the formal register's version of bare little, and one of the few quantifying words that takes countable and uncountable nouns alike.

  1. There is scant evidence that the program worked.
  2. The report paid scant attention to what any of it would cost.
  3. That is scant comfort to the families still waiting for answers.
  4. They did the whole job with scant resources and no permanent staff. (plural countable)
  5. He has shown scant regard for the deadline since the day he took over.
  6. Add a scant teaspoon of baking soda, not a heaping one. (singular — "barely a full")
See also
minimal, slight, sparse, little, precious few / precious little

slight#

Pronunciation
/slaɪt/ · SLYTE
Русский
незначительный, лёгкий — малая степень или мера чего-либо
Type
adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner
Position
attributive after a determiner (a slight delay, the slightest chance) or predicative (the difference was slight)
Register
neutral
Goes with
uncountable nouns without an article (slight improvement, slight discomfort) and singular countable nouns with an article (a slight delay, a slight chance). With plural countable nouns it is possible but uncommon (slight variations). Because it is an adjective, it cannot replace the article the way a determiner can: ✗ slight delay held us up → ✓ a slight delay held us up.

With of: Not used — this word takes no of-phrase.

Pattern
a slight [singular countable noun] · slight [uncountable noun] · the slightest [noun] · not the slightest [noun] · [noun] is slight (predicative)
Contrast
slight measures degree or intensity, not bulk — a slight delay is short, a slight accent is faint, a slight chance is improbable. That is what separates it from minimal, which measures quantity, and from little, which is a determiner and takes no article. Its superlative slightest is a negative-polarity favorite: not the slightest interest, without the slightest hesitation, hasn't the slightest idea. Keep it apart from the unrelated noun and verb slight meaning "an insult" / "to snub."
Variants
slightly (adverb), slighter, the slightest, not the slightest, the merest

Describes a small degree or extent of something — faint, mild, or barely appreciable.

  1. There's been a slight delay with the connecting flight.
  2. She has a slight accent that's hard to place.
  3. We saw slight improvement after the second week.
  4. There's a slight chance of snow tonight.
  5. He hasn't the slightest idea what he's agreeing to.
  6. The difference between the two samples was slight.
  7. There were slight variations between the two batches. (plural countable — possible, but uncommon)
See also
minimal, scant, sparse, a hint of, a shade of, a little

sparse#

Pronunciation
/spɑrs/ · SPARSS
Русский
редкий, разреженный — малочисленный и разбросанный по площади
Type
adjective with quantifying force — not a true determiner
Position
attributive (sparse vegetation, the sparse crowd) or predicative (the data are sparse)
Register
neutral, leaning descriptive and technical
Goes with
uncountable nouns (sparse vegetation, sparse data, sparse attendance) and plural countable nouns (sparse crowds, sparse trees). With a singular countable noun it needs an article: a sparse crowd.

With of: Not used — this word takes no of-phrase.

Pattern
sparse [uncountable / plural countable] · [a/the] sparse [noun] · sparsely populated (adverb) · [noun] is sparse (predicative) · sparser / more sparse
Contrast
sparse adds distribution to smallness: the quantity is small and thinly spread over an area, which is why it fits vegetation, hair, population, attendance, and data points, and why it sounds wrong for a compact quantity (sparse money is odd). Among the adjectives here, minimal measures amount, slight measures degree, and sparse measures density. Its nearest determiner-side relative is a smattering of, which does the same scattering work as a partitive phrase. The opposite is dense.
Variants
sparsely (adverb), sparser, sparsity / sparseness, thin, scattered

Describes something small in quantity and thinly distributed over an area or a set.

  1. The vegetation gets sparse above the tree line.
  2. Attendance was sparse for a Tuesday matinee.
  3. Data on the early years is sparse and unreliable.
  4. He ran a hand through his sparse hair.
  5. Sparse crowds gathered along the parade route.
  6. The northern counties are sparsely populated.
See also
minimal, scant, slight, a smattering of, a trickle of
