Distributive, universal, and negative determiners#

The determiners that quantify over a whole set — covering it one member at a time, all at once, two at a time, or not at all.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

What unites this group is that none of its members states an amount. They state coverage — how much of a set the sentence reaches. Each walks the set one member at a time, every and all take the whole of it, both, either, and neither work over a set of exactly two, and no, none of, and not any reach none of it at all. The trap running through the group is number: each, every, either, and neither all mean something plural but take a singular noun and a singular verb — each student has, every window was, neither plan works — while both and all take the plural. Getting that backwards produces ✗ each students and ✗ every windows, the most common errors in the entire determiner system.

The second rule running through the group is of, and it is worth memorizing as three lists. All and both make it optional before another determiner (all (of) the students, both (of) my parents) but required before a pronoun (all of them, never ✗ all them). Each, either, neither, and any require it before anything already determined (each of the students, ✗ each my cousins). And every and no forbid it outright — there is no ✗ every of the students and no ✗ no of the students — which is the entire reason every one of, every bit of, and none of exist as separate headwords.

Negation has rules of its own here. Standard American English allows one negative marker per clause, so no and any never combine: ✗ I have no any money is impossible in every variety of English, and the two grammatical repairs are I have no money and I don't have any money. Doubling the negative onto both the verb and the determiner (I don't have no money) is negative concord — rule-governed and completely systematic in African American English, Southern vernacular, and many other spoken varieties, but stigmatized in edited American writing. Recognize it, label it, and keep it out of a cover letter. Distinguish it, too, from partial negation: not all and not every leave some members standing, where no and none of leave none.

Finally, most of these words are also pronouns. The determiner precedes a noun; the pronoun stands alone. All the tickets sold out (determiner) against All is well (pronoun); Each student paid against They each paid. The pure pronouns of this family — everyone, everything, no one, nobody, nothing, and bare none — are never determiners and live with the pronouns. The correlative pairs both … and, either … or, and neither … nor are conjunctions and live with the conjunctions. And several near neighbors are catalogued elsewhere in this dictionary: some, the polarity partner of any, and hardly any sit with the small quantifiers; much, many, most sit with the large ones; and the wh- free-choice words sit with the interrogatives and relatives.

Universal and distributive#

The words that take in a whole set — either sweeping it up at once or walking through it member by member. Watch the number agreement in every entry: all and both are plural, everything else here is singular. One near neighbor is deliberately absent. Each additional and every additional are not determiners in their own right but each or every plus the ordinary postdeterminer additional, which keeps its full descriptive meaning — "one more, beyond the base" — and is catalogued with the numerals at further, additional. The test is that it can be swapped for another adjective without breaking anything (each extra passenger, each remaining passenger), which is exactly what the bleached intensifiers of every single, every last, and every blessed cannot do.

all#

Pronunciation
/ɔl/ · AWL · /ɑl/ for the many Americans who merge cot and caught; there is no weak form of all itself, but the /l/ vocalizes before a consonant and all right reduces casually to [ɔˈɹaɪt] or even [aɪt]; in the partitive the of is weak, /əv/ reducing to /ə/, so all of the runs to [ˈɔlə ðə] and all of them to [ˈɔlə ðəm]
Русский
весь, все — охватывает всё множество или всё количество целиком, без остатка
Type
quantifier (universal)
Position
predeterminer before another determiner (all the money); central determiner before a bare noun (all children)
Register
neutral
Goes with
plural countable nouns (all students) and uncountable nouns (all information); a singular countable only when another determiner follows (all my life, all this trouble) or the noun names a span of time (all day, all summer). Never a bare singular countable: ✗ all book, ✗ all student.

With of: Optional before another determiner — all (of) the students, all (of) my cousins. Required before a personal pronoun — all of them, all of us; ✗ all them. Impossible before a bare noun — all children, ✗ all of children.

Pattern
all [plural/uncountable noun] · all (of) [the/my/these + noun] · all of [them/us/you] · [plural subject] + auxiliary + all + verb (floated) · all [day/week/year]
Contrast
all covers three or more, or an undivided mass; both covers exactly two. Against every, the difference is grammatical rather than semantic: all takes a plural noun and a plural verb and sees the group as one body (all the students were notified), while every takes a singular noun and a singular verb and runs down the list (every student was notified). Against not all, watch the scope — All the seats weren't taken is ambiguous in speech and should be rewritten as Not all the seats were taken (some were empty) or None of the seats were taken (all were empty). As a determiner all precedes a noun; standing alone it is a pronoun (All is well, That's all).
Variants
all of, all the, floated all, all day / all week / all year, all but

Sweeps in the entire set or the entire quantity, with nothing left out. The verb agrees with the noun all is quantifying, not with all itself — All the money is gone but All the coins are gone. Like both and each, all can float away from its subject and land next to the verb: All the guests have left → The guests have all left.

  1. All the students passed the exam.
  2. She spent all her savings on the down payment.
  3. All of them showed up an hour early.
  4. All this trouble started with one missed email. — a singular countable, grammatical only because this follows
  5. We waited all day for a callback from the insurance company.
  6. All information on this form is confidential.
  7. The guests have all gone home.
  8. All children learn to read at their own pace.

all and singular#

Pronunciation
/ˌɔl ən ˈsɪŋɡjəlɚ/ · awl-uhn-SING-gyuh-lur · and takes its weak form /ən/ or a syllabic /n̩/, giving [ˌɔl n̩ ˈsɪŋɡjəlɚ]; all keeps /ɔl/, or /ɑl/ for the many Americans who merge cot and caught; the main stress lands on singular, whose /j/ is often dropped in fast speech to give [ˈsɪŋɡəlɚ]
Русский
устар. юр. все и каждый в отдельности — формула из купчих, завещаний и описей
Type
quantifier (universal, emphatic legal doublet)
Position
predeterminer — it stands in front of the central determiner, exactly where all stands in all the goods
Register
archaic legal boilerplate. An American meets it in deeds, wills, probate inventories, admiralty writs, and old insurance policies, and essentially nowhere else; plain-language drafting guides delete it on sight
Goes with
plural countable nouns, almost always inside a legal doublet of their own — all and singular the goods and chattels, all and singular the lands and tenements. It takes an uncountable noun only in a few frozen formulas (all and singular the personal property). Never a bare singular countable: ✗ all and singular the house, ✗ all and singular house.

With of: Never — ✗ all and singular of the goods. The doublet sits directly on top of the, exactly as predeterminer all does; the of form a modern reader expects, all of the goods, is the rewrite rather than a variant.

Pattern
all and singular the [plural noun] · all and singular the [noun] and [noun] (the doublet doubled again) · to have and to hold all and singular the [noun] · all and singular, the [noun] (with the comma, as older deeds set it)
Contrast
Singular here does not mean "one" or "unusual." It is the Latin singuli, "one by one," arriving through Law French, so the phrase means "the whole set, and every item in it taken separately" — which makes it all and each welded into one predeterminer. It is the courtroom ancestor of the two doublets already in this file: any and all, which does the same belt-and-suspenders work in modern contracts, and each and every, which does it in speech. Against all and sundry, which looks parallel but is a pronoun meaning "everybody" and takes no noun at all (the party was open to all and sundry), all and singular always has a noun phrase after it; see sundry.
Variants
all and singular the, all and every (older), any and all, each and every, all and sundry (a pronoun — a different word)

An archaic legal predeterminer meaning "the entire set, and each member of it individually." It exists to defeat two opposite readings at once — that a grant covers the collection but not the pieces, or the pieces but not the collection — which is the same anxiety that produces any and all. The verb agrees with the plural noun, not with singular: All and singular the parcels are conveyed. Modern American drafters write all and stop.

  1. The deed conveys all and singular the lands and tenements described in Schedule A. — a conveyance
  2. I give and bequeath all and singular my goods and chattels to my sister. — a will
  3. The executor filed an inventory of all and singular the personal property of the estate. — probate
  4. The policy insures all and singular the vessels named in the endorsement. — marine insurance, where the phrase is oldest
  5. To have and to hold all and singular the premises, with the appurtenances thereto belonging. — the habendum clause of an old deed
  6. A modern contract says all the property described above and loses nothing. — the plain-language rewrite

both#

Pronunciation
/boʊθ/ · BOHTH · the final /θ/ is often lost before a following consonant in fast speech: both sides → [boʊ ˈsaɪdz]; the of of the partitive takes its weak /əv/ and usually drops to /ə/, giving both of them [ˈboʊθə ðəm]
Русский
оба, обе — двое как пара; глагол всегда во множественном числе
Type
quantifier (universal, dual)
Position
predeterminer before another determiner (both the twins, both my parents); central determiner before a bare plural (both hands)
Register
neutral
Goes with
plural countable nouns only, and only when the set has exactly two — both hands, both candidates. Never a singular countable (✗ both hand), never an uncountable (✗ both furniture, ✗ both information), and never a set of three or more (✗ both of the five finalists).

With of: Optional before another determiner — both (of) the twins, both (of) these windows. Required before a personal pronoun in this order — both of them, both of us; ✗ both them, though the reversed order them both is perfectly good (I saw them both). Not used before a bare plural — both parents, ✗ both of parents.

Pattern
both [plural countable noun] · both (of) [the/my/these + plural noun] · both of [them/us/you] · [plural subject] + are/have + both + … (floated)
Contrast
both is the plural member of the dual set, and the three of them split the verb neatly: both takes the two together and a plural verb (Both plans work), either takes one of the two and a singular verb (Either plan works), neither takes zero of the two and a singular verb (Neither plan works). Against all, both is capped at two — for three or more, all is the only choice. The correlative conjunction both … and joins two items and is a different word class entirely. As a determiner both precedes a noun; standing alone it is a pronoun (Both are broken).
Variants
both of, both the, floated both, them both / us both

Refers to the two members of a two-member set together, as a pair. The verb is always plural, even though the sense is "each of the two" — Both of my parents are retired. Both floats like all and each: Both of my parents are retired → My parents are both retired. It sits badly with a negative — Both of them didn't come is ambiguous and avoided; say Neither of them came for zero, or Only one of them came for one.

  1. Both windows in the front room leak.
  2. Both of my parents grew up in Cleveland.
  3. She broke both her wrists in the fall.
  4. Both candidates dodged the question.
  5. I've read both of them, and neither is any good.
  6. My sisters are both teachers.
See also
all, either, neither, each, both … and

each#

Pronunciation
/itʃ/ · EECH · the /tʃ/ links straight into a following /j/, so each year runs together as [ˈitʃɪɹ]; the obligatory of is unstressed and reduces from /əv/ to /ə/, so each of the comes out [ˈitʃə ðə]
Русский
каждый (по отдельности) — перебирает членов множества поодиночке
Type
distributive
Position
central determiner; also floats to a position beside the verb, and postposes as an adverb (ten dollars each)
Register
neutral
Goes with
singular countable nouns only — each student, each page, each day. Never a plural countable (✗ each students), never an uncountable (✗ each water, ✗ each advice). To reach a plural noun, each must go through of: each of the students.

With of: Required before any noun phrase that already has a determiner, and before every pronoun — each of the students, each of my cousins, each of them. ✗ each my cousins and ✗ each them are ungrammatical. Bare each + singular noun takes no of: ✗ each of student.

Pattern
each [singular countable noun] · each of [the/my/these + plural noun] · each of [them/us/you] · [plural subject] + each + [plural verb] (floated) · [noun] + cost/weigh … each (postposed)
Contrast
each against every is the central distinction of this file. Each looks at members individually, one at a time; every looks at the set collectively. Each works with a set of two, every needs three or more. Each takes of and can float away from its subject; every can do neither. Almost, nearly, and practically modify every, not each — ✓ almost every day, ✗ almost each day. As a determiner each precedes a noun; standing alone it is a pronoun (Each was inspected).
Variants
each of, each one, each and every, floated each, postposed each (a dollar each)

Picks out the members of a group one by one, treating them separately. The noun and the verb are both singular — Each student has a locker — but when each floats away from a plural subject and lands next to the verb, the verb goes back to plural: The students each have a locker, We each have our own copy. Each also survives at the end of a phrase as an adverb of price or weight: The tickets are twenty dollars each.

  1. Each student receives a locker and a combination.
  2. Each of the four applicants was interviewed twice.
  3. She wrote a note to each of them.
  4. The winners each got a check for five hundred dollars.
  5. Each day brought a new complication.
  6. Limes are seventy-nine cents each this week.
See also
every, each and every, either, both, every one of

each and every#

Pronunciation
/ˌitʃ ən ˈɛvri/ · eech-uhn-EV-ree · and reduces to the weak form /ən/ or to a syllabic /n̩/; the main stress lands on every
Русский
каждый без исключения — эмфатический сплав each и every, «все до одного»
Type
distributive (emphatic)
Position
central determiner
Register
emphatic; natural in speech and rhetoric, and routinely cut by copy editors as redundant
Goes with
singular countable nouns only — each and every student. Never a plural (✗ each and every students), never an uncountable (✗ each and every water), exactly like both of its parts.

With of: Not with the bare phrase — ✗ each and every of the students. The partitive is each and every one of the students, with one inserted to carry the of.

Pattern
each and every [singular countable noun] · each and every one of [the/my + plural noun or pronoun]
Contrast
Semantically it adds nothing to each or to every — it is a rhetorical doubling that means "with no exceptions whatsoever, and I mean it." Grammatically it inherits every's behavior rather than each's: it cannot float (✗ The students each and every paid) and cannot take a bare of. Its counterpart at the opposite end of the register scale is the legal doublet any and all.
Variants
each and every one of, every single, every last

An emphatic fusion of each and every, used when the speaker wants to insist that nothing was skipped. The verb is singular. It is common in speeches, sales pitches, and coaching talk, and because it draws attention to itself it wears out fast in writing.

  1. Each and every employee will get the same bonus this year.
  2. I checked each and every box on that form twice.
  3. Each and every one of you needs to sign in at the front desk.
  4. She answers each and every email the same day.
  5. Each and every seat in the theater was taken.
  6. He thanked each and every volunteer by name.
See also
each, every, every single, every last, any and all

either#

Pronunciation
/ˈiðɚ/ · EE-thur · /ˈaɪðɚ/ (EYE-thur) is fully standard, but /ˈiðɚ/ is the majority American pronunciation — the reverse of the British preference; whichever vowel a speaker uses, they normally use the same one in neither; the required of is weak, so either of them runs to [ˈiðɚə ðəm]
Русский
любой из двух; в оборотах on either side — и тот и другой, с обеих сторон
Type
distributive (dual)
Position
central determiner
Register
neutral
Goes with
singular countable nouns only, and only from a set of exactly two — either route, either parent. Never a plural (✗ either routes), never an uncountable (✗ either milk), and never a set of three or more — for three or more the word is any.

With of: Required before a determined noun phrase or a pronoun — either of the routes, either of them. ✗ either the routes and ✗ either them are ungrammatical. Note that the noun after of is plural while the verb stays singular: Either of the plans works.

Pattern
either [singular countable noun] · either of [the/my/these + plural noun] · either of [them/us/you] · on either side of [noun]
Contrast
either is the positive dual and neither its negation: Either answer is acceptable against Neither answer is acceptable. Against any, the difference is set size — either for two, any for three or more (Either day works for a weekend, Any day works for a week). Against both, either takes one of the two where both takes them together. As a determiner either precedes a noun; standing alone it is a pronoun (Either will do), and either at the end of a negative sentence is an adverb (I don't like it either) — three different word classes for one spelling.
Variants
either of, either one, either way, on either side

Two distinct uses. (1) One or the other of two, it doesn't matter which — the ordinary sense: Either route gets you there. (2) Each of two, both — a fossil sense surviving in fixed spatial phrases: trees on either side of the road means both sides, not one of them. Because sense (2) is still live, either is occasionally ambiguous, and careful writers switch to each or both when they mean sense (2) anywhere other than either side, either end, either hand. The verb is singular in careful writing — Either of the plans works — though work is very common in speech.

  1. Either route will get you to the airport by six.
  2. Either of these applicants would do the job well.
  3. There were cornfields on either side of the highway.
  4. I'm happy with either answer.
  5. You can pay in cash or by card — either one is fine.
  6. Did either of them call you back?

every#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛvri/ · EV-ree · two syllables in ordinary American speech; the careful three-syllable /ˈɛvəri/ exists but sounds deliberate
Русский
каждый, всякий — всё множество разом; существительное и глагол в ед. ч.
Type
distributive (universal)
Position
central determiner
Register
neutral
Goes with
singular countable nouns only — every window, every chance. Never a plural countable (✗ every windows), never an uncountable (✗ every water, ✗ every advice — for those, see every bit of). The one systematic exception is the frequency pattern, where every takes a numeral plus a plural: every three days, every ten minutes, every few weeks, every two years.

With of: Never. ✗ every of the students does not exist in English. The repair is every one of the students for plural countables and every bit of the paint for uncountables — which is precisely why those two phrases exist.

Pattern
every [singular countable noun] · every [numeral] [plural time noun] (frequency) · every one of [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] · almost/nearly/practically every [singular noun]
Contrast
every against each is the distinction to learn: every views the set collectively and wants at least three members; each views the members individually and works with two. Every takes no of and cannot float (✗ The students every arrived); each does both. Every accepts almost, nearly, and practically; each does not. Against all, every is singular where all is plural — every student was notified = all the students were notified. Against not every, note that negation is partial, not total.
Variants
every single, every last, every other, each and every, every one of, every bit of

Covers the entire membership of a set of three or more, viewed as a complete sweep rather than as separate individuals. Both the noun and the verb are singular — Every seat is taken — but the pronoun that refers back is normally singular they in modern American usage: Every applicant should bring their own laptop. Every is the base for a whole family of emphatic and alternating phrases: every single, every last, every other, each and every.

  1. Every window on the block was broken.
  2. She checks her email every ten minutes.
  3. Every applicant has to submit two references.
  4. Almost every restaurant on that street closed during the pandemic.
  5. I've read every book he's written.
  6. Every one of those excuses is a lie.

every bit of#

Pronunciation
/ˌɛvri ˈbɪt əv/ · ev-ree-BIT-uv · of takes its weak form /əv/ and often reduces further to /ə/, giving [ˌɛvri ˈbɪɾə]; the stress sits on bit
Русский
весь до последней капли — способ применить every к неисчисляемому существительному
Type
distributive (universal, partitive)
Position
central determiner heading a partitive phrase — every determines bit, and the of-phrase supplies the real noun
Register
neutral, mildly emphatic
Goes with
uncountable nouns, reached through of — every bit of evidence, every bit of the paint, every bit of energy. It also takes a singular countable construed as a mass (every bit of that old house). Never a plural countable: ✗ every bit of the students — that is every one of the students.

With of: Of is required; carrying the of is the entire function of bit. ✗ every bit evidence is impossible, and so is ✗ every evidence, which is why the phrase exists.

Pattern
every bit of [uncountable noun] · every bit of [the/my/this + uncountable noun] · every bit of [it/this/that] · every last bit of [uncountable noun]
Contrast
every bit of is to uncountable nouns what every one of is to plural countables: both exist because every can take neither of nor a non-singular noun. Against all of, the difference is insistence — all of the paint reports the quantity, every bit of the paint insists that none was left. Do not confuse the determiner with the adverbial every bit as (every bit as good as the original), which modifies an adjective and quantifies nothing.
Variants
every last bit of, every single bit of, every ounce of, every scrap of, every drop of

The partitive that lets every quantify something uncountable, insisting that the whole of it was covered with nothing left over. The verb stays singular, agreeing with bit: Every bit of the evidence was thrown out.

  1. Every bit of evidence pointed the same direction.
  2. She used every bit of the fabric and had nothing left over.
  3. He put every bit of energy he had into that last mile.
  4. We scraped every bit of ice off the windshield.
  5. Every bit of the money went to the shelter.
  6. I meant every bit of it.

every blessed#

Pronunciation
/ˌɛvri ˈblɛsɪd/ · ev-ree-BLESS-id · blessed has two syllables here, not one — the adjective /ˈblɛsɪd/, never the past-tense /blɛst/ of he blessed the meal. The stress moves onto blessed, which is where the exasperation lives; there is no weak form, because the whole point of the word is that it is leaned on
Русский
устар., разг. каждый распроклятый — эмфатическое every с досадой; blessed вместо брани
Type
distributive (emphatic, euphemistic)
Position
central determiner — every fills the slot and blessed is a fixed intensifier with no religious content
Register
dated, folksy, and emphatic. An American meets it in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century fiction, in older rural and Southern speech, and from grandparents; in current writing it reads as deliberately quaint rather than wrong
Goes with
singular countable nouns only — every blessed day, every blessed one. Never a plural (✗ every blessed days), never an uncountable (✗ every blessed money), following every exactly. In practice it attaches to a short list of nouns and does not spread much beyond it: day, night, time, minute, one, thing, word, penny.

With of: Not with the bare phrase — ✗ every blessed of them. The partitive is every blessed one of: every blessed one of them.

Pattern
every blessed [singular time noun] · every blessed one of [the/my + plural noun or pronoun] · every blessed [thing/word/penny] · the whole blessed [noun] (the same intensifier in the other frame)
Contrast
Blessed here is a minced oath — a euphemism standing in for damned, blasted, or cursed — which is why the phrase carries irritation rather than reverence: every blessed day means "every last miserable day," not a day that was blessed. That bleaching puts it in the same family as old in any old, single in not a single, and earthly in no earthly, where an ordinary adjective has lost its meaning and kept only its force. Against every single, which counts members off, and every last, which exhausts a supply, every blessed adds exasperation and nothing else. Do not confuse it with the literal adjective in every blessed sacrament, where blessed means what it says and the phrase means what it looks like.
Variants
every blessed one of, every blasted, every livelong (as in the livelong day), every last, every single

Emphatic every with a note of grievance in it — the speaker is complaining about the relentlessness of whatever the noun names. The noun and the verb are both singular, exactly as after plain every: Every blessed one of them was late. It has aged out of neutral American English and now works mainly as period flavor, as affectionate imitation of an older speaker, or as comic exaggeration.

  1. He calls me at six in the morning every blessed day.
  2. She sat by that window every blessed night for a year.
  3. We spent every blessed penny we had on the roof.
  4. Every blessed one of those forms had to be signed twice.
  5. "I've told you every blessed thing I know," he said, and shut the door. — the phrase as fiction sets it down
  6. The machine broke down every blessed time we ran it.
  7. Current American writing says every single day here; every blessed day is the grandparent's version of the same sentence. — the register in one line

every last#

/ˌɛvri ˈlæst/ · ev-ree-LAST · the stress moves onto last, and the /t/ is usually unreleased before a consonant: every last dime → [ˌɛvri ˈlæs daɪm]

Type
distributive (emphatic)
Position
central determiner
Register
informal and emphatic — common in speech, rare in formal writing
Goes with
singular countable nouns only — every last dime, every last crumb. Never a plural (✗ every last dimes), never an uncountable (✗ every last money), following every exactly.

With of: Not with the bare phrase. Use every last one of for countables — every last one of them — and every last bit of for uncountables.

Contrast
every last and every single are near-twins; every last leans toward the exhaustion of a supply (every last dollar, every last drop), while every single leans toward counting off members of a set (every single person in the room). Variants

Emphatic every, insisting that the set has been used up or accounted for down to the final member. It carries a note of effort or grievance — the speaker is usually pointing out that this took some doing, or that nothing was left over.

Negative#

The determiners that reduce a set to zero — plus the two that reduce it only partway, and the rules that keep English from stacking two negatives in one clause. Related negatives of small quantity (hardly any, scarcely any, barely any, next to no, a shred of) are in quantifiers of small quantity; the dialectal and archaic negatives — ain't no, ary / ary a, nary a, never a, and negative concord — have their own section below.

One phrase that looks as though it belongs in this section does not. An absence of is not a determiner at all but a full lexical noun heading its own noun phrase: it takes the and a possessive (the absence of evidence, in his absence), it pluralizes (three unexplained absences), it accepts modifiers (a complete absence of, a conspicuous absence of), and — the decisive test — the verb agrees with absence rather than with the noun after of: ✓ An absence of witnesses was noted, ✗ were noted. In a genuine partitive determiner like every bit of or a shred of, the of-phrase supplies the real noun; here it does not. To deny a quantity, the determiners are no and none of; to name the smallest imaginable amount and then deny even that, see a shred of and not so much as a.

Keep not in front of all, because moving it onto the verb creates the ambiguity back again: All the seats weren't taken is read both ways and should be rewritten. The singular counterpart is not every.
Variants

Denies that the whole set is covered, without denying any particular member — "some, but not the whole." The verb agrees with the noun, exactly as with all: Not all the money is accounted for, Not all the checks have cleared.

  2. Not all of the applicants were interviewed.
  3. Not all the money has been accounted for.
  4. Not all insurance policies cover flood damage.
  5. Not all of them agreed with the decision.
  6. She works most weekends, though not all of them.

Dialectal, archaic, and nonstandard#

A learner will meet all five of these constantly in speech, in song lyrics, in fiction, and in older writing, and needs to recognize them and know where they belong. Two of them — ain't no and concord no — are the same phenomenon seen from two angles, and both are stigmatized in edited writing. The other three carry no stigma at all; they are costumes. Nary a and its positive twin ary a are Appalachian and rural Southern dialect used on purpose, and never a — the ancestor of both, by way of the contracted ne'er a — is Early Modern English surviving in a handful of frozen phrases. None of those three doubles its negative, which is precisely what separates them from the two that do.

Русский

Type

Position

Register
nonstandard in edited American English — systematic and rule-governed in African American English, in Southern and Appalachian vernacular, and in casual speech everywhere, but out of place in academic writing, business correspondence, and any formal register
