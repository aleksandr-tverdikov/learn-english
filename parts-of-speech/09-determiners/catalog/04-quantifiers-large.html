Quantifiers of large quantity#

The determiners that answer how much? and how many? with a big number — and the file where the much/many and amount/number lines get drawn.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

What unites this group is one question, how much or how many, and one fault line running through every answer to it. Countability decides the word: much, a great deal of, and a large amount of take nouns you measure; many, a great many, and a large number of take nouns you count; and the phrases built on a lot — a lot of, lots of, plenty of, tons of — take either, which is exactly why they took over the spoken language. Cross the line and you produce the errors this file exists to prevent: ✗ much books, ✗ many information, ✗ a large amount of people, ✗ a great deal of reasons. Read the Goes with line first in every entry below, because it is the only line you cannot guess.

The second thing to learn here is register, which American English splits in a way textbooks rarely mention. Much and many are the neutral written forms and are at home everywhere in questions and negatives — How much time do we have?, There aren't many left — but bare much in a plain affirmative statement is stilted, and nobody says We have much time. In affirmative speech the defaults are a lot of and lots of, with a bunch of and tons of a rung further down; a rung up, in formal prose, sit a great deal of, a large number of, and numerous. Picking the wrong rung is not ungrammatical, merely audible.

Below those neutral rungs the file keeps four groups an American will meet without ever having been taught them, and each is labeled rather than smoothed over. Two words follow their noun instead of preceding it (bargains galore, reasons aplenty). A block of regional and slang forms belongs to particular places and particular people — a passel of and a right smart of to Appalachia, a mess of and a sight of and a power of to the South, hella to Northern California, mad to New York, beaucoup to the military — and one of them, a shitload of, is obscene and carries a line saying where it must never go. A block of archaic and literary forms lives only inside quotation marks (enow, full many a, many's the). And a block of legal and technical terms names a threshold fixed by a document rather than a quantity judged by a speaker (a plurality of, a quorum of, a supermajority of). Read the Register line in any of those entries as a boundary and not as flavor: not one of them is a neutral synonym for a lot of.

Two mechanical rules then apply almost everywhere in the file. Of: the partitive phrases (a lot of, plenty of, tons of) carry of always and cannot drop it, while the bare determiners (much, many, more, most, enough) take it only in front of another determiner or a pronoun — much of the report, many of them, but never ✗ much the report and never ✗ many of students. That of is never stressed: it reduces to /əv/ and in ordinary speech to a bare /ə/ — a lotta time, a buncha people, tonsa work — and the a that opens these phrases is the weak article /ə/, never /eɪ/. Agreement: after an of-phrase the verb agrees with the noun after of, not with the quantity word in front of it — A lot of people are waiting beside A lot of time was wasted. And watch three phrases that mean the opposite of what they look like: quite a few means many, quite a bit of means much, and no end of means a great deal of rather than none.

One note on scope, since every determiner has exactly one home in this catalog. Several words that mean "a lot" are quantified elsewhere and are only linked from here — the table at the end of this page lists them all. This file links to every one of them and duplicates none.

The core pair and the comparison series#

Much and many are the same quantifier split down the countability line, and more and most are the comparative and superlative that serve both of them at once. Learn these five and you have the skeleton of the whole category; everything else in this file is a phrase built to do one of these jobs in a different register.

many#

Pronunciation: /ˈmɛni/ · MEN-ee Type: quantifier · Position: central when it opens the noun phrase (many people); postdeterminer after a central determiner (the many problems, her many friends, these many years) · Register: neutral — the standard written form; in plain affirmative speech Americans usually reach for a lot of Goes with: plural countable nouns only — many people, many reasons, many times. Never an uncountable noun (✗ many information, ✗ many money, ✗ many advice — use much or a lot of) and never a singular countable noun (✗ many reason). The single exception is the fixed frame many a + singular noun, which has its own entry below. With of: of is required before another determiner or a pronoun — many of the students, many of my friends, many of them. Never ✗ many the students. No of before a bare plural: many students. Pattern: many [plural countable] · many of [the/my/these + plural] · many of [them/us] · [the/her/these] many [plural] · how many [plural] · too / so / as many [plural] · many more [plural] Contrast: Many counts, much measures: many books but much reading. Both are perfectly natural in questions and negatives (Do you have many?, There aren't many), and both sound stiff in a plain affirmative statement in American English, where a lot of is the spoken default — A lot of people came rather than Many people came, unless you are writing. Many stays comfortable in affirmatives when it is modified (so many, too many, as many, how many, many more) and throughout formal prose. Determiner against pronoun: many people left has a noun after it, many left does not. Variants: so many, too many, as many, how many, many more, the many, a great many, a good many

Marks a large but unspecified number of countable things.

1. Before a bare plural noun it names a big number drawn from an open set — many Americans, many companies. 2. After the, a possessive, or a demonstrative it is a postdeterminer and simply describes an already-identified set as a large one — the many problems we inherited. 3. As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun: Many were called, few were chosen.

  1. Many Americans commute more than an hour each way.
  2. How many people are coming to dinner?
  3. There aren't many seats left for the early show.
  4. She has too many projects going at once.
  5. Many of the applicants had never written a line of code.
  6. The many delays finally cost the contractor the job.

See also: much, more, a lot of, a great many, quite a few, few, how many

many a#

Pronunciation: /ˈmɛni ə/ · MEN-ee uh · the a keeps its weak /ə/ and never takes stress here Type: quantifier · Position: predeterminer — the article a is locked inside the phrase, so no other determiner can appear: ✗ many a the student · Register: formal, literary, or deliberately old-fashioned; alive in proverbs, sermons, and sports commentary Goes with: singular countable nouns only — many a time, many a young writer, many a night. Never a plural noun (✗ many a times) and never an uncountable noun (✗ many a money). This is the one corner of English where many takes a singular, and the singular runs through the whole clause: Many a student has asked that question, never ✗ have asked. With of: never takes an of-phrase — ✗ many a of the students. Before a vowel sound the article changes as usual: many an evening. The only of-adjacent form is the archaic pronoun many a one. Pattern: many a [singular countable] + singular verb · many an [vowel-initial singular noun] · many's the [singular noun] (fixed idiom) Contrast: Many a student was late and many students were late mean the same thing with opposite grammar: many takes a plural noun and a plural verb, many a takes a singular of each. Do not confuse it with a good many and a great many, which are ordinary intensified many and take the plural — a good many students were late. The singular agreement is obligatory, not a stylistic option: ✗ many a parent have asked is an error. Modern American prose almost always prefers plain many; reach for many a only when the sentence wants that older cadence. Variants: many an, many's the, many a one (archaic pronoun)

Names a large number of countable things while treating them grammatically one at a time. The singular is the whole point: it makes the reader picture the cases individually rather than as a crowd, which is why the phrase sounds weighty and old-fashioned.

  1. Many a student has asked that same question.
  2. He had made that drive many a time.
  3. Many an evening ended with the two of them arguing about the thermostat.
  4. Many a good idea has died in a committee meeting.
  5. Many a parent learns that lesson the hard way.
  6. Many's the night I fell asleep at that desk.

See also: many, a great many, a good many, every, each, many's the, full many a

more#

Pronunciation: /mɔr/ · MOR Type: quantifier, comparative · Position: central when it opens the noun phrase (more time); postdeterminer after a determiner or a numeral (some more coffee, no more excuses, two more days, many more people) · Register: neutral Goes with: both plural countable nouns (more chairs, more people) and uncountable nouns (more time, more money). This is what makes it easy: more is the comparative of both many and much, so it is one of the few quantifiers here that never asks you to decide countability. It does not take a bare singular countable noun (✗ more chair). With of: of is required before another determiner or a pronoun — more of the cake, more of my time, more of them. Never ✗ more the cake. No of before a bare noun: more cake. Pattern: more [plural/uncountable] · more [noun] than [phrase/clause] · more of [the/my/this + noun] · more of [them/it] · [some / no / any / two / many / much / a lot] more [noun] · the more … the more … Contrast: More is the comparative of both many and much, and its two opposites split along the countability line: fewer for things you count, less for things you measure. Determiner against pronoun: more volunteers has a noun after it, we need more does not. Keep the determiner apart from the degree adverb — more chairs puts more in front of a noun, while more careful and more slowly put it in front of an adjective or adverb, where it is not a determiner at all. Watch the negatives too: no more means "none from here on" (No more excuses), not … anymore is about time (I don't work there anymore), and before a noun the two-word spelling is the right one (any more questions). Variants: some more, no more, any more, a little more, a lot more, many more, much more, two more, the more … the more

Names a larger number or amount than something else — than a stated comparison, than before, or than what is already on hand. As a pronoun it stands alone with no noun after it: We need more, Would you like some more?

  1. We need more volunteers for Saturday.
  2. Is there any more coffee in the pot?
  3. She has more patience than I do.
  4. Give me two more days and I'll have the draft.
  5. More of the money went to overhead than to programs.
  6. No more excuses — the report is due Friday.
  7. There's soup on the stove if anyone wants more.

See also: most, many, much, fewer, less, how many more

most#

Pronunciation: /moʊst/ · MOHST Type: quantifier, superlative · Position: central when it opens the noun phrase (most people); after the it forms the superlative of quantity (the most points) · Register: neutral Goes with: both plural countable nouns (most people, most cities) and uncountable nouns (most information, most traffic). It does not take a bare singular countable noun (✗ most city), though most of the city is fine, because of reattaches it to an already-determined phrase. With of: required before another determiner or a pronoun — most of the students, most of my time, most of them. ✗ most the students is not standard written English. No of before a bare noun: most people. Pattern: most [plural/uncountable] · most of [the/my/these + noun] · most of [them/it] · the most [noun] (superlative) · a most [adjective + singular noun] (formal intensifier) Contrast: The bare/of split is a meaning split, not a style choice. Most people is generic — people at large. Most of the people is specific — the majority of one identified group. Against more, most is the superlative to its comparative, and both cover countables and uncountables alike, unlike their small-quantity opposites fewest and least, which do not. Three lookalikes to keep separate: the degree adverb (the most interesting talk), the formal intensifier meaning "very" (a most unusual request), and the colloquial regional most meaning "almost" (most everybody, most all of them), which is at home in American conversation and out of place in writing. Variants: most of, the most, a most (intensifier), most all / most everybody (informal, "almost")

Names the majority — more than half, and usually a good deal more than half. Bare most generalizes about a whole class; most of picks the larger part out of a specific set. As a pronoun it stands alone: Most had already gone home.

  1. Most people would rather not fly on a Sunday night.
  2. Most of the money is already spent.
  3. She spends most of her time on the road.
  4. Most information on that site is out of date.
  5. Which team scored the most points?
  6. Most of them had never seen snow before.

See also: more, many, much, a majority of, the majority of, the minority of, least, all

much#

Pronunciation: /mʌtʃ/ · MUCH Type: quantifier · Position: central — it opens the noun phrase (much time); the identical degree adverb is a different animal (much better, thanks very much) · Register: neutral in questions and negatives; in a plain affirmative statement it is formal enough to sound stilted in American speech Goes with: uncountable nouns only — much time, much money, much information. Never a plural countable noun (✗ much books, ✗ much people — use many) and never a singular countable noun (✗ much book). With of: of is required before another determiner or a pronoun — much of the report, much of my time, much of it. Never ✗ much the report. Bare much takes no of before a bare noun: much time. Pattern: much [uncountable noun] · much of [the/my/this + noun] · much of [it/that] · how much [uncountable] · too / so / as / very / that much [uncountable] · much more [uncountable] Contrast: Much is to uncountables what many is to plural countables. In affirmative statements Americans avoid bare much — We have much time is grammatical but nobody says it — and reach for a lot of or plenty of instead. Much survives in full health in questions (How much time do we have?), in negatives (not much time), and after too, so, as, how, very, and that. It is also entirely natural in affirmative writing the moment it is followed by of or fronted as a subject: Much of the report was wrong, Much remains to be done. Variants: too much, so much, as much, how much, very much, that much, much of, much more, not much

Marks a large but unspecified amount of something that cannot be counted. As a pronoun it stands alone: There isn't much to say.

  1. We don't have much time.
  2. How much money did the repair cost?
  3. There isn't much to say about it.
  4. She spends too much energy worrying about things she can't control.
  5. Much of the report was out of date before it was printed.
  6. I didn't get much sleep last night.

See also: many, more, a lot of, a great deal of, quite a bit of, little, not much, how much

The spoken defaults: a lot of and its family#

These are the phrases that actually carry large quantity in American conversation. Every one of them takes plural countables and uncountables alike — a bunch of only in casual speech, the rest without restriction — which is exactly why they won: a speaker who says a lot of never has to decide between much and many. All of them are built the same way — a noun of quantity plus obligatory of — and in all of them the verb agrees with the noun after of, not with the quantity word itself.

a bunch of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈbʌntʃ əv/ · uh BUNCH uhv · casually /ə ˈbʌntʃə/, "a buncha" Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central — the whole phrase fills the determiner slot · Register: informal American; everywhere in speech, out of place in formal writing Goes with: plural countable nouns (a bunch of people, a bunch of emails) and, in casual American speech, uncountable nouns as well (a bunch of money, a bunch of work, a bunch of time). The literal use is a different thing: a bunch of grapes, a bunch of keys, a bunch of flowers name a physical cluster, and there bunch is an ordinary head noun — the giveaway is that it pluralizes and takes its own modifiers (three big bunches of grapes), which the quantifier never does. With of: of is obligatory and welded to the phrase — ✗ a bunch people. Nothing changes before another determiner or a pronoun: a bunch of the emails, a bunch of them. Pattern: a bunch of [plural countable] · a bunch of [uncountable] · a bunch of [the/my/these + noun] · a bunch of [them/us] · a whole bunch of [noun] Contrast: A bunch of is the informal American twin of a lot of, and in speech the two swap freely. Agreement follows the noun after of, not bunch: A bunch of people are waiting (plural) beside A bunch of money is missing (singular). A prescriptive grammar would want is in the first, since bunch is singular, but notional plural agreement is what Americans actually say and write in informal registers. Against a couple of and a handful of, which name small numbers, a bunch of names a big one. Variants: a whole bunch of, bunches of, a bunch (pronoun: thanks a bunch)

Names a large, uncounted quantity in casual speech — the conversational equivalent of a lot of, with no claim about how large.

  1. A bunch of us are going to the game on Saturday.
  2. He left a bunch of dishes in the sink again.
  3. That's going to cost a bunch of money we don't have.
  4. A bunch of the emails never went out.
  5. I've got a whole bunch of questions about the lease.
  6. She picked up a bunch of bananas on the way home.

See also: a lot of, lots of, tons of, a slew of, a couple of, a handful of

a lot of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈlɑt əv/ · uh LOT uhv · in ordinary speech the t flaps and of reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈlɑɾə/, "a lotta" Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: neutral to informal — the default in American speech and in most everyday writing; formal prose prefers much, many, a great deal of, or a large number of Goes with: both plural countable nouns (a lot of people) and uncountable nouns (a lot of money). That coverage is the whole reason it took over the spoken language: it spares the speaker the much/many decision. Not used with a bare singular countable noun (✗ a lot of chair). With of: of is obligatory before a noun and cannot be dropped — ✗ a lot people. It stays put before another determiner or a pronoun: a lot of the money, a lot of them. Bare a lot with no of is either a pronoun (I have a lot) or an adverb (It helped a lot, a lot better). Pattern: a lot of [plural/uncountable] · a lot of [the/my/these + noun] · a lot of [them/it] · quite a lot of [noun] · a whole lot of [noun] · an awful lot of [noun] Contrast: A lot of is what Americans say where writing uses much and many. Verb agreement is decided by the noun after of and never by lot: A lot of people are waiting but A lot of time was wasted. Two spelling traps come with it — it is always two words (✗ alot), and the plural variant lots of is a genuine alternative, not an error. Against plenty of, a lot of only says the quantity is big; plenty of adds the promise that it is enough. Variants: lots of, a whole lot of, quite a lot of, an awful lot of, a lotta (casual spelling)

Names a large quantity of anything, countable or not, without measuring it. It is the workhorse of spoken American quantity and the safe answer whenever much and many feel risky.

  1. A lot of people showed up for a Tuesday.
  2. That job takes a lot of patience.
  3. A lot of the paperwork was already done when we got there.
  4. We wasted a lot of time on the wrong approach.
  5. A lot of them had never voted in a primary before.
  6. There's an awful lot of traffic on the bridge tonight.

See also: lots of, quite a lot of, plenty of, a bunch of, much, many

lots of#

Pronunciation: /ˈlɑts əv/ · LOTS uhv · casually /ˈlɑtsə/ Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: informal and conversational — a step more casual than a lot of, and the form adults use talking to children Goes with: both plural countable nouns (lots of friends) and uncountable nouns (lots of sugar). Never a bare singular countable noun (✗ lots of chair). With of: of is obligatory before a noun — ✗ lots people. It stays before another determiner or a pronoun: lots of the candy, lots of them. Bare lots is a pronoun (We have lots). Pattern: lots of [plural/uncountable] · lots of [the/my + noun] · lots of [them/it] · lots and lots of [noun] Contrast: Lots of and a lot of mean exactly the same thing and differ only in register. The plural -s does not force a plural verb: agreement still follows the noun after of, so Lots of people are here but Lots of sugar is bad for you. Both are too casual for a research paper, where the equivalents are many, much, a great deal of, and a large number of. Variants: lots and lots of, a lot of, loads of

A casual, warm-toned a lot of. It reports a big quantity and makes no attempt to be precise about it.

  1. There were lots of kids at the pool this afternoon.
  2. We still have lots of time before the movie starts.
  3. She's got lots of ideas about how to fix the schedule.
  4. Lots of the seats were still empty at kickoff.
  5. He put lots of hot sauce on everything.
  6. Lots of them come back a second year.

See also: a lot of, a bunch of, tons of, loads of, plenty of

plenty of#

Pronunciation: /ˈplɛnti əv/ · PLEN-tee uhv · the nt commonly reduces in American speech: /ˈplɛni ə/, "plenny a" Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: neutral to informal; usable almost anywhere short of a legal brief Goes with: both plural countable nouns (plenty of chairs, plenty of reasons) and uncountable nouns (plenty of room, plenty of time). Never a bare singular countable noun (✗ plenty of chair). With of: of is obligatory before a noun — ✗ plenty chairs. Bare plenty is a pronoun (We have plenty) or, in casual American speech, an adverb meaning "quite" (plenty big, plenty warm enough). Pattern: plenty of [plural/uncountable] · plenty of [the/my + noun] · plenty of [them/it] · plenty more [noun] · plenty [adjective] (informal adverb) Contrast: Plenty of is not a synonym for a lot of. It adds sufficiency: the quantity is large and it is enough for the purpose, which is why it is the natural answer to worry — Don't rush, there's plenty of time. A lot of makes no such promise, and too much overshoots. Against enough, which marks the line exactly, plenty of sits comfortably above it. Agreement follows the noun after of: Plenty of seats are left, Plenty of time remains. Variants: plenty more, plenty (pronoun), plenty (informal adverb), in plenty

Marks a quantity that is large and, more to the point, sufficient — as much as anyone needs and then some.

  1. There's plenty of time before the train leaves.
  2. We have plenty of parking behind the building.
  3. He gave me plenty of reasons to be skeptical.
  4. Plenty of people would take that job tomorrow.
  5. There's plenty of room in the back seat for all three of you.
  6. Don't worry about the food — there's plenty left.

See also: enough, ample, a lot of, sufficient, aplenty, galore

quite a lot of#

Pronunciation: /ˌkwaɪt ə ˈlɑt əv/ · kwyt uh LOT uhv Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: neutral to informal; a shade more measured and more British-sounding than plain a lot of Goes with: both plural countable nouns (quite a lot of people) and uncountable nouns (quite a lot of money). Never a bare singular countable noun. With of: of is obligatory before a noun — ✗ quite a lot people. Bare quite a lot is a pronoun (She's read quite a lot) or an adverb (It helped quite a lot). Pattern: quite a lot of [plural/uncountable] · quite a lot of [the/my + noun] · quite a lot of [them/it] · quite a lot (pronoun or adverb) Contrast: Quite in front of a lot of, a few, and a bit of is an intensifier, not a hedge: quite a lot of trouble is more trouble than a lot of trouble, not less. The same quite in front of a bare adjective can go the other way in American English (quite good often means only "fairly good"), and that split is what confuses learners. Treat quite a lot of, quite a few, and quite a bit of as three fixed phrases that all mean "a large amount or number." Variants: quite a bit of, quite a few, quite a number of, quite a lot (pronoun)

Names a quantity the speaker considers notably large — a mild intensification of a lot of, often with a raised eyebrow attached.

  1. That's quite a lot of money for a used dishwasher.
  2. Quite a lot of people asked the same question afterward.
  3. We got quite a lot of snow overnight.
  4. He's put quite a lot of work into that yard.
  5. Quite a lot of the data turned out to be duplicated.
  6. She travels quite a lot for work.

Large amounts of uncountable things: the amount and deal family#

Almost everything in this section takes uncountable nouns, and this is where the classic red-pen error lives. Amount measures, number counts: a large amount of water but a large number of bottles, never ✗ a large amount of bottles. The deal phrases go one better — they are uncountable-only with no countable version at all, so ✗ a great deal of people has to be repaired as a great many people or a large number of people. Two entries stretch past the mass line and say so in their own Goes with line: a body of takes a plural that is being treated as a single collection (a body of laws), and an excess of takes a count measured against an expected figure (an excess of deaths). One more is here for its ancestry rather than its usefulness — bare a deal of is a nineteenth-century form, not a modern option.

a body of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈbɑdi əv/ · uh BOD-ee uhv · the article is the weak /ə/ and of reduces to /ə/ in ordinary speech: /ə ˈbɑdi ə/ Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: neutral to formal; standard in academic, legal, and journalistic prose Goes with: uncountable abstract nouns that name something accumulated — a body of evidence, a body of work, a body of research, a body of law, a body of knowledge, a body of literature. It reaches a plural only when the plural is itself being treated as one collection (a body of laws, a body of texts); ordinary countable plurals are out (✗ a body of chairs, ✗ a body of emails), and so are physical substances (✗ a body of sugar). The literal senses are a different word: a body of water is a geographic term and a body of troops an older military one, and in both body is a real head noun that pluralizes freely — two bodies of water. With of: of is obligatory — ✗ a body evidence. It stays before another determiner: a body of the case law that predates the statute. Pattern: a body of [mass abstract] · a body of [the + mass abstract] · a growing / substantial / considerable / large body of [noun] · the body of [noun] (definite, naming the whole) Contrast: A body of is not a plain a lot of: it claims the quantity is large and coherent — one accumulated mass that hangs together and can be argued about. That is why a growing body of evidence is the standard phrase and ✗ a body of noise is not. Against a wealth of, which approves of what it counts, and a great deal of, which measures and nothing more, a body of organizes. The verb is singular, since body is the head and the noun after of is a mass: A body of research suggests otherwise. Variants: a growing body of, a substantial body of, a large body of, the body of, bodies of

Names a large, accumulated, coherent mass of evidence, work, or knowledge — quantity presented as one organized whole rather than as a heap.

  1. A growing body of evidence links the two conditions.
  2. Her body of work spans four decades and three countries.
  3. There is a substantial body of research on the question and no consensus anywhere in it.
  4. The ruling unsettles a body of case law going back to the 1970s.
  5. A body of knowledge that takes a decade to acquire cannot be replaced in a summer.
  6. Lake Michigan is the largest body of water inside a single state.

See also: a wealth of, a great deal of, a large amount of, considerable, a multitude of

a deal of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈdil əv/ · uh DEEL uhv · the article is the weak /ə/, never /eɪ/, and of reduces to /əv/ or a bare /ə/ Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: archaic and dialectal. The bare form dropped out of standard American English in the nineteenth century and survives now only in period prose, in reported country speech, and in British and Irish dialect. With an adjective wedged in, the same phrase is perfectly current: a good deal of, a great deal of Goes with: uncountable nouns — a deal of trouble, a deal of money, a deal of talk. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ a deal of people, ✗ a deal of reasons. Never a bare singular countable noun. With of: of is obligatory — ✗ a deal trouble. Adjectives that quantify#

These seven are adjectives, not determiners, and the difference is testable. A determiner cannot sit inside another determiner's phrase and cannot be graded — ✗ the much time, ✗ a much money, ✗ more much — while an adjective sits happily after a determiner (the numerous errors, his abundant energy), grades and compares (more numerous, most abundant), and can stand after the verb (the errors were numerous). Many is the one word that muddies the test, because it doubles as a postdeterminer and therefore does follow the (the many problems); it still refuses grading (✗ more many) and still heads the of-phrase (many of them) that none of these adjectives can. They are here because they do a large quantifier's semantic work and because the countability rules apply to them exactly as they do to the determiners: countless and numerous count and cannot take a mass noun, and considerable measures and cannot take an ordinary plural.

Sufficiency: enough and sufficient#

Sufficiency is a large quantity measured against a need rather than against zero. Enough marks the line exactly; plenty of and ample sit comfortably above it; too much and too many overshoot it; and below it lie the small quantifiers not enough, too few, and too little. Enough is also the strangest-behaving determiner in English — it is the only one that can follow its noun, and the only degree word that follows the adjective it modifies.

enough#

Pronunciation: /əˈnʌf/ · uh-NUF · carefully /ɪˈnʌf/; the first syllable is always reduced, never /iː/ Type: quantifier (sufficiency) · Position: central determiner before the noun (enough time); in formal and fixed usage it may also follow the noun (time enough, reason enough, money enough), which no other determiner in English can do; as a degree adverb it follows its adjective (good enough) · Register: neutral Goes with: both plural countable nouns (enough chairs, enough volunteers) and uncountable nouns (enough time, enough money). Not used with a bare singular countable noun (✗ enough chair). With of: of is required before another determiner or a pronoun — enough of the food, enough of my time, enough of this, enough of them. No of before a bare noun: enough food. Pattern: enough [plural/uncountable] · [noun] enough (formal/fixed) · enough of [the/my/this + noun] · enough of [them/it] · [adjective/adverb] + enough · enough to [verb] · not enough [noun] Contrast: Enough names the sufficiency line itself: below it are not enough, too few, and too little; above it are too many and too much; comfortably above it are plenty of and ample. Two structural oddities are worth memorizing. First, as a degree word it follows what it modifies — warm enough, quickly enough, never ✗ enough warm — which is the reverse of very, too, and so. Second, it is the only determiner that can trail its own noun in standard English (There's time enough for that), a survival of older word order now limited to a handful of nouns. As a pronoun it stands alone: That's enough. Variants: not enough, more than enough, enough to, enough of, sure enough, oddly enough, fair enough, enough is enough

The informal intensives#

Hyperbole, all of it, and nearly all built on the same frame: a noun of bulk — usually plural — plus obligatory of. Every one takes plural countables and uncountables alike, none is literal, and the verb agrees with the noun after of — tons of people are, tons of work is. What separates them is geography and age. Tons of is the American default and a heap of its singular, Southern-flavored cousin; loads of and masses of are British-leaning though universally understood; heaps of is chiefly Australian and British; oodles of is dated and jocular; and no end of is the odd one out, because it contains a negative and means a great deal.

The postpositive pair: galore and aplenty#

Two quantity words in American English go after the noun instead of in front of it. Bargains galore, reasons aplenty — the noun comes first and the quantifier trails it, which is why neither can open a noun phrase (✗ galore bargains, ✗ aplenty reasons) and why neither takes of (✗ galore of bargains). They attach to a bare plural or a bare mass noun and bring no article of their own. Both are marked: galore is jocular and skews to good news, aplenty is literary and takes trouble as happily as bargains. They are the last survivors of a word order English otherwise abandoned, and the only other determiner that can still do it is enough in fixed phrases like time enough.

Pattern: [plural or uncountable noun] + aplenty · there is / there are [noun] aplenty · compare prenominal plenty of [noun] Contrast: Aplenty is plenty of turned around, and the reversal is the whole difference: plenty of food is neutral reporting, food aplenty is a flourish. Regional, dialect, and slang quantifiers#

Every word in this section means "a lot," and not one of them is neutral. Some belong to a place: a passel of and a right smart of to Appalachia and the Upper South, a mess of and a sight of and a power of to Southern and older country speech, hella to Northern California, mad to New York City. One belongs to a group — beaucoup came home from the wars with American servicemen and still travels with them. One, a shitload of, is obscene, and its entry says where it must never go. They are catalogued because Americans really do meet them, in conversation, in lyrics, in fiction, and in quoted speech, and because the Register line in each entry is the load-bearing one: using a regional form outside its home is not a style choice but a costume, and none of these belongs in writing that is not quoting somebody. Grammatically they are mostly ordinary partitive phrases with obligatory of; beaucoup, hella, and mad are the exceptions that sit bare in the determiner slot with no article and no of at all.

It cannot follow a determiner in this sense, and the moment one appears the meaning flips: the mad people is about insanity, not about numbers · Register: regional American slang — New York City, out of hip-hop and Black and Latino youth speech of the 1980s and 1990s and spread from there. Still strongly New York-flavored. Casual speech only; nothing about it belongs in writing that is not quoting somebody Goes with: both plural countable nouns (mad people, mad cars) and uncountable nouns (mad money, mad respect, mad love, mad traffic). Never a bare singular countable noun: ✗ mad car. With of: it takes no of — ✗ mad of people — and no article: ✗ a mad people. A lot of anything, countable or not, in New York City slang.

  1. There were mad people outside the venue by eight o'clock.
  2. I've got mad respect for what she pulled off with that budget.
  3. He's been putting in mad hours since the promotion.
  4. There's mad traffic on the expressway every afternoon.
  5. The crowd gave the opening act mad love.
  6. Mind the article: mad skills is a great deal of skill, and a mad scientist is something else.

Archaic and literary quantifiers#

These are the large-quantity forms an American meets in reading rather than in conversation — in the hymnal and the King James Bible, in Gray and FitzGerald and Poe, in Scott and Dickens, in a nineteenth-century deed or deposition. Not one of them is current. Enow is dead outside quotation. Full many a and many and many a are verse intensifications of many a and belong to poetry. A parcel of survives only as an insult. Many's the is the one item here that living American speakers still produce, in a deliberately old-fashioned and reminiscent voice. They are catalogued because reading requires them, and because a learner who meets Paradise enow deserves better than a guess — but write none of them in your own prose unless you are writing period dialogue.

a parcel of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈpɑrsəl əv/ · uh PAR-sul uhv · of reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈpɑrsələ/ Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: archaic, and wherever it survives, contemptuous. In present-day American English it is alive only in the dismissive frame a parcel of lies, a parcel of fools, a parcel of nonsense, and even there it sounds like a quotation. Its country descendant a passel of is the living form of the same word Goes with: plural countable nouns naming people held in contempt (a parcel of rogues, a parcel of fools, a parcel of idlers) and uncountable nouns naming falsehood or foolishness (a parcel of lies, a parcel of nonsense). It is not used of neutral things: a parcel of chairs reads as the literal noun, a wrapped package of them. With of: of is obligatory — ✗ a parcel lies. The literal noun is a separate and thoroughly current word — a parcel of land, a parcel from the post office — and there parcel pluralizes freely and carries no scorn. Pattern: a parcel of [rogues / fools / lies / nonsense] · a whole parcel of [noun] · compare literal a parcel of land Contrast: Parcel and passel are one word split in two: the standard spelling kept the contempt, the dialect spelling kept the quantity. Burns fixed the phrase in English with "such a parcel of rogues in a nation," and nearly every modern use is echoing him or the register he wrote in. Where a present-day writer wants the same scorn, the living idiom is a pack of lies — which is exactly what a parcel of lies turned into. Variants: a passel of, a pack of (lies), a whole parcel of

A collection of people or claims the speaker holds in contempt — archaic, and never neutral.

  1. "We're bought and sold for English gold — such a parcel of rogues in a nation."
  2. The whole defense was a parcel of lies and the jury knew it within an hour.
  3. He called the commissioners a parcel of fools and was removed from the room.
  4. The pamphlet is a parcel of nonsense dressed up in footnotes.
  5. An 1840s witness dismissed the whole claim as a parcel of idle talk.
  6. Today the same insult comes out as a pack of lies.

See also: a passel of, a plethora of, a host of, a deal of, a mess of

enow#

Pronunciation: /əˈnaʊ/ · uh-NOW · it rhymes with now, not with know, and the first syllable is the weak /ə/ exactly as in enough Type: quantifier (sufficiency) · Position: before its noun (enow provisions) or, far more often in verse, after it — Paradise enow, gold enow — exactly as enough once could and still can in a few fixed phrases · Register: archaic. It is the old northern English and Scots form of enough, dead in American English since the nineteenth century and alive now only in quotation — above all in FitzGerald's Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, which is where nearly every American who knows the word learned it Goes with: both plural countable nouns (friends enow, books enow) and uncountable nouns (gold enow, trouble enow). Historically enow was the plural-countable form and enough the mass form — books enow beside bread enough — the same countability split that separates many from much; but the distinction had broken down before the word died, and the surviving quotations do not observe it. With of: in the surviving quotations it takes no of-phrase. Its modern replacement does: enough of the money, enough of them. Pattern: [noun] + enow (postpositive, the usual order in verse) · enow [noun] · compare current enough Contrast: Enow is not a separate word to learn but the ancestor of one: read it as enough every time and the line will make sense. Its historical job — countable plural against mass enough — is the only place in English where sufficiency was ever divided the way many and much divide quantity, and nothing of it survives. Modern American English has no trace of the form at all: write enough. Variants: enough, anow, enow (Scots and northern English)

The archaic form of enough — a sufficient or plentiful quantity, met today only inside quotation marks.

  1. "A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou / Beside me singing in the Wilderness — / Oh, Wilderness were Paradise enow!"
  2. The old ballad promises gold enow to any man who dares the crossing.
  3. He had friends enow while the money lasted.
  4. "There is trouble enow in this house without yours," the housekeeper said.
  5. A nineteenth-century hymn asks whether grace is enow for the day.
  6. Every one of those sentences takes enough in modern English.

full many a#

Pronunciation: /ˌfʊl ˈmɛni ə/ · fool MEN-ee uh · full here has the vowel of pull, /fʊl/, and is unstressed; the phrase carries its stress on many, and the article stays the weak /ə/ Type: quantifier · Position: predeterminer — the article a is locked inside the phrase, so nothing may precede it: ✗ the full many a gem · Register: archaic and poetic. It is a verse intensification of many a, at home in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English poetry, and the line most Americans have met it in is Gray's Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard. No living register produces it Goes with: singular countable nouns only, exactly as many a does — full many a gem, full many a flower, full many a time. Never a plural (✗ full many a gems) and never an uncountable noun. The singular runs through the whole clause: Full many a flower is born to blush unseen. With of: it never takes of — ✗ full many a of them. Before a vowel sound the article changes as usual: full many an hour. Pattern: full many a [singular countable] + singular verb · full many an [vowel-initial singular noun] Contrast: Full here is the old adverb meaning "very," the same one preserved in full well (you know full well) and in the archaic full sore; it is not the adjective, and it never agrees with anything. Strip it off and what remains is plain many a, which is formal but alive. Its companion intensification is many and many a, which repeats rather than amplifies. Both belong to verse, and in modern prose both read as pastiche. Variants: many a, many and many a, full well (the surviving adverb)

A poetic intensification of many a — a great number of things, considered one at a time.

  1. "Full many a gem of purest ray serene / The dark unfathom'd caves of ocean bear."
  2. "Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, / And waste its sweetness on the desert air."
  3. The hymn recalls full many a year of wandering before the promise was kept.
  4. Full many an evening had ended the same way in that house.
  5. Full many a traveler had turned back at the pass, and the guide could name them all.
  6. Strip the old adverb off and the living phrase remains: many a traveler turned back.

See also: many a, many and many a, many's the, many, enow

many and many a#

Pronunciation: /ˌmɛni ən ˈmɛni ə/ · MEN-ee uhn MEN-ee uh · the linking and is always the weak /ən/ or a bare /n/, never the full /ænd/; the second many carries the main stress Type: quantifier · Position: predeterminer, with the article a locked inside the phrase — nothing may precede it · Register: archaic and literary; a reduplication used in nineteenth-century verse and storytelling for emphasis and rhythm. Poe's "It was many and many a year ago, / In a kingdom by the sea" is the line that keeps it in circulation. Living speakers produce it only in a deliberately storybook voice Goes with: singular countable nouns only — many and many a year, many and many a time, many and many a night — with a strong preference for nouns of time. Plural and uncountable nouns are both impossible: ✗ many and many a years, ✗ many and many a money. Singular agreement runs through the clause. With of: it never takes of — ✗ many and many a of them. Before a vowel sound the article changes: many and many an hour. Pattern: many and many a [singular countable, usually a time word] + singular verb · many and many an [vowel-initial noun] · compare plain many a and intensified full many a Contrast: The three members of the family differ only in weight. Many a night is formal but current; full many a night is poetic and amplified; many and many a night is poetic and repeated, and the repetition does as much work for "again and again" as for "a great number." It is the most sing-song of the three and the least usable in prose. The doubling is the same pattern behind again and again and round and round. Variants: many a, full many a, many and many a time, many's the

An emphatic, repeated many a — a great many occasions, one after another, in a storytelling voice.

  1. "It was many and many a year ago, / In a kingdom by the sea."
  2. Many and many a night he sat up with the lamp burning.
  3. The old woman had told that story many and many a time.
  4. Many and many an hour was lost at that crossing before the bridge went in.
  5. Many and many a traveler passed that way and left nothing behind.
  6. In plain modern English the sentence says simply many nights.

many's the#

Pronunciation: /ˈmɛniz ðə/ · MEN-eez thuh · the contracted 's is /z/ and the article is the weak /ðə/, becoming /ði/ only before a vowel Type: quantifier in a fixed clause frame · Position: predeterminer inside a fronted noun phrase: the phrase opens the sentence and a relative clause completes it — Many's the night I fell asleep at that desk. It cannot appear anywhere else in the sentence · Register: old-fashioned but not dead — still produced by living American speakers reaching for a folksy, reminiscent tone, and common in older fiction and in Irish and Irish-American speech. It is the one form in this section you might actually hear said out loud Goes with: singular countable nouns only — many's the night, many's the time, many's the man, many's the story — with nouns of time dominating. Never a plural (✗ many's the nights) and never an uncountable noun. With of: it never takes of. The frame instead requires a clause: many's the + singular noun + a relative clause, with the relative pronoun usually left out — Many's the time I have wondered rather than Many's the time that I have wondered. Pattern: many's the [singular countable] + [relative clause] · many's the man who … · many's the time [subject + verb] · compare many a [singular noun] in ordinary subject position Contrast: The frame is an inversion. It means there have been many nights when …, with the quantity pushed to the front as a predicate, and that is why the sentence cannot continue as an ordinary clause: ✗ Many's the night was cold is wrong, because many's the night is not the subject of what follows. Plain many a night is an ordinary subject (Many a night was cold) and can sit anywhere in the sentence, where many's the can only open one. Variants: many a, many's the time, many's the man, many and many a

There have been a great many occasions of the kind named — a fronted, reminiscent way of saying many a.

  1. Many's the night I fell asleep at that desk.
  2. Many's the time she warned him about that road in the winter.
  3. Many's the man who has lost a boat in that channel.
  4. Many's the story that came out of the mill and never made the paper.
  5. Many's the dollar the two of them spent trying to keep it running.
  6. The ordinary version is flatter and says the same thing: many a night I fell asleep there.

Legal and technical quantity terms#

Three of the four phrases here name a threshold rather than an impression. A plurality, a quorum, and a supermajority are counted, and what counts as one is fixed somewhere else — in a statute, a charter, a set of bylaws — so the word tells you that a line exists without telling you where it is. That is what makes them technical rather than merely formal, and it is why using one loosely, as a dressed-up many, misleads a reader who knows the field. The fourth, so much of … as, is not a threshold but a drafting frame: it is how legal English says "the part of X that." All four take of, all four live in statutes, bylaws, minutes, and the reporting that quotes them, and outside that world most Americans meet them on election night.

a plurality of#

Pronunciation: /ə plʊˈræləɾi əv/ · uh pluh-RAL-uh-tee uhv · the t flaps to /ɾ/ in American speech, as it does in majority and variety Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: technical — election law and political reporting in the first sense, patent drafting in the second. Outside those two worlds an American meets it on election night and nowhere else Goes with: plural countable nouns — a plurality of voters, a plurality of the votes cast, a plurality of the members. In patent claims it takes any countable noun at all: a plurality of apertures. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ a plurality of money. With of: of is obligatory — ✗ a plurality voters. The noun after of is normally definite or generic: a plurality of the votes, a plurality of Americans. Bare a plurality is common with the set understood: She won a plurality. Pattern: a plurality of [plural countable] · a plurality of [the + plural] · win / take a plurality of the vote · patent claim: comprising a plurality of [noun] Contrast: Two technical senses, and neither means what it looks like. 1. In elections a plurality is the largest share, not necessarily a majority: a candidate who takes 42 percent of a three-way race has a plurality and not a majority, and that distinction is what decides whether a runoff is required. 2. In United States patent practice a plurality of means simply two or more — a plurality of vents claims at least two vents and nothing more — which is why patent claims are full of a phrase that in ordinary English sounds like a crowd. Do not reach for it as a formal synonym for many: a reader in either field will read it as a number. Variants: a majority of, pluralities of, win by a plurality, a plurality (bare)

Names the largest share of a vote or a group — and, in patent drafting, nothing grander than "more than one."

1. In elections and in any counted body, the largest share, whether or not it passes half — a plurality of the vote. 2. In United States patent claims, exactly "two or more" and no more than that — a plurality of vents.

  1. She won a plurality of the vote in a five-way primary and never came near fifty percent.
  2. A plurality of the members favored postponement, but the bylaws require a majority.
  3. Under state law the office goes to whoever takes a plurality of the votes cast.
  4. The claim recites a housing comprising a plurality of vents.
  5. A plurality of respondents named cost as the main obstacle; no single answer drew half.
  6. A plurality is not a majority, and a plurality of forty-two percent is the usual reason for a runoff.

See also: a majority of, a supermajority of, a quorum of, most, the majority of, the minority of

a quorum of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˈkwɔrəm əv/ · uh KWOR-uhm uhv · of reduces to /ə/: /ə ˈkwɔrəmə/ Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: technical — parliamentary procedure, corporate governance, statutes, and the minutes of any body that meets and votes. Neutral inside that world and out of place outside it Goes with: plural countable nouns naming the members of a defined body — a quorum of the board, a quorum of members, a quorum of the senate. It cannot take an uncountable noun (✗ a quorum of money) and cannot take a set with no membership rule behind it (✗ a quorum of customers). With of: of is obligatory when the body is named — ✗ a quorum the board. Bare a quorum with the body understood is the commoner form in practice: We have a quorum, A quorum was present, The meeting lacked a quorum. Pattern: a quorum of [the + defined body] · a quorum was present · lack / constitute / establish a quorum · in the absence of a quorum Contrast: A quorum is a minimum, and the minimum is set elsewhere — by statute, charter, or bylaws — so the phrase names a number without telling you what it is: a quorum may be a majority, two-thirds, or five people in a room. That is what separates it from a majority of, which is arithmetic and needs no governing document. Note what the threshold is for: a quorum makes a body able to act at all, which is a separate question from how many votes any particular motion then needs. The verb is singular, since quorum is the head noun: A quorum of the board was present. The word is Latin for "of whom," lifted out of the wording of old commissions. Variants: a quorum, quorum call, lack a quorum, quorate (chiefly British)

The number of a body's members that must be present before it can transact business — a threshold fixed by rule, not by the speaker.

  1. A quorum of the board was present, and the meeting was called to order.
  2. Without a quorum of the members, the committee may debate but cannot vote.
  3. The bylaws set a quorum of five, which the club has not managed since March.
  4. A quorum of the commission failed to appear and the hearing was continued.
  5. The clerk noted the absence of a quorum and the chamber adjourned.
  6. State law requires a quorum of two-thirds for any vote to amend the charter.

See also: a supermajority of, a plurality of, a majority of, enough, all

a supermajority of#

Pronunciation: /ə ˌsupɚməˈdʒɔrəɾi əv/ · uh soo-per-muh-JOR-uh-tee uhv · main stress on -jor-, secondary stress on su-; the t flaps to /ɾ/ as in majority Type: quantifier (partitive phrase) · Position: central · Register: technical — constitutional and corporate law, legislative reporting, and bylaws. It has become common enough in political journalism since the 1970s that most Americans meet it every budget season Goes with: plural countable nouns naming members or votes — a supermajority of the senate, a supermajority of shareholders, a supermajority of the votes cast. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ a supermajority of support. With of: of is obligatory when the body is named — ✗ a supermajority the senate. Bare a supermajority is common with the body understood: The amendment needs a supermajority. Pattern: a supermajority of [the + body] · a two-thirds / three-fifths supermajority of [body] · require / reach / fall short of a supermajority · supermajority requirement Contrast: A supermajority is any threshold above a simple majority, and like a quorum the actual fraction is set by the governing document — most often two-thirds, sometimes three-fifths or three-quarters. That puts the three threshold words in a series: a plurality is the largest share, a majority is more than half, a supermajority is more than a majority by a stated amount. The verb is singular, since supermajority is the head noun: A supermajority of the members was required. The word is American and modern; older documents simply say a two-thirds vote. Variants: a two-thirds supermajority, a three-fifths supermajority, supermajority requirement, a majority of

A share of a body larger than a simple majority, at a level fixed by statute, charter, or bylaws.

  1. Overriding the veto takes a supermajority of both chambers.
  2. The bylaws require a supermajority of shareholders to approve any sale of assets.
  3. A supermajority of the council voted to rezone the block over the mayor's objection.
  4. The measure fell two votes short of a supermajority.
  5. Several states require a supermajority of the legislature to raise any tax.
  6. A supermajority of three-fifths is written into the district's charter.

See also: a majority of, a plurality of, a quorum of, most, the majority of, the minority of

so much of … as#

Pronunciation: /ˌsoʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv … æz/ · soh MUCH uhv … AZ · read aloud, the as is normally weak, /əz/, and of reduces to /əv/ or /ə/ Type: quantifier in a correlative frame · Position: central — so much fills the determiner slot and the as-clause that completes it follows the noun · Register: legal and formal — the ordinary drafting idiom of statutes, wills, appropriations, and contracts, and stiff to the point of parody anywhere else. Plain-language drafting is steadily replacing it with the part of … that Goes with: uncountable nouns and mass-like abstractions in the much version — so much of the fund as remains, so much of the Act as relates to fees. The countable version is so many … as: so many of the directors as are present. Never a bare singular countable noun. With of: of is obligatory, and so is the as-clause: both halves are required. ✗ so much of the fund alone is incomplete, and so much as by itself is a different construction entirely, an emphatic negative-polarity item meaning "even" (he never so much as looked at it). Pattern: so much of [the + noun] as [verb phrase] · so many of [the + plural] as [verb phrase] · plain-language equivalent: the part of [noun] that [verb phrase] Contrast: The frame is a restrictive quantifier: it does not report a quantity, it carves one out. So much of the appropriation as is unexpended means exactly the unexpended part and no more, which is why drafters reach for it — they must define a share without knowing its size. Keep it clear of two lookalikes. So much as on its own means "even" and needs a negative to lean on. As much … as is the ordinary comparative: as much of the fund as we can spare compares two amounts, where so much of the fund as remains defines one. In your own prose, write the part of the fund that remains. Variants: so many … as, so much as (= even), as much … as, such … as

The part of something, and only that part, which meets the stated condition — the legal drafter's way of quantifying by definition instead of by number.

  1. So much of the appropriation as remains unexpended on June 30 shall revert to the general fund.
  2. So much of the Act as relates to licensing takes effect immediately.
  3. The trustee shall distribute so much of the income as is necessary for the beneficiary's support.
  4. So many of the directors as are present may adjourn the meeting to a later date.
  5. The court considered so much of the record as had been designated on appeal.
  6. A plain-language redraft says the same thing: the part of the appropriation that is unspent reverts.

See also: much, most, enough, a quorum of, so, too, as
