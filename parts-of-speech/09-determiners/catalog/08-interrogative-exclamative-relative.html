Interrogative, exclamative, and relative determiners#

The wh- determiners: the words that ask which noun you mean, exclaim about it, or fasten a whole clause onto it.

← The grammar of determiners · All categories

Almost every word in this file begins with wh-, and that is no accident: they descend from a single Old English question stem and they all still do the same underlying job — they point at a noun without naming it. What separates them is the sentence they land in. In a question the wh- determiner asks the hearer to identify the noun (which gate?); in an exclamation it registers a reaction to it (what a gate!); in a relative clause it hooks a clause onto a noun the sentence already has (the gate whose sign was missing).

Two traps run the length of the file. The first is determiner or pronoun. A determiner has a noun immediately after it — which flight, whose coat, how many boxes. A pronoun stands alone — which is yours?, whose is that?, how many were left? The reliable test is of: the moment you add it, the wh- word has become a pronoun, and the noun inside the of-phrase keeps its own determiner. Which books is a determiner; which of the books is a pronoun. That one test settles the matter for which, what, whatever, whichever, how much, and how many alike. Whose is the single exception, and only because it has no of-partitive at all (✗ whose of these is yours?); for whose the noun test alone does the work.

The second trap is the article rule in exclamations. What and such are predeterminers, so they stand in front of a/an: what a long day, such a long day. How is not a determiner at all — it is a degree adverb that takes an adjective, and it leaves the article exactly where it always was: how long the day was. Nearly every exclamative error in English is some scramble of those two patterns.

One pronunciation note covers the whole file. Most Americans have the wine–whine merger, so what, which, and whatever begin with a plain /w/ and which is a homophone of witch; older speakers and many Southern speakers keep an /hw-/ onset. Whose and whosever are different — their w has been silent for centuries and they begin with /h/. And because determiners are almost never accented, the weak forms are the ones you will actually hear: what as /wət/, of as /əv/ or /ə/, and the t of what flapped into any following vowel, so what a mess comes out [wʌɾəˈmɛs].

Interrogative determiners#

These stand at the front of a question and ask the hearer to identify the noun or measure it. All of them front the entire noun phrase, not just the wh- word: Which gate does it leave from?, never Gate which does it leave from? In an indirect question the same phrase fronts inside the subordinate clause, but the clause keeps statement word order: I asked which gate it left from, not I asked which gate did it leave from. That single rule accounts for more errors than every countability rule in this file combined.

how big a, how good a, how much of a#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ ˈbɪɡ ə/, /ˌhaʊ ˈɡʊd ə/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv ə/ · how-BIG-uh, how-GOOD-uh, how-MUCH-uhv-uh · the article is always the weak /ə/ here and never surfaces as /eɪ/; of reduces to /əv/ and in fast speech to a single syllabic [ə], so how much of a problem runs to [haʊ ˈmʌtʃ əvə ˈprɑbləm]. How itself has no weak form Type: interrogative (degree), and exclamative in the uninverted version · Position: predeterminer — the degree phrase stands in front of a/an, which then does its ordinary job on the noun; how much of a is the of-partitive member of the same slot · Register: neutral in both speech and writing; how much of a is the more colloquial of the two frames Goes with: singular countable nouns only, because the whole construction is built around a/an — how big a house, how good a teacher, how much of a problem. Never a plural (✗ how big a houses, ✗ how big houses) and never an uncountable (✗ how heavy a traffic); with those you drop back to the ordinary quantifiers, which take no article at all — how many houses, how much traffic. How much of a additionally wants a noun that can be graded: ✓ how much of a risk, ✓ how much of a difference, ✗ how much of a chair. With of: How big a and how good a take no of in edited writing: ✗ how big of a house. That said, how big of a is thoroughly established in spoken American English — Midland, Western, and increasingly general — and it is a real spoken pattern rather than a slip of logic; editors still delete it. How much of a is the one member of the trio where of is obligatory and standard: ✗ how much a problem is it? Pattern: How [adjective] a/an [singular countable noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much of a [singular countable noun] [verb]? · How [adjective] a/an [noun] [subject] [verb]! (exclamative, no inversion) · [clause] how [adjective] a/an [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · spoken only: how [adjective] of a [noun] Variants: how big of a, how good of a (spoken American, nonstandard in edited writing), that big a (informal), so/too/as + adjective + a (the statement partners) Contrast: this is the one construction in English where the adjective jumps in front of the article: ordinary a big house inverts to how big a house, never ✗ a how big house. The statement partners are so, too, as — too big a risk, as good a time as any — and how is simply the question-and-exclamation member of that family. Against how (exclamative), the difference is whether a noun is present: How big the house is! has only an adjective and leaves the article behind, while How big a house it is! brings the noun along in front of it. Against what a, what an, where the wh- word takes the article directly and the adjective stays put: ✓ What a big house!, ✗ What big a house! And how big a against how much of a is a difference of scale type — how big a problem measures the problem, how much of a problem asks whether it counts as one at all.

Asks — or exclaims — how far along a scale a single countable thing sits: what size, what quality, or what degree of a thing it really is.

  1. How big a house do you actually need for three people?
  2. How good a lawyer do you have to be to argue that in front of a jury?
  3. How much of a problem is the noise going to be at night?
  4. I hadn't realized how serious a mistake the second signature was.
  5. How much of a difference does the premium gas really make?
  6. How long a drive is it from the airport to the hotel?
  7. She asked how big a deposit the landlord was going to want.

how few#

Pronunciation: /haʊ ˈfju/ · how-FYOO · the stress falls on few, because the point of the phrase is the smallness Type: interrogative (usually exclamative in force) · Position: central — the two words together fill one central-determiner slot · Register: neutral; more often rhetorical than a genuine request for a number Goes with: plural countable nouns only — how few voters, how few options. Never an uncountable (✗ how few money → how little money) and never a singular countable (✗ how few voter). With of: Required before another determiner or a pronoun — how few of the applicants, how few of them. In that construction how few is a pronoun, not a determiner, and the noun inside the of-phrase keeps its own determiner. Never before a bare plural: ✗ how few of applicants. Pattern: how few [plural countable] · how few of [the/these/my + plural noun] · how few of [them/us] · How few [plural countable] [verb]! (exclamative) · [clause] how few [plural countable] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) Contrast: how few is to plural countables what how little is to uncountables, and it is the loaded twin of how many. How many students showed up? is neutral and simply asks for a number; How few students showed up! presupposes that the number was small and usually registers dismay. The negative loading comes from bare few, which means 'not many' — and because the loading is the whole point, there is no ✗ how a few.

Asks — or, far more often, exclaims — about a small number, treating the smallness as the point.

  1. How few people actually read the terms is the frightening part.
  2. How few votes does it take to block the motion?
  3. You would be amazed how few restaurants downtown open on Mondays.
  4. How few of these applicants have any lab experience?
  5. It is shocking how few students can name their own senator.
  6. How few hours can I sleep and still function at work?

See also: how little, how many, few, fewer

how little#

Pronunciation: /haʊ ˈlɪtəl/ · how-LIT-ul · the t is a flap in General American, so it comes out as how-LID-ul Type: interrogative (usually exclamative in force) · Position: central — the two words fill one slot · Register: neutral Goes with: uncountable nouns only — how little time, how little sleep, how little evidence. Never a plural countable (✗ how little friends → how few friends). It also runs as a degree adverb with no noun at all (how little it matters), and that use is not a determiner. With of: Required before another determiner or a pronoun — how little of the budget, how little of it. How little is a pronoun there. Bare how little + bare uncountable takes no of. Pattern: how little [uncountable] · how little of [the/this/my + uncountable] · How little [uncountable] [subject] [verb]! · how little [verb] (degree adverb, no noun) Contrast: the uncountable partner of how few, and the loaded twin of how much. The noun test separates the determiner from the adverb: how little sleep do you need has a noun after it and is a determiner; how little he cares has none and is an adverb of degree. As with how few, the bare negative-loading form is the only one available: ✗ how a little.

Asks or exclaims about a small amount, with the smallness taken for granted.

  1. How little sleep can you get by on?
  2. It is amazing how little money they spent on the whole trip.
  3. How little notice do we actually have to give?
  4. I had not realized how little time was left.
  5. How little of the budget is left for marketing?
  6. How little effort would it take just to answer the email?

See also: how few, how much, little, less

how many#

Pronunciation: /ˈhaʊ ˌmɛni/ · HOW-men-ee · how carries the accent when a noun follows, and the sentence nucleus lands on the noun: HOW many PEOPLE came? Type: interrogative · Position: central — the two words fill one slot · Register: neutral Goes with: plural countable nouns only — how many people, how many times. Never an uncountable (✗ how many money → how much money), never a singular countable (✗ how many person). A measure word makes an uncountable countable and brings how many back: how many gallons of gas, how many pieces of advice. With of: Required before another determiner or a pronoun — how many of the seats, how many of them. Never ✗ how many them and never ✗ how many of seats. In the of-construction how many is a pronoun. Pattern: How many [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How many [plural noun] [verb]? (subject question) · How many of [the/these/my + plural noun]? · [clause] how many [plural noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · How many? (pronoun, noun understood) Contrast: how many is to plural countables what how much is to uncountables — the single most common countability error learners make. Against how few, how many is neutral and expects a number of any size. Note the answer types as well: how many is answered with a number, how much with an amount or a price. In a subject question the verb agrees with the plural noun, not with how many: ✓ How many people are coming?, ✗ How many people is coming?

Asks for the number of a countable set.

  1. How many people are coming to dinner?
  2. I don't know how many times I've explained this.
  3. How many of these boxes can I recycle?
  4. How many hours a week do you actually work?
  5. She asked how many copies we needed.
  6. How many pieces of ID does the DMV want?

See also: how much, how few, how many of, how much of, many

how many fewer, how much less#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ ˌmɛni ˈfjuɚ/, /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈlɛs/ · how-men-ee-FYOO-er, how-much-LESS · fewer is two syllables, /ˈfju.ɚ/, not fyoor Type: interrogative (comparative) · Position: how many / how much fills the central slot; fewer and less are postdeterminers inside the phrase · Register: neutral; common in analytical, business, and journalistic speech Goes with: how many fewer + plural countable (how many fewer flights); how much less + uncountable (how much less sugar). The split is the ordinary fewer/less split, so ✗ how much less flights and ✗ how many fewer sugar. How much less also runs as a degree adverb with no noun (how much less does it cost?), which is not the determiner use. With of: Required before another determiner or a pronoun — how much less of the budget, how many fewer of them. Pronoun there, not determiner. Pattern: How many fewer [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much less [uncountable] …? · How much less [adjective/adverb]? (degree) · … than [comparison] Variants: how many less (widespread in speech, nonstandard in edited writing) Contrast: the downward mirror of how many more and how much more. Fewer counts, less measures. In edited American English less with a plural countable (how many less calories, less calories) is a usage error — though it is extremely common in speech and is fully standard in the fixed expressions less than five miles and one less thing, where the plural is measured rather than counted.

Asks for the size of a decrease — how much smaller the number or the amount will be.

  1. How many fewer flights will the airline run this winter?
  2. How much less sugar does the new recipe use?
  3. I want to know how many fewer people showed up than last year.
  4. How much less of the budget goes to overhead now?
  5. How many fewer hours would you have to work?
  6. How much less rent are you paying in the new place?

See also: how many more, how much more, fewer, less

how many more#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ ˌmɛni ˈmɔɹ/ · how-men-ee-MOR · in an impatient question the accent jumps to more: how many MORE times? Type: interrogative (comparative) · Position: how many fills the central slot; more is a postdeterminer between it and the noun · Register: neutral, frequently impatient or rhetorical Goes with: plural countable nouns only — how many more chairs, how many more times. Never an uncountable (✗ how many more water → how much more water). With of: Partitive how many more of the / of them is possible and is required before a pronoun; how many more is a pronoun there. Never before a bare plural: ✗ how many more of chairs. Pattern: How many more [plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How many more [plural noun] are there? · How many more of [them/these]? · [clause] how many more [plural noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded) · How many more? (pronoun) Contrast: pairs with how much more exactly as many pairs with much. Keep the determiner apart from the pronoun: a noun follows the determiner (how many more chairs), nothing follows the pronoun (how many more do we need?). Word order inside the phrase is fixed: ✓ how many more chairs, ✗ how more many chairs.

Asks for the number still to come, or the number in addition to a quantity already counted.

  1. How many more chairs do we need for the reception?
  2. How many more times do I have to say it?
  3. She wanted to know how many more applications were still unread.
  4. How many more days until the deadline?
  5. How many more of these do you have in the back?
  6. How many more people can the elevator hold?

how many of, how much of#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni əv/, /ˌhaʊ ˈmʌtʃ əv/ · how-MEN-ee-uhv, how-MUCH-uhv · of is nearly always the weak /əv/, and in fast speech reduces to /ə/ — how many of them [ˌhaʊ ˈmɛni ə ðəm] Type: interrogative (partitive — a pronoun construction, not a determiner) · Position: not a determiner slot at all: how many / how much heads its own noun phrase and the of-phrase completes it, so the following noun keeps its own determiner · Register: neutral Goes with: how many of + a definite plural (of the emails, of these people, of them); how much of + a definite uncountable or a whole singular thing (of the money, of this pie, of it). The complement of of must already be determined: ✓ how many of the emails, ✗ how many of emails. With of: Of is the construction itself and is obligatory. Drop it and you are back to the determiner: how many emails, not ✗ how many the emails. Pattern: How many of [the/these/my + plural noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much of [the/this/my + uncountable] …? · How many of [them/us/you]? · How much of [it/that]? · embedded: [clause] how many of [noun phrase] [subject] [verb] Contrast: this is the of test that runs through the whole file, in its clearest form. How many emails did you read is a determiner — the noun follows directly. How many of the emails did you read is a pronoun — an of-phrase follows and the noun inside it keeps its own determiner. The same split separates which from which of, and what from what of. Verb agreement follows the noun in the of-phrase: How many of the files were deleted?

Asks what part or what proportion of an already identified set or mass is involved.

  1. How many of the emails did you actually read?
  2. How much of the budget is already committed?
  3. How many of them showed up on time?
  4. How much of this is he paying for himself?
  5. I wonder how many of those reviews are real.
  6. How much of the movie did you sleep through?

See also: how many, how much, which of, what of

how much#

Pronunciation: /ˈhaʊ ˌmʌtʃ/ · HOW-much · how takes the accent when a noun follows; standing alone as a price question the phrase is usually how MUCH? Type: interrogative · Position: central — the two words fill one slot · Register: neutral Goes with: uncountable nouns only — how much money, how much time, how much sugar. Never a plural countable (✗ how much people → how many people) and never a singular countable (✗ how much chair). With no noun at all it is a pronoun or an adverb asking about price or degree (How much is it?, How much do you care?), not a determiner. With of: Required before another determiner or a pronoun — how much of the money, how much of it. Bare how much + bare uncountable takes no of: ✗ how much of money. Pattern: How much [uncountable] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much [uncountable] [verb]? (subject question) · How much of [the/this/my + uncountable]? · [clause] how much [uncountable] [subject] [verb] (embedded) · How much? (pronoun) Contrast: how much is to uncountables what how many is to plural countables. Note also that how much is the price question in English — How much is that jacket? — where many other languages use their word for what; ✗ What costs the jacket? is a direct-translation error that never sounds native. In a subject question the verb is singular, matching the uncountable noun: ✓ How much money is left?, ✗ How much money are left?

Asks for the amount, quantity, or price of something that is not counted in units.

  1. How much money do you have on you?
  2. How much time do we have before the train?
  3. She asked how much experience I had with Python.
  4. How much salt does the recipe call for?
  5. How much rent are you paying now?
  6. How much of the report did you finish?
  7. How much is that jacket in the window?

See also: how many, how little, how many of, how much of, much

how much more#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈmɔɹ/ · how-much-MOR Type: interrogative (comparative) · Position: how much fills the central slot; more is a postdeterminer between it and the noun · Register: neutral, often impatient or rhetorical Goes with: uncountable nouns only — how much more time, how much more evidence. Never a plural countable (✗ how much more people → how many more people). It is also common with no noun at all, as a degree adverb (how much more expensive is it?) or an endurance question (how much more can I take?) — neither of those is the determiner use. With of: Partitive how much more of the / of this / of it is possible and is required before a pronoun, where how much more is a pronoun. Pattern: How much more [uncountable] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · How much more of [this/it]? · How much more [adjective]? (degree — how much more expensive) · How much more [verb]? · embedded: [clause] how much more [uncountable] [subject] [verb] Contrast: pairs with how many more as much pairs with many. Do not confuse the determiner with the degree adverb: how much more expensive is it? modifies an adjective and is an adverb; how much more money do you need? has a noun after it and is a determiner. The rhetorical use — how much more evidence do you want? — expects no answer and means 'that is already enough.'

Asks for the additional amount — what remains to be added, endured, or paid.

  1. How much more time do you need?
  2. How much more evidence does the jury want?
  3. How much more money is this going to cost me?
  4. I don't know how much more patience I have for this.
  5. How much more work is left on the kitchen?
  6. How much more of this do I have to sit through?

See also: how many more, how much, how many fewer, how much less, more

what (interrogative)#

Pronunciation: /wʌt/, /wɑt/ · WUHT, WAHT · weak form /wət/ when unstressed; the final /t/ flaps into a following vowel, so what a is [ˈwʌɾə] and what is it runs to [ˈwʌɾɪzɪt]. Most Americans have the wine–whine merger and begin the word with plain /w/; older and Southern speakers keep /hwʌt/ Type: interrogative · Position: central · Register: neutral Goes with: any noun type — singular countable (what time, what color), plural countable (what books, what options), uncountable (what music, what evidence). What it cannot do is share the slot with another central determiner: ✗ the what book, ✗ what the book, ✗ what my size. With no noun after it, it is a pronoun, not a determiner: What happened? With of: What of + noun phrase exists, but it is a fixed formal idiom meaning 'and what about' — see what of — not the determiner's partitive. The everyday partitive question uses which of: ✓ Which of these is yours?, ✗ What of these is yours? Pattern: What [noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · What [noun] [verb]? (subject question — What train leaves at six?) · [clause] what [noun] [subject] [verb] (embedded, statement order) · What [noun]! (exclamative — a separate entry) Contrast: what against which is the central choice of this file. Which asks you to pick from a closed set — one both speakers can see, count, or already have in mind. What leaves the set open. On a plane: What airline do you usually fly? (any of hundreds) against Which seat is mine? (a fixed, printed list). Where the choice is spelled out as alternatives, only which is natural: ✓ Which do you want, tea or coffee? Where the field is wide open, what is natural: What music do you like? rather than which music. In a great many everyday cases both are grammatical and the difference is only how bounded the speaker takes the options to be. Remember too that embedded questions keep statement order: ✓ I asked what time it was, ✗ I asked what time was it.

Asks the hearer to identify or specify the noun, with no assumption that the answer comes from a limited list.

  1. What time does the meeting start?
  2. What books did you take with you?
  3. What music do you listen to when you work?
  4. What size shoe does she wear?
  5. I never found out what excuse he gave.
  6. What color should we paint the hallway?
  7. What evidence do they actually have?

See also: which (interrogative), what (exclamative), what (relative), whose

what all#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt ˈɔl/ · wuht-AWL · /ˌwʌt ˈɑl/ for the majority of Americans who merge cot and caught; the two words run together over a flap, [wʌˈɾɔl] Type: interrogative · Position: central — the two words fill one slot · Register: regional and spoken — Southern and Midland American English. Rule-governed and completely natural in those varieties, but regionally marked elsewhere and out of place in edited writing Goes with: plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns in the determiner use — what all classes, what all paperwork. Not used with a singular countable. It is far more often a pronoun standing alone (What all did she say?), and that is the use most non-Southern readers will meet first. With of: Not used. ✗ what all of the classes — the ordinary partitive is which of the classes. Pattern: What all [plural/uncountable noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · What all [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (pronoun) · parallel regional forms: who all, which all, where all, whose all Variants: who all, which all, whose all, what-all (hyphenated in dialect writing) Contrast: the all adds an exhaustive-list reading that plain what does not carry: What did you buy? invites one answer, What all did you buy? asks for the whole list. This is a genuine grammatical feature of Southern and Midland English, not an error of logic — but it is dialect, so flag it as such and use what things, what sorts of things, or everything that in standard writing.

Asks for a complete list rather than a single item. Regional — Southern and Midland American English.

  1. What all classes are you taking this semester?
  2. What all paperwork does the closing require?
  3. What all questions did they ask you in the interview?
  4. She wanted to know what all documents we needed.
  5. What all did you buy at the hardware store?
  6. What all has he told you about the job?

See also: what (interrogative), all, y'all's

what kind of, what sort of, what type of#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt ˈkaɪnd əv/ · wuht-KYND-uhv · kind of reduces heavily to [ˈkaɪnə], and what flaps into it, so the whole phrase is commonly [wəˈkaɪnə] — what kinda car Type: interrogative · Position: central — the whole phrase fills one slot, and the head noun that follows normally takes no article of its own · Register: neutral; what sort of leans British, what type of leans technical Goes with: a following noun that is normally bare — singular countable (what kind of car), plural countable (what kind of shoes, what kinds of shoes), uncountable (what kind of music). Number agreement runs on kind, not on the head noun: what kind of dog for one category, what kinds of dogs for several. Do not insert an article: the neutral form is article-less, and what kind of a car is a marked variant that adds scorn (What kind of a question is that?). With of: Of is part of the item and is obligatory: ✗ what kind car. Pattern: What kind of [bare noun] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? · What kinds of [plural noun] …? · What kind of [noun] is it? · What kind of a [noun] …? (scornful) · What kind [verb]? (pronoun, noun elided) Variants: what sort of, what type of, what brand of, what make of, what manner of (archaic, literary) Contrast: the demonstrative partners are this kind of and these kinds of — the same construction, pointing instead of asking. Against plain what: what car do you drive asks for the identity of one vehicle, what kind of car do you drive asks for the category. Against which kind of: which narrows to a set already on the table (Which kind of icing, buttercream or fondant?).

Asks for the category, class, or variety rather than for the individual item.

  1. What kind of car does she drive?
  2. What kind of music do you put on when you cook?
  3. What sort of person leaves a note like that?
  4. What kinds of documents do I need to bring?
  5. What type of insulation did the contractor use?
  6. What kind of dog is that?
  7. What kind of a question is that?

See also: this kind of, these kinds of, what manner of, what (interrogative), which (interrogative)

what manner of#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt ˈmænɚ əv/ · wuht-MAN-er-uhv · of is the weak /əv/ and reduces further to /ə/, and the t of what flaps straight into manner, so the phrase runs to [wʌɾ ˈmænɚ ə] Type: interrogative · Position: central — the whole phrase fills one slot, and the noun that follows takes no article of its own · Register: archaic and literary. It is alive today only in deliberate period style, in the one fixed phrase what manner of man, and in mock-solemn or comic use Goes with: a bare noun with no determiner of its own — singular countable (what manner of man), plural countable (what manner of creatures), uncountable (what manner of mischief). The noun is usually singular, because the fossilized phrase pulls it that way. Manner itself never pluralizes in this frame: ✗ what manners of man. Whose, whosever, and of which: the genitive wh- words#

Whose is the one wh- determiner that marks possession, and being a possessive it has its home in the possessives file rather than here. It is catalogued there once and covers both jobs it does in this file: whose asks a question (Whose car is that?) and introduces a relative clause (the engineer whose design won), with the free relative whosever and the formal of which alongside it. The archaic legal intensives whosesoever and, in older printing, the two-word whose soever — the genitive members of the -soever family that gives this file whatsoever and whichsoever — are variants of whosever and are listed with it there rather than duplicated here. Four points from those entries are worth repeating here, because they are what sends readers to this file in the first place.

1. Whose works with things, not only with people. English has no separate inanimate genitive relative, so whose covers both: ✓ a house whose roof leaks, ✓ a novel whose ending nobody likes, ✓ a car whose brakes failed. Older handbooks called this an error and told writers to reserve whose for humans. That advice never matched real usage, and the alternative it recommends is clumsy.

2. Of which is the formal alternative — and only for inanimates. A house the roof of which leaks is grammatical and belongs to legal, academic, and technical registers; a house whose roof leaks is the ordinary English sentence. With a human antecedent only whose is possible: ✓ the man whose car was stolen, ✗ the man the car of which was stolen. Note also that of which is not a determiner at all — it is a postmodifying prepositional phrase, which is exactly why the head noun keeps its own article (the roof of which).

3. Whose against who's. Whose asks or states possession; who's is the contraction of who is or who has. Expand it to test: Who's calling? = who is calling, but Whose call is it? has no expansion, so it takes whose. See who's (not a determiner).

4. The determiner-or-pronoun test applies here too. Whose coat is this? has a noun after it and is a determiner; Whose is this? stands alone and is a pronoun. And whose has no of-partitive of its own — that question is built on which: ✓ Which of these is yours?, ✗ Whose of these is yours?

Exclamative determiners#

These stand in front of the article and mark the noun as extreme, remarkable, or hard to believe. Two determiners do the work — what and such — and both obey the same rule: the predeterminer goes before a/an, never after it. ✓ what a long day, ✓ such a long day, and ✗ a such long day, ✗ a what long day. In front of plurals and uncountables the article simply disappears (what beautiful flowers, such nonsense), because a/an was never going to be there. How heads the section even though it is not a determiner at all, because half of every exclamative error in English comes from crossing it with what. The degree predeterminers quite a and rather a fill the identical slot in a cooler register — quite a storm, rather a nuisance — and follow the same article rule, but they assess rather than exclaim.

Relative determiners#

A relative determiner hooks a clause onto a noun while still determining that noun. Two kinds live here. The bound relative determiner needs an antecedent already in the sentence and repeats it with a noun of its own — he arrived at noon, by which time we had left. The fused relative determiner supplies its own antecedent and packages noun and clause into one phrase — what money I had means 'the money that I had,' with nothing standing in front of it. The -ever forms (whatever, whichever) are fused relatives that also do concessive duty: whatever excuse he gives means 'no matter what excuse.' As always, the test for determiner status is whether a noun follows immediately: whatever seat you like is a determiner, take whatever you like is a pronoun.

in which case#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˌwɪtʃ ˈkeɪs/ · in-wich-KAYSS · the noun takes the accent — at which POINT, by which TIME Type: relative · Position: which is the central determiner of the noun, inside a fronted prepositional phrase; the phrase as a whole opens its clause · Register: neutral to formal; standard in careful writing, contracts, and business speech Goes with: a small closed set of abstract nouns naming a situation, moment, or reason — case, point, time, event, instance, circumstances, reason, occasion, juncture, stage, respect. It cannot take an ordinary concrete noun in this relative use: ✗ He sat down, in which chair we later found his keys, ✗ by which sandwich. (The interrogative In which chair did he sit? is a different construction and is fine.) With of: None — the preposition is already supplied by the phrase itself (in, at, by, for, after, during, since).   1. The flight may be canceled, in which case we'll drive.
  2. He arrived at noon, by which time we had already left.
  3. The tenant may refuse, at which point the matter goes to arbitration.
  4. She could still appeal, in which event the hearing would be reopened.
  5. The check might bounce, in which case you'll hear from me.
  6. We waited until Friday, by which time the offer had expired.

Pronunciation: /wʌt/ · WUHT · weak form /wət/, since the fused relative is rarely accented — what money I had runs to [wətˈmʌni] Type: relative (fused relative — it contains its own antecedent) · Position: central · Register: neutral, with a slightly literary edge Goes with: uncountable nouns (what money I had) and plural countable nouns (what photographs survived). A bare singular countable is marginal and normally rephrased (✗? what car I owned → the car I owned). The noun is nearly always small in quantity — the construction carries an undertone of 'the little there was,' which is why the fixed expansions what little and what few exist. With of: None in the determiner use. what of is a separate, non-determiner idiom and means something else entirely. Pattern: what [noun] [subject] [verb] — what money I had · [verb] what [noun] [subject] [verb] — He spent what savings he had · what [noun] [verb] — What evidence remains is inconclusive · expansions: what little [uncountable], what few [plural] Variants: what little, what few, such … as (the formal older equivalent) Contrast: the fused relative has no antecedent — it supplies its own. Against interrogative what, the test is whether an answer is being requested: I asked what money he had embeds a question, He spent what money he had asks nothing. Nonstandard warning: the relative pronoun what in the book what I read is a well-established feature of several British and American vernaculars and is entirely systematic there, but it is not standard written English — use that or which.

'The (little) X that' — packages a noun together with its relative clause into a single phrase, with the quantity implied to be limited.

  1. He spent what money he had on the repair.
  2. She sold what furniture would fit in the truck.
  3. What evidence remains is inconclusive.
  4. Take what supplies you need from the closet.
  5. They saved what photographs survived the flood.
  6. I'll give you what help I can.

Pronunciation: /wɪtʃ/ · WICH · unreduced even though unstressed; /hwɪtʃ/ for speakers who keep the wh- onset Type: relative · Position: central, normally inside a fronted prepositional phrase · Register: formal; only in which case and its siblings are ordinary in everyday American English Goes with: abstract nouns naming a time, point, case, event, or reason, in a supplementary (nonrestrictive) clause — by which time, at which point, in which case, for which reason. With of: of which is a different construction — a relative genitive that names something the antecedent has, not this determiner.   4. They proposed a settlement, which offer my client declined.
  5. Payment may be late, in which event a fee applies.

Notes and tricky cases#

The determiner-or-pronoun test. Every word in this file can be placed by asking one question: does a noun follow it immediately?

Verb agreement follows the noun, not the wh- word. How many and how few take a plural verb because the noun they determine is plural — How many people are coming?, How few options remain? — while how much and how little take a singular — How much money is left?, How little evidence was there? What, which, whatever, and whichever simply inherit their noun's number: Which gate is ours? against Which gates are open? In the of-partitives the controller is the noun inside the of-phrase, never the wh- word in front of it: Which of the files were deleted?, How much of the report is finished?, How many of them have replied? The fused relatives behave the same way — What few records survive are in the archive, What little evidence remains is inconclusive.

Which or what — the practical rule. Use which when the options are countable, visible, or already listed, and what when the field is open. Where the alternatives are spelled out in the question itself, which is effectively obligatory: ✓ Which do you want, tea or coffee? Where no set is presupposed at all, what is the natural choice: What music do you like? In the wide middle ground both are grammatical, and the choice only reflects how bounded the speaker takes the options to be.

Embedded questions keep statement order. This is the commonest error made with every interrogative determiner in the file: ✓ I asked which gate it left from, ✗ I asked which gate did it leave from; ✓ She wants to know how much it costs, ✗ She wants to know how much does it cost.

Nonstandard and regional forms — recognize them, do not imitate them in writing. What all, and its relatives who all, which all, and whose all, are systematic in Southern and Midland American English but regionally marked everywhere else. The relative pronoun what in the book what I read is standard in several vernaculars and not in edited American English; use that or which. Whatsoever and whichsoever in their relative senses are archaic rather than dialectal — legal and biblical, not wrong. So are the which and what manner of, which belong to Chaucer, Shakespeare, and the King James Bible rather than to any living American register, and such other, which is not archaic at all but is confined to drafted documents. One further archaism gets no entry in this file: whether in the old sense 'which of two,' as in Whether of them twain did the will of his father? and Whether is greater, the gold, or the temple? In every text a modern reader will actually meet, that whether either stands alone or takes an of-partitive — which, by the test above, makes it a pronoun, not a determiner. The attributive use died out in Middle English, and the living replacement is which of the two.
