# Numerals, ordinals, fractions, and multipliers

> The determiners that count, rank, divide, and multiply a noun phrase — how many, which one in the sequence, what share of it, and how many times over.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Everything in this file answers a question about quantity or sequence: *how many* (cardinals), *which one in order* (ordinals and general ordinals), *what share* (fractions and part-words), *how many times over* (multipliers), and *roughly how many* (approximators). What unites them grammatically is that most of them are **not central determiners** — they stack around one. Multipliers and fractions sit in the **predeterminer** slot, ahead of *the*, *my*, or *this* (*double **the** price*, *half **that** amount*); cardinals and ordinals sit in the **postdeterminer** slot, behind it (*the **first three** chapters*). That is why *all the first three chapters* is a legal noun phrase with four determiners in it — *all*, *the*, *first*, *three* — and only one of them, *the*, central. English allows exactly one central determiner per noun phrase, so the predeterminers and postdeterminers are the only way to pile more quantity onto the same noun.

Two ordering rules run through the whole group and account for most of the errors. **Ordinal before cardinal**: it is *the first three* questions, never ✗ *the three first questions*. And **counting words stay singular**: *three hundred people*, *two dozen eggs*, *ten percent* — the *-s* appears only when the number is indefinite and *of* follows (*hundreds of people*, *dozens of eggs*). The third trap is smaller but constant: a singular article can stand in front of a plural noun when a quantity word blocks the two apart, because the quantity counts as one unit — *a good twenty miles*, *a mere three days*, *another two hundred dollars*, *a full two weeks*.

One more thing runs through every entry below: **these words are unstressed almost every time you say them.** *A* is /ə/, *an* is /ən/, *the* is /ðə/ before a consonant and /ði/ before a vowel, *of* is /əv/, *to* is /tə/, and *than* is /ðən/. The only members of this class that keep a full stressed vowel are the numbers themselves — *one* is always /wʌn/, never ✗ /wən/ — and that stress difference is what separates *one hour* from *an hour* in speech.

## Cardinal numerals

Cardinals state how many. They are postdeterminers, they take plural countable nouns (except *one*, which takes a singular), and they never take an *-s* when a number governs them. Several members break the postdeterminer pattern and are labeled accordingly below: *zero* behaves like a central determiner, since nothing can stand in front of it (✗ *the zero calories*); *a pair of* and *a gross of* head partitive phrases of their own; and *twain*, the archaic form of *two*, follows its noun instead of preceding it. The inexact small numbers — [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few), [one or two](05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several) — behave like quantifiers rather than numerals and live with the [small quantifiers](05-quantifiers-small.md).

### a couple three

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈkʌpəl ˈθri/ &middot; *uh KUP-uhl THREE* &middot; *a* is weak, /ə/, and in running speech *couple* loses its /l/ into the number — *a couple three days* is usually /ə ˈkʌpə ˈθri ˈdeɪz/; the fuller *a couple two three* /ə ˈkʌpəl tu ˈθri/ runs all three numbers together with no pause
**Русский:** пара-тройка — разг., регионализм Среднего Запада: два-три
**Type:** cardinal numeral (indefinite small count, = two or three) &middot; **Position:** a quantity phrase filling the determiner slot of a bare plural noun; nothing central may join it &middot; **Register:** **regional and colloquial** — Upper Midwestern and Great Lakes speech above all (Wisconsin, Minnesota, Michigan, Chicago), where it passes unnoticed in conversation, and casual speech more widely. It is not standard written English and appears in print only in dialogue.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *a couple three days*, *a couple three guys*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *a couple three day*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *a couple three water* → *a couple three glasses of water*).
**With *of*:** **No *of* before a bare plural noun** — *a couple three beers* — and speakers who use this construction drop *of* from plain *a couple* too (*a couple beers*). *Of* returns before a determiner or a pronoun: *a couple three of the guys*, *a couple three of them*.
**Pattern:** *a couple three* [plural noun] &middot; *a couple two three* [plural noun] &middot; *two-three* [plural noun] &middot; *a couple three of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *a couple three* (standing alone)
**Contrast:** This is *two or three* with the *or* worn away, and the stacked *a couple two three* is one of the clearest markers of Upper Midwestern speech. The count is genuinely approximate — nobody saying it is promising three. In writing, use *two or three*, or [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), which is the neutral form of the same idea. Against [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few): *a few* is vaguer and can run to five or six, while *a couple three* stays pinned at two or three. Agreement is plain plural: *A couple three guys **were** waiting*.
**Variants:** *a couple two three*, *two-three*, *two or three*, *a couple of*, *a few*

Names an indefinite small count of two or three, in Upper Midwestern and casual American speech.

1. Give me *a couple three* days and I'll have the estimate.
2. "There was *a couple three* guys out front waiting on you."
3. We're *two-three* weeks out from opening, tops.
4. He pulled *a couple two three* beers out of the cooler and sat down.
5. It's *a couple three* miles up the county road, past the silo.
6. In writing, make it two or three: ✗ *a couple three days* → ✓ *two or three days*.

**See also:** [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), [a few](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-few), [one or two](05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two), [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [one or more](#one-or-more)

### a gross of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈɡroʊs əv/ &middot; *uh GROHSS uv* &middot; *a* /ə/ and *of* /əv/ are weak; the vowel is the /oʊ/ of *grow*, and the *s* is voiceless — /ɡroʊs/, never ✗ /ɡroʊz/
**Русский:** гросс — 144 штуки, дюжина дюжин (торг., в накладных и каталогах)
**Type:** cardinal numeral (collective, = 144, a dozen dozen) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner of *gross*; the whole phrase heads a partitive &middot; **Register:** **trade and wholesale** — purchase orders, invoices, and stationery and hardware catalogs. An American who has never worked a stockroom may never meet it, and in ordinary prose *144* is clearer.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns, and only the small manufactured things that are actually sold by the gross — *a gross of pencils*, *two gross of screws*, *a gross of buttons*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *a gross of water*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *a gross of pencil*).
**With *of*:** **Required** in the ordinary *a gross of pencils* — ✗ *a gross pencils*. As with *dozen*, the order desk drops it when a number governs a bare plural (*ten gross envelopes*), but *ten gross of envelopes* is the safer written form. *Of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun: *a gross of the small ones*, *a gross of them*.
**Pattern:** *a gross of* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *gross of* [plural noun] &middot; *half a gross of* [plural noun] &middot; *a great gross of* [plural noun] (= 1,728, effectively dead) &middot; *by the gross*
**Contrast:** **The count word stays singular under a number, exactly like *dozen* and *hundred*:** *two gross of screws*, never ✗ *two grosses of screws*. Unlike *dozen*, though, *gross* has no everyday life left outside the supply room, so write *144* unless you are quoting an invoice. Do not confuse the numeral with the far commoner adjective *gross* meaning "before deductions" (*gross income*, *gross weight*) or the slang *gross* meaning disgusting — same spelling, unrelated words. Agreement follows the plural noun: *A gross of pencils **were** delivered*.
**Variants:** *two gross*, *half a gross*, *a great gross* (= 1,728), *a dozen dozen*, *by the gross*, *144*

A hundred and forty-four of something, counted as one wholesale unit.

1. The order sheet says *a gross of* number-two pencils.
2. They shipped *two gross of* machine screws in the wrong size.
3. We go through *a gross of* zip ties a month at the shop.
4. *Half a gross of* envelopes will cover the whole mailing.
5. The invoice reads *3 gross*, which is four hundred thirty-two units.
6. Outside a stockroom nobody says *a gross of* anything — they say 144.

**See also:** [dozen](#dozen), [a pair of](#a-pair-of), [score](#score), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion)

### a jillion

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈdʒɪljən/ &middot; *uh JIL-yuhn* &middot; *a* is weak, /ə/, exactly as in *a million*; the variant *forty-eleven* is /ˌfɔrti ɪˈlɛvən/, *FOR-tee-ih-LEV-uhn*, with the main beat on *-lev-*
**Русский:** тьма, вагон, мильон — сленг, гипербола: несметное количество, никогда буквально
**Type:** cardinal numeral (hyperbolic indefinite — an uncountably large number) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, in the same slot as *million*, after *a* &middot; **Register:** **slang and hyperbolic, never literal.** *A jillion*, *a zillion*, and *a bazillion* are ordinary American informal speech and writing — texts, complaints, sports talk. *Forty-eleven* is narrower: Southern and Appalachian folk speech, often addressed to a child, and it sounds regional to everyone else. None of them belongs anywhere a real number is being reported.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a jillion reasons*, *forty-eleven forms*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *a jillion reason*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *a jillion homework* → *a jillion assignments*).
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare plural noun: *a jillion reasons*. *Of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun (*a jillion of those forms*, *a jillion of them*), and the plural *jillions of* always takes it, exactly like *millions of*.
**Pattern:** *a jillion* [plural noun] &middot; *jillions of* [plural noun] &middot; *forty-eleven* [plural noun] &middot; *a jillion times* &middot; *a zillion / bazillion / gazillion* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** **These are not numbers; they are complaints and boasts wearing a number's clothes**, which is why nothing that measures can sit beside them: ✗ *about a jillion*, ✗ *a jillion and six*, ✗ *at least a jillion*. [umpteen](04-quantifiers-large.md#umpteen) is the same idea one register up, and the matching ordinals live at [nth, umpteenth](#nth-umpteenth). *Forty-eleven* is built the way a child builds a big number — a real ten stacked on a real teen — and that is its flavor in Southern speech: *I've told you forty-eleven times*. Agreement is plain plural: *A jillion things **are** wrong with it*.
**Variants:** *a zillion*, *a bazillion*, *a gazillion*, *a kajillion*, *jillions of*, *forty-eleven* (Southern and Appalachian), *umpteen*, *a hundred and one*

Names a comically large, uncounted number — hyperbole rather than arithmetic.

1. There are *a jillion* reasons not to refinance right now.
2. I've got *a zillion* emails and not one that needs answering.
3. Mama's told you *forty-eleven* times to stay off that porch rail.
4. *Jillions of* mosquitoes came off the river after the rain.
5. He's got *forty-eleven* cousins scattered between here and Knoxville.
6. That's *a bazillion* dollars in equipment sitting in an unlocked shed.

**See also:** [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [nth, umpteenth](#nth-umpteenth), [umpteen](04-quantifiers-large.md#umpteen), [countless](04-quantifiers-large.md#countless), [dozens of](04-quantifiers-large.md#dozens-of)

### a pair of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈpɛr əv/ &middot; *uh PAIR uv* &middot; both *a* /ə/ and *of* /əv/ are weak and unstressed; only *pair* carries a beat
**Русский:** пара — два предмета; счётное слово для парных существительных: брюки, ножницы
**Type:** cardinal numeral (unit counter, = two, and the counter for plural-only nouns) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner of *pair*; the whole phrase heads a partitive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — either a plural-only noun that has no singular at all (*a pair of scissors*, *a pair of pants*, *a pair of glasses*, *a pair of tweezers*) or an ordinary noun naming things that come in twos (*a pair of shoes*, *a pair of gloves*, *a pair of twins*). Never a singular countable noun (✗ *a pair of shoe*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *a pair of water*, ✗ *a pair of advice*).
**With *of*:** **Required and fixed** — ✗ *a pair scissors*. A second *of* builds a partitive on top: *a pair of the good scissors*, *two of the pairs I bought*.
**Pattern:** *a pair of* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *pairs of* [plural noun] &middot; *this/that pair of* [plural noun] &middot; *another pair of* [plural noun] &middot; *a pair of* [pronoun] (rare)
**Contrast:** **This is the only way to count a plural-only noun.** *Scissors*, *pants*, *jeans*, *shorts*, *glasses*, *pliers*, *tweezers*, and *binoculars* have no singular form, so a cardinal cannot touch them directly: **two scissors*, ✗ *one pant*, ✗ *three jeans*. The number counts the pairs instead — *two pairs of scissors*, *one pair of pants*. ✗ *Agreement follows *pair*, not the noun after *of*:** *One pair of scissors **is** missing*, *Two pairs of scissors **are** missing* — but the bare noun by itself stays plural, *The scissors **are** missing*, never ✗ *The scissors is missing*. With ordinary countables, *a pair of* means exactly two where [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of) means roughly two.
**Variants:** *pairs of*, *a set of*, *a couple of* (approximate two), *a brace of* (game birds, archaic), *pant* / *scissor* (attributive singulars only: *a pant leg*, *a scissor kick*)

Counts a noun that has no singular of its own, or one that comes in twos, by counting the units instead.

1. I need *a pair of* scissors that actually cuts.
2. She bought two *pairs of* jeans and returned both.
3. Do you have *a pair of* pliers in the truck?
4. *A pair of* glasses was sitting on the dashboard.
5. There isn't *a pair of* clean socks left in the house.
6. He owns exactly one *pair of* dress shoes.
7. *A pair of* twins joined the class in October.

**See also:** [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [dozen](#dozen), [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several), [both](06-distributives-negatives.md#both)

### dozen

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʌzən/ &middot; *DUZ-uhn* &middot; the article in front is weak: *a dozen* /ə ˈdʌzən/, never ✗ /eɪ ˈdʌzən/
**Русский:** дюжина — двенадцать; после числа без -s: two dozen eggs
**Type:** cardinal numeral (collective, = twelve) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, after *a*, *the*, a possessive, or another cardinal &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a dozen eggs*, *two dozen roses*. Not uncountable nouns (✗ *a dozen water* → *a dozen bottles of water*) and not singular countable nouns (✗ *a dozen egg*).
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare plural noun: *a dozen eggs*, ✗ *a dozen of eggs*. *Of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun: *a dozen of the eggs*, *a dozen of them*. The plural *dozens* always takes *of*: *dozens of complaints*.
**Pattern:** *a/one dozen* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *dozen* [plural noun] &middot; *a dozen of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *dozens of* [plural noun] &middot; *half a dozen* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** Like *hundred* and *thousand*, *dozen* stays singular when a number counts it — *two dozen eggs*, never ✗ *two dozens eggs*. It goes plural only when the count is vague and *of* follows: *dozens of eggs*. The same rule governs *score* (twenty), which survives mainly in *four score and seven*. Agreement follows the plural noun: *A dozen eggs **were** on the counter*. And *dozen* doubles as a **pronoun** — as a determiner it precedes a noun (*a dozen eggs*), as a pronoun it stands alone (*I'll take a dozen*, *he sent two dozen*).
**Variants:** *half a dozen*, *a baker's dozen* (thirteen), *dozens of*, *score* (= twenty, archaic)

Twelve of something, counted as a unit.

1. Pick up *a dozen* eggs on your way home.
2. They ordered *three dozen* chairs for the reception.
3. *Half a dozen* people had already complained.
4. *Dozens of* flights were canceled before noon.
5. I've read *a dozen of* these reports and they all say the same thing.
6. She sent *two dozen* roses and no note.
7. We don't have *a dozen* chairs, let alone three dozen.

**See also:** [a pair of](#a-pair-of), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of), [two, three, four](#two-three-four)

### hundred, thousand, million, billion

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhʌndrəd/, /ˈθaʊzənd/, /ˈmɪljən/, /ˈbɪljən/ &middot; *HUN-druhd, THOW-zuhnd, MIL-yuhn, BIL-yuhn* &middot; the article in front is weak — *a hundred* /ə ˈhʌndrəd/, *a million* /ə ˈmɪljən/; the full *one hundred* /ˈwʌn ˈhʌndrəd/ is the stressed, precise alternative
**Русский:** сто, тысяча, миллион, миллиард — после числительного без -s: three hundred
**Type:** cardinal numeral (large, multiplicative) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *a hundred reasons*, *two million dollars*. Never an uncountable noun directly (✗ *a thousand water* → *a thousand gallons of water*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *a hundred reason*).
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare plural noun: *three hundred people*, ✗ *three hundred of people*. *Of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun (*three hundred of the applicants*, *a million of them*) and after the plural forms (*hundreds of people*, *thousands of dollars*).
**Pattern:** *a/one hundred* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *hundred* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *hundred* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *hundreds of* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *hundred of* [the/my + plural noun]
**Contrast:** **The singular rule is the whole entry.** When a number governs them they never take *-s*: *three hundred people*, *five thousand miles*, *two million dollars* — ✗ *three hundreds people*. The *-s* form exists only for an indefinite, uncounted quantity, and it always requires *of*: *hundreds of people*, *millions of dollars*. Compare *dozen*, which behaves identically. Agreement splits the same way it does for the small cardinals: a plural count takes a plural verb (*Two hundred permits **were** issued*), but a sum of money, a distance, or a stretch of time treated as one amount takes a singular (*Two million dollars **is** a lot to write off*, *Five thousand miles **is** a long way to drive*).
**Variants:** *one hundred* (precise, formal), *a hundred* (ordinary), *hundreds of*, *a hundred and one* (idiomatic "very many"), *trillion*, *zillion* (informal)

A round large number used to count a plural noun.

1. The city issued *two hundred fifty* permits last month.
2. *A thousand* things could go wrong between now and Friday.
3. *Hundreds of* commuters were stranded at the station.
4. The settlement came to *four million* dollars.
5. Only *three hundred of* the tickets were ever sold.
6. She has *a hundred and one* reasons not to go.

**Note on *and*.** The standard American reading of 253 is *two hundred fifty-three*; *two hundred and fifty-three* is standard British and perfectly acceptable in the United States. Keep *and* for the decimal point in dictation (*two hundred and five tenths*) and out of whole numbers in American technical writing.

**See also:** [dozen](#dozen), [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [percent](#percent), [a couple of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-couple-of)

### one

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn/ &middot; *WUN* &middot; **it has no weak form.** *One* keeps its full stressed vowel every time, which is exactly what separates it from the article: *an hour* /ən ˈaʊɚ/ against *one hour* /ˈwʌn ˈaʊɚ/. If the syllable reduces to /ə/ or /ən/, the word was *a/an*, not *one*.
**Русский:** один — числительное; после the значит «единственный»: the one exception
**Type:** cardinal numeral &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer as a numeral (*the one exception*); functions alone as the determiner of a bare noun phrase (*one reason*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns only — *one reason*, *one child*. Never a plural countable noun (✗ *one reasons*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *one information* → *one piece of information*).
**With *of*:** Required before a determiner or a pronoun, and the following noun is **plural** while the verb stays **singular**: *one of the students was late*, *one of them*. Never ✗ *one of student*.
**Pattern:** *one* [singular noun] &middot; *the/my/this one* [singular noun] &middot; *one of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *one of* [pronoun] &middot; *one* [singular noun] *or another*
**Contrast:** *One* against *a/an* is the core contrast. Both precede a singular countable noun, but *one* counts and *a* merely introduces: *I waited an hour* reports duration, *I waited one hour* insists it was exactly one and not two. Use *a* unless the number is the point. Note also that *one* is a pronoun (*I'll take the red one*, *One never knows*) — as a determiner it precedes a noun, as a pronoun it stands alone.
**Variants:** *one and only*, *one or two*, *one another*, *the one* (emphatic), *a one* (informal: *that's a one*)

Counts exactly one of something, as opposed to any other number.

1. **The number.** Exactly one, in contrast to any other number.
2. **The emphatic singular.** *The one* + noun singles out a unique member: *the one thing I asked for*.
3. **"A certain."** Formal, slightly legal or literary: *One Robert Hayes has filed a claim*.

1. *One* witness changed her story twice.
2. I'll be there in *one* hour, two at the most.
3. *One of* the elevators has been out since March.
4. That's *the one* mistake we can't afford to make.
5. Give me *one* good reason to stay.
6. *One* Mr. Delgado called while you were out.

**See also:** [a](01-articles.md#a), [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [first](#first), [another](#another), [once](#once)

### one or more

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌn ɚ ˈmɔr/ &middot; *WUN ur MOR* &middot; *or* is weak here, /ɚ/, and never carries a beat; *one* and *more* both keep their full vowels — the phrase is two stresses with a schwa between them
**Русский:** один или более — юр./тех. формула со значением «не менее одного»
**Type:** cardinal numeral (open-ended lower bound) &middot; **Position:** fills the determiner slot of a bare plural noun; it admits no central determiner in front of it (✗ *the one or more copies*) &middot; **Register:** **formal, legal, and technical** — statutes, standards, specifications, and software documentation, where it is the standard way to write "at least one." It is stiff in conversation, which prefers *one or two* or *some*.
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** — *one or more witnesses*, *one or more of the following conditions*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *one or more witness*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *one or more evidence* → *one or more pieces of evidence*).
**With *of*:** **Required** before a determiner or a pronoun, and the noun after *of* stays plural: *one or more of the conditions*, *one or more of them*, *one or more of the following*.
**Pattern:** *one or more* [plural noun] &middot; *one or more of* [the/these + plural noun] &middot; *one or more of the following* &middot; *of one or more* [plural noun] &middot; *one or more* (standing alone)
**Contrast:** **The verb goes plural, which surprises everyone who sees *one* first:** *One or more of the panels **are** loose*, *If one or more conditions **are** met…*. That is the reverse of [one of](#one), where the verb is singular (*one of the panels **is** loose*) — coordinating with *more* is what pluralizes it. Against *at least one*, which means the same thing in plainer words but takes a **singular** noun (*at least one witness*): drafters who want the plural noun reach for *one or more*. Against [one or two](05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two), a casual estimate with a ceiling on it, this one has no ceiling at all. In plain-language editing, *one or more of the following* is usually just *any of the following*.
**Variants:** *at least one*, *one or two*, *any of*, *one or more of the following*, *≥ 1* (technical)

Sets a floor of one with no ceiling above it: at least one, possibly many.

1. The form must be signed by *one or more* witnesses.
2. Coverage lapses if *one or more* of the conditions are not met.
3. Each account may be linked to *one or more* devices.
4. The statute applies wherever *one or more* parties reside in the state.
5. Check *one or more* of the boxes below.
6. *One or more* of the sensors were reporting bad data all week.

**See also:** [one](#one), [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [one or two](05-quantifiers-small.md#one-or-two), [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [two, three, four](#two-three-four)

### score

**Pronunciation:** /skɔr/ &middot; *SKOR* &middot; the article in front is weak: *a score of years* /ə ˈskɔr əv ˈjɪrz/; in the compounds the stress stays on the first element, *threescore* /ˈθriskɔr/, *fourscore* /ˈfɔrskɔr/
**Русский:** двадцать — устар., книжн.: four score and seven — восемьдесят семь
**Type:** cardinal numeral (collective, = twenty) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, after *a*, *the*, or another cardinal &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** An American meets it in three places and almost nowhere else: the King James Bible (*threescore and ten*), the Gettysburg Address (*Four score and seven years ago*), and the living journalistic plural [scores of](04-quantifiers-large.md#scores-of), which no longer means twenty at all. Using it for a real count of twenty is a period costume.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns — *fourscore years*, *a score of witnesses*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *a score of water*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *a score of year*).
**With *of*:** **None in the old bare use:** *fourscore years*, *threescore and ten*. *Of* is normal once the indefinite article appears — *a score of years*, *a score of witnesses* — and it is obligatory with the modern vague plural, *scores of complaints*.
**Pattern:** [cardinal] *score* [plural noun] &middot; *a score of* [plural noun] &middot; *threescore and ten* &middot; *fourscore and seven* &middot; *scores of* [plural noun] (modern)
**Contrast:** **It stays singular under a number, like *dozen* and *hundred*:** *four score years*, ✗ *four scores years*. The *-s* belongs only to the vague modern plural, which always takes *of*. *Threescore* (60) and *fourscore* (80) are single words frozen inside their famous phrases — *threescore and ten* is the biblical span of a life, seventy years, and *fourscore and seven* is eighty-seven. Note that *score* keeps a whole separate life as an ordinary noun — a tally, a grudge, a piece of written music — and none of that is a determiner.
**Variants:** *threescore* (= 60), *fourscore* (= 80), *a score of*, *scores of* (modern, vague), *twoscore*, *three score and ten*

Twenty of something counted as a unit — an archaic collective numeral surviving in scripture, oratory, and quotation.

1. *Four score* and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent a new nation.
2. The days of our years are *threescore* years and ten. (Psalm 90, King James Version)
3. He had been dead a *score of* years before anyone opened the trunk.
4. *Fourscore* militiamen answered the muster that morning.
5. *Scores of* protesters gathered outside the statehouse — the only form still in ordinary use.
6. Write twenty, not *a score of*, unless you are quoting somebody.

**See also:** [dozen](#dozen), [a gross of](#a-gross-of), [scores of](04-quantifiers-large.md#scores-of), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [twain](#twain)

### twain

**Pronunciation:** /tweɪn/ &middot; *TWAYN* &middot; one syllable, rhyming with *rain*; **there is no weak form** — *twain* is always fully stressed, which is part of why it survives only inside set phrases
**Русский:** два, двое — устар., поэт.; стоит после существительного: in twain — надвое
**Type:** cardinal numeral (archaic, = two) &middot; **Position:** **postpositive or fused-head** — it follows its noun (*days twain*) or stands alone after a determiner (*the twain*, *in twain*). It does not sit in front of a noun in any living construction. &middot; **Register:** **archaic and literary.** An American meets it in the King James Bible (*they twain shall be one flesh*), in Kipling (*never the twain shall meet*), in the frozen *cut in twain*, and in Mark Twain's pen name, taken from the leadsman's river cry *mark twain* — two fathoms. Nowhere else.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns, and only in the archaic postpositive frame — *brethren twain*, *days twain*. It cannot precede a noun: ✗ *twain days*, ✗ *twain reasons*. As a fused head it takes *the* or *they* and stands alone: *the twain*, *they twain*.
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*. The surviving phrases carry prepositions instead: *in twain*, *between the twain*.
**Pattern:** [plural noun] *twain* (archaic postpositive) &middot; *the twain* (fused head) &middot; *in twain* (= into two pieces) &middot; *they twain* (biblical) &middot; *mark twain* (river sounding)
**Contrast:** **This is *two* in the shape *two* used to have.** English once had more than one form of the numeral, and *twain* is the survivor of the one used when the number stood after its noun or alone — which is exactly why it never occupies a determiner slot the way [two, three, four](#two-three-four) does. *Days twain* is a poetic inversion of *two days*, not a different determiner, and *never the twain shall meet* is a fused-head pronoun meaning "the two of them." Do not treat it as a fancy synonym you can drop into ordinary prose: outside quotation and deliberate pastiche it reads as costume.
**Variants:** *two* (the living form), *in twain*, *the twain*, *they twain*, *mark twain* (two fathoms), *both*

The archaic form of *two*, surviving only after its noun, in fixed phrases, and in quotation.

1. *…and they twain shall be one flesh.* (Matthew 19:5, King James Version)
2. Oh, East is East, and West is West, and never the *twain* shall meet.
3. The veil of the temple was rent *in twain* from the top to the bottom.
4. He took his soundings and called "mark *twain*" — two fathoms, safe water.
5. In older verse the number follows the noun: *days twain* for two days.
6. Nothing in modern American English lets it stand in front of a noun: ✗ *twain days* → ✓ *two days*.

**See also:** [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [both](06-distributives-negatives.md#both), [score](#score), [a pair of](#a-pair-of), [one](#one)

### two, three, four

**Pronunciation:** /tu/, /θri/, /fɔr/ &middot; *TOO, THREE, FOR* &middot; the numbers are always stressed, but the function words around them are not: *three of the witnesses* is /ˈθri əv ðə ˈwɪtnəsəz/, with *of* /əv/ and *the* /ðə/ both reduced
**Русский:** два, три, четыре — количественные числительные; идут после артикля и после порядкового
**Type:** cardinal numeral &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows any central determiner and follows an ordinal &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *two witnesses*, *three days*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *two witness*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *three information*, ✗ *four advice* → *three pieces of information*, *four pieces of advice*). The one systematic exception is the hyphenated premodifier, where the noun goes **singular**: *a three-day weekend*, *a two-mile run*, *a six-foot fence*.
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare plural noun: *three witnesses*. *Of* is required before a determiner or a pronoun: *three of the witnesses*, *two of them*, *four of my clients*.
**Pattern:** [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *the/my/these* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *the* [ordinal] [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *a* [cardinal]*-*[singular measure noun] [noun]
**Contrast:** **Ordinal before cardinal, always:** *the first two questions*, ✗ *the two first questions*; *her last three albums*, ✗ *her three last albums*. And note the agreement split: a plural count takes a plural verb (*Three years have passed since then*), but a measured quantity treated as one unit takes a singular (*Three years is a long time to wait*, *Twenty miles is too far to walk*). Nouns that are permanently plural — *scissors*, *pants*, *glasses*, *tweezers* — cannot be counted directly at all (✗ *two scissors*); they take [a pair of](#a-pair-of), and the number counts the pairs: *two pairs of scissors*. **Nonstandard:** *them two*, *them three* (*them two guys never showed*) replaces the demonstrative *those* with object-form *them*; it is widespread in casual and regional American speech and is not standard written English — see [them](02-demonstratives.md#them).
**Variants:** *twenty-one* through *ninety-nine* (hyphenated), *two-and-a-half*, *a pair of* (with permanently plural nouns), *nought/naught* (rare), *them two* (nonstandard)

Any exact number above one, counting a plural noun.

1. *Three* witnesses gave *two* conflicting accounts.
2. The *first two* chapters are free; after that you pay.
3. She ran *twenty-six* miles in *four* hours.
4. *Two of* the panelists never showed up.
5. We're taking a *three-day* weekend at the end of the month.
6. *Five* years is a long time to hold a grudge.

**See also:** [one](#one), [a pair of](#a-pair-of), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [first](#first), [third, fourth, fifth](#third-fourth-fifth), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### zero

**Pronunciation:** /ˈzɪroʊ/, also /ˈziroʊ/ &middot; *ZEER-oh*
**Русский:** ноль, нулевой — нулевое количество; в отличие от прочих чисел заменяет собой артикль
**Type:** cardinal numeral (null quantity) &middot; **Position:** **central determiner in practice** — although it is a number, and numbers are normally postdeterminers, *zero* fills the slot *no* would fill and admits nothing in front of it: ✗ *the zero calories*, ✗ *my zero absences*. It is a cardinal that behaves like a central determiner. &middot; **Register:** neutral in technical and commercial writing; emphatic in speech
**Goes with:** **plural** countable nouns (*zero calories*, *zero absences*) and uncountable nouns (*zero tolerance*, *zero evidence*). Not a singular countable noun (✗ *zero reason* → *no reason*). The plural is the surprise: every number but *one* takes a plural noun, so it is *zero degrees* and *0.5 degrees*, not ✗ *zero degree*. Agreement follows the noun: *Zero injuries **were** reported*, but *Zero tolerance **is** the policy*.
**With *of*:** Rare and best avoided; use *none of* instead — *none of the applicants*, not ✗ *zero of the applicants* (heard informally, but *none of* is the standard partitive).
**Pattern:** *zero* [plural noun] &middot; *zero* [uncountable noun] &middot; *zero* [noun] *for* [noun]
**Contrast:** *No* is the ordinary negative determiner and takes singular countable, plural countable, and uncountable nouns alike (*no reason*, *no reasons*, *no evidence*). *Zero* is narrower — plural and uncountable only — and carries a technical or emphatic edge: *zero tolerance* is a policy, *no tolerance* is a description. For the partitive, *none of* replaces both.
**Variants:** *0*, *nil* (sports, British), *naught/nought*, *zilch* (slang), *no* (the general negative determiner)

Names an absence of quantity as a counted amount rather than as a plain denial.

1. The district has a *zero* tolerance policy for weapons.
2. This brand advertises *zero* calories and *zero* sugar.
3. She had *zero* interest in relitigating the argument.
4. The forecast calls for *zero* chance of rain through Sunday.
5. Two of the sensors reported *zero* degrees overnight.
6. There were *zero* injuries, which the chief called a miracle.

**See also:** [one](#one), [none of](06-distributives-negatives.md#none-of), [a fraction of](#a-fraction-of)

## Ordinals

Ordinals rank an item within a sequence. They are postdeterminers, they almost always follow *the* or a possessive, and they come **before** any cardinal in the same phrase.

### final

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfaɪnəl/ &middot; *FY-nuhl*
**Русский:** последний, окончательный — завершающий весь ряд
**Type:** general ordinal (adjective in the postdeterminer slot) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, after a central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the final episode*), plural countable nouns when a cardinal follows or the set is plural (*the final two episodes*, *the final results*), and uncountable nouns in fixed phrases (*the final word*, *final approval*).
**With *of*:** Takes *of* only through a noun: *the final of the three*, *the final of the series*. Not a partitive determiner on its own.
**Pattern:** *the/my/this final* [noun] &middot; *the final* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *final* [noun] (bare, in fixed compounds: *final notice*, *final approach*)
**Contrast:** *Final* and *last* overlap, and the difference is definiteness of ending. *Final* says nothing more will follow, ever — *the final episode* of a canceled show. *Last* can simply mean most recent (*last week*, *the last email you sent*), which *final* can never mean. When you want "no more after this," *final* is unambiguous; *last* is not.
**Variants:** *the very final*, *the final-ever* (informal), *ultimate* (formal)

Marks the item that ends a sequence with nothing after it.

1. The *final* two episodes were filmed back to back.
2. This is your *final* notice before we send the account to collections.
3. She missed the *final* deadline by nine minutes.
4. The judge reserved the *final* word for herself.
5. Their *final* three attempts all failed for the same reason.
6. We're waiting on *final* approval from the state.

**See also:** [last](#last), [next](#next), [first](#first), [remaining](#remaining)

### first

**Pronunciation:** /fɚst/ &middot; *FURST* &middot; the article in front is weak and takes its consonant form: *the first* /ðə ˈfɚst/, never ✗ /ði ˈfɚst/
**Русский:** первый — порядковое числительное
**Type:** ordinal numeral &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the first draft*), plural countable nouns when a cardinal or a plural set follows (*the first three days*, *the first responders*), and mass nouns naming an instance (*the first snow of the season*). It normally requires a central determiner in front of it — *the*, a possessive, or a demonstrative — and is ungrammatical bare before a common noun in most contexts (✗ *first draft was rough* → *the first draft was rough*), though it stands bare in fixed phrases (*first thing tomorrow*, *first base*, *first prize*, *at first light*).
**With *of*:** *of* is required for the partitive: *the first of the month*, *the first of three children*, *the first of them to arrive*.
**Pattern:** *the/my/this first* [noun] &middot; *the first* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a first* [noun] &middot; *the first of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *first* [noun] (fixed phrases)
**Contrast:** **Ordinal before cardinal.** It is *the first three chapters*, never ✗ *the three first chapters* — this is the single most common ordering error in the whole determiner system. Note also *the first* against *a first*: *the first attempt* identifies a unique one, *a first attempt* treats it as one of a possible series (*a first attempt at bread*). *First* is also a **pronoun** — every ordinal is — and it drops the noun rather than the determiner: as a determiner it precedes a noun (*the first chapter*), as a pronoun it stands alone with *the* still attached (*She was the first to arrive*, *I'll take the first*). Bare *first* with no *the* is the adverb (*First, sign here*).
**Variants:** *the very first*, *first-ever*, *the first-time* (attributive), *foremost*

Ranks an item at position one in a sequence.

1. The *first* three chapters are free.
2. This is *my first* time flying out of Newark.
3. She was *the first of* four children.
4. *First* thing tomorrow, call the insurance company.
5. It was *a first* attempt, and it showed.
6. All *the first* six rows were reserved for family.

**See also:** [second](#second), [third, fourth, fifth](#third-fourth-fifth), [last](#last), [next](#next), [one](#one), [former, latter](#former-latter)

### last

**Pronunciation:** /læst/ &middot; *LAST*
**Русский:** последний; прошлый — во временном значении без артикля: last week — на прошлой неделе
**Type:** general ordinal &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer; in the time sense it stands alone with no article (*last week*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the last chapter*, *last night*), plural countable nouns (*the last two weeks*, *my last three jobs*), and uncountable nouns (*the last milk in the fridge*, *the last of the money*).
**With *of*:** *of* is required for the partitive: *the last of the milk*, *the last of them*, *the last of the survivors*.
**Pattern:** *the/my/this last* [noun] &middot; *the last* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *last* [time noun] (no article) &middot; *the last of* [the/my + noun] &middot; *the last* [noun] *to* [verb]
**Contrast:** **The article changes the meaning, and this is the trap.** *Last week* (bare) means the calendar week before this one; *the last week* means the most recent seven days, whichever days those are. Same for *last year* vs. *the last year*, *last month* vs. *the last month*. Never insert a preposition into the bare form: *I saw her last week*, ✗ *in last week*. Against *final*: *last* can mean merely most recent, *final* cannot.
**Variants:** *the very last*, *last-minute* (attributive), *the last but one* (penultimate), *last but not least*

Ranks an item at the end of a sequence, or points back to the one immediately before now.

1. **Final in a sequence.** The one at the end, with nothing after it.
2. **Most recent.** The one immediately before now — this is the article-less use with time nouns.
3. **Least likely.** In *the last* + noun + relative clause: *the last person I'd ask*.

1. The *last* two questions on the exam were the hardest.
2. I saw her *last* week at the farmers market.
3. Sales are up nine percent over *the last* six months.
4. He drank *the last of* the coffee and didn't make more.
5. She's *the last* person I'd trust with a secret.
6. *Last* night's storm knocked out power on three blocks.

**See also:** [next](#next), [final](#final), [first](#first), [penultimate, second-to-last, next-to-last](#penultimate-second-to-last-next-to-last), [previous, prior, preceding](#previous-prior-preceding), [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of)

### next

**Pronunciation:** /nɛkst/ &middot; *NEKST*
**Русский:** следующий; будущий — во временном значении без артикля: next week — на следующей неделе
**Type:** general ordinal &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer; in the time sense it stands alone with no article (*next week*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the next flight*, *next Tuesday*) and plural countable nouns (*the next three trains*, *the next few weeks*). **Not uncountable nouns** — a sequence needs discrete items to have a next one, so ✗ *the next money*, ✗ *the next funding*, ✗ *the next advice*. Where the noun is a mass, use *the rest of* (*the rest of the funding*) or count it in units (*the next round of funding*, *the next two gallons*).
**With *of*:** Only in the fixed legal phrase *next of kin* and in the fused-head partitive, where the noun after *of* is plural or a number: *the next of the three*, *the next of them*. It is not a general partitive determiner — ✗ *the next of the milk*.
**Pattern:** *the next* [noun] &middot; *the next* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *next* [time noun] (no article) &middot; *the next* [noun] *to* [verb] &middot; *next door / next of kin*
**Contrast:** Mirror image of *last*, with the same article trap. *Next week* (bare) is the calendar week after this one; *the next week* is the seven days from now. And *next Tuesday* is genuinely ambiguous in American speech — it can mean the Tuesday of this coming week or the Tuesday after that, so name the date if it matters. Against *following*: *next* is measured from now, *following* from some other point already established (*the meeting was Monday; the following day she resigned*).
**Variants:** *the very next*, *next-day* (attributive), *next of kin*, *the next but one*

1. **Immediately after this one in a sequence.** *The next flight*, *the next chapter*.
2. **The coming one, counted from now.** With time nouns and no article: *next week*, *next summer*.

1. *The next* three flights to Denver are already full.
2. I'll call you *next* Tuesday about the invoice.
3. *The next* person who interrupts is leaving.
4. We spent *the next* two hours on hold.
5. Her office is *next* door to the copy room.
6. They listed his brother as *next* of kin.

**See also:** [last](#last), [subsequent, following](#subsequent-following), [another](#another), [final](#final)

### nth, umpteenth

**Pronunciation:** /ɛnθ/, /ˌʌmpˈtinθ/ &middot; *ENTH, ump-TEENTH*
**Русский:** энный (тех.); в сотый раз, надцатый (разг., с досадой)
**Type:** indefinite ordinal &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, effectively always after *the* &middot; **Register:** *nth* is neutral to technical; *umpteenth* is informal and always exasperated
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns — *the nth term*, *the umpteenth delay*. Not plural countable nouns (✗ *the umpteenth delays*) and not uncountable nouns.
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*.
**Pattern:** *the nth* [singular noun] &middot; *to the nth degree* &middot; *for the nth/umpteenth time* &middot; *the umpteenth* [singular noun]
**Contrast:** *Nth* is a real variable in mathematics (*the nth term of the series*) that has leaked into everyday speech as an intensifier (*to the nth degree*). *Umpteenth* has no mathematical life at all — it is purely a complaint, meaning "one more in a series I have lost count of and resent." *Zillionth* and *millionth* work the same way informally.
**Variants:** *the nth degree*, *umpteen* (the cardinal), *zillionth*, *for the millionth time* (hyperbolic)

Ranks an item at an unspecified high position in a sequence.

1. For *the nth* time, the deadline has not moved.
2. The formula gives you *the nth* term without computing the ones before it.
3. He proofreads everything to *the nth* degree.
4. This is *the umpteenth* delay on this project.
5. She explained it for *the umpteenth* time and gave up.
6. That's *the zillionth* email about the parking lot this week.

**See also:** [third, fourth, fifth](#third-fourth-fifth), [penultimate, second-to-last, next-to-last](#penultimate-second-to-last-next-to-last), [another](#another), [same](#same), [umpteen](04-quantifiers-large.md#umpteen)

### penultimate, second-to-last, next-to-last

**Pronunciation:** /pəˈnʌltəmət/, /ˌsɛkənd tə ˈlæst/, /ˌnɛkst tə ˈlæst/ &middot; *puh-NUL-tuh-mit, SEK-uhnd-tuh-LAST, NEKST-tuh-LAST* &middot; the *to* inside the compounds is weak, /tə/, and is never stressed; *the penultimate* is /ðə pəˈnʌltəmət/
**Русский:** предпоследний — penultimate книжн., два других обиходные
**Type:** general ordinal (counted backward from the end) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, after *the* or a possessive &middot; **Register:** *penultimate* formal and literary; *second-to-last* and *next-to-last* are the ordinary American forms
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the penultimate chapter*, *the second-to-last row*) and plural countable nouns when a cardinal follows (*the second-to-last three entries*, rare). **Not uncountable nouns** — ✗ *the penultimate money*, ✗ *the next-to-last evidence* — because counting backward, like counting forward, needs discrete items. All three forms normally require *the* or a possessive: ✗ *penultimate chapter* bare, though hyphenated attributives appear without one (*next-to-last place*).
**With *of*:** *of* is required for the partitive: *the penultimate of the four movements*, *the second-to-last of them*, *the next-to-last of the survivors*.
**Pattern:** *the penultimate* [singular noun] &middot; *the second-to-last* [singular noun] &middot; *the next-to-last* [singular noun] &middot; *the* [ordinal]*-to-last* [noun] &middot; *the penultimate of* [the + plural noun]
**Contrast:** **The counting runs backward, and it is off by one for most people.** *Penultimate* means **second from the end**, not "the ultimate, only more so" — that mistaken reading is common enough that plain *second-to-last* is safer anywhere it matters. Going further back, *antepenultimate* is third from the end; past that, English gives up and says *fourth from the end*. Against [last](#last): *last* is one from the end, *second-to-last* is two from the end. The British *the last but one* means exactly *second-to-last* and reads as an import in American prose. *Second-to-last* and *next-to-last* are interchangeable in American English; both take hyphens.
**Variants:** *antepenultimate* (third from the end), *the last but one* (British), *second from the end*, *third-to-last*, *the runner-up* (of a ranking, a noun rather than a determiner)

Ranks an item by counting backward from the end of a sequence.

1. The *penultimate* chapter is the one everybody remembers.
2. She was sitting in *the second-to-last* row.
3. *The next-to-last* question tripped up half the room.
4. Stress falls on *the penultimate* syllable in most Spanish words.
5. Isn't this *the second-to-last* box we have to move?
6. He finished *third-to-last* and still made the team.
7. *The penultimate of* the four movements is the only quiet one.

**See also:** [last](#last), [final](#final), [third, fourth, fifth](#third-fourth-fifth), [former, latter](#former-latter), [remaining](#remaining)

### second

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛkənd/ &middot; *SEK-uhnd*
**Русский:** второй — порядковое числительное
**Type:** ordinal numeral &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the second draft*, *a second chance*), plural countable nouns when a cardinal follows or the set is inherently plural (*the second two rows*, *second helpings*, *second thoughts*), and uncountable nouns only in fixed phrases (*second nature*, *on second thought*). Note that *a second helping* and *second helpings* are ordinary countables, not mass nouns — the fixed-phrase exemption covers only the abstract pair.
**With *of*:** *of* is required for the partitive: *the second of the month*, *the second of her three sons*.
**Pattern:** *the/my/this second* [noun] &middot; *a second* [noun] &middot; *the second of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *every second* [singular noun] &middot; *second* [noun] (fixed: *second base*, *second thought*)
**Contrast:** **The article is a meaning switch.** *The second chance* is chance number two in a numbered series; *a second chance* just means one more, a synonym of *another*. *A second opinion*, *a second helping*, *a second look* — all of these are the "additional" sense, not the ranking sense. Note also *every second* + singular noun, which means alternate ones (*every second row*), not "each pair."
**Variants:** *a second*, *second-best*, *second-hand*, *second to none*, *every second*

1. **Rank two in a sequence.** *The second chapter*, *the second attempt*.
2. **One more of the same kind.** With *a*: *a second chance*, *a second opinion* — equivalent to *another*.

1. *The second* witness contradicted the first.
2. She asked for *a second* opinion before scheduling surgery.
3. Nobody wants *a second* meeting about the same memo.
4. He was born on *the second of* March.
5. They water the plants *every second* day.
6. Her *second* novel sold better than her first.

**See also:** [first](#first), [another](#another), [third, fourth, fifth](#third-fourth-fifth), [former, latter](#former-latter)

### third, fourth, fifth

**Pronunciation:** /θɚd/, /fɔrθ/, /fɪfθ/ &middot; *THURD, FORTH, FIFTH* &middot; **this is where *the* switches forms**: /ðə/ before a consonant (*the third* /ðə ˈθɚd/, *the fifth* /ðə ˈfɪfθ/) but /ði/ before a vowel (*the eighth* /ði ˈeɪtθ/, *the eleventh* /ði ɪˈlɛvənθ/). It is never stressed either way.
**Русский:** третий, четвёртый, пятый — порядковые числительные
**Type:** ordinal numeral &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the third try*, *the fifth attempt*) and plural countable nouns when a cardinal follows or two ordinals are coordinated (*the third two weeks*, rare; *the third and fourth chapters*). **Not uncountable nouns** — ✗ *the third water*, ✗ *the fourth advice*; a mass noun has to be counted in units before it can be ranked (*the third glass of water*, *the fourth piece of advice*). Like all ordinals it normally requires a central determiner in front of it, and it stands bare only in fixed compounds — *third base*, *third rail*, *fifth wheel*, which are ordinary singular countable nouns rather than exceptions to the rule.
**With *of*:** *of* is required for the partitive: *the third of the four defendants*, *the fifth of July*.
**Pattern:** *the/my/this* [ordinal] [singular noun] &middot; *a* [ordinal] [singular noun] &middot; *the* [ordinal] *of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *the* [ordinal] [noun] *to* [verb]
**Contrast:** Ordinals still come before cardinals (*the third two weeks* is legal but odd; *the first three weeks* is the normal shape). Watch the spelling of the irregulars, which is where most written errors land: *first*, *second*, *third*, *fifth* (not ✗ *fiveth*), *eighth* (one *t*), *ninth* (no *e*), *twelfth*, *twentieth*. In compound ordinals only the **last** element takes the ordinal form: *twenty-first*, ✗ *twentieth-first*.
**Variants:** *sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth, twelfth, twentieth, twenty-first, hundredth*, *3rd / 4th / 5th* (numerals)

Ranks an item at position three, four, five, and upward in a sequence.

1. This is *the third* time the server has gone down this week.
2. Her *fourth* album is the one people actually remember.
3. He finished *fifth* out of forty and was thrilled.
4. The *twenty-first* floor is mechanical; the elevator skips it.
5. We meet on *the third* Thursday of every month.
6. *The ninth* juror asked to be excused.

**See also:** [first](#first), [second](#second), [two, three, four](#two-three-four), [a quarter, a third, two-thirds](#a-quarter-a-third-two-thirds), [nth, umpteenth](#nth-umpteenth)

## General ordinals

These express sequence without a number — before, after, the one already mentioned, one more of the same. They sit in the postdeterminer slot with the true ordinals, with two exceptions: *another* and the dialect *t'other* are **central** determiners, because an article is baked into each of them — *an* into the first, *the* into the second.

### another

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnʌðɚ/ &middot; *uh-NUTH-ur* &middot; the first syllable **is** the indefinite article, reduced to /ə/ — *another* is *an* + *other* welded together, and the stress never moves onto it (✗ /eɪˈnʌðɚ/)
**Русский:** ещё один; другой — включает в себя артикль an, поэтому другого определителя не терпит
**Type:** general ordinal / indefinite determiner (historically *an* + *other*) &middot; **Position:** **central determiner** — it contains the indefinite article, which is why nothing else central can join it &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns** — *another chance*, *another delay*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *another information* → *more information*) and never a plural countable noun on its own (✗ *another books*). **The one exception is a quantity phrase**, where a plural noun is allowed because the quantity counts as a single unit: *another three days*, *another two hundred dollars*, *another few minutes*, *another ten percent*.
**With *of*:** **Required** for the partitive, and the noun after *of* must be **plural and determined** while the verb stays **singular**: *another of her clients **has** filed*, *another of them*. ✗ *another of client*, ✗ *another of clients*.
**Pattern:** *another* [singular noun] &middot; *another* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *another of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *another* [singular noun] *like* [noun] &middot; *one another*
**Contrast:** ***Another* vs. *other*** is the pair to memorize. *Another* is one word, always singular, and never follows a determiner: ✗ *the another day*. *Other* takes a determiner in front of it and works with plurals and uncountables: *the other day*, *other books*, *other evidence*. So a bare singular countable noun requires *another*, never bare *other*: ✓ *another reason*, ✗ *other reason*. Also distinguish the two senses of *another* itself — "one more of the same" (*another beer*) and "a different one" (*let's try another route*). And note that *another* is a **pronoun** as well as a determiner: as a determiner it precedes a noun (*another cup*), as a pronoun it stands alone (*I'll have another*, *one after another*). The verb stays singular either way — *Another **is** on the way*.
**Variants:** *one another*, *another one*, *yet another*, *a whole nother* (informal tmesis, common in American speech; write *a whole other*)

1. **One more of the same kind.** *Another cup of coffee*, *another delay*.
2. **A different one.** *We'll have to find another route.*

1. Give me *another* minute and I'll have it.
2. *Another* three days of rain and the basement floods.
3. That's *yet another* reason to leave early.
4. She's *another of* his former students.
5. If this route is closed, we'll take *another* one.
6. *Another* two hundred dollars won't fix the transmission.
7. Isn't there *another* way into the building?
8. He finished his coffee and asked for *another*.

**See also:** [other](#other), [second](#second), [next](#next), [further, additional](#further-additional), [a](01-articles.md#a)

### former, latter

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɔrmɚ/, /ˈlætɚ/ &middot; *FOR-mur, LAT-ur*
**Русский:** первый (из двух) — последний (из двух); книжн. отсылка к уже названному
**Type:** general ordinal (anaphoric — it points back to something already named) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, effectively always after *the* &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; *the former … the latter* is a writing device more than a speaking one
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the former proposal*, *the latter option*), plural countable nouns (*the latter two candidates*, *the former tenants*), and uncountable nouns when the two things being contrasted are masses (*the former evidence was hearsay; the latter testimony was not*, *the latter funding*). Both require *the* or a possessive in the anaphoric sense — ✗ *latter option* bare.
**With *of*:** *of* appears in two places, and neither is a partitive on a bare noun. After a quantity noun inside the phrase: *the latter half of the year*, *the former third of the book*. And in the fused-head partitive, where *former/latter* has dropped its noun: *the former of the two*, *the latter of them*. Never on the noun itself — ✗ *the latter of the option*.
**Pattern:** *the former* [noun] &middot; *the latter* [noun] &middot; *the former … the latter* &middot; *the latter* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *her/his former* [noun] (the "ex-" sense)
**Contrast:** **They work only for exactly two items.** With three or more, *the latter* is wrong — use *the last* or name the thing. *Former* has a second life meaning "ex-," and there it does not need a pair: *her former employer*, *the former mayor*. *Latter* has no such second life. Careful readers also object to *latter* when the two items are far back in the text; if the reader has to hunt, repeat the noun. Both words are **determiner and pronoun at once**, and in practice the pronoun use is the commoner one: as determiners they precede a noun (*the former proposal*), as fused-head pronouns they stand alone with *the* still attached (*the former is faster*). The verb agrees with what they stand for — *The latter **is** cheaper* for one thing, *The latter **are** cheaper* for a pair of them.
**Variants:** *the latter half*, *the former of the two*, *ex-* (for the "former" sense), *the first … the second*

Points back to the first (*former*) or the second (*latter*) of two things already mentioned.

1. Between arbitration and litigation, *the former* is faster and *the latter* is more thorough.
2. *The latter* two witnesses were never called.
3. She spent *the latter* half of her career in public defense.
4. Her *former* employer is contesting the unemployment claim.
5. Of the two routes, *the former* is scenic and *the latter* is quick.
6. The *former* mayor still keeps an office downtown.
7. *The former* testimony was hearsay; *the latter* evidence was not.

**See also:** [first](#first), [last](#last), [previous, prior, preceding](#previous-prior-preceding), [subsequent, following](#subsequent-following)

### further, additional

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɚðɚ/, /əˈdɪʃənəl/ &middot; *FUR-thur, uh-DISH-uh-nuhl*
**Русский:** дополнительный, ещё — further нейтр., additional офиц. и делов.
**Type:** general ordinal of addition &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer; both appear bare, with no central determiner, before plural and uncountable nouns &middot; **Register:** *further* is neutral to formal; *additional* is formal and common in business and legal writing
**Goes with:** *further* takes **uncountable** nouns (*further information*, *further delay*, *further notice*) and **plural countable** nouns (*further questions*, *further cuts*); it does not take a bare singular countable noun (✗ *further reason* → *a further reason*). *Additional* takes all three — *an additional charge*, *additional seats*, *additional funding*.
**With *of*:** Neither takes *of*. ✗ *further of the information*.
**Pattern:** *further* [uncountable/plural noun] &middot; *a further* [singular noun] &middot; *no further* [noun] &middot; *additional* [noun] &middot; *an additional* [singular noun] &middot; *until further notice*
**Contrast:** ***Further* vs. *farther*** is the American split worth keeping: *farther* for physical distance (*three miles farther down the road*), *further* for quantity, degree, and everything abstract — and only *further* works as a determiner. *Further* against *more*: *more* is the plain comparative of quantity, *further* adds the idea of continuing something already begun, which is why *further discussion* implies discussion has already happened and *more discussion* does not.
**Variants:** *no further*, *until further notice*, *further to* (formal correspondence), *extra* (informal), *supplementary* (formal)

Adds a quantity to one already in play.

1. We'll release *further* details on Monday.
2. The office is closed until *further* notice.
3. Are there any *further* questions before we vote?
4. There's *an additional* charge for checked bags.
5. They requested *additional* funding in the spring.
6. Without *further* delay, here's the summary.

**See also:** [another](#another), [other](#other), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more), [remaining](#remaining)

### other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðɚ/ &middot; *UTH-ur*
**Русский:** другой, остальной, прочий — не этот, а иной из того же множества
**Type:** general ordinal / indefinite determiner &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows a central determiner, or stands bare before a plural or uncountable noun &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **plural countable** nouns (*other books*, *the other two witnesses*), **uncountable** nouns (*other evidence*, *other information*), and **singular countable** nouns **only when a determiner precedes it** (*the other book*, *my other car*, *some other reason*). It cannot precede a bare singular countable noun: ✗ *other book* → *another book*.
**With *of*:** Marginal as a determiner. The partitive is built with the pronoun *others* or with a number: *the other of the two*, *others of his generation* (formal). Ordinary usage prefers *the others*.
**Pattern:** *the/my/some/any other* [noun] &middot; *other* [plural noun] &middot; *other* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the other* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *every other* [singular noun] &middot; *the other day*
**Contrast:** ***Other* vs. *another*** — see [another](#another): bare + singular countable requires *another*, and *another* never takes a determiner in front of it. ***Other* vs. *others*** — *other* is the determiner and precedes a noun (*the other students*), *others* is the pronoun and stands alone (*the others left*). Never combine them: ✗ *the others students*. Note also *every other*, which means alternate ones (*every other week* = biweekly), not "all the remaining ones."
**Variants:** *others* (pronoun), *the other day*, *every other*, *each other*, *other than* (preposition), *otherwise*

1. **Different from the one named.** *Some other route*, *other evidence*.
2. **The remaining one or ones of a known set.** With *the*: *the other shoe*, *the other three.*
3. **Alternate.** In *every other* + singular noun.

1. *Other* airlines charge for the second bag too.
2. *The other* two witnesses never testified.
3. Is there *any other* evidence you'd like to submit?
4. I ran into him *the other* day at the hardware store.
5. She works from home *every other* Friday.
6. We'll need *some other* explanation for the missing hours.

**See also:** [another](#another), [same](#same), [remaining](#remaining), [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of), [every other](06-distributives-negatives.md#every-other)

### previous, prior, preceding

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpriviəs/, /ˈpraɪɚ/, /prɪˈsidɪŋ/ &middot; *PREE-vee-uhs, PRY-ur, prih-SEE-ding*
**Русский:** предыдущий, предшествующий — prior юр./офиц., preceding книжн., о тексте
**Type:** general ordinal (backward-pointing) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** *previous* neutral; *prior* formal, legal, and administrative; *preceding* formal and textual
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the previous owner*, *the preceding paragraph*), plural countable nouns (*the two previous attempts*, *prior convictions*), and uncountable nouns (*prior notice*, *prior experience*, *previous employment*). *Previous* and *preceding* normally need *the* or a possessive; *prior* is the one that appears comfortably bare, especially in legal and business phrasing (*without prior notice*, *prior authorization*).
**With *of*:** None of the three is a partitive determiner. *Prior to* is a separate item — a formal preposition meaning *before* (*prior to the hearing*), and plain *before* is almost always better.
**Pattern:** *the previous* [noun] &middot; *the* [cardinal] *previous* [plural noun] &middot; *prior* [uncountable noun] &middot; *without prior* [noun] &middot; *the preceding* [noun] &middot; *prior to* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Previous* is the everyday word; *preceding* means specifically the one **immediately** before, which is why it belongs to documents (*the preceding paragraph*, *the preceding fiscal year*); *prior* means "already existing beforehand" and is at home in law and paperwork (*a prior conviction*, *no prior experience*). Against *former*: *former* contrasts two named things, *previous* just points backward in time. Against *last*: *last week* is a fixed calendar unit, *the previous week* is measured from some other point in the narrative.
**Variants:** *prior to*, *the preceding*, *the aforementioned* (formal), *earlier*, *foregoing* (legal)

Points to the item that came before the one under discussion.

1. *The previous* owner repainted everything beige.
2. She has two *prior* convictions for the same offense.
3. No changes may be made without *prior* written consent.
4. See *the preceding* paragraph for the exact wording.
5. Both *previous* attempts failed for the same reason.
6. *Prior* experience with the software is helpful but not required.

**See also:** [subsequent, following](#subsequent-following), [last](#last), [former, latter](#former-latter), [next](#next)

### remaining

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈmeɪnɪŋ/ &middot; *rih-MAY-ning*
**Русский:** оставшийся — тот, что ещё не израсходован или не назван
**Type:** general ordinal (participial adjective in the postdeterminer slot) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer, normally after *the* or a possessive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*the remaining two seats*, *the remaining applicants*) and uncountable nouns (*the remaining money*, *the remaining time*). A singular countable noun works only when the noun is genuinely unique (*the remaining copy*). It normally requires a central determiner, though notices and headlines drop it: *Remaining seats are limited*.
**With *of*:** Not a partitive determiner itself. The partitive is *the rest of* or *the remainder of*.
**Pattern:** *the remaining* [plural/uncountable noun] &middot; *the remaining* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *my/our remaining* [noun] &middot; *remaining* [plural noun] (bare, in notices)
**Contrast:** *Remaining* precedes the noun and modifies it; *the rest of* and *the remainder of* are partitive phrases that take *of* and a determined noun after them. They mean the same thing and differ only in shape: *the remaining seats* = *the rest of the seats*. Against *other*: *other* means different, *remaining* means left over.
**Variants:** *the remainder*, *the rest*, *left over / leftover*, *outstanding* (of balances and tasks)

Marks what is left of a set after part of it is gone.

1. *The remaining* two seats are in the back row.
2. We'll split *the remaining* money three ways.
3. All *remaining* applicants will hear back by Friday.
4. She used *her remaining* vacation days in December.
5. *The remaining* balance is due on the first.
6. Only three of *the remaining* twelve sites are still active.

**See also:** [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of), [other](#other), [further, additional](#further-additional), [last](#last)

### same

**Pronunciation:** /seɪm/ &middot; *SAYM*
**Русский:** тот же самый — требует перед собой the, this или that
**Type:** general ordinal (identity determiner) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it requires a central determiner, nearly always *the*, *this*, or *that* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** all three noun types — singular countable (*the same problem*), plural countable (*the same two people*), and uncountable (*the same information*, *the same advice*). What it cannot do is stand bare before a noun in ordinary prose: ✗ *same problem came up again* → *the same problem came up again*. It stands bare only in fixed and commercial compounds (*same-day shipping*, *same-sex marriage*) and in elliptical replies (*Same here*, *Same to you*).
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*. The comparison is made with *as* (*the same as before*) or with a relative clause (*the same one that broke*) — ✗ *the same of*.
**Pattern:** *the same* [noun] &middot; *this/that same* [noun] &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *the same* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *the very same* [noun] &middot; *same-day / same-sex* (compound modifiers)
**Contrast:** *The same* points at identity — one thing, met twice. *Similar* points at resemblance — two things that are alike. American English uses *the same as* for the comparison (*the same as last year*); *the same like* is not English. Note that *the same* also works as a pronoun (*I'll have the same*), and that *this same* and *that same* are legal because *same* is a postdeterminer, so a demonstrative can occupy the central slot in front of it.
**Variants:** *the very same*, *this/that same*, *one and the same*, *the selfsame* (literary), *same old* (informal)

Marks the noun as identical to one already known, rather than merely similar.

1. *The same* problem came up in the last audit.
2. We ordered from *that same* restaurant twice in one week.
3. Her answer was *the same as* it was in March.
4. *The same* two people always volunteer.
5. They offer *same-day* shipping on anything ordered before noon.
6. It's *the very same* car he drove in high school.
7. Every lawyer we called gave us *the same* advice.

**See also:** [other](#other), [another](#another), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this), [former, latter](#former-latter)

### subsequent, following

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbsəkwənt/, /ˈfɑloʊɪŋ/ &middot; *SUB-suh-kwuhnt, FAH-loh-ing*
**Русский:** последующий, следующий — subsequent офиц., юр. и науч.
**Type:** general ordinal (forward-pointing) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** *subsequent* formal, legal, and academic; *following* neutral to formal
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the following day*, *the subsequent hearing*), plural countable nouns (*the subsequent two years*, *the following pages*), and uncountable nouns (*subsequent correspondence*, *subsequent damage*). Both normally take *the*, though *subsequent* appears bare in formal writing (*subsequent revisions were minor*).
**With *of*:** Neither takes *of*. *Subsequent to* is a formal preposition meaning *after* (*subsequent to the merger*) and plain *after* is nearly always better.
**Pattern:** *the following* [noun] &middot; *the subsequent* [noun] &middot; *the subsequent* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *subsequent to* [noun phrase] &middot; *the following:* [list]
**Contrast:** *Following* is measured from a point already established in the text or the narrative (*the meeting was Monday; the following day she resigned*), while *next* is measured from **now**. That is the whole difference, and it is why a report says *the following year* and a conversation says *next year*. *The following* also has a job no other word here has — introducing a list, with a colon: *Please bring the following: a photo ID, proof of address, and the signed form.*
**Variants:** *subsequent to*, *the following*, *ensuing*, *succeeding*, *thereafter* (adverb)

Points to the item that comes after the one under discussion.

1. *The following* morning she filed the complaint.
2. *Subsequent* revisions were minor and mostly cosmetic.
3. Attendance dropped in each of *the subsequent* three years.
4. Please bring *the following:* a photo ID, proof of address, and the signed form.
5. *The following* pages list every exception.
6. In *the ensuing* confusion, two boxes went missing.

**See also:** [next](#next), [previous, prior, preceding](#previous-prior-preceding), [further, additional](#further-additional), [last](#last)

### t'other

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtʌðɚ/ &middot; *TUTH-ur* &middot; one word, two syllables, no pause at the apostrophe — the *t-* is simply the first sound of the word. The commonest weakening in running speech is the whole phrase, not the word: *t'other day* /ˈtʌðɚ ˈdeɪ/
**Русский:** тот, другой — диал./устар. стяжение the other; аппалачское и южное просторечие
**Type:** general ordinal (dialect form of *the other*) &middot; **Position:** **central determiner** — it has the definite article baked into it, so nothing else central can join it: ✗ *the t'other side* &middot; **Register:** **regional and archaic.** In American English it is Appalachian and Southern mountain speech first, older rural speech elsewhere second, and in print it belongs to dialect writing — Twain, Joel Chandler Harris, local-color fiction — and to the fixed pair *one … t'other*. It is not standard written English, and a reader who knows it hears it as country, not as an error.
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*t'other side*, *t'other day*) and plural countable nouns (*t'other ones*, *t'other two*) — the same range as *the other*. Not uncountable nouns in ordinary dialect use.
**With *of*:** Not a partitive determiner. In the fused-head pronoun use it simply stands alone, normally paired against *one*: *one or t'other*, *each blamed t'other*.
**Pattern:** *t'other* [singular noun] &middot; *t'other* [plural noun] &middot; *one … t'other* (paired) &middot; *one or t'other* &middot; *t'other day / t'other side / t'other night*
**Contrast:** **It is *the other*, article and all**, which is why a second determiner cannot stand in front of it and why standardizing it means dropping the *t*: *t'other day* → *the other day*. The apostrophe is a misreading that history left behind — the *t-* is a fossil of the old demonstrative *that*, not a clipped *the*, and the older spelling is plain *tother*. The paired frame *one … t'other* is the dialect equivalent of *the one … the other* and of the bookish [former, latter](#former-latter). For the standard word, see [other](#other).
**Variants:** *tother* (older spelling), *tuther* (eye dialect), *one … t'other*, *t'other one*, *the other* (standard)

The dialect form of *the other* — of two, the one not just named.

1. "You take one end and I'll take *t'other*."
2. He come by *t'other* day asking after the mule.
3. One of 'em was lying and *t'other* one knew it.
4. The cabin sits on *t'other* side of the creek, past the fence line.
5. It don't matter which — one or *t'other* will do.
6. In standard English write *the other*: *t'other day* → *the other day*.

**See also:** [other](#other), [another](#another), [former, latter](#former-latter), [th'](01-articles.md#th), [the](01-articles.md#the)

## Fractions and part-words

These name a share of something rather than a count of it. *Half* and *all* are the odd ones out: both are true predeterminers that can skip *of* entirely (*half the cake*, *all the cake*). Every other fraction and part-word needs *of* before a determined noun phrase, and all of them take **notional agreement** — the verb follows the noun after *of*, not the fraction: *Half the cake **is** gone*, *Half the guests **are** gone*.

### a chunk of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈtʃʌŋk əv/ &middot; *uh CHUNK uv* &middot; *a* is weak /ə/ and *of* reduces to /əv/ or simply /ə/ in fast speech — *a chunk of the money* is usually /ə ˈtʃʌŋk ə ðə ˈmʌni/
**Русский:** изрядная часть, кусок — разг.: заметная доля чего-либо
**Type:** part-word (partitive quantifier, informal) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner of *chunk*; the whole phrase heads a partitive &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — conversational American, ordinary in business talk and journalism, out of place in a contract or a specification
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*a chunk of the budget*, *a chunk of time*) and singular countable nouns naming a divisible whole (*a chunk of the retaining wall*, *a chunk of the afternoon*). With plural countable nouns it is marginal — *a chunk of the applicants* is heard, but *a lot of the applicants* is better — because *chunk* divides a mass and does not select from a group.
**With *of*:** **Required.** ✗ *a chunk the budget*. The noun after *of* may be bare when it is a mass (*a chunk of money*, *a chunk of change*) or determined for the true partitive (*a chunk of **the** budget*).
**Pattern:** *a chunk of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *a good/big/fair/sizable chunk of* [noun phrase] &middot; *a chunk of* [pronoun] &middot; *a chunk of change* (fixed, = a lot of money)
**Contrast:** Against [a fraction of](#a-fraction-of), which always means small, *a chunk of* always means **big** — and *a good chunk of* means bigger still, close to *most of*. Against [part of](#part-of), which is neutral about size and at home in formal prose, this one is informal and carries the picture of something bitten out of a whole. Agreement is notional, following the noun after *of*: *A chunk of the money **is** missing*, *A chunk of the shipments **were** late*. The fixed *a chunk of change* means a large sum and is never used of a small one.
**Variants:** *a good chunk of*, *a big chunk of*, *a fair chunk of*, *a sizable chunk of*, *a chunk of change*, *a hunk of* (of solid matter)

Names a large, roughly measured piece of a whole, in conversational register.

1. *A chunk of* the budget disappears into insurance every year.
2. *A good chunk of* the audience left at intermission.
3. Rewriting the intake form ate *a chunk of* my afternoon.
4. *A chunk of* the retaining wall came down in the storm.
5. They still owe us *a good chunk of* it.
6. Replacing the transmission is *a chunk of change*.

### a fraction of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈfrækʃən əv/ &middot; *uh FRAK-shuhn uv*
**Русский:** малая доля, ничтожная часть — подчёркивает, как мало это от целого
**Type:** fraction (partitive quantifier) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner of *fraction*; the whole phrase heads a partitive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*a fraction of the cost*), plural countable nouns (*a fraction of the applicants*), and singular countable nouns naming a divisible whole (*a fraction of the building*). Not a bare noun: ✗ *a fraction of applicants* is odd — the noun after *of* is normally determined.
**With *of*:** **Required.** *A fraction* cannot precede a noun directly: ✗ *a fraction the cost*.
**Pattern:** *a fraction of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *a fraction of* [pronoun] &middot; *a small/tiny fraction of* [noun phrase] &middot; *at a fraction of* [the + noun]
**Contrast:** Unlike *a third* or *two-thirds*, *a fraction of* names no specific share — it means "a very small part," and it is always understood as small. So *a fraction of the cost* is a bargain, while *a portion of the cost* is neutral about size. Verb agreement is notional: *A fraction of the budget was spent*, *A fraction of the residents were notified*.
**Variants:** *a small fraction of*, *a tiny fraction of*, *a portion of*, *a percentage of*

Names a small, unspecified share of a whole.

1. You can get the same coverage at *a fraction of* the price.
2. Only *a fraction of* the applicants made it past the first round.
3. *A fraction of* the money ever reached the schools.
4. The repair took *a fraction of* the time we budgeted.
5. *A small fraction of* users ever change the default settings.
6. She read *a fraction of* it and put it down.

### a proportion of

**Pronunciation:** /ə prəˈpɔrʃən əv/ &middot; *uh pruh-POR-shuhn uv* &middot; *a* /ə/, the first syllable of *proportion* /prə/, and *of* /əv/ are all weak; the phrase carries one beat, on *-por-*
**Русский:** доля, некоторая часть — книжн., науч.: часть от общего числа
**Type:** fraction (proportional partitive) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner of *proportion*; the whole phrase heads a partitive &middot; **Register:** formal — statistics, research writing, public health, and policy. In ordinary prose a percentage or *some of* is plainer.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*a proportion of the respondents*) and uncountable nouns (*a proportion of the funding*). A singular countable noun only when it names a divisible whole (*a proportion of the estate*). The noun after *of* is normally determined.
**With *of*:** **Required.** ✗ *a proportion the applicants*. Without *of*, *proportion* is an ordinary noun about ratio and shape (*out of proportion*, *the proportions of the room*) and not a determiner at all.
**Pattern:** *a proportion of* [the/my/these + noun] &middot; *a large/small/significant proportion of* [noun phrase] &middot; *a proportion of* [pronoun] &middot; *the proportion of* [noun] *who/that* [verb] &middot; *what proportion of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **Bare *a proportion of* says almost nothing** — every share is a proportion, so the phrase does no work until an adjective sizes it: *a large proportion of*, *a small proportion of*. That is what separates it from [a fraction of](#a-fraction-of), which is understood as small on its own, and from [the majority of](#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), which is understood as more than half. In plain writing a number says it better: *a proportion of respondents* → *thirty percent of respondents*. Note the separate *the proportion of*, which names the ratio itself rather than a share of a group: *The proportion of first-generation students **has** doubled*. Agreement is otherwise notional: *A proportion of the funding **was** withheld*, *A proportion of the respondents **were** undecided*.
**Variants:** *a large proportion of*, *a small proportion of*, *a significant proportion of*, *the proportion of*, *a percentage of*, *a share of*

Names a share of a group or an amount, sized only by whatever adjective is put in front of it.

1. *A large proportion of* the respondents skipped the last page.
2. *A small proportion of* the funding is restricted to capital projects.
3. *A proportion of* every gift goes straight to the endowment.
4. *What proportion of* the caseload is housing?
5. *The proportion of* first-generation students has risen every year since 2018.
6. Only *a proportion of* them will ever renew.

### a quarter, a third, two-thirds

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈkwɔrtɚ/, /ə ˈθɚd/, /ˌtu ˈθɚdz/ &middot; *uh KWOR-tur, uh THURD, too THURDZ* &middot; the *a* is weak, /ə/, and so is the *of* that follows: *a third of the class* /ə ˈθɚd əv ðə ˈklæs/
**Русский:** четверть, треть, две трети — перед определённой группой требуют of: a third of the cake
**Type:** fraction &middot; **Position:** predeterminer **in the fraction series, but not a working predeterminer the way *half* is.** Unlike *half*, these cannot sit bare in front of a central determiner: ✓ *half the cake*, ✗ *a third the cake*. Before a determined noun phrase they take *of* and head a partitive instead (*a third **of** the cake*), and the bare predeterminer use survives only in measure comparisons — *a third the size*, *two-thirds the price*. &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*two-thirds of the water*), plural countable nouns (*a third of the students*), and singular countable nouns naming a divisible whole (*a quarter of the pie*, *a quarter of an hour*).
**With *of*:** **Required before a determined noun phrase:** *two-thirds of the students*, ✗ *two-thirds the students*. *Of* is **optional** only before a measure noun in a comparison: *a third the size*, *two-thirds the price*, *a quarter the weight*. Before a pronoun it is required: *a third of them*.
**Pattern:** *a/one third of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; [cardinal]*-*[ordinal plural] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *a quarter of* [pronoun] &middot; *a third* [the + measure noun] (comparison) &middot; *three-quarters of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *Half* is the exception in this family — it drops *of* freely (*half the cake*) where the others cannot (✗ *a third the cake*). Hyphenation: hyphenate a fraction used as a modifier and, by American convention, as a noun too — *two-thirds*, *three-quarters*, *seven-eighths*. Agreement is notional: *Two-thirds of the budget **is** gone*, *Two-thirds of the seats **are** taken*.
**Variants:** *a fourth* (American alternative to *a quarter*), *three-quarters*, *five-eighths*, *one-third* (formal/technical), *a quarter of an hour*

Names an exact share of a whole.

1. *A third of* the class failed the midterm.
2. *Two-thirds of* the water in the reservoir evaporated.
3. She finished in *a quarter of* the time it took me.
4. *Three-quarters of* the respondents said they'd switch.
5. The new unit is *a third* the size of the old one.
6. Wait *a quarter of* an hour and try the door again.

### all of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔl əv/ &middot; *AWL uv* &middot; *of* is weak, /əv/, and drops to /ə/ before a consonant — *all of the money* is usually /ˈɔl ə ðə ˈmʌni/, while before a pronoun the /v/ links forward: *all of it* /ˈɔl əv ɪt/. *All* itself keeps its full vowel.
**Русский:** все, всё из — весь объём уже определённой группы: all of the students
**Type:** part-word (totality partitive) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer *all* plus *of*; the phrase heads a partitive over an already-determined noun phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*all of the students*), uncountable nouns (*all of the money*, *all of the time*), singular countable nouns naming a divisible whole or a stretch (*all of the cake*, *all of the afternoon*), and — the use that makes the *of* obligatory — pronouns: *all of us*, *all of them*, *all of it*.
**With *of*:** **Optional before a determiner:** *all (of) the students*, *all (of) my money*, with the *of*-less form the more common American choice. **Required before a pronoun:** *all of them*, ✗ *all them* (heard in casual and regional speech, not standard). **Impossible before a bare noun:** ✗ *all of students* → *all students*.
**Pattern:** *all of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *all of* [pronoun] &middot; *all* [the/my + noun] (no *of*) &middot; *all of* [proper noun] &middot; *not all of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** This is the **totality** end of the same series as *half of* and *two-thirds of*, and it behaves like [half](#half) in the one way that matters: both may drop *of* in front of a determiner where the other fractions may not — ✓ *all the cake*, ✓ *half the cake*, ✗ *a third the cake*. Against bare *all* plus a bare noun, which is generic: *all students need an ID* is about students in general, *all of the students* is about a specific known set. Agreement is notional, following the noun after *of*: *All of the cake **is** gone*, *All of the cookies **are** gone*.
**Variants:** *all the*, *all my*, *not all of*, *the whole of*, *every one of*, *all them* (nonstandard)

Takes in every part or every member of a known whole — the totality member of the fractions-and-part-words family. For the word's full entry as a predeterminer, including floating *all*, see [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all).

1. *All of* the milk had gone sour by Thursday.
2. *All of* us were in the parking lot when the alarm went off.
3. She read *all of* it twice before signing anything.
4. *All of* the applicants who made the second round were local.
5. Not *all of* the damage is covered.
6. *All of* Ohio was under a wind advisory by noon.

### half

**Pronunciation:** /hæf/ &middot; *HAF* &middot; the *l* is silent; everything around it reduces — *half the cake* /ˌhæf ðə ˈkeɪk/, *half a dozen* /ˌhæf ə ˈdʌzən/, *half an hour* /ˌhæf ən ˈaʊɚ/, *half of it* /ˌhæf əv ɪt/
**Русский:** половина, пол- — предопределитель: стоит перед the/my/that (half the cake)
**Type:** fraction &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** — it stands ahead of the central determiner (*half the cake*, *half my paycheck*, *half that amount*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** all three noun types — singular countable (*half the cake*, *half an hour*), plural countable (*half the students*), and uncountable (*half the water*, *half my time*). It also precedes bare nouns in fixed price and time phrases: *half price*, *half fare*, *half time*.
**With *of*:** **Optional** before a determiner: *half (of) the cake*, *half (of) my paycheck* — both are standard, and the *of*-less form is more common in American speech. **Required** before a pronoun: *half of it*, *half of them*, ✗ *half them*.
**Pattern:** *half* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *half of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *half of* [pronoun] &middot; *half a/an* [singular noun] &middot; *a half* [singular noun] &middot; *half* [bare price/time noun]
**Contrast:** *Half* is the only fraction that can skip *of* before a determiner — ✗ *a third the cake* but ✓ *half the cake*. On *a half hour* vs. *half an hour*: both are standard American English; *a half an hour* is common in speech but redundant in writing. Agreement is notional, following the noun after *half*: *Half the cake **is** gone*, *Half the guests **are** gone*. As a pronoun it stands alone (*I'll take half*); as a determiner it precedes a noun phrase.
**Variants:** *a half*, *one half*, *half a*, *half an*, *half again*, *better half*, *half past*, *and a half* (*a year and a half*)

Divides a quantity into two equal parts and names one of them.

1. *Half* the class failed the midterm.

3. Give me *half an* hour and I'll have the numbers.
4. *Half of* it was still frozen in the middle.
5. Kids under six ride at *half* price.
6. *Half* the time he doesn't even read the email.

### one in three, nine out of ten

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌn ɪn ˈθri/, /ˈnaɪn aʊt əv ˈtɛn/ &middot; *WUN in THREE, NYN owt uv TEN* &middot; the numbers carry the beats and the joining words reduce — *in* is /ɪn/ or /ən/, and *out of* runs together as /ˈaʊɾə/, so *nine out of ten dentists* is /ˈnaɪn ˈaʊɾə ˈtɛn ˈdɛntɪsts/
**Русский:** каждый третий; девять из десяти — способ выразить долю или частоту
**Type:** fraction (proportional frame) &middot; **Position:** the whole frame fills the determiner slot of the noun, or heads a partitive when *of* follows &middot; **Register:** neutral — the ordinary American way to state a rate in speech, in journalism, and in public-health writing; *nine out of ten* is also a fixed advertising rhythm
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** — *one in three households*, *nine out of ten dentists*. Never an uncountable noun (✗ *one in three funding*) and never a singular countable noun (✗ *one in three household*). The noun drops out entirely when it is understood: *One in three said no*.
**With *of*:** The *out of* frame carries its own fixed *of*, which is **not** a partitive: *nine out of ten*, ✗ *nine out ten*. A real partitive needs another *of* and a determined noun — *one in three of the households*, *nine out of ten of them* — and before a pronoun it is required: *one in three of us*.
**Pattern:** *one in* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *out of* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *one in* [cardinal] *of* [the/my + plural noun] &middot; *one in every* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *in* [cardinal] (standing alone)
**Contrast:** **Agreement is the trap, and American usage is split.** The frame is singular in form and plural in sense, so *One in three households **is** uninsured* is the conservative choice, with the verb following *one*, while *One in three households **are** uninsured* is very common and increasingly accepted. Choose one and hold it through a document; the plural reads better when a plural pronoun follows (*One in three renters say **they**…*). The two frames mean the same thing (*one in three* = *a third*), and the difference from [a quarter, a third, two-thirds](#a-quarter-a-third-two-thirds) is rhetorical: a fraction divides a mass, a ratio counts people one at a time, which is why health reporting prefers *one in three*. Keep the larger number second — ✗ *ten out of nine* — and do not blend the frames: ✗ *nine in out of ten*.
**Variants:** *one out of five*, *one out of three*, *one in ten*, *two in five*, *one in every three*, *a third* (the fraction equivalent)

3. *One out of five* prescriptions is never picked up.
4. *One in every* eight flights out of the airport was canceled that week.
5. Roughly *one in three of* them had been through the program before.

**Русский:** процент — после числа без -s: ten percent

### the aggregate of

**Pronunciation:** /ði ˈæɡrəɡət əv/ &middot; *thee AG-ruh-gut uv* &middot; **the article switches form**: *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *aggregate*, never ✗ /ðə/. The noun's last syllable is a schwa, /-ɡət/, where the verb *aggregate* ends /-ɡeɪt/; *of* is weak, /əv/.
**Русский:** совокупность, общая сумма — юр., фин. и страховой термин
**Type:** part-word (summative partitive) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase; *the* is fixed in front of it &middot; **Register:** **legal, financial, and technical** — insurance policies, contracts, and accounting, where *the aggregate* is a defined term. In ordinary prose *the total of* or plain *all* says it.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*the aggregate of the payments*, *the aggregate of all claims*) and uncountable nouns naming an amount (*the aggregate of the indebtedness*). Not a singular countable noun unless it names a divisible whole.
**With *of*:** **Required**, and *the* is required in front: ✗ *aggregate of the payments*, ✗ *the aggregate the payments*. Without *of*, *in the aggregate* is a fixed adverbial meaning "taken all together," and *aggregate* standing before a noun is an adjective (*the aggregate limit*, *aggregate demand*), not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *the aggregate of* [the/all + plural noun] &middot; *the aggregate of* [the + uncountable amount noun] &middot; *an aggregate of* [number] &middot; *in the aggregate* &middot; *aggregate* [noun] (adjective)
**Contrast:** Against [the totality of](#the-totality-of), which insists nothing was left out, *the aggregate of* insists the parts were **added up** — it is a sum, not a whole. Against [a total of](#a-total-of), which introduces a figure in a news report, this one introduces a noun phrase in a contract: *a total of forty-one claims* against *the aggregate of all claims*. The technical sense is worth knowing because it costs money — an insurance policy's *aggregate limit* caps everything paid in a policy period, where the per-occurrence limit caps a single claim. Agreement follows *aggregate* and stays singular: *The aggregate of the payments **exceeds** the cap*.
**Variants:** *in the aggregate*, *an aggregate of*, *aggregate limit* (insurance), *the sum of*, *the total of*, *the whole of*

Names the sum of a set of separate amounts, taken together as one figure.

1. *The aggregate of* the monthly payments exceeds the purchase price by a third.
2. Coverage is capped at *the aggregate of* all claims filed in the policy year.
3. *The aggregate of* the fines came to just under nine thousand dollars.
4. Taken *in the aggregate*, the returns look better than any single quarter does.
5. *The aggregate of* the outstanding indebtedness is disclosed in Note 7.
6. In plain English, *the aggregate of* the payments is the payments added up.

**See also:** [the totality of](#the-totality-of), [a total of](#a-total-of), [the bulk of](#the-bulk-of), [the whole (of)](#the-whole-of), [all of](#all-of)

### the better part of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈbɛtɚ ˈpɑrt əv/ &middot; *thuh BET-ur PART uv* &middot; *the* is /ðə/ before the consonant and *of* is weak /əv/; the *tt* of *better* is a flap, /ˈbɛɾɚ/, so the phrase runs together as /ðə ˈbɛɾɚ ˈpɑrɾ əv/
**Русский:** бо́льшая часть, добрых — почти весь: добрых два часа, и всё впустую
**Type:** part-word (majority partitive, idiomatic) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase; *the* is fixed in front of it &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — conversational and journalistic, and it carries a faint complaint: the time went and there is nothing to show for it
**Goes with:** **uncountable nouns and singular countable nouns naming a span**, above all spans of time — *the better part of an hour*, *the better part of a decade*, *the better part of the afternoon*, *the better part of a tank of gas*. With plural countable nouns it is unidiomatic: ✗ *the better part of the applicants* → *most of the applicants*.
**With *of*:** **Required**, and *the* is required in front: ✗ *better part of an hour*, ✗ *the better part an hour*.
**Pattern:** *the better part of* [a/an + time noun] &middot; *the better part of* [the + uncountable noun] &middot; *the better part of* [a + measure noun] &middot; *the best part of* [noun phrase] (British)
**Contrast:** It means **most of, nearly all of** — not "the superior half." *The better part of an hour* is fifty minutes, not thirty. Against [the bulk of](#the-bulk-of) and *most of*, which are neutral and handle counts of people and things, this one is idiomatic, wants a span rather than a set, and implies the span was wearing: *We spent the better part of a week on hold*. The British *the best part of* means exactly the same and reads as an import. Do not confuse it with the unrelated *discretion is the better part of valor*, where *better part* means the wiser portion. Agreement is normally singular, since the noun after *of* is normally a mass or a stretch: *The better part of the afternoon **was** gone*.
**Variants:** *the best part of* (British), *most of*, *the bulk of*, *the greater part of*, *nearly all of*

Names most of a span or an amount — usually a stretch of time, usually with a note of impatience.

1. We spent *the better part of* an hour on hold with the insurance company.
2. She's been at that firm *the better part of* a decade.
3. Rewiring the panel took *the better part of* two days.
4. *The better part of* the shipment was still sitting in Newark on Friday.
5. He drank *the better part of* a pot of coffee before nine.
6. It rained *the better part of* the week we were there.

**See also:** [the bulk of](#the-bulk-of), [a chunk of](#a-chunk-of), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most), [the majority of, the minority of](#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), [half](#half)

### the bulk of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈbʌlk əv/ &middot; *thuh BULK uv*
**Русский:** основная масса, львиная доля — подавляющая часть
**Type:** part-word (majority partitive) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase; *the* is fixed &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in journalism and business writing
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns (*the bulk of the work*, *the bulk of the money*) and plural countable nouns (*the bulk of the applicants*). Not a singular countable noun unless it names a divisible mass (*the bulk of the shipment*).
**With *of*:** **Required**, and *the* is required in front: ✗ *bulk of the work*, ✗ *the bulk the work*.
**Pattern:** *the bulk of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *the bulk of* [pronoun] &middot; *the bulk of* [possessive + noun]
**Contrast:** *The bulk of* and *the majority of* both mean "most," but *the bulk of* prefers mass and volume (*the bulk of the funding*) while *the majority of* prefers countable sets and is the only one at home with people and votes (*the majority of voters*). Plain *most of* covers both and is shorter — *the bulk of* earns its keep mainly when the writer wants weight and heft in the sentence. Agreement is notional: *The bulk of the work **is** done*, *The bulk of the applicants **were** rejected*.
**Variants:** *most of*, *the greater part of*, *the lion's share of*, *the preponderance of* (formal)

Names the largest share of something, viewed as mass rather than as a count.

1. *The bulk of* the work happens in the first two weeks.
2. *The bulk of* the settlement went to legal fees.
3. She wrote *the bulk of* it on a plane.
4. *The bulk of* the shipment is still sitting in Newark.
5. *The bulk of* the applicants never finished the second form.
6. *The bulk of* our revenue comes from three clients.

**See also:** [the majority of, the minority of](#the-majority-of-the-minority-of), [part of](#part-of), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most), [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of)

### the majority of, the minority of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə məˈdʒɔrəti əv/, /ðə məˈnɔrəti əv/ &middot; *thuh muh-JOR-uh-tee uv, thuh muh-NOR-uh-tee uv*
**Русский:** большинство — меньшинство (из чего-либо)
**Type:** part-word (proportional partitive) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Goes with:** **plural countable** nouns first and foremost — *the majority of voters*, *the majority of the applicants*. With uncountable nouns it is awkward at best (✗ *the majority of the water* → *most of the water*), which is the trap: reach for *most of* whenever the noun is a mass.
**With *of*:** **Required** before a noun phrase or pronoun: *the majority of them*. *Majority* can also stand alone as a noun with no *of* (*The majority ruled*), and in that use it is not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *the majority of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *a majority of* [plural noun] &middot; *the vast majority of* [plural noun] &middot; *the majority of* [pronoun] &middot; *a small minority of* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** ***A* majority vs. *the* majority.** *A majority* is a counting fact — more than half, the winning side of a vote. *The majority* is the greater part of a known group, closer to *most*. Agreement follows the noun after *of*: *The majority of voters **were** undecided* (plural), but *The majority **was** decisive* when *majority* stands alone as a single body. Prefer *most of* with mass nouns and in plain prose.
**Variants:** *a majority*, *the vast majority of*, *the overwhelming majority of*, *a small minority of*, *most of*

Names the larger (*majority*) or smaller (*minority*) share of a countable group.

1. *The majority of* voters were still undecided a week out.
2. *A majority of* the committee voted to postpone.
3. *The vast majority of* complaints concern one department.
4. Only *a small minority of* users ever contact support.
5. *The majority of* them had never flown before.
6. In *the minority of* cases where it fails, the fix is manual.

**See also:** [the bulk of](#the-bulk-of), [percent](#percent), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most), [a fraction of](#a-fraction-of)

### the rest of, the remainder of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈrɛst əv/, /ðə rɪˈmeɪndɚ əv/ &middot; *thuh REST uv, thuh rih-MAYN-dur uv*
**Русский:** остальное, остаток — remainder книжн., фин. и мат.
**Type:** part-word (complement partitive) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase; *the* is fixed &middot; **Register:** *the rest of* neutral; *the remainder of* formal, financial, and mathematical
**Goes with:** all three noun types — singular countable (*the rest of the cake*), plural countable (*the rest of the cookies*), and uncountable (*the rest of the money*, *the rest of the time*).
**With *of*:** **Required** before any noun phrase or pronoun: *the rest of us*, *the rest of the day*. Without *of*, *the rest* is a pronoun standing alone: *You take these; I'll handle the rest.*
**Pattern:** *the rest of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *the rest of* [pronoun] &middot; *the remainder of* [noun phrase] &middot; *the rest* (pronoun, standing alone)
**Contrast:** *The rest of* names everything not already accounted for — it is the complement of whatever share you just mentioned. *The remaining* does the same work as a premodifier with no *of*: *the remaining seats* = *the rest of the seats*. *The remainder of* is the formal twin of *the rest of*, and in arithmetic it has a technical meaning (what is left after division) that *the rest* does not. Agreement is notional: *The rest of the cake **is** gone*, *The rest of the cookies **are** gone*.
**Variants:** *the remainder*, *the rest*, *what's left of*, *the balance of* (financial), *the residue of* (legal)

Names everything left over once a part has been taken out.

1. *The rest of* the milk had gone sour.
2. He spent *the rest of* the afternoon on hold.
3. *The rest of* us waited in the parking lot.
4. *The remainder of* the balance is due within thirty days.
5. Take what you need and leave *the rest of* it.
6. *The rest of* the questions were multiple choice.

**See also:** [remaining](#remaining), [part of](#part-of), [the bulk of](#the-bulk-of), [other](#other), [half](#half)

### the totality of

**Pronunciation:** /ðə toʊˈtæləti əv/ &middot; *thuh toh-TAL-uh-tee uv* &middot; *the* is /ðə/ and *of* is weak /əv/; the *t* between vowels inside *totality* is a flap, /toʊˈtæləɾi/
**Русский:** вся совокупность, всё в целом — юр. и книжн.; ср. «по совокупности обстоятельств»
**Type:** part-word (totality) &middot; **Position:** heads a partitive phrase; *the* is fixed in front of it &middot; **Register:** **formal, legal, and academic.** In American law it is a term of art — *the totality of the circumstances* is the standard a court applies when no single factor decides the question. Outside that world it is a heavy way to say *all of*.
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*the totality of the circumstances*, *the totality of his holdings*) and uncountable nouns (*the totality of the evidence*, *the totality of human knowledge*). A singular countable noun only when it names a whole system or record (*the totality of the record*).
**With *of*:** **Required**, and *the* is required in front: ✗ *totality of the evidence*, ✗ *the totality the evidence*. The one *of*-less use is the adverbial *in its totality*.
**Pattern:** *the totality of* [the + plural noun] &middot; *the totality of* [the + uncountable noun] &middot; *the totality of the circumstances* (legal standard) &middot; *in its totality*
**Contrast:** Against [all of](#all-of), the plain everyday form, *the totality of* insists on the whole considered **as one thing** — which is the legal point of *the totality of the circumstances*: no single fact controls, the court weighs them together. Against [the aggregate of](#the-aggregate-of), which adds separate amounts into a sum, this one gathers items into a whole with no arithmetic. Against [the whole (of)](#the-whole-of), which is ordinary English and prefers a singular noun, this is formal and prefers a plural or a mass. In plain writing, *the totality of the evidence* is *all the evidence*. Agreement stays singular, following *totality*: *The totality of the circumstances **supports** the stop*.
**Variants:** *in its totality*, *the totality of the circumstances* (legal), *the entirety of*, *the whole of*, *all of*

Names everything in a set considered together as one indivisible whole.

1. The court reviews *the totality of* the circumstances, not any single factor.
2. *The totality of* the evidence points one direction.
3. Officers may consider *the totality of* what they observed at the scene.
4. *The totality of* his holdings came out only after the audit.
5. The report has to be read in *its totality* to make any sense.
6. In plain English, *the totality of* the evidence is all the evidence.

**See also:** [the whole (of)](#the-whole-of), [all of](#all-of), [the aggregate of](#the-aggregate-of), [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all)

### the whole (of)

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈhoʊl (əv)/ &middot; *thuh HOHL (uv)*
**Русский:** весь, целиком — требует определителя перед собой: the whole day, my whole life
**Type:** part-word (totality) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it requires a central determiner in front of it (*the whole*, *a whole*, *my whole*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns** (*the whole story*, *a whole hour*, *my whole life*, *the whole afternoon*) and **uncountable nouns** (*the whole truth*, *the whole point*, *my whole attention*). **Never a plural countable noun** in this sense — ✗ *the whole students* → *all the students*. It does precede a plural noun when a cardinal comes between them, because the number makes the stretch one unit: *the whole three days*. The plural *whole grains*, *whole numbers*, *whole milk* is a different word — a plain adjective meaning "undivided," not a determiner.
**With *of*:** **Optional and formal** before a determined noun phrase: *the whole of the morning*, *the whole of Europe* — the bare *the whole morning* is the American default. **Required** before a pronoun: *the whole of it*, though *all of it* is the more idiomatic American choice.
**Pattern:** *the whole* [singular/uncountable noun] &middot; *a whole* [singular noun] &middot; *my/his/their whole* [noun] &middot; *the whole* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *the whole of* [the + noun] &middot; *the whole of* [pronoun]
**Contrast:** ***Whole* and *all* split the work by noun type and by position.** *Whole* is a **postdeterminer** and takes a **singular** noun (*the whole cake*, *my whole life*); *all* is a **predeterminer** and takes plural and uncountable nouns, standing in front of the determiner (*all the cakes*, *all my life*). Where both are possible they mean different things: *the whole cake* is one cake with nothing missing, *all the cake* is however much cake there happens to be. *Whole* is also the complement of *half* and *part of* — it insists nothing was left out.
**Variants:** *a whole*, *the whole of*, *the whole thing*, *a whole nother* (informal, for *a whole other*), *entire* (formal), *wholly* (adverb)

Names something in its entirety, with no part of it missing or left out. This entry covers *whole* as the totality member of the fractions-and-part-words family — the complement of *half*, *part of*, and *the rest of*. For the word's full entry as a general postdeterminer, see [whole](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#whole).

1. Tell me *the whole* story, not the version you gave the police.
2. She waited *a whole* hour and then left without saying anything.
3. *My whole* family showed up unannounced on a Tuesday.
4. He spent *the whole* afternoon on hold with the insurance company.
5. *The whole of* Europe was watching the vote.
6. It rained *the whole* three days we were there.

**See also:** [whole](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#whole), [half](#half), [part of](#part-of), [the rest of, the remainder of](#the-rest-of-the-remainder-of), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every)

## Multipliers

Multipliers state how many times over. Grammatically they are **predeterminers**: they stand in front of a complete, already-determined noun phrase (*double **the** price*, *twice **that** amount*) and they take no *of* — ✗ *double of the price*. Five entries here are **not** multiplier determiners at all and say so in their own lines: *once* is a frequency adverb, *twofold* and its family are adjectives and adverbs of change, *a factor of* and *an order of magnitude* are fixed phrases that multiply a verb or a comparison rather than a noun, and *per* is the preposition that states a rate.

### a factor of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈfæktɚ əv/ &middot; *uh FAK-tur uv* &middot; *a* /ə/ and *of* /əv/ are both weak; the beats fall on *fac-* and on the number — *by a factor of ten* is /baɪ ə ˈfæktɚ əv ˈtɛn/, with *by* unstressed too
**Русский:** во столько-то раз — тех.: by a factor of ten — в десять раз
**Type:** multiplier phrase — **not a determiner** &middot; **Position:** never in a determiner slot; it is the object of *by* and modifies a verb or a comparison, not a noun &middot; **Register:** technical, scientific, financial, and journalistic — the register of reports rather than conversation
**Goes with:** nothing, as a determiner. It attaches to a **number** inside the fixed frame *by a factor of* + [cardinal] and reports how much something grew, shrank, or missed: *rose by a factor of ten*, *off by a factor of two*. It never precedes a noun: ✗ *a factor of ten the price*, ✗ *a factor of ten people*.
**With *of*:** The *of* is **required and fixed** — part of the phrase, not a partitive: *by a factor of three*, ✗ *by a factor three*.
**Pattern:** [verb] *by a factor of* [cardinal] &middot; *off by a factor of* [cardinal] &middot; [comparative] *by a factor of* [cardinal] &middot; *a factor of* [cardinal] *higher/lower/faster*
**Contrast:** **This entry exists to block an error.** *Double*, *triple*, and *three times* are predeterminers that stand in front of a noun phrase (*triple the price*); *a factor of* cannot — it multiplies a **verb or a comparison**, never a noun. So ✓ *The cost rose by a factor of three* and ✓ *three times the cost*, but ✗ *a factor of three the cost*. Watch the arithmetic that comes with the word: *by a factor of ten* means ten times as large, and *reduced by a factor of ten* means down **to** a tenth, not down **by** a tenth — which is why careful writing says *to one-tenth*. Compare [an order of magnitude](#an-order-of-magnitude), the same idea locked to powers of ten.
**Variants:** *by a factor of*, *an order of magnitude*, *X times*, *X-fold*, *times over*

Multiplies a quantity by a stated number, from outside the noun phrase rather than inside it.

1. Processing time dropped *by a factor of* four after the rewrite.
2. The original estimate was off *by a factor of* ten.
3. Rents in that corridor have risen *by a factor of* three since 2010.
4. Their error rate is lower *by a factor of* two, which is less impressive than it sounds.
5. Reducing the dose *by a factor of* five is not the same as reducing it by five.
6. ✗ *It costs a factor of three the original price.* → ✓ It costs *three times* the original price.

**See also:** [three times](#three-times), [an order of magnitude](#an-order-of-magnitude), [twofold, threefold, tenfold](#twofold-threefold-tenfold), [double](#double), [percent](#percent)

### an order of magnitude

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈɔrdɚ əv ˈmæɡnəˌtud/ &middot; *un OR-dur uv MAG-nuh-tood* &middot; *an* /ən/ and *of* /əv/ are weak, so the run in front of the comparative flattens to /ənˈɔrdɚəv/; the beats fall on *or-* and *mag-*
**Русский:** порядок величины — науч./тех.: разница в десять раз
**Type:** multiplier phrase (= a power of ten) — **not a determiner** &middot; **Position:** never in a determiner slot; it modifies a comparative adjective or adverb (*an order of magnitude cheaper*) or follows *by* (*grew by an order of magnitude*) &middot; **Register:** technical and scientific first, then business and journalism; in conversation *ten times* says it without the lab coat
**Goes with:** nothing, as a determiner. It attaches to a **comparative** (*an order of magnitude larger*, *orders of magnitude faster*) or to a verb of change through *by* (*fell by an order of magnitude*). It never precedes a noun: ✗ *an order of magnitude the price*, ✗ *an order of magnitude people*.
**With *of*:** Both *of*s are **fixed** parts of the phrase and neither is a partitive: *an order of magnitude*, ✗ *an order magnitude*, ✗ *an order of the magnitude*.
**Pattern:** *an order of magnitude* [comparative adjective/adverb] &middot; [verb] *by an order of magnitude* &middot; *orders of magnitude* [comparative] &middot; *two orders of magnitude* (= a hundred times) &middot; *within an order of magnitude of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **One order of magnitude is a factor of ten, two is a hundred, three is a thousand** — the count is of powers of ten, not of multiples, and that is where the phrase gets misused: *two orders of magnitude* is a hundredfold, not twofold. The bare plural *orders of magnitude faster* means "very much faster" and is honest hyperbole in speech, dishonest in a specification. Against [a factor of](#a-factor-of), which takes any number at all: *by a factor of three* is available where *an order of magnitude* is not, since this one comes only in tens. And like *a factor of*, it is not a predeterminer — [three times](#three-times) and [double](#double) stand in front of a noun phrase, this cannot.
**Variants:** *orders of magnitude*, *by an order of magnitude*, *a factor of ten*, *tenfold*, *ten times*

Multiplies or divides a quantity by a power of ten, from outside the noun phrase.

1. Solar has gotten *an order of magnitude* cheaper since 2010.
2. The new index is *orders of magnitude* faster on the same hardware.
3. Their estimate and ours differ *by an order of magnitude*.
4. Two *orders of magnitude* is a hundredfold, not twice.
5. The risk is *an order of magnitude* higher in that age group.
6. ✗ *an order of magnitude the cost* → ✓ *ten times* the cost.

**See also:** [a factor of](#a-factor-of), [three times](#three-times), [twofold, threefold, tenfold](#twofold-threefold-tenfold), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [percent](#percent)

### double

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʌbəl/ &middot; *DUB-uhl* &middot; the central determiner it stands in front of stays weak: *double the price* /ˈdʌbəl ðə ˈpraɪs/, *double an ordinary dose* /ˈdʌbəl ən ˈɔrdənɛri ˈdoʊs/
**Русский:** вдвое больше, двойной — предопределитель: double the price — вдвое дороже
**Type:** multiplier &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer**, ahead of the central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** an already-determined noun phrase naming an amount — *double the price*, *double that dosage*, *double my usual order*. It does not attach to a bare noun as a multiplier: *double price* is not the multiplier use, and phrases like *a double room*, *double doors*, *double vision* are the adjective, not the determiner.
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *double of the price*. The one *of* that appears is inside the noun phrase that follows: *double the number of applicants*.
**Pattern:** *double* [the/my/this/that + noun] &middot; *double the number of* [plural noun] &middot; *double* [the + measure noun] &middot; *a double* [noun] (adjective, different word class)
**Contrast:** *Double* and *twice* mean the same multiplication and differ in shape. *Double* attaches to a noun phrase (*double the price*); *twice* does that too (*twice the price*) but is also the one that works before *as*: *twice as expensive*, ✗ *double as expensive*. For comparisons with an adjective, use *twice*; for a plain doubled amount, either. *Double* additionally lives as an adjective (*a double bed*), a verb (*prices doubled*), and a noun (*a stunt double*) — only the predeterminer use belongs in this file.
**Variants:** *twice*, *twofold*, *two times*, *double the amount*, *doubly* (adverb)

Multiplies the following quantity by two.

1. They charged us *double* the advertised price.
2. The new pump moves *double* the water in half the time.
3. She's making *double* what she made at her last job.
4. *Double* the number of applicants showed up.
5. Take *double* that dosage only if the doctor says so.
6. The storm dropped *double* the normal rainfall for March.

**See also:** [twice](#twice), [triple, quadruple](#triple-quadruple), [three times](#three-times), [half](#half), [twofold, threefold, tenfold](#twofold-threefold-tenfold)

### half again as much

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæf əˈɡɛn əz ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *haf uh-GEN az MUCH*
**Русский:** в полтора раза больше — амер., разг.
**Type:** multiplier (fractional increase, = 1.5 times) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer phrase; the *as … as* frame carries the comparison &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; distinctly American
**Goes with:** *as much* takes uncountable nouns (*half again as much money*); *as many* takes plural countable nouns (*half again as many people*). Choose the one that matches the noun — this is the *much/many* split, imported whole.
**With *of*:** No *of*. The second half of the frame is *as* + a noun phrase: *half again as much money as last year*.
**Pattern:** *half again as much* [uncountable noun] &middot; *half again as many* [plural noun] &middot; *half again as* [adjective] &middot; *half again* [the + noun] &middot; [verb] *half again as much*
**Contrast:** It means **one and a half times**, not "half as much again more" and not "twice." The British form is *half as much again*, which means exactly the same thing and confuses American readers just as much in reverse. Because both are misread often, technical writing should say *one and a half times* or *fifty percent more* and be done with it.
**Variants:** *half as much again* (British), *one and a half times*, *fifty percent more*, *half again as many*

Increases a quantity by fifty percent.

1. The new lease costs *half again as much* as the old one.
2. They hired *half again as many* seasonal workers this year.
3. The second engine is *half again as* powerful.
4. It took *half again as much* time as we budgeted.
5. She sold *half again as many* units in the fourth quarter.
6. The premium version holds *half again* the charge.

**See also:** [twice](#twice), [double](#double), [half](#half), [three times](#three-times), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### once

**Pronunciation:** /wʌns/ &middot; *WUNSS* &middot; the article in the frequency frame is weak and unstressed: *once a week* /ˈwʌns ə ˈwik/, *once an hour* /ˈwʌns ən ˈaʊɚ/
**Русский:** (один) раз, однажды — наречие частотности, а не определитель
**Type:** frequency adverb — **not** a multiplier determiner &middot; **Position:** adverbial; it does not occupy a determiner slot before a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** nothing, as a determiner. It heads a frequency phrase with *a/an* or *every* (*once a week*, *once every three days*) and works as a plain adverb of time (*I met him once*).
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*.
**Pattern:** *once a/an* [time noun] &middot; *once every* [cardinal] [plural time noun] &middot; *once* [adverbial] &middot; *at once* &middot; *once* [clause] (conjunction — a separate word)
**Contrast:** **This entry exists to block an error.** *Twice* and *three times* are multipliers that precede a noun phrase (*twice the price*, *three times the price*), and learners reasonably extend the pattern down to *once* — but ✗ *once the price* is not English. For a one-times comparison, say *the same price* or *equal to the price*. *Once* keeps only the frequency sense (*once a week*) and the time-adverb sense (*we met once*). It is also a subordinating conjunction (*once you sign, it's final*), which lives with the conjunctions.
**Variants:** *at once*, *once more*, *once again*, *once in a while*, *once upon a time*

Names a single occurrence or a rate of one per period. It is not the "one times" member of the multiplier series.

1. The committee meets *once* a month.
2. Water the plant *once* every ten days.
3. I only asked *once*.
4. She rewrote the opening *once* more and stopped.
5. Take the pills *once* daily with food.
6. ✗ *It costs once the price of the original.* → ✓ It costs *the same as* the original.

**See also:** [twice](#twice), [three times](#three-times), [one](#one), [double](#double)

### per

**Pronunciation:** /pɚ/ &middot; *pur* &middot; **unstressed and reduced in ordinary use** — *ten dollars per hour* is /ˈtɛn ˈdɑlɚz pɚ ˈaʊɚ/. The full /pɝ/ shows up only under contrastive stress and in the Latin tags *per se* /pɚ ˈseɪ/ and *per diem* /pɚ ˈdiəm/.
**Русский:** за, на, в — предлог нормы: per hour — в час; тех. и коммерч. регистр
**Type:** preposition (distributive rate marker) — **not a determiner** &middot; **Position:** heads a prepositional phrase after a quantity; it fills no determiner slot, and it allows no determiner after it &middot; **Register:** **technical and commercial** — invoices, contracts, science, and specifications. In speech and ordinary prose the article does this job: *sixty miles an hour*, not *per hour*, unless you are reading a gauge.
**Goes with:** a **bare singular unit noun** of time, weight, distance, volume, or head count — *per hour*, *per pound*, *per mile*, *per person*, *per unit*, *per share*, *per capita*. **It takes no determiner at all**, and that is the rule worth memorizing: ✗ *per an hour*, ✗ *per the hour*, ✗ *per each person*. Never a plural (✗ *per hours*) and never a mass noun (✗ *per water* → *per gallon*).
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*.
**Pattern:** [amount] *per* [bare unit noun] &middot; [number] *per* [bare unit noun] &middot; *per capita / per diem / per annum / per se* (Latin) &middot; *as per* [noun phrase] (business jargon) &middot; *per* [noun phrase] (= according to)
**Contrast:** ***Per* and the distributive *a/an* are one job in two registers.** *Six dollars a pound* and *six dollars per pound* mean exactly the same thing; the first belongs in speech and the second on the invoice — see [a (per)](01-articles.md#a-per). The hard rule is that *per* refuses an article: *$22 an hour* or *$22 per hour*, never ✗ *$22 per an hour*. Against *each*, which follows the noun and prices items rather than stating a rate (*Tickets are $12 each*), and against *by the*, which names the unit a thing is sold or paid in (*sold by the pound*, *paid by the hour*). Two business habits draw complaints: *as per your request* (write *as you requested*) and *per* meaning "according to" (*per the contract*), which is fine in a memo and stiff anywhere else.
**Variants:** *a / an* (the spoken form), *per capita*, *per diem*, *per annum*, *per se*, *as per*, *each*, *by the*

States a rate by naming the unit it is measured against: so much for every one of these.

1. The lot charges four dollars *per* hour after the first two.
2. Output is measured in gallons *per* minute.
3. Reimbursement is capped at sixty-five dollars *per* diem.
4. The state spends about twelve thousand dollars *per* student.
5. Earnings came to $1.42 *per* share.
6. In speech it's sixty miles *an* hour; on the gauge it's miles *per* hour.

**See also:** [a (per)](01-articles.md#a-per), [once](#once), [twice](#twice), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [percent](#percent)

### three times

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθri ˌtaɪmz/ &middot; *THREE TYMZ* &middot; the determiner behind it never takes stress: *three times the price* /ˈθri ˌtaɪmz ðə ˈpraɪs/
**Русский:** в три раза, втрое — предопределитель: three times the price
**Type:** multiplier &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** an already-determined noun phrase naming an amount — *three times the price*, *four times my salary*, *ten times that number*. Also used before *as many* + plural countable and *as much* + uncountable: *three times as many applicants*, *three times as much water*.
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *three times of the price*. Any *of* belongs to the noun phrase inside: *three times the number of complaints*.
**Pattern:** [cardinal] *times* [the/my/that + noun] &middot; [cardinal] *times as many* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *times as much* [uncountable noun] &middot; [cardinal] *times as* [adjective] &middot; [cardinal] *times the number of* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** ***Times* stays plural from three up** — *three times*, *ten times* — while *twice* replaces ✗ *two times* in idiomatic usage (*two times the price* is grammatical but reads as arithmetic; *twice the price* is what people say). Below that, *once* is not a multiplier at all. Match *as many* to countables and *as much* to uncountables, exactly as with *many* and *much*.
**Variants:** *four times*, *ten times*, *n times*, *twice* (for two), *threefold*, *X times over*

Multiplies the following quantity by a stated number.

1. The house sold for *three times* what they paid for it.
2. This model uses *four times* the electricity of the older one.
3. *Ten times* as many people showed up as we planned for.
4. She earns *three times* my salary and works fewer hours.
5. The storm dumped *five times* the normal rainfall.
6. It's *three times as* expensive in the airport shop.

**See also:** [twice](#twice), [double](#double), [triple, quadruple](#triple-quadruple), [once](#once), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### triple, quadruple

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtrɪpəl/, /kwɑˈdrupəl/, also /ˈkwɑdrəpəl/ &middot; *TRIP-uhl, kwah-DROO-puhl*
**Русский:** тройной, втрое; четверной, вчетверо — quadruple и выше книжн. или тех.
**Type:** multiplier &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer** &middot; **Register:** neutral; *quadruple* and above are formal or technical
**Goes with:** an already-determined noun phrase naming an amount — *triple the amount*, *quadruple the dose*. As with *double*, the attributive adjective is a different word class: *a triple play*, *triple glazing*.
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *triple of the amount*.
**Pattern:** *triple* [the/my/that + noun] &middot; *quadruple* [the + noun] &middot; *triple the number of* [plural noun] &middot; *a triple* [noun] (adjective)
**Contrast:** After *double*, American English increasingly prefers the *n times* form: *triple the price* is fine, but *quadruple the price* already sounds like a lab report, and *quintuple* and *sextuple* are effectively technical only. Above two, *three times*, *four times*, and *five times* are the natural everyday multipliers. Note that *triple* and *quadruple* are also verbs (*enrollment tripled*) and adjectives (*a triple bypass*).
**Variants:** *treble* (British), *quintuple*, *sextuple*, *threefold*, *fourfold*, *three times*, *four times*

Multiplies the following quantity by three, four, and upward.

1. The clinic ordered *triple* the usual supply.
2. Rebuilding it now would cost *quadruple* the original estimate.
3. They're seeing *triple* the traffic they saw last spring.
4. The recipe calls for *triple* the amount of garlic, which is correct.
5. *Quadruple* the dose is dangerous, not merely ineffective.
6. He wants *triple* what the car is worth.

**See also:** [double](#double), [three times](#three-times), [twofold, threefold, tenfold](#twofold-threefold-tenfold), [twice](#twice)

### twice

**Pronunciation:** /twaɪs/ &middot; *TWYSS* &middot; whatever follows stays weak: *twice the price* /ˌtwaɪs ðə ˈpraɪs/, *twice a year* /ˌtwaɪs ə ˈjɪr/, *twice as much* /ˌtwaɪs əz ˈmʌtʃ/
**Русский:** вдвое; дважды — предопределитель: twice the size — вдвое больше
**Type:** multiplier &middot; **Position:** **predeterminer**, ahead of the central determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** an already-determined noun phrase naming an amount — *twice the price*, *twice that amount*, *twice my weight*. Also *twice as many* + plural countable, *twice as much* + uncountable, and *twice a/an* + time noun in the frequency sense (*twice a week*).
**With *of*:** **Never.** ✗ *twice of the price*.
**Pattern:** *twice* [the/my/that + noun] &middot; *twice as many* [plural noun] &middot; *twice as much* [uncountable noun] &middot; *twice as* [adjective] &middot; *twice a/an* [time noun] &middot; *twice the number of* [plural noun]
**Contrast:** *Twice* and *double* both multiply by two; *twice* is the one that also fits the *as … as* frame (*twice as expensive*, not ✗ *double as expensive*) and the frequency frame (*twice a week*, not ✗ *double a week*). *Two times* is grammatical but unidiomatic where *twice* will do. And *once* does not join this series before a noun phrase — see [once](#once).
**Variants:** *two times*, *double*, *twofold*, *twice over*, *twice as many/much*

Multiplies the following quantity by two, or names a rate of two per period.

1. The storm dropped *twice* the normal rainfall.
2. They pay *twice* what the job is worth, and still can't fill it.
3. There were *twice as many* people as chairs.
4. It uses *twice as much* electricity as the old unit.
5. The board meets *twice* a year.
6. This one is *twice as* heavy but half the price.

**See also:** [double](#double), [three times](#three-times), [once](#once), [half again as much](#half-again-as-much), [half](#half)

### twofold, threefold, tenfold

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtufoʊld/, /ˈθrifoʊld/, /ˈtɛnfoʊld/ &middot; *TOO-fohld, THREE-fohld, TEN-fohld*
**Русский:** двукратный, трёхкратный, десятикратный — книжн.; rose tenfold — вырос в десять раз
**Type:** multiplier (adjective before a noun, adverb after a verb) — **not** a determiner &middot; **Position:** attributive adjective inside the noun phrase, after the determiner (*a tenfold increase*), or a bare adverb after the verb (*rose tenfold*) &middot; **Register:** formal; the register of reports, science writing, and business news
**Goes with:** as an adjective, singular and plural countable nouns naming a change — *a twofold increase*, *threefold gains*. It does not attach to an ordinary noun: ✗ *tenfold people*, ✗ *a tenfold price* — the noun has to name growth, a rise, a risk, or a difference.
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*.
**Pattern:** *a* [n]*fold* [increase/rise/jump/difference] &middot; [verb] [n]*fold* &middot; *a twofold* [problem/purpose] (the "having two parts" sense)
**Contrast:** Unlike *double* and *twice*, these never stand in the determiner slot — you cannot say ✗ *tenfold the price*, only *ten times the price* or *a tenfold increase in price*. *Twofold* has a second sense that has nothing to do with multiplication: "having two parts" (*the purpose is twofold*), and that sense is far more common than the arithmetic one. Note that a *tenfold increase* means the amount became ten times as large, not eleven times.
**Variants:** *manifold* (literary), *hundredfold*, *a twofold purpose*, *n-fold* (technical)

Expresses multiplication as a property of a change rather than as a determiner on a noun.

1. The company reported a *tenfold* increase in support tickets.
2. Enrollment rose *threefold* between 2015 and 2020.
3. The risk is roughly *twofold* higher in that age group.
4. Our purpose here is *twofold*: to explain and to warn.
5. A *hundredfold* return is a fantasy, not a forecast.
6. Costs grew *fourfold* while revenue stayed flat.

**See also:** [double](#double), [three times](#three-times), [triple, quadruple](#triple-quadruple), [percent](#percent)

## Approximators

These attach to a numeral rather than to the noun, and they hedge it — roughly this many, at least this many, no more than this many. Two of them sit here for contrast rather than for hedging: *a total of* attaches to a numeral in exactly the same way and hedges nothing at all, and *-plus* attaches **after** the number instead of in front of it, as *-odd* does. Watch the article: when the quantity is treated as a single block, a singular *a* can stand in front of a plural noun (*a good twenty miles*, *a full three days*).

### a good, a solid, an easy, a full

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈɡʊd/, /ə ˈsɑləd/, /ən ˈizi/, /ə ˈfʊl/ &middot; *uh GUUD, uh SAH-lid, un EE-zee, uh FUUL*
**Русский:** добрых, целых, никак не меньше — усиление минимума: a good twenty miles
**Type:** approximator (emphatic minimum) &middot; **Position:** *a/an* is the central determiner; the adjective premodifies the cardinal in the postdeterminer slot &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*a good twenty miles*, *a solid three hours*) or a cardinal + measure phrase (*a full two percent*). The noun is plural while the article is singular, because the whole quantity is one block: ✓ *a good twenty miles*, and note that ✗ *good twenty miles* without the article is not English.
**With *of*:** No *of* before the numeral. *Of* appears only in a partitive built on top: *a good twenty of them*.
**Pattern:** *a good* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a solid* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *an easy* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a full* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a good* [cardinal] *of* [pronoun]
**Contrast:** All four mean **at least, and probably more** — the opposite of *about*, which hedges in both directions. *A full* is the most neutral and simply insists the count is complete (*a full three days*, not two and a half). *A good* and *a solid* are conversational; *an easy* implies the estimate is conservative (*an easy two hundred people were there*). Against *a mere*, which belittles the same number: *a mere twenty miles* is nothing, *a good twenty miles* is a haul.
**Variants:** *a good many*, *a good few*, *a solid*, *a full*, *an easy*, *at least*

Marks a number as a floor rather than an estimate: this much, if not more.

1. It's *a good* twenty miles from here to the interstate.
2. We waited *a solid* three hours before anyone came out.
3. There were *an easy* two hundred people in the lobby.
4. She's been at it *a full* two weeks without a day off.
5. *A good* half of them never responded.
6. That'll take *a solid* forty minutes in this traffic.

**See also:** [a mere](#a-mere), [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [no fewer than, no less than, as many as](#no-fewer-than-no-less-than-as-many-as), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of)

### a mere

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈmɪr/ &middot; *uh MEER*
**Русский:** всего лишь, каких-то — принижает количество
**Type:** approximator (diminishing) &middot; **Position:** *a* is the central determiner; *mere* premodifies the cardinal or the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; rhetorical
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*a mere three days*), a quantity phrase (*a mere two percent*, *a mere fraction*), and a singular countable noun in the non-numerical sense (*a mere child*, *a mere formality*).
**With *of*:** No *of* before the numeral. *Of* appears in a partitive built on top: *a mere two of the forty applicants*.
**Pattern:** *a mere* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a mere* [singular noun] &middot; *a mere* [percentage/fraction] &middot; *the merest* [noun]
**Contrast:** *A mere* does the opposite work of [a good](#a-good-a-solid-an-easy-a-full): both attach to the same number, but *a mere twenty miles* dismisses it and *a good twenty miles* respects it. As with those, the article is singular before a plural noun. *Only* and *just* hedge the same way in ordinary speech; *a mere* is the written, rhetorical version.
**Variants:** *merely*, *the merest*, *only*, *just*, *no more than*

Marks a number as smaller or slighter than the reader might expect.

1. The whole repair took *a mere* twenty minutes.
2. *A mere* two percent of applicants were accepted.
3. She won by *a mere* forty votes.
4. It's *a mere* formality at this point.
5. He was *a mere* child when the family left.
6. They rebuilt it in *a mere* three months.

**See also:** [a good, a solid, an easy, a full](#a-good-a-solid-an-easy-a-full), [a fraction of](#a-fraction-of), [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately)

### a total of

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈtoʊtəl əv/ &middot; *uh TOH-tuhl uv* &middot; *a* /ə/ and *of* /əv/ are weak, and the *t* between vowels is a flap — *a total of forty* is /ə ˈtoʊɾəl əv ˈfɔrti/
**Русский:** в общей сложности, всего — суммарный итог
**Type:** numeral frame (summative) — it states a sum instead of hedging it &middot; **Position:** heads a quantity phrase in front of the numeral; the phrase then fills the determiner slot of the noun &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the register of news reports, minutes, and press releases, where it is often padding
**Goes with:** a cardinal plus a plural countable noun (*a total of forty-one applicants*), an amount of money, time, or measure (*a total of $4.2 million*, *a total of six hours*), and a percentage. It requires a **number** after it: ✗ *a total of applicants*, ✗ *a total of some people*.
**With *of*:** **Required and fixed** — part of the phrase, not a partitive: ✗ *a total forty people*. A real partitive is built on top with a second *of*: *a total of nine of the twelve jurors*.
**Pattern:** *a total of* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *a total of* [amount of money/time] &middot; *a total of* [number] *percent* &middot; *a combined total of* [number] &middot; *in total*
**Contrast:** **It hedges nothing**, which is what separates it from everything else in this section: *a total of forty* is exactly forty, where *about forty*, *at least forty*, and *up to forty* are not. What it adds is that the number is a **sum counted up from parts**, which earns its keep in a report covering six precincts and is pure filler in *A total of one person complained*. Agreement follows the plural noun after *of*, not *total*: *A total of forty-one applicants **were** interviewed*, while the abstract sum takes a singular — *The total **was** forty-one*. Style guides tell writers not to begin a sentence with a numeral, and *a total of* exists largely to solve that problem.
**Variants:** *a combined total of*, *in total*, *all told*, *altogether*, *an aggregate of*, *a sum of*

Introduces a number as the sum of everything counted, without hedging it in either direction.

1. *A total of* forty-one applicants sat for the exam.
2. The county reported *a total of* $4.2 million in storm damage.
3. *A total of* six agencies signed the memorandum.
4. She missed *a total of* nine days all year.
5. *A combined total of* eleven inches fell across the three counties.
6. *A total of* one person objected, which is a sentence that should have read "one person objected."

**See also:** [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [the aggregate of](#the-aggregate-of), [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [hundred, thousand, million, billion](#hundred-thousand-million-billion), [percent](#percent)

### about, around, roughly, approximately

**Pronunciation:** /əˈbaʊt/, /əˈraʊnd/, /ˈrʌfli/, /əˈprɑksəmətli/ &middot; *uh-BOWT, uh-ROWND, RUF-lee, uh-PRAHK-suh-mit-lee*
**Русский:** около, примерно, приблизительно — approximately офиц. и тех.
**Type:** approximator (two-directional estimate) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or quantity phrase — it sits outside the determiner system and takes whatever follows &middot; **Register:** *about* and *around* neutral to conversational; *roughly* neutral; *approximately* formal and technical
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*about twenty people*), a quantity or measure phrase (*roughly two gallons*, *around ten percent*), a fraction (*about half the class*), and a time expression (*around three o'clock*). It never attaches directly to a bare noun: ✗ *about people*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits whatever the quantity phrase after it requires: *about a third of the class*.
**Pattern:** *about* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *around* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *roughly* [fraction] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *approximately* [number] [measure noun] &middot; *about* [time]
**Contrast:** These four hedge in **both** directions — the real number could be higher or lower — which is what separates them from *a good* and *upwards of* (at least this many) and from *no more than* (at most this many). Register is the only meaningful difference among the four: *around* is the most American and most conversational, *about* is the neutral default, *roughly* signals a deliberate rounding, and *approximately* belongs in specifications and lab reports. Do not stack them with the postposed hedges: ✗ *about twenty or so* is redundant, though it is common in speech.
**Variants:** *round about* (informal), *some* (before a round number), *give or take*, *more or less*, *ballpark* (informal)

Marks a number as an estimate that could fall on either side of the figure given.

1. *About* twenty people showed up in the rain.
2. The repair will run *around* four hundred dollars.
3. *Roughly* half the class had already dropped.
4. The tank holds *approximately* two hundred gallons.
5. She got here *around* three o'clock, maybe a little after.
6. *Roughly* a third of the responses arrived after the deadline.

**See also:** [nearly, almost](#nearly-almost), [some (approximative)](#some-approximative), [twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so](#twenty-odd-twenty-ish-or-so), [a good, a solid, an easy, a full](#a-good-a-solid-an-easy-a-full)

### anywhere from … to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniwɛr frəm … tə/ &middot; *EN-ee-wair fruhm … tuh* &middot; both joining words are weak — *from* /frəm/ and *to* /tə/ — and the beats fall on *any-* and on the two numbers: *anywhere from twenty to thirty* /ˈɛniwɛr frəm ˈtwɛnti tə ˈθɚti/
**Русский:** где-то от … до — разг. оценка диапазона
**Type:** approximator (open range) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of a pair of numerals; it sits outside the determiner system and takes whatever quantity phrase follows &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal — conversational and journalistic, the standard shape of an estimate from a contractor or an analyst
**Goes with:** two cardinals with a plural countable noun after the second (*anywhere from twenty to thirty people*), two amounts of money, time, or measure (*anywhere from two to six hours*), two percentages, and two fractions. The noun is stated once, after the second number. It never attaches to a bare noun: ✗ *anywhere from people*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits whatever the quantity phrase after it requires — *anywhere from a third to half of the class*.
**Pattern:** *anywhere from* [number] *to* [number] [plural noun] &middot; *anywhere from* [amount] *to* [amount] &middot; *anywhere between* [number] *and* [number] &middot; *anywhere from* [fraction] *to* [fraction] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *anywhere up to* [number]
**Contrast:** The range is what makes the estimate honest and *anywhere* is what makes it wide: plain *from twenty to thirty people* states a range, *anywhere from twenty to thirty people* admits the speaker has no idea where inside it the answer falls. **Keep the frame intact** — *anywhere between* takes *and*, never *to* (✗ *anywhere between twenty to thirty*), and a word frame does not mix with a dash (✗ *anywhere from 20–30 people* → *anywhere from 20 to 30 people* or *20–30 people*). Against [up to](#up-to), which names only a ceiling, and [at least](#at-least-at-most), which names only a floor, this one names both ends and commits to neither.
**Variants:** *anywhere between … and*, *anywhere up to*, *from … to*, *between … and*, *somewhere in the range of*, *give or take*

States an estimate as an open range between two numbers, with no claim about where inside it the truth sits.

1. The job takes *anywhere from* two *to* six hours, depending on the wiring.
2. They're quoting *anywhere from* $400 *to* $900 for the same part.
3. *Anywhere from* twenty *to* thirty people usually show up on a Tuesday.
4. Damage estimates run *anywhere from* nine *to* fourteen million dollars.
5. *Anywhere between* a third *and* half the crop was lost.
6. Turnaround is *anywhere from* three days *to* three weeks, and they will not narrow it down.

**See also:** [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [up to](#up-to), [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [more than, over, under, less than](#more-than-over-under-less-than), [twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so](#twenty-odd-twenty-ish-or-so)

### at least, at most

**Pronunciation:** /ət ˈlist/, /ət ˈmoʊst/ &middot; *uht LEEST, uht MOHST* &middot; *at* reduces to /ət/ and is never stressed here; the stress falls on *least* and *most*
**Русский:** по меньшей мере — самое большее; границы включаются
**Type:** approximator (inclusive bound — floor and ceiling) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or quantity phrase; it sits outside the determiner system and takes whatever follows &middot; **Register:** neutral; *at least* is the plainest way to state a minimum in American English
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*at least twenty people*), a cardinal + singular countable noun when the number is *one* (*at least one witness*), a measure or money phrase (*at most two gallons*, *at least fifty dollars*), a fraction (*at least half the budget*), and — unusually for this section — a following determiner or quantifier (*at least some warning*, *at most a few minutes*). It never attaches to a bare noun with no quantity in it: ✗ *at least people*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits whatever the quantity phrase after it requires — *at least three of the jurors*, *at most half of them*.
**Pattern:** *at least* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *at most* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *at least one* [singular noun] &middot; *at least/at most* [fraction] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *at least* [amount of money/time]
**Contrast:** **These are the inclusive bounds, and that is what separates them from *more than* and *under*.** *At least twenty* includes twenty; *more than twenty* excludes it. *At most twenty* includes twenty; *under twenty* excludes it. Against *about* and *around*, which hedge in both directions, each of these hedges in exactly one. Against *no fewer than* and *as many as*, which mark the number as surprisingly high, *at least* is rhetorically flat — which is why contracts, recipes, and specifications use it. Note that *at least* has a second life as a discourse softener with no number in sight (*It's ugly, but at least it's cheap*); there it is an adverb, not a numeral modifier.
**Variants:** *not less than* (contracts), *a minimum of*, *a maximum of*, *no more than*, *up to*, *at the very least*

Fixes the lower or upper limit of a quantity, with the stated number itself included inside the limit.

1. There were *at least* forty people in line ahead of us.
2. The repair takes *at most* two days if the part is in stock.
3. You need *at least one* witness to sign the form in person.
4. *At least* half the budget was already committed by March.
5. She spends *at most* an hour a week on the newsletter.
6. *At least* three of the jurors had made up their minds before lunch.

**See also:** [more than, over, under, less than](#more-than-over-under-less-than), [up to](#up-to), [no fewer than, no less than, as many as](#no-fewer-than-no-less-than-as-many-as), [a good, a solid, an easy, a full](#a-good-a-solid-an-easy-a-full), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of)

### circa

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɚkə/ &middot; *SUR-kuh* &middot; the second syllable is a weak schwa and never takes stress; the abbreviations *c.* and *ca.* are read aloud either as *circa* or simply as "about"
**Русский:** около, приблизительно (о дате) — книжн., из латыни; сокр. c. или ca.
**Type:** approximator (date hedge) — a Latin preposition, not a determiner &middot; **Position:** premodifier of a year or a date, and postposed after a noun phrase in the informal use; outside the determiner system either way &middot; **Register:** **formal and technical** — museum labels, auction and dealer catalogs, academic history, genealogy, and library cataloging, abbreviated *c.* or *ca.* In running prose *around 1890* says the same thing and is not mistaken for a typo.
**Goes with:** a **year or a date** above all (*circa 1890*, *c. 1650*), and by extension a named period (*circa the Depression*). Loosely, in business speech, a round money figure (*circa $2 million*) — a use British commerce made ordinary and American editors still flag. It does not attach to a plain count of things: ✗ *circa twenty people* → *about twenty people*.
**With *of*:** Does not take *of*.
**Pattern:** *circa* [year] &middot; *c.* [year] &middot; *ca.* [year] &middot; *circa* [the + period noun] &middot; [noun phrase], *circa* [year] (postposed)
**Contrast:** **It hedges dates, and in careful use only dates.** *About*, *around*, and *roughly* hedge anything at all (see [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately)); *circa* narrows to the year on the object's label. The abbreviations split by field — art history and cataloging prefer *ca.*, general publishing prefers *c.* — and both sit tight against the date with no comma: *c. 1890*, ✗ *c., 1890*. There is a second, arch use in American speech where *circa* means "back in the era of" and is hung on the end of anything: *his politics, circa 1968*. That one is postposed, informal, and always a little of a joke.
**Variants:** *c.*, *ca.*, *around*, *about*, *approximately*, *fl.* (*floruit*, of a person's active years), postposed *circa* (informal)

Marks a date as approximate — the thing belongs near this year rather than exactly in it.

1. The catalog lists it as a Shaker side chair, *circa* 1840.
2. The photograph is dated *c.* 1912 in the finding aid.
3. Her research covers Neapolitan glassmaking *circa* 1600 to 1650.
4. The deed puts the barn on the property *ca.* 1878.
5. He still dresses like a graduate student, *circa* 1994.
6. In running prose write *around* 1890 rather than *circa* 1890 unless it is a caption.

**See also:** [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [some (approximative)](#some-approximative), [nearly, almost](#nearly-almost), [twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so](#twenty-odd-twenty-ish-or-so)

### more than, over, under, less than

**Pronunciation:** /mɔr ðən/, /ˈoʊvɚ/, /ˈʌndɚ/, /lɛs ðən/ &middot; *MOR thuhn, OH-vur, UN-dur, LESS thuhn* &middot; *than* is always weak, /ðən/ or /ðn̩/, never /θæn/
**Русский:** более, свыше — менее, меньше чем; строго, без включения самого числа
**Type:** approximator (exclusive comparative bound) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or quantity phrase; outside the determiner system &middot; **Register:** neutral; *over* and *under* are the conversational pair, *more than* and *less than* the written pair
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*more than twenty people*, *over a hundred entries*), a measure, money, time, or distance phrase (*under two gallons*, *over an hour*, *less than thirty dollars*), a fraction (*more than half the seats*), and a percentage (*under ten percent*). With a plural count of separate things, careful writing prefers *fewer than* to *less than*: *fewer than twenty applicants*. It never attaches to a bare noun: ✗ *more than people*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits the requirement of whatever follows — *more than half of the residents*, *fewer than ten of them*.
**Pattern:** *more than* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *over* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *fewer than* [cardinal] [plural countable noun] &middot; *less than* [amount/uncountable noun] &middot; *under* [amount] &middot; *more than* [fraction] *of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **The *fewer/less* split runs through all four.** *Fewer* counts, *less* measures: *fewer than twenty applicants* but *less than twenty dollars*. Money, time, distance, and weight take *less* even when the number is plural, because they are single amounts rather than counts of separate things: *less than five miles*, *less than two hours*, *less than ten pounds*. The old objection to *over* meaning *more than* ("*over* is only spatial") was never a real rule and the AP Stylebook dropped it in 2014 — *over a hundred entries* is ordinary American English. All four bounds are **exclusive**: *more than twenty* does not include twenty. For a bound that does include it, use [at least](#at-least-at-most) and *at most*.
**Variants:** *fewer than*, *no more than*, *up to*, *north of / south of* (informal, financial), *better than* (informal, = more than), *shy of* (informal, = just under)

Places a quantity strictly above or strictly below the figure named.

1. *More than* two hundred people signed the petition in a week.
2. *Over* half the seats were empty by the third inning.
3. The whole procedure took *under* an hour.
4. *Fewer than* ten of the original tenants are still in the building.
5. The house sold for *less than* half what they paid for it.
6. Everything on that rack is *under* thirty dollars.

**See also:** [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [up to](#up-to), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [no fewer than, no less than, as many as](#no-fewer-than-no-less-than-as-many-as), [fewer](05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### nearly, almost

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɪrli/, /ˈɔlmoʊst/ &middot; *NEER-lee, AWL-mohst*
**Русский:** почти, без малого — приближение снизу
**Type:** approximator (upper bound, approached from below) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of a numeral, quantifier, or determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*nearly a hundred people*), a fraction (*almost half the budget*), and — unusually for this section — a following **determiner**: *almost every seat*, *nearly all the money*, *almost no one*. It does not attach to a bare noun: ✗ *nearly people*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits the requirement of whatever follows: *nearly all of them*, *almost half of the class*.
**Pattern:** *nearly* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *almost* [fraction] [noun phrase] &middot; *almost every* [singular noun] &middot; *nearly all* [the + noun] &middot; *almost no* [noun]
**Contrast:** *Nearly* and *almost* set a ceiling the number falls just short of, so they are one-directional where *about* and *around* go both ways: *nearly a hundred* is 95, never 105. The two are interchangeable before numbers; only *almost* is fully comfortable in *almost every*, *almost no*, and *almost anyone*, and only *nearly* takes *not nearly* (*not nearly enough*). Both attach to *all*, *every*, and *no*, but neither attaches to *some*: ✗ *almost some*.
**Variants:** *not nearly*, *just about*, *close to*, *practically*, *virtually*, *all but*

Marks a quantity as falling just short of the figure named.

1. *Nearly* a hundred commuters were stranded overnight.
2. *Almost* half the budget went to a single contractor.
3. *Almost every* seat was taken by seven o'clock.
4. We got *nearly* all of it done before the power went out.
5. *Almost no* one filled out the second page.
6. That's *not nearly* enough for a deposit.

**See also:** [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [half](#half)

### no fewer than, no less than, as many as

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈfjuɚ ðən/, /noʊ ˈlɛs ðən/, /əz ˈmɛni əz/ &middot; *noh FYOO-ur thuhn, noh LESS thuhn, az MEN-ee az*
**Русский:** не менее чем, целых — подчёркнутый минимум, книжн. и журн.
**Type:** approximator (emphatic floor) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; journalistic
**Goes with:** *no fewer than* and *as many as* take a cardinal + **plural countable** noun (*no fewer than forty applicants*, *as many as three hundred homes*). *No less than* and *as much as* take **amounts and uncountable** nouns (*no less than half the budget*, *as much as two gallons*). *No less than* is also used before a plural count in speech (*no less than forty applicants*), and careful writers change it to *no fewer than*.
**With *of*:** No *of* before the numeral; *of* comes from the partitive built on top: *no fewer than half of the residents*.
**Pattern:** *no fewer than* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *no less than* [amount/uncountable noun] &middot; *as many as* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *as much as* [amount] &middot; *no less a* [noun] *than* [name]
**Contrast:** **The *fewer/less* split is the whole trap.** *Fewer* counts, *less* measures: *no fewer than twelve jurors*, *no less than a gallon*. All three phrases mark the number as surprisingly high — they are rhetorical, not neutral, so a specification that just needs a minimum should say *at least*. Note the separate idiom *no less than* meaning "none other than": *The complaint came from no less than the mayor herself*.
**Variants:** *at least*, *not less than* (contracts), *as few as*, *as little as*, *no more than* (the ceiling version)

Marks a number as a minimum, and marks it as surprisingly large.

1. *No fewer than* forty families were displaced by the fire.
2. The storm dropped *as much as* eight inches in some counties.
3. *As many as* three hundred homes may be without power tonight.
4. The renovation will cost *no less than* half a million dollars.
5. *No fewer than* six agencies have jurisdiction over that stretch of river.
6. The objection came from *no less than* the chief of staff.

**See also:** [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [a good, a solid, an easy, a full](#a-good-a-solid-an-easy-a-full), [fewer](05-quantifiers-small.md#fewer), [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less)

### north of, south of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɔrθ əv/, /ˈsaʊθ əv/ &middot; *NORTH uv, SOWTH uv* &middot; *of* is weak, /əv/, and reduces to /ə/ before a consonant — *north of ten grand* /ˈnɔrθ ə ˈtɛn ˈɡrænd/; the beat is on the compass word and on the number
**Русский:** больше чем — меньше чем; разг. и образно, обычно о деньгах
**Type:** approximator (exclusive bound, figurative) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or amount; outside the determiner system &middot; **Register:** **informal and figurative** — business talk, financial journalism, and sports commentary, where it is stock phrasing for money. It is never the register for a contract or a spec sheet.
**Goes with:** an amount of **money** above all (*north of two million dollars*), and by extension any figure people picture on a dial or a chart — an age, a speed, a percentage, a temperature (*south of forty percent*, *north of ninety miles an hour*). It also attaches to a bare figure standing alone: *The bid came in north of forty*. Not a bare noun: ✗ *north of applicants*.
**With *of*:** The *of* is **required and fixed** — part of the phrase, not a partitive. To build a real partitive on top, repeat it: *north of half of the fleet*.
**Pattern:** *north of* [amount of money] &middot; *south of* [amount] &middot; *north of* [number] *percent* &middot; *just north/south of* [amount] &middot; *north of* [number] (standing alone)
**Contrast:** *North of* means **more than** and *south of* means **less than**, from the compass on a wall map where up is more. They belong with [more than, over, under, less than](#more-than-over-under-less-than) and are exclusive in the same way — *north of forty* does not include forty — but they carry a swagger those plain words do not, which is why an annual report says *in excess of* and a broker says *north of*. **They live on money.** *North of two hundred thousand dollars* is idiomatic; *north of two hundred thousand applicants* is not, and counts of people and things want *more than*. *South of* is much rarer and usually turns up for the sake of the pair. The older informal *better than* (*better than forty of them showed*) does the same job.
**Variants:** *just north of*, *just south of*, *better than* (informal), *upwards of*, *in excess of*, *shy of* (= just under)

Places an amount above (*north of*) or below (*south of*) a figure, in informal money talk.

1. The renovation came in *north of* two million dollars.
2. Their approval rating is *south of* forty percent and still falling.
3. He's making *north of* six figures at the new firm.
4. The truck was doing *north of* ninety when it left the road.
5. Anything *south of* thirty grand isn't going to buy that lot.
6. She's *just north of* fifty and running marathons.

**See also:** [more than, over, under, less than](#more-than-over-under-less-than), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [up to](#up-to), [-plus](#-plus)

### on the order of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˈɔrdɚ əv/ &middot; *ahn thee OR-dur uv* &middot; **the article switches form**: *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *order*, never ✗ /ðə/. *On* and *of* are weak too, /ən/ and /əv/, so everything in front of the number flattens to /ənðiˈɔrdɚəv/.
**Русский:** порядка — тех./науч.: примерно такой величины
**Type:** approximator (two-directional estimate, round magnitudes) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or quantity phrase; outside the determiner system &middot; **Register:** formal and technical — engineering, science writing, and analysis. In conversation the word is *about*.
**Goes with:** a round cardinal plus a plural countable noun (*on the order of two hundred cases*), an amount of money, time, or measure (*on the order of ten million dollars*, *on the order of six months*), and a percentage. Like [some (approximative)](#some-approximative) it wants a **round** number: ✗ *on the order of two hundred seven cases*. Not a bare noun: ✗ *on the order of cases*.
**With *of*:** The *of* is **required and fixed** — part of the phrase. A real partitive needs a second *of*: *on the order of half of the fleet*.
**Pattern:** *on the order of* [round cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *on the order of* [amount] &middot; *on the order of* [number] *percent* &middot; *of the order of* [number] (older and British)
**Contrast:** It hedges in **both** directions like *about*, but it hedges harder: *about two hundred* claims to be close to two hundred, while *on the order of two hundred* claims only the right ballpark — the right power of ten and roughly the right leading digit. That is why it sits next to [an order of magnitude](#an-order-of-magnitude), which counts those powers of ten exactly. Do not confuse it with the ordinary *in the order of*, meaning "in sequence" (*deal with them in the order of arrival*); *on* is the American form of the hedge and *of the order of* is the older, more British one. And do not stack it: ✗ *on the order of magnitude of ten million*.
**Variants:** *of the order of* (British), *in the ballpark of*, *something like*, *roughly*, *approximately*, *give or take*

Marks a number as right only to the nearest round magnitude.

1. The repair will cost *on the order of* ten million dollars.
2. They handle *on the order of* two hundred cases a year.
3. Response time is *on the order of* a few milliseconds.
4. We're talking *on the order of* six months, not six weeks.
5. The county expects *on the order of* five thousand new residents by 2035.
6. Losses ran *on the order of* fifteen percent across the whole portfolio.

**See also:** [an order of magnitude](#an-order-of-magnitude), [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [some (approximative)](#some-approximative), [anywhere from … to](#anywhere-from--to), [a factor of](#a-factor-of)

### -plus

**Pronunciation:** /plʌs/ &middot; *PLUSS* &middot; it hangs on the number with no pause and takes **no stress of its own**, though it keeps its full vowel — *twenty-plus years* is /ˈtwɛnti plʌs ˈjɪrz/, with the beats on *twen-* and *years*
**Русский:** с лишним, и более — присоединяется после числа: 20-plus years — двадцать с лишним лет
**Type:** approximator (postposed floor) &middot; **Position:** attaches **after** the numeral, inside the quantity phrase; the phrase then modifies the noun &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — journalism, job postings, résumés, and advertising, where it is compact and completely standard
**Goes with:** a cardinal plus a plural countable noun (*twenty-plus applicants*, *50-plus years*), an amount of money, an age, or a measure (*$500-plus*, *a 300-plus-page report*), and an age band standing alone (*the 65-plus crowd*). It never attaches to a bare noun (✗ *plus applicants*), and with the number *one* it is unidiomatic — use [one or more](#one-or-more).
**With *of*:** No *of*. A partitive is built separately: *twenty-plus of the original crew*.
**Pattern:** [cardinal]*-plus* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal]*-plus* (standing alone) &middot; *a* [cardinal]*-plus-*[singular measure noun] [noun] &middot; [amount]*-plus* &middot; *the* [age]*-plus* [noun]
**Contrast:** **It marks a floor, exactly like *upwards of*, and that is what separates it from *-odd*, which hedges both ways:** *twenty-odd years* could be nineteen, *twenty-plus years* could not. Both are postposed, which separates them in turn from *about*, *some*, and *at least*, and none of them should be stacked — ✗ *about twenty-plus years*. Keep the hyphen: *twenty-plus years* is a quantity, *twenty plus years* reads as arithmetic. In the attributive the whole thing hyphenates and the measure noun goes singular, exactly as it does after a plain cardinal: *a 30-plus-year veteran*.
**Variants:** *-odd*, *or more*, *and up*, *and counting*, *and change* (of money and time), *upwards of*

Marks a number as a floor that the real figure meets or passes, hung on the end of the number itself.

1. She has *twenty-plus* years on the same beat.
2. The posting asks for *five-plus* years of supervisory experience.
3. Turnout was *200-plus*, which nobody expected in a primary.
4. They handed us a *300-plus*-page report the night before the vote.
5. Tickets ran *$80-plus* once the fees were added.
6. The *65-plus* crowd votes in every municipal election.
7. ✗ *about twenty-plus people* → ✓ *twenty-plus people*, or *about twenty people*.

**See also:** [twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so](#twenty-odd-twenty-ish-or-so), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [at least, at most](#at-least-at-most), [more than, over, under, less than](#more-than-over-under-less-than), [north of, south of](#north-of-south-of)

### some (approximative)

**Pronunciation:** /sʌm/ — always the **strong** form &middot; *SUM*
**Русский:** около, примерно — книжн. перед круглым числом: some fifty people
**Type:** approximator &middot; **Position:** premodifier of a round cardinal &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, journalistic; it sounds bookish in conversation
**Goes with:** a round cardinal + plural countable noun — *some twenty people*, *some fifty years*. It does not go with a bare noun in this sense, and it does not go with a small or precise number: ✗ *some three people*, ✗ *some twenty-two people* — the number has to be a round approximation.
**With *of*:** Not in this sense. The partitive *some of the students* is the ordinary quantifier *some*, a different word with a different pronunciation.
**Pattern:** *some* [round cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *some* [round cardinal] [plural time noun] *ago* &middot; *some* [round cardinal] [measure noun]
**Contrast:** **Pronunciation separates the two *somes*.** The ordinary quantifier is unstressed and reduced — *some water* /səm ˈwɔtɚ/ — while the approximator is stressed and full: *some twenty people* /ˈsʌm ˈtwɛnti ˈpipəl/. Meaning separates them too: this one means *about*, and it is the only *some* that can be replaced by *approximately*. For the everyday quantifier, see [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some) — this entry covers only the numeral-hedging use.
**Variants:** *some … odd*, *about*, *approximately*, *upwards of*

Hedges a round number in the same way *about* does, in a more formal register.

1. *Some* twenty people were waiting outside the courthouse.
2. The building has stood there *some* two hundred years.
3. *Some* fifty thousand ballots remain uncounted.
4. She left the company *some* three years ago.
5. The fire burned *some* forty acres before it was contained.
6. *Some* six hundred pages of testimony were entered into the record.

**See also:** [about, around, roughly, approximately](#about-around-roughly-approximately), [upwards of, in excess of](#upwards-of-in-excess-of), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), [twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so](#twenty-odd-twenty-ish-or-so)

### twenty-odd, twenty-ish, or so

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtwɛnti ˌɑd/, /ˈtwɛntiɪʃ/, /ɚ ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *TWEN-tee-ahd, TWEN-tee-ish, ur SOH*
**Русский:** двадцать с чем-то, где-то двадцать, примерно — разг., ставится после числа
**Type:** approximator (postposed) &middot; **Position:** attaches **after** the numeral, not before it &middot; **Register:** informal; *-odd* is neutral in speech and casual writing, *-ish* is conversational
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*twenty-odd people*), or a bare numeral or time expression standing alone (*twenty or so*, *around fiveish*). *-ish* also attaches to times and ages (*sixish*, *fortyish*).
**With *of*:** No *of*. The partitive is built separately: *twenty-odd of them*.
**Pattern:** [cardinal]*-odd* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal] *or so* &middot; [cardinal] *or so* [plural noun] &middot; [cardinal]*-ish* &middot; [cardinal] *odd years* &middot; [cardinal] *or thereabouts*
**Contrast:** **The hyphen in *-odd* carries meaning.** *Twenty-odd people* means about twenty; *twenty odd people* means twenty peculiar people. Always hyphenate. *-odd* attaches only to round numbers and always means "a bit more" — *twenty-odd* is never nineteen. *Or so* and *-ish* hedge in both directions like *about*, and *-ish* alone can answer a question (*"What time?" "Sixish."*). All three are postposed, which is what distinguishes them from *about* and *some*, and they should not be stacked with those: ✗ *about twenty-odd people*.
**Variants:** *or thereabouts*, *give or take*, *-something* (*twenty-something*), *and change* (*twenty dollars and change*), *plus* (*twenty plus*)

Hedges a number from behind, marking it as approximate or as a floor.

1. There were *twenty-odd* people in the waiting room.
2. Give me ten minutes *or so* and I'll be ready.
3. He's *fortyish*, maybe a little younger.
4. She's been teaching there *thirty-odd* years.
5. We're meeting at *sixish* if that works.
6. It cost two hundred dollars *or so*, plus tax.

### up to

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌp tə/, before a vowel /ˌʌp tu/ &middot; *up tuh, up too* &middot; *to* is weak, /tə/, and never carries stress; the beats fall on *up* and on the number — *up to twenty* /ˌʌp tə ˈtwɛnti/
**Русский:** до, вплоть до — верхняя граница включительно
**Type:** approximator (inclusive ceiling) &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral or quantity phrase; it sits outside the determiner system and takes whatever follows &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standard way to state a ceiling in advertising, specifications, and warranties
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*up to twenty passengers*), a measure, money, or time phrase (*up to two gallons*, *up to fifty dollars*, *up to six hours*), a fraction (*up to half the class*), and a percentage (*up to forty percent*). With the number *one* it takes a **singular countable** noun, as any *one*-phrase does: *up to one guest per resident*. It never attaches to a bare noun with no quantity in it: ✗ *up to passengers*, ✗ *up to money*.
**With *of*:** No *of* of its own; it inherits whatever the quantity phrase after it requires — *up to half of the residents*, *up to three of the four seats*, *up to ten percent of the total*.
**Pattern:** *up to* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *up to one* [singular noun] &middot; *up to* [amount of money/time] &middot; *up to* [fraction] *of* [noun phrase] &middot; *up to* [number] *percent* &middot; *up to and including* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** **It sets a ceiling, not an estimate, and that is exactly what advertising trades on.** *Up to fifty percent off* promises nothing but the ceiling — one item marked down fifty percent satisfies it. Like [at most](#at-least-at-most) the bound is **inclusive**: *up to twenty* includes twenty, where *under twenty* and *fewer than twenty* exclude it. Against *about* and *around*, which hedge in both directions, *up to* hedges only downward — the real figure can be anything from nothing at all up to the number named. That is also what separates it from *upwards of*, which points the other way and means *more than*. In contracts, the fuller *up to and including* removes any doubt about whether the endpoint counts.
**Variants:** *up to and including*, *at most*, *no more than*, *a maximum of*, *not to exceed* (contracts), *as many as* (the rhetorical version)

Sets an inclusive ceiling: anything from nothing at all up to the figure named.

1. The van holds *up to* twelve passengers.
2. Shipping takes *up to* six business days.
3. They're advertising *up to* fifty percent off, which guarantees almost nothing.
4. Are you allowed *up to* two carry-ons on this fare?
5. The grant covers *up to* half the tuition.
6. The fine can run *up to* five thousand dollars.
7. Each household may register *up to one* vehicle per space.
8. It doesn't hold *up to* two gallons, whatever the box says.

### upwards of, in excess of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌpwɚdz əv/, /ɪn ɪkˈsɛs əv/ &middot; *UP-wurdz uv, in ik-SESS uv*
**Русский:** свыше, более — in excess of юр. и фин.
**Type:** approximator (floor, "more than") &middot; **Position:** premodifier of the numeral; *of* is part of the fixed phrase &middot; **Register:** *upwards of* neutral to journalistic; *in excess of* formal, legal, and financial
**Goes with:** a cardinal + plural countable noun (*upwards of two hundred applicants*) and a cardinal + measure or amount phrase (*in excess of ten million dollars*, *upwards of six hours*). Not a bare noun: ✗ *upwards of applicants*.
**With *of*:** The *of* is **required and fixed** — it is part of the phrase, not a partitive. To build a real partitive on top, repeat it: *upwards of half of the residents*.
**Pattern:** *upwards of* [cardinal] [plural noun] &middot; *in excess of* [cardinal] [measure noun] &middot; *upwards of* [cardinal] *percent* &middot; *upward of* [cardinal] (variant spelling)
**Contrast:** Both mean **more than** the number given, which puts them with *no fewer than*, against the two-directional *about*, and directly opposite [up to](#up-to) — *up to two hundred* is a ceiling, *upwards of two hundred* is a floor, and swapping them reverses the claim. *Upwards of* has a second, looser use in American speech meaning "roughly, and probably a bit more" (*upwards of an hour*), so avoid it where the number is a legal threshold. *In excess of* is the contract-and-annual-report register and always means strictly greater than; in plain prose *more than* says the same thing in two syllables.
**Variants:** *upward of*, *over*, *more than*, *north of* (informal, financial), *better than* (informal)

Marks the stated number as a lower bound that the real figure passes.

1. *Upwards of* two hundred people applied for six positions.
2. The verdict was *in excess of* ten million dollars.
3. The drive takes *upwards of* six hours in summer traffic.
4. Losses *in excess of* the deductible are covered.
5. *Upwards of* forty percent of the fleet is out of service.
6. They've raised *upwards of* a million dollars since March.
