# Predeterminers, postdeterminers, and general determiners

> The words that stack around the central determiner — one slot in front of it, one slot behind it — plus the vague indefinites that fill the central slot themselves when no article will do.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

English allows exactly **one central determiner** per noun phrase, which is why ✗ *the my book* and ✗ *a this book* are impossible. Everything else that wants to modify the noun has to find a different slot: *all*, *both*, *half*, *such*, *quite*, *rather*, *many a*, the placeholder *such-and-such*, and the binominal intensifiers *a hell of a*, *a whale of a*, and *something of a* squeeze in **in front** of the central determiner as **predeterminers**, and *other*, *same*, *own*, *only*, *whole*, *next*, *past*, and the numerals line up **behind** it as **postdeterminers**. A third group — *certain*, *various*, *sundry*, *diverse*, *assorted*, *multiple* — can fill the central slot itself, which is why *various reasons* and *certain questions* need no article at all. What that third group never does is stand beside a **second** central determiner: ✗ *a various reason*, ✗ *various the reasons*. When an article or a possessive is already present, these words do not disappear — they slide back one slot and behave as postdeterminers (*the various reasons*, *his assorted grievances*, *the miscellaneous items*). The trap running through the whole file is **word order**: get the slot wrong and the phrase collapses, because ✗ *the all cake* and ✗ *whole the cake* are both ungrammatical for the same reason that ✗ *the every day* is.

This file is the positional one. It gives the ordering template that every determiner obeys, the *of*-alternation that the predeterminers share, and full entries for the words whose only home is one of these two outer slots. Members of the class that are catalogued in other files for their meaning — *all* and *both* with the distributives, *half* and *double* with the numerals, *such a* and *what a* with the exclamatives — are listed with links at the end.

## The three slots

The full template for an English noun phrase runs left to right in a fixed order, and no slot may be filled twice:

**[predeterminer] &rarr; [central determiner] &rarr; [postdeterminer] &rarr; [adjective] &rarr; [noun]**

- *all* &rarr; *the* &rarr; *other* &rarr; *unopened* &rarr; *boxes*
- *both* &rarr; *my* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *hands*
- *half* &rarr; *an* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *hour*
- *such* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *long* &rarr; *day*
- *quite* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *while*
- *rather* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *nuisance*
- *many* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *young* &rarr; *writer*
- *a hell of* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *long* &rarr; *day*
- *something of* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *legend*
- *such-and-such* &rarr; *a* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *date*
- *&mdash;* &rarr; *the* &rarr; *first three* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *chapters*
- *&mdash;* &rarr; *her* &rarr; *only other* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *sister*
- *&mdash;* &rarr; *various* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *unrelated* &rarr; *reasons*
- *&mdash;* &rarr; *the* &rarr; *various* &rarr; *&mdash;* &rarr; *reasons*

Two consequences follow. First, a predeterminer is the **only** way to get a totality word in front of *the*: since *all* cannot be a central determiner alongside *the*, it must stand outside the phrase, and that is exactly what *all the students* is. Second, a word can change slots and change meaning as it goes — *whole* is a postdeterminer (*the whole cake*) while its synonym *all* is a predeterminer (*all the cake*), and swapping them produces ✗ *whole the cake* and ✗ *the all cake*. The last two lines of the template show the third pattern: *various* takes the central slot when nothing else claims it and drops back to the postdeterminer slot when *the* arrives.

## Weak forms in the stack

Because the predeterminer carries the stress, the central determiner it leans on is nearly always **unstressed**, and learners who give it a full vowel sound wrong even when the word order is right. The reductions to expect: *a* /ə/, *an* /ən/, *the* /ðə/ before a consonant and /ði/ before a vowel, *some* /səm/, *your* /jɚ/, *our* /ɑr/. So *all of the students* is /ˌɔl əv ðə ˈstudn̩ts/, *quite an evening* is /ˌkwaɪt ən ˈivnɪŋ/, *half of your time* is /ˌhæf əv jɚ ˈtaɪm/, and *both of our cars* is /ˌboʊθ əv ɑr ˈkɑrz/. The vowel-fronted forms matter most with the postdeterminers in this file that begin with a vowel: *the entire day* is /ði ɪnˈtaɪɚ ˈdeɪ/ and *the only exit* is /ði ˈoʊnli ˈɛɡzɪt/, both with /ði/, not /ðə/.

## Nonstandard fillers of these slots

Three spoken forms fill these slots in ways edited writing does not accept, and they are labeled where they appear below. *A whole nother* splits *another* around *whole* and is **informal spoken American only** — see [a whole nother](01-articles.md#a-whole-nother). *Most all* (as in *most all the students*) is a **regional American** predeterminer for *almost all*. And ✗ *all manners of things* is simply an **error** for *all manner of things*. Two further nonstandard patterns belong to neighboring files but stack in the same slots: the demonstrative-for-*those* of *them apples* and *this here book* (see [them](02-demonstratives.md#them), [this here](02-demonstratives.md#this-here)), and negative concord in *ain't no reason* (see [no (negative concord)](06-distributives-negatives.md#no-negative-concord)). The countability error of *less* with a countable noun (✗ *less chairs* for *fewer chairs*) is treated at [less](05-quantifiers-small.md#less).

## The *of* rule

Predeterminers alternate between a bare form and an *of*-form, and the choice is not free. The rule has three parts:

1. **Before another determiner, *of* is optional** with *all*, *both*, and *half*: *all (of) the students*, *both (of) my hands*, *half (of) the cake*. Americans use the *of* rather more than the British do; neither is wrong.
2. **Before a pronoun, *of* is required**: *all of them*, *both of us*, *half of it*. Never ✗ *all them*, ✗ *both us*, ✗ *half it*. The one systematic exception is the **floating** order, in which the pronoun comes first and the quantifier trails behind it with no *of* at all: *you all*, *we both*, *them all*, *us both*, *I saw them all*. Put the quantifier first and *of* comes straight back: *all of you*, *both of us*.
3. **Before a bare noun, *of* is impossible**: *all children*, *both hands*, *half price* — never ✗ *all of children*.

The pattern breaks in three places, and they break in different directions.

- ***Each*** and ***none*** **require** *of* whenever another determiner or a pronoun follows: *each of the students*, *none of them*, never ✗ *each the students*, ✗ *none them*. *Each* keeps a bare form before a bare singular noun (*each student*); *none* has no bare determiner form at all and is always *none of*.
- ***Every*** **never takes *of* at all.** ✗ *every of the students* is impossible in every register — there is no optional-*of* version and no required-*of* version. The partitive must be built on *one*: ***every one of** the students*, ***every one of** them*. This is the single sharpest split between *every* and its near-twin *each*, which does take *of*.
- The fixed phrases below — *all kinds of*, *all manner of*, *some kind of* — carry *of* inside them as part of the word, and no determiner may follow it: *all kinds of problems*, never ✗ *all kinds of the problems*. To select from a known set, switch to the real partitive: *all of the problems*.

## Agreement

The verb agrees with what the determiner actually quantifies, and the outer slots do not change that:

- ***Each*, *every*, *either*, *neither*** take a **singular** verb, even when a plural *of*-phrase intervenes: *Each of the students **has** a locker*. *Every one of them **is** accounted for*. *Neither of the answers **works***. The plural noun next to the verb is a distraction; the head is the singular quantifier.
- ***Both*, *all*, *several*** take a **plural** verb: *Both of my hands **are** cold*. *Several of the seats **were** empty*. *All* is the exception inside the exception — it goes plural with a plural noun (*All the students **were** there*) and singular with an uncountable one (*All the water **was** gone*).
- ***None*** takes **either**, and the choice is a real one. *None of the seats **were** taken* is standard and far commoner in American English; *None of the seats **was** taken* is the formal, strictly-singular option some editors still enforce. Neither is an error. With an uncountable noun only the singular is possible: *None of the water **was** left*.
- ***All but*** takes the verb its plural set calls for, not the number of the exception clipped onto the end: *All but one of the founders **have** retired*, not ✗ *has retired*. The adjacent *one* pulls hard toward the singular; resist it.

See [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [either](06-distributives-negatives.md#either), [neither](06-distributives-negatives.md#neither), and [none of](06-distributives-negatives.md#none-of) for the full entries.

## Predeterminers: the slot in front of the article

Eight entries live here in full, and three of them — *a hell of a*, *a whale of a*, and *something of a* — are one **binominal frame** with different nouns dropped into it: [*a* + evaluating noun + *of*] set in front of an ordinary *a* + noun, with the *of* welded in and the second article doing the real determining. The remaining members of the class — *all*, *both*, *half*, *double*, *twice*, *three times*, *such a*, *what a*, *many a*, and the negated *not all* — are catalogued in the files listed at the end of this page, but the ordering template above governs all of them alike. Note how many of these constructions reorder the phrase around the article rather than simply sitting in front of it.

### a hell of a, one hell of a, one heck of a

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈhɛl əv ə/, /ˌwʌn ˈhɛl əv ə/, /ˌwʌn ˈhɛk əv ə/ &middot; *uh HELL uv uh*, *wun HELL uv uh*, *wun HECK uv uh* &middot; **weak forms carry the whole phrase**: *of* drops to /əv/ and then to /ə/, both articles are /ə/ (or /ən/ before a vowel), and the run-together result is the eye-spelling *helluva* /ˈhɛləvə/ — *HELL-uh-vuh*. *One* is the exception: it resists reduction because it carries the emphasis, so it stays /wʌn/ and never becomes ✗ /wən/
**Type:** quantifier (degree, intensifying) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer (fixed *a … of a* frame) &middot; **Register:** informal and spoken; *hell* is mildly profane, *heck* is the broadcast-safe swap, and *helluva* is a spelling of the sound rather than a word for edited prose
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, because the frame ends in *a/an* — *a hell of a game*, *one hell of a mess*, *one heck of an argument*. Never a plural (✗ *a hell of a games*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *a hell of a traffic*, ✗ *a hell of a money*). To reach the other two noun types you hang the frame on a quantifier instead: *a hell of a lot of people*, *a hell of a lot of money*. The noun itself may be good, bad, or neutral — the frame only supplies the intensity, and the direction comes from the noun.
**With *of*:** The *of* is **welded into the frame** and cannot be dropped: ✗ *a hell a game*. It is not the partitive *of*, so no determiner may follow it (✗ *a hell of the game*) and no pronoun may either (✗ *a hell of a them*) — for a pronoun you go through the *lot* version, *a hell of a lot of them*.
**Pattern:** *a/one hell of a(n)* + [singular countable noun] &middot; *a/one hell of a(n)* + [adjective] + [noun] &middot; *one heck of a(n)* + [noun] (euphemism) &middot; *a hell of a lot of* + [plural or uncountable] &middot; *the hell out of* (unrelated verb intensifier)
**Contrast:** This is *quite a* with the volume up and the collar down — *quite a game* and *a hell of a game* fill the identical slot and make the identical claim, and only register separates them. Against *such a*, the word order matches (*a hell of a long day*, *such a long day*, adjective after the article), which is what keeps both of them apart from *so*, the one degree word that drags the adjective in front (*so long a day*). *One hell of a* is simply the emphatic form of *a hell of a*; the *one* is not counting anything, which is why ✗ *two hells of a mess* is nonsense. Keep the determiner apart from the adverbial *the hell* of *what the hell* and *get the hell out*, which intensifies verbs and question words rather than nouns. **On the euphemism:** *heck* is not merely politer but weaker — *one heck of a storm* is what the local news anchor says, *one hell of a storm* is what the man whose roof came off says.
**Variants:** *a helluva*, *one helluva* (eye-spelling), *a heck of a*, *one heck of a* (euphemism), *a hell of a lot of*, *a devil of a* (dated), *the hell out of* (unrelated adverbial)

Marks the noun as an extreme instance of its kind, in either direction: *a hell of a mechanic* is very good and *a hell of a mess* is very bad, and only the noun tells you which. It is among the commonest intensifiers in spoken American English and among the rarest in edited writing — which is exactly why *one heck of a* exists.

1. That was *a hell of a* game, right down to the last out.
2. He is *one hell of a* mechanic — nobody else could find that leak.
3. We got *one heck of a* storm out of that front Tuesday night.
4. Losing the server on a Friday was *a hell of a* way to end the week.
5. She has had *a hell of a* year, and not in a good way.
6. That is *a helluva* view for eighty dollars a night.
7. It would take *one hell of an* argument to change his mind now.
8. There is still *a hell of a lot of* work to do before the inspection.

**See also:** [a whale of a](#a-whale-of-a), [quite a](#quite-a), [rather a](#rather-a), [something of a](#something-of-a), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [what a, what an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-a-what-an), [a lot of](04-quantifiers-large.md#a-lot-of)

### a whale of a

**Pronunciation:** /ə ˈweɪl əv ə/ &middot; *uh WAYL uv uh* &middot; most Americans use a plain /w/ rather than the older /ʍ/, so *whale* is a homophone of *wail*; the weak forms are the frame's usual ones — *of* to /əv/ and then /ə/, both articles to /ə/ (/ən/ before a vowel) — and the phrase surfaces as a single run, /ə ˈweɪləvə/, *uh WAY-luh-vuh*
**Type:** quantifier (degree, intensifying) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer (fixed *a … of a* frame) &middot; **Register:** informal, American, and **mildly dated** — the clean cousin of *a hell of a*. An American meets it in sportscasting, in advertising copy, and from relatives over sixty, and above all in the frozen phrase *have a whale of a time*
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *a whale of a time*, *a whale of a story*, *a whale of an idea*. Never a plural (✗ *a whale of a stories*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *a whale of a fun*). Unlike *a hell of a* it leans **positive**: *a whale of a time* is a good time, and *a whale of a mess* is possible but sounds like a joke. There is no *a whale of a lot of* in current use, so for plurals and uncountables you switch to *a lot of* or *a great deal of*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is welded into the frame and cannot be dropped: ✗ *a whale a time*. It is not partitive, so nothing definite may follow it — ✗ *a whale of the time*, ✗ *a whale of it*. Compare the literal noun, where the *of* is doing real work: *the skeleton of a whale*.
**Pattern:** *a whale of a(n)* + [singular countable noun] &middot; *a whale of a(n)* + [adjective] + [noun] &middot; *have a whale of a time* (fixed collocation) &middot; *a monster/beast/bear of a* + [noun] (the same frame, other animals)
**Contrast:** *A whale of a* and *a hell of a* are one construction with a different noun dropped into the frame, and they split on register and direction: the whale is clean, folksy, and usually complimentary, the hell is profane, current, and can go either way. Against *quite a*, the whale is louder and warmer — *quite a time* is what you write, *a whale of a time* is what your grandmother says. The frame stays productive in casual speech with any noun of size (*a monster of a truck*, *a beast of a hill*, *a bear of a man*), and those obey the same rules; what earns *a whale of a* its own entry is that it has frozen into *have a whale of a time*, where no other animal will substitute. It has nothing to do with the literal noun *whale*, and nothing at all to do with the verb *wail*.
**Variants:** *have a whale of a time* (fixed), *a monster of a*, *a beast of a*, *a bear of a*, *a devil of a* (dated), *a hell of a* (the profane equivalent)

An intensifier meaning "a very big and usually very good one of these," built on the size of the animal. It is the mildest member of the *a … of a* family and the one member you can use in front of anybody's children.

1. The kids had *a whale of a* time at the lake.
2. That was *a whale of a* story, and I do not believe a word of it.
3. We ran into *a whale of a* problem with the foundation.
4. She has *a whale of an* argument for keeping the old building.
5. They threw *a whale of a* party when he finally retired.
6. It turned out to be *a whale of a* deal once they threw in the warranty.
7. He hit *a whale of a* home run to end the game in the ninth.

**See also:** [a hell of a, one hell of a, one heck of a](#a-hell-of-a-one-hell-of-a-one-heck-of-a), [quite a](#quite-a), [rather a](#rather-a), [something of a](#something-of-a), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [a lot of](04-quantifiers-large.md#a-lot-of)

### all but

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈbʌt/ &middot; *awl BUT* &middot; *but* keeps its full vowel here, unlike the conjunction, which reduces to /bət/
**Type:** quantifier (universal with exception) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly formal
**Goes with:** whatever *all* itself goes with, since it is *all* with an exception clipped on. **Plural countable nouns** (*all but two passengers*), **uncountable nouns** (*all but a cup of the flour*, *all but a trickle of the water*), and **singular countable nouns only when a determiner follows** (*all but the final chapter*). Never a bare singular countable: ✗ *all but chapter*. The exception phrase after *but* must be a noun phrase, a numeral, or a pronoun. **Agreement:** the verb follows the **plural set**, not the singular exception standing next to it — *All but one of the founders **have** retired*, never ✗ *has retired*. With an uncountable noun the verb is singular: *All but a trickle of the water **was** gone*.
**With *of*:** *Of* attaches to the exception, not to *all*. It is **required** before a pronoun — *all but two of them*, *all but one of us*, never ✗ *all but two them*. With a following determiner it is optional in the same way as bare *all*: *all but (a few) of the seats*.
**Pattern:** *all but* + [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *all but* + [numeral] + [plural noun] &middot; *all but* + [numeral] + *of* + [the/them] &middot; *all but one* &middot; *all but* + [adjective/verb] (the unrelated adverb)
**Contrast:** As a determiner *all but* means "every one except," and the exception is a real countable thing you could point to. As an **adverb** it means "almost" and modifies an adjective or a verb: *the project was all but finished* does not mean everything except the finishing. The test is what follows — a noun phrase means determiner, an adjective or participle means adverb. Against *most*, *all but* claims far more: *most of the seats* leaves the count vague, while *all but three of the seats* names exactly what is missing. Against *not all*, it points the other way: *not all of the seats sold* says some did not, *all but three of the seats sold* says almost every one did. **Determiner or pronoun:** both. With the noun spelled out it is a determiner (*all but three passengers*); with the noun understood from context the phrase stands alone as a **pronoun** (*Twelve boarded, and all but three walked away*), and the pronoun use keeps the plural verb.
**Variants:** *all but one*, *all but a handful*, *all but the last*, *all but* + adjective (adverb, meaning "almost")

Sweeps in the entire set and then carves out a named exception, which is why it feels more precise than *most* — the speaker is telling you exactly how much is left over.

1. *All but* three of the passengers walked away from the crash.
2. She has read *all but* the last chapter.
3. *All but* one of the original founders have retired.
4. The storm knocked out power to *all but* a handful of houses on the street.
5. *All but* two of them voted to postpone the decision.
6. They sold off *all but* the downtown property.
7. We used *all but* a cup of the flour on the first batch.
8. Has *all but* the last shipment cleared customs yet?
9. Twelve people boarded, and *all but* three walked away.

### quite a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkwaɪt ə/ &middot; *KWYTE-uh* &middot; often flapped to /ˈkwaɪɾə/ in fast speech; before a vowel the article takes its weak *an* /ən/ — *quite an evening* /ˌkwaɪt ən ˈivnɪŋ/
**Type:** quantifier (degree) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral and conversational
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** in this predeterminer use — *quite a while*, *quite a mess*, *quite an evening*. Never a plural (✗ *quite a friends*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *quite a traffic*, ✗ *quite a money*). To reach the other two noun types you switch phrases: **plural countable** takes *quite a few* (*quite a few neighbors*), and **uncountable** takes *quite a bit of* (*quite a bit of money*) or article-free *quite some* (*quite some time*, *quite some trouble*).
**With *of*:** No *of* on *quite a* itself — ✗ *quite a of the time*. The repair phrases do take it: *quite a few of them*, *quite a bit of the money*. *Quite some* takes none: ✗ *quite some of time*.
**Pattern:** *quite a(n)* [singular countable noun] &middot; *quite a(n)* [adjective] + [singular countable noun] &middot; *quite the* [singular countable noun] &middot; *quite a few* [plural countable] &middot; *quite a bit of* [uncountable] &middot; *quite some* [uncountable]
**Contrast:** *quite a* and *rather a* fill the identical slot with opposite attitudes — *quite a* is admiring and impressed, *rather a* is understated and mildly negative. American English uses *quite a* constantly and *rather a* almost never. Note also the transatlantic trap in bare *quite* + adjective: American *quite good* means "very good," while British *quite good* often means "fairly good, and not much more." Word order matters too — *quite a long while* is right and ✗ *a quite long while* is not, because the adjective belongs after the article, not after *quite*. **In the negative the word flips sense entirely**: *not quite a year* means "almost a year, and slightly short of it," the opposite of the intensifying *quite a year*. Negate the sentence and you get the intensifier back (*That wasn't quite a disaster* = almost one); negate nothing and *quite a* only ever exaggerates.
**Variants:** *quite the*, *quite a few*, *quite a bit of*, *quite some* (as in *quite some time*), *not quite a* (= "almost")

**1.** As an intensifier, marks the noun as a notable, impressive, or extreme instance of its kind — *that was quite a storm* means the storm was remarkable. **2.** With measure nouns it simply means "a considerable amount of" — *quite a while*, *quite a distance*, *quite a drive*.

1. That was *quite a* game last night.
2. We waited *quite a* while before anyone came to the door.
3. She has made *quite a* name for herself in the last two years.
4. It is *quite a* drive from here — about four hours with traffic.
5. He is *quite the* storyteller once he gets going.
6. *Quite a few* of my neighbors have put solar panels on the roof.
7. They spent *quite a bit of* money getting the roof replaced.
8. We waited *quite some* time before the doors opened.
9. Isn't that *quite an* improvement over last year's numbers?
10. It has been *not quite a* year since she started — she is short by two weeks.

### rather a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðɚ ə/ &middot; *RA-thur-uh* &middot; first syllable rhymes with *bath*, not with *father*; the article stays weak and becomes /ən/ before a vowel — *rather an ordeal* /ˌræðɚ ən ɔrˈdil/
**Type:** quantifier (degree, downtoning) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer &middot; **Register:** formal and British-leaning; in American English it reads as bookish
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *rather a nuisance*, *rather a shame*, *rather a long meeting*. Never a plural (✗ *rather a problems*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *rather a traffic*). With plurals and uncountables the phrase becomes *rather a lot of*: *rather a lot of people*, *rather a lot of traffic*.
**With *of*:** No *of* on *rather a* itself. *Rather a lot of* carries it obligatorily: *rather a lot of money*, *rather a lot of them*.
**Pattern:** *rather a(n)* [singular countable noun] &middot; *rather a(n)* [adjective] + [singular countable noun] &middot; *a rather* [adjective] + [noun] (the preferred American order) &middot; *rather a lot of* [plural/uncountable]
**Contrast:** *rather a* downtones and apologizes where *quite a* admires: *that was quite a performance* is praise and *that was rather a disappointment* is a soft complaint. The bigger practical point is word order — American English moves the word into the ordinary adverb slot and says *a rather long meeting*, while *rather a long meeting* is the British-leaning predeterminer order. Both are grammatical; only one sounds American. Note that when *rather* means "instead" (*I'd rather walk*) it is not a determiner at all.
**Variants:** *a rather* (preferred American order), *rather a lot (of)*, *rather too*, *would rather* (unrelated verb idiom)

Marks the noun as more so than expected, in a mild and faintly negative direction — the sense of "somewhat of a" or "a bit of a." The understatement is the point: *rather a problem* is a real problem being described politely.

1. The whole business turned into *rather a* nuisance.
2. It was *rather a* shame the concert got rained out.
3. Losing the file that late in the day was *rather a* problem.
4. He gave *rather a* long answer to a very simple question.
5. She struck me as *rather a* serious person.
6. That is *rather a* lot of money for a used bike.

**See also:** [quite a](#quite-a), [many a](04-quantifiers-large.md#many-a), [a mere](07-numerals-multipliers.md#a-mere), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [so, too, as](#so-too-as)

### so, too, as

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/, /tu/, /æz/ &middot; *SOH*, *TOO*, *AZ* &middot; *as* reduces to /əz/ when unstressed, *too* never does
**Type:** quantifier (degree) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the construction is written rather than spoken
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, because the pattern is built around *a/an* — *so long a day*, *too big a risk*, *as good a time as any*. Never a plural (✗ *too big a risks*, ✗ *too big risks*) and never an uncountable (✗ *too heavy a traffic*). With plurals and uncountables the pattern is simply unavailable: you rephrase (*days that are so long*) or switch to the ordinary quantifiers *so many*, *too much*, and *as many*, which take no article at all.
**With *of*:** No *of*. The tails these words take are correlative, not partitive — *as ... as* (*as good a time as any*) and *too ... to* (*too big a risk to take*).
**Pattern:** *so* + [adjective] + *a(n)* + [singular noun] &middot; *too* + [adjective] + *a(n)* + [singular noun] (+ *to* + verb) &middot; *as* + [adjective] + *a(n)* + [singular noun] + *as* [X] &middot; *how* + [adjective] + *a(n)* + [singular noun] (interrogative) &middot; *that* + [adjective] + *a(n)* + [singular noun] (informal)
**Contrast:** This is the one construction in English where the **adjective jumps in front of the article**. Ordinary order is *a long day*; add one of these degree words and it inverts to *so long a day* — never ✗ *a so long day*. Compare *such*, which is a predeterminer in its own right and leaves the adjective where it was: *such a long day*. That is why *such a* is the everyday choice and *so long a day* reads as literary. Keep this pattern apart from *so many*, *too much*, and *as many*, which are ordinary quantifiers of plural and uncountable nouns with no article and no inversion.
**Variants:** *such a* (the plain alternative), *how* + adjective + *a*, *that* + adjective + *a* (informal), *no less* + adjective + *a*

Marks the degree of an adjective when the noun phrase is a singular countable, and pays for it by dragging the adjective in front of *a/an*. In current American English this is a written construction; in speech people nearly always reach for *such a* or a relative clause instead.

1. I had never seen *so* large a crowd at a school board meeting.
2. It was *too* big a risk to take with someone else's money.
3. This is *as* good a time as any to tell her.
4. *How* serious a problem are we actually talking about?
5. He is not *so* young a man that he can afford to wait another year.
6. That is *too* fine a distinction for a jury to follow.
7. Is it really *that* big a deal if we go on Sunday instead?

**See also:** [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [how (exclamative)](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#how-exclamative), [quite a](#quite-a), [rather a](#rather-a), [a](01-articles.md#a)

### something of a

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmθɪŋ əv ə/ &middot; *SUM-thing uv uh* &middot; **weak forms run deep here**: *of* goes to /əv/ and then to bare /ə/, the article is /ə/ (/ən/ before a vowel), and *something* itself reduces to /ˈsʌmpm̩/ in ordinary fast speech, so the whole phrase can land as /ˈsʌmpm̩ əvə/, *SUMP-m-uh-vuh*. Only the first syllable ever carries stress
**Type:** quantifier (degree, downtoning and hedging) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer (fixed *… of a* frame) &middot; **Register:** neutral and literate — the natural register of profiles, reviews, and obituaries, and the American print equivalent of British *rather a*
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only**, and overwhelmingly nouns of **role, type, or reputation** — *something of a legend*, *something of a specialist*, *something of a disaster*. Never a plural (✗ *something of a legends*) and never a bare uncountable (✗ *something of a trouble*). The noun has to be one that a person or thing can *be*: ✓ *something of an expert*, and *something of a chair* is not English. For quantity rather than category the frame changes shape entirely — *something like forty people*, *a certain amount of luck*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is built into the frame and cannot be dropped: ✗ *something a legend*. It is not partitive, so no determiner may follow it: ✗ *something of the legend*. Keep it apart from the free partitive *something of* meaning "a portion of," which takes no article after it and is a different construction: *He has something of his father in him.*
**Pattern:** [subject] + *be/become* + *something of a(n)* + [singular countable noun] &middot; *something of a(n)* + [adjective] + [noun] &middot; *something like* + [numeral] (approximation, unrelated) &middot; *quite something* (predicative, unrelated)
**Contrast:** Where *a hell of a* exaggerates, *something of a* **hedges** — *she is something of an expert* claims deliberately less than *she is an expert*, which makes it the exact opposite number of *quite a*, a frame that claims more than the plain noun would. It is closest in tone to *rather a*, but not in geography: *rather a nuisance* is British-leaning and bookish, while *something of a nuisance* is what an American newspaper prints for the same idea. Against *a certain*, note what each one withholds — *a certain charm* withholds the **identity** of the thing, *something of a charmer* withholds the **degree**. The disputed sibling is *somewhat of a*, which many editors treat as an error for *something of a*; it is common in American speech and still worth avoiding on paper. The informal spoken equivalent is *a bit of a* (*a bit of a mess*).
**Variants:** *something of an*, *a bit of a* (informal equivalent), *somewhat of a* (criticized in writing), *something like* (approximation), *quite something* (predicative)

Hedges the noun: the thing counts as a member of the category, but only loosely, and the speaker wants credit for the qualification. That built-in reservation is why obituaries and profiles lean on it so hard — *something of a recluse* is the polite way to report the fact.

1. She is *something of a* legend in the county clerk's office.
2. The evening turned into *something of a* disaster once the power went out.
3. He has become *something of an* expert on rural broadband.
4. The ruling came as *something of a* surprise to everyone in the room.
5. The old mill is *something of a* landmark, though nobody has worked there since 1978.
6. Her second novel was *something of a* departure from the first.
7. He was *something of a* recluse in his last years.
8. That is *something of an* overstatement, if you look at the actual numbers.

**See also:** [quite a](#quite-a), [rather a](#rather-a), [a hell of a, one hell of a, one heck of a](#a-hell-of-a-one-hell-of-a-one-heck-of-a), [a whale of a](#a-whale-of-a), [certain](#certain), [some kind of](#some-kind-of), [a bit of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-bit-of)

### such-and-such (a)

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *such-un-SUCH* &middot; the middle word is *and* in its **weak form only** — /ən/ or syllabic /n̩/, never the full /ænd/, which is why the spelling looks longer than the word sounds. The article behind it weakens too, to /ə/ or /ən/, so *such-and-such a date* is /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˌsʌtʃ ə ˈdeɪt/ and *such-and-such an amount* is /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˌsʌtʃ ən əˈmaʊnt/
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (placeholder) &middot; **Position:** predeterminer before *a/an*; central determiner in the looser bare-noun use &middot; **Register:** neutral and spoken, faintly impatient. An American meets it wherever someone is walking a listener through a procedure whose real values do not matter yet — a teacher setting up a word problem, a lawyer explaining a filing deadline, a customer-service script
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns through the article** — *such-and-such a date*, *such-and-such an amount*, *on such-and-such a day*. It can also attach straight to a **plural countable** (*such-and-such chapters*) or an **uncountable** (*such-and-such information*) with nothing in front, though that bare use is much rarer and reads as looser speech; the article version is the standard one. It never follows an article itself: ✗ *a such-and-such day*, ✗ *the such-and-such date*. And it takes no adjective between itself and the noun in the bare use: *such-and-such a long delay* is fine, ✗ *such-and-such long delays* is not.
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *such-and-such of the dates*. To select from a set that really exists, switch to *whichever* or the formal *certain of*.
**Pattern:** *such-and-such a(n)* + [singular countable] &middot; *such-and-such* + [plural or uncountable noun] (looser) &middot; *on/at/by such-and-such a* + [time noun] &middot; *such-and-such* standing alone (pronoun) &middot; *so-and-so* (the person equivalent)
**Contrast:** *Such-and-such* is the **placeholder** to *such*'s real demonstrative: *such a day* points at a day that actually happened, while *such-and-such a day* is an empty slot waiting for a date nobody has supplied. Its siblings divide the territory — *so-and-so* stands in for a **person** (*talk to so-and-so in accounting*) and doubles as a mild insult (*the old so-and-so*); *thingamajig* and *whatchamacallit* stand in for an object whose name has escaped the speaker; *blah blah blah* stands in for the content of what was said. Against *a certain*, the difference is who is being kept in the dark: *a certain date* means the speaker knows it and will not say, *such-and-such a date* means nobody has picked one. **Determiner or pronoun:** both. In front of a noun it is a determiner (*such-and-such a form*); with the noun understood it stands alone as a **pronoun** (*he claimed he had done such-and-such*), exactly as *so-and-so* does. Hyphens are standard in edited writing; the open spelling *such and such* is common and is not an error.
**Variants:** *such and such* (open spelling), *so-and-so* (person placeholder), *thus and so* (dated), *what's-his-name*, *thingamajig*, *whatchamacallit*

A deliberate blank. The speaker names the slot instead of the value, either because the value is beside the point of the example or because it has not been decided yet — *you file by such-and-such a date, and if you miss it the clerk does such-and-such*.

1. You have to file the form by *such-and-such a* date or the case is dismissed.
2. The letter always says the same thing: the account was reviewed on *such-and-such a* day and closed.
3. Suppose the contractor bills you for *such-and-such an* amount and you dispute it.
4. He kept saying he had been at *such-and-such a* meeting with so-and-so from the mayor's office.
5. Every syllabus tells you to read *such-and-such* chapters by Friday and says nothing about how.
6. The form just asks you to enter *such-and-such* information and press submit.
7. Do not tell me you were held up by *such-and-such* — I want the actual reason.

**See also:** [such](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such), [such a, such an](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such-a-such-an), [certain](#certain), [given](#given), [some kind of](#some-kind-of), [any old](06-distributives-negatives.md#any-old), [whatever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever)

## Postdeterminers: the slot behind the article

These words cannot open a noun phrase on their own with a singular countable noun — they need a central determiner in front of them, and they stand between it and any descriptive adjective. Their partners *other*, *another*, *same*, *own*, *next*, *last*, *final*, *previous*, *remaining*, *further*, *additional*, *more*, *few*, *several*, and the numerals are catalogued elsewhere; see the closing list. Watch two things as you read: which of them demand a **definite** determiner in front (*only*, *very*, *selfsame*, *sole*, *respective*, and *past* all do, while *entire*, *extra*, and *whole* take *a/an* happily), and which noun types each one allows, since the slot they share does not make them interchangeable.

### entire

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈtaɪɚ/ &middot; *in-TYRE* &middot; the word begins with a vowel, so the article in front of it takes its pre-vowel forms: *the entire day* /ði ɪnˈtaɪɚ ˈdeɪ/, *an entire block* /ən ɪnˈtaɪɚ ˈblɑk/
**Type:** quantifier (totality) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral, emphatic
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*an entire day*, *the entire team*), uncountable nouns naming a bounded span or mass (*the entire time*, *the entire output*), and plural countables when the plural names a fixed collection (*my entire savings*, *the entire proceeds*). It **cannot** open a noun phrase by itself: ✗ *Entire building burned down*. There must be a central determiner in front of it.
**With *of*:** **No *of*** — ✗ *the entire of the day*. This is the sharpest difference from its synonym *whole*, where *the whole of the day* is fine. The *of*-form belongs to the noun *entirety*: *the entirety of the day*.
**Pattern:** [the/a/an/my/this] + *entire* + [noun] &middot; [the] + *entire* + [ordinal or numeral] + [noun] &middot; *the entirety of* [noun phrase]
**Contrast:** *entire* and *whole* occupy the same slot and mean the same thing, but *whole* is the everyday word and *entire* the emphatic, slightly more formal one. Two grammatical splits separate them: *whole* takes an *of*-phrase and *entire* does not, and *whole* has the informal infixed form *a whole nother* which *entire* lacks. Against *all*, the difference is purely positional — *all* is a predeterminer and comes before the article (*all the cake*), while *entire* is a postdeterminer and comes after it (*the entire cake*), which is why ✗ *the all cake* and ✗ *entire the cake* are both impossible.
**Variants:** *entirely* (adverb), *entirety* (noun), *the entirety of*

The whole of something with nothing left out, said with emphasis. The speaker is not merely reporting the extent but insisting that it really was total.

1. He spent the *entire* afternoon on hold with the airline.
2. The fire took out an *entire* block of downtown.
3. She read the *entire* contract before signing anything.
4. My *entire* family showed up — all fourteen of them.
5. He was on his phone the *entire* time we were at dinner.
6. The *entire* proceeds from the sale go to the food bank.

**See also:** [whole](#whole), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [half](07-numerals-multipliers.md#half), [only](#only), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

### extra

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛkstrə/ &middot; *EK-struh* &middot; vowel-initial, so the determiner in front weakens to /ən/ or /ði/ — *an extra key* /ən ˈɛkstrə ˈki/, *the extra time* /ði ˈɛkstrə ˈtaɪm/
**Type:** quantifier (additive) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral and everyday
**Goes with:** all three noun types, which makes it unusually flexible — singular countable (*an extra key*), plural countable (*three extra chairs*, *extra towels*), and uncountable (*extra time*, *extra money*, *extra sugar*). With a plural or an uncountable it can also open the phrase with no central determiner at all: *Extra chairs are in the closet.* With a singular countable it needs one: ✗ *Extra key is under the mat*.
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *extra of the chairs*. To quantify from a known set, use *another*, *more of*, or a numeral: *two more of the chairs*.
**Pattern:** [a/an/the/my] + *extra* + [singular noun] &middot; [numeral] + *extra* + [plural noun] &middot; *extra* + [plural or uncountable noun] &middot; *a little extra* [uncountable]
**Contrast:** *extra* and *additional* fill the same slot and mean nearly the same thing; *extra* is the plain spoken word and *additional* the formal written one, and *extra* carries a hint of "spare, beyond what was strictly needed" that *additional* lacks. Against *more*, note that *more* is comparative and can stand alone as a pronoun (*I want more*) while *extra* as a determiner needs its noun. Do not confuse the determiner with the **adverb** *extra* meaning "unusually" (*extra careful*, *extra spicy*), which modifies adjectives rather than nouns.
**Variants:** *a little extra*, *some extra*, *extras* (noun), *extra-* (prefix)

Beyond the number or amount already counted on — one more, or some more, added to a baseline that both speaker and listener already have in mind.

1. Bring an *extra* charger in case mine dies on the road.
2. We put two *extra* chairs at the end of the table.
3. She stayed an *extra* night in Denver to finish the audit.
4. There is *extra* cash in the glove compartment.
5. The *extra* time helped, but we still missed the deadline.
6. Grab *extra* napkins on your way out.

**See also:** [further, additional](07-numerals-multipliers.md#further-additional), [another](07-numerals-multipliers.md#another), [more](04-quantifiers-large.md#more), [other](07-numerals-multipliers.md#other), [enough](04-quantifiers-large.md#enough)

### only

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊnli/ &middot; *OHN-lee* &middot; vowel-initial, so *the* in front of it is /ði/, not /ðə/ — *the only exit* /ði ˈoʊnli ˈɛɡzɪt/
**Type:** quantifier (uniqueness) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the only door*), plural countable nouns (*the only two seats left*), and uncountable nouns (*the only advice she gave me*, *the only evidence*). But it comes with a requirement the other postdeterminers do not have: it needs a **definite** central determiner in front of it — *the*, a possessive, or a demonstrative. Never after *a/an* (✗ *an only chance*), the single exception being the fixed noun *an only child*. Never bare: ✗ *Only exit is locked*.
**With *of*:** No *of* on the determiner itself. The *of* in *the only one of them* belongs to *one*, not to *only*.
**Pattern:** [the/my/his/this] + *only* + [noun] &middot; *the only* + [numeral] + [plural noun] &middot; *the only* + [noun] + [relative clause] &middot; *an only child* (fixed)
**Contrast:** The determiner *only* sits **after** a definite determiner and means "sole"; the far commoner **adverb** *only* sits **in front** of whatever it modifies and means "no more than." The slot is the test, and it changes the meaning completely: *the only three people who came were her cousins* identifies a group of three and says nobody else was there, while *only three people came* is a bare count and makes no claim about who they were. Against *sole*, the two words mean the same thing and differ only in register — *sole* is legal and written, *only* is what people say. In the negative it stays a determiner and simply denies uniqueness: *she is not my only sister* means there are others.
**Variants:** *the one and only*, *an only child*, *only ever*, *the only other*

Marks the noun as the single member of its class in the situation at hand: there are no others, and that absence is the point of the sentence.

1. That is the *only* exit on this side of the building.
2. She is my *only* sister.
3. The *only* two seats left were in the back row.
4. His *only* regret was not calling sooner.
5. That was the *only* advice anyone gave me all week.
6. He is an *only* child, and it shows.
7. She is not my *only* sister — there are three of us.
8. Is this the *only* copy of the lease you have?

**See also:** [sole](#sole), [very](#very), [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [one](07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each)

### particular

**Pronunciation:** /pɚˈtɪkjəlɚ/ &middot; *pur-TIK-yuh-lur* &middot; in fast American speech the medial schwa drops and both *r*s stay: /pɚˈtɪkjlɚ/, *pur-TIK-lur*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (specificity) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns** above all (*a particular restaurant*, *this particular case*, *no particular reason*), and uncountable nouns after *no* or *any* (*no particular order*, *no particular hurry*). Plural countables work only under *these/those/no* (*no particular reasons*, *these particular files*). The requirement that defines it: there must be a determiner in front — *a*, *no*, *any*, *this*, *that*, or *the*. Bare ✗ *particular people objected* is not the determiner use, and *particular people* on its own reads as the unrelated adjective meaning "fussy."
**With *of*:** No *of* on the determiner. The fixed adverbial *in particular* (*nobody in particular*, *in particular, the second draft*) is a prepositional phrase, not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *a particular* + [singular countable] &middot; *no particular* + [noun] &middot; *this/that particular* + [singular countable] &middot; *any particular* + [singular countable] &middot; *in particular* (fixed adverbial)
**Contrast:** *Particular* and *certain* both narrow a noun to a specific instance, and they split on what they do with the identity. *A certain restaurant* means the speaker knows which one and is not telling; *a particular restaurant* means one specific one, with no implication of concealment. *Particular* also runs freely under *no* and *this*, where *certain* cannot: *no particular reason* is idiomatic and ✗ *no certain reason* is not. Keep the determiner apart from the **adjective** *particular* meaning "fussy, hard to please," which goes after *be*: *she is very particular about her coffee*.
**Variants:** *no particular*, *any particular*, *this particular*, *in particular*, *particularly* (adverb)

Narrows the noun to one specific instance rather than the general class. Under *no* it does the opposite job and cancels the narrowing — *no particular reason* means the reason was not any specific one.

1. I had a *particular* restaurant in mind when I suggested dinner.
2. She left for no *particular* reason that anyone could name.
3. This *particular* case is the one the court will hear first.
4. Put the files back in no *particular* order.
5. Is there any *particular* reason you are asking me this now?
6. That *particular* model was recalled two years ago.
7. These *particular* files were never backed up.
8. He named no *particular* names, but everyone knew who he meant.

**See also:** [certain](#certain), [given](#given), [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [only](#only), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this)

### past

**Pronunciation:** /pæst/ &middot; *PAST*
**Type:** temporal postdeterminer &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Goes with:** singular and plural countable nouns that name a stretch of time or a recurring event — *the past week*, *the past three elections*, *these past few months*. It does **not** take ordinary object nouns (✗ *the past chair*) and does not count uncountables (✗ *the past water*). One separate use escapes the determiner slot: generic attributive *past* before an abstract noun with no article at all — *past experience*, *past performance*, *past behavior*.
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *the past of three weeks*. The noun *the past* is a different thing (*in the past*, *a thing of the past*).
**Pattern:** *the past* + [numeral or quantifier] + [plural time noun] &middot; *the past* + [singular time noun] &middot; *these/this past* + [time noun] &middot; *past* + [abstract noun] (generic, no article)
**Contrast:** *past* and *last* both count backward from now, and in the plural they are interchangeable: *the past three weeks* and *the last three weeks* say the same thing. The difference appears in the singular and with the article. *The past year* means the twelve months just ended, while *last year* with no *the* means the previous calendar year — two different spans. And *last* carries a second sense that *past* never has, "final in a series" (*the last chapter*), so *the past chapter* is not English.
**Variants:** *this past*, *these past*, *in the past*, *past due* (fixed adjective)

Counts a span backward from the present moment, covering the period that runs right up to now rather than a period that ended and closed.

1. The *past* three weeks have been a blur.
2. Sales are up eleven percent over the *past* year.
3. She has been in and out of the hospital these *past* few months.
4. This *past* weekend was the first sunny one all spring.
5. In the *past* decade the neighborhood has changed completely.
6. *Past* performance is no guarantee of future results.

**See also:** [last](07-numerals-multipliers.md#last), [next](07-numerals-multipliers.md#next), [previous, prior, preceding](07-numerals-multipliers.md#previous-prior-preceding), [former, latter](07-numerals-multipliers.md#former-latter), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this)

### respective

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈspɛktɪv/ &middot; *rih-SPEK-tiv*
**Type:** distributive postdeterminer &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** formal and written — business, legal, and academic prose
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *their respective fields*, *the respective departments*, *our respective shares*. Never a singular (✗ *his respective office*) and never an uncountable (✗ *their respective luggage*), because the word's entire job is to hand out one item to each of several possessors, which requires more than one of each. It also needs a determiner in front of it, almost always a possessive: ✗ *Respective owners were notified*.
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *the respective of the totals*.
**Pattern:** [their/our/your/the] + *respective* + [plural noun] &middot; [plural subject] + verb + *to their respective* + [plural noun] &middot; [list] + *respectively* (adverb)
**Contrast:** *respective* distributes where *same* collects. *They went back to their respective offices* means each person went to a different office, one apiece; *They went back to the same office* means one office for everybody. Against *own*, which also follows a possessive, *own* stresses ownership against an outsider (*her own car*) while *respective* stresses the one-to-one matching inside a group. Do not confuse it with *respectful* ("showing respect"), an unrelated adjective.
**Variants:** *respectively* (adverb), *respective owners*, *respective fields*

Assigns one item to each member of a plural set — "each one's own," matched up one to one, with the matching itself being the information the word contributes.

1. After the meeting everyone went back to their *respective* desks.
2. The two companies kept their *respective* logos after the merger.
3. Both authors are leaders in their *respective* fields.
4. The delegates flew home to their *respective* countries on Sunday.
5. Please list the *respective* totals for each quarter.
6. My brother and I inherited our *respective* shares of the house.

**See also:** [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [own](03-possessives.md#own), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [their](03-possessives.md#their)

### selfsame

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɛlfˌseɪm/ &middot; *SELF-same*
**Type:** identity postdeterminer (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** literary and emphatic — rare in speech, a written flourish
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns overwhelmingly (*the selfsame moment*, *the selfsame bench*), occasionally plural countables (*the selfsame words*), and uncountable abstract nouns in stretched literary use (*that selfsame stubbornness*). It requires *the* or a demonstrative in front of it — never *a/an* (✗ *a selfsame day*), because asserting identity presupposes a referent the reader can already find. Never bare: ✗ *Selfsame bench sat there for years*.
**With *of*:** No *of*.
**Pattern:** [the/this/that] + *selfsame* + [noun] &middot; *the very selfsame* + [noun] (double emphasis) &middot; *that selfsame* + [time noun]
**Contrast:** This is *same* with the volume turned up. *The same day* is neutral reporting; *the selfsame day* insists on the coincidence and asks the reader to be struck by it. *Very* does a similar emphatic job (*that very day*), and the two stack into *that very selfsame day*. In ordinary American English *the very same* is the natural phrase and *selfsame* the deliberately bookish one — use it in fiction, not in a memo.
**Variants:** *self-same* (hyphenated spelling), *the very selfsame*, *the very same* (the plain modern equivalent)

Marks two things as not merely alike but numerically one and the same, with the writer stressing how remarkable the coincidence is.

1. They met again on the *selfsame* bench thirty years later.
2. That *selfsame* argument had already cost him one job.
3. She wore the *selfsame* dress to both weddings.
4. At that *selfsame* moment the power went out across the city.
5. He repeated the *selfsame* words his father had used on him.
6. The letter arrived on the *selfsame* day she decided to leave.

**See also:** [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [very](#very), [own](03-possessives.md#own), [that](02-demonstratives.md#that), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this)

### sole

**Pronunciation:** /soʊl/ &middot; *SOHL* &middot; a homophone of *soul* and of *sole* the fish
**Type:** quantifier (uniqueness) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** formal and written — legal, journalistic, and business prose; in speech Americans say *only*
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*the sole heir*, *the sole survivor*) and uncountable abstract nouns (*its sole purpose*, *sole responsibility*). Plural countables are possible but stilted (*the sole survivors*), where *only* is the natural choice. It normally needs *the* or a possessive in front of it — ✗ *a sole survivor* — with one systematic exception: fixed legal collocations take the **zero article** before an uncountable, as in *sole custody*, *sole discretion*, and *sole ownership*.
**With *of*:** No *of* on the determiner. The *of* in *the sole author of the report* belongs to *author*, not to *sole*.
**Pattern:** [the/my/its/our] + *sole* + [noun] &middot; *the sole* + [noun] + *of* + [phrase] &middot; *sole custody / sole discretion / sole proprietor* (fixed, no article)
**Contrast:** *sole* and *only* are one idea in two registers — *the sole survivor* is what the newspaper prints and *the only survivor* is what people say. *Sole* is more absolute and legalistic, and it locks into fixed phrases where *only* would be wrong: ✗ *only custody*, ✗ *at our only discretion*. Do not confuse it with the unrelated nouns *sole* (bottom of a foot or shoe) and *sole* (a flatfish), nor with *solely*, the adverb.
**Variants:** *solely* (adverb), *sole proprietor*, *sole custody*, *sole discretion*, *sole source*

The one and only — chosen over *only* when the exclusivity is the legally or factually load-bearing point of the sentence.

1. She was the *sole* survivor of the crash.
2. The company's *sole* purpose is to license the patent.
3. He was named *sole* executor of the estate.
4. Refunds are issued at our *sole* discretion.
5. Their *sole* source of income is the rent from the garage.
6. She has *sole* custody of both children.

**See also:** [only](#only), [one](07-numerals-multipliers.md#one), [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each)

### very

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɛri/ &middot; *VEH-ree*
**Type:** identity postdeterminer (emphatic) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral, emphatic
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns above all (*the very moment*, *this very room*), plural countable nouns when they form a fixed set (*his very words*, *the very people who complained*), and uncountable abstract nouns (*the very existence of the program*). It requires a **definite** central determiner — *the*, a demonstrative, or a possessive — and never follows *a/an* in this use: ✗ *a very house* meaning "exactly that house."
**With *of*:** No *of*.
**Pattern:** [the/this/that/my] + *very* + [noun] &middot; *the very* + [ordinal or superlative] + [noun] &middot; *the very same* + [noun] &middot; *the very* + [noun] + [relative clause]
**Contrast:** This determiner *very* is a different word from the **adverb** *very*, which intensifies adjectives and adverbs (*very tired*, *very quickly*). What follows tells them apart: a noun means determiner (*the very house*), an adjective means adverb (*a very old house*). Against *same*, the two stack in *the very same day* but differ alone — *very* means "exactly this one and no other," while *same* means "identical to the one already mentioned."
**Variants:** *the very same*, *this very*, *the very first*, *the very best*, *the very idea*

**1.** Marks the noun as exactly the one in question and no other, usually with a note of surprise, irony, or emphasis. **2.** With edge nouns and superlatives it marks the extreme limit — *the very top*, *the very end*, *the very first*.

1. That is the *very* thing I was looking for.
2. We are standing in the *very* room where the treaty was signed.
3. He used my *very* words against me in the meeting.
4. She quit on the *very* day her bonus vested.
5. The *very* idea of moving again exhausts her.
6. Their names were at the *very* bottom of the list.
7. Come here this *very* minute.
8. The *very* existence of the program is now in question.
9. Those were his *very* words, and I wrote them down.

**See also:** [selfsame](#selfsame), [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [only](#only), [first](07-numerals-multipliers.md#first), [this](02-demonstratives.md#this), [that](02-demonstratives.md#that)

### whole

**Pronunciation:** /hoʊl/ &middot; *HOHL* &middot; the *w* is silent; a homophone of *hole*
**Type:** quantifier (totality) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral — the everyday word for totality
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*a whole pizza*, *the whole team*) and uncountable nouns (*the whole truth*, *my whole attention*). With a numeral it can also front a plural time expression treated as one block: *the whole three days*, *the whole ten dollars*. Bare plurals belong to a **different**, adjectival sense meaning "intact, not cut up" — *whole grains*, *whole peppercorns*. It cannot open a phrase with a singular countable: ✗ *Whole cake was gone*.
**With *of*:** **Optional and structural.** *The whole summer* and *the whole of the summer* are both good, and the *of*-version needs a determiner after it in American usage — *the whole of the summer*, where bare *the whole of summer* reads as British. Before a pronoun *of* is **required**: *the whole of it*, never ✗ *the whole it*.
**Pattern:** [the/a/my/this] + *whole* + [noun] &middot; [the] + *whole* + [numeral] + [plural noun] &middot; *the whole of* + [the/my + noun] &middot; *the whole of it* &middot; *a whole nother* (nonstandard)
**Contrast:** *whole* and *entire* mean the same and sit in the same slot, but only *whole* takes an *of*-phrase, and *whole* is the plain word where *entire* is the emphatic one. The slot is what separates *whole* from *all*: *all* is a predeterminer and goes before the central determiner (*all the cake*), *whole* is a postdeterminer and goes after it (*the whole cake*) — hence ✗ *whole the cake* and ✗ *the all cake*. With a plural noun *all* is the only option: *all the students*, never ✗ *the whole students*.
**Variants:** *the whole of*, *a whole lot of* (informal, large quantity), *the whole nine yards*, *a whole nother* (nonstandard)

The complete extent of something with nothing missing — the same idea as *all*, differing only in which slot it occupies. Note the widespread spoken form [*a whole nother*](01-articles.md#a-whole-nother), which splits *another* around *whole*; it is **nonstandard in writing** and very common in American speech, but the written form is *a whole other*. For *whole* as the totality member of the fractions family, see [the whole (of)](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-whole-of).

1. He ate a *whole* pizza by himself.
2. She told me the *whole* story on the drive home.
3. I have lived in this town my *whole* life.
4. The *whole* team showed up to help him move.
5. It rained the *whole* three days we were there.
6. We spent the *whole* of the summer painting the house.
7. That is a *whole* other problem, and we will get to it later.

**See also:** [entire](#entire), [the whole (of)](07-numerals-multipliers.md#the-whole-of), [a whole nother](01-articles.md#a-whole-nother), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [half](07-numerals-multipliers.md#half), [other](07-numerals-multipliers.md#other), [most](04-quantifiers-large.md#most)

## General and indefinite determiners

These words fill the central determiner slot when nothing else claims it — *various reasons*, *certain questions*, *assorted cookies*, *miscellaneous charges*, all with no article. What none of them can do is stand beside a **second** central determiner: ✗ *a various reason*, ✗ *various the reasons*, and *some kind of animal*, never ✗ *some kind of an animal* in careful writing. But most of them are **slot-flexible**: put *the*, a possessive, or a demonstrative in front and they simply drop back into the postdeterminer slot — *the various options*, *his assorted grievances*, *the sundry complaints in the file*, *our diverse staff*, *the miscellaneous items*. Three members are rigid and take the central slot only: *divers*, *suchlike*, and the fixed *of*-phrases *all kinds of*, *all manner of*, and *some kind of*, which swallow the whole slot and admit no determiner on either side. Several of these words form a family with one meaning and five registers — *various* is the current word, *assorted* implies a deliberate mixture, *miscellaneous* is the leftover bin, *sundry* and *divers* are the antiques, and *manifold* is the one that survives chiefly in the King James Bible and the hymnal.

### all kinds of, all sorts of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈkaɪndz əv/, /ˌɔl ˈsɔrts əv/ &middot; *awl KYNDZ uv*, *awl SORTS uv* &middot; *of* reduces to /əv/ or just /ə/
**Type:** quantifier (variety and large number) &middot; **Position:** central determiner (fixed phrase) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, heavily spoken
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*all kinds of birds*, *all sorts of problems*) and uncountable nouns (*all kinds of trouble*, *all sorts of advice*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *all kinds of book*, ✗ *all sorts of mistake*. The phrase occupies the whole central slot, so no article may follow it and none may precede it.
**With *of*:** *Of* is **built into the phrase** and cannot be dropped: ✗ *all kinds problems*. It is not the partitive *of*, so no determiner may follow it: ✗ *all kinds of the problems*. To select from a known set, switch to *all of the problems*.
**Pattern:** *all kinds of* [plural countable] &middot; *all kinds of* [uncountable] &middot; *all sorts of* [plural or uncountable] &middot; *all kinds of* [adjective] + [plural noun]
**Contrast:** Read literally the phrase says "every variety of," but in ordinary American use it has drifted to mean simply "a lot of, and varied" — *we had all kinds of trouble* means plenty of trouble, not trouble of every possible type. *All sorts of* is identical in meaning and marginally more British-sounding; *all manner of* is the formal cousin. Against *what kind of*, which asks a question about type, this phrase asserts quantity and gives up on type.
**Variants:** *all sorts of*, *all types of*, *all manner of*, *all kinds of things*

A large and varied quantity — "many, and of many different kinds," with the variety usually mattering less to the speaker than the sheer amount.

1. There were *all kinds of* birds at the feeder this morning.
2. We ran into *all sorts of* problems with the building permit.
3. He gave me *all kinds of* advice I had not asked for.
4. The store sells *all kinds of* used camera gear.
5. She has had *all sorts of* trouble with that car since she bought it.
6. *All kinds of* people showed up, from students to retirees.

**See also:** [all manner of](#all-manner-of), [some kind of](#some-kind-of), [various](#various), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all), [what kind of](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-kind-of-what-sort-of-what-type-of), [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of)

### all manner of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈmænɚ əv/ &middot; *awl MAN-ur uv*
**Type:** quantifier (variety) &middot; **Position:** central determiner (fixed phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal and literary — the written cousin of *all kinds of*
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*all manner of excuses*) and uncountable nouns (*all manner of mischief*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *all manner of excuse*. Note the fossilized singular inside the phrase — *manner* never pluralizes here: ✗ *all manners of things*, even though *manners* is a perfectly good noun meaning "polite behavior."
**With *of*:** *Of* is part of the phrase and obligatory. No determiner may follow it: ✗ *all manner of the excuses*.
**Pattern:** *all manner of* [plural countable] &middot; *all manner of* [uncountable] &middot; *all manner of* [adjective] + [noun]
**Contrast:** Same meaning as *all kinds of* and *all sorts of*, one register higher — it belongs in essays and reportage rather than conversation. The fossilized singular is the error to watch: *all manner of things* is right and ✗ *all manners of things* is not. Compare the archaic bare *manner of* that survives only in *what manner of man*.
**Variants:** *all manner of things*, *manner of* (archaic, as in *what manner of man*), *all kinds of*, *all sorts of*

Every variety of — a formal or literary way of saying "a great many different kinds of," typically used when the writer wants the list to feel exhaustingly long.

1. The attic was full of *all manner of* forgotten junk.
2. He offered *all manner of* excuses and none of them held up.
3. The treaty was later blamed for *all manner of* troubles.
4. She keeps *all manner of* strange ingredients in the pantry.
5. The report catalogs *all manner of* accounting irregularities.
6. Children get into *all manner of* mischief on a rainy afternoon.

**See also:** [all kinds of, all sorts of](#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of), [various](#various), [sundry](#sundry), [diverse](#diverse), [all](06-distributives-negatives.md#all)

### assorted

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsɔrɾɪd/, carefully /əˈsɔrtɪd/ &middot; *uh-SOR-tid*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** neutral; standard on packaging and in commercial writing
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns almost exclusively** — *assorted cookies*, *assorted sizes*, *assorted complaints*. Uncountables work only when the noun already names a collection (*assorted hardware*). A singular countable is impossible: ✗ *an assorted cookie*. It can open the phrase alone (*assorted chocolates*) or follow a central determiner (*the assorted cables in the drawer*, *his assorted grievances*).
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *assorted of the cookies*. The *of*-construction belongs to the noun: *an assortment of cookies*.
**Pattern:** *assorted* + [plural countable] &middot; [the/his/these] + *assorted* + [plural countable] &middot; *assorted* + [adjective] + [plural noun] &middot; *an assortment of* + [plural or uncountable]
**Contrast:** *Assorted* and *various* both mean "of several different kinds," but *assorted* implies a mixture put together **on purpose** — a box of assorted chocolates was assembled by someone — while *various* says nothing about how the variety arose. Applied to people or problems, *assorted* turns faintly dismissive (*assorted hangers-on*). Because it takes no singular, *an assortment of* is the way to get an article in front.
**Variants:** *an assortment of*, *assorted sizes*, *assorted colors*, *assorted flavors*

Of several different kinds mixed together, usually as a deliberate selection rather than an accident.

1. She brought a tin of *assorted* cookies to the potluck.
2. The drawer was full of *assorted* cables and dead batteries.
3. They sell the shirts in *assorted* colors and sizes.
4. His *assorted* complaints about the neighbors filled two pages.
5. The bin held *assorted* screws, none of them the right length.
6. *Assorted* relatives turned up for the reading of the will.
7. The garage was stacked with *assorted* hardware he would never use.
8. Do they come in *assorted* sizes, or only the one?

**See also:** [various](#various), [miscellaneous](#miscellaneous), [diverse](#diverse), [sundry](#sundry), [all kinds of, all sorts of](#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of)

### certain

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɚtn̩/ &middot; *SUR-tn* &middot; unstressed in the determiner use, with the article in front weakening to /ə/ — *a certain look* /ə ˌsɚtn̩ ˈlʊk/; stressed only when it means "sure"
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (particular but unnamed) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer after *a/an*; central determiner before plurals and uncountables &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly formal, often faintly coy
**Goes with:** all three noun types, but each through a different door. **Singular countable nouns** take it **only with *a/an* in front** — *a certain restaurant*, *a certain look*, *a certain Mr. Alvarez*; never bare, so ✗ *certain man came to the door*. **Plural countable nouns** (*certain questions*, *certain people*) and **uncountable nouns** (*certain evidence*, *certain information*) take it with **nothing in front at all** — the word is the central determiner there, and ✗ *a certain people* and ✗ *a certain information* are both wrong. The one crossing case is *a certain* + **abstract uncountable** noun (*a certain charm*, *a certain restlessness*, *a certain menace*), where the article does not make the noun countable but hedges the quality; that is sense 3 below, and it is the only place *a* and an uncountable noun meet in this entry. It does not follow *the* in any of these senses; *the certain answer* can only mean "the sure answer."
**With *of*:** Mostly none: *certain people*, never ✗ *certain of people*. But formal writing does allow *certain of* before another determiner — *certain of the members objected* — a bookish partitive that many editors avoid in favor of *some of* or *several of*.
**Pattern:** *a certain* + [singular countable] &middot; *certain* + [plural countable] &middot; *certain* + [uncountable] &middot; *a certain* + [proper name] &middot; *certain of* + [the/my + plural noun] (formal) &middot; *a certain amount of* + [uncountable]
**Contrast:** The determiner *certain* is unstressed and means "particular, and I am not naming which"; the **adjective** *certain* is stressed and means "sure." The test is the verb *be*: *I am certain* works for the adjective, but you cannot say ✗ *the people are certain* meaning "the people are particular ones." Against *various*, *certain* points at specific members the speaker could name but declines to, while *various* only reports that there were several kinds. Against *some*, *certain* is more pointed and often insinuating: *certain people should know better* has an accusation in it that *some people should know better* softens.
**Variants:** *a certain amount of*, *a certain number of*, *a certain someone*, *to a certain extent*

**1.** Points at particular members of a set that the speaker knows but chooses not to name — *certain people should know better*. **2.** Before a personal name, marks the person as unknown to the listener — *a certain Dr. Reyes called*. **3.** In *a certain* + abstract noun, hedges a quality the speaker cannot quite pin down — *a certain charm*.

1. *Certain* people should know better than to bring that up.
2. A *certain* Mr. Kowalski has been calling for you all morning.
3. The offer is good only under *certain* conditions.
4. There is a *certain* charm to the old station, run down as it is.
5. *Certain* information was withheld from the final report.
6. A *certain* amount of delay was built into the schedule.
7. *Certain* of the members objected, but the motion carried anyway.
8. She had a *certain* restaurant in mind but would not say which.
9. Are *certain* departments exempt from the freeze, or is it across the board?

**See also:** [various](#various), [given](#given), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), [said](02-demonstratives.md#said), [a](01-articles.md#a)

### divers

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdaɪvɚz/ &middot; *DYE-vurz* &middot; stress on the first syllable, unlike *diverse*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety and number) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** **archaic and legal** — recognize it, do not produce it
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *divers persons*, *divers occasions*, *divers dates*. Never a singular (✗ *divers person*) and never an uncountable (✗ *divers evidence*). In its surviving legal uses it takes the central slot with nothing in front of it and no article after it; the older *his divers offices* pattern, with the word behind a determiner, is period English and not worth reviving.
**With *of*:** No *of*.
**Pattern:** *divers* + [plural countable noun] &middot; *on divers occasions* &middot; *divers and sundry* + [plural noun]
**Contrast:** *Divers* and *diverse* are one word that split in two. *Divers* /ˈdaɪvɚz/ kept the old meaning "several, sundry" and first-syllable stress; *diverse* /dɪˈvɚs/ took the modern meaning "varied, of many kinds" and second-syllable stress. Modern writing uses *diverse*; *divers* survives almost only in indictments (*on divers dates*) and period prose. There is a homograph trap too: *divers* is spelled and pronounced exactly like the plural of *diver*, the person who dives, so only context tells them apart.
**Variants:** *diverse* (the modern form), *divers and sundry*, *sundry*

Several, various — an archaic quantifier now confined to legal boilerplate and historical writing, where it signals age rather than adding meaning.

1. The indictment alleged theft on *divers* occasions between March and July.
2. *Divers* witnesses were called before the grand jury.
3. He had traveled to *divers* countries in the course of his work.
4. The estate was divided among *divers* cousins nobody had ever met.
5. *Divers* complaints were laid before the council that spring.
6. She had, on *divers* dates, signed for packages she never received.

**See also:** [diverse](#diverse), [sundry](#sundry), [various](#various), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several), [said](02-demonstratives.md#said)

### diverse

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈvɚs/, also /daɪˈvɚs/ &middot; *dih-VURSS* &middot; stress on the second syllable, unlike *divers*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; heavily used in institutional and academic prose
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*diverse backgrounds*, *diverse opinions*) and **collective or uncountable nouns** naming a body or a body of work (*a diverse workforce*, *diverse programming*). A singular countable works only when it names a collection — *a diverse team* is fine, ✗ *a diverse book* is not. It opens the phrase alone (*diverse opinions*) or follows a determiner (*the diverse interests of the members*, *our diverse staff*).
**With *of*:** No *of* on the determiner. The construction *a diverse range of* puts the *of* on *range*.
**Pattern:** *diverse* + [plural countable] &middot; [the/our/a] + *diverse* + [collective noun] &middot; *a diverse range of* + [plural noun] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *diverse* (predicative)
**Contrast:** *Diverse* stresses that the members differ from **each other**; *various* only reports that there were several kinds; *several* counts without commenting on variety at all. The grammatical tell is the predicative slot: *the panel is diverse* is perfectly normal, which is why *diverse* is usually classed as an adjective that also does determiner work, whereas ✗ *the panel is various* is not English. Keep it apart from archaic *divers* /ˈdaɪvɚz/, which means "several" and is stressed on the first syllable.
**Variants:** *diversity* (noun), *diversify* (verb), *a diverse range of*, *diversely* (adverb)

Made up of members that differ markedly from one another in origin, kind, or character — the difference among them being the information the word supplies.

1. The committee heard *diverse* opinions before it voted.
2. She works with a *diverse* team spread across four countries.
3. *Diverse* interests pulled the coalition apart within a year.
4. The catalog covers a *diverse* range of subjects, from beekeeping to bookbinding.
5. The city's population is far more *diverse* now than it was in 1990.
6. Their portfolio is deliberately *diverse* so that no one sector can sink it.

**See also:** [divers](#divers), [various](#various), [assorted](#assorted), [miscellaneous](#miscellaneous), [multiple](#multiple), [sundry](#sundry)

### given

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪvən/ &middot; *GIV-un*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (arbitrary instance) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; common in technical, statistical, and sports writing
**Goes with:** **singular countable nouns only** — *any given moment*, *a given category*, *any given Sunday*. Never a plural (✗ *any given days*) and never an uncountable (✗ *a given water*), because the phrase exists to pick out exactly one arbitrary instance. It requires *a* or *any* in front of it and never follows *the* in this sense.
**With *of*:** No *of*.
**Pattern:** *any given* + [singular countable] &middot; *a given* + [singular countable] &middot; *at any given* + [time noun] &middot; *in a given* + [period or category]
**Contrast:** *Any given* and plain *any* both choose freely, but *any given* fixes on one arbitrary instance and then reasons about it: *on any given day about forty people come through* is a claim about a single representative day, while *any day works for me* offers the entire range at once. Do not confuse the determiner with the **preposition** *given* meaning "considering" (*Given the weather, we should leave early*), nor with the **noun** *a given* meaning "something taken for granted" (*That's a given*).
**Variants:** *any given*, *at any given time*, *at any given moment*, *on any given day*

Any one instance you care to pick, considered on its own as a representative stand-in for all the others.

1. On any *given* day about forty people come through the clinic.
2. At any *given* moment there are roughly ten thousand planes in the air.
3. In a *given* month she will drive maybe six hundred miles.
4. Any *given* Sunday, either team could win that game.
5. The rule applies to all employees in a *given* pay grade.
6. Only three cases are open at any *given* time.

**See also:** [any](06-distributives-negatives.md#any), [any old](06-distributives-negatives.md#any-old), [certain](#certain), [each](06-distributives-negatives.md#each), [every](06-distributives-negatives.md#every), [whatever](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#whatever)

### manifold

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmænəfoʊld/ &middot; *MAN-uh-fohld* &middot; first-syllable stress and a full /oʊ/ in the last syllable, as in *twofold*; the middle vowel is a schwa that can drop out entirely in fast speech, /ˈmænfoʊld/. Being a content word it has **no weak form of its own** — what reduces is the determiner in front of it, as in *the manifold blessings* /ðə ˈmænəfoʊld ˈblɛsɪŋz/ and *his manifold talents* /ɪz ˈmænəfoʊld ˈtælənts/
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety and number) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** **archaic, literary, and scriptural** — recognize it, and produce it only when the King James sound is what you are after
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*manifold reasons*, *manifold blessings*) and **abstract uncountable nouns** naming something that comes in kinds (*manifold wisdom*, *manifold grace*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *a manifold reason*. It opens the phrase with no article at all (*manifold difficulties*) or drops back behind a determiner (*the manifold failures of the agency*, *his manifold talents*). It also sits **predicatively** after *be* — *the benefits are manifold* — which is both the tell that it is an adjective doing determiner work, in the way *diverse* is, and its commonest surviving use in modern American writing.
**With *of*:** No *of* — ✗ *manifold of the reasons*. The partitive goes through *many of* or *several of*. The unrelated **noun** *a manifold* (the exhaust manifold of an engine, a manifold in topology) carries its own article and its own *of*: *the manifold of a six-cylinder engine*.
**Pattern:** *manifold* + [plural countable] &middot; *manifold* + [abstract uncountable] &middot; [the/his/its] + *manifold* + [plural noun] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *manifold* (predicative — the live modern use) &middot; *a manifold* (noun, unrelated)
**Contrast:** *Manifold* belongs to the variety family with *various*, *sundry*, *divers*, and *diverse*, and it is the most elevated of them: *various reasons* is what you write, *manifold reasons* is what a hymn sings. **Where an American actually meets it:** the King James Bible (*the manifold wisdom of God*, Ephesians 3:10; *manifold temptations*, 1 Peter 1:6), the hymns and prayers built on that vocabulary, nineteenth-century prose, and the one formula that survives intact in ordinary speech and business writing, *the benefits are manifold*. Against *myriad*, which stresses sheer number, *manifold* stresses **kinds** — many, and folded over into many sorts. Against *multiple*, which is flat and clinical, it is warm and elevated, which is why an incident report says *multiple injuries* and a eulogy says *his manifold kindnesses*. It is built like the multipliers *twofold* and *tenfold* but does not count: ✗ *sales rose manifold* is not the way to say *sales rose tenfold*. Do not misread it as *manifest*, an unrelated word.
**Variants:** *manifoldly* (rare adverb), *a manifold* (noun: engine part, mathematical object), *multifarious*, *myriad*, *manifest* (unrelated look-alike)

Many and of many kinds at once — the old word for a number that is large because the varieties are. In front of a noun in ordinary modern prose it reads as deliberate period costume; after *be* it is still perfectly current.

1. The committee's failures were *manifold* and well documented.
2. He thanked the congregation for its *manifold* kindnesses that winter.
3. *Manifold* difficulties delayed the expedition for the whole of that summer.
4. The King James translators wrote of the *manifold* wisdom of God, and the phrase has stuck.
5. Her *manifold* talents were wasted on a job that only ever asked her to file invoices.
6. The benefits of the merger are *manifold*, and the costs all sit in one column.
7. The old prayer book gives thanks for God's *manifold* mercies.
8. The shop had to order a new exhaust *manifold*, which is a different word altogether.

**See also:** [various](#various), [divers](#divers), [diverse](#diverse), [sundry](#sundry), [multiple](#multiple), [assorted](#assorted), [myriad](04-quantifiers-large.md#myriad), [numerous](04-quantifiers-large.md#numerous), [twofold, threefold, tenfold](07-numerals-multipliers.md#twofold-threefold-tenfold)

### miscellaneous

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɪsəˈleɪniəs/ &middot; *miss-uh-LAY-nee-us*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (leftover variety) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the standard label on forms, budgets, and inventories
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*miscellaneous charges*, *miscellaneous parts*) and **uncountable nouns** naming an undifferentiated mass (*miscellaneous debris*, *miscellaneous paperwork*). A singular countable is odd unless it names a collection: ✗ *a miscellaneous receipt*. It opens the phrase alone (*miscellaneous expenses*) or follows a determiner (*the miscellaneous items at the back of the shelf*).
**With *of*:** No *of* on the determiner. The noun *miscellany* carries it: *a miscellany of odds and ends*.
**Pattern:** *miscellaneous* + [plural countable] &middot; *miscellaneous* + [uncountable] &middot; [the] + *miscellaneous* + [noun] &middot; *a miscellany of* + [plural noun]
**Contrast:** The variety words differ in what they imply about how the collection came to be. *Assorted* is a deliberate mixture; *various* is a neutral several-kinds; *diverse* stresses that the members differ; and *miscellaneous* is the **leftover bin** — things grouped only because they fit nowhere else. That is exactly why it is the standard heading for the last line of a budget and why ✗ *a miscellaneous team* makes no sense where *a diverse team* does.
**Variants:** *misc.* (abbreviation), *a miscellany of*, *miscellanea*, *miscellaneously* (adverb)

Of assorted kinds with no organizing principle — the catch-all category for whatever is left over after the real categories have been filled.

1. File the receipts under *miscellaneous* expenses.
2. The box held *miscellaneous* parts from three different bikes.
3. She spent the whole afternoon on *miscellaneous* paperwork.
4. *Miscellaneous* charges accounted for a third of the bill.
5. The last drawer is for *miscellaneous* junk we cannot classify.
6. The *miscellaneous* items at the back of the shelf had been there for years.

**See also:** [assorted](#assorted), [various](#various), [sundry](#sundry), [diverse](#diverse), [multiple](#multiple), [other](07-numerals-multipliers.md#other)

### multiple

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌltəpəl/ &middot; *MUL-tuh-pul* &middot; the middle syllable reduces to a schwa or drops entirely in fast speech: /ˈmʌltəpl̩/
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (number above one) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** neutral; the default word in journalism, medical writing, and incident reports
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *multiple reasons*, *multiple witnesses*, *multiple times*. Never a singular countable (✗ *a multiple reason*, ✗ *multiple witness*) and **never an uncountable** (✗ *multiple advice*, ✗ *multiple information*, ✗ *multiple evidence*), which is the error to watch, since the meaning tempts writers into it. For an uncountable you must go through a countable head: *multiple pieces of evidence*, *multiple forms of aid*, *multiple rounds of funding*. It opens the phrase alone (*multiple complaints*) or follows a central determiner (*the multiple failures of the system*, *his multiple injuries*). **Agreement:** plural verb, always — *Multiple witnesses **were** called*, never ✗ *was called*.
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare noun — ✗ *multiple of the witnesses* is not English. The partitive goes through *several of* or *a number of*: *several of the witnesses*. The mathematical *a multiple of six* is the **noun** *multiple*, a different word that carries its own *of*.
**Pattern:** *multiple* + [plural countable] &middot; [the/his/these] + *multiple* + [plural countable] &middot; *multiple* + [adjective] + [plural noun] &middot; *multiple* + [countable head] + *of* + [uncountable] &middot; *a multiple of* + [number] (noun, unrelated)
**Contrast:** *Multiple*, *several*, and *various* split three ways. *Several* counts loosely — more than two, fewer than many. *Various* adds that the members differed in kind. *Multiple* says only "more than one" and says it flatly, with no estimate and no flavor, which is exactly why reporting and clinical prose reach for it: *multiple injuries*, *multiple sources*, *multiple offenses*. It is also the one of the three that sits comfortably behind a determiner in ordinary modern prose — *the multiple failures* is unremarkable, while *the several failures* survives only in legal English, where *the several* means "the respective." Keep the determiner apart from the **noun** *multiple* (*a multiple of twelve*) and from the compound *multiple-choice*.
**Variants:** *multiply* (adverb and verb), *multiplicity* (noun), *multiple-choice* (compound), *a multiple of* (noun)

More than one, stated without warmth or estimate — the plain modern word for a count the speaker wants on the record but does not want to characterize.

1. *Multiple* witnesses described the same car leaving the lot.
2. She gave *multiple* reasons for leaving, and none of them was money.
3. The building failed *multiple* inspections before it was condemned.
4. He has been treated for *multiple* injuries since the accident.
5. The *multiple* failures that night were all traced to one relay.
6. Were there *multiple* offers on the house, or just the one?
7. The report rests on *multiple* pieces of evidence, not a single document.
8. Do not send *multiple* copies of the same form.

**See also:** [various](#various), [diverse](#diverse), [assorted](#assorted), [respective](#respective), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several), [numerous](04-quantifiers-large.md#numerous), [a number of](05-quantifiers-small.md#a-number-of)

### some kind of

**Pronunciation:** /səm ˈkaɪnd əv/ &middot; *sum KYND uv* &middot; *some* takes its weak form /səm/ here and is never the stressed /sʌm/
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (vague type) &middot; **Position:** central determiner (fixed phrase) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, heavily spoken
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns **with their article absorbed** (*some kind of animal*, *some kind of mistake*) and uncountable nouns (*some kind of trouble*, *some kind of oil*). For a plural the whole phrase pluralizes: *some kinds of birds never migrate*. The form *some kind of a mistake*, with an extra *a* wedged in after *of*, is common in casual American speech but is **nonstandard in writing** — drop the *a*.
**With *of*:** *Of* is built into the phrase and cannot be dropped: ✗ *some kind mistake*. It is not partitive, so no determiner follows it: ✗ *some kind of the animal*.
**Pattern:** *some kind of* + [singular countable, no article] &middot; *some kind of* + [uncountable] &middot; *some sort of* + [noun] &middot; *some kinds of* + [plural noun]
**Contrast:** *Some kind of* hedges the **type** of the thing — *some kind of bird* means it is a bird but I cannot say which kind. Plain *some* hedges its **identity or amount** — *some bird* means a bird, and I am not saying which one. The question form *what kind of* asks for the type instead of giving up on it. Keep the determiner apart from the adverbial hedge *kind of* (*kind of tired*, reduced to *kinda*), which modifies adjectives and verbs, not nouns.
**Variants:** *some sort of*, *some type of*, *some kinds of*, *some kind of a* (criticized in writing)

Marks the noun as belonging to a category the speaker cannot or will not specify — "a something-or-other of this general type," with the vagueness openly admitted.

1. There is *some kind of* animal living under the porch.
2. It looked like *some kind of* mistake on the invoice.
3. He is in *some kind of* trouble with the bank again.
4. They put *some kind of* oil on it to stop the squeaking.
5. She is *some sort of* consultant, but nobody knows for what.
6. *Some kinds of* birds never migrate at all.
7. Is this *some kind of* joke, or are you serious?
8. It was not *some kind of* accident — somebody unplugged it.

**See also:** [all kinds of, all sorts of](#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of), [certain](#certain), [any old](06-distributives-negatives.md#any-old), [some](05-quantifiers-small.md#some), [what kind of](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#what-kind-of-what-sort-of-what-type-of), [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of)

### suchlike

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌtʃlaɪk/ &middot; *SUCH-lyke*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (similarity) &middot; **Position:** central determiner &middot; **Register:** **dated**; more British than American — recognize it, do not reach for it
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns (*suchlike creatures*, *suchlike tools*) and uncountable nouns (*suchlike nonsense*, *suchlike talk*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *a suchlike creature*. It takes no article in front of it, and it far more often stands **alone as a pronoun** at the end of a list: *swords, shields, and suchlike*.
**With *of*:** No *of*.
**Pattern:** *suchlike* + [plural or uncountable noun] &middot; [list] + *and suchlike* (standing alone) &middot; *or suchlike*
**Contrast:** *Suchlike* is *such* plus *-like*, and in the determiner slot it means much the same thing, "of that sort." The everyday American equivalents are *that kind of* and the trailing *and things like that*. Two differences from *such* matter: *such* is fully current in American English while *suchlike* is not, and *such* takes an article in the singular (*such a day*) while *suchlike* never does.
**Variants:** *such like* (older two-word spelling), *and suchlike*, *or suchlike*, *and such*

Of that sort — used to gesture at the rest of a category rather than spell it out, usually with a touch of impatience toward the things being waved away.

1. He had no patience for horoscopes, crystals, and *suchlike* nonsense.
2. The shed was full of rakes, hoes, and *suchlike* tools.
3. She never believed in ghosts or *suchlike* creatures.
4. They talked about mortgages, insurance, and *suchlike* dull matters.
5. Bring rope, tarps, and *suchlike* — whatever you would take camping.
6. *Suchlike* talk was common enough in the village at the time.

**See also:** [such](08-interrogative-exclamative-relative.md#such), [all kinds of, all sorts of](#all-kinds-of-all-sorts-of), [sundry](#sundry), [this kind of](02-demonstratives.md#this-kind-of)

### sundry

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌndri/ &middot; *SUN-dree*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** **formal and dated**, sometimes jocular; alive in accounting and in the fixed phrase *all and sundry*
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns** (*sundry documents*, *sundry relatives*) and uncountable nouns naming a mass of odds and ends (*sundry paperwork*). Never a singular countable: ✗ *a sundry document*. It opens the phrase alone (*sundry expenses*) or follows a determiner (*the sundry complaints in the file*).
**With *of*:** No *of*. In the fossilized phrase *all and sundry* the word is a **pronoun** meaning "everybody," not a determiner.
**Pattern:** *sundry* + [plural countable] &middot; [the] + *sundry* + [plural noun] &middot; *all and sundry* (fixed pronoun) &middot; *sundries* (noun, "small miscellaneous items")
**Contrast:** *Sundry*, *various*, *assorted*, and *miscellaneous* all mean "of several kinds," and only register and flavor separate them: *various* is neutral and current, *assorted* implies a deliberate mixture, *miscellaneous* is the leftover bin, and *sundry* is the old-fashioned bookkeeper's word. It pairs with the equally archaic *divers* in the legal doublet *divers and sundry*.
**Variants:** *sundries* (plural noun), *all and sundry*, *divers and sundry*

Various, of several sorts — a dated word for a mixed handful of small items, still standard in bookkeeping where *sundry expenses* is a real line on a ledger.

1. The invoice lists *sundry* charges that nobody can explain.
2. She kept receipts, stamps, and *sundry* odds and ends in the top drawer.
3. *Sundry* relatives arrived over the course of the weekend.
4. Post the small amounts to *sundry* expenses.
5. He answered questions from reporters, neighbors, and *sundry* passersby.
6. The invitation went out to all and *sundry*.
7. There is *sundry* paperwork still waiting on your desk.
8. The auditors could not tie *sundry* expenses to any invoice.

**See also:** [various](#various), [divers](#divers), [assorted](#assorted), [miscellaneous](#miscellaneous), [diverse](#diverse)

### various

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɛriəs/ &middot; *VAIR-ee-us*
**Type:** general/indefinite determiner (variety and number) &middot; **Position:** central determiner, or postdeterminer after one &middot; **Register:** neutral — the current, unmarked member of its family
**Goes with:** **plural countable nouns only** — *various reasons*, *various people*, *the various options*. Never a singular countable (✗ *a various reason*) and **never an uncountable** (✗ *various information*, ✗ *various advice*), which is the commonest error with this word. For uncountables you must go through a countable head: *various kinds of evidence*, *various forms of aid*.
**With *of*:** No *of* before a bare noun. Formal writing does allow *various of* before another determiner — *various of the delegates walked out* — but it is rare and many editors dislike it; *several of the delegates* is the safe alternative.
**Pattern:** *various* + [plural countable] &middot; [the/its/these] + *various* + [plural countable] &middot; *various kinds of* + [plural or uncountable] &middot; *various* + [adjective] + [plural noun]
**Contrast:** *Various* says two things at once — there were several, and they differed. *Several* gives the number without the variety; *diverse* stresses the difference more strongly and can follow *be* (*the group is diverse*), which *various* barely tolerates (*his reasons were various* sounds literary, and ✗ *the group is various* is not English). Against *certain*, *various* is open about the variety and vague about which ones, while *certain* points at particular ones and declines to name them.
**Variants:** *various kinds of*, *variously* (adverb), *variety* (noun), *various and sundry*

Several, and of more than one kind — the ordinary modern word for a mixed handful, and the default choice whenever *sundry*, *divers*, or *assorted* would sound wrong.

1. He gave *various* reasons for quitting, none of them convincing.
2. The *various* departments each keep their own records.
3. *Various* people have asked me the same question this week.
4. The drug is sold under *various* brand names.
5. She has worked in *various* capacities at the museum since 2011.
6. The report cites *various* kinds of evidence, most of it circumstantial.

**See also:** [diverse](#diverse), [divers](#divers), [assorted](#assorted), [sundry](#sundry), [miscellaneous](#miscellaneous), [multiple](#multiple), [certain](#certain), [several](05-quantifiers-small.md#several)

## Look-alikes that are not determiners

Some words stand exactly where a postdeterminer stands and are still not determiners, and the same two tests separate them every time: a determiner cannot be **graded**, and it cannot be turned into a **predicate** after *be* with its meaning intact. Anything that passes both tests is describing the noun rather than picking it out, which makes it an adjective.

- ***current*, *relevant*, *applicable*, *appropriate*, *pertinent*.** *The current tenant*, *the relevant section*, and *the applicable rate* look like *the present case*, but each of these words grades freely (*more current data*, *the most relevant example*, *the least applicable of the three rules*) and each goes after *be* without changing meaning (*Is your passport current?*, *That section is not relevant*, *The surcharge is applicable only to new accounts*). *Applicable* even takes a postnominal slot no determiner can reach: *the rate applicable to new accounts*. The genuine article in that neighborhood is [the present](02-demonstratives.md#the-present), which does neither — ✗ *a more present chapter*, and *the chapter is present* says something else entirely — and *the current* is already carried there as a variant of it. Contract English leans on *the applicable* and *the relevant* so hard (*the Applicable Rate*, *the relevant Party*) that they start to feel like articles; a defined term in a contract is still an adjective plus a noun.
- ***the like of*, *the likes of*.** *We will never see the likes of him again*; *a storm the like of which nobody remembered*. This one fails a different test: it never stands in front of a bare noun at all. ✗ *the likes of problems* is impossible, because *like* and *likes* are the **head noun** of their own phrase and *of* + X is the complement, with X normally definite (*the likes of him*, *the likes of Amazon*, *the like of which*). The whole string is a noun phrase, not a determiner on one. For the determiners that do that job, see [suchlike](#suchlike) and [all manner of](#all-manner-of).
