# Demonstrative determiners

> The four pointing words — *this*, *that*, *these*, *those* — that place a noun near the speaker or away from it, in space, in time, or in the conversation itself.

[← The grammar of determiners](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Demonstratives are the pointing determiners: where an article says only whether the hearer can pick the noun out, a demonstrative locates it — near the speaker or away from the speaker, in the room, in time, or in the text that came before. English runs a two-way system, near *this/these* against far *that/those*, crossed with number, and these are the only determiners in the language that inflect for it — *much/many* and *little/few* are two separate words apiece, but *this → these* and *that → those* are one word changing form. That number contrast is the trap the whole file runs on: *this* and *that* take singular countable nouns **and** uncountable nouns (*this furniture*, *that advice*), while *these* and *those* take plural countable nouns and nothing else, which makes ✗ *these furniture* and ✗ *this shoes* the two commonest demonstrative errors in English. The second trap is that every word here is also a pronoun — the determiner leans on a noun (*this book*), the pronoun stands alone (*this is mine*).

**A note on position.** All four demonstratives are **central** determiners, and English allows exactly one central determiner per noun phrase. That single fact predicts a whole family of impossible strings: ✗ *the this book*, ✗ *this my car*, ✗ *a that idea*, ✗ *that each week* — article, possessive, and demonstrative are all competing for one slot. There are two ways around it. Possession moves behind the noun into the double genitive (*this car of yours*, *that dog of his*), and the **predeterminers** *all*, *both*, and *half* sit in front of the central slot rather than in it, which is why *all these years*, *both those windows*, and *half that amount* are perfectly ordinary. Numerals and other postdeterminers stack behind the demonstrative instead: *these three files*, *those first two days*.

**A note on stress.** Determiners are normally unstressed, and most of them have a weak form that a learner has to be taught to hear: *a* /ə/, *an* /ən/, *the* /ðə/ before a consonant sound and /ði/ before a vowel sound, *some* /səm/, *your* /jɚ/, *our* /ɑr/. Demonstratives are the exception. They are pointing words, they carry stress, and they resist the reduction that flattens the articles — which is why the pronunciation lines below give full vowels. Two forms qualify the rule. The nonstandard *them* of *them apples* does reduce, to /ðəm/, but only because it is the object pronoun repurposed, weak form and all. And an unstressed *this* can slip toward /ðəs/ in fast casual speech (*this morning*), the one reduction the standard demonstratives allow — and even that vanishes the moment the word is doing real pointing work. The reduction most worth learning here, though, is not a weak demonstrative at all but the trap next door: demonstrative *that* is always /ðæt/, while the relative and complementizer *that* — a different word wearing the same spelling — reduces to /ðət/ and can usually be deleted outright (*the car (that) I bought*).

## The near set: *this* and *these*

The proximal pair. Both point at what is near the speaker — near in the room, near in the present, or near in the part of the conversation currently open. The only thing that separates them is number, and it is a hard boundary: *this* takes singular countables and uncountables, *these* takes plural countables and nothing else.

### these

**Pronunciation:** /ðiz/ &middot; *THEEZ*  
**Русский:** эти — ближний указательный определитель, только перед мн. числом (✗ these furniture)
**Type:** demonstrative (also a demonstrative pronoun: *these are mine*) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *these chairs*, *these people*, *these two weeks*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *these chair*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *these furniture*, ✗ *these information*, ✗ *these advice*); for an uncountable, use *this* (*this furniture*) or a counter (*these pieces of furniture*). *These wines*, *these cheeses*, *these coffees* are fine only because the noun has been recounted as "kinds of" or "servings of."  
**With *of*:** None as a determiner — ✗ *these of the books*. A partitive has to get its *of* from a quantifier standing in front: *both of these books*, *several of these*, *two of these*. Nor does the pronoun take one: the idiom is *those of us / those of you*, never ✗ *these of us*.  
**Pattern:** *these* [plural countable noun] &middot; *these* [number] [plural noun] (*these three files*) &middot; [predeterminer] *these* [plural noun] (*all these years*, *both these windows*) &middot; *these* [plural noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] (*these shoes of mine*) &middot; *these* standing alone as a pronoun (*I'll take these*)  
**Contrast:** Two axes cross here. Number: *these* is the plural of *this*, so *this* handles singular countables and uncountables while *these* handles plural countables and nothing else. Distance: *these* points at what is near the speaker in space, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the talk currently in hand (*these days*, *these numbers*), where *those* points at what is away, past, or finished (*those days*). Determiner vs pronoun: the determiner leans on a noun (*these seats are taken*), the pronoun stands alone and is itself the whole noun phrase (*these are taken*, *I'll take these*, *with these*). In dialect speech *these* is also replaced by *them* (*them apples*), which is nonstandard.  
**Variants:** *these here* (nonstandard emphatic), *them* (nonstandard, for both these and those)

Points at more than one thing near the speaker — in the room, in the present stretch of time, or in the part of the discussion currently in hand.

**1. In space.** The things pointed at are close to the speaker, or closer than a contrasting set: *these seats, not those.*

**2. In time.** *These days*, *these last few months*, *these next two weeks* — a plural period that contains, or is about to contain, the present moment.

**3. In discourse.** Refers to plural items just mentioned, or about to be listed: *these three problems*, *these figures*.

**4. Narrative *these* (informal).** The plural of the storytelling *this*, introducing participants the hearer has never heard of, where the grammar calls for a bare plural: *So these two guys knock on my door at midnight.*

1. *These* shoes are killing me.
2. Are *these* seats taken?
3. *These* days I'm up before five.
4. I don't recognize *these* charges on my statement.
5. *These* two chapters are the hardest in the book.
6. *These* wines all come from the same vineyard.
7. So *these* two guys knock on my door at midnight asking about the car.

### this

**Pronunciation:** /ðɪs/, casual /ðəs/ before a stressed noun &middot; *THIS*  
**Русский:** этот, эта, это — ближний указательный определитель: ед. ч. и неисчисляемые
**Type:** demonstrative (also a demonstrative pronoun: *this is mine*) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*this chair*) and uncountable nouns (*this water*, *this advice*, *this furniture*). Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *this books*, ✗ *this shoes* → *these books*, *these shoes*. Two apparent exceptions are worth knowing, and neither breaks the rule. The degree phrase *this many* (*this many people*) has *this* modifying the quantifier *many*, not the noun. And a plural **measure** phrase counted as a single block takes *this* and a singular verb — *This five dollars is all I have*, *This last three weeks has been brutal* — the same singular-unit logic that gives *Ten dollars is a lot*. Outside those two, a plural noun after *this* is an error.  
**With *of*:** None — ✗ *this of the wine*. And because *this* is a central determiner it cannot stack with an article or a possessive (✗ *the this book*, ✗ *this my car*), so possession is added behind the noun instead, in the double genitive: *this friend of mine*, *this car of yours*.  
**Pattern:** *this* [singular countable noun] &middot; *this* [uncountable noun] &middot; *this* [time noun] (*this morning*, *this Friday*, *this past week*, *this coming spring*) &middot; [predeterminer] *this* [noun] (*all this trouble*, *half this amount*) &middot; *this* [noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] &middot; *this much* [uncountable] / *this many* [plural countable] &middot; *this* [adjective/adverb] (degree: *this big*, *this late*)  
**Contrast:** *this* vs *that* is proximity: *this* is near the speaker in space, in time, or in attention; *that* is away, finished, or belongs to the hearer's side of the conversation. *this* vs *these* is number, and it is the commonest demonstrative error there is — *this* takes singular countables and uncountables, *these* takes plural countables only. Determiner vs pronoun: the determiner leans on a noun (*this report is late*), the pronoun stands alone (*this is late*).  
**Variants:** *this here* (nonstandard emphatic), *this'n* (dialectal pronoun, = this one)

Points at a single thing — or at a mass of something — that is near the speaker: near in the room, near in time, or near in the flow of the conversation.

**1. In space.** Nearer to the speaker than the alternative: *this chair*, the one I am touching, against *that chair* across the room.

**2. In time.** The period containing the present moment: *this morning*, *this week*, *this year*. American English sharpens it two ways: *this past Tuesday* is the one most recently gone by, *this coming Tuesday* the one ahead. Bare *this Tuesday* is genuinely ambiguous, which is why speakers repair it so often.

**3. In discourse.** *This* points forward to what the speaker is about to say (*Listen to this:*) and back to whatever is still live in the conversation (*This is why I called.*). Careful writers avoid a bare *this* whose antecedent is a whole clause and hang a noun on it instead: not *This surprised everyone* but *This decision surprised everyone*.

**4. Narrative *this* (informal).** In spoken storytelling, *this* introduces a brand-new participant the listener has never heard of, in the slot where the grammar calls for *a*: *So this guy walks up to me…* Ordinary in casual speech, out of place in formal writing.

**5. As a degree word (informal to neutral).** In front of an adjective or adverb, *this* means "to the degree I am showing or describing": *I didn't know it was this bad. Hold your hands this far apart.*

1. *This* seat is taken, but the one behind it is free.
2. *This* coffee has been on the burner all morning.
3. Is *this* water safe to drink?
4. I'll call you *this* afternoon.
5. *This* advice came straight from our attorney.
6. I don't need *this* aggravation on a Monday.
7. Read *this* paragraph again before you sign anything.
8. *This* past Tuesday we lost power for six hours.
9. *This* five dollars is all I have until payday.
10. So *this* guy walks up to me and asks to see my badge.
11. I didn't know the drive was going to be *this* long.

## The far set: *that* and *those*

The distal pair, split by number on exactly the same line as *this/these*. Beyond physical distance they carry two extra jobs the near pair does not: pointing back at something already said and finished, and holding a referent at arm's length in a way that often reads as judgment (*that dog of yours*).

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt/ (never reduced as a demonstrative) &middot; *THAT*  
**Русский:** тот, та, то — дальний указательный определитель: ед. ч. и неисчисляемые
**Type:** demonstrative (also a demonstrative pronoun, *that is mine* — and a wholly separate word as the relative and complementizer *that*, as in *the car that broke down*, *I know that she left*) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns (*that box*) and uncountable nouns (*that noise*, *that money*, *that information*). Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *that boxes* → *those boxes*. The apparent exception, *that many people*, has *that* modifying the quantifier *many*.  
**With *of*:** None as a determiner — ✗ *that of the money*. The pro-form *that of* is a different construction and is fully standard in comparisons, where *that* replaces a noun already named: *The climate of Houston is milder than that of Chicago* (= than the climate of Chicago). Possession goes behind the noun in the double genitive: *that dog of yours*.  
**Pattern:** *that* [singular countable noun] &middot; *that* [uncountable noun] &middot; *that* [time noun] (*that summer*, *that year*) &middot; [predeterminer] *that* [noun] (*all that noise*, *half that amount*) &middot; *that* [noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] &middot; *that much* [uncountable] / *that many* [plural countable] &middot; *not* … *that* [adjective] (degree: *not that expensive*) &middot; *that same* [noun]  
**Contrast:** *that* vs *this* is distance — away versus near, in space, in time, or in the speaker's attention. *that* vs *those* is number: singular countable and uncountable versus plural countable. Determiner vs demonstrative pronoun: the determiner needs a noun to lean on (*that answer was wrong*), while the pronoun stands alone and can take a whole preceding clause as its antecedent (*He resigned by text. That was a mistake.*). The harder split is *that* the determiner against *that* the relative pronoun and complementizer, which are different words wearing the same spelling: the determiner keeps a full vowel /ðæt/, carries stress, and can be swapped for *this* (*that book* → *this book*); the relative and the complementizer reduce to /ðət/ and can usually just be deleted (*I know (that) she left*, *the car (that) I bought*).  
**Variants:** *that there* (nonstandard emphatic), *that same* (with anaphoric *same*), *yon, yonder* (archaic/regional, for what is far from both speakers)

Points at a single thing, or at a mass, that is away from the speaker — across the room, back in the past, or already dealt with in the conversation.

**1. In space.** Farther from the speaker, or nearer the hearer: *Hand me that wrench.* A gesture or a glance normally goes with it.

**2. In time.** A stretch of time cut off from now: *that summer*, *that winter*, *that year we lived in Denver*.

**3. In discourse (anaphoric).** Refers back to something already said and treated as closed: *He resigned by text. That decision followed him for years.* Standing alone as a pronoun, *that* can take a whole preceding clause as its antecedent — *He resigned by text. That was a mistake.*

**4. Shared knowledge, and attitude.** *That* marks a referent the speaker assumes the hearer can already call to mind: *You know that guy from accounting?* Combined with a possessive it usually carries a judgment: *that dog of yours*.

**5. As a degree word (informal to neutral).** In front of an adjective, an adverb, or *much/many*, *that* means "to the degree just mentioned": *It's not that expensive. I didn't know she was that young.* It lives mainly in negatives and questions; the affirmative *It was that bad* is informal.

1. *That* building on the corner used to be a bank.
2. Hand me *that* wrench, would you?
3. Is *that* information still accurate?
4. I've never forgotten *that* summer.
5. *That* decision cost the company millions.
6. Who made all *that* noise last night?
7. I don't want *that* responsibility.
8. *That* dog of yours dug up my tulips again.
9. It's not *that* expensive — about forty dollars.
10. We ran into her again *that* same afternoon.

**See also:** [this](#this), [those](#those), [that much](#that-much), [that many](#that-many), [that which](#that-which), [that there](#that-there), [self](#self), [selfsame](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame), [the](01-articles.md#the)

### those

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊz/ &middot; *THOHZ*  
**Русский:** те — дальний указательный определитель, только перед мн. числом
**Type:** demonstrative (also a demonstrative pronoun: *those are mine*) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *those boxes*, *those people*, *those three years*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *those box*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *those furniture*, ✗ *those luggage*, ✗ *those homework*). *Those* can also head a noun phrase with no noun in it at all, and two different things go on when it does. As a plain demonstrative pronoun it points at something already identifiable in the situation and needs nothing after it: *Those are mine. I'll take those.* In the generic sense "the ones" or "the people," a relative clause is obligatory: *those who wait*, *those we lost* — never a bare ✗ *Those are rewarded* meaning "people are rewarded."  
**With *of*:** None as a determiner — ✗ *those of the boxes* meaning "those boxes." As a pronoun, though, *those of* is standard in two patterns: the partitive *those of us / those of you / those of the applicants who…*, and the comparative pro-form *Prices are higher than those of last year* (*those* = the prices).  
**Pattern:** *those* [plural countable noun] &middot; *those* [number] [plural noun] (*those first two days*) &middot; [predeterminer] *those* [plural noun] (*all those years*, *both those windows*) &middot; *those* [plural noun] *of* [possessive pronoun] (*those shoes of hers*) &middot; *those who/that/which* [relative clause] &middot; *those of* [us/you/them/the + noun]  
**Contrast:** *those* is the plural of *that* and the far counterpart of *these*: *these files* are the ones here and now, *those files* the ones over there or back then. Because it is plural-only, it cannot cover an uncountable the way *that* can — *that furniture*, never ✗ *those furniture*. Determiner vs pronoun: the determiner sits on a noun (*those boxes are heavy*), the pronoun replaces the noun phrase outright (*those are heavy*). And *those* is the standard form where dialect speech uses *them*: *them apples* is nonstandard for *those apples*.  
**Variants:** *those there* (nonstandard emphatic), *them, them there* (nonstandard)

Points at more than one thing away from the speaker — across the room, back in the past, or already settled in the conversation.

**1. In space.** Farther from the speaker than a contrasting set, or simply out of reach: *Those boxes go in the garage.*

**2. In time.** A plural period cut off from now: *back in those days*, *those years after the mill closed*.

**3. In discourse (anaphoric).** Refers to plural items already mentioned: *The 2019 numbers were wrong, and those figures are still on the website.*

**4. As a bare head with a relative clause.** *Those* alone means "the people" or "the ones," and requires the relative clause to complete it: *those who arrived early*, *those that still work*.

**5. As a pro-form after a comparison.** Standing in for a repeated noun: *Our margins are thinner than those of our competitors.*

1. *Those* boxes go in the garage.
2. Back in *those* days, gas was under a dollar.
3. I can't read *those* road signs from here.
4. Are *those* your keys on the counter?
5. All *those* years of practice finally paid off.
6. *Those* people you met at the wedding are my cousins.
7. Today's prices are nearly double *those* of ten years ago.

## Demonstratives of degree and quantity

*this* and *that* also work as degree words, and their commonest landing spot is directly in front of *much* and *many*. The countability rule carries straight through the compound: *much* keeps its uncountables, *many* keeps its plural countables, and the demonstrative only says whether the amount is being shown (*this*) or referred back to (*that*).

### that many

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðæt ˌmɛni/ &middot; *THAT-men-ee*  
**Русский:** столько (штук) — «так много, как названо»; чаще в отрицаниях и вопросах
**Type:** demonstrative degree word + quantifier &middot; **Position:** central (the quantifier *many* fills the slot; *that* is a degree modifier in front of it) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *that many people*, *that many copies*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *that many money*, ✗ *that many time* → *that much money*, *that much time*. It also stands alone as a pronoun (*I didn't think there were that many*).  
**With *of*:** *that many of the / of my / of these* + plural noun, and *that many of them* with a pronoun, where *of* is required: *We don't need that many of them.* Before a bare plural noun there is no *of*: *that many chairs*, not ✗ *that many of chairs*.  
**Pattern:** *that many* [plural countable noun] &middot; *that many of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *that many of* [them/us/you] &middot; *not / never* … *that many* [plural noun] &middot; *that many* standing alone (*half that many*)  
**Contrast:** *that many* is to plural countables what *that much* is to uncountables — the same split as *many/much*. Against *this many*: *this many* shows the amount, usually with a gesture or with the amount in plain sight, while *that many* points back at a figure already named or already in the hearer's mind. Like the bare degree word *that*, it prefers negatives and questions; the affirmative *There were that many* is informal.  
**Variants:** *not that many*, *half that many*, *twice that many*

Names a number by pointing at one already mentioned or already known — "as many as that." Most at home under a negative or in a question, where it denies or queries a number the other person has implied.

1. I didn't invite *that many* people — maybe twelve.
2. Are there really *that many* flights a day out of Midway?
3. We don't have *that many* options left.
4. *That many* signatures would take a month to collect.
5. I had never seen *that many* deer in one field.
6. Only about half *that many* showed up.

**See also:** [that much](#that-much), [this many](#this-many), [that](#that), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### that much

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðæt ˌmʌtʃ/ &middot; *THAT-much*  
**Русский:** столько (о неисчисляемом) — «так много, как названо»; чаще в отрицаниях и вопросах
**Type:** demonstrative degree word + quantifier &middot; **Position:** central (the quantifier *much* fills the slot; *that* is a degree modifier in front of it) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *that much time*, *that much salt*, *that much trouble*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *that much people* → *that many people*. It also stands alone as a pronoun (*It didn't cost that much*) and modifies a comparative adjective or adverb (*that much better*, *that much sooner*).  
**With *of*:** *that much of the / of my / of this* + noun, and *that much of it* with a pronoun, where *of* is required: *I wouldn't spend that much of it on one repair.* Before a bare uncountable noun there is no *of*: *that much flour*, not ✗ *that much of flour*.  
**Pattern:** *that much* [uncountable noun] &middot; *that much of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *that much of* [it/them] &middot; *not* … *that much* [uncountable] &middot; *that much* [comparative] (*that much harder*) &middot; *that much* standing alone  
**Contrast:** *that much* takes uncountables, *that many* takes plural countables — the *much/many* line, unchanged. Against *this much*: *this much* displays the amount ("about this much"), *that much* refers back to an amount already stated. Bare *that much* also has an idiomatic concessive use meaning "at least that part": *That much I understood.*  
**Variants:** *not that much*, *half that much*, *twice that much*, *that much more/better/harder*

Names an amount of something uncountable by pointing at one already mentioned or already known — "as much as that." Like the degree word *that*, it lives mainly in negatives and questions, and in front of a comparative it means "by that margin": *the new tires made the ride that much smoother.*

1. It doesn't cost *that much* — I checked this morning.
2. I didn't drink *that much* coffee. Two cups.
3. *That much* I understood; the rest lost me.
4. She really spent *that much of* her savings on one trip?
5. If you cut the budget *that much*, we'll lose the program.
6. New tires made the ride *that much* smoother.

**See also:** [that many](#that-many), [this much](#this-much), [that](#that), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much)

### this many

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɪs ˌmɛni/ &middot; *THIS-men-ee*  
**Русский:** вот столько (штук) — количество, которое видно или показывают жестом
**Type:** demonstrative degree word + quantifier &middot; **Position:** central (the quantifier *many* fills the slot; *this* is a degree modifier in front of it) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *this many people*, *this many boxes*. Never an uncountable noun: ✗ *this many traffic*, ✗ *this many information* → *this much traffic*, *this much information*. It also stands alone as a pronoun (*I've never seen this many*).  
**With *of*:** *this many of the / of my / of these* + plural noun, and *this many of them* with a pronoun, where *of* is required: *Do we really need this many of them?* Before a bare plural noun there is no *of*: *this many chairs*.  
**Pattern:** *this many* [plural countable noun] &middot; *this many of* [the/my/these + plural noun] &middot; *this many of* [them/us] &middot; *never* … *this many* [plural noun] &middot; *this many* standing alone  
**Contrast:** *this many* counts, *this much* measures — the plain *many/much* split carried up into the demonstrative. Against *that many*: *this many* presents a number that is visible, present, or being demonstrated right now, while *that many* points back to a number already named. It is the main environment in which *this* legally stands in front of a plural noun, and even there *this* is modifying *many*, not the noun; the only other one is a plural measure counted as a single block (*this five dollars*, with a singular verb).  
**Variants:** *about this many*, *twice this many*

Names a number by showing it rather than stating it — the number in front of us, or the one the speaker is indicating with a gesture. It is most natural in exclamations, questions, and negatives, where the speaker is reacting to a count that turned out larger than expected.

1. I've never seen *this many* people at a school board meeting.
2. Why did you print *this many* copies?
3. We didn't budget for *this many* guests.
4. *This many* applicants means we'll need a second reviewer.
5. Do we really need *this many of* the small screws?
6. I can carry about *this many* books before my arm gives out.

**See also:** [this much](#this-much), [that many](#that-many), [this](#this), [many](04-quantifiers-large.md#many)

### this much

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɪs ˌmʌtʃ/ &middot; *THIS-much*  
**Русский:** вот столько (о неисчисляемом) — количество, которое показывают жестом
**Type:** demonstrative degree word + quantifier &middot; **Position:** central (the quantifier *much* fills the slot; *this* is a degree modifier in front of it) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** uncountable nouns only — *this much time*, *this much flour*, *this much money*. Never a plural countable noun: ✗ *this much people* → *this many people*. It also stands alone as a pronoun (*I've only got this much*) and heads the fixed opener *This much is clear:*  
**With *of*:** *this much of the / of my / of this* + noun, and *this much of it* with a pronoun, where *of* is required: *I've read this much of the report.* Before a bare uncountable noun there is no *of*: *this much flour*, not ✗ *this much of flour*.  
**Pattern:** *this much* [uncountable noun] &middot; *this much of* [the/my/this + noun] &middot; *this much of* [it/them] &middot; *this much* standing alone (*that's all I need — this much*) &middot; *This much is clear/certain:* [clause]  
**Contrast:** *this much* measures, *this many* counts. Against *that much*: *this much* shows the amount — fingers apart, a line on a glass, a stack on the desk — while *that much* refers back to an amount already named. Note the fixed discourse formula *This much is clear:*, where *this much* means "this part of the matter, at least."  
**Variants:** *about this much*, *This much is clear/certain* (fixed opener)

Names an amount of something uncountable by showing it rather than stating it — the amount indicated by a gesture, or the amount visibly on hand.

1. I've only got *this much* time before my next call.
2. Add *this much* flour, then stop.
3. He wants *this much* money for a used mower?
4. I've read *this much of* the report and I'm already worried.
5. You can pour *this much* and no more.
6. *This much* is clear: nobody read the contract.

**See also:** [this many](#this-many), [that much](#that-much), [this](#this), [much](04-quantifiers-large.md#much), [little](05-quantifiers-small.md#little)

## Demonstratives with *kind*, *sort*, and *type*

A frame in which the demonstrative determines a classifier noun and the *of*-phrase carries the noun being classified. All four demonstratives take it — *this/that kind of* with a singular head, *these/those kinds of* with a plural one — and the near/far contrast survives inside the frame: *this kind of* for the category at hand, *that kind of* for one already named or held at a distance, very often disapprovingly. The rule that governs the whole frame is number agreement, and the mixed *these kind of things* is the single most-corrected demonstrative error in American editing.

### that kind of

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt ˈkaɪnd əv/ &middot; *that-KYND-uv*  
**Русский:** такого рода, такой — о категории уже названной; часто с оттенком осуждения
**Type:** demonstrative + classifier noun (partitive frame) &middot; **Position:** central — *that* determines the head noun *kind*, and the *of*-phrase carries the noun being classified &middot; **Register:** neutral, but frequently evaluative  
**Goes with:** after *of*, a bare singular countable noun (*that kind of engine*) or an uncountable noun (*that kind of behavior*, *that kind of weather*). A plural after the singular frame — *that kind of engines* — is dispreferred in edited American English; keep everything singular (*that kind of engine*) or pluralize the whole frame (*those kinds of engines*). The verb agrees with the singular head *kind*: *that kind of mistake is expensive*.  
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory: ✗ *that kind car* → *that kind of car*. The article in *that kind of a car* is informal and regional and is edited out of American writing. Putting *the* after *of* (*that kind of the problem*) makes the phrase specific and defeats the classifying sense. The reduced *kinda* is speech only.  
**Pattern:** *that kind of* [singular countable noun] &middot; *that kind of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *that sort of / that type of* [noun] &middot; [subject] *that kind of* [noun] + singular verb &middot; *that kind of thing* (fixed, summarizing)  
**Contrast:** Against *this kind of*: the same frame at the other end of the deictic scale — *this kind of* names the category in front of us, *that kind of* one already introduced or held at arm's length, and the distancing is what gives *that kind of* its habitual note of disapproval (*that kind of language*, *that kind of person*). Against *those kinds of*: number, and the frame has to move as a unit — *that kind of problem* or *those kinds of problems*, never the mixed ✗ *those kind of problems*. Against plain *that*: *that problem* points at one particular problem, *that kind of problem* at the whole category it belongs to.  
**Variants:** *that sort of*, *that type of*, *that kind of a* (informal/regional), *that kinda* (speech only)

Classifies a thing by the category it belongs to rather than pointing at the individual, with the category set at a distance — already named, already known to the hearer, or deliberately held away from the speaker. In American speech that distancing is very often a judgment.

1. We don't tolerate *that kind of* behavior here.
2. *That kind of* mistake is expensive to fix later.
3. I've never driven *that kind of* truck.
4. *That sort of* remark doesn't belong in a staff meeting.
5. Is *that kind of* coverage included in the basic plan?
6. He isn't *that kind of* person, whatever the neighbors say.
7. *That type of* loan carries a prepayment penalty.

**See also:** [this kind of](#this-kind-of), [those kinds of](#those-kinds-of), [these kinds of](#these-kinds-of), [that](#that)

### these kinds of

**Pronunciation:** /ðiz ˈkaɪndz əv/ &middot; *theez-KYNDZ-uv*  
**Русский:** такого рода (мн. ч.) — о категориях, о которых идёт речь сейчас
**Type:** demonstrative + classifier noun (partitive frame) &middot; **Position:** central — *these* determines the head noun *kinds*, and the *of*-phrase carries the noun being classified &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** a plural countable noun after *of* — *these kinds of injuries*, *those kinds of delays*. An uncountable after *of* is possible but rare and usually rewritten with the singular frame (*this kind of weather*, not *these kinds of weather*). Everything in the phrase agrees as a plural: *these kinds of errors are common*, with a plural verb.  
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory: ✗ *these kinds problems* → *these kinds of problems*. Adding an article after *of* (*these kinds of the problems*) makes the phrase specific and is usually wrong for the classifying sense.  
**Pattern:** *these/those kinds of* [plural countable noun] &middot; *these/those sorts of* [plural noun] &middot; *these/those types of* [plural noun] &middot; [subject] *these kinds of* [plural noun] + plural verb  
**Contrast:** The number agreement is the whole point: *this kind of problem* (all singular) and *these kinds of problems* (all plural) are the two clean patterns. The mixed *these kind of problems* — plural demonstrative, singular *kind* — has been in the language since Shakespeare and is still ordinary in speech, but American editors treat it as an error; fix it by making everything singular or everything plural. *Type* is the most technical of the three nouns, *sort* the most British-sounding, *kind* the everyday American choice.  
**Variants:** *these sorts of*, *these types of*, *those kinds of*, *those sorts of*, *those types of*, *these kind of* (nonstandard/disputed)

Classifies plural things by category rather than pointing at individuals: not these particular injuries but injuries of the sort just indicated. The demonstrative locates the category in the discussion — *these* for the type under discussion now, *those* for a type already dealt with or held at a distance.

1. *These kinds of* injuries take months to heal.
2. *These sorts of* questions come up in every audit.
3. *These types of* accounts earn almost no interest.
4. We see *these kinds of* errors whenever the form is filled out by hand.
5. Do *these kinds of* delays show up in the quarterly report?
6. Contractors don't warranty *these kinds of* materials.
7. *These kinds of* repairs are never cheap.

**See also:** [this kind of](#this-kind-of), [those kinds of](#those-kinds-of), [that kind of](#that-kind-of), [these](#these), [those](#those)

### this kind of

**Pronunciation:** /ðɪs ˈkaɪnd əv/ &middot; *this-KYND-uv*  
**Русский:** такой, такого рода — классифицирует по виду, а не указывает на предмет
**Type:** demonstrative + classifier noun (partitive frame) &middot; **Position:** central — *this* determines the head noun *kind*, and the *of*-phrase carries the noun being classified &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Goes with:** after *of*, a bare singular countable noun (*this kind of engine*) or an uncountable noun (*this kind of weather*, *this kind of behavior*). A plural after the singular frame — *this kind of engines* — is dispreferred in edited American English; either drop to the singular (*this kind of engine*) or pluralize the whole frame (*these kinds of engines*).  
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory: ✗ *this kind car* → *this kind of car*. Inserting an article after *of* (*this kind of a car*) is informal and regional, and is edited out of American writing. The reduced *kinda* is speech only.  
**Pattern:** *this/that kind of* [singular countable noun] &middot; *this/that kind of* [uncountable noun] &middot; *this/that sort of* [noun] &middot; *this/that type of* [noun] &middot; [subject] *this kind of* [noun] + singular verb  
**Contrast:** Keep the number consistent across the frame: *this kind of problem* or *these kinds of problems*, never the mixed ✗ *these kind of problems*. Against a plain demonstrative: *this problem* points at one particular problem, *this kind of problem* points at the whole category it belongs to. Against [*that kind of*](#that-kind-of): the same frame at a distance, and usually with a note of dismissal (*We don't tolerate that kind of behavior*).  
**Variants:** *this sort of*, *this type of*, *that kind of*, *that sort of*, *that type of*, *this kind of a* (informal/regional)

Classifies a thing by category instead of pointing at the individual: not this engine but an engine of the sort indicated. The demonstrative says which category — *this* for the one at hand, *that* for one already named, frequently with a note of disapproval.

1. *This kind of* weather always knocks out the power.
2. I've never worked on *this kind of* engine before.
3. *This sort of* mistake is easy to catch in review.
4. *This kind of* thing happens every spring.
5. Does *this kind of* policy cover flood damage?
6. *This type of* account earns almost no interest.
7. We don't see *this kind of* wear on a newer machine.

**See also:** [these kinds of](#these-kinds-of), [that kind of](#that-kind-of), [those kinds of](#those-kinds-of), [this](#this), [that](#that)

### those kinds of

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊz ˈkaɪndz əv/ &middot; *thohz-KYNDZ-uv*  
**Русский:** такого рода (мн. ч.) — о категориях уже названных или отдалённых
**Type:** demonstrative + classifier noun (partitive frame) &middot; **Position:** central — *those* determines the head noun *kinds*, and the *of*-phrase carries the noun being classified &middot; **Register:** neutral, but frequently evaluative  
**Goes with:** a plural countable noun after *of* — *those kinds of delays*, *those kinds of arguments*. An uncountable after *of* is possible but rare and is normally rewritten with the singular frame (*that kind of weather*, not *those kinds of weather*). Everything in the phrase agrees as a plural, the verb included: *those kinds of errors are common*.  
**With *of*:** *of* is obligatory: ✗ *those kinds problems* → *those kinds of problems*. Adding an article after *of* (*those kinds of the problems*) makes the phrase specific and kills the classifying sense.  
**Pattern:** *those kinds of* [plural countable noun] &middot; *those sorts of* [plural noun] &middot; *those types of* [plural noun] &middot; [subject] *those kinds of* [plural noun] + plural verb  
**Contrast:** Against *these kinds of*: distance rather than number — *these kinds of* for the categories under discussion now, *those kinds of* for ones already dealt with or held away, which is why *those kinds of* so readily carries dismissal (*those kinds of people*). Against *that kind of*: number, and the frame moves as a unit — *that kind of delay* or *those kinds of delays*, never the mixed ✗ *those kind of delays*, which is ordinary in speech but is treated as an error in American editing. Against plain *those*: *those delays* points at particular delays, *those kinds of delays* at the categories they belong to.  
**Variants:** *those sorts of*, *those types of*, *those kind of* (nonstandard/disputed)

Classifies plural things by the categories they belong to rather than pointing at individuals, with the category set at a distance — already named, already known, or deliberately held away from the speaker.

1. *Those kinds of* delays cost us contracts.
2. *Those kinds of* arguments never convince anybody.
3. *Those sorts of* rumors take years to die.
4. Are *those kinds of* fees still legal?
5. We don't hire for *those kinds of* roles anymore.
6. *Those types of* engines were phased out in the nineties.
7. *Those kinds of* mistakes are why we have a checklist.

**See also:** [these kinds of](#these-kinds-of), [that kind of](#that-kind-of), [this kind of](#this-kind-of), [those](#those)

## Fused constructions: the demonstrative as head

In these patterns the demonstrative takes no noun at all — it heads the noun phrase itself and a relative clause completes it. They are demonstratives functioning as pronouns, listed here because learners meet them as fixed units and because the *who / which / that* choice inside them is not free.

### that which

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *that-WICH*  
**Русский:** то, что — книжн., формальный эквивалент what
**Type:** fused relative — demonstrative pronoun *that* + relative *which* &middot; **Position:** not a determiner: *that* is the head of the noun phrase here, not a modifier of a following noun &middot; **Register:** formal, literary; slightly archaic  
**Goes with:** no noun at all — *that which* takes no head noun and cannot be followed by one (✗ *that which book*). What follows is a relative clause. It refers to things and abstractions, never to people; for people the fused form is *those who* (or *he who* in older prose). The verb agreeing with it is singular: *that which remains is ours*.  
**With *of*:** None. The look-alike *that of* is a different construction, in which *that* is a pro-form standing in for a noun already named: *a climate like that of Florida*.  
**Pattern:** *that which* [relative clause] &middot; [verb] *that which* [relative clause] &middot; *that which* [relative clause] + singular verb &middot; *all that which* (rare, emphatic)  
**Contrast:** Modern American English says *what*: *that which cannot be measured* = *what cannot be measured*. Choose *that which* only for deliberate formality or aphorism. Against *those who*: *that which* is singular and refers to things, *those who* is plural and refers to people. Against the ordinary determiner *that*: the determiner needs a noun after it (*that book*), while *that which* stands on its own as a whole noun phrase.  
**Variants:** *what* (the ordinary modern equivalent), *those which* (plural), *he who / they that* (archaic, of persons)

Means "the thing that" — a formal, written equivalent of *what*, used when a writer wants the weight of the demonstrative behind an abstraction.

1. *That which* does not kill us makes us stronger.
2. He spent his life wanting *that which* he could not have.
3. The report separates *that which* can be measured from *that which* is merely claimed.
4. Keep *that which* is good, and let the rest go.
5. We are judged by *that which* we leave behind.
6. *That which* the committee approved bears no resemblance to the draft.

**See also:** [those who](#those-who), [those which](#those-which), [that](#that)

### those which

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊz ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thohz-WICH*  
**Русский:** те, которые — выделяет подмножество из уже названного
**Type:** fused relative — demonstrative pronoun *those* + relative *which* (or *that*) &middot; **Position:** not a determiner: *those* is the head of the noun phrase, not a modifier of a following noun &middot; **Register:** formal (*those which*); neutral (*those that*)  
**Goes with:** no noun — a relative clause follows instead (✗ *those which boxes*). It refers to things; *those that* can stretch to people in careful prose, but *those who* is the normal choice there. The verb is plural: *those that remain are damaged*.  
**With *of*:** None in this construction. The partitive *those of the applicants that…* inserts *of* before a noun phrase and is a different pattern.  
**Pattern:** *those which* [relative clause] &middot; *those that* [relative clause] &middot; [preposition] *those which* [relative clause] &middot; *only those that* [relative clause] &middot; *those* [relative clause with the relative omitted] (*those we lost*)  
**Contrast:** *those which* is the plural of *that which*, and *those that* is its everyday American equivalent — *which* sounds legal or literary here, *that* sounds normal. Against *those who*: *which/that* for things, *who* for people. The relative may be dropped when it is the object of its clause (*those we lost*), but not when it is the subject (✗ *those remain are damaged*).  
**Variants:** *those that*, *the ones that* (informal), *that which* (singular)

Means "the ones that" — picks out a subset of things already under discussion and defines it by the relative clause that follows.

1. Of all the proposals, we funded only *those which* had community support.
2. Discard *those that* are cracked or warped.
3. The rule applies to *those which* were signed before June.
4. I kept *those that* still fit and donated the rest.
5. Species like *those which* live in caves tend to lose their pigment.
6. Only *those that* pass inspection get a sticker.

**See also:** [that which](#that-which), [those who](#those-who), [those](#those)

### those who

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊz ˈhu/ &middot; *thohz-HOO*  
**Русский:** те, кто — называет людей по признаку, а не по имени
**Type:** fused relative — demonstrative pronoun *those* + relative *who* &middot; **Position:** not a determiner: *those* heads the noun phrase and takes no noun after it &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Goes with:** no noun — ✗ *those who people*. It refers to people, always plural, and the relative clause is obligatory: *those who arrive late* is complete, while bare *those* meaning "people" is not. The verb inside the relative clause is plural (*those who were waiting*), and so is the verb of the main clause (*those who wait are rewarded*).  
**With *of*:** *of* appears in the partitive expansions *those of us who*, *those of you who*, *those of them who*, where *of* is required before a pronoun: *Those of us who commute know the 8:10 is always late.* The verb still agrees with *those*, so it stays plural.  
**Pattern:** *those who* [relative clause] + plural verb &middot; *those of us/you/them who* [relative clause] &middot; [preposition] *those who* [relative clause] &middot; *one of those* [plural noun] *who* [plural verb] &middot; *those* [reduced relative] (*those left behind*)  
**Contrast:** *those who* is for people, *those which / those that* for things, and *that which* is the singular of both in formal prose. Against the determiner *those*: the determiner sits on a noun (*those people*), the fused form replaces the noun entirely. One agreement trap comes with it: in *one of those people who…*, the relative clause describes *people*, so the verb is plural — *He's one of those people who always show up early*, not ✗ *who always shows up early*.  
**Variants:** *those that* (of people, less common), *people who* (plainer), *he who / they who* (archaic), *those of us who*

Means "the people who" — names a group not by who they are but by what is true of them, which is why it turns up in rules, proverbs, and announcements.

1. *Those who* arrive after seven will be seated at intermission.
2. The scholarship is meant for *those who* need it most.
3. *Those who* can, do.
4. *Those of us who* commute know the 8:10 is never on time.
5. He's one of *those* people *who* never answer email.
6. *Those who* disagreed said nothing at the meeting.

**See also:** [those](#those), [those which](#those-which), [that which](#that-which)

## Nonstandard emphatics and dialect forms

Every one of these is **nonstandard**. They are listed because a learner will hear them constantly — in fiction, film, country music, and everyday speech across large parts of the United States — and needs to parse them, not because they should ever be produced. Two features recur: *there* or *here* moved in front of the noun instead of behind it, and object-pronoun *them* used in place of *those*.

### that there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈðɛr/ &middot; *that-THAIR*  
**Русский:** вон тот, этот вот — прост./диал. усиление that; значение то же самое
**Type:** demonstrative + reinforcing adverb (nonstandard emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central — *that there* fills a single central-determiner slot &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; rural and Southern American speech, dialect writing, and jocular imitation  
**Goes with:** the same nouns as plain *that*: singular countable nouns (*that there truck*) and uncountable nouns (*that there coffee*). Never a plural countable noun — the plural forms are the equally nonstandard *those there* and *them there*.  
**With *of*:** None, exactly as with *that*.  
**Pattern:** *that there* [singular countable noun] &middot; *that there* [uncountable noun] &middot; *that there* standing alone as a pronoun (*that there's the problem*) — all nonstandard  
**Contrast:** Standard English says the same thing with the adverb behind the noun: *that truck there*, *that book there*. Moving *there* in front of the noun is what makes the phrase nonstandard, so *that there truck* is dialect while *that truck there* is ordinary English. The near counterpart is *this here*. Learners should recognize the form — it is everywhere in fiction, country lyrics, and comedy — and not produce it.  
**Variants:** *that'ere*, *'at there* (eye-dialect spellings), *those there* / *them there* (plural)

A dialect intensifier of *that*: the pointing is reinforced by tucking the adverb *there* between the determiner and the noun. It adds emphasis and a strong regional flavor, nothing more — the meaning is exactly that of *that*.

1. *That there* wrench is the only one that fits. (nonstandard)
2. *That there* truck hasn't run since '98. (nonstandard)
3. You'll want to move *that there* ladder before the wind picks up. (nonstandard)
4. *That there* coffee's been sitting since dawn. (nonstandard)
5. I bought *that there* rifle at an estate sale. (nonstandard)
6. *That there*'s the man I told you about. (nonstandard, pronoun use)

**See also:** [this here](#this-here), [those there](#those-there), [them there](#them-there), [that](#that)

### them

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛm/, weak /ðəm/ before the noun &middot; *THEM, weak thuhm*  
**Русский:** те, эти — прост./диал. вместо those; в норме them — объектная форма they
**Type:** demonstrative (nonstandard); in standard English *them* is only the object form of the pronoun *they* &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; widespread in rural, Southern, Appalachian, and working-class American speech, and in dialect writing  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *them apples*, *them boys*, *them tools*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *them apple*) and never an uncountable noun; it stands in for both *those* and *these*, so it carries no near/far contrast of its own.  
**With *of*:** None as a determiner. In standard English *of them* is the pronoun (*two of them*, *all of them*), which is a different thing entirely and perfectly correct.  
**Pattern:** *them* [plural countable noun] &middot; *them there* [plural noun] (doubly emphatic) &middot; [verb] *them* [plural noun] — all nonstandard  
**Contrast:** The standard equivalent is *those* (or *these*): *those apples*, not ✗ *them apples*. The distinction to hold onto is that *them* before a noun is nonstandard, while *them* after a verb or preposition is the ordinary object pronoun and completely standard — *I ate them* is correct English, *I ate them apples* is not. The form is old rather than careless. *The* and *those* come down from the Old English demonstrative *sē / þæt / þā*, while *they, them, their* were borrowed from Old Norse *þeir, þeim* — but the Norse words grew from the same Germanic demonstrative root, so *them* in front of a noun is a demonstrative going back to demonstrative work, not a random slip.  
**Variants:** *them there* (intensified), *them's* (dialect, = those are)

The nonstandard plural demonstrative determiner: it does the job of *those* (and sometimes *these*) in front of a plural noun. Learners need to recognize it, because it is one of the most frequent markers of dialect speech in American fiction, film, and song — but it should never be produced in writing or in careful speech.

1. Hand me *them* pliers. (nonstandard)
2. *Them* apples came off our own tree. (nonstandard)
3. *Them* boys have been up to something. (nonstandard)
4. One of *them* days, I'll finish the barn. (nonstandard)
5. We put *them* tomatoes up in jars every August. (nonstandard)
6. I haven't seen *them* people since the reunion. (nonstandard)

**See also:** [those](#those), [these](#these), [them there](#them-there), [those there](#those-there)

### them there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛm ˈðɛr/ &middot; *them-THAIR*  
**Русский:** вон те — резко сниженное прост./диал.; по значению просто those
**Type:** nonstandard demonstrative + reinforcing adverb &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; strongly marked rural and Southern American speech, and the stock phrase of dialect comedy  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only — *them there hills*, *them there apples*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *them there hill*) and never an uncountable noun.  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *them there* [plural countable noun] — nonstandard throughout  
**Contrast:** It doubles two nonstandard features at once: *them* in place of *those*, and *there* wedged in front of the noun instead of behind it. The standard rendering keeps the adverb after the noun: *those hills over there*. Compare the singular *that there*, and the *these*-based *these here*.  
**Variants:** *them thar* (eye-dialect: *them thar hills*), *that there* (singular)

The most heavily marked demonstrative in American English: nonstandard *them* reinforced by a preposed *there*. It is almost never used neutrally — in print it signals dialect, in speech it is often deliberate humor.

1. *Them there* apples are ready to pick. (nonstandard)
2. There's gold in *them there* hills. (nonstandard; a fixed catchphrase)
3. Watch out for *them there* potholes on the county road. (nonstandard)
4. *Them there* dogs bark all night. (nonstandard)
5. I keep *them there* tools in the shed. (nonstandard)
6. You can't drive over *them there* tracks in a low car. (nonstandard)

**See also:** [them](#them), [that there](#that-there), [those there](#those-there), [those](#those)

### these here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðiz ˈhɪr/ &middot; *theez-HEER*  
**Русский:** вот эти — прост./диал. усиление these; меняется только регистр
**Type:** demonstrative + reinforcing adverb (nonstandard emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; rural, Southern, and Appalachian American speech, and dialect writing  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only, exactly like plain *these* — *these here boots*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *these here boot*) and never an uncountable noun (✗ *these here water*).  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *these here* [plural countable noun] &middot; *these here* standing alone as a pronoun (*these here are mine*) — nonstandard  
**Contrast:** Standard English puts the adverb after the noun: *these boots here*. The singular counterpart is *this here*; the far counterparts are *those there* and *them there*. Recognize it, do not write it.  
**Variants:** *these'ere* (eye-dialect spelling)

A dialect intensifier of *these*, with the adverb *here* moved in front of the noun to reinforce the pointing. The meaning is identical to plain *these*; only the register changes.

1. *These here* tomatoes came out of my own garden. (nonstandard)
2. *These here* boots have another season in them. (nonstandard)
3. Put *these here* boxes in the truck. (nonstandard)
4. *These here* young folks don't remember the flood. (nonstandard)
5. I got *these here* nails at the feed store. (nonstandard)
6. *These here* are the papers you wanted. (nonstandard, pronoun use)

**See also:** [this here](#this-here), [those there](#those-there), [these](#these)

### this here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɪs ˈhɪr/ &middot; *this-HEER*  
**Русский:** вот этот — прост./диал. усиление this; значение то же, что у this
**Type:** demonstrative + reinforcing adverb (nonstandard emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; rural, Southern, and Appalachian American speech, dialect writing, and joking imitation  
**Goes with:** the same nouns as plain *this*: singular countable nouns (*this here wrench*) and uncountable nouns (*this here coffee*). Never a plural countable noun — the plural is the equally nonstandard *these here*.  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *this here* [singular countable noun] &middot; *this here* [uncountable noun] &middot; *this here* standing alone as a pronoun (*this here's my brother*) — nonstandard  
**Contrast:** The standard version of the same emphasis puts *here* behind the noun: *this wrench here*, *this book here*. That postnominal *here* is ordinary English; the preposed one is not. The far counterpart is *that there*, the plural is *these here*.  
**Variants:** *this'ere* (eye-dialect spelling), *this here now* (intensified, rare)

A dialect intensifier of *this*: the adverb *here* is squeezed in between the determiner and the noun to sharpen the pointing. It means nothing more than *this*, but it marks the speaker's variety unmistakably.

1. *This here* wrench is the only one that fits. (nonstandard)
2. *This here* dog won't hunt. (nonstandard)
3. I've had *this here* truck since I got out of the Army. (nonstandard)
4. You put *this here* box on top. (nonstandard)
5. *This here* coffee's been on the burner since dawn. (nonstandard)
6. *This here*'s my brother's place. (nonstandard, pronoun use)

**See also:** [that there](#that-there), [these here](#these-here), [this](#this)

### those there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðoʊz ˈðɛr/ &middot; *thohz-THAIR*  
**Русский:** вон те — прост./диал. усиление those; значение то же
**Type:** demonstrative + reinforcing adverb (nonstandard emphatic) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; rural and Southern American speech and dialect writing  
**Goes with:** plural countable nouns only, like plain *those* — *those there hills*. Never a singular countable noun (✗ *those there hill*) and never an uncountable noun.  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *those there* [plural countable noun] &middot; *those there* standing alone as a pronoun — nonstandard  
**Contrast:** Standard English keeps the adverb behind the noun: *those hills over there*. *Those there* is less common than *them there* in dialect speech, because speakers who preposition *there* usually use *them* for the determiner as well. The near counterpart is *these here*, the singular is *that there*.  
**Variants:** *them there* (more frequent in the same dialects)

A dialect intensifier of *those*, with *there* moved forward in front of the noun. Same meaning as *those*, different register entirely.

1. *Those there* hills were all farmland once. (nonstandard)
2. Hand me *those there* pliers. (nonstandard)
3. *Those there* cattle belong to my uncle. (nonstandard)
4. I don't trust *those there* new machines. (nonstandard)
5. *Those there* windows leak every winter. (nonstandard)
6. We store *those there* jars down in the cellar. (nonstandard)

**See also:** [them there](#them-there), [these here](#these-here), [that there](#that-there), [those](#those)

## Archaic and regional: the lost third distance

English once had a three-way deictic system — *this* by me, *that* by you, *yon* over there beyond us both, much as Spanish still keeps *este / ese / aquel*. Only the *yon* family survives, and it survives unevenly: as determiners all three forms are archaic, while the adverb *over yonder* is ordinary regional American speech. Note that none of them inflects for number.

### yon

**Pronunciation:** /jɑn/ &middot; *YAHN*  
**Русский:** вон тот (вдали) — устар./книжн.; третья, дальняя ступень указания
**Type:** demonstrative (distal, third-degree — neither near the speaker nor near the hearer) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic; alive in poetry, hymns, historical fiction, and some Appalachian and Ozark speech  
**Goes with:** singular or plural countable nouns without any change of form — *yon hill*, *yon hills*. This is its most striking feature: unlike *this/these* and *that/those*, it does not inflect for number. Uncountables are possible but rare (*yon water*).  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *yon* [singular countable noun] &middot; *yon* [plural countable noun] &middot; [preposition] *yon* [noun] (*past yon barn*) &middot; *yon* standing alone as a pronoun (archaic)  
**Contrast:** *yon* is the survivor of an older three-way system — *this* (by me), *that* (by you), *yon* (over there, away from us both) — of the kind Spanish still keeps in *este/ese/aquel*. Modern standard English has collapsed it into two, so *that* now covers everything *yon* once did. Against *yonder*: the two are interchangeable as determiners, but only *yonder* is still used as an adverb (*over yonder*), which is why *yonder* survives in speech and *yon* mostly does not.  
**Variants:** *yond* (Shakespearean), *yonder* (the living form)

Points at something visible but distant from both speaker and hearer — the third term of a deictic system English has otherwise lost. In current American English it is a period marker: it makes a line sound old, rustic, or biblical.

1. *Yon* ridge marks the county line.
2. We camped under *yon* pines.
3. He pointed to *yon* steeple and said the town began there.
4. Round *yon* Virgin, Mother and Child. ("Silent Night")
5. *Yon* stranger has been watching the road all morning.
6. Take the path past *yon* barn and turn left at the creek.

**See also:** [yonder](#yonder), [yond](#yond), [that](#that), [those](#those)

### yond

**Pronunciation:** /jɑnd/ &middot; *YAHND*  
**Русский:** вон тот — устар., шекспировское; то же, что yon
**Type:** demonstrative (distal, third-degree) &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; effectively confined to Shakespeare and to writing that imitates him  
**Goes with:** singular or plural countable nouns, with no change of form — *yond star*, *yond hills*. Like *yon*, it does not inflect for number.  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *yond* [noun] &middot; *yond same* [noun] (a Shakespearean favorite) &middot; [preposition] *yond* [noun]  
**Contrast:** *yond*, *yon*, and *yonder* are three forms of one word, and all three mean "that, over there." *yonder* is the only one an American might still say; *yon* survives in verse and regional speech; *yond* is a quotation, not a usable word. A learner needs it for reading, not for writing.  
**Variants:** *yon*, *yonder*

The oldest-sounding of the three distal demonstratives, meaning "that … over there." Encountered almost exclusively in Elizabethan text.

1. *Yond* Cassius has a lean and hungry look. (Shakespeare, *Julius Caesar*)
2. *Yond* same star that's westward from the pole. (Shakespeare, *Hamlet*)
3. *Yond* tower was built by the first settlers.
4. He gestured toward *yond* hills and said nothing.
5. *Yond* ship flies no colors.
6. We shall meet again beneath *yond* oak.

**See also:** [yon](#yon), [yonder](#yonder), [that](#that)

### yonder

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjɑndɚ/ &middot; *YAHN-der*  
**Русский:** вон тот, вон там — устар. как определитель, диал. (юг США) как наречие места
**Type:** demonstrative determiner; also, and far more commonly, an adverb of place &middot; **Position:** central &middot; **Register:** archaic as a determiner; regional (Southern, Appalachian, Ozark, and rural Midwestern) as an adverb, where it is thoroughly alive  
**Goes with:** singular or plural countable nouns with no change of form — *yonder hill*, *yonder hills*. It does not inflect for number, which sets it apart from every other demonstrative in the language except *yon* and *yond*.  
**With *of*:** None.  
**Pattern:** *yonder* [singular or plural noun] &middot; *over yonder* (adverb) &middot; *out/up/down yonder* (adverb) &middot; *the wild blue yonder* (fixed noun phrase)  
**Contrast:** As a determiner it is interchangeable with *yon* and *yond* and equally archaic; as an adverb it is ordinary regional American speech, and that is the use a learner will actually meet — *He's out yonder*, *the cabin over yonder*. Against *that*: *that* is simply "not here," while *yonder* adds "and you can see it from here," a distance judged by eye rather than by ownership or topic.  
**Variants:** *yon*, *yond*, *over yonder* (adverb), *the wild blue yonder* (noun)

Points at something at a middle-to-far distance that is nonetheless in view. As a determiner it belongs to older and regional English; as an adverb of place it is current, informal American usage.

1. *Yonder* field flooded again last spring.
2. *Yonder* hills turn blue at dusk.
3. She lives in *yonder* house with the green roof.
4. The cabin is just over *yonder*. (adverb)
5. Go on down *yonder* road about two miles. (regional)
6. They lit out for the wild blue *yonder*. (fixed noun phrase)

**See also:** [yon](#yon), [yond](#yond), [that](#that), [those](#those)

## Archaic identity: the demonstrative of sameness

One more demonstrative has fallen out of the language, and this one points not at a place but at a referent: *self*, meaning "the same — the very one already named." It never filled the central slot. It sat behind *the* or a demonstrative, in the position *same* occupies today (*that self hour*, *the self place*), and as a free word it is now dead, surviving only where it fused with its own gloss into [*selfsame*](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame). It is listed here because anyone reading Shakespeare, Spenser, or the King James translators meets it constantly, and because a modern eye mis-parses it every time — as the noun *self*, or as a *self-* prefix that has lost its hyphen.

### self

**Pronunciation:** /sɛlf/, with no weak form — *self* is an identifying word, it carries stress, and it keeps its full vowel wherever it stands; the only reduction it ever undergoes is inside the fused compound, where the second element gives up its stress and *self same* /ˌsɛlf ˈseɪm/ becomes *selfsame* /ˈsɛlfˌseɪm/ &middot; *SELF*  
**Русский:** тот самый — устар.; тождество, а не сходство; предок живого selfsame
**Type:** identity determiner (demonstrative in function — it marks a noun as the very one already named), archaic; elsewhere the ordinary noun *self* (*a sense of self*) and the bound second element of the reflexive pronouns (*myself*, *herself*) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows *the*, a demonstrative, or *one*, and can never fill the central slot itself (✗ *self hour was appointed*, ✗ *a self day*) &middot; **Register:** archaic, and as a free word effectively obsolete. It belongs to Early Modern English — Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton, the King James translators — and to modern writing that quotes or imitates them. An American meets bare *self* in a literature class, in an old hymn, or in an editor's footnote, and nowhere else; what survives in living prose is the fused *selfsame*.  
**Goes with:** singular countable nouns overwhelmingly — *that self hour*, *the self place*, *that self mould*, *one self king*. Plural countables turn up in older verse but are rare (*the self words*), and an abstract uncountable is possible in the same register (*that self blood*). Because it asserts identity with a referent the text has already fixed, it requires a definite word in front of it: *the*, a demonstrative, or the numeral *one*. Never *a/an* — ✗ *a self day* — since identity presupposes something the reader can already find; never bare — ✗ *Self hour was appointed*; never a proper noun — ✗ *that self London*, where the noun has to be a common one (*that self town*); and never a pronoun — ✗ *the self it*. Like *aforesaid* and *the subject*, it cannot introduce a referent, only re-identify one.  
**With *of*:** None — ✗ *the self of hours*. Two look-alikes do take *of*, and neither of them is this word: the **noun** *self* takes *of*-phrases and possessives freely (*a sense of self*, *his better self*, *the death of the self*), and the **prefix** *self-* builds compounds meaning "by or to itself" (*self-evident*, *self-employed*, *self-defense*), which is a different sense altogether.  
**Pattern:** [*the* / *this* / *that* / *one*] *self* [singular countable noun] &middot; [*the* / *that*] *self* [abstract noun] &middot; [preposition] [*the* / *that*] *self* [noun] (*in that self hour*, *at the self moment*) &middot; *that self same* [noun] (the older spaced writing) &middot; *selfsame* [noun] (the fused modern survival)  
**Contrast:** Against *same*, the word that replaced it: *the self hour* is *the same hour*, and outside a pastiche no American writes the first. Against [*selfsame*](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame): one word at two ages — the free *self* is dead, the fusion is alive as a literary flourish, which is why *the selfsame bench* reads as ornate English while ✗ *the self bench* reads as a typo. Against *very* (*that very day*): the same identifying job, still current, and older writing stacked the two (*that very self same day*). Against the noun and the prefix: the determiner takes a noun after it and a determiner before it (*that self hour*), the noun heads a phrase of its own and takes its own determiner (*a sense of self*, *his former self*), and the prefix is bound, hyphenated, and means "by itself" rather than "the same" — *self-taught* is not "the same taught." Against the reflexive: *-self* is bound into a pronoun and takes the stress (*herself* /hɚˈsɛlf/), and is not a determiner at all. One note on sense: *self* claimed only **numerical** identity, one and the same thing, never "of the same kind" — the second reading that modern *the same car* can carry was never available to it.  
**Variants:** *selfsame*, *self-same* (the fused compound — the only living form), *that self same* (older spaced writing), *the same*, *the very same* (the modern equivalents)

Marks a noun as the identical one already named — "that very same." It is the free-standing ancestor of *selfsame*, and it does the work modern English hands to *same*: not sameness of kind but sameness of identity, the claim that two mentions have landed on one and the same thing.

1. *That self* hour the bell in the chapel began to toll. (period narrative)
2. Her sweet perfections, with one *self* king! (Shakespeare, *Twelfth Night*)
3. That bed, that womb, that metal, that *self* mould that fashion'd thee made him a man. (Shakespeare, *Richard II*; some editions print *self-mould*)
4. He was buried in *the self* ground where his father lay. (historical fiction)
5. Upon *the self* day appointed, the fleet weighed anchor and stood out to sea. (imitation of a sixteenth-century chronicle)
6. And in *that self* same hour the fever left him. (biblical cadence; the King James translators write it fused, *selfsame*)

**See also:** [selfsame](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#selfsame), [same](07-numerals-multipliers.md#same), [very](09-predeterminers-postdeterminers.md#very), [that](#that), [aforesaid](#aforesaid)

## Anaphoric determiners of formal and legal prose

Demonstrative in function but not in form: these words point into the document rather than outward into the room. Three of them point backward, marking a noun as one already named on the page (*aforementioned*, *aforesaid*, *said*). Three more point at the here-and-now instead, and they are the register's version of *this*, split by what exactly they aim at: *the present* at the document itself, *the within* at the paper the words are written on, *the subject* at the one thing the whole file is about. They belong to contracts, pleadings, and administrative writing, and in nearly every case a plain *the*, *this*, or *that* would be clearer.

### aforementioned

**Pronunciation:** /əˈfɔrˌmɛnʃənd/ &middot; *uh-FOR-men-shund*  
**Русский:** вышеупомянутый — юр./канц., отсылка к названному ранее в тексте
**Type:** anaphoric determiner-adjective (demonstrative in function — it points backward into the text) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it normally follows *the* (*the aforementioned report*) rather than filling the central slot itself &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, bureaucratic; stiff in ordinary prose  
**Goes with:** any noun already named earlier in the same document — singular countable (*the aforementioned clause*), plural countable (*the aforementioned witnesses*), or uncountable (*the aforementioned evidence*). It cannot introduce anything new: if the noun has not been mentioned before, the word is simply false.  
**With *of*:** None of its own. Partitives come from a quantifier in front: *each of the aforementioned factors*, *none of the aforementioned parties*.  
**Pattern:** *the aforementioned* [noun] &middot; *the aforementioned* standing alone as a pronoun (*the aforementioned is incorporated by reference*) &middot; [noun] *aforementioned* (postposed, in older drafting) &middot; *the above-mentioned* [noun]  
**Contrast:** Against *said* and *aforesaid*: all three point back to an earlier mention, but *said* usually stands without an article (*said vehicle*) while *aforementioned* and *aforesaid* usually follow *the*. Against a plain demonstrative: *that report* and *the aforementioned report* do identical work, and outside a contract the demonstrative is better English. American style guides recommend replacing all of them with *the* or *this/that*.  
**Variants:** *above-mentioned*, *abovementioned*, *above-named*, *the foregoing*, *aforesaid*, *said*

Marks a noun as one already introduced earlier in the same text — a demonstrative that points at a place on the page instead of a place in the room. It belongs to legal and administrative drafting and reads as padding anywhere else.

1. The *aforementioned* report was never released to the public.
2. Each of the *aforementioned* factors carries equal weight.
3. The *aforementioned* witnesses were unavailable for deposition.
4. The plaintiff relies on the *aforementioned* exception.
5. The *aforementioned* is incorporated herein by reference.
6. For the reasons *aforementioned*, the motion is denied.

**See also:** [aforesaid](#aforesaid), [said](#said), [the present](#the-present), [that](#that), [the](01-articles.md#the)

### aforesaid

**Pronunciation:** /əˈfɔrˌsɛd/ &middot; *uh-FOR-sed*  
**Русский:** вышеназванный, вышеозначенный — юр., устар.; вне документов не употребляется
**Type:** anaphoric determiner-adjective (demonstrative in function) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer after *the* (*the aforesaid agreement*); bare, article-less use (*aforesaid parties*) survives in older drafting &middot; **Register:** legal, archaic; unusable in ordinary writing  
**Goes with:** any noun already named in the document — singular countable (*the aforesaid agreement*), plural countable (*the aforesaid parties*), uncountable (*the aforesaid property*). Like all anaphoric determiners it requires an earlier mention to point back to.  
**With *of*:** None of its own; a quantifier supplies it (*any of the aforesaid conditions*).  
**Pattern:** *the aforesaid* [noun] &middot; *aforesaid* [noun] (bare, older style) &middot; *as aforesaid* (fixed adverbial: *as aforesaid, the tenant shall…*) &middot; [noun] *aforesaid* (postposed)  
**Contrast:** *aforesaid* is the older and more legalistic of the pair *aforesaid / aforementioned*; *said* is the shortest and the most common in American drafting. The fixed adverbial *as aforesaid* has no counterpart in the other two. In every case a demonstrative or a plain article does the same work more clearly.  
**Variants:** *aforementioned*, *said*, *as aforesaid* (adverbial), *the foregoing*

Identifies a noun as the one already specified earlier in the same instrument. Its whole function is anaphoric: it substitutes for a demonstrative and buys the drafter a claim of precision.

1. The *aforesaid* agreement shall govern any dispute between the parties.
2. *Aforesaid* parties waive their right to a jury trial.
3. The witness confirmed the *aforesaid* dates.
4. Nothing in this section limits the *aforesaid* obligations.
5. The *aforesaid* premises were inspected on June 1.
6. Subject to the conditions *aforesaid*, the permit is granted.

**See also:** [aforementioned](#aforementioned), [said](#said), [the present](#the-present), [that](#that)

### said

**Pronunciation:** /sɛd/ &middot; *SED*  
**Русский:** означенный, упомянутый — юр./канц.; в обиходной речи шутливо
**Type:** anaphoric determiner (demonstrative in function); elsewhere the past tense of *say* &middot; **Position:** central determiner when it stands bare (*said vehicle*, where no article is possible); postdeterminer in the older *the said vehicle* &middot; **Register:** legal and bureaucratic; jocular in general speech (*I returned said sandwich*)  
**Goes with:** any noun already introduced in the same document — singular countable (*said defendant*), plural countable (*said documents*), uncountable (*said property*, *said equipment*). It never introduces a new referent, and it takes no article when used bare, because it is itself occupying the determiner slot.  
**With *of*:** None of its own. *Of* arrives only with a quantifier in front: *any of said documents*, *none of said parties*.  
**Pattern:** *said* [singular countable noun] &middot; *said* [plural countable noun] &middot; *said* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the said* [noun] (older style) &middot; [quantifier] *of said* [noun]  
**Contrast:** Against *that/those*: identical in meaning — *said vehicle* is *that vehicle* — but restricted to legal register, and criticized even there; American style authorities recommend replacing it with *the*. Against *aforesaid* and *aforementioned*: *said* is the bare, article-free form, the other two normally follow *the*. And against the verb: *said* the determiner sits in front of a noun (*said report*), while *said* the verb takes a subject (*she said*).  
**Variants:** *the said*, *aforesaid*, *aforementioned*

Marks a noun as the one already named in the document — the legal draftsman's demonstrative. Outside contracts and pleadings it survives mainly as a joke, where the mock-legal tone is the point.

1. *Said* vehicle was recovered in Tulsa on March 3.
2. The tenant shall keep *said* premises in good repair.
3. *Said* documents must be produced within ten days.
4. Payment for *said* services is due on receipt.
5. The court found that *said* defendant had waived the argument.
6. All rights in *said* property pass to the buyer at closing.

**See also:** [aforesaid](#aforesaid), [aforementioned](#aforementioned), [the present](#the-present), [that](#that), [the](01-articles.md#the)

### the present

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈprɛzənt/ &middot; *thuh-PREZ-uhnt*  
**Русский:** настоящий (документ, дело) — книжн./юр. замена this: the present case
**Type:** anaphoric determiner-adjective (demonstrative in function — it points at the document, case, or occasion in hand) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows *the* and cannot fill the central slot on its own (✗ *present case is distinguishable*) &middot; **Register:** formal: legal, academic, and administrative writing  
**Goes with:** any noun naming the text, proceeding, or occasion at hand — singular countable (*the present case*, *the present agreement*), plural countable (*the present proceedings*, *the present regulations*), or uncountable (*the present litigation*, *the present research*). Because it means "this one, the one we are inside of," it is odd with a noun that has nothing to do with the document: *the present weather* is just *this weather*, badly dressed.  
**With *of*:** None of its own; a quantifier in front supplies it (*each of the present exhibits*, *none of the present parties*). The look-alikes *at present* and *for the present* are fixed adverbials, not determiner uses.  
**Pattern:** *the present* [singular countable noun] &middot; *the present* [plural countable noun] &middot; *the present* [uncountable noun] &middot; *in the present case / instance* (fixed) &middot; *the present writer / author* (formal self-reference) &middot; *the instant* [noun] (American legal variant)  
**Contrast:** Against *aforementioned*, *aforesaid*, and *said*: those three point **backward** to a noun already named, while *the present* points at the here-and-now of the document — it is the proximal member of the set, the formal register's *this*. Against plain *this*: *the present case* and *this case* say the same thing, and outside a brief or a journal article *this* is the better English. Against *the instant*: interchangeable in American court writing (*the instant case*, *the instant motion*), with *instant* the more strictly legal of the two. And against the verb *present* /prɪˈzɛnt/: the determiner-adjective takes first-syllable stress, /ˈprɛzənt/.  
**Variants:** *the instant* (legal: *the instant case*), *the current*, *the present-day*, *this* (the plain equivalent)

Marks a noun as the one belonging to the text, case, or occasion in hand — the proximal counterpart of *aforesaid*, and a formal-register substitute for *this*.

1. *The present* case is distinguishable on its facts.
2. *The present* study replicates an experiment from 1998.
3. Nothing in *the present* agreement waives that right.
4. *The present* writer takes no position on the question.
5. Do *the present* regulations apply to leases signed before 2019?
6. *The present* research does not address long-term outcomes.
7. *The instant* motion was filed one day out of time. (legal variant)

**See also:** [aforementioned](#aforementioned), [aforesaid](#aforesaid), [said](#said), [this](#this), [the](01-articles.md#the)

### the subject

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈsʌbdʒɪkt/, with *the* in its weak form /ðə/ and the unstressed second syllable reduced to /dʒəkt/ in ordinary speech &middot; *thuh-SUB-jikt*  
**Русский:** данный, рассматриваемый — офиц.: the subject property = «рассматриваемый объект»
**Type:** anaphoric determiner-adjective (demonstrative in function — it points at the one thing the document exists to deal with) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows *the* and cannot fill the central slot on its own in running prose (✗ *Subject property was appraised in May*), though headings, captions, and exhibit labels drop the article (*Subject Property: 412 Elm Street*) &middot; **Register:** formal; the house style of appraisals, insurance claims, inspection reports, real-estate contracts, police reports, and litigation  
**Goes with:** any noun naming the thing the file is about — singular countable (*the subject property*, *the subject vehicle*, *the subject loan*, *the subject patent*), plural countable (*the subject properties*, *the subject employees*), a plural-only noun (*the subject premises*), or uncountable (*the subject land*, *the subject equipment*). It cannot precede a proper noun (✗ *the subject Elm Street* → *the subject property at 412 Elm Street*), a pronoun (✗ *the subject it*), or a noun with no stake in the document — *the subject weather* is nonsense unless the weather is what is being adjudicated. And like every anaphoric determiner it needs a referent the document has already fixed; it cannot introduce one.  
**With *of*:** None of its own. A partitive comes from a quantifier in front: *each of the subject properties*, *none of the subject vehicles*. Do not confuse the frame with *the subject of* (*the subject of the appraisal*, *the subject of the sentence*), where *subject* is the head noun and the *of*-phrase belongs to it — the determiner use takes no complement at all.  
**Pattern:** *the subject* [singular countable noun] &middot; *the subject* [plural countable noun] &middot; *the subject* [uncountable noun] &middot; *the subject property / vehicle / premises* (the three fixed collocations) &middot; *Subject* [noun] (bare, in headings, captions, and exhibit labels) &middot; [quantifier] *of the subject* [plural noun]  
**Contrast:** Against *the present*: both are proximal, but they aim at different things — *the present agreement* is the paper in your hand, *the subject property* is the house that paper is about. Against *said*, *aforesaid*, and *aforementioned*: those three mark a noun as one already named earlier on the page, while *the subject* marks it as the one the whole file concerns, which is exactly why an appraisal calls the house being valued *the subject property* and the houses it is measured against *the comparables*. Against a plain demonstrative: *this property* and *the subject property* do identical work, and outside a report *this* is the better English. Against the unrelated adjective in *subject to*: that one is predicative and takes a complement (*the property is subject to a lien*), while this one never appears after a verb (✗ *the property is subject*) and never takes a complement. And against the verb *subject* /səbˈdʒɛkt/ (*to subject a sample to testing*): the determiner-adjective takes first-syllable stress, /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt/, exactly as *the present* does against *present*.  
**Variants:** *subject* (bare, in headings and captions: *Subject Vehicle*), *the subject premises* (fixed, plural-only noun), *the instant* (legal near-equivalent), *the said* (older drafting), *this* (the plain equivalent)

Marks a noun as the one thing the document exists to deal with — the house being appraised, the car being adjusted, the building being inspected. It is a demonstrative in a business suit: *the subject property* is *this property*, written by someone who is writing for a file rather than for a reader.

1. *The subject* property sold twice in the last decade, most recently in 2019. (appraisal)
2. *The subject* vehicle sustained damage to the left rear quarter panel. (insurance claim)
3. Access to *the subject* premises is limited to normal business hours. (commercial lease)
4. Officers recovered *the subject* vehicle three blocks from the scene. (police report)
5. Each of *the subject* properties lies within the same flood zone. (quantifier in front)
6. *The subject* loan closed two weeks before the rule took effect. (lending compliance)
7. *Subject* Property: 412 Elm Street, photographed on the morning of the inspection. (bare, in a caption)

### the within

**Pronunciation:** /ðə wɪˈðɪn/, weak /ðə wəˈðɪn/ with the unstressed first syllable reduced; /ðə wɪˌðɪn ˈneɪmd/ in the compound *the within-named*, where the main stress moves onto *named* &middot; *thuh-wih-THIN*  
**Русский:** настоящий (документ) — юр., устар.: «то, что написано на этой бумаге»
**Type:** anaphoric determiner-adjective (demonstrative in function — it points at the document the words are written on) &middot; **Position:** postdeterminer — it follows *the* (*the within action*) and cannot open the phrase in modern use; it also fuses into a compound modifier (*the within-named defendant*) and heads a bare noun phrase in endorsements (*a true copy of the within*) &middot; **Register:** legal and archaic. It survives in New York court practice (*the within motion*), on the backs of folded pleadings and subpoenas, and on old deeds and promissory notes. An American who is not a lawyer meets it about once — on the reverse of a legal paper somebody has just handed them.  
**Goes with:** nouns naming the instrument or the proceeding that the paper itself contains — singular countable (*the within action*, *the within motion*, *the within instrument*, *the within affidavit*), plural countable (*the within proceedings*, *the within exhibits*), and, inside the compound, a noun naming a person (*the within-named defendant*, *the within-named parties*). It will not stand in front of an ordinary object noun — ✗ *the within truck*, ✗ *the within house* — because the word means "contained in this document," not "over here": there has to be a document for the noun to be inside of.  
**With *of*:** None of its own — ✗ *the within of the motion*. A quantifier in front supplies any partitive: *each of the within exhibits*, *any of the within-named parties*. The trap is the ordinary preposition *within*, which does take an object and is many times commoner (*within thirty days*, *within the meaning of section 4*); that *within* is not a determiner at all.  
**Pattern:** *the within* [document or proceeding noun] &middot; *the within-named* [person noun] &middot; *the within* standing alone as a noun phrase (*a true copy of the within*) &middot; *within* [noun] (bare, in older drafting) &middot; [quantifier] *of the within* [plural noun] &middot; *the within-written* / *the within-mentioned* (older compounds)  
**Contrast:** Against *the present*: near-synonyms — *the present motion* and *the within motion* both mean "this one" — but *the present* is the neutral formal choice while *the within* is a courthouse habit that survived in New York and in fossils elsewhere. Against *aforementioned*, *aforesaid*, and *said*: those point **backward** to a noun already named on the page, while *the within* points **inward** at the paper as a physical object, which puts it with *the present* on the proximal side. Against the preposition *within*: the preposition takes an object (*within ten days*), the determiner modifies a following noun (*the within action*), and the test is whether *this* can be swapped in — *the within action* → *this action*, yes; *within ten days* → ✗ *this ten days*, no.  
**Variants:** *the within-named*, *the within-mentioned*, *the within-written* (older), *within* (bare, in older drafting), *the annexed* (the sibling endorsement word, for what is stapled to the paper), *this* (the plain equivalent)

Means "this — the one written on the paper you are holding." Its deixis points neither at the room nor backward up the page but at the document as an object, which is why it lives on endorsements: the drafter is writing on the outside of a folded paper and needs a word for what is on the inside.

1. *The within* motion is denied without prejudice. (New York practice)
2. Service of *the within* summons and complaint is hereby admitted. (endorsement on the back of a pleading)
3. *The within-named* defendant has not appeared or answered. (compound modifier)
4. Please take notice of *the within* affidavit and the exhibits annexed thereto. (notice of motion)
5. The undersigned certifies that *the within* is a true copy of the original. (standing alone, on a certification)
6. All rights and remedies under *the within* instrument pass to the assignee. (older conveyancing)
7. Payment of *the within* note was duly demanded and refused. (nineteenth-century promissory note)
