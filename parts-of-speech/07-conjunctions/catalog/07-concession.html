Subordinators of concession and contrast#

The conjunctions that hand the other side a point and then take the sentence back anyway.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Every word in this file sets up the same two-part deal: one clause grants something inconvenient, and the other clause wins in spite of it. That makes them the subordinating answer to but — where but balances two equal halves, although demotes one of them, so the clause you hang the conjunction on is the clause you are giving away. A few of these words draw a flat contrast instead of a concession (whereas, where) or subtract an exception from what was just claimed (except that, save that), and a whole family of them concede every possible case at once (whatever, no matter how, however hard). One punctuation rule runs through all of it: front the subordinate clause and you always need a comma after it, while a trailing clause is a judgment call — a concession is supplementary, so it usually keeps its comma (She took the job, although the pay was worse), but a trailing even if, even when, or no matter how clause is integrated into what the main clause claims and usually loses it. The fixed formulas at the end of the file (be that as it may, granted, howbeit) are adverbs, not conjunctions, and take a period or a semicolon before them — never a comma alone.

Core concessive subordinators#

The everyday words for "this is true, and it doesn't change anything." Most of them can be fronted or trailed — the inverted as pattern (tired as I was) is the one that only ever lives at the front of the sentence — and several — although, though, while, whilst — can drop the subject and the verb be when the subject is the same in both clauses: Although tired, she finished the shift.

Introduces a fact that makes the main clause surprising.   4. As much as he complains about the commute, he has never once been late.
  5. Much as the committee wanted to fund every proposal, the budget wouldn't stretch.

Punctuation
The comma is what separates the two meanings. Contrastive while takes one — He's methodical, while his partner works on instinct — and temporal while does not: She read while I cooked. Fronted, it always takes a comma after the clause.

While does three jobs as a conjunction — time, contrast, and concession — and American readers sort them by position and comma. Concessive while ("although") is normal at the front of a sentence; contrastive while ("whereas") is normal in the middle with a comma in front of it. If a sentence could be read either as time or as contrast, rewrite it. Outside the conjunction the same spelling is a noun (it took a while, worth your while) and a verb (we whiled away the afternoon), which is why awhile and a while trip people up: the one-word adverb modifies a verb, the two-word noun phrase follows a preposition.

  1. While I agree with the goal, I think the timeline is fantasy.
  2. He's methodical, while his partner works on instinct.
  3. While the paint is technically dry, I wouldn't lean on it.
  4. Our office got new chairs, while the warehouse crew got nothing.
  5. While nobody disputes the numbers, everybody disputes what they mean.

Contrast: two things side by side#

These do not concede anything. They set two propositions against each other and leave both standing, which is why a comma before them is close to mandatory — the second clause is a comparison, not a condition on the first. Two of them, except that and save that, do something narrower still: instead of comparing, they subtract, taking back one piece of the claim the main clause just made. Four are not clause conjunctions at all — contra, contrary to, and pace take a noun phrase, and on the flip side is a conjunctive adverb needing a period or a semicolon in front of it — but they draw the same line between two positions, so they belong here.

Comma before a trailing except that clause — it withdraws part of what was just claimed, so it is supplementary, never integrated. Variants

Subtracts a piece of the main clause instead of conceding a point against it, and that is what keeps it out of the although family: although the room was quiet grants a fact and moves on, while except that the fan rattled takes something back.   1. The apartment would be perfect, except that there's no parking on the block.
  2. Nothing about the trip went wrong, except that the rental car smelled like smoke.
  3. I'd have finished the draft, except that the power went out at nine.
  4. The two engines are identical, except that the newer one carries a longer warranty.
  5. She remembers the whole evening. Except that she can't say who drove her home.
  6. The branch is open every day, except when the holiday falls on a Monday.

Formal and phrasal concessives#

Multi-word chunks, learned whole. Most exist because a preposition — despite, in spite of, regardless of, aside from, notwithstanding — cannot take a clause on its own and needs the fact that bolted on to do it. Nearly every one can be cut to although or except that, and usually should be. The exception is the pair of notwithstanding override formulas that American contracts are built out of — notwithstanding anything to the contrary and notwithstanding the foregoing — which are terms of art, and cutting them changes what the document means.

Compact and fixed-phrase concessives#

Single words and frozen formulas. Most of them — all the same, anyhow, at that, be that as it may, even then, granted, howbeit, mind you, natheless, no doubt, not for nothing, still and all, to be sure — are conjunctive adverbs rather than conjunctions, so when they stand between two independent clauses they need a period or a semicolon in front of them; a comma there is a comma splice. Albeit is a genuine subordinator but takes a phrase, not a clause, and maugre takes a noun phrase only. The archaic end of the shelf — be it ever so, maugre, natheless, not but that, what though — is period reading, not modern usage.

Concedes something in advance, and the concession is usually thin: no doubt she meant well has a but waiting behind it almost every time, which makes the phrase a polite way of not really conceding at all. That ironic edge is the whole difference from undoubtedly, where the speaker actually agrees. Doubtless does the same job a register higher.

  1. No doubt the intentions were good, but the rollout was a disaster.
  2. She'll have a reason, no doubt.
  3. No doubt you've heard a different version of this story.
  4. Doubtless the committee has its reasons; it has not shared them with the faculty.
  5. The contractor was no doubt busy, which does not explain three unreturned calls.
  6. Doubtless he meant it kindly, and it landed badly all the same.

Русский

Type
subordinating — a frozen stacked negative
Register
archaic and formal for not but that; not but what is dialectal and nonstandard — regional American and British speech, never edited prose

The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century ancestor of not that: a stacked negative meaning "it is not that … not," which comes out as "though of course."   1. She never spoke of the money, not but that she needed it.
  2. He declined the invitation — not but that he was flattered to be asked.
  3. I have no quarrel with the arrangement, not but that I should have preferred to be consulted.
  4. "He's a decent enough landlord, not but what the roof's wanted fixing these ten years." (dialectal)
  5. The family said nothing at the time, not but that the whole village knew.

Type
conjunctive adverb (phrasal) — a concessive disjunct
Register
formal to neutral — a writer's phrase; in speech it sounds bookish
Clause type
none — a sentence adverbial conceding a point, almost always ahead of a but

  3. He was not the most experienced candidate, to be sure; he was plainly the most prepared.

Type
subordinating — a rhetorical question standing in for a concessive clause
Register
archaic and poetic — Shakespeare, Milton, the hymnal, and nineteenth-century verse; dead in modern prose
Clause type

A rhetorical question doing a concessive clause's work: what though the field be lost? means "granted the field is lost — it changes nothing." The subjunctive verb (be, were) is part of the formula. Milton gave it its most famous outing and the hymn writers kept it alive through the nineteenth century; an American now meets it in poetry and in older hymns, nowhere else.

  4. What though he had neither name nor fortune, the girl would have him.
  5. "What though our lot be hard, we are not therefore without hope."
