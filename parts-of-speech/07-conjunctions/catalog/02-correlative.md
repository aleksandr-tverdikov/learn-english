# Correlative conjunctions (paired)

> The fixed two-part joiners — *both … and*, *either … or*, *no sooner … than* — where one half marks each of the two things being joined and neither half can stand alone.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

A correlative conjunction is a **pair**: the first half sits in front of the first element, the second half in front of the second, and both are obligatory — drop either one and the sentence breaks. (The systematic exceptions are the *then* of *if … then*, the *yet* of *though … yet*, and the *there* of *where … there* — all three adverbs rather than conjunctions, and all three droppable.) Because they coordinate, the two elements they join have to be grammatically parallel, which makes **placement** the rule that governs the entire class: each half goes immediately before the element it marks, and the two halves move inward or outward together. *She not only wrote the script but also directed the film* balances two verb phrases; *she not only wrote the script but also the film* balances a verb phrase against a noun and is broken. Three of these pairs also change subject-verb agreement: with *either … or*, *neither … nor*, and *not only … but also*, the verb agrees with whichever subject sits **nearer** to it, while *both … and* is always plural.

Punctuation across the class is mostly a matter of leaving it alone: **no comma between the halves** when the pair joins words or phrases. Two exceptions are worth memorizing, and one look-alike is worth resisting. First, when a pair ends up joining two full independent clauses, the second half takes a comma before it, exactly like any coordinating conjunction — *Either you tell him, or I will.* Second, when the first half fronts a clause or an adjunct ahead of the main clause, close that fronted stretch with a comma at the seam: *The more you practice, the easier it gets*; *Just as a garden needs weeding, so a budget needs pruning*; *If it rains, then we'll move inside*; *Whether you like it or not, we're going*; *As soon as the door closed, everyone started talking*; *Though the evidence was thin, yet the jury convicted*; *What with the traffic and the rain, we were late.* The rule behind all of them is the general one — **a fronted subordinate half takes the comma; the same half sitting second does not.** Reverse them and the comma normally goes away: *we're going whether you like it or not*, *a budget needs pruning just as a garden needs weeding*, *everyone started talking as soon as the door closed*. Concession is the single exception: a trailing *though* clause keeps its comma, because it contrasts with the main clause instead of restricting it — *nobody paid to fix it, though the roof leaked all spring*. The look-alike to resist is the immediate-sequence group (*no sooner … than*, *hardly/scarcely/barely … when*). Fronting *no sooner* looks like the fronting above but takes **no** comma; what it takes is subject-auxiliary inversion — *No sooner had we sat down than…*, never *No sooner we had sat down*.

## Addition, alternative, and exclusion

The coordinating pairs. These join two elements of equal grammatical rank and carry the parallelism and agreement rules that define the class.

### as well … as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈwɛl/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *az-WEL … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (the split form of the quasi-coordinator *as well as*; the first *as* is a degree adverb and *well* an adverb, so only the second *as* is the conjunction — unsplit, the same three words are also the equative of *she plays as well as her brother*) &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in legal and older literary prose
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — joins noun phrases, prepositional phrases, and adjectives
**Pattern:** *as well* [X] *as* [Y] &middot; *as well* [prepositional phrase] *as* [prepositional phrase] &middot; [verb] *as well* [object] *as* [object] &middot; *as well* [subject] *as* [subject] + plural verb &middot; *as well* [adjective] *as* [adjective]
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. The commas that fence the modern *as well as* when it interrupts a subject and its verb (*The manager, as well as the interns, was invited*) have no equivalent here, because the split frame coordinates the two items instead of adding one parenthetically.
**Variants:** *as well as* (modern, unsplit), *both … and* (the ordinary modern equivalent), *not only … but also*

The old split arrangement of *as well as*: the *well* stays with the first item and the second *as* introduces the other, so *as well the rich as the poor* means both of them. Because both items sit inside the frame it coordinates like *both … and*, where the modern unsplit *as well as* leaves the first item grammatically in charge and the verb singular. Nothing but fossilized phrasing survives in current American English — write *both X and Y* or *X as well as Y*.

1. The statute binds *as well* the buyer *as* the seller.
2. The fire took *as well* the barn *as* the house.
3. He was remembered *as well* for his temper *as* for his generosity.
4. *As well* the officers *as* the men were paid out of the same fund.
5. The collection holds letters *as well* in French *as* in English.
6. The charter protected *as well* those born in the town *as* those who settled there later.

**See also:** [both … and](#both--and), [not only … but also](#not-only--but-also), [as well as (addition)](01-coordinating.md#as-well-as), [as well as (comparison)](08-comparison.md#as-well-as)

### at once … and

**Pronunciation:** /æt ˈwʌns/ … /ænd/, /ən/ &middot; *at-WUNSS … AND, uhn*
**Type:** correlative (*at once* is elsewhere an adverb of time meaning "immediately" — *come at once*; the *and* half is the ordinary coordinator) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — most often two adjectives or two noun phrases in predicate position
**Pattern:** [subject] + *be* + *at once* [adjective] *and* [adjective] &middot; *at once* [noun phrase] *and* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *at once* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *at once* [adjective], [adjective], *and* [adjective] (three or more) &middot; *at once both* [X] *and* [Y]
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves in the two-item form. With three or more items, punctuate the run as an ordinary series: *at once cheap, fast, and reliable.*
**Variants:** *both … and* (neutral), *at the same time … and*, *at once … and yet* (adds contrast)

Says that both descriptions hold simultaneously, and usually implies they sit oddly together — *at once familiar and strange*. It is the literary cousin of *both … and*, and the temporal reading of *at once* ("immediately") drops away as soon as the *and* half arrives.

1. The house was *at once* grand *and* uninhabitable.
2. Her answer was *at once* polite *and* devastating.
3. The book is *at once* a memoir *and* a history of the town.
4. He managed to be *at once* the funniest person in the room *and* the loneliest.
5. The ruling is *at once* narrow *and* far-reaching.
6. The score is *at once* cheap, fast, *and* impossible to argue with.

**See also:** [both … and](#both--and), [not only … but also](#not-only--but-also), [and](01-coordinating.md#and), [at the same time](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#at-the-same-time)

### both … and

**Pronunciation:** /boʊθ/ … /ænd/, /ənd/, /ən/ &middot; *BOHTH … AND, uhn*
**Type:** correlative (*both* on its own is a determiner and a pronoun — *both books*, *both of them*; the *and* half is the ordinary coordinator of *bread and butter*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — joins words, phrases, and matching subordinate clauses, but not two independent clauses
**Pattern:** *both* [X] *and* [Y] (X and Y the same kind of element) &middot; *both* [subject] *and* [subject] + plural verb &middot; [subject] *both* [verb phrase] *and* [verb phrase] &middot; [verb] *both* [object] *and* [object] &middot; *both* [prepositional phrase] *and* [prepositional phrase] &middot; *both* [subordinate clause] *and* [subordinate clause] (*both that he knew and that he said nothing*)
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves — never *both the coach, and the players*. Since it joins phrases rather than clauses, a comma before this *and* is almost always an error.
**Variants:** *both … as well as* is a common mix-up; use *both X and Y* or *X as well as Y*, not the hybrid

Adds two things together and puts emphasis on the fact that there are two — plain *and* reports the pair, *both … and* insists on it. It takes exactly two elements, and unlike *either … or*, a *both … and* subject always takes a plural verb.

1. She speaks *both* Spanish *and* Portuguese at home.
2. *Both* the coach *and* the players were fined.
3. The apartment is *both* cheaper *and* closer to campus.
4. He *both* wrote the score *and* conducted the premiere.
5. We ate *both* before the movie *and* after it.
6. The bill has *both* Republican *and* Democratic sponsors.

**See also:** [either … or](#either--or), [neither … nor](#neither--nor), [not only … but also](#not-only--but-also), [between … and](#between--and), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### either … or

**Pronunciation:** /ˈiðər/, /ˈaɪðər/ … /ɔr/, /ər/ &middot; *EE-thur (also EYE-thur) … OR, er*
**Type:** correlative (*either* on its own is a determiner, a pronoun, and a negative-echo adverb — *either way*, *I don't either*; the *or* half is the ordinary coordinator) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
**Pattern:** *either* [X] *or* [Y] &middot; *either* [subject] *or* [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one &middot; [subject] *either* [verb phrase] *or* [verb phrase] &middot; [verb] *either* [object] *or* [object] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *either* [complement] *or* [complement] &middot; *Either* [independent clause], *or* [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves when they join words or phrases. Put a comma before *or* only when it joins two independent clauses: *Either you tell him, or I will.* Two short imperatives sharing one understood subject count as coordinated verb phrases rather than clauses, so they take no comma: *Either finish the report or hand it off.*
**Variants:** *either … or else* (informal, adds a threat); *or … or* (archaic and poetic — the *either* dropped and the coordinator doubled instead: *or by force or by guile*)

Offers two alternatives and asserts that one of them holds. Against plain *or*, the *either* warns the listener in advance that a choice is coming and closes the list at two. The verb agrees with whichever subject stands nearer to it, which is the difference that trips learners up: *Either the twins or Maria **is** driving.*

1. We can *either* drive *or* take the train.
2. *Either* the twins *or* Maria is driving tonight.
3. *Either* you tell him, *or* I will.
4. The paint comes in *either* matte *or* gloss.
5. She's *either* brilliant *or* completely reckless.
6. *Either* finish the report *or* hand it off — I don't care which.

**See also:** [neither … nor](#neither--nor), [both … and](#both--and), [whether … or](#whether--or), [or … or](#or--or), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

### neither … nor

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniðər/, /ˈnaɪðər/ … /nɔr/ &middot; *NEE-thur (also NYE-thur) … NOR*
**Type:** correlative (*neither* on its own is a determiner, a pronoun, and an adverb — *neither one*, *me neither*; *nor* is also a plain coordinator) &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning formal in speech
**Clause type:** coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
**Pattern:** *neither* [X] *nor* [Y] &middot; *neither* [subject] *nor* [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one &middot; [subject] *neither* [verb phrase] *nor* [verb phrase] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *neither* [complement] *nor* [complement] &middot; [verb] *neither* [object] *nor* [object] &middot; *Neither* [independent clause], *nor* [inverted independent clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between short paired elements. Use a comma before *nor* when it joins two independent clauses, each of which then inverts: *Neither did he call, nor did he write.*
**Variants:** *nor … nor* (literary and archaic — the *neither* dropped and the coordinator doubled instead: *nor rain nor snow*); *neither … or* is nonstandard — the second half must be *nor*

Denies both alternatives at once; it is the exact negative of *both … and*. The pair already carries the negation, so the verb stays positive — *Neither of them was invited*, never *wasn't invited*. Everyday American speech usually reaches for *not … or* instead and saves *neither … nor* for writing.

1. *Neither* the manager *nor* the assistants were told about the merger.
2. He *neither* apologized *nor* offered to pay for the window.
3. The soup was *neither* hot *nor* cold.
4. *Neither* Maria *nor* the twins are driving.
5. She *neither* confirmed *nor* denied the report.
6. *Neither* rain *nor* snow keeps her off that trail.
7. *Neither* did he call, *nor* did he write.

**See also:** [either … or](#either--or), [both … and](#both--and), [not … but](#not--but), [nor … nor](#nor--nor), [nor](01-coordinating.md#nor)

### nor … nor

**Pronunciation:** /nɔr/ … /nɔr/, reduced /nər/ &middot; *NOR … NOR, nur* (the doubled form is rhetorical, so both halves normally keep full stress; only in running prose does the first flatten)
**Type:** correlative (both halves are the ordinary coordinator *nor*, the one FANBOYS member that carries its own negation; the same *nor* is the second half of *neither … nor* and the clause coordinator of *He didn't call, nor did he write*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic — an American meets it in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, hymn texts, and nineteenth-century verse, and essentially nowhere else
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — joins noun phrases, adjectives, and prepositional phrases, all of them negated
**Pattern:** *nor* [X] *nor* [Y] &middot; *nor* [subject] *nor* [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one &middot; [verb] *nor* [object] *nor* [object] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *nor* [complement] *nor* [complement] &middot; *nor* [X], *nor* [Y], *nor* [Z] (extended run)
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves in a two-item pair. Older printing commas every item in a longer run — *nor brass, nor stone, nor earth* — and that is the style to copy in period voice. The negation sits in the *nor* itself, so the verb stays positive: *nor gold nor threat could move him*, never *couldn't move him*.
**Variants:** *neither … nor* (the ordinary modern form), *nor … nor … nor* (extended run), *not … nor*, *neither … or* (nonstandard)

The *neither* dropped and the coordinator doubled in its place, so the opening *nor* does the announcing that *neither* normally does. The meaning is identical to *neither … nor*; only the register differs, and it differs enormously — this is verse and scripture, not a choice available in modern writing. Agreement follows the same rule as its modern twin: the verb matches whichever subject stands nearer to it.

1. *Nor* gold *nor* threat could move him from his purpose.
2. *Nor* sun *nor* star was seen for three days together.
3. There is *nor* peace *nor* profit in that quarrel.
4. *Nor* the captain *nor* his officers were ashore that night.
5. *Nor* by force *nor* by persuasion would the old woman give up the deed.
6. The wind spared *nor* roof, *nor* rick, *nor* fence along the whole valley.
7. *Nor* death *nor* life shall part us from that love.

**See also:** [neither … nor](#neither--nor), [or … or](#or--or), [both … and](#both--and), [not … but](#not--but), [nor](01-coordinating.md#nor)

### not … but

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt/ … /bʌt/, /bət/ &middot; *NOT … BUT, buht*
**Type:** correlative (*but* is also a plain coordinator and a preposition meaning "except" — *everyone but me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — corrective coordination
**Pattern:** *not* [X] *but* [Y] &middot; [subject] [verb] *not* [X] *but* [Y] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *not* [X] *but* [Y] &middot; *not* [X] *but rather* [Y] &middot; *not because* [clause] *but because* [clause] &middot; *not to* [verb] *but to* [verb] &middot; *not that* [clause] *but that* [clause]
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves when the elements are short. A comma before *but* is acceptable, and common, when the elements run long or the contrast is being pointed: *It wasn't the heat, but the humidity.*
**Variants:** *not … but rather*, *not … but instead*, *not so much … as* (softer)

Rejects one description and substitutes the correct one, so the weight of the sentence lands on the second half. Plain *but* adds a contrasting fact; *not … but* replaces — the first element is denied outright rather than qualified.

1. That was *not* a request *but* an order.
2. He came *not* to argue *but* to listen.
3. The problem is *not* the price *but* the delivery time.
4. She's *not* angry, *but* disappointed.
5. We canceled *not because* the numbers were bad *but because* the timing was wrong.
6. The best players are *not* the strongest *but* the most patient.

**See also:** [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [not only … but also](#not-only--but-also), [rather … than](#rather--than), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### not only … but also

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ˈoʊnli/ … /bʌt ˈɔlsoʊ/, /bət ˈɔlsoʊ/ &middot; *not-OHN-lee … but-AWL-soh*
**Type:** correlative (*but* is also a plain coordinator; *also* is a mobile adverb, which is why it can slide down the second half or surface as *as well* or *too*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
**Pattern:** [subject] *not only* [verb phrase] *but also* [verb phrase] &middot; *not only* [noun phrase] *but also* [noun phrase] &middot; [subject] + *be* + *not only* [complement] *but also* [complement] &middot; *not only* [subject] *but also* [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one &middot; *Not only* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb], *but* [subject] *also* [verb] &middot; *Not only* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb], *but* [clause] *as well*.
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the pair when it joins phrases. When *not only* is fronted and the clause inverts, put a comma before *but*: *Not only did he apologize, but he also sent flowers.*
**Variants:** *not only … but*, *not only … but … as well*, *not only … but … too*, *not just … but (also)*, *not merely … but* (formal)

Adds a second item and flags it as the more surprising or more important of the two, so the sentence builds toward the *but also* half. Fronting *not only* forces subject-auxiliary inversion the way any fronted negative does; leaving it mid-sentence does not. Like *either … or*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject.

1. He wants *not only* a raise *but also* a corner office.
2. *Not only* did she apologize, *but* she sent flowers *as well*.
3. The storm *not only* flooded the basement *but also* took out the fence.
4. *Not only* is the software free, *but* it runs on almost anything.
5. She's *not just* a good writer *but* a fast one.
6. *Not only* the players *but also* the coach was fined.

**See also:** [both … and](#both--and), [not … but](#not--but), [as much … as](#as-much--as), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### not so much … as

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt soʊ ˈmʌtʃ/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *not-soh-MUCH … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (*as* on its own is a preposition — *as a teacher* — and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner) &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly literary
**Clause type:** coordinate elements — corrective comparison
**Pattern:** [subject] + *be* + *not so much* [X] *as* [Y] &middot; [subject] [verb] *not so much* [phrase] *as* [phrase] &middot; *not so much* [prepositional phrase] *as* [prepositional phrase] &middot; *not so much because* [clause] *as because* [clause] &middot; *It's not so much that* [clause] *as that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves; a comma before *as* is optional and marks emphasis.
**Variants:** *not so much … as rather*, *less … than* (plainer), *more … than* (positive version)

Says the second description fits better than the first without throwing the first out entirely. It is the softened cousin of *not … but*, which rejects the first element flatly.

1. It's *not so much* a complaint *as* an observation.
2. He was *not so much* angry *as* embarrassed.
3. The trouble is *not so much that* she's late *as that* she never calls.
4. What sank the deal was *not so much* the price *as* the deadline.
5. She sang the last line *not so much* to the audience *as* to herself.
6. They lost *not so much because* the defense collapsed *as because* they kept giving the ball away.

**See also:** [not … but](#not--but), [less … than](#less--than), [more … than](#more--than), [as … as](#as--as)

### or … or

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr/ … /ɔr/, reduced /ər/ &middot; *OR … OR, er* (both halves take full stress in verse; in running speech the second flattens to *er*)
**Type:** correlative (both halves are the ordinary coordinator *or*; the first *or* simply stands where *either* would in the modern pair, which is why nothing else in the sentence has to change) &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic — Elizabethan drama, Milton, hymn texts, and nineteenth-century verse; in modern American prose it reads either as a typo or as costume
**Clause type:** coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
**Pattern:** *or* [X] *or* [Y] &middot; *or* [prepositional phrase] *or* [prepositional phrase] &middot; *or* [subject] *or* [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one &middot; [subject] [modal] *or* [bare infinitive] *or* [bare infinitive] &middot; *Or* [independent clause], *or* [independent clause]. &middot; *or* [X], *or* [Y], *or* [Z] (extended run)
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves when they join words or phrases. Put a comma before the second *or* when it joins two independent clauses, exactly as with *either … or*, and comma an extended run throughout.
**Variants:** *either … or* (the ordinary modern form), *or … or else*, *whether … or*, *be it … or*

The positive twin of *nor … nor*: the *either* is dropped and the coordinator itself doubles, so the opening *or* is what warns that a choice is coming. It asserts exactly what *either … or* asserts and differs only in register — *or by force or by guile* is period voice, and outside pastiche modern American English has no use for it. Like its modern twin it closes the list at two unless the run is deliberately extended.

1. *Or* by force *or* by guile, the town would be taken before winter.
2. He must *or* sell the land *or* mortgage it.
3. *Or* king *or* commoner, the fever made no distinction.
4. She would have her answer *or* tonight *or* never.
5. *Or* the sergeant *or* his men were lying, and the court meant to learn which.
6. The word will come *or* by rider *or* by post, but come it will.
7. *Or* fight *or* flee — there was no third road out of that valley.

**See also:** [either … or](#either--or), [nor … nor](#nor--nor), [whether … or](#whether--or), [be it … or](#be-it--or), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

### whether … or

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər/ … /ɔr/, /ər/ &middot; *WEH-thur … OR, er* (a homophone of *weather* for most Americans)
**Type:** correlative (*whether* is the rare half with no second job — it is only ever a subordinator; contrast *if*, which is both a conditional subordinator and a complementizer. The *or* half is the ordinary coordinator) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question); with *or not*, an adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** [verb] *whether* [clause] *or* [clause] &middot; [verb] *whether* [X] *or* [Y] &middot; [preposition] *whether* [clause] *or* [clause] &middot; *whether to* [verb] *or* [verb] &middot; *Whether* [clause] *or not*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whether* [clause] *or not*.
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the pair. When a concessive *whether … or …* clause opens the sentence, close it with a comma: *Whether you like it or not, we're going.* When the same clause trails the main clause, no comma: *We're going whether you like it or not.* A *whether* noun clause never takes a comma in either position.
**Variants:** *whether … or not*, *whether or not*, *whether … or whether*

Lays out two possibilities without choosing between them. As a noun clause it names an open question; with *or not* it turns concessive, meaning both possibilities lead to the same result. Unlike *if*, *whether* can follow a preposition, take *to* + infinitive, and open a sentence — *whether to go*, never *if to go*.

1. I can't tell *whether* he's serious *or* joking.
2. *Whether* you like it *or not*, the deadline stands.
3. She hasn't decided *whether* to fly *or* drive.
4. It depends on *whether* the permit clears *or* the city stalls again.
5. Ask him *whether* the store opens at eight *or* nine.
6. *Whether or not* they call back, I'm taking the other offer.
7. He'll say something at the meeting *whether* anyone asks him *or not*.

**See also:** [either … or](#either--or), [if … then](#if--then), [as much … as](#as-much--as), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

## Alternation, distribution, and enumeration

Pairs that divide something into two shares rather than joining two things. Not one of these halves is a conjunction in the strict sense — they are adverbs and adverbial phrases — but each first half opens a slot that the second half has to fill, and that expectation is what makes them behave as correlatives. Because both halves are adjuncts rather than coordinated elements, the parallelism rule loosens and the punctuation rule tightens: every pair here takes a comma between the halves, and where the halves front two independent clauses, that comma has to become a semicolon or a period.

### for one thing … for another (thing)

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈwʌn θɪŋ/ … /fɔr əˈnʌðər/ &middot; *for-WUN-thing … for-uh-NUH-thur*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are prepositional phrases used as sentence adverbials, not conjunctions; *for* is elsewhere a preposition and the causal coordinator of the FANBOYS) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, mostly spoken
**Clause type:** none — each half is a sentence adverbial standing in front of its own independent clause
**Pattern:** *For one thing*, [clause]. *For another*, [clause]. &middot; *For one thing*, [clause]; *for another*, [clause]. &middot; *For one thing*, [clause]. *For another thing*, [clause]. *And for a third*, [clause]. &middot; [clause] — *for one thing* [clause], *for another* [clause] &middot; *For one thing*, [clause]. (second half never supplied)
**Punctuation:** A comma after each half, separating it from the clause it introduces. Between the two clauses use a period or a semicolon — a comma alone leaves a splice, since both halves are adverbials and neither one joins anything.
**Variants:** *for one thing … for another thing*, *for one … for another*, *for a start … and then* (British-leaning), *first … second* (neutral)

Enumerates reasons for a judgment already announced, and claims by its shape that there is more than one. It is the conversational alternative to *first … second*, and the second half is often never supplied at all — *for one thing* on its own already promises a list.

1. *For one thing*, the rent is too high. *For another*, the commute runs an hour each way.
2. I wouldn't buy that car — *for one thing* it's out of warranty, *for another* the parts take weeks.
3. *For one thing*, nobody asked us; *for another*, nobody paid us.
4. She'd never last as a line cook: *for one thing*, she hates crowds; *for another*, she can't stand the heat.
5. *For one thing*, the data is two years old. *For another thing*, half the responses came from one county.
6. He shouldn't be driving. *For one thing*, he can't read the signs anymore.

**See also:** [on the one hand … on the other hand](#on-the-one-hand--on-the-other-hand), [partly … partly](#partly--partly), [first, second, third](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#first-second-third), [for example](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#for-example), [for](01-coordinating.md#for)

### now … now

**Pronunciation:** /naʊ/ … /naʊ/ &middot; *NOW … NOW*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are the time adverb *now* in an old distributive sense, "at one moment"; neither is a conjunction, which is why a coordinator can sit between them — *now hot, now cold* or *now hot and now cold*) &middot; **Register:** literary, archaic
**Clause type:** none — it pairs two adverbials, adjectives, or parallel clauses describing alternating states
**Pattern:** *now* [adjective], *now* [adjective] &middot; *now* [verb phrase], *now* [verb phrase] &middot; *now* [clause], *now* [clause] &middot; *now* [X], *now* [Y], *now* [Z] (extended alternation) &middot; *now* [X] *and now* [Y]
**Punctuation:** Comma between the halves — the pair reads as a list of alternating states, not as a joined phrase. Where the halves are full clauses, use a semicolon.
**Variants:** *now … and now*, *now … now again*, *sometimes … sometimes* (neutral), *one minute … the next* (conversational), *at one moment … at the next*

Marks a state that keeps switching back and forth: at one moment this, at the next that. The *now* here has nothing to do with present time — it is a distributive survival that lives on in poetry and heightened prose, and it should not be confused with the causal and temporal subordinator *now that*.

1. The wind was *now* at our backs, *now* in our faces.
2. She played the part *now* as comedy, *now* as tragedy.
3. The river runs *now* fast, *now* slow, depending on the month.
4. His voice rose and fell, *now* a whisper, *now* a shout.
5. The lamp swung over the water, *now* bright, *now* almost gone.
6. Prices climbed all winter, *now* by pennies *and now* by dollars.

**See also:** [one minute … the next](#one-minute--the-next), [partly … partly](#partly--partly), [now that](03-time.md#now-that), [meanwhile](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#meanwhile)

### on the one hand … on the other hand

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈwʌn hænd/ … /ɑn ði ˈʌðər hænd/ &middot; *on-thuh-WUN-hand … on-thee-UH-thur-hand*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are prepositional phrases working as conjunctive adverbs, not conjunctions; the second half has a full independent life without the first) &middot; **Register:** neutral, at home in essays and analysis
**Clause type:** none — each half is a sentence adverbial in front of its own independent clause
**Pattern:** *On the one hand*, [clause]; *on the other hand*, [clause]. &middot; *On the one hand*, [clause]. *On the other*, [clause]. &middot; *On the one hand* [noun phrase], *on the other* [noun phrase] &middot; [clause]; *on the other hand*, [clause]. (first half dropped) &middot; *On the one hand*, [clause]; *but on the other*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma after each half. Between the two clauses use a semicolon or a period — a comma alone is a splice, exactly as with *however*. Mind the article, which does not match across the halves: *the one hand* but *the other hand*.
**Variants:** *on the one hand … on the other*, *on one hand … on the other hand* (informal, the first *the* dropped), *on the one hand … but then again*, *while … on the other hand*

Lays out two sides of a question and weighs them against each other, framing an argument rather than joining grammatical elements. It is the one pair in the file whose halves are unequal in independence: *on the other hand* is a standard conjunctive adverb that needs no setup, while a first half with nothing after it leaves the reader waiting.

1. *On the one hand*, the rent is cheap; *on the other hand*, the commute is brutal.
2. *On the one hand* she wanted the promotion; *on the other*, she dreaded the travel.
3. *On the one hand*, the drug works. *On the other*, almost nobody can afford it.
4. The plan saves money. *On the other hand*, it costs us a month.
5. *On the one hand*, the numbers look strong; *on the other hand*, they come from a single quarter.
6. *On the one hand* a bigger kitchen, *on the other* a longer drive — we took the kitchen.

**See also:** [either … or](#either--or), [partly … partly](#partly--partly), [for one thing … for another (thing)](#for-one-thing--for-another-thing), [on the other hand](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#on-the-other-hand), [on the contrary](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#on-the-contrary)

### one minute … the next

**Pronunciation:** /wʌn ˈmɪnɪt/ … /ðə ˈnɛkst/ &middot; *wun-MIN-it … thuh NEKST*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are bare noun phrases used adverbially, the same construction as *this morning*; neither half is a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** informal, conversational
**Clause type:** none — each half is a time adverbial fronting its own clause
**Pattern:** *One minute* [clause], *the next* [clause]. &middot; *One minute* [X], *the next* [Y]. (both verbs dropped) &middot; *One minute* [clause] *and the next* [clause]. &middot; *One second / one moment / one day* [clause], *the next* [clause]. &middot; *One minute* [clause]; *the next*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma between the halves. Both halves front independent clauses, so careful writing upgrades that comma to a semicolon or a dash when the clauses run long; the plain comma survives in speech-like prose.
**Variants:** *one moment … the next*, *one second … the next*, *one day … the next*, *now … now* (literary), *first … then* (plain sequence, no surprise)

Reports a sudden switch between two states, with the shortness of the named interval doing all the emphasis. It is the everyday counterpart of literary *now … now*, and unlike a plain sequence it always implies the change was abrupt or out of character.

1. *One minute* the sun was out, *the next* it was pouring.
2. *One minute* he's your best friend, *the next* he won't answer a text.
3. *One minute* the server was fine; *the next*, half the site was down.
4. *One day* the store was busy, *the next* it was closed for good.
5. *One moment* she was laughing, *the next* she was in tears.
6. The market gave it all back — *one minute* we were up eight percent, *the next* we were flat.

**See also:** [now … now](#now--now), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [the minute](03-time.md#the-minute), [the moment](03-time.md#the-moment)

### partly … partly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑrtli/ … /ˈpɑrtli/ &middot; *PART-lee … PART-lee*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are the ordinary degree adverb *partly*; nothing here is a conjunction, and a coordinator between the halves is optional) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — it pairs two adjuncts, most often two *because* clauses or two prepositional phrases of cause
**Pattern:** *partly* [X], *partly* [Y] &middot; *partly because* [clause], *partly because* [clause] &middot; *partly* [prepositional phrase] *and partly* [prepositional phrase] &middot; *partly* [adjective], *partly* [adjective] &middot; *partly* [X], *partly* [Y], *and partly* [Z] (three or more shares)
**Punctuation:** Comma between the halves, since the second *partly* opens a fresh adjunct rather than a second element of one phrase: *partly because of the weather, partly because of the cost.* With *and* between the halves the comma is optional.
**Variants:** *partly … and partly*, *in part … in part* (formal), *half … half* (informal: *half amused, half annoyed*), *partly … mostly* (unequal shares)

Splits a cause, a description, or a composition into two shares, neither of which accounts for the whole. That is the difference from *both … and*, which asserts two complete facts: *partly … partly* concedes in advance that each half is only some of the story.

1. The delay was *partly* our fault, *partly* the weather.
2. She took the job *partly because* it paid more, *partly because* it was closer to home.
3. The wall is *partly* brick *and partly* plaster.
4. His expression was *partly* amusement, *partly* alarm.
5. The shortage is *partly* a supply problem *and partly* a pricing one.
6. The trip was *partly* research, *partly* vacation, *and partly* an excuse to see her sister.

**See also:** [both … and](#both--and), [on the one hand … on the other hand](#on-the-one-hand--on-the-other-hand), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

## Comparison and proportion

These pairs measure one thing against another. The second half is a genuine subordinator introducing the standard of comparison, and that clause is almost always elliptical — *as good as the original (is)*. Between them they cover the four possible verdicts: *more … than* ranks up, *less … than* ranks down, *as … as* levels, and *the same … as* claims identity.

### as … as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ … /æz/, reduced /əz/ … /əz/ &middot; *AZ … AZ* (both halves usually flatten to *uhz* in running speech)
**Type:** correlative (the first *as* is a degree adverb, the second the subordinator; *as* on its own is also a preposition — *as a teacher* — and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, usually elliptical
**Pattern:** *as* [adjective/adverb] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *as* [adjective/adverb] *as* [clause] &middot; *as* [adjective] *a(n)* [singular noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *as many/much* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *not as* [adjective] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *As* [adjective/adverb] *as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair. But when the whole *as … as* stretch is a fronted adverbial clause, close it with a comma at the seam, like any other fronted subordinate clause: *As soon as the door closed, everyone started talking.* Sitting in its ordinary place after the main clause it takes none: *Everyone started talking as soon as the door closed.*
**Variants:** *not as … as* (negative), *not so … as* (formal negative), *as … as ever / as … as possible* (fixed intensifiers)

States equality of degree: the first *as* is a degree adverb, the second is the conjunction that introduces the standard. Where *more … than* ranks one thing above another, *as … as* levels them. One trap in the noun pattern: with a singular count noun the article jumps to the far side of the adjective — *as good a manager as*, never *as a good manager as*.

1. The sequel is *as* good *as* the original.
2. Call me *as* soon *as* you land.
3. It didn't cost *as* much *as* I expected.
4. She works *as* hard *as* anyone on the floor.
5. Get here *as* early *as* you can.
6. He's *as* tall *as* his father is now.
7. She's *as* good a manager *as* the company has ever had.
8. *As* soon *as* the door closed, everyone started talking.

**See also:** [as much … as](#as-much--as), [not so … as](#not-so--as), [the same … as](#the-same--as), [more … than](#more--than), [so … that](#so--that)

### as … even so

**Pronunciation:** /æz/, /əz/ … /ˌivən ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *AZ, uhz … ee-vun-SOH* (the opening *as* is unstressed and normally reduces; the weight of the sentence lands on *so*)
**Type:** correlative (only the first half is a conjunction — *as* is elsewhere a preposition and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner; *even so* is the resumptive adverb *so* with an intensifier in front of it, which is why it can shrink to bare *so* without changing the grammar) &middot; **Register:** archaic and biblical — the register of the King James Bible, of hymn texts, and of pulpit and platform oratory in the older style
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, fronted, with the main clause following *even so*
**Pattern:** *As* [clause], *even so* [main clause]. &middot; *As* [clause], *even so* [verb] [subject]. (inverted main clause) &middot; *As* [noun phrase], *even so* [main clause]. (verbless first half) &middot; *Just as* [clause], *even so* [main clause]. &middot; *As* [clause], *even so also* [main clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma between the halves is required — the fronted *as* clause has to close before *even so* opens the main clause. Never a comma after *even so* here, and that is the fastest way to tell the pair from its look-alike: the free-standing conjunctive adverb *even so* does take one (*Even so, we went*) and needs a semicolon or a period in front of it, because it links two independent clauses instead of finishing a comparison.
**Variants:** *just as … so*, *as … so*, *as … so also*, *even as … so* (the same emphasis moved to the first half), *like as … so* (older still)

The emphatic form of *as … so*: *even* leans on the *so* and insists the second case matches the first point for point, where plain *so* merely draws the parallel. It is how scripture and the preaching built on it make an analogy, and outside that world — sermons, hymnody, older oratory, deliberate pastiche — an American will not meet it. Do not confuse the paired *even so* with the free-standing conjunctive adverb *even so*, which concedes what came before and then holds out against it rather than matching it.

1. *As* the sparks fly upward, *even so* man is born to trouble.
2. *As* the rain returns not to the cloud, *even so* the word goes not out in vain.
3. *As* the seed is sown, *even so* shall the harvest be.
4. *As* the body without the spirit is dead, *even so* is faith without works.
5. *As* the vine is pruned that it may bear, *even so* is a people chastened that it may endure.
6. *As* the fathers bore the heat of the day, *even so* must this generation bear it.
7. *As* the republic was founded in argument, *even so* will it be preserved by argument.

**See also:** [just as … so](#just-as--so), [as … as](#as--as), [the … the](#the--the), [as … so](08-comparison.md#as--so), [like as](08-comparison.md#like-as), [even so](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#even-so)

### as much … as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈmʌtʃ/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *az-MUCH … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (*as much as* is also a fixed concessive subordinator meaning "although," and an adverbial of approximation — *as much as $400*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison (quantity or degree), often elliptical
**Pattern:** *as much* [uncountable noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *as many* [countable noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *as much as* [clause] &middot; *not as much* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *As much as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair. When *as much as* opens a concessive clause, close that clause with a comma: *As much as I like him, I wouldn't hire him.*
**Variants:** *as many … as* (countable nouns), *not as much … as*, *as much as* (adverbial, "roughly")

The quantity version of *as … as*: *as much* with uncountable nouns, *as many* with countable ones. It has a second life as a fixed concessive, where *as much as I'd love to* simply means *although I'd love to*.

1. We don't have *as much* time *as* we thought.
2. She earns *as much* now *as* both her parents did.
3. There weren't *as many* people *as* last year.
4. He complained *as much as* everyone else did.
5. *As much as* I like the neighborhood, I can't afford it.
6. Take *as much* cake *as* you want.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [more … than](#more--than), [less … than](#less--than), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as)

### just as … so

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʒʌst æz/ … /soʊ/, reduced /ˈdʒəs əz/ &middot; *JUST-az … SOH* (the stress sits on *just*, not on *as*)
**Type:** correlative (*so* is also a coordinator — *I was tired, so I left* — a degree adverb, and a pro-form standing in for a whole clause: *I think so*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** *Just as* [clause], *so* [clause]. &middot; *Just as* [clause], *so too* [clause]. &middot; *Just as* [clause], *so* [verb] [subject]. (inverted second half) &middot; *As* [clause], *so* [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma between the halves is required — the *just as* clause is fronted and has to be closed off before *so* begins the main clause.
**Variants:** *as … so*, *just as … so too*, *just as … so also*, *as … even so* (biblical and emphatic: *as the sparks fly upward, even so man is born to trouble*)

Sets up a proportion: what holds in the first situation holds in the same way in the second. It is the pulpit-and-essay relative of *the … the*, which does the same comparison in ordinary speech.

1. *Just as* the coach demanded loyalty, *so* he gave it.
2. *Just as* a garden needs weeding, *so* a budget needs pruning.
3. *As* the twig is bent, *so* grows the tree.
4. *Just as* athletes taper before a race, *so too* should writers rest before a deadline.
5. *Just as* the city rebuilt after the fire, *so* it will rebuild after the flood.
6. *Just as* a bad hire costs a year, *so* a good one pays for a decade.

**See also:** [the … the](#the--the), [as … even so](#as--even-so), [as … as](#as--as), [so … that](#so--that)

### less … than

**Pronunciation:** /lɛs/ … /ðæn/, /ðən/ &middot; *LES … THAN, thuhn*
**Type:** correlative (*less* is also a determiner, a pronoun, and a degree adverb; *than* is prepositional in *less than me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, usually elliptical
**Pattern:** *less* [adjective/adverb] *than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; *less* [uncountable noun] *than* [noun phrase] &middot; [verb] *less than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; *less of a* [noun] *than* [noun phrase] &middot; *fewer* [countable noun] *than* [noun phrase/clause]
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *fewer … than* (countable nouns), *not as … as* (everyday equivalent), *less of a … than*

Ranks the first item below the second — the mirror image of *more … than*. Careful American usage keeps *less* for uncountable amounts and *fewer* for things you can count one by one, though *less* with countables is everywhere in speech and on grocery-store signs.

1. This route is *less* direct *than* the highway.
2. The repair cost *less than* we were quoted.
3. There are *fewer* seats *than* there were last season.
4. He seemed *less* interested *than* his partner did.
5. It took *less* effort *than* I expected.
6. The rewrite was *less of a* rescue *than* a demolition.

**See also:** [more … than](#more--than), [as … as](#as--as), [the same … as](#the-same--as), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [rather … than](#rather--than)

### more … than

**Pronunciation:** /mɔr/ … /ðæn/, /ðən/ &middot; *MOR … THAN, thuhn*
**Type:** correlative (*more* is also a determiner, a pronoun, and a degree adverb; *than* is prepositional in *taller than me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, usually elliptical
**Pattern:** *more* [adjective/adverb] *than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; [short adjective]*-er than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; *more* [noun] *than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; [verb] *more than* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; *more* [adjective] *than* [adjective] (two properties of one thing: *more tired than hungry*)
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *-er … than* (short adjectives: *bigger than*, *sooner than*), *no more … than*, *rather … than* (preference, not degree)

Ranks the first item above the second; the *-er* ending is the same correlative with the first half fused onto the adjective. When the second half is a bare pronoun, formal usage takes the subject form because a verb is understood — *taller than I (am)* — while ordinary speech says *than me*.

1. The drive is *more* scenic *than* the flight.
2. She got here *sooner than* we did.
3. It rained *more* this June *than* in the last three Junes combined.
4. He knows *more* about the code *than* anyone left on the team.
5. The book is *better than* the movie.
6. There were *more* volunteers *than* we had jobs for.
7. By the third day she was *more* bored *than* homesick.

**See also:** [less … than](#less--than), [as … as](#as--as), [the same … as](#the-same--as), [rather … than](#rather--than), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than)

### not so … as

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt soʊ/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *not-SOH … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (*so* is also a coordinator and a degree adverb; *as* is also a preposition and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner) &middot; **Register:** formal, old-fashioned (everyday American uses *not as … as*)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, usually elliptical
**Pattern:** *not so* [adjective/adverb] *as* [noun phrase/clause] &middot; *not so* [adjective] *as to* [verb] &middot; *not so much as* [verb] (= not even) &middot; *not so much* [X] *as* [Y]
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *not as … as* (neutral), *not so much … as*, *not so much as* + verb (= not even), *so … as to* (result)

The older negative of *as … as*, kept alive in careful writing and in set phrases like *not so fast* and *not so bad as all that*. American English overwhelmingly prefers *not as … as* in speech, and the positive *so … as* (*so good as this*) is now archaic outside fixed expressions.

1. The winter was *not so* harsh *as* the one before it.
2. He is *not so* young *as* he looks.
3. The rules are *not so* strict *as* people assume.
4. She was *not so* foolish *as to* sign it without reading it.
5. It's *not so* simple *as* it sounds.
6. She didn't *so much as* glance at the check.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [so … as to](#so--as-to), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [less … than](#less--than)

### such … as

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *SUCH … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (*such* is a determiner, not a conjunction — only *as* is; *as* is elsewhere a preposition and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** comparison clause of kind; introduces an example or a standard
**Pattern:** *such* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *such a* [singular noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *such* [noun] *as* [clause] &middot; *such … as to* [verb] &middot; [noun phrase], *such as* [examples], [rest of clause]
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as* when the *such* phrase restricts the noun. When *such as* introduces a nonessential list after an already complete noun phrase, set the list off with commas: *citrus fruits, such as lemons and limes, keep for weeks*.
**Variants:** *such as* (standalone, "for example"), *such … as to* + infinitive, *such … that* (result — a different construction)

Points at a kind: *such* names the type and *as* introduces the example or standard that fixes it. Keep it apart from *such … that*, which measures a degree and reports a consequence.

1. We don't stock *such* items *as* these anymore.
2. He had never faced *such a* decision *as* this one.
3. Bring *such* documents *as* the clerk asks for.
4. Root vegetables, *such as* carrots and parsnips, keep all winter.
5. Her tone was *such as to* end the discussion.
6. I've never heard *such* nonsense *as* came out of that meeting.

**See also:** [such … that](#such--that), [as … as](#as--as), [the same … as](#the-same--as), [so … as to](#so--as-to)

### the … the

**Pronunciation:** /ðə/ … /ðə/ (before a vowel /ði/) &middot; *thuh … thuh*
**Type:** correlative (neither half is the definite article; both are the Old English instrumental *þy*, and this *the* has no other use in the language) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** paired comparative clauses — the first is subordinate, the second is the main clause
**Pattern:** *The* [comparative] [subject] [verb], *the* [comparative] [subject] [verb]. &middot; *The* [comparative] [noun phrase], *the* [comparative] [clause]. (verb omitted in the first half: *The higher the rate, …*) &middot; *The more* [noun] [clause], *the more* [noun] [clause]. &middot; *The* [comparative], *the* [comparative]. (both verbs dropped: *The sooner the better.*)
**Punctuation:** Comma between the halves in the full-clause version: *The more you practice, the easier it gets.* Short verbless pairs normally drop it: *The sooner the better.*
**Variants:** *the more … the more*, *the more … the less*, *the sooner … the better*, *the … the merrier*

States that two quantities move together — change one and the other follows. The *the* here is not the article but a fossil of an old instrumental form meaning "by that much," which is why it can sit in front of a comparative.

1. *The* more you practice, *the* easier it gets.
2. *The* longer we waited, *the* worse the line got.
3. *The* sooner *the* better.
4. *The* less he said, *the* more suspicious she became.
5. *The* higher the interest rate, *the* fewer people qualify.
6. *The* more people we invite, *the* more food we'll need.

**See also:** [just as … so](#just-as--so), [more … than](#more--than), [as … as](#as--as)

### the same … as

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈseɪm/ … /æz/, /əz/ &middot; *thuh SAME … AZ, uhz*
**Type:** correlative (*the same* is article + adjective; only *as* is the conjunction, and that *as* is elsewhere a preposition — *as a teacher* — and a subordinator of time, reason, and manner; swapping in *that* turns the second half into a relative clause and changes the meaning) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison (identity), usually elliptical
**Pattern:** [X] *is the same as* [Y] &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [noun phrase] &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [clause] &middot; [verb] *the same as* [clause] &middot; *the same* [noun] *that* [relative clause] (identity of the one thing, not of the kind)
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as*.
**Variants:** *same as* (informal, article dropped), *much the same … as* (hedged), *the same … as ever*, *the same … that* (relative clause — a different construction)

Completes the comparison set: *more … than* ranks one thing above another, *less … than* below, *as … as* levels them, and *the same … as* claims they are of one kind. The choice between *as* and *that* after *same* decides what is being asserted — *the same truck as we rented* means one like it, *the same truck that we rented* means literally that vehicle. American speech drops the article freely in the standalone form (*same as always*), which is informal but not an error.

1. Her handwriting looks *the same as* her mother's.
2. He ordered *the same* sandwich *as* I did.
3. The new lease has *the same* terms *as* the one it replaced.
4. Filing online works *the same as* mailing the form in.
5. Nothing in town tasted *the same as* it had before the fire.
6. *Same as* always — black coffee, no sugar.
7. She still drives *the same* truck *that* she bought in 2004.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [more … than](#more--than), [less … than](#less--than), [such … as](#such--as), [the same as (standalone)](08-comparison.md#the-same-as)

## Degree and result

The first half measures an amount; the second half reports what that amount causes. Which pair you need depends on what the degree word attaches to: *so* takes adjectives and adverbs, *such* takes noun phrases, and *too* and *enough* take an infinitive instead of a clause.

### enough … to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈnʌf/ … /tu/, /tə/ &middot; *ih-NUF … TOO, tuh*
**Type:** correlative (*enough* is also a determiner, a pronoun, and a degree adverb; the *to* here is the infinitive marker, not the preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** infinitival clause of degree and result
**Pattern:** [adjective/adverb] *enough to* [verb] &middot; *enough* [noun] *to* [verb] &middot; [adjective] *enough for* [noun] *to* [verb] &middot; [verb] *enough to* [verb] &middot; [adjective] *enough that* [clause]
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *enough … that* + clause (*warm enough that we ate outside*), *sufficiently … to* (formal)

Says the degree reaches the threshold the action requires — the positive counterpart of *too … to*. Mind the word order, which is unlike anything else in English: *enough* follows an adjective or adverb (*old enough*) but precedes a noun (*enough chairs*).

1. She's old *enough to* vote.
2. We have *enough* chairs *to* seat forty.
3. He didn't run fast *enough to* catch the bus.
4. The ice was thick *enough for* the truck *to* cross.
5. The offer was good *enough to* take seriously.
6. It got warm *enough that* we ate on the porch.
7. He saved *enough* over two summers *to* pay for the first year.

**See also:** [too … to](#too--to), [so … that](#so--that), [so … as to](#so--as-to)

### so … as to

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ … /æz tu/, reduced /əz tə/ &middot; *SOH … az-too, uhz-tuh* (the degree word *so* carries the stress; *as to* is unstressed and normally reduces)
**Type:** correlative (*so* is also a coordinator and a degree adverb; *as to* elsewhere is a two-word preposition meaning "regarding" — *no word as to when*) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** infinitival clause of result
**Pattern:** *so* [adjective/adverb] *as to* [verb] &middot; *not so* [adjective] *as to* [verb] &middot; *be so* [adjective] *as to* [verb] (courtesy formula: *be so kind as to*) &middot; *such … as to* [verb] (with a noun phrase)
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *so … that* (everyday form), *such … as to*, *so much as to*

The compact, formal version of *so … that*: it names a degree and its consequence without building a second clause. Outside legal and academic prose most of its working life is spent in the fixed politeness formula *would you be so kind as to*.

1. The margin was *so* narrow *as to* be meaningless.
2. Would you be *so* kind *as to* sign at the bottom?
3. His account was *so* detailed *as to* leave no doubt.
4. She was not *so* careless *as to* leave the door unlocked.
5. The evidence is *so* thin *as to* invite an appeal.
6. She spoke *so* softly *as to* be almost inaudible.

**See also:** [so … that](#so--that), [not so … as](#not-so--as), [such … as](#such--as), [too … to](#too--to)

### so … that

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ … /ðæt/, /ðət/ &middot; *SOH … THAT, thuht*
**Type:** correlative (*so* is also a coordinator — *I was tired, so I left* — and half of the purpose subordinator *so that*; *that* is also a complementizer, a relative pronoun, and a demonstrative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result
**Pattern:** *so* [adjective/adverb] *that* [clause] &middot; *so* [adjective/adverb] [clause] (*that* dropped) &middot; *so much/many/little/few* [noun] *that* [clause] &middot; [verb] *so much that* [clause] &middot; *So* [adjective] *was* [subject] *that* [clause] (formal inversion)
**Punctuation:** No comma before *that*. Do not confuse this pair with the purpose conjunction *so that*, where the two words form a single unit and are not split by an adjective — *He left early so that he could beat the traffic*.
**Variants:** *so … as to* (formal), *so much … that*, *so many … that*, *such … that* (with noun phrases)

Measures a degree in the first half and reports its consequence in the second. *So* attaches to adjectives, adverbs, and quantity words; when the degree word needs to sit on a noun phrase instead, the pair becomes *such … that*.

1. It was *so* hot *that* we gave up and went inside.
2. She spoke *so* quietly *that* nobody in back could hear.
3. There were *so many* applicants *that* they extended the deadline.
4. The line moved *so* slowly we left.
5. He had *so little* time *that* he skipped lunch.
6. *So* convincing was the demo *that* they signed on the spot.

**See also:** [such … that](#such--that), [so … as to](#so--as-to), [too … to](#too--to), [enough … to](#enough--to), [so](01-coordinating.md#so)

### such … that

**Pronunciation:** /sʌtʃ/ … /ðæt/, /ðət/ &middot; *SUCH … THAT, thuht*
**Type:** correlative (*such* is a determiner, not a conjunction — only *that* is; *that* is elsewhere a complementizer, a relative pronoun, and a demonstrative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result
**Pattern:** *such a/an* [adjective] [singular noun] *that* [clause] &middot; *such* [adjective] [plural or uncountable noun] *that* [clause] &middot; *such* [noun] [clause] (*that* dropped) &middot; *Such was* [noun phrase] *that* [clause] (formal inversion)
**Punctuation:** No comma before *that*.
**Variants:** *such … as to* (formal), *such … Ø* (*that* dropped in speech), *so … that* (with adjectives and adverbs)

The noun-phrase counterpart of *so … that* — the degree word attaches to a noun instead of an adjective. Watch where the article lands: *such a good movie that…* but *so good a movie that…*.

1. It was *such* a long meeting *that* two people walked out.
2. They had *such* bad luck *that* they stopped entering.
3. She showed *such* patience *that* the whole room calmed down.
4. It's *such* a small town *that* everyone knows your car.
5. *Such* was the noise *that* we had to shout to be heard.
6. He told *such* a good story nobody noticed the check had come.

**See also:** [so … that](#so--that), [such … as](#such--as), [so … as to](#so--as-to)

### too … to

**Pronunciation:** /tu/ … /tu/, /tə/ &middot; *TOO … too, tuh* (the first *too* keeps its full vowel; the *to* reduces)
**Type:** correlative (*too* is also the additive adverb of *me too* and a bare intensifier — *too loud*; the *to* here is the infinitive marker, not the preposition) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** infinitival clause of degree and result
**Pattern:** *too* [adjective/adverb] *to* [verb] &middot; *too* [adjective] *for* [noun] *to* [verb] &middot; *too much/many* [noun] *to* [verb] &middot; [verb] *too* [adverb] *to* [verb] &middot; *only too* [adjective] *to* [verb]
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *too … for* + noun (no infinitive), *not too … to*, *only too … to* (emphatic and positive: *only too glad to help*)

Says the degree passes a limit, so the action does not happen — it carries a built-in negative that *so … that* does not. *He was too tired to drive* means he did not drive; *He was so tired that he drove badly* means he did.

1. He was *too* tired *to* drive home.
2. The box is *too* heavy *to* lift by yourself.
3. She spoke *too* fast *for* me *to* follow.
4. There's *too much* going on *to* think straight.
5. It's *too* early *to* call the race.
6. I was *only too* glad *to* help.

**See also:** [enough … to](#enough--to), [so … that](#so--that), [so … as to](#so--as-to)

## Immediate sequence

Four pairs that say the second event landed almost on top of the first. All four put the earlier event in the past perfect, all four require subject-auxiliary inversion when the negative adverb is fronted, and all four take no comma between the halves. The one thing that separates them is the second half: *no sooner* is a comparative and demands *than*, while *hardly*, *scarcely*, and *barely* take *when* (or, a little less often, *before*). *No sooner … when* and *hardly … than* are the two crossover errors to watch for.

### barely … when

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɛrli/ … /wɛn/ &middot; *BAIR-lee … WEN*
**Type:** correlative (*barely* is also a plain degree adverb — *barely enough*; *when* is also an interrogative and a relative adverb) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** [subject] *had barely* [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; *Barely had* [subject] [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had barely* [past participle] *before* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *was barely* [complement] *when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. Fronting *barely* requires inversion of subject and auxiliary; leaving it after the subject does not.
**Variants:** *barely … before*, *hardly … when*, *scarcely … when*

Says the second event arrived before the first had finished settling. Of the three *when* pairs it is the most conversational, and Americans far more often leave *barely* in place after the subject than front it.

1. We had *barely* sat down *when* the fire alarm went off.
2. *Barely had* the game started *when* the rain came.
3. She had *barely* hung up *when* he called back.
4. I'd *barely* opened the box *when* I saw the crack.
5. The plane had *barely* left the gate *when* they turned it around.
6. We were *barely* through the first song *before* the power cut out.

**See also:** [hardly … when](#hardly--when), [scarcely … when](#scarcely--when), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than)

### hardly … when

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɑrdli/ … /wɛn/ &middot; *HARD-lee … WEN*
**Type:** correlative (*hardly* is also a plain degree adverb — *hardly anyone*; *when* is also an interrogative and a relative adverb) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Hardly had* [subject] [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had hardly* [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had hardly* [past participle] *before* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *was hardly* [complement] *when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. Fronted *hardly* requires subject-auxiliary inversion — *Hardly had we left*, never *Hardly we had left*.
**Variants:** *hardly … before*, *scarcely … when*, *barely … when*

Marks two events as nearly simultaneous, the first barely complete before the second began. Its second half is *when* or *before* — pairing it with *than* is the classic crossover error with *no sooner … than*.

1. *Hardly had* she left *when* the phone rang.
2. We had *hardly* unpacked *when* the neighbors came over.
3. *Hardly had* the words left his mouth *when* he regretted them.
4. The ink was *hardly* dry *when* the terms changed.
5. I had *hardly* started the engine *before* the light turned red.
6. *Hardly had* the season opened *when* their best pitcher went down.

**See also:** [scarcely … when](#scarcely--when), [barely … when](#barely--when), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than)

### no sooner … than

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈsunər/ … /ðæn/, /ðən/ &middot; *noh-SOO-nur … THAN, thuhn*
**Type:** correlative (*sooner* is the comparative of the adverb *soon*, which is why the pair takes *than*; *than* on its own is prepositional in *than me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *No sooner had* [subject] [past participle] *than* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had no sooner* [past participle] *than* [clause]. &middot; *No sooner was* [subject] [complement] *than* [clause]. &middot; *No sooner does* [subject] [verb] *than* [clause]. (present-tense habitual)
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. Fronted *no sooner* forces inversion of the subject and the auxiliary.
**Variants:** none — *no sooner … when* is a frequent error; the second half is always *than*

The tightest of the immediate-sequence pairs: the second event follows so fast it seems to interrupt the first. *Sooner* is a comparative, which is exactly why the pair takes *than*, unlike *hardly*, *scarcely*, and *barely*, which take *when* or *before*.

1. *No sooner had* we sat down *than* the fire alarm went off.
2. *No sooner had* she fixed one bug *than* two more appeared.
3. He had *no sooner* signed the lease *than* he lost the job.
4. *No sooner was* the tent up *than* the wind knocked it flat.
5. *No sooner had* the news broken *than* the stock dropped.
6. *No sooner does* one machine come back online *than* another goes down.

**See also:** [hardly … when](#hardly--when), [scarcely … when](#scarcely--when), [barely … when](#barely--when), [more … than](#more--than)

### scarcely … when

**Pronunciation:** /ˈskɛrsli/ … /wɛn/ &middot; *SKAIRS-lee … WEN*
**Type:** correlative (*scarcely* is also a plain degree adverb — *scarcely audible*; *when* is also an interrogative and a relative adverb) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Scarcely had* [subject] [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had scarcely* [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; *Scarcely* [noun phrase] *had* [past participle] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had scarcely* [past participle] *before* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves; inversion is required whenever *scarcely* is fronted.
**Variants:** *scarcely … before*, *hardly … when*, *barely … when*

The bookish member of the group — identical in meaning to *hardly … when*, but rare in American speech and mostly confined to written narrative.

1. *Scarcely had* the door closed *when* the arguing started.
2. He had *scarcely* finished speaking *when* the lights failed.
3. *Scarcely had* they reached the summit *when* the storm broke.
4. The town had *scarcely* recovered *when* the second flood came.
5. *Scarcely* a week had passed *when* the letter arrived.
6. The ship had *scarcely* cleared the harbor *before* the fog closed in.

**See also:** [hardly … when](#hardly--when), [barely … when](#barely--when), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than)

## Preference and substitution

Two pairs that put one option ahead of another. The first half names what is chosen and the second half what is turned down, so the parallelism rule bites hard here: whatever form stands after the first half has to stand after *than* as well.

### rather … than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðər/ … /ðæn/, /ðən/ &middot; *RA-thur … THAN, thuhn*
**Type:** correlative (*rather* alone is a degree adverb — *rather cold* — and a corrective discourse marker — *or rather*; *than* is prepositional in *than me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate elements; with a bare infinitive, an adverbial clause of preference
**Pattern:** [X] *rather than* [Y] &middot; [verb] [X] *rather than* [Y] &middot; *Rather than* [bare infinitive or *-ing*], [main clause]. &middot; [subject] *chose to* [verb] *rather than* [bare infinitive] &middot; [subject] *would rather* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive]
**Punctuation:** No comma when *rather than* joins two parallel elements inside a clause. When a *rather than* phrase is fronted, close it with a comma: *Rather than argue, he left.* A comma before a trailing *rather than* is optional and marks it as an afterthought.
**Variants:** *instead of* (takes a noun or *-ing* only), *as opposed to*, *sooner … than*

Marks the first element as chosen and the second as rejected. It differs from *instead of* in what it can take: *rather than* accepts a bare infinitive (*rather than wait*), while *instead of* cannot (*instead of waiting*). Keep both sides in the same grammatical form.

1. We took the train *rather than* the bus.
2. *Rather than* argue about it, he just paid the fee.
3. She'd call it a delay *rather than* a cancellation.
4. The change helps borrowers *rather than* banks.
5. He chose to resign *rather than* be fired.
6. *Rather than* raising prices, they cut the portion size.

**See also:** [would rather … than](#would-rather--than), [more … than](#more--than), [not … but](#not--but), [less … than](#less--than)

### would rather … than

**Pronunciation:** /wʊd ˈræðər/, contracted /d ˈræðər/ … /ðæn/, reduced /ðən/ &middot; *wood-RA-thur … THAN, thuhn* (usually heard as *I'd RA-thur … thuhn*)
**Type:** correlative (*rather* alone is a degree adverb and a corrective discourse marker; *than* is prepositional in *than me*; *would rather* is a fixed modal idiom that becomes a correlative only when *than* arrives) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate bare-infinitive phrases; with a new subject, a clause of unreal preference
**Pattern:** [subject] *would rather* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive] &middot; [subject]*'d rather* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive] &middot; *Would* [subject] *rather* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive]? &middot; [subject] *would rather have* [past participle] *than* [past participle] &middot; [subject] *would rather* [new subject] [past-form verb] (unreal: *I'd rather you didn't*)
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the pair.
**Variants:** *'d rather … than*, *would sooner … than*, *would just as soon … as*, *had rather … than* (archaic)

States a personal preference between two actions, both in the bare infinitive — no *to* on either side. When the preference is about someone else's behavior, the second half becomes a clause with a past-tense verb that carries present meaning: *I'd rather you didn't smoke.*

1. I'd *rather* walk *than* wait for the shuttle.
2. She'd *rather* pay the fine *than* go to traffic school.
3. *Would* you *rather* meet Tuesday *than* Thursday?
4. I'd *rather* you didn't mention it to him.
5. He'd *rather have* stayed home *than* sat through that.
6. They'd *sooner* close the store *than* sell it.

**See also:** [rather … than](#rather--than), [more … than](#more--than), [whether … or](#whether--or)

## Condition, concession, range, and cause

Pairs whose halves are not both conjunctions in the strict sense — *between*, *from*, and *what with* are prepositions, *be it* is an inverted subjunctive verb, and the *then* of *if … then*, the *yet* of *though … yet*, and the *there* of *where … there* are adverbs — but which behave as correlatives because the first half fixes what the second half has to be. Three of them, *if … then*, *though … yet*, and *where … there*, are also the only pairs in the category whose second half can simply be dropped; everywhere else, losing a half breaks the sentence.

### be it … or

**Pronunciation:** /bi ɪt/ … /ɔr/, /ər/ &middot; *bee-IT … OR, er*
**Type:** correlative (an inverted present subjunctive of *be* plus the ordinary coordinator *or*; the same fossilized inversion drives *were it not for* and *had I known*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition (= *whether it is X or Y*)
**Pattern:** *Be it* [X] *or* [Y], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *be it* [X] *or* [Y]. &middot; *Be it* [X], [Y], *or* [Z], [main clause]. (three or more) &middot; *Be they* [X] *or* [Y], [main clause]. (plural subject) &middot; *Be it ever so* [adjective], [main clause]. (fixed: *be it ever so humble*)
**Punctuation:** Set the whole *be it* phrase off with a comma wherever it sits, fronted or trailing — unlike most subordinate halves in this file, it keeps its comma in both positions, because it comments on the main clause rather than restricting it. No comma between the halves.
**Variants:** *be it … or be it*, *be they … or*, *whether it be … or* (uninverted), *whether … or* (neutral), *no matter whether … or*

Says the main claim holds whichever of the listed possibilities turns out to be true — the compressed, inverted form of *whether it is X or Y*. The verb is the bare subjunctive *be*, never *is*, and the inversion is what marks the clause as concessive rather than as a command: *Be it rain or shine, the market opens at six.*

1. *Be it* rain *or* shine, the market opens at six.
2. Every application gets the same review, *be it* from a freshman *or* a full professor.
3. *Be it* a typo *or* a lie, the number in that filing is wrong.
4. The fee is the same, *be it* one night *or* seven.
5. *Be they* citizens *or* visitors, everyone waits in the same line.
6. *Be it* ever so humble, the cabin was warm and the roof held.

**See also:** [whether … or](#whether--or), [either … or](#either--or), [whether or not](07-concession.md#whether-or-not), [come what may](07-concession.md#come-what-may), [were](06-condition.md#were)

### between … and

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈtwin/ … /ænd/, /ən/ &middot; *bih-TWEEN … AND, uhn*
**Type:** correlative frame (*between* is a preposition, not a conjunction — only *and* is, and that *and* is the ordinary coordinator; *between* is also an adverb in *in between*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — it pairs two noun phrases inside a prepositional phrase, coordinating without creating a clause
**Pattern:** *between* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *between* [number] *and* [number] &middot; *the difference between* [X] *and* [Y] &middot; *between* [*-ing* phrase] *and* [*-ing* phrase] &middot; *Between* [X] *and* [Y], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. When a *between … and* phrase opens the sentence as an adverbial of cause, close it with a comma: *Between the traffic and the rain, we were an hour late.*
**Variants:** *from … to* (ranges and paths), *among* (three or more not treated pairwise)

Fixes two endpoints — of a range, a relationship, or a comparison — and *and* is the only legal second half. *Between X to Y* is always an error. *Between* is fine with more than two items when each is being paired against the others (*trade between the fifty states*), but a plain list of three usually wants *among*.

1. The store is open *between* nine *and* six.
2. Somewhere *between* Tulsa *and* Amarillo, the radio died.
3. What's the difference *between* a lease *and* a rental agreement?
4. *Between* the traffic *and* the rain, we missed the first act.
5. The bill runs *between* forty *and* fifty dollars a month.
6. *Between* fixing the roof *and* replacing the water heater, the savings are gone.

**See also:** [from … to](#from--to), [what with … and](#what-with--and), [both … and](#both--and), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### from … to

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm/, /frəm/ … /tu/, /tə/ &middot; *FRUM, fruhm … TOO, tuh*
**Type:** correlative frame (both halves are prepositions, not conjunctions; the *to* here is the preposition, not the infinitive marker of *too … to*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — it pairs two noun phrases as the endpoints of a range or a path
**Pattern:** *from* [X] *to* [Y] &middot; *from* [time] *to* [time] &middot; *from* [X] *through* [Y] (inclusive) &middot; [verb] *from* [place] *to* [place] &middot; *everything from* [X] *to* [Y] (spread of kinds)
**Punctuation:** No comma between the halves. Never mix the words with a dash: write *from 1999 to 2003* or *1999–2003*, but not *from 1999–2003*.
**Variants:** *from … through* (inclusive of the last unit), *from … until*, *from … till*, *between … and*

Marks a starting point and an ending point — in space, in time, or across a spread of kinds. Where *between … and* names two endpoints as a bounded interval, *from … to* traces extent or movement across it, and only *from … to* can describe a path.

1. The shop is open *from* ten *to* six.
2. We drove *from* Denver *to* Santa Fe in a day.
3. The class covers everything *from* soldering *to* firmware.
4. Prices dropped *from* thirty dollars *to* nineteen.
5. She read the report *from* cover *to* cover.
6. The permit runs *from* June *through* September.

**See also:** [between … and](#between--and), [the … the](#the--the), [what with … and](#what-with--and)

### if … then

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf/ … /ðɛn/ &middot; *IF … THEN*
**Type:** correlative (*if* is also a complementizer in indirect questions — *ask if he's home*; *then* is an adverb, not a conjunction, which is exactly why it can be dropped) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** *If* [clause], *then* [main clause]. &middot; *If* [clause], [main clause]. (*then* dropped — the ordinary form) &middot; [main clause] *if* [clause]. (no comma, no *then*) &middot; *If not* [X], *then* [Y].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *if* clause when it comes first, whether or not *then* follows; the comma goes before *then*, never after it. When the *if* clause trails the main clause, use no comma — and *then* is not available in that order.
**Variants:** *then* is always optional; compare *unless … then*, *when … then*

One of only two correlatives whose second half can be dropped freely — the other is *though … yet*. *Then* adds no meaning, only logical sharpness, which is why it survives in mathematics, contracts, and code and is usually left out of ordinary prose. Examples 6 and 7 show the two forms that lose it: the ordinary comma'd version with *then* dropped, and the reversed order, where the trailing *if* clause takes no comma and cannot take *then* at all.

1. *If* it rains, *then* we'll move the ceremony inside.
2. *If* the card is declined, *then* the order is canceled automatically.
3. *If* you're not going, *then* neither am I.
4. *If* that's the best offer, *then* I'll pass.
5. *If not* this week, *then* sometime next month.
6. *If* the meeting runs long, I'll text you.
7. Call the front desk *if* the heat doesn't come on.

**See also:** [whether … or](#whether--or), [either … or](#either--or), [though … yet](#though--yet), [the … the](#the--the)

### though … yet

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊ/ … /jɛt/ &middot; *THOH … YET*
**Type:** correlative (*though* is also a plain concessive subordinator — *though it rained, we went* — and a sentence-final adverb meaning "however": *Nice, though.*; *yet* is also one of the seven coordinators and a time adverb — *not yet*) &middot; **Register:** formal and old-fashioned in the paired form — modern American keeps *though* and drops *yet*; the full pair survives mainly in sermons, legal prose, and older literature
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Though* [clause], *yet* [main clause]. &middot; *Although* [clause], *yet* [main clause]. &middot; *Though* [clause], *still* [main clause]. &middot; *Though* [adjective or noun phrase], *yet* [main clause]. (verbless first half) &middot; *Though* [clause], [main clause]. (*yet* dropped — the ordinary modern form) &middot; [main clause], *though* [clause]. (no *yet* available in this order)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *though* clause whether or not *yet* follows; the comma goes before *yet*, never after it. Concession is the one place where reversing the order does **not** remove the comma — a trailing *though* clause keeps it, because it contrasts with the main clause rather than restricting it: *Nobody would pay to fix it, though the roof leaked all spring.* Compare the trailing *if* clause of *if … then*, which takes none.
**Variants:** *although … yet*, *though … still*, *though … nevertheless*, *while … yet*; in current American English the second half is normally just left out

Concedes a point in the first half and asserts the main claim anyway in the second, with *yet* marking the turn. The pairing is a survival: Old and Middle English routinely propped up a fronted concessive clause with a resumptive adverb, and *yet* is what is left of that. A modern editor will usually cut it as redundant — *though* has already done the concessive work — so use the full pair only when you want the deliberate weight of older prose.

1. *Though* the evidence was thin, *yet* the jury convicted.
2. *Although* the company posted record sales, *yet* the stock fell.
3. *Though* she had every reason to walk out, *yet* she stayed to the end.
4. *Though* young, *yet* he ran the whole shop by himself.
5. *Though* the roof leaked all spring, *still* nobody would pay to fix it.
6. *Though* the roof leaked all spring, nobody would pay to fix it.
7. Nobody would pay to fix it, *though* the roof leaked all spring.

**See also:** [if … then](#if--then), [just as … so](#just-as--so), [though](07-concession.md#though), [although](07-concession.md#although), [yet](01-coordinating.md#yet)

### what with … and

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt wɪð/, /ˈwɑt wɪð/ … /ænd/, /ən/ &middot; *WUT-with … and, uhn* (some Americans end *with* in /θ/; the stress sits on *what*)
**Type:** correlative frame (*what with* is a compound preposition — only *and* is a conjunction, and it is the ordinary coordinator; *what* elsewhere is an interrogative and a relative pronoun) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** none — a prepositional adjunct of cause, so both halves take noun phrases or *-ing* phrases
**Pattern:** *What with* [X] *and* [Y], [main clause]. &middot; *What with* [*-ing* phrase] *and* [*-ing* phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *What with* [X], [Y], *and* [Z], [main clause]. (three or more causes) &middot; *What with* [X] *and* [Y] *and all*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *what with* [X] *and* [Y].
**Punctuation:** The whole *what with* phrase is set off from the main clause by a comma, wherever it sits in the sentence.
**Variants:** *what with … and all*, *between … and* (same job, more neutral)

Piles up two or more causes as an informal explanation, usually for something that went wrong. It takes nouns and *-ing* phrases and never a finite clause — *what with the baby crying*, not *what with the baby cried*.

1. *What with* the traffic *and* the rain, we got there an hour late.
2. *What with* work *and* the new baby, we haven't unpacked a box.
3. She's exhausted, *what with* the move *and* the audit *and* everything.
4. *What with* the layoffs *and* the hiring freeze, morale is shot.
5. We skipped the trip, *what with* gas prices *and* the car making that noise.
6. *What with* the dog barking *and* the neighbors hammering, I couldn't hear a word.

**See also:** [between … and](#between--and), [both … and](#both--and), [what with](04-cause.md#what-with), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### where … there

**Pronunciation:** /wɛr/ … /ðɛr/ &middot; *WAIR … THAIR*
**Type:** correlative (*where* is the subordinator; *there* is a resumptive adverb, not a conjunction, which is exactly why it can be dropped — the same arrangement as the *then* of *if … then*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary, proverbial
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place, routinely generalized to circumstance
**Pattern:** *Where* [clause], *there* [main clause]. &middot; *Where* [clause], *there* [verb] [subject]. (inverted main clause) &middot; *Where* [clause], *there* [main clause] *also*. &middot; *Where* [clause], [main clause]. (*there* dropped — the ordinary modern form) &middot; [main clause] *where* [clause]. (no *there* available in this order) &middot; *Wherever* [clause], *there* [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *where* clause whether or not *there* follows; the comma goes before *there*, never after it. Reversed, the trailing *where* clause takes no comma and cannot take *there* at all. One look-alike to resist: in *Where there's smoke, there's fire* the second *there* is the dummy subject of *there is*, not the resumptive adverb, and the pair is doing the same job by accident.
**Variants:** *wherever … there*, *where … there also*, *where* alone (the ordinary modern form); compare *when … then*

Names a place, or more often a kind of situation, in the first half and asserts what holds in it in the second, with *there* pointing back at the *where* clause. Like the *then* of *if … then*, the *there* adds emphasis rather than meaning and is normally dropped in modern prose; when it is kept, formal style often inverts the main clause behind it — *there will your heart be also*.

1. *Where* the road ends, *there* the trail begins.
2. *Where* your treasure is, *there* will your heart be also.
3. *Where* the water runs deepest, *there* the current is slowest.
4. *Where* the statute is silent, *there* custom governs.
5. *Where* two or three inspectors disagree, *there* the paperwork multiplies.
6. *Where* the tax was actually collected, the roads got paved.
7. Weeds grow thickest *where* the shade is deepest.

**See also:** [if … then](#if--then), [though … yet](#though--yet), [where (place)](09-place-relative.md#where), [wherever](09-place-relative.md#wherever), [where (concessive)](07-concession.md#where)
