Coordinating conjunctions#

The seven FANBOYS and their relatives — the words that join two things of equal grammatical rank and mark how they relate.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Coordinating conjunctions join two elements of equal grammatical rank — word to word, phrase to phrase, or whole sentence to whole sentence. Neither half is demoted; either one could usually stand on its own. English has exactly seven true coordinators, the FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so), plus a short list of fixed two-word coordinators (and/or, or else, but then) and a fringe of coordinator-like items (plus, minus, times, versus, as well as, along with, let alone, if not) that link equal partners without quite behaving like the seven.

What unites all of them is position: a coordinator sits between the two things it joins and can never move to the front of the first one. That single test separates this class from subordinators like although, which head their own clause and can front the sentence, and from conjunctive adverbs like however, which can slide around inside their clause.

One comma rule runs through the entire category: when a coordinator joins two independent clauses, put a comma before it; when it joins anything smaller than a clause, don't. She called, and I answered takes the comma; bread and butter does not. Two riders: in a list of three or more, American editing adds a serial comma before the final and or or (red, white, and blue), and for always takes its comma, because without one it reads as the preposition.

The seven FANBOYS#

The only seven words in English that are coordinating conjunctions and nothing else in that role. Learn them as a closed set: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

and#

Pronunciation
/ænd/, weak /ənd/, /ən/, /n̩/ · AND, weak uhnd / uhn / n
Русский
и — основной соединительный союз: добавление, последовательность, результат
Type
coordinating
Register
neutral
Clause type
coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases of any rank
Pattern
[X] and [Y]. · [X], [Y], and [Z]. · [independent clause], and [independent clause]. · [imperative] and [result]. · And [clause]. (sentence-initial)
Punctuation
No comma between two joined words, phrases, or verbs sharing one subject (bread and butter; She stood up and left). Comma before and when it joins two independent clauses (She called, and I answered), droppable when both clauses are very short. In a list of three or more, American editing puts a serial comma before the final and (red, white, and blue).
Variants
& (ampersand — company names, notes, informal writing) · 'n' (rock 'n' roll, mac 'n' cheese) · reduced /ən/ in ordinary speech

The default coordinator: it adds, and it will add anything to anything as long as the two halves are the same grammatical kind. Beyond plain addition it carries sequence (She got up and left), result (He tripped and fell), and, after an imperative, a condition (Move and I'll shoot means "if you move"). Of all the addition words it is the only one that makes a subject genuinely plural — plus is informal and as well as leaves the verb alone.

  1. She ordered a salad and a glass of iced tea.
  2. We packed the car, and the kids were asleep before we hit the highway.
  3. Add the flour, the sugar, and a pinch of salt.
  4. Say that one more time and I'm leaving.
  5. He came home, dropped his bag, and went straight to bed.
  6. And then, out of nowhere, the power went out.

but#

Pronunciation
/bʌt/, weak /bət/ · BUT, weak buht
Русский
но, а — второе идёт вразрез с тем, чего ждёшь после первого
Type
coordinating (also a preposition meaning "except", and an adverb in fixed phrases)
Register
neutral
Clause type
coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases
Pattern
[X] but [Y]. · [independent clause], but [independent clause]. · not [X] but [Y]. · But [clause]. (sentence-initial)
Punctuation
Comma before but when it joins two independent clauses (I called, but nobody answered). No comma when it joins two words or phrases (small but tidy). In the not X but Y frame the comma is optional and marks emphasis (It's not the money, but the principle). No comma after But at the head of a sentence unless what follows is itself parenthetical.
Variants
but then · but rather · but also (in not only … but also) · prepositional but (everyone but me) is a different word class

Marks the second element as running against what the first led you to expect. It is the plain, neutral contrast word, and the differences around it are worth memorizing: yet adds a note of "in spite of that," however means the same but is an adverb and needs a semicolon before it, and although subordinates instead of coordinating, which changes which half of the sentence carries the point.

  1. I called twice, but nobody picked up.
  2. The soup was bland but filling.
  3. It's not the money but the principle.
  4. But nobody had checked the budget.
  5. She said she'd think about it, but I could tell she had already decided.
  6. We can go Friday but not Saturday.
See also
yet, and yet, but then, but rather, only, however, although

for#

Pronunciation
/fɔr/, weak /fɚ/ · FOR, weak fer
Русский
ибо, ведь — книжн./устар. союз причины; всегда с запятой перед ним
Type
coordinating (far more often a preposition — a gift for you — and the marker of infinitival clauses: for her to leave now would be rude)
Register
formal, literary
Clause type
coordinate clause of reason
Pattern
[independent clause], for [independent clause]. — normally medial · For [clause]. (sentence-initial; scripture and consciously elevated prose only, linking back to the sentence before)
Punctuation
Always put a comma before conjunctive for; without one the word reads as the preposition (He waited for the bus vs. He waited, for the bus was late). It never joins anything smaller than a clause. Sentence-initial For belongs to scripture and deliberately elevated writing (For the wages of sin is death), where it points back across the period and takes no comma after it.
Variants
none as a conjunction · prepositional for (a gift for you) is a different word class · infinitival for (for her to leave now would be rude)

Supplies a reason or a justification for what was just said — roughly because or since, but grammatically a coordinator. In American English it is close to literary now: outside scripture, older fiction, and deliberately elevated prose, people say because. Its behavior is unlike because too — its clause cannot be moved ahead of the main clause (For she had never lied to me, I trusted her is impossible), it cannot answer why?, and it cannot be the focus of a cleft.

  1. I trusted her, for she had never lied to me.
  2. We turned back, for the trail had washed out below the ridge.
  3. Do not weep, for the child is not dead but sleeping.
  4. He spoke slowly, for he wanted every word to land.
  5. The town emptied out in October, for there was no work to be had after the harvest.
  6. She kept the letters, for they were all she had left of him.
  7. He refused the money. For he had given his word, and that was the end of it.
See also
so, and so, because, since, therefore

nor#

Pronunciation
/nɔr/, weak /nɚ/ · NOR, weak ner
Русский
и не, также не — второе отрицание; требует инверсии: nor did he call
Type
coordinating (also the second half of the correlative neither … nor)
Register
formal to neutral
Clause type
coordinate clause, negative
Pattern
[negative clause], nor [auxiliary or be] [subject] [rest]. · neither [X] nor [Y]. · [negative clause], nor [noun phrase]. (with the verb gapped) · Nor [auxiliary] [subject] … (sentence-initial, formal)
Punctuation
Comma before nor when it joins two independent clauses (He didn't call, nor did he write). No comma inside a neither … nor pair joining words or phrases (neither hot nor cold). The clause after clause-linking nor is inverted — auxiliary or be before the subject. Sentence-initial Nor follows a period or a semicolon, inverts the same way, and takes no comma after it.
Variants
neither … nor (correlative) · nor yet (literary, archaic)

Adds a second negative to a negative already on the table: "and not that either." Two traps come with it. Word order inverts (nor did he call, never nor he called), and the clause takes no second not, because nor already carries the negation.

  1. He didn't call, nor did he answer my texts.
  2. The company never admitted fault, nor did it offer a refund.
  3. Neither the manager nor the assistants were told about the merger.
  4. She isn't unkind, nor is she especially warm.
  5. I have no time for this, nor the patience.
  6. Nor should we forget who paid for the road in the first place.
See also
or, and, much less, neither … nor, either … or

or#

Pronunciation
/ɔr/, weak /ɚ/ · OR, weak er
Русский
или — альтернатива, обычно невзаимоисключающая (можно и то и другое)
Type
coordinating
Register
neutral
Clause type
coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases
Pattern
[X] or [Y]. · [X], [Y], or [Z]. · [independent clause], or [independent clause]. · [imperative], or [consequence]. · either [X] or [Y]. · [term], or [other name for the same thing], …
Punctuation
No comma between two joined words or phrases (coffee or tea). Comma before or joining two independent clauses (Hurry, or we'll miss it), droppable only when both clauses are very short. Serial comma before the final or in a list of three or more (by car, by train, or by bus). A comma before or also signals the renaming use, and a second comma closes it (the humerus, or upper arm bone, is …).
Variants
or else · or rather · and/or · either … or (correlative)

Offers an alternative. English or is normally inclusive — coffee or tea does not forbid both — and only context or an explicit either … or forces the exclusive reading. Two side jobs are worth learning: after an imperative it states the consequence of not complying, and after a comma it renames the thing just mentioned. Note also that after a negative it is or, not and, that spreads the negation: I don't eat meat or fish.

  1. Do you want the aisle or the window?
  2. We could drive, or we could fly.
  3. Would you rather have coffee, tea, or something cold?
  4. Hurry up, or we'll miss the last train.
  5. I don't eat meat or fish.
  6. She studies the humerus, or upper arm bone, in the first week of the course.
  7. Either you tell him or I will.

so#

Pronunciation
/soʊ/ · SOH
Русский
поэтому, так что — следствие: сначала причина, потом результат
Type
coordinating (also subordinating in so that, a degree adverb, and a discourse marker)
Register
neutral
Clause type
coordinate clause of result
Pattern
[independent clause], so [independent clause]. · So [clause]. (conversational opener) · compare subordinating [clause] so (that) [purpose clause]. — no comma
Punctuation
Comma before so when it joins two independent clauses (It rained, so we stayed in). No comma when so means "so that" and introduces a purpose (I left early so I could beat traffic) — the presence or absence of that comma is the main clue to which reading is intended. Sentence-initial discourse So takes a comma after it only as a conversational aside (So, what happened?).
Variants
and so · so that (subordinating, purpose) · so … that (correlative, degree)

States the result of what came before: cause first, effect after so. It is the mirror image of because, which puts the effect first and the cause second. The high-value distinction is against so (that): I left early, so I beat traffic reports what happened, while I left early so I could beat traffic reports what was intended.

  1. Traffic was terrible, so we missed the opening.
  2. The store was out of basil, so I used parsley.
  3. He never wrote back, so I stopped emailing.
  4. I left early so I could beat the traffic.
  5. So, what did the doctor say?
  6. She's allergic to cats, so we met at my place instead.
See also
and so, for, therefore, so that, because

yet#

Pronunciation
/jɛt/ · YET
Русский
но всё же, и тем не менее — противопоставление с оттенком неожиданности
Type
coordinating (also a time adverb: not yet, has she left yet?)
Register
neutral; a shade more formal than but in writing
Clause type
coordinate clause of concessive contrast
Pattern
[X] yet [Y]. · [independent clause], yet [independent clause]. · [clause] and yet [clause]. · Yet [clause]. (sentence-initial)
Punctuation
Comma before yet when it joins two independent clauses (She apologized, yet nothing changed). No comma when it joins two words or phrases (firm yet fair). And yet takes the same comma. Sentence-initial Yet takes no comma after it.
Variants
and yet · adverbial yet (I haven't finished yet) is a different word class

Contrast plus surprise: it asserts that both facts hold even though they look incompatible. But merely opposes; yet adds "and in spite of that." It is also one of only two FANBOYS words that ordinary usage lets you put and in front of — and yet and and so — a clue that both are half adverb; and but, and or, and and for are impossible in any variety, and and nor is British — American editing writes nor alone.

  1. She apologized, yet nothing actually changed.
  2. The apartment is small yet surprisingly bright.
  3. He had every reason to quit, yet he showed up every morning.
  4. It was a simple question, yet nobody in the room could answer it.
  5. Yet something about the story didn't add up.
  6. She had barely known him and yet she cried through the whole service.
See also
but, and yet, but then, nevertheless, although

Compound coordinators#

Fixed two-word sequences that function as a single coordinator. Each has a comma rule of its own, and none of them can be broken apart or reordered.

and/or#

Pronunciation
/ˌænd ˈɔr/ · and-OR
Русский
и/или — юр., канц.: «одно, другое или оба сразу»
Type
coordinating (compound)
Register
formal; legal, technical, and administrative writing
Clause type
coordinate phrase; joins words and phrases, rarely clauses
Pattern
[noun phrase] and/or [noun phrase]. · [subject] and/or [subject] [verb] … · [verb phrase] and/or [verb phrase]. · [adjective] and/or [adjective]. · recommended rewrite: [X] or [Y] or both.
Punctuation
Written with a slash and no spaces around it. Punctuate the surrounding sentence exactly as you would for or: no comma between the two joined elements, none after the slash. Avoid it in a list of three or more, where its scope becomes genuinely ambiguous. Note too that subject–verb agreement after an and/or subject is unsettled (the tenant and/or the guarantor is — or are — liable), which is one more reason editors rewrite it.
Variants
and or (nonstandard spacing) · or/and (rare) · recommended rewrite: X or Y or both

A written shorthand for "either one or both," invented to close the loophole that inclusive or supposedly leaves open in contracts. Plain-language guides and many courts dislike it, since ordinary or is already inclusive and the slash breaks down past two items; the standard fix is X or Y or both.

  1. Bring a passport and/or a driver's license.
  2. Applicants must show two years of experience and/or a relevant certificate.
  3. The fee may be paid by the tenant and/or the guarantor.
  4. Damage caused by fire and/or flooding is excluded from coverage.
  5. Please copy your manager and/or the project lead on the final draft.
  6. Signatures may be collected in person and/or electronically.
See also
or, and, or else, either … or

and so#

Pronunciation
/ˌænd ˈsoʊ/, weak /ənd ˈsoʊ/, /ən ˈsoʊ/ · and-SOH, weak uhn-SOH
Русский
и потому, и вот — итоговое следствие, весомее простого so
Type
coordinating (compound)
Register
neutral; narrative or slightly formal
Clause type
coordinate clause of result
Pattern
[independent clause], and so [independent clause]. · And so [clause]. (sentence-initial, narrative)
Punctuation
Comma before and so when it joins two independent clauses; never a comma between and and so, and none after so. Sentence-initial And so takes no comma after it.
Variants
and therefore (formal) · do not confuse with and so + inversion (and so did I = "I did too")

The same result link as bare so, but heavier and more deliberate — it sums up rather than merely reports, which is why it lands so often at the end of a story or an argument. Keep it apart from additive and so with inversion (She was exhausted, and so was I), where so means "likewise" and is an adverb.

  1. The flight was canceled, and so we spent the night in Denver.
  2. No one else volunteered, and so the job fell to me.
  3. And so the summer ended, the way summers do.
  4. The evidence never surfaced, and so the case was dropped.
  5. We had promised the kids a beach day, and so a beach day is what they got.
  6. The road washed out in April, and so the town was cut off for a week.

and then#

Pronunciation
/ˌænd ˈðɛn/, weak /ənd ˈðɛn/, /ən ˈðɛn/ · and-THEN, weak uhn-THEN
Русский
а потом, и затем — закрепляет порядок событий, который and лишь подразумевает
Type
coordinating (compound)
Register
neutral; conversational in narrative
Clause type
coordinate clause of sequence
Pattern
[X] and then [Y]. · [subject] [verb phrase] and then [verb phrase]. · [independent clause], and then [independent clause]. · [X], [Y], and then [Z]. · And then [clause]. (sentence-initial)
Punctuation
No comma when and then joins two verb phrases sharing one subject (We had dinner and then walked down to the water). Comma before and then when it joins two independent clauses (First the check bounced, and then the landlord called) — the ordinary and rule, though fast narrative drops it between short clauses. Never a comma between and and then, and none after then; sentence-initial And then takes no comma either.
Variants
and then some (idiom: "and more besides") · and afterward (formal)

Fixes the order of events, which plain and only implies. It has a second, rhetorical life in complaint and storytelling, where each and then stacks one new development on the last.

  1. We had dinner and then walked down to the water.
  2. She read the letter twice and then put it back in the drawer.
  3. First the check bounced, and then the landlord called.
  4. And then he tells me the whole thing was my idea.
  5. Boil the pasta, drain it, and then toss it with the sauce.
  6. The power flickered twice and then went out for good.

and yet#

Pronunciation
/ˌænd ˈjɛt/, weak /ənd ˈjɛt/, /ən ˈjɛt/ · and-YET, weak uhn-YET
Русский
и всё же, и тем не менее — усиленное yet: признаёт первое и дивится второму
Type
coordinating (compound)
Register
neutral; common in writing
Clause type
coordinate clause of concessive contrast
Pattern
[independent clause], and yet [independent clause]. · [X] and yet [Y]. · And yet [clause]. (sentence-initial)
Punctuation
Comma before and yet between two independent clauses. Sentence-initial And yet normally takes no comma after it, though writers add one for a beat of hesitation.
Variants

Intensifies the surprise already in yet: it concedes the first fact and then registers the second as genuinely hard to square with it. English tolerates a doubled coordinator here only because yet is halfway to an adverb — the same license that produces and so — which is why and but and and or are impossible.

  1. He had every advantage, and yet he threw it away.
  2. The book is barely two hundred pages and yet took him nine years to write.
  3. She's been asked a hundred times, and yet she still answers as if it were new.
  4. And yet nobody thought to check the basement.
  5. The town has no industry left, and yet the population keeps growing.
  6. It's the most obvious explanation, and yet it's wrong.

but rather#

Pronunciation
/ˌbʌt ˈræðɚ/, weak /bət ˈræðɚ/ · but-RA-ther, weak buht-RA-ther
Русский
а (не X, а Y) — отменяет отвергнутое и подставляет верное; нужно отрицание впереди
Type

Register

Clause type
coordinate phrase or clause, corrective
Pattern

Punctuation
In the not X but rather Y frame a comma before but rather is optional and adds emphasis; never a comma after it. When the first element is a full clause, use the comma.

Replaces a rejected item with the correct one, so it needs a negative in front of it to do its work. Where plain but contrasts two things that can both be true, but rather cancels the first outright and substitutes the second.

  1. The problem wasn't the code but rather the data it was fed.
  2. She didn't refuse, but rather asked for more time.
  3. It's not that he's rude, but rather that he's completely unaware of other people.
  4. We're not raising prices, but rather trimming the menu.
  5. The delay wasn't caused by weather but rather by a paperwork error.
  6. He isn't lazy but rather exhausted.

  4. But then again, he did warn us.

nor yet#

Pronunciation
/ˌnɔr ˈjɛt/, weak /nɚ ˈjɛt/ · nor-YET, weak ner-YET
Русский
и уж тем более не — устар., книжн.: усиленное nor
Type
coordinating (compound; the reinforced form of nor)
Register
formal, literary; archaic in flavor
Clause type
coordinate clause or phrase, negative
Pattern
[negative clause], nor yet [noun phrase]. · [negative clause], nor yet [auxiliary or be] [subject] [rest]. · neither [X], nor [Y], nor yet [Z]. · not [X], nor yet [Y].
Punctuation
Comma before nor yet, never one between the two words or after them. When a full clause follows, it inverts exactly as it does after bare nor (nor yet did he write). In a three-part negative list it marks the last item and takes the same comma the other items take.
Variants
nor (the plain modern form) · nor even · neither … nor · and not … either (plain-English rewrite)

A reinforced nor: it adds one more negative and marks it as the least expected item of the set, the one a reader would have thought least likely. Hymns, scripture translations, and nineteenth-century prose use it freely; present-day American writing has all but dropped it, and bare nor carries the same load.

  1. He had no money, no prospects, nor yet any wish to acquire either.
  2. She was not beautiful, nor yet plain.
  3. The committee has no budget for this, nor yet the authority to ask for one.
  4. I never learned my father's trade, nor yet wanted to.
  5. The treaty settled neither the border, nor the fishing rights, nor yet the question of who would pay.
  6. There was no answer at the door, nor yet a light in any of the windows.

or else#

Pronunciation
/ˌɔr ˈɛls/ · or-ELS
Русский
а то, иначе — последствие или угроза при невыполнении первого
Type
coordinating (compound)
Register
neutral to informal
Clause type
coordinate clause of alternative consequence
Pattern
[imperative], or else [consequence]. · [independent clause], or else [independent clause]. · [warning] … or else. (elliptical threat)
Punctuation
Comma before or else when it joins two clauses; the comma is often dropped when the first clause is a short imperative. The bare threat Or else. stands as its own sentence and takes a period, and a dash before it is common.

  1. Leave a note, or else they'll worry.
  2. We need to leave by six, or else we'll hit rush hour.
  3. Write it down, or else you'll forget by morning.
  4. Pay the ticket by Friday or else it doubles.
  5. Have the car back by ten — or else.
  6. Book it now, or else the fare goes up.

/ˌɔr ˈræðɚ/ · or-RA-ther
Русский

Clause type

  6. The meeting is Tuesday, or rather Wednesday now.

Quasi-coordinators and coordinator-like items#

Words that join equal partners but fail at least one test for true coordination — they may not link finite clauses, may not make a subject plural, or may be classed as prepositions by strict grammars. They are grouped here because learners meet them in coordinator slots and need the same comma rules.

along with#

Pronunciation
/əˈlɔŋ wɪð/, also /wɪθ/ · uh-LONG-with
Русский
вместе с, наряду с — добавляет второго участника, не меняя числа подлежащего
