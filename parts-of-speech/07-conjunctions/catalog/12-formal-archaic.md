# Formal, legal, literary, and archaic conjunctions

> The conjunctions an American meets in contracts, statutes, hymns, King James scripture, and nineteenth-century novels — and almost nowhere else.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What holds this category together is not meaning but **survival**. Every word here does a job that plain modern English can already do — *lest* is *so that … not*, *whereby* is *by which*, *save that* is *except that*, *ere* is *before* — and each has lasted only because some institution kept using it: the courts, the church, the poets, or the drafters of contracts. So the entries below are sorted less by what they mean than by where you will actually run into them, and every one carries a register label you should take literally. Writing *whilst* or *howbeit* in an American email is not a small stylistic choice; it is a costume.

Three habits run through the whole class. First, **they front their clause**. These words like the front of the sentence far more than their modern equivalents do (*Inasmuch as the plaintiff failed to appear …*, *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary …*), and a fronted clause is closed by a **comma** before the main clause — with one systematic exception: in contract recitals a fronted *WHEREAS* clause is closed by a **semicolon**, because recitals are stacked in a list rather than joined to a main clause. Second, **several of them keep the old subjunctive**: after *lest*, *except* (= unless), *an*, and *without* you write *lest he forget*, not *lest he forgets*, and *but that* takes a past subjunctive when it means *were it not that*. Third, **many are not conjunctions at all** — *pursuant to*, *subject to*, *per*, *hereby*, *therefor*, and *witnesseth* are prepositions, pro-adverbs, and formulas that merely sit in the connective slot of legal prose. They are included here because you meet them in the same paragraph as the real conjunctions, and each is labeled honestly for what it is.

## Formal concession and contrast

Words for *but* and *although* that have been promoted into formal registers. They divide by what they attach to: *howsoever*, *much as*, and *whilst* take a full finite clause; *albeit* and *notwithstanding* normally take a phrase; and *be that as it may* floats free as a sentence adverb between two independent clauses — which is why it, alone in this group, requires a semicolon or a period in front of it.

### albeit

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈbiɪt/, /ælˈbiɪt/ &middot; *awl-BEE-it, al-BEE-it*
**Русский:** хотя и — книжн., присоединяет уступительный оборот, а не полное придаточное
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** reduced concessive clause — normally a phrase, not a full clause
**Pattern:** [main clause], *albeit* [adjective / adverb / noun phrase / prepositional phrase]. &middot; [article] [adjective], *albeit* [adjective], [noun]. &middot; [main clause], *albeit* *that* [clause] (rare and dated).
**Punctuation:** Comma before *albeit* when it introduces a trailing phrase; commas on both sides when the phrase sits inside the clause. No semicolon — it is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb.
**Variants:** *all be it* (the original three-word form, no longer written), *albeit that* (the rare full-clause form), *although* (the plain equivalent)

A formal concessive meaning *although it be*. Its one distinguishing habit is that, unlike *although*, it almost never takes a full finite clause in modern American English — it heads a bare phrase (*albeit reluctantly*, *albeit a costly one*), which is exactly why it is so convenient in edited prose. Writing *albeit he was tired* is not wrong historically but reads as a mistake today.

1. The plan worked, *albeit* more slowly than we had hoped.
2. She agreed to help, *albeit* reluctantly.
3. It was a victory, *albeit* a costly one.
4. The restaurant serves very good food, *albeit* at prices that make you wince.
5. He is a talented, *albeit* difficult, collaborator.
6. The policy has been revised, *albeit* only in the margins.

**See also:** [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [much as](#much-as), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [howbeit](#howbeit), [whilst](#whilst)

### be that as it may

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbi ðæt əz ɪt ˈmeɪ/ &middot; *bee-that-uhz-it-MAY*
**Русский:** как бы то ни было — книжн., уступает предыдущий довод и всё же настаивает на своём
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (fixed phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of concession — modifies the whole following clause
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Be that as it may*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *be that as it may*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** Takes a **semicolon or a period before it** and a **comma after it**. A comma alone before it produces a comma splice — this is a sentence adverb, not a conjunction.
**Variants:** *that said* (neutral), *even so* (neutral)

A frozen subjunctive clause — literally *let that be as it may be* — used to concede the previous point and then set it aside. It differs from *however* in what it concedes: *however* signals mere contrast, while *be that as it may* grants that the other side may well be right and says the conclusion stands anyway.

1. The budget is tight. *Be that as it may*, the roof still has to be replaced.
2. He may have meant no harm; *be that as it may*, the damage is done.
3. *Be that as it may*, I would like to hear the other side before I decide.
4. She apologized, and the apology seemed sincere. *Be that as it may*, the policy requires a written report.
5. The data is three years old. *Be that as it may*, it is the only data we have.

**See also:** [albeit](#albeit), [howbeit](#howbeit), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [howsoever](#howsoever)

### howsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** как бы ни, каким бы образом ни — книжн./устар., усиленная форма however
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, archaic-leaning
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition ("no matter how")
**Pattern:** *Howsoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *Howsoever* [adjective/adverb] [subject] (*may be*), [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *howsoever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [noun/participle] *howsoever* [prepositional phrase] (legal boilerplate, no commas).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *howsoever* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. Inside a noun phrase in legal boilerplate (*any claim arising howsoever out of this Agreement*) it takes no punctuation at all. Never a semicolon — the semicolon rule belongs to the conjunctive adverb *however*, not to this word.
**Variants:** *howsoe'er* (poetic), *whosoever*, *whomsoever*, *whatsoever*, *wheresoever*, *whensoever*

The emphatic *-soever* form of *however* in its subordinating sense: *no matter how*, *in whatever way*. Its whole family — *whosoever*, *wheresoever*, *whensoever*, *whatsoever* — is built the same way and survives in legal boilerplate (*any claim whatsoever*) and in scripture. Do not confuse it with the conjunctive adverb *however* (= nevertheless), which needs a semicolon; *howsoever* never does.

1. *Howsoever* the vote goes, we will respect the outcome.
2. The trustee may invest the funds *howsoever* he sees fit.
3. *Howsoever* slight the risk, it must be disclosed to the patient.
4. Any claim arising *howsoever* out of this Agreement shall be settled by arbitration.
5. *Howsoever* she explained it, the committee remained unconvinced.

**See also:** [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [whereupon](#whereupon), [whither](#whither)

### much as

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌtʃ æz/ &middot; /ˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *MUCH az*
**Русский:** как ни, при всём том что — книжн., уступка по степени: «хотя очень»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (= *although … very much*)
**Pattern:** *Much as* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *much as* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *As much as* [subject] [verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *much as* clause — and it is fronted the great majority of the time. Comma **before** a trailing *much as* clause as well, because it always comments on the whole main clause rather than restricting it. Never a semicolon: it is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb.
**Variants:** *as much as* (the same conjunction with the fuller opening), *for all that*, *although* (the plain equivalent)

A concessive that concedes an **intensity**: *Much as I admire her, I cannot vote for her* means "although I admire her a great deal." Because the degree word is doing the concessive work, the verb has to be one that admits of degree — *admire*, *want*, *like*, *regret*, *tempt*. Keep it apart from the comparative *as much as* meaning "the same quantity as" (*He earns as much as I do*): the concessive fronts its clause and takes a comma, the comparative does neither.

1. *Much as* I admire her work, I cannot vote for her.
2. *Much as* he wanted the job, he turned it down once he read the contract.
3. She refused to sell the house, *much as* the offer tempted her.
4. *Much as* it pains me to admit it, the first draft was the better one.
5. *As much as* we would like to help, the fund is already committed for the year.
6. *Much as* the team improved after the trade, it still finished last in the division.

**See also:** [albeit](#albeit), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [whilst](#whilst)

### notwithstanding

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ/, /ˌnɑtwɪðˈstændɪŋ/ &middot; *not-with-STAN-ding*
**Русский:** несмотря на — юр., книжн.; может стоять и после своего дополнения
**Type:** subordinating (with *that*); preposition and postposition elsewhere &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession with *that*; otherwise a concessive prepositional phrase
**Pattern:** *Notwithstanding* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *notwithstanding*, [main clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *notwithstanding* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase in all three fronted patterns, including the postposed one (*Her objections notwithstanding, the board approved it*). No comma when a plain *notwithstanding* + noun phrase trails the main clause (*The contract remains in force notwithstanding any change of ownership*) — but a comma **is** required when the postposed form trails, since that is an absolute construction (*He signed the lease, his lawyer's warnings notwithstanding*). Never a semicolon: it introduces a phrase or a subordinate clause, not a second independent clause.
**Variants:** *notwithstanding that* (the clause-taking form), *this notwithstanding*, *that notwithstanding*

The heavyweight concessive of American legal drafting, where *notwithstanding anything to the contrary herein* is the standard way to make one clause override another. Its distinguishing trick — unique among English concessives — is that it can follow its own object (*the delay notwithstanding*), a leftover word order no other preposition allows.

1. *Notwithstanding* the weather, the ceremony went ahead outdoors.
2. The contract remains in force *notwithstanding* any change of ownership.
3. Her objections *notwithstanding*, the board approved the merger.
4. *Notwithstanding that* the deadline had passed, the court accepted the filing.
5. *Notwithstanding* anything to the contrary in Section 4, the Buyer shall pay all closing costs.
6. He signed the lease, his lawyer's warnings *notwithstanding*.

**See also:** [albeit](#albeit), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [subject to](#subject-to), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [howsoever](#howsoever)

### whilst

**Pronunciation:** /waɪlst/ &middot; *WHYLST*
**Русский:** пока; тогда как — брит. вариант while, в американском английском не пишут
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal — **British, not American**
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (simultaneity) or of contrast
**Pattern:** *Whilst* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whilst* [subject] [verb] (simultaneity). &middot; [main clause], *whilst* [subject] [verb] (contrast). &middot; [main clause] *whilst* [-ing phrase / reduced clause].
**Punctuation:** Identical to *while*: comma after the *whilst* clause when it comes first; no comma before a trailing clause of simultaneity (*She studied Latin whilst she was at Cambridge*), but a comma before a trailing clause that draws a contrast (*The museum is open daily, whilst the archive opens only by appointment*).
**Variants:** *while* (the American form), *amongst, amidst, whiles* (the same -st family)

A British variant of *while* with no difference in meaning — it covers both simultaneity and contrast exactly as *while* does. It is listed here because American learners meet it constantly in British books, journals, and corporate boilerplate and reasonably conclude it is the more formal English word. It is not: **an American editor will change every instance to *while***, and using it in American prose reads as affectation. Its cousins *amongst* and *amidst* are prepositions rather than conjunctions, but carry the same British flavor and get the same treatment from an American editor. One limit worth knowing: only the **conjunction** has an *-st* form. *While* is also a noun (*stay a while*, *worth your while*) and a verb (*while away an afternoon*), and neither of those is ever *whilst*, in Britain or anywhere else.

1. *Whilst* the report is thorough, it stops short of a recommendation. *(British usage)*
2. She studied Latin *whilst* she was at Cambridge. *(British usage)*
3. Passengers are asked to remain seated *whilst* the aircraft is taxiing. *(British usage)*
4. *Whilst* I appreciate the offer, I must decline. *(British usage)*
5. He read the paper *whilst* his tea went cold. *(British usage)*
6. The museum is open daily, *whilst* the archive opens only by appointment. *(British usage)*

**See also:** [albeit](#albeit), [sith](#sith), [ere](#ere)

## Formal cause, purpose, and result

The *-asmuch* family plus the two purpose conjunctions that still turn up in serious prose. *Inasmuch as* and *insomuch as* look almost identical and point in opposite directions — cause versus result — which is the most common error in this section.

### forasmuch as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɔrəzˈmʌtʃ æz/ &middot; /ˌfɔrəzˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *for-uhz-MUCH az*
**Русский:** поскольку, ввиду того что — устар., книжн., язык Библии и старых актов
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal and liturgical
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause
**Pattern:** *Forasmuch as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *forasmuch as* [clause] (rare).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *forasmuch as* clause when it is fronted, which is nearly always. A trailing *forasmuch as* clause is possible but rare even in period prose, and it takes a comma before it, because it comments on the whole main clause rather than restricting it. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *for as much as* (older spacing), *inasmuch as* (the surviving form)

A causal conjunction meaning *seeing that*, *since*. Where *inasmuch as* narrowed to "to the extent that" and stayed alive, *forasmuch as* stayed purely causal and died everywhere except the Book of Common Prayer, the King James Bible, and the preambles of old statutes and judgments. An American meets it at funerals and in nineteenth-century legal reports.

1. *Forasmuch as* it hath pleased Almighty God to take unto himself the soul of our departed brother, we commit his body to the ground. *(after the Book of Common Prayer)*
2. *Forasmuch as* many have taken in hand to set forth in order a declaration of those things which are most surely believed among us … *(King James Bible)*
3. *Forasmuch as* the parties have agreed to settle all claims between them, this action is dismissed with prejudice.
4. *Forasmuch as* the defendant has admitted liability, the trial will address damages only.
5. *Forasmuch as* no objection was filed within the statutory period, the plan is hereby confirmed.
6. The award will not be disturbed, *forasmuch as* no fraud has been shown. *(the rarer trailing order)*

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [insomuch as](#insomuch-as), [for that](#for-that), [sith](#sith), [whereas](#whereas)

### in that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈðæt/ &middot; /ɪn ðət/ &middot; *in-THAT*
**Русский:** тем, что; в том отношении, что — книжн., указывает, в чём именно верно сказанное
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause or respect
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Normally **no comma** before *in that* — it restricts the main clause rather than commenting on it. It cannot be fronted: *In that it assumes constant demand, the argument is flawed* is not idiomatic. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *in the respect that*, *for that* (the archaic ancestor), *because* (the plain causal equivalent), *insofar as* (when the point is scope rather than respect)

A formal causal conjunction that specifies the *respect* in which the main clause is true, rather than the reason it happened. Do not read it as the preposition *in* plus the demonstrative *that* (*the receipts are in that folder*): the conjunction is a fixed two-word unit and is always followed by a complete clause, never by a noun. Contrast *because*: *The argument is flawed because it assumes constant demand* says the assumption caused the flaw; *The argument is flawed in that it assumes constant demand* says the assumption **is** the flaw. It is the standard connective of academic and judicial prose for exactly that reason, and it is the only entry in this section a modern American would write unselfconsciously.

1. The argument is flawed *in that* it assumes the very thing it sets out to prove.
2. This case is unusual *in that* both parties agree on every material fact.
3. She was fortunate *in that* her injuries turned out to be minor.
4. The new statute differs from the old one *in that* it applies to contractors as well as employees.
5. The study is limited *in that* it draws on a single hospital system.

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [insofar as](#insofar-as), [insomuch as](#insomuch-as), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as)

### inasmuch as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnəzˈmʌtʃ æz/ &middot; /ˌɪnəzˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *in-uhz-MUCH az*
**Русский:** поскольку; в той мере, в какой — книжн., причина или степень
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause, or of degree/respect
**Pattern:** *Inasmuch as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *inasmuch as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *inasmuch as* clause when it is fronted; normally no comma when it follows. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *in as much as* (older spacing), *insomuch as* (a different word — see below)

Two related senses: *because* (*Inasmuch as the plaintiff failed to appear, the case is dismissed*) and *to the extent that* (*He is responsible inasmuch as he approved the transfer*). The second is the live one in modern legal and academic writing. Keep it apart from **insomuch as**, which means *to such a degree that* and introduces a result rather than a cause — the two are confused constantly, and the *-a-* versus *-o-* is the only signal.

1. The ruling is significant *inasmuch as* it overturns forty years of precedent.
2. *Inasmuch as* the plaintiff failed to appear, the case is dismissed for want of prosecution.
3. He is responsible *inasmuch as* he approved the transfer without reading it.
4. *Inasmuch as* we are all volunteers here, nobody is going to be fired over a missed meeting.
5. The two cases are alike *inasmuch as* both turn on the meaning of the word "reasonable."
6. *Inasmuch as* ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me. *(King James Bible)*

**See also:** [insomuch as](#insomuch-as), [insofar as](#insofar-as), [in that](#in-that), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as)

### insomuch as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnsəˈmʌtʃ æz/ &middot; /ˌɪnsəˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *in-suh-MUCH az*
**Русский:** настолько, что; до такой степени, что — устар., книжн., вводит следствие
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result or degree
**Pattern:** [main clause], *insomuch that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *insomuch as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *insomuch that* when it introduces a consequence, because the result comments on the whole main clause rather than restricting it. **No comma** before the degree sense *insomuch as*, which does restrict the main clause (*He was trusted insomuch as he was given a key*). It is not fronted, and it never takes a semicolon: what follows is a subordinate clause, not a second independent one.
**Variants:** *insomuch that* (the commoner form), *in so much that*

Means *to such a degree that* — it introduces the **result** of what the main clause described (*The wind rose, insomuch that the ferry was canceled*). Its near-twin **inasmuch as** introduces a **cause**. A useful test: *insomuch* looks forward to a consequence and can usually be replaced by *so … that*; *inasmuch* looks backward to a reason and can be replaced by *since*.

1. The wind rose through the afternoon, *insomuch that* the last ferry was canceled.
2. The evidence was overwhelming, *insomuch that* the defense rested without calling a single witness.
3. The crowd pressed forward, *insomuch that* the officers had to link arms.
4. He was trusted *insomuch as* he was given a key to the building.
5. The drought ran three straight years, *insomuch that* the riverbed cracked open.

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that), [to the end that](#to-the-end-that)

### lest

**Pronunciation:** /lɛst/ &middot; *LEST*
**Русский:** чтобы не; как бы не — книжн., отрицательная цель, требует сослагательного наклонения
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative purpose, or complement of an expression of fear
**Pattern:** [main clause] *lest* [subject] [**bare verb**]. &middot; [main clause] *lest* [subject] *should* [verb]. &middot; *Lest* [subject] [bare verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *lest* when it follows the main clause and states a purpose; a comma is used when it comments on the whole preceding clause (*The committee moved quickly, lest the opportunity be lost*). Comma after a fronted *lest* clause. Never a semicolon — *lest* subordinates a clause, it does not join two independent ones.
**Variants:** *lest … should* (British-leaning variant), *for fear that* (the neutral equivalent)

Introduces the outcome to be **avoided**, so it is inherently negative: *lest* already contains the *not*, and writing *lest he should not forget* reverses your meaning. Two things mark it as formal. It takes the **bare subjunctive** — *lest he forget*, never *lest he forgets* — and it is one of the last places in American English where that mood is obligatory rather than optional.

1. He wrote the address on the back of his hand *lest* he forget it.
2. The committee moved quickly, *lest* the opportunity be lost.
3. She kept her voice down *lest* the neighbors hear.
4. *Lest* anyone think this is a minor matter, the fine is fifty thousand dollars.
5. I am putting it in writing *lest* there be any confusion later.
6. He hesitated to ask, *lest* she should take offense.

**See also:** [to the end that](#to-the-end-that), [except](#except), [but that](#but-that), [save that](#save-that)

### to the end that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ði ˈɛnd ðæt/ &middot; *tuh-thee-END-that*
**Русский:** с тем чтобы, дабы — юр., книжн.; в придаточном обычно may/shall/should
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to the end that* [subject] *may/might/shall/should* [verb]. &middot; [main clause], *to the end that* [clause]. &middot; *To the end that* [subject] *may/might/shall/should* [verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** When it trails, a comma before it is optional and signals that the purpose comments on the whole main clause rather than restricting it. When it is fronted — much rarer, and confined to drafting — a comma closes the *to the end that* clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *in order that* (the neutral formal equivalent), *so that* (the everyday equivalent)

The ceremonial purpose conjunction: *so that*, dressed for court. It strongly prefers a modal in its clause — *may*, *might*, *shall*, or *should* — and sounds wrong with a bare present tense. The bare literary *that* of *Speak, that I may hear thee* is the same construction with the noun phrase worn away.

1. The records are sealed *to the end that* witnesses may testify without fear of reprisal.
2. Notice shall be published once a week for three weeks, *to the end that* all creditors may present their claims.
3. He left the whole of his estate to the college, *to the end that* no student should be turned away for want of money.
4. The rules were tightened *to the end that* such an accident might never happen again.
5. We publish these findings *to the end that* others may build on them.
6. *To the end that* the record may be complete, the clerk shall file all correspondence received in the matter.

**See also:** [lest](#lest), [insomuch as](#insomuch-as), [now, therefore](#now-therefore)

## Formal condition, exception, and proviso

The exceptive conjunctions: ways of saying *except* that outrank *except*. Several still take the bare subjunctive, and the legal proviso *provided, however, that* carries a comma convention of its own.

### but

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt/ &middot; /bət/ &middot; *BUT*
**Русский:** кроме, за исключением — книжн., в оборотах none but, nothing but, no choice but
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — exceptive; prepositional before a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** exceptive phrase; before a bare infinitive it introduces a reduced clause
**Pattern:** [no one / none / nothing / nobody / all / who] *but* [noun phrase]. &middot; [nothing / no choice / no option] *but* [bare infinitive].
**Punctuation:** No comma. The exceptive *but* binds tightly to what follows it and is never punctuated like the coordinating *but*, which does take a comma before it when it joins two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *but for* (= if not for), *all but* (= nearly), *save*, *except*

This is not the FANBOYS *but* — it means **except**, and it survives in fixed negative frames: *none but*, *nothing but*, *no one but*, *all but*, *no choice but*. Before a bare infinitive it is a genuine subordinator (*nothing to do but wait*). One old refinement is still argued over: because it was historically a conjunction, formal usage takes the nominative after it (*no one but he knew*), while ordinary American English treats it as a preposition and says *no one but him*. Few English words spread across more classes: *but* is a coordinating conjunction (*small but sturdy*), this exceptive preposition, a degree adverb meaning *only* (*he is but a child*, *there were but three left*), a counterfactual in the compound **but for**, and even a noun (*no ifs, ands, or buts*). Only the coordinating one takes a comma before it when it joins two independent clauses.

1. None *but* the brave deserves the fair. *(Dryden)*
2. There was nothing to do *but* wait for the tow truck.
3. No one *but* a fool would sign a lease like that.
4. All *but* two of the seats were already taken.
5. He had no choice *but* to resign.
6. Who *but* a native speaker would ever catch that?

**See also:** [but for](#but-for), [but that](#but-that), [save](#save), [except](#except), [but (coordinating)](01-coordinating.md#but)

### but for

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt ˈfɔr/ &middot; /bət ˈfɔr/ &middot; *but-FOR*
**Русский:** если бы не — юр., книжн., называет обстоятельство, без которого исход был бы иным
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — counterfactual prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** counterfactual conditional phrase; the main clause carries *would* or *would have*
**Pattern:** *But for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would* / *would have*]. &middot; [main clause with *would* / *would have*] *but for* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *but for* phrase; no comma when it trails the main clause. Never a semicolon — it introduces a phrase, not a second independent clause. Hyphenated when it is pressed into service as a modifier: *but-for causation*, *the but-for world*.
**Variants:** *if not for*, *were it not for*, *had it not been for*, *absent* (the drafting equivalent: *absent the storm*)

The counterfactual *but*: it names the one circumstance whose absence would have changed the outcome, so the main clause is contrary to fact and nearly always carries *would* or *would have*. It is a fixture of American tort law, where **but-for causation** is the basic test — an act is a cause of the harm if the harm would not have happened but for it. Keep it apart from the exceptive *but* directly above: exceptive *but* subtracts a member from a set (*no one but the pilot*), while *but for* subtracts a condition from a history.

1. *But for* the storm, the ceremony would have been held outdoors.
2. She would have made partner years ago *but for* the merger.
3. *But for* a single misplaced decimal, the audit would have passed without comment.
4. The plaintiff must show that the injury would not have occurred *but for* the defendant's negligence.
5. *But for* his testimony, there would have been no case at all.
6. The building would still be standing *but for* a faulty relay in the alarm panel.

**See also:** [but](#but), [except](#except), [save](#save), [provided always that](#provided-always-that)

### but that

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt ˈðæt/ &middot; /bət ˈðæt/ &middot; *but-THAT*
**Русский:** что (после отрицаемого сомнения); если бы не — книжн., устар.
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, archaic
**Clause type:** noun clause after a negated expression of doubt; or adverbial clause of exception
**Pattern:** [negated doubt/denial] *but that* [clause]. &middot; *But that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [not so ADJ] *but that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *but that* clause. No comma after a verb of doubting or denying, where the clause is the verb's own object (*I do not doubt but that …*), and none after *not so* + adjective. A comma **before** a trailing *but that* clause when it means *were it not that* (*She would have left years ago, but that her mother needed looking after*). Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *but what* (dialectal), *that* (the plain modern equivalent)

Three formal uses. After a negated verb of doubting or denying it simply fills in for *that* (*There is no question but that the statute applies*). Fronted, it means *were it not that* (*But that I trusted him, I would never have handed over the keys*) and takes past-tense or subjunctive forms. And after *not so …*, it means *that … not* (*no case so hopeless but that something can be salvaged*).

1. I do not doubt *but that* the money will be repaid in full.
2. There is no question *but that* the statute applies to these facts.
3. *But that* I trusted him completely, I would never have handed over the keys.
4. She would have left years ago, *but that* her mother needed looking after.
5. No case is so hopeless *but that* something can be salvaged from it.

**See also:** [but](#but), [save that](#save-that), [except that](#except-that), [lest](#lest)

### except as otherwise provided

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt əz ˈʌðərwaɪz prəˈvaɪdɪd/ &middot; *ik-SEPT az UH-ther-wize pruh-VY-did*
**Русский:** если иное не предусмотрено — юр., стандартная договорная оговорка
**Type:** subordinating — reduced exceptive clause &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of exception; the *as* clause stands in for whatever the other provision says
**Pattern:** *Except as otherwise provided* [in Section X / herein], [main clause]. &middot; *Unless otherwise provided* [in X], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *except as otherwise provided in* [X].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted formula, which is where drafters deliberately put it so that the reader meets the carve-out before the rule. No comma when it trails the main clause. Never a semicolon — what follows the formula is the main clause itself, not a second independent one.
**Variants:** *unless otherwise provided*, *except as provided in*, *except as otherwise set forth herein*, *unless otherwise specified*, *unless otherwise agreed in writing*

The standard American drafting formula for making one provision yield to another, and the mirror image of *notwithstanding*: *except as otherwise provided in Section 9* means Section 9 wins, while *notwithstanding Section 9* means this clause wins. *Unless otherwise provided* is the same formula with a conditional opener instead of an exceptive one and is used interchangeably. The danger both carry is vagueness — a bare *except as otherwise provided herein* points at the whole document at once, which is why careful drafters name the section.

1. *Except as otherwise provided* in this Article, all notices required by this Agreement must be given in writing.
2. *Unless otherwise provided* by law, the annual meeting shall be held at the principal office of the corporation.
3. *Except as otherwise provided* herein, each party bears its own costs and attorneys' fees.
4. The Tenant shall pay all utilities furnished to the premises, *except as otherwise provided* in Section 6.3.
5. *Unless otherwise provided* in the applicable purchase order, delivery terms are FOB destination.
6. *Except as otherwise provided* in subsection (b), an application must be filed within ninety days of the denial.

**See also:** [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [subject to](#subject-to), [save and except](#save-and-except), [provided always that](#provided-always-that), [except that](#except-that)

### except that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt ðæt/ &middot; /ɪkˈsɛpt ðət/ &middot; *ik-SEPT that*
**Русский:** кроме того что, за исключением того что — нейтр.-книжн., вычитает одну деталь
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (bare *except* is informal)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of exception
**Pattern:** [main clause], *except that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *except that* [clause]. &middot; *Except that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma before *except that* is usual when the exception qualifies the whole main clause, and omitted when it attaches to a single element. Comma after a fronted *except that* clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *except* (informal, clause-taking), *excepting that*, *save that* (formal), *only* (very informal)

The everyday exceptive conjunction and the modern replacement for *save that*. It subtracts one detail from a claim that is otherwise true. Dropping *that* is fully idiomatic in speech (*I would have called, except my phone was dead*), but the two-word form is what edited prose uses. Note that this is a different word from the archaic *except* meaning **unless**. *Except* runs across three classes in all: preposition (*everyone except me*), conjunction (this entry and the archaic *unless* sense), and verb (*present company excepted*) — and only the clause-taking uses are punctuated as above.

1. The apartment is close to perfect, *except that* the radiator clanks all night.
2. Nothing about the place had changed, *except that* the sign over the door was new.
3. I would have called you, *except that* my phone was dead.
4. The two drafts are identical *except that* the second adds a severability clause.
5. He remembers nothing at all about the accident, *except that* it was raining.
6. *Except that* it rained hard on Saturday, the trip went exactly as planned.

**See also:** [save that](#save-that), [but that](#but-that), [save](#save), [except](#except), [but](#but)

### provided always that

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈvaɪdɪd ˈɔlweɪz ðæt/ &middot; *pruh-VY-did AWL-wayz that*
**Русский:** при условии, что — юр., оговорка, ограничивающая только что сказанное
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (a proviso limiting the clause before it)
**Pattern:** [main clause]; *provided always that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause]; *provided, however, that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *provided that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Both marks are in use, and they mean different things. A **semicolon** is the drafting convention when the proviso cuts back the entire preceding provision, and it is effectively obligatory before *provided, however, that*; a **comma** is normal when the proviso attaches to the immediately preceding clause only, and it is what ordinary formal prose uses with plain *provided that*. In the *provided, however, that* form, **both commas around *however* are required** — dropping them is one of the most visible tells of sloppy drafting. Note that the semicolon here does not make this a conjunctive adverb: what follows is a subordinate clause, not an independent one.
**Variants:** *provided that*, *providing that*, *provided*, *always provided that*, *provided, however, that*

The contract proviso: a condition attached after the fact to cut back the grant just made. The plain conditional *provided that* is ordinary formal English and belongs with the everyday conditionals; *provided always that* and *provided, however, that* are drafting formulas whose job is to signal that an exception to the foregoing is coming. Careful drafters now prefer *except that* or a separate sentence, because a proviso's scope — how far back it reaches — is a classic source of litigation.

1. The Tenant may sublet the premises; *provided always that* the Landlord's written consent is first obtained.
2. Payment shall be made within thirty days, *provided always that* a conforming invoice has been received.
3. The license granted herein is perpetual; *provided, however, that* it terminates immediately upon a breach of Section 7.
4. The Trustee may sell any asset of the trust, *provided always that* the sale is at fair market value.
5. Employees may work remotely, *provided always that* their supervisor approves the arrangement in advance.

**See also:** [subject to](#subject-to), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [save that](#save-that), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to)

### provided further that

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈvaɪdɪd ˈfɜrðər ðæt/ &middot; /prəˈvaɪdɪd ˈfɜrðər ðət/ &middot; *pruh-VY-did FUR-ther that*
**Русский:** и при этом при условии, что — юр., вторая оговорка, добавленная к первой
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition — a **second** proviso stacked on the first
**Pattern:** [main clause]; *provided that* [clause]; *provided further that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause]; *provided further, however, that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** A **semicolon before it**, effectively always: by the time a second proviso arrives the sentence has at least three parts, and the semicolon is what keeps them apart. *Further* takes **no commas** around it, unlike the *however* of *provided, however, that*. The semicolon here is a drafting convention rather than the conjunctive-adverb rule — what follows is a subordinate clause, not an independent one.
**Variants:** *and provided further that*, *provided further, however, that*, *provided also that*, *provided always that*

The marker of a **stacked** proviso: it adds a second condition to the one just stated rather than cutting it back. That is the whole difference from *provided, however, that*, which is adversative. Strings of these — *provided that … provided further that … provided further that* — are the signature of nineteenth-century American statutes and appropriations bills, and are exactly why modern drafting manuals tell you to break the sentence apart and number the conditions instead.

1. The Secretary may extend the deadline; *provided that* the applicant has shown good cause; *provided further that* no extension shall exceed ninety days.
2. The Tenant may sublet the premises; *provided that* the Landlord consents in writing; *provided further that* the subtenant assumes every obligation of this Lease.
3. The sum appropriated shall remain available until expended; *provided further that* not more than ten percent may be used for administration.
4. Payment shall be made within thirty days of invoice; *provided further that* interest accrues on any unpaid balance from the date of the invoice.
5. The permit may be renewed annually; *provided further, however, that* renewal shall be refused while any fee remains unpaid.

**See also:** [provided always that](#provided-always-that), [provided, however, that](#provided-however-that), [except as otherwise provided](#except-as-otherwise-provided), [save and except](#save-and-except), [subject to](#subject-to)

### provided, however, that

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈvaɪdɪd haʊˈɛvər ðæt/ &middot; *pruh-VY-did how-EV-er that*
**Русский:** при условии, однако, что — юр., оговорка, отбирающая часть только что данного
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition — an **adversative** proviso cutting back what precedes
**Pattern:** [main clause]; *provided, however, that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause]; *provided, however, that* [clause]; *and provided further that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** A **semicolon before it** and **commas on both sides of *however***. Both commas are required, and dropping either is the most visible tell of careless drafting. The semicolon does not make this a conjunctive adverb: *however* is buried inside a subordinator here, and what follows is a subordinate clause. A comma in place of the semicolon is not a comma splice, but it blurs how far back the proviso reaches — the single most litigated question about provisos.
**Variants:** *provided, nevertheless, that*, *provided always that*, *provided that*, *except that* (the plainer equivalent)

The adversative proviso: *however* announces that the condition about to be stated **takes something back** from the grant just made. Against *provided further that*, which adds a second condition, this one contradicts the first; against plain *provided that*, which is ordinary formal English, this one is pure drafting formula. American style authorities — Garner most insistently — advise replacing the whole thing with *but* or *except that*, or with a separate sentence.

1. The license granted herein is perpetual; *provided, however, that* it terminates immediately upon a breach of Section 7.
2. Either party may terminate on thirty days' written notice; *provided, however, that* termination shall not affect obligations already accrued.
3. The Company shall reimburse all reasonable travel expenses; *provided, however, that* any expense over one thousand dollars requires prior written approval.
4. The Buyer may inspect the goods at any reasonable time; *provided, however, that* no inspection shall delay shipment.
5. This Agreement is governed by the law of Delaware; *provided, however, that* the arbitration clause is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act.

**See also:** [provided always that](#provided-always-that), [provided further that](#provided-further-that), [except that](#except-that), [except as otherwise provided](#except-as-otherwise-provided), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding)

### save

**Pronunciation:** /seɪv/ &middot; *SAYV*
**Русский:** кроме, за исключением — книжн., лит., при all, none, nothing; чаще как save for
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — exceptive; prepositional before a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** exceptive phrase subtracted from the main clause
**Pattern:** [all / every / none / nothing / no one] [predicate] *save* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *save for* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *save for* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma when *save* attaches directly to the exhaustive word it subtracts from (*all the passengers save the pilot*). A comma when the exception trails the whole clause as an afterthought, which is the usual pattern with *save for* (*The room was empty, save for a folding chair*). Never a semicolon — it introduces a phrase, not a second independent clause.
**Variants:** *save for*, *saving*, *excepting*, *but*, *except*

The literary *except*. It sits between the exhaustive word (*all*, *every*, *none*, *nothing*) and the one item pulled out of it. *Save for* is the commoner modern form and is the one you will actually meet in journalism and fiction. Do not confuse it with the verb *save*: *He saved one* and *all save one* look alike and are unrelated. Four word classes share the spelling — verb (*save the file*), noun (*the goalie's save*), this exceptive preposition, and, with *that* added, a full subordinating conjunction — and only the last of those takes a clause.

1. Every window on the street was dark *save* one.
2. The room was empty, *save for* a folding chair and a coffee can full of nails.
3. All the passengers *save* the pilot survived the landing.
4. Nothing remained of the barn *save* the foundation.
5. He told no one *save* his wife.

**See also:** [save that](#save-that), [but](#but), [except](#except), [except that](#except-that)

### save and except

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪv ənd ɪkˈsɛpt/ &middot; /ˌseɪv n̩ ɪkˈsɛpt/ &middot; *sayv-and-ik-SEPT*
**Русский:** за исключением — юр., парная формула в актах на недвижимость и недра
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — exceptive; subordinating with *that* &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** exceptive phrase carving one thing out of a larger grant; with *that*, an adverbial clause of exception
**Pattern:** [grant of the whole] *save and except* [noun phrase]. &middot; [grant], *save and except that* [clause]. &middot; *SAVE AND EXCEPTING* therefrom [description of the parcel reserved].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it attaches directly to the thing it subtracts from, which is the ordinary case in a deed. A comma when the exception trails the whole clause as an afterthought. Never a semicolon — it introduces a phrase, or with *that* a subordinate clause, and neither is a second independent clause.
**Variants:** *save and excepting*, *saving and excepting*, *less and except* (the oil-and-gas variant), *excepting and reserving*, *except*, *save*

A legal doublet: *save* is the native English exceptive and *except* the Latin-derived one, welded together by the old drafting habit of pairing an English word with its Romance twin — the same instinct behind *will and testament* and *free and clear*. It survives in American deeds and mineral conveyances, where a *save and except* clause is how a seller keeps back a strip, a road, or the mineral rights while conveying everything else. Plain-drafting guides would have you write *except*; title examiners will be reading the doublet for as long as there are deeds.

1. The Grantor conveys all of Lot 14, *save and except* the westerly twenty feet reserved for the county road.
2. All of the Grantor's interest in the land described above is conveyed, *save and except* all oil, gas, and other minerals, which are reserved unto the Grantor.
3. The sale includes every fixture in the building, *save and except* the walk-in cooler.
4. *SAVE AND EXCEPTING* therefrom that portion heretofore conveyed to the school district by deed recorded in Book 212, Page 40.
5. The release covers claims of every kind, *save and except that* it does not extend to claims for indemnity under Section 9.

**See also:** [save](#save), [save that](#save-that), [except that](#except-that), [except as otherwise provided](#except-as-otherwise-provided), [but](#but)

### save that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈseɪv ðæt/ &middot; *SAYV that*
**Русский:** за исключением того, что — книжн., юр., клаузальный вариант save (= except that)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of exception
**Pattern:** [main clause], *save that* [clause]. &middot; *Save that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *save that* when it trails the main clause, and after the clause when it is fronted. Never a semicolon — what follows is a subordinate clause, not an independent one.
**Variants:** *saving that*, *except that* (the neutral equivalent), *but that*

The clause-taking counterpart of *save*, and a straight formal substitute for *except that*. It is the standard way in contract drafting to carve one exception out of a provision you have just stated, and in literary prose it introduces the one detail that survives an otherwise blank memory (*He remembered nothing, save that it had ended badly*).

1. The two texts are identical, *save that* the later one drops a footnote.
2. He remembered nothing of the evening, *save that* it had ended badly.
3. The terms are unchanged, *save that* the annual fee increases by three percent.
4. *Save that* she signed the register, there is no evidence she was ever in the building.
5. Nothing in this Section limits the Buyer's remedies, *save that* consequential damages are excluded.

**See also:** [save](#save), [except that](#except-that), [but that](#but-that), [provided always that](#provided-always-that)

## Degree, extent, and proportion

Two connectives that limit or scale a claim rather than assert it outright. Both are hedges, and both are alive in academic and legal writing where a flat statement would overclaim.

### according as

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋ æz/ &middot; /əˈkɔrdɪŋ əz/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding az*
**Русский:** смотря по тому, как; в зависимости от того — устар., книжн., соответствие
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of proportion or correspondence
**Pattern:** [main clause] *according as* [clause]. &middot; *According as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause; comma after a fronted *according as* clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *according to whether* (the modern equivalent), *in proportion as*

Sets up a correspondence: the main clause varies **in step with** the subordinate one. Modern English says *according to whether* or simply *depending on whether*, which is why *according as* now appears only in older statutes, actuarial and tax language, and nineteenth-century prose. Note that *according to* takes a noun phrase and *according as* takes a clause — that is the whole difference.

1. The tax is calculated *according as* the property is residential or commercial.
2. Members will be seated *according as* they arrive.
3. The grant is renewed or withdrawn *according as* the trustees judge the work to be sound.
4. He praised or blamed the crew *according as* the day had gone well or badly.
5. The payment rises or falls *according as* the index rises or falls.
6. *According as* the harvest was good or poor, the rent was raised or forgiven.

**See also:** [insofar as](#insofar-as), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that)

### insofar as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnsəˈfɑr æz/ &middot; /ˌɪnsəˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *in-suh-FAR az*
**Русский:** постольку, поскольку; в той мере, в какой — книжн., ограничивает утверждение
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of degree or extent
**Pattern:** [main clause] *insofar as* [clause]. &middot; *Insofar as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause; comma after a fronted *insofar as* clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *in so far as* (British spelling), *so far as*, *to the extent that* (the plainer equivalent)

Limits a claim to the degree it actually holds: *The rule applies insofar as it does not conflict with federal law* concedes that beyond that point it does not apply. Americans write it closed up as one word; the British write *in so far as*. Against **inasmuch as**, which is chiefly causal, *insofar as* is chiefly about scope, and *to the extent that* is the plain-English substitute a modern drafting guide will push you toward.

1. The state rule applies *insofar as* it does not conflict with federal law.
2. *Insofar as* I can tell, the two accounts agree on every material point.
3. He was helpful *insofar as* he handed over the file, but he would not explain any of it.
4. The company is liable *insofar as* its employees were acting within the scope of their duties.
5. *Insofar as* the treaty covers fishing rights, it remains in force.

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that), [according as](#according-as), [howsoever](#howsoever)

## The *wh-* relatives: *whence*, *whither*, and the *where-* compounds

A closed set built on one pattern: *where-* plus a preposition, meaning *preposition + which*. *Whereby* is fully alive, *wherein* and *whereupon* survive in legal and formal narrative prose, and the rest are archaic. Every one is a **relative** — it points back at something already named — which is why most cannot start a sentence. The one member built on a different plan is *whensoever*, *when* plus the intensifier *-soever*: it is a plain subordinator rather than a relative, but it keeps the shape and the register of the rest of the group.

### whence

**Pronunciation:** /wɛns/, /hwɛns/ &middot; *WENSS*
**Русский:** откуда — устар., лит.; предлог «от» уже заключён в самом слове
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb; also interrogative &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** relative clause of place/source, or interrogative noun clause
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whence* [clause]. &middot; [verb of knowing/saying] *whence* [clause]. &middot; *Whence* [verb] [subject]? &middot; [main clause], *whence* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *whence* when it comments on the whole preceding clause (*a crawlspace, whence a raccoon emerged*); no comma when it restricts a noun, and none before an indirect question (*no one could say whence the rumor had started*). Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *from whence* (long established, though strictly redundant), *whencesoever*

Means *from where* or *from which* — the *from* is already inside the word. That makes *from whence* a redundancy, which purists have objected to for three centuries and which the King James Bible uses anyway (*from whence cometh my help*), so it is best called long-established rather than wrong. It completes a set with **whither** (to where) and *where* (at where).

1. They went back to the village *whence* they had come.
2. No one could say *whence* the rumor had started.
3. The envelope gave no clue *whence* it had been mailed.
4. She traced the noise to a crawlspace, *whence* a very large raccoon eventually emerged.
5. *Whence* comes this sudden generosity?
6. I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from *whence* cometh my help. *(King James Bible)*

**See also:** [whither](#whither), [thence](#thence), [whereof](#whereof), [wherein](#wherein), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### whensoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛnsoʊˈɛvər/, /ˌhwɛnsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *wen-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** когда бы ни — устар., юр. канцелярит, усиленное whenever
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, formal — alive in legal boilerplate
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of indefinite time ("at whatever time", "no matter when")
**Pattern:** *Whensoever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whensoever* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *whensoever* [participle / prepositional phrase] (boilerplate, no commas).
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *whensoever* clause; no comma when it follows the main clause. Inside a noun phrase in boilerplate (*any claim whensoever arising*) it takes no punctuation at all. Never a semicolon — it is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb.
**Variants:** *whensoe'er* (poetic), *whenever* (the plain equivalent), *howsoever*, *wheresoever*, *whatsoever*

The *-soever* form of *whenever*: *at whatever time*. Like the rest of its family it survives in exactly two places — the King James Bible and the belt-and-suspenders language of contracts and releases, where *whensoever arising* is written to keep any argument about timing from getting started. In ordinary American English *whenever* covers every use, and *whensoever* in modern prose reads as costume.

1. *Whensoever* the Board determines that a vacancy exists, it may appoint a successor to serve out the unexpired term.
2. The Indemnitor shall pay all losses, *whensoever* arising, out of the use or operation of the equipment.
3. For ye have the poor with you always, and *whensoever* ye will ye may do them good. *(King James Bible)*
4. Any claim under this Agreement, *whensoever* and howsoever arising, shall be submitted to arbitration.
5. *Whensoever* the King's peace was broken, the sheriff of the county was answerable for it.

**See also:** [howsoever](#howsoever), [whenas](#whenas), [whereupon](#whereupon), [whither](#whither), [wheresoever](09-place-relative.md#wheresoever)

### whereas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæz/ &middot; /wərˈæz/ &middot; *wair-AZ*
**Русский:** принимая во внимание, что — юр., зачин преамбулы договора (не «тогда как»)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** recital clause — a stacked background statement, not joined to a main clause
**Pattern:** *WHEREAS*, [clause]; &middot; *WHEREAS*, [clause]; *NOW, THEREFORE*, [operative clause]. &middot; *Whereas* [clause], [resolving clause].
**Punctuation:** In recitals each *WHEREAS* clause ends with a **semicolon**, not a comma, because the recitals are a stacked list rather than subordinate clauses attached to a main clause; the last one runs into *NOW, THEREFORE*. The word is conventionally capitalized and often followed by a comma. In the resolving use, where the clause does reach a main clause (*Whereas the Tenant has failed to pay rent, the Landlord hereby terminates the lease*), it is punctuated like any fronted subordinate clause: a comma closes it. The everyday contrastive *whereas* — a different word, filed with the concessives — takes a **comma before** a trailing clause: *I like tea, whereas he likes coffee.*
**Variants:** *whereas clause* (noun use), *given that, considering that* (the plain equivalents)

Two different words share this spelling. The everyday one is the contrastive subordinator (*I like tea, whereas he likes coffee*), which belongs with the other concessives. This entry is the **legal recital** *whereas*, meaning roughly *given that* — the word that opens each background paragraph of a contract, resolution, or preamble. Its syntax is unique: the clause never gets a main clause of its own, and the whole stack is discharged by *NOW, THEREFORE*.

1. *WHEREAS*, the Company desires to retain the Consultant's services;
2. *WHEREAS*, the Consultant is willing to provide such services on the terms set forth herein;
3. *WHEREAS* the parties entered into a Master Services Agreement dated March 3, 2021, and *WHEREAS* the parties now wish to amend that Agreement;
4. *Whereas* it has pleased the Assembly to honor our late colleague, be it resolved that this chamber stand adjourned for the day.
5. *Whereas* the Tenant has failed to pay rent for three consecutive months, the Landlord hereby terminates the lease.

**See also:** [now, therefore](#now-therefore), [witnesseth](#witnesseth), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [wherefore](#wherefore)

### whereat

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæt/ &middot; *wair-AT*
**Русский:** на что, в ответ на что — устар., лит., реакция на только что случившееся
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** sentence-relative clause of reaction or point in time
**Pattern:** [clause], *whereat* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Always a comma before it. Like all the *where-* relatives it points back at the clause before it, so it cannot open a sentence.
**Variants:** *at which point* (the modern equivalent), *whereupon*

Means *at which* — most often *at which point*, introducing somebody's reaction to what has just happened. It is the rarest of the surviving *where-* compounds and is functionally interchangeable with **whereupon**, except that *whereat* leans toward a reaction and *whereupon* toward a next event.

1. He called her a liar, *whereat* she picked up her coat and walked out.
2. The judge asked twice for silence, *whereat* the courtroom finally stilled.
3. She read the telegram, *whereat* all the color left her face.
4. The vote was announced, *whereat* a cheer went up from the gallery.
5. He proposed a toast to the bride, *whereat* the whole table rose.

**See also:** [whereupon](#whereupon), [wherein](#wherein), [whereof](#whereof), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### whereby

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *wair-BY*
**Русский:** посредством чего, благодаря чему — книжн., при слове со значением механизма
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** relative clause modifying a noun of mechanism (*system*, *process*, *arrangement*)
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whereby* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase], *whereby* [clause] (nonrestrictive, rarer).
**Punctuation:** Normally no comma — it restricts the noun it follows, and a comma there is usually an error. The exception is the nonrestrictive use, where the noun is already fully identified and *whereby* merely adds information about it (*the 1994 accord, whereby each side gave up its claims*); that one takes a comma. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *by which* (the plain equivalent)

Means *by which* or *by means of which*, and it is by a wide margin the healthiest of the *where-* compounds: American lawyers, journalists, and policy writers use it daily. It needs a noun of mechanism in front of it — a *system*, *scheme*, *process*, *arrangement*, *mechanism*, *deal* — and sounds wrong attached to anything else.

1. The company has a system *whereby* employees can report problems anonymously.
2. The two sides reached an arrangement *whereby* each dropped its claims against the other.
3. The statute creates a process *whereby* records can be sealed after ten years.
4. There is no mechanism *whereby* a sitting member can be expelled.
5. He described a scheme *whereby* early investors would be paid out of new deposits.
6. The 1994 accord, *whereby* each side gave up its claims to the border strip, has held ever since. *(nonrestrictive — hence the commas)*

**See also:** [wherein](#wherein), [whereof](#whereof), [whereon](#whereon), [wherewith](#wherewith), [thereby](#thereby)

### wherefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛrfɔr/ &middot; *WAIR-fore*
**Русский:** почему; вследствие чего — устар.; в юр. — зачин просительной части иска
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (legal); interrogative adverb (archaic) &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of consequence; or interrogative noun clause of reason
**Pattern:** [clause]; *wherefore*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]. *Wherefore*, [clause]. &middot; *WHEREFORE*, [petitioner] prays [clause]. &middot; *Wherefore* [verb] [subject]?
**Punctuation:** As a consequence marker it behaves like *therefore*: **semicolon or period before, comma after**. A comma alone before it makes a comma splice.
**Variants:** *the whys and wherefores* (fixed noun phrase), *therefore* (the modern equivalent)

Means *for what reason* (the archaic question word) or *for which reason* (the legal consequence marker). Its famous line is routinely misread: *Wherefore art thou Romeo?* asks **why** Romeo has to be a Montague, not where he is standing. In American litigation it survives as the fixed opener of the prayer for relief, *WHEREFORE, Plaintiff respectfully requests …*, which is the last paragraph of nearly every complaint filed in the country.

1. *Wherefore* art thou Romeo? *(Shakespeare — "why are you Romeo," not "where are you")*
2. *WHEREFORE*, Plaintiff respectfully requests that this Court enter judgment in her favor and award costs.
3. The defendant has failed to answer within the time allowed; *wherefore*, default is hereby entered.
4. *Wherefore* dost thou weep, when the thing is already done?
5. The board found no misconduct; *wherefore*, the complaint is dismissed.

**See also:** [now, therefore](#now-therefore), [whereas](#whereas), [ergo](#ergo), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### wherein

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɪn/ &middot; *wair-IN*
**Русский:** в котором; в чём именно — книжн., юр.
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** relative clause of place or respect; or interrogative noun clause
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *wherein* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *wherein* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it restricts a noun; a comma when it comments on the whole preceding clause.
**Variants:** *in which* (the plain equivalent), *in what respect*

Means *in which* — either literally inside something (*a contract wherein the seller disclaims all warranties*) or, more often, *in what respect* (*He could not see wherein he had gone wrong*). It is the second-healthiest of the *where-* compounds after *whereby*, and it is standard in judicial opinions.

1. This is a case *wherein* the facts matter a great deal more than the law.
2. The contract contains a clause *wherein* the seller disclaims all warranties.
3. He could not see *wherein* he had gone wrong.
4. She described in detail the manner *wherein* the funds had been transferred.
5. The report identifies three areas *wherein* the agency exceeded its statutory authority.
6. She signed without reading the addendum, *wherein* the arbitration clause was buried. *(comment on the whole clause — hence the comma)*

**See also:** [whereby](#whereby), [whereof](#whereof), [whereat](#whereat), [therefor](#therefor)

### whereinto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrɪnˈtu/ &middot; *wair-in-TOO*
**Русский:** куда, во что — устар., библ., юр.; динамическая пара к wherein
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, legal
**Clause type:** relative clause of entry or destination (*into which*)
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whereinto* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase], *whereinto* [clause] (nonrestrictive). &middot; [noun phrase] *whereinto* *to* [verb] (rare).
**Punctuation:** No comma when it restricts the noun it follows, which is the ordinary case; a comma in the nonrestrictive use, where the noun is already identified. Like every *where-* relative it points back at a noun already on the page, so it cannot open a sentence. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *thereinto* (the free-standing counterpart), *into which*, *wherein* (the static counterpart)

Means *into which* — the motion counterpart of **wherein**, which is static: *the room wherein he sat* against *the room whereinto he was led*. It draws exactly the line that *in* and *into* draw in ordinary English, and it is worth noticing because *wherein* is often written where *whereinto* is meant. Outside scripture, deeds, and period narrative it is dead; *into which* is the living phrase.

1. There was a garden, *whereinto* he entered, and his disciples. *(King James Bible)*
2. The deed describes an open cellar *whereinto* the surface water has drained for sixty years.
3. He led her down to a low chamber *whereinto* no daylight ever came.
4. This is the pit *whereinto* every argument of the kind eventually falls.
5. They came at last to a cleft in the rock, *whereinto* the whole company was obliged to creep one at a time.

**See also:** [wherein](#wherein), [whereto](#whereto), [whereon](#whereon), [whereof](#whereof), [whither](#whither)

### whereof

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈʌv/, /wɛrˈɑv/ &middot; *wair-UV*
**Русский:** которого, о котором — устар., юр.; живёт в формуле in witness whereof
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** relative clause modifying a noun (*of which*, *of whom*)
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whereof* [clause]. &middot; IN WITNESS *WHEREOF*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the fixed attestation formula — nothing separates *WITNESS* from *WHEREOF* — though a comma does close the formula before the main clause (*IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the parties have executed …*). Elsewhere, no comma when it restricts a noun. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *of which*, *of whom*

Means *of which* or *of whom*. Nearly all of its modern life is inside one frozen formula — **IN WITNESS WHEREOF**, the signature line that closes almost every American contract, where it means *in witness of which*. Outside that formula it is purely literary.

1. IN WITNESS *WHEREOF*, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
2. He spoke at length of matters *whereof* he had no direct knowledge.
3. *Whereof* one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent. *(Wittgenstein, in translation)*
4. The document bears the seal *whereof* mention is made in Section 2.
5. She testified to events *whereof* she was the only living witness.

**See also:** [wherein](#wherein), [whereon](#whereon), [wherewith](#wherewith), [witnesseth](#witnesseth), [therefor](#therefor)

### whereon

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɑn/, /wɛrˈɔn/ &middot; *wair-ON*
**Русский:** на котором — устар., юр.; и буквально, и переносно («на чём основано»)
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** relative clause of location or basis (*on which*)
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whereon* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *whereon* *to* [verb]. &middot; [noun phrase], *whereon* [clause] (nonrestrictive).
**Punctuation:** No comma when it restricts the noun it follows, which is the ordinary case. A comma only in the nonrestrictive use, where the noun is already identified (*the north field, whereon the barn once stood*). Never a semicolon, and it cannot open a sentence — like every *where-* relative it points back at a noun already on the page.
**Variants:** *thereon* (the free-standing *there-* counterpart), *whereupon* (in its older locative sense), *on which* (the plain equivalent)

Means *on which*, both physically (*the rock whereon the church is built*) and abstractly (*the ground whereon the claim rests*). It is the locative half of a word that split in two: **whereupon** took over the time sense — *immediately after which* — and left *whereon* with the literal *on*, which is why *whereon* now sounds the older of the twins although the two were once interchangeable. In American prose it survives in deeds, land descriptions, and scriptural echo, and it takes a *to*-infinitive as readily as **wherewith** does.

1. This is the rock *whereon* the whole argument is built.
2. The deed describes the parcel *whereon* the mill formerly stood.
3. He pointed to the chair *whereon* the coat had been left.
4. She had no ground *whereon* to base an appeal.
5. The north field, *whereon* the barn once stood, has been planted in corn for forty years.
6. Every claim *whereon* the plaintiff relies was pleaded in the original complaint.

**See also:** [whereupon](#whereupon), [wherein](#wherein), [wherewith](#wherewith), [thereupon](#thereupon)

### whereto

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈtu/ &middot; *wair-TOO*
**Русский:** к которому, куда; для чего — устар., юр.
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb; also interrogative &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** relative clause of destination or purpose (*to which*); or interrogative noun clause (*to what end*)
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *whereto* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *whereto* [subject] [*is/are* + past participle]. &middot; *Whereto* [verb] [subject]? (interrogative).
**Punctuation:** No comma when it restricts the noun it follows; a comma in the nonrestrictive use. Never a semicolon, and it cannot open a sentence except as a direct question.
**Variants:** *whereunto* (the emphatic older form), *thereto* (the free-standing counterpart), *to which*, *to what end* (the interrogative sense)

Means *to which*, and in its older sense *to what end*. It is the destination relative, matching *thereto* ("to it") in the legal *there-* family and **whither** among the archaic direction words. American drafting kept the *there-* member and let this one go: a modern contract says *the exhibits attached thereto*, never *whereto*, and ordinary prose says *to which*.

1. This is the schedule *whereto* the exhibits are attached.
2. The office *whereto* he was appointed had stood vacant for the better part of a year.
3. *Whereto* serves mercy but to confront the visage of offence? *(Shakespeare, "Hamlet")*
4. The end *whereto* all this labor tended was never once explained to the men.
5. He named in his will the fund *whereunto* the residue of the estate should fall.

**See also:** [whereinto](#whereinto), [whereon](#whereon), [wherein](#wherein), [whither](#whither), [therefor](#therefor)

### whereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; *wair-uh-PON*
**Русский:** после чего, вслед за чем — книжн., второе событие, вызванное первым
**Type:** subordinating — sentence-relative adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** sentence-relative clause of immediate sequence
**Pattern:** [clause], *whereupon* [clause].
**Punctuation:** **A comma before it, never a period.** Because it is a relative, it must stay attached to the clause it refers back to; putting a period in front of it creates a fragment. Use **thereupon** if you want to start a new sentence.
**Variants:** *at which point* (the plain equivalent), *whereat*

Means *immediately after which* — it chains a second event onto the first and implies that the first triggered it. It is fully alive in formal narrative, judicial fact-recitals, and meeting minutes. The distinction that matters: *whereupon* joins clauses and cannot begin a sentence, whereas its *there-* twin **thereupon** is an ordinary adverb and can.

1. The alarm sounded, *whereupon* the entire floor emptied in under ninety seconds.
2. He handed over the envelope, *whereupon* the officers arrested him on the spot.
3. The motion was seconded, *whereupon* the chair called for a vote.
4. She announced that she was leaving, *whereupon* the room went completely quiet.
5. The deposit clears on Friday, *whereupon* title passes to the buyer.

**See also:** [thereupon](#thereupon), [whereat](#whereat), [whereby](#whereby), [wherefore](#wherefore)

### wherewith

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈwɪθ/, /wɛrˈwɪð/ &middot; *wair-WITH*
**Русский:** чем, посредством чего — устар., лит.; обычно о недостающем средстве
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** relative clause of means, usually followed by *to* + infinitive
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *wherewith* *to* [verb]. &middot; [noun phrase] *wherewith* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma — it restricts the noun it follows.
**Variants:** *wherewithal* (the surviving noun: *the wherewithal to pay*), *with which*

Means *with which* or *by means of which*, and it almost always names the resource somebody lacks (*no money wherewith to pay the fine*). The word has effectively been replaced by its own derivative: the noun **wherewithal**, meaning the means or money to do something, is ordinary modern English, while *wherewith* itself is confined to scripture and period prose.

1. They had no money *wherewith* to pay the fine.
2. He lacked the words *wherewith* to answer her.
3. If the salt has lost its savor, *wherewith* shall it be salted? *(after the King James Bible)*
4. She was given a small allowance *wherewith* to furnish the house.
5. The trust provides the funds *wherewith* the school is maintained.

**See also:** [whereof](#whereof), [whereby](#whereby), [wherein](#wherein), [whence](#whence)

### whither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪðər/ &middot; *WITH-er*
**Русский:** куда — устар., лит.; произносится со звонким th, не путать с wither
**Type:** subordinating — relative adverb; also interrogative &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** relative clause of destination, or interrogative noun clause
**Pattern:** *Whither* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *whither* [clause]. &middot; [verb of knowing/saying] *whither* [clause]. &middot; *Whither* [noun phrase]?
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *whither* clause. No comma when it restricts a noun, and none before an indirect question (*no one knew whither the caravan had turned*). The headline formula *Whither X?* is a verbless sentence and takes only its question mark. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *whithersoever*, *whither away* (archaic greeting)

Means *to where* or *to which* — the direction word matching **whence** (from where) and *where* (at where). It survives in two places: the King James Bible (*Whither thou goest, I will go*) and the headline formula *Whither X?*, meaning "where is X headed?" Note the pronunciation trap — it is /ˈwɪðər/ with a voiced *th*, a near-homophone of *wither*, and the two are constantly confused in print.

1. *Whither* thou goest, I will go. *(King James Bible)*
2. No one in the camp knew *whither* the caravan had turned.
3. *Whither* American democracy, now that the compromise has collapsed?
4. He followed the path *whither* it led, without much hope.
5. They were bound for a town *whither* none of them had ever traveled.

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [thence](#thence), [whereupon](#whereupon), [howsoever](#howsoever), [ere](#ere)

## Legal drafting connectives

The joinery of a contract. Only some of these are conjunctions; the rest are prepositions, pro-adverbs, and one archaic verb. They are grouped here because they appear in the same few paragraphs of the same documents, and a reader who knows *pursuant to* but not *therefor* is still stuck. Two families run in parallel and are worth learning as a pair: the *here-* words point at the document in your hand (*hereby*, *herein*, *hereto*), and the *there-* words point back at something already named (*therefor*, *thereby*, *thereupon*, *thence*).

### by reason whereof

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈrizən wɛrˈʌv/ &middot; *by-REE-zuhn wair-UV*
**Русский:** вследствие чего — юр., устар., в исковых заявлениях старого образца
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (fixed legal phrase) — sentence-relative &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, archaic-leaning
**Clause type:** sentence-relative connective of consequence — points back at the whole statement before it
**Pattern:** [clause], *by reason whereof* [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *by reason whereof*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma before it when it stays welded to the clause it points back at, exactly as with *whereupon*; a semicolon when the drafter wants two heavier clauses kept apart. Because it contains the relative *whereof* it cannot open a sentence — for that you need *by reason thereof* or plain *therefore*.
**Variants:** *by reason thereof* (the free-standing form), *by means whereof*, *by reason of which*, *as a result of which*, *whereby*

Old pleading language for *by reason of which*: it names the facts just recited as the cause of the damage about to be claimed, which is why it lives in the damages paragraph of a common-law complaint. It is the *where-* relative **whereof** frozen inside a longer phrase. Modern American pleading writes *as a result*, and only form books and older reports keep the full formula.

1. The defendant failed to maintain the stairway, *by reason whereof* the plaintiff fell and was grievously injured.
2. The vessel was unseaworthy at the time of sailing, *by reason whereof* the cargo was wholly lost.
3. No notice was ever given to the surety, *by reason whereof* the bond is discharged.
4. The tenant suffered the premises to fall into ruin, *by reason whereof* the lease became forfeit.
5. The contract was procured by fraud; *by reason whereof*, the plaintiff prays that it be rescinded and the consideration returned.

**See also:** [whereof](#whereof), [whereupon](#whereupon), [thereby](#thereby), [wherefore](#wherefore), [now, therefore](#now-therefore)

### hereby

**Pronunciation:** /hɪrˈbaɪ/, /ˈhɪrbaɪ/ &middot; *heer-BY*
**Русский:** настоящим — юр., «этим самым документом»; делает действие совершаемым, а не описанным
**Type:** performative adverb — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of instrument; marks its own clause as the act rather than a report of the act
**Pattern:** [subject] *hereby* [performative verb] [object]. &middot; [subject] *is hereby* [past participle]. &middot; [noun phrase] *herein* / *hereto* / *hereof* / *hereunder* — the same *here-* slot with a different preposition.
**Punctuation:** No commas — *hereby* sits between the subject and its verb, or between *is* and the participle, and is never set off. This is exactly where it parts company with **thereby**, which takes a comma before it when it heads a trailing participial phrase. Never a semicolon: it connects nothing and cannot join two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *herein*, *hereto*, *hereof*, *hereunder*, *hereinafter*, *heretofore*, *herewith*, *hereinabove*

The *here-* family is the mirror of the *there-* family under **therefor**: *here-* plus a preposition means that preposition plus *this document*. *Herein* is *in this agreement*, *hereto* is *to this agreement*, *hereunder* is *under this agreement*. *Hereby* is the odd one and the important one, because it means *by this very document* and is what makes an instrument **operate** instead of merely describe: *I hereby convey* performs the conveyance, while *I convey* only reports one. American plain-language guides tell drafters to delete most of the family and write *in this Agreement* rather than *herein*, but they defend *hereby* in the operative verb, because there the word is doing legal work. One structural difference from the *there-* family is worth holding on to: *here-* words point at the paper, so they can open a document, while *therefor* and *thereby* point backward at an antecedent and cannot.

1. The Seller *hereby* conveys to the Buyer all right, title, and interest in the Property.
2. Notice of termination is *hereby* given, effective thirty days from the date written above.
3. I *hereby* certify that the foregoing is a true and correct copy of the original.
4. The parties *hereby* waive any right to a jury trial in any action arising out of this Agreement.
5. The obligations described *herein* survive the closing, and the exhibits attached *hereto* form part of this Agreement.
6. The Executive Committee is *hereby* authorized to negotiate a lease on the terms set out below.

**See also:** [thereby](#thereby), [therefor](#therefor), [witnesseth](#witnesseth), [now, therefore](#now-therefore), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to)

### hereinafter

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrɪnˈæftər/ &middot; *heer-in-AF-ter*
**Русский:** далее по тексту, в дальнейшем именуемый — юр.
**Type:** cataphoric adverb — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial pro-form meaning *later in this document*; most often inside a defining parenthetical
**Pattern:** [full name] (*hereinafter* "[short name]"). &middot; [full name] (*hereinafter referred to as* "[short name]"). &middot; the [noun] described *hereinafter*.
**Punctuation:** No commas around it inside a defining parenthetical — *(hereinafter "the Buyer")* — and none when it modifies a verb. The defined term itself takes quotation marks. Never a semicolon: it connects nothing.
**Variants:** *hereinafter referred to as*, *hereafter*, *hereinbelow*, *below* (the plain equivalent)

*Here-* plus *in* plus *after*: **from this point on in this document**. Its real work in American drafting is not direction but definition — it is the word that christens a party or a term for the rest of the instrument. Keep it apart from *hereafter*, which means *from this time forward* in the world rather than *from this point on in the paper*, and from its backward-looking twin **hereinbefore**. Plain-language drafters delete it and write *(the "Seller")*, which does the same job with nothing lost.

1. Ada Reyes, of Multnomah County, Oregon (*hereinafter* "the Seller"), agrees to convey the property described below.
2. Northline Freight, Inc. (*hereinafter referred to as* "the Carrier") shall maintain insurance in the amounts set out in Schedule C.
3. The rules of construction set forth *hereinafter* govern the interpretation of this Agreement.
4. The Association (*hereinafter* "the HOA") may levy assessments as provided in Article VIII.
5. Each of the foregoing is *hereinafter* referred to individually as a "Party" and collectively as the "Parties."

### hereinbefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrɪnbɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *heer-in-bee-FOR*
**Русский:** выше по тексту, ранее в настоящем документе — юр., устар.
**Type:** anaphoric adverb — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, archaic-leaning
**Clause type:** adverbial pro-form meaning *earlier in this document*
**Pattern:** the [noun] *hereinbefore* [past participle]. &middot; as *hereinbefore* [provided / described / set forth]. &middot; except as *hereinbefore* provided, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No commas — it sits directly in front of the participle it modifies (*the sum hereinbefore mentioned*) and is never set off. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *hereinabove*, *heretofore* (= before this time), *hereinafter* (its forward-looking twin), *above* (the plain equivalent)

*Earlier in this instrument* — the backward-looking mate of **hereinafter**. Three words in this family are swapped constantly and mean three different things: *hereinbefore* points **earlier in the paper**, *heretofore* points **earlier than now**, and **theretofore** points earlier than some other past moment. American plain-drafting guides retire all three in favor of *above* and a section number, but they survive in deeds, wills, and older form books.

1. The sum *hereinbefore* mentioned shall be paid on the first day of each month.
2. Subject to the conditions *hereinbefore* set forth, the Grantor conveys the land to the Grantee in fee simple.
3. The Trustee shall hold the property upon the trusts *hereinbefore* declared.
4. Except as *hereinbefore* provided, no waiver of any covenant shall be effective unless made in writing.
5. The parties *hereinbefore* named have executed this instrument on the day and year first above written.

**See also:** [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [hereby](#hereby), [theretofore](#theretofore), [therefor](#therefor), [hereupon](#hereupon)

### hereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrəˈpɑn/, /ˌhɪrəˈpɔn/ &middot; *heer-uh-PON*
**Русский:** тут же, вслед за этим — устар., книжн., от «этого» момента повествования
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, formal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of immediate sequence (= *immediately after this*)
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Hereupon* [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *hereupon*, [clause]. &middot; *Hereupon* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** A **semicolon or a period before it** when it stands between two independent clauses; a comma there is a comma splice. A comma after it is optional. It is not the relative *whereupon* and must not be welded to the previous clause with a comma.
**Variants:** *thereupon* (the commoner form, pointing back at a past event), *whereupon* (the relative), *hereon*, *at this point*

The third member of the *upon* trio: **whereupon** is the relative that has to stay attached to the clause before it, **thereupon** points back at a past event, and *hereupon* points at **this** moment — the one the narrator has just brought the reader to. That deictic *here* is why it is the rarest of the three: it wants a narrator standing in the scene, which is how Bunyan, Milton, and the eighteenth-century pamphleteers use it. In a modern American document it is essentially never the right word.

1. The messenger delivered his letter. *Hereupon* the whole council fell to arguing at once. *(18th-century narrative)*
2. He confessed the debt in open court; *hereupon* the plaintiff moved for judgment.
3. *Hereupon* the pilgrim laid down his burden and wept aloud. *(after Bunyan)*
4. The bell was rung twice. *Hereupon*, the doors were shut and no one else admitted.
5. She refused outright to sign the paper; *hereupon* the notary declined to proceed.

**See also:** [thereupon](#thereupon), [whereupon](#whereupon), [hereby](#hereby), [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [whereat](#whereat)

### in lieu of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlu əv/ &middot; /ɪn ˈlu ə/ &middot; *in-LOO uhv*
**Русский:** вместо, взамен — книжн., юр.; не «ввиду» — это частая ошибка
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of substitution (= *instead of*) modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in lieu of* [noun phrase / -ing form]. &middot; *In lieu of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [noun] *in lieu of* [noun].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *in lieu of* phrase; no comma when it trails, which is the ordinary case. Never a semicolon. Note the shape of the phrase: American English requires the *of* — bare *in lieu* is British and reads as an error here.
**Variants:** *instead of*, *in place of*, *in substitution for*, *in exchange for*

French *lieu* is "place," so *in lieu of X* is *in the place of X*: one thing is given and another withheld. It is ordinary formal English in American business and legal writing, where it also names specific transactions — *deed in lieu of foreclosure*, *payment in lieu of taxes*, *pay in lieu of notice*. Watch the trap: it means *instead of*, never *in light of*, and using it for "in view of" is a common and conspicuous mistake.

1. The employee received four weeks' pay *in lieu of* notice.
2. *In lieu of* flowers, the family asks that donations be made to the county library.
3. The borrower offered the bank a deed *in lieu of* foreclosure rather than fight the case for a year.
4. The university will accept a portfolio *in lieu of* the standard entrance examination.
5. The town receives an annual payment *in lieu of* taxes from the hospital.
6. *In lieu of* a jury trial, the parties agreed to submit the dispute to a single arbitrator.

### in witness whereof

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈwɪtnəs wɛrˈʌv/ &middot; *in-WIT-nuhs wair-UV*
**Русский:** в удостоверение чего — юр., формула перед подписями сторон
**Type:** sentence adverbial (fixed legal formula) — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** attestation formula opening the execution clause of an instrument
**Pattern:** *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, [parties] have executed this [instrument] as of [date]. &middot; *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, I have hereunto set my hand and seal.
**Punctuation:** Conventionally set in **capitals and closed by a comma**; nothing separates *WITNESS* from *WHEREOF*. It opens the last paragraph of the document, immediately above the signature lines. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *in testimony whereof*, *witness my hand and seal*, *in witness thereof* (an error — the formula takes *whereof*)

*In witness of which* — the closing formula of nearly every American contract, deed, will, and certificate, matching **know all men by these presents** at the top and **witnesseth** before the recitals. The *whereof* points back at the entire instrument: the signatures are offered as evidence of everything above them. Plain-language drafters replace it with a bare **Signatures** heading, but form books and county recorders keep it in circulation.

1. *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
2. *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, I have hereunto set my hand and affixed my official seal this ninth day of June.
3. *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, the Grantor has signed and sealed this deed on the day and year first above written.
4. *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, the undersigned has caused this certificate to be executed by its duly authorized officer.
5. *IN WITNESS WHEREOF*, I set my hand this day and declare this instrument to be my last will and testament.

### including but not limited to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈkludɪŋ bʌt nɑt ˈlɪmɪtɪd tu/ &middot; /ɪnˈkludɪŋ bət nɑt ˈlɪmɪtɪd tə/ &middot; *in-KLOO-ding but not LIM-it-id too*
**Русский:** включая, но не ограничиваясь — юр., перечень заведомо неполон
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective introducing a nonexhaustive list &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of exemplification attached to the general noun before it
**Pattern:** [general noun], *including but not limited to*, [examples]. &middot; [general noun] *including without limitation* [examples]. &middot; [general noun], *including, but not limited to*, [examples].
**Punctuation:** A comma before it when the list is an aside on a noun already complete in itself, and a **matching comma after the whole list** — the closing comma is the one drafters forget. No commas at all when the phrase is welded into a defined term. Never a semicolon before it; when the listed items themselves contain commas, the items are divided by semicolons.
**Variants:** *including without limitation*, *including, without limitation*, *including but not limited to the following*, *such as*, *including* (the plain form)

The drafter's insurance policy: it announces that the examples to follow do not exhaust the general word in front of them. Lawyers write it because of *ejusdem generis*, the canon that reads a general term as limited to things like the specific ones listed, and the phrase exists to defeat that reading. Against **inter alia**, which merely reports that a list is incomplete, this one **instructs** the reader to take it as incomplete. Plain-language authorities point out that a bare *including* already means this; American drafters keep writing the long form anyway.

1. The Tenant shall be responsible for all utilities, *including but not limited to* water, sewer, gas, and electricity.
2. The Company may terminate for cause, *including but not limited to* fraud, theft, or willful misconduct.
3. Damages of every kind, *including without limitation* lost profits and loss of goodwill, are excluded by this Section.
4. The permit covers commercial vehicles *including but not limited to* trailers, tractors, and semitrailers.
5. All intellectual property, *including but not limited to* patents, trademarks, copyrights, and trade secrets, passes to the Buyer at closing.

**See also:** [without limiting the generality of the foregoing](#without-limiting-the-generality-of-the-foregoing), [inter alia](#inter-alia), [exempli gratia](#exempli-gratia), [videlicet](#videlicet), [save and except](#save-and-except)

### know all men by these presents

**Русский:** настоящим доводится до всеобщего сведения — юр., устар., зачин доверенности
**Type:** performative formula (imperative clause) — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** attestation formula opening a deed, bond, or power of attorney; followed by a *that* clause
**Pattern:** *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*, that [clause]. &middot; *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*: [clause]. &middot; *KNOW ALL PERSONS BY THESE PRESENTS*, that [clause].
**Punctuation:** Conventionally capitalized and closed by a **comma before the *that* clause**, or by a colon. No punctuation inside the formula itself. It opens the instrument, so nothing precedes it and it never takes a semicolon.
**Variants:** *know all persons by these presents* (the modern degendered form), *know ye*, *know all men whom these presents may concern*, *to all to whom these presents shall come*

The old opener of a deed, bond, or power of attorney, matching **in witness whereof** at the foot of the page. *These presents* means *this present document* — *presents* is a noun here, from the legal French *ces présentes*, and has nothing to do with gifts — so the whole formula is an imperative addressed to the world: *let everyone know, by this document, that …*. 1. *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*, that I, Owen Pike, of Suffolk County, do hereby appoint Ada Reyes my true and lawful attorney-in-fact.
2. *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*, that we, the Principal and the Surety, are held and firmly bound unto the State of Ohio in the sum of fifty thousand dollars.
3. *KNOW ALL PERSONS BY THESE PRESENTS*, that the Grantor, for value received, conveys the land described in Exhibit A.
4. *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*: the undersigned releases all claims arising out of the collision of March 3.
5. *KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS*, that the above-named obligor is firmly bound as set forth below.

### now, therefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaʊ ˈðɛrfɔr/ &middot; *now-THAIR-fore*
**Русский:** исходя из вышеизложенного — юр., переход от преамбулы к обязательствам договора
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (fixed legal formula) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial marking the transition from recitals to operative provisions
**Pattern:** *NOW, THEREFORE*, in consideration of [X], the parties agree as follows: &middot; *NOW, THEREFORE*, BE IT RESOLVED that [clause].
**Punctuation:** Conventionally set in capitals with a **comma after *NOW* and a comma after *THEREFORE***, then a comma closing the consideration phrase that follows. The recital immediately before it ends with a semicolon, not a period.
**Variants:** *now therefore* (without the internal comma), *now, therefore, be it resolved*

The hinge of a contract or resolution: it closes the stack of *WHEREAS* recitals and opens the part that actually binds. It is *therefore* with a ceremonial *now* attached, and it works as a structural signpost rather than a genuine logical inference — nothing in the recitals really entails what follows.

1. *NOW, THEREFORE*, in consideration of the mutual covenants set forth herein, the parties agree as follows:
2. *NOW, THEREFORE*, BE IT RESOLVED that the Board authorizes the President to execute the lease.
3. *Now, therefore*, the Seller conveys to the Buyer all right, title, and interest in the Property.
4. *Now, therefore*, in reliance on the representations set out above, the Lender agrees to extend the maturity date to June 30.
5. *NOW, THEREFORE*, for good and valuable consideration, the receipt of which is hereby acknowledged, the parties covenant as follows:

### per

**Pronunciation:** /pɜr/ &middot; /pər/ &middot; *PUR*
**Русский:** согласно, по (per Section 12); в расчёте на (per annum) — книжн., деловой жаргон
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** formal, business jargon
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of source or authority modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** *Per* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *per* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As per* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *per* phrase; no comma when it trails the clause.
**Variants:** *as per*, *per annum, per diem, per capita* (the distributive sense)

Two jobs. The old distributive one is *for each* (*five percent per annum*). The connective one, borrowed from legal Latin, is *according to* — *per our conversation*, *per Section 12* — and this is the one that dominates American business email. The expanded **as per** is entrenched but widely disliked as redundant; *per* alone, or plain *according to*, is safer in anything that will be read closely.

1. *Per* our conversation this morning, I have attached the revised estimate.
2. The flooring was installed *per* the manufacturer's written instructions.
3. *As per* your request, the meeting has been moved to Thursday afternoon.
4. Interest accrues at five percent *per* annum on the unpaid balance.
5. *Per* Section 12, notice of termination must be given in writing.

### pursuant to

**Pronunciation:** /pərˈsuənt tu/ &middot; /pərˈsuənt tə/ &middot; *per-SOO-uhnt too*
**Русский:** во исполнение, в соответствии с — юр., со ссылкой на норму или пункт
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of authority modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** *Pursuant to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *pursuant to* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *pursuant to* phrase; no comma when it trails the clause.
**Variants:** *in accordance with*, *under* (the plain-English equivalent)

Means *under the authority of* or *in accordance with*, and it is the standard way an American lawyer cites the rule that licenses an action (*pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6)*). Against **per**, which is loose and commercial, *pursuant to* is precise and points at a specific legal instrument. Plain-language drafting guides recommend replacing it with *under*, and courts increasingly do.

1. *Pursuant to* Section 8 of the Agreement, the Buyer hereby gives notice of termination.
2. The records were released *pursuant to* a valid and properly served subpoena.
3. *Pursuant to* Rule 12(b)(6), the defendant moves to dismiss the complaint in its entirety.
4. The search was conducted *pursuant to* a warrant issued that same morning.
5. *Pursuant to* the court's order of May 4, the parties have conferred in good faith.

### subject to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tu/ &middot; /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt tə/ &middot; *SUB-jikt too*
**Русский:** с учётом, при условии соблюдения — юр.; противоположность notwithstanding
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of condition or subordination modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** *Subject to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *subject to* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *subject to* phrase. A trailing one takes a comma **only** when it qualifies the whole clause as an afterthought (*The merger will close on October 1, subject to the Board's approval*); it takes **no** comma when it is part of the predicate, which is the commoner case (*The offer is subject to financing*). Never a semicolon. Note the stress: the connective is **SUB**-ject, first syllable, unlike the verb sub-**JECT**.
**Variants:** *subject always to*, *without prejudice to* (a near-synonym in drafting)

The mirror image of **notwithstanding**: *subject to Section 4* means Section 4 wins, while *notwithstanding Section 4* means this clause wins. That single pair does most of the work of resolving conflicts between provisions in an American contract, and reversing them inverts the deal. In ordinary use it simply means *conditional on* (*subject to financing*).

1. *Subject to* Section 4, the Company shall reimburse all reasonable travel expenses.
2. The offer is *subject to* financing and to a satisfactory inspection.
3. *Subject to* the Board's approval, the merger will close on October 1.
4. All payments under this Agreement are *subject to* withholding required by applicable law.
5. The lease is *subject to* the rights of the existing tenants.

### thence

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛns/ &middot; *THENSS*
**Русский:** оттуда, далее — устар., лит.; в межевых описаниях земли — живое рабочее слово
**Type:** anaphoric adverb of source — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary in prose; fully alive in deeds and surveys
**Clause type:** adverbial pro-form meaning *from there* / *from that*; continues a route or a chain of reasoning
**Pattern:** [clause], *thence* *to* [place]. &middot; [clause] and *thence* [direction / prepositional phrase]. &middot; *THENCE* [bearing] [distance] *to* [monument] (survey call). &middot; [clause]; *thence* [clause] (the old inferential sense).
**Punctuation:** A comma or *and* before it when it continues an itinerary (*they sailed to Lisbon, thence to Tangier*). In a metes-and-bounds description each *thence* opens a new leg and the legs are divided by semicolons, with no comma after the word itself. It is **not** a conjunction, so it cannot join two independent clauses on a comma — that is a comma splice, exactly as it would be with *therefore*; use a semicolon or a period.
**Variants:** *from thence* (long established, though strictly redundant), *hence* (= from here), *whence* (= from where), *thenceforth*, *thenceforward*

The demonstrative member of the little set that **whence** and **whither** belong to: *whence* is *from where*, *hence* is *from here*, *thence* is *from there*. The *from* is already inside the word, which makes *from thence* a redundancy of exactly the same kind as *from whence* — and, since the King James translators wrote it too, exactly as long established. Two lives survive in American English. In literary and travel prose it chains an itinerary (*to Denver, and thence to the coast*). In land law it is not archaic at all: every metes-and-bounds description in an American deed is a chain of *thence* calls, and a title examiner reads dozens in a working week. A third, faded sense — *from that it follows* — has been handed over to *hence*.

1. They sailed first to Lisbon, *thence* to Tangier.
2. The family moved to Chicago in 1911 and *thence* to Detroit two years later.
3. *THENCE* North 12 degrees East a distance of 210.5 feet to an iron pin; *THENCE* South 78 degrees East 96 feet to the point of beginning. *(deed description)*
4. Water is pumped to the reservoir on the hill and *thence* to every house in the valley.
5. The trail climbs to the ridge, *thence* down into the next valley.
6. The premise is granted; *thence* the conclusion follows without further argument. *(the old inferential sense; modern English says "hence")*

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [whither](#whither), [thereupon](#thereupon), [therefor](#therefor), [hereby](#hereby)

### thereby

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrˈbaɪ/, /ˈðɛrbaɪ/ &middot; *thair-BY*
**Русский:** тем самым, посредством этого — книжн., называет механизм полученного результата
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of result; most often heads a participial phrase
**Pattern:** [clause], *thereby* [-ing phrase]. &middot; [clause] and *thereby* [verb phrase]. &middot; [clause]; *thereby* [clause].
**Punctuation:** When it heads a participial phrase, put a comma **before** *thereby* and **none after** it (*…, thereby cutting the commute*). When it joins two independent clauses on its own, it needs a **semicolon** before it, like any conjunctive adverb.
**Variants:** *hereby* (= by this document), *by that means*, *thus*

Means *by that means* — it names the mechanism by which the main clause produced its effect. Its favorite construction by far is a trailing participle (*thereby reducing errors*), which is also where writers get into trouble: the participle must attach to the subject of the main clause, or you have a dangling modifier. Distinguish **hereby**, which means *by this very document* and is what makes a legal instrument operate (*I hereby convey*).

1. The city widened the road, *thereby* cutting the average commute by six minutes.
2. She signed the release, *thereby* giving up any claim to the property.
3. He missed the filing deadline and *thereby* forfeited the appeal.
4. The deed was recorded on Monday; *thereby* the buyer's title became a matter of public record.
5. Automating the intake form eliminated three handoffs, *thereby* reducing errors by roughly half.

**See also:** [hereby](#hereby), [thereupon](#thereupon), [therefor](#therefor), [whereby](#whereby), [ergo](#ergo)

### therefor

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈfɔr/ &middot; *thair-FOR*
**Русский:** за это, для этого — юр., «for it»; не путать с therefore («поэтому»)
**Type:** anaphoric pro-adverb — not a true conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** adverbial pro-form standing for *preposition + it/that*; binds the clause to something already named
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *therefor* (= *for it* / *for that*). &middot; [verb] [object] *therefor*. &middot; [noun phrase] *thereto* / *therein* / *thereof* / *thereunder* — the same slot with a different preposition.
**Punctuation:** No commas — these words attach directly to what they modify and are never set off.
**Variants:** *thereto*, *therein*, *thereof*, *thereunder*, *therewith*, *thereat*, *thereon*, *therefrom*, *thereafter*

A whole family of legal pro-adverbs: *there-* plus a preposition, meaning that preposition plus *it* or *that*. *Therefor* is *for it*, *thereto* is *to it*, *therein* is *in it*, *thereunder* is *under it*. They are not conjunctions, but they do a conjunction's cohesive work, tying each clause back to an antecedent without repeating it. **Watch the stress**: *therefor* (thair-FOR, = for it) and *therefore* (THAIR-fore, = consequently) differ by one letter and one stress, and swapping them changes the meaning of a sentence entirely.

1. The Contractor shall supply all materials and submit a single invoice *therefor*.
2. If the equipment is damaged, the Lessee shall pay the reasonable cost of repair *therefor*.
3. This Agreement, together with all exhibits *thereto*, constitutes the entire understanding of the parties.
4. The Landlord shall maintain the building and all mechanical systems *therein*.
5. The Company grants the license and shall receive the royalty provided *therefor* in Schedule B.

**See also:** [hereby](#hereby), [thereby](#thereby), [thereupon](#thereupon), [whereof](#whereof), [wherein](#wherein)

### theretofore

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrtəˈfɔr/ &middot; *thair-tuh-FOR*
**Русский:** до того времени — юр., книжн.; отсчёт от момента в прошлом, а не от «сейчас»
**Type:** anaphoric adverb — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, archaic-leaning
**Clause type:** adverbial pro-form of time meaning *before that time*, anchored to a past reference point rather than to now
**Pattern:** [past-tense clause] *theretofore* [past participle]. &middot; the [noun] *theretofore* [held / paid / existing]. &middot; [main clause], as *theretofore* provided.
**Punctuation:** No commas — it sits directly before the participle or verb it modifies, like the rest of the *there-* family. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *heretofore* (= before now), *hereinbefore* (= earlier in this document), *thitherto*, *up to that time* (the plain equivalent)

*Before that time* — the past-anchored twin of *heretofore*, which means *before now*. The distinction is the whole reason the word exists: in a narrative or a set of findings that has already moved into the past, *heretofore* would wrongly reach back from the present day, while *theretofore* reaches back only as far as the moment under discussion. It survives in judicial opinions, deeds, and older statutes; plain-language drafters write *previously* or *before then*.

1. On March 3 the company canceled the policy that had *theretofore* covered the entire fleet.
2. The court set aside the judgment *theretofore* entered against the surety.
3. The parties abandoned the practice *theretofore* followed in settling their accounts.
4. She took possession of the farm, which had *theretofore* belonged to her uncle.
5. All sums *theretofore* advanced became due and payable upon the default.

**See also:** [hereinbefore](#hereinbefore), [hereinafter](#hereinafter), [thereupon](#thereupon), [therefor](#therefor), [thence](#thence)

### thereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; *thair-uh-PON*
**Русский:** вслед за тем; на этом основании — книжн., юр.
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, literary
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of immediate sequence or consequence
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Thereupon*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *thereupon* [clause]. &middot; [clause] and *thereupon* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** As a conjunctive adverb it takes a **semicolon or period before it**; a comma after it is optional. A comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *thereon*, *whereupon* (the relative counterpart), *at that point*

Means *immediately after that* or *on that basis*. It is the free-standing twin of **whereupon**, and the difference is purely syntactic: *whereupon* is a relative and must stay welded to the previous clause with a comma, while *thereupon* is an ordinary adverb and can open a brand-new sentence.

1. The chairman ruled the motion out of order. *Thereupon*, three members walked out.
2. She named her price; *thereupon* the negotiation ended.
3. The check cleared on Thursday, and *thereupon* the deed was recorded.
4. He confessed in full. *Thereupon* the investigation was closed.
5. The court found the contract void and *thereupon* dismissed the suit.

**See also:** [whereupon](#whereupon), [thereby](#thereby), [therefor](#therefor), [whereat](#whereat)

### therewithal

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrwɪˈðɔl/, /ˌðɛrwɪˈθɔl/ &middot; *thair-with-AWL*
**Русский:** вместе с тем; вслед за тем; к тому же — устар., поэт.
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of accompaniment or immediate sequence (= *with that*, *thereupon*, *besides*)
**Pattern:** [clause]; *therewithal* [clause]. &middot; [clause], and *therewithal* [verb phrase]. &middot; [clause]. *Therewithal*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** A **semicolon or a period before it** when it joins two independent clauses, exactly as with *thereupon*; a comma alone there is a comma splice. After *and* it takes no punctuation at all, which is its commonest position in period prose.
**Variants:** *therewith*, *withal* (the bare form), *thereupon*, *besides*, *at the same time*

*There* + *with* + *all*: *with that*, and by extension *thereupon* and *besides*. Period writers slide between two senses — the instrumental (*he drew his sword and therewithal cut the rope*) and the additive (*she was clever, and therewithal kind*) — and the reader has to take the sense from the context. Chaucer, Malory, and the King James translators use it; the modern words are *with that*, *thereupon*, and *besides*.

1. He drew a letter from his coat, and *therewithal* a small brass key.
2. She was quick and clever, and *therewithal* kinder than anyone else in that house.
3. The steward brought in bread and wine; *therewithal* he set a dish of olives on the board.
4. He gave his promise, and *therewithal* his hand upon it.
5. The storm broke a little after midnight; *therewithal* every light along the quay went out.

**See also:** [thereupon](#thereupon), [thereby](#thereby), [therefor](#therefor), [eftsoons](#eftsoons), [whereupon](#whereupon)

### without limiting the generality of the foregoing

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˌðaʊt ˈlɪmɪtɪŋ ðə ˌdʒɛnəˈrælɪti əv ðə fɔrˈɡoʊɪŋ/ &middot; *with-OWT LIM-it-ing thuh jen-uh-RAL-uh-tee uhv thuh for-GOH-ing*
**Русский:** не ограничивая общности вышеизложенного — юр., канцелярская формула
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (fixed legal formula) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial tying a specific provision back to the general one before it
**Pattern:** [general provision]. *Without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, [specific provision]. &middot; [general provision]; *without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, [specific provision].
**Punctuation:** A **period or a semicolon before it** and a **comma after it**. It stands between two independent clauses, so a comma alone in front of it is a comma splice, exactly as with *however*. In numbered drafting it usually opens its own subsection.
**Variants:** *without limiting the foregoing*, *without limitation*, *without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing* (the British form), *in particular but without limitation*

The long-form cousin of **including but not limited to**: it lets a drafter give one specific instance of a general obligation without the reader concluding that the general words have been cut down to that instance. Like the shorter phrase it exists to defeat *ejusdem generis*, the canon that reads general language as limited by the specifics attached to it. Plain-language authorities treat it as one of the worst mouthfuls in American drafting, and the honest replacements are *for example* or a separate numbered clause.

1. The Contractor shall comply with all applicable law. *Without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, the Contractor shall obtain every permit required by the county before work begins.
2. Tenant shall keep the premises in good repair; *without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, Tenant shall have the heating system serviced twice each year.
3. The Lender may take any action it deems necessary to protect the collateral. *Without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, it may take possession of the equipment without prior notice.
4. Each party shall keep the other's information confidential. *Without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, neither party shall disclose the pricing terms to any competitor.
5. The Seller makes no representation of any kind; *without limiting the generality of the foregoing*, the Seller disclaims all implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose.

**See also:** [including but not limited to](#including-but-not-limited-to), [inter alia](#inter-alia), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [save and except](#save-and-except), [hereby](#hereby)

### witnesseth

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪtnəsɪθ/, /ˈwɪtnəsəθ/ &middot; *WIT-nuh-sith*
**Русский:** удостоверяет — устар., юр., заголовок перед преамбулой договора (форма на -eth)
**Type:** archaic verb form used as a formulaic heading — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** structural heading standing between the parties clause and the recitals
**Pattern:** THIS [instrument], made [date], between [parties], *WITNESSETH*: &middot; THIS [instrument] *WITNESSETH that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Conventionally capitalized and followed by a **colon** when it heads the recitals, or by *that* when it runs straight into the operative language. No comma before it.
**Variants:** *witnesseth that*, *Recitals / Background* (the modern plain-English headings)

The third-person singular present of *witness* with the old *-eth* ending — literally "this document bears witness." It is not a conjunction, but it sits in the connective slot of a deed or indenture, immediately before the *WHEREAS* recitals, which is why it belongs alongside them. Modern American drafting has largely replaced it with a plain **Background** or **Recitals** heading, but it survives in deeds, trust instruments, and form books.

1. THIS INDENTURE, made this tenth day of April, between Ada Reyes and Owen Pike, *WITNESSETH*:
2. THIS AGREEMENT *WITNESSETH* that, in consideration of the sum of one dollar, the Grantor conveys the land described below.
3. THIS TRUST AGREEMENT *WITNESSETH*: That the Settlor hereby transfers to the Trustee the property described in Schedule A.
4. THIS BILL OF SALE *WITNESSETH* that the Seller, for value received, sells and delivers the goods listed below.
5. THIS LEASE, between the Landlord and the Tenant named above, *WITNESSETH* as follows:

## Archaic conjunctions: Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and the hymnal

Dead in speech, permanent in print. You will not write these, but you will read them, and each has a modern equivalent you can substitute on sight. Several are traps: *ere* is not *e'er*, *an* is not the article, and *without* is not the preposition.

### an

**Pronunciation:** /æn/ &middot; /ən/ &middot; *AN*
**Русский:** если — устар., шекспировское; не путать с артиклем an
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** *An* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *an* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *an* clause. A comma before a trailing one is usual, but that follows the loose comma practice of the period rather than the modern rule, which leaves a trailing conditional clause unpunctuated (*I will do it if you wish it*). In modernized editions it is often printed *an'* with an apostrophe. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *an't* (= an it), *and* (the older spelling of the same word), *an if*

The archaic conditional *if*, everywhere in Shakespeare and Middle English (*an it please your honor*). It is the same word as the coordinating *and*, which once carried a conditional sense too — the fossil survives in *and if* and in dialect. **Do not confuse it with the article *an***: the article precedes a noun, this precedes a whole clause.

1. *An* it please your honor, I shall wait outside. *(Shakespeare-era usage)*
2. I will do it, *an* you wish it.
3. *An* he be a true knight, he will keep his word.
4. Come tomorrow, *an* you can.
5. He would speak plainly, *an* he dared.

**See also:** [except](#except), [without](#without), [sith](#sith), [ere](#ere)

### anent

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnɛnt/ &middot; *uh-NENT*
**Русский:** касательно, относительно — устар., шотл., юр.
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** archaic; chiefly Scots and Scottish legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of topic (= *concerning*, *about*) modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** *Anent* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *anent* [noun phrase]. &middot; [noun of writing or speech] *anent* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *anent* phrase; no comma when it trails the clause. Never a semicolon — it takes a noun phrase, not a clause.
**Variants:** *anentis* (Middle Scots), *concerning*, *regarding*, *as touching* (the scriptural equivalent), *in re* (the legal Latin)

Means *concerning* or *about*. It is Scots and northern English rather than general archaic English, and an American meets it in Walter Scott, in Burns, in Scottish court reports and kirk-session records, and in the deliberately antique voice of nineteenth-century correspondence. Against **as touching**, the King James Bible's phrase for the same job, *anent* is legal and epistolary; both are dead in American prose, where *concerning* or plain *about* is the only live choice.

1. I have your letter *anent* the disputed march between our two farms. *(19th-century Scottish correspondence)*
2. The Session has taken no action *anent* the complaint laid before it in March. *(kirk-session records)*
3. *Anent* the matter of the entail, my client will say nothing further at present.
4. He held views *anent* the education of women that scandalized the whole parish.
5. The Sheriff pronounced an interlocutor *anent* the expenses of the cause. *(Scots legal usage)*
6. *Anent* your inquiry of the twelfth, the books were sold at Edinburgh in 1843.

**See also:** [as touching](#as-touching), [per](#per), [pursuant to](#pursuant-to), [in lieu of](#in-lieu-of), [inter alia](#inter-alia)

### as touching

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈtʌtʃɪŋ/ &middot; /æz ˈtʌtʃɪŋ/ &middot; *az-TUCH-ing*
**Русский:** что касается, относительно — устар., библ.
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of topic (= *concerning*, *as regards*) modifying the main clause
**Pattern:** *As touching* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as touching* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Touching* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, which is where it nearly always sits; no comma when it trails the clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *as concerning*, *touching* (the bare form), *touching upon*, *concerning* / *as regards* (the modern equivalents)

The King James Bible's way of announcing a change of subject: *As touching the resurrection of the dead, have ye not read …*. All three members of the set are participial prepositions — *touching* is the present participle of *touch* pressed into service exactly as *concerning* and *regarding* were — and *as concerning* and the bare *touching* do the same work. An American meets them in scripture, in Shakespeare, and in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century correspondence; the modern equivalents are *concerning*, *as for*, and *when it comes to*.

1. Now *as touching* things offered unto idols, we know that we all have knowledge. *(King James Bible)*
2. *As touching* the resurrection of the dead, have ye not read that which was spoken unto you by God? *(King James Bible)*
3. *Touching* the matter of the lease, I have written to Mr. Havisham this morning.
4. *As concerning* the sum advanced last Michaelmas, no account has yet been rendered.
5. He would say nothing whatever *as touching* his own part in the affair.
6. *Touching* your request for leave, the Colonel has returned no answer.

**See also:** [anent](#anent), [per](#per), [inter alia](#inter-alia), [whereof](#whereof), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as)

### eftsoons

**Pronunciation:** /ɛftˈsunz/ &middot; *eft-SOONZ*
**Русский:** вскоре после того; снова — устар., поэт.
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of immediate sequence or repetition (= *soon afterward*, *again*)
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Eftsoons* [clause]. &middot; [clause], and *eftsoons* [verb phrase]. &middot; [clause]; *eftsoons* [clause].
**Punctuation:** As a sentence adverb it takes a **semicolon or a period before it** when it stands between two independent clauses, exactly like *thereupon*; a comma there is a comma splice. No comma after it in verse, where it usually sits inside the line.
**Variants:** *eftsoon*, *eft* (the bare Old English adverb), *soon afterward*, *thereupon* (the surviving equivalent)

Old English *eft* ("again") plus *soon*: *soon afterward*, or simply *again*. It was already antique when Spenser revived it for *The Faerie Queene*, and Coleridge took it from Spenser for the line most American readers know it by — *Eftsoons his hand dropt he*, in "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner." You will meet it there, in Spenser, and essentially nowhere else; **thereupon** is the living word for the same job.

1. *Eftsoons* his hand dropt he. *(Coleridge, "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner")*
2. The knight rode forth at daybreak, and *eftsoons* came to a darksome wood. *(after Spenser)*
3. She rebuked him sharply; *eftsoons* he was begging her pardon.
4. *Eftsoons* the trumpet sounded a second time, and the lists were cleared.
5. He swore he would never come back to that house, yet *eftsoons* he was at the gate again.

**See also:** [thereupon](#thereupon), [therewithal](#therewithal), [whereupon](#whereupon), [ere](#ere), [verily](#verily)

### ere

**Pronunciation:** /ɛr/ &middot; *AIR*
**Русский:** прежде чем, до — устар., поэт.
**Type:** subordinating; also prepositional before a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (anteriority)
**Pattern:** *Ere* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *ere* [clause]. &middot; *ere* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *ere* clause; no comma when it follows the main clause — exactly like *before*.
**Variants:** *ere long* (= soon), *or ere* (emphatic archaic doubling), *ere now*

Simply *before*, in time. It is the standard poetic and scriptural form and is common in hymns, carols, and nineteenth-century verse. **Three near-homophones cause trouble**: *ere* (before), *e'er* (a contraction of *ever*), and *air* are all /ɛr/ in American English, and *err* (to make a mistake) is close enough to add to the confusion.

1. *Ere* the sun sets, we shall be home.
2. He was gone *ere* I could thank him.
3. *Ere* long the whole town knew every detail.
4. Many a night I have watched the harbor *ere* the boats came in.
5. She died *ere* the letter ever reached her.
6. *Ere* you go, take this with you.

**See also:** [sith](#sith), [an](#an), [whither](#whither), [whilst](#whilst)

### except

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt/ &middot; *ik-SEPT*
**Русский:** если не, разве что — устар. (= unless), библ.; требует сослагательного наклонения
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic (in this sense)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative condition (= *unless*)
**Pattern:** *Except* [subject] [**bare verb**], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *except* [subject] [bare verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *except* clause; no comma when it trails the main clause, exactly as with *unless*. The verb is in the **bare subjunctive**: *except he repent*, not *except he repents*. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *except that* (the modern exceptive), *unless* (the modern equivalent), *excepting*

This is the archaic *except* meaning **unless**, not the everyday one meaning *apart from*. It is the King James Bible's standard negative conditional — *Except a man be born again*, *Except ye repent* — and it takes the subjunctive as reliably as *lest* does. The modern exceptive sense is a separate entry, **except that**. Three word classes share the spelling: preposition (*everyone except me*), verb (*present company excepted*), and conjunction — and the conjunction itself splits in two, the archaic *unless* of this entry and the ordinary *except that*.

1. *Except* a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God. *(King James Bible)*
2. *Except* the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it. *(King James Bible)*
3. *Except* ye repent, ye shall all likewise perish. *(King James Bible)*
4. I will not go *except* you come with me.
5. No one may enter the hall *except* he bear the seal of the order.

**See also:** [except that](#except-that), [without](#without), [lest](#lest), [save](#save), [an](#an)

### for that

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˈðæt/ &middot; /fər ˈðæt/ &middot; *for-THAT*
**Русский:** потому что — устар., ранненовоанглийское because
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for that* [clause]. &middot; *For that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *for that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it restricts the main clause; a comma when it comments on the whole of it. Comma after a fronted *for that* clause. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *forasmuch as*, *in that* (the surviving formal descendant), *because* (the modern equivalent), *for* (the coordinator it is built on)

The Early Modern English *because*, built from the causal *for* plus the complementizer *that* — the same recipe that gave French *parce que* and Spanish *porque*. Shakespeare and the King James translators use it freely; it went out with the seventeenth century and left behind its narrower relative **in that**, which kept the construction but restricted it from plain cause to cause-in-a-specified-respect. Do not read it as the preposition *for* plus the demonstrative *that* (*this envelope is for that client*): the conjunction is followed by an entire clause, never by a noun.

1. He was punished *for that* he had spoken against the king.
2. *For that* the night was bitterly cold, they built the fire high and kept a watch.
3. The men gave thanks, *for that* the ship had come safely into harbor.
4. She would not open the door, *for that* she knew not who stood outside.
5. *For that* no answer was returned within the term, the suit was taken as confessed.
6. I love him not, *for that* he keeps such company.

**See also:** [in that](#in-that), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [sith](#sith), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### howbeit

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈbiɪt/ &middot; *how-BEE-it*
**Русский:** тем не менее, однако — устар., библ.
**Type:** conjunctive adverb; occasionally subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of concession; or adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Howbeit*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *howbeit*, [clause]. &middot; *Howbeit* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** As a sentence adverb it takes a **semicolon or period before, comma after**, exactly like *however*. As a subordinator (rarer) it takes a comma after its clause.
**Variants:** *how be it* (the original three-word form), *albeit* (the surviving cousin)

*However it may be* — that is, *nevertheless*. It is the exact concessive twin of **albeit**, built the same way from *how* + *be* + *it*, but where *albeit* survived into modern formal prose, *howbeit* died out completely after the seventeenth century. You will meet it in the King James Bible and in Shakespeare's contemporaries and essentially nowhere else.

1. *Howbeit*, the ship held together until morning.
2. He gave his word freely; *howbeit*, he did not keep it.
3. *Howbeit* we did not know it at the time, the decision had already been taken.
4. *Howbeit* when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth. *(King James Bible)*
5. She was warned twice. *Howbeit*, she went alone.

**See also:** [albeit](#albeit), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [sith](#sith)

### less'n

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɛsən/ &middot; *LESS-uhn*
**Русский:** если не, разве что — диал., простореч. (Юг, Аппалачи, Озарк) = unless
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** dialectal, nonstandard — Southern, Appalachian, and Ozark speech
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative condition (= *unless*)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *less'n* [clause]. &middot; *Less'n* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause; comma after a fronted *less'n* clause — the punctuation of *unless* exactly. In print it keeps the apostrophe, which marks the worn-away *un-*. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *lessen*, *'less*, *unlessen*, *without* (the same job in the same dialects), *unless* (the standard form)

*Unless* with the first syllable worn off — heard in Southern, Appalachian, and Ozark speech and used constantly by writers rendering it, from Twain and Joel Chandler Harris through a century of American dialect fiction. It is **not** archaic literary English and not available for ordinary writing: outside dialogue an American writes *unless*. It runs in harness with the conjunction **without** (= unless), the other nonstandard exceptive of the same regions.

1. I ain't going in there *less'n* you come with me.
2. He won't say a word about it *less'n* you ask him twice.
3. *Less'n* the rain holds off, there'll be no hay cut this week.
4. Don't you open that gate *less'n* I tell you to.
5. She'd never have known a thing about it *less'n* somebody told her.
6. There's no getting up that road *less'n* you've got chains on the tires.

**See also:** [without](#without), [except](#except), [an](#an), [save](#save), [unless](06-condition.md#unless)

### maugre

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɔɡər/ &middot; *MAW-gur*
**Русский:** вопреки, несмотря на — устар., поэт.
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — prepositional connective; not a clause introducer &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic
**Clause type:** concessive prepositional phrase (= *in spite of*)
**Pattern:** *Maugre* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *maugre* [noun phrase]. &middot; *maugre* [possessive] *teeth* (the fixed old idiom).
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *maugre* phrase; no comma when it trails. Never a semicolon — it takes a noun phrase, never a clause.
**Variants:** *malgre*, *mauger*, *in spite of*, *despite*, *notwithstanding* (the surviving formal equivalent)

From Old French *maugré*, "ill will" — literally *in the teeth of*, hence *in spite of*. Chaucer, Spenser, and Milton use it freely, and it kept one fossilized idiom for centuries: *maugre his teeth*, meaning "against his will, whatever he could do about it." It is a preposition and not a conjunction, and **notwithstanding** is the word that took over its work in modern formal English.

1. *Maugre* all his oaths, he was back in the tavern by nightfall.
2. She would have her way *maugre* her father and her uncles both.
3. *Maugre* his teeth, the knight was carried from the field. *(the fixed old idiom)*
4. The town held out *maugre* famine, fire, and three assaults in a single week.
5. *Maugre* the storm, the packet sailed on the evening tide.

**See also:** [natheless](#natheless), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [albeit](#albeit), [howbeit](#howbeit), [without](#without)

### natheless

**Pronunciation:** /ˈneɪθləs/, /ˈnæθləs/ &middot; *NAYTH-luhs*
**Русский:** тем не менее — устар., поэт., прямой предок nevertheless
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of concession (= *nevertheless*)
**Pattern:** [clause]; *natheless* [clause]. &middot; [clause]. *Natheless*, [clause]. &middot; *Natheless* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** A **semicolon or a period before it**, exactly as with *nevertheless*; a comma alone between the two clauses is a comma splice. A comma after it is optional and is usually dropped in verse for the meter.
**Variants:** *nathless* (Milton's spelling), *ne the less* (the original three words), *natheless that* (rare, clause-taking), *nevertheless* (the modern descendant)

Old English *ná þe lǽs*, "none the less" — the direct ancestor of *nevertheless*, built with the negative *ne* where the modern word has *never*. Milton's *Nathless he so endur'd* is where most American readers meet it; Spenser and the nineteenth-century medievalists supply the rest. Nothing about it is available for modern use: write *nevertheless*.

1. *Nathless* he so endur'd, till on the beach of that inflamed sea he stood. *(Milton, "Paradise Lost")*
2. The night was bitter cold; *natheless* they kept the watch till dawn.
3. He was warned twice of the danger. *Natheless*, he went alone into the wood.
4. Her fortune was gone; *natheless* she bore herself as a queen to the end.
5. *Natheless* the ship held her course, though the mast was sprung and the pumps were failing.

**See also:** [maugre](#maugre), [howbeit](#howbeit), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [albeit](#albeit), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding)

### sith

**Pronunciation:** /sɪθ/ &middot; *SITH*
**Русский:** поскольку; с тех пор как — устар., предок since
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause or of time
**Pattern:** *Sith* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *sith* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *sith* clause; no comma when it follows — the same rules that govern *since*.
**Variants:** *sithence*, *sith that*, *since* (the modern form)

The Middle and Early Modern English ancestor of *since*, and it carries the same double life: *because* (*Sith it is so, let us make the best of it*) and *from the time that* (*Much has changed sith last we met*). Spenser and Shakespeare use it freely; by the eighteenth century *since* had taken over completely, and *sith* survives only as deliberate archaism. Its longer form **sithence** — *sith* with the old adverbial *-s* on the end, respelled *-ce* — is the same conjunction in fuller dress, and is the form much sixteenth-century prose prefers; it died with the shorter one, which is why it is listed here rather than on its own.

1. *Sith* thou art gone, the house is silent.
2. *Sith* it is so, let us make the best of it.
3. Much has changed *sith* last we met.
4. *Sith* the king commands it, we must obey.
5. He has not spoken a word *sith* the trial began.
6. *Sithence* the Conquest, no such charter hath been granted. *(16th-century usage)*

**See also:** [ere](#ere), [an](#an), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### so soon as

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈsun æz/ &middot; /soʊ ˈsun əz/ &middot; *soh-SOON az*
**Русский:** как только — устар., старая форма as soon as
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (immediate anteriority)
**Pattern:** *So soon as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *so soon as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *so soon as* clause; no comma when it follows the main clause — the punctuation of *as soon as* exactly. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *as soon as* (the modern form), *so soon as ever* (emphatic), *the moment*, *immediately* (the British conjunction)

The older form of *as soon as*, standard through the eighteenth century and still ordinary in Austen, Scott, and the nineteenth-century novel: the *so … as* frame was the usual English pattern before *as … as* took it over in positive clauses. It is not an American form today, and in modern prose it reads as period costume rather than formality.

1. *So soon as* the letter was written, she carried it herself to the post. *(19th-century usage)*
2. He resolved to speak to her *so soon as* they were left alone.
3. *So soon as* the tide serves, the ship will drop down the river.
4. She knew him for a scoundrel *so soon as* he opened his mouth.
5. *So soon as* the news reached the town, every shop in the street was shut up.

**See also:** [ere](#ere), [whenas](#whenas), [whensoever](#whensoever), [sith](#sith), [as soon as](03-time.md#as-soon-as)

### verily

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɛrəli/ &middot; *VAIR-uh-lee*
**Русский:** истинно, воистину — устар., библ., ручается за сказанное вслед
**Type:** emphatic sentence adverb — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical
**Clause type:** discourse-initial sentence adverbial of assertion — vouches for the clause it opens
**Pattern:** *Verily* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Verily*, [clause]. &middot; *Verily, verily*, I say unto you, [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma after it in modern typesetting when it opens a sentence; the King James Bible prints *Verily I say unto you* with no comma at all, and quotations should keep the original pointing. It joins nothing, so nothing precedes it — it opens its own sentence, and a semicolon before it would be wrong.
**Variants:** *verily, verily* (the doubled scriptural form), *of a truth*, *truly*, *indeed* (the modern equivalent)

*Truly, in truth* — a swearing-word rather than a connective: it stakes the speaker's credit on what follows. Its home is the King James Gospels, where *Verily I say unto you* renders the Greek *amēn* and marks the most solemn sayings; John doubles it. An American meets it there, in hymns, and in the deliberately biblical cadence of nineteenth-century oratory, and in modern speech only as a joke about that cadence.

1. *Verily* I say unto you, this generation shall not pass, till all these things be fulfilled. *(King James Bible)*
2. *Verily, verily*, I say unto thee, except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. *(King James Bible)*
3. *Verily* the man was righteous, and his house prospered after him.
4. *Verily*, I have seen the like of it but once in fifty years.
5. Their cause is just, and *verily* it shall prevail. *(19th-century pulpit oratory)*

**See also:** [nota bene](#nota-bene), [to wit](#to-wit), [whenas](#whenas), [eftsoons](#eftsoons), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as)

### whenas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnˈæz/, /hwɛnˈæz/ &middot; *wen-AZ*
**Русский:** когда; тогда как — устар., поэт., елизаветинское
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, poetic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time; occasionally of cause or contrast (= *whereas*)
**Pattern:** *Whenas* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whenas* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *whenas* clause, which is where it nearly always sits in verse; no comma when it trails. Never a semicolon.
**Variants:** *when as* (the older spacing), *when that* (the same doubling with *that*), *whereas* (the surviving cousin), *when* (the modern equivalent)

*When*, with the old expletive *as* welded on — the same doubling that produced *whereas*, *whiles that*, and *when that*, all of them ways of marking a bare adverb unmistakably as a conjunction. It is chiefly Elizabethan and Jacobean, and its most quoted line is Herrick's *Whenas in silks my Julia goes*. A second and weaker sense, *seeing that* or *whereas*, turns up in seventeenth-century prose and in old statutes.

1. *Whenas* in silks my Julia goes, then, then, methinks, how sweetly flows that liquefaction of her clothes. *(Herrick)*
2. *Whenas* the sun went down, the watch was set upon the walls.
3. He was silent *whenas* the question was put to him directly.
4. *Whenas* the King had need of money, a Parliament was called. *(17th-century prose)*
5. *Whenas* they had eaten, the elder rose up and spoke.

**See also:** [whensoever](#whensoever), [so soon as](#so-soon-as), [ere](#ere), [sith](#sith), [whereas](#whereas)

### without

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/, /wɪˈθaʊt/ &middot; *with-OWT*
**Русский:** если не, пока не — устар., диал. союз (= unless); не путать с предлогом «без»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic, regional
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative condition (= *unless*)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *without* [clause]. &middot; *Without* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause; comma after a fronted *without* clause.
**Variants:** *unless* (the standard equivalent), *'thout* (eye-dialect spelling)

The conjunction *without*, meaning **unless**, is a different animal from the ordinary preposition. Standard until the eighteenth century, it is now nonstandard in edited American English but alive in Southern and rural speech and used constantly in dialogue by writers rendering it. Test which one you have: the preposition takes a noun phrase (*without a coat*), the conjunction takes a whole clause (*without you come with me*). A third class rounds it out — the adverb of place surviving in *a sound from without* and *the view from without* — so preposition, adverb, and conjunction all share the one spelling.

1. I won't go *without* you come with me.
2. He'll not lend a dime *without* he sees the deed first.
3. Don't you touch that *without* I tell you to.
4. Nothing gets planted *without* the ground thaws first.
5. She never came into town *without* it was market day.
6. *Without* you sign it yourself, they won't take it at the courthouse.

**See also:** [except](#except), [an](#an), [lest](#lest), [save](#save)

## Latin and appositional connectives

The abbreviations that introduce a restatement, an example, or a list. Their American punctuation is fixed and frequently botched: *i.e.* and *e.g.* take a period after each letter **and** a comma after the whole abbreviation. Several members connect nothing at all — *inter alia* is a parenthetical hedge on a list, *nota bene* is a directive addressed to the reader, *cf.* and *q.v.* send the reader somewhere else entirely, and *imprimis* and *item* merely number the entries of an old will — and all are here because they travel with the others and are punctuated by contrast with them.

### confer

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈfɜr/ &middot; /ˌsi ˈɛf/ &middot; *kuhn-FUR, see-EF*
**Русский:** ср., сравни — лат. cf., отсылка к источнику, с которым стоит сопоставить
**Type:** reader-directive cross-reference marker — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly, legal
**Clause type:** stands outside the clause structure; points the reader at a differing or comparable source
**Pattern:** [claim] (*cf.* [source]). &middot; [claim]; *cf.* [source]. &middot; *Cf.* [source], which reaches the opposite conclusion.
**Punctuation:** Written **cf.** with a single period and **no comma after it** — this is exactly where it differs from *i.e.* and *e.g.*, which both require one. It normally sits inside parentheses, a footnote, or a citation sentence of its own, and what follows it is a source, not a restatement.
**Variants:** *cf.*, *compare*, *contra* (the source flatly disagrees), *see also*, *q.v.*

Latin *confer*, the imperative "compare." It tells the reader to look at another source and notice how it differs — not to look up a definition, and not to read an illustration. Two errors are worth heading off: *cf.* is not an abbreviation of *see* (that is plain *see*, or **quod vide** for a cross-reference inside the same work), and it never takes the comma that *i.e.* and *e.g.* require. In American legal citation *cf.* signals support by analogy, while *contra* signals the opposite.

1. Most surveys put the figure near forty percent (*cf.* Alvarez 2019, who reports twenty-two).
2. The court applied the same test to municipal employees. *Cf.* Garcetti v. Ceballos, 547 U.S. 410 (2006).
3. The manuscript is usually dated to about 1340; *cf.* Whitfield, who argues for a generation later.
4. Nesting begins in early April (*cf.* the coastal population, which begins three weeks sooner).
5. She treats the 1919 strike as a failure — *cf.* Reyes, who calls it the beginning of the modern union.

**See also:** [quod vide](#quod-vide), [et alii](#et-alii), [nota bene](#nota-bene), [exempli gratia](#exempli-gratia), [id est](#id-est)

### ergo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɜrɡoʊ/, /ˈɛrɡoʊ/ &middot; *UR-goh, AIR-goh*
**Русский:** следовательно, итак — лат.; строгий вывод, часто с шутливо-напыщенным оттенком
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, or deliberately jokey
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial of logical consequence
**Pattern:** [clause]; *ergo*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]. *Ergo*, [clause]. &middot; [premise] — *ergo*, [conclusion].
**Punctuation:** Like *therefore*, it needs a **semicolon or a period before it** when it joins two independent clauses, and it takes a comma after it. A dash before it is common in compressed argument.
**Variants:** *therefore*, *hence*, *thus*

Latin for *therefore*, and unlike most Latin survivals it is spoken aloud as often as written. It marks a strict logical inference rather than a loose consequence, which gives it two registers at once: perfectly serious in philosophy and law, and mock-pompous everywhere else, where an American reaches for it to signal that the conclusion is meant to be obvious.

1. The lock was undamaged; *ergo*, whoever came in had a key.
2. All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; *ergo*, Socrates is mortal.
3. He was not at the meeting. *Ergo*, he cannot have heard the announcement.
4. The file was modified after the deadline; *ergo*, it was not the version submitted.
5. No fingerprints, no forced entry, nothing missing — *ergo*, no burglary.

**See also:** [wherefore](#wherefore), [now, therefore](#now-therefore), [thereby](#thereby), [id est](#id-est)

### et alii

**Pronunciation:** /ɛt ˈeɪliaɪ/ &middot; /ɛt ˈæl/ &middot; *et-AY-lee-eye, et-AL*
**Русский:** и др., и другие — лат. et al., заменяет остальные фамилии в списке
**Type:** quasi-coordinator — abbreviated coordination closing a list of names &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly, legal
**Clause type:** closes a noun phrase; stands in for the remaining coordinates of a list of people
**Pattern:** [Name] *et al.*, [year]. &middot; [Name] *et al.* [verb]. &middot; [Name] v. [Name] *et al.* (case caption).
**Punctuation:** Written **et al.** — no period after *et*, which is a whole Latin word, and one period after *al.*, which is an abbreviation. American style does **not** italicize it, and it takes **no comma before it**: *Weinberg et al. found*, never *Weinberg, et al. found*. At the end of a sentence its period serves as the full stop as well.
**Variants:** *et al.*, *and others*, *et alia* (neuter), *et alios* (accusative), *inter alia* (a different word — *among other things*)

Latin *and others*, and the only member of this section that actually coordinates: it replaces the second and later names in a list of authors, parties, or defendants. Its confusion with **inter alia** is worth heading off — *et al.* stands for **people left out of a list of names**, while *inter alia* warns that a **list of things** is incomplete. Note also that it abbreviates a coordination, not a plural: *Weinberg et al.* means Weinberg and at least two others, and the verb agrees with the whole group.

1. Weinberg *et al.* reported the same effect in a much larger cohort.
2. The suit names the city, the transit authority, *et al.* as defendants.
3. See Okafor *et al.*, "Coastal Subsidence in the Gulf," 2021.
4. The complaint in Alvarez *et al.* v. Northline Freight was filed in March.
5. The grant was awarded jointly to Dr. Pierce *et al.* at the state laboratory.

**See also:** [inter alia](#inter-alia), [quod vide](#quod-vide), [confer](#confer), [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit)

### exempli gratia

**Pronunciation:** /ɪɡˌzɛmpli ˈɡrɑtiə/ &middot; *eg-ZEM-plee GRAH-tee-uh*
**Русский:** например — лат. e.g., вводит выборочный, заведомо неполный перечень
**Type:** conjunctive adverb — appositional &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** appositional phrase introducing an illustrative sample
**Pattern:** [clause] — *e.g.*, [examples]. &middot; [clause] (*e.g.*, [examples]). &middot; [noun phrase], *e.g.*, [examples].
**Punctuation:** American style: **a period after each letter and a comma after the whole abbreviation** — *e.g.,* — with a comma, a dash, or an opening parenthesis before it. Never close the list with *etc.*, which repeats what *e.g.* already said.
**Variants:** *e.g.*, *for example*, *such as*, *for instance*

Latin *for the sake of example*, and it introduces a **partial** list — a sample, with more left unsaid. Its constant confusion with **id est** is the most common Latin error in American writing: *e.g.* gives you some of the class, *i.e.* restates the whole of it. The standard mnemonic is *e.g.* = **e**xample **g**iven. Almost nobody says the Latin aloud; readers say "for example" or spell out the letters.

1. Bring something to write with (*e.g.*, a pen or a sharpened pencil).
2. The policy covers large durable goods — *e.g.*, refrigerators, washers, and stoves.
3. Some English verbs take two objects, *e.g.*, *give* and *send*.
4. Please avoid strong solvents, *e.g.*, acetone or lacquer thinner.
5. Certain fees are nonrefundable (*e.g.*, the application fee).

**See also:** [id est](#id-est), [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit), [inter alia](#inter-alia), [nota bene](#nota-bene)

### id est

**Pronunciation:** /ɪd ˈɛst/ &middot; *id-EST*
**Русский:** то есть — лат. i.e., вводит уточняющий пересказ, а не пример
**Type:** conjunctive adverb — appositional &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** appositional phrase introducing a restatement of what precedes
**Pattern:** [clause] — *i.e.*, [restatement]. &middot; [clause] (*i.e.*, [restatement]). &middot; [noun phrase], *i.e.*, [restatement].
**Punctuation:** American style: **a period after each letter and a comma after the whole abbreviation** — *i.e.,* — preceded by a comma, a dash, or an opening parenthesis.
**Variants:** *i.e.*, *that is*, *that is to say*, *namely*

Latin *that is*. It introduces a **restatement**: the same thing said again more precisely, covering the whole of what came before. Against **exempli gratia**, which offers a sample, *i.e.* offers an equivalent — *the fee is due at signing, i.e., before you get the keys* redefines the moment rather than illustrating it. Read it aloud as "that is," not as Latin.

1. The fee is due at signing — *i.e.*, before you receive the keys.
2. She works the graveyard shift, *i.e.*, eleven at night to seven in the morning.
3. Only the principal is refundable (*i.e.*, not the accrued interest).
4. Please respond by close of business, *i.e.*, five o'clock Eastern time.
5. The device is rated IP68, *i.e.*, it can be fully submerged without damage.

**See also:** [exempli gratia](#exempli-gratia), [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit), [ergo](#ergo)

### imprimis

**Pronunciation:** /ɪmˈpraɪmɪs/, /ɪmˈprimɪs/ &middot; *im-PRY-mis, im-PREE-mis*
**Русский:** во-первых, прежде всего — лат., устар., зачин старой описи или завещания
**Type:** enumerative discourse marker — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** opens the first item of a formal enumeration; stands outside the clause it introduces
**Pattern:** *Imprimis*, [first item]. *Item*, [second item]. &middot; *Imprimis*: [first item].
**Punctuation:** Followed by a **comma**, or by a colon in an inventory, and it opens its own sentence or line. Nothing precedes it — it is by definition the first thing in the list — so the question of a semicolon before it never arises.
**Variants:** *in primis* (the two-word Latin), *item* (the marker for every entry after the first), *first*, *firstly*

Latin *in the first place* — the opening marker of the old itemized will, inventory, or account, always answered by a string of **item** entries beneath it. An American meets the pair in probate files, in colonial inventories of estates, and in Shakespeare's parody of the form in *Twelfth Night*. The modern replacements are a numbered list and the word *First*.

1. *Imprimis*, I give and bequeath unto my wife Elizabeth the dwelling house and the ground thereto belonging. *(will, 1698)*
2. *Imprimis*: one bay mare, with saddle and bridle, valued at eight pounds. *(inventory of an estate)*
3. *Imprimis*, the Committee finds that no notice of the meeting was ever given to the membership.
4. *Imprimis*, we are to consider what the statute meant by the word "resident."
5. *Imprimis*, two feather beds; *item*, one iron pot; *item*, six pewter dishes. *(colonial probate inventory)*

**See also:** [item](#item), [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit), [nota bene](#nota-bene), [inter alia](#inter-alia)

### inter alia

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪntər ˈeɪliə/, /ˌɪntər ˈɑliə/ &middot; *in-ter-AY-lee-uh*
**Русский:** среди прочего — лат., юр.; подчёркивает, что перечень неполон
**Type:** parenthetical adverbial phrase — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, legal, scholarly
**Clause type:** parenthetical adverbial meaning *among other things*; marks the list around it as incomplete
**Pattern:** [verb], *inter alia*, [object]. &middot; [subject], *inter alia*, [predicate]. &middot; [noun phrase] (*inter alia*).
**Punctuation:** **Commas on both sides**, or a pair of parentheses — it interrupts a clause rather than joining two, and dropping the closing comma is the usual error, the same one that spoils *to wit*. Never a semicolon, and never a comma on one side only.
**Variants:** *among other things* (the plain equivalent), *inter alios* (= among other **people**), *among others*, *et al.*

Latin *among other things*, and in force the exact opposite of **videlicet**: *viz.* promises that the enumeration is complete, *inter alia* warns that it is not. Its home is the brief and the judicial opinion — *the court held, inter alia, that the statute was not retroactive* — where it lets a writer report one item from a holding without seeming to report the whole of it, a difference that in litigation is substantive rather than stylistic. Two cautions. It is *inter alia* for things and *inter alios* for people, and the second is regularly botched. And outside law the plain *among other things* costs nothing and loses nothing, which is what most American editors will make you write.

1. The court held, *inter alia*, that the notice period had never been properly triggered.
2. The complaint alleges, *inter alia*, breach of contract and unjust enrichment.
3. The agency cited, *inter alia*, the cost of compliance for small carriers.
4. The report recommends unannounced inspections (*inter alia*) and a single reporting deadline.
5. She has advised, *inter alios*, two former governors and a sitting senator.
6. The statute applies, *inter alia*, to trusts created before its effective date.

**See also:** [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit), [exempli gratia](#exempli-gratia), [id est](#id-est), [nota bene](#nota-bene)

### item

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaɪtəm/ &middot; *EYE-tuhm*
**Русский:** также, пункт — лат., устар., зачин каждой следующей статьи описи; отсюда сущ. item
**Type:** enumerative discourse marker — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** archaic, legal
**Clause type:** opens each entry after the first in a formal enumeration
**Pattern:** *Imprimis*, [first entry]. *Item*, [next entry]. *Item*, [next entry]. &middot; *Item*: [entry] (inventory or account).
**Punctuation:** Followed by a **comma**, or by a colon in an account book, and it opens its own line or sentence. The entries are stacked with no connective between them: the repeated *Item* **is** the connective, which is why no *and* ever appears in these lists.
**Variants:** *imprimis* (the marker for the first entry), *ditto*, *also*, *likewise*

Latin *likewise* — the word that opened every entry after the first in a will, inventory, or bill of particulars, and the reason English has the noun *an item*: the marker for a listed thing became the name of the thing. An American meets the adverb in probate files, colonial account books, and Shakespeare's mockery of the form in *Twelfth Night*, and meets its descendant the noun everywhere.

1. *Item*, I give to my son John my silver watch and my best coat. *(will, 1712)*
2. *Item*, two feather beds; *item*, one iron pot; *item*, six pewter dishes. *(estate inventory)*
3. *Item*: to mending the parish gate, three shillings. *(churchwarden's accounts)*
4. *Item*, two lips, indifferent red; *item*, two grey eyes, with lids to them. *(Shakespeare, "Twelfth Night")*
5. *Item*, the tenant covenants to keep the fences and gates in repair.

**See also:** [imprimis](#imprimis), [videlicet](#videlicet), [to wit](#to-wit), [nota bene](#nota-bene), [inter alia](#inter-alia)

### nota bene

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊtə ˈbɛneɪ/, /ˌnoʊtə ˈbini/ &middot; *noh-tuh-BEN-ay, noh-tuh-BEE-nee*
**Русский:** нотабене, заметь хорошо — лат. N.B., помета «обратите внимание»
**Type:** reader-directive marker — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** stands outside the clause structure; flags the note that follows
**Pattern:** *N.B.*: [clause]. &middot; *N.B.* — [clause]. &middot; *Nota bene*: [clause].
**Punctuation:** Written **N.B.** with a period after each letter, and followed by a colon, a dash, or nothing at all — but **not** by a comma, which is where it differs from *i.e.* and *e.g.* It normally opens its own sentence or parenthetical note.
**Variants:** *N.B.*, *NB* (British, often without periods), *note that*

Latin *note well*, an imperative addressed to the reader. It is included here because it travels with *i.e.*, *e.g.*, and *viz.* and is punctuated by contrast with them, but it is worth being clear that it connects nothing: it is a flag on a note, not a link between clauses. Modern American writing increasingly just writes *Note:* instead.

1. *N.B.*: The gallery is closed on Mondays and all federal holidays.
2. *N.B.* — all measurements in this table are given in millimeters.
3. Payment is due on the first of the month. *N.B.*: late fees apply after the fifth.
4. *Nota bene*: the second edition renumbers every chapter after the fourth.
5. *N.B.* The deposit becomes nonrefundable once the work begins.

**See also:** [videlicet](#videlicet), [id est](#id-est), [exempli gratia](#exempli-gratia), [to wit](#to-wit)

### quod vide

**Pronunciation:** /kwɑd ˈvaɪdi/ &middot; /ˌkju ˈvi/ &middot; *kwod-VY-dee, kyoo-VEE*
**Русский:** смотри там же — лат. q.v., отсылка к другой статье того же издания
**Type:** reader-directive cross-reference marker — not a conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly
**Clause type:** stands outside the clause; sends the reader to an entry elsewhere in the same work
**Pattern:** [term] (*q.v.*). &middot; [term], *q.v.* &middot; [two terms] (*qq.v.*).
**Punctuation:** Written **q.v.** with a period after each letter, normally inside parentheses immediately after the term it points at, and **not** followed by a comma. The plural, for two or more cross-references at once, is *qq.v.*
**Variants:** *q.v.*, *qq.v.* (plural), *see*, *see also*, *cf.* (a different instruction — compare, not consult)

Latin *quod vide*, "which see" — an instruction to go and look up the term just named somewhere else in the same book. Its home is the reference work: encyclopedias, dictionaries, and legal treatises use it to mark a term that has an entry of its own. Do not confuse it with **confer**, which tells you to compare a source that differs; *q.v.* says the work itself covers the term elsewhere. Modern American editing largely replaces it with *see* and a page number, or with a link.

1. The doctrine descends from the old writ of assumpsit (*q.v.*).
2. Compare the treatment of consideration (*q.v.*) in the chapter on contract formation.
3. The style was borrowed from the Aesthetic Movement (*q.v.*) a generation earlier.
4. On this point the reader should consult the entries for estoppel and laches (*qq.v.*).
5. The bird is closely related to the marsh harrier, *q.v.*

**See also:** [confer](#confer), [nota bene](#nota-bene), [videlicet](#videlicet), [et alii](#et-alii), [id est](#id-est)

### to wit

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈwɪt/, /tu ˈwɪt/ &middot; *tuh-WIT*
**Русский:** а именно — юр., английская калька videlicet
**Type:** conjunctive adverb — appositional &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** appositional phrase naming exactly what the preceding noun refers to
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], *to wit*, [specification]. &middot; [clause], *to wit*: [list].
**Punctuation:** **Commas on both sides** — *one item, to wit, the deed* — or a comma before and a colon after when a list follows. Leaving off the second comma is a common error.
**Variants:** *namely*, *videlicet / viz.*, *that is to say*

The English calque of **videlicet**, from the old verb *wit*, meaning *to know*. Its home is the criminal charging document, where the offense is named in general terms and then pinned down precisely: *one count of forgery, to wit, signing the endorsement on a check payable to another*. Outside law it sounds theatrically stiff, which is exactly why writers reach for it to get a laugh.

1. The defendant is charged with one count of forgery, *to wit*, signing the endorsement on a check made payable to another.
2. Two documents were produced, *to wit*: the lease and the addendum.
3. He was found in possession of the stolen property, *to wit*, a gold pocket watch.
4. Only one person had the combination, *to wit*, the branch manager.
5. The statute names three exceptions, *to wit*, fraud, duress, and mutual mistake.

### videlicet

**Pronunciation:** /vɪˈdɛləsɛt/, /vaɪˈdɛlɪsɛt/ &middot; *vih-DEL-ih-set*
**Русский:** а именно — лат. viz., вводит полный перечень (ср. e.g. — выборочный)
**Type:** conjunctive adverb — appositional &middot; **Register:** formal, scholarly, legal
**Clause type:** appositional phrase introducing a complete specification or list
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], *viz.*, [full list]. &middot; [clause], *viz.* [specification].
**Punctuation:** Written **viz.** with a single period, and normally followed by a comma in American style. Read aloud as "namely," not as the Latin.
**Variants:** *viz.*, *scilicet / sc.*, *namely*

Latin *namely*, contracted in medieval manuscripts to *viz.* — the *z* is really a scribal shorthand mark rather than a letter, which is why the pronunciation has nothing to do with it. It introduces the **complete** enumeration of what was just named in general terms, and that is what separates it from **exempli gratia**, which gives only a sample, and from **inter alia**, which says outright that the list is partial. Its rarer cousin **scilicet** (*sc.*) does a slightly different job: it supplies a word the text has left out, as in *the poem's "she" (sc. the moon)*.

1. Three species were recorded that night, *viz.*, the barn owl, the screech owl, and the great horned owl.
2. Only one remedy is available to the plaintiff, *viz.*, specific performance.
3. The manuscript names its sources, *viz.* Bede, Gildas, and Nennius.
4. He held two offices simultaneously, *viz.*, treasurer and clerk.
5. The tax applies to a single class of property, *viz.*, unimproved land.
