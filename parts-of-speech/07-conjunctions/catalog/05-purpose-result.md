# Subordinators of purpose and result

> Words that attach a clause naming the goal an action aims at, or the outcome that action produced.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Purpose answers *what for*; result answers *what happened*. The same forms often serve both — *so that* is the clearest case — and English separates the two senses not with different words but with a comma and a modal verb: a purpose clause looks forward and almost always carries *can, could, will, would,* or *may* (*I left early so that I could beat traffic*), while a result clause reports something that actually occurred (*I left early, so that I beat traffic*). Purpose also comes in a non-finite form built on *to*, *in order to*, and *so as to*, and those forms require the main clause and the purpose phrase to share a subject; when the subjects differ you have to switch to a finite *so that* clause or to *in order for X to Y*.

One punctuation rule runs through the whole category and splits it down the middle. The subordinators — *so that, in order that, lest, such that, with the result that* — attach with a comma or with nothing at all, and never with a semicolon, because the clause they introduce is not independent. The result connectives at the end of this file — *therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly, as a result* — are conjunctive adverbs rather than conjunctions, so joining two independent clauses with one of them takes a semicolon or a period: *It rained; therefore, we canceled* is correct, and *It rained, therefore we canceled* is a comma splice. One member of that group behaves differently: *thereby* normally heads an *-ing* phrase rather than a second independent clause, so it takes a plain comma and a semicolon in front of it would be wrong.


## Purpose: finite clauses

Subordinators that take a full clause with its own subject and verb. The verb in the purpose clause is normally a modal — *can, could, will, would, may, might* — because the goal has not happened yet. Fronting the clause always puts a comma after it. In the ordinary order the comma is normally absent, and that absence carries meaning: with *so* and *so that* a comma in front of the clause flips the reading from purpose to result, and *to the end that* picks one up in legal drafting when the aim is an add-on. None of them ever takes a semicolon, because what follows is not an independent clause.

### for fear that

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ˈfɪr ðæt/, reduced /fɚ ˈfɪr ðət/ &middot; *fer-FEER-thut*  
**Русский:** из страха, что; боясь, что — отрицательная цель: то, чего действием стараются избежать
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for fear that* [clause]. &middot; *For fear that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for fear of* [gerund/noun].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted, which is rare.  
**Variants:** *for fear* (that-less), *for fear of* + gerund

Negative purpose: the clause names the thing the subject is acting to avoid. It covers the same ground as *lest* but stays inside ordinary modern American prose, and unlike *lest* it takes a normal indicative or modal verb rather than the subjunctive — *for fear that he would find out*, not *for fear that he find out*.

1. She kept the receipt *for fear that* the store would refuse the return.
2. He never mentioned the offer *for fear* his boss would hear about it.
3. They moved the ceremony indoors *for fear that* the storm would arrive early.
4. Nobody said a word *for fear that* the wrong person was listening.
5. I checked the lock twice *for fear of* another break-in.
6. Parents pulled their kids out of the pool *for fear that* lightning was coming.
7. *For fear that* the news would leak overnight, the company briefed its staff and the press the same morning.

**See also:** [lest](#lest), [in case](#in-case), [in the hope that](#in-the-hope-that), [so that](#so-that), [in order that](#in-order-that)

### in case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkeɪs/ &middot; *in-KAYSS*  
**Русский:** на случай, если — предупредительная цель; не «если» (if): действие делается заранее
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of precautionary purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in case* [clause]. &middot; *In case* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], just *in case*. &middot; *In case of* [noun], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** No comma before *in case* in the normal order. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. The tag *just in case* at the end of a sentence does take a comma before it.  
**Variants:** *just in case*, *in case of* + noun (prepositional)

Precautionary purpose — you do the main action now so that you are ready for a possibility. That is exactly what separates it from *if*: *Take an umbrella in case it rains* means take it regardless, while *Take an umbrella if it rains* means take it only once the rain has started. American English uses the present tense for the future contingency (*in case it rains*, never *in case it will rain*).

1. Bring a jacket *in case* the theater is cold.
2. I wrote the address on my hand *in case* my phone died.
3. *In case* the power goes out, there are candles in the drawer.
4. We booked the second room *in case* her parents decided to come after all.
5. Save the confirmation email, just *in case*.
6. *In case of* fire, use the stairs.

**See also:** [for fear that](#for-fear-that), [in the hope that](#in-the-hope-that), [lest](#lest), [so that](#so-that)

### in order that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ ðæt/, reduced /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ ðət/ &middot; *in-OR-der-thut*  
**Русский:** чтобы, с тем чтобы — книжн. союз цели, обычно с модальным глаголом
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in order that* [clause with modal]. &middot; *In order that* [clause with modal], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted.

The formal written twin of *so that*, and almost always followed by a modal — *may, might, can, could, will, would, shall*. Americans say *so that* or bare *so* in speech; *in order that* survives mainly in legal, academic, and ceremonial prose. Use *in order to* instead when both clauses share a subject and an infinitive will do.

1. The records are sealed *in order that* the witnesses may be protected.
2. *In order that* everyone can attend, the meeting was moved to Friday afternoon.
3. Funds were set aside *in order that* the district might hire a second counselor.
4. The statute was amended *in order that* small businesses would not be penalized.
5. He repeated the instructions slowly *in order that* no one would misunderstand them.
6. *In order that* the record may be complete, the clerk will now read the motion.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [in order to](#in-order-to), [to the end that](#to-the-end-that), [that](#that), [lest](#lest)

### in the hope that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp ðæt/, reduced /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp ðət/ &middot; *in-thuh-HOHP-thut*  
**Русский:** в надежде, что — цель, в достижимости которой говорящий не уверен
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of tentative purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in the hope that* [clause]. &middot; *In the hope that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in hopes that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the hope of* [gerund/noun].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate, not independent.  
**Variants:** *in hopes that*, *in the hopes that*, *in the hope of* + gerund, *in hopes of* + gerund

Purpose with the confidence taken out of it. *So that* presents the goal as reachable; *in the hope that* concedes it may not work, which is why it takes an ordinary indicative verb (*in the hope that someone would answer*) instead of the modal-heavy clause *in order that* demands. American English uses the plural *in hopes that* as freely as the singular.

1. She left a note on the windshield *in the hope that* the driver would call.
2. They cut the price twice *in hopes that* somebody would bid.
3. *In the hope that* a deal could still be reached, the union delayed the strike vote.
4. He mailed the manuscript to twelve agents *in the hope of* a single reply.
5. The city planted two hundred maples *in the hope that* the block would be shaded by 2035.
6. We left the porch light on all night *in hopes that* the cat would find her way back.
7. He drove past the house twice *in hopes of* catching her at home.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [in order that](#in-order-that), [for fear that](#for-fear-that), [in case](#in-case), [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of)

### lest

**Pronunciation:** /lɛst/ &middot; *LEST*  
**Русский:** чтобы не, дабы не — книжн., устар.; отрицание уже внутри слова, второе «не» лишнее
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, literary  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. &middot; [main clause] *lest* [subject] *should* [verb]. &middot; *Lest* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted, as in the fixed phrase *Lest we forget, ...*

Negative purpose, equal to *so that ... not* or *for fear that*. It takes the present subjunctive — a bare verb with no *-s* (*lest he be late*, not *lest he is late*) — or *should*. The negation already sits inside the word, which is the classic trap: *lest anyone hear* means "so that nobody hears," so adding *not* reverses the sense. Everyday American speech uses *so that ... doesn't* or *in case*.

1. He whispered the address *lest* anyone overhear.
2. She hid the letter *lest* her sister find it.
3. *Lest* we forget, the same promise was made five years ago.
4. The committee moved carefully *lest* it be accused of favoritism.
5. Keep the receipt *lest* there should be a dispute later.
6. I said nothing *lest* I make the argument worse.

**See also:** [for fear that](#for-fear-that), [in case](#in-case), [so that](#so-that), [in order that](#in-order-that)

### so

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/, reduced /sə/ in rapid connected speech &middot; *SOH*  
**Русский:** чтобы — разг. замена so that; с запятой перед ним — «так что, поэтому» (результат)
**Type:** subordinating (reduced *so that*); also coordinating in the result sense &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose; coordinate clause in the result sense  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *so* [clause with modal]. — purpose &middot; [independent clause], *so* [independent clause]. — result &middot; [subject] [verb] *so* [adjective] *that* [clause]. — degree  
**Punctuation:** One comma decides the meaning. **No comma = purpose** (*I left early so I could beat traffic*). **Comma = result** (*I left early, so I beat traffic*), which is the ordinary rule for a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses. A semicolon before result *so* is defensible only when both clauses are long and already full of commas; in an ordinary sentence it is wrong, and it is always wrong before purpose *so*.  
**Variants:** *so that* (full form), *so as to* (infinitival), *so ... that* (degree)

In casual American English bare *so* stands in for *so that* in the purpose sense, and the dropped *that* is completely standard in speech. The same two letters do at least four other jobs, which is why that comma has to work so hard: the coordinating conjunction of result, one of the FANBOYS (*The bus never came, so we walked*); the degree adverb of *so ... that* and of bare intensifying *so* (*I'm so tired*); the pro-form standing in for a whole clause (*I think so*, *If so, call me*); and the discourse opener that starts a turn in conversation (*So, where were we?*).

1. Text me when you land *so* I know you got there.
2. She sat in the front row *so* she could hear the questions.
3. We left the porch light on *so* the sitter wouldn't have to hunt for the keyhole.
4. Write it on the calendar *so* you don't forget again.
5. The bus never came, *so* we walked the last mile.
6. Move the couch over *so* there's room for the tree.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [so ... that](#so--that), [so as to](#so-as-to), [so (coordinating)](01-coordinating.md#so)

### so that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ ðæt/, reduced /ˈsoʊ ðət/ &middot; *SOH-thut*  
**Русский:** чтобы — основной союз цели; запятая перед ним меняет смысл на «так что» (результат)
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose, or of result when preceded by a comma  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *so that* [clause with modal]. — purpose &middot; [main clause], *so that* [clause]. — result &middot; *So that* [clause], [main clause]. — fronted, formal  
**Punctuation:** The central rule of this category. ✗ *No comma before *so that* = purpose**: *He spoke slowly so that everyone would understand* (that was the goal). ✗ *Comma before *so that* = result**: *He spoke slowly, so that everyone understood* (that is what happened). A semicolon is always wrong here.  
**Variants:** *so* (that-dropped, informal)

The default purpose subordinator of American English, and the one to reach for when the two clauses have different subjects. The purpose clause normally carries *can, could, will, would,* or *may*; drop the modal and the sentence tips toward the result reading. Compare *in order that* (formal), bare *so* (casual), and *in order to* (same subject, infinitive).

1. Label the boxes *so that* the movers know which room each one goes in.
2. I set two alarms *so that* I wouldn't oversleep on the first day.
3. The city widened the sidewalk *so that* strollers and wheelchairs could pass each other.
4. She froze half the soup *so that* there would be something ready on Thursday.
5. The window was left open all night, *so that* the whole apartment smelled like rain.
6. *So that* nobody would be left out, they made the party a potluck.

**See also:** [so](#so), [in order that](#in-order-that), [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [so ... that](#so--that), [lest](#lest)

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt/, reduced /ðət/ &middot; *THAT*  
**Русский:** дабы, чтобы — устар., книжн. союз цели, только в паре с may/might
**Type:** subordinating (purpose use of the complementizer) &middot; **Register:** literary, archaic  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *that* [subject] *may/might* [verb]. &middot; [imperative], *that* [subject] *may/might* [verb]. &middot; [main clause] *that* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb].  
**Punctuation:** No comma is required, and none appears when the purpose clause is the point of the sentence (*He gave up his seat that she might sit down*). A comma is usual after an imperative or a short main clause, where the purpose clause reads as an added statement of aim (*Speak plainly, that all may understand*). Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate. Modern edited prose simply rewrites the whole thing as *so that*.  
**Variants:** *so that* and *in order that* are the modern equivalents

Bare *that* as a purpose subordinator survives in scripture, hymns, oratory, poetry, and deliberate archaism: *Come, that we may see*. Only the purpose *that* pairs with *may* or *might*, which is the test for spotting it, because the same word does four commoner jobs: the complementizer that opens a noun clause (*I know that he left*), the relative pronoun (*the book that I read*), the demonstrative determiner and pronoun (*that book*, *give me that*), and the degree adverb of informal speech (*It wasn't that cold*).

1. Speak plainly, *that* all may understand.
2. He gave up his seat *that* the older woman might sit down.
3. Come closer, *that* we may see your face.
4. They fought *that* their children might live free.
5. Write these things down, *that* the record be preserved.
6. She worked all her life *that* her name might outlast her.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [in order that](#in-order-that), [to the end that](#to-the-end-that), [lest](#lest)

### to the end that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ði ˈɛnd ðæt/, reduced /tə ði ˈɛnd ðət/ &middot; *tuh-thee-END-thut*  
**Русский:** с тем чтобы, дабы — юр., книжн.; end здесь «цель», а не «конец»
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to the end that* [clause with modal]. &middot; [main clause], *to the end that* [clause with modal]. &middot; *To the end that* [clause with modal], [main clause]. — rare  
**Punctuation:** A comma before it is common in legal drafting, where the clause states an added aim; leave the comma out when the purpose clause is essential to the sentence. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate.  
**Variants:** *to the intent that* (legal, rarer)

A ceremonial *in order that*, at home in statutes, contracts, charters, and older sermons and nowhere else. *End* here means "purpose, goal," not "finish," which is what makes the phrase opaque to learners. In any ordinary sentence, write *so that*.

1. The board publishes its minutes, *to the end that* the public may follow its work.
2. Records shall be retained for seven years *to the end that* an audit may be conducted.
3. The treaty was drafted *to the end that* neither party could later claim surprise.
4. He endowed the chair *to the end that* the subject might never be dropped from the curriculum.
5. All parties shall cooperate, *to the end that* the transfer may close on schedule.
6. The rules are circulated in advance *to the end that* no member is taken unawares.
7. *To the end that* the fund may continue in perpetuity, the trustees shall reinvest all income.

**See also:** [in order that](#in-order-that), [to the intent that](#to-the-intent-that), [so that](#so-that), [that](#that), [to which end](#to-which-end), [whereby](#whereby)

### to the intent that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðæt/, reduced /tə ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðət/ &middot; *tuh-thee-in-TENT-thut*  
**Русский:** с тем намерением, чтобы — устар. юр., язык старых завещаний и актов
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** archaic legal — conveyancing and drafting boilerplate  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *to the intent that* [clause with *may/might/shall*]. &middot; [main clause] *to the intent that* [clause with *may/might/shall*]. &middot; [main clause] *to the intent and purpose that* [clause]. — the doubled drafting formula &middot; *To the intent that* [clause], [main clause]. — rare  
**Punctuation:** A comma before it is the drafting norm, because the purpose clause is hung on a provision that is already complete; leave the comma out when the purpose restricts the main clause rather than commenting on it. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate, not independent.  
**Variants:** *to the intent and purpose that*, *to the end that* (the commoner twin), *with the intent that* (the modern American drafting equivalent)

An American meets this one in old deeds, wills, and trust instruments, in English conveyancing precedents, and in nineteenth-century statutes and charters — never in modern American drafting, which writes *with the intent that* or plain *so that*. It differs from its twin *to the end that* only in what the noun names: *end* names the goal aimed at, while *intent* names what was in the drafter's mind, which is why this version outlived the other in instruments where somebody's intention is itself the operative fact. The clause takes *may*, *might*, or *shall*, or occasionally the bare present subjunctive.

1. The lands are conveyed to the trustees, *to the intent that* the same may be sold and the proceeds divided among my children.
2. This release is given *to the intent that* no further claim shall be made upon the estate.
3. I give my library to the parish, *to the intent that* it may be kept together and lent to any inhabitant.
4. The Act was passed *to the intent that* the ancient rights of common might be preserved to the freeholders.
5. The shares were transferred into her name alone, *to the intent that* she should take the whole beneficially.
6. Notice shall be published for three weeks, *to the intent that* no creditor may afterwards plead ignorance of the proceeding.
7. The parties have set their hands hereunto, *to the intent and purpose that* the covenants herein may bind their heirs and assigns.

**See also:** [to the end that](#to-the-end-that), [with the intent that](#with-the-intent-that), [in order that](#in-order-that), [so that](#so-that), [to which end](#to-which-end)

### to which end

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˌwɪtʃ ˈɛnd/, reduced /tə wɪtʃ ˈɛnd/ &middot; *tuh-wich-END*  
**Русский:** с каковой целью, ради чего — книжн., юр.; отсылает к цели, названной в предыдущей части
**Type:** relative connective of purpose (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal  
**Clause type:** sentential relative clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *to which end* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *To which end,* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *to which end* [subject] *shall/may* [verb].  
**Punctuation:** Comma before it, never a semicolon and never nothing — it is a supplementary relative, so it takes the punctuation of *which is why* rather than the punctuation of *therefore*. A comma after it is optional and turns up mainly when the phrase opens a new sentence.  
**Variants:** *to that end*, *to this end* (the demonstrative versions, which start a fresh sentence)

The relative twin of *to that end*: it reaches back, treats the whole preceding clause as the goal, and then names the step taken to reach it. *End* here means "purpose," exactly as in *to the end that*, and not "finish." It belongs to charters, trusts, and contracts; in ordinary prose start a new sentence with *to that end*.

1. The trust must be preserved in perpetuity, *to which end* the trustees shall reinvest all income.
2. The parties wish to close by March 1, *to which end* each shall deliver its schedules within ten days.
3. The society was founded to keep the language alive, *to which end* it runs a summer school every July.
4. He meant the farm to pass to his grandchildren intact, *to which end* he broke none of it up in the will.
5. The council resolved to clear the backlog by fall, *to which end* two additional clerks were hired in April.
6. The charter directs the board to publish every vote, *to which end* the minutes go up within a week of each meeting.

**See also:** [to the end that](#to-the-end-that), [in order that](#in-order-that), [whereby](#whereby), [which is why](#which-is-why), [to that end](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#to-that-end)

### with the intent that

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðæt/, reduced /wɪð ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðət/ &middot; *with-thee-in-TENT-thut*  
**Русский:** с намерением, чтобы — юр.: фиксирует умысел действующего лица, а не итог
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *with the intent that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with the intention that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with intent to* [base verb]. — the criminal-law formula &middot; *With the intent that* [clause], [main clause]. — rare  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause, which is where it almost always sits. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate.  
**Variants:** *with the intention that*, *with intent to* + base verb, *with the object that* (legalistic)

States the actor's purpose as a matter of record, which is why it lives in statutes, wills, and pleadings: what is being asserted is what was in somebody's mind, not what actually came about. The clause takes a modal — *would, should, might* — or, in drafting, the present subjunctive: *with the intent that the fund be divided equally*. Its everyday relatives *with the aim of* and *with the intention of* take an *-ing* form and cannot govern a clause of their own.

1. She signed the deed over in March *with the intent that* her sister would keep the house.
2. The clause was inserted *with the intent that* neither side could walk away without notice.
3. He transferred the shares *with the intention that* the dividends should fund the scholarship.
4. The photographs were released *with the intent that* they be read as evidence, not as art.
5. Congress wrote the exemption narrowly *with the intent that* only family farms would qualify.
6. He was charged with entering the building *with intent to* commit theft.
7. The memo went to every department *with the intention that* nobody could later plead ignorance.

## Purpose: infinitives and phrases

Non-finite purpose markers. These take a bare infinitive or an *-ing* form instead of a clause, and because the purpose phrase has no subject of its own it borrows the subject of the main clause — *He left early to catch the train* can only mean that he catches it. When the subjects differ, switch to *so that* or to *in order for X to Y*. Most of them take a comma when fronted and none when they follow. Two members sit outside that description: *for to*, the archaic doubled marker, does not front at all, and *with this in mind* takes no complement of any kind, because it points back at a goal already stated instead of naming a new one.

### for the purpose of

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ðə ˈpɝpəs əv/ &middot; *fer-thuh-PUR-puss-uv*  
**Русский:** с целью, для — книжн., юр.; дальше только -ing или существительное, не инфинитив
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a gerund) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (gerund phrase)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for the purpose of* [gerund phrase]. &middot; *For the purpose of* [gerund phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *For purposes of* [noun], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted.  
**Variants:** *for purposes of*, *for the purposes of*

The purpose marker of statutes, contracts, and policy documents. It takes an *-ing* form and never an infinitive — *for the purpose of collecting data*, not *for the purpose of to collect data*. The *for purposes of X* variant does something slightly different: it limits a definition to one document or context.

1. The data is retained *for the purpose of* verifying eligibility.
2. *For the purpose of* this agreement, "employee" includes independent contractors.
3. She entered the building *for the purpose of* serving the summons.
4. The fund was created *for the purpose of* rebuilding the pier.
5. Cameras were installed *for the purpose of* deterring theft, not monitoring staff.
6. *For purposes of* the tax code, the two entities are treated as one.

**See also:** [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [to](#to), [with a view to](#with-a-view-to), [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of)

### for the sake of

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk əv/, reduced /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/ &middot; *fer-thuh-SAYK-uv*  
**Русский:** ради, для блага — цель через того или то, что от неё выигрывает
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a noun or a gerund) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (noun or gerund phrase)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for the sake of* [noun/gerund]. &middot; *For the sake of* [noun/gerund], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for* [possessive] *sake*. &middot; *For the sake of argument,* [main clause]. — frozen hedge  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted, which is common. Never a semicolon — a phrase follows, not an independent clause.  
**Variants:** *for* [possessive] *sake* (*for the children's sake*), *for the sake of argument*, *for goodness' sake* (interjection, not purpose)

Purpose named through the person or thing that benefits — *for the sake of the kids* — or through the quality being served, as in *for the sake of clarity*. It takes a noun or an *-ing* form and never an infinitive. Two things sit next to it and are not it: the possessive shortcut *for her own sake*, which is the same construction turned inside out, and the exasperated interjection *for heaven's sake*, which names no goal at all.

1. They stayed in the same city *for the sake of* the kids.
2. *For the sake of* clarity, the report uses the 2019 boundaries throughout.
3. She learned the software *for the sake of* keeping the job, not because she liked it.
4. Both sides agreed to a short recess *for the sake of* letting tempers cool.
5. Don't rewrite the whole chapter *for the sake of* one sentence.
6. *For the sake of* argument, suppose the early numbers are right.
7. He gave up the corner office *for* his partner's *sake*.

**See also:** [in the interest of](#in-the-interest-of), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of), [in order to](#in-order-to), [so that](#so-that)

### for to

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ˈtu/, usually reduced /fɚ tə/ &middot; *fer-TOO*  
**Русский:** чтобы — устар., диал.; язык баллад и южных говоров, в норме просто to
**Type:** subordinating (infinitival), nonstandard &middot; **Register:** archaic, dialectal, balladry  
**Clause type:** non-finite adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for to* [base verb]. &middot; [main clause] *for to* [base verb] [object]. &middot; [verb of motion] *for to* [base verb]. — the commonest frame  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it. It does not front, and it never takes a semicolon.  
**Variants:** plain *to* and *in order to* are the standard equivalents; *for* [subject] *to* [verb] is a different and fully standard pattern

A doubled purpose marker — the preposition *for* stacked on the infinitive *to* — that was ordinary in Middle and Early Modern English and survives now only in ballads, the King James Bible, Appalachian and Southern regional speech, and Irish and Scots English. The trap is the standard pattern that looks like it: in *He waited for the rain to stop* a subject sits between the two words and the sentence is unremarkable, while *He waited for to see her* is dialect. In edited American prose, write plain *to*.

1. We come *for to* sing you a song about the river.
2. He went down to the courthouse *for to* pay the fine himself.
3. A sower went out *for to* sow his seed.
4. She saved all winter *for to* buy the ticket home.
5. They gathered on the bank *for to* watch the boats come in.
6. I only stopped by *for to* say goodbye before the train.
7. The old ballad has him riding out *for to* seek his fortune.

**See also:** [to](#to), [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [that](#that), [to the end that](#to-the-end-that)

### in an effort to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ən ˈɛfɚt tu/, reduced /ɪn ən ˈɛfɚt tə/ &middot; *in-un-EFF-ert-too*  
**Русский:** пытаясь, в стремлении — газетный оборот: попытка, об успехе которой ничего не сказано
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional phrase plus infinitive) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the house style of American news writing  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (to-infinitive)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in an effort to* [base verb]. &middot; *In an effort to* [base verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in an attempt to* [base verb]. &middot; [main clause] *in a bid to* [base verb]. — headline register  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted, which is common in reporting. Never a semicolon — an infinitive phrase follows, not an independent clause.  
**Variants:** *in an attempt to*, *in a bid to* (headlinese), *in hopes of* + gerund

Purpose with a hedge built in. *In order to* and *so as to* simply name the aim; *in an effort to* reports that somebody was trying, and quietly declines to say it worked, which is why reporters reach for it when the outcome is still open or has already failed. Like every infinitival purpose marker it borrows the subject of the main clause, so the person making the effort has to be the person in the main clause. *In a bid to* is the compressed newspaper version and sounds odd outside a headline or a lede.

1. The city cut fares in half *in an effort to* get riders back on the buses.
2. *In an effort to* clear the backlog, the clinic added Saturday hours.
3. Growers flooded the fields *in an attempt to* keep the frost off the buds.
4. He rewrote his resume three times *in an effort to* get past the screening software.
5. The league changed the overtime rule *in an effort to* cut down on head injuries.
6. *In an attempt to* settle before trial, both sides agreed to one more day of mediation.

**See also:** [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of), [to](#to), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of)

### in order to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ tu/, reduced /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ tə/ &middot; *in-OR-der-too*  
**Русский:** чтобы, для того чтобы — явный маркер цели перед инфинитивом
**Type:** subordinating (infinitival) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in order to* [base verb]. &middot; *In order to* [base verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in order not to* [base verb]. &middot; [main clause] *in order for* [subject] *to* [base verb].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *in order to* phrase. No comma when it follows the main clause.  
**Variants:** *in order not to*, *in order for ... to*

The explicit, slightly formal version of the plain purpose infinitive *to*. Its real value is disambiguation: *He wrote to the senator* is unclear, *He wrote in order to reach the senator* is not. The negative is *in order not to* — careful writing puts *not* before *to*, though *in order to not* is common in speech. Use *in order for X to Y* when the two clauses have different subjects.

1. He took a second job *in order to* pay off the loan faster.
2. *In order to* qualify, applicants must have lived in the county for a year.
3. She left the office at four *in order not to* hit rush hour.
4. The store closed early *in order for* the staff to attend the funeral.
5. We rewrote the opening *in order to* make the point in the first paragraph.
6. *In order to* be safe, back up the drive before you run the update.

**See also:** [to](#to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [in order that](#in-order-that), [so that](#so-that), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [in an effort to](#in-an-effort-to)

### in the interest of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ˈɪntrəst əv/, also /ˈɪntərəst/ &middot; *in-thee-IN-trust-uv*  
**Русский:** в интересах, ради — называет ценность или сторону, которой служит решение
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a noun or a gerund) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (noun or gerund phrase)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in the interest of* [noun/gerund]. &middot; *In the interest of* [noun/gerund], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the interests of* [noun]. &middot; *In the public interest,* [main clause]. — fixed phrase  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted, which is the commoner order. Never a semicolon.  
**Variants:** *in the interests of*, *in the public interest*, *in the interest of* [somebody's] *safety/privacy/time*

Names the value or the party a decision is meant to serve: *in the interest of safety*, *in the interest of the shareholders*. It takes a noun or an *-ing* form and never an infinitive. It also carries a faint note of justification, which is why it turns up in announcements, rulings, and apologies — places where somebody is explaining a decision that not everyone is going to like. The plural *in the interests of* is equally standard in American English.

1. *In the interest of* time, we'll take two more questions and then break.
2. The hearing was closed *in the interest of* protecting the witnesses.
3. The airline canceled the flight *in the interest of* safety after the second warning light.
4. *In the interests of* full disclosure, I should say the author is a friend of mine.
5. The board sold the building *in the interest of* the membership, over the staff's objections.
6. He dropped the appeal *in the interest of* getting back to work.

**See also:** [for the sake of](#for-the-sake-of), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [with a view to](#with-a-view-to), [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of), [in an effort to](#in-an-effort-to)

### so as to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ əz tə/, full /ˈsoʊ æz ˌtu/ &middot; *SOH-az-tuh*  
**Русский:** чтобы, так чтобы — книжн.; особенно частотна форма so as not to «чтобы не»
**Type:** subordinating (infinitival) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — the negative *so as not to* is everyday American  
**Clause type:** non-finite adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *so as to* [base verb]. &middot; [main clause] *so as not to* [base verb]. &middot; *So as to* [base verb], [main clause]. — rare  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase in the rare fronted order.  
**Variants:** *so as not to*

A formal equivalent of *in order to* that is most alive in its negative, *so as not to*, which is the natural American way to say "in order to avoid." Like every infinitival purpose marker it shares the subject of the main clause; if the person acting and the person affected are different, you need *so that*.

1. He rewrote the email *so as to* sound less annoyed.
2. She closed the door quietly *so as not to* wake the baby.
3. The trail loops north *so as to* avoid the private property.
4. Fold the paper in thirds *so as to* fit it in the envelope.
5. They scheduled the vote for Monday *so as not to* conflict with the holiday.
6. *So as to* keep the discussion short, the chair set a three-minute limit.

**See also:** [in order to](#in-order-to), [to](#to), [so that](#so-that), [so](#so), [in order that](#in-order-that)

### to

**Pronunciation:** /tu/, reduced /tə/ before a consonant &middot; *TOO*  
**Русский:** чтобы — обычный инфинитив цели; не путать с предлогом to «к, в, на» перед существительным
**Type:** subordinating (infinitival purpose marker) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** non-finite adverbial clause of purpose  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to* [base verb]. &middot; *To* [base verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to* [base verb], not *to* [base verb]. — contrastive &middot; [main clause] *only to* [base verb]. — unexpected result  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted purpose infinitive: *To save time, we skipped lunch*. No comma when it follows the main clause. A comma does appear before a contrastive second infinitive (*to explain, not to excuse*) and before *only to*.  
**Variants:** *in order to* and *so as to* are the explicit forms

The plain to-infinitive is the ordinary, unmarked way to state purpose in American English — *I came to help* simply means "in order to help," and adding *in order* changes nothing but the formality. Switch to the longer forms only when the bare infinitive could be misread as a complement or a result, as in *He stopped to talk* (purpose) beside *He stopped talking* (aspect). Keep the infinitive marker apart from the preposition spelled the same way: in *to the coast* and *listen to me*, *to* takes a noun phrase and states direction or a target, while the purpose *to* takes a bare verb — which is why *look forward to* is followed by an *-ing* form (*to leaving*), a preposition and not this word at all.

1. I stayed late *to* finish the quarterly report.
2. *To* get the discount, enter the code at checkout.
3. She called the vet *to* ask about the dosage.
4. We drove out to the coast *to* watch the meteor shower.
5. He raised his hand *to* get the waiter's attention.
6. They cut every other line item *to* keep the doors open.
7. I'm telling you this *to* explain what happened, not *to* excuse it.

**See also:** [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [so that](#so-that), [only to](#only-to), [too ... to](#too--to)

### with a view to

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ə ˈvju tu/, reduced /wɪð ə ˈvju tə/, also /wɪθ/ &middot; *with-uh-VYOO-too*  
**Русский:** с прицелом на, в расчёте на — офиц.; дальняя цель, дальше обязательно -ing
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a gerund) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (gerund phrase)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *with a view to* [gerund phrase]. &middot; *With a view to* [gerund phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with an eye to* [gerund phrase or noun].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase in the less common fronted order, like any fronted adjunct. A comma in the normal order is acceptable only when the phrase is tacked on as an afterthought.  
**Variants:** *with a view toward*, *with an eye to*, *with an eye toward*

Business and diplomatic register, marking a longer-range aim than *in order to*. The *to* here is a preposition, so what follows has to be an *-ing* form — *with a view to expanding*, never *with a view to expand*. That is one of the most reliably repeated errors in formal English writing, and *with an eye to* behaves exactly the same way.

1. The firm opened a Denver office *with a view to* expanding into the mountain states.
2. They restructured the debt *with a view to* refinancing it next spring.
3. She started taking night classes *with an eye to* changing careers.
4. The talks resumed *with a view toward* a cease-fire by June.
5. He saved every receipt *with an eye to* the audit he assumed was coming.
6. The land was purchased *with a view to* building a school on it.
7. *With a view to* cutting turnover, the hospital rewrote its whole scheduling policy.

**See also:** [with the aim of](#with-the-aim-of), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [in order to](#in-order-to), [so as to](#so-as-to)

### with the aim of

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ði ˈeɪm əv/ &middot; *with-thee-AYM-uv*  
**Русский:** с целью — называет заявленную цель действующего лица, дальше -ing
**Type:** quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a gerund) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite purpose adjunct (gerund phrase)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *with the aim of* [gerund phrase]. &middot; *With the aim of* [gerund phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with the intention of* [gerund phrase].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted.  
**Variants:** *with the goal of*, *with the intention of*, *with the object of* (legalistic)

States the actor's declared objective, and is the workhorse of journalism, grant writing, and reports. It takes an *-ing* form. Choose *with the intention of* when you want to stress what was in the actor's mind rather than what the action achieved — it is the version that survives a failed plan.

1. The program was launched *with the aim of* cutting emergency-room visits.
2. *With the goal of* finishing by June, they brought on a second crew.
3. He moved to Austin *with the intention of* starting a business.
4. The amendment was written *with the aim of* closing exactly that loophole.
5. She entered the race *with the goal of* breaking three hours.
6. They bought the old bank *with the intention of* converting it into apartments.

**See also:** [with a view to](#with-a-view-to), [with the intent that](#with-the-intent-that), [for the purpose of](#for-the-purpose-of), [in order to](#in-order-to), [in an effort to](#in-an-effort-to)

### with this in mind

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ˈðɪs ɪn ˈmaɪnd/ &middot; *with-THISS-in-MYND*  
**Русский:** с учётом этого, исходя из этого — отсылает к уже сказанному, новой цели не вводит
**Type:** linking adverbial (prepositional phrase functioning as a conjunctive adverb) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** independent clause (it does not subordinate anything)  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]. *With this in mind*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]; *with this in mind*, [independent clause]. &middot; *With that in mind*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] ... *with this in mind*.  
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it between two independent clauses, and a comma after it when it opens the clause. A comma alone in front of it is a comma splice. In the clause-final position — *we wrote the policy with this in mind* — it takes no punctuation at all.  
**Variants:** *with that in mind*, *bearing this in mind*, *keeping that in mind*

Points back at a goal, a fact, or a constraint just stated, and marks what follows as the step taken in view of it. It is the everyday version of *to that end*, and looser: *to that end* needs a goal in front of it, while *with this in mind* can point back at any consideration at all. It names no new purpose of its own, which is what separates it from every other phrase in this section.

1. The grant runs out in June. *With this in mind*, the director has already started raising money for next year.
2. Most of our readers open the newsletter on a phone; *with this in mind*, we cut the images in half.
3. *With that in mind*, let's look at what happened in the second quarter.
4. The soil here drains fast. *With this in mind*, plant the lavender on the high side of the bed.
5. The committee wants a decision by Friday, and the agenda was written *with that in mind*.
6. Two of the panelists have never met the applicants. *With this in mind*, the chair asked for written summaries in advance.

## Result: degree correlatives

Paired constructions in which the first half sets a degree and the second half reports what that degree caused. Both halves have to appear — *so* without *that*, or *too* without *to*, leaves the sentence unfinished. No comma belongs inside the construction, with one exception: in speech and informal writing a comma can stand in for a dropped *that* after *so* (*It was so loud, I left*).

### enough ... to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈnʌf ... tu/, also /əˈnʌf/ &middot; *ee-NUFF ... too*  
**Русский:** достаточно ..., чтобы — степени хватает, и результат наступает
**Type:** correlative (degree and result) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** non-finite result clause (to-infinitive); finite with *that*  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] [adjective/adverb] *enough to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *enough* [noun] *to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [adjective] *enough for* [object] *to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [adjective] *enough that* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *enough that* [clause]. — bare adverb, no adjective in front of it  
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere inside the construction, in any of its versions.  
**Variants:** *enough ... that*, *enough that* (bare adverb after the verb), *enough ... for ... to*

The positive counterpart of *too ... to*: the degree is sufficient, so the result does happen. The word-order rule is the trap, and it is absolute — *enough* follows an adjective or adverb (*warm enough, clearly enough*) but precedes a noun (*enough chairs*). Add *for* plus an object when the person doing the second verb is not the subject. The word floats across three classes, and the position tells you which one you have: adverb of degree after an adjective, determiner before a noun, and bare pronoun on its own (*I've had enough*).

1. The water was warm *enough to* swim in by the second week of June.
2. He spoke loudly *enough for* the back row *to* hear every word.
3. We have *enough* chairs *to* seat forty people.
4. She was tired *enough that* she fell asleep in the car on the way home.
5. The ice looked thick *enough to* walk on, but nobody tried it.
6. He knows the city well *enough to* get around without a map.
7. It rained *enough that* the field behind the school flooded again.

**See also:** [too ... to](#too--to), [so ... as to](#so--as-to), [so ... that](#so--that), [such ... that](#such--that), [to such an extent that](#to-such-an-extent-that)

### so ... as to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ ... əz tə/, full /ˈsoʊ ... æz ˌtu/ &middot; *SOH ... az-tuh*  
**Русский:** настолько ..., что — книжн. вариант so ... that; be so kind as to «будьте добры»
**Type:** correlative (degree and result) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** non-finite result clause (to-infinitive)  
**Pattern:** [subject] [be] *so* [adjective] *as to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *so* [adverb] *as to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [be] *not so* [adjective] *as to* [base verb]. — understatement &middot; *Be so kind/good as to* [base verb]. — frozen request formula  
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere inside the construction, in any of its versions.  
**Variants:** *such ... as to*, *not so ... as to*

The infinitival twin of *so ... that*: the degree named in the first half is high enough to produce the result named in the second, but the result arrives as an infinitive instead of a finite clause. It is a step more formal than *so ... that* and lives in three places — technical description (*so arranged as to prevent backflow*), understatement with *not* (*not so foolish as to sign it*), and the frozen courtesy *would you be so kind as to*. Do not confuse it with the purpose marker [so as to](#so-as-to), which has no degree word standing in front of it: *He moved the ladder so as to reach the gutter* states an aim, while *The gutter was so high as to be unreachable* states a degree and its consequence.

1. The vents sit *so* high *as to* be useless in a room this size.
2. He was not *so* careless *as to* leave the keys in the ignition.
3. The panels are fitted *so* tightly *as to* leave no gap for water.
4. Would you be *so* kind *as to* hold the door for a second?
5. Her answer was *so* vague *as to* tell us nothing at all.
6. The margin was *so* small *as to* fall inside the polling error.
7. The two accounts differ *so* sharply *as to* raise a question about the source.

**See also:** [so ... that](#so--that), [so as to](#so-as-to), [such ... that](#such--that), [too ... to](#too--to), [enough ... to](#enough--to)

### so ... that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ ... ðæt/, reduced /ðət/ &middot; *SOH ... thut*  
**Русский:** так, настолько ..., что — степень и её следствие; so при прилагательном или наречии
**Type:** correlative (degree and result) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *so* [adjective/adverb] *that* [result clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *so much/many/little/few* [noun] *that* [result clause]. &middot; *So* [adjective] [be] [subject] *that* [result clause]. — literary inversion  
**Punctuation:** No comma before *that*. When *that* is dropped in speech and informal writing, a comma often takes its place: *It was so loud, I left*.  
**Variants:** *so ... (that)* with *that* dropped, *so much ... that*, *so many ... that*, *so little ... that*, *so few ... that*

Marks a degree high enough to produce a result. *So* attaches to an adjective or an adverb; the same job for a whole noun phrase belongs to *such* — *so cold that* beside *such a cold night that*. With a noun you have to insert a quantity word: *so many applicants*, not *so applicants*.

1. The line was *so* long *that* we gave up and ate at home.
2. She talks *so* fast *that* I miss about half of it.
3. There were *so* many applicants *that* the website crashed twice.
4. He was *so* tired he fell asleep with his shoes on.
5. The room got *so* quiet *that* you could hear the clock in the hall.
6. *So* thick was the fog *that* the ferries stopped running altogether.

**See also:** [such ... that](#such--that), [such that](#such-that), [so much so that](#so-much-so-that), [so ... as to](#so--as-to), [so that](#so-that), [so](#so), [too ... to](#too--to), [enough ... to](#enough--to)

### such ... that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌtʃ ... ðæt/, reduced /ðət/ &middot; *SUCH ... thut*  
**Русский:** такой ..., что — то же, что so ... that, но при существительном
**Type:** correlative (degree and result) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *such a/an* [adjective] [singular noun] *that* [result clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *such* [plural or mass noun] *that* [result clause]. &middot; *Such* [be] [noun] *that* [result clause]. — formal inversion  
**Punctuation:** No comma before *that*.  
**Variants:** *such a ... that* with singular count nouns

The noun-phrase counterpart of *so ... that*: use it when the degree word has to attach to a noun rather than to a bare adjective. Article order is where learners slip — *such a good movie*, never *a such good movie* — and note that the *so* version flips the article to the other side: *so good a movie*, which is formal but correct.

1. It was *such* a good movie *that* we watched it again the next night.
2. They serve *such* enormous portions *that* nobody ever orders dessert.
3. He spoke with *such* confidence *that* no one questioned the numbers.
4. The storm did *such* damage *that* the school stayed closed for a week.
5. *Such* was the noise from the street *that* we moved the table to the back patio.
6. She had *such* a headache *that* she left work before noon.

**See also:** [so ... that](#so--that), [such that](#such-that), [so that](#so-that)

### too ... to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtu ... tu/, second *to* reduced /tə/ &middot; *TOO ... tuh*  
**Русский:** слишком ..., чтобы — избыток, из-за которого действие не выходит; отрицание уже в too
**Type:** correlative (degree and result) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** non-finite result clause (to-infinitive), negative in force  
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *too* [adjective/adverb] *to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *too* [adjective] *for* [object] *to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *too much/many* [noun] *to* [base verb]. &middot; [subject] [be] *too* [adjective] *a* [singular noun] *to* [base verb]. — article inversion  
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere inside the construction.  
**Variants:** *too ... for ... to*, *too ... a ... to*

Excess that blocks a result: *too tired to think* means "so tired that I can't think." Two things follow from the negation being locked inside *too*. The infinitive stays positive — *too heavy to lift*, never *too heavy not to lift* — and the object slot at the end stays empty: *The soup is too hot to drink*, with no *it* after *drink*. With a singular count noun the article moves in behind the adjective, exactly as it does after *so*: *too big a risk to take*. Keep the degree word apart from the other *too*, the sentence adverb meaning "also" (*I'm coming too*), which never sets up a following infinitive.

1. I'm *too* tired *to* argue about this tonight.
2. The box was *too* heavy *for* one person *to* carry up the stairs.
3. It's *too* early *to* tell whether the treatment is working.
4. He was driving *too* fast *to* stop at the light.
5. There were *too* many variables *to* draw any conclusion.
6. The coffee is still *too* hot *to* drink.
7. Buying at that price was *too* big *a* risk *to* take on one appraisal.

**See also:** [enough ... to](#enough--to), [so ... as to](#so--as-to), [so ... that](#so--that), [such ... that](#such--that), [to](#to)


## Result: clause connectors

Subordinators, infinitivals, and relatives that attach a clause reporting an outcome. Because the clause they introduce is dependent, every one of them takes a comma or nothing — a semicolon in front of any word in this section is an error. That is exactly what separates them from the conjunctive adverbs in the last section.

### in such a way that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈsʌtʃ ə ˈweɪ ðæt/, reduced /ɪn ˈsʌtʃ ə ˈweɪ ðət/ &middot; *in-SUCH-uh-WAY-thut*  
**Русский:** таким образом, что; так, чтобы — способ действия и его результат в одном обороте
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and result  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in such a way that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in such a way as to* [base verb]. &middot; [main clause] *in such a manner that* [clause]. — formal &middot; [main clause] *in a way that* [clause]. — plainer  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate, not independent.  
**Variants:** *in such a manner that* (formal, legal), *in such a way as to* + base verb, *in a way that* (plainer)

Manner and result folded into one phrase: it says how something was done and, in the same breath, what that manner brought about. It is the long form of *such that*, and the choice between them is register — *Arrange the panels such that no two identical colors touch* is technical, and *in such a way that* is the version that reads naturally in ordinary prose. *In such a manner that* is the drafting variant and belongs to specifications, codes, and contracts.

1. Stack the boxes *in such a way that* the labels all face the aisle.
2. The question was worded *in such a way that* a yes could later be read as a no.
3. He resigned *in such a way that* nobody could accuse him of walking out on the project.
4. The rear seats fold *in such a way as to* leave the floor completely flat.
5. Fixtures shall be mounted *in such a manner that* they can be serviced without removing the ceiling.
6. She told the story *in a way that* made everyone at the table the hero of one line.
7. The bylaws were rewritten *in such a way that* no single member can block a vote.

**See also:** [such that](#such-that), [so that](#so-that), [so ... as to](#so--as-to), [to such an extent that](#to-such-an-extent-that), [whereby](#whereby)

### it follows that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt ˈfɑloʊz ðæt/, reduced /ɪt ˈfɑloʊz ðət/ &middot; *it-FOL-ohz-thut*  
**Русский:** отсюда следует, что — вывод, вынуждаемый сказанным; отриц. «отсюда не следует, что»
**Type:** matrix-clause result connector (takes a *that*-complement) &middot; **Register:** formal, academic, legal  
**Clause type:** independent clause with a *that*-clause complement  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]. *It follows that* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause]; *it follows that* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], and *it follows that* [clause]. &middot; *From this it follows that* [clause]. &middot; *It does not follow that* [clause]. — the negative, which does most of the work  
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it, because it opens a full independent clause of its own — or a coordinating conjunction plus a comma, which is the ordinary way to join two independent clauses. A comma by itself is a comma splice. No comma after *that*, and none before it.  
**Variants:** *from this it follows that*, *it would follow that* (hedged), *it does not follow that* (negative)

Marks what comes next as a conclusion forced by what came before. Grammatically it is not a conjunction at all — *it* is a dummy subject, *follows* is the verb, and the *that*-clause is that verb's complement — but it does a conjunction's work in argument, standing where *therefore* would stand and carrying more logical weight. Its most useful shape is the negative: *it does not follow that* is the standard way to reject an inference without disputing the premise it was drawn from.

1. Every witness was inside the building at nine. *It follows that* none of them saw the car leave.
2. The two sets have the same number of elements; *it follows that* a one-to-one pairing exists.
3. The contract was never signed, and *it follows that* neither party is bound by it.
4. She was on a plane all afternoon. *It follows that* she could not have sent the message at four.
5. The sample came from a single county, so *it does not follow that* the result holds statewide.
6. If the earlier reading is correct, *it would follow that* the manuscript predates the fire.
7. *From this it follows that* the second experiment was measuring something else entirely.

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [thus](#thus), [which means](#which-means), [consequently](#consequently), [as a corollary](#as-a-corollary)

### meaning (that)

**Pronunciation:** /ˈminɪŋ ðæt/, reduced /ˈminɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *MEE-ning-thut*  
**Русский:** то есть, а значит — поясняет следствие сказанного простыми словами
**Type:** participial connector (supplementary *-ing* clause) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal  
**Clause type:** supplementary participial clause of result or restatement  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *meaning* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *meaning that* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause] — *meaning* [noun phrase]. &middot; [noun phrase], *meaning* [noun phrase]. — gloss  
**Punctuation:** A comma or a dash before it, always; never a semicolon, because what follows is a participial phrase rather than an independent clause. No comma after *meaning*. The *that* is optional and is normally dropped in speech.  
**Variants:** *which means*, *meaning* + noun phrase (gloss), *which is to say* (formal)

Spells out the consequence, or the plain-English translation, of what was just said. It is the participial cousin of *which means*, and the two are close to interchangeable, with one difference of feel: *which means* draws a conclusion, while *meaning* more often restates the point in words the reader can act on. Keep the connector apart from the ordinary participle after a noun — *a sign meaning "no entry"* — which glosses a single word instead of the whole clause.

1. The office closes at four on Fridays, *meaning* you'll have to file the form on Monday.
2. The tunnel is down to one lane, *meaning that* the detour adds twenty minutes each way.
3. She's the only signer on the account — *meaning* nothing moves without her.
4. The rate is fixed for five years, *meaning that* the payment will not change until 2031.
5. Two of the three servers sit in the same building, *meaning* one outage takes both of them down.
6. The deposit is nonrefundable, *meaning* you lose it if you cancel for any reason at all.
7. He came in second by a tenth of a second, *meaning* he missed the final by one place.

**See also:** [which means](#which-means), [which is why](#which-is-why), [thereby](#thereby), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that), [so](#so)

### only to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊnli tu/, reduced /ˈoʊnli tə/ &middot; *OHN-lee-too*  
**Русский:** и в итоге лишь..., только чтобы (обнаружить) — неожиданный и обидный исход вместо цели
**Type:** subordinating (infinitival result marker) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** non-finite clause of unexpected result  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *only to* [base verb]. &middot; [independent clause], *only to find/discover/learn* [that-clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *only to have* [object] [past participle]. &middot; [independent clause], *only for* [subject] *to* [base verb].  
**Punctuation:** Comma before *only to*, and here the comma is required rather than optional, because the phrase comments on the whole clause instead of naming the aim of the verb. Never a semicolon: what follows is an infinitive phrase, not an independent clause.  
**Variants:** *only for ... to* (when the subjects differ), *only to have* + object

The disappointment infinitive. It has the shape of a purpose infinitive but reports the opposite: not what the subject was aiming at, but what actually happened, and what happened defeated the effort just described. *He drove four hours to see the exhibit* is purpose; *He drove four hours, only to find it closed* is result, and an unwelcome one. Switch to *only for X to Y* when the person let down is not the one who acted. Keep this *only* apart from the far commoner focusing adverb (*only on Tuesdays*, *I only asked*), which never sets up an infinitive.

1. She sprinted the length of the terminal, *only to* watch the plane push back from the gate.
2. We repainted the whole kitchen, *only to* decide a month later that we hated the color.
3. He saved for two years, *only to have* the tuition raised again in April.
4. The crew rebuilt the deck in October, *only for* a storm to take it out in January.
5. I called the office three times, *only to* get the same recording.
6. The council passed the ordinance in June, *only to* repeal it before it ever took effect.
7. We drove all the way out to the orchard, *only to find* that it had closed for the season.

**See also:** [to](#to), [in order to](#in-order-to), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that), [which is why](#which-is-why)

### so much so that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ ˈmʌtʃ soʊ ðæt/, reduced /ˌsoʊ ˈmʌtʃ soʊ ðət/ &middot; *soh-MUCH-soh-thut*  
**Русский:** настолько, что — отсылает назад к степени, названной в предыдущей части фразы
**Type:** correlative result connector (anaphoric *so ... that*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result, hanging on a degree already stated  
**Pattern:** [clause containing an adjective or adverb], *so much so that* [result clause]. &middot; [clause] — *so much so that* [result clause]. &middot; [clause], *so much so*. — result left implied  
**Punctuation:** A comma or a dash before it, always. Unlike split *so ... that*, it cannot sit tight against the word it measures, because it is reaching back to a degree stated in the previous clause. Never a semicolon — the clause it introduces is subordinate. No comma after it.  
**Variants:** *so much so* used alone

The pro-form of *so ... that*. Split *so ... that* wraps around the degree word (*so spicy that I couldn't finish it*); *so much so that* leaves the degree word where it stands in the first clause and then points back at it (*It was spicy, so much so that I couldn't finish it*). The middle *so* is doing pro-form duty — it stands in for whatever adjective just appeared — which is why the phrase looks like a stutter and is not one. Reach for it when the adjective sits too far back for the split construction to reach, or when the result deserves its own beat.

1. The room was cold, *so much so that* we could see our breath.
2. Turnout was down, *so much so that* two precincts closed an hour early.
3. She has reread the book maybe thirty times — *so much so that* she quotes it in ordinary conversation.
4. Rents climbed all spring, *so much so that* the council called an emergency session.
5. He was convincing, *so much so that* nobody thought to check the dates.
6. The trail is popular now, *so much so that* the lot fills by seven on Saturdays.
7. "Is the neighborhood really that expensive?" "*So much so.*"

**See also:** [so ... that](#so--that), [such ... that](#such--that), [to the point that](#to-the-point-that), [such that](#such-that), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that)

### such that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌtʃ ðæt/, reduced /ˈsʌtʃ ðət/ &middot; *SUCH-thut*  
**Русский:** такой, что; так, чтобы — книжн., тех.; в математике «такой, что» при задании условия
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal, technical  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result, or a specification clause after a noun  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *such that* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] *such that* [clause]. — mathematical specification  
**Punctuation:** No comma before *such that* in either use.

Two jobs in one phrase. In ordinary prose it reports result: the state of affairs was of a kind that produced what follows. In mathematics, logic, and technical writing it states the condition an object has to satisfy — *a number x such that x squared is four*. Keep it distinct from split *such ... that*, where *such* modifies a noun earlier in the sentence and *that* comes later.

1. Arrange the panels *such that* no two identical colors touch.
2. The lighting in the room was *such that* every photo came out orange.
3. Choose a value of n *such that* the sum is divisible by three.
4. His schedule is *such that* we only overlap on Thursday mornings.
5. The wording was changed *such that* both readings are now defensible.
6. Set the timer *such that* the pump runs an hour before sunrise.

**See also:** [such ... that](#such--that), [so that](#so-that), [in such a way that](#in-such-a-way-that), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that), [whereby](#whereby)

### to such an extent that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈsʌtʃ ən ɪkˈstɛnt ðæt/, reduced /tə ˈsʌtʃ ən ɪkˈstɛnt ðət/ &middot; *tuh-such-un-ick-STENT-thut*  
**Русский:** до такой степени, что — книжн.; не путать с to the extent that «постольку, поскольку»
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result at a degree  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to such an extent that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to such a degree that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *to such an extent that* [clause]. &middot; *To such an extent* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *that* [clause]. — literary inversion  
**Punctuation:** Usually no comma. Writers add one when the result reads as a separate escalation rather than as part of the same measurement, exactly as with *to the point that*. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate.  
**Variants:** *to such a degree that*, *to such a point that*, *to the extent that* (which states a condition, not a result)

The formal member of the *so ... that* family. Where *so ... that* wraps around a single adjective and *to the point that* names a threshold that was crossed, *to such an extent that* measures the whole action, which is why it belongs to reports and academic prose. Do not swap in *to the extent that*: dropping *such* changes the meaning completely, because that phrase means "insofar as" and states a condition rather than an outcome.

1. The river silted up *to such an extent that* barges could no longer reach the mill.
2. Costs rose *to such an extent that* the second phase was abandoned before it broke ground.
3. He has revised the opening *to such a degree that* almost nothing of the first draft is left in it.
4. The building had settled *to such an extent that* none of the doors closed properly.
5. Attendance fell *to such a degree that* the festival was called off after twenty-one years.
6. The species has recovered *to such an extent that* it came off the protected list last spring.
7. The two dialects have diverged *to such an extent that* speakers now watch each other's films with subtitles.

**See also:** [to the point that](#to-the-point-that), [so ... that](#so--that), [so much so that](#so-much-so-that), [such that](#such-that), [in such a way that](#in-such-a-way-that)

### to the point that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ðə ˈpɔɪnt ðæt/, reduced /tə ðə ˈpɔɪnt ðət/ &middot; *tuh-thuh-POYNT-thut*  
**Русский:** до того, что; до такой степени, что — порог, который ситуация перешла
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result at a threshold  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to the point that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to the point where* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to the point of* [gerund/noun].  
**Punctuation:** Usually no comma. Writers add one when the clause reads as a separate escalation: *It rained all week, to the point that the field flooded*.  
**Variants:** *to the point where*, *to the point of* + gerund or noun

Names a threshold the situation crossed, so the result comes across as an extreme rather than a simple consequence. It overlaps with *so ... that* but attaches to the whole clause instead of to a single adjective, which makes it the natural choice when the escalation is gradual. *To the point where* is the more conversational of the two.

1. The noise went on *to the point that* neighbors started calling the police.
2. She reworked the essay *to the point where* it no longer sounded like her.
3. Prices rose *to the point that* people simply stopped eating out.
4. He apologized *to the point of* embarrassment.
5. The software slowed down *to the point where* it was faster to do it by hand.
6. They rehearsed *to the point that* nobody needed a script.

**See also:** [so ... that](#so--that), [such that](#such-that), [so much so that](#so-much-so-that), [to such an extent that](#to-such-an-extent-that), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that)

### whereby

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *wair-BY*  
**Русский:** посредством чего, согласно которому — книжн., юр.; всегда при существительном-механизме
**Type:** relative adverb (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal  
**Clause type:** relative clause of means or result  
**Pattern:** [noun naming a mechanism: agreement, rule, system, process, plan] *whereby* [clause]. — defining, no comma &middot; [fully identified noun], *whereby* [clause]. — supplementary, comma &middot; [main clause] *under/by* [determiner] [noun naming a mechanism] *whereby* [clause]. — the prepositional frame it most often sits in  
**Punctuation:** No comma before *whereby* when the clause defines the noun, which is the normal case. Use a comma only when the clause is a supplementary aside about a noun already fully identified: *the 1997 settlement, whereby the state took over the fund*. Never a semicolon, and it can never open a sentence.  
**Variants:** *by which* is the plainer equivalent; *by means of which* is the full form the word compresses; *by means of* + noun is the bare preposition behind both and joins no clauses at all

Means "by means of which" or "as a result of which," and it always hangs on a preceding noun that names a mechanism — a deal, a policy, a scheme, a process. It cannot modify a whole clause and it cannot open a sentence, which is what separates it from a general result connector. If you cannot name the mechanism noun, write *by which* or recast the sentence.

1. They reached an agreement *whereby* the city would maintain the trail.
2. The company has a policy *whereby* unused vacation days roll over one year.
3. Congress created a program *whereby* borrowers could refinance at a fixed rate.
4. There is an unwritten rule *whereby* the last person out locks up.
5. The settlement set up a process *whereby* claims are reviewed by an outside panel.
6. He described a scheme *whereby* the same votes would be counted twice.
7. The 1997 consent decree, *whereby* the state took over the fund, expires next year.

**See also:** [such that](#such-that), [thereby](#thereby), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that), [to the end that](#to-the-end-that)

### which is why

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ ɪz ˈwaɪ/ &middot; *which-iz-WHY*  
**Русский:** поэтому, вот почему — относит. оборот: всё сказанное перед ним — причина
**Type:** relative (sentential), used as a result connector &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** sentential relative clause of result  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *which is why* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *which is how* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *which is the reason* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause] — *which is why* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Always a comma before it, never a semicolon and never nothing. It is a supplementary relative clause, so the comma is both required and sufficient — the opposite of the rule for *therefore*.  
**Variants:** *which is how*, *which is the reason*

Reaches back and treats the entire preceding clause as the reason for what comes next. In meaning it works like *therefore*; in grammar it is a relative clause, which is why it is punctuated with a comma rather than the semicolon a conjunctive adverb would demand. It is also the most conversational result connector in this file.

1. The bridge was closed all morning, *which is why* everybody showed up late.
2. She grew up bilingual, *which is why* the accent fooled me.
3. Our server sits in Oregon, *which is why* the timestamps look three hours off.
4. He never reads the group chat, *which is why* he came on the wrong day.
5. The recipe has no eggs in it, *which is how* it stays vegan.
6. They cut the budget back in March, *which is why* there is no summer program.
7. Nobody signed off on the change, *which is the reason* it never made it into the release notes.

**See also:** [which means](#which-means), [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [as a result](#as-a-result), [only to](#only-to), [so](#so)

### which means

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ ˈminz/ &middot; *which-MEENZ*  
**Русский:** а значит, из чего следует — разговорный аналог therefore: вывод, а не причина
**Type:** relative (sentential), used as a result connector &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** sentential relative clause of inference or consequence  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *which means* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *which means that* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause] — *which means* [noun phrase]. &middot; [independent clause], *which would mean* [clause]. — hypothetical  
**Punctuation:** Always a comma or a dash before it, never a semicolon and never nothing. It is a supplementary relative clause, so the comma is both required and sufficient. The *that* after it is optional and is usually dropped in speech.  
**Variants:** *which means that*, *which would mean*, *meaning (that)*

Reaches back, treats the whole preceding clause as a premise, and states what follows from it. It is the conversational *therefore*. Its neighbor *which is why* looks almost identical and runs the other direction: *which is why* says the first clause caused the second, while *which means* says the first clause proves the second.

1. The last bus leaves at ten, *which means* we should order now.
2. Both accounts show the same login, *which means* one of them is not hers.
3. The deposit cleared this morning, *which means that* the funds are available today.
4. He's third on the list and the two people ahead of him withdrew, *which means* he's in.
5. The lease renews automatically in August, *which would mean* another year at this rent.
6. The battery is original, *which means* it holds about half the charge it used to.
7. Two inspectors are out sick — *which means* nothing ships before Thursday.

**See also:** [which is why](#which-is-why), [meaning (that)](#meaning-that), [it follows that](#it-follows-that), [therefore](#therefore), [so](#so)

### with the result that

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ðə rɪˈzʌlt ðæt/, reduced /wɪð ðə rɪˈzʌlt ðət/ &middot; *with-thuh-rih-ZULT-thut*  
**Русский:** в результате чего, так что — книжн.: называет только исход, целью прочитан быть не может
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of result  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *with the result that* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *with the effect that* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma before it. Never a semicolon: the clause is subordinate, so it cannot take the punctuation that *therefore* and *consequently* require.  
**Variants:** *with the consequence that*, *with the effect that*

Labels what followed, in so many words. It belongs to reports, academic writing, and legal prose; in speech Americans say *so*. Unlike *so that*, it can never be read as purpose — it states an outcome and nothing else, which makes it useful precisely where *so that* would be ambiguous.

1. The vendor missed two deadlines, *with the result that* the launch slipped to fall.
2. Rainfall was half the average, *with the result that* the reservoirs never refilled.
3. The form was redesigned last year, *with the result that* error rates fell by a third.
4. Two inspectors retired the same month, *with the result that* the backlog doubled.
5. Interest rates rose sharply, *with the effect that* refinancing dried up overnight.
6. The rule was never enforced, *with the consequence that* everyone ignored it.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [such that](#such-that), [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [as a result](#as-a-result)


## Result: conjunctive adverbs

These express result but are adverbs, not conjunctions, and they cannot join two independent clauses on a comma. Between two independent clauses each one needs a semicolon or a period before it and normally a comma after it: *The signature was forged; therefore, the contract is void*. They also move around inside their clause, which a real conjunction cannot do — that mobility is the test for spotting them.

The semicolon rule is a rule about what follows, not about the word itself. When the adverb heads something less than an independent clause — a verbless tag after *hence*, an *-ing* phrase after *thus* or *thereby* — there is no second clause to join, so a plain comma or a dash is correct and a semicolon is wrong: *The printer jammed twice, hence the delay*.

### accordingly

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋli/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding-lee*  
**Русский:** соответственно, и поэтому — книжн.: в ответ на сказанное были приняты меры
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** independent clause (it does not subordinate anything)  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *accordingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Accordingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject], *accordingly*, [verb] ... &middot; [subject] [verb] *accordingly*. — manner sense  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it joins two independent clauses, and a comma after it. A comma alone in front of it is a comma splice. In the clause-final manner use (*planned accordingly*) it takes no punctuation at all.

"And so, in keeping with that." It is the most agentive of the result adverbs: *therefore* draws a conclusion, while *accordingly* says somebody took action in response. It also keeps a plain manner sense at the end of a clause, where it simply means "in the way just described."

1. The forecast called for freezing rain; *accordingly*, the district canceled classes.
2. Sales fell for three straight quarters. *Accordingly*, the board froze hiring.
3. The statute is ambiguous; *accordingly*, the court turned to the legislative history.
4. We were told to expect a smaller crowd, and we planned *accordingly*.
5. The deadline moved up two weeks; the team, *accordingly*, dropped two features.
6. *Accordingly*, the motion is denied.

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [thus](#thus), [hence](#hence), [as a result](#as-a-result)

### as a corollary

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə ˈkɔrəˌlɛri/, reduced /əz ə ˈkɔrəˌlɛri/ &middot; *az-uh-KOR-uh-lair-ee*  
**Русский:** как следствие, в качестве побочного вывода — книжн., науч.: попутное, не главное следствие
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (prepositional phrase functioning as one) &middot; **Register:** formal, academic  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *as a corollary*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *As a corollary*, [independent clause]. &middot; *As a corollary of* [noun], [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb], *as a corollary*, [complement].  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses, and a comma after it when it opens the clause. A comma alone in front of it is a comma splice. *As a corollary of* plus a noun is an ordinary prepositional phrase and follows the ordinary comma rules for one.  
**Variants:** *as a corollary of/to* + noun, *a corollary of that is*, *correspondingly* (looser)

Marks the next statement as a secondary consequence — something that falls out of the first result rather than being the point of it. That makes it narrower than *consequently*: a corollary is an extra that comes with a proof or a policy whether anyone wanted it or not, which is why the phrase is at home in mathematics, philosophy, and analytical writing and sounds stiff anywhere else.

1. The theorem fixes the maximum at four; *as a corollary*, no five-color map can be minimal.
2. Remote work cut the commute. *As a corollary*, downtown lunch counters lost half their traffic.
3. The statute bars the agency from setting prices; *as a corollary*, it cannot set them indirectly through fees.
4. *As a corollary of* the merger, three regional offices will close in the spring.
5. The team drafted for defense and, *as a corollary*, scored fewer points for two straight seasons.
6. Every share is now held electronically; *as a corollary*, the transfer agent's paper vault stands empty.

**See also:** [consequently](#consequently), [therefore](#therefore), [thus](#thus), [accordingly](#accordingly), [it follows that](#it-follows-that)

### as a result

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə rɪˈzʌlt/, reduced /əz ə rɪˈzʌlt/ &middot; *az-uh-rih-ZULT*  
**Русский:** в результате, поэтому — самый нейтральный из результативных оборотов, годится и в речи
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (prepositional phrase functioning as one) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *as a result*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *As a result*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] ... *as a result*. &middot; *As a result of* [noun], [independent clause].  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses, and a comma after it when it opens a clause. A comma alone before it is a comma splice. Clause-final *as a result* usually takes no comma, and *as a result of* plus a noun is a preposition that follows the ordinary comma rules for a phrase.  
**Variants:** *as a consequence*, *as a result of* + noun (prepositional)

The plainest of the result connectives and the only one that sounds natural in email and speech, where *thus* and *hence* would sound stiff. Watch the two shapes: *as a result* connects clauses, while *as a result of* takes a noun phrase and cannot introduce a clause.

1. The supplier raised prices twice; *as a result*, we changed vendors.
2. Two lanes were closed for repaving. *As a result*, the commute doubled.
3. She missed the filing deadline and lost the appeal *as a result*.
4. Attendance dropped sharply *as a result of* the schedule change.
5. The bridge reopened early; *as a consequence*, downtown traffic eased by Friday.
6. *As a result*, nobody trusted the numbers in the second report.

**See also:** [consequently](#consequently), [therefore](#therefore), [thus](#thus), [accordingly](#accordingly), [with the result that](#with-the-result-that)

### consequently

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑnsəkwɛntli/, /ˈkɑnsəkwəntli/ &middot; *KON-suh-kwent-lee*  
**Русский:** следовательно, вследствие этого — книжн.: реальное последствие, а не логический вывод
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *consequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Consequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] and, *consequently*, [verb] ... &middot; [subject] [auxiliary] *consequently* [verb] ...  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses, and a comma after it when it opens the clause. Joining two independent clauses with a comma plus *consequently* is a comma splice.  
**Variants:** *in consequence* (formal, dated)

Marks the second clause as the effect of the first. It is interchangeable with *therefore* in most sentences, with a slight difference of flavor: *therefore* leans logical, drawing a conclusion, while *consequently* leans causal, reporting a real-world effect.

1. The samples were contaminated; *consequently*, the study was withdrawn.
2. He never filed the paperwork. *Consequently*, the permit expired in April.
3. Snow closed the pass for two days; *consequently*, deliveries ran a week late.
4. The departments use different systems and, *consequently*, duplicate half their work.
5. *Consequently*, the committee recommends no change to the policy.
6. Funding was cut in March; *consequently*, the clinic reduced its hours.

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [thus](#thus), [hence](#hence), [accordingly](#accordingly), [as a result](#as-a-result)

### hence

**Pronunciation:** /hɛns/ &middot; *HENSS*  
**Русский:** отсюда, потому и — книжн.; часто без глагола: hence the delay «отсюда и задержка»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, literary  
**Clause type:** independent clause; also used with no verb before a noun phrase  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *hence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Hence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *hence* [noun phrase]. — verbless &middot; [independent clause] — *hence* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when a full clause follows, and the comma after it is optional. In the verbless pattern (*hence the delay*) a comma or a dash is enough, because what follows is not an independent clause and so needs no semicolon.

"For that reason, from this." Drier and more compressed than *therefore*, and its signature move is the verbless tag: *The printer jammed twice, hence the delay*. Do not confuse the result adverb with the time adverb spelled the same way, as in *two weeks hence*, which means "from now." *From hence* is redundant in both senses.

1. The data was collected by volunteers; *hence*, some inconsistency is expected.
2. Both trains were canceled — *hence* the crowd on the platform.
3. The lease ends in June, *hence* the rush to find a subletter.
4. He grew up on the Chesapeake, *hence* the accent.
5. The bridge is one lane in each direction; *hence*, backups are routine.
6. Sample sizes were small, *hence* the wide confidence intervals.
7. The original files were lost in the move. *Hence*, the timeline had to be rebuilt from old emails.

**See also:** [thus](#thus), [thereby](#thereby), [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [accordingly](#accordingly)

### so then

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈðɛn/ &middot; *soh-THEN*  
**Русский:** и вот тогда; так что же — разг.: в рассказе последовательность, в споре вывод-вопрос
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (discourse marker of inference and sequence) &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]. *So then*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *So then* [independent clause]. — narrative, no comma &middot; *So then*, [question]? — checking an inference &middot; [independent clause]; *so then* [independent clause].  
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it when it opens a new sentence. A comma in front of it is defensible only because the *so* inside it can serve as a coordinating conjunction; with the inferential reading, treat it like *therefore* and use the stronger stop. The comma after it is optional and is usually dropped in narrative.  
**Variants:** *and then* (pure sequence), *so anyway* (resuming a story), bare *so* (the plain result conjunction)

Two jobs, told apart by intonation. In narrative it simply moves the story forward — *So then she gets up and walks out* — where it is barely more than a sequencing filler. In argument it draws an inference out of what the other person just said, very often as a question: *So then you're saying it was my fault?* Writers who want the inferential reading on the page often put a comma inside the phrase and print it *so, then*. Both uses belong to speech; in edited prose, use *therefore*, *so*, or *next*.

1. The car wouldn't start. *So then* we called a cab and missed the first act anyway.
2. *So then*, you're telling me the invoice was never sent?
3. He said the office was closed. *So then* why is the light on?
4. She finished the coursework in June; *so then* the only thing left was the thesis.
5. *So then* the whole row of us had to stand up so she could get past.
6. *So then*, where does that leave the Friday deadline?

**See also:** [so](#so), [therefore](#therefore), [accordingly](#accordingly), [that way / this way](#that-way--this-way), [thus](#thus)

### that way / this way

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðæt ˌweɪ/, /ˈðɪs ˌweɪ/ &middot; *THAT-way*, *THISS-way*  
**Русский:** тогда, так — разг.: «сделай так — тогда не забудешь»; устная замена so that
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (noun phrase functioning as one) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]. *That way*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]; *that way*, [independent clause]. &middot; [imperative]. *That way* [independent clause]. &middot; *This way*, [independent clause]. — pointing at a plan being proposed now  
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it between two independent clauses, and a comma after it in careful writing, though speech and casual prose drop that comma routinely. A comma alone in front of it is a comma splice, the same error as with *therefore*.  
**Variants:** *this way*, *that way you* (fixed conversational opener)

The spoken American answer to *so that*. It points back at the action just proposed and names the benefit that action buys: *Write it on the calendar. That way you won't forget* is *Write it on the calendar so that you won't forget*, split across two sentences. *That way* points at something already said, while *this way* points at what the speaker is proposing at that moment. Two lookalikes are not connectives at all — the manner phrase (*hold the bracket that way*) and the direction adverb (*the restrooms are that way*) — and neither one links a pair of clauses.

1. Park behind the building. *That way* you won't have to feed the meter.
2. Send the draft tonight; *that way*, she can read it on the plane.
3. Let's split the list in half. *This way*, we're both done by five.
4. Freeze half the dough. *That way* there's a pizza ready on a Wednesday.
5. I put her number in my phone. *That way*, I don't have to dig out the card every time.
6. We could meet at the bakery instead — *this way* nobody has to drive downtown.
7. Label the cables before you unplug anything. *That way* you'll know what goes back where.

**See also:** [so that](#so-that), [so](#so), [so then](#so-then), [accordingly](#accordingly), [to that end](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#to-that-end)

### thereby

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛrˈbaɪ/, also /ˈðɛrbaɪ/ &middot; *thair-BY*  
**Русский:** тем самым — книжн., обычно перед оборотом с -ing, а не перед целым предложением
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (result by means of what was just said) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** heads an *-ing* participial phrase; rarely a full independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *thereby* [-ing participle phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] and *thereby* [verb] ... &middot; [subject] [auxiliary] *thereby* [verb] ... &middot; [independent clause]; *thereby*, [independent clause]. — rare  
**Punctuation:** A plain comma before it in the ordinary participial pattern, because what follows is a phrase and not an independent clause — *The city rezoned the block, thereby allowing apartments* must not take a semicolon. In the rare case where a full independent clause does follow, the semicolon-or-period rule that governs *therefore* and *consequently* applies, and a comma alone would be a comma splice. No comma after it in any pattern.  
**Variants:** *hereby* (performative, not result), *whereby* (hangs on a noun)

"By that means, and as a result of it." It is the adverb to *whereby*'s relative: *whereby* has to hang on a noun that names a mechanism, while *thereby* points back at the whole preceding clause. Nearly all its modern life is spent in front of an *-ing* form, which is why the comma, not the semicolon, is its normal punctuation — writers who reach for it after a semicolon usually want *thus* or *therefore* instead. It also survives in the frozen idiom *thereby hangs a tale*, where it means "in connection with that."

1. The company retired its two oldest plants, *thereby* cutting emissions by nearly a fifth.
2. The renewal clause was struck before signing, *thereby* leaving the contract silent on what happens in year four.
3. Each district drew its own map and *thereby* protected its own incumbents.
4. She paid the filing fee out of pocket, *thereby* keeping the appeal alive.
5. Blanch the greens first, *thereby* holding the color through a long braise.
6. The update caches the font locally and *thereby* removes one round trip from every page load.

**See also:** [whereby](#whereby), [thus](#thus), [hence](#hence), [therefore](#therefore), [as a result](#as-a-result)

### therefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɛrˌfɔr/ &middot; *THAIR-for*  
**Русский:** поэтому, следовательно — книжн.: вывод; не союз, перед ним нужна точка с запятой
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** independent clause  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *therefore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Therefore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [auxiliary] *therefore* [verb] ... &middot; [subject] [verb], *therefore*, [complement].  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses, and a comma after it at the head of a clause. Mid-clause it takes a comma on each side or none at all, consistently. *It rained, therefore we canceled* is a comma splice; write *It rained; therefore, we canceled*.  
**Variants:** *ergo* (jocular or strictly logical)

Draws a conclusion from what came before, and is strongest in argument, proof, and formal decisions. It is not a coordinating conjunction, and treating it as one is the single most common punctuation error in American student writing. In speech, people simply say *so*.

1. The signature was forged; *therefore*, the contract is void.
2. All three witnesses contradicted each other. *Therefore*, the jury acquitted.
3. The tests came back clean; the recall was *therefore* lifted on Monday.
4. She had already left the company and was, *therefore*, not on the call.
5. *Therefore*, we recommend rejecting the bid in its current form.
6. Costs rose while revenue stayed flat; *therefore*, margins narrowed.

**See also:** [thus](#thus), [hence](#hence), [thereby](#thereby), [consequently](#consequently), [accordingly](#accordingly), [as a result](#as-a-result), [so (coordinating)](01-coordinating.md#so)

### thus

**Pronunciation:** /ðʌs/ &middot; *THUSS*  
**Русский:** таким образом, тем самым — книжн., науч.: одновременно и способ, и следствие
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, academic  
**Clause type:** independent clause; also modifies a following participle  
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *thus*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Thus*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *thus* [-ing participle phrase]. &middot; [independent clause] — *thus* [noun phrase]. — verbless tag &middot; [subject] [auxiliary] *thus* [verb] ...  
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses, and the comma after it is optional. Before an *-ing* participle (*thus leaving the panel short*) a plain comma is correct, because no second independent clause follows, and the same goes for the verbless tag (*thus the decision to close the campus*), which takes a comma or a dash.  
**Variants:** *thusly* is nonstandard; avoid it

"In this way" and "as a consequence" at once. That double sense is why it can head a participial phrase, a pattern *therefore* cannot take: *The mold was reused, thus cutting costs*. It is standard in academic prose and stiff in conversation.

1. The sensor recalibrates every hour; *thus*, drift stays under one percent.
2. Two reviewers dropped out, *thus* leaving the panel one member short.
3. The mold was reused, *thus* cutting production costs nearly in half.
4. The evidence was obtained without a warrant and was *thus* excluded at trial.
5. *Thus*, the hypothesis cannot be rejected on the present data.
6. Enrollment fell for a third year — *thus* the decision to close the campus.
