# Subordinators of concession and contrast

> The conjunctions that hand the other side a point and then take the sentence back anyway.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every word in this file sets up the same two-part deal: one clause grants something inconvenient, and the other clause wins in spite of it. That makes them the subordinating answer to *but* — where *but* balances two equal halves, *although* demotes one of them, so the clause you hang the conjunction on is the clause you are giving away. A few of these words draw a flat contrast instead of a concession (*whereas*, *where*) or subtract an exception from what was just claimed (*except that*, *save that*), and a whole family of them concede every possible case at once (*whatever*, *no matter how*, *however hard*). One punctuation rule runs through all of it: front the subordinate clause and you always need a comma after it, while a trailing clause is a judgment call — a concession is supplementary, so it usually keeps its comma (*She took the job, although the pay was worse*), but a trailing *even if*, *even when*, or *no matter how* clause is integrated into what the main clause claims and usually loses it. The fixed formulas at the end of the file (*be that as it may*, *granted*, *howbeit*) are adverbs, not conjunctions, and take a period or a semicolon before them — never a comma alone.

## Core concessive subordinators

The everyday words for "this is true, and it doesn't change anything." Most of them can be fronted or trailed — the inverted *as* pattern (*tired as I was*) is the one that only ever lives at the front of the sentence — and several — *although*, *though*, *while*, *whilst* — can drop the subject and the verb *be* when the subject is the same in both clauses: *Although tired, she finished the shift.*

### although

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈðoʊ/ (also /ɑlˈðoʊ/ for speakers with the *cot–caught* merger) &middot; *awl-THOH*
**Русский:** хотя, несмотря на то что — вводит уступительное придаточное; книжнее, чем though
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Although* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *although* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *although* clause when it comes first. When it follows, put a comma before *although* — the concession is supplementary, unlike a trailing *because* clause, which takes no comma. Drop it only when the two clauses are short and tightly fused.
**Variants:** *altho*, *tho'* (informal or poetic writing only; not standard prose)

Introduces a fact that makes the main clause surprising. Unlike *but*, it subordinates — the *although* clause is the concession and the main clause carries the point, so which clause you put it on changes what the sentence is arguing. Against *though* it is the heavier, more written of the two, and it can never be tacked onto the end of a sentence as an afterthought.

1. *Although* the store closed at nine, they let us finish shopping.
2. She took the job, *although* the pay was worse than what she had been making.
3. *Although* it rained all weekend, the festival went ahead.
4. He never mentioned the diagnosis, *although* he must have known for months.
5. *Although* I understand the decision, I don't agree with it.
6. The recipe works with frozen berries, *although* fresh ones give you better color.

**See also:** [though](#though), [even though](#even-though), [whereas](#whereas), [albeit](#albeit), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ &middot; unstressed /əz/ &middot; *AZ, reduced to uhz*
**Русский:** хотя, как ни — уступка с инверсией: try as he might «как он ни старался»
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also a preposition (*works as a paralegal*), half of the comparative correlative *as … as*, a relative pronoun after *such* and *the same* (*such delays as we saw*), and a causal and a temporal subordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary in this concessive use
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (with inversion)
**Pattern:** [adjective/adverb/bare verb] *as* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *As* [adjective] *as* [subject] [verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *as* clause. This construction is essentially always fronted; it does not trail comfortably.
**Variants:** *as … as* (the doubled form, more common in American speech: *as tired as I was*)

Concessive *as* takes the word being conceded and throws it in front of the conjunction — *try as he might*, *tired as I was*, *strange as it sounds*. That inversion is the only signal, which is why this *as* has to be kept apart from causal *as* (*As it was raining, we drove*) and temporal *as* (*She called as I was leaving*): same word, same spelling, different word order. And the conjunction is only part of the word's day job — *as* is also a preposition (*She works as a paralegal*), the second half of the comparative *as … as* (*as cold as it looks*), and a relative pronoun after *such* and *the same* (*such problems as we found*). Nothing but position tells them apart.

1. Try *as* he might, he could not get the lawn mower to start.
2. Tired *as* I was, I stayed up to watch the returns.
3. Strange *as* it sounds, the cheaper model outlasted the expensive one.
4. Cold *as* it was, the kids refused to come inside.
5. Search *as* they did, the divers never found the wreck.
6. Hard *as* the team fought, they were down twenty at the half.

**See also:** [much as](#much-as), [though](#though), [however](#however), [albeit](#albeit), [even though](#even-though)

### even as

**Pronunciation:** /ˈivən æz/ &middot; unstressed /ˈivən əz/ &middot; *EE-vun az*
**Русский:** в то время как, тогда как — одновременность с оттенком противоречия
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (a staple of journalism)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession with simultaneity
**Pattern:** *Even as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *even as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. A comma before a trailing *even as* clause is optional and usually left out, since the two events are being presented as one moment.

Marks two things happening at the same time that ought not to go together — a contrast in the calendar rather than in the logic. Plain *as* would give you nothing but simultaneity; *even as* adds the sting. It is the standard way American reporters frame a contradiction they are not allowed to call one.

1. *Even as* the company announced layoffs, it was posting new job listings.
2. She kept smiling *even as* the interviewer cut her off for the third time.
3. *Even as* the levees held, forecasters warned that the second surge would be worse.
4. He insisted the deal was dead *even as* his lawyers were drafting the terms.
5. *Even as* ticket prices climbed, attendance hit a record.

**See also:** [while](#while), [when](#when), [whereas](#whereas), [even though](#even-though)

### even though

**Pronunciation:** /ˌivən ˈðoʊ/ &middot; *ee-vun THOH*
**Русский:** хотя, несмотря на то что — уступаемый факт подаётся как реальный (ср. even if)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Even though* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *even though* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause when it comes first; comma before it when it follows.

The emphatic *although*. It insists that the fact in its clause is genuinely known and genuinely true, and that is the whole line between it and *even if*, where the fact is only supposed: *even though it's raining* means it is raining, *even if it rains* leaves the rain open.

1. *Even though* we left at five, we still hit traffic on the bridge.
2. He paid the full invoice, *even though* half the work was never done.
3. *Even though* she's allergic, she keeps two cats.
4. They kept the store open through the winter, *even though* it lost money every month.
5. *Even though* I've seen it four times, I cried again at the end.

**See also:** [although](#although), [though](#though), [even if](#even-if), [even when](#even-when), [much as](#much-as)

### even when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌivən ˈwɛn/ (also /ˌivən ˈhwɛn/) &middot; unstressed /ˌivən wən/ &middot; *ee-vun WEN*
**Русский:** даже когда — уступка по всем случаям, включая самый маловероятный
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over occasions
**Pattern:** *Even when* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *even when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. No comma when it trails — the clause names a case the main claim has to survive, so it is integrated rather than supplementary.

The member of the *even* family that quantifies over occasions instead of a single fact or a single hypothetical. *Even though he's tired* concedes one thing that is true right now; *even if he's tired* leaves it open; *even when he's tired* says the main clause holds on every such occasion, the least likely one included. It is the plain alternative to *no matter when* and to a *whenever* clause pushed onto its hardest case.

1. *Even when* the store is empty, there's a wait at the register.
2. She takes the stairs *even when* she's carrying groceries.
3. *Even when* he's flatly wrong, he sounds completely sure of himself.
4. The heat doesn't kick on *even when* it drops below freezing.
5. *Even when* we book months ahead, we end up in the middle seats.

**See also:** [even though](#even-though), [even if](#even-if), [even as](#even-as), [whenever](#whenever), [no matter when](#no-matter-when)

### much as

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌtʃ æz/ &middot; unstressed /ˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *MUCH az*
**Русский:** как ни, при всём том что — хотя очень: much as I'd like to «как бы мне ни хотелось»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Much as* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *As much as* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *much as* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, which is where it sits in nearly every case. It can trail, and there it keeps the comma, because a concession is supplementary: *I'll have to pass, much as I'd like to be there.*
**Variants:** *as much as* (the more common form in American speech)

A concession about the speaker's own feelings: "I like this a great deal, and the main clause still stands." It almost always attaches to a verb of liking, admiring, wanting, or hating, which makes it the standard polite way to decline. *Although I'd like to* reports a fact; *much as I'd like to* reports a fact and a regret.

1. *Much as* I admire her, I wouldn't want to work for her.
2. *As much as* I love that neighborhood, we can't afford it.
3. *Much as* it pains me to say it, the second album is better.
4. *As much as* he complains about the commute, he has never once been late.
5. *Much as* the committee wanted to fund every proposal, the budget wouldn't stretch.
6. I'll have to pass on Saturday, *much as* I'd like to be there.

**See also:** [as](#as), [although](#although), [even though](#even-though), [though](#though)

### much though

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌtʃ ðoʊ/ &middot; *MUCH thoh*
**Русский:** книжн. как ни — книжный вариант much as, чаще британский
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal or literary; *much as* is the ordinary American form
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Much though* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *much though* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, which is where it nearly always sits. Comma before it when it trails, because a concession is supplementary. It is a subordinator, not an adverb, so no semicolon question arises.
**Variants:** *much as* (the standard American form), *as much as*

The bookish twin of *much as* — the same meaning, the same attachment to verbs of liking, wanting, and admiring, but a register higher and far commoner in British writing than in American. Americans meet it in literary prose and in speeches reaching for a formal note; what actually comes out in speech is *much as* or *as much as*.

1. *Much though* I admire her scholarship, this chapter does not hold together.
2. *Much though* the town wanted the factory, nobody wanted the traffic that came with it.
3. He declined the nomination, *much though* it flattered him to be asked.
4. *Much though* it pains me to agree with the man, he is right about the drainage.
5. *Much though* she loved the house, the commute finally decided it.

**See also:** [much as](#much-as), [as](#as), [though](#though), [although](#although), [even though](#even-though)

### though

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊ/ &middot; *THOH*
**Русский:** хотя; в конце фразы — «впрочем, всё же»; разговорнее, чем although
**Type:** subordinating (also a conjunctive adverb in final position) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the final-position adverb is informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Though* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *though* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [adjective/noun/bare verb] *though* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [statement], *though*. (final adverb)
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *though* clause and before a trailing one. As a sentence-final adverb it is always set off by a comma: *I like it, though.*

The lighter, more spoken twin of *although*, and the only member of the family that also works as a stand-alone adverb meaning "however," parked at the end of a sentence. It takes the inverted concessive pattern too (*cold though it was*), which *although* cannot.

1. *Though* the recipe calls for buttermilk, regular milk works fine.
2. We took the long way, *though* nobody was in a hurry.
3. Cold *though* it was, we ate out on the patio.
4. "The car's twelve years old." "It runs, *though*."
5. *Though* she'd never admit it, the surprise party got to her.
6. It's a small apartment. The light is incredible, *though*.

**See also:** [although](#although), [even though](#even-though), [albeit](#albeit), [as](#as), [however](#however)

### though that

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊ ðət/ &middot; *THOH that*
**Русский:** устар. хотя — то же though с избыточным that; Шекспир и XVII век
**Type:** subordinating, with the older pleonastic complementizer *that* attached &middot; **Register:** archaic — Early Modern English; you meet it in Shakespeare and in seventeenth-century verse, and in no modern register
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Though that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *though that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, exactly as with bare *though*. Modern editions of older texts keep the *that* and punctuate the clause as though it were not there.
**Variants:** *though* (the modern form), *if that*, *when that*, *for that*, *because that* (the same doubled pattern on other conjunctions)

Early Modern English let a subordinator take a redundant *that* as a clause marker — *though that*, *if that*, *when that*, *for that* — and the doubled form died out over the seventeenth century. Nothing separates *though that* from *though* in meaning; the extra word is grammatical scaffolding, and for a poet it was also a spare syllable. Read straight past it.

1. "*Though that* his joy be joy, / Yet throw such changes of vexation on't." (*Othello*, I.i)
2. "If I do prove her haggard, / *Though that* her jesses were my dear heart-strings, / I'd whistle her off." (*Othello*, III.iii)
3. *Though that* the night was far spent, the watch kept its post.
4. *Though that* he were the king's own son, the law would have him.
5. The town gave up nothing, *though that* the siege ran on through the winter.

**See also:** [though](#though), [although](#although), [what though](#what-though), [for that](12-formal-archaic.md#for-that), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### try as one might

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtraɪ əz wʌn ˈmaɪt/ &middot; *TRY az wun MYTE*
**Русский:** как ни старайся, сколько ни пытайся — за оборотом обычно следует неудача
**Type:** subordinating — a frozen instance of the inverted concessive *as* &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary; fully current in American writing, a little bookish in speech
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, with the verb thrown in front of the conjunction
**Pattern:** *Try as* [subject] *might*, [main clause]. &middot; *Try as* [subject] *would/will/did*, [main clause]. &middot; *Try as* [subject] *might to* [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [bare verb] *as* [subject] *might*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause. The construction is fronted in nearly every case; trailing it (*he never got it started, try as he might*) is possible, and there the comma is required, since it lands as an afterthought.
**Variants:** *try as I might*, *try as he might*, *try as they might* (the pronoun tracks the subject), *try as one will*, *search as they did*, *push as he would* (the same frame on other verbs)

The most frozen instance of concessive *as*: a bare verb is thrown in front of the conjunction and the modal is left standing behind it, giving "no matter how hard the subject tried." The verb is not limited to *try* — *search as they did*, *push as he would* — but *try as one might* is the shape Americans actually reach for, and the main clause behind it is almost always a failure.

1. *Try as he might*, he could not get the pilot light to stay lit.
2. *Try as I might*, I cannot make the deposits match the register.
3. *Try as they might*, the auditors never found where the money went.
4. *Try as she might* to sound neutral, her opinion came through in every paragraph.
5. The stain came back every spring, *try as we might* to seal the ceiling.
6. *Search as they did*, the divers never located the second engine.

**See also:** [as](#as), [much as](#much-as), [however](#however), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [though](#though)

### when

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn/ (also /hwɛn/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*) &middot; unstressed /wən/ &middot; *WEN*
**Русский:** хотя, тогда как — упрёк: зачем платить, когда рядом бесплатно
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also the ordinary temporal subordinator, an interrogative adverb (*When did you get in?*), a relative adverb (*the year when we moved*), a complementizer in noun clauses (*I don't know when it ships*), and a noun (*the when and the where*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (an extension of the clause of time)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *when* [clause]. &middot; *When* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Normally no comma before a trailing concessive *when* clause — it is integrated, not supplementary. Comma after it if you front it.

The same word that marks time also marks concession, and only sense tells them apart: *Why buy it when you can borrow it?* is not about a moment, it is about a contradiction. Concessive *when* almost always follows the main clause and usually carries a note of complaint or challenge that *although* does not.

1. Why pay for parking *when* the lot two blocks over is free?
2. He asked for a raise *when* he'd been there six weeks.
3. She apologized *when* the whole thing was my fault.
4. *When* you already own the tools, hiring somebody makes no sense.
5. They cut the arts budget *when* enrollment in those classes was going up.

**See also:** [while](#while), [even as](#even-as), [whereas](#whereas), [though](#though)

### while

**Pronunciation:** /waɪl/ (also /hwaɪl/) &middot; *WYLE*
**Русский:** хотя; тогда как — уступка или противопоставление (тот же союз значит и «пока»)
**Type:** subordinating; also a noun (*stay a while*, *worth your while*) and a verb (*while away an afternoon*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession or contrast (also of time)
**Pattern:** *While* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *while* [clause].
**Punctuation:** The comma is what separates the two meanings. Contrastive *while* takes one — *He's methodical, while his partner works on instinct* — and temporal *while* does not: *She read while I cooked.* Fronted, it always takes a comma after the clause.

*While* does three jobs as a conjunction — time, contrast, and concession — and American readers sort them by position and comma. Concessive *while* ("although") is normal at the front of a sentence; contrastive *while* ("whereas") is normal in the middle with a comma in front of it. If a sentence could be read either as time or as contrast, rewrite it. Outside the conjunction the same spelling is a noun (*it took a while*, *worth your while*) and a verb (*we whiled away the afternoon*), which is why *awhile* and *a while* trip people up: the one-word adverb modifies a verb, the two-word noun phrase follows a preposition.

1. *While* I agree with the goal, I think the timeline is fantasy.
2. He's methodical, *while* his partner works on instinct.
3. *While* the paint is technically dry, I wouldn't lean on it.
4. Our office got new chairs, *while* the warehouse crew got nothing.
5. *While* nobody disputes the numbers, everybody disputes what they mean.

**See also:** [whereas](#whereas), [although](#although), [whilst](#whilst), [when](#when), [even as](#even-as)

### whilst

**Pronunciation:** /waɪlst/ (also /hwaɪlst/) &middot; *WYLST*
**Русский:** брит. хотя, тогда как — британский вариант while; в США звучит манерно
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** British; dated or affected in American English
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession or contrast (also of time)
**Pattern:** *Whilst* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *whilst* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Same as *while*: comma after a fronted clause, comma before a trailing contrastive one.
**Variants:** *while* (the standard American form)

The British counterpart of *while*, in every one of its senses. Americans read it as affected or as a signal that the writer is British, and it turns up in American prose almost only when someone is reaching for formality. Use *while*. Its cousins *amongst*, *amidst*, and *learnt* carry the same flag.

1. *Whilst* the committee acknowledged the delay, it declined to apologize.
2. She studied in Leeds, *whilst* her brother stayed in Cork.
3. *Whilst* I take your point, the deadline has not moved.
4. The tenants were told to vacate *whilst* repairs were carried out.
5. *Whilst* rare, such errors are not unheard of.

**See also:** [while](#while), [although](#although), [whereas](#whereas)

## Contrast: two things side by side

These do not concede anything. They set two propositions against each other and leave both standing, which is why a comma before them is close to mandatory — the second clause is a comparison, not a condition on the first. Two of them, *except that* and *save that*, do something narrower still: instead of comparing, they subtract, taking back one piece of the claim the main clause just made. Four are not clause conjunctions at all — *contra*, *contrary to*, and *pace* take a noun phrase, and *on the flip side* is a conjunctive adverb needing a period or a semicolon in front of it — but they draw the same line between two positions, so they belong here.

### contra

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrə/ &middot; *KON-truh*
**Русский:** юр. напротив, вопреки — отсылка к источнику противоположного мнения
**Type:** conjunctive adverb by function — a Latin preposition in form, taking a noun phrase or a citation and never a clause &middot; **Register:** formal — American legal citation and academic prose; it exists nowhere else
**Clause type:** none — it points at an authority or a writer holding the opposite view
**Pattern:** [statement]. *Contra*, [citation]. &middot; *Contra* [name], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] (*contra* [name]).
**Punctuation:** In a citation sentence, a comma after it and then the authority: *Contra*, Erie R.R. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938). Before a name in running prose it takes no comma of its own — *contra* Quine — and the whole phrase is fenced with commas or parentheses when it interrupts. It is not a conjunction and cannot join two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *but see*, *but cf.* (the English citation signals that do the same job), *pace* (which bows before it disagrees), *contra proferentem*, *contra bonos mores* (fixed legal phrases — a different word class)

Latin "against," and in American usage a pointer rather than a connector: it flags an authority or a writer holding the opposite of what the sentence just said. Citation manuals keep it as the signal for directly contrary authority, and scholarship borrows it to name the person being argued with. *Contra* Smith means "Smith says otherwise"; *pace* Smith means "Smith says otherwise, and I mean no disrespect."

1. *Contra*, Erie R.R. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938).
2. Every circuit to reach the question has read the clause narrowly. *Contra*, a single unpublished order from 1974.
3. *Contra* Quine, the analytic–synthetic distinction survives in a weakened form.
4. Most editors treat the manuscript as a scribal copy (*contra* Hollander, who calls it an autograph).
5. The brief cites four cases for the proposition and, *contra*, none against it.

**See also:** [pace](#pace), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [whereas](#whereas), [on the contrary](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#on-the-contrary), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### contrary to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrɛri tu/ &middot; casually /ˈkɑntrɛri tə/ &middot; *KON-trair-ee too*
**Русский:** вопреки, в противоположность — требует существительного, а не придаточного
**Type:** conjunctive adverb by function — a two-word preposition, taking a noun phrase or a fused relative *what* clause and never a bare finite clause &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none of its own — a fronted adverbial that sets the main clause against a belief, an expectation, or a rule
**Pattern:** *Contrary to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Contrary to what* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *contrary to* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, and that comma is not optional — the phrase is a full adverbial. It cannot join two independent clauses: *The trail is open, contrary to what you heard, it closes in November* is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *contrary to popular belief*, *contrary to expectation* (fixed collocations), *contrary to the fact that* (wordy — cut it), *contra* (the Latin citation form)

Sets the main clause against something someone believes, expects, or has ordered. Its everyday use is corrective — *contrary to what you've heard* — and its legal use is stricter, where a thing done *contrary to* a rule was done in violation of it. It needs a noun phrase, so to hang it on a clause you have to insert *what* (*contrary to what the sign says*) or *the fact that*.

1. *Contrary to* popular belief, the bridge is older than the highway.
2. *Contrary to what* the listing said, the unit has no washer-dryer hookup.
3. She kept the receipts, *contrary to* the office's usual practice.
4. *Contrary to* the doctor's advice, he flew home the next morning.
5. *Contrary to* every forecast we saw, the storm turned east at the last minute.
6. The permit was issued *contrary to* the zoning ordinance and was later revoked.

**See also:** [contra](#contra), [whereas](#whereas), [on the flip side](#on-the-flip-side), [notwithstanding anything to the contrary](#notwithstanding-anything-to-the-contrary), [on the contrary](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#on-the-contrary)

### except that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt ðət/ &middot; *ik-SEPT that*
**Русский:** за исключением того, что; только вот — отнимает часть уже сказанного
**Type:** subordinating; *except* on its own is a preposition (*everyone except me*) and, in frozen phrases, a verb (*present company excepted*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of exception and contrast
**Pattern:** [main clause], *except that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause]. *Except that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *except* [when/where/if] [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before a trailing *except that* clause — it withdraws part of what was just claimed, so it is supplementary, never integrated. It essentially never fronts ahead of its own main clause: *Except that the fan rattled, the room was quiet* is not idiomatic. When the exception needs weight of its own, give it a period or a dash and let it stand as a fragment.
**Variants:** *except*, *except for the fact that* (wordy — cut it), *only* (the spoken equivalent: *I'd have called, only I lost your number*)

Subtracts a piece of the main clause instead of conceding a point against it, and that is what keeps it out of the *although* family: *although the room was quiet* grants a fact and moves on, while *except that the fan rattled* takes something back. American speech does the same work with bare *but* or *only*; *except that* is what writing reaches for when the exception has to be named precisely.

1. The apartment would be perfect, *except that* there's no parking on the block.
2. Nothing about the trip went wrong, *except that* the rental car smelled like smoke.
3. I'd have finished the draft, *except that* the power went out at nine.
4. The two engines are identical, *except that* the newer one carries a longer warranty.
5. She remembers the whole evening. *Except that* she can't say who drove her home.
6. The branch is open every day, *except* when the holiday falls on a Monday.

**See also:** [save that](#save-that), [aside from the fact that](#aside-from-the-fact-that), [whereas](#whereas), [where](#where), [but](01-coordinating.md#but), [only](01-coordinating.md#only)

### on the flip side

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈflɪp ˌsaɪd/ &middot; casually /ɑn ə ˈflɪp saɪd/ &middot; *on thuh FLIP side*
**Русский:** разг. с другой стороны — обратная сторона медали, о двух сторонах одной сделки
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal — spoken American English; too casual for a brief, a contract, or a formal report
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial linking two independent clauses
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *On the flip side*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *on the flip side*, [independent clause]. &middot; *On the flip side of* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it. Because it is an adverb and not a conjunction, a comma alone in front of it between two independent clauses is a comma splice — use a period or a semicolon: *The rent is cheap; on the flip side, the commute is an hour.*
**Variants:** *on the other hand* (the neutral form), *then again*, *flip side*, *the flip side is (that)* (the same image as a noun)

The casual American cousin of *on the other hand*, borrowed from the B-side of a 45. It introduces the offsetting half of a trade-off, and it works best when the two halves really are two faces of one thing — cheap rent and a long commute — rather than two unrelated facts. In anything more formal than an email, write *on the other hand*.

1. The job pays well. *On the flip side*, you're on call every other weekend.
2. We got the house under asking; *on the flip side*, the roof is going this year.
3. *On the flip side*, nobody in Tucson has ever had to shovel a driveway.
4. Streaming gutted album sales. *On the flip side*, a nobody in Ohio can reach ten thousand listeners overnight.
5. She's the fastest coder on the team; *on the flip side*, nobody else can read what she writes.

**See also:** [on the other hand](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#on-the-other-hand), [whereas](#whereas), [while](#while), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [in contrast](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#in-contrast)

### pace

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpeɪsi/ (also /ˈpɑtʃeɪ/, /ˈpɑkeɪ/) — not the English word *pace* /peɪs/ &middot; *PAY-see*
**Русский:** книжн. при всём уважении к — вопреки мнению названного автора
**Type:** conjunctive adverb by function — a Latin ablative used as a preposition, taking a name or a noun phrase and never a clause &middot; **Register:** formal, academic — criticism, philosophy, and the more literary reviews; a scholar's word, and unknown outside that world
**Clause type:** none — a parenthetical adverbial naming the authority being contradicted
**Pattern:** *Pace* [name], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *pace* [name]. &middot; [main clause] (*pace* [name]).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase; commas or parentheses on both sides when it interrupts. No comma between *pace* and the name it governs. It cannot join two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *with all due respect to*, *with apologies to* (the plain-English forms), *contra* (blunter, and without the courtesy)

Latin for "by the leave of," and it means "with apologies to X, who thinks otherwise." It concedes that a respected authority holds the opposite view and then proceeds anyway, which makes it the politest adversative in academic English — and the most easily misread, since it looks like the ordinary English word and is said *PAY-see*. Against *contra*, which simply files an opposing citation, *pace* bows first.

1. *Pace* Dr. Johnson, the poem is not a failure.
2. The tax, *pace* its defenders, falls hardest on the people it was written to help.
3. *Pace* the committee's report, enrollment has been flat for a decade.
4. Chaucer was not writing for a courtly audience here, *pace* Robertson.
5. *Pace* my colleagues in the department, the second edition is the better text.

**See also:** [contra](#contra), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [granted](#granted), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [albeit](#albeit)

### save that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈseɪv ðət/ &middot; *SAYV that*
**Русский:** книжн., устар. за исключением того, что — формальный двойник except that
**Type:** subordinating; *save* is far commoner as a preposition (*all save one*) and as an ordinary verb &middot; **Register:** formal and literary, verging on archaic — not neutral, and out of place in ordinary American prose
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of exception and contrast
**Pattern:** [main clause], *save that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *save* [noun phrase]. (preposition) &middot; [main clause], *save for* [noun phrase]. (preposition)
**Punctuation:** Comma before the *save that* clause, exactly as with *except that*. It does not front.
**Variants:** *save*, *save for*, *saving that* (archaic), *except that* (the neutral everyday form)

The formal twin of *except that*, and the register gap between them is wide: *save that* belongs to literary narration, appellate opinions, and deliberate period voice, and in a memo or an email it reads as a costume. Use *except that*. What is still alive in American English is the preposition *save* or *save for* — *the lot was empty save for one truck* — and even that sits a notch above neutral.

1. The record is complete, *save that* two pages are missing from the appendix.
2. Nothing in the house had changed, *save that* someone had moved the piano.
3. The statute reaches every carrier, *save that* municipal fleets are exempt.
4. He remembered nothing of the crossing, *save that* it had been bitterly cold.
5. The street was dark, *save for* a light in the second-floor window. (preposition, not a conjunction)

**See also:** [except that](#except-that), [albeit](#albeit), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [whereas](#whereas), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### where

**Pronunciation:** /wɛr/ (also /hwɛr/) &middot; *WAIR*
**Русский:** тогда как, там где — контраст двух версий одного и того же
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also the ordinary locative subordinator (*Put it where it belongs*), an interrogative adverb (*Where is it?*), a relative adverb (*the town where she grew up*), and a noun (*the where and the when*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of contrast (also of place)
**Pattern:** *Where* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *where* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause and before the trailing one. That comma is also what tells a reader you mean contrast and not place.

Contrastive *where* sets one case against another — "in this version X, in that one Y." It is the reviewer's and the analyst's conjunction. Where *whereas* balances two whole propositions, contrastive *where* usually balances two versions of the same thing.

1. *Where* his first novel was tight and mean, this one sprawls.
2. The old system logged every request, *where* the new one samples.
3. *Where* other teams rebuild, this one just keeps signing veterans.
4. She writes in long paragraphs, *where* he can't get past two sentences.
5. *Where* the state requires a permit, the county only asks for a notice.

**See also:** [whereas](#whereas), [while](#while), [whilst](#whilst), [when](#when), [except that](#except-that)

### whereas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæz/ (also /hwɛrˈæz/) &middot; *wair-AZ*
**Русский:** тогда как, в то время как — чистое противопоставление, без уступки
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of contrast
**Pattern:** [main clause], *whereas* [clause]. &middot; *Whereas* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Always a comma before a trailing *whereas* clause — it is supplementary, never integrated. Comma after it when fronted. In legal preambles (*Whereas the parties agree …*) the older sense "given that" survives, and each *whereas* clause is closed with a semicolon.

Pure contrast, no concession: it says the two clauses differ, not that one defeats the other. That is the line against *although* — *Although I like coffee, I drink tea* implies a tension, while *I drink coffee, whereas she drinks tea* implies only a difference. *While* does the same job in a lower register.

1. My brother loves horror movies, *whereas* I can't get through the trailers.
2. *Whereas* the coastal counties voted one way, the interior went the other.
3. Diesel engines gain efficiency under load, *whereas* gas engines lose it.
4. She was hired for her research, *whereas* he was hired to run the lab.
5. *Whereas* the first draft ran ninety pages, the final version is thirty.

**See also:** [while](#while), [where](#where), [although](#although), [whilst](#whilst)

## Concessive conditionals

These concede a condition rather than a fact — the thing granted has not happened and may never happen, and the main clause holds either way. The trailing clause here is integrated, so unlike a plain concession it normally takes no comma.

### arguendo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑrɡjuˈɛndoʊ/ &middot; *ar-gyoo-EN-doh*
**Русский:** юр. допустим ради спора — в обороте assuming arguendo that
**Type:** subordinating inside the fixed frame *assuming arguendo that*; the bare word is a Latin adverb ("in the course of argument") and connects nothing by itself &middot; **Register:** formal, legal — American briefs and judicial opinions, and essentially nowhere else
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Assuming arguendo that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Assuming, arguendo, that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Even assuming arguendo* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [verb phrase] *arguendo*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. The adverb itself is often fenced with commas inside the frame — *assuming, arguendo, that the statute applies* — and either punctuation is standard.
**Variants:** *assuming arguendo*, *for the sake of argument*, *assuming for the sake of argument that* (the plain-English forms), *conceding the point arguendo*

A lawyer's *even if*. It grants a premise the writer does not accept, purely to show that the argument fails even on the opponent's own terms, and the Latin is there so that nobody can later quote the concession as an admission. In a brief it is standard and expected; anywhere else, write *even if* or *for the sake of argument*.

1. *Assuming arguendo that* the search was lawful, the evidence was still gathered outside the scope of the warrant.
2. *Even assuming arguendo* the plaintiff has standing, the claim is time-barred.
3. The court accepted, *arguendo*, that the notice was defective, and affirmed on other grounds.
4. *Assuming, arguendo, that* a contract was formed, no party ever performed under it.
5. Counsel conceded the point *arguendo* and moved on to the damages calculation.

**See also:** [even if](#even-if), [granted that](#granted-that), [conceding that](#conceding-that), [admitting that](#admitting-that), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether)

### even if

**Pronunciation:** /ˌivən ˈɪf/ &middot; unstressed /ˌivən əf/ &middot; *ee-vun IF*
**Русский:** даже если — уступка предположению, а не факту (ср. even though)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Even if* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *even if* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause when it comes first; normally no comma when it follows, because the condition is part of what the main clause is claiming.

Grants a condition that has not happened, then says the main clause holds anyway. That is the whole difference from *even though*, which grants a fact. It often pairs with *would* or *still*, and with a past-perfect verb it turns counterfactual: *even if you had told me*.

1. *Even if* the flight is on time, I won't get in before midnight.
2. I'm going *even if* nobody else does.
3. *Even if* you had told me, I'm not sure I could have stopped it.
4. The policy wouldn't have covered the damage *even if* she had filed the day it happened.
5. *Even if* the numbers hold up, the conclusion still doesn't follow.

**See also:** [even though](#even-though), [if](#if), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [no matter what](#no-matter-what)

### if

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf/ &middot; unstressed /əf/ &middot; *IF*
**Русский:** книжн. хотя и — при прилагательном: a bold, if risky plan «смелый, хотя и рискованный»
**Type:** subordinating; the same word heads conditional clauses (*If it rains, we'll move inside*) and noun clauses after verbs of asking and knowing (*Ask if they're open*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary in this concessive use; conditional *if* is neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, usually reduced to a phrase
**Pattern:** [statement], *if* [adjective]. &middot; [determiner] [adjective], *if* [adjective], [noun]. &middot; *If* [adjective/noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides when concessive *if* interrupts — *a clever, if flawed, argument*. A fronted reduced clause takes a comma after it. Never leave it uncommaed, or a reader will take it for the conditional.

A compressed *although*, almost always attached to an adjective rather than to a full clause, and almost always in writing. It concedes one quality while asserting another: *a useful, if expensive, tool*. Conditional *if* asks whether something is true; concessive *if* takes for granted that it is and grants it anyway.

1. The plan is bold, *if* risky.
2. *If* poor, he was never once late with the rent.
3. It was a clear, *if* unwelcome, answer.
4. She gave a short, *if* pointed, reply to the board.
5. The remodel produced a functional, *if* charmless, kitchen.
6. *If* not elegant, the solution at least worked.

**See also:** [even if](#even-if), [although](#although), [albeit](#albeit), [though](#though)

### regardless of whether

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈɡɑrdləs əv ˈwɛðər/ (also /ˈhwɛðər/) &middot; casually /rɪˈɡɑrdləs ə ˈwɛðər/ &middot; *rih-GARD-luss uv WETH-er*
**Русский:** офиц. независимо от того, ... ли — канцелярский вариант whether or not
**Type:** subordinating; bare *regardless* is a conjunctive adverb (*Regardless, the flight left on time*) and *regardless of* is a preposition (*regardless of cost*) — neither can take a clause until *whether* or *the fact that* is bolted on &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Regardless of whether* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *regardless of whether* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *irrespective of whether* (more formal). *Irregardless* is nonstandard in every register — do not use it in writing.

Says the main clause holds across both branches of an open question. It is the contractual version of *whether or not*: same meaning, four more syllables, and a home in policies, statutes, and memos rather than in speech.

1. *Regardless of whether* the grant comes through, the study starts in March.
2. Tenants must be given sixty days' notice *regardless of whether* the sale closes.
3. *Regardless of whether* you attend, please submit your comments in writing.
4. The application fee is nonrefundable *regardless of whether* the applicant is admitted.
5. *Regardless of whether* the recall is expanded, the plant stays closed through August.

**See also:** [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [even if](#even-if)

### whether or not

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər ər ˈnɑt/ (also /ˈhwɛðər/) &middot; *WETH-er er NOT*
**Русский:** независимо от того, ... или нет — верно при любом из вариантов
**Type:** subordinating, and correlative in the split form *whether … or (not)*; the same *whether* also heads noun clauses (*I asked whether it was open*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Whether or not* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Whether* [clause] *or not*, [main clause]. &middot; *Whether* [clause A] *or* [clause B], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the whole fronted clause, and no comma between the *whether* half and the *or* half. No comma when the clause trails.
**Variants:** *whether … or*, *whether … or not*

Runs through every alternative and says the outcome is the same in all of them. Keep it apart from noun-clause *whether*: *I don't know whether she's coming* is an object, takes no comma, and cannot lose the *or not*, while concessive *whether or not you agree* is an adverbial and always can.

1. *Whether or not* you agree, the vote stands.
2. The bill comes on the first *whether* you use the service *or* not.
3. *Whether* the forecast is right *or* the storm turns, we're canceling the trip.
4. She's going to say what she thinks *whether or not* the cameras are on.
5. *Whether or not* it was intentional, the effect was the same.

**See also:** [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [even if](#even-if), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [come what may](#come-what-may), [like it or not](#like-it-or-not)

## Universal concessives: the *-ever* words and *no matter*

These concede every case at once — every degree, every thing, every person, every place, every time. The *-ever* words carry a trap the *no matter* phrases do not: they can also head a noun clause, and then the comma disappears, because the clause is the subject or object of the main verb rather than a concession bolted onto it.

### however

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-EV-er*
**Русский:** как бы ни, насколько бы ни — союз, не наречие «однако»; however hard he tried
**Type:** subordinating (a different word from the conjunctive adverb *however*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal — "no matter how"
**Pattern:** *However* [adjective/adverb] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *However* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *However many/much* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *however* [adjective/adverb] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the whole *however* clause and **no comma after *however* itself**. That is the test that separates the two words: *However, he tried* is the adverb; *However hard he tried,* is the subordinator. The adverb also needs a semicolon or a period before it when it stands between two independent clauses; the subordinator never does.
**Variants:** *howsoever* (archaic or legal)

Means "no matter how," and it grabs the adjective or adverb it scopes over: *however hard*, *however carefully*, *however many*. Learners meet the conjunctive adverb *however* ("but") first and then misread this one; the giveaway is that the subordinator is never followed by a comma and always has a clause hanging off it.

1. *However* hard he tried, he could not get the door back on its hinges.
2. *However* you slice the numbers, the program loses money.
3. She answers every email *however* long it takes.
4. *However* many times I explain it, somebody asks again.
5. *However* carefully you pack them, a few glasses always break.

**See also:** [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [whatever](#whatever), [whichever](#whichever), [though](#though), [whenever](#whenever)

### howsomever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊsəmˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-sum-EV-er*
**Русский:** диал., устар. как бы ни; впрочем — просторечная форма however
**Type:** subordinating, and also a conjunctive adverb meaning "however" — it inherits both jobs from *however* &middot; **Register:** dialectal and archaic — nineteenth-century American rural speech and the local-color fiction that reproduced it, and still heard from older Appalachian and Ozark speakers. It is not standard in any written register.
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal — "no matter how"; also a sentence adverbial
**Pattern:** *Howsomever* [adjective/adverb] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *Howsomever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [statement]; *howsomever*, [statement]. (adverb)
**Punctuation:** As a subordinator, a comma after the whole clause and **no comma after *howsomever* itself** — the same test that separates the two *howevers*. As an adverb it needs a period or a semicolon in front of it between two independent clauses, and a comma after.
**Variants:** *however* (the standard form), *howsoever* (the formal and legal form), *howsumever*, *howsever*, *howsomdever* (other dialect spellings)

A folk extension of *howsoever*, and the extra syllable is exactly what marks it: *howsoever* turns up in a deed, *howsomever* turns up in a character's mouth. Americans meet it in dialect writing — Twain and the local-color novelists — and in the speech of older rural speakers, and dropping it into ordinary prose reads as either a mistake or a costume.

1. "*Howsomever*, I done it, and I warn't sorry for it afterwards, neither."
2. *Howsomever* you figure it, that fence is on our side of the line.
3. He never learned to read; *howsomever*, he could price a load of timber in his head.
4. *Howsomever* hard the winter came on, the stock got fed first.
5. "It ain't much of a road, *howsomever* it'll carry you to the river."

**See also:** [however](#however), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [whatever](#whatever), [howsoever](12-formal-archaic.md#howsoever), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### no matter how

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈhaʊ/ &middot; *no MAT-er HOW*
**Русский:** как бы ни, сколько бы ни — уступка по степени
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over degree
**Pattern:** *No matter how* [adjective/adverb] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *No matter how many/much* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter how* [adjective/adverb] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. When it trails, a comma before it is optional — include it if the clause lands as an afterthought.

The plain-English twin of subordinating *however*, and by far the more common of the two in American speech. It scopes over a degree — how hard, how long, how many — and says the main clause survives all of them.

1. *No matter how* hard I try, I can't parallel park.
2. The stain comes back *no matter how* many times we scrub it.
3. *No matter how* early we leave, we hit the same traffic.
4. He won't sell, *no matter how* much they offer.
5. *No matter how* carefully you read the contract, something is buried in there.

**See also:** [however](#however), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [no matter when](#no-matter-when), [whatever](#whatever)

### no matter that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ðət/ &middot; *no MAT-er that*
**Русский:** пусть даже, неважно, что — с оттенком пренебрежения к признанному факту
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal or literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** [main clause] — *no matter that* [clause]. &middot; *No matter that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Usually set off with a dash, a comma, or a sentence of its own, because it arrives as an afterthought rather than as a condition. Comma after it when fronted.

The one member of the *no matter* family that takes a plain statement instead of a question word, and it means "although," not "regardless of which." It comes with a dismissive shrug built in: the conceded fact is being brushed aside, not weighed.

1. He was introduced as the founder — *no matter that* he joined in year four.
2. *No matter that* the lease says otherwise, they've been parking there for years.
3. The story ran as fact, *no matter that* two sources had already retracted.
4. She was blamed for the delay, *no matter that* she had flagged the problem in April.
5. *No matter that* it was pouring, the coach kept them on the field.

**See also:** [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [although](#although), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [for all that](#for-all-that)

### no matter what

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈwʌt/ (also /ˈhwʌt/) &middot; *no MAT-er WUT*
**Русский:** что бы ни; в конце фразы — «во что бы то ни стало»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over things and outcomes
**Pattern:** *No matter what* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *No matter what* [verb phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *No matter what* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter what* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [main clause], *no matter what*. (fixed tag, nothing following)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; comma before the bare tag *no matter what* at the end of a sentence.
**Variants:** *no matter which* (when the choice comes from a known set — it has its own entry below)

Ranges over every possible thing or outcome. Unlike *whatever*, it can only be adverbial — you cannot make it the subject or object of anything — which is why *No matter what you decide, I'm with you* works and *No matter what you decide is fine* does not.

1. *No matter what* the inspector finds, we're closing on Friday.
2. I'll be at the airport, *no matter what*.
3. *No matter what* I cook, he asks whether there's pizza.
4. She was going to defend him *no matter what* the tape showed.
5. *No matter what* you've heard, the trail is open all winter.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [no matter which](#no-matter-which), [no matter who](#no-matter-who), [come what may](#come-what-may)

### no matter when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈwɛn/ (also /ˈhwɛn/) &middot; *no MAT-er WEN*
**Русский:** когда бы ни — время не имеет значения
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over time
**Pattern:** *No matter when* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.

Says the timing makes no difference. It competes with *whenever*, which is far more common in the plain time sense; a writer reaches for *no matter when* when the concession is the point and the schedule is not.

1. *No matter when* you call, somebody will pick up.
2. The bakery has a line out the door *no matter when* we go.
3. *No matter when* the funding clears, construction can't start before spring.
4. He shows up at the same table *no matter when* his shift ends.
5. *No matter when* they schedule it, half the department will be traveling.

**See also:** [whenever](#whenever), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [no matter where](#no-matter-where), [even as](#even-as)

### no matter where

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈwɛr/ (also /ˈhwɛr/) &middot; *no MAT-er WAIR*
**Русский:** где бы ни, куда бы ни — место не имеет значения
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over place
**Pattern:** *No matter where* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter where* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.

Says the place makes no difference. Against *wherever*, which can also simply name a place (*Put it wherever you like*), *no matter where* is only ever a concession.

1. *No matter where* you sit, you can hear the kitchen.
2. The dog finds the one sunny spot *no matter where* we move the couch.
3. *No matter where* the company relocates, she says she isn't moving.
4. Cell service drops out *no matter where* you stand in that building.
5. *No matter where* we've lived, the first thing we look for is a decent diner.

**See also:** [wherever](#wherever), [no matter when](#no-matter-when), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [where](#where)

### no matter which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈwɪtʃ/ (also /ˈhwɪtʃ/) &middot; *no MAT-er WICH*
**Русский:** какой бы ни, который бы ни — выбор из известного набора
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over a closed set
**Pattern:** *No matter which* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *No matter which* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter which* [noun] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *no matter what* (when the field is wide open rather than a known set)

*No matter what* ranges over everything; *no matter which* ranges over a list the speaker and the listener already have in front of them — two candidates, three routes, four plans. It stands to *whichever* exactly as *no matter what* stands to *whatever*: same meaning, but adverbial only, so it can never be the subject or object of the main verb.

1. *No matter which* route you take, you'll sit in traffic on the bridge.
2. The premium goes up *no matter which* plan we pick.
3. *No matter which* of the two finalists they hire, somebody on the committee is going to be unhappy.
4. She'll be up all night *no matter which* night they schedule it.
5. *No matter which* way you read that clause, it doesn't cover this.

**See also:** [whichever](#whichever), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [no matter who](#no-matter-who), [whatever](#whatever)

### no matter who

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈhu/ &middot; *no MAT-er HOO*
**Русский:** кто бы ни — только обстоятельство, в отличие от whoever
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over persons
**Pattern:** *No matter who* [verb phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *No matter who* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. (when *who* is the object inside its own clause) &middot; [main clause] *no matter who* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *no matter whom* (formal, and only when the pronoun is an object: *no matter whom they appoint*)

Ranges over every person. Like the rest of the family it is adverbial only, and that is the split from *whoever*: *Whoever wins gets the trophy* has *whoever* doing subject duty, while *No matter who wins, the trophy stays here* bolts a concession onto a sentence that was already complete.

1. *No matter who* wins in November, the deficit is still there in January.
2. She gives the same answer *no matter who* asks.
3. *No matter who* signed off on it, the design is wrong.
4. The rule applies *no matter who* you know.
5. *No matter who* they hire, that job is going to eat somebody alive.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [whichever](#whichever), [no matter why](#no-matter-why)

### no matter why

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈmæɾər ˈwaɪ/ (also /ˈhwaɪ/) &middot; *no MAT-er WY*
**Русский:** по какой бы причине ни — мотив не имеет значения
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over reasons
**Pattern:** *No matter why* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no matter why* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *whatever the reason* (the commoner everyday phrasing), *for whatever reason* (a noun-phrase adverbial, not a clause)

Says the reason makes no difference. It is the thinnest-used member of the family, for two reasons: English already has two shorter ways to say it — *whatever the reason*, *for whatever reason* — and *why* has no live *-ever* form in American English, so *no matter why* has no *whyever* pushing against it the way *whenever* pushes against *no matter when*. Reach for it when the sentence is refusing to litigate motive.

1. *No matter why* he left, the position is open now.
2. The late fee applies *no matter why* the payment missed the due date.
3. *No matter why* the numbers moved, the board is going to want an explanation.
4. They'll suspend your license *no matter why* you skipped the hearing.
5. *No matter why* you're calling, you'll be on hold for twenty minutes.

**See also:** [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [no matter who](#no-matter-who), [whatever](#whatever)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvər/ (also /wɑtˈɛvər/, and /hwʌtˈɛvər/ for speakers who keep the *wh-*) &middot; casually /wəˈɾɛvər/, with the *t* flapped &middot; *wut-EV-er*
**Русский:** что бы ни (уступка); всё, что (именное придаточное) — различает запятая
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also a determiner (*whatever excuse she gave*), a fused relative pronoun heading noun clauses (*whatever falls on the floor*), and — informally — a one-word dismissive interjection (*Whatever.*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal — also heads noun clauses
**Pattern:** *Whatever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *Whatever* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whatever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [verb] *whatever* [subject] [verb] (noun clause — no comma)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause when it is a concession at the front of a sentence. **No comma** when the *whatever* clause is the subject or object of the main verb — that is a noun clause, not an adverbial. *Whatever you decide is fine* takes no comma; *Whatever you decide, I'll back you* does.
**Variants:** *whatsoever* (dead as a subordinator; alive only as an emphatic tag after a negative: *no reason whatsoever*)

Two words in one shape. Concessive *whatever* means "no matter what" and can be lifted out without breaking the sentence; nominal *whatever* means "anything that" and cannot. The comma test above is the fastest way to tell them apart.

1. *Whatever* you do, don't touch the red switch.
2. He finds something to complain about *whatever* we order.
3. *Whatever* the reason, the shipment never left the warehouse.
4. *Whatever* excuse she gave, the landlord didn't buy it.
5. *Whatever* happens tonight, I'm proud of you.
6. The dog eats *whatever* falls on the floor. (noun clause — no comma, no concession)

**See also:** [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [whichever](#whichever), [however](#however), [whoever](#whoever)

### whenever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnˈɛvər/ (also /hwɛnˈɛvər/) &middot; *wen-EV-er*
**Русский:** когда бы ни; всякий раз, когда — чаще о повторе, реже уступка
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also the ordinary time subordinator meaning "every time" and a fused relative heading noun clauses (*Whenever you get here is fine*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over time — also of time proper
**Pattern:** *Whenever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whenever* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whenever*. (bare tag, nothing following: *Call me whenever*) &middot; *Whenever* [clause] [main-clause verb phrase]. (fused relative heading a noun clause — no comma: *Whenever you get here is fine*)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. No comma when it trails in the time sense; a comma is fine when the trailing clause is a genuine concession. **No comma** when the *whenever* clause is the subject or object of the main verb — that is a noun clause, not an adverbial.

Mostly a time word meaning "every time," but it turns concessive when the point is that the timing is irrelevant rather than repeated. *Whenever you get here is fine* is scheduling, *Whenever there's a potluck she brings the same casserole* is a pattern, and *Whenever that photo was taken, it wasn't 1962* is a concession.

1. *Whenever* the deal actually closes, the terms won't change.
2. Call me *whenever* — I'm up late anyway.
3. *Whenever* they schedule the hearing, we'll be ready.
4. She brings the same casserole *whenever* there's a potluck.
5. *Whenever* that photo was taken, it wasn't 1962.

**See also:** [no matter when](#no-matter-when), [wherever](#wherever), [whatever](#whatever), [when](#when)

### wherever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɛvər/ (also /hwɛrˈɛvər/) &middot; *wair-EV-er*
**Русский:** где бы ни, куда бы ни — в любом месте, и это неважно
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also the ordinary place subordinator (*Sit wherever you like*) and a fused relative heading noun clauses &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over place — also of place proper
**Pattern:** *Wherever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *wherever* [clause]. &middot; [verb/preposition] *wherever* [clause]. (fused relative heading a noun clause — no comma) &middot; *Wherever* [clause] [main-clause verb phrase]. (fused relative as subject — no comma)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted concessive clause; no comma when it trails. **No comma** when the *wherever* clause is the subject or object of the main verb — that is a noun clause naming a place, not a concession.
**Variants:** *wheresoever* (archaic or legal)

"In any place, and it makes no difference which." The concessive reading is the fronted one; trailing, it usually just names a place, and there the clause is an ordinary adverbial of place rather than a concession.

1. *Wherever* they end up, they'll make it work.
2. He takes that ridiculous pillow *wherever* he travels.
3. *Wherever* the money came from, it wasn't the general fund.
4. You can pick up the order *wherever* is closest to you. (fused relative naming a place — no comma, no concession)
5. *Wherever* you look in that town, somebody's cousin owns the building.

**See also:** [no matter where](#no-matter-where), [whenever](#whenever), [whatever](#whatever), [where](#where)

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃˈɛvər/ (also /hwɪtʃˈɛvər/) &middot; *wich-EV-er*
**Русский:** какой бы ни, который бы ни — из закрытого, известного набора
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also a determiner (*whichever route you take*) and a fused relative pronoun heading noun clauses (*Take whichever you like*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over a known set — also heads noun clauses
**Pattern:** *Whichever* [noun] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; *Whichever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. (as a bare pronoun, no noun following) &middot; [main clause] *whichever* [noun] [subject] [verb]. &middot; [verb] *whichever* [noun] [subject] [verb] (noun clause — no comma)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted concessive clause, and a comma before a trailing concessive one, which is supplementary: *The winner gets the corner office, whichever of them it turns out to be.* **No comma** when the *whichever* clause is the subject or object of the main verb — that is a noun clause, not a concession.
**Variants:** *whichsoever* (archaic or legal)

Like *whatever*, except that the alternatives are a closed, known set — two candidates, three routes, four plans. If the speaker has a list in mind, *whichever*; if the field is wide open, *whatever*.

1. *Whichever* route you take, you'll cross the same bridge.
2. The winner gets the corner office, *whichever* of them it turns out to be.
3. *Whichever* plan the board picks, somebody's department loses a position.
4. *Whichever* way the vote goes, this will be in court by spring.
5. Take *whichever* seat you like. (noun clause — no comma)

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [whoever](#whoever), [no matter what](#no-matter-what), [however](#however)

### whoever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-EV-er*
**Русский:** кто бы ни; тот, кто — падеж выбирается по роли внутри своего придаточного
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also a fused relative pronoun heading noun clauses (*Give it to whoever answers*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over persons — also heads noun clauses
**Pattern:** *Whoever* [verb phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Whoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause]. (when *whoever* is the object inside its own clause) &middot; [main clause] *whoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb/preposition] *whoever* [verb phrase] (noun clause — no comma)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted concessive clause; a trailing concessive clause normally goes uncommaed, since it names a case the main claim has to survive. **No comma** when the clause is the subject or object of the main verb: *Whoever left this here owes me a mug.*
**Variants:** *whomever* (formal, and only when the pronoun is the object inside its own clause), *whosoever* (archaic or legal)

"No matter which person." The case trap is real: pick *whoever* or *whomever* by the job the word does inside its own clause, not by what comes before it — *Give it to whoever answers* is correct, because *whoever* is the subject of *answers*, not the object of *to*.

1. *Whoever* took the last of the coffee, please start a new pot.
2. *Whoever* wins the primary, this district is going to be expensive.
3. They'll blame the front office *whoever* they end up hiring.
4. *Whoever* they assign to the case, it settles before trial.
5. *Whoever* signed for the package, it wasn't anybody in this office.
6. The prize goes to *whoever* finishes first. (noun clause — no comma, no concession)

## Formal and phrasal concessives

Multi-word chunks, learned whole. Most exist because a preposition — *despite*, *in spite of*, *regardless of*, *aside from*, *notwithstanding* — cannot take a clause on its own and needs *the fact that* bolted on to do it. Nearly every one can be cut to *although* or *except that*, and usually should be. The exception is the pair of *notwithstanding* override formulas that American contracts are built out of — *notwithstanding anything to the contrary* and *notwithstanding the foregoing* — which are terms of art, and cutting them changes what the document means.

### admitting that

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈmɪɾɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *ud-MIT-ing that*
**Русский:** книжн. признавая, что; пусть даже — уступка, которой до этого сопротивлялись
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (participial)
**Pattern:** *Admitting that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Even admitting that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause. It is fronted essentially always; trailing, it reads as a dangling participle.
**Variants:** *even admitting that*, *while admitting that*

Concedes a point for the sake of argument and then rejects the conclusion drawn from it. It belongs to debate and briefs rather than to conversation, and it sits right next to *granted that* — the difference is that *admitting that* implies the speaker had been resisting the point up to now.

1. *Admitting that* the sample was small, the direction of the result is still striking.
2. *Even admitting that* he was provoked, the response was out of all proportion.
3. *Admitting that* the deadline slipped, the team delivered more than was asked for.
4. *Admitting that* the neighborhood has changed, the zoning argument still doesn't follow.
5. *Even admitting that* costs have risen, a forty percent increase is hard to defend.

**See also:** [granted that](#granted-that), [granted](#granted), [for all that](#for-all-that), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### aside from the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsaɪd frəm ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *uh-SIDE frum thuh FAKT that*
**Русский:** не считая того, что; если не считать, что — исключение выносится вперёд
**Type:** subordinating; bare *aside from* is a preposition (*aside from the rent*) and cannot take a clause until *the fact that* is bolted on &middot; **Register:** neutral, and wordy
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of exception and contrast
**Pattern:** *Aside from the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *aside from the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *Apart from the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Aside from* [noun phrase], [main clause]. (preposition)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; usually no comma when it trails. Fronting is the normal position, because the exception has to be quarantined before the main clause can make its claim.
**Variants:** *apart from the fact that* (the same thing, a shade more bookish in American English), *other than the fact that*, *aside from* + noun phrase (a preposition, not a conjunction)

The exception-flavored member of the *the fact that* family. *Despite the fact that* concedes an obstacle and pushes past it; *aside from the fact that* sets one item off to the side and then rates everything else, which is why it so often carries a dry joke — the thing set aside is usually the thing that matters most. Against *except that* the split is position and timing: *except that* trails and takes something back from a claim already made, while *aside from the fact that* fronts and fences the exception off before the claim arrives.

1. *Aside from the fact that* the car has no brakes, it's in great shape.
2. The proposal is workable *aside from the fact that* nobody has budgeted for it.
3. *Apart from the fact that* she has never managed anyone, she'd be a fine choice.
4. He had no objection to the paint color *aside from the fact that* he had picked a different one.
5. *Aside from the fact that* it rained all week, the trip was everything we wanted.
6. *Aside from* the noise, the apartment is perfect. (preposition, not a conjunction)

**See also:** [except that](#except-that), [despite the fact that](#despite-the-fact-that), [save that](#save-that), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [not that](#not-that)

### conceding that

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsidɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *kun-SEE-ding that*
**Русский:** книжн. соглашаясь с тем, что — уступает то, что охотнее оставил бы себе
**Type:** subordinating (participial) &middot; **Register:** formal — briefs, editorials, and argument generally
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (participial)
**Pattern:** *Conceding that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *While conceding that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Even conceding that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause. It is fronted almost always, and the subject of the main clause has to be the one doing the conceding — otherwise the participle dangles.
**Variants:** *while conceding that*, *even conceding that*, *granting that*, *admitting that*

Puts the other side's point on the record and then denies that the point decides anything. Of the three participial concessives it is the most deliberate: *admitting that* suggests the speaker had been resisting, *granting that* treats the premise as merely arguable, and *conceding that* hands over something the speaker would rather have kept.

1. *Conceding that* the notice was late, counsel argued that no party had been prejudiced by the delay.
2. *While conceding that* enrollment has fallen, the superintendent opposed closing the school.
3. *Even conceding that* the first study was flawed, three later ones found the same effect.
4. *Conceding that* she had the votes, he still refused to bring the bill to the floor.
5. *Conceding that* the repairs were overdue, the landlord denied that the ceiling had ever leaked.

**See also:** [admitting that](#admitting-that), [granted that](#granted-that), [granted](#granted), [arguendo](#arguendo), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### despite the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈspaɪt ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *dih-SPYTE thuh FAKT that*
**Русский:** несмотря на то что — многословный вариант although, обычно лишний
**Type:** subordinating; bare *despite* is a preposition only (*despite the rain*) and can never take a clause &middot; **Register:** neutral, and wordy
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Despite the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *despite the fact that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; usually no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *despite* + noun phrase (a preposition, not a conjunction)

A five-word way of saying *although*, built only so that the preposition *despite* can take a clause — which it cannot do on its own. Every American style guide tells you to cut it, and they are right, though the length does make the conceded fact land as an established one.

1. *Despite the fact that* the road was closed, three cars were waved through.
2. The bill passed *despite the fact that* nobody had read it.
3. *Despite the fact that* she had never flown before, she slept the whole way.
4. They renewed his contract *despite the fact that* attendance was down a third straight year.
5. *Despite the fact that* the warranty had expired, the shop fixed it for free.

**See also:** [in spite of the fact that](#in-spite-of-the-fact-that), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [aside from the fact that](#aside-from-the-fact-that), [although](#although), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### for all

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈɔl/ &middot; *fer AWL*
**Русский:** при всём, несмотря на — с существительным: for all his money «при всех его деньгах»
**Type:** prepositional in form, concessive in force — it takes a noun phrase; *for all that* is the version that takes a clause &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Clause type:** none of its own — a concessive prepositional phrase standing in for a whole clause
**Pattern:** *For all* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for all* [noun phrase]. &middot; *For all* [possessive] [noun], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. Trailing, it normally goes uncommaed — *he never leaves the county for all his talk of traveling* — because there it is integrated into the claim rather than supplementary to it.
**Variants:** *for all that* (the clause-taking form, immediately below), *for all I know*, *for all I care*, *for all the good it did* (fixed idioms in which *for all* does take a clause), *despite*, *in spite of* (the neutral prepositions)

"In spite of," with a shrug in it. What separates it from plain *despite* is the implication that the thing named should have been enough and wasn't: *for all his money* says the money ought to have bought something and did not. It attaches most naturally to a possessive noun phrase, and that is the shape American speech keeps alive.

1. *For all* his money, he drives a fifteen-year-old pickup.
2. *For all* the noise the committee made, nothing in the budget changed.
3. The alarm system did nothing *for all* the money we spent on it.
4. *For all* her training, the first week on the floor nearly broke her.
5. *For all* the talk of a rebuild, they signed two more thirty-year-olds.
6. He might as well have stayed home, *for all* the good it did.

**See also:** [for all that](#for-all-that), [despite the fact that](#despite-the-fact-that), [in spite of the fact that](#in-spite-of-the-fact-that), [maugre](#maugre), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### for all that

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈɔl ˈðæt/ as the adverb, where *that* is a stressed demonstrative &middot; /fər ˈɔl ðət/ as the subordinator, where *that* is an unstressed complementizer &middot; *fer AWL THAT*
**Русский:** хотя, при том что; как наречие — «и всё же, тем не менее»
**Type:** subordinating; also a conjunctive adverb meaning "even so," and the bare *for all* is a preposition (*for all his money*) &middot; **Register:** formal or literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *For all that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [statement]. *For all that*, [statement]. (adverb) &middot; *For all* [noun phrase], [main clause]. (preposition)
**Punctuation:** As a subordinator, comma after the fronted clause. As an adverb meaning "even so," set it off with commas — and use a period or semicolon, never a comma alone, if it stands between two independent clauses.

Two related jobs. Followed by a clause it means "although": *For all that he complains, he never leaves.* Standing alone it means "nevertheless": *He's rude. For all that, he's the best we have.* The related *for all* plus a noun phrase — *for all his money* — is a preposition, not a conjunction at all.

1. *For all that* he grumbles about the drive, he has never once called out.
2. The house is drafty and the roof leaks. *For all that*, we love it.
3. *For all that* the reviews were brutal, the show sold out every night.
4. She was underprepared and, *for all that*, still the sharpest person in the room.
5. *For all that* the technology has changed, the job is the same one my father had.

**See also:** [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [no matter that](#no-matter-that), [albeit](#albeit), [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [granted](#granted)

### granted that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd ðət/ (also /ˈɡrænɪd/ with the *t* dropped) &middot; *GRAN-tid that*
**Русский:** допустим, что; пусть — уступает посылку, чтобы оспорить вывод
**Type:** subordinating; bare *granted* is a conjunctive adverb with its own entry below, and it is also the plain past participle of the verb *grant* (*the permit was granted*) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Granted that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Granting that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Granted* [clause], [main clause]. (with *that* dropped)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause. Always fronted.
**Variants:** *granting that*, *allowing that*, *granted* with the *that* dropped

Concedes a premise in order to attack the inference drawn from it — the arguer's move. *Granted that* treats the fact as settled, where *even if* would leave it open. Bare *granted*, used alone as a reply or a sentence opener, is the same word doing a much more common and much more casual job.

1. *Granted that* the numbers are accurate, they still don't support that conclusion.
2. *Granting that* she had the authority, the process was not followed.
3. *Granted that* the building is historic, it is also unsafe.
4. *Granting that* the two cases look alike, the statutes are completely different.
5. *Granted that* we were late, the ticket agent didn't have to be rude about it.

**See also:** [granted](#granted), [admitting that](#admitting-that), [even if](#even-if), [although](#although), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### in spite of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈspaɪt əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *in SPYTE uv thuh FAKT that*
**Русский:** несмотря на то что — самый длинный способ сказать although
**Type:** subordinating; bare *in spite of* is a preposition only (*in spite of the rain*) and can never take a clause; the noun *spite* inside it is frozen and carries none of its ordinary meaning &middot; **Register:** neutral, and wordy
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *In spite of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in spite of the fact that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; usually no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *in spite of* + noun phrase (a preposition, not a conjunction)

The longest way to say *although* that is still standard English. It differs from *despite the fact that* by one word and a shade of tone — *in spite of* carries a faint sense of defiance that *despite* does not. Both are cuttable, and in most sentences both should be cut.

1. *In spite of the fact that* the forecast called for snow, nobody salted the roads.
2. He kept the appointment *in spite of the fact that* he could barely stand up.
3. *In spite of the fact that* she had been passed over twice, she stayed another six years.
4. The exhibit opened on time *in spite of the fact that* half the crates arrived the night before.
5. *In spite of the fact that* everyone knew the answer, nobody said it out loud.

**See also:** [despite the fact that](#despite-the-fact-that), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [although](#although), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that)

### notwithstanding anything to the contrary

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ ˈɛniθɪŋ tə ðə ˈkɑntrɛri/ (also /ˌnɑtwɪð-/) &middot; *not-with-STAN-ding EN-ee-thing tuh thuh KON-trair-ee*
**Русский:** юр. несмотря ни на какие противоречащие положения — оговорка приоритета
**Type:** conjunctive adverb by function — a prepositional phrase that takes a noun phrase and never a clause &middot; **Register:** formal, legal — contract and statutory boilerplate, with no life at all outside drafted documents
**Clause type:** none — a fronted adverbial that overrides other provisions of the same instrument
**Pattern:** *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary* [in this Agreement / herein / contained herein], [main clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding anything in* [Section X] *to the contrary*, [main clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the whole phrase, always — the override has to close before the operative sentence begins. No commas inside it. It is not a conjunction and cannot join two clauses.
**Variants:** *notwithstanding anything herein to the contrary*, *notwithstanding anything contained herein to the contrary*, *notwithstanding any other provision of this Agreement*, *notwithstanding the foregoing* (narrower — it reaches only the text just above)

The override switch of American contract drafting: whatever else the document says, this sentence wins. Drafters and courts both treat it warily, because an agreement with four of them scattered through it has no readable order of priority — style guides tell you to name the provision you mean to override instead. Its reach is the whole instrument, which is what separates it from *notwithstanding the foregoing*.

1. *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in this Agreement*, Licensor's total liability shall not exceed the fees paid in the preceding twelve months.
2. *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary herein*, either party may seek injunctive relief in any court of competent jurisdiction.
3. *Notwithstanding anything in Section 8 to the contrary*, the confidentiality obligations survive termination.
4. *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary*, no provision of this Article shall be construed to waive a Tenant's statutory rights.
5. *Notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in the Plan*, no award may be repriced without shareholder approval.

**See also:** [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [notwithstanding the foregoing](#notwithstanding-the-foregoing), [contrary to](#contrary-to), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [provided always that](12-formal-archaic.md#provided-always-that)

### notwithstanding that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ ðət/ (also /ˌnɑtwɪðˈstændɪŋ/) &middot; *not-with-STAN-ding that*
**Русский:** юр. несмотря на то что, хотя — язык договоров и судебных решений
**Type:** subordinating; the same word is also a preposition that can stand either before its object (*notwithstanding the delay*) or after it (*the delay notwithstanding*), and a conjunctive adverb in its own right &middot; **Register:** formal, chiefly legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Notwithstanding that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *notwithstanding that* [clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding* [noun phrase], [main clause]. (preposition) &middot; [main clause], [noun phrase] *notwithstanding*. (postposed preposition)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it trails. As a preposition it takes no comma at all — *notwithstanding the delay* — and neither does the postposed form, *the delay notwithstanding*.
**Variants:** *notwithstanding the fact that*, *notwithstanding* + noun phrase (preposition)

"Although," in the register of contracts and court opinions. Its oddity is that the prepositional form can sit before its object or after it: *notwithstanding any provision to the contrary* and *any provision to the contrary notwithstanding* are both standard, and the postposed version is a legal signature.

1. *Notwithstanding that* the lease had expired, the tenant remained in possession.
2. The board approved the transfer *notwithstanding that* two directors objected.
3. *Notwithstanding that* he had signed the waiver, the court allowed the claim.
4. *Notwithstanding the fact that* the deadline had passed, the agency accepted the filing.
5. The contract stands, the earlier memorandum *notwithstanding*.

**See also:** [despite the fact that](#despite-the-fact-that), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [albeit](#albeit), [no matter that](#no-matter-that)

### notwithstanding the foregoing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ ðə fɔrˈɡoʊɪŋ/ (also /ˌnɑtwɪð-/) &middot; *not-with-STAN-ding thuh for-GO-ing*
**Русский:** юр. несмотря на вышеизложенное — исключение из предыдущего пункта
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) — an anaphoric adverbial pointing back at the text just read &middot; **Register:** formal, legal — contracts, bylaws, board resolutions, and statutes; in ordinary prose it reads as a parody of a lawyer
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial carving an exception out of the provision immediately above it
**Pattern:** *Notwithstanding the foregoing*, [main clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding the foregoing* [sentence/Section/subsection], [main clause]. &middot; *The foregoing notwithstanding*, [main clause]. (postposed)
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, and a period or a semicolon in front of it when what precedes is a complete sentence — it is an adverbial, not a conjunction, so a comma alone there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *the foregoing notwithstanding* (postposed), *notwithstanding the provisions of the preceding sentence*, *notwithstanding anything to the contrary* (broader — it reaches the entire document)

The drafting equivalent of "except for what I am about to say." It carves an exception out of the provision the reader has just finished, and its scope is exactly that — unlike *notwithstanding anything to the contrary*, it does not claim the whole agreement, though drafters argue endlessly about how far back "the foregoing" runs, which is why careful ones name the section instead.

1. *Notwithstanding the foregoing*, the Company may terminate this Agreement immediately upon the Executive's conviction of a felony.
2. Subscriptions renew automatically. *Notwithstanding the foregoing*, a subscriber may cancel within thirty days of renewal for a full refund.
3. *Notwithstanding the foregoing sentence*, no notice is required in the event of an emergency repair.
4. *The foregoing notwithstanding*, the Board retains discretion to accelerate vesting.
5. *Notwithstanding the foregoing*, nothing in this Section limits either party's indemnification obligations.

**See also:** [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [notwithstanding anything to the contrary](#notwithstanding-anything-to-the-contrary), [regardless of the fact that](#regardless-of-the-fact-that), [save that](#save-that), [provided always that](12-formal-archaic.md#provided-always-that)

### regardless of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈɡɑrdləs əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *rih-GARD-luss uv thuh FAKT that*
**Русский:** невзирая на то что — не взвешивает факт, а отметает его
**Type:** subordinating; bare *regardless* is a conjunctive adverb (*Regardless, they went ahead*) and *regardless of* is a preposition (*regardless of the weather*) &middot; **Register:** formal, and wordy
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Regardless of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *regardless of the fact that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; usually no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *irrespective of the fact that* (more formal). *Irregardless* is nonstandard — avoid it.

Concedes a known fact and dismisses it as beside the point. It runs a shade colder than *despite the fact that*: *despite* acknowledges the obstacle, while *regardless* refuses to weigh it at all. Cut it to *although* or *even though* unless that dismissiveness is exactly what you want heard.

1. *Regardless of the fact that* the permit had been denied, construction continued.
2. They billed us for the full month *regardless of the fact that* we moved out on the ninth.
3. *Regardless of the fact that* she was the only qualified applicant, the search was reopened.
4. He repeated the claim *regardless of the fact that* it had been corrected twice on air.
5. *Regardless of the fact that* the ballot was printed wrong, the results were certified.

**See also:** [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [despite the fact that](#despite-the-fact-that), [in spite of the fact that](#in-spite-of-the-fact-that), [no matter that](#no-matter-that)

## Compact and fixed-phrase concessives

Single words and frozen formulas. Most of them — *all the same*, *anyhow*, *at that*, *be that as it may*, *even then*, *granted*, *howbeit*, *mind you*, *natheless*, *no doubt*, *not for nothing*, *still and all*, *to be sure* — are conjunctive adverbs rather than conjunctions, so when they stand between two independent clauses they need a period or a semicolon in front of them; a comma there is a comma splice. *Albeit* is a genuine subordinator but takes a phrase, not a clause, and *maugre* takes a noun phrase only. The archaic end of the shelf — *be it ever so*, *maugre*, *natheless*, *not but that*, *what though* — is period reading, not modern usage.

### albeit

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈbiɪt/ (also /ælˈbiɪt/) &middot; *awl-BEE-it*
**Русский:** хотя и, пусть и — только с оборотом, не с целым придаточным: albeit reluctantly
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, usually verbless
**Pattern:** [main clause], *albeit* [adjective/adverb/noun phrase/prepositional phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *albeit* [determiner] [adjective] [noun]. &middot; [main clause], *albeit* [adverb], [rest of the sentence].
**Punctuation:** Always a comma before *albeit*, and commas on both sides when it interrupts. It is not a coordinator, so it cannot join two full independent clauses across a comma — if a subject and a tensed verb follow, use *although*.

A fossil of *all be it* ("although it be"), and it still behaves like one: it takes a phrase rather than a full clause, and it concedes a qualification rather than a fact. *He agreed, albeit reluctantly* is right; *He agreed, albeit he was reluctant* is the classic overreach, and *although* is the fix.

1. The meeting produced a decision, *albeit* a temporary one.
2. She agreed to testify, *albeit* under protest.
3. Sales recovered, *albeit* slowly, over the following two quarters.
4. It's a real solution, *albeit* an expensive one.
5. He offered an apology, *albeit* one that blamed everybody else.

**See also:** [though](#though), [if](#if), [granted](#granted), [notwithstanding that](#notwithstanding-that), [although](#although)

### all the same

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ðə ˈseɪm/ &middot; *awl thuh SAME*
**Русский:** всё равно, всё-таки, тем не менее — разговорный аналог nevertheless
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial pointing back at a concession already made
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *All the same*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *all the same*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *all the same*. &middot; [Independent clause], *all the same*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position. At the end of a sentence it takes a comma when it lands as an afterthought and none when it is fused to the verb phrase (*she went all the same*). Between two independent clauses it needs a period or a semicolon — a comma alone is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *just the same* (identical in meaning, a shade more casual), *all the same to me/you* (a different idiom altogether — indifference, not concession)

"That changes nothing." It points backward at a concession the speaker has just granted and refuses to let it govern, which puts it next to *nevertheless* — the difference is tone, since *all the same* is what people say and *nevertheless* is what they write. Keep it apart from the frozen *it's all the same to me*, where the words mean no preference rather than no surrender.

1. The reviews were brutal. *All the same*, the theater was packed every night.
2. He apologized, and I appreciated it; *all the same*, I'm not lending him the truck again.
3. She knew the trail was closed. She went *all the same*.
4. *All the same*, I'd feel better if somebody called ahead.
5. The rent was more than we wanted to pay. We took the place, *just the same*.
6. It's a long drive for one night; *just the same*, I'm glad we went.

**See also:** [be that as it may](#be-that-as-it-may), [granted](#granted), [still and all](#still-and-all), [anyhow](#anyhow), [nevertheless](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#nevertheless)

### anyhow

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛnihaʊ/ &middot; *EN-ee-how*
**Русский:** разг. всё равно, так или иначе — форма anyways нелитературна
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** informal for *anyhow*; *anyways* is nonstandard — casual regional American speech, and a red flag in writing
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial dismissing what was just granted
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Anyhow*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *anyhow*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *anyhow*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and **no** comma before it in final position (*we went anyhow*). A period or a semicolon in front of it when it opens a new independent clause; a comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *anyway* (the standard form, with a fuller entry among the conjunctive adverbs), *anyways* (nonstandard; common in casual speech across the South and Midwest, never in edited prose), *any how* (not a word — always one word)

The concessive job of the *anyway* family: the fact just granted is real, and the main clause happened regardless. *Anyhow* is fully standard and a touch more casual than *anyway*; *anyways* is the dialectal form carrying the old adverbial *-s*, which an American hears constantly and should write only in dialogue. All three also do discourse work — returning to a dropped thread, closing a phone call — and that sense belongs with *anyway*.

1. The forecast said rain all day. We drove up *anyhow*.
2. Nobody asked my opinion; *anyhow*, here it is.
3. *Anyhow*, the point is that the deposit never cleared.
4. "It's probably too late to call." "Call him *anyhow*."
5. The store was closing in ten minutes, but they let us in *anyhow*.
6. He told her the lease forbade dogs. She got the dog *anyways*. (nonstandard — casual speech only)

**See also:** [anyway](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#anyway), [all the same](#all-the-same), [still and all](#still-and-all), [even then](#even-then), [at any rate](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#at-any-rate)

### at that

**Pronunciation:** /ət ˈðæt/ — *at* reduces to /ət/ inside the phrase &middot; *ut THAT*
**Русский:** и при этом, к тому же; в устар. уступительном смысле — «и всё-таки, вопреки всему»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal, clause-final) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; the concessive sense is dated and now mostly regional, while the additive sense is everywhere
**Clause type:** none — a clause-final tag
**Pattern:** [Independent clause] *at that*. &middot; [Independent clause], *at that*. &middot; [noun phrase], *and* [adjective] *one at that*. (the additive sense)
**Punctuation:** It sits at the very end of its clause. A comma in front of it is normal when it arrives as an afterthought and optional when it is fused to what precedes. It is not a conjunction and can never join two clauses.
**Variants:** *at that* in the additive sense ("and moreover": *a long drive, and a boring one at that*), *let it go at that*, *leave it at that* (fixed idioms, not connectors)

Two senses share the shape, and only the surrounding sentence sorts them out. The common one is additive — *a used car, and an old one at that* — which belongs with *moreover*. The concessive one, "in spite of all that, contrary to what you'd expect," is older and now much rarer: *he might win at that* means the speaker has just talked themselves out of their own objection. Americans meet that sense mostly in older fiction and in regional speech.

1. He may be right *at that*. (concessive — "in spite of what I just said")
2. I laughed when he suggested walking, and then we walked, and it wasn't bad *at that*.
3. "Nobody will notice." "They might, *at that*."
4. The old wiring came through the storm *at that*, which is more than the new panel did.
5. It was a two-hour meeting, and a pointless one *at that*. (the common additive sense — "and moreover")
6. We agreed to leave it *at that*. (fixed idiom, not a connector)

**See also:** [even then](#even-then), [all the same](#all-the-same), [still and all](#still-and-all), [though](#though), [moreover](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#moreover)

### be it ever so

**Pronunciation:** /bi ɪt ˈɛvər soʊ/ &middot; *bee it EV-er soh*
**Русский:** устар. как бы ни был — be it ever so humble «пусть даже самый убогий»
**Type:** subordinating — a frozen subjunctive with inversion &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary — hymns, proverbs, nineteenth-century prose, and one song lyric every American knows
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession, universal over degree — "no matter how"
**Pattern:** *Be it ever so* [adjective], [main clause]. &middot; *Be* [subject] *ever so* [adjective], [main clause]. &middot; *Be* [subject] *ne'er so* [adjective], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause, which is always fronted. It is a clause and not an adverbial, so the semicolon question never arises.
**Variants:** *be it never so* (the older form), *be he ne'er so*, *be she ne'er so* (the subject changes, and *ne'er* stands for *never*), *however* + adjective, *no matter how* (the modern forms)

The subjunctive *be* thrown to the front of its clause, exactly as in *come what may*, conceding every degree of the adjective at once: *be it ever so humble* means "no matter how humble it is." Modern English says *however humble it is*. What keeps the formula alive for Americans is the song — *be it ever so humble, there's no place like home* — and beyond that it belongs to Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and the hymnal.

1. "*Be it ever so* humble, there's no place like home." (John Howard Payne, "Home, Sweet Home," 1823)
2. *Be it ever so* slight, an error in the survey has to be corrected before the deed is recorded.
3. *Be he ne'er so* poor, no man was turned away from that door.
4. *Be she ne'er so* fair, she has not a penny to her name.
5. "*Be it never so* true, it were not fit to be spoken here."
6. *Be it ever so* cold, the mill opened at six.

**See also:** [come what may](#come-what-may), [however](#however), [no matter how](#no-matter-how), [howsomever](#howsomever), [what though](#what-though)

### be that as it may

**Pronunciation:** /bi ˈðæt əz ɪt ˈmeɪ/ &middot; *bee THAT az it MAY*
**Русский:** как бы то ни было, при всём том — отметает всё сказанное ранее
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial, not a clause connector
**Pattern:** [statement]. *Be that as it may*, [statement]. &middot; [statement]; *be that as it may*, [statement].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it. Because it is an adverb and not a conjunction, it **cannot** join two independent clauses with a comma alone — put a period or a semicolon in front of it, exactly as you would with *however* or *nevertheless*.

A frozen subjunctive meaning "granting all of that." It concedes everything the other side has just said and then sets the whole pile aside, which makes it a favorite of judges, editors, and people trying to end an argument. It always points backward, at something already stated.

1. The costs are real. *Be that as it may*, the project is going forward.
2. He may well have been provoked; *be that as it may*, he threw the first punch.
3. "The forecast says clear skies." "*Be that as it may*, I'm bringing a jacket."
4. The data are three years old. *Be that as it may*, nothing since then contradicts them.
5. She never signed the form; *be that as it may*, everyone treated her as a member.

**See also:** [for all that](#for-all-that), [granted](#granted), [come what may](#come-what-may), [howbeit](#howbeit)

### believe it or not

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˌliv ɪt ər ˈnɑt/ &middot; casually /bəˌliv ɪɾ ər ˈnɑt/, with the *t* of *it* flapped &middot; *bih-leev-it-er-NOT*
**Русский:** хотите верьте, хотите нет — предупреждение о неожиданном факте
**Type:** subordinating — a frozen concessive-conditional clause, built exactly like *like it or not* &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition (frozen imperative plus alternative)
**Pattern:** *Believe it or not*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *believe it or not*. &middot; [main clause] — *believe it or not* — [continuation]. &middot; *Whether you believe it or not*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Always set off by a comma, at the front, at the end, or on both sides when it interrupts. It is an adverbial, not a conjunction: *Believe it or not, the check cleared, the bank called to confirm* is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *whether you believe it or not* (the full form, and blunter), *if you can believe it*, *believe you me* (a different idiom — emphasis, not concession)

A *whether* clause with the *whether* dropped and the verb left standing in the imperative, exactly like *like it or not*. It runs through both branches — you believe it, you don't — and reports that the main clause holds either way, which in practice makes it a flag on a surprising fact more than a real concession. *Like it or not* warns; *believe it or not* invites.

1. *Believe it or not*, the permit came through in nine days.
2. He had never seen the ocean before that trip, *believe it or not*.
3. *Believe it or not*, that's the third time this month the main has broken.
4. The cheapest flight was the nonstop — *believe it or not* — and it left at a civilized hour.
5. *Whether you believe it or not*, I never got the invoice.
6. She reads every word of the terms of service, *believe it or not*.

### come what may

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌm wʌt ˈmeɪ/ &middot; *kum wut MAY*
**Русский:** будь что будет, что бы ни случилось — звучит как обещание
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal or literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition (frozen, with inversion)
**Pattern:** *Come what may*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *come what may*.
**Punctuation:** Always set off by a comma, at the front or at the end.

"No matter what happens" — another frozen subjunctive, this one with the verb thrown to the front. It looks forward, where *be that as it may* looks back, and it reads as a vow rather than as an argument.

1. *Come what may*, the wedding is happening on the fourteenth.
2. She promised to see the project through, *come what may*.
3. *Come what may*, this family has dinner together on Sundays.
4. He had made up his mind to enlist, *come what may*.
5. *Come what may*, the doors open at seven and the curtain goes up at eight.

### even then

**Pronunciation:** /ˌivən ˈðɛn/ &middot; *ee-vun THEN*
**Русский:** даже тогда, и всё равно — отсылает к уже названному условию или моменту
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial pointing back at a condition or a moment already named
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Even then*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *even then*, [independent clause]. &middot; [conditional clause], *and even then* [main clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *even then*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position. Between two independent clauses it takes a period or a semicolon, never a bare comma. Riding behind *and* it needs no punctuation of its own: *if the parts arrive Monday, and even then it's tight*.
**Variants:** *even so* (which points back at a granted fact rather than a hypothetical), *and even then* (the commonest shape in speech), *even at that*

The adverb that finishes what *even if* starts: the *if* clause names the best case and *even then* says the main claim survives it. It is anaphoric — it has to point back at a condition or a time already on the table — and that is the split from *even so*, which points back at a fact. It also does plain temporal work (*he was nine, and even then he could name every state capital*), and only context sorts the two.

1. We might get the permit by June. *Even then*, nothing gets poured before August.
2. You'd have to sell both cars, and *even then* you'd come up short.
3. The drug helps maybe a third of patients; *even then*, the effect is modest.
4. Assume every witness is telling the truth. *Even then*, the timeline doesn't work.
5. They may reopen the road tonight. *Even then*, I'd take the long way around.
6. He was nine when they moved here, and *even then* he could name every state capital. (temporal — "as early as that")

### granted

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd/ (also /ˈɡrænɪd/ with the *t* dropped) &middot; *GRAN-tid*
**Русский:** согласен, спору нет, положим — разговорная уступка перед возражением
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — a supplementary adverbial, often a whole turn by itself
**Pattern:** *Granted*, [statement]. &middot; [statement], *granted*, [continuation]. &middot; [independent clause]; *granted*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause] — *granted* — [continuation]. &middot; "*Granted.*" (as a complete reply)
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a sentence, commas on both sides when it interrupts. Because it is an adverb and not a conjunction, it cannot join two independent clauses with a comma alone: put a period or a semicolon in front of it, exactly as you would with *however* or *nevertheless*. *It's expensive, granted, it's also the only one that fits* is a comma splice; *It's expensive; granted, it's also the only one that fits* is not.

Concedes the other person's point, usually so that the speaker can turn around and qualify it. In American conversation it is the standard one-word way of saying "fair enough, but." The subordinating *granted that* is the same word doing a heavier job.

1. *Granted*, the first hour is slow, but the last twenty minutes are worth it.
2. "It's expensive." "*Granted.* It's also the only one that fits."
3. The house needs work, *granted*, though the lot alone is worth the price.
4. *Granted*, I've only driven it once, and I already want one.
5. She's the youngest person on the staff — *granted* — and she's also the only one who has shipped anything.
6. It's a long drive; *granted*, the last twenty miles make up for it.

### howbeit

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈbiɪt/ &middot; *how-BEE-it*
**Русский:** устар. тем не менее, впрочем — предок albeit, из Библии короля Иакова
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (formerly also subordinating) &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** none in modern use — a sentence adverbial meaning "nevertheless"
**Pattern:** [statement]; *howbeit*, [statement]. &middot; *Howbeit* [clause], [main clause]. (obsolete subordinating use)
**Punctuation:** As with any conjunctive adverb, a semicolon or a period in front of it and a comma after. The obsolete subordinating form takes a comma after its clause.
**Variants:** *howbe*, *howsobeit*

The dead ancestor of *albeit*, built the same way out of *how be it*. It survives in the King James Bible, in deliberate period pastiche, and in the occasional antique-sounding legal opinion; no living American register uses it. Read it as *nevertheless*.

1. "*Howbeit* we speak wisdom among them that are perfect." (1 Corinthians 2:6, King James Version)
2. The general was warned; *howbeit*, he pressed the attack at dawn.
3. *Howbeit* the terms were harsh, the town accepted them.
4. She had every right to refuse; *howbeit*, she signed.
5. *Howbeit*, the matter was never spoken of again.

### like it or not

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlaɪk ɪt ər ˈnɑt/ &middot; casually /ˌlaɪk ɪɾ ər ˈnɑt/, with the *t* of *it* flapped &middot; *lyke-it-er-NOT*
**Русский:** хочешь не хочешь, нравится это или нет — чаще предупреждение, чем уступка
**Type:** subordinating — a frozen concessive-conditional clause; the bare word *like* is also a preposition (*a house like ours*), a verb (*I like it*), a noun (*likes and dislikes*), an informal subordinator of manner (*like I said*), and an informal quotative (*she was like, no*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal; the full *whether you like it or not* is blunter still
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition (frozen, imperative plus alternative)
**Pattern:** *Like it or not*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *like it or not*. &middot; *Like* [noun phrase] *or not*, [main clause]. &middot; *Whether* [subject] *like(s) it or not*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Always set off by a comma, at the front or at the end. It is an adverbial, not a conjunction that joins clauses, so it cannot splice two independent clauses together: *We're going, like it or not, the tickets are bought* is a comma splice — put a period or a semicolon after *not*.
**Variants:** *whether you like it or not* (the full form), *ready or not*, *want to or not*

The spoken cousin of *whether or not*: a *whether* clause with the *whether* dropped and the verb left standing in the imperative. It runs through both branches — you like it, you don't — and reports that the main clause holds either way. More often a warning than a concession, which is why it lands hardest at the front of a sentence, and why it so rarely appears in writing that is trying to be polite.

1. *Like it or not*, the deadline moved up to the fifteenth.
2. We're all on the new system Monday, *like it or not*.
3. *Like it or not*, that stretch of highway is the only way in.
4. She's the deciding vote, *like it or not*.
5. *Whether you like it or not*, the two of you are going to have to share an office.
6. *Ready or not*, the doors open at nine.

### maugre

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɔɡər/ &middot; *MAW-gur*
**Русский:** устар. вопреки, несмотря на — предлог, вымерший в XVII веке
**Type:** prepositional in form, concessive in force — it takes a noun phrase and never a clause &middot; **Register:** archaic — Middle and Early Modern English; Chaucer, Spenser, Shakespeare, and Sir Walter Scott, and nothing written since
**Clause type:** none — a concessive prepositional phrase
**Pattern:** *Maugre* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *maugre* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Maugre* [possessive] [noun], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase and none when it trails — the rules that govern *despite*. It cannot join two clauses.
**Variants:** *mauger*, *malgre*, *maulgre* (older spellings — the word was never settled), *in spite of*, *despite* (the modern forms), *malgré* (the living French word it came from)

Old French *maugré*, "ill will": to do a thing *maugre* someone was to do it against their spite, which is where the modern phrase *in spite of* gets its noun. It dropped out of English in the seventeenth century and survives only in period text and deliberate antiquarian writing. An American meets it in *Twelfth Night* and in Scott's novels, and nowhere else.

1. "I love thee so that, *maugre* all thy pride, / Nor wit nor reason can my passion hide." (*Twelfth Night*, III.i)
2. "*Maugre* thy strength, place, youth, and eminence, … thou art a traitor." (*King Lear*, V.iii)
3. The abbey held its lands *maugre* the bishop's decree.
4. *Maugre* every warning, the young laird rode north at first light.
5. He kept his seat at the table, *maugre* the whispering of the whole company.
6. *Maugre* wind and tide, the packet made harbor before dark.

### mind you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmaɪnd ju/ &middot; casually /ˈmaɪn jə/ &middot; *MYND yoo*
**Русский:** разг. заметьте, имейте в виду — оговорка к только что сказанному
**Type:** conjunctive adverb by function — a frozen imperative used as a parenthetical connector &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken; more British and Irish than American, but current in American conversation and in dialogue
**Clause type:** none — a parenthetical adverbial hanging a qualification on what was just said
**Pattern:** [Statement], *mind you*, [continuation]. &middot; [Statement] — *mind you*, [statement]. &middot; *Mind you*, [statement]. &middot; [Statement], *mind you*.
**Punctuation:** Always fenced with commas, dashes, or parentheses. Because it is an adverbial and not a conjunction, it cannot hold two independent clauses together: *He paid, mind you, he took his time about it* is a comma splice — write *He paid — mind you, he took his time about it.*
**Variants:** *mind*, *mind you now* (older and more regional), *note that* (the formal equivalent), *granted*, *to be fair* (the American near-equivalents)

Flags the next thing — or the thing just said — as a qualification the listener had better not overlook: "I'm granting this, so don't hold it against me later." It is the spoken hedge that keeps a compliment from being total or a complaint from being unfair. In American English it carries a faint British accent; *granted* and *to be fair* do the same work without one.

1. He's the best hitter on the team. He can't field, *mind you*.
2. *Mind you*, I only heard this secondhand.
3. The place was spotless — *mind you*, they'd had a week's notice we were coming.
4. She never complained, *mind you*, but you could see what the shifts were costing her.
5. I'm not saying he's wrong. *Mind you*, I'm not saying he's right either.
6. It's a good price. *Mind you*, you'll spend it twice over on repairs.

### natheless

**Pronunciation:** /ˈneɪθləs/ (also /ˈnæθləs/) &middot; *NAYTH-luss*
**Русский:** устар., поэт. тем не менее — предок nevertheless, у Мильтона и романтиков
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic — Spenser, Milton, Keats, and nineteenth-century verse; dead in every modern register
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial meaning "nevertheless"
**Pattern:** [Statement]; *natheless*, [statement]. &middot; *Natheless*, [statement]. &middot; [statement] *natheless* [continuation]. (bare inside a line of verse)
**Punctuation:** Modern editing gives it a semicolon or a period in front and a comma after, like any conjunctive adverb; the poets who used it often left it unpunctuated inside the line.
**Variants:** *nathless*, *natheles*, *nathelesse* (the spelling was never fixed), *ne'ertheless*, *nevertheless* (the surviving form)

The ancestor of *nevertheless*, from Old English *na þe læs*, "not the less." It had fallen out of ordinary use by the seventeenth century and survived after that as a poetic archaism — Milton reached for it in *Paradise Lost*, and the Romantics reached for it because Milton had. An American meets it in a poetry anthology; in prose it reads as pastiche.

1. "*Nathless* he so endur'd, till on the beach / Of that inflamed sea he stood." (Milton, *Paradise Lost*, I)
2. The road was washed out; *natheless* the mail came through.
3. *Natheless*, the lady kept her counsel and said nothing to the court.
4. He was warned of the ford; *natheless* he rode into it.
5. *Natheless* she loved him, and would not hear a word said against him.

### no doubt

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈdaʊt/ &middot; *no DOWT*
**Русский:** спору нет, конечно — уступка, за которой почти всегда прячется «но»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) — a concessive disjunct &middot; **Register:** neutral; *doubtless* is more literary
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial conceding a point before the objection arrives
**Pattern:** *No doubt* [independent clause], *but* [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] *no doubt* [verb phrase], *but* [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *no doubt*. &middot; *Doubtless* [independent clause], *but* [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** A fronted *no doubt* needs no comma after it — it leans on the clause it opens — though one is fine when the phrase is meant to stand apart. Comma before it at the end of a sentence. It is not a conjunction, so keep the *but* when you are joining two clauses.
**Variants:** *doubtless* (compressed, and more literary), *doubtlessly* (widely disliked — write *doubtless*), *undoubtedly* (emphatic rather than concessive: it agrees instead of conceding), *no doubt about it* (a fixed emphatic tag)

Concedes something in advance, and the concession is usually thin: *no doubt she meant well* has a *but* waiting behind it almost every time, which makes the phrase a polite way of not really conceding at all. That ironic edge is the whole difference from *undoubtedly*, where the speaker actually agrees. *Doubtless* does the same job a register higher.

1. *No doubt* the intentions were good, but the rollout was a disaster.
2. She'll have a reason, *no doubt*.
3. *No doubt* you've heard a different version of this story.
4. *Doubtless* the committee has its reasons; it has not shared them with the faculty.
5. The contractor was *no doubt* busy, which does not explain three unreturned calls.
6. *Doubtless* he meant it kindly, and it landed badly all the same.

### not but that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt bət ˈðæt/ &middot; *not but THAT*
**Русский:** устар. хотя, конечно же — «не то чтобы не»; современная форма — not that
**Type:** subordinating — a frozen stacked negative &middot; **Register:** archaic and formal for *not but that*; *not but what* is dialectal and nonstandard — regional American and British speech, never edited prose
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (parenthetical disclaimer)
**Pattern:** [statement], *not but that* [clause]. &middot; [statement]. *Not but that* [clause]. &middot; [statement], *not but what* [clause]. (nonstandard)
**Punctuation:** A comma, a dash, or a period in front of it — it always arrives as an afterthought. Never run it onto the previous clause with no punctuation at all.
**Variants:** *not but what* (dialectal), *not but* (the bare older form), *not that* (the living modern form)

The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century ancestor of *not that*: a stacked negative meaning "it is not that … not," which comes out as "though of course." *She never spoke of the money, not but that she needed it* says she needed it badly. Americans meet it in Austen, Dickens, and Trollope; *not but what* is the same phrase in country speech, and modern writing has handed the whole job to *not that*.

1. She never spoke of the money, *not but that* she needed it.
2. He declined the invitation — *not but that* he was flattered to be asked.
3. I have no quarrel with the arrangement, *not but that* I should have preferred to be consulted.
4. "He's a decent enough landlord, *not but what* the roof's wanted fixing these ten years." (dialectal)
5. The family said nothing at the time, *not but that* the whole village knew.

### not for nothing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt fər ˈnʌθɪŋ/ &middot; casually /ˌnɑt fɚ ˈnʌθɪn/ &middot; *not fer NUTH-ing*
**Русский:** разг. не в обиду будь сказано (сев.-вост. США); устар. недаром, не зря
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) in the concessive sense; the older sense inverts the clause behind it &middot; **Register:** informal and regional — the concessive opener is a New York, New Jersey, and Boston signature, familiar nationally from movies and television
**Clause type:** none — a discourse-initial adverbial prefacing an objection
**Pattern:** *Not for nothing*, *but* [independent clause]. &middot; *Not for nothing*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Not for nothing* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb phrase]. (the older inverted sense)
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, and the *but* that usually follows keeps its ordinary place. It cannot join two independent clauses on its own.
**Variants:** *no offense, but*, *with all due respect* (the same softener without the regional accent), *not for nothing did* + subject (the older "for good reason" sense), *not for nothing is it called* …

Two senses that pull in opposite directions. The older one means "for good reason" and inverts the clause after it: *not for nothing is that stretch called the worst merge in the state*. The newer one — the one an American actually hears — is a Northeastern discourse opener that softens an objection just before it lands, exactly like *no offense, but*: it announces that the speaker knows the next sentence may sting.

1. *Not for nothing*, but the last two times we tried this, the server went down.
2. *Not for nothing*, but you're the one who picked the restaurant.
3. "*Not for nothing*, Counselor, your client's story has changed twice."
4. *Not for nothing*, I've been doing this twenty years and I've never seen a rider like that.
5. *Not for nothing* is that stretch of I-95 called the worst merge in the state. (the older sense — "for good reason")
6. *Not for nothing* did the neighbors start calling the block "the canyon." (older sense, inverted)

### not that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɑt ðæt/ &middot; unstressed /ˈnɑt ðət/ &middot; *NOT that*
**Русский:** не то чтобы — отводит вывод, который собеседник вот-вот сделает
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession (parenthetical disclaimer)
**Pattern:** [statement]. *Not that* [clause]. &middot; *Not that* [clause], but [main clause]. &middot; [statement] — *not that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Set it off with a period, a dash, or parentheses; when *but* follows, a comma goes between the two halves. Never attach it to the previous sentence with a comma alone.

Heads off an inference the speaker expects the listener to draw, and denies it — usually with a wink, since *not that it matters* concedes that it does matter, or the sentence would not exist. It is one of the most common concessive moves in American speech and almost never appears in formal writing.

1. He's moving to Denver. *Not that* he told any of us.
2. *Not that* I'm counting, but this is the fourth time she has rescheduled.
3. The place has no sign and no website — *not that* they need one.
4. I'd have picked a different color. *Not that* anybody asked.
5. *Not that* it's my business, but the contractor's truck hasn't moved in a week.

### still and all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌstɪl ən ˈɔl/ &middot; *stil un AWL*
**Русский:** разг. и всё же, всё равно — просторечное удвоение still
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal and regional — colloquial American speech with a rural, older-generation flavor; not standard written English
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial dismissing what was just granted
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Still and all*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *still and all*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Still and all*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it. Between two independent clauses it needs a period or a semicolon in front — a comma alone is a comma splice, exactly as with *however*.
**Variants:** *still* (the standard one-word form), *all the same*, *just the same*, *when all's said and done*

A colloquial doubling of *still*, where *and all* adds nothing but breath and warmth. It grants everything just said and then holds the line anyway. It is a spoken form — an American hears it from an older relative or reads it in dialogue — and an editor cuts it to *still* or *all the same* in anything formal.

1. The truck has two hundred thousand miles on it. *Still and all*, it starts every morning.
2. She was rude about it; *still and all*, she wasn't wrong.
3. *Still and all*, somebody should have called the family first.
4. We knew the odds. *Still and all*, it stung to lose that way.
5. The place is falling apart. *Still and all*, I'd rather be there than anywhere else.

### to be sure

**Pronunciation:** /tə bi ˈʃʊr/ &middot; *tuh bee SHOOR*
**Русский:** книжн. разумеется, спору нет — письменная уступка перед неизбежным «но»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) — a concessive disjunct &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral — a writer's phrase; in speech it sounds bookish
**Clause type:** none — a sentence adverbial conceding a point, almost always ahead of a *but*
**Pattern:** [Independent clause], *to be sure*, *but* [independent clause]. &middot; *To be sure*, [independent clause]. *But* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], *to be sure*.
**Punctuation:** Commas around it wherever it sits. It is an adverbial and not a conjunction, so the *but* that so often follows still has to be there — drop it and two independent clauses are left spliced.
**Variants:** *admittedly*, *granted* (the same move, one conversational and one neutral), *it is true that*, *sure* (the informal one-word version: *sure, it's expensive, but* …)

The essayist's concession: it grants the strongest point on the other side in order to show that the argument survives it. It nearly always has a *but* or a *yet* behind it — a *to be sure* with no reversal after it has left its sentence unfinished. Against *granted*, which is conversational, this is the written form, and it carries a faint whiff of the nineteenth-century critic.

1. The evidence is thin, *to be sure*, but every piece of it points the same way.
2. *To be sure*, the first two seasons are uneven. The third is one of the best things ever put on television.
3. He was not the most experienced candidate, *to be sure*; he was plainly the most prepared.
4. *To be sure*, nobody expected the program to pay for itself in a single year.
5. The translation loses the puns, *to be sure*, and it keeps the argument perfectly intact.

### what though

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈðoʊ/ (also /ˌhwʌt ˈðoʊ/) &middot; *wut THOH*
**Русский:** устар., поэт. что с того, что; пусть даже — риторический вопрос вместо уступки
**Type:** subordinating — a rhetorical question standing in for a concessive clause &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic — Shakespeare, Milton, the hymnal, and nineteenth-century verse; dead in modern prose
**Clause type:** interrogative in form, concessive in force — "what does it matter that …"
**Pattern:** *What though* [clause]? [main clause]. &middot; *What though* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *What though* [noun phrase]? [main clause].
**Punctuation:** A question mark after the *what though* clause when it is put as a question, a comma when it runs straight into the main clause. Both are standard in older text, and modern editions differ on which to print.
**Variants:** *though*, *even though* (the modern forms), *what if* (the surviving relative — it asks rather than concedes), *what matter though*

A rhetorical question doing a concessive clause's work: *what though the field be lost?* means "granted the field is lost — it changes nothing." The subjunctive verb (*be*, *were*) is part of the formula. Milton gave it its most famous outing and the hymn writers kept it alive through the nineteenth century; an American now meets it in poetry and in older hymns, nowhere else.

1. "*What though* the field be lost? / All is not lost." (Milton, *Paradise Lost*, I)
2. "*What though* the spicy breezes / Blow soft o'er Ceylon's isle." (Reginald Heber, "From Greenland's Icy Mountains," 1819)
3. *What though* the night be long? The watch is set and the gate is barred.
4. *What though* he had neither name nor fortune, the girl would have him.
5. "*What though* our lot be hard, we are not therefore without hope."
