# Subordinators of time

> The connectors that attach one clause to another and say *when* — before it, after it, during it, from it, or up to it.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These are the words and phrases that take a full clause and locate the main clause in time relative to it. Most are ordinary time words pressed into service (*when, while, once, till*), and several moonlight in other categories: *since* and *as* also give reasons, *while* also marks contrast, *when* also heads noun clauses and relative clauses, *than* elsewhere is the comparative conjunction, *once*, *directly*, and *immediately* are adverbs before they are anything else, and *after*, *before*, *since*, *till*, and *until* are prepositions as well. The test is what follows — a conjunction takes a subject and a verb (*after the show ended*), a preposition takes a noun phrase (*after the show*). That is also why *pending*, *upon*, *following*, *prior to*, *subsequent to*, *as of*, and *as from* are not in this file: they take noun phrases only (*pending approval*, *upon arrival*, *following the vote*, *prior to closing*, *as of Monday*), which makes them prepositions rather than conjunctions.

Two rules run through the whole category. **Punctuation:** when the time clause comes first, put a comma after it — *Before you leave, lock the back door* — and when it follows the main clause, leave the comma out: *Lock the back door before you leave.* **Tense and word order:** a time clause pointing at the future takes the present, never *will* — *when he gets here*, *as soon as I land*, *until she calls* — and the two fronted-negative patterns, *no sooner … than* and *hardly … when*, additionally invert the subject and the auxiliary. The last section of this file covers the conjunctive adverbs of time (*meanwhile, then, thereafter*), which are not conjunctions at all: they cannot join two independent clauses with a comma and require a semicolon or a period instead.

## The core subordinators

The ten single-word subordinators that carry most of the traffic. Every one of them also belongs to another word class, so the clause test above matters here more than anywhere else in the file.

### after

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftər/ &middot; *AF-ter*
**Русский:** после того как — событие главного предложения следует за событием придаточного
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is also a preposition and an adverb)
**Pattern:** *After* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *after* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] [measure phrase] *after* [clause] (*ten minutes after everyone left*). &middot; *Just / right / soon / shortly / long after* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *after* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *right after*, *just after*, *soon after*, *shortly after*, *long after* (the modifier goes in front)

Marks the main clause event as following the *after* clause event. With a clause it is a conjunction (*after the show ended*); with a noun phrase or an *-ing* form (*after the show*, *after eating*) the same word is a preposition; standing alone behind a measure phrase it is an adverb (*she called two days after*). Do not put *will* inside the clause: *after I get home*, never *after I will get home*.

1. *After* the movie ended, we walked to the diner.
2. She called me *after* she landed in Denver.
3. *After* you finish the dishes, could you take the trash out?
4. The alarm went off ten minutes *after* everyone had already left.
5. *After* the storm passed, the whole street smelled like wet pine.
6. Just *after* I hit send, I noticed the typo.

**See also:** [before](#before), [once](#once), [since](#since), [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [by the time](#by-the-time), [afterward](#afterward)

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ &middot; *AZ*; weak form /əz/ &middot; *uhz*
**Русский:** по мере того как, когда — два действия идут параллельно; тот же союз значит и «так как»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word also builds clauses of cause, manner, comparison, and concession, and works as a preposition)
**Pattern:** *As* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as* [clause]. &middot; *Just as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *as* clause when it comes first; usually no comma when it follows. The comma does not disambiguate the time reading from the cause reading, so rewrite with *while* or *because* if a sentence could go either way.
**Variants:** *just as* (exact simultaneity), *even as* (literary), *as … as* (comparison — a different construction)

Sets two events side by side in time, usually with a sense that they unfold together rather than meeting at a single point. *When* names a moment and *while* spans a stretch; *as* is the one that runs two things in parallel, which makes it the natural choice for gradual change (*as the days got shorter*). It is the most overloaded word in this file: besides time it gives a reason (*as it was late, we left*), a manner (*do as I do*), a comparison (*as tall as his brother*), and a grudging concession (*tired as he was, he finished*), and with a noun phrase it is a preposition instead (*as a child*, *works as a nurse*). That crowding is exactly why *as* is a bad choice whenever a reader might hear "because."

1. *As* the bell rang, half the class was already standing.
2. She hummed *as* she worked.
3. *As* the years went by, the letters came less often.
4. Just *as* I sat down, the phone rang.
5. The crowd got quieter *as* the speaker went on.
6. *As* we drove west, the mountains came into view.

**See also:** [when](#when), [while](#while), [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [as long as](#as-long-as), [the moment](#the-moment)

### before

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈfɔr/ &middot; *bih-FOR*; weak /bəˈfɔr/
**Русский:** прежде чем, до того как — зеркало after: главное событие предшествует придаточному
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is also a preposition and an adverb)
**Pattern:** *Before* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *before* [clause]. &middot; It was [measure phrase] *before* [clause] (*a good ten minutes before anybody spoke*). &middot; *Just / right / shortly / long before* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *before* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *just before*, *right before*, *shortly before*, *long before*

The exact mirror of *after*: the main clause event precedes the *before* clause event. Keep the clause in the present when the reference is future (*before she gets here*). Only the clause use is the conjunction — with a noun phrase it is a preposition (*before noon*), and on its own it is an adverb (*I've seen this before*). Note the idiomatic "and it never happened" reading in sentences like *He hung up before I could thank him*, where the *before* event is exactly the one that did not occur.

1. *Before* you leave, make sure the back door is locked.
2. We got the tent up *before* it started raining.
3. He hung up *before* I could explain.
4. *Before* the bridge was built, the trip took two hours.
5. Call me *before* you buy anything.
6. It was a good ten minutes *before* anybody said a word.

**See also:** [after](#after), [until](#until), [by the time](#by-the-time), [till](#till), [previously](#previously), [ere](#ere)

### once

**Pronunciation:** /wʌns/ &middot; *WUNSS*
**Русский:** как только, раз уж — с момента, когда порог пройден, и дальше он остаётся пройденным
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is separately an adverb, and that is its commoner job — *I met him once*, *the once-great franchise*)
**Pattern:** *Once* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *once* [clause]. &middot; *Once* [past participle or adjective phrase], [main clause] (reduced clause: *once installed*, *once cool enough to handle*).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *once* clause when it comes first; usually no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** none as a conjunction — *once* is separately an adverb meaning "one time" and "formerly" (*I met him once*, *the once-great franchise*)

Means "from the moment that," with the added sense that the event is a threshold: *once the paperwork clears* implies everything else was waiting on it. *When* is neutral about that dependency and *as soon as* stresses speed; *once* stresses that the condition, having been met, stays met. It also reduces cleanly in instructions (*once installed*, *once cool enough to handle*), where the subject and *be* drop out. The word is an adverb far more often than it is a conjunction, and the clause test sorts them: *I met him once* has nothing following, so there is no conjunction there.

1. *Once* the paperwork clears, the money moves the same day.
2. You'll like him *once* you get to know him.
3. *Once* she made up her mind, there was no talking her out of it.
4. The engine runs fine *once* it warms up.
5. *Once* we crossed the state line, the radio stations all changed.
6. It gets easier *once* you stop overthinking it.
7. *Once* cool enough to handle, the pan goes in the top rack. *(reduced clause)*

**See also:** [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [when](#when), [after](#after), [now that](#now-that)

### since

**Pronunciation:** /sɪns/ &middot; *SINSS*
**Русский:** с тех пор как — начало отрезка, длящегося до сих пор; в главном требует перфекта
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word also builds clauses of cause, and works as a preposition and an adverb)
**Pattern:** *Since* [clause], [main clause with a perfect tense]. &middot; [main clause with a perfect tense] *since* [clause]. &middot; *Ever since* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *since* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *ever since* (time only — the *ever* blocks the "because" reading)

Marks the start of a stretch that runs up to now, which is why the main clause almost always takes a perfect tense: *I've lived here since I was ten*, not *I live here since I was ten*. The clause itself normally takes the simple past. It is a conjunction only in front of a clause: with a noun phrase it is a preposition (*since Tuesday*) and standing alone it is an adverb (*I haven't seen her since*). The collision with the cause reading is real — *Since you took over, things have gone smoothly* genuinely has two meanings, and *ever since* or *because* is the fix.

1. She's been quieter *since* her sister moved out.
2. *Since* the new manager started, the schedule has actually made sense.
3. I haven't touched a cigarette *since* I got out of the hospital.
4. Ever *since* we got the dog, nobody sleeps past six.
5. *Since* they repaved the road, the drive has taken fifteen minutes less.
6. He's called twice *since* you left.

**See also:** [ever since](#ever-since), [from the time](#from-the-time), [until](#until), [now that](#now-that), [after](#after), [since then](#since-then), [thereafter](#thereafter)

### till

**Pronunciation:** /tɪl/ &middot; *TIL*; weak /təl/
**Русский:** пока не, до тех пор пока — то же, что until, но разг. и не в начале фразы
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is also a preposition: *till Friday*, *till noon*)
**Pattern:** *Till* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *till* [clause]. &middot; [negative main clause] *till* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *till* clause when it comes first, which is uncommon; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *'til*, *til* (both widely used, both rejected by most style guides), *until* (the fuller, more formal word)

Means exactly what *until* means, and takes a clause or a noun phrase for the same reason *until* does — conjunction in front of a clause, preposition in front of a noun. Despite appearances *till* is the older word and not a clipping of *until*, which is why the apostrophe in *'til* is a back-formation that editors delete. Use *until* at the start of a sentence and in formal writing; *till* is at home in speech and casual prose.

1. Stir it *till* the sugar dissolves.
2. We stayed *till* the last band finished.
3. I didn't know he was sick *till* somebody told me at work.
4. Just hold the door *till* I get the stroller through.
5. Nobody moved *till* the coach blew the whistle.
6. *Till* I saw the bill, I had no idea how much we'd spent.
7. She kept refreshing the page *till* the grades posted.

**See also:** [until](#until), [before](#before), [as long as](#as-long-as), [until such time as](#until-such-time-as)

### until

**Pronunciation:** /ənˈtɪl/ &middot; *un-TIL*; also /ʌnˈtɪl/
**Русский:** пока не, до тех пор пока — конечная точка длящейся ситуации; not … until = «только в»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is also a preposition: *until noon*)
**Pattern:** *Until* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *until* [clause]. &middot; [negative main clause] *until* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *until* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *till*, *'til* (less formal), *up until*, *until such time as* (legal)

Marks the endpoint of a situation that continues up to it. In front of a clause it is a conjunction; in front of a noun phrase (*until Friday*) the same word is a preposition. Keep the clause in the present for future reference (*until she gets back*). The most useful pattern is the negative one: *not … until* is how English says "only at that point" — *The store doesn't open until ten* is a statement about ten o'clock, not about the store being closed forever.

1. Wait here *until* I call you.
2. *Until* the results came back, nobody said anything out loud.
3. The store doesn't open *until* ten.
4. We kept the heat on *until* the pipes thawed.
5. He didn't tell his parents *until* the semester was over.
6. *Until* you've tried it yourself, don't knock it.

**See also:** [till](#till), [before](#before), [as long as](#as-long-as), [by the time](#by-the-time), [until such time as](#until-such-time-as)

### when

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn/ &middot; *WEN*; weak /wən/. Some Southern and older speakers keep the older /ʍɛn/ &middot; *HWEN*.
**Русский:** когда — основной временной союз: указывает на момент, а не на отрезок
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word also introduces noun clauses and relative clauses — *I know when he left*, *the year when we moved* — and is the interrogative adverb in questions: *When did you leave?*)
**Pattern:** *When* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *when* [clause]. &middot; *when* + [-ing form or past participle] (reduced clause: *when finished*, *when driving*).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *when* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. Use a comma before a trailing *when* clause only when it is an afterthought or a surprise turn, and note that the comma is the only thing marking that reading: *She was halfway through the story when her phone rang* (plain time) versus *She was halfway through the story, when the lights went out* (the interruption).
**Variants:** *whenever* (every time), *just when*, *right when*, *back when*

The default time subordinator: it puts the main clause at the point named by the *when* clause. *While* spreads the event across a stretch, *as* runs the two together, and *when* simply says "at that time." Never use *will* inside it — *when he gets here*, not *when he will get here*.

1. *When* the power went out, the whole block came outside.
2. Call me *when* you get in.
3. *When* I was a kid, we drove to Michigan every August.
4. She was halfway through the story *when* her phone rang.
5. *When* the check finally cleared, I felt like I could breathe.
6. Text me *when* you're five minutes out.

**See also:** [whenever](#whenever), [while](#while), [as](#as), [the moment](#the-moment), [hardly … when](#hardly--when)

### whenever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnˈɛvər/ &middot; *wen-EV-er*; weak /wənˈɛvər/
**Русский:** всякий раз когда; когда угодно — повторяемость или безразличие ко времени
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (also concessive, "no matter when")
**Pattern:** *Whenever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whenever* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *whenever* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *whensoever* (archaic and legal); *when ever* as two words only in questions (*When ever did you find the time?*)

Means "every time that" or "at whatever time," where *when* means "at that one time." The frequency reading is the common one: *whenever it rains* covers all the rainy days, while *when it rained* points at one. The second reading — "it doesn't matter when" — shows up in offers and permissions.

1. *Whenever* it rains, that corner floods.
2. She calls her mother *whenever* she has a long drive.
3. *Whenever* you're ready, we'll start.
4. The dog hides *whenever* the vacuum comes out.
5. *Whenever* I try to cook rice, it turns to glue.
6. Stop by *whenever* you want — we're home all weekend.

**See also:** [when](#when), [every time](#every-time), [each time](#each-time), [any time](#any-time)

### while

**Pronunciation:** /waɪl/ &middot; *WYLE*; some speakers /ʍaɪl/ &middot; *HWYLE*
**Русский:** пока, в то время как — на протяжении отрезка; также «тогда как» (противопоставление)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word also builds clauses of contrast and of concession, and is separately a noun and a verb)
**Pattern:** *While* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *while* [clause]. &middot; *while* + [-ing form, past participle, adjective phrase, or prepositional phrase] (reduced clause).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *while* clause when it comes first. When it follows, no comma in the time sense — but do use a comma when *while* means "whereas," because that reading is contrastive and non-restrictive: *She read while I cooked* (time) versus *She loves horror movies, while I can't watch them* (contrast).
**Variants:** *whilst* (British — see its own entry), *all the while*, *the whole while*

Spans a stretch of time and puts the main clause inside it. *When* names a point and *as* runs two events together; *while* is the one that means "throughout." The reduced form (*while waiting*, *while in college*) is extremely common in edited prose, and the contrast sense is common enough that the comma rule above is worth memorizing. Beyond the conjunction, *while* is a noun (*stay a while*, *worth your while*) and a verb (*while away an afternoon*), so the clause test decides the reading here too.

1. *While* the sauce simmers, chop the parsley.
2. She read *while* I cooked.
3. Somebody took my seat *while* I was in the restroom.
4. *While* I was living in Tucson, I never once used the heater.
5. He learned Portuguese *while* working on a rig off Brazil.
6. *While* we waited for the tow truck, three people stopped to ask if we were okay.

**See also:** [as](#as), [when](#when), [as long as](#as-long-as), [the whole time](#the-whole-time), [whilst](#whilst), [meanwhile](#meanwhile)

## Phrasal and correlative subordinators

Multi-word connectors, most of them built on a time noun (*the moment*, *by the time*, *every time*). They punctuate exactly like the single-word subordinators above — comma after a fronted clause, no comma after a trailing one — with two families of exceptions flagged in their own entries. The inverting group (*no sooner … than*, *hardly … when*, *not until*, *only when*) forces subject–auxiliary inversion when the connector is fronted and takes no comma at the break. The connective relatives (*after which*, *at which time*, *by which time*, *during which time*, *since when*, *whereafter*, *whereupon*) point backward at the clause in front of them, which means they can never open a sentence and are always preceded by a comma or a semicolon and never followed by one.

### after which

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftər wɪtʃ/ &middot; *AF-ter-WICH*; *which* is /ʍɪtʃ/ &middot; *HWICH* for speakers who keep the *wh*
**Русский:** после чего — относительный оборот, отсылает ко всему сказанному; только в конце фразы
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial relative clause of time, always non-restrictive; the relative *which* points back at the whole preceding clause rather than at any single noun
**Pattern:** [main clause], *after which* [clause]. &middot; [long main clause]; *after which* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *after which* [clause], [continuation]. &middot; There is no fronted skeleton — *After which* [clause], [main clause] is not a sentence.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. Like *whereupon*, its clause cannot move to the front, so *after which* can never open a sentence in edited prose — a stray *After which…* is a fragment, whatever effect the writer was after.
**Variants:** *whereafter* (rare and formal), *after that*, *and then* (the plain equivalents), *at which point*, *at which time*

The plain modern way to keep a narrative moving without starting a new sentence: *which* reaches back over everything just said, and *after* puts the next event behind it. *Whereupon* says nearly the same thing but adds a hint that the first event triggered the second, and it carries a distinctly literary tone; *after which* is neutral about cause and is at home in minutes, reports, procedures, and news writing. Because the relative looks backward, the clause is stuck at the end of its sentence.

1. The committee heard forty minutes of testimony, *after which* the chair called for a vote.
2. She finished her residency in Cleveland, *after which* she moved to Santa Fe and opened a practice.
3. The system runs a full backup at two in the morning, *after which* the nightly reports are generated.
4. He was suspended for three games, *after which* he was quietly traded.
5. Guests check in at the front desk, *after which* a shuttle takes them out to the cabins.
6. The tenant has thirty days to cure the default, *after which* the landlord may terminate the lease.

**See also:** [whereafter](#whereafter), [whereupon](#whereupon), [at which time](#at-which-time), [during which time](#during-which-time), [thereafter](#thereafter), [after](#after)

### any time

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛni ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *EN-ee-time*
**Русский:** в любое время когда, всякий раз когда — как whenever, но с оттенком разрешения
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Any time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *any time* [clause]. &middot; *Any time that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *any time that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *any time* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. The optional *that* is usually dropped.
**Variants:** *anytime* (the standard one-word spelling when it works as a conjunction or an adverb), *any time that*

A near-synonym of *whenever* with a permissive edge: *any time you need help* offers all the occasions, where *every time* merely counts them. Write it as one word in the conjunction and adverb uses (*anytime you want*); keep two words after a preposition and when *any* modifies a real quantity (*at any time*, *do you have any time?*).

1. *Anytime* you need a ride, just text me.
2. She gets a headache *any time* the pressure drops.
3. *Anytime* somebody mentions the merger, he changes the subject.
4. Call the front desk *any time* the heat cuts out.
5. *Anytime* I've asked him for a favor, he's said yes.
6. You can return it *any time* the store is open.

**See also:** [whenever](#whenever), [every time](#every-time), [each time](#each-time), [as long as](#as-long-as)

### as long as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈlɔŋ æz/ &middot; *az-LAWNG-az*; reduced /əz ˈlɔŋ əz/ &middot; *uhz-LAWNG-uhz*
**Русский:** пока, всё то время что; при условии что — время или условие, решает контекст
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same phrase also builds clauses of condition)
**Pattern:** *As long as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as long as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for as long as* [clause]. &middot; *So long as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *as long as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *so long as* — the same phrase, slightly more common in the conditional sense and a touch more formal in American English

In the time sense it means "throughout the whole period that": the main clause holds for exactly the stretch the clause describes. In the conditional sense it means "provided that." Context decides — *as long as the music played* is duration, *as long as you pay on time* is a condition. The comparison *as long as* ("of equal length") is a third construction entirely.

1. The porch light stays on *as long as* somebody's still out.
2. *As long as* the generator held out, we had lights.
3. She'll keep coaching *as long as* the kids keep showing up.
4. *So long as* you're standing there, hand me the pliers.
5. I'll stay *as long as* you need me.
6. *As long as* the ice held, the whole town skated on it.

**See also:** [while](#while), [until](#until), [till](#till), [the whole time](#the-whole-time), [now that](#now-that), [any time](#any-time)

### as often as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈɔfən æz/ &middot; *az-AWF-un-az*; reduced /əz ˈɔfən əz/ &middot; *uhz-AWF-un-uhz*; many speakers have /ˈɑfən/ with the cot–caught merger, and the *t* is silent for nearly all of them — the spelling pronunciation /ˈɔftən/ &middot; *AWF-tun* is a minority form and not the default
**Русский:** всякий раз когда, столько раз сколько — частотность, часто с оттенком «уж сколько раз»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (frequency)
**Pattern:** *As often as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as often as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as often as* [noun phrase] (comparison — a different construction).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *as often as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *as many times as*, *however often*, *no matter how often*

The frequency member of the family: it counts the occasions the way *every time* does, but folds in a note of concession — *as often as I've told him* means "on all those occasions, and there have been plenty of them." Keep it apart from the comparative *as often as*, which measures one frequency against another (*She calls as often as I do*) and is not a time clause at all.

1. *As often as* I've driven that road, I still miss the turn.
2. He brings it up *as often as* he can work it into a conversation.
3. *As often as* that machine jams, somebody ought to replace it.
4. She gets out to see her grandmother *as often as* the weather lets her.
5. *As often as* we've been through this, you'd think I'd remember the password.
6. The alarm goes off *as often as* a truck backs into the loading dock.

**See also:** [every time](#every-time), [each time](#each-time), [whenever](#whenever), [any time](#any-time)

### as soon as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈsun æz/ &middot; *az-SOON-az*; reduced /əz ˈsun əz/, often collapsing to /əˈsunəz/ &middot; *uh-SOO-nuhz*
**Русский:** как только — сразу вслед за событием, без промежутка
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *As soon as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as soon as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *as soon as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *just as soon as* (informal intensifier), *soon as* (the clipped spoken American form: *soon as I get there, I'll call*); *the moment*, *the minute*, *the second*, *the instant* are the emphatic equivalents

Says the main clause happens immediately after the clause event, with no gap in between. *When* allows a delay, *once* stresses that a threshold has been crossed, and *as soon as* stresses speed. Use the present for future reference — *as soon as I land*, never *as soon as I will land*.

1. I'll call you *as soon as* I land.
2. *As soon as* the light turned green, the guy behind me leaned on the horn.
3. She recognized the voice *as soon as* he said hello.
4. *As soon as* we're done here, I'm going home.
5. Text me *as soon as* you hear anything.
6. *As soon as* the check cleared, he booked the flight.

**See also:** [once](#once), [when](#when), [the moment](#the-moment), [the minute](#the-minute), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [immediately](#immediately)

### at such time as

**Pronunciation:** /æt sʌtʃ ˈtaɪm əz/ &middot; *at-such-TIME-uhz*; *at* reduces to /ət/ and the final *as* rarely carries stress
**Русский:** юр. в тот момент, когда — канцелярское «когда» с намеренно неизвестной датой
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal and bureaucratic)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *At such time as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *at such time as* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *shall* [verb] *at such time as* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *at such time as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *until such time as* (the endpoint version — see its own entry), *at such time or times as* (drafting boilerplate), *at such time that* (a common miswriting); *when* is the plain equivalent

*When*, dressed for a contract. The phrase names a future point whose date nobody yet knows, which is exactly why drafters reach for it: *at such time as the board determines* leaves the timing open in a way *on July 1* cannot. It is the point-in-time partner of [until such time as](#until-such-time-as), and an American meets it in stock plans, trust instruments, court orders, and personnel manuals and almost nowhere else. Outside those registers it is padding, and editors cut it back to *when*.

1. The remaining shares vest *at such time as* the company completes an initial public offering.
2. *At such time as* the tenant assigns the lease, the guaranty terminates automatically.
3. Distributions shall be made *at such time as* the trustee in her sole discretion determines.
4. The department will reopen the position *at such time as* funding is restored.
5. *At such time as* the court enters final judgment, the escrowed funds shall be released.
6. Plain English: The remaining shares vest *when* the company goes public.

**See also:** [until such time as](#until-such-time-as), [at which time](#at-which-time), [when](#when), [whereafter](#whereafter), [henceforth](#henceforth)

### at which time

**Pronunciation:** /æt wɪtʃ ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *at-wich-TIME*; *at* reduces to /ət/
**Русский:** и тогда, в этот момент — книжн./канц.; второе событие приходится ровно на тот момент
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial relative clause of time, always non-restrictive; *which* points back at the whole preceding clause or at a date named in it
**Pattern:** [main clause], *at which time* [clause]. &middot; [long main clause]; *at which time* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *at which point* [clause] (the everyday variant). &middot; No fronted skeleton — the clause cannot open a sentence.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. It can never start a sentence, and the comma in front of it is not optional, because the clause is non-restrictive.
**Variants:** *at which point* (commoner in ordinary prose), *whereupon* (literary), *by which time* (a different relation — see its own entry), *when* after a named date (*on June 1, when…*)

The plain-prose replacement for *whereupon*: it drops the second event onto exactly the moment the first one reached. *At which point* is the same connector with less starch and is what most Americans actually say; *at which time* is the one that turns up in agendas, contracts, and meeting minutes. Keep it apart from its two neighbors in the family — *after which* only says "later than that," and *by which time* says the second event was already finished.

1. The public comment period closes on March 3, *at which time* the board will take up the ordinance.
2. We waited until the last flight was called, *at which time* the agent finally admitted the crew was stuck in Denver.
3. The note matures in five years, *at which time* the full principal comes due.
4. Training runs through Friday, *at which time* new hires are assigned to a shift.
5. The alarm sounded twice, *at which point* everybody stopped pretending it was a drill.
6. The deposition was suspended at 4:15 p.m., *at which time* counsel conferred off the record.

**See also:** [by which time](#by-which-time), [after which](#after-which), [during which time](#during-which-time), [whereupon](#whereupon), [when](#when), [then](#then)

### by the time

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ðə ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *by-thuh-TIME*
**Русский:** к тому времени как — тянет в главное предложение перфект (had left, will have landed)
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *By the time* [clause], [main clause with *had* + past participle]. &middot; *By the time* [clause], [main clause with *will have* + past participle]. &middot; [main clause] *by the time* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *by the time* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *by the time that* (the *that* is optional and usually dropped)

Means "at or before the moment when," and it pulls a perfect tense into the main clause: past perfect for past reference (*by the time we got there, they had left*) and future perfect for future reference (*by the time you read this, I'll have landed*). *When* just marks the point; *by the time* says the main clause was already complete at that point.

1. *By the time* we got there, they had already left.
2. *By the time* you read this, I'll have landed in Seattle.
3. The soup was cold *by the time* everyone sat down.
4. *By the time* she turned thirty, she'd lived in six cities.
5. *By the time* the inspector showed up, we'd fixed it ourselves.
6. He'll be asleep *by the time* the game ends.

**See also:** [before](#before), [when](#when), [until](#until), [after](#after)

### by which time

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ wɪtʃ ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *by-wich-TIME*
**Русский:** к этому времени — отсылает назад; главное действие к тому моменту уже завершилось
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial relative clause of time, always non-restrictive; *which* points back at a moment already named
**Pattern:** [main clause], *by which time* [clause with a perfect tense]. &middot; [long main clause]; *by which time* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *by which point* [clause]. &middot; No fronted skeleton — the clause cannot open a sentence.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. It can never start a sentence.
**Variants:** *by which point*, *by then* (the adverb equivalent, which needs no preceding clause); *by the time* is the ordinary subordinator that names the moment instead of pointing back at it

[By the time](#by-the-time) turned around. Instead of naming the deadline in a clause of its own, it points back at a moment already on the page, and it drags the same perfect tense along with it: *by which time everyone had left*. It nearly always carries a note of too late. Set it against its neighbors: *at which time* puts the second event right at that moment, while *by which time* says the second event was over before the moment arrived.

1. The tow truck showed up at eleven, *by which time* we had already changed the tire ourselves.
2. Her flight was rerouted through Charlotte, *by which time* the connection had long since departed.
3. The report reached the committee in June, *by which time* two of its authors had left the agency.
4. He apologized the following spring, *by which time* nobody could remember what the fight had been about.
5. Repairs are scheduled for next October, *by which time* the building will have stood empty for three years.
6. The recall notice finally went out in the middle of March, *by which point* most of the affected units had already been installed in new houses.

### during the time that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʊrɪŋ ðə ˈtaɪm ðət/ &middot; *DOOR-ing-thuh-TIME-thut*; also /ˈdjʊrɪŋ/ &middot; *DYOOR-ing* for older and Southern speakers
**Русский:** книжн./юр. в течение того времени, пока — многословное while без оттенка контраста
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal; wordy in ordinary prose
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (duration)
**Pattern:** *During the time that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *during the time that* [clause]. &middot; *During the period that* / *during the time when* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *during the time that* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. Unlike most of the optional *that*s in this file, this one does not drop comfortably: *during the time he was in office* is possible but reads as a slip.
**Variants:** *during the period that*, *during the time when*, *during such time as* (legal); *while* is the one-word equivalent

A four-word *while*, kept alive by contracts, testimony, and academic prose, where a writer wants the stretch stated as a stretch. It differs from *while* in weight and in one honest particular: *while* has a contrast sense (*she likes it, while I don't*) that this phrase cannot have, so it is unambiguous where *while* is not. That is the only real argument for it. In ordinary writing, cut it to *while*.

1. *During the time that* he served as treasurer, the fund never missed a filing deadline.
2. No interest accrues *during the time that* the account is under review.
3. *During the time that* the plant sat idle, the company continued to pay full benefits.
4. She was living in Seattle *during the time that* the bridge was being rebuilt.
5. *During the time that* the witness was in the room, did anyone speak to her about the report?
6. Plain English: *While* he was treasurer, the fund never missed a filing deadline.

### during which time

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʊrɪŋ wɪtʃ ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *DOOR-ing-wich-TIME*; also /ˈdjʊrɪŋ/ &middot; *DYOOR-ing*
**Русский:** в течение какового времени — книжн., отсылает назад к уже названному отрезку
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial relative clause of time (duration), always non-restrictive; *which* points back at a stretch already named
**Pattern:** [main clause naming a stretch], *during which time* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *during which* [clause] (the short form). &middot; [long main clause]; *during which time* [clause]. &middot; No fronted skeleton — the clause cannot open a sentence.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. It can never start a sentence, and because the clause is non-restrictive the comma in front of it is required, not optional.
**Variants:** *during which* (the bare form, used when the antecedent is already a time noun: *a six-week strike, during which nothing shipped*), *in which time*, *over which time*, *all that time* (the adverb equivalent)

The duration member of the connective-relative family. *After which* and *at which time* point at a moment; this one covers a span, and it needs a span in front of it to point back at — *for eleven days, during which time the detour added forty minutes* works because *eleven days* is sitting there to be picked up. Drop the word *time* when the antecedent is already a time noun and the reference is obvious; keep it when a bare *which* would otherwise reach for the nearest ordinary noun instead.

1. The road was closed for eleven days, *during which time* the detour added forty minutes to every commute.
2. He spent two years in Manila, *during which time* he learned enough Tagalog to argue with a landlord.
3. The system was offline from midnight to six, *during which time* no transactions were recorded.
4. She sat through a four-hour hearing, *during which* not one member asked her a question.
5. The lease ran through 2019, *during which time* the rent was never once raised.
6. There was a long silence, *during which* everybody at the table studied the tablecloth.

### each time

**Pronunciation:** /ˈitʃ ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *EECH-time*
**Русский:** каждый раз когда — случаи берутся по одному, чуть более обдуманно, чем every time
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Each time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *each time* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *each time* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *each time that*

Means "on every one of the occasions when," and it differs from *every time* the way *each* differs from *every* everywhere else in the language: *each* takes the occasions one at a time, *every* takes them as a set. The difference is small enough that the two are usually interchangeable; *each time* sounds slightly more deliberate.

1. *Each time* the door opened, everyone looked up.

3. *Each time* I've called, I've gotten a different answer.

### every time

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvri ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *EV-ree-time*

**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Every time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *every time* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *every time* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *every time that*, *every single time* (emphatic)

The most common way to say "on all the occasions when." It overlaps almost completely with *whenever*, but *every time* counts the occasions and often carries a note of exasperation, where *whenever* is neutral and can also mean "at whatever time suits you."

1. *Every time* I wash the car, it rains.

### ever since

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛvər ˈsɪns/ &middot; *ev-er-SINSS*; run together in casual speech as /ˈɛvərsɪns/ &middot; *EV-er-sinss*
**Русский:** с тех самых пор как — since с наглухо закрытым причинным прочтением, только время
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same two words are also a bare adverb — *He hasn't called ever since* — and a preposition in front of a noun phrase: *ever since college*)
**Pattern:** *Ever since* [clause], [main clause with a perfect tense]. &middot; [main clause with a perfect tense] *ever since* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *ever since* (bare adverb — nothing follows). &middot; *Ever since* [noun phrase], [main clause] (preposition, not conjunction).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *ever since* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. Nothing changes when it is the bare adverb at the end of a sentence: no comma there either.
**Variants:** *ever since then* (pointing back at a moment already named), *ever since the day*, *right from the time*; the bare *since* is the same conjunction with the ambiguity left in

*Since* with the ambiguity welded shut. The *ever* is not decoration — it blocks the causal reading outright, so *ever since you took over* can only be about time, where *since you took over* can be read either way. In every other respect it behaves like [since](#since): it pulls a perfect tense into the main clause, takes a simple past in its own clause, and adds a quiet insistence that the stretch has run unbroken. Because the same two words are also an adverb and a preposition, the clause test still decides: a subject and a verb after it make it a conjunction, a noun alone makes it a preposition, and nothing at all makes it an adverb.

1. *Ever since* the plant closed, the diner has been half empty.
2. He's been afraid of dogs *ever since* one bit him in third grade.
3. *Ever since* she started running, she's slept better.
4. The back porch has leaked *ever since* we bought the place.
5. *Ever since* they added the third shift, the parking lot has been a mess.
6. We had one argument about it in 2019, and he's brought it up *ever since*. *(bare adverb — no clause follows)*
7. She's kept the same phone number *ever since* college. *(preposition — a noun phrase, not a clause)*

### from the time

**Русский:** с того времени как — однозначное «с тех пор как», без риска прочтения «поскольку»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral

The unambiguous way to say what *since* says: it marks the start of a stretch, with no chance of being misread as "because." It also does not force a perfect tense on the main clause, so it can frame a stretch that ended long ago — *from the time he was six, he played piano every day*.

**Type:** correlative (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the uninverted order (*We had hardly sat down when…*); formal to literary when *hardly* is fronted and the subject inverts
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (*when* here is locked to the correlative and cannot be traded for *than*; on its own the same word heads noun clauses and relative clauses too — see [when](#when))

**Variants:** *the next time*, *next time that*

### no sooner … than

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈsunər … ðæn/ &middot; *noh-SOO-ner … THAN*; *than* reduces to /ðən/ &middot; *thun*
**Русский:** не успел … как — самое выразительное «сразу вслед»; парой идёт than, никогда не when
**Type:** correlative (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** formal to literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (*than* here is the correlative partner of *no sooner*, not the comparative conjunction of *taller than I am* and not the preposition of *older than me*)
**Pattern:** *No sooner* had [subject] [past participle] *than* [clause]. &middot; *No sooner* [*did* / *was* / *were*] [subject] [verb] *than* [clause]. &middot; [Subject] had *no sooner* [past participle] *than* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *than*. Fronting *no sooner* forces subject–auxiliary inversion: *No sooner had we sat down than…*, never *No sooner we had sat down than…* When the clause has no auxiliary of its own, *do* supplies one: *No sooner did I sit down than…*
**Variants:** *no sooner … but* (archaic and now nonstandard); *than* here is the correlative partner word, not the comparative conjunction of *bigger than I thought*

The most emphatic way in English to say that one event followed another instantly. The partner word is *than*, never *when* — *No sooner had she left when the phone rang* is the single most common mistake made with this pattern, and *hardly*, *scarcely*, and *barely* are the words that take *when*.

1. *No sooner* had we sat down *than* the waiter took our order.
2. *No sooner* had the game started *than* the power went out.
3. She had *no sooner* hung up *than* the phone rang again.
4. *No sooner* did I get comfortable *than* somebody knocked on the door.
5. *No sooner* was the paint dry *than* the kids put handprints on it.
6. He had *no sooner* unpacked *than* the company transferred him again.

**See also:** [hardly … when](#hardly--when), [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [the moment](#the-moment), [the instant](#the-instant)

### not until

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ənˈtɪl/ &middot; *not-un-TIL*; in the ordinary order the negative contracts — *didn't … until* /ˈdɪdənt/ &middot; *DID-unt*
**Русский:** только когда, не раньше чем — при выносе в начало требует инверсии
**Type:** subordinating (with negative fronting) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the ordinary order; formal to emphatic when fronted
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time, negated
**Pattern:** [negative main clause] *until* [clause] (the ordinary order: *He didn't call until Tuesday*). &middot; *Not until* [clause] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] (fronted, with inversion). &middot; *Not until* [noun phrase] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]. &middot; *It was not until* [clause or noun phrase] *that* [clause] (the cleft, which needs no inversion).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *until* in the ordinary order, and — this is the exception worth memorizing — no comma after the fronted *not until* clause either. It is the one fronted time clause in this file that takes no comma, because the inversion already marks the break. Fronting forces subject–auxiliary inversion: *Not until the results came back did anyone relax*, never *Not until the results came back anyone relaxed*. When the clause has no auxiliary of its own, *do* supplies one.
**Variants:** *not till* (informal), *only when*, *only after* (the positive equivalents), *it was not until … that* (the cleft), *not before*

English's way of saying "at that point and no earlier." The plain order is unremarkable — *The store doesn't open until ten* — but fronting *not until* turns the sentence into one of the negative-inversion patterns, alongside *no sooner ... than* and *hardly ... when*, and it is what journalists and speechwriters reach for when they want the length of the delay to land. The *it was not until … that* cleft says the same thing without the inversion and is much easier to get right.

1. *Not until* the last box was on the truck did anybody sit down.
2. He didn't say a word about the diagnosis *until* the semester was over. *(ordinary order — no inversion)*
3. *Not until* 1974 did the state require seat belts in school buses.
4. It was *not until* she read the second page *that* she understood what he was actually asking. *(cleft)*
5. *Not until* the power came back on did we realize how cold the house had gotten.
6. *Not until* you have done it yourself will you understand why it takes three days.

**See also:** [until](#until), [till](#till), [only when](#only-when), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [hardly … when](#hardly--when), [before](#before)

### now that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnaʊ ðæt/ &middot; *NOW-that*; *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** теперь когда, раз уж — время и причина сразу: обстановка только что изменилась
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time and cause
**Pattern:** *Now that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *now that* [clause]. &middot; *Now* [clause], [main clause] (informal, with *that* dropped).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *now that* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *now* on its own, informal (*Now you mention it…*)

Fuses time and cause: it means "from this point on, and because of it." *Since* and *because* give a reason with no time anchor, while *now that* insists that the situation has just changed. The clause almost always takes the present or the present perfect.

1. *Now that* the semester's over, I can finally sleep in.
2. He's much easier to work with *now that* he's not commuting.
3. *Now that* you mention it, I did hear something about that.
4. *Now that* the kids are both in school, the house is unnervingly quiet.
5. We can afford it *now that* the car is paid off.
6. *Now that* she's seen the actual numbers, she's asking better questions.

**See also:** [since](#since), [once](#once), [as long as](#as-long-as), [when](#when)

### only when

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊnli ˈwɛn/ &middot; *OHN-lee-WEN*
**Русский:** только когда — сужает главное действие до одного момента, исключая все прочие
**Type:** subordinating (with focusing *only*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the trailing order; formal to emphatic when fronted
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time, focused
**Pattern:** [main clause] *only when* [clause] (the ordinary order). &middot; *Only when* [clause] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] (fronted, with inversion). &middot; *Only after* [clause or noun phrase] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Only once* [clause] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]. &middot; *It is only when* [clause] *that* [clause] (the cleft).
**Punctuation:** No comma in the trailing order, and no comma after the fronted clause either — like *not until*, the inversion marks the break, so *Only when the rain stopped did they start* takes none. Fronting forces subject–auxiliary inversion: *Only after she left did he speak*, never *Only after she left he spoke*; when there is no auxiliary, *do* supplies one.
**Variants:** *only after*, *only once*, *only then*, *only if* (conditional, not time), *not until* (the negative equivalent), *it is only when … that* (the cleft)

*Only* clamped onto a time clause narrows the main clause down to that one occasion and rules out every other: *he relaxes only when the shift ends* says there is no other moment. Swap the subordinator to move the point — *only when* names the moment, *only after* puts the main clause behind it, *only once* adds the sense of a threshold finally crossed — and the construction stays the same in all three. Fronted, it behaves exactly like [not until](#not-until) and inverts, and that inverted form is both the emphatic one and the one learners most often get wrong.

1. *Only when* the last guest had gone did she admit how tired she was.
2. The heat kicks on *only when* the temperature drops below sixty.
3. *Only after* the audit was finished did the board see the actual numbers.
4. *Only once* the paperwork clears will the money move.
5. He talks about his father *only when* somebody else brings him up first.
6. *Only when* you have taught it yourself do you understand how hard it is.

**See also:** [not until](#not-until), [when](#when), [after](#after), [once](#once), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [until](#until)

### since when

**Pronunciation:** /sɪns ˈwɛn/ &middot; *sinss-WEN*
**Русский:** книжн. с какового времени; а также «с каких это пор?» — возмущённый вопрос без ответа
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) in the linking use; interrogative adverb in the challenge use &middot; **Register:** formal and British-leaning as a connective; sharply informal in the rhetorical question
**Clause type:** adverbial relative clause of time, always non-restrictive, with *when* pointing back at a date or event already named. The question use builds no clause at all and connects nothing.
**Pattern:** [main clause naming a point in time], *since when* [clause with a perfect tense]. &middot; *Since when* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? (the rhetorical question — a separate construction). &middot; No fronted linking skeleton: the connective clause cannot open a sentence.
**Punctuation:** As a connective it is always preceded by a comma and never followed by one, and it cannot start a sentence. As a question it opens the sentence and takes a question mark: *Since when do you drink coffee?*
**Variants:** *since which time* (heavier and clearer), *and since then*, *since then* (the ordinary American choice)

Two different words wearing one spelling. The connective use ties a clause to a date already given — *the mill closed in 1998, since when the town has lost a third of its population* — and it is formal, faintly British, and uncommon in American prose, where *and since then* does the job. The other use is a challenge rather than a connector: *Since when is that my job?* expects no answer and means "that was never true." Only the first belongs in a grammar of conjunctions, but the second is far more likely to reach an American ear.

1. The mill closed in 1998, *since when* the town has lost a third of its population.
2. She was appointed to the bench in 2014, *since when* she has written more dissents than opinions.
3. The rule was suspended during the pandemic, *since when* nobody has bothered to reinstate it.
4. He gave up coffee in March, *since when* he has been impossible to talk to before ten.
5. *Since when* do you know anything about carburetors? *(rhetorical question — not a connector)*
6. Ordinary American English: The mill closed in 1998, and *since then* the town has lost a third of its population.

**See also:** [since](#since), [since then](#since-then), [ever since](#ever-since), [after which](#after-which), [during which time](#during-which-time)

### the day

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈdeɪ/ &middot; *thuh-DAY*
**Русский:** в тот день, когда — модель «the + слово времени + придаточное» в роли союза
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The day* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the day* [clause]. &middot; *The* [time noun] [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the day* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows. The optional *that* is almost always dropped.
**Variants:** *the night*, *the week*, *the month*, *the year*, *the summer*, *the winter* — any time noun takes the same frame; also *the day that*

The pattern behind *the moment* and *the minute*, opened up to any unit of time: *the* plus a time noun plus a bare clause makes a time conjunction. Longer units give a period rather than a point, so *the year we moved* frames a stretch the way *while* does, while *the day we moved* names a single occasion.

1. *The day* the factory closed, half the town started looking for work.
2. I knew *the day* I met him that he'd never leave Ohio.
3. *The year* we lived in Boston, it snowed into April.
4. *The night* the storm came through, we lost three trees.
5. She quit *the week* her contract ran out.
6. *The summer* I turned twelve, I read every book in the house.

**See also:** [the moment](#the-moment), [the first time](#the-first-time), [from the time](#from-the-time), [when](#when)

### the first time

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈfɜrst ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *thuh-FURST-time*
**Русский:** в первый раз, когда — выделяет один случай из ряда; работает любое порядковое
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The first time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the first time* [clause]. &middot; *The* [ordinal] *time* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the first time* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *the first time that*, *the second time*, *the third time* — the whole ordinal series takes the same frame

Singles out one occasion in a series and locates the main clause there. Because any ordinal works, the frame is a workhorse of narrative: *the first time* sets up a pattern that a later occasion breaks.

1. *The first time* I drove in Boston, I got honked at within a block.
2. She said no *the first time* he asked.
3. *The first time* the alarm went off, everybody filed out politely.
4. *The third time* the check bounced, the landlord called a lawyer.
5. I stopped worrying about it *the first time* somebody told me it was normal.
6. *The first time* you make it, follow the recipe exactly.

**See also:** [the last time](#the-last-time), [next time](#next-time), [every time](#every-time), [each time](#each-time)

### the instant

**Pronunciation:** /ði ˈɪnstənt/ &middot; *thee-IN-stunt*
**Русский:** книжн. в то самое мгновение, как — самый выразительный из группы «как только»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The instant* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the instant* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the instant* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *the very instant*, *the instant that*

The most emphatic and the most bookish member of the *moment / minute / second / instant* set. All four mean "as soon as"; this one insists on a vanishing interval and shows up far more in written narrative than in speech.

1. *The instant* the doors opened, the crowd surged forward.
2. He knew *the instant* he saw the number on the screen.
3. *The instant* she stepped outside, the wind took her umbrella.
4. The alarm trips *the instant* the current drops.
5. *The instant* the verdict came in, the room went silent.
6. She recognized the handwriting *the instant* she opened the envelope.

**See also:** [the moment](#the-moment), [the second](#the-second), [the minute](#the-minute), [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [immediately](#immediately)

### the last time

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈlæst ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *thuh-LAST-time*
**Русский:** в последний раз, когда — либо «в самый недавний», либо «в самый последний»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The last time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the last time* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the last time* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *the last time that*, *the only time*

Points at the most recent occasion, or at the final one. The ambiguity between those two readings is real and usually harmless: *the last time we spoke* normally means "most recently," but in the right context it means "for good."

1. *The last time* I saw him, he still had the beard.
2. The car made that same noise *the last time* we drove to Chicago.
3. *The last time* we tried this, the server crashed.
4. *The last time* the pipes froze, it cost us four hundred dollars.
5. *The last time* she came through town, we missed each other by a day.
6. *The last time* you asked, I gave you the same answer.

**See also:** [the first time](#the-first-time), [next time](#next-time), [every time](#every-time), [since](#since)

### the minute

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈmɪnɪt/ &middot; *thuh-MIN-it*
**Русский:** разг. как только, в ту же минуту как
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The minute* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the minute* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the minute* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *the minute that*

"As soon as," with a conversational edge. Of the four members of this set, *the minute* and *the second* are the spoken ones, *the moment* is the neutral one, and *the instant* is the literary one; the grammar is identical across all four.

1. *The minute* he walked in, everybody stopped talking.
2. Call me *the minute* you hear anything.
3. *The minute* that bell rings, the hallway is a stampede.
4. She knew something was wrong *the minute* she saw his face.
5. *The minute* I sat down, the baby woke up.
6. We'll head out *the minute* the rain lets up.

**See also:** [the moment](#the-moment), [the second](#the-second), [the instant](#the-instant), [as soon as](#as-soon-as)

### the moment

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈmoʊmənt/ &middot; *thuh-MOH-munt*
**Русский:** в тот момент как, как только — нейтральный и самый ходовой член этой группы
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The moment* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the moment* [clause]. &middot; *From the moment* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the moment* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows. The optional *that* is normally dropped.
**Variants:** *the moment that*, *the very moment*, *from the moment*

The neutral and most usable member of the *moment / minute / second / instant* family: it means "as soon as," with more force than *when* and none of the bookishness of *the instant*. Adding *from* converts it from a point into a starting line.

1. *The moment* the doors opened, people started pushing.
2. I knew *the moment* I walked in that I was underdressed.
3. *The moment* she started singing, the room went quiet.
4. Text me *the moment* you land.
5. *From the moment* he took the job, he was looking for the next one.
6. The dog hears the leash *the moment* you touch it.

**See also:** [the minute](#the-minute), [the second](#the-second), [the instant](#the-instant), [as soon as](#as-soon-as), [from the time](#from-the-time)

### the second

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈsɛkənd/ &middot; *thuh-SEK-und*
**Русский:** разг. как только, в ту же секунду как — обычно с досадой или удивлением
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The second* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the second* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the second* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *the second that*, *the split second* (emphatic)

The most colloquial and most impatient of the four: it means "as soon as," and it usually carries a note of exasperation or surprise. Watch the collision with the ordinal — *the second time* means the second occasion, while *the second* on its own means "instantly."

1. *The second* the whistle blew, both benches cleared.
2. He starts complaining *the second* the check comes.
3. *The second* I turned my back, the dog was up on the counter.
4. Let me know *the second* you hear from her.
5. *The second* the movie ended, my phone lit up.
6. She was out the door *the second* her shift ended.

**See also:** [the minute](#the-minute), [the moment](#the-moment), [the instant](#the-instant), [as soon as](#as-soon-as)

### the whole time

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈhoʊl ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *thuh-HOHL-time*
**Русский:** всё то время, пока — отрезки совпадают полностью, часто звучит как упрёк
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *The whole time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the whole time* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the whole time* (bare — an adverb, not a conjunction).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the whole time* clause when that clause comes first; no comma when it follows. The optional *that* is normally dropped.
**Variants:** *the entire time*, *the whole time that*, *all the while*, *the whole while*

The duration member of the *the* + time-noun family: where *the moment* and *the second* name a point, this one covers every bit of a stretch. *While* says two things overlapped; *the whole time* insists the overlap was total, which is why it so often carries an accusation (*he knew the whole time*). It doubles as a bare adverb, and that is the test — in *He knew the whole time* there is no following clause, so there is no conjunction.

1. *The whole time* we were talking, he was checking his phone.
2. She knew about the layoffs *the whole time* we were planning the retreat.
3. *The whole time* the inspector was in the basement, nobody said a word.
4. The dog sat by the front door *the entire time* we were gone.
5. *The whole time* I lived in Chicago, I never made it to a Cubs game.
6. He was standing right behind me *the whole time*. *(bare adverb — no clause follows)*

**See also:** [while](#while), [as long as](#as-long-as), [the moment](#the-moment), [from the time](#from-the-time), [meanwhile](#meanwhile)

### until such time as

**Pronunciation:** /ənˈtɪl sʌtʃ ˈtaɪm əz/ &middot; *un-TIL-such-TIME-uhz*; the final *as* rarely carries stress
**Русский:** юр. до тех пор, пока не — канцелярское until, в обычной речи лишнее
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal and bureaucratic)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Until such time as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *until such time as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *until such time as* [clause] *and no longer*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *until such time as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *till such time as*; *until such time that* is a common miswriting of it

A four-word way to say *until*, at home in contracts, court orders, and policy manuals and almost nowhere else. In ordinary prose it is padding, and editors cut it back to *until*. It survives in legal drafting because that register wants an endpoint stated as an event whose date is unknown.

1. The account remains frozen *until such time as* the court rules otherwise.
2. *Until such time as* a permanent director is appointed, Ms. Reyes will serve in an acting capacity.
3. Benefits continue *until such time as* the employee secures comparable coverage.
4. *Until such time as* the tenant cures the default, the landlord may withhold consent.
5. The materials shall be held in escrow *until such time as* both parties have signed.
6. *Until such time as* the board votes, the current policy stands.

**See also:** [until](#until), [till](#till), [whereupon](#whereupon), [henceforth](#henceforth)

### whereafter

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrˈæftər/ &middot; *wair-AF-ter*
**Русский:** юр./устар. после чего — редчайший вариант after which, почти вне американской практики
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** formal and rare; legal drafting
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time and sequence, always non-restrictive
**Pattern:** [main clause], *whereafter* [clause]. &middot; [long main clause]; *whereafter* [clause]. &middot; There is no fronted skeleton — *Whereafter* [clause], [main clause] is not a sentence.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. Like *whereupon*, it can never open a sentence.
**Variants:** *after which* (the ordinary form), *whereupon* (the same construction with a sense of immediate consequence), *thereafter* (an adverb, not a connector of clauses)

The *where-* compound for "after which," and the rarest member of the family in American English: [whereupon](#whereupon) still lives in literary narrative and *after which* covers everything else, which leaves *whereafter* to contracts, arbitration clauses, and engineering specifications. It is markedly commoner in legal English written in Dutch- and Afrikaans-influenced traditions than in anything drafted in the United States. Use *after which* unless a house style specifically wants the *where-* form.

1. The parties shall confer in good faith for thirty days, *whereafter* either may demand arbitration.
2. The notice was served on the seventeenth, *whereafter* the period for objection began to run.
3. The specimen is heated to four hundred degrees, *whereafter* it is quenched in oil.
4. The chair declared the motion carried, *whereafter* the meeting was adjourned.
5. The tenant shall vacate the premises by noon, *whereafter* any property left behind is deemed abandoned.
6. Ordinary English: The parties confer for thirty days, *after which* either one can demand arbitration.

**See also:** [after which](#after-which), [whereupon](#whereupon), [thereafter](#thereafter), [at which time](#at-which-time), [henceforth](#henceforth)

### whereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; *wair-uh-PON*; also /ˌwɛrəˈpɔn/
**Русский:** книжн. после чего, вслед за чем — с намёком, что первое событие вызвало второе
**Type:** subordinating (connective relative) &middot; **Register:** formal to literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time and consequence, always non-restrictive
**Pattern:** [main clause], *whereupon* [clause]. &middot; [long main clause]; *whereupon* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *whereupon* [clause], [continuation]. &middot; There is no fronted skeleton — *Whereupon* [clause], [main clause] is ungrammatical.
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, or by a semicolon in a long sentence; never followed by one. Unlike every other subordinator in this file, its clause cannot move to the front of the sentence.
**Variants:** *at which point*, *at which time* (the everyday paraphrases), *whereat* (archaic)

Means "and immediately after that," with a hint that the second event was triggered by the first. Because it can only follow its main clause, it works as a narrative connector rather than a scene-setter — which is the opposite of *when* and *after*. *At which point* says the same thing in plain modern English.

1. The witness refused to answer, *whereupon* the judge cleared the courtroom.
2. He announced that he was resigning, *whereupon* the room erupted.
3. The motion failed by one vote, *whereupon* the chair called a recess.
4. She read the letter twice, *whereupon* she folded it and put it in her pocket.
5. The alarm sounded, *whereupon* every door in the building locked automatically.
6. We waited an hour, *at which point* somebody finally told us the flight was canceled.

**See also:** [thereafter](#thereafter), [then](#then), [after](#after), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than)

## British, archaic, and dialect forms

Twelve time subordinators that a learner will meet in reading but should not produce in American English. *Directly* and *immediately* are current British usage, and *whilst* is current British usage with an American afterlife as an affectation. The rest are archaic: *ere*, *or ever*, *so soon as*, *the while*, *what time*, *when that*, *whenas*, and *whiles* belong to the King James Bible, to Shakespeare and his contemporaries, and to nineteenth-century verse and narrative, while *against* is archaic in print but still audible from older speakers in the southern mountains.

### against

**Pronunciation:** /əˈgɛnst/ &middot; *uh-GENST*; also /əˈgeɪnst/ &middot; *uh-GAYNST*; the clipped *'gainst* /gɛnst/ &middot; *GENST* in verse
**Русский:** устар. к тому времени как — с добавочным смыслом «успев подготовиться к этому моменту»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic; still heard from older speakers in Appalachian and Ozark English
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is overwhelmingly a preposition today — *against the wall*, *against the rules* — and only the clause use is the conjunction)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *against* [clause]. &middot; *Against* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [imperative] *against* [clause] (the commonest shape: *have it ready against he comes*).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *against* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. The clause takes the present or the present subjunctive even for future reference — *against he come*, *against the weather turns* — and that bare subjunctive is one of the marks of the old form.
**Variants:** *'gainst* (poetic clipping), *agin* (eye-dialect spelling of the regional pronunciation); *by the time* is the modern equivalent, and *in time for* carries the preparation sense

The old conjunction meaning "by the time that," with an extra sense of getting ready in advance for the moment named: *bake the bread against they come home* is about preparation, not merely sequence. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare and his contemporaries, and in nineteenth-century fiction that reproduces country speech, and it is still occasionally audible from older speakers in the southern mountains. Do not write it: *by the time* carries the timing, *in time for* carries the preparation, and modern readers will simply see the preposition and stall.

1. They made the guest room ready *against* her father came up from Richmond.
2. Have the horses watered *against* the wagons roll at first light.
3. She had put up four quarts of preserves *against* the winter came on.
4. *Against* the parson arrives, somebody had better sweep the porch.
5. Lay in the wood now, *against* the weather turns.
6. Modern equivalent: They had the guest room ready *by the time* her father came up from Richmond.

### directly

**Pronunciation:** /dəˈrɛktli/ &middot; *duh-REKT-lee*; also /daɪˈrɛktli/ &middot; *dye-REKT-lee*
**Русский:** брит. как только — в американском английском как союз не употребляется
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** regional (British and Irish); not current American usage
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is an ordinary adverb in both varieties — *go directly home* — and only the conjunction is foreign to American English)
**Pattern:** *Directly* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *directly* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *directly* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *directly that* (archaic); *as soon as* is the American equivalent

British English uses *directly* as a conjunction meaning "as soon as." American English does not: to an American ear the sentence sounds unfinished, as though *directly* were still an adverb. The adverb is alive and well in the United States — "straight" (*go directly home*), "immediately" (*I'll deal with it directly*), and in Southern speech "in a little while" (*I'll be there directly*) — but only the conjunction is foreign.

1. *Directly* he arrived, the meeting started. *(British)*
2. She recognized him *directly* he spoke. *(British)*
3. *Directly* the rain stopped, they were back on the field. *(British)*
4. I'll write *directly* I hear from the solicitor. *(British)*
5. *Directly* the door closed, the room went quiet. *(British)*
6. American equivalent: *As soon as* he arrived, the meeting started.

### ere

**Pronunciation:** /ɛr/ &middot; *AIR*
**Русский:** устар./поэт. прежде чем, до того как
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Ere* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *ere* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *ere* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *ere long* (an adverb phrase meaning "soon"), *or ever* (biblical)

The archaic word for *before*. It survives in hymns, poetry, Shakespeare, and deliberate period style; in ordinary modern prose it reads as a joke or an affectation. Do not confuse it with *e'er*, which is a contraction of *ever*, or with *err* /ɜr/, which is a verb.

1. *Ere* the sun sets, we must reach the pass.
2. He was gone *ere* the household stirred.
3. *Ere* the last note faded, the audience was on its feet.
4. She vowed to finish the letter *ere* the ship sailed.
5. *Ere* another winter comes, the roof will have to be replaced.
6. Modern equivalent: *Before* the sun sets, we have to reach the pass.

### immediately

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈmidiətli/ &middot; *ih-MEE-dee-ut-lee*
**Русский:** брит. как только — американцы так не говорят и пишут as soon as

**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same word is an ordinary adverb in both varieties — *call me immediately* — and only the conjunction is foreign to American English)
**Pattern:** *Immediately* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *immediately* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *immediately* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *immediately after* (fully standard in American English, with a clause or a noun phrase), *directly*

British English lets *immediately* introduce a clause on its own: *Immediately the results came in, she called.* American English does not — Americans hear the adverb and wait for the sentence to continue — and writes *as soon as* or *immediately after* instead. The plain adverb *immediately* is of course standard in both varieties.

1. *Immediately* the results came in, she called her supervisor. *(British)*
2. He knew *immediately* the door opened that something was wrong. *(British)*
3. *Immediately* the ceremony ended, the caterers moved in. *(British)*
4. *Immediately* the engine cut out, the cabin went silent. *(British)*
5. The porter locked the gates *immediately* the last guest had gone. *(British)*

### or ever

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr ˈɛvər/ &middot; *or-EV-er*
**Русский:** устар. прежде чем ещё, прежде чем вообще — усиленное библейское «до того как»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic and biblical
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *Or ever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *or ever* [clause]. &middot; *Or ever* [clause with a present subjunctive verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *or ever* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. The clause frequently takes the present subjunctive in the texts where the phrase actually appears: *or ever the silver cord be loosed*.
**Variants:** *or e'er* (the poetic spelling, easily confused with *ere*), *or ere* (Shakespearean), *ere*, *before ever*; *before* is the modern equivalent

An emphatic archaic *before*, meaning "before ever" or "before so much as." The *or* has nothing to do with the coordinating conjunction — it is a separate old word that already meant "before," and the *ever* piles emphasis on top of it. An American meets the phrase in the King James Bible, in *Hamlet*, and in hymn texts, and nowhere else; it is not available for modern writing, and even deliberate period pastiche usually reaches for [ere](#ere) instead.

1. *Or ever* the silver cord be loosed, remember thy Creator.
2. He was taken from us *or ever* he had reached his fortieth year.
3. *Or ever* the sun was risen, the whole household was astir.
4. Would I had died *or ever* I saw that letter.
5. They were seized *or ever* they came at the bottom of the stair.
6. Modern equivalent: He was taken from us *before* he ever reached forty.

**See also:** [ere](#ere), [before](#before), [what time](#what-time), [whenas](#whenas), [against](#against)

### so soon as

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈsun æz/ &middot; *soh-SOON-az*; the final *as* reduces to /əz/ &middot; *uhz*
**Русский:** устар. как только — старая форма as soon as, вышедшая из употребления
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic; standard English into the early nineteenth century, obsolete since
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *So soon as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *so soon as* [clause]. &middot; *So soon as ever* [clause], [main clause] (emphatic).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *so soon as* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows — the same rules its modern replacement takes.
**Variants:** *as soon as* (the modern form), *so soon as ever* (emphatic, archaic); compare *so long as*, the one member of the *so* series that survived

The older form of [as soon as](#as-soon-as), ordinary in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, and in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose, and gone from American English by about the time of the Civil War. The paired history is the interesting part: English kept *so long as* alongside *as long as* and still uses both, but let *so soon as* go completely, so a modern reader hears the phrase as a period marker rather than as a live variant. Write *as soon as*.

1. *So soon as* the letter was sealed, he sent it away by the evening post.
2. She resolved to speak with him *so soon as* the company should withdraw.
3. *So soon as* the gates were opened, the crowd pressed in.
4. He begged that word be sent him *so soon as* the child was born.
5. *So soon as ever* the ship was sighted, the whole town went down to the quay.
6. Modern equivalent: *As soon as* the letter was sealed, he sent it out with the evening mail.

### the while

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈwaɪl/ &middot; *thuh-WYLE*
**Русский:** устар./поэт. пока, в то время как — в живом языке осталось лишь all the while
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic; the bare adverb survives in *all the while*
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (duration). The same two words are far commoner as a bare adverb meaning "meanwhile," which builds no clause at all.
**Pattern:** [main clause] *the while* [clause]. &middot; *The while* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the while* (bare adverb — nothing follows). &middot; *All the while* [clause], [main clause] (the surviving modern form).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *the while* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows. No commas at all around the bare adverb at the end of a clause: *he watched her, smiling the while*.
**Variants:** *all the while*, *the whole while* (both fully current), *whiles* (archaic), *while*, *meanwhile*

The archaic conjunction *while* with the definite article welded on, at home in verse, hymn texts, and nineteenth-century narrative: *she sang, the while the fire burned low*. Modern English kept only two pieces of it — the bare adverb (*smiling the while*) and the intensified conjunction *all the while*, which is ordinary today — and left the plain conjunction behind. Read it as a period marker and write *while* or *all the while*.

1. He kept his eyes on the road, *the while* she read the letter aloud.
2. *The while* the choir sang, the candles were carried down the aisle.
3. She sat with her hands folded, praying *the while*. *(bare adverb — no clause follows)*
4. The old man mended his nets, *the while* his grandson counted gulls.
5. *The while* the storm held, not a soul stirred from the inn.
6. Current English: He kept his eyes on the road *all the while* she read the letter aloud.

### what time

**Pronunciation:** /wɑt ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *wot-TIME*; also /wʌt ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *wut-TIME*, and /ʍɑt/ &middot; *HWOT* for speakers who keep the *wh*
**Русский:** устар. в то время когда — библейское и поэтическое «когда»
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic; biblical
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (the same two words open an ordinary question — *What time does it start?* — which is a different construction entirely)
**Pattern:** *What time* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *what time* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *what time* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *what time that* (older still), *when* (the modern form); the interrogative *what time* (*What time is the train?*) is a separate construction, not a variant

The archaic conjunction meaning "at the time when." It is preserved for most American readers in a single line of the King James Psalms — *What time I am afraid, I will trust in thee* — and it turns up elsewhere in Milton, in hymnody, and in nineteenth-century verse. Nothing in modern prose licenses it, and the collision with the everyday question is fatal: a reader who meets *what time the reapers went out* hears a question about the clock and has to back up. Write *when*.

1. *What time* I am afraid, I will trust in thee.
2. *What time* the morning star arose, the watch was set upon the wall.
3. He came to her *what time* the household slept.
4. *What time* the reapers went into the field, the sky was still dark.
5. The bells were rung *what time* the news reached the village.
6. Modern equivalent: *When* I am afraid, I trust in you.

### when that

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn ˈðæt/ &middot; *wen-THAT*; *that* usually reduces to /ðət/ &middot; *thut*
**Русский:** устар. когда — «когда» со старой подчинительной частицей that
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic; standard in Middle and early Modern English
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** *When that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *when that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *when that* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *whan that* (the Middle English spelling), *if that*, *though that*, *before that* (the same old *that*-tagging on other subordinators), *when* (the modern form)

Plain *when* with the old subordinating particle *that* still attached. Middle and early Modern English tagged *that* onto most subordinators — *when that*, *if that*, *though that* — and the language shed the particle across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, keeping it only where it had fused into a new word (*now that*, *so that*, *in that*). Modern readers meet it at the head of the *Canterbury Tales* and in Feste's closing song in *Twelfth Night*. Do not reproduce it outside period pastiche.

1. *When that* I was and a little tiny boy, the rain it raineth every day.
2. *When that* April with his showers sweet had pierced the drought of March, then folk longed to go on pilgrimage.
3. He shall be sorely missed *when that* the summer comes.
4. *When that* the trumpet sounds, the dead shall be raised incorruptible.
5. She wept *when that* the letter was read out to the household.
6. Modern equivalent: *When* I was a little boy, the rain it raineth every day.

### whenas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnˈæz/ &middot; *wen-AZ*; also /ˈwɛnəz/ &middot; *WEN-uhz*
**Русский:** устар. когда, в то время как — елизаветинское слово, сегодня полностью мёртвое
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic (sixteenth- and seventeenth-century English)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time; in some older texts adverbial of contrast instead, where it means "whereas"
**Pattern:** *Whenas* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Whenas* [clause], *then* [main clause] (the correlative shape common in verse). &middot; [main clause] *whenas* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *whenas* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *when as* (two words, the older spelling), *whereas* (the surviving relative of the contrast sense), *when* (the modern form of the time sense)

An Elizabethan and Jacobean conjunction meaning "when" or "at the time when," and occasionally "whereas." The line most Americans have met it in is Herrick's — *Whenas in silks my Julia goes* — and outside seventeenth-century verse it is genuinely dead, not merely formal or literary but obsolete. Note how the family split: the contrast sense survived as *whereas*, which is standard today in contracts and in ordinary argument, while the time sense left the language altogether.

1. *Whenas* in silks my Julia goes, then, then methinks how sweetly flows that liquefaction of her clothes.
2. *Whenas* the day was far spent, they came unto the city.
3. He was ever the first to speak, *whenas* the others held their peace.
4. *Whenas* the frost is on the ground, the roads are hard and quick.
5. *Whenas* she looked upon him, her colour rose.
6. Modern equivalent: *When* my Julia walks by in silks, I think how sweetly her clothes flow.

### whiles

**Pronunciation:** /waɪlz/ &middot; *WYLZ*; also /ʍaɪlz/ &middot; *HWYLZ* for speakers who keep the *wh*
**Русский:** устар. пока, в то время как — старая форма while из Библии короля Иакова
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic; the separate adverb *whiles* meaning "sometimes" survives in Scots and Northern English
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (duration)
**Pattern:** *Whiles* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whiles* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *whiles* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *while*, *whilst*, *the while* — the same word carrying different old endings; the adverbial *whiles* ("sometimes," Scots) is a separate use and not a variant of the conjunction

The old *-s* form of *while*, ordinary in the King James Bible — *agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him* — and out of English prose by the eighteenth century. It sits at one end of a set of four: *while* is the American standard, *whilst* is current British, and *whiles* and *the while* are archaic. Recognize it in reading and leave it in the seventeenth century.

1. Agree with thine adversary quickly, *whiles* thou art in the way with him.
2. *Whiles* the bridegroom tarried, they all slumbered and slept.
3. He kept the watch *whiles* the others rested.

### whilst

**Pronunciation:** /waɪlst/ &middot; *WYLST*
**Русский:** брит. пока, в то время как — то же, что while; в США редко и звучит манерно
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** British; in American English an affectation
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time (also contrast)
**Pattern:** *Whilst* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whilst* [clause]. &middot; *whilst* + [-ing form].
**Punctuation:** The same rules as *while*: comma after the *whilst* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows in the time sense, and a comma when it means "whereas."
**Variants:** *while* (the American form); *amongst* and *amidst* are the same British *-st* pattern on *among* and *amid*, and American editing prefers the shorter form in all three pairs

Identical in meaning to *while* and about as common as *while* in British writing. In American English it is rare, and when it does appear it reads as either British or self-consciously fancy; American style guides recommend *while* in every case. It is in this file so that learners recognize it and know not to reach for it.

1. *Whilst* the committee deliberated, the applicants waited outside. *(British)*
2. She read the report *whilst* the kettle boiled. *(British)*
3. *Whilst* in London, he kept a diary. *(British)*

5. *Whilst* I take your point, the deadline hasn't moved. *(British; contrast sense)*

## Conjunctive adverbs of time

These are not conjunctions. They are adverbs that connect ideas, which means they cannot join two independent clauses with a comma — *We ate, meanwhile the kids played* is a comma splice. Put a semicolon or a period in front of them and a comma after them, and remember the movement test: if the word can slide into the middle or the end of its clause, it is an adverb. A comma is legal in front of them only when a coordinating conjunction is doing the joining: *We ate, and then the kids played.* Four of them make the ordinary narrative sequence — *at first*, *then*, *later*, *finally* — and the rest of the section fills in the formal, the backward-looking, and the overlapping cases.

### afterward

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftərwərd/ &middot; *AF-ter-werd*
**Русский:** потом, после этого — наречие, а не союз: отсылает назад к уже названному событию
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — it modifies a clause; the clause it sits in stays independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *afterward*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Afterward*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *afterward*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *We ate, afterward we walked* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause; no commas at all when it sits at the end (*We walked afterward*).
**Variants:** *afterwards* (standard but British-leaning; American editing prefers *afterward*), *after that*, *later*

The adverb counterpart of the conjunction *after*: it points back at something already mentioned instead of naming it again. Because it is an adverb it can slide around inside its clause, which is exactly the test that separates it from a real conjunction.

1. The ceremony ran long; *afterward*, nobody wanted to stand around for photos.
2. We toured the plant. *Afterward*, they fed us in the cafeteria.
3. She apologized *afterward*, which surprised everybody.
4. He said almost nothing during the meeting and even less *afterward*.
5. Take the pill with food; *afterward*, drink a full glass of water.
6. They stayed friends for a while *afterward*, then drifted apart.

**See also:** [after](#after), [then](#then), [thereafter](#thereafter), [subsequently](#subsequently), [previously](#previously), [later](#later), [meanwhile](#meanwhile)

### at first

**Pronunciation:** /æt ˈfɜrst/ &middot; *at-FURST*; *at* reduces to /ət/ &middot; *uht*
**Русский:** сначала, поначалу — обещает перелом и требует продолжения «а потом»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverbial phrase; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** *At first*, [independent clause]; [*later* / *then* / *eventually*], [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *At first*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *at first*, but [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *at first*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *At first he said no, later he changed his mind* is a comma splice. A comma works only with a coordinating conjunction: *At first he said no, but later he changed his mind.* Comma after it when it opens a clause, though a phrase this short is often left bare (*At first nobody said anything*), and both are standard. No comma when it sits at the end.
**Variants:** *initially* (formal), *first*, *first of all*, *to begin with*, *at the outset* (formal); *at first glance* and *at first blush* are different phrases that concede an appearance rather than mark a time

The opener of a two-part timeline: it plants a first state that something later overturns, which is why it almost always arrives with a partner — *later*, *then*, *eventually*, *but*. Left without one it strands the reader, because *At first, the engine ran fine* is a promise that the engine stopped running fine. *Initially* is the report-writing equivalent and carries the same promise; plain *first* is a list marker and carries none.

1. *At first*, nobody believed the number; then accounting ran it again.
2. She hated the job *at first*, but the people won her over.
3. *At first* the engine ran fine. By the second lap it was smoking.
4. The pay looked good *at first*.
5. *At first*, we thought the noise was the furnace; eventually we found the raccoon.
6. He wasn't going to say anything *at first*, and then he couldn't help himself.

**See also:** [eventually](#eventually), [finally](#finally), [later](#later), [previously](#previously), [then](#then)

### at the same time

**Pronunciation:** /æt ðə ˈseɪm ˌtaɪm/ &middot; *at-thuh-SAME-time*; *at* reduces to /ət/
**Русский:** в то же время — «одновременно», но чаще «и всё же», как уступка без смысла времени
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverbial phrase; its clause remains independent. The variant *at the same time that* is a true subordinator and does take a clause.
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *at the same time*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *At the same time*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] and, *at the same time*, [verb] … &middot; *At the same time that* [clause], [main clause] (the subordinating variant).
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *It's expensive, at the same time nothing else works* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause, and commas on both sides mid-clause. The subordinating *at the same time that* takes the ordinary subordinator rules instead: comma after the clause when it comes first, none when it follows.
**Variants:** *simultaneously* (formal), *meanwhile*, *at the same time that* (subordinating), *all the same* (concessive only — a different phrase)

Does two jobs, and the second one has quietly taken over. Literally it means "simultaneously," the way *meanwhile* does. In practice it far more often means "and yet," conceding a point while holding the line: *It's expensive; at the same time, nothing else does the job.* Read the whole sentence before deciding which is meant, because the concessive use has no time content at all.

1. The store was hiring; *at the same time*, corporate was closing two locations.
2. She wanted the promotion. *At the same time*, she dreaded the travel. *(concessive)*
3. He's the best writer on staff and, *at the same time*, the hardest to edit.
4. *At the same time that* the city was cutting bus routes, it was breaking ground on a streetcar. *(subordinating variant)*
5. The proposal saves money; *at the same time*, it costs us a year.
6. *At the same time*, nobody wants to be the one who says no.

**See also:** [meanwhile](#meanwhile), [in the meantime](#in-the-meantime), [while](#while), [the whole time](#the-whole-time)

### eventually

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈvɛntʃuəli/ &middot; *ih-VEN-choo-uh-lee*; commonly compressed to /ɪˈvɛntʃəli/ &middot; *ih-VEN-chuh-lee*
**Русский:** в конце концов, со временем — после долгого ожидания; ложный друг: не «возможно»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *eventually*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Eventually*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] *eventually* [verb] … &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *eventually*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *We waited, eventually somebody came* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause; no commas at all when it sits inside the clause (*She eventually agreed*) or at the end (*He'll come around eventually*).
**Variants:** *in the end*, *ultimately*, *finally*, *sooner or later* (forward-looking), *at length* (literary)

Puts the second event at the far end of an unspecified delay: "after a while, and it took a while." *Then* is neutral about the gap and *afterward* only says "later"; *eventually* is the one that reports the waiting. It is also a standing trap for learners of Romance languages — Spanish *eventualmente* and French *éventuellement* mean "possibly," not "at last."

1. The line barely moved; *eventually*, they opened a second register.
2. We called four different plumbers. *Eventually*, one of them showed up.
3. She *eventually* stopped asking about it.
4. *Eventually*, somebody is going to have to tell him.
5. The paperwork sat on his desk for a month; *eventually*, HR called him about it.
6. He'll come around *eventually*.

**See also:** [then](#then), [subsequently](#subsequently), [afterward](#afterward), [thereafter](#thereafter), [finally](#finally), [at first](#at-first)

### finally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfaɪnəli/ &middot; *FYE-nuh-lee*; commonly compressed to /ˈfaɪnli/ &middot; *FYNE-lee* in fast speech
**Русский:** наконец — последний пункт перечисления; или «наконец-то», с облегчением или досадой
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *finally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Finally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] *finally* [verb] … &middot; *First* …, *then* …, *finally* … &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *finally*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *We waited two hours, finally somebody came out* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause; no commas when it sits inside the clause (*She finally answered*) or at the end. In a list of steps it takes the comma the last item would take anyway: *…, and finally add the zest.*
**Variants:** *at last*, *in the end*, *ultimately*, *eventually*, *lastly* (list position only), *at length* (literary)

Does two jobs, and a reader has to tell them apart. In a sequence it simply marks the last item — *first … then … finally* — and carries no feeling at all. In a narrative it means "at last, after too long a wait," and carries relief or irritation: *The line finally moved.* [Eventually](#eventually) reports the delay flatly; *finally* reports how the delay felt. *Lastly* only ever does the list job, which is why *The line lastly moved* is impossible.

1. We waited two hours; *finally*, somebody came out to explain.
2. Whisk the eggs, fold in the flour, and *finally* add the zest.
3. She *finally* answered on the fourth try.
4. *Finally*, I'd like to thank the volunteers who set up the room.
5. The permit sat in review all spring. *Finally*, in July, it cleared.
6. He said he'd call back, and after three days he *finally* did.

**See also:** [eventually](#eventually), [at first](#at-first), [then](#then), [subsequently](#subsequently), [thereafter](#thereafter)

### henceforth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛnsˈfɔrθ/ &middot; *hens-FORTH*; also /ˈhɛnsfɔrθ/ &middot; *HENS-forth*
**Русский:** офиц. отныне, впредь — с этого момента и далее действует новое правило
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal (legal and ceremonial)
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *henceforth*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Henceforth*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] shall *henceforth* [verb].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; comma after it when it opens one. No comma when it sits inside a verb phrase: *shall henceforth be known as*.
**Variants:** *henceforward*, *hereafter*, *from now on* (the plain equivalent), *thenceforth* (past reference)

Marks a boundary after which a new rule applies: "starting now and continuing." It belongs to statutes, bylaws, and proclamations; in ordinary prose *from now on* does the same work without the costume. *Thereafter* is its past-reference twin.

1. The building shall *henceforth* be known as the Hall of Sciences.
2. The old form is discontinued; *henceforth*, all requests go through the portal.
3. *Henceforth*, no employee may approve their own expenses.
4. The rate is fixed for two years; *henceforth*, it floats with the index.
5. The committee will *henceforth* meet quarterly rather than monthly. *(mid-clause — no commas)*
6. I've said my piece, and *henceforth* I'll leave the matter to the board.

**See also:** [thereafter](#thereafter), [since then](#since-then), [subsequently](#subsequently), [until such time as](#until-such-time-as), [ere](#ere)

### in the meantime

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈminˌtaɪm/ &middot; *in-thuh-MEEN-time*
**Русский:** тем временем, а пока — в ожидании чего-то предстоящего, часто перед указанием
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverbial phrase; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *in the meantime*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In the meantime*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject], *in the meantime*, [verb] …
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; a comma alone is a splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause, and commas on both sides when it sits mid-clause.
**Variants:** *meantime* (bare, informal), *in the interim* (formal), *meanwhile*

Fills the gap between now and an expected event: "while we wait for that to happen." *Meanwhile* simply reports that two things overlap; *in the meantime* almost always implies a pending outcome, which is why it so often introduces an instruction.

1. The permit takes six weeks; *in the meantime*, we can order materials.
2. Your replacement laptop ships Monday. *In the meantime*, use the loaner.
3. *In the meantime*, nobody is to speak to the press.
4. She's still waiting on the diagnosis and, *in the meantime*, trying not to read anything online.
5. The road reopens in the spring; *in the meantime*, take the county highway.
6. *In the meantime*, I'd keep a copy of everything you send them.

**See also:** [meanwhile](#meanwhile), [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [while](#while), [until](#until), [then](#then), [later](#later)

### later

**Pronunciation:** /ˈleɪtər/ &middot; *LAY-ter*; the *t* is a flap in ordinary American speech, /ˈleɪɾər/
**Русский:** позже, спустя — просто «дальше по времени», без указания величины промежутка
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent (the same word is also the comparative form of the adjective *late*, where it modifies a noun and connects nothing: *a later flight*)
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *later*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Later*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *later*. &middot; [measure phrase] *later*, [independent clause] (*Two weeks later, the check arrived*).
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *We ate dinner, later we walked to the pier* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause; no comma when it sits at the end (*We walked to the pier later*). The measure-phrase opener takes a comma after the whole phrase: *Ten minutes later, the power came back.*
**Variants:** *later on* (conversational), *afterward*, *subsequently* (formal), *[measure] later* (*an hour later*, *years later*); *see you later* is a fixed farewell and not a connector

The plainest way to put one event after another without committing to how big the gap was. [Afterward](#afterward) points specifically back at the event just named; *later* only says "further along the line." Its most useful form is the measure-phrase opener, which is how English narrative moves the clock in a single stroke: *Ten minutes later, the power came back.* Watch the collision with the adjective — *a later flight* is a modifier inside a noun phrase and joins nothing to anything.

1. We ate dinner at the hotel; *later*, we walked down to the pier.
2. Two weeks *later*, the check finally arrived.
3. She said she'd think about it. *Later*, she admitted she'd already decided.
4. He apologized, though only much *later*.
5. The store closed in March; *later on*, a bakery took the space.
6. I'll deal with the email *later*.

**See also:** [afterward](#afterward), [subsequently](#subsequently), [thereafter](#thereafter), [eventually](#eventually), [then](#then), [previously](#previously)

### meanwhile

**Pronunciation:** /ˈminˌwaɪl/ &middot; *MEEN-wyle*
**Русский:** тем временем — наречие: два события идут параллельно, но оба остаются самостоятельными
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *meanwhile*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Meanwhile*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject], *meanwhile*, [verb] …
**Punctuation:** This is the rule learners get wrong most often. *Meanwhile* is an adverb, not a conjunction, so *The kids napped, meanwhile I cleaned* is a comma splice. Use a semicolon or a period before it and a comma after it; mid-clause it takes commas on both sides.
**Variants:** *meantime*, *in the meantime*, *all the while*

The adverb that does the job of the conjunction *while*: it reports that a second situation was going on at the same time, without subordinating either one. Use *while* when you want one clause tucked inside the other; use *meanwhile* when both deserve to stand as sentences.

1. The kids napped; *meanwhile*, I cleaned the entire kitchen.
2. Sales fell for the third straight quarter. *Meanwhile*, costs kept climbing.
3. He was still arguing with the gate agent. His flight, *meanwhile*, was boarding.
4. *Meanwhile*, back at the office, nobody had noticed he was gone.
5. The city debated the plan for two years; *meanwhile*, the building sat empty.
6. She trained all winter. *Meanwhile*, her old teammates had moved up a division.

**See also:** [while](#while), [in the meantime](#in-the-meantime), [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [then](#then), [afterward](#afterward)

### next thing you know

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛkst θɪŋ ju ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *NEKST-thing-yuh-NOH*; *you* almost always reduces to /jə/, and the whole phrase runs together as /ˈnɛksθɪŋjəˈnoʊ/ &middot; *NEKS-thing-yuh-NOH*
**Русский:** разг. и вот уже, глядь — резкий, незапланированный поворот в рассказе
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken narrative)
**Clause type:** none — a fixed adverbial formula; the clause it sits in front of stays independent
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Next thing you know*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]; *next thing you know*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], and *the next thing I knew*, [independent clause]. &middot; *The next thing* [subject] *knew*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *He borrowed the truck, next thing you know it's in Tulsa* is a comma splice, common as that is in speech. Comma after it when it opens a sentence; in the run-on spoken form joined by *and*, the comma after it is usually left out.
**Variants:** *the next thing I knew*, *the next thing we knew*, *the next thing you knew* (past narrative), *before you know it* (forward-looking), *first thing you know* (regional)

The storyteller's jump cut. It marks a turn that was fast, unplanned, and slightly out of the speaker's hands, which is why it almost always introduces something absurd or unwelcome — nobody says *next thing you know, everything went according to plan*. The present tense keeps the story vivid even when the events are decades old, while the past form (*the next thing I knew*) is the one that fits written narrative. Grammatically it connects nothing: it is an adverbial formula parked in front of an independent clause, so it takes a period or a semicolon in front of it and never a bare comma.

1. He said he'd stay for one drink. *Next thing you know*, he's doing karaoke.
2. I let the kid borrow the truck for an afternoon; *next thing you know*, it's registered in Tulsa.
3. You start with one tomato plant, and *the next thing you know* the whole yard is tomatoes.
4. The car spun once on the ice, and *the next thing I knew* we were facing the wrong way in the median.
5. She agreed to help with one bake sale. *Next thing you know*, she was running the PTA.
6. A pipe froze in January; *next thing you know*, we were replacing the entire kitchen floor.

**See also:** [then](#then), [meanwhile](#meanwhile), [eventually](#eventually), [the second](#the-second), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [later](#later)

### previously

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpriviəsli/ &middot; *PREE-vee-uss-lee*
**Русский:** книжн. ранее, до этого — зеркало subsequently, обычное в отчётах и новостях
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *previously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Previously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] had *previously* [past participle]. &middot; *previously* [past participle] (inside a modifier: *previously unreported payments*).
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *The bridge reopened Monday, previously traffic went through Elmwood* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause. No commas when it sits inside a verb phrase (*had previously testified*) or in front of a participle (*previously undisclosed*).
**Variants:** *earlier*, *before that*, *beforehand*, *formerly* (a former identity rather than an earlier event), *hitherto* (formal, "up to now")

The mirror of *subsequently*: it points back to something that happened before the moment under discussion. Reports and news writing lean on it because it keeps a timeline explicit without spending a whole *before* clause on it. *Formerly* is not a substitute — *formerly the county courthouse* names an old identity, while *previously scheduled* names an earlier event.

1. The bridge reopened Monday; *previously*, traffic had been routed through Elmwood.
2. She *previously* worked for the state auditor.
3. The company denied the report. *Previously*, it had refused to comment at all.
4. Two of the samples had *previously* tested clean.
5. *Previously*, only department heads could approve overtime.
6. The filing lists several *previously* undisclosed payments.

**See also:** [subsequently](#subsequently), [afterward](#afterward), [at first](#at-first), [before](#before), [thereafter](#thereafter), [since then](#since-then)

### since then

**Pronunciation:** /sɪns ˈðɛn/ &middot; *sinss-THEN*
**Русский:** с тех пор — наречие вместо союза since; так же тянет за собой перфект
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverbial phrase; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *since then*, [independent clause with a perfect tense]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Since then*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] [has/have] [past participle] *since then*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — *He moved out in March, since then the place has been quiet* is a comma splice. Comma after it when it opens a clause; no comma when it sits at the end.
**Variants:** *ever since* (as a bare adverb: *He hasn't called ever since*), *from then on*, *since that time* (formal), *thenceforth* (archaic)

The adverb that does what the conjunction *since* does, except that it points back at a moment already on the page instead of naming one: *since then* is *since* plus an anaphor. Like the conjunction it pulls a perfect tense into its clause. *From then on* is the forward-rolling twin and does not need the perfect.

1. The plant closed in 2011; *since then*, the population has dropped by half.
2. She had one bad experience with a landlord. *Since then*, she's read every lease twice.
3. Nobody has heard from him *since then*.
4. *Since then*, the rules have changed twice.
5. They repaved the road in June; *since then*, the potholes have stayed away.
6. He apologized once, and *from then on* the subject never came up again.

### subsequently

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbsəkwəntli/ &middot; *SUB-suh-kwunt-lee*
**Русский:** офиц. впоследствии, затем — только «после», но не «вследствие» (ср. consequently)
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *subsequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Subsequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] was *subsequently* [past participle].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; comma after it when it opens one. No commas when it sits inside a verb phrase: *was subsequently withdrawn*.
**Variants:** *later*, *afterward*, *thereafter* (less formal or more literary equivalents)

The formal-register way to say "afterward," standard in reports, case histories, and news writing. It carries no implication of cause, which is the trap: *subsequently* means "after," *consequently* means "therefore," and swapping them changes the claim.

1. The drug was approved in 2019 and *subsequently* withdrawn.
2. He denied the allegation; *subsequently*, he resigned.
3. *Subsequently*, the committee revised its own recommendation.
4. Two of the three witnesses *subsequently* changed their statements.
5. The property was sold at auction and *subsequently* demolished.
6. The paper was published in March. *Subsequently*, three labs failed to replicate it.

### then

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛn/ &middot; *THEN*; weak /ðən/
**Русский:** потом, затем — главный разговорный маркер последовательности; также «тогда» в следствии
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *then* [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], and *then* [independent clause]. &middot; [subject] *then* [verb] … &middot; *First* …, *then* …, *finally* …
**Punctuation:** *Then* is an adverb, not a conjunction, so a comma alone cannot hold two independent clauses together with it: *We ate, then we left* is a comma splice. Use a semicolon or a period, or add a coordinating conjunction — *We ate; then we left*, *We ate. Then we left.*, *We ate, and then we left.* Unlike *however* and *meanwhile*, *then* normally takes no comma after it. In the *if … then* pairing the comma goes before *then*, not after it.
**Variants:** *and then*, *but then*, *right then*, *just then*, *back then*, *by then*

The workhorse sequencer of spoken English: it puts one event after another. The comma splice with *then* is so common in speech-like prose that it barely registers, but it is still a splice, and adding *and* is the painless fix. *Then* also has a resultative use (*if that's true, then we have a problem*) and a noun-modifying one (*the then governor*).

1. Brown the onions; *then* add the garlic.
2. We finished unloading the boxes, and *then* somebody suggested pizza.
3. She looked at the bill, *then* looked at me. *(no splice — one subject, two verbs)*
4. First rinse the beans; *then* soak them overnight.
5. If the light's still red, *then* the sensor never reset.
6. He was quiet for a long moment. *Then* he laughed.

**See also:** [at first](#at-first), [finally](#finally), [later](#later), [afterward](#afterward), [subsequently](#subsequently), [thereafter](#thereafter), [meanwhile](#meanwhile), [when](#when)

### thereafter

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrˈæftər/ &middot; *thair-AF-ter*
**Русский:** офиц. после этого, с того времени — обращённый в прошлое двойник henceforth
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** none — an adverb; its clause remains independent
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *thereafter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Thereafter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [time expression] *thereafter*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; comma after it when it opens one. No comma inside a fixed phrase like *monthly thereafter* or *shortly thereafter*.
**Variants:** *shortly thereafter*, *soon thereafter*, *immediately thereafter*, *thenceforth* (archaic)

"After that time," pointing back at a moment already established. It is the past-reference partner of *henceforth* and lives in contracts, obituaries, and formal narrative, where *afterward* is the everyday word. The frozen phrase *shortly thereafter* is the form most Americans actually produce.

1. The first payment is due on signing, and monthly *thereafter*.
2. He was elected in 1974 and served four terms *thereafter*.
3. The plant closed in March. *Thereafter*, the town lost a third of its population.
4. She left the company in 2019 and shortly *thereafter* started her own firm.
5. The lease renews automatically each year *thereafter* unless either party objects.
6. The alarm was disabled at 2:14 a.m.; *thereafter*, the cameras recorded nothing.
