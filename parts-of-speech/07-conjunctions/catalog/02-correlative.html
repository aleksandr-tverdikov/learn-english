Correlative conjunctions (paired)#

The fixed two-part joiners — both … and, either … or, no sooner … than — where one half marks each of the two things being joined and neither half can stand alone.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

A correlative conjunction is a pair: the first half sits in front of the first element, the second half in front of the second, and both are obligatory — drop either one and the sentence breaks. (The systematic exceptions are the then of if … then, the yet of though … yet, and the there of where … there — all three adverbs rather than conjunctions, and all three droppable.) Because they coordinate, the two elements they join have to be grammatically parallel, which makes placement the rule that governs the entire class: each half goes immediately before the element it marks, and the two halves move inward or outward together. She not only wrote the script but also directed the film balances two verb phrases; she not only wrote the script but also the film balances a verb phrase against a noun and is broken. Three of these pairs also change subject-verb agreement: with either … or, neither … nor, and not only … but also, the verb agrees with whichever subject sits nearer to it, while both … and is always plural.

Punctuation across the class is mostly a matter of leaving it alone: no comma between the halves when the pair joins words or phrases. Two exceptions are worth memorizing, and one look-alike is worth resisting. First, when a pair ends up joining two full independent clauses, the second half takes a comma before it, exactly like any coordinating conjunction — Either you tell him, or I will. Second, when the first half fronts a clause or an adjunct ahead of the main clause, close that fronted stretch with a comma at the seam: The more you practice, the easier it gets; Just as a garden needs weeding, so a budget needs pruning; If it rains, then we'll move inside; Whether you like it or not, we're going; As soon as the door closed, everyone started talking; Though the evidence was thin, yet the jury convicted; What with the traffic and the rain, we were late. The rule behind all of them is the general one — a fronted subordinate half takes the comma; the same half sitting second does not. Reverse them and the comma normally goes away: we're going whether you like it or not, a budget needs pruning just as a garden needs weeding, everyone started talking as soon as the door closed. Concession is the single exception: a trailing though clause keeps its comma, because it contrasts with the main clause instead of restricting it — nobody paid to fix it, though the roof leaked all spring. The look-alike to resist is the immediate-sequence group (no sooner … than, hardly/scarcely/barely … when). Fronting no sooner looks like the fronting above but takes no comma; what it takes is subject-auxiliary inversion — No sooner had we sat down than…, never No sooner we had sat down.

Addition, alternative, and exclusion#

The coordinating pairs. These join two elements of equal grammatical rank and carry the parallelism and agreement rules that define the class.

as well … as#

Pronunciation
/æz ˈwɛl/ … /æz/, /əz/ · az-WEL … AZ, uhz
Русский
устар. как …, так и … — старая расщеплённая форма as well as: и то, и другое
Type
correlative (the split form of the quasi-coordinator as well as; the first as is a degree adverb and well an adverb, so only the second as is the conjunction — unsplit, the same three words are also the equative of she plays as well as her brother)
Register
archaic; survives in legal and older literary prose
Clause type
coordinate elements — joins noun phrases, prepositional phrases, and adjectives
Pattern
as well [X] as [Y] · as well [prepositional phrase] as [prepositional phrase] · [verb] as well [object] as [object] · as well [subject] as [subject] + plural verb · as well [adjective] as [adjective]
Punctuation
No comma between the halves. The commas that fence the modern as well as when it interrupts a subject and its verb (The manager, as well as the interns, was invited) have no equivalent here, because the split frame coordinates the two items instead of adding one parenthetically.
Variants
as well as (modern, unsplit), both … and (the ordinary modern equivalent), not only … but also

The old split arrangement of as well as: the well stays with the first item and the second as introduces the other, so as well the rich as the poor means both of them. Because both items sit inside the frame it coordinates like both … and, where the modern unsplit as well as leaves the first item grammatically in charge and the verb singular. Nothing but fossilized phrasing survives in current American English — write both X and Y or X as well as Y.

  1. The statute binds as well the buyer as the seller.
  2. The fire took as well the barn as the house.
  3. He was remembered as well for his temper as for his generosity.
  4. As well the officers as the men were paid out of the same fund.
  5. The collection holds letters as well in French as in English.
  6. The charter protected as well those born in the town as those who settled there later.

/ˈiðər/, /ˈaɪðər/ … /ɔr/, /ər/ · EE-thur (also EYE-thur) … OR, er
Русский
или …, или …; либо …, либо … — глагол согласуется с ближайшим подлежащим
Type
correlative (either on its own is a determiner, a pronoun, and a negative-echo adverb — either way, I don't either; the or half is the ordinary coordinator)
Register
neutral
Clause type
coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
Pattern
either [X] or [Y] · either [subject] or [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one · [subject] either [verb phrase] or [verb phrase] · [verb] either [object] or [object] · [subject] + be + either [complement] or [complement] · Either [independent clause], or [independent clause].

No comma between the halves when they join words or phrases. Put a comma before or only when it joins two independent clauses: Either you tell him, or I will.   3. Either you tell him, or I will.
  4. The paint comes in either matte or gloss.
  5. She's either brilliant or completely reckless.
  6. Either finish the report or hand it off — I don't care which.

neither … nor#

Pronunciation
/ˈniðər/, /ˈnaɪðər/ … /nɔr/ · NEE-thur (also NYE-thur) … NOR
Русский
ни …, ни … — отрицание уже в самой паре, поэтому глагол остаётся положительным
Type
correlative (neither on its own is a determiner, a pronoun, and an adverb — neither one, me neither; nor is also a plain coordinator)
Register
neutral, leaning formal in speech
Clause type
coordinate elements, or two coordinate independent clauses
Pattern
neither [X] nor [Y] · neither [subject] nor [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one · [subject] neither [verb phrase] nor [verb phrase] · [subject] + be + neither [complement] nor [complement] · [verb] neither [object] nor [object] · Neither [independent clause], nor [inverted independent clause].
Punctuation
No comma between short paired elements. Use a comma before nor when it joins two independent clauses, each of which then inverts: Neither did he call, nor did he write.

Denies both alternatives at once; it is the exact negative of both … and. The pair already carries the negation, so the verb stays positive — Neither of them was invited, never wasn't invited. Everyday American speech usually reaches for not … or instead and saves neither … nor for writing.

  1. Neither the manager nor the assistants were told about the merger.
  2. He neither apologized nor offered to pay for the window.
  3. The soup was neither hot nor cold.
  4. Neither Maria nor the twins are driving.
  5. She neither confirmed nor denied the report.
  6. Neither rain nor snow keeps her off that trail.
  7. Neither did he call, nor did he write.

nor … nor#

Pronunciation
/nɔr/ … /nɔr/, reduced /nər/ · NOR … NOR, nur (the doubled form is rhetorical, so both halves normally keep full stress; only in running prose does the first flatten)
Русский
устар., поэт. ни …, ни … — то же, что neither … nor, но только в стихах и Библии
Type
correlative (both halves are the ordinary coordinator nor, the one FANBOYS member that carries its own negation; the same nor is the second half of neither … nor and the clause coordinator of He didn't call, nor did he write)
Register
archaic and poetic — an American meets it in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, hymn texts, and nineteenth-century verse, and essentially nowhere else
Clause type
coordinate elements — joins noun phrases, adjectives, and prepositional phrases, all of them negated
Pattern
nor [X] nor [Y] · nor [subject] nor [subject] + verb agreeing with the nearer one · [verb] nor [object] nor [object] · [subject] + be + nor [complement] nor [complement] · nor [X], nor [Y], nor [Z] (extended run)
Punctuation
No comma between the halves in a two-item pair. Older printing commas every item in a longer run — nor brass, nor stone, nor earth — and that is the style to copy in period voice. The negation sits in the nor itself, so the verb stays positive: nor gold nor threat could move him, never couldn't move him.
Variants
neither … nor (the ordinary modern form), nor … nor … nor (extended run), not … nor, neither … or (nonstandard)

The neither dropped and the coordinator doubled in its place, so the opening nor does the announcing that neither normally does. The meaning is identical to neither … nor; only the register differs, and it differs enormously — this is verse and scripture, not a choice available in modern writing. Agreement follows the same rule as its modern twin: the verb matches whichever subject stands nearer to it.

  1. Nor gold nor threat could move him from his purpose.
  2. Nor sun nor star was seen for three days together.
  3. There is nor peace nor profit in that quarrel.
  4. Nor the captain nor his officers were ashore that night.
  5. Nor by force nor by persuasion would the old woman give up the deed.
  6. The wind spared nor roof, nor rick, nor fence along the whole valley.
  7. Nor death nor life shall part us from that love.

Pronunciation
/nɑt/ … /bʌt/, /bət/ · NOT … BUT, buht
Русский
не …, а … — первое отвергается и заменяется вторым, верным
Type
correlative (but is also a plain coordinator and a preposition meaning "except" — everyone but me)
Register

Pattern

  3. The problem is not the price but the delivery time.
  4. She's not angry, but disappointed.
  5. We canceled not because the numbers were bad but because the timing was wrong.
  6. The best players are not the strongest but the most patient.

Alternation, distribution, and enumeration#

Pairs that divide something into two shares rather than joining two things. Not one of these halves is a conjunction in the strict sense — they are adverbs and adverbial phrases — but each first half opens a slot that the second half has to fill, and that expectation is what makes them behave as correlatives. Because both halves are adjuncts rather than coordinated elements, the parallelism rule loosens and the punctuation rule tightens: every pair here takes a comma between the halves, and where the halves front two independent clauses, that comma has to become a semicolon or a period.

Pronunciation
/fɔr ˈwʌn θɪŋ/ … /fɔr əˈnʌðər/ · for-WUN-thing … for-uh-NUH-thur
Русский
разг. во-первых …, во-вторых … — перечисление доводов к уже высказанному мнению
Type
correlative frame (both halves are prepositional phrases used as sentence adverbials, not conjunctions; for is elsewhere a preposition and the causal coordinator of the FANBOYS)
Register
informal to neutral, mostly spoken
Clause type
none — each half is a sentence adverbial standing in front of its own independent clause
Pattern

A comma after each half, separating it from the clause it introduces. Between the two clauses use a period or a semicolon — a comma alone leaves a splice, since both halves are adverbials and neither one joins anything.
Variants

Enumerates reasons for a judgment already announced, and claims by its shape that there is more than one. It is the conversational alternative to first … second, and the second half is often never supplied at all — for one thing on its own already promises a list.

  1. For one thing, the rent is too high. For another, the commute runs an hour each way.
  2. I wouldn't buy that car — for one thing it's out of warranty, for another the parts take weeks.
  3. For one thing, nobody asked us; for another, nobody paid us.
  4. She'd never last as a line cook: for one thing, she hates crowds; for another, she can't stand the heat.
  5. For one thing, the data is two years old. For another thing, half the responses came from one county.
  6. He shouldn't be driving. For one thing, he can't read the signs anymore.

Immediate sequence#

Four pairs that say the second event landed almost on top of the first. All four put the earlier event in the past perfect, all four require subject-auxiliary inversion when the negative adverb is fronted, and all four take no comma between the halves. The one thing that separates them is the second half: no sooner is a comparative and demands than, while hardly, scarcely, and barely take when (or, a little less often, before). No sooner … when and hardly … than are the two crossover errors to watch for.

Condition, concession, range, and cause#

Pairs whose halves are not both conjunctions in the strict sense — between, from, and what with are prepositions, be it is an inverted subjunctive verb, and the then of if … then, the yet of though … yet, and the there of where … there are adverbs — but which behave as correlatives because the first half fixes what the second half has to be. Three of them, if … then, though … yet, and where … there, are also the only pairs in the category whose second half can simply be dropped; everywhere else, losing a half breaks the sentence.
