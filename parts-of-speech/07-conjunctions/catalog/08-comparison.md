# Subordinators of comparison and manner

> The conjunctions that measure one thing against another — more, less, or equal — and the ones that say in what manner something is done.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every conjunction in this file answers one of two questions: *how much* compared to what, or *in what way*. What unites them grammatically is that they almost never work alone — the subordinator is the second half of a frame whose first half sits earlier in the sentence, in a comparative word (*taller*, *more slowly*, *fewer*) or a correlative marker (*as*, *so*, *the same*, *no sooner*). Learn the pairs, not the single words: *than* is meaningless without a comparative in front of it, and a lone *as* is a different conjunction from *as … as*.

The clause they introduce is normally **elliptical** — English deletes from the comparative clause everything the main clause already supplied, which is why *She's taller than I am* shrinks to *than I* and then, in speech, to *than me*. Both are current American English: *than I am* and *as tall as he is* are the safe written forms, the bare objective (*than me*, *as tall as him*) is the normal spoken one, and *than whom* is the single place where the objective case is fixed.

**The punctuation rule that runs through the whole category: comparative clauses are integral to the sentence, so they take no comma.** Write *She finished faster than anyone expected*, never *faster, than anyone expected*. There are four exceptions, and they are worth memorizing. (1) The paired frames whose halves are two full clauses — *the more … the more* and *as … so* — take a comma at the seam. (2) Any clause in this file that is moved to the **front** of the main clause takes a comma after it: *As far as I know, the office is closed*. (3) The supplementary markers — *whereas*, contrastive *while*, *as compared with*, *in comparison with*, *in contrast to*, *unlike*, *as opposed to*, *as against*, *as distinct from*, *as distinguished from*, *as with*, and often *compared to* — are set off with commas, because they comment on the main clause rather than complete it. The everyday *compared to* is the loose one: fronted it always takes a comma (*Compared to last winter, this one has been mild*), but a short trailing phrase usually drops it (*The rent is reasonable compared to what friends pay downtown*). (4) *Than whom* takes commas on both sides, because it heads a supplementary relative clause instead of finishing a comparison.

**One rule from outside this file belongs here, because it is where these commas actually go wrong.** *However* leads two lives. The subordinator catalogued below — the one meaning "in whatever way" — takes a comma only when its clause is fronted: *However you slice it, the numbers don't work*. The **conjunctive adverb** *however*, the one that means "but," cannot join two independent clauses with a comma. That is a comma splice: ✗ *We were late, however they held our table.* It requires a semicolon or a period — *We were late; however, they held our table* or *We were late. However, they held our table.* The same holds for every conjunctive adverb of comparison and contrast: *likewise*, *similarly*, *conversely*, *by contrast*, *instead*, *on the other hand*, *in comparison*. A comma alone will not carry any of them. Only the true coordinating conjunctions — *and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet* — join two independent clauses with a comma **before** them (*The northern route is faster, but the coastal road is prettier*), and none of the seven live in this file.

## Comparison of inequality: the *than* family

These attach to a comparative form earlier in the sentence. The comparative clause itself never takes a comma before *than*: *faster than anyone expected*, not *faster, than anyone expected*. Four entries in this section are exceptions, and each is an exception because it is not a plain comparison — *than whom* heads a supplementary relative clause and takes commas on both sides, *rather than* takes commas when it is a parenthetical afterthought rather than a linked alternative, the rhetorical *how much more / how much less* is punctuated off from the clause that sets it up, and the Latin *a fortiori* is not a subordinator at all but a conjunctive adverb, which is why it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it between two independent clauses.

### a fortiori

**Pronunciation:** /ˌeɪ ˌfɔrʃiˈɔraɪ/, also /ˌeɪ ˌfɔrtiˈɔri/ &middot; *ay for-shee-OR-eye*, also *ay for-tee-OR-ee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (Latin) of comparative inference; also attributive, as an adjective before a noun (*an a fortiori argument*) &middot; **Register:** formal; Latin — the register of judicial opinions, briefs, law reviews, and analytic philosophy
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial marking its clause as following with even greater force from what the previous clause already granted
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *a fortiori*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *A fortiori*, [independent clause]. &middot; *If* [weaker case], *then a fortiori* [stronger case]. &middot; [main clause], *and a fortiori* [phrase]. &middot; *an a fortiori* [argument / case / reason] (attributive).
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a clause. Between two independent clauses it is an adverb, not a conjunction, so it takes a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a comma splice: ✗ *The rule bars a dinner, a fortiori it bars a car.* ✓ *The rule bars a dinner; a fortiori, it bars a car.* Only *and* can carry it across with a comma (*…, and a fortiori…*). No commas in the attributive use. Set it in roman in American legal writing; it has been naturalized long enough not to need italics.
**Variants:** *how much more* (the English rhetorical frame), *all the more so*, *even more so*, *a maiore ad minus* and *a minore ad maius* (the two directions, in legal Latin)

Latin for "from the stronger [argument]": it says that a point already conceded in a weaker case must hold with greater force in a stronger one. It is the Latin name of the *how much more* frame, and the difference is what each one does with the move — *how much more* asks a rhetorical question, while *a fortiori* asserts a conclusion, which is why briefs and philosophy papers use it where a sermon uses the English. An American reader meets it in court opinions, appellate briefs, and academic argument, and essentially nowhere else; in ordinary prose *all the more so* carries the same idea without the Latin.

1. If a warrant is required to search a car, then *a fortiori* one is required to search a home.
2. The statute bars a legislator from accepting a fifty-dollar dinner; *a fortiori*, it bars accepting a car.
3. Congress may regulate the sale of the drug; *a fortiori*, it may regulate its manufacture.
4. She had no duty to volunteer the information, and *a fortiori* no duty to repeat it.
5. The court found the weaker record in *Ramirez* insufficient, and this record is thinner still — an *a fortiori* case if there ever was one.
6. If a grown adult could not have understood the waiver, *a fortiori* a fourteen-year-old could not.

**See also:** [how much more / how much less](#how-much-more--how-much-less), [no less than](#no-less-than), [mutatis mutandis](#mutatis-mutandis), [than](#than), [qua](#qua)

### any more than

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛni ˈmɔr ðən/, /ˌɛni ˈmɔr ðæn/ &middot; *EN-ee MOR thuhn*
**Type:** correlative comparative frame (subordinating), negative-polarity — it needs a negative, a question, or another non-affirmative context earlier in the sentence &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison (paired denial)
**Pattern:** [negative clause] *any more than* [clause]. &middot; [negative clause] *any more than* [noun phrase] (elliptical). &middot; *Not* [X] *any more than* [Y]. &middot; [question] *any more than* [clause]?
**Punctuation:** No comma before *any more than*, and none inside the frame.
**Variants:** *no more … than* (the affirmative twin), *not … any more than*, *no more than*

The negative-polarity form of *no more … than*: it denies the main clause and the compared clause in one stroke, so *I can't rebuild a transmission any more than I can land a plane* says that both are beyond me. It cannot stand in a plain positive sentence — something non-affirmative (*not*, *never*, *hardly*, or a question) has to license it. Keep the two words apart: *any more* here is a degree phrase, not the adverb *anymore* meaning "these days."

1. I can't rebuild a transmission *any more than* I can land a plane.
2. She doesn't set the schedule *any more than* the rest of us do.
3. A new logo won't fix the company *any more than* fresh paint fixes a cracked foundation.
4. He never asked for this *any more than* you did.
5. Nobody enjoys the paperwork *any more than* the inspectors do.
6. Does a title make him an expert *any more than* a uniform makes me a pilot?

**See also:** [no more than](#no-more-than), [no less than](#no-less-than), [than](#than), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as)

### how much more / how much less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈmɔr/, /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *how much MOR, how much LESS*
**Type:** rhetorical comparative frame (a fortiori) — a *wh-* degree phrase heading an elliptical clause, not a plain subordinator &middot; **Register:** formal, rhetorical, literary
**Clause type:** exclamatory or interrogative comparative clause, usually elliptical, hanging off a clause that supplies the standard
**Pattern:** *If* [clause], *how much more* [phrase]? &middot; [clause]; *how much less* [phrase]. &middot; *How much more* [adj/adv] [noun phrase] [verb phrase]! &middot; [clause] — *how much more so* [phrase].
**Punctuation:** A comma or a semicolon at the seam, because the second half is a separate rhetorical move rather than a completion of the first. A question mark or an exclamation point at the end, depending on whether it is put as a question or an exclamation. No comma inside the frame.
**Variants:** *how much the more* (archaic), *how much more so*, *all the more*, *a fortiori* (the Latin name for the move)

The a-fortiori frame: it takes a case the reader has already granted and argues that the point holds with even greater force in the second case. *How much more* pushes the conclusion up from a small case to a large one, and *how much less* pushes it down after a negative. The second half is almost always elliptical, and it is a rhetorical question — no answer is expected or wanted.

1. If one missed payment dents your credit, *how much more* a foreclosure?
2. They wouldn't listen to their own auditor; *how much less* to a stranger with a spreadsheet.
3. If the draft was too long, *how much more* the version with the appendices.
4. Children notice when adults are tired — *how much more* when adults are angry.
5. If we can't keep one office running, *how much less* a national network of them.
6. *How much more* difficult the climb becomes once the weather turns.

**See also:** [no less than](#no-less-than), [no more than](#no-more-than), [than](#than), [as … so](#as--so)

### no less than

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈlɛs ðən/, /noʊ ˈlɛs ðæn/ &middot; *noh LESS thuhn*
**Type:** correlative comparative frame (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** [X] *is no less* [adj] *than* [Y] (*is*). &middot; [clause] [verb] *no less than* [clause]. &middot; *No less than* [quantity / name] [verb phrase]. &middot; [X] *is no less a* [noun] *than* [Y].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *than*. No comma inside the frame.
**Variants:** *none the less than* (archaic), *no less a … than* (with a noun: "no less a figure than")

Asserts that the first term measures up to the second, usually with the rhetorical suggestion that people underrate it. Where *no more … than* denies the quality of both terms at once, *no less … than* affirms it of both, and the bare *no less than* before a number or a name means "and that is a lot" rather than "at most."

1. The sequel is *no less* ambitious *than* the original.
2. Her second novel was *no less* well received *than* her first.
3. He was *no less* surprised *than* I was to see her name on the list.
4. *No less than* the mayor herself showed up to cut the ribbon.
5. The repairs will cost *no less than* they did last spring.
6. Two summers on a job site taught him *no less than* four years of classes had.
7. Losing the grant was *no less a* setback *than* losing the lease had been.

**See also:** [no more than](#no-more-than), [than](#than), [as much as](#as-much-as), [as … as](#as--as)

### no more than

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈmɔr ðən/, /noʊ ˈmɔr ðæn/ &middot; *noh MOR thuhn*
**Type:** correlative comparative frame (subordinating) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the paired-denial frame is formal and rhetorical
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** [X] *is no more* [adj] *than* [Y] (*is*). &middot; [X] *is no more a* [noun] *than* [Y] (*is*). &middot; [clause] [verb] *no more than* [clause]. &middot; [clause] [verb] *no more than* [quantity].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *than*.
**Variants:** *not any more … than* (informal), *no more a … than* (with a noun: "no more a lawyer than")

In its argument frame this denies the property of both terms together: *He is no more a scientist than I am* says neither of us is one. Learners regularly read it as an ordinary "less than" comparison; it is actually a paired denial, and the second term is chosen precisely because it is obviously false. Before a bare quantity it means simply "at most."

1. A rented tux makes him *no more* a groom *than* a costume makes me a pilot.
2. The committee has *no more* authority here *than* the audience does.
3. She's *no more* to blame *than* you are.
4. It took *no more than* twenty minutes to change the tire.
5. He earns *no more than* he did five years ago.
6. That was *no more* an accident *than* the fire last spring.

**See also:** [no less than](#no-less-than), [than](#than), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [as … as](#as--as)

### no sooner … than

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈsunər … ðən/ &middot; *noh SOO-nur … thuhn*
**Type:** correlative &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; the fronted form is literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time built on a comparative frame
**Pattern:** *No sooner* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *than* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *had no sooner* [past participle] *than* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma at the seam — *than* joins the halves directly. Fronting *No sooner* forces subject–auxiliary inversion: *No sooner had we sat down than…*, never *No sooner we had sat down*.
**Variants:** *no sooner had … than*, *hardly … when* and *scarcely … when* (same meaning, different second half)

Formally a comparison, semantically pure sequence: it says the second event followed the first immediately. Because the frame is comparative, the second half must be *than*; *no sooner … when* is the commonest error in this construction, and it comes from confusing it with *hardly … when* and *scarcely … when*, which do take *when*.

1. *No sooner* had we sat down *than* the fire alarm went off.
2. *No sooner* did the rain stop *than* the mosquitoes came out.
3. She had *no sooner* hung up *than* the phone rang again.
4. *No sooner* was the ink dry *than* they wanted changes.
5. We had *no sooner* unpacked *than* the landlord called about a leak.
6. *No sooner* had the news broken *than* the stock dropped nine points.

**See also:** [sooner than](#sooner-than), [than](#than), [just as](#just-as)

### rather than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðər ðən/ &middot; *RA-thur thuhn*
**Type:** subordinating; also works as a quasi-coordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of preference and substitution; also links two parallel phrases
**Pattern:** *Rather than* [bare infinitive / -ing], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *rather than* [bare infinitive]. &middot; [X] *rather than* [Y] (two matching phrases). &middot; [subject] *would rather* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *rather than* phrase. No comma when it links two parallel elements inside a clause. Commas on both sides only when it is a parenthetical afterthought.
**Variants:** *rather … than*, *would rather … than*

Marks the rejected alternative. Its nearest neighbor is *instead of*, and they take different complements: *rather than* takes a bare infinitive when the main clause has one (*Rather than pay, we walked out*), while *instead of* requires *-ing* (*Instead of paying, we walked out*). Whatever sits on the two sides of *rather than* must be the same kind of phrase.

1. *Rather than* argue about it, I just paid the fee.
2. We took the train *rather than* fly.
3. She sees it as a delay *rather than* a defeat.
4. *Rather than* wait for approval, the team shipped a draft.
5. He wants honest feedback *rather than* praise.
6. The problem is structural *rather than* personal.

**See also:** [sooner than](#sooner-than), [than](#than), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

### sooner than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsunər ðən/ &middot; *SOO-nur thuhn*
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral, emphatic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of preference; also a plain comparison of time
**Pattern:** [subject] *would* [bare infinitive] *sooner than* [bare infinitive] (preference). &middot; [subject] *'d sooner* [bare infinitive] *than* [bare infinitive]. &middot; *Sooner than* [bare infinitive], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *sooner than* [clause] (literal time).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *sooner than*. Comma after a fronted *Sooner than* phrase.
**Variants:** *would sooner … than*, *'d sooner … than*

The emphatic sibling of *rather than*: it states a preference the speaker treats as obvious, usually a refusal dressed up as a choice. It also has a literal time reading (*She got here sooner than we expected*), and the bare infinitive after it is the signal that you are in the preference sense rather than the time sense.

1. I'd quit *sooner than* work another holiday weekend.
2. He'd sell the house *sooner than* ask his brother for money.
3. *Sooner than* sit through that meeting again, I'll take the early flight.
4. The package arrived *sooner than* the tracking page promised.
5. She'd walk home in the rain *sooner than* call a cab.
6. They finished *sooner than* anyone thought possible.

**See also:** [rather than](#rather-than), [no sooner … than](#no-sooner--than), [than](#than)

### than

**Pronunciation:** /ðæn/, weak /ðən/, /ðn̩/ &middot; *THAN, thuhn*
**Type:** subordinating (comparative); prepositional in the *than me* usage &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison, attached to a comparative word earlier in the sentence
**Pattern:** [clause + comparative adj/adv] *than* [clause]. &middot; [clause + *more / less / fewer* + noun] *than* [clause]. &middot; [clause + comparative] *than* [noun phrase] (elliptical: *than me*, *than last year's*). &middot; [clause + *other / rather / sooner / else / different*] *than* [phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *than* in a comparison. A comma appears only when the *than* phrase is a deliberate afterthought set off from the rest of the sentence.
**Variants:** *than what* (nonstandard, as in "bigger than what I expected"), *than whom* (formal; see its own entry)

The workhorse of unequal comparison. It cannot start a comparison by itself — something earlier must license it, either a comparative form (*taller*, *more slowly*, *fewer*) or one of a short list of trigger words (*other*, *rather*, *sooner*, *else*, *different*). The clause after it is almost always elliptical, which is where the case argument lives: *taller than I (am)* is the conservative written form and *taller than me* the ordinary spoken one, and both are defensible in American English. One last thing it is not: *then*. *Than* compares; *then* tells time or draws a consequence. They sound alike in unstressed speech — both reduce to /ðən/ — and that is exactly why the misspelling is the single commonest error in the category. Test it by stressing the word: if the sentence still means "at that time," you want *then*.

1. She finished the course faster *than* anyone expected.
2. This apartment costs more *than* our last one did.
3. There were fewer people at the hearing *than* the organizers had hoped.
4. He knows the neighborhood better *than* I do.
5. I'd rather drive *than* sit in an airport for six hours.
6. The recipe calls for something other *than* butter.
7. That's a bigger crowd *than* last year's.

**See also:** [than whom](#than-whom), [rather than](#rather-than), [as … as](#as--as), [the same as](#the-same-as), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### than whom

**Pronunciation:** /ðæn ˈhum/ &middot; *than HOOM*
**Type:** subordinating conjunction + relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** nonrestrictive relative clause built on a comparative
**Pattern:** [noun phrase], *than whom* [no one / few / nobody] [verb phrase], [rest of main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase], *than which* [nothing / few / no + noun] [verb phrase], [rest of main clause]. &middot; [noun phrase], *than whom* [subject] [verb] [no one / nothing] *more*, [rest of main clause].
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides, always — the clause is supplementary, never restrictive. This is the one place in the *than* family where a comma stands directly before *than*.
**Variants:** *than which* (for things)

A fossilized formal construction, and the one place in modern English where *than* is unmistakably followed by the objective case — the historical argument for calling *than* a preposition. It reads as deliberately elevated and nearly always pairs with a negative that gives it superlative force (*than whom no one*, *than which nothing*).

1. Dr. Reyes, *than whom* no one is better qualified, will chair the panel.
2. She studied under Kowalski, *than whom* the department has known no stricter grader.
3. The Chrysler Building, *than which* few American towers have aged better, still stops traffic.
4. He named his old editor, *than whom* he trusted no one more, as his literary executor.
5. The award went to Ellis, *than whom* the league has produced no steadier defender.

**See also:** [than](#than), [no less than](#no-less-than)

## Comparison of equality: the *as … as* family

Equatives come in two halves that straddle the compared word: the first half is a degree word sitting inside the main clause — the adverb *as* or *so*, or the determiner *the same* — and only the second half is the actual subordinator. Nothing inside the frame takes a comma. The one thing that attracts one is fronting — move a whole *as* clause to the head of the sentence and it takes a comma after it: *As much as I'd like to help, I'm booked all week*.

### as … as

**Pronunciation:** /æz … əz/ — the first *as* carries the stress and stays full, the second reduces to /əz/ &middot; *AZ … uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative comparison) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** [clause] *as* [adj/adv] *as* [clause/phrase]. &middot; [clause] *not as* [adj/adv] *as* [clause/phrase]. &middot; *as* [adj] *a(n)* [noun] *as* [phrase]. &middot; [clause] *as much / as many* [noun] *as* [clause]. &middot; [clause] [twice / half / three times] *as* [adj/adv] *as* [phrase].
**Punctuation:** No commas anywhere inside the frame, in any of those orders.
**Variants:** *not as … as* (ordinary negative), *not so … as* (formal, older), *just as … as* (emphatic)

States that two things match on some scale. The trap is word order in the noun pattern: when a singular count noun is compared, the article jumps to the far side of the adjective — *as fine a meal as I've had*, not *as a fine meal as I've had*. No other English frame moves the article that way.

1. The drive is *as* long *as* the flight once you count security.
2. She's *as* good a manager *as* the company has ever had.
3. Bring *as many* chairs *as* you can fit in the car.
4. It's not *as* cold today *as* it was yesterday.
5. He explained it *as* clearly *as* anyone could have.
6. The copy reads *as* well *as* the original did.

**See also:** [so … as](#so--as), [as much as](#as-much-as), [as many as](#as-many-as), [as well as](#as-well-as), [the same as](#the-same-as), [than](#than)

### as … so

**Pronunciation:** /æz … ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *az … SOH*
**Type:** correlative &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison with a resumptive main clause
**Pattern:** *As* [clause], *so* [clause]. &middot; *Just as* [clause], *so (too)* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma at the seam between the two halves. *So* opens the second half and often triggers inversion (*so grows the tree*, *so too should writers rest*).
**Variants:** *just as … so*, *just as … so too*, *as … so also*

Sets up an analogy: as the first proposition goes, so goes the second. This is the elevated, written-out version of the comparison that ordinary speech makes with *just like*, and it belongs in sermons, editorials, and formal argument rather than conversation.

1. *As* the twig is bent, *so* grows the tree.
2. *Just as* the coasts warmed early, *so* the interior followed a decade later.
3. *As* the harvest failed, *so* the price of bread climbed.
4. *Just as* athletes taper before a race, *so too* should writers rest before a deadline.
5. *As* a city treats its parks, *so* it treats its people.

**See also:** [just as](#just-as), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [the more … the more](#the-more--the-more), [as](#as)

### as far as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɑr əz/, weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *az FAR uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) with a clause; prepositional with a noun phrase in the distance sense &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison and extent; also literal distance
**Pattern:** *As far as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as far as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as far as* [noun phrase] (literal distance; prepositional). &middot; *As far as* [noun phrase] *is concerned / goes*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as far as* [noun phrase] *is concerned / goes*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause whenever it is fronted (*As far as I know, …*). No comma when it follows the main clause — in the extent sense (*He'll help as far as he's able*) and in the literal-distance sense (*The trail runs as far as the old fire tower*) alike.
**Variants:** *so far as* (formal), *as far as … is concerned*, *as far as I'm concerned*, *as far as … goes*

Limits a claim to the reach of somebody's knowledge, authority, or physical distance. In edited American prose it should keep its verb: write *as far as the budget is concerned*, not the clipped *as far as the budget*, which is common in speech but still corrected in print. Its formal twin is *insofar as*.

1. *As far as* I know, the office closes at four on Fridays.
2. The trail runs *as far as* the old fire tower.
3. *As far as* the budget is concerned, nothing has changed.
4. He'll help *as far as* he's able.
5. *As far as* anyone could tell, the machine was working fine.
6. You can see *as far as* the state line from up here.

**See also:** [insofar as](#insofar-as), [as … as](#as--as), [so … as](#so--as), [according as](#according-as)

### as lief … as

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈlif … əz/ &middot; *az LEEF … uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative of preference) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison expressing preference
**Pattern:** [subject] *had as lief* [bare infinitive] *as* [bare infinitive]. &middot; [subject] *would as lief* [bare infinitive] *as* [bare infinitive]. &middot; [subject] *'d as lief* [bare infinitive] *as not*. &middot; *As lief* [bare infinitive] *as* [bare infinitive] — [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the frame.
**Variants:** *had as lief*, *would as lief*, *'d as lief*, *as lief … as not*, *as leave* (a respelling from after the word went obsolete)

The Elizabethan ancestor of *would just as soon*. *Lief* is a dead adverb meaning "willingly" (the root survives in German *lieb*), and the frame claims equal willingness to do either thing — which in practice is a courteous refusal of the first. Note the verb the old idiom takes: *had as lief*, not *would as lief*, is what the printed texts have, and anything written this century should say *I'd just as soon*.

1. I *had as lief* walk *as* wait an hour for the next bus.
2. He'd *as lief* pay the fine *as* argue with a clerk about it.
3. "I *had as lief* not be *as* live to be in awe of such a thing as I myself." — *Julius Caesar*
4. She would *as lief* have stayed home, and said so twice.
5. They'd *as lief* see the building torn down *as* watch it rot another decade.
6. I'd *as lief* not, if it's all the same to you.

**See also:** [sooner than](#sooner-than), [rather than](#rather-than), [as … as](#as--as), [as much as](#as-much-as)

### as many as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈmɛni əz/, weak /əz ˈmɛni əz/ &middot; *az MEN-ee uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative, count) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** [clause] *as many* [plural noun] *as* [clause/phrase]. &middot; [clause] *as many of* [noun phrase] *as* [clause]. &middot; [clause] [twice / half / three times] *as many* [plural noun] *as* [phrase]. &middot; *As many as* [number] [verb phrase]. &middot; [clause] *as many as* [number].
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the frame.
**Variants:** *not as many as*, *half as many as*, *twice as many as*

The countable half of the equative pair: *as many as* takes plural count nouns and *as much as* takes mass nouns, and mixing them (*as much chairs*) is one of the most visible learner errors in English. Standing alone before a number it means "up to — and that is a surprising number."

1. Take *as many* forms *as* you need.
2. She's read *as many* of his books *as* I have.
3. *As many as* two hundred people were turned away at the door.
4. We didn't sell *as many* tickets *as* we did last spring.
5. Bring *as many* helpers *as* will fit in the van.
6. There were *as many* questions *as* there were answers.

**See also:** [as much as](#as-much-as), [as … as](#as--as), [no more than](#no-more-than)

### as much as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈmʌtʃ əz/, weak /əz ˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *az MUCH uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative, degree) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison; also an adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** [clause] *as much as* [clause/phrase]. &middot; [clause] *as much* [mass noun] *as* [clause]. &middot; [clause] [twice / half] *as much* [mass noun] *as* [phrase]. &middot; *As much as* [clause], [main clause] (concessive). &middot; [clause] *as much as* [quantity].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the degree sense. In the concessive sense the clause comes first and takes a comma after it: *As much as I'd like to, I can't.*
**Variants:** *as much … as*, *not as much as*, *twice as much as*

Measures degree, or quantity of a mass noun. It leads a second life at the front of a sentence, where *As much as I like her* means "although I like her very much" — identical words, completely different job, and only position and the comma tell you which one you are reading. The formal equivalent of that concessive use is *much as*.

1. He worries about the deadline *as much as* you do.
2. Pour in *as much* stock *as* the pan will hold.
3. *As much as* I'd like to help, I'm booked all week.
4. The repair cost *as much as* the car was worth.
5. She travels *as much as* her job allows.
6. *As much as* it hurt, quitting was the right call.

**See also:** [as many as](#as-many-as), [as well as](#as-well-as), [much as](#much-as), [as … as](#as--as), [no less than](#no-less-than)

### as well as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈwɛl əz/, weak /əz ˈwɛl əz/ &middot; *az WELL uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative) in the comparison sense; quasi-coordinator in the additive sense &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison (manner and degree); the additive sense links noun phrases instead
**Pattern:** [clause] *as well as* [clause]. &middot; [clause] *as well as* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *as well as* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; *As well as* [-ing], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the comparison sense. In the additive sense the commas depend on where the phrase sits: commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb (*The manager, as well as the players, was fined*), a comma after it when it is fronted (*As well as teaching two sections, she advises the debate team*), and **no** comma at all when it simply trails as the second item in a pair (*They serve breakfast as well as lunch*). Whichever way it is punctuated, it does **not** change the verb's number — *was fined*, singular, unlike *and*.
**Variants:** *just as well as*, *every bit as well as*

Two grammars share these three words. As an equative it compares skill or quality: *She plays as well as her teacher* means she plays equally well. As a quasi-coordinator it means "in addition to," and that is the one that breaks agreement rules for learners, because unlike *and* it leaves the subject singular.

1. He writes *as well as* anyone on the staff.
2. The new model handles *as well as* the one it replaced.
3. The coach, *as well as* two assistants, was suspended.
4. She speaks Portuguese *as well as* she speaks Spanish.
5. They serve breakfast *as well as* lunch.
6. The car stops *as well as* it did the day we bought it.
7. *As well as* teaching two sections, she advises the debate team.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [as much as](#as-much-as), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### insofar as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnsəˈfɑr əz/, /ˌɪnsoʊˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *in-suh-FAR uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison and extent
**Pattern:** *Insofar as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *insofar as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *only insofar as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *insofar as* [noun phrase] *is concerned* (heavy; usually better as a clause).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows the main clause.
**Variants:** *in so far as* (chiefly British spacing), *insomuch as* (rare, same sense)

Restricts a statement to the degree that something holds — the legal-and-academic register of *as far as*. Its lookalike *inasmuch as* is a different conjunction meaning "because," and swapping the two reverses the logic of a sentence, so keep the *-so-* and the *-as-* straight: **in-so-far** measures extent, **in-as-much** gives a reason. Spell it closed up in American prose; the spaced *in so far as* is chiefly British, and *insomuch as* is rare enough now to read as an affectation.

1. The clause applies *insofar as* state law permits.
2. *Insofar as* the data are reliable, the trend is real.
3. He supported the plan *insofar as* it protected existing tenants.
4. *Insofar as* anyone can judge from the outside, the merger went smoothly.
5. The theory holds *insofar as* the sample stays representative.

**See also:** [as far as](#as-far-as), [according as](#according-as), [as much as](#as-much-as)

### just as

**Pronunciation:** /dʒəst ˈæz/, /dʒʌst ˈæz/ &middot; *just AZ*
**Type:** subordinating (intensified equative and manner); also temporal &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison or manner; adverbial clause of time in the "at that moment" sense
**Pattern:** *Just as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *just as* [clause] (manner or time). &middot; [clause] *just as* [adj/adv] *as* [phrase] (comparison). &middot; *Just as* [clause], *so (too)* [clause] (formal correlative).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *just as* clause. No comma when it follows the main clause.
**Variants:** *just as … as* (equative), *just as … so* (formal correlative)

*Just* sharpens *as* into three readings: comparison ("exactly as much as," *just as expensive as*), manner ("in precisely the way," *just as I predicted*), and time ("at the very moment," *just as we sat down*). Nothing in the words themselves distinguishes them — what follows decides, and a second *as* means you are in the comparison sense.

1. *Just as* I predicted, the flight was delayed.
2. The used one is *just as* reliable *as* the new model.
3. She left the kitchen *just as* she'd found it.
4. *Just as* his father had, he went into teaching.
5. The power went out *just as* the game went to overtime.
6. *Just as* the recipe warns, the sauce broke when I raised the heat.

**See also:** [as](#as), [as … so](#as--so), [just like](#just-like), [the way](#the-way)

### not so much … as

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt soʊ ˈmʌtʃ … əz/ &middot; *not soh MUCH … uhz*
**Type:** correlative &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison (corrective)
**Pattern:** [X] *is not so much* [noun phrase] *as* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X] *is not so much* [adj] *as* [adj]. &middot; [clause] *not so much* [phrase] *as* [phrase]. &middot; [clause] *not so much because* [clause] *as because* [clause]. &middot; *Not so much* [phrase] *as* [phrase] — [main clause] (fronted, dash-linked).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as*. The two compared elements have to be the same kind of phrase.
**Variants:** *not so much … but rather*, *not so much … as rather*, *less … than* (informal equivalent)

Rejects one description and substitutes a better one — "less this, more that." It is the polite corrective: it concedes that the first label was a reasonable guess before replacing it, whereas *rather than* simply rules the first option out. Do not confuse it with the fixed phrase *not so much as* meaning "not even" (*She didn't so much as glance at the menu*), which has no second element at all.

1. It's *not so much* a job *as* a calling.
2. The problem is *not so much* the cost *as* the timing.
3. She was *not so much* angry *as* disappointed.
4. What sank the project was *not so much* bad luck *as* bad planning.
5. He's *not so much* retired *as* between projects.
6. The bill stalled *not so much because* the votes weren't there *as because* nobody wanted to schedule the vote.
7. *Not so much* a renovation *as* a demolition — that's what the contractor delivered.

**See also:** [so … as](#so--as), [rather than](#rather-than), [than](#than), [no more than](#no-more-than)

### so … as

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ … əz/ &middot; *SOH … uhz*
**Type:** correlative (equative, negative and interrogative contexts) &middot; **Register:** formal, old-fashioned
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** [subject] *is not so* [adj/adv] *as* [clause/phrase]. &middot; *Is* [subject] *so* [adj] *as* [clause]? (interrogative). &middot; [clause] *so* [adj] *as to* [bare infinitive]. &middot; [subject] *didn't so much as* [bare infinitive] (= not even). &middot; *Nothing / no one is so* [adj] *as* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** No commas inside the frame.
**Variants:** *not so … as*, *so far as*, *not so much as*

The older negative equative. Modern American English says *not as … as* nearly everywhere, and *not so … as* survives mainly in careful prose and in fixed phrases (*not so much as a thank-you*, *so far as I can tell*, *so kind as to*). Do not confuse it with the purpose marker *so as to* or the result frame *so … that*, which are different constructions that happen to start with the same word.

*So* changes word class as it moves, and only one of its jobs takes a comma. Here it is a degree adverb sitting inside an equative frame, and nothing in the frame is punctuated. In *It was late, so we left* it is a **coordinating conjunction** joining two independent clauses, and that one takes a comma **before** it. In *so that they'd hear us* it opens a purpose clause; in *so tired* it is a bare intensifier; in *So, where were we?* it is a discourse marker. Sort out which *so* you have before you reach for any punctuation.

1. The second act is not *so* strong *as* the first.
2. He isn't *so* young *as* he looks.
3. She didn't *so much as* glance at the menu.
4. Would you be *so* kind *as* to hold the door?
5. Nothing is *so* useless *as* a meeting that could have been an email.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [not so much … as](#not-so-much--as), [as far as](#as-far-as)

### the same as

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈseɪm əz/ &middot; *thuh SAME uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (equative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of comparison; also a comparison complement after *be*
**Pattern:** [X] *is the same as* [Y]. &middot; [X] *is the same* [noun] *as* [Y / clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the same as* [clause]. &middot; [X] *is the same* [noun] *that* [clause] (relative — that very one). &middot; *Same as* [noun phrase] — [main clause] (clipped, informal).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as*.
**Variants:** *the same … as*, *same as* (informal, article dropped), *the same … that* (relative clause)

Asserts identity rather than resemblance. The choice between *as* and *that* after *same* changes the meaning: *the same house as we rented* means one like it, while *the same house that we rented* means literally that house — *as* compares two things, *that* opens a relative clause about one of them.

1. The chili tastes *the same as* my uncle's.
2. He wore *the same* coat *as* the man in the photograph.
3. *Same as* always — coffee, black, no sugar.
4. Filing online works *the same as* mailing the form.
5. She ordered *the same as* I did.
6. Nothing looked *the same as* it had before the renovation.

**See also:** [as … as](#as--as), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [like](#like), [than](#than)

### to the degree that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ðə dɪˈɡri ðət/ &middot; *tuh thuh dih-GREE thut*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of extent and degree; also proportion
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to the degree that* [clause]. &middot; *To the degree that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *only to the degree that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to the extent that* [clause] (the commoner twin).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause when it is fronted; no comma when it trails the main clause.
**Variants:** *to the extent that* (far commoner), *to the degree to which*, *to such a degree that* (a different construction — result, not extent), *insofar as*

Ties the truth of the main clause to how much of something is the case — plain-English *insofar as*. Keep it clear of the result frame *to such a degree that*, which looks almost identical and points the other way: this one measures a condition, that one reports a consequence. In American prose *to the extent that* is the default, and *to the degree that* is what you reach for when *extent* is already doing work elsewhere in the sentence.

1. The policy helps renters *to the degree that* landlords actually register their units.
2. *To the degree that* the forecast is reliable, we should move the ceremony indoors.
3. He is responsible *to the degree that* he signed off on the numbers.
4. *To the degree that* anyone can plan for a flood, the town had planned.
5. The warranty covers the repair *only to the degree that* the damage wasn't deliberate.
6. Test scores predict college success *to the degree that* they measure preparation rather than money.

**See also:** [insofar as](#insofar-as), [as far as](#as-far-as), [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as), [according as](#according-as)

## Manner and resemblance

These say *how*, not *how much*. The whole *like*-versus-*as* argument lives in this section, and the safe way through it is the plain, unobjectionable *the way*.

### analogously

**Pronunciation:** /əˈnælədʒəsli/ &middot; *uh-NAL-uh-jus-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb of resemblance; prepositional in the heavier *analogously to* &middot; **Register:** formal; academic, mathematical, scientific, and legal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial introducing a parallel case that runs the same way as the one just described
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *analogously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Analogously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [main clause], *and analogously* [clause]. &middot; [subject], *analogously*, [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *analogously to* [noun phrase] (prepositional, heavy).
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a sentence or a clause, and commas on both sides when it sits inside one. Between two independent clauses it is an adverb, so it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice: ✗ *The odd case runs by induction, analogously the even case does too.* ✓ *The odd case runs by induction; analogously, the even case does too.* No comma in the prepositional *analogously to*.
**Variants:** *similarly*, *likewise*, *by analogy*, *in an analogous way*, *correspondingly*

The technical register's *similarly*: it claims that the second case is built the same way as the first, point for point, rather than merely resembling it. That is what separates it from its everyday neighbors — *similarly* asserts a likeness and *likewise* only adds a second item, while *analogously* says the structure carries over, which is why it belongs to proofs, protocols, and doctrinal argument. It sounds pretentious the moment all you mean is "also."

1. The proof for the odd case runs by induction; *analogously*, the even case follows from the same lemma.
2. Sodium reacts violently with water. *Analogously*, potassium does, only faster.
3. Federal courts read the exemption narrowly; *analogously*, most state courts have read their own versions the same way.
4. Wages track productivity in manufacturing, and *analogously* in freight.
5. A vaccine trains the immune system before there is an infection. *Analogously*, a fire drill trains a building's occupants before there is smoke.
6. The second experiment was designed *analogously to* the first, with temperature held constant instead of pressure.

**See also:** [by analogy with](#by-analogy-with), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [correspondingly](#correspondingly), [by the same token](#by-the-same-token), [similarly](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#similarly)

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/, weak /əz/ &middot; *AZ, uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (manner and comparison); the same word also subordinates time and cause, and is a **preposition** in the role sense (*as a teacher*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal in the manner sense
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner or comparison; adverbial clause of time or cause in its other senses; prepositional phrase in the role sense
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as* [clause]. &middot; *As* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as* [clause] (supplementary comment). &middot; *as* + [past participle / adjective] (reduced: *as expected*, *as noted above*). &middot; *As* [noun phrase], [main clause] (role; prepositional: *As a teacher, she…*).
**Punctuation:** No comma when the *as* clause follows the main clause and states manner: *Do as I say.* Comma when it is fronted (*As I mentioned, the deposit is due Friday*) and comma before it when it trails as a comment on the whole sentence rather than on the verb (*The bill passed, as everyone expected*). That comma is the only thing separating the two readings of *She left as I asked*: without it, in the way I asked; with it, and I had asked her to.
**Variants:** *just as*, *as usual*, *as follows*, *as expected*, *as such*

The neutral manner subordinator, meaning "in the way that." It is the always-safe alternative to *like*, and unlike *like* it takes a full clause with no objection from anyone. Watch the overload: the same three letters also mark time (*as we walked*), cause (*as it was late*), and role (*as a teacher, she…*), and only context sorts them out — which is exactly why *as* is one of the hardest words in English for learners.

1. Fold the paper *as* the diagram shows.
2. *As* I mentioned on the phone, the deposit is due Friday.
3. Leave the room *as* you found it.
4. She dressed *as* her mother had taught her.
5. The bill passed, *as* everyone expected.
6. Season the sauce *as* you like.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [like](#like), [the way](#the-way), [just as](#just-as), [as … as](#as--as)

### as if

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈɪf/, /əz ɪf/ &middot; *az IF*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison, often hypothetical
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as if* [clause]. &middot; [*look / sound / feel / smell / seem*] *as if* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as if* *to* [verb phrase]. &middot; *As if* [clause / reduced phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *As if!* (standalone retort, informal).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as if* when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted.
**Variants:** *as though* (interchangeable, slightly more formal), *like* (informal), *As if!* (standalone retort)

Introduces an unreal or unverified comparison — how something looks, sounds, feels, or seems. Mood matters: when the comparison is contrary to fact, careful writing keeps the past subjunctive (*as if he were sorry*), and when the situation may well be true, the indicative is normal (*It looks as if the flight is on time*).

1. He talks *as if* he owns the place.
2. She looked *as if* she hadn't slept in days.
3. It smells in here *as if* something's burning.
4. The dog tilted its head *as if* to ask a question.
5. *As if* nothing had happened, he sat down and ordered lunch.
6. It sounds *as if* they're going to reschedule.

**See also:** [as though](#as-though), [like](#like), [as](#as), [the way](#the-way)

### as though

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈðoʊ/, weak /əz ˈðoʊ/ &middot; *az THOH*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison, often hypothetical
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as though* [clause]. &middot; [look/sound/feel/seem] *as though* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as though* *to* [verb phrase]. &middot; *As though* [clause / reduced phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as though* when it follows the main clause. Comma after a fronted *as though* clause or reduced phrase: *As though on cue, the lights came back on.*
**Variants:** *as if* (same meaning, more common in speech)

The near-twin of *as if*, and the two are genuinely interchangeable in meaning. The only reliable difference is texture: *as though* reads a shade more literary and is slightly favored after verbs of feeling and appearance (*It felt as though hours had passed*), while *as if* dominates speech and owns the fixed retort *As if!*

1. She spoke *as though* the decision had already been made.
2. It felt *as though* hours had passed in that waiting room.
3. He shrugged *as though* the whole thing bored him.
4. The house looked *as though* no one had lived there in years.
5. *As though* on cue, the lights came back on.
6. They acted *as though* we hadn't been standing there for an hour.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [like](#like), [as](#as), [much as](#much-as)

### as who should say

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈhu ʃəd ˌseɪ/ &middot; *az HOO shud say*
**Type:** subordinating (fossilized manner clause) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner, elliptical and supplementary
**Pattern:** [main clause], *as who should say* [reported words]. &middot; [main clause], *as who should say*, "[direct speech]." &middot; [main clause], *as much as to say* [words] (the surviving paraphrase).
**Punctuation:** Comma before it — it comments on the main clause instead of completing it — and another comma after it when a direct quotation follows.
**Variants:** *as much as to say*, *as if to say* (the modern equivalent), *as who would say*

A fossil of Elizabethan English meaning "as one might say" or "as if to say." The *who* is the old indefinite pronoun "anyone," a use that has otherwise died out, which is why the phrase looks ungrammatical to a modern reader rather than merely old. It survives in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and deliberate pastiche; write *as if to say* in anything current.

1. He shrugged and turned away, *as who should say*, "Suit yourself."
2. She raised an eyebrow, *as who should say* the matter was already settled.
3. The porter waved us through, *as who should say* our business was no business of his.
4. He held up the empty bottle, *as who should say*, "You see how it is."
5. They smiled at each other, *as who should say* they had heard that excuse before.
6. The old man nodded slowly, *as who should say* he had expected nothing else.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [as though](#as-though), [like as](#like-as), [as](#as)

### how

**Pronunciation:** /haʊ/ &middot; *HOW*
**Type:** subordinating (nominal relative); complementizer in indirect questions &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause of manner; also an indirect question
**Pattern:** [verb] *how* [clause] (object of the verb). &middot; *How* [clause] + [main-clause verb phrase] (the *how* clause is the subject). &middot; [verb] [indirect object] *how* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *how to* [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *how* [clause] (manner, = *the way that*).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *how*. It heads an integrated nominal clause, so it never takes one, in any of those positions.
**Variants:** *how come* (informal, = why), *how ever* (two words, emphatic question form)

Covers both "in what manner" — an indirect question — and the plain "the way that" (*I love how she phrases things*). That second use is fully standard American English and now common in edited prose, though *the way* remains the more conservative choice in formal writing, where some editors still reserve *how* for questions.

1. I like *how* you rearranged the living room.
2. Show me *how* the latch works.
3. *How* she found the address is still a mystery.
4. He never explained *how* he got the job.
5. Do it *how* you want — I don't have a preference.
6. It's amazing *how* quickly kids pick up a language.

**See also:** [the way](#the-way), [however](#however), [as](#as), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that)

### however

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-EV-ur*
**Type:** subordinating (manner and degree free relative) — not the conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner or concessive condition
**Pattern:** *However* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *however* [clause]. &middot; *However* [adj/adv] [subject] [verb], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *however* [adj/adv] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *however* clause, and nothing else — no comma when the clause trails. This is **not** the conjunctive adverb. The transitional *however* that means "but" cannot join two independent clauses with a comma; that is a comma splice (✗ *We were late, however they held our table*). It requires a semicolon before it and a comma after (*We were late; however, they held our table*), or a period and a fresh sentence. If you can swap in *no matter how* or *in whatever way*, you are looking at this subordinator and no semicolon belongs anywhere near it.
**Variants:** *no matter how*, *in whatever way*, *howsoever* (archaic)

Means "in whatever manner" or "to whatever degree," and it makes the main clause hold across every alternative. The movement test separates the two *howevers*: the conjunctive adverb slides freely around inside its own clause (*They, however, held our table*), while this one is locked to the front of the clause it introduces.

1. *However* you slice it, the numbers don't work.
2. Arrange the chairs *however* you like.
3. *However* hard I try, I can't parallel park.
4. She'll finish the job *however* long it takes.
5. *However* the vote goes, we'll need a new budget by June.
6. Get here *however* you can.

**See also:** [how](#how), [the way](#the-way), [as much as](#as-much-as), [much as](#much-as)

### in the same manner as

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈseɪm ˌmænər əz/ &middot; *in thuh SAME man-ur uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) with a clause; prepositional with a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** formal; procedural, legal, and scientific
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in the same manner as* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *in the same manner as* [clause]. &middot; *In the same manner as* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the same manner that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in like manner* (the clipped adverb).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase; no comma when it trails.
**Variants:** *in the same manner that*, *in a manner similar to*, *in like manner* (dated adverb), *in the same way that*

The procedural register of *in the same way that* — the phrasing of manuals, statutes, and lab protocols, where *manner* is the term of art for how a step is carried out. It is the heaviest way to say this, so it earns its place only where the method itself is what is being prescribed or described. Anywhere else, *the same way* or *just as* does the identical work in half the words.

1. Renewals are processed *in the same manner as* new applications.
2. Cut the second board *in the same manner as* the first.
3. *In the same manner as* the federal statute, the state law exempts family farms.
4. Interest accrues *in the same manner as* it does on an ordinary savings account.
5. The samples were prepared *in the same manner as* those in the 2019 trial.
6. Members are elected *in the same manner that* officers are.

**See also:** [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [the way](#the-way), [just as](#just-as), [the same as](#the-same-as), [as](#as)

### in the same way that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈseɪm ˌweɪ ðət/ &middot; *in thuh SAME way thut*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in the same way that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the same way* [clause] (*that* dropped). &middot; *In the same way that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the same way as* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows the main clause.
**Variants:** *in the same way as*, *in much the same way that*, *the same way* (informal, clipped)

Spells an analogy out at full length — the explicit, unambiguous version of *like* or *just as*, and the safest choice in formal writing where *like* would draw objections. It is heavy, so it earns its place only when the analogy is the point of the sentence; otherwise *just as* or *the way* does the same work in half the words.

1. A good index works *in the same way that* a map does.
2. *In the same way that* muscles need rest, attention needs breaks.
3. The tax credit phases out *in the same way that* the old one did.
4. She coaches *in the same way* she was coached.
5. *In the same way that* no two fingerprints match, no two rooms sound alike on tape.

**See also:** [the same as](#the-same-as), [the way](#the-way), [just as](#just-as), [as … so](#as--so)

### just like

**Pronunciation:** /dʒəst ˈlaɪk/ &middot; *just LYKE*
**Type:** subordinating (informal) with a clause; prepositional with a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison
**Pattern:** [main clause] *just like* [noun phrase] (prepositional). &middot; [main clause] *just like* [clause] (informal). &middot; *Just like* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *Just like* [clause], [main clause] (informal).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *just like*. Comma after a fronted *just like* phrase or clause.
**Variants:** *exactly like*, *just as* (the formal clause equivalent), *much like*

The everyday intensified comparison. Followed by a noun phrase (*just like her mother*) it is unremarkable in any register; followed by a full clause (*just like I told you*) it is conversational, and edited prose substitutes *just as*. Note that it points at resemblance, not identity — *just like the original* means indistinguishable from it, not the same object.

1. She laughs *just like* her mother.
2. It went *just like* I told you it would.
3. *Just like* last year, the parade started twenty minutes late.
4. The knockoff looks *just like* the real thing.
5. He handled the interview *just like* a pro.
6. It tastes *just like* the version they used to sell.

**See also:** [like](#like), [just as](#just-as), [the same as](#the-same-as), [as … so](#as--so)

### like

**Pronunciation:** /laɪk/ &middot; *LYKE*
**Type:** subordinating (informal) before a clause; **preposition** before a noun phrase; and outside the conjunction system a verb (*I like it*), a noun (*the likes of*), a suffix (*businesslike*), and — in speech only — a quotative (*she was like, no*) and a filler &middot; **Register:** informal as a conjunction; neutral as a preposition
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison
**Pattern:** [main clause] *like* [noun phrase] (prepositional; standard everywhere). &middot; [main clause] *like* [clause] (manner, = *as*; informal). &middot; [look/sound/seem/feel] *like* [clause] (= *as if*; informal). &middot; *Like* [clause], [main clause] (fronted comment: *Like I said, …*). &middot; *Like* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *like* in a comparison. Comma after a fronted *like* clause or phrase. A comma before it appears only when the *like* phrase is a supplementary aside tacked on to the end of the sentence.
**Variants:** *just like*, *kind of like* / *sort of like* (informal), *more like* (corrective)

*Like* before a noun phrase — *She sings like her father* — has never been controversial. *Like* before a full clause with its own verb — *She sings like her father did*, *It looks like it's going to rain* — is what draws objections, and it is one of the longest-running arguments in American usage.

**On the *like* / *as* question, the facts are these.** Clause-introducing *like* is universal in speech, standard in journalism and fiction, and still corrected in academic and formal business writing. When you need it to pass anywhere, substitute *as* for the manner sense (*as her father did*) and *as if* or *as though* for the appearance sense (*as if it's going to rain*). The substitution does not run the other way: with a bare noun phrase, *as* changes the meaning, because *She works like her father* compares her to him while *She works as her father* would mean in the role of her father.

1. He drives *like* his grandfather taught him.
2. It looks *like* we're going to need a bigger table.
3. Nobody makes chili *like* my uncle does.
4. She writes *like* she talks.
5. The engine sounds *like* something's loose.
6. *Like* I said, the store opens at ten.

**See also:** [as](#as), [as if](#as-if), [as though](#as-though), [just like](#just-like), [the way](#the-way)

### like as

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlaɪk əz/ &middot; *LYKE uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound; archaic), usually correlative with a resumptive *so* &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison, answered by *so* in the second half
**Pattern:** *Like as* [clause], *so* [clause]. &middot; *Like as* [noun phrase], *so* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *like as* [clause] (dialectal).
**Punctuation:** Comma at the seam before the resumptive *so*, exactly as in *as … so*. Comma after the fronted clause when there is no *so* to answer it.
**Variants:** *even as*, *like as if* (dialectal), *just as … so* (the modern equivalent)

The King James Bible's way of opening a simile: *Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him*. Both halves are needed — *like as* paints the picture and *so* applies it — which makes it a doubled-up cousin of *as … so* rather than a plain manner clause. It is wholly archaic in American English outside scripture, hymnody, and pastiche, and modern translations of those same verses print *As* or *Just as*.

1. *Like as* a father pitieth his children, *so* the Lord pitieth them that fear him.
2. *Like as* the waves make toward the pebbled shore, *so* do our minutes hasten to their end.
3. *Like as* a garden left untended runs to weed, *so* a language left unspoken thins out.
4. *Like as* a lamp burns lower toward morning, *so* the talk died down.
5. *Like as* the seasons turn, *so* the old quarrels come back around.
6. *Like as* we were taught, *so* we taught our own children.

**See also:** [as … so](#as--so), [just as](#just-as), [like](#like), [as](#as), [as who should say](#as-who-should-say)

### much as

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *MUCH uhz*
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison; adverbial clause of concession when fronted
**Pattern:** *Much as* [clause], [main clause] (concessive). &middot; [main clause] *much as* [clause] (comparative). &middot; [main clause] *much as* [noun phrase] [auxiliary] (elliptical: *much as a cold does*). &middot; [main clause] *much like* [noun phrase] (the prepositional twin).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted concessive clause. No comma when the comparison clause follows the main clause.
**Variants:** *as much as* (everyday equivalent of the concessive sense), *much like* (with a noun phrase), *much the same as*

Two readings split by position. Fronted, it is concessive: *Much as I admire him* means "although I greatly admire him." Following the main clause, it is comparative: "in much the same way that." Both sit a register above *as much as* and *just like*, which is why *much as* shows up in editorials and rarely in conversation.

1. *Much as* I admire her work, I wouldn't want her job.
2. The market corrected in 2019 *much as* it had a decade earlier.
3. *Much as* we'd love to stay, we have an early flight.
4. He runs the shop *much as* his father did.
5. *Much as* it pains me to say it, they were right.
6. The rumor spread *much as* a cold does in a kindergarten.

**See also:** [as much as](#as-much-as), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [however](#however), [whereas](#whereas)

### mutatis mutandis

**Pronunciation:** /mjuˌteɪtɪs mjuˈtændɪs/, also /muˌtɑtɪs muˈtɑndɪs/ &middot; *myoo-TAY-tis myoo-TAN-dis*, also *moo-TAH-tis moo-TAHN-dis*
**Type:** adverbial phrase (Latin) of qualified analogy — a manner adverbial in the predicate, a sentence adverbial when set off; never a clause-taking conjunction &middot; **Register:** formal; Latin — contract, treaty, and statutory boilerplate, and academic argument
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of manner and comparison, carrying an earlier rule or argument over to a new case
**Pattern:** [provision] *shall apply mutatis mutandis to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *mutatis mutandis*. &middot; *Mutatis mutandis*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *mutatis mutandis*, [continuation]. &middot; [main clause] *mutatis mutandis* (bare, trailing).
**Punctuation:** No commas in the fixed drafting formula, where it is integral to the predicate: *Article 12 applies mutatis mutandis to any renewal term*. Comma before it when it trails as a supplement (*the same holds for the city's ordinance, mutatis mutandis*), comma after it when it is fronted, commas on both sides when it interrupts. Roman type in legal drafting; academic style often italicizes it. It never inflects — both words end in *-is*, and there is no singular.
**Variants:** *with the necessary changes*, *with the appropriate modifications*, *with suitable changes made*, *mutatis mutandis* (invariable)

Latin for "with those things changed that need to be changed": it imports a rule or an argument whole into a second case while conceding that the names, dates, and parties will differ. An American meets it in contracts, treaties, and statutes, where it saves a drafter from setting out the same clause twice, and in academic writing, where it saves an argument from being rehearsed twice — it is boilerplate rather than prose, and outside those settings *with the necessary changes* says the same thing to a reader who has not studied law. Its near neighbor *a fortiori* moves an argument to a stronger case; this one moves it sideways to a parallel one.

1. The provisions of Article 12 shall apply *mutatis mutandis* to any renewal term.
2. The indemnity in Section 8 applies, *mutatis mutandis*, to the Purchaser's affiliates.
3. What the court said about the county's ordinance holds, *mutatis mutandis*, for the city's.
4. *Mutatis mutandis*, the same objection can be raised against the second version of the argument.
5. The parties agree that the arbitration rules shall govern any appeal *mutatis mutandis*.
6. His account of the nineteenth-century railroads applies *mutatis mutandis* to broadband today.

**See also:** [a fortiori](#a-fortiori), [in the same manner as](#in-the-same-manner-as), [by analogy with](#by-analogy-with), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [qua](#qua)

### sort of like

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɔrt əv ˌlaɪk/, reduced /ˈsɔrtə ˌlaɪk/ &middot; *SORT uv lyke*
**Type:** hedged comparison marker — prepositional before a noun phrase, subordinating (informal) before a clause &middot; **Register:** informal, chiefly spoken
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner and comparison, hedged
**Pattern:** [X] *is sort of like* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *sort of like* [clause] (informal). &middot; *It's sort of like* [-ing]. &middot; *Sort of like* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *is kind of like* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it. Comma after a fronted *sort of like* phrase. Never hyphenate it in this use: *sort of like*, not ✗ *sort-of-like*.
**Variants:** *kind of like*, *kinda like*, *sorta like*, *something like*, *a bit like*, *more or less like*

*Like* with the confidence turned down: it offers a comparison and concedes in the same breath that it is approximate, which is why speakers reach for it when explaining something unfamiliar. It belongs to conversation and to writing that imitates conversation; edited prose either drops the hedge (*It is like a…*) or spells it out (*roughly comparable to*). *Kind of like* is the same move in a different key, and both reduce in speech to *sorta* and *kinda*.

1. A mandolin is *sort of like* a violin you strum.
2. The new job is *kind of like* the old one, only with more meetings.
3. It felt *sort of like* being back in high school.
4. Renting there is *sort of like* paying a mortgage on somebody else's house.
5. He talks *kind of like* his brother does, all questions and no answers.
6. *Sort of like* the last outage, this one started with a bad update.

**See also:** [like](#like), [just like](#just-like), [as if](#as-if), [the way](#the-way), [as though](#as-though)

### the way

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈweɪ/ &middot; *thuh WAY*
**Type:** subordinating (compound; nominal relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of manner
**Pattern:** [main clause] *the way* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *the way that* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *the way* [clause] (object of *like*, *hate*, *remember*: *I hate the way he says it*). &middot; *The way* [clause], [main clause] (idiomatic — "given that," "by his account"). &middot; [main clause] *the way in which* [clause] (formal).
**Punctuation:** No comma before *the way* when it follows the main clause. Comma after it when the clause is fronted: *The way he tells it, he saved the company.*
**Variants:** *the way that*, *in the way that*, *the way in which* (formal)

The everyday manner subordinator, and the word that solves the *like*-versus-*as* problem: it is standard in every register, takes a full clause without complaint, and never sounds stiff. Note the idiomatic fronted use, where *The way he tells it* means "according to his account" and *The way prices are climbing* means "given that."

1. Do it *the way* I showed you.
2. *The way* he tells it, he practically saved the company.
3. I didn't like *the way* she said that.
4. The house is exactly *the way* we left it.
5. She organizes her notes *the way* a lawyer builds a case.
6. *The way* prices are climbing, we should book the flights now.

**See also:** [how](#how), [as](#as), [like](#like), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that)

### the way I see it

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈweɪ aɪ ˈsi ɪt/ &middot; *thuh WAY eye SEE it*
**Type:** fixed stance formula built on the manner subordinator *the way*; functions as a sentence adverbial &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** fronted adverbial clause of manner, commenting on the whole sentence
**Pattern:** *The way I see it*, [main clause]. &middot; *The way I see it*, [clause], *and* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *the way I see it*. &middot; *As far as I'm concerned*, [main clause] (the equivalent formula).
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, always — it is a supplement, not part of the clause it introduces. Comma before it when it trails as an afterthought.
**Variants:** *the way I look at it*, *the way I figure it*, *as I see it*, *as far as I'm concerned*, *to my mind*

The everyday way of labeling an opinion as an opinion: literally "in the manner in which I see it," frozen into a discourse formula that softens a claim by owning it. It is the idiomatic fronted use of *the way*, and it belongs to speech, columns, and email rather than to reports. *As far as I'm concerned* is the same move with an edge — it marks the speaker's view as settled for their own purposes, which is why it so often introduces a refusal.

1. *The way I see it*, we either raise the price or cut the hours.
2. *The way I see it*, nobody in that meeting had read the contract.
3. *As far as I'm concerned*, the matter is closed.
4. *The way I look at it*, a used car with service records beats a new one without.
5. She's already apologized, *the way I see it*, and that should be the end of it.
6. *As far as I'm concerned*, they can keep the deposit — I'm not going back.

## Proportion and correspondence

Five entries that say the halves of a sentence vary together, or that pair their members off one for one. They are punctuated differently, so do not generalize from one to the other. *The more … the more* joins two full clauses of equal weight and takes a comma at the seam. *According as* and *in proportion as* are ordinary subordinators: comma after the clause when it is fronted, no comma when it trails. The other two are not conjunctions at all but adverbs, and that changes everything about the commas — *correspondingly* standing between two independent clauses needs a semicolon or a period in front of it, exactly like *however*, and *respectively* sits at the end of its own clause behind a single comma and joins nothing to anything.

### according as

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋ əz/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal, dated
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of proportion and correspondence
**Pattern:** [main clause] *according as* [clause]. &middot; *According as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *according as* [clause] *or* [clause] (the paired-outcome form). &middot; [main clause] *according to whether* [clause] (the modern paraphrase). &middot; [main clause] *according to* [noun phrase] (prepositional — a different word class).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows the main clause. No comma before the internal *or* that pairs the two outcomes.
**Variants:** *according to whether* (ordinary modern paraphrase), *according to which*

Means "in proportion to whether" or "depending on which of these turns out to be true" — the outcome varies with the condition. It survives in legal, mathematical, and older literary prose. Note that it is a conjunction taking a clause, unlike the everyday preposition *according to*, which takes a noun phrase; everyday American English says *depending on whether* instead.

1. The rate rises or falls *according as* the index moves.
2. *According as* the weather allows, the ceremony will be held outdoors or in the hall.
3. Each parcel is taxed *according as* it is classified residential or commercial.
4. The sign of the result changes *according as* n is even or odd.
5. They will be judged *according as* they have acted.

**See also:** [according to](#according-to), [insofar as](#insofar-as), [as far as](#as-far-as), [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as), [the more … the more](#the-more--the-more)

### correspondingly

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɔrəˈspɑndɪŋli/, also /ˌkɑrəˈspɑndɪŋli/ &middot; *kor-uh-SPON-ding-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb of correspondence; also an ordinary degree adverb inside the clause (*a correspondingly larger bill*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; reporting, policy, and technical prose
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial saying its clause moves in step with the one before it; degree modifier in the adjectival use
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *correspondingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Correspondingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [main clause], *and* [subject] [verb phrase] *correspondingly*. &middot; [subject], *correspondingly*, [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *a correspondingly* [comparative adjective] [noun] (degree use).
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a clause; commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb. Between two independent clauses it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice: ✗ *The levy rose, correspondingly receipts fell.* ✓ *The levy rose; correspondingly, receipts fell.* No comma at all in the degree use, where it simply modifies an adjective: *a correspondingly smaller refund*.
**Variants:** *proportionately*, *in proportion*, *to a corresponding degree*, *equally*, *by the same token*

The adverb of matched movement: whatever the first clause reports going up or down, the second clause is said to follow it. That makes it narrower than *similarly*, which claims only a resemblance — *correspondingly* claims a linkage between two quantities, and it rings false when nothing is actually varying. Its clause-taking equivalents are *in proportion as* and *the more … the more*, which say the same thing with a subordinator instead of an adverb, and a comma instead of a semicolon.

1. The district enrolled two hundred more students; *correspondingly*, it hired six more teachers.
2. Freight volumes fell all spring. *Correspondingly*, rail revenue came in under forecast.
3. Premiums went up nine percent, and coverage narrowed *correspondingly*.
4. As the deductible rises, the monthly payment drops *correspondingly*.
5. The new building is twice the size of the old one and carries a *correspondingly* larger tax bill.
6. Testing slowed over the holiday; *correspondingly*, the reported case counts dipped and then caught up.

**See also:** [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as), [the more … the more](#the-more--the-more), [according as](#according-as), [respectively](#respectively), [analogously](#analogously)

### in proportion as

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən əz/ &middot; *in pruh-POR-shun uhz*
**Type:** subordinating (compound); the shortened *in proportion to* is a **preposition** taking a noun phrase &middot; **Register:** formal, dated
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of proportion and correspondence
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in proportion as* [clause]. &middot; *In proportion as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in proportion to* [noun phrase] (prepositional — a different word class). &middot; *In proportion as* [clause], *so* [clause] (with a resumptive *so* opening the second half).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause when it is fronted; no comma when it follows the main clause. Nothing inside the clause takes one.
**Variants:** *in proportion to* (prepositional), *in the same proportion as*, *proportionately as* (rare), *the more … the more* (everyday equivalent)

Says that two quantities move together — as one rises or falls, so does the other. It is the clause-taking twin of the everyday preposition *in proportion to*, and the difference is only what follows: the conjunction takes a full clause (*in proportion as the debt grows*), the preposition takes a noun phrase (*in proportion to the debt*). In American English the conjunction is confined to formal argument, older prose, mathematics, and physical law; ordinary writing reaches for *the more … the more* or *as … so*, which say the same thing and sound like this century.

1. Wages rose *in proportion as* the cost of housing climbed.
2. *In proportion as* the light fades, the colors go flat.
3. A tax is fair *in proportion as* it tracks the ability to pay.
4. The pressure increases *in proportion as* the volume shrinks.
5. *In proportion as* the town grew, the old rivalries lost their heat.
6. He grew more careful *in proportion as* the stakes rose.
7. *In proportion as* the population aged, *so* the tax base narrowed.

**See also:** [according as](#according-as), [the more … the more](#the-more--the-more), [as … so](#as--so), [insofar as](#insofar-as)

### respectively

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈspɛktɪvli/, weak /rəˈspɛktɪvli/ &middot; *rih-SPEK-tiv-lee*
**Type:** adverb of one-to-one correspondence — **not** a conjunction; it distributes the members of one list across the members of another and joins nothing &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; statistical, scientific, and journalistic
**Clause type:** adverbial of correspondence, almost always clause-final
**Pattern:** [list A] [verb] [list B], *respectively*. &middot; [X] *and* [Y] *were* [A] *and* [B], *respectively*. &middot; [X], [Y], *and* [Z] [verb] [A], [B], *and* [C], *respectively*. &middot; [main clause] ([A] *and* [B], *respectively*). &middot; [X] *and* [Y], *respectively*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** A comma before it at the end of the clause, which is where it almost always sits; commas on both sides in the rarer medial position. Because it is an adverb with no linking function at all, it can never stand between two independent clauses in any punctuation — not with a comma and not with a semicolon. Do not spell it *respectfully*, which means "with respect" and is a different word.
**Variants:** *in that order*, *in order*, *correspondingly* (loosely — it does not match lists)

Pairs off two lists in sequence: *Chicago and Denver grew four and seven percent, respectively* assigns four to Chicago and seven to Denver. It only works when both lists have the same number of members in the same order, and it is empty when there is only one item on a side (✗ *Sales rose in Ohio, respectively*). It is catalogued here as the last member of the correspondence family and as a warning: writers reach for it as though it meant *in turn*, *accordingly*, or *each in its own way*, and it means none of those.

1. The two bills cleared the House and the Senate by margins of twelve and three votes, *respectively*.
2. Chicago and Denver grew four and seven percent, *respectively*, over the decade.
3. Gold, silver, and bronze went to Norway, Japan, and Canada, *respectively*.
4. The control and treatment groups reported 14 and 31 adverse events, *respectively*.
5. Her first and second novels sold nine hundred and ninety thousand copies, *respectively*.
6. The north and south wings were built in 1904 and 1927, *respectively*.

**See also:** [correspondingly](#correspondingly), [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as), [according as](#according-as), [the more … the more](#the-more--the-more)

### the more … the more

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈmɔr … ðə ˈmɔr/ &middot; *thuh MOR … thuh MOR*
**Type:** correlative (proportional comparative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** paired comparative clauses — the first is subordinate, the second is the main clause
**Pattern:** *The* [comparative], *the* [comparative]. &middot; *The more* [clause], *the more* [clause]. &middot; *The* [comparative] [noun], *the* [comparative] [noun] (verbless).
**Punctuation:** Comma at the seam between the two halves — required in edited prose and the single rule learners most often miss. No comma inside either half. Very short verbless pairs are sometimes printed without it, but *The sooner, the better* is the standard form.
**Variants:** *the more … the less*, *the sooner … the better*, *the bigger … the harder*, and any other pair of comparatives

Says that two quantities rise or fall together. The *the* here is not the article but a surviving Old English instrumental form meaning "by that much," which is why it can stand in front of a comparative and why both halves need one. Word order is fixed: the comparative phrase moves to the front of each clause, so it is *The more he explained*, never *He explained the more*.

1. *The more* he explained, *the less* I understood.
2. *The sooner* you file, *the sooner* you get the refund.
3. *The colder* it gets, *the more* the pipes rattle.
4. *The harder* she practiced, *the better* she played.
5. *The bigger* the crowd, *the longer* the wait.
6. *The sooner*, *the better*.

**See also:** [as … so](#as--so), [according as](#according-as), [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as), [as much as](#as-much-as)

## Phrasal comparison markers

Reduced, participial, and prepositional phrases that name the baseline of a comparison, plus the two clause-taking contrast subordinators — *whereas* and *while* — that punctuate the same way. The contrast markers among them comment on the main clause instead of completing it, which is why they are normally set off with commas: always after a fronted one, and normally before a trailing one. The loose member of that set is the everyday *compared to*, which drops the comma when a short phrase trails. The conformity markers — *according to*, *consistent with*, *in conformity with*, *in keeping with*, *in line with*, *relative to*, *when it comes to* — split the difference: fronted they take a comma like any other long adverbial, but trailing they belong to the predicate and take none (*Seats were assigned according to rank*).

Watch the boundary with the conjunctive adverbs, because it is where the commas in this section go wrong. *Compared to*, *in comparison with*, and *in contrast to* keep their *to* or *with*, take a phrase, and are set off with commas. Strip the *to* and the same words become adverbs — *by comparison*, *in contrast*, *on the other hand* — and an adverb standing between two independent clauses needs a semicolon or a period in front of it: *Rural counties shrank; in contrast, the suburbs grew*, never ✗ *Rural counties shrank, in contrast the suburbs grew*.

### according to

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tu/, weak /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tə/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding too*
**Type:** preposition (complex) — it takes a noun phrase or a nominal *wh-* clause, never a finite clause; the clause-taking form is the conjunction *according as* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of source, standard, or correspondence; supplementary when fronted
**Pattern:** *According to* [noun phrase], [main clause] (attribution). &middot; [main clause], *according to* [noun phrase] (attribution, trailing). &middot; [main clause] *according to* [noun phrase] (standard or correspondence). &middot; [main clause] *according to whether* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *according to* [wh-clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. In the attribution sense a trailing phrase takes a comma before it (*The flight is on time, according to the airline*); in the correspondence sense a trailing phrase is integral to the predicate and takes none (*Seats were assigned according to rank*).
**Variants:** *according as* (the conjunction), *according to whether*, *in accordance with* (formal, conformity only), *per* (business shorthand)

Two jobs share one phrase. In the attribution sense it names whose account you are relaying (*according to the police*), and it carries a faint note of distance — you are reporting the claim, not vouching for it, which is why *According to me* sounds wrong and *in my opinion* is the phrase for your own view. In the correspondence sense it names the standard something varies with (*paid according to experience*), and that is the sense whose clause-taking twin is *according as*.

1. *According to* the airline, the delay is weather.
2. Seats were assigned *according to* seniority.
3. The bill will be scored *according to* the committee's own formula.
4. *According to* the label, the paint needs two hours between coats.
5. Attendance rose and fell *according to* the weather.
6. The fee changes *according to* whether you file online or by mail.
7. It rained all weekend in the mountains, *according to* my sister.

**See also:** [according as](#according-as), [in conformity with](#in-conformity-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [consistent with](#consistent-with), [in proportion as](#in-proportion-as)

### along the same lines

**Pronunciation:** /əˌlɔŋ ðə seɪm ˈlaɪnz/ &middot; *uh-LONG thuh same LYNEZ*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker — prepositional with *as* before a noun phrase; a **conjunctive adverb** when it stands alone &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase of resemblance; adverbial in the bare form
**Pattern:** [main clause] *along the same lines as* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Along the same lines*, [main clause] (adverbial). &middot; [main clause] *along the same lines* (bare, trailing). &middot; [main clause] *along much the same lines as* [noun phrase]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *along the same lines*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *along the same lines as* when it completes the comparison. Comma after the bare form when it opens a sentence. Standing between two independent clauses it is an adverb and needs a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice.
**Variants:** *along similar lines*, *along much the same lines*, *on the same lines* (chiefly British), *in the same vein*

Says that the second thing follows the pattern of the first without matching it exactly — the register of memos, workshops, and editorial notes, where it turns a suggestion into a direction. With *as* it names the model it is following; bare, it points back at whatever was just said. Its neighbor *in the same vein* covers tone and style, while this one covers approach and design.

1. The second proposal runs *along the same lines as* the first, with a smaller budget.
2. *Along the same lines*, the committee wants a shorter application form.
3. We're thinking *along the same lines* — let's compare notes.
4. She wants a mural *along the same lines as* the one in the lobby.
5. The state bill was drafted *along much the same lines as* the federal one.
6. The two reports reach different numbers, but they argue *along the same lines*.

**See also:** [in the same vein](#in-the-same-vein), [in keeping with](#in-keeping-with), [in line with](#in-line-with), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [likewise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#likewise)

### as against

**Pronunciation:** /æz əˈɡɛnst/, weak /əz əˈɡɛnst/ &middot; *az uh-GENST*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase, most often a figure, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** formal; the register of reporting and statistics
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase naming the figure or the case being set against
**Pattern:** [figure] *as against* [figure]. &middot; [main clause], *as against* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As against* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X], *as against* [Y], [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the tight figure-against-figure pair (*eleven percent as against nine*). Comma before it when it trails as a supplement, comma after it when it is fronted, commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb.
**Variants:** *as distinct from*, *as compared with*, *as opposed to*, *versus* (the everyday shorthand), *against*

The statistician's contrast: it sets one measured value beside another and lets the gap speak for itself. British writing also uses it freely in the *as opposed to* sense, but American English mostly keeps it for numbers and reaches for *as opposed to* or *versus* everywhere else. Whatever follows has to be the same kind of quantity as what precedes it, or the comparison means nothing.

1. Turnout was thirty-one percent, *as against* nineteen in the last off-year election.
2. The county recorded 412 permits, *as against* 380 a year earlier.
3. *As against* the projected shortfall, the actual gap was small.
4. He was sentenced to two years, *as against* the ten the statute allows.
5. Revenue grew four percent, *as against* an industry average of one.
6. *As against* what the district spends per student, the neighboring county looks generous.

**See also:** [as compared with](#as-compared-with), [as distinct from](#as-distinct-from), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [relative to](#relative-to)

### as compared with

**Pronunciation:** /æz kəmˈpɛrd wɪð/, weak /əz kəmˈpɛrd wɪθ/ &middot; *az kum-PAIRD with*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (reduced participial phrase, functioning as a preposition) — it takes a noun phrase or a nominal *wh-* clause, never an ordinary finite clause &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of comparison, supplementary
**Pattern:** [main clause], *as compared with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As compared with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X], *as compared with* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *as compared with* [wh-clause].
**Punctuation:** Set off with commas — comma after it when fronted, comma before it when it trails. It is a supplement, not an integral part of the clause.
**Variants:** *compared with*, *as compared to*, *as against*

The report-and-abstract way of naming the baseline of a comparison. Traditionally *compared with* measures like against like on one scale (two quarters, two cities) and *compared to* likens unlike things; in current American usage the two have largely merged, but *with* remains the safer choice in technical writing. Make sure the thing being compared is the subject of the main clause, or the phrase dangles.

1. Emissions fell four percent, *as compared with* the same quarter last year.
2. *As compared with* the pilot program, the statewide rollout cost twice as much.
3. Response times improved, *as compared with* the baseline measured in March.
4. The new alloy is brittle, *as compared with* standard steel.
5. *As compared with* her earlier novels, this one is almost cheerful.
6. Enrollment is up nine percent, *as compared with* what the district projected in the spring.

**See also:** [compared to](#compared-to), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [than](#than), [whereas](#whereas)

### as distinct from

**Pronunciation:** /æz dɪˈstɪŋkt frəm/, weak /əz dɪˈstɪŋkt frəm/ &middot; *az dih-STINKT frum*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (adjectival preposition) — it takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase drawing a line between two categories
**Pattern:** [X], *as distinct from* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *as distinct from* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As distinct from* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as distinct from* [-ing].
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb; comma before it when it trails as a supplement; comma after it when fronted. No comma inside a tight paired phrase (*advice as distinct from instruction*).
**Variants:** *as distinguished from*, *as against*, *as opposed to*, *not to be confused with*

Draws a categorical line rather than stating a preference: it says the two things differ in kind, and it assumes the reader might have run them together. That is what separates it from *as opposed to*, which names an alternative that was rejected — *as distinct from* rejects nothing, it only sorts. Keep the two halves the same kind of phrase, and make the second one the thing the reader was likelier to have meant.

1. The report covers full-time staff, *as distinct from* contractors.
2. What she offered was advice, *as distinct from* an instruction.
3. *As distinct from* the state tax, the city levy applies only to earned income.
4. He studies climate, *as distinct from* weather, and the difference matters to his funding.
5. The lease covers repairs, *as distinct from* improvements the tenant chooses to make.
6. We're counting how many people read the email, *as distinct from* how many opened it.

**See also:** [as distinguished from](#as-distinguished-from), [as against](#as-against), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [unlike](#unlike)

### as distinguished from

**Pronunciation:** /æz dɪˈstɪŋɡwɪʃt frəm/, weak /əz dɪˈstɪŋɡwɪʃt frəm/ &middot; *az dih-STING-gwisht frum*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (participial preposition) — it takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and academic
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase drawing a technical distinction
**Pattern:** [X], *as distinguished from* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *as distinguished from* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As distinguished from* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as distinguished from* [-ing].
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb; comma before it when it trails; comma after it when fronted.
**Variants:** *as distinct from*, *in contradistinction to* (heavier still), *as opposed to*, *unlike*

The courtroom's version of *as distinct from*, and a shade more deliberate: *distinguished* implies that somebody has drawn the line on purpose, which is exactly what a judge does in distinguishing one case from another. Reserve it for genuinely technical distinctions in formal prose, where the line being drawn carries consequences. Everywhere else it is three syllables of ceremony that *as distinct from* supplies for free.

1. The statute reaches negligence, *as distinguished from* deliberate harm.
2. Ordinary income, *as distinguished from* capital gains, is taxed at the full rate.
3. *As distinguished from* the earlier ruling, this opinion turns on intent.
4. She was hired as an employee, *as distinguished from* an independent contractor.
5. The term refers to the printed record, *as distinguished from* the recording of the hearing.
6. Fair use, *as distinguished from* a negotiated license, requires no permission at all.

**See also:** [as distinct from](#as-distinct-from), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [unlike](#unlike), [whereas](#whereas)

### as for

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɔr/, weak /əz fər/ &middot; *az FOR*, reduced *uhz fur*
**Type:** fixed topic-shifting marker — prepositional in effect, taking a noun phrase, a pronoun, an *-ing* form, or a nominal *wh-* clause, never a finite clause of its own &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** fronted adverbial phrase of respect, naming the topic the main clause is about to settle
**Pattern:** *As for* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *As for* [pronoun], [main clause]. &middot; *As for* [-ing], [main clause]. &middot; *As for* [wh-clause], [main clause]. &middot; *As for the rest* / *as for that*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the phrase, always — it is a fronted supplement, not part of the clause that follows. Unlike *when it comes to*, it does not trail the main clause, so the trailing comma question never arises: write *As for the budget, we'll take that up in September*, not ✗ *We'll take that up in September as for the budget.*
**Variants:** *as to* (formal, and not restricted to the front), *as regards*, *regarding*, *when it comes to*, *as far as … is concerned*

Hands the sentence over to a new topic — usually one the reader has been waiting for — by picking something up and saying "now that one." That is what separates it from *when it comes to*, which narrows a general claim to one domain instead of switching subjects, and it is why *as for* so often carries a faint note of dismissal: *As for the neighbors, they can think what they like.* It takes a phrase and never a clause, so write *as for whether the road reopens*, not ✗ *as for the road reopens*.

1. *As for* the budget, we'll take that up in September.
2. The kids loved the museum. *As for* me, I loved the café.
3. *As for* whether the road reopens by Friday, nobody at the county will say.
4. He's agreed to the price. *As for* the closing date, that's still open.
5. *As for* getting there early, don't bother — the doors don't open until seven.
6. The engine is sound. *As for* the transmission, I'd get a second opinion.

**See also:** [when it comes to](#when-it-comes-to), [as far as](#as-far-as), [relative to](#relative-to), [vis-à-vis](#vis-à-vis), [the way I see it](#the-way-i-see-it)

### as in

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈɪn/, weak /əz ɪn/ &middot; *az IN*
**Type:** reduced comparison marker — the manner subordinator *as* with an elliptical clause, prepositional in effect &middot; **Register:** neutral; the clarifier use is informal
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of comparison; also an exemplifying phrase
**Pattern:** [main clause], *as in* [noun phrase] (parallel case). &middot; [main clause] *as in* [noun phrase] (integral). &middot; [sound / sense], *as in* "[example]" (illustration). &middot; *As in* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [statement] — *as in*, [restatement] (spoken clarifier).
**Punctuation:** Comma before it when it adds a supplementary parallel or an illustration (*a soft g, as in "giraffe"*). No comma when it is integral to the predicate (*The vowel is long as in "make"*). Comma after it when fronted, and a comma after it in the spoken clarifier use.
**Variants:** *as with*, *just as in*, *such as in*, *like in* (informal)

A compression of *as it is in*: it points at another case where the same thing holds, and in its commonest use it names the example that pins down a pronunciation or a sense. Speech has stretched it into a clarifier — *We need it soon, as in today* — where it introduces a restatement rather than a comparison, and that use stays out of formal writing. Whatever follows is a noun phrase, so it never takes a clause of its own.

1. The vowel is short, *as in* "bet."
2. Federal aid arrived late, *as in* every previous flood.
3. *As in* the pilot program, the classes are capped at fifteen.
4. It's a soft g, *as in* "giraffe."
5. Turnout collapsed in the suburbs, *as in* 2018.
6. She wants it done soon — *as in*, before the board meets.

**See also:** [as with](#as-with), [as](#as), [just as](#just-as), [like](#like)

### as opposed to

**Pronunciation:** /æz əˈpoʊzd tu/, weak /əz əˈpoʊzd tə/ &middot; *az uh-POHZD too*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (participial preposition) — it takes a noun phrase, an *-ing* form, or a nominal *wh-* clause, never a finite clause of its own &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase naming the rejected alternative
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as opposed to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *as opposed to* [-ing]. &middot; *As opposed to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X], *as opposed to* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *as opposed to* [wh-clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. A comma before it when it trails as a supplement (*The grant is a subsidy, as opposed to a loan*); none when the two halves are a tight pair inside the clause (*leasing as opposed to buying*). Commas on both sides when it interrupts subject and verb.
**Variants:** *as against*, *in contrast to*, *rather than* (the equivalent that can take a bare infinitive)

Names the alternative that was *not* chosen, and presses on the distinction harder than *rather than* does — it is the phrase you reach for when you expect the reader to have confused the two things. Being a preposition, it takes a noun phrase, an *-ing* form, or a noun clause, never a bare infinitive: *as opposed to renting*, not *as opposed to rent*, whenever the other half of the pair is a gerund. Keep the two halves the same shape and it never goes wrong.

1. The grant is a subsidy, *as opposed to* a loan the city expects back.
2. *As opposed to* last year's format, this conference runs three days.
3. She was hired as a consultant, *as opposed to* a full-time employee.
4. We're talking about the printed edition, *as opposed to* whatever is on the website.
5. They're leasing this time *as opposed to* buying.
6. The tax applies to income, *as opposed to* assets.

**See also:** [compared to](#compared-to), [rather than](#rather-than), [unlike](#unlike), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [whereas](#whereas), [as compared with](#as-compared-with)

### as with

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈwɪð/, weak /əz wɪθ/ &middot; *az WITH*
**Type:** reduced comparison marker — the manner subordinator *as* with an elliptical clause; prepositional in effect, taking a noun phrase or an *-ing* form &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of comparison, supplementary
**Pattern:** *As with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As with* [-ing], [main clause]. &middot; *As with* [noun phrase], *so with* [noun phrase] (the balanced pair).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. Comma before it when it trails, because it comments on the whole clause instead of completing it.
**Variants:** *as is the case with*, *as happens with*, *as in*, *like* (informal)

Short for *as is the case with*: it names a parallel case and claims that the main clause runs the same way there. It is the workhorse opener of explanatory prose — *As with most rules, there are exceptions* — because it lets a writer generalize and qualify in three words. Watch for the dangler: whatever follows *as with* has to be genuinely comparable to the subject of the main clause.

1. *As with* most rules, there are exceptions.
2. *As with* the first dose, the second one left her arm sore for a day.
3. The fee is waived for students, *as with* the museum's other programs.
4. *As with* buying a house, the closing costs are where the surprises live.
5. *As with* her earlier books, the plot matters less than the voice.
6. Snow closed the pass again, *as with* every storm this winter.

**See also:** [as in](#as-in), [as](#as), [like](#like), [in the same way that](#in-the-same-way-that), [as compared with](#as-compared-with)

### by analogy with

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ əˈnælədʒi wɪð/ &middot; *by uh-NAL-uh-jee with*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** formal; academic, legal, and technical
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase naming the model a claim or a coinage is reasoned from
**Pattern:** [main clause] *by analogy with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *By analogy with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *was coined / formed / decided by analogy with* [Y]. &middot; [main clause], *by analogy with* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. No comma when it trails as part of the predicate (*coined by analogy with "workaholic"*); a comma before it only when it is a genuine aside.
**Variants:** *by analogy to*, *on the analogy of*, *on the model of*, *analogous to*

Names the case a conclusion was reasoned *from*, not merely a case it is being measured against — which is what separates it from *compared to*. Linguists use it for the process that gives us *chocoholic* out of *alcoholic*; lawyers use it for reasoning from a decided case to an undecided one. In American technical prose prefer *with*; *by analogy to* is common but reads a shade looser.

1. The word was coined *by analogy with* "workaholic."
2. *By analogy with* the postal rule, the court treated the email as sent on dispatch.
3. Early chemists named the gas *by analogy with* the elements they already knew.
4. *By analogy with* municipal bonds, the new instrument pays tax-free interest.
5. Children build past tenses *by analogy with* the regular verbs they know, which is why "goed" turns up before "went."
6. The argument runs *by analogy with* the flu vaccine, and it breaks down for the same reasons.

### by the same token

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ðə ˌseɪm ˈtoʊkən/ &middot; *by thuh same TOH-kun*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (fixed phrase) of parallel inference &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** sentence adverbial marking its clause as following from the same reasoning that produced the last one
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *by the same token*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *By the same token*, [independent clause]. &middot; [main clause], *and by the same token* [clause]. &middot; [subject], *by the same token*, [verb phrase]. &middot; *If* [clause], *then by the same token* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a clause; commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb. Standing between two independent clauses it is an adverb, so it takes a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice: ✗ *He can't vote on it, by the same token neither can she.* ✓ *He can't vote on it; by the same token, neither can she.* Only a coordinating *and* can carry it across with a comma.
**Variants:** *by that token*, *by the same reasoning*, *equally*, *likewise*, *conversely* (the opposite move)

Says that the reason just given cuts a second way as well — not that two things merely resemble each other, but that one argument covers both. That is the line between it and its neighbors: *likewise* and *similarly* point at a resemblance, while *by the same token* points at a shared premise, which is why it so often introduces the less welcome half of a principle. The *token* is the old sense "sign, evidence," not a coin.

1. A landlord can't be forced to rent to anyone he pleases; *by the same token*, a tenant can't be forced to stay.
2. If experience should count toward the promotion, then *by the same token* it should count toward the pay.
3. She refused to comment on the lawsuit. *By the same token*, she wouldn't discuss the settlement talks.
4. Cheap money made the boom possible, and *by the same token* it made the crash worse.
5. The rule keeps outside firms from bidding; *by the same token*, it keeps the town from finding a cheaper contractor.
6. He never asked anyone for help and, *by the same token*, never offered any.

**See also:** [analogously](#analogously), [correspondingly](#correspondingly), [along the same lines](#along-the-same-lines), [in the same vein](#in-the-same-vein), [likewise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#likewise)

### compared to

**Pronunciation:** /kəmˈpɛrd tu/, /kəmˈpɛrd tə/ &middot; *kum-PAIRD too*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (reduced participial phrase, functioning as a preposition) — it takes a noun phrase, an *-ing* form, or a nominal *wh-* clause, never an ordinary finite clause; *compare* is also a plain transitive verb (*He compared the two drafts*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of comparison
**Pattern:** *Compared to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *compared to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *compared to* [wh-clause]. &middot; [main clause] *compared to* [-ing]. &middot; [X], *compared to* [Y], [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. When it trails the main clause the comma is optional and usually dropped if the phrase is short and integral (*The repair was cheap compared to replacing the whole unit*); keep it when the phrase is a genuine afterthought. Commas on both sides when it interrupts subject and verb.
**Variants:** *compared with*, *as compared to*, *in comparison to*

The everyday version of the same move, and the one American speakers actually use. Classically *compared to* likens two unlike things to make a point (*He compared the city to an anthill*) while *compared with* sets two like things side by side, but as a sentence-opening adverbial *compared to* now covers both. The fronted form must still describe the subject: *Compared to last winter, this one has been mild* works because both are winters.

1. *Compared to* last winter, this one has been mild.
2. The rent is reasonable *compared to* what friends pay downtown.
3. *Compared to* the original, the remake feels rushed.
4. Two hours is nothing *compared to* the drive we made last summer.
5. *Compared to* most of his classmates, he reads several grades ahead.
6. The repair was cheap *compared to* replacing the whole unit.

**See also:** [as compared with](#as-compared-with), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [than](#than), [the same as](#the-same-as)

### consistent with

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɪstənt wɪð/ &middot; *kun-SIS-tunt with*
**Type:** adjective + preposition; a phrasal marker of conformity, and a supplementary adverbial when fronted &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; heavily used in science, medicine, and law
**Clause type:** predicative complement after *be*; adverbial phrase of conformity when fronted or set off
**Pattern:** [X] *is consistent with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Consistent with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *consistent with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *consistent with* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [X] *is consistent with* [wh-clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the predicative use (*The results are consistent with the model*). Comma after the fronted adverbial (*Consistent with earlier work, the trial found no effect*), and a comma before it when it trails as a supplement.
**Variants:** *in a manner consistent with*, *not inconsistent with* (the lawyer's hedge), *in keeping with*, *in line with*

Says that two things fit together without contradiction — and note how much weaker that is than saying they match. In scientific and medical writing it is a deliberate hedge: *findings consistent with pneumonia* means the picture fits pneumonia, not that pneumonia is proved, and the double negative *not inconsistent with* hedges further still. Fronted, it becomes a conformity adverbial that ties a new result to an established one.

1. The blood work is *consistent with* a viral infection.
2. *Consistent with* earlier studies, the trial found no effect on mortality.
3. His account is *consistent with* what the neighbors told police.
4. The city's decision is *consistent with* the zoning code, whatever the neighbors think.
5. *Consistent with* the new policy, all requests now go through the portal.
6. The wear on the tire is *consistent with* driving on it flat.

### in comparison with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kəmˈpɛrɪsən wɪð/, /ɪn kəmˈpɛrɪsən wɪθ/ &middot; *in kum-PAIR-ih-sun with*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a finite clause; the clipped *by comparison* and *in comparison* are **conjunctive adverbs** &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** reduced adverbial clause of comparison, supplementary
**Pattern:** *In comparison with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in comparison with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *in comparison with* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *in comparison with* [-ing]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *by comparison*, [independent clause] (the clipped adverb).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase; comma before it when it trails and is supplementary. The clipped forms punctuate differently, because they are adverbs and not prepositions: between two independent clauses *by comparison* needs a semicolon or a period before it (*The first draft was long; by comparison, the second is a pamphlet*), and a comma there is a splice.
**Variants:** *in comparison to*, *by comparison with*, *by comparison* (standalone)

The heaviest member of the family, at home in reports and academic prose. It says exactly what *compared with* says in three more words, so prefer the shorter form unless the sentence already has a *compared* doing other work. The clipped *by comparison* can stand alone after a full clause, which the long form cannot.

1. *In comparison with* its neighbors, the state spends very little per student.
2. The delay was trivial *in comparison with* the problems that followed.
3. *In comparison with* the 1990s, violent crime is down sharply.
4. Her early paintings look tentative *in comparison with* the later work.
5. The second proposal is modest *in comparison with* the first.
6. The first draft ran three hundred pages; *by comparison*, the second is a pamphlet. *(the clipped form is an adverb — semicolon, not comma)*

### in conformity with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˈfɔrməti wɪð/ &middot; *in kun-FOR-muh-tee with*
**Type:** phrasal marker of conformity (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, regulatory, and diplomatic
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of conformity; supplementary when fronted
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in conformity with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In conformity with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *is in conformity with* [Y]. &middot; [main clause], *in conformity with* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. No comma when it trails as part of the predicate; a comma before it only when it is a genuine aside.
**Variants:** *in conformance with*, *in accordance with*, *in compliance with*, *conformably to* (archaic), *in keeping with*

The rule-book register: it says the thing described has been measured against a stated standard and does not depart from it. *In accordance with* is its everyday twin and reads better in most sentences, while *in compliance with* is the one to use when the standard is mandatory and somebody could be penalized for missing it. Keep it for documents that actually name the standard being met; anywhere else it is bureaucratic padding for *under* or *following*.

1. The audit was conducted *in conformity with* generally accepted accounting principles.
2. *In conformity with* the treaty, both governments filed annual reports.
3. The building was inspected and found to be *in conformity with* the fire code.
4. Ballots are counted *in conformity with* procedures published before the election.
5. *In conformity with* long practice, the chair recognized the minority leader first.
6. The device is manufactured *in conformity with* the European standard as well as the American one.

### in contrast to

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑntræst tu/, weak /ɪn ˈkɑntræst tə/ &middot; *in KAHN-trast too*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase, an *-ing* form, or a nominal *wh-* clause, never a finite clause; strip the *to* and bare *in contrast* is a **conjunctive adverb** &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase of contrast
**Pattern:** *In contrast to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *in contrast to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *in contrast to* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; *In contrast to* [-ing], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *in contrast to* [wh-clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in contrast*, [independent clause] (the bare adverb).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. Comma before it when it trails, because it comments on the main clause rather than completing it; the comma disappears only in the tight predicate use (*small in contrast to the effort*), which is rarer here than with *compared to*. Commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb. The stripped-down *in contrast* is where writers go wrong: it is an adverb, so between two independent clauses it needs a semicolon or a period, never a comma. ✗ *Rural counties shrank, in contrast the suburbs grew.* ✓ *Rural counties shrank; in contrast, the suburbs grew.*
**Variants:** *in contrast with*, *in sharp / stark / marked contrast to*, *by contrast with*, *in contrast* (the bare conjunctive adverb)

Names the thing the subject differs from, and unlike the *compare* family it is only ever about difference — there is no reading in which it finds two things alike. Its nearest neighbors split the work three ways: *unlike* says the same thing in one word and takes the same commas, *as opposed to* points at a rejected alternative rather than a plain difference, and *whereas* does the job with a full clause instead of a phrase. Because the phrase has no verb of its own, whatever follows *to* has to be the same kind of thing as the subject of the main clause, or it dangles: ✗ *In contrast to last year, the mayor added two positions* compares a year to a mayor.

1. *In contrast to* her sister, she never took a music lesson in her life.
2. The new regulation, *in contrast to* the old one, covers part-time workers.
3. Attendance held steady downtown, *in contrast to* the sharp drop in the suburbs.
4. *In contrast to* what the brochure promised, the room faced the parking lot.
5. Rents fell here, *in contrast to* almost every other city on the coast.
6. *In contrast to* borrowing at a fixed rate, a variable loan moves with the index.
7. The coasts lost residents; *in contrast*, the mountain states gained them. *(bare adverb — semicolon, not comma)*

### in keeping with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkipɪŋ wɪð/ &middot; *in KEE-ping with*
**Type:** phrasal marker of conformity (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of conformity and harmony; supplementary when fronted
**Pattern:** *In keeping with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in keeping with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X] *is in keeping with* [Y]. &middot; *In keeping with* [-ing], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *is out of keeping with* [Y] (the negative).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. No comma when it trails as part of the predicate (*a plain stone in keeping with the rest of the churchyard*); comma before it when it is a supplement.
**Variants:** *out of keeping with* (the negative), *in keeping* (bare, after *be*), *in the spirit of*, *in line with*

Conformity with a tone, a tradition, or a style rather than with a rule — which is what separates it from *in accordance with*, and why it lives in obituaries, architecture criticism, and dress codes. It has a working negative, *out of keeping with*, that no other member of this family really offers, and that negative is where the phrase does its sharpest work. What follows it has to be something a manner could harmonize with, not a number.

1. *In keeping with* family tradition, the service was small and early.
2. The addition uses local stone, *in keeping with* the older wing.
3. Her decision to walk away is entirely *in keeping with* everything she has written.
4. *In keeping with* the season, the menu is all root vegetables.
5. The tone of the letter was *out of keeping with* the rest of the file.
6. Guests dressed *in keeping with* the invitation, which had asked for "garden formal."

### in line with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪn wɪð/ &middot; *in LYNE with*
**Type:** phrasal marker of conformity (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase, never a finite clause &middot; **Register:** neutral; business, journalism, and policy writing
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of conformity and correspondence; supplementary when fronted
**Pattern:** [X] *is in line with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In line with* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in line with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X] *is broadly / roughly in line with* [Y]. &middot; *bring / keep* [X] *in line with* [Y].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. No comma when it trails, in the predicative use (*Rents are in line with the county*) or the adverbial one alike.
**Variants:** *broadly in line with*, *out of line with* (the negative), *in keeping with*, *consistent with*

The quarterly-report phrase: earnings come in *in line with* expectations, and the whole job of the words is to say that a figure landed where it was supposed to. It also has an active use — *bring state law in line with the federal statute* — meaning to make one thing match another. The literal ancestor still shows through, so what follows should be something a number or a policy could be lined up against.

1. Revenue came in *in line with* what analysts expected.
2. *In line with* the new guidance, the district moved its start time back an hour.
3. The proposal would bring state law *in line with* the federal statute.
4. Rents here are roughly *in line with* the rest of the county.
5. His testimony was *out of line with* everything in the written record.
6. *In line with* past practice, the committee released the transcript a week later.

### in the same vein

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə seɪm ˈveɪn/ &middot; *in thuh same VAYN*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker — prepositional with *as* before a noun phrase; a **conjunctive adverb** when it stands alone &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase of tone and style; adverbial in the bare form
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in the same vein as* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In the same vein*, [main clause] (adverbial). &middot; [main clause], *in the same vein* (trailing). &middot; [Independent clause]; *in the same vein*, [independent clause]. &middot; *In a similar vein*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *in the same vein as* when it completes the comparison. Comma after the bare form when it opens a sentence. Between two independent clauses it is an adverb and takes a semicolon or a period before it, never a comma: ✗ *The first essay was funny, in the same vein the second is grim.*
**Variants:** *in a similar vein*, *in that vein*, *in the same spirit*, *along the same lines*

Points at a shared manner, tone, or line of thought rather than at a measurable likeness — the metaphor is a vein of ore running through rock, so what continues is a seam of the same material. Bare, it works as a transition between two remarks of the same kind; with *as*, it names the work or the comment it is echoing. Keep it for style and register, and use *along the same lines* when the resemblance is one of method.

1. Her second collection is *in the same vein as* the first, only funnier.
2. *In the same vein*, the mayor proposed cutting parking fees downtown.
3. The sequel continues *in the same vein* for another four hundred pages.
4. He told one story about his father; *in the same vein*, she told one about her mother.
5. *In a similar vein*, the committee asked for a plainer summary.
6. The painting is *in the same vein as* his early work, before the murals.

### qua

**Pronunciation:** /kweɪ/, also /kwɑ/ &middot; *KWAY*
**Type:** preposition (Latin) — "in the capacity of," "considered as"; it takes a bare noun phrase, never a clause &middot; **Register:** formal; philosophical, academic, and legal
**Clause type:** prepositional phrase of role and respect
**Pattern:** [noun] *qua* [the same or a related noun] (*the novel qua novel*). &middot; [X], *qua* [role], [verb phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *qua* [noun phrase]. &middot; *considered qua* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** No commas around it in the tight noun-*qua*-noun pair — it binds to the nouns on either side. Commas only when the whole *qua* phrase interrupts a subject and its verb (*The judge, qua judge, has no opinion*). Set it in roman; it has been English long enough not to need italics.
**Variants:** *as such*, *considered as*, *in the capacity of*, *as* (the ordinary English equivalent)

Latin for "in the capacity of," and the word philosophers use to isolate one aspect of a thing from everything else that is true about it: *the novel qua novel* means the novel considered purely as a novel, setting aside its politics, its sales, and its author. English *as* does the same job in the role sense, so *qua* earns its keep only where repeating the noun is the point. Used loosely for *and* or *versus* it is simply wrong — what follows *qua* is a role, not a second party.

1. We are studying the building *qua* architecture, not *qua* real estate.
2. The judge, *qua* judge, has no opinion about the wisdom of the statute.
3. Money *qua* money is worthless; it matters only for what it buys.
4. He was writing *qua* historian rather than *qua* citizen.
5. The question is whether a corporation *qua* corporation can hold a religious belief.
6. Sociology treats the group *qua* group, not as a pile of individuals.

**See also:** [as](#as), [as in](#as-in), [relative to](#relative-to), [vis-à-vis](#vis-à-vis), [as such](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#as-such)

### relative to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɛlətɪv tu/, weak /ˈrɛlətɪv tə/ &middot; *REL-uh-tiv too*
**Type:** phrasal comparison marker (prepositional) — it takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* form, never a finite clause; *relative* is also a plain adjective (*relative humidity*) and a noun (*a distant relative*) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; technical, financial, and academic
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of comparison naming the reference point; also a phrase of topic in the "concerning" sense
**Pattern:** [main clause] *relative to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Relative to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *is* [comparative] *relative to* [Y]. &middot; [figure] *relative to* [figure]. &middot; [main clause] *relative to* [-ing].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase. No comma when it trails, because it completes the measurement rather than commenting on it.
**Variants:** *in relation to*, *with respect to*, *as against*, *compared to*, *vis-à-vis*

Names the baseline a measurement is taken from, which makes it the phrase of choice wherever a number only means something against a reference — velocity, exchange rates, cost of living, performance against a benchmark. That is what separates it from *compared to*: *compared to* invites the reader to notice a difference, while *relative to* says the second term is the frame the first is being measured in. It has a looser second life meaning "concerning" (*documents relative to the sale*), which reads as legal boilerplate and is better written *about*.

1. The dollar has weakened *relative to* the euro.
2. *Relative to* its population, the state sends an outsized delegation to Washington.
3. Housing costs are high here *relative to* wages.
4. The fund underperformed *relative to* its benchmark for a third straight quarter.
5. *Relative to* last quarter, complaints are down by a third.
6. Measure the shadow *relative to* the height of the pole.

### unlike

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌnˈlaɪk/ &middot; *un-LYKE*
**Type:** preposition of contrast — the negative of prepositional *like*; also an adjective in *It's unlike her to be late* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** supplementary comparative phrase; it has no clause-taking form at all
**Pattern:** *Unlike* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *unlike* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *unlike* [Y], [verb phrase]. &middot; *It is unlike* [person] *to* [verb phrase] (adjective).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. Comma before it when it trails as a supplement. Commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb: *My sister, unlike me, reads the manual.*
**Variants:** *not like* (informal), *in contrast to*, *as opposed to*, *unlike with* (loose, in the "in the case of" sense)

The negative counterpart of prepositional *like*, and the one member of this family with no conjunction use whatsoever — there is no *unlike he does*, only *unlike him*. Because it is a bare phrase, it dangles easily: *Unlike most cities, the streets here are numbered* compares cities to streets. Whatever follows *unlike* has to be the same kind of thing as the subject of the main clause. It carries a second, idiomatic "out of character" sense (*It's unlike her to be late*), where it is an adjective rather than a preposition.

1. *Unlike* her brother, she never learned to drive.
2. The new lease, *unlike* the old one, lets us sublet.
3. *Unlike* most of the coast, this stretch has no public access.
4. He took the criticism well, *unlike* the last person in the job.
5. It's *unlike* Marco to leave without saying goodbye.
6. *Unlike* the earlier models, this one runs quietly enough to sit next to.

### vis-à-vis

**Pronunciation:** /ˌviz ə ˈvi/ &middot; *veez-uh-VEE*
**Type:** preposition (from French) — "in relation to," "as against"; it takes a noun phrase, never a clause, and it is also a noun meaning "counterpart" &middot; **Register:** formal; diplomatic, business, and academic
**Clause type:** adverbial phrase of relation and comparison
**Pattern:** [main clause] *vis-à-vis* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X]'s position *vis-à-vis* [Y]. &middot; *Vis-à-vis* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [X] *is* [comparative] *vis-à-vis* [Y].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase; no comma when it trails. Keep both hyphens, and keep the grave accent — *vis-à-vis* is the dictionary spelling in American English, and the bare *vis-a-vis* is a common second best.
**Variants:** *vis-a-vis* (unaccented), *in relation to*, *with respect to*, *as against*, *face to face with* (the literal French sense)

Literally "face to face," and the surviving sense keeps that picture: two parties lined up opposite each other, with one measured against the other. Analysts and diplomats use it for standing and leverage — *the union's position vis-à-vis management* — where *compared to* would sound merely arithmetical. In loose American usage it has drifted toward a vague "regarding," and that drift draws complaints; keep it where two parties genuinely face each other.

1. The bank's position *vis-à-vis* its European competitors has improved.
2. *Vis-à-vis* the state, a city has only the powers the legislature grants it.
3. She wanted to know where the department stood *vis-à-vis* the new funding rules.
4. The treaty leaves the smaller countries weaker *vis-à-vis* their neighbors.
5. Our costs are unremarkable *vis-à-vis* the rest of the industry.
6. He was hired to handle the company's dealings *vis-à-vis* the regulators.

**See also:** [relative to](#relative-to), [as against](#as-against), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [compared to](#compared-to), [qua](#qua)

### when it comes to

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn ɪt ˈkʌmz tu/, also /hwɛn/ &middot; *wen it KUMZ too*
**Type:** fixed topic-limiting phrase — prepositional in effect, taking a noun phrase or an *-ing* form; the clause is frozen, and *when it came to* is the only variation it allows &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of respect, narrowing the main clause to one topic
**Pattern:** *When it comes to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *When it comes to* [-ing], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *when it comes to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *When it came to* [noun phrase], [main clause] (past narrative).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, always. No comma when it trails the main clause.
**Variants:** *when it came to*, *as far as … is concerned*, *where … is concerned*, *as for*, *in the matter of* (formal)

Narrows the sentence to a single subject before the claim arrives — "on the topic of." It reads as speech written down: useful in a column or an email, and usually deletable in formal prose, where *As for* or a plain preposition does the same work in fewer words. Note that it is not a time clause at all, despite the *when*: nothing in it points to a moment.

1. *When it comes to* paperwork, she is the most patient person I know.
2. *When it comes to* raising rates, the board has been cautious.
3. He's fearless on the water and useless *when it comes to* spiders.
4. *When it came to* naming a successor, the founder stalled for two years.
5. *When it comes to* barbecue, everyone in this town has a theory and a cousin.
6. The company is generous with equipment and stingy *when it comes to* time off.

**See also:** [as far as](#as-far-as), [the way I see it](#the-way-i-see-it), [as with](#as-with), [insofar as](#insofar-as)

### whereas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæz/, /hwɛrˈæz/ &middot; *wair-AZ*
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; the "given that" sense is legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of contrast, comparing two parallel facts
**Pattern:** [main clause], *whereas* [clause]. &middot; *Whereas* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [X] [verb phrase], *whereas* [Y] [verb phrase] (the balanced pair). &middot; *Whereas* [clause]; *whereas* [clause]; now, therefore, [main clause] (legal preamble).
**Punctuation:** Comma before *whereas* even when it follows the main clause — unlike most subordinators it is always supplementary, never integral. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. In legal preambles, where each *Whereas* clause is a separate recital, the recitals are closed with semicolons rather than commas. Note that *whereas* is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb: the comma before it is correct and no semicolon is needed. Write *however* the other way — *We were late; however, they held our table* — because that one is an adverb, not a conjunction.
**Variants:** *where as* (misspelling), *whereas … whereas* (stacked, in legal preambles)

Sets two facts side by side to point out that they differ, so the contrast is the entire content of the comparison. Compare its neighbors: *although* concedes, meaning the main clause wins the argument, while *whereas* balances, asserting both halves equally; and *while* is ambiguous between "at the same time" and "by contrast," where *whereas* can only mean the contrast. Note that *whilst* is British — it is a synonym of *while*, not of *whereas*, and it has no place in American prose.

1. My brother loves horror movies, *whereas* I can't get through the trailer.
2. The northern route is faster, *whereas* the coastal road is prettier.
3. *Whereas* the first draft ran long, the second barely filled ten pages.
4. She was trained as a chemist, *whereas* her partner came up through sales.
5. Enrollment rose in the suburbs, *whereas* the city schools lost students.
6. *Whereas* the parties wish to settle all outstanding claims, the following terms are agreed.

**See also:** [while](#while), [much as](#much-as), [compared to](#compared-to), [in contrast to](#in-contrast-to), [in comparison with](#in-comparison-with), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### while

**Pronunciation:** /waɪl/, also /hwaɪl/ &middot; *WYLE*
**Type:** subordinating — contrast here, but the same word also subordinates time and concession, and outside the conjunction system it is a noun (*stay a while*) and a verb (*while away an afternoon*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; a step below *whereas* in formality and far commoner in speech
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of contrast, setting two parallel facts side by side
**Pattern:** [main clause], *while* [clause]. &middot; *While* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [X] [verb phrase], *while* [Y] [verb phrase] (the balanced pair). &middot; [subject], *while* [reduced phrase], [predicate] (concessive reduction: *The plan, while ambitious, is affordable* — see [while (concession)](07-concession.md#while)).
**Punctuation:** Comma before a trailing contrastive *while* clause and after a fronted one. That comma is doing real work, because a trailing **temporal** *while* clause takes no comma: *He waited while she made the call* means "during," and *He waited, while she made the call* means "she, by contrast, made the call." Do not reach for a semicolon here — *while* is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb, so a comma is the correct mark; it is simply not optional in the contrast sense.
**Variants:** *whereas* (the formal equivalent, contrast only), *whilst* (**British** — dated or affected in American prose), *where* (contrastive, more clipped)

Three jobs, one word: time ("during"), concession ("although"), and contrast ("whereas"). Only context and that comma sort them out, which is why careful American writers switch to *whereas* whenever a sentence could be read two ways. In the contrast sense *while* balances two facts and leaves both standing, exactly as *whereas* does, but at a conversational level. Note that the British *whilst* is a synonym of *while* in all three senses, not of *whereas*, and it has no place in American prose; the same flag applies to *amongst* and *amidst*.

1. The morning session ran long, *while* the afternoon finished early.
2. *While* the coasts get the coverage, the growth is happening inland.
3. Her first album was all guitars, *while* this one is mostly synths.
4. *While* federal law caps the fee, the state sets no limit at all.
5. Deliveries are up, *while* the number of drivers has barely moved.
6. He handles the money, *while* she does everything else.
