# Complementizers and noun-clause introducers

> The words that shrink a whole clause down to the size of a noun, so that one sentence can serve as another sentence's subject or object.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every word in this file does the same structural job: it takes a clause and makes it available as a noun — the subject of a verb, the object of a verb or a preposition, the complement of an adjective, or an appositive to a noun. Some of them (*that*, *whether*, *if*, *for*) are pure markers that carry no meaning and fill no role inside the clause they introduce; the wh-words (*what*, *who*, *when*, *how*) both introduce the clause and occupy a slot in it; the *-ever* forms fold a head noun and a relative clause into a single word. What separates the whole category from the rest of the conjunction system is that a noun clause is an **argument** — delete it and the sentence collapses (*I know* … *what?*) — while an adverbial clause is a modifier the sentence can survive without.

Three rules run through everything below. **Never put a comma between a verb and its noun clause**: *He said that he was tired*, not *He said, that he was tired* — the comma belongs only with a direct quotation (*He said, "I'm tired."*). **An embedded question keeps statement word order and takes no question mark**: *I asked where the office is*, never *I asked where is the office?* And **the *however* in this file is not the *however* that means "nevertheless"** — that one is a conjunctive adverb, and between two independent clauses it requires a semicolon or a period before it and a comma after it (*We left early; however, the traffic was already bad*); a comma in the semicolon's place is a comma splice. Any fronted-clause comma you see below (*Whatever happens, call me*) belongs to a different construction — the concessive adverbial — and not to the noun clause.

## The bare complementizers

Markers that fill no slot inside their own clause and take no comma at the junction with the verb, noun, or adjective they complete — not even when the clause they head is the subject of the sentence. Most are pure grammar and contribute no meaning of their own; *like*, *as if*, and *as though* carry a faint hypothetical tint, which is exactly what formal writing weighs when it chooses among the three. Three of these headwords (*as if*, *as though*, *whether or not*) also do adverbial work elsewhere, and in that other job — never in this one — a fronted clause takes a comma.

### as if

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈɪf/ &middot; *az IF* — the first word almost always reduces to /əz/, and the stress sits on *if*
**Type:** complementizer (compound); the same pair is also a manner-and-comparison subordinator and the standalone retort *As if!* &middot; **Register:** neutral — the formal counterpart of complementizer *like*
**Clause type:** noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (*seem, look, sound, feel, appear*), and of *be* in the fixed *It's not as if* frame
**Pattern:** It [seems/looks/sounds/appears] *as if* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] *as if* [clause]. &middot; It [verb of appearance] *to* [object] *as if* [clause]. &middot; It's not *as if* [clause]. &middot; It's not *as if* [clause], but [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as if* in this use: it stands between a verb and its own complement, and that junction never takes a comma. The comparison *as if* is the one with a comma rule, and only when it is fronted (*As if nothing had happened, he sat down*). Deletion tells you which one you have — *It seems* by itself is unfinished, so that *as if* clause is an argument and takes no comma; *He talks* by itself is a whole sentence, so that one is a modifier and can be moved.
**Variants:** *as though* (interchangeable, a shade more literary); *like* (informal); *that* after *seem* and *appear* (*It seems that nobody read it*)

The formal substitute for complementizer *like*, and the form to reach for the moment the writing stops being conversational. Mood is the detail Americans split on: when the clause is contrary to fact, careful writing keeps the past subjunctive (*It looked as if he were joking*), and when the situation may well be true the indicative is normal and far commoner (*It looks as if the flight is on time*). Keep it apart from the manner *as if* of *He talks as if he owns the place*: that one modifies a verb the sentence survives without and can be fronted, while this one is an argument and cannot move anywhere.

1. It seems *as if* nobody bothered to open the attachment.
2. It's not *as if* I had a choice in the matter.
3. The engine sounds *as if* something came loose underneath.
4. I feel *as if* we skipped a step somewhere in this recipe.
5. From up here it looks *as if* the whole street lost power.
6. It appeared to the auditors *as if* the invoices had been backdated.

**See also:** [as though](#as-though), [like](#like), [that](#that), [whether](#whether), [as if (comparison)](08-comparison.md#as-if)

### as though

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈðoʊ/ &middot; *az THOH* — the first word reduces to /əz/; the stress falls on *though*
**Type:** complementizer (compound); the same pair is also a manner-and-comparison subordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal — a shade more literary than *as if*
**Clause type:** noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (*seem, look, sound, feel, appear*), and of *be* in the *It's not as though* frame
**Pattern:** It [seems/looks/sounds/appears] *as though* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] *as though* [clause]. &middot; It [verb of appearance] *to* [object] *as though* [clause]. &middot; It's not *as though* [clause]. &middot; It's not *as though* [clause], but [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as though* when it completes a verb of appearance or sensation — the junction between a verb and its complement never takes one. The comparison *as though* is the one that takes a comma, and only when fronted: *As though on cue, the lights came back on.* That construction is a modifier, not an argument.
**Variants:** *as if* (same meaning, commoner in speech); *like* (informal); *that* after *seem* and *appear*

The near-twin of *as if*, genuinely interchangeable with it in meaning and differing only in texture: *as though* reads slightly more literary and is mildly favored after verbs of feeling (*It felt as though hours had passed*). Like *as if*, it does double duty — a complementizer when the clause completes *seem*, *look*, *sound*, *feel*, or *appear*, and an adverbial of manner when it modifies an ordinary verb (*He shrugged as though the whole thing bored him*). Only the adverbial one can be fronted, and only the adverbial one ever takes a comma.

1. It felt *as though* hours had passed in that waiting room.
2. It seems *as though* every form here has to be filed twice.
3. It's not *as though* nobody warned us about the fees.
4. The house looked *as though* no one had lived there in years.
5. It sounded *as though* the furnace kicked on and then quit.
6. It seemed to everyone in the room *as though* the decision had already been made.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [like](#like), [that](#that), [as though (comparison)](08-comparison.md#as-though)

### if

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf/ &middot; *IF* — reduced to /əf/ in connected speech (*I don't know əf he's home*)
**Type:** complementizer; the same spelling is also the subordinating conjunction of condition &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning informal
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect yes-no question)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *if* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *if* [clause]. &middot; [imperative verb] *if* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *if* [clause] *or not*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before *if* and none inside the clause. Conditional *if* is the one that takes a comma when its clause is fronted (*If it rains, we'll drive*); a noun-clause *if* can never be fronted at all, so no comma question ever arises.
**Variants:** *if … or not* — the *or not* can only trail the clause (*if he's coming or not*), never sit next to *if*

The everyday spoken alternative to *whether* for indirect yes-no questions. It cannot go everywhere *whether* goes: no *if*-clause may follow a preposition, precede a *to*-infinitive, or serve as the subject of a sentence. It is also ambiguous between condition and question — *Let me know if he calls* can mean "in the event he calls" or "tell me whether he calls" — and swapping in *whether* is the standard fix. Keep it separate from the conditional *if*, which is a different word wearing the same three letters: that one heads an adverbial clause the sentence survives without, moves to the front, and takes a comma there; this one is an argument the verb cannot do without and cannot move anywhere.

1. I don't know *if* the pharmacy on Third is still open.
2. She asked *if* I'd ever driven a stick shift.
3. Check *if* the milk's expired before you pour it.
4. He wondered *if* anyone had noticed him slip out.
5. Nobody could tell me *if* the flight was delayed *or not*.
6. See *if* the hardware store carries that size.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [that](#that), [as to whether](#as-to-whether), [if (conditional)](06-condition.md#if)

### like

**Pronunciation:** /laɪk/ &middot; *LYKE* — unstressed in this use and often reduced to /lək/ (*It's not lək I had a choice*)
**Type:** complementizer (informal); the same spelling is also a preposition, a comparison subordinator, a verb, a noun, a quotative (*she was like, "no way"*), and a discourse particle &middot; **Register:** informal — universal in speech, ordinary in fiction and journalism, still corrected in academic and formal business writing
**Clause type:** noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (*seem, look, sound, feel, act*), and of *be* in the fixed *It's not like* frame
**Pattern:** It [seems/looks/sounds] *like* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] *like* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb of acting] *like* [clause]. &middot; It's not *like* [clause]. &middot; It's not *like* [clause], but [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *like* and none inside the clause: it stands between a verb and its own complement, and that junction never takes a comma. The discourse particle *like* is the one that gets commas (*It was, like, three hours*), and it is a different word entirely.
**Variants:** formal substitutes *as if* / *as though* (*It seems as though nobody read it*); *that* after *feel* and *seem* (*I feel that we're missing a step*)

The complement-clause *like*, as opposed to the comparison *like* of *She sings like her mother did*. The test is whether the clause is an argument: *It seems* and *I feel* are unfinishable without what follows, so the *like*-clause is filling a noun slot, not modifying anything. Americans use it constantly and edited prose still resists it, so the usable rule is to keep it in speech and dialogue and swap in *as if*, *as though*, or *that* the moment the writing turns formal. The *It's not like…* frame is fixed and idiomatic, and no substitution improves it.

1. It seems *like* nobody bothered to open the attachment.
2. I feel *like* we skipped a step somewhere in this recipe.
3. It's not *like* I had a choice in the matter.
4. The engine sounds *like* something came loose underneath.
5. She acts *like* she's never seen a parking meter before.
6. Honestly, it looks *like* the whole thing was decided before the meeting started.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [as though](#as-though), [that](#that), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [not that](#not-that), [like (comparison)](08-comparison.md#like)

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt/ &middot; *THAT* — as a complementizer it is nearly always reduced to /ðət/ &middot; *thut*; the full vowel signals the demonstrative (*I said* /ðæt/)
**Type:** complementizer; the same spelling is also a relative pronoun, a demonstrative determiner and pronoun, and a degree adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause) — subject, object, subject complement, adjective complement, or noun appositive
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *that* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *that* [clause]. &middot; *That* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; It [verb] [object] *that* [clause]. &middot; [noun] *that* [clause]. &middot; [adjective] *that* [clause]. &middot; The [noun] is *that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Never a comma before complementizer *that* — *He said, that he was tired* is an error. A parenthetical inserted right after it takes commas on both sides: *He said that, if it rains, they'll cancel.* No comma either when the *that*-clause is a fronted subject: *That he lied surprised no one.*
**Variants:** zero *that* — see [zero *that*](#zero-that)

The workhorse of the category: a pure marker that packages a statement into a noun-sized chunk a verb, noun, or adjective can take as its complement. Four different words share the spelling, and only this one is a conjunction. Unlike relative *that* (*the box that came yesterday*), it fills no role inside its own clause and leaves no gap, which is exactly why it can usually be deleted; unlike demonstrative *that* (*I said that*), it carries no meaning and takes no stress; unlike the degree adverb *that* (*I'm not that hungry*), it modifies nothing. Where *whether* and *if* introduce a question, *that* introduces an assertion.

1. She admitted *that* she'd forgotten the deadline entirely.
2. *That* he never called back tells you everything.
3. It's obvious *that* they rehearsed this.
4. There's a rumor *that* the plant is closing in March.
5. I'm just glad *that* you two finally met.
6. The problem is *that* nobody wants to pay for the repairs.

**See also:** [zero *that*](#zero-that), [whether](#whether), [if](#if), [like](#like), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [not that](#not-that), [but that](#but-that)

### whether

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər/ &middot; *WEH-thur* — a homophone of *weather* for most Americans; a conservative /ˈhwɛðər/ survives in parts of the South and Midwest
**Type:** complementizer; also the first half of the correlative pair *whether … or* &middot; **Register:** neutral, leaning formal
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect yes-no question, or alternative question)
**Pattern:** [verb] *whether* [clause]. &middot; *Whether* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *whether* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *whether to* [verb]. &middot; [verb] *whether* [clause] *or* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *whether*, none inside the clause, and none after a fronted subject clause: *Whether he comes is up to him.* A fronted concessive *whether … or …* clause is a different construction and does take a comma: *Whether you like it or not, it's happening.*
**Variants:** [whether or not](#whether-or-not); archaic *whether or no*

The complementizer of indirect yes-no questions and of stated alternatives, and the neutral choice everywhere *if* is barred: after a preposition (*depends on whether*), before an infinitive (*whether to go*), at the head of a subject clause, and any time *if* would be misread as a condition. Against *that*, the difference is settled versus open — *I know that she's coming* asserts it; *I know whether she's coming* only claims to have the answer.

1. It depends entirely on *whether* the landlord approves the sublet.
2. I still can't decide *whether* to drive or fly.
3. *Whether* she was joking is anybody's guess.
4. He asked me *whether* the offer was still good.
5. The real question is *whether* anyone will notice.
6. We argued about *whether* to tell him now *or* wait until Monday.

**See also:** [if](#if), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [as to whether](#as-to-whether), [that](#that), [whatever](#whatever), [whether to / what to / how to / where to](#whether-to--what-to--how-to--where-to), [the question whether](#the-question-whether)

### whether or not

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛðər ər ˈnɑt/ &middot; *weh-thur er NAHT* — the middle syllable is almost always the reduced /ər/
**Type:** complementizer; also subordinating in its concessive use &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question); also adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** [verb] *whether or not* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *whether* [clause] *or not*. &middot; *Whether or not* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *whether or not* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use. In the concessive use ("regardless of whether"), a fronted clause takes a comma after it and a trailing one normally takes a comma before it. *If or not* is not English in either use.
**Variants:** split *whether … or not*; archaic *whether or no*

Two constructions sharing one shape. As a complementizer it is plain *whether* with the alternative spelled out, and the *or not* is padding most editors cut. As a concessive it means "no matter whether," and there the *or not* is structural and cannot be dropped — which is the quick test for telling the two apart.

1. Let me know *whether or not* you can make it Saturday.
2. I asked *whether* the check had cleared *or not*.
3. *Whether or not* the weather cooperates, the ceremony starts at four.
4. Nobody has told us *whether or not* the position is still open.
5. He's showing up, *whether or not* anyone invited him.
6. The board votes tonight on *whether or not* to renew the lease.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [if](#if), [whatever](#whatever), [however](#however)

### zero *that*

**Pronunciation:** ∅ — unpronounced; the clause simply begins with its own subject
**Type:** complementizer (null) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause), object or complement only
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] ∅ [clause]. &middot; [adjective] ∅ [clause]. &middot; The [noun] is ∅ [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma, ever — and the missing word makes the wrong comma more tempting: *I think, we took a wrong turn* is an error, not a pause.
**Variants:** the overt [that](#that)

American English drops complementizer *that* freely after common reporting and mental verbs (*say, think, know, hope, feel, guess, bet*) and after adjectives (*glad, sure, afraid*), and the omission is fully standard in speech and in most edited prose. Omission is blocked in five places: when the clause is the sentence's subject (*That he lied surprised me*), after a noun (*the rumor that…*), when anything comes between the verb and the clause (*He told me yesterday that he'd quit*), in the second of two coordinated clauses (*He said he was tired and that he wanted to leave*), and in fixed frames like *the fact that* and *not that*. After heavy reporting verbs — *contend, assert, maintain, note* — dropping it is possible but reads badly in formal writing.

1. I think ∅ we took a wrong turn back at the light.
2. She said ∅ she'd call back after lunch.
3. Everyone knows ∅ the parking around the stadium is terrible.
4. I'm just glad ∅ it's over.
5. He swears ∅ he mailed the check on Monday.
6. The trouble is ∅ nobody kept the receipts.

**See also:** [that](#that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [not that](#not-that), [the thing is / here's the thing](#the-thing-is--heres-the-thing), [trouble is / problem is / fact is / truth is](#trouble-is--problem-is--fact-is--truth-is), [no wonder / small wonder / little wonder](#no-wonder--small-wonder--little-wonder)

## Wh-words that head noun clauses

These are not pure complementizers: each one introduces the clause *and* fills a slot inside it, which is why none of them can be deleted the way *that* can. Two rules govern all of them. Keep **statement word order** — no auxiliary inversion (*I asked what he wants*, never *what does he want*) — and never end the containing sentence with a question mark unless the whole sentence is itself a question. All but *why* and *how come* also allow a *to*-infinitive — *what to do*, *how to ask*, *where to go*, *who to call*, but not *why to go* or *how come to go* — and that construction has an entry of its own at [whether to / what to / how to / where to](#whether-to--what-to--how-to--where-to).

### how

**Pronunciation:** /haʊ/ &middot; *HOW*
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative adverb); the same word is also an exclamative adverb (*How quickly this year went!*) and, in the comparison family, a manner subordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause — embedded wh-question of manner, means, or degree; also a fused relative of manner, and an embedded exclamative
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *how* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *how* [clause]. &middot; *How* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *how to* [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *how* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *how* [adjective/adverb] [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere — not before *how*, not inside its clause, and not before a fronted *how*-clause subject (*How she found the address is still a mystery*). Statement order inside the clause: *I asked how it works*, not *I asked how does it work*.

Introduces an embedded question about manner, means, or — with an adjective or adverb attached — extent (*how long*, *how much*). It also heads a fused relative meaning "the way in which," where it competes with *the way that*; formal writing prefers *how* to the redundant *the way how*, which is not standard. A third use embeds an exclamative rather than a question — *It's amazing how quickly kids pick it up* — and that clause, like the others, keeps statement order and ends the sentence with a period.

1. Nobody ever explained *how* the billing cycle actually works.
2. *How* she found the address is still a mystery to me.
3. I never learned *how* to parallel park.
4. It depends on *how* much overtime they approve.
5. Watch *how* he shifts his grip on the second swing.
6. She wanted to know *how* long the drive takes.
7. It's amazing *how* fast that dog learned the fence line.

**See also:** [how come](#how-come), [why](#why), [what](#what), [however](#however), [how that](#how-that), [whether to / what to / how to / where to](#whether-to--what-to--how-to--where-to)

### how come

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˈkʌm/ &middot; *how-KUM*
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative phrase) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question of reason)
**Pattern:** *How come* [clause]? &middot; [subject] [verb] *how come* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [object] *how come* [clause]. &middot; [imperative verb] [indirect object] *how come* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma. Unlike *why*, *how come* never triggers inversion even in a direct question: *How come he left?*, never *How come did he leave?* A direct question with it still takes a question mark; an embedded one still takes a period (*She never explained how come she quit.*).

A colloquial *why* that takes statement word order in main clauses as well as embedded ones, which makes it the one wh-question American learners can never get inverted by mistake. Its embedded use (*I don't know how come*) is common in speech and avoided in edited writing, where *why* is the safe substitute. It also refuses the *to*-infinitive that most wh-words allow: *how come to leave* is not English.

1. *How come* nobody told me the meeting moved?
2. I have no idea *how come* the charge showed up twice.
3. She never explained *how come* she quit so suddenly.
4. "*How come* you're up so early?" "Couldn't sleep."
5. Ask him *how come* the car's still in the shop.

**See also:** [why](#why), [how](#how)

### what

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/ &middot; *WUHT* — also /wɑt/ &middot; *WAHT*; reduced to /wət/ in connected speech
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative pronoun and determiner; also fused relative pronoun and exclamative determiner) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause — embedded wh-question, fused ("headless") relative, or embedded exclamative
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *what* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *what* [clause]. &middot; *What* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *what to* [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *what* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *what* [noun] [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *what a* [noun] [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma, and none before a fronted *what*-clause subject: *What he said surprised us.* Never follow it with *that*: *what that he wants* is not English.

Three jobs in one word. As an interrogative it heads an indirect question (*I asked what he wants*); as a fused relative it means "the thing(s) which" and needs no antecedent (*What he wants is a raise*); as an exclamative determiner it embeds the same way an exclamation does, minus the exclamation point (*What a mess this is!* → *You wouldn't believe what a mess this is*). The fused-relative use is where learners overreach: *what* replaces an entire noun-plus-relative sequence, so *the thing what he wants* and *everything what I own* are both wrong — after an expressed noun you need *that* or *which*, and the *what* relative of *the man what fixed the roof* is dialect, not standard American English.

1. I never found out *what* the second envelope contained.
2. *What* really bothers me is the tone of the email.
3. Tell me *what* you need and I'll grab it on the way.
4. She has no idea *what* to wear to a rehearsal dinner.
5. The final bill depends on *what* the inspector finds.
6. *What* he said and *what* he meant were two very different things.
7. You wouldn't believe *what* a mess the kitchen was when we got home.

**See also:** [which](#which), [whatever](#whatever), [that](#that), [how](#how), [whether to / what to / how to / where to](#whether-to--what-to--how-to--where-to)

### when

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn/ &middot; *WEN* — reduced to /wən/; conservative /hwɛn/ in some regional speech
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative adverb); the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time, a concessive subordinator, and a relative adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question of time) — distinct from the adverbial *when* of the time-clause family
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *when* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *when* [clause]. &middot; *When* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *when to* [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *when* [clause]. &middot; [linking verb] *when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, including when the clause is a fronted subject: *When they'll announce it is anyone's guess.* Adverbial *when* is the one that takes a comma when fronted: *When they announce it, we'll know.*

One word, two grammars. In the noun-clause use the *when*-clause is an argument — substitute *something* and the sentence still stands — and it never takes a comma; in the adverbial use it modifies the main clause and follows the ordinary fronted-subordinator comma rule. There is also a tense test: the noun clause allows a future (*I don't know when he'll arrive*), while the adverbial one forbids it (*when he arrives*, not *when he will arrive*).

1. Nobody will tell me *when* the results get posted.
2. *When* the renovation actually finishes is anybody's guess.
3. She's still deciding *when* to give notice.
4. The whole schedule hinges on *when* the permit clears.
5. Ask the dispatcher *when* the next bus comes through.
6. I honestly don't know *when* he'll get here.
7. The only real question now is *when* they'll announce it.

**See also:** [whenever](#whenever), [where](#where), [why](#why), [how](#how)

### where

**Pronunciation:** /wɛr/ &middot; *WAIR* — conservative /hwɛr/ in some regional speech
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative adverb; also fused relative); the same word is a place subordinator, a relative adverb, and a concessive subordinator elsewhere &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question of place); also a fused relative of place
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *where* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *where* [clause]. &middot; *Where* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *where to* [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *where* [clause]. &middot; [linking verb] *where* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *where*, none inside the clause, and none after a fronted *where*-clause subject: *Where the money went has never been explained.* Adverbial *where* is the one with a comma rule when fronted (*Where the sidewalk ends, the gravel starts*). Statement order: *I asked where it is*, not *I asked where is it*.

Heads an embedded question about place, and also a fused relative meaning "the place at which" (*This is where I grew up*). Distinguish it from the adverbial *where* of *Sit where you like*, which modifies the verb rather than filling an argument slot and belongs to the place-clause family, not to this one.

1. She wouldn't say *where* she'd been all afternoon.
2. *Where* the money actually went has never been explained.
3. We spent an hour arguing about *where* to put the couch.
4. That bend in the road is *where* the fault line runs.
5. Text me *where* you parked so I'm not circling the lot.
6. It matters a great deal *where* the closing takes place.

### whether to / what to / how to / where to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər tə/, /ˈwʌt tə/, /ˈhaʊ tə/, /ˈwɛr tə/ &middot; *WEH-thur tuh, WUHT tuh, HOW tuh, WAIR tuh* — the stress stays on the wh-word and *to* always reduces to /tə/, flapping to /ɾə/ after a vowel (*what to order* comes out *whaddu order*)
**Type:** complementizer + infinitive marker (infinitival wh-clause); each member is one of the wh-words in this section, or *whether*, followed by bare *to* &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (infinitival embedded question) — object of a verb, object of a preposition, subject, or subject complement
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *[wh-word] to* [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *[wh-word] to* [verb]. &middot; *[Wh-word] to* [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *[wh-word] to* [verb]. &middot; [linking verb] *[wh-word] to* [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *whether to* [verb] *or to* [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere, and no subject inside the clause — the whole point of the construction is that the subject goes unexpressed and is recovered from the main clause. Never add *should* on top of the *to* (*what to do* or *what I should do*, never *what to should do*), and never insert *that* (*how to fix it*, not *how that to fix it*).
**Variants:** the finite paraphrase with *should* or *can* (*where to park* → *where I should park*); *whether or not to* (*whether or not to sign*); *who to* — the ordinary American form, where the stiffest prose writes *whom to*

The infinitival half of the embedded-question system: the wh-word supplies the question, *to* supplies a verb with no tense, and the subject is left out and read off the main clause — *She's deciding whether to go* can only mean she is deciding about herself. It is open to *whether* and to every wh-word in this section except *why* and *how come* (*why to go* and *how come to leave* are not English), and it is the one embedded question that sits comfortably after a preposition (*a question of what to charge*). What it buys is compression: *nobody told us where to file it* says in six words what *nobody told us where we were supposed to file it* needs ten to say.

1. She still hasn't decided *whether to* take the job or stay put.
2. Nobody told us *where to* drop off the paperwork.
3. He's never learned *how to* back a trailer down a driveway.
4. *What to* wear to the rehearsal dinner turned into a two-day argument.
5. The real problem is *who to* call at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night.
6. Half the meeting went to arguing about *whether to* renew the contract *or to* rebid it.
7. The manual says nothing about *which to* replace first.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [what](#what), [how](#how), [where](#where), [who](#who), [why](#why), [whether or not](#whether-or-not)

### which

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ &middot; *WICH* — a homophone of *witch* for most Americans; conservative /hwɪtʃ/
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative pronoun or determiner); the same word is also a relative pronoun, restrictive and nonrestrictive &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question, selective)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *which* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *which* [noun] [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *which of* [noun phrase] [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *which* ([noun]) *to* [verb]. &middot; *Which* [noun] [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; *Which* [noun] [subject] [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *which* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use. Relative *which* is the one with comma rules (*the car, which was new, wouldn't start*); the noun-clause *which* never takes one, no matter how long the clause runs.

The selective interrogative: it asks for a choice from a known, closed set, where *what* asks open-endedly. *I don't know which he picked* presumes a menu; *I don't know what he picked* does not. It works alone or with a noun attached (*which flight*, *which switch*).

1. He wouldn't tell me *which* of the two offers he'd accepted.
2. I can never remember *which* switch runs the porch light.
3. *Which* candidate they endorse will decide the primary.
4. She's still weighing *which* to keep and *which* to sell.
5. The label doesn't say *which* ingredient causes the reaction.
6. *Which* team wins tonight decides the whole bracket.
7. Everything now depends on *which* version they actually installed.

**See also:** [what](#what), [whichever](#whichever), [who](#who), [whose](#whose)

### who

**Pronunciation:** /hu/ &middot; *HOO* — reduced to /hʊ/ in fast speech
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative pronoun); the same word is also a relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question, personal)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *who* [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *who* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *who to* [verb]. &middot; *Who* [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; It [linking verb] [adjective] *who* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *who* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma, and statement order: *I asked who he is*, not *I asked who is he*. When *who* is itself the subject of the embedded clause, nothing shifts — *I asked who called* already looks like a statement. Relative *who* is the one with a comma rule: nonrestrictive, it takes commas on both sides (*my brother, who lives in Denver, called*); restrictive, it takes none (*the neighbor who called*).

Heads an embedded question about a person. In everyday American English it also covers the object slot that formal grammar reserves for *whom* (*I don't know who they picked*), and only the stiffest prose insists otherwise. Distinguish it from relative *who*, which requires an antecedent noun (*the woman who called*) and is a relative pronoun, not a conjunction.

1. Nobody could tell me *who* signed off on the invoice.
2. *Who* leaked the memo is now the only question anyone cares about.
3. I forget *who* was supposed to bring the ice.
4. She asked *who* I'd been talking to out in the hallway.
5. It's still unclear *who* has the authority to cancel the order.
6. I never did figure out *who* to send the complaint to.
7. The whole seating chart depends on *who* actually shows up.

### whom

**Pronunciation:** /hum/ &middot; *HOOM*
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative pronoun, objective case); the same word is also an objective relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question, personal, object function)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *whom* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *whom* [subject] [verb] … [preposition]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [preposition] *whom* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *whom to* [verb]. &middot; *Whom* [subject] [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; It [linking verb] [adjective] *whom* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma, including before a fronted *whom*-clause subject: *Whom they ultimately choose is up to the board.* Relative *whom* is the one with a comma rule — nonrestrictive, it takes commas (*the director, whom nobody had met, sat in back*); restrictive, none. The case test is substitution inside the clause: if *him* or *her* fits, *whom* is correct (*they hired him* → *I know whom they hired*).

The object form of *who*, and genuinely formal in American English rather than merely careful. It survives mainly right after a preposition (*to whom*, *for whom*, *with whom*); everywhere else *who* is the ordinary spoken choice, and a *whom* pressed into subject position (*the man whom I think is guilty*) reads as an overcorrection.

1. I have no idea *whom* the committee actually interviewed.
2. The report doesn't say *whom* the funds were paid to.
3. *Whom* they ultimately choose is up to the board.
4. She asked to *whom* she should address the appeal.
5. It remains unsettled *whom* the estate names as executor.
6. The style guide never says *whom* to credit when two people wrote the piece.

**See also:** [who](#who), [whomever](#whomever), [whose](#whose)

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ* — a homophone of *who's*
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative determiner); the same word is also a possessive relative determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question, possessive)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *whose* [noun] [verb phrase]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *whose* [noun] [subject] [verb]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *whose* [noun] [clause]. &middot; *Whose* [noun] [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; [linking verb] *whose* [noun] [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *whose* [noun] [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere in the noun-clause use, including before a fronted *whose*-clause subject: *Whose signature is on the second page turned out to matter.* Relative *whose* is the one with a comma rule — nonrestrictive, it takes commas (*the tenant, whose lease was up, moved out*); restrictive, none. And no apostrophe in either: *whose* is the possessive, *who's* is *who is* or *who has*. The two are pronounced identically, so this is a writing error rather than a speaking one.

Asks which person something belongs to, and it always leads a noun (*whose car*, *whose idea*, *whose turn*). Relative *whose* does the same possessive work inside a modifying clause (*the man whose car got towed*); the noun-clause *whose* is the one that answers a question rather than identifying a noun.

1. Nobody knew *whose* car was blocking the loading dock.
2. *Whose* signature is on the second page turned out to matter enormously.
3. She wouldn't say *whose* idea the whole trip had been.
4. It all depends on *whose* insurance covers the rental.
5. The clerk asked *whose* name the account was under.
6. Nobody told us *whose* budget the overtime was coming out of.
7. The only real question now is *whose* name goes on the deed.

### why

**Pronunciation:** /waɪ/ &middot; *WY* — conservative /hwaɪ/ in some regional speech
**Type:** complementizer (interrogative adverb); the same spelling is also a relative adverb after *reason* and, in older and regional speech, an interjection (*Why, of course*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question of reason)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb] *why* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] [indirect object] *why* [clause]. &middot; *Why* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [linking verb] *why* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *why* [clause]. &middot; the reason *why* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma, and no question mark on the containing sentence: *I asked why he left.* is a statement and ends with a period. The interjection *why* is the only one of these spellings that takes a comma, and it takes one always: *Why, I hadn't thought of that.*

The only wh-word here with no adverbial twin — a *why*-clause is always a noun clause, which is why it never needs a comma under any arrangement. It is also the one wh-word that refuses a *to*-infinitive: *what to do* and *how to ask* are fine, but *why to go* is not English, and only the fixed *why not* takes a bare verb (*Why not ask her?*).

1. He never explained *why* the shipment ran two weeks late.
2. *Why* they scheduled it on a holiday weekend is beyond me.
3. I'd genuinely like to understand *why* this keeps happening.
4. That is exactly *why* I stopped answering his calls.
5. Nobody thought to ask *why* the office was empty at noon.
6. Nobody ever gave us the reason *why* the account was frozen.
7. It all comes down to *why* he waited three weeks to say anything.

## The *-ever* and *-soever* forms: fused relatives

Each of these folds a head noun and a relative clause into one word — *whoever wins* means "the person who wins" — so the clause fills a noun slot with no antecedent anywhere in sight. The rule that governs the whole subgroup is that **case and function are assigned from inside the clause, not from outside it**, which is why *Give it to whoever wants it* is correct: the preposition takes the entire clause as its object, so it cannot reach in and force *whomever*. All of them also have a concessive adverbial use meaning "no matter what/who/where," and that use — and only that use — takes a comma when fronted; every entry below states the rule for its own word, and no noun-clause use in this section takes a comma in any position. The last two members, *whomsoever* and *whosoever*, are built on the older emphatic suffix *-soever*; they behave exactly like *whomever* and *whoever* and differ only in register, which is archaic.

### however

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); a homograph of the conjunctive adverb *however* ("nevertheless"), which is a different word with the opposite punctuation &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause or adverbial clause of manner (fused relative, "in whatever way"); also concessive ("no matter how")
**Pattern:** [main clause] *however* [clause]. &middot; *However* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *However* [adjective/adverb] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *however* [adjective/adverb] [clause]. &middot; *However* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; *However* [adjective/adverb] [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *however* [clause].
**Punctuation:** This *however* punctuates like any other subordinator: no comma when its clause follows the main clause (*Do it however you like*), and a comma after the clause when it is fronted in the "no matter how" sense (*However carefully you write it, he'll find something*). No comma ever goes right after the word itself. That pointing belongs to the conjunctive adverb *however*, which is a different word and takes the opposite treatment: joining two independent clauses it **requires a semicolon or a period before it and a comma after it** — *We left early; however, the traffic was already bad.* Putting a comma where that semicolon belongs is a comma splice, not a stylistic choice: *We left early, however, the traffic was already bad.* is an error.

A fused relative meaning "in whatever way" or "no matter how," spelled and pronounced exactly like the conjunctive adverb but punctuated the opposite way — a swap that produces one of the most common comma errors in American writing. Two tests separate them. Substitution: if *no matter how* or *in whatever way* fits, it is this word; if *nevertheless* fits, it is the adverb. Movement: the conjunctive adverb slides freely around inside its own clause (*The traffic, however, was already bad*), while this *however* is welded to the front of the clause it introduces and cannot move at all.

1. Do it *however* you like — I really don't have a preference.
2. *However* carefully you write it, he'll find something to fix.
3. She dresses *however* she feels when she gets up.
4. *However* long it takes is *however* long it takes.
5. The system sorts the files *however* the last user configured it.
6. She'll finish the job *however* long it takes.
7. The refund gets calculated based on *however* the agent coded the ticket.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [how](#how), [whichever](#whichever), [however (conjunctive adverb)](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#however)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvər/ &middot; *wuht-EV-ur* — the *t* is normally a flap, [wʌɾˈɛvɚ]; also /wɑtˈɛvər/
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); the same word is also a determiner, a concessive subordinator, and a standalone dismissive interjection &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative); also adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** [verb] *whatever* [clause]. &middot; *Whatever* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *whatever* [noun] [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *whatever* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *whatever* [noun] [clause]. &middot; *Whatever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Whatever* [noun] [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, including as a fronted subject: *Whatever she paid was too much.* A fronted "no matter what" clause does take a comma: *Whatever happens, call me.*
**Variants:** *whatsoever* (emphatic, and only after a negated noun — *no reason whatsoever* — never a clause introducer)

Means "anything that" when it fills a noun slot and "no matter what" when it modifies the main clause, and the comma is the only visible difference between the two. It can also work as a determiner with a noun attached (*whatever excuse he gives*). The standalone dismissive *Whatever.* is an interjection and has nothing to do with this entry.

1. Order *whatever* looks good to you.
2. *Whatever* she paid for that couch, it was too much.
3. He agrees with *whatever* the last person in the room said.
4. *Whatever* excuse he comes up with, don't fall for it.
5. I'll take *whatever* is left once the deposit clears.
6. *Whatever* happens tomorrow, we've done what we could.
7. She'll go along with *whatever* plan the committee settles on.

**See also:** [what](#what), [whichever](#whichever), [whoever](#whoever), [however](#however), [whether or not](#whether-or-not)

### whenever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛnˈɛvər/ &middot; *wen-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); the same word is far more often a subordinating conjunction of time, and it also runs concessive ("no matter when") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative of time); more often an adverbial clause of time
**Pattern:** [verb] *whenever* [clause]. &middot; *Whenever* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; *Whenever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whenever* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use. As a time adverbial, a comma after the clause when it is fronted and no comma when it trails — the ordinary subordinator rule.

Mostly a time adverbial meaning "every time that" or "at any time that," but it does head genuine noun clauses in subject and object position: *Whenever he shows up is fine with me*. Against *when*, it trades a specific point in time for an open one — *I'll leave when it stops* names an occasion, *I'll leave whenever it stops* accepts any.

1. *Whenever* he finally shows up is fine with me.
2. Pick *whenever* works best for your schedule.
3. She calls *whenever* something in the house breaks.
4. *Whenever* you're ready, we'll get started.
5. The meeting can be *whenever* the conference room is free.

**See also:** [when](#when), [wherever](#wherever), [whatever](#whatever)

### wherever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɛvər/ &middot; *wair-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of place and a concessive ("no matter where") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative of place); also adverbial clause of place
**Pattern:** [verb] *wherever* [clause]. &middot; *Wherever* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; *Wherever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *wherever* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use. A fronted "no matter where" clause takes a comma; a trailing place clause takes none.

"Any place that" or "no matter where." It heads a noun clause when it fills an argument slot (*Wherever they put it is fine*) and a place adverbial when it modifies the verb (*Sit wherever you like*). Against *where*, it is the indifferent option — *where* names a place, *wherever* declines to.

1. *Wherever* they end up moving is fine with me.
2. We'll ship it *wherever* you need it delivered.
3. *Wherever* you go this weekend, take the charger.
4. He seems to make friends *wherever* he lands.
5. The reception drops out *wherever* the trees get thick.

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃˈɛvər/ &middot; *wich-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); the same word is also a determiner and a concessive subordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative, selective)
**Pattern:** [verb] *whichever* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *whichever* [noun] [clause]. &middot; *Whichever* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; *Whichever* [noun] [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; *Whichever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Whichever* [noun] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [preposition] *whichever* [clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] or [noun phrase], *whichever* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, in any position — not even when the clause is a fronted subject (*Whichever vendor bids lowest gets the contract*). Two other constructions do take one. A fronted concessive ("no matter which") takes a comma before the main clause: *Whichever route the driver takes, we're going to hit traffic.* And the fixed summarizing tag *whichever comes first* is set off by a comma from the list it closes: *three years or 36,000 miles, whichever comes first.*

The selective *-ever* form: "any one of these that." It presumes a defined set where *whatever* is open-ended — *whichever you want* offers a menu, *whatever you want* offers the world. It is standard boilerplate in contracts and warranties in the tags *whichever comes first* and *whichever is greater*.

1. Take *whichever* seat you want; they're all the same price.
2. *Whichever* vendor bids lowest gets the contract.
3. She'll be happy with *whichever* one you pick out.
4. The warranty expires after three years or 36,000 miles, *whichever* comes first.
5. I'll go with *whichever* is cheaper to ship.
6. *Whichever* route the driver takes, we're going to hit traffic.
7. *Whichever* they end up choosing, somebody on the committee is going to complain.

### whoever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative); the same word also heads concessive adverbials ("no matter who") &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative, personal)
**Pattern:** *Whoever* [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *whoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] [indirect object] *whoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *whoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [linking verb] *whoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; *Whoever* [verb phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, in any position — a fronted subject clause takes none (*Whoever parked in my spot is about to get towed*), and neither does one sitting after a verb or a preposition. The concessive ("no matter who") is the one comma: fronted, its clause is closed off before the main clause arrives — *Whoever ends up with the account, the deadline doesn't move.* The case rule matters far more than any comma here: use *whoever*, not *whomever*, whenever it is the subject of its own clause — even directly after a preposition, as in *Give it to whoever wants it.*

"The person who" or "anyone who." A whole clause, not a bare pronoun, is what follows a preposition, so the preposition never reaches inside to force *whomever*; choosing the case by looking at the word to the left is the single most common error in this entry. If a verb comes right after, you want *whoever*.

1. *Whoever* parked in my spot is about to get towed.
2. Give the extra ticket to *whoever* asks for it first.
3. I'd like to thank *whoever* shoveled the front walk.
4. *Whoever* wrote this manual has clearly never used the product.
5. She'll hire *whoever* can start on Monday.
6. *Whoever* ends up with the account, the deadline doesn't move.
7. The new supervisor will be *whoever* survives the reorganization.

### whomever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈmɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-MEV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative, objective case); like *whoever*, it also heads concessive adverbials ("no matter whom") &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative, personal, object function); also adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [verb] [indirect object] *whomever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *whomever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Whomever* [subject] [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; [linking verb] *whomever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Whomever* [subject] [verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, including as a fronted subject: *Whomever they nominate will face a tough confirmation.* The concessive is the one that takes a comma, and only when fronted: *Whomever the board picks, the union is going to push back.* Use the word at all only when it is the object *inside* its own clause; substitute *him* to check — *the client designates him* → *whomever the client designates*.

The object form of *whoever*, and formal even by *whom*'s standards. Americans overcorrect to it after prepositions, where *whoever* is usually the right form; the reliable signal is what comes next — a subject and verb call for *whomever*, a bare verb calls for *whoever*.

1. Send the invoice to *whomever* the client designates.
2. *Whomever* they nominate will face a tough confirmation.
3. She'll work with *whomever* management assigns her.
4. Address the letter to *whomever* you spoke with last week.
5. The prize goes to *whomever* the judges rank highest.
6. *Whomever* the board picks, the union is going to push back.
7. The new liaison will be *whomever* the two departments can agree on.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [whosoever](#whosoever)

### whomsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhumsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoom-so-EV-ur* — primary stress on *-EV-*, a secondary beat on *hoom-*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative, emphatic, objective case); the object form of *whosoever*, and like it a concessive subordinator ("no matter whom") &middot; **Register:** archaic — confined to legal and testamentary drafting, formal boilerplate, and King James English; never a neutral choice
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative, personal, object function); also adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [preposition] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Whomsoever* [subject] [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *whomsoever* it may [verb]. &middot; *Whomsoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, including as a fronted subject: *Whomsoever the executor names takes the residue.* A fronted concessive takes a comma before the main clause: *Whomsoever the court appoints, the estate pays the fee.* Case is settled inside the clause, exactly as with [whomever](#whomever): *whomsoever* only when a subject and verb follow it, *whosoever* when a verb follows directly — *to whomsoever it may concern* is right, *to whomsoever comes first* is not.
**Variants:** [whosoever](#whosoever) (subjective); the parallel *-soever* forms *whatsoever*, *whichsoever*, *wheresoever*, *howsoever*

The object partner of *whosoever*, and the *-soever* form Americans are likeliest to have met, since the boilerplate *to whom it may concern* keeps its longer cousin in circulation. Nothing in modern prose needs it — *whomever* is already formal and *whoever* covers the ordinary cases — and the one thing to get right is that the preposition in front of it governs the whole clause, not the pronoun, so the case is decided by what follows the word rather than what precedes it. Drafters who reach for it as a general-purpose upgrade produce the standard hypercorrection: *to whomsoever shall apply* should be *to whosoever shall apply*.

1. The estate shall be distributed to *whomsoever* the executor designates.
2. *Whomsoever* the parties later substitute assumes every obligation under this clause.
3. The letter was addressed, half in earnest, to *whomsoever* it might concern.
4. He vowed to hand the deed to *whomsoever* his father had named in the will.
5. *Whomsoever* the board may choose, the appointment still requires a public hearing.
6. The trustee may pay the income to *whomsoever* the beneficiary directs in writing.

**See also:** [whosoever](#whosoever), [whomever](#whomever), [whoever](#whoever), [whom](#whom)

### whosoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhusoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-so-EV-ur*
**Type:** complementizer (fused relative, emphatic); *whomsoever* is its objective form &middot; **Register:** archaic — alive only in legal drafting, hymnody, and King James English; never a neutral choice
**Clause type:** noun clause (fused relative, personal, universal); also adverbial clause of concession
**Pattern:** *Whosoever* [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *whosoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *whosoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [linking verb] *whosoever* [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb]. &middot; *Whosoever* [verb phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma in the noun-clause use, in any position, including as a fronted subject: *Whosoever enters these gates leaves his rank at the door.* A fronted concessive is the exception, as everywhere else in this section: *Whosoever holds the office, the duties do not change.* The case rule is [whoever](#whoever)'s exactly: *whosoever* when it is the subject of its own clause, *whomsoever* only when it is the object inside that clause — so *to whomsoever it may concern* is right and *to whomsoever comes first* is not.
**Variants:** [whomsoever](#whomsoever) (objective); the parallel *-soever* forms *whatsoever*, *whichsoever*, *wheresoever*, *howsoever*

The emphatic *whoever*, built with the old intensifying suffix *-soever*, meaning what *whoever* means with the universality underlined: "any person at all who." Nothing in modern American English needs it — *whoever* covers every use — and reaching for it in ordinary prose reads as costume. It survives for two reasons: estate and contract drafters want language with no seams in it, and the King James Bible fixed *whosoever believeth* permanently in the ear of the language.

1. *Whosoever* presents this note is entitled to payment in full.
2. The trust pays out to *whosoever* survives the settlor.
3. Address the notice to *whomsoever* the court appoints.
4. *Whosoever* enters these gates leaves his rank at the door.
5. He promised the reward to *whosoever* found the dog.
6. Blessed is *whosoever* keeps the faith to the end.
7. *Whosoever* holds the office, the duties do not change.

**See also:** [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whoever](#whoever), [whomever](#whomever), [whatsoever](09-place-relative.md#whatsoever), [wheresoever](09-place-relative.md#wheresoever)

## Phrasal, formal, and archaic complementizers

Multi-word frames and survivals. Several of them (*as to*, *as to whether*, *the fact that*, *the idea that*, *the question whether*, *for*) exist mainly to solve a structural problem — English will not let a bare *that*- or wh-clause sit after most prepositions and most nouns — and the rest are period pieces, dialect survivals, or narrow specialists. Almost none of them takes a comma between the frame and its clause; the two conversational *be*-frames that close the section (*the thing is*, *trouble is*) are the exception, and each states its own rule.

### as how

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈhaʊ/ &middot; *uz HOW* — *as* is always the reduced /əz/, and the two words run together as one unstressed-then-stressed unit
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal); the same two words also close the causal frames *seeing as how* and *being as how* &middot; **Register:** regional and dialectal — Southern, Appalachian, and Midland American speech, plus the folksy narrative voice that imitates it; not standard in edited writing
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause) after a verb of saying, thinking, or conceding
**Pattern:** [subject] *allow* / *reckon* / *figure* / *say* *as how* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb of saying] [indirect object] *as how* [clause]. &middot; [subject] *allowed as how* [clause], [coordinated clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *as how*, none between the two words, and none inside the clause — it stands between a verb and its own complement. Causal *seeing as how* is the one with a comma rule, and only when its clause is fronted (*Seeing as how you've done this before, you go first*); that phrase belongs to the cause family, not to this one.
**Variants:** the fixed verb frames it lives in: *allow as how*, *reckon as how*, *figure as how*, *say as how*; plain *that* or zero *that* in standard prose (*He allowed that it might rain*)

A dialectal *that*: after *allow*, *reckon*, *figure*, and *say* it marks a plain statement and keeps no trace of manner, exactly as *how that* did in Early Modern English. The giveaway is that *how* has been bleached out of it — *He allowed as how it might rain* reports what he said, not the way he said it, so the *how* can be neither questioned nor answered. Keep it apart from causal *seeing as how* and *being as how*, which mean "since" and modify the main clause instead of filling a slot in it; in writing, both words simply come out.

1. He *allowed as how* the price seemed a little steep for a used mower.
2. She *reckoned as how* the storm would blow through before supper.
3. The foreman *said as how* they'd be two hands short all week.
4. Granddad finally *admitted as how* he'd never actually read the manual.
5. I *figured as how* nobody would miss one folding chair.
6. He *allowed as how* he might come by Sunday, though he wouldn't promise.

**See also:** [how that](#how-that), [but what](#but-what), [but that](#but-that), [that](#that), [seeing that](04-cause.md#seeing-that), [being that](04-cause.md#being-that)

### as to (+ wh-)

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæz ˈtu/ &middot; *az TOO* — both words reduce in running speech to /əz tə/ &middot; *uz tuh*, and the pair never carries a stress of its own
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal); *as to* is also a compound preposition in front of a noun phrase (*as to the cost*) &middot; **Register:** formal — the register of contracts, memos, and administrative prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (embedded wh-question) — complement of a noun or an adjective, occasionally of a verb
**Pattern:** [noun] *as to* [wh-word] [clause]. &middot; [adjective] *as to* [wh-word] [clause]. &middot; [verb] *as to* [wh-word] [clause]. &middot; [determiner] [noun] *as to* [wh-word] [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [noun/adjective] *as to* [wh-word] *to* [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it, between its two words, or after its clause — not even when the whole *as to* phrase is buried inside the subject of the sentence. It never fronts, so the fronted-subordinator comma is never in play.
**Variants:** [as to whether](#as-to-whether) — the commonest instance, with an entry of its own; *as to* + noun phrase (*guidance as to the deadline*); the plainer *about*, *of*, *on*, *over* (*a question about what it costs*)

The general frame that [as to whether](#as-to-whether) is one case of. English will not park a bare wh-clause after most nouns and adjectives, and *as to* supplies the missing preposition: *a question as to what caused the delay*, *silent as to who owns the strip*, *vague as to when it happened*. It earns its keep after nouns and adjectives that govern no preposition of their own; after a plain verb it is padding, since *He inquired as to what it cost* says nothing that *He asked what it cost* does not. Where an ordinary preposition already fits — *about*, *on*, *over* — that preposition is the better word, and *as to* reads as officialese.

1. The deed is silent *as to* who maintains the shared driveway.
2. There was some confusion *as to* what the deposit actually covered.
3. Nobody could give us guidance *as to* how the surcharge gets calculated.
4. Her testimony was vague *as to* when the second call came in.
5. A dispute *as to* which company owned the easement stalled the sale for a year.
6. The bylaws say nothing *as to* how many proxies one member may hold.
7. The committee is still undecided *as to* whom the award should honor.

**See also:** [as to whether](#as-to-whether), [the question whether](#the-question-whether), [whether](#whether), [what](#what), [how](#how), [the fact that](#the-fact-that)

### as to whether

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæz tə ˈwɛðər/ &middot; *az tuh WEH-thur*
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect yes-no question)
**Pattern:** [noun] *as to whether* [clause]. &middot; [adjective] *as to whether* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *as to whether* [clause]. &middot; [determiner] [noun] *as to whether* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [noun/adjective] *as to whether* [clause] *or* [clause]. &middot; [noun/adjective] *as to whether to* [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it, inside it, or after its clause — not even when the whole *as to whether* clause is packed inside the subject of the sentence (*A dispute as to whether the easement runs with the land has held up the closing*). It never fronts, so no fronted-subordinator comma is ever in play.
**Variants:** [as to (+ wh-)](#as-to--wh-) — the same frame with the other wh-words (*as to what*, *as to how*, *as to who*, *as to which*); *as to* + noun phrase (*as to the cost*)

A formal connector for the spots where a noun or adjective needs a preposition in front of a *whether*-clause: *doubt as to whether*, *silent as to whether*, *a question as to whether*. After a plain verb it is padding — *He questioned as to whether* is simply worse than *He questioned whether* — so the usable rule is to keep it after nouns and adjectives and delete it after verbs.

1. There is some doubt *as to whether* the signature is genuine.
2. The contract is silent *as to whether* renewals are automatic.
3. The memo raised a question *as to whether* the policy still applies.
4. Opinions differ sharply *as to whether* the fee is refundable.
5. He gave no guidance *as to whether* we should keep the originals.
6. A dispute *as to whether* the easement runs with the land has held up the closing for months.
7. The panel was divided *as to whether* to reopen the case *or* let the ruling stand.

**See also:** [as to (+ wh-)](#as-to--wh-), [whether](#whether), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [the question whether](#the-question-whether), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [if](#if)

### but that

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt ˈðæt/ &middot; *but THAT* — reduced to /bət ðət/
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic, surviving in legal and literary prose
**Clause type:** noun clause after a negated verb or noun of doubting or denying
**Pattern:** [negated verb of doubt/denial] *but that* [clause]. &middot; There is no [doubt/question] *but that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma. Do not confuse it with the ordinary sequence *but* + a *that*-clause (*I know he's late, but that isn't the issue*), where *but* coordinates and *that* starts a fresh clause — that one does take the coordinating comma.
**Variants:** bare *but* in the same frame (*I don't doubt but he'll come*); [*but what*](#but-what) (regional and dialectal)

An old negative complementizer meaning roughly "that … not," alive today only after *doubt*, *deny*, and *question* in the negative. Contemporary American English uses a plain *that*: *I don't doubt that he acted in good faith*. Note the built-in double negative that makes it read backwards to modern eyes — *I never doubted but that she'd win* means the speaker expected her to win.

1. I do not doubt *but that* he acted in good faith.
2. There is no question *but that* the notice was properly served.
3. She never denied *but that* the money had changed hands.
4. It cannot be denied *but that* the practice was widespread at the time.
5. Who knows *but that* he was right all along?

**See also:** [that](#that), [but what](#but-what), [how that](#how-that), [lest](#lest), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### but what

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt ˈwʌt/ &middot; *but WUHT* — reduced to /bət wət/ in running speech
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** regional and dialectal — Southern, Appalachian, and Midland American speech; not standard in edited writing anywhere
**Clause type:** noun clause after a negated verb or noun of doubting, denying, or knowing
**Pattern:** [negated verb of doubt/denial] *but what* [clause]. &middot; There is no [doubt/question] *but what* [clause]. &middot; It won't be long *but what* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma, exactly as with [but that](#but-that). Do not confuse it with the ordinary coordination *but* + *what*-clause (*I heard the story, but what he actually meant was different*), where *but* joins two independent clauses and therefore does take a comma before it.
**Variants:** [but that](#but-that) (the older and more literary form); bare *but* (*I don't doubt but he'll come*)

The spoken cousin of *but that*, and the form far more Americans have actually heard: *I don't doubt but what she'll be there*, *There's no question but what he knew*. It carries the same built-in double negative — *I never doubted but what she'd win* means the speaker expected her to win — and the same restriction to negated verbs of doubting and denying. Dictionaries mark it dialectal rather than archaic, which is the real difference from *but that*: this one is alive, just not in print. In writing, use a plain *that*.

1. I don't doubt *but what* she'll be there before we are.
2. There's no question *but what* he knew about the leak.
3. It won't be long *but what* somebody notices the sign is gone.
4. Nobody denied *but what* the road needed paving.
5. I wouldn't be surprised *but what* they close the store by fall.

**See also:** [but that](#but-that), [that](#that), [how that](#how-that), [as how](#as-how), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### for

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr/ &middot; *FOR* — in this use almost always reduced to /fər/ &middot; *fur*
**Type:** complementizer (infinitival *for … to*); the same spelling is also a coordinating conjunction meaning "because" and one of the most common prepositions in the language &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (infinitival clause with an overt subject)
**Pattern:** *For* [subject] *to* [verb] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *for* [subject] *to* [verb]. &middot; It [verb] *for* [subject] *to* [verb]. &middot; [adjective] *for* [subject] *to* [verb]. &middot; [noun] *for* [subject] *to* [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma between *for* and its subject and none before *to*. A fronted *for*-clause subject takes no comma either: *For him to say that was out of line.* The coordinating *for* meaning "because" is the one with a comma rule — joining two independent clauses, it takes a comma before it: *We turned back, for the road was washed out.* The preposition takes none at all.
**Variants:** *for … to* — the full name of the frame, since the two words function as one discontinuous complementizer and neither half stands without the other; bare *to* + verb when the subject is understood from context (*It's hard to say*)

The complementizer that lets an infinitive clause carry its own subject: *for* marks the subject, *to* marks the verb, and the pair wraps a whole clause into a noun slot. It is neither the coordinating *for* meaning "because" nor the ordinary preposition — the test is that a *to*-infinitive always follows. Its subject takes object case: *for him to go*, never *for he to go*.

1. *For* him to apologize now would surprise everyone.
2. It's important *for* the kids to hear it from you first.
3. I'd honestly prefer *for* someone else to make that call.
4. She's waiting *for* the paint to dry before she pulls the tape.
5. *For* a first attempt to go that smoothly is genuinely unusual.
6. There's no reason *for* us to leave this early.

**See also:** [that](#that), [whether](#whether), [for (coordinating)](01-coordinating.md#for)

### how that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ˈðæt/ &middot; *how THAT*
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause)
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb of telling or knowing] *how that* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb of telling] [indirect object] *how that* [clause]. &middot; [imperative verb] *how that* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb of writing or saying] *how that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *how that*, none between *how* and *that*, and none inside the clause. Modern American English uses a plain *that* or a plain *how*, never the pair.
**Variants:** *how* alone in narrative use (*He told us how he'd walked all night*)

A doubled complementizer common in Early Modern English and preserved in the King James Bible, where *how* had drifted away from manner and become a bare statement-marker. It is dead in contemporary American English except as deliberate period flavor; the modern equivalent of *He told them how that the ship had gone down* is simply *He told them that the ship had gone down*.

1. Ye know *how that* it is an unlawful thing for a man of my nation to keep company with a stranger.
2. He told them *how that* the ship had gone down within sight of the harbor.
3. Remember *how that* our fathers came into this country with nothing at all.
4. The old preacher explained *how that* mercy is never earned.
5. She wrote *how that* the winter had been the hardest in living memory.

**See also:** [that](#that), [how](#how), [but that](#but-that), [as how](#as-how)

### lest

**Pronunciation:** /lɛst/ &middot; *LEST*
**Type:** complementizer after verbs of fearing; subordinating (negative purpose) elsewhere &middot; **Register:** formal to literary
**Clause type:** noun clause after a verb, adjective, or noun of fearing; adverbial clause of negative purpose otherwise
**Pattern:** [subject] [verb of fearing] *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. &middot; [subject] *be* [adjective of fearing] *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. &middot; [noun of fearing] *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. &middot; [main clause] *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. &middot; [main clause], *lest* [subject] [bare subjunctive verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *lest* in the noun-clause use — it sits between a verb, adjective, or noun and its own complement. In the purpose use a comma is optional and usually omitted; use one only when the *lest* clause is a clearly parenthetical afterthought. *Lest* never fronts in either use, so the fronted-subordinator comma never applies to it.
**Variants:** *lest* + *should* (*lest he should fall*) — dated, and more British than American

A negative complementizer meaning roughly "that … might," used after *fear*, *afraid*, *anxious*, *worry*, and *apprehension*: *She feared lest the news get out* means "feared that the news might get out." It takes the bare subjunctive — *lest he be*, *lest it go*, with no *-s* on a third-person verb — and that is the detail Americans most often get wrong. It is never neutral; ordinary American speech uses *that … might* or *for fear that*.

1. He was afraid *lest* his silence be taken for agreement.
2. The family lived in fear *lest* the loan be called in.
3. She hesitated, anxious *lest* she say the wrong thing.
4. Speak quietly *lest* the neighbors hear you.
5. The committee worried *lest* the delay cost them the grant.
6. He kept every receipt in a folder, *lest* anyone question the expense later.

**See also:** [that](#that), [whether](#whether), [but that](#but-that), [would that](#would-that)

### no wonder / small wonder / little wonder

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈwʌndər/, /ˌsmɔl ˈwʌndər/, /ˌlɪtl ˈwʌndər/ &middot; *noh WUN-dur, smawl WUN-dur, LIT-l WUN-dur* — the stress lands on *wonder* in all three, and *no wonder* is normally said as a single falling unit
**Type:** complementizer frame (reduced matrix predicate); the unreduced form is *It is no wonder that* &middot; **Register:** *no wonder* neutral to informal; *small wonder* and *little wonder* neutral to formal, and commoner on the page than in the mouth
**Clause type:** noun clause (extraposed subject content clause), normally with the *that* dropped
**Pattern:** *No wonder* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *No wonder that* [clause]. &middot; It is *no wonder that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] — *no wonder* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *Small wonder that* [clause]. &middot; *Little wonder* ∅ [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the frame and its clause: *No wonder he was tired*, not *No wonder, he was tired*. When it trails a complete sentence it needs a period, a semicolon, or a dash in front of it — *The heater's been off since Friday; no wonder the pipes froze* — because both halves are independent clauses and a comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** the unreduced *It's no wonder that*; *is it any wonder (that)* in questions; *no surprise*, *small surprise* — the same frame with a different noun

A whole matrix clause squeezed down to two words: *It is no wonder that he was tired* drops *it is* and *that* and comes out *No wonder he was tired*, with the content clause still doing the work of a subject. That reduction is what makes it look like a conjunction, and the proof that it still is one is that the full form can always be put back. *Small wonder* and *little wonder* mean the same thing in a more written register; only *no wonder* is at home in speech, and only *no wonder* stands alone as an exclamation (*No wonder!*).

1. *No wonder* the car wouldn't start — the dome light had been on all night.
2. *Small wonder that* enrollment fell the year they doubled the fee.
3. She's been up since four with the baby. *No wonder* she fell asleep at the table.
4. *Little wonder* the neighbors finally called the city about the noise.
5. They cut the staff in half and kept the same hours; *no wonder* the rest of them quit.
6. It's *no wonder that* nobody reads the terms of service.

**See also:** [that](#that), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [not that](#not-that), [trouble is / problem is / fact is / truth is](#trouble-is--problem-is--fact-is--truth-is)

### not that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈðæt/ &middot; *naht THAT* — the second word reduces to /ðət/ in fast speech
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** noun clause, negated — parenthetical, or a denial of an inference
**Pattern:** *Not that* [clause], but [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] — *not that* [clause]. &middot; It's *not that* [clause]; it's *that* [clause]. &middot; *Not that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** When it opens the sentence, a comma closes its clause before the main clause arrives: *Not that it matters, but he left early.* When it trails, it needs a comma, a dash, or a full sentence break in front of it: *He never returned the call — not that I expected him to.* In the *It's not that X; it's that Y* frame the two halves are both independent clauses, so a semicolon (or a period, or a dash) is required between them; a comma there is a comma splice, common in casual writing and still an error in edited prose.
**Variants:** the fixed phrases *not that it matters*, *not that I'm complaining*, *not that I know of*

A complementizer that denies an inference the listener might be drawing — usually right before the speaker goes ahead and makes the point anyway. In the *It's not that … it's that …* frame it does the more literal work of rejecting one *that*-clause in favor of another. It is one of the few environments where the *that* cannot be dropped.

1. *Not that* anyone asked, but I thought the second draft was better.
2. He never returned the call — *not that* I expected him to.
3. It's *not that* I don't trust her; it's *that* I don't trust the process.
4. *Not that* it matters now, but the reservation was under my name.
5. "Is he still working there?" "*Not that* I know of."

**See also:** [that](#that), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that)

### the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *thuh FAKT thut*
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (nominalized content clause)
**Pattern:** *The fact that* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [preposition] *the fact that* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *the fact that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere in the phrase and none between *that* and its clause. Never drop this *that* — after a noun the complementizer is obligatory.
**Variants:** *the fact of the matter is that*; the same frame with other nouns — *the idea that*, *the claim that*, *the possibility that*, *the news that*

A packaging device that turns a clause into a full noun phrase so it can follow a preposition or head a sentence, since a bare *that*-clause cannot do either after most prepositions: *despite that he lied* is ungrammatical, *despite the fact that he lied* is fine. Editors flag it as padding wherever a plain *that*-clause or a gerund would carry the same load — *I resent the fact that he lied* shrinks cleanly to *I resent that he lied*.

1. *The fact that* nobody objected doesn't make it legal.
2. They settled despite *the fact that* liability was never established.
3. She kept coming back to *the fact that* the door had been unlocked.
4. *The fact that* he apologized at all surprised everyone in the room.
5. We're stuck with *the fact that* the deadline hasn't moved an inch.

### the idea that / the notion that / the claim that

**Pronunciation:** /ði aɪˈdiə ðət/, /ðə ˈnoʊʃən ðət/, /ðə ˈkleɪm ðət/ &middot; *thee eye-DEE-uh thut, thuh NOH-shun thut, thuh KLAYM thut* — the *that* is always the reduced /ðət/; a full /ðæt/ turns it into a demonstrative and breaks the phrase
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal — abstract noun + *that*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** noun clause (appositive content clause completing an abstract noun)
**Pattern:** *The* [noun] *that* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *the* [noun] *that* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *the* [noun] *that* [clause]. &middot; [determiner] [noun] *that* [clause]. &middot; *The* [noun] *that* [clause] *is* [complement].
**Punctuation:** No comma between the noun and *that*, and none between *that* and its clause. This *that* can never be dropped — *the claim he was misquoted* reads as a relative clause with a hole in it, which is a different sentence. And no comma before the verb even when the whole phrase is the subject: *The notion that anyone reads these surprises me.*
**Variants:** the same frame with any abstract noun that takes a content clause — *the fact that*, *the possibility that*, *the belief that*, *the news that*, *the rumor that*, *the assumption that*, *the suggestion that*; the interrogative counterpart [the question whether](#the-question-whether)

The productive frame behind [the fact that](#the-fact-that): an abstract noun naming a piece of mental content — *idea*, *notion*, *claim*, *belief*, *rumor*, *possibility* — takes a *that*-clause that says what the content is. The clause is appositive, not relative, and the test is substitution rather than deletion: *the claim that he was misquoted* means "the claim, namely that he was misquoted," and nothing has been lifted out of the clause. Compare *the claim that he made*, where *that* is a relative pronoun standing in for the object of *made* and can be deleted. The choice among the nouns is attitude, not structure — *the fact that* concedes the content is true, *the idea that* and *the notion that* usually telegraph doubt, and *the claim that* withholds endorsement outright.

1. *The idea that* one more meeting will fix this is optimistic at best.
2. She rejected *the notion that* the delay was anybody's fault but the vendor's.
3. *The claim that* the road was already flooded turned out to be wrong.
4. Everything rests on *the assumption that* the grant gets renewed.
5. We spent the afternoon with *the possibility that* the offer might be pulled.
6. *The rumor that* the plant is closing has been going around since March.
7. He never quite let go of *the belief that* the inspection had been rigged.

**See also:** [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [the question whether](#the-question-whether), [that](#that), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [not that](#not-that)

### the question whether

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈkwɛstʃən ˌwɛðər/ &middot; *thuh KWES-chun weh-thur* — primary stress on *ques-*, a lighter secondary beat on *weh-*
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal — noun + *whether*) &middot; **Register:** formal — legal, academic, and editorial prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (appositive indirect yes-no question completing an abstract noun)
**Pattern:** *The question whether* [clause] [verb phrase]. &middot; [verb] *the question whether* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *the question of whether* [clause]. &middot; *The question whether to* [verb]. &middot; *The question whether* [clause] *or* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between *question* and *whether*, none inside the clause, and none after the whole phrase when it is the sentence's subject: *The question whether the statute applies has never been briefed.* Never *the question that* — a yes-no question takes *whether*, and never *the question if*, since an *if*-clause cannot follow a noun at all.
**Variants:** *the question of whether* — commoner in American English and perfectly acceptable, only a little heavier; *the question as to whether* (see [as to whether](#as-to-whether)); the same frame with other nouns — *the issue whether*, *a doubt whether*, *a dispute whether*

The interrogative counterpart of [the idea that](#the-idea-that--the-notion-that--the-claim-that): when the abstract noun names an open question instead of a settled content, *whether* replaces *that*. Bare *the question whether* is the traditional legal form and is still standard; American usage more often slips in *of*, and that *of* is not an error. *The question as to whether* is the same phrase with a third word most editors strike. The one form to avoid entirely is *the question if*.

1. *The question whether* the easement survives the sale has never been litigated.
2. The board tabled *the question whether* to reopen the north entrance.
3. Everything now turns on *the question of whether* the notice was timely.
4. *The question whether* he knew is separate from *the question whether* he should have.
5. She raised *the question whether* the committee had jurisdiction at all.
6. *The question whether* the fee is refundable *or* forfeited is buried in an appendix.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [as to whether](#as-to-whether), [as to (+ wh-)](#as-to--wh-), [the idea that / the notion that / the claim that](#the-idea-that--the-notion-that--the-claim-that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [if](#if)

### the thing is / here's the thing

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈθɪŋ ɪz/, /ˌhɪrz ðə ˈθɪŋ/ &middot; *thuh THING iz, heerz thuh THING* — *the thing is* ends on a held, slightly rising *is*, and that held syllable is exactly what the comma represents on the page
**Type:** complementizer frame (specificational *be* + content clause); *here's the thing* is the same frame turned into a discourse marker &middot; **Register:** informal — ordinary in speech, dialogue, and conversational writing; out of place in formal prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause) as subject complement, normally with the *that* dropped
**Pattern:** *The thing is* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *The thing is,* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *The thing is that* [clause]. &middot; *Here's the thing:* [clause]. &middot; *Here's the thing* — [clause]. &middot; [main clause], but *the thing is* ∅ [clause].
**Punctuation:** This is one of the two frames in the section that regularly takes a comma at the junction, and it is not the usual complementizer error: the comma stands for the pause after *is* and is standard in edited dialogue (*The thing is, nobody asked her*). Put the *that* back in and the comma goes away (*The thing is that nobody asked her*). *Here's the thing* takes a colon, a dash, or a period after it — never a bare comma, which would splice two independent clauses.
**Variants:** [trouble is / problem is / fact is / truth is](#trouble-is--problem-is--fact-is--truth-is) — the article-dropping members of the same family; *the thing about it is*; *here's the thing about* + noun phrase

A specificational sentence doing duty as a conversational hinge: *the thing* is an empty placeholder noun and the clause after *is* supplies its content. What it actually signals is that a complication is coming — *The thing is* almost always introduces the catch, the objection, or the reason the previous suggestion will not work. *Here's the thing* makes the same move with the placeholder pushed to the front, announcing the complication a beat before naming it. Neither belongs in formal writing, where the sentence gets stated without the runway.

1. *The thing is,* nobody actually told the landlord we were subletting.
2. *Here's the thing:* the warranty ran out two months before it broke.
3. I'd love to help, but *the thing is* I'm already committed Saturday.
4. *The thing is that* the part is discontinued, not just backordered.
5. *Here's the thing* — she never signed the second page.
6. He kept promising to pay us back. *The thing is,* he never did.

**See also:** [trouble is / problem is / fact is / truth is](#trouble-is--problem-is--fact-is--truth-is), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [that](#that), [not that](#not-that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that)

### trouble is / problem is / fact is / truth is

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtrʌbl ɪz/, /ˈprɑbləm ɪz/, /ˈfækt ɪz/, /ˈtruθ ɪz/ &middot; *TRUB-l iz, PRAH-blum iz, FAKT iz, TROOTH iz* — with *the* gone the noun carries the stress and the phrase opens on a full beat, which is most of what makes the clipped form sound conversational
**Type:** complementizer frame (specificational *be* + content clause, with the article dropped) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral — the article-less form is conversational; restoring *the* makes any of them fit edited prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (declarative content clause) as subject complement, normally with the *that* dropped
**Pattern:** *Trouble is* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *Fact is,* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *The trouble is that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *but the problem is* ∅ [clause]. &middot; *Truth is,* ∅ [clause], *and* [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma after *is* is standard wherever the pause is real (*Fact is, nobody checked*), and no comma at all when the sentence runs straight through (*The trouble is nobody checked*). With an overt *that* there is never a comma. When one of these follows a complete sentence it needs a period, a semicolon, or a dash in front of it, since both halves are independent: *We budgeted for three months; trouble is, the permit took five.*
**Variants:** the full forms *the trouble is*, *the problem is*, *the fact is*, *the truth is*; [the thing is / here's the thing](#the-thing-is--heres-the-thing); *thing is*, *point is*; *question is* — the interrogative member, which takes *whether* rather than *that*

The same specificational frame as [the thing is](#the-thing-is--heres-the-thing) with the article knocked off the front, which is a real feature of American conversational syntax rather than carelessness: *Trouble is, the permit took five months* is a fixed pattern, not a typo for *The trouble is*. Dropping *the* is what marks the register, and putting it back is the entire fix when the sentence has to go into formal writing. The four nouns do not carry equal force — *trouble is* and *problem is* introduce an obstacle, while *fact is* and *truth is* introduce a correction the listener is expected to resist.

1. *Trouble is,* the permit took five months instead of three.
2. *Fact is,* nobody on that shift had been trained on the new machine.
3. We could repaint it, but *the problem is* the trim would still be wrong.
4. *Truth is,* I'd forgotten the whole conversation until she brought it up.
5. *The trouble is that* the replacement costs more than the repair.
6. Everyone keeps blaming the storm. *Fact is,* the roof was already failing.
7. He says the check is in the mail; *truth is,* he hasn't written it yet.

**See also:** [the thing is / here's the thing](#the-thing-is--heres-the-thing), [zero *that*](#zero-that), [that](#that), [the fact that](#the-fact-that), [no wonder / small wonder / little wonder](#no-wonder--small-wonder--little-wonder)

### would that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʊd ðət/ &middot; *WOOD thut* — *would* takes the stress and never contracts here; the *that* is the reduced /ðət/
**Type:** complementizer (phrasal, optative) — the fossilized remains of *I would that* &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary — poetry, elevated rhetoric, and deliberate period voice; never a neutral choice
**Clause type:** noun clause (content clause expressing a wish), verb in the past subjunctive
**Pattern:** *Would that* [subject] [past subjunctive verb]. &middot; *Would that* [subject] *were* [complement]. &middot; *Would that* [subject] *had* [past participle]. &middot; *Would that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Would to God that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma between *would* and *that*, and none before the clause. The sentence it heads is an exclamation of wish and takes a period or an exclamation point, never a question mark, despite the inverted look of the leading *would*. If a main clause follows, a comma closes the wish first: *Would that it were true, but the numbers say otherwise.*
**Variants:** *would that* with the *that* dropped (*Would it were so*); *would to God that*; the living paraphrases *if only* and *I wish*

An optative frame: *would* is the old volitional verb meaning "wish" with its subject worn away, and *that* is the ordinary complementizer introducing the thing wished for. It takes the past subjunctive — *were*, not *was*, and *had* plus a participle for a wish about the past — and the tense is counterfactual, so *Would that it were true* says outright that it is not. Modern American English says *If only it were true* or *I wish it were true*; *would that* survives in speech only as a set piece of raised-eyebrow formality.

1. *Would that* it were as simple as signing one form.
2. *Would that* he had said so before we bought the tickets.
3. *Would that* the two sides could agree on even the basic facts.
4. *Would that* I had listened to my sister about the roof.
5. *Would that* the world were run by people who read the reports.
6. *Would that* it were true, but the numbers say otherwise.
