Subordinators of condition#

The words that hang one clause on another as a condition — saying what has to be true, what would have to have been true, or what will happen if it isn't.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

What unites this category is that every member marks its clause as unasserted: the speaker is not claiming the condition is true, only saying what follows if it is. That is why If the check cleared, we're fine tells you nothing about whether the check cleared, while Because the check cleared, we're fine tells you it did. Some members take a full clause (if, unless, provided that), some take only a noun phrase (but for, absent, subject to), and three take no conjunction at all, marking the condition by inverting an auxiliary (Had I known…, Should you need…, Were it up to me…).

Two rules run through the whole set. Punctuation: a conditional clause placed before the main clause takes a comma after it, and the same clause placed after the main clause normally takes none — with one important exception, since a fronted only if clause triggers inversion in the main clause and takes no comma at all (Only if you ask will she tell you). Tense: the conjunction never changes to show likelihood; the verb does. A present-tense condition is real (if it rains), a past-tense one is unlikely (if it rained), and a past-perfect one is already lost (if it had rained) — and a future condition still takes the present, never will.

Several members of this list are not conjunctions at all, and the punctuation follows the part of speech rather than the meaning. Otherwise, failing that, either way, and in that case are conjunctive adverbs, so two independent clauses joined by one of them need a semicolon or a period in front — a comma alone is a comma splice (We should leave now; otherwise, we'll be late). Or else is coordinating and therefore takes a plain comma before it, the same as or. In which case heads a relative clause and also takes a comma. Absent, barring, short of, but for, without, subject to, and contingent on are prepositions that take a noun phrase, and had, should, and were are auxiliaries doing the job by inversion. Pure concessives that state facts rather than conditions — although, though, even though — belong to the concession file; what lives here is the concessive condition, where the clause is still open (even if, whether or not, regardless of whether). Negative purpose — lest, for fear that — is the neighboring category and lives in the purpose file.

The core conditionals#

The words that do the basic work: one open condition, three negative ones, and three that adjust how much the condition covers.

except if#

Pronunciation

[main clause] except if [clause]. · Except if [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] except when [clause]. · [main clause] except where [clause].

Carves an exception out of a statement that otherwise holds — the same job unless does in one word instead of two. American editors treat it as wordy and swap in unless, and it earns its keep in only two places: where the exception is one of several being listed in parallel (except if, except when, except where), and where unless would be read as conditioning the whole sentence rather than the one item it is attached to.

  1. The office is open every weekday except if it's a federal holiday.
  2. Don't call me at work except if it's an emergency.
  3. Coverage continues except if the premium goes unpaid for sixty days.
  4. Returns are accepted within thirty days except if the tag has been removed.
  5. She answers every email the same day, except if she's traveling.
  6. Except if the weather turns, the crew works straight through Saturday.

Provisos and stipulations#

Conditions imposed as terms — the language of permissions, contracts, and rules. Most could be rewritten with if; each one adds the sense that somebody set the requirement. Two of them, subject to and contingent on, take a noun phrase instead of a clause, and three more — as and when, if and when, and unless and until — are fixed doublets that fold a time limit into the condition.

Pattern
[main clause] as and when [clause]. · [main clause] as and when [past participle or adjective]. · As and when [clause], [main clause].

A drafter's doublet meaning "at whatever times, and each time that" — it leaves the schedule of an obligation open instead of fixing a date, and it covers repetition as well as timing. Against if and when, which doubts the event will happen at all, as and when takes it for granted and questions only the calendar: payable as and when the invoices fall due assumes there will be invoices.

  1. Dividends are payable as and when declared by the board.
  2. The contractor will submit invoices as and when the work is completed.
  3. Rent shall be paid as and when it falls due.
  4. Additional units will be released as and when demand warrants.
  5. The trustee may sell the securities as and when she thinks fit.
  6. As and when further records come to light, the docket will be updated.

  2. Her bonus is contingent upon hitting the third-quarter target.
  3. Contingent on board approval, we'll announce the merger Monday.
  4. Everything is contingent on whether the permit comes through.
  5. The second payment is contingent on delivery of a working prototype.
  6. Federal funding is contingent on the state's matching the grant dollar for dollar.

  5. To the extent that anyone remembers the case at all, they remember the dissent.

Counterfactuals#

Conditions known to be false, or — with without — simply not met. Four of the six take a noun phrase rather than a clause, and wherever the condition is genuinely counterfactual the main clause carries would or would have. Legal, Latin, and archaic conditionals#

The conditional formulas that live in contracts, statutes, briefs, and old books rather than in ordinary American speech — recognizing them matters far more than using them. The Latin tags ceteris paribus, ex hypothesi, and arguendo belong to economics, philosophy, and appellate argument; the doublet if and to the extent that and the fallback clause in default of belong to drafting; and the subjunctive forms be it that, if so be that, but and if, and peradventure belong to the King James Bible, to Shakespeare's contemporaries, and to the nineteenth-century novel. In no event is the odd member of the set: it is not a conjunction at all but a fronted negative adverbial, and it earns its place here as the exact negative of in the event that.

The old word for "by chance," used as a conjunction meaning "if by chance" and, after a verb of fearing, "lest." Its everyday afterlife is not conditional at all: what an American actually meets is the adverb in the King James Bible (Peradventure there be fifty righteous within the city) and the frozen legal noun in beyond peradventure of doubt. The conjunction itself died out with the eighteenth century.

  1. "Peradventure there be fifty righteous within the city." (Genesis 18:24)
  2. "Peradventure the woman will not be willing to follow me." (Genesis 24:5)
  3. If peradventure the ship be lost, the underwriters shall answer for the whole.
  4. Set a watch upon the gate, lest peradventure he come upon thee unawares.
  5. Peradventure the heir die without issue, the land shall revert to the donor.

whether or no#

Pronunciation
/ˈwɛðər ər ˈnoʊ/ · WETH-ur-ur-NOH · identical to whether or not except in the last word, and the no takes exactly the stress the not would
Русский
устар., книжн.: независимо от того, … или нет — старая форма whether or not
Type
subordinating; also a complementizer in noun clauses
Register
archaic, literary
Clause type
adverbial clause of concessive condition; also a noun clause
Pattern
Whether or no [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] whether or no [clause]. · [verb of knowing or asking] whether or no [clause]. · [main clause] whether [clause] or no (the split form).
Punctuation
Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows, and none before a noun clause — the same rules that govern whether or not.
Variants
whether or not (the modern form), the split whether … or no, whether or nay (rarer still)

The older shape of whether or not, with the archaic sentential no where modern English puts not. It is not an error, only dated: it is thick on the ground in the King James Bible, in Milton, and in nineteenth-century novels, and an American who writes it today will be read as reaching for an antique effect.

  1. Whether or no the enterprise succeed, the attempt does him honor.
  2. She meant to have her way, whether or no her brother approved.
  3. I know not whether or no he will come.
  4. Whether or no the treaty be ratified, the boundary is settled in fact.
  5. He would have the boy schooled, whether or no the money could be found.
