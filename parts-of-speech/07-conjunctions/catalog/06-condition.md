# Subordinators of condition

> The words that hang one clause on another as a condition — saying what has to be true, what would have to have been true, or what will happen if it isn't.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What unites this category is that every member marks its clause as **unasserted**: the speaker is not claiming the condition is true, only saying what follows if it is. That is why *If the check cleared, we're fine* tells you nothing about whether the check cleared, while *Because the check cleared, we're fine* tells you it did. Some members take a full clause (*if*, *unless*, *provided that*), some take only a noun phrase (*but for*, *absent*, *subject to*), and three take no conjunction at all, marking the condition by inverting an auxiliary (*Had I known…*, *Should you need…*, *Were it up to me…*).

Two rules run through the whole set. **Punctuation:** a conditional clause placed before the main clause takes a comma after it, and the same clause placed after the main clause normally takes none — with one important exception, since a fronted *only if* clause triggers inversion in the main clause and takes no comma at all (*Only if you ask will she tell you*). **Tense:** the conjunction never changes to show likelihood; the verb does. A present-tense condition is real (*if it rains*), a past-tense one is unlikely (*if it rained*), and a past-perfect one is already lost (*if it had rained*) — and a future condition still takes the present, never *will*.

Several members of this list are not conjunctions at all, and the punctuation follows the part of speech rather than the meaning. *Otherwise*, *failing that*, *either way*, and *in that case* are conjunctive adverbs, so two independent clauses joined by one of them need a semicolon or a period in front — a comma alone is a comma splice (*We should leave now; otherwise, we'll be late*). *Or else* is coordinating and therefore takes a plain comma before it, the same as *or*. *In which case* heads a relative clause and also takes a comma. *Absent*, *barring*, *short of*, *but for*, *without*, *subject to*, and *contingent on* are prepositions that take a noun phrase, and *had*, *should*, and *were* are auxiliaries doing the job by inversion. Pure concessives that state facts rather than conditions — *although*, *though*, *even though* — belong to [the concession file](07-concession.md); what lives here is the concessive *condition*, where the clause is still open (*even if*, *whether or not*, *regardless of whether*). Negative purpose — *lest*, *for fear that* — is the neighboring category and lives in [the purpose file](05-purpose-result.md).

## The core conditionals

The words that do the basic work: one open condition, three negative ones, and three that adjust how much the condition covers.

### even if

**Pronunciation:** /ˈivən ɪf/ &middot; *EE-vuhn-if* &middot; reduced /ˈivn̩ ɪf/ &middot; the stress sits on *even*; *if* takes it only under contrast
**Русский:** даже если — уступительное условие; в отличие от even though, факт не утверждается
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Even if* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *even if* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the *even if* clause when it comes first; no comma when it follows.

Grants a condition the maximum benefit of the doubt and says the main clause holds anyway. The distinction learners need is with *even though*: *Even though it rained, we went* means it rained, while *Even if it rains, we're going* leaves the rain an open possibility.

1. *Even if* the flight leaves on time, we'll miss the connection in Denver.
2. She would have taken the job *even if* they had offered less.
3. *Even if* you apologize now, the damage is done.
4. I'm walking there *even if* it's ten degrees out.
5. *Even if* every member votes yes, the measure still needs the governor's signature.
6. He wouldn't sell the house *even if* somebody offered him double.

**See also:** [if](#if), [only if](#only-if), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [granted that](#granted-that), [even though](07-concession.md#even-though)

### except if

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈsɛpt ɪf/ &middot; *ik-SEPT-if* &middot; the final /t/ links straight into the vowel of *if*, and casual speech drops it altogether: [ɪkˈsɛp ɪf]
**Русский:** кроме тех случаев, когда; разве что — двухсловный аналог unless
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral, though editors usually change it to *unless*
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative condition (exception)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *except if* [clause]. &middot; *Except if* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *except when* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *except where* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it nearly always sits; comma after it on the rare occasions the clause is fronted.
**Variants:** *except when* (the time version), *except where* (place, and standard in statutes), *except that* (which states a fact rather than a condition — see [12-formal-archaic.md](12-formal-archaic.md#except-that)), *unless* (the one-word equivalent)

Carves an exception out of a statement that otherwise holds — the same job *unless* does in one word instead of two. American editors treat it as wordy and swap in *unless*, and it earns its keep in only two places: where the exception is one of several being listed in parallel (*except if*, *except when*, *except where*), and where *unless* would be read as conditioning the whole sentence rather than the one item it is attached to.

1. The office is open every weekday *except if* it's a federal holiday.
2. Don't call me at work *except if* it's an emergency.
3. Coverage continues *except if* the premium goes unpaid for sixty days.
4. Returns are accepted within thirty days *except if* the tag has been removed.
5. She answers every email the same day, *except if* she's traveling.
6. *Except if* the weather turns, the crew works straight through Saturday.

**See also:** [unless](#unless), [not unless](#not-unless), [if not](#if-not), [barring](#barring), [except that](12-formal-archaic.md#except-that)

### if

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf/ &middot; *IF* &middot; reduced /əf/ in fast speech
**Русский:** если — основной союз условия; в косвенном вопросе — частица «ли»
**Type:** subordinating (also a complementizer in indirect questions) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition; also a noun clause (indirect yes/no question)
**Pattern:** *If* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *if* [clause]. &middot; [verb of knowing or asking] *if* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *if* clause; normally no comma when the *if* clause follows. Never put a comma before *if* introducing a noun clause: *I don't know if he's coming.*
**Variants:** *if'n* (regional, dialectal); reduced fragments *if any*, *if anything*, *if ever*, *if at all*

The all-purpose conditional. The word itself never changes to show how likely the condition is — the tense of its clause does that job, with the present for real conditions, the simple past for unlikely ones, and the past perfect for impossible ones. Note that a future condition takes the present tense: *If it rains tomorrow*, never *If it will rain tomorrow*.

1. *If* it rains tomorrow, the game moves to Sunday.
2. Call me *if* you get lost.
3. *If* I had left ten minutes earlier, I would have made the train.
4. She'd take the promotion *if* they moved her back to Denver.
5. *If* you're finished with that, I'll take it.
6. Let me know *if* the package arrives.

**See also:** [unless](#unless), [whether](#whether), [only if](#only-if), [if only](#if-only), [in case](#in-case), [provided that](#provided-that)

### if and only if

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪf ən ˈoʊnli ɪf/ &middot; *if-uhn-OHN-lee-if* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/; written *iff* in mathematics, but always read aloud in full
**Русский:** тогда и только тогда, когда — двустороннее условие (мат., лог., юр.)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal, technical (mathematics, logic, contracts)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (biconditional)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *if and only if* [clause]. &middot; [proposition] *iff* [proposition] (written mathematics and logic only).
**Punctuation:** No commas at all — the phrase is doing precise work and a comma weakens the link. It sits after the main clause; fronting it is not idiomatic.
**Variants:** *iff* (written abbreviation in mathematics and logic; not a typo)

States that the condition is both sufficient and necessary, closing both directions at once. Plain *if* gives only sufficiency and *only if* only necessity, so this is the one that says the two statements stand or fall together.

1. A triangle is equilateral *if and only if* all three of its angles are equal.
2. An integer is even *if and only if* its last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.
3. The grant is renewed *if and only if* the recipient files an annual report.
4. Payment becomes due *if and only if* the goods arrive undamaged.
5. A set is closed *iff* it contains all of its limit points.
6. The clause is triggered *if and only if* both parties sign before the deadline.

**See also:** [only if](#only-if), [if](#if), [unless](#unless), [provided that](#provided-that)

### not unless

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ənˈlɛs/ &middot; *not-uhn-LESS* &middot; the /t/ flaps into the following vowel, [nɑɾ ənˈlɛs]; both words are stressed when the phrase stands alone as a reply
**Русский:** разве что; нет, если только не — отказ, называющий единственное снимающее его условие
**Type:** subordinating (elliptical — *unless* with the negated main clause left unsaid) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standalone reply is conversational
**Clause type:** an *unless* clause with its negative main clause reduced to the bare *not*
**Pattern:** *Not unless* [clause]. (a complete turn in conversation) &middot; [negative main clause], *not unless* [clause]. &middot; [negative main clause] — *not unless* [clause].
**Punctuation:** As a standalone reply it takes a period and no internal comma. Tacked onto a negative statement it reads as an afterthought and takes a comma or a dash in front of it. It never takes a comma inside the pair.
**Variants:** *not until* (the time counterpart), *only if* (the same deal stated as a rule), *not without* + noun phrase

Answers a question by naming the one condition that would turn the no into a yes — *unless* with the refusal left implicit. It is the conversational twin of *only if*: *Not unless she asks* and *Only if she asks* set the same terms, one as a refusal and one as a policy.

1. "Are you going to the reunion?" "*Not unless* Danny goes."
2. "Can we still get a table?" "*Not unless* somebody cancels."
3. He isn't signing anything, *not unless* his lawyer reads it first.
4. The plant doesn't reopen — *not unless* the inspection clears.
5. "Will she change her mind?" "*Not unless* you apologize first."
6. We're not driving up there in February, *not unless* the roads are clear.

**See also:** [unless](#unless), [only if](#only-if), [if not](#if-not), [or else](#or-else), [until](03-time.md#until)

### only if

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊnli ɪf/ &middot; *OHN-lee-if* &middot; the stress is on *only*, which is what makes the condition sound exclusive
**Русский:** только если — необходимое условие, ничто другое не подойдёт
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (necessary condition)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *only if* [clause]. &middot; *Only if* [clause] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma before *only if* when it follows the main clause. When it is fronted it triggers subject–auxiliary inversion in the main clause and takes **no** comma: *Only if you ask will she tell you.*
**Variants:** *if and only if* (the two-way version); *only when*, *only after* (time equivalents)

Marks the condition as necessary rather than merely sufficient — nothing else will do. *If you sign, we ship* leaves other routes open; *We ship only if you sign* closes them all.

1. We'll refund the deposit *only if* you cancel a week in advance.
2. *Only if* the tests come back clean will they reopen the plant.
3. He answers his phone *only if* he recognizes the number.
4. The policy pays out *only if* the damage exceeds the deductible.
5. *Only if* both parents sign can the child travel alone.
6. You may retake the exam *only if* you attend the review session.

**See also:** [if and only if](#if-and-only-if), [if](#if), [unless](#unless), [on condition that](#on-condition-that)

### unless

**Pronunciation:** /ənˈlɛs/ &middot; *uhn-LESS* &middot; full form /ʌnˈlɛs/
**Русский:** если не; кроме случаев, когда — единственное исключение из сказанного
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of negative condition (exception)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *unless* [clause]. &middot; *Unless* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *unless* clause; normally no comma when it follows. The clause takes the present tense for future conditions, never *will*.
**Variants:** *unless and until* (legal doublet); the negative *if not* covers much of the same ground

Means "except on the condition that" — it carves one exception out of a statement that otherwise holds. It is close to *if … not* but narrower: *unless* names the single circumstance that would overturn the main clause, and it cannot be used for counterfactuals (*If I hadn't called…*, never *Unless I had called…*).

1. The store closes at six *unless* there's a holiday sale.
2. *Unless* somebody objects, I'll book the room for Thursday.
3. Don't sign anything *unless* your lawyer has read it.
4. We'll be there by noon *unless* traffic on the interstate is terrible.
5. *Unless* you've already eaten, there's a plate in the oven.
6. She never takes a day off *unless* she's genuinely sick.

**See also:** [if](#if), [if not](#if-not), [or else](#or-else), [otherwise](#otherwise), [unless and until](#unless-and-until), [barring](#barring)

## Provisos and stipulations

Conditions imposed as terms — the language of permissions, contracts, and rules. Most could be rewritten with *if*; each one adds the sense that somebody set the requirement. Two of them, *subject to* and *contingent on*, take a noun phrase instead of a clause, and three more — *as and when*, *if and when*, and *unless and until* — are fixed doublets that fold a time limit into the condition.

### as and when

**Pronunciation:** /æz ən ˈwɛn/ &middot; *az-uhn-WEN* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ and never carries stress; *as* keeps its full vowel because the phrase opens on it
**Русский:** по мере того как; когда именно — юр.: срок не фиксируется, событие предполагается
**Type:** subordinating (fixed doublet) &middot; **Register:** formal (contracts, corporate resolutions, statutes)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of time and condition at once
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as and when* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as and when* [past participle or adjective]. &middot; *As and when* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it almost always sits; comma after a fronted clause. Never split the three words with a comma — they work as one conjunction.
**Variants:** *if and when* (which leaves the whether open), *as and when required*, *as and when due*, *as and when declared* (fixed drafting collocations)

A drafter's doublet meaning "at whatever times, and each time that" — it leaves the schedule of an obligation open instead of fixing a date, and it covers repetition as well as timing. Against *if and when*, which doubts the event will happen at all, *as and when* takes it for granted and questions only the calendar: *payable as and when the invoices fall due* assumes there will be invoices.

1. Dividends are payable *as and when* declared by the board.
2. The contractor will submit invoices *as and when* the work is completed.
3. Rent shall be paid *as and when* it falls due.
4. Additional units will be released *as and when* demand warrants.
5. The trustee may sell the securities *as and when* she thinks fit.
6. *As and when* further records come to light, the docket will be updated.

**See also:** [if and when](#if-and-when), [unless and until](#unless-and-until), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [when](03-time.md#when)

### as long as

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈlɔŋ əz/ &middot; *uhz-LAWNG-uhz* &middot; full form /æz ˈlɔŋ æz/; /ˈlɑŋ/ for speakers with the cot–caught merger
**Русский:** при условии что — постоянное требование; те же слова значат и «пока», о длительности
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (also of time — see below)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as long as* [clause]. &middot; *As long as* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *as long as* clause; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *so long as* (a shade more formal, identical in meaning), *just as long as* (emphatic)

Sets a standing requirement: the main clause holds for exactly as many cases as the condition does. The same three words also measure duration (*Stay as long as you like*), and only the sense of the sentence tells them apart — the conditional reading describes a state of affairs, the temporal one describes a stretch of time.

1. You can borrow the truck *as long as* you bring it back with a full tank.
2. *As long as* everyone's comfortable with the date, I'll send the invitations.
3. The warranty holds *as long as* you keep the receipt.
4. *So long as* the numbers hold up, we'll expand in the fall.
5. I don't care where we eat *as long as* it isn't that pizza place again.
6. He'll keep quiet about it *as long as* nobody asks him directly.

**See also:** [provided that](#provided-that), [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [if](#if), [as long as (duration)](03-time.md#as-long-as)

### contingent on

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈtɪndʒənt ɑn/ &middot; *kuhn-TIN-juhnt-on* &middot; *contingent upon* is /kənˈtɪndʒənt əˌpɑn/, *kuhn-TIN-juhnt-uh-pon*
**Русский:** зависящий от; обусловленный — исход ждёт будущего события (книжн., юр.)
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase, a gerund, or a *whether* clause) &middot; **Register:** formal (contracts, finance, real estate)
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — a noun phrase, an *-ing* phrase, or a *whether* clause naming what the outcome hangs on
**Pattern:** [main clause] *contingent on* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *contingent on* [gerund]. &middot; [main clause] *contingent on whether* [clause]. &middot; *Contingent on* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it almost always sits. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted. Never put a comma between *contingent* and *on*.
**Variants:** *contingent upon* (a shade more formal), *conditioned on*, *conditional on*, *dependent on*

Hangs an outcome on something else happening, named as a noun rather than spelled out as a clause. It is the close cousin of *subject to*, and contracts use the two almost interchangeably, but they point at different things: *subject to* defers to a rule or an approval that already exists, while *contingent on* waits on a future event that may never occur. Note that *contingent* is an adjective outside this use, and a noun in *a contingent of volunteers*.

1. The offer is *contingent on* the buyer securing financing.
2. Her bonus is *contingent upon* hitting the third-quarter target.
3. *Contingent on* board approval, we'll announce the merger Monday.
4. Everything is *contingent on* whether the permit comes through.
5. The second payment is *contingent on* delivery of a working prototype.
6. Federal funding is *contingent on* the state's matching the grant dollar for dollar.

**See also:** [subject to](#subject-to), [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [provided that](#provided-that), [if](#if)

### if and when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪf ən ˈwɛn/ &middot; *if-uhn-WEN* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ and never carries stress
**Русский:** если и когда — открыты сразу и сам факт, и срок
**Type:** subordinating (fixed doublet) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (business, legal, administrative)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition and time at once
**Pattern:** [main clause] *if and when* [clause]. &middot; *If and when* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *if and when* clause; no comma when it follows. Never split the pair with a comma — the three words work as one conjunction. Like plain *if*, the clause takes the present tense for future conditions, never *will*.
**Variants:** *when and if* (same meaning, less common); the negative counterpart is *unless and until*

The positive mirror of *unless and until*: it leaves the whether and the when open at once and promises nothing about either. That doubt is the whole difference from plain *when* — *We'll deal with it when he calls* expects the call, *if and when he calls* does not.

1. We'll cross that bridge *if and when* we come to it.
2. *If and when* the funding clears, we'll start hiring.
3. The company will comment *if and when* the investigation ends.
4. *If and when* she decides to sell, she has my number.
5. Those shares vest *if and when* the merger closes.
6. I'll worry about the tax bill *if and when* it actually shows up.

**See also:** [unless and until](#unless-and-until), [if](#if), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [when](03-time.md#when)

### on condition that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn kənˈdɪʃən ðət/ &middot; *on-kuhn-DISH-uhn-thuht* &middot; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/; the full /ðæt/ belongs to the demonstrative
**Русский:** при условии, что — оговорка, которую кто-то поставил (книжн.)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on condition that* [clause]. &middot; *On condition that* [clause], [main clause] (rare).
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it almost always sits. On the rare occasions the clause is fronted, a comma follows it. Formal writing often puts the base-form subjunctive in the clause: *on condition that he stay*, not *stays*.
**Variants:** *on the condition that*, *on one condition: that…*, *on the express condition that* (legal), *on the understanding that* (softer — a shared expectation rather than a term)

States a term that somebody imposes — a stipulation attached to a permission or a deal, so there is always a grantor standing behind it. *Provided that* merely names a requirement; *on condition that* implies that a person set it.

1. He agreed to testify *on condition that* his name never appear in print.
2. The museum accepted the collection *on condition that* it be displayed together.
3. She'll release the recording *on the condition that* both parties approve the transcript.
4. They let him out *on condition that* he stay within the county.
5. The loan was granted *on condition that* the company cut its dividend.
6. *On condition that* the school keeps her name off the building, she'll fund the whole wing.

**See also:** [provided that](#provided-that), [as long as](#as-long-as), [subject to](#subject-to), [only if](#only-if)

### on the understanding that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˌʌndərˈstændɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *on-thee-un-dur-STAN-ding-thuht* &middot; *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *understanding*; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** с тем пониманием, что — негласная договорённость, а не жёсткая оговорка
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (negotiations, letters, minutes)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (a shared expectation treated as a term)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the understanding that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with the understanding that* [clause]. &middot; *On the understanding that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it nearly always sits; comma after a fronted clause. A comma before it is possible when the qualification is an afterthought rather than part of the deal.
**Variants:** *with the understanding that* (the commoner American wording, identical in meaning), *on the express understanding that* (emphatic), *on the clear understanding that*, *understanding that* (reduced)

Attaches a condition that both sides are taken to have agreed to without anyone writing it down — the softest member of the proviso family. *On condition that* names a term somebody imposed and can enforce; *on the understanding that* names an expectation, and the usual reason for saying it out loud is that the speaker suspects it may not be shared.

1. She agreed to chair the committee *on the understanding that* it would meet twice a year.
2. We released the figures *with the understanding that* they would not be published before Monday.
3. The city leased the lot to the club *on the understanding that* the field stays open to the public.
4. He took the loan *with the understanding that* repayment would start after graduation.
5. *On the understanding that* nothing here is final, I'll circulate a draft this week.
6. They dropped the objection *on the understanding that* the hearing would be rescheduled, not canceled.

**See also:** [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [provided that](#provided-that), [with the proviso that](#with-the-proviso-that), [subject to](#subject-to), [assuming that](#assuming-that)

### provided that

**Pronunciation:** /prəˈvaɪdɪd ðət/ &middot; *pruh-VY-did-thuht* &middot; the medial /d/ is usually a flap: [prəˈvaɪɾəd]; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** при условии что; если только — требование как оговорка
**Type:** subordinating (participial — frozen from the verb *provide*, which is why it never inflects here) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (standard in rules and contracts)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *provided (that)* [clause]. &middot; *Provided (that)* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *provided (that)* clause. When it follows, the comma is optional: use one if the proviso is an afterthought, none if it is part of the core statement. Legal drafting sets it off as *; provided, however, that…* — a style worth avoiding everywhere else.
**Variants:** *provided*, *providing*, *providing that* (the *-ing* forms are a little more informal and rarer in edited American prose)

Introduces a requirement that has to be met for the main clause to hold. It does the same job as *if*, but flags the condition as a stipulation rather than a possibility, which is why it prefers things somebody can actually control.

1. You may return the item *provided that* you have the receipt.
2. *Provided that* nothing changes, the closing is on the ninth.
3. Employees can carry over vacation days *provided* they use them by March.
4. The city will fund the repairs *providing that* the county matches the grant.
5. *Provided that* both signatures are notarized, the transfer takes effect immediately.
6. She'll speak at the conference, *provided* her travel is covered.

**See also:** [as long as](#as-long-as), [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [if](#if), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [subject to](#subject-to)

### subject to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbdʒɛkt tə/ &middot; *SUB-jekt-tuh* &middot; stress on the first syllable; the verb *subject* is /səbˈdʒɛkt/, sub-JEKT
**Русский:** при условии; с учётом — зависит от согласования, правила или события (перед сущ.)
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal (contracts, offers, schedules)
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming the condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *subject to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Subject to* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *subject to* phrase; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *subject to the condition that* + clause (the full clausal form)

Makes a statement conditional on an approval, a rule, or an event named as a noun. Because it takes a noun phrase, it cannot carry a subject and verb the way *provided that* can, which is exactly why contracts like it — the condition stays compact and quotable.

1. The offer is *subject to* a satisfactory home inspection.
2. *Subject to* board approval, the merger closes in March.
3. All fares are *subject to* change without notice.
4. The permit was issued *subject to* three conditions.
5. Delivery dates are *subject to* availability.
6. *Subject to* the terms below, the license is worldwide and perpetual.

**See also:** [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [provided that](#provided-that), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [absent](#absent)

### to the extent that

**Pronunciation:** /tə ði ɪkˈstɛnt ðət/ &middot; *tuh-thee-ik-STENT-thuht* &middot; *to* reduces to /tə/ and *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *extent*; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** в той мере, в какой — отвечает на «насколько», а не на «да или нет»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (contracts, statutes, academic prose)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition limiting the scope of the main clause
**Pattern:** [main clause] *to the extent that* [clause]. &middot; *To the extent that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *only to the extent that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to the extent of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause; comma after a fronted clause. Never put a comma inside the phrase, and do not write *to the extent, that*.
**Variants:** *insofar as* (the one-word formal equivalent), *to the degree that*, *to the extent of* + noun phrase, *to such extent as* (older drafting), *if and to the extent that* (the legal doublet)

Makes the main clause true in proportion to how far the condition holds instead of all at once — it answers "how far?" rather than "whether?" That partial quality is the whole difference from *if*: *The rule applies if it doesn't conflict with federal law* is a yes-or-no test, while *to the extent that it doesn't conflict* keeps whatever part of the rule survives and drops the rest.

1. State law governs *to the extent that* it does not conflict with the federal statute.
2. *To the extent that* the deposit covers the damage, no further payment is due.
3. The company will reimburse expenses *to the extent that* they are documented.
4. I agree with her *to the extent that* the schedule is unrealistic, but not about the budget.
5. *To the extent that* anyone remembers the case at all, they remember the dissent.
6. Coverage applies *only to the extent that* the loss exceeds the deductible.

**See also:** [as long as](#as-long-as), [provided that](#provided-that), [if and to the extent that](#if-and-to-the-extent-that), [to the degree that](08-comparison.md#to-the-degree-that), [insofar as](12-formal-archaic.md#insofar-as)

### unless and until

**Pronunciation:** /ənˈlɛs ən ənˈtɪl/ &middot; *uhn-LESS-uhn-uhn-TIL* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ between the two stresses
**Русский:** если и пока не — юр. дублет: условие и срок сразу
**Type:** subordinating (fixed doublet) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal and administrative)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition and time at once
**Pattern:** [main clause, usually negative] *unless and until* [clause]. &middot; *Unless and until* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *unless and until* clause; no comma before it when it follows the main clause, which is where it usually sits. Never split the pair with a comma — the three words work as one conjunction.
**Variants:** *until and unless* (same meaning, less common); the positive counterpart is *if and when*

A lawyer's doublet that closes two gaps at once: nothing happens *unless* the condition is met, and nothing happens *until* it is met. Outside legal and administrative writing, plain *until* almost always says everything the sentence needs.

1. No payment is due *unless and until* the work passes inspection.
2. The account stays frozen *unless and until* a court says otherwise.
3. *Unless and until* the tenant cures the default, the lease remains in breach.
4. The company will make no further comment *unless and until* the investigation concludes.
5. Benefits are suspended *unless and until* the claimant files the missing form.

**See also:** [unless](#unless), [if and when](#if-and-when), [if](#if), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [until](03-time.md#until)

### with the proviso that

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ðə prəˈvaɪzoʊ ðət/ &middot; *with-thuh-pruh-VY-zoh-thuht* &middot; many Americans say /wɪθ/ with the consonant of *thin*, and both are standard; the plural is *provisos*, not *provisoes*
**Русский:** с оговоркой, что — ограничение, приложенное к уже данному разрешению
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional phrase plus complementizer) &middot; **Register:** formal (contracts, minutes, committee resolutions)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition — a stipulation cutting back what was just granted
**Pattern:** [main clause], *with the proviso that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *with the proviso that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *subject to the proviso that* [clause]. &middot; *With the proviso that* [clause], [main clause]. (rare)
**Punctuation:** A comma before it is usual, because the proviso is added to a grant already made; a comma after it is wrong, since what follows is its own clause. Comma after the phrase on the rare occasions it is fronted. Formal drafting uses the base-form subjunctive inside the clause: *with the proviso that the work stop at seven*, not *stops*.
**Variants:** *subject to the proviso that*, *with the one proviso that*, *with the proviso* (the clause dropped), *provided that* (the plain conjunction), *provided always that* (the legal formula)

Names a limit attached to permission that has just been given, and announces in advance that the qualification is coming. Where *provided that* simply states a requirement, *with the proviso that* frames it as a carve-out from the grant, which is why it lives in minutes, resolutions, and letters of acceptance rather than in ordinary prose.

1. The board approved the budget, *with the proviso that* the hiring freeze stay in place.
2. She accepted the appointment *with the proviso that* she could keep teaching one course.
3. The council granted the permit, *with the proviso that* work stop at seven each evening.
4. We'll fund the second year *with the proviso that* the first year's results are reviewed.
5. The library will take the collection, *with the proviso that* nothing be sold off.
6. He agreed to the interview *with the proviso that* the tape not be edited.

**See also:** [provided that](#provided-that), [on condition that](#on-condition-that), [on the understanding that](#on-the-understanding-that), [subject to](#subject-to), [provided always that](12-formal-archaic.md#provided-always-that)

## Precautions and contingencies

Conditions about things that might go wrong. Four take a clause, three take a bare noun phrase, and all seven attach to a plan made in advance. The negative-purpose subordinators that sit right next door — *lest*, *for fear that* — are filed under [purpose](05-purpose-result.md#lest).

### absent

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæbsənt/ &middot; *AB-suhnt* &middot; the adjective is the same; the verb *absent oneself* shifts to /æbˈsɛnt/, ab-SENT
**Русский:** при отсутствии; в отсутствие — книжн.-юр. предлог со значением «если нет»
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal, academic, journalistic)
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming what is missing
**Pattern:** *Absent* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *absent* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *absent* phrase, which is where it usually appears; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *in the absence of* (the unabbreviated equivalent)

An American legal-register preposition meaning "if there is no." It states a negative condition in two words, and it has spread from court opinions into policy writing and journalism, though it still sounds stiff in conversation.

1. *Absent* a written agreement, the default rules apply.
2. The bill has no path forward *absent* bipartisan support.
3. *Absent* evidence of fraud, the court will not reopen the case.
4. *Absent* some change in the weather, the launch window closes tonight.
5. Enrollment will keep falling *absent* a serious recruiting effort.

**See also:** [barring](#barring), [short of](#short-of), [but for](#but-for), [without](#without), [unless](#unless), [subject to](#subject-to)

### barring

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɑrɪŋ/ &middot; *BAR-ing* &middot; identical to the *-ing* form of the verb *bar*
**Русский:** если не случится; за исключением — единственная помеха уверенному прогнозу
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase); frozen from the verb *bar*, so it never behaves like a live participle and takes no subject &middot; **Register:** neutral, slightly formal
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming the one thing that would prevent the outcome
**Pattern:** *Barring* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *barring* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *barring* phrase; a comma before it when it follows is optional and common.
**Variants:** fixed collocations *barring a miracle*, *barring the unforeseen*, *barring something unexpected*

Means "unless there is" — it names the single event that would derail the main clause, which is why it attaches to confident predictions. It is the everyday cousin of *absent*, which sounds legal by comparison.

1. *Barring* a last-minute delay, we land at six.
2. The roof should last another decade, *barring* a bad hailstorm.
3. *Barring* an injury, she'll start every game this season.
4. We're on schedule *barring* something we haven't thought of.
5. *Barring* a miracle, this bill dies in committee.

**See also:** [absent](#absent), [short of](#short-of), [unless](#unless), [but for](#but-for), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that)

### if need be

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈnid bi/ &middot; *if-NEED-bee* &middot; the stress is on *need*, and *be* keeps its full vowel because it ends the phrase; the three words are said as one unit
**Русский:** если понадобится; в случае необходимости
**Type:** subordinating (a frozen clause — *need* is a bare noun subject and *be* the old present subjunctive) &middot; **Register:** neutral; common in speech and in ordinary writing
**Clause type:** a fixed adverbial clause of condition that takes no subject, tense, or negation of its own
**Pattern:** [main clause] *if need be*. &middot; *If need be*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *if need be*, [continuation].
**Punctuation:** Comma before it when it trails the main clause, since it reads as an afterthought; comma after it when it opens the sentence; commas on both sides in the middle of a clause. The phrase never inflects — there is no *if need was* and no *if needs be* in American English, the second being the British form.
**Variants:** *if necessary* (the everyday equivalent), *should the need arise* (formal), *if it comes to that*, *if needs be* (British)

Marks a fallback the speaker is willing to resort to — "if it turns out to be necessary." It is a fossil: *need* is the subject noun and *be* the present subjunctive, which is why it cannot be put into any other tense. Against *in case*, which acts on a possibility ahead of time, *if need be* holds the action back and takes it only if the need actually shows up.

1. I can drive you to the airport *if need be*.
2. *If need be*, we'll hold the meeting over Zoom.
3. The clinic will stay open past six, *if need be*, until everyone has been seen.
4. She'll testify *if need be*, though she'd rather not.
5. We can cut the last two slides *if need be*.
6. *If need be*, the county can draw on the emergency fund.

**See also:** [in case](#in-case), [should](#should), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [on the off chance that](#on-the-off-chance-that), [if](#if)

### in case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkeɪs/ &middot; *in-KAYSS*
**Русский:** на случай если — предосторожность заранее, в отличие от if («если»)
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of precautionary condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in case* [clause]. &middot; *In case* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *in case of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *in case* clause; no comma when it follows. American English does not write *in the case that* for this sense.
**Variants:** *just in case* (emphatic; also stands alone: *Bring it just in case*), *in case of* + noun phrase (signs and instructions)

Marks a precaution taken in advance against a possibility, so the main clause happens first, because of that possibility. Compare *if*: *Take an umbrella if it rains* means wait and see, while *Take an umbrella in case it rains* means take it now.

1. Take an umbrella *in case* it rains.
2. I wrote down the address *in case* my phone dies.
3. *In case* you're wondering, the meeting got moved to Thursday.
4. Leave a key with the neighbor *just in case*.
5. *In case of* fire, use the stairs.
6. She keeps a spare charger in her bag *in case* somebody needs one.

**See also:** [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [on the off chance that](#on-the-off-chance-that), [if](#if), [should](#should), [lest](05-purpose-result.md#lest)

### in the event that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ði ɪˈvɛnt ðət/ &middot; *in-thee-ih-VENT-thuht* &middot; *the* is /ði/ before the vowel of *event*; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** в случае если — офиц. эквивалент if, о событии, а не о состоянии
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal (legal, insurance, administrative)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** *In the event that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in the event that* [clause]. &middot; *in the event of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *in the event of* + noun phrase, *in the event* (with *that* dropped), *in event of* (older contract style)

The formal-register equivalent of *if*, reserved for conditions that are occurrences rather than states — a cancellation, a default, a death, a claim. In ordinary prose it is longer than *if* without being any clearer.

1. *In the event that* the flight is canceled, the airline will rebook you at no charge.
2. Benefits are paid to the beneficiary *in the event of* the policyholder's death.
3. *In the event that* neither candidate reaches a majority, the House chooses the president.
4. The deposit is refundable *in the event that* the inspection fails.
5. *In the event of* a power outage, the generator starts automatically.

**See also:** [in case](#in-case), [should](#should), [if](#if), [subject to](#subject-to), [unless and until](#unless-and-until)

### on the off chance that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˈɔf tʃæns ðət/ &middot; *on-thee-AWF-chanss-thuht* &middot; /ɑf/ for speakers with the cot–caught merger; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** на случай, вдруг; авось — разг., о маловероятной возможности
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of remote precautionary condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the off chance (that)* [clause]. &middot; *On the off chance (that)* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it in ordinary use; a comma is fine when the phrase is tacked on as an afterthought. Comma after it when the whole clause is fronted.
**Variants:** *on the off chance*, *on the chance that*, *on the outside chance that*

*In case* with the odds marked low — the speaker is hedging against something unlikely and admitting as much. That admission is the whole point of choosing this phrase over *in case*.

1. I texted him *on the off chance that* he's still in town.
2. She applied *on the off chance* the position was still open.
3. We brought a cooler *on the off chance that* the power went out again.
4. I'm asking *on the off chance* you know somebody who does drywall.
5. He checks the numbers every Monday *on the off chance* he won.
6. *On the off chance that* she's already left, I'll try her cell instead.

**See also:** [in case](#in-case), [if](#if), [what if](#what-if), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [short of](#short-of)

### short of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʃɔrt əv/ &middot; *SHORT-uhv* &middot; the /t/ flaps into the vowel of *of*: [ˈʃɔɹɾ əv]
**Русский:** разве что; кроме как — крайняя мера, без которой исход не изменить
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase or a gerund) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase or an *-ing* phrase naming the extreme measure that would change the outcome
**Pattern:** *Short of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *short of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Short of* [gerund], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *short of* [gerund].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *short of* phrase, which is where it usually appears; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *nothing short of* (an intensifier, not a condition: *nothing short of a miracle* means "an outright miracle"), *barring*, *absent*

Names the extreme step that would be needed to change the outcome and implies that nothing less will do. It overlaps with *barring*, but the two run in opposite directions: *barring a strike, the trains run on time* names the one thing that could spoil a good outcome, while *short of a strike, nothing gets management's attention* names the one thing that could rescue a bad one. Outside this conditional use, *short of* is an ordinary adjective phrase meaning "not enough of" (*short of cash*, *short of breath*), and what follows tells the two apart: the conditional names an action or an event, the adjective names a supply.

1. *Short of* a miracle, this bill dies in committee.
2. *Short of* selling the house, I don't see how they cover the bills.
3. There's nothing more I can do *short of* calling her myself.
4. *Short of* an act of Congress, that rule is not changing.
5. They tried everything *short of* replacing the engine.
6. *Short of* rewriting the whole chapter, I'm not sure how to fix it.

**See also:** [barring](#barring), [absent](#absent), [but for](#but-for), [unless](#unless)

## Suppositions and granted premises

Conditions the speaker puts on the table to reason from — offered, conceded, or floated as a question rather than genuinely tested.

### all else being equal

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈɛls ˌbiɪŋ ˈikwəl/ &middot; *awl-ELSS-bee-ing-EE-kwuhl* &middot; /ɑl/ for speakers with the cot–caught merger; *being* routinely collapses to one syllable in speech, [biŋ]
**Русский:** при прочих равных (условиях)
**Type:** subordinating (absolute construction — a subject plus a participle, with no conjunction present) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal (economics, science writing, argument)
**Clause type:** an absolute phrase doing the work of an adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** *All else being equal*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *all else being equal*. &middot; [subject], *all else being equal*, [verb phrase]. &middot; *Other things being equal*, [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens the sentence, which is where it usually sits; comma before it when it trails; commas on both sides in the middle. It is a phrase, not a conjunction, so it cannot join two independent clauses on its own — a comma splice here is the same error it is with *otherwise*.
**Variants:** *all other things being equal*, *other things being equal*, *everything else being equal*, *all things being equal* (very common in speech, though it says more than the speaker means), *ceteris paribus* (the Latin)

Freezes every factor except the one under discussion, so that a comparison can be made about that factor alone. It is the working-English form of *ceteris paribus*, and it hedges as much as it conditions: the speaker is conceding that in the world outside the sentence other things are rarely equal.

1. *All else being equal*, the cheaper flight wins.
2. A heavier bat, *all else being equal*, produces a slower swing.
3. *Other things being equal*, students who read more write better.
4. *All else being equal*, I'd rather hire someone local.
5. Raising the price lowers demand, *all else being equal*.
6. *All other things being equal*, the shorter route should be faster — but it runs straight through downtown.

**See also:** [ceteris paribus](#ceteris-paribus), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [given that](#given-that), [ex hypothesi](#ex-hypothesi), [if](#if)

### assuming that

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsumɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *uh-SOO-ming-thuht* &middot; American /əˈsumɪŋ/ drops the yod, British /əˈsjuːmɪŋ/ keeps it; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** если предположить, что; допустим, что — посылка принята условно
**Type:** subordinating (participial) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition (a premise taken for granted)
**Pattern:** *Assuming (that)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *assuming (that)* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. When it follows, a comma is usual, because the clause reads as a hedge added after the fact.
**Variants:** *assuming*, *presuming that*, *on the assumption that* (formal)

Introduces a premise the speaker is provisionally treating as true in order to reason from it. Unlike *if*, it signals that the premise is unverified and worth flagging; unlike *given that*, it makes no claim that the premise is established.

1. *Assuming* the numbers hold, we break even in the third quarter.
2. We'll be there by seven, *assuming* traffic isn't awful.
3. *Assuming that* she accepts, when would she start?
4. The plan works, *assuming* nobody quits between now and June.
5. *Assuming* no one objects, I'll go ahead and order the equipment.
6. He can finish by Friday, *assuming that* the parts arrive Monday.

**See also:** [given that](#given-that), [granted that](#granted-that), [supposing that](#supposing-that), [provided that](#provided-that), [if](#if)

### given that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪvən ðət/ &middot; *GIV-uhn-thuht* &middot; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** учитывая, что; раз уж — посылка признана данной, значение близко к причине
**Type:** subordinating (participial — frozen from the past participle of *give*); also a preposition before a bare noun phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition shading into cause
**Pattern:** *Given (that)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *given (that)* [clause]. &middot; *given* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; a comma is usual when it follows, too, since it reads as a supplied premise.
**Variants:** *given* + noun phrase (*given the circumstances*), *given the fact that* (wordy)

Sets out a premise as already established and reasons forward from it — halfway between *if* and *because*. Where *assuming that* flags the premise as unproven, *given that* presents it as agreed, which is why it drifts toward cause: *Given that the road is closed, we'll take the long way* is barely different from *because*.

1. *Given that* the road is closed, we'll take the long way.
2. The turnout was impressive, *given that* it poured all morning.
3. *Given* the circumstances, I think she handled it well.
4. *Given that* nobody has objected in six weeks, I'll assume the plan is approved.
5. It's a fair price, *given* what the market has been doing.
6. *Given that* you've already paid, the balance is zero.

**See also:** [assuming that](#assuming-that), [granted that](#granted-that), [because](04-cause.md#because), [since](04-cause.md#since)

### granted that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd ðət/ &middot; *GRAN-tid-thuht* &middot; casual American speech drops the /t/ of *granted*: [ˈɡrænəd]; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** допустим, что; пусть даже — уступка, за которой следует возражение
**Type:** subordinating (participial — frozen from the past participle of *grant*); bare *granted* is a conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** *Granted (that)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *granted that* [clause]. &middot; *Granting that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Granted*, [sentence].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. When the clause trails the main clause it takes a comma before it, because it reads as a concession added after the fact. Standing alone as a concession, *Granted* takes a comma after it and usually opens the sentence.
**Variants:** *granted*, *granting that*, *admittedly* (adverbial equivalent)

Concedes a point for the sake of argument and then pushes past it: even accepting that the clause is true, the main clause still stands. Where *given that* accepts a premise and reasons from it, *granted that* accepts one and reasons against it.

1. *Granted that* the design is elegant, it's still far too expensive to build.
2. *Granted*, the first season is slow, but it pays off.
3. *Granting that* the witness is credible, the timeline still doesn't work.
4. *Granted that* she had every reason to be angry, the email was a mistake.
5. He's the best closer we've had in years, *granted that* the bar was pretty low.
6. *Granted that* the numbers are only estimates, they all point the same direction.

**See also:** [given that](#given-that), [even if](#even-if), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [although](07-concession.md#although), [even though](07-concession.md#even-though)

### how about if

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ əˈbaʊt ɪf/ &middot; *how-uh-BOWT-if* &middot; in fast speech *about* loses its first vowel and often its /t/ as well: *how 'bout if*, [haʊˈbaʊʔ ɪf]
**Русский:** а что, если; давай…? — разг. предложение в форме вопроса
**Type:** subordinating (fixed phrase heading a conditional proposal) &middot; **Register:** informal, spoken
**Clause type:** a conditional clause standing alone as a question
**Pattern:** *How about if* [clause]? &middot; *What about if* [clause]? &middot; *How about* [gerund]? &middot; *How about* [noun phrase]?
**Punctuation:** Ends with a question mark even though nothing is inverted and no main clause follows. The fragment is standard in speech and in writing that reports speech, not an error.
**Variants:** *what about if* (identical in meaning), *how 'bout if* (spelled this way in dialogue), *how about* and *what about* + noun phrase or gerund, *what if* (shorter and more neutral)

Floats a suggestion as a conditional question — "suppose we did this; would that work?" It differs from *what if*, which is as often a worry as a proposal (*What if it rains?*): *how about if* is nearly always an offer, and it expects an answer rather than a shudder.

1. *How about if* we meet at six instead?
2. *What about if* I drive and you navigate?
3. *How about if* she takes the early shift this week?
4. "The nine o'clock is full." "*How about if* we fly out the night before?"
5. *What about if* we split it three ways?
6. *How about if* I call you back after the meeting?

**See also:** [what if](#what-if), [say](#say), [supposing that](#supposing-that), [if](#if)

### say

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ/ &middot; *SAY*
**Русский:** допустим; скажем — разг. ввод гипотезы; во вставке — «скажем, в четверг»
**Type:** subordinating (from the imperative of the verb *say*) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition, usually with no *that*
**Pattern:** *Say* [clause] — [question or consequence]? &middot; *Say* [clause]. [question]? &middot; *Let's say* [clause]; [main clause]. &middot; [phrase], *say*, [example].
**Punctuation:** The hypothetical clause is followed by a dash, a comma, or a period before the consequence. The separate "for example" *say* is fenced by commas on both sides: *in a few days — say, Thursday*.
**Variants:** *let's say*, *say that*, *supposing*

The conversational way to launch a hypothetical, meaning "suppose" and belonging to speech and casual writing where *supposing that* would sound stiff. It nearly always sets up a question or a thought experiment rather than a rule.

1. *Say* you miss the connection — what's your backup?
2. *Say* we leave at five. Do we still hit rush hour?
3. *Let's say* the bid comes in over budget; then what?
4. *Say* somebody offered you the job tomorrow. Would you take it?
5. Give it a few days — *say*, until Thursday — and call them again.

**See also:** [supposing that](#supposing-that), [what if](#what-if), [if](#if), [assuming that](#assuming-that)

### supposing that

**Pronunciation:** /səˈpoʊzɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *suh-POH-zing-thuht* &middot; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** предположим, что; а если — приглашение представить гипотезу, обычно в вопросе
**Type:** subordinating (participial) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of hypothetical condition
**Pattern:** *Supposing (that)* [clause], [main clause or question]? &middot; *Suppose* [clause], [main clause or question]?
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause — often a dash or a question mark instead, since the construction usually sets up a question.
**Variants:** *supposing*, *suppose*, *suppose that*, *supposin'* (eye dialect)

Invites the listener to entertain a hypothetical, most often as the opening of a question. Unlike *if*, it is essentially clause-initial: *We'd be stuck supposing the ferry stopped running* does not work.

1. *Supposing* the ferry stops running, how do we get back?
2. *Suppose* you had a week off — where would you go?
3. *Supposing that* the tests come back negative, what's the next step?
4. *Suppose* we split the difference and call it four hundred.
5. *Supposing* nobody shows up, do we still pay for the room?

**See also:** [say](#say), [what if](#what-if), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [if](#if)

### what if

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt ˈɪf/ &middot; *wut-IF* &middot; the /t/ flaps between vowels in fast speech: [wʌɾ ˈɪf]
**Русский:** а что, если — гипотеза как предложение или как опасение
**Type:** subordinating (fixed phrase heading a conditional fragment) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** a conditional clause standing alone as a question
**Pattern:** *What if* [clause]? &middot; *What if* [clause] — [consequence]? &middot; [verb of asking] *what if* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Ends with a question mark even though there is no main clause and no inversion. The fragment is standard, not an error.
**Variants:** *so what if* (dismissive), *what if I told you* (rhetorical opener), *and what if*

Raises a hypothetical as a proposal or a worry, leaving the consequence for the listener to supply. Tense does the work: *What if we leave early?* proposes something real, while *What if we left early?* holds it further away.

1. *What if* it rains on Saturday?
2. *What if* we just told them the truth?
3. *What if* the flight gets canceled — do we still have the hotel?
4. She kept asking *what if* nobody came.
5. *So what if* he's ten minutes late? The doors don't open until eight.
6. *What if* I said I could get you a table at seven?

**See also:** [say](#say), [supposing that](#supposing-that), [if only](#if-only), [if](#if)

## Alternative conditions

The *whether* family, which covers both branches of a yes/no split instead of picking one.

### depending on

**Pronunciation:** /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ ɑn/ &middot; *dih-PEN-ding-on* &middot; the *-ing* is regularly [ɪn] in casual speech, *dih-PEN-din-on*; *depending upon* is /dɪˈpɛndɪŋ əˌpɑn/, *dih-PEN-ding-uh-pon*
**Русский:** в зависимости от — исход меняется вместе с условием, а не просто включается
**Type:** subordinating (participial — takes a noun phrase, or a full clause after *whether*, *how*, *what*, *where*, *who*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause after the bare form — a noun phrase; a full clause after *depending on whether* and the other *wh-* words
**Pattern:** [main clause] *depending on* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *depending on* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *depending on whether* [clause]. &middot; *Depending on whether* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *depending on how / what / where / who* [clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma before it is usual when the phrase is an afterthought qualifying the whole main clause (*We'll leave Friday or Saturday, depending on the weather*) and none when it is part of the core statement (*The fee changes depending on the plan*). Comma after a fronted clause.
**Variants:** *depending upon* (a shade more formal), *depending on whether* (the clause-taking form), *according as* (the archaic equivalent), *contingent on* (which waits on one event rather than varying with it)

Says the outcome varies with the condition instead of simply switching on or off — the everyday American way to write *according as*. Against *contingent on*, which makes one outcome wait on one event, *depending on* keeps both branches live: *Contingent on the vote, we expand* means the expansion waits, while *We expand or we don't, depending on the vote* means either answer is a real answer.

1. We'll drive or fly, *depending on* the weather.
2. The fee changes *depending on whether* you file jointly.
3. *Depending on whether* the grant comes through, the study runs one year or three.
4. She's either brilliant or impossible, *depending on* who you ask.
5. Delivery takes two to five days, *depending on* where you live.
6. The room seats forty or sixty, *depending on how* you set up the chairs.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [contingent on](#contingent-on), [if](#if), [according as](12-formal-archaic.md#according-as)

### regardless of whether

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈɡɑrdləs əv ˈwɛðər/ &middot; *rih-GARD-luhss-uv-WETH-ur*
**Русский:** независимо от того, … ли — главное верно при обоих исходах
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional phrase plus complementizer) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition
**Pattern:** [main clause] *regardless of whether* [clause]. &middot; *Regardless of whether* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *regardless of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows. Note that *irregardless* is nonstandard and should never appear in writing.
**Variants:** *irrespective of whether* (formal), *regardless of* + noun phrase, *no matter whether*

Says the main clause holds on both branches of a yes/no split. *Even if* grants one branch and dismisses it; *regardless of whether* covers both explicitly, which is why contracts and policies prefer it.

1. The fee is due *regardless of whether* you use the service.
2. *Regardless of whether* the funding comes through, the study goes forward.
3. She'd have said the same thing *regardless of whether* anyone was listening.
4. *Regardless of* the outcome, the audit findings stand.
5. Practice starts at six *regardless of whether* it's raining.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [either way](#either-way), [even if](#even-if), [no matter what](07-concession.md#no-matter-what), [although](07-concession.md#although)

### whether

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər/ &middot; *WETH-ur* &middot; identical to *weather* for most Americans; some speakers keep a breathy /ˈʍɛðər/
**Русский:** ли — вводит косвенный вопрос; whether … or — «… ли … или», альтернативное условие
**Type:** complementizer; subordinating in the *whether … or* condition &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect yes/no question); adverbial clause of alternative condition
**Pattern:** [verb, noun, or adjective] *whether* [clause]. &middot; [preposition] *whether* [clause]. &middot; *Whether* [clause] [verb phrase] (the noun clause as subject). &middot; *Whether* [clause] *or* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Whether* [clause] *or not*, [main clause]. &middot; *whether to* [infinitive].
**Punctuation:** No comma before a *whether* noun clause. When a *whether … or* pair opens the sentence as a condition, put a comma after it: *Whether you like it or not, the rule stands.*
**Variants:** *whether or not*, *whether … or*, *whether or no* (archaic, literary)

Frames a yes/no alternative rather than a single case. Against conditional *if*, it is the form that survives in four places *if* cannot go — after a preposition (*depends on whether*), before an infinitive (*whether to go*), as a subject (*Whether he comes is up to him*), and directly before *or not*. It also removes the ambiguity in *Let me know if he calls*, which can mean either "report either way" or "only in the event that he does."

1. I can't tell *whether* she's joking.
2. It depends on *whether* the landlord approves the sublet.
3. *Whether* we drive or fly, we'll need to leave by noon.
4. She hasn't decided *whether* to accept the offer.
5. *Whether* he meant it or not, the comment landed badly.
6. Let me know *whether* the check cleared.
7. *Whether* the bond passes comes down to turnout in the suburbs.

**See also:** [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [if](#if), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [even if](#even-if)

### whether or not

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər ər ˈnɑt/ &middot; *WETH-ur-ur-NOT*
**Русский:** независимо от того, … или нет; ли … или нет — исход один при обоих вариантах
**Type:** subordinating; also a complementizer in noun clauses &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition; also a noun clause
**Pattern:** *Whether or not* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whether or not* [clause]. &middot; [verb, noun, or adjective] *whether or not* [clause] (the noun clause). &middot; [main clause] *whether* [clause] *or not* (the split form).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows, and none before a noun clause.
**Variants:** the split form *whether … or not*, with *or not* moved to the end of the clause

Locks in "in both cases": the outcome is the same on either branch. In the noun-clause use, *or not* is merely emphatic and can be dropped (*I don't know whether (or not) he's coming*); in the adverbial use it cannot, because dropping it changes the sentence.

1. *Whether or not* you agree with the policy, you have to follow it.
2. The train leaves at six *whether or not* everyone is on board.
3. *Whether or not* it rains, the ceremony is outdoors.
4. I still don't know *whether or not* she got the message.
5. He's going to say something *whether* we ask him to *or not*.

**See also:** [whether](#whether), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [either way](#either-way), [even if](#even-if), [if](#if)

## Counterfactuals

Conditions known to be false, or — with *without* — simply not met. Four of the six take a noun phrase rather than a clause, and wherever the condition is genuinely counterfactual the main clause carries *would* or *would have*. *If only* and *were it not that* take a clause instead, and *if only* is the odd one out on a second count as well: it can stand alone as a bare wish with no main clause to govern.

### but for

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbʌt ˈfɔr/ &middot; *but-FOR* &middot; reduced /bət ˈfɔr/, with the /t/ unreleased before the /f/ — it does not flap here, because no vowel follows it
**Русский:** если бы не — книжн.-юр., только перед существительным
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal, literary)
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming the decisive factor
**Pattern:** *But for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would* or *would have*]. &middot; [main clause] *but for* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted *but for* phrase; no comma when it follows.
**Variants:** *if not for*, *if it weren't for*, *were it not for*, *had it not been for*

Names the one factor without which the main clause would have gone the other way, so the main clause must carry *would* or *would have*. This *but* is not the coordinating conjunction — it is the older *but* meaning "except," and the construction is the "but-for test" of causation in American law.

1. *But for* the storm, the ceremony would have been outdoors.
2. *But for* your help, I'd still be sitting in that parking lot.
3. The company would have folded in 2009 *but for* an emergency loan.
4. *But for* a clerical error, the deed would have been recorded in June.
5. She would never have applied *but for* a professor's encouragement.

**See also:** [if not for](#if-not-for), [without](#without), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [absent](#absent), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### if not for

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈnɑt fɔr/ &middot; *if-NOT-for*
**Русский:** если бы не — обиходный эквивалент but for
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming what made the difference
**Pattern:** *If not for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would*]. &middot; [main clause with *would*] *if not for* [noun phrase]. &middot; *If it weren't for* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *If it hadn't been for* [noun phrase], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, which is where it almost always sits; no comma when it trails the main clause.
**Variants:** *if it weren't for*, *if it wasn't for* (informal), *if it hadn't been for*, *were it not for* (formal)

The everyday American counterfactual — plain English for *but for*. The longer clausal forms pin down the time: *if it weren't for* points at a present state, *if it hadn't been for* at a past event.

1. *If not for* the traffic, we would have been on time.
2. *If it weren't for* the noise, this would be a perfect apartment.
3. *If it hadn't been for* her, nobody would have caught the mistake.
4. The season would have been a disaster *if not for* two late wins.
5. *If it weren't for* bad luck, he'd have no luck at all.

**See also:** [but for](#but-for), [without](#without), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [if only](#if-only), [if](#if)

### if only

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈoʊnli/ &middot; *if-OHN-lee*
**Русский:** если бы только; вот бы — сожаление или желание о несбывшемся
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition; also an exclamative wish standing alone
**Pattern:** *If only* [clause with past or past perfect]! &middot; *If only* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *if only to* [infinitive].
**Punctuation:** Period or exclamation point on the standalone wish; comma after the clause when a main clause follows. A comma before *if only to* when it trails the main clause.
**Variants:** *if only to* + infinitive (a different sense: "even if for no other reason than")

Expresses a wish that the condition were met, with the strong implication that it isn't — the verb shifts back one tense, so *if only I had* means I don't and *if only I had had* means I didn't. Unlike plain *if*, it needs no main clause: the regret is the whole sentence.

1. *If only* I had known you were in town.
2. *If only* she'd asked, I would have driven her.
3. *If only* it were that simple.
4. *If only* the rent were two hundred dollars cheaper, I'd take the place.
5. Call him back, *if only* to end the argument.
6. *If only* we'd left an hour earlier.

**See also:** [if](#if), [were](#were), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [what if](#what-if)

### were it not for

**Pronunciation:** /wər ɪt ˈnɑt fɔr/ &middot; *wur-it-NOT-for* &middot; the /t/ of *it* is normally a glottal stop before the /n/ of *not*, not a flap: [wɚ ɪʔ ˈnɑt fɚ]
**Русский:** если бы не — книжн., инверсия were вместо if, перед существительным
**Type:** subordinating (inverted subjunctive with a prepositional complement) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** no clause after *for* — a noun phrase; the condition is carried by the inverted *were*
**Pattern:** *Were it not for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would*]. &middot; [main clause with *would*] *were it not for* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Had it not been for* [noun phrase], [main clause with *would have*].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, which is where it almost always sits; no comma when it trails the main clause. No *if* — the inversion replaces it, and writing *if were it not for* is an error.
**Variants:** *had it not been for*, *if it were not for*, *were it not that* + clause

The formal, inverted counterfactual: the same content as *if it weren't for*, lifted in register by moving *were* in front of the subject. *Were it not for* takes a present or general state, *had it not been for* a past event.

1. *Were it not for* the volunteers, the shelter would have closed years ago.
2. *Had it not been for* a delayed flight, they never would have met.
3. *Were it not for* her testimony, the case would have collapsed.
4. The town would be half its size *were it not for* the university.
5. *Had it not been for* the seatbelt, the injuries would have been far worse.

**See also:** [but for](#but-for), [if not for](#if-not-for), [without](#without), [were](#were), [had](#had)

### were it not that

**Pronunciation:** /wər ɪt ˈnɑt ðət/ &middot; *wur-it-NOT-thuht* &middot; the /t/ of *it* is a glottal stop before the /n/ of *not*, not a flap: [wɚ ɪʔ ˈnɑt ðət]; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** если бы не то, что — книжн.; вводит целое придаточное, а не существительное
**Type:** subordinating (inverted past subjunctive plus a complementizer) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of counterfactual condition — a full clause, not a noun phrase
**Pattern:** *Were it not that* [clause], [main clause with *would*]. &middot; [main clause with *would*] *were it not that* [clause]. &middot; *Had it not been that* [clause], [main clause with *would have*].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, which is where it almost always sits; no comma when it trails the main clause. No *if* — the inversion of *were* replaces it, and writing *if were it not that* is an error.
**Variants:** *if it were not that*, *had it not been that* (past), *were it not for* + noun phrase (the commoner form), *but that* (the archaic equivalent)

The clause-taking twin of *were it not for*. Where *were it not for* names a thing (*were it not for the rain*), this one states a whole proposition (*were it not that the rain had washed out the road*), so it is the form to reach for when the decisive fact will not compress into a noun.

1. *Were it not that* the roads were closed, we would have driven up that night.
2. The bill would have passed, *were it not that* two members were absent.
3. *Were it not that* she had kept every receipt, the audit would have gone badly.
4. I'd take the job tomorrow *were it not that* it means moving.
5. *Had it not been that* the alarm failed, the fire would have been caught in minutes.
6. The manuscript would be lost *were it not that* a copy went to the printer in March.

**See also:** [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [but for](#but-for), [if not for](#if-not-for), [were](#were), [but that](12-formal-archaic.md#but-that)

### without

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðaʊt/ &middot; *with-OWT* &middot; stress on the second syllable; many Americans say /wɪˈθaʊt/ with the consonant of *thin* instead, and both are standard; reduced /wəˈðaʊt/ in fast speech
**Русский:** без — «если бы не», условие через отсутствие чего-либо
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase or a gerund); also the ordinary preposition of lacking (*coffee without sugar*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase or an *-ing* phrase naming what is missing
**Pattern:** *Without* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *without* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Without* [gerund], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *without* [gerund].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *without* phrase; no comma when it follows. No comma ever separates *without* from the noun phrase it governs.
**Variants:** *but for*, *absent*, *if not for* (the same job in higher registers); *without which* + clause (formal, relative)

The plain-English member of the *but for* family, and the one word in it that nobody would call stiff. It names what is missing and lets the main clause say what follows. When the missing thing really is absent, the sentence is counterfactual and the main clause takes *would* (*Without her, we'd have folded*); when it is only hypothetical, the main clause stays in the present (*Without a permit, you can't build*). The conditional reading appears only when *without* governs the whole situation — in *He left without saying goodbye* it is the everyday preposition of lacking, modifying the verb, and no condition is being stated at all.

1. *Without* the volunteers, the food bank would have closed in March.
2. You can't board *without* a valid ID.
3. *Without* a second income, they never would have kept the house.
4. The whole argument falls apart *without* that one footnote.
5. *Without* checking the tape, the referee would have missed the call.
6. She'd still be waiting on a diagnosis *without* her sister's pushing.

**See also:** [but for](#but-for), [if not for](#if-not-for), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [absent](#absent), [short of](#short-of)

## Conditionals without a conjunction: inversion

English can mark a condition by moving an auxiliary in front of the subject, with no conjunction at all. Only three auxiliaries do this in modern American English, and the fronted clause never takes *if*.

### had

**Pronunciation:** /hæd/ &middot; *HAD* &middot; reduced /(h)əd/; the inverted conditional keeps the full form
**Русский:** инверсия вместо if в нереальном условии прошлого: Had I known — «если бы я знал»
**Type:** subordinating by inversion — no conjunction present; the word is otherwise the auxiliary and main verb *have* &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal in writing; uncommon in casual speech
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of past counterfactual condition
**Pattern:** *Had* [subject] [past participle], [subject] *would have* [past participle]. &middot; *Had* [subject] *not* [past participle], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause with *would have*] *had* [subject] [past participle]. &middot; uninverted: *If* [subject] *had* [past participle], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma between the inverted clause and the main clause when the inverted clause comes first, which is the usual order; no comma when it trails — *We'd be there by now had we left ten minutes earlier.* Never add *if* — write *If I had known* or *Had I known*, never both.
**Variants:** the uninverted *if … had*; the fixed phrase *had it not been for*

Replaces *if* in past counterfactuals by moving the auxiliary *had* in front of the subject. The meaning is identical to *if I had known*, but the inverted form is more formal and more emphatic. Like the *should* and *were* inversions, it usually opens the sentence but can just as well follow the main clause. The negative cannot contract: *Had I not known*, never *Hadn't I known*, which is a question.

1. *Had* I known you were coming, I would have made more food.
2. *Had* the company disclosed the defect, the recall would have been far smaller.
3. *Had* she not spoken up, the error would have gone into print.
4. *Had* we left ten minutes earlier, we'd be there by now.
5. *Had* the vote gone the other way, the whole department would have been reorganized.
6. I'd have driven you myself *had* I known your car was in the shop.

**See also:** [should](#should), [were](#were), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [if](#if)

### should

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʊd/ &middot; *SHUUD* &middot; reduced /ʃəd/; the inverted conditional keeps the full form
**Русский:** инверсия вместо if: «если вдруг», «случись так, что» — значения долженствования здесь нет
**Type:** subordinating by inversion — no conjunction present; the word is otherwise a modal auxiliary of obligation or expectation &middot; **Register:** formal (business, customer service, legal)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of open future condition
**Pattern:** *Should* [subject] [bare infinitive], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *should* [subject] [bare infinitive]. &middot; uninverted: *If* [subject] *should* [bare infinitive], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma between the fronted inverted clause and the main clause; no comma when the inverted clause follows. Never combine the inversion with *if*.
**Variants:** *if … should*, *in the event that* (same job, no inversion), *should it happen that*

Marks a future condition as possible but not expected — the tentative conditional. It carries none of the obligation sense *should* has elsewhere: *Should you need anything* means "if you happen to need anything," not "you ought to need anything."

1. *Should* you need anything, the front desk is open all night.
2. *Should* the shipment arrive damaged, photograph it before signing.
3. *Should* any of this change, I'll email everyone.
4. *Should* the tenant fail to pay, the landlord may terminate the lease.
5. Call this number *should* your symptoms return.

**See also:** [had](#had), [were](#were), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [if](#if)

### were

**Pronunciation:** /wɜr/ &middot; *WUR* &middot; unstressed /wər/
**Русский:** инверсия сослагательного were вместо if: Were I you — «будь я на вашем месте»
**Type:** subordinating by inversion — no conjunction present; the past subjunctive of *be*, which is otherwise just the plural and second-person past tense &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of present or future counterfactual condition
**Pattern:** *Were* [subject] [complement], [main clause with *would*]. &middot; *Were* [subject] *to* [infinitive], [main clause with *would*]. &middot; [main clause with *would*] *were* [subject] [complement]. &middot; uninverted: *If* [subject] *were* [complement], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma between the inverted clause and the main clause when the inverted clause comes first, which is the usual order; no comma when it trails — *We'd start an hour later were it up to me.* No *if* with the inversion, and no contraction: *Were it not*, never *Weren't it*.
**Variants:** the uninverted subjunctive *if I were*; *were it not for*; *if I was* (informal speech, still flagged in edited prose)

The past subjunctive *were* used for a hypothetical present or future, inverted in place of *if*. *Were I you* is the formal twin of *if I were you*, and *were … to* pushes the condition further into the unlikely future. The inverted clause normally comes first, but it can follow the main clause exactly as the *had* and *should* inversions do.

1. *Were* I in your position, I'd ask for the raise in writing.
2. *Were* the bridge to close, the detour would add an hour.
3. *Were* she any less patient, that meeting would have ended in a fight.
4. *Were* it up to me, we'd start an hour later.
5. *Were* the company to relocate, most of the staff would not follow.
6. I'd say something to him myself *were* I not his sister.

**See also:** [had](#had), [should](#should), [were it not for](#were-it-not-for), [if only](#if-only)

## Pro-forms and follow-on connectives

Words that stand in for a condition already stated rather than spelling out a new one. The punctuation here follows the part of speech every time, and the parts of speech differ: *either way*, *in either case*, *failing that*, *in that case*, and *otherwise* are conjunctive adverbs and need a semicolon or a period in front of them; *or else* is a coordinator and takes a plain comma; *in which case* heads a relative clause and takes a comma; *if not* and *if so* are reduced conditional clauses that need a period, a semicolon, or a dash before them; and the scalar tag *if that* rides at the end of its own clause behind a comma, joining nothing at all.

### either way

**Pronunciation:** /ˈiðər ˌweɪ/ &middot; *EE-thur-way* &middot; the stress is on *either*; /ˈaɪðər/ is the minority American pronunciation and is not an error
**Русский:** в любом случае; так или иначе — из двух уже названных вариантов
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a pro-form standing in for a *whether* alternative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — it points back at a two-branch condition already stated
**Pattern:** [sentence]. *Either way*, [sentence]. &middot; [sentence]; *either way*, [sentence]. &middot; [clause], *but either way* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *either way* (final, no comma).
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it, comma after it when it opens a clause — a comma alone in front of it is a comma splice, exactly as with *otherwise* and *in that case*. A coordinating conjunction may carry it instead, in which case the comma goes before the coordinator: *You can pay now or at pickup, but either way the card has to match.* Riding at the end of its own clause it takes no punctuation at all: *The fee is the same either way.*
**Variants:** *one way or the other*, *whichever way it goes*, *in any case* and *regardless* (which also cover more than two branches)

Answers a yes/no split already on the table by saying the outcome is the same on both branches — the pro-form counterpart of *whether or not* and *regardless of whether*, which spell the branches out instead of pointing back at them. It is strictly two-way: with three or more options open, *in any case* or *regardless* is the word.

1. We can drive or fly; *either way*, we need to leave by noon.
2. He may take the offer or he may not. *Either way*, the position gets filled.
3. The fee is the same *either way*.
4. She might apologize, or she might dig in — *either way*, I'm done arguing about it.
5. You can pay now or at pickup, but *either way* the card has to match the ID.
6. *Either way*, somebody has to tell the landlord before Friday.

**See also:** [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [in that case](#in-that-case), [otherwise](#otherwise), [failing that](#failing-that)

### failing that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfeɪlɪŋ ðæt/ &middot; *FAY-ling-that* &middot; this *that* is the demonstrative pronoun, so it keeps its full /ðæt/ — unlike the complementizer *that* of *provided that* and *given that*, which reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** если не выйдет; в противном случае — вводит запасной вариант
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (participial phrase used as a connective) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** no clause — it stands in for the whole condition just stated
**Pattern:** [sentence]. *Failing that*, [sentence]. &middot; [sentence]; *failing that*, [sentence]. &middot; [main clause] or, *failing that*, [alternative]. &middot; *Failing* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *failing which* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after *failing that* when it opens a sentence, and commas on both sides when it is parenthetical. It cannot join two independent clauses with a comma alone — use a period or a semicolon before it. *Failing which* is the exception in this entry: like *in which case* it heads a relative clause, so it takes an ordinary comma before it and no semicolon.
**Variants:** *failing which* (formal, legal), *failing* + noun phrase (*failing agreement*)

Means "if that doesn't work": it points back at the option just named and introduces the fallback. It belongs to writing and planned speech — conversation uses *if not* or *otherwise*.

1. Try the manager first; *failing that*, put your complaint in writing.
2. We'll fly out Thursday or, *failing that*, first thing Friday.
3. The parties will negotiate in good faith, *failing which* the dispute goes to arbitration.
4. Ask a librarian first and, *failing that*, try the county archive.
5. *Failing* a quorum, the vote is postponed to the next meeting.
6. Reset the router. *Failing that*, call the number on the back of the modem.

**See also:** [if not](#if-not), [otherwise](#otherwise), [or else](#or-else), [in which case](#in-which-case), [in that case](#in-that-case)

### if not

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈnɑt/ &middot; *if-NOT*
**Русский:** если нет; в противном случае; а также скалярное «если не»: hundreds, if not thousands
**Type:** subordinating (elliptical clause) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** a reduced conditional clause — the verb is recovered from the previous sentence
**Pattern:** [statement or question]. *If not*, [main clause]. &middot; [statement or question] — *if not*, [main clause]. &middot; [statement]; *if not*, [main clause]. &middot; [adjective or number], *if not* [stronger adjective or number].
**Punctuation:** Comma after *If not* when it opens a sentence, and a period, semicolon, or dash in front of it — a bare comma between the two clauses is a splice. In the scalar pattern the comma goes before *if not* and none after.
**Variants:** *if not, then*; *if that's not the case*; the positive counterpart *if so*

Stands in for a whole negative condition already on the table: "if that is not the case." Keep it apart from the scalar *if not*, which raises the bar instead of negating — *hundreds, if not thousands* means possibly even thousands, not "otherwise thousands."

1. Let me know by Friday. *If not*, I'll give the spot to somebody else.
2. Is the room free at two? *If not*, we can meet in the lobby.
3. Try the pharmacy on Third — *if not*, there's one by the hospital.
4. The repair will cost hundreds, *if not* thousands.
5. He's the best pitcher in the division, *if not* the league.

**See also:** [if so](#if-so), [otherwise](#otherwise), [or else](#or-else), [unless](#unless), [failing that](#failing-that)

### if so

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *if-SOH*
**Русский:** если так; если да — подхватывает только что высказанную возможность
**Type:** subordinating (elliptical clause) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** a reduced conditional clause standing in for a positive condition
**Pattern:** [statement or question]. *If so*, [main clause]. &middot; [statement]; *if so*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *if so*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after *If so* when it opens a sentence. Like *if not*, it takes a period, a semicolon, or a dash in front of it, never a bare comma.
**Variants:** *if that's the case*, *if that's so*, *if that is true*; the negative counterpart *if not*

Picks up a possibility just raised and says what follows from it, without repeating the clause. It is the compact written form of *if that is the case*, and it does most of its work in email, forms, and instructions.

1. Have you already filed? *If so*, ignore this notice.
2. The lease may renew automatically. *If so*, you have thirty days to opt out.
3. Are you driving? *If so*, I'll ride with you.
4. She may have left already; *if so*, we'll catch her tomorrow.
5. Check whether the charge posted twice, and dispute it *if so*.
6. Did the tracking number ever update? *If so*, forward me the email.

**See also:** [if not](#if-not), [in that case](#in-that-case), [in which case](#in-which-case), [if](#if)

### if that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈðæt/ &middot; *if-THAT* &middot; this *that* is the stressed demonstrative and keeps its full vowel, unlike the complementizer *that* of *provided that*, which reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** и то вряд ли; от силы — оценка сверху, ставится после числа
**Type:** subordinating (elliptical clause used as a scalar tag) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral, chiefly spoken
**Clause type:** a reduced conditional clause — "if it is even that much"
**Pattern:** [quantity or measure], *if that*. &middot; [main clause] [quantity], *if that*. &middot; [measure], *if that*, [continuation].
**Punctuation:** A comma before it and nothing after it, because it closes the clause. It joins nothing and cannot stand between two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *if even that*, *at most*, *if I'm being generous*; the upward counterpart is scalar *if not* (*hundreds, if not thousands*)

Tacked onto a number or a measure to say the figure is a ceiling and the truth may well be less. It is the mirror image of scalar *if not*, which pushes the estimate up: *hundreds, if not thousands* means possibly more, while *a hundred, if that* means probably fewer.

1. The whole meeting took ten minutes, *if that*.
2. There were forty people in the room, *if that*.
3. He's twenty-five, *if that*.
4. We got two inches of snow, *if that*.
5. The repair will run you fifty dollars, *if that*.
6. She's been here a month, *if that*, and she already runs the place.

**See also:** [if not](#if-not), [if so](#if-so), [if](#if), [if only](#if-only)

### in either case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈiðər keɪs/ &middot; *in-EE-thur-kayss* &middot; /ˈaɪðər/, *EYE-thur*, is the minority American pronunciation and is not an error
**Русский:** в обоих случаях; и в том, и в другом случае — письменный аналог either way
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a pro-form standing in for a two-branch alternative) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; writing more than speech
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — it points back at two possibilities already named
**Pattern:** [sentence]. *In either case*, [sentence]. &middot; [sentence]; *in either case*, [sentence]. &middot; [clause], and *in either case* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in either case* (final, no comma).
**Punctuation:** **Semicolon or period before it, comma after it** when it opens a clause — a comma alone in front of it is a comma splice, exactly as with *otherwise* and *in that case*. A coordinating conjunction may carry the join instead, and then the comma goes before the coordinator. Riding at the end of its own clause it takes no punctuation at all: *The answer is the same in either case.*
**Variants:** *in either event* (a shade more formal), *either way* (the conversational form), *in both cases* (which reports rather than conditions), *in any case* (which covers more than two branches)

Says the consequence is the same on both branches of an alternative that has just been spelled out. It is the written counterpart of *either way* — the same job, but at home in reports and letters where *either way* would sound too casual, and unlike *either way* it wants the two branches actually stated first.

1. The committee may approve the plan or send it back; *in either case*, we hear by Friday.
2. She can appeal or she can refile. *In either case*, the clock starts over.
3. The tenant may cure the default or vacate; *in either case*, the landlord's costs are recoverable.
4. The result is the same *in either case*.
5. Rain would postpone the game and lightning would shorten it — *in either case*, nobody gets home before ten.
6. *In either case*, somebody has to tell the client before the end of the day.

**See also:** [either way](#either-way), [in that case](#in-that-case), [in which case](#in-which-case), [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [otherwise](#otherwise)

### in that case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈðæt keɪs/ &middot; *in-THAT-kayss* &middot; *that* keeps its full vowel here because it is the stressed demonstrative, not the complementizer
**Русский:** в таком случае; тогда — ответ на только что названное условие
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (demonstrative adverbial phrase) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — it points back at a condition somebody has just put on the table
**Pattern:** [sentence]. *In that case*, [sentence]. &middot; [sentence]; *in that case*, [sentence]. &middot; [clause] and, *in that case*, [clause]. &middot; [subject] *in that case* [verb phrase] (medial, rarer).
**Punctuation:** **Period or semicolon before it, comma after it** when it opens a sentence. A comma alone in front of it is a comma splice: write *The store's closed; in that case, we'll order in*, never *The store's closed, in that case we'll order in.* This is exactly where it parts company with *in which case*, which is a relative and does take a plain comma.
**Variants:** *in which case* (the relative version, inside the same sentence), *then*, *if that's the case*, *if so*

Picks up a condition that has just been stated — usually by somebody else — and says what follows from it. The difference from *in which case* is structural rather than semantic: *in which case* continues the sentence that raised the possibility, while *in that case* starts a new one, which is why it is the natural reply in conversation.

1. "The 6:40 is sold out." "*In that case*, we'll drive."
2. You'd rather not fly? *In that case*, I'll price out the train.
3. The permit may take another month; *in that case*, we push the opening to spring.
4. She's already eaten. *In that case*, we can skip the reservation.
5. *In that case*, I'd rather hear it from him directly.
6. If the intake numbers are wrong, the whole model is wrong, and *in that case* we start over.

**See also:** [in which case](#in-which-case), [if so](#if-so), [otherwise](#otherwise), [failing that](#failing-that), [either way](#either-way)

### in which case

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈwɪtʃ keɪs/ &middot; *in-WICH-kayss*
**Русский:** в каковом случае; и тогда — продолжает то же предложение
**Type:** subordinating (sentential relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** a non-restrictive relative clause commenting on the whole clause before it
**Pattern:** [clause], *in which case* [clause]. &middot; [sentence]. *In which case*, [sentence]. (informal)
**Punctuation:** A comma before it — it heads a relative clause attached to what came before, so in careful writing it does not start a new sentence, although email and speech often let it. When it does open a sentence, put a comma after it.
**Variants:** *in that case* (a demonstrative, not a relative — it starts a fresh sentence and usually answers somebody else)

Chains a consequence onto a possibility already stated, with *which* pointing back at that whole possibility. The difference from *in that case* is structural: *in which case* continues the same sentence, *in that case* replies to something just said.

1. The flight may be delayed, *in which case* we'll rebook you on the morning departure.
2. She might not have gotten the email, *in which case* I'll resend it.
3. The inspection could turn up mold, *in which case* the sale falls through.
4. He may already be on his way, *in which case* calling won't help.
5. Rain is possible, *in which case* the reception moves indoors.

**See also:** [in that case](#in-that-case), [if so](#if-so), [otherwise](#otherwise), [failing that](#failing-that), [if](#if)

### or else

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr ˈɛls/ &middot; *or-ELSS* &middot; reduced /ər ˈɛls/
**Русский:** а то; иначе; а не то — угроза или предупреждение (разг. в чистом виде)
**Type:** coordinating (fixed phrase) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the bare threat use is informal
**Clause type:** coordinate clause stating the negative alternative
**Pattern:** [imperative or statement], *or else* [clause]. &middot; [imperative], *or else*.
**Punctuation:** Comma before *or else* when it joins two clauses — the same rule as plain *or*. The bare threat takes a period, and nothing after it.
**Variants:** *or* alone in the same warning sense (*Hurry, or we'll be late*); bare *else* as a conjunction is archaic in American English outside programming

Names what happens if the first clause is not carried out — the coordinated twin of *unless*. *Do it, or else you're fired* and *You're fired unless you do it* state the same deal from opposite ends — and note that the coordinator takes its comma while the subordinator does not. Left bare, *or else* is an entire threat by itself.

1. Leave now, *or else* you'll hit rush hour.
2. Back up the drive tonight, *or else* you'll be sorry tomorrow.
3. Pay the balance by the first, *or else* the account goes to collections.
4. "Turn the music down, *or else*."
5. We need to leave by six, *or else* we're eating dinner at ten.

**See also:** [otherwise](#otherwise), [unless](#unless), [if not](#if-not), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

### otherwise

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðərˌwaɪz/ &middot; *UTH-ur-wyze*
**Русский:** иначе; в противном случае — наречие-связка, требует точки с запятой перед собой
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — it links two independent clauses as an adverb
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *otherwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Otherwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause] — *otherwise* [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]; [subject] *would otherwise* [verb phrase] (medial).
**Punctuation:** **Semicolon or period before it, comma after it.** *We should leave now; otherwise, we'll be late.* A comma alone in front of *otherwise* is a comma splice — the single most common punctuation error with this word, because it feels like a conjunction and is not one.
**Variants:** *or otherwise* is a different word — the plain adverb meaning "in other ways" (*damaged or otherwise*), which carries none of the punctuation rules above

States what will happen if the preceding statement is not acted on — the conjunctive-adverb equivalent of *or else* and the mirror image of *unless*. It also works backward as an inference marker: *She must have left; otherwise her coat would still be here.*

1. Take the earlier train; *otherwise*, you'll miss the opening.
2. We need a deposit by Friday. *Otherwise*, the room goes back into inventory.
3. Write it down; *otherwise*, you'll forget by morning.
4. She must have left already — *otherwise* her coat would still be here.
5. The tickets are nonrefundable; *otherwise*, I'd have canceled weeks ago.
6. The deadline is firm; we'd *otherwise* be glad to give you another week.

**See also:** [or else](#or-else), [unless](#unless), [if not](#if-not), [in that case](#in-that-case), [in which case](#in-which-case), [failing that](#failing-that), [either way](#either-way)

## Legal, Latin, and archaic conditionals

The conditional formulas that live in contracts, statutes, briefs, and old books rather than in ordinary American speech — recognizing them matters far more than using them. The Latin tags *ceteris paribus*, *ex hypothesi*, and *arguendo* belong to economics, philosophy, and appellate argument; the doublet *if and to the extent that* and the fallback clause *in default of* belong to drafting; and the subjunctive forms *be it that*, *if so be that*, *but and if*, and *peradventure* belong to the King James Bible, to Shakespeare's contemporaries, and to the nineteenth-century novel. *In no event* is the odd member of the set: it is not a conjunction at all but a fronted negative adverbial, and it earns its place here as the exact negative of *in the event that*.

### assuming arguendo that

**Pronunciation:** /əˈsumɪŋ ˌɑrɡjuˈɛndoʊ ðət/ &middot; *uh-SOO-ming-ar-gyoo-EN-doh-thuht* &middot; *arguendo* is read as an English word, four syllables with the stress on *-en-*; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/
**Русский:** юр.: допустим даже, что — уступка исключительно ради спора, без признания
**Type:** subordinating (participial, with a Latin adverb wedged inside it) &middot; **Register:** legal (briefs, opinions, oral argument); it appears essentially nowhere else
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition — a premise conceded for the sake of argument only
**Pattern:** *Assuming arguendo that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Assuming, arguendo, that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *assuming arguendo that* [clause]. &middot; [verb] *arguendo* [that-clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. Courts print it both ways — *assuming arguendo that* and *assuming, arguendo, that* with the Latin fenced in commas — and either is safe so long as a single brief keeps to one of them. *Arguendo* is italicized in most American legal style, though a few court styles set it roman.
**Variants:** *arguendo* alone, *assuming for the sake of argument that*, *even assuming that*, *granting arguendo that*

Concedes a point without accepting it, purely in order to show that the concession does not save the other side — the lawyer's way of saying "even if you are right about that, you still lose." *Assuming that* takes a premise on board provisionally and reasons from it; *assuming arguendo that* takes it on board while making plain that the writer denies it.

1. *Assuming arguendo that* the statute applies, the plaintiff still filed too late.
2. *Assuming, arguendo, that* the search was unlawful, the evidence would have been inevitably discovered.
3. The court observed that, *assuming arguendo that* a duty existed, no breach had been shown.
4. *Assuming arguendo that* the contract is ambiguous, the extrinsic evidence favors our reading.
5. Counsel conceded *arguendo* that the notice was late and argued the absence of prejudice instead.
6. *Assuming arguendo that* the officer had reasonable suspicion, the stop still lasted far too long.

**See also:** [assuming that](#assuming-that), [granted that](#granted-that), [even if](#even-if), [ex hypothesi](#ex-hypothesi), [given that](#given-that)

### be it that

**Pronunciation:** /bi ɪt ˈðæt/ &middot; *bee-it-THAT* &middot; *be* keeps its full vowel because it is the subjunctive verb carrying the construction, and *that* is stressed as the demonstrative-turned-complementizer of older English
**Русский:** устар.: пусть даже; допустим, что — старая формула с сослагательным be
**Type:** subordinating (inverted present subjunctive of *be* plus a complementizer) &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition — a premise granted for argument
**Pattern:** *Be it that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *So be it that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *Be it so that* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, which is the only position the phrase takes. No *if* — inverting the subjunctive *be* in front of its subject does that work, exactly as *were* and *had* still do in modern counterfactuals.
**Variants:** *so be it that*, *be it so that*, *be it granted that*; the living equivalents are *granted that* and *even supposing that*

An old subjunctive formula meaning "granting that" or "even supposing that," built by putting *be* in front of its subject. An American meets it in Shakespeare and his contemporaries, in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century prose, and in the recitals of old deeds; the only fragments of it still alive are the concessive *be it rain or shine* and the standalone shrug *so be it*, neither of which is this conjunction.

1. *Be it that* he hath wronged thee, thy revenge is no remedy.
2. *Be it that* the debt was lawfully contracted, no action lay after the term had run.
3. *So be it that* the tenant pay the rent reserved, the lease shall continue in force.
4. *Be it that* she erred in judgment, her intent was honest, and the court so found.
5. *Be it that* the witness spoke truly, the account still wants corroboration.

**See also:** [if so be that](#if-so-be-that), [granted that](#granted-that), [even if](#even-if), [supposing that](#supposing-that), [but and if](#but-and-if)

### but and if

**Pronunciation:** /bət ən ˈɪf/ &middot; *buht-uhn-IF* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ and the three words run together as one unit, with the stress falling on *if*
**Русский:** устар., библ.: а если — формула Библии короля Иакова, значит просто «но если»
**Type:** subordinating (a fossilized doublet — the old conditional *and* reinforced by *if*) &middot; **Register:** archaic, biblical
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition opening a new turn in a narrative
**Pattern:** *But and if* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *And if* [clause], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause, which is its only position. The King James text often sets the clause off with a colon or a semicolon instead, following seventeenth-century pointing rather than modern rules; a modern editor quoting it leaves the original punctuation alone.
**Variants:** *and if*, *but if*; the bare conditional *an* and *an if* of Shakespeare's English

A King James Bible formula meaning nothing more than "but if." The *and* in it is not the coordinator: it is the old conditional *an*, itself meaning "if," so the phrase stacks three conditional words on top of one another — which is why every translation after 1611 dropped it. An American meets it in the KJV, in preaching that quotes the KJV, and nowhere else.

1. "*But and if* that evil servant shall say in his heart, My lord delayeth his coming…" (Matthew 24:48)
2. "*But and if* she depart, let her remain unmarried, or be reconciled to her husband." (1 Corinthians 7:11)
3. "*But and if* thou marry, thou hast not sinned." (1 Corinthians 7:28)
4. "*But and if* ye suffer for righteousness' sake, happy are ye." (1 Peter 3:14)
5. *But and if* the tenant fail to pay the rent reserved, the term shall cease and determine.

**See also:** [if](#if), [be it that](#be-it-that), [if so be that](#if-so-be-that), [peradventure](#peradventure), [an](12-formal-archaic.md#an)

### ceteris paribus

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɛtərɪs ˈpærɪbəs/ &middot; *KET-uh-ris-PAIR-ih-buhs* &middot; also /ˌsɛtərɪs/, *SET-uh-ris*, from the English tradition of reading Latin *c* as /s/; both are heard in American economics departments and neither is corrected
**Русский:** при прочих равных — лат. формула экономистов, книжн.
**Type:** subordinating (a Latin ablative absolute borrowed whole; an adverbial phrase, not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal, technical Latin (economics, philosophy, legal argument)
**Clause type:** no clause — a fixed Latin phrase standing in for a whole condition
**Pattern:** *Ceteris paribus*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *ceteris paribus*. &middot; [subject], *ceteris paribus*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a sentence, comma before it when it trails, commas on both sides in the middle. American style italicizes it, though economics journals often set it roman because it has become a term of art in the field. Being an adverbial and not a connector, it cannot join two independent clauses with a comma.
**Variants:** *cet. par.* (abbreviated in equations and footnotes), *all else being equal*, *other things being equal*, *mutatis mutandis* (a different tag altogether — "with the necessary changes made")

The Latin for *all else being equal*, and the standard hedge of economic reasoning: it isolates one variable by declaring every other one frozen. It survives because economics needed a compact way to say it, and outside economics, philosophy, and the occasional judicial opinion an American writer is better served by the English.

1. *Ceteris paribus*, a rise in price reduces the quantity demanded.
2. The model predicts, *ceteris paribus*, that real wages track productivity.
3. *Ceteris paribus*, the defendant with counsel fares better than the one without.
4. Higher tariffs raise domestic prices, *ceteris paribus*.
5. *Ceteris paribus*, a country that saves more will invest more.
6. The court reasoned that, *ceteris paribus*, the earlier filing should control.

**See also:** [all else being equal](#all-else-being-equal), [ex hypothesi](#ex-hypothesi), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [given that](#given-that), [if](#if)

### ex hypothesi

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛks haɪˈpɑθəsaɪ/ &middot; *eks-hy-POTH-uh-sy* &middot; the final syllable is *-sy*, /saɪ/, in the traditional English pronunciation of Latin; /ˌɛks haɪˈpɑθəsi/, *-see*, is also heard
**Русский:** по принятому допущению; по условию — лат., в философии и логике
**Type:** subordinating (a Latin prepositional phrase used as a sentence adverbial; not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal, technical Latin (analytic philosophy, logic, appellate opinions)
**Clause type:** no clause — a fixed Latin phrase pointing back at a hypothesis already granted
**Pattern:** *Ex hypothesi*, [main clause]. &middot; [subject] is, *ex hypothesi*, [complement]. &middot; [main clause] *ex hypothesi*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it when it opens a sentence and commas on both sides in the middle; a writer who runs it into the clause without commas (*the premise is ex hypothesi true*) is treating it as an ordinary adverb, which is also standard. Italicized in American style. It links nothing, so two independent clauses on either side of it still need a period or a semicolon.
**Variants:** *by hypothesis*, *on the hypothesis*, *by definition* (looser and not the same claim), *ex vi termini* (the rarer legal cousin)

Means "by the hypothesis already granted" — it flags that what follows is not a fresh claim but something the assumed premise already contains. Where *ceteris paribus* freezes the other variables, *ex hypothesi* reaches back to a supposition made earlier in the argument and draws out what it entails.

1. The being in question is, *ex hypothesi*, perfect, so the objection cannot arise.
2. *Ex hypothesi*, the two sets have no member in common.
3. If the promise was gratuitous, then *ex hypothesi* no consideration passed.
4. The second premise is *ex hypothesi* true, and the argument turns entirely on the first.
5. *Ex hypothesi* the defendant knew nothing of the scheme; the question is whether he should have.

**See also:** [ceteris paribus](#ceteris-paribus), [assuming arguendo that](#assuming-arguendo-that), [assuming that](#assuming-that), [given that](#given-that), [all else being equal](#all-else-being-equal)

### if and to the extent that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪf ən tə ði ɪkˈstɛnt ðət/ &middot; *if-uhn-tuh-thee-ik-STENT-thuht* &middot; *and* reduces to /ən/ and the whole run is unstressed until *extent*
**Русский:** юр.: если и в той мере, в какой — сразу «да или нет» и «насколько»
**Type:** subordinating (fixed legal doublet) &middot; **Register:** legal (credit agreements, indentures, tax and indemnity provisions)
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition combining a yes-or-no test with a scope test
**Pattern:** [main clause] *if and to the extent that* [clause]. &middot; *If and to the extent that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *to the extent, if any, that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *if and to the extent* [past participle].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; no comma when it follows the main clause, which is the usual position. Never break the doublet with a comma — the exception is the inverted *to the extent, if any, that*, where the commas around *if any* are required.
**Variants:** *to the extent, if any, that*, *if and to the extent*, *to the extent that* (the plain form), *if, and only to the extent that*

A drafter's belt-and-suspenders phrase asking two questions at once: does this apply at all, and if so, how far? Modern drafting guides call it redundant, on the ground that *to the extent that* already answers both — nothing applies to a zero extent — but it stays standard in indentures and tax language, where drafters would rather repeat themselves than litigate the point.

1. The Borrower shall indemnify the Lender *if and to the extent that* such losses arise from the Borrower's negligence.
2. *If and to the extent that* any provision of this Agreement is held unenforceable, that provision shall be severed.
3. Amounts withheld shall be credited *if and to the extent that* they are actually remitted to the taxing authority.
4. The guaranty applies *if and to the extent that* the primary obligor fails to pay when due.
5. *If and to the extent that* the Trustee has actual knowledge of a default, it shall notify the holders.
6. This policy responds *to the extent, if any, that* the underlying policy does not.

**See also:** [to the extent that](#to-the-extent-that), [if and when](#if-and-when), [unless and until](#unless-and-until), [provided that](#provided-that), [insofar as](12-formal-archaic.md#insofar-as)

### if so be that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf soʊ ˈbi ðət/ &middot; *if-soh-BEE-thuht* &middot; the stress sits on *be*, the subjunctive verb that carries the condition; the complementizer *that* reduces to /ðət/ and is often dropped altogether
**Русский:** устар.: если случится так, что; при условии что
**Type:** subordinating (archaic — *if* plus the present subjunctive *be*, with an optional complementizer) &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition
**Pattern:** *If so be that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *If so be* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *if so be that* [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. Older printings use a colon or a semicolon at the seam instead, on seventeenth-century principles rather than modern ones, and a quotation should keep whatever the source had.
**Variants:** *if so be*, *so be that*, *if it so be that*; the modern equivalents are plain *if* and *provided that*

An old way of saying "if it should turn out that" or "provided that," with the subjunctive *be* marking the condition as genuinely open. It is a piled-up conditional — *if* plus *so be* plus *that* — of the kind Early Modern English liked and modern English trimmed away. An American meets it in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare's contemporaries, and in eighteenth-century deeds.

1. "…*if so be* that the Spirit of God dwell in you." (Romans 8:9)
2. "*If so be* ye have tasted that the Lord is gracious." (1 Peter 2:3)
3. "*If so be* they will hearken, and turn every man from his evil way…" (Jeremiah 26:3)
4. "…*if so be* that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together." (Romans 8:17)
5. *If so be that* the said lands descend to the heirs male, the entail shall stand.

**See also:** [be it that](#be-it-that), [but and if](#but-and-if), [provided that](#provided-that), [if](#if), [supposing that](#supposing-that)

### in default of

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn dɪˈfɔlt əv/ &middot; *in-dih-FAWLT-uhv* &middot; /dɪˈfɑlt/ for speakers with the cot–caught merger; note that the computing noun *default* is stressed on the first syllable, *DEE-fawlt*, and this legal phrase never is
**Русский:** юр.: за отсутствием; при неисполнении — формула завещаний и закладных
**Type:** subordinating (prepositional — takes a noun phrase) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal (wills, trusts, mortgages, older statutes)
**Clause type:** no clause — a noun phrase naming the act that fails to happen
**Pattern:** *In default of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in default of* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *in default whereof* [clause]. (older drafting)
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted phrase, which is where it usually appears; no comma when it follows. *In default whereof* takes a comma before it and none after, since like *failing which* it heads a relative clause.
**Variants:** *in default whereof*, *failing* + noun phrase, *for want of*, *in the absence of*, *absent* (the modern American equivalent)

The drafting formula for "if that does not happen": it names an act that was required and provides for its not being done. *In default of issue*, *in default of payment*, and *in default of appointment* are set phrases in wills and mortgages, and outside that world an American would write *absent* or simply *if there is no*.

1. *In default of* payment, the mortgagee may enter and sell.
2. The estate passes to the testator's nephews *in default of* issue.
3. *In default of* agreement between the parties, the matter shall be referred to arbitration.
4. The power passes to the survivor *in default of* appointment by either spouse.
5. *In default of* an answer within twenty days, judgment may be entered against you.
6. The tenant shall repair within thirty days, *in default whereof* the landlord may re-enter.

**See also:** [absent](#absent), [failing that](#failing-that), [without](#without), [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [short of](#short-of)

### in no event

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈnoʊ ɪˌvɛnt/ &middot; *in-NOH-ih-vent* &middot; the stress sits on *no*, which is what makes the negative absolute; *event* keeps a secondary stress
**Русский:** ни в коем случае; ни при каких обстоятельствах — юр., требует инверсии
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a fronted negative adverbial; not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal (limitation-of-liability, indemnity, and cap provisions)
**Clause type:** no clause of its own — a sentence adverbial that forces subject–auxiliary inversion when it is fronted
**Pattern:** *In no event shall* [subject] [bare infinitive]. &middot; *In no event will* [subject] [verb phrase]. &middot; *In no event may* [subject] [bare infinitive]. &middot; uninverted: [subject] shall *in no event* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** **No comma after it.** It is part of the clause it inverts, not a sentence adverb like *however*, and a comma would cut the adverbial off from its own verb: write *In no event shall the Company be liable*, never *In no event, shall the Company be liable.* Because it joins nothing, two independent clauses on either side of it still need a period or a semicolon.
**Variants:** *under no circumstances*, *on no account*, *in no case*, *at no time*; the positive counterpart is *in any event*

The drafter's absolute negative — the flat denial that opens a limitation-of-liability clause, and the mirror image of *in the event that*, which opens one condition where this closes all of them. The inversion is obligatory once the phrase is fronted, by the same negative-fronting rule that gives *never have I seen* and *not once did she complain*.

1. *In no event shall* the Company be liable for indirect, incidental, or consequential damages.
2. *In no event will* aggregate liability exceed the fees paid in the preceding twelve months.
3. *In no event shall* the term of this Lease extend beyond December 31, 2045.
4. *In no event may* a claim be brought more than two years after the loss.
5. Interest shall *in no event* exceed the maximum rate permitted by law.
6. *In no event shall* silence be construed as acceptance of an offer.

**See also:** [in the event that](#in-the-event-that), [unless and until](#unless-and-until), [absent](#absent), [otherwise](#otherwise), [or else](#or-else)

### peradventure

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpɛrədˈvɛntʃər/ &middot; *pair-ud-VEN-chur* &middot; four syllables with the stress on *-ven-*; the second half is the ordinary word *adventure* in its older sense of "chance"
**Русский:** устар.: если случайно, а вдруг; как наречие — «быть может»
**Type:** subordinating (archaic); the same word is an adverb meaning "perhaps" and a noun meaning "doubt" &middot; **Register:** archaic
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of condition, or of apprehension after a verb of fearing
**Pattern:** *Peradventure* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *If peradventure* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *lest peradventure* [clause]. &middot; *beyond peradventure of doubt* (the surviving noun idiom).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause. As the bare adverb meaning "perhaps" it opens the sentence and takes its own comma in modern printing, though seventeenth-century texts leave that comma out and a quotation should not add it.
**Variants:** *if peradventure*, *lest peradventure*, *peradventure that*; the noun survives in *beyond peradventure* and *beyond peradventure of doubt*

The old word for "by chance," used as a conjunction meaning "if by chance" and, after a verb of fearing, "lest." Its everyday afterlife is not conditional at all: what an American actually meets is the adverb in the King James Bible (*Peradventure there be fifty righteous within the city*) and the frozen legal noun in *beyond peradventure of doubt*. The conjunction itself died out with the eighteenth century.

1. "*Peradventure* there be fifty righteous within the city." (Genesis 18:24)
2. "*Peradventure* the woman will not be willing to follow me." (Genesis 24:5)
3. *If peradventure* the ship be lost, the underwriters shall answer for the whole.
4. Set a watch upon the gate, *lest peradventure* he come upon thee unawares.
5. *Peradventure* the heir die without issue, the land shall revert to the donor.

**See also:** [if so be that](#if-so-be-that), [but and if](#but-and-if), [be it that](#be-it-that), [in case](#in-case), [lest](05-purpose-result.md#lest)

### whether or no

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛðər ər ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *WETH-ur-ur-NOH* &middot; identical to *whether or not* except in the last word, and the *no* takes exactly the stress the *not* would
**Русский:** устар., книжн.: независимо от того, … или нет — старая форма whether or not
**Type:** subordinating; also a complementizer in noun clauses &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concessive condition; also a noun clause
**Pattern:** *Whether or no* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whether or no* [clause]. &middot; [verb of knowing or asking] *whether or no* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whether* [clause] *or no* (the split form).
**Punctuation:** Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows, and none before a noun clause — the same rules that govern *whether or not*.
**Variants:** *whether or not* (the modern form), the split *whether … or no*, *whether or nay* (rarer still)

The older shape of *whether or not*, with the archaic sentential *no* where modern English puts *not*. It is not an error, only dated: it is thick on the ground in the King James Bible, in Milton, and in nineteenth-century novels, and an American who writes it today will be read as reaching for an antique effect.

1. *Whether or no* the enterprise succeed, the attempt does him honor.
2. She meant to have her way, *whether or no* her brother approved.
3. I know not *whether or no* he will come.
4. *Whether or no* the treaty be ratified, the boundary is settled in fact.
5. He would have the boy schooled, *whether or no* the money could be found.

**See also:** [whether or not](#whether-or-not), [whether](#whether), [regardless of whether](#regardless-of-whether), [either way](#either-way), [even if](#even-if)
