Subordinators of place, and relative connectors#

The words that pin a clause to a location, and the wh- words that hook a whole clause onto a noun already standing in the sentence.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Two families share one shelf here. The first is small and genuinely adverbial: where, wherever, everywhere, anywhere and their archaic cousins whence and whither build clauses that answer where? about the main verb. The second is the relative system — who, whom, whose, which, that, when, where, why — which does not modify the verb at all, but reaches back and fastens a clause onto a noun. The two families overlap because where does both jobs, and they share a shape because every word in this file descends from the same Old English hw- stem.

One rule runs through the entire category, and it is a comma rule. A clause that narrows which thing you mean is restrictive and takes no commas: the house where I grew up. A clause that merely adds information about something already identified is nonrestrictive and is fenced off by commas on both sides: our old house, where I grew up, is finally for sale. That one distinction decides three separate things at once — whether you write commas, whether you may use that, and whether you may drop the relative word entirely. Restrictive clauses allow that and allow the zero relative (the book I read); nonrestrictive clauses allow neither and require a wh- word. In edited American prose the nonrestrictive relative for things is which, never that.

Word order is fixed and worth stating once: every word in this file goes at the front of its own clause, no matter what job the word does inside that clause. The people with whom he shared the house moves the preposition to the front along with it; the people he shared the house with leaves the preposition stranded at the end. Both are correct American English, and stranding is the normal spoken option.

Subordinators of place#

These build adverbial clauses that answer where? about the main verb. Most of them can be fronted for emphasis, and every one that can follows the same comma rule: comma after a fronted place clause, no comma when the place clause trails the main clause.

anyplace#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛniˌpleɪs/ · EN-ee-playss
Русский
где угодно; куда угодно — разг., амер. вариант anywhere; редактор правит на anywhere
Type
subordinating; also a place adverb (we didn't go anyplace) and a pronoun (anyplace is fine)
Register
informal, chiefly American — ordinary in speech and casual writing, changed to anywhere by copy editors
Clause type
adverbial clause of place (free choice)
Pattern
[main clause] anyplace [that] [clause]. · Anyplace [that] [clause], [main clause]. · [negated main clause] anyplace [clause] — the negative-polarity use. · [main clause] anyplace [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in anyplace the dog can't get to.
Punctuation
Exactly the anywhere rule: no comma when the clause follows the main clause, comma after it when the clause is fronted, and never a comma on either side of the optional that.
Variants
anywhere (the standard equivalent, available in every position anyplace occupies); any place as two words is the literal noun phrase, not this pro-form

The informal American twin of anywhere, built on place instead of the -where stem. It is a real Americanism — British English does not have it — and it is unremarkable in conversation and in fiction dialogue, but it is a register choice and not a meaning choice, so edited prose puts anywhere back. Note the spelling: closed up for the pro-form, two words for the ordinary noun (any place you can name).

  1. Sit anyplace you want — nobody saved seats.
  2. We couldn't find anyplace that was still open past nine.
  3. Anyplace the road narrows to one lane, expect a wait.
  4. He'd have followed that team anyplace they played.
  5. You can't smoke anyplace there's a kid within fifty feet.
  6. She'll eat anyplace they keep the pie under glass.

  3. Anywhere the trail crosses water, expect mud for the next half mile.
  4. She would have followed that band anywhere they toured.

anywheres#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛniˌwɛrz/ · EN-ee-wairz
Русский
где угодно — диал., прост.: нестандартная форма anywhere с -s
Type
subordinating; more often a plain place adverb
Register
dialectal and nonstandard — regional American speech and the dialect writing that records it; never correct in edited prose
Clause type
adverbial clause of place (free choice); relative clause hosted by a place pro-form
Pattern
[negated main clause] anywheres [clause]. · [main clause] anywheres [that] [clause]. · [main clause] anywheres [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded. · not … anywheres near [noun] — the frame in which it is heard most often.
Punctuation
The anywhere rule unchanged: no comma when the clause trails, comma after a fronted clause, no comma around the optional that. The -s affects nothing but the register.
Variants
anywhere (the standard form); somewheres, nowheres, everywheres (the same -s on the rest of the set)

The -s is the old adverbial genitive that standard English froze in always, besides, nowadays, and unawares and then stopped adding; regional American speech kept adding it. So anywheres is a survival rather than a blunder — but it is firmly nonstandard now, and an American meets it in Twain, in dialogue by Faulkner and Caldwell, and in rural speech from northern New England through the Ozarks, essentially nowhere else in print.

  1. There wasn't anywheres we could tie up the raft before dark.
  2. He never went anywheres his mother didn't know about.
  3. You won't cut hay like that anywheres the ground stays this wet.
  4. I don't reckon there's anywheres around here a body can get a hot meal.
  5. She won't shop anywheres they don't take a check.
  6. It ain't anywheres near as far as the map makes it look.

no place#

Pronunciation
/noʊ ˈpleɪs/ · noh-PLAYSS
Русский
негде, некуда; нет места, где — разг., амер. вариант nowhere; вперёд не выносится
Type
subordinating (negative); also a place adverb and a noun phrase (forty miles of no place)
Register
informal, chiefly American
Clause type
relative clause hosted by a negative place pro-form
Pattern
[there is/was] no place [that] [clause]. · [subject] [verb] no place [that] [clause]. · [main clause] no place [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in no place I'd want to eat at.
Punctuation
No comma before the clause — it always narrows, so it is restrictive. And note the one syntactic difference from nowhere: no place does not front for inversion. Variants
nowhere (the standard equivalent, and the only one of the two that can be fronted); noplace (one word, more informal still)

The informal American negative of the -place set, matching anyplace, someplace, and everyplace. It substitutes for nowhere in the ordinary post-verbal slot and nowhere else: the emphatic fronted construction, the noun (the middle of nowhere), and the fixed idioms all belong to nowhere alone.

  1. There was no place we could park within six blocks of the ballpark.
  2. By ten o'clock there was no place in town still serving food.
  3. The dog had no place to run that wasn't fenced.
  4. He had no place he could go once the shift ended.
  5. We looked for an hour and found no place the kids could swim.
  6. Off that exit there's no place I'd want to spend the night.

subordinating (negative); also a place adverb and a noun (the middle of nowhere)

The negative member of the somewhere / anywhere / everywhere set: it denies that any location satisfies the clause.   4. The kid had nowhere he could go after school, so the library became it.
  5. Nowhere is the difference clearer than in the third-quarter numbers.
  6. Off that exit there's nowhere I'd want to eat at.
  7. We drove around for an hour and found no place the dog could run off leash.

  4. She wouldn't go nowheres her sister hadn't been first.
  5. That's nowheres near enough lumber to finish the porch.

whence#

Pronunciation
/wɛns/ (older and Southern /ʍɛns/) · WENS
Русский
откуда — устар., книжн.: место-источник
Type
subordinating; also a relative adverb
Register
archaic, literary
Clause type
adverbial clause of place (source); relative clause of origin; noun clause (indirect question)
Pattern
[main clause] whence [clause]. · [place noun], whence [clause]. · [place noun] whence [clause] — restrictive, no comma. · [verb of knowing/asking] whence [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. · [main clause] from whence [clause] — the long-established redundant form.
Punctuation
Comma before whence when the clause merely adds information about a place already named; no comma when the clause completes the main clause or answers an implied question.
Variants
from whence (redundant but long established); whencesoever (archaic)

Means "from what place" or "from which place." The from is already baked into the word, which makes from whence strictly redundant — but it is in the King James Bible and in Shakespeare, so it has four centuries of cover. Set against its partners: whence is the source, whither the destination, where the location.

  1. Return the stone whence it came.
  2. He never said whence the money had come.
  3. Investigators traced the outbreak to a single farm, whence the shipments had gone out to nine states.
  4. "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, from whence cometh my help."
  5. The company moved its charter back to Delaware, whence it had come twenty years earlier.
  6. The village whence his family came no longer appears on any map.
  7. Nobody could establish whence the rumor had started.

Русский

Type
subordinating; also a relative adverb

archaic; survives in scriptural cadence and in old customs and revenue drafting

The -soever intensification of whence, meaning "from absolutely any place that."   1. The duty is charged on goods whencesoever imported into the territory.
  2. The trustee shall gather in the income of the estate, whencesoever arising.
  3. Whencesoever the report had come, it was on the governor's desk by noon.
  4. He would take help whencesoever it was offered, and ask no questions after.
  5. The parish was bound to relieve any traveler in want, whencesoever he came.
  6. Rumor, whencesoever it begins, is not easily called back.

where#

Pronunciation
/wɛr/ (older and Southern /ʍɛr/) · WAIR — unstressed as a conjunction but not reduced; it never falls together with were /wər/
Русский
где; куда; там, где — место; также относительное «в котором» и косвенный вопрос «где»
Type
subordinating; also a relative adverb and an interrogative
Register
neutral
Clause type
adverbial clause of place; relative clause modifying a place noun; noun clause (indirect question)
Pattern
[main clause] where [clause]. · Where [clause], [main clause]. · [place noun] where [clause]. · [place noun], where [clause], [rest of sentence]. · [verb of knowing/asking] where [clause].
Punctuation
No comma when the place clause follows the main clause. Comma after a fronted where clause. As a relative adverb the general rule applies: no commas if the clause narrows which place is meant (the town where I was born), commas on both sides if the place is already identified (Tulsa, where I was born, has changed). Never a comma before an indirect question (ask where it is, not ask, where it is).
Variants
none in standard American English; at which / in which is the formal relative equivalent

The workhorse of the category, and the one word that belongs to both families in this file. As an adverbial subordinator it locates the whole event (put it where I can see it). As a relative adverb it modifies a place noun and can be swapped for in which (the room where we met = the room in which we met). It also drifts into abstract territory, meaning "in cases in which" (where the law is silent, custom governs), which is standard in legal and academic prose.

  1. Put the boxes back where you found them.
  2. Where the sidewalk ends, the gravel road starts.
  3. This is the diner where we had breakfast every Sunday for six years.
  4. We spent August in Bar Harbor, where my grandmother still keeps a house.
  5. Nobody could tell me where the registration desk was.
  6. Where the contract is silent, state law fills the gap.
  7. He'll argue where most people would just walk away.

where all#

Pronunciation
/ˈwɛr ˌɔl/ · also /ˌwɛr ˈɔl/ · WAIR-awl
Русский
где именно, во всех каких местах — диал. (юг США): вопрос требует перечислить все места
Type
subordinating (interrogative); also an interrogative adverb in direct questions
Register
dialectal — Southern and South Midland American; unremarkable in speech across that region and absent from edited prose everywhere
Clause type
noun clause (indirect question, exhaustive)
Pattern
Where all [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — direct question, with inversion. · [verb of asking/knowing] where all [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. · Where all [subject] [verb] [preposition]? — stranded. · Where [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] all? — the all floated to the end of the clause.
Punctuation
No comma between where and all, and none before the embedded clause. The direct question takes a question mark; the embedded clause takes none.
Variants
who all, what all (the same all on the other wh- words); all the places (that) (the standard paraphrase)

The all here is not the ordinary quantifier but an exhaustiveness marker welded to the wh- word: Where did you go? invites one answer, Where all did you go? asks for the whole itinerary. Standard English has no single word for the distinction and has to paraphrase, which is why the construction is so stable in the regions that have it — and why it is worth recognizing rather than correcting when you meet it.

  1. Where all did you go on that trip?
  2. I don't know where all she's lived since college.
  3. Where all have they put these stores now?
  4. He couldn't remember where all he'd left his tools.
  5. Ask her where all the family is coming in from.
  6. Where all did y'all look before you called me?
See also
where, who all, what all, whereabouts

Pronunciation
/wɛr … æt/ · the at is unstressed and normally reduced to /ət/ · WAIR … at
Русский
где — прост.: избыточное at в конце придаточного, в русском не отражается
Type
subordinating — plain where with a pleonastic stranded at
Register
nonstandard; extremely widespread in casual American speech in every region, and one of the most heavily corrected features in American schools
Clause type
noun clause (indirect question of place); adverbial clause of place
Pattern
[verb of asking/knowing] where [subject] [verb] at. · Where [auxiliary] [subject] at? — the direct question. · [main clause] where [subject] [verb] at. · where it's at — the frozen idiom, which is a different animal.
Punctuation
No comma anywhere in it; it points exactly like plain where. Note that the at attaches to the end of its own clause, not to where — which is why the headword is where … at and not a single word, and why it can be separated from where by an entire clause (where you said the key was at).
Variants

Where already means "at what place," so the trailing at is redundant, and that redundancy is the whole basis of the schoolroom joke ("Where are you at?" "Between the A and the T"). It is nonetheless one of the most common features of casual American speech, cutting across region, race, and class — treat it as informal rather than illiterate, but keep it out of writing. One offshoot escaped the stigma completely: the frozen idiom where it's at, meaning "the thing that actually matters," is standard colloquial English and no editor blinks at it.

  1. Do you know where the extra chairs are at?
  2. "Where you at?" is the entire text message in this family.
  3. Nobody could tell me where the trailhead was at.
  4. He asked where I was at on the quarterly report.
  5. She never says where she's at, only when she'll be back.
  6. Comfortable shoes and a ten-minute commute — that's where it's at.

Built from where + as, and originally meaning "at which place" — which is why it sits in this file. Modern American English keeps two descendants of that original sense. The where- compounds#

These are connectors built from where plus a preposition, and they mean exactly what the parts say: whereby = "by which," wherein = "in which," whereof = "of which," whereupon = "upon which," wherefore = "for which reason." All of them are formal, and — this is the trap — none of them has anything to do with place. They inherited the shape of where and the meaning of the preposition. The same pattern once produced whereat, whereon, whereto, and wherewith, which are now fully archaic; two more, wherefrom and whereunto, are documented below because they still turn up in old deeds and in scriptural prose.

All of them but one are true relative connectors: they subordinate, they cannot begin a sentence, and a comma is the most punctuation they ever take. Wherefore is the odd one out — it behaves like the conjunctive adverb therefore, which means it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it and it can open a sentence in legal drafting. Sorting whereupon (comma) from wherefore (semicolon) is the single thing to get right in this section.

No comma before whereby — the clause defines the arrangement it follows, so it is restrictive by nature. A nonrestrictive whereby is possible but so rare it reads as an error; use by which with commas instead. Whereby never begins a sentence, and it never takes a semicolon.

Relative pronouns#

A relative pronoun does two jobs at once: it links its clause to a noun in the main sentence, and it fills a grammatical slot inside its own clause — subject, object, or object of a preposition. That double duty is why there is always a "gap" after a relative pronoun (the book that I read ∅) and why case matters (who vs whom).

Three rules govern the whole group. First, the comma rule: restrictive (identifying) clauses take no commas; nonrestrictive (adding) clauses take commas on both sides. Second, the that/which rule: in edited American prose, restrictive clauses about things take that, nonrestrictive clauses about things take which — and that can never follow a comma. British and older American writing uses restrictive which freely, so you will meet it. Third, the zero relative: in a restrictive clause you may delete the relative pronoun when it is the object (the email I sent), but not when it is the subject (the email that arrived — you cannot drop that). Nonrestrictive clauses never allow deletion. That deletion is common enough to be its own headword, zero relative, at the end of this section.

Relative adverbs#

A relative adverb replaces a preposition-plus-which: the day when we met = the day on which we met; the town where I grew up = the town in which I grew up; the reason why he left = the reason for which he left. There are exactly three, and each is locked to one kind of antecedent — when to a time noun, where to a place noun, why to the noun reason. All three follow the standard comma rule, and all three can be dropped in a restrictive clause (the day we met), which is what makes them feel so much lighter than on which.

Where is documented in full among the subordinators of place above, since that is where learners meet it first.

Fused relatives (the -ever words)#

A fused relative packs the antecedent and the relative word into one word: whoever means "the person who," whatever means "the thing that," wherever means "the place where." Because the head is fused in, the whole clause behaves as a noun phrase — it can be the subject of the main verb, the object of a verb, or the object of a preposition.

That produces the one punctuation rule worth memorizing for this group. When the -ever clause fills a slot in the main clause, there is no comma: Whoever parked here is getting towed. When the same word instead introduces a concessive clause meaning "no matter who/what/which," it is an adverbial, it is fronted, and it does take a comma: Whatever you decide, I'll back you. Ask whether the clause is doing the job of a noun or commenting on the sentence, and the comma follows.

Case is decided inside the clause, not outside it: in Give it to whoever answers, the word is the subject of answers, so whoever is correct despite following the preposition to. The place member of this family, wherever, is documented among the place subordinators above.

The group also has an older, heavier wing built on -soever rather than -ever — whatsoever, whichsoever, whosoever, whomsoever, and the wheresoever filed with the place words. These are not intensified versions of the modern forms so much as period spellings of them: they carry the same grammar and the same commas, but the register jumps from neutral to scriptural or statutory, and a writer who reaches for one in ordinary prose has changed costume, not emphasis. A thinner wing still is the poetic one, in which -ever is elided to -e'er to save the line a syllable; the whole set of those is gathered under whate'er.

whosoever#

Pronunciation
/ˌhusoʊˈɛvər/ · hoo-soh-EV-er
Русский
кто бы ни; всякий, кто — устар., библ.: усиленное whoever
Type
fused relative pronoun (subject case); also a concessive subordinator
Register
archaic; survives in scripture, hymn text, and legal drafting — never neutral
Clause type
nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
Pattern
Whosoever [verb phrase] + [main verb] — clause as subject. · [verb/preposition] whosoever [verb phrase] — clause as object of the outer verb, with whosoever still the subject inside. · Whosoever [clause], [main clause] — concessive. · to whomsoever [clause] — the object partner, used after a preposition that governs the pronoun itself.
Punctuation
No comma between a subject whosoever clause and the main verb, and none between a preposition and whosoever. Comma after a fronted concessive clause, exactly as with whoever. The King James comma after the subject clause (Whosoever believeth in him, should not perish) is seventeenth-century practice and is an error today.
Variants
whoever (the living equivalent in every sense); whomsoever (object case); whoso (older still); whosesoever (rare possessive)

The -soever member of the whoever family, built with the same intensifying suffix as wheresoever and whatsoever, and meaning "any person at all who." It is not a stylistic upgrade on whoever — it is a period costume, and outside scripture, hymnody, and statutory drafting it reads as parody. Its object form whomsoever follows the same rule as whomever: it is correct only when the pronoun is the object inside its own clause, so to whomsoever shall apply is a hypercorrection for to whosoever shall apply.

  1. "Whosoever shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father."
  2. The trust shall pay the balance to whosoever survives the settlor.
  3. Whosoever drafted this indemnity clause did not read the rest of the contract.
  4. Whosoever the next tenant turns out to be, the easement runs with the land.
  5. The reward is offered to whosoever returns the manuscript intact.
  6. She addressed the letter, half seriously, to whomsoever it might concern.
