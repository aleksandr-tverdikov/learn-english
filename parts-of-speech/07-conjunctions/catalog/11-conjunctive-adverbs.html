Conjunctive adverbs and transitional connectors#

The words that carry a conjunction's meaning — addition, contrast, result, sequence — while behaving grammatically as adverbs, which is why they take a semicolon or a period rather than a comma.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Every word in this file expresses a relationship a real conjunction could express — however means roughly what but means, therefore means roughly what so means — but not one of them is a conjunction. They are adverbs, and the difference is not academic: a conjunction joins two independent clauses across a comma, while a conjunctive adverb joins nothing at all. Write It rained, however we went anyway and you have produced a comma splice, the most common punctuation error in American English. Those two clauses need a semicolon or a period between them: It rained; however, we went anyway. / It rained. However, we went anyway.

What these words get in exchange for the heavier punctuation is mobility, and mobility is the test that identifies them. A conjunctive adverb can slide anywhere inside its clause — However, the results held. / The results, however, held. / The results held, however. — while but is welded to the seam between the two clauses and cannot move an inch. So one three-part rule runs through this entire category: semicolon or period before; comma after it in front position, or commas on both sides in the middle; and never a bare comma joining the clauses. Two riders. Short adverbs (then, thus, hence, still, also, next, instead) routinely drop the following comma, while the multi-word phrases (on the other hand, as a result, in other words) essentially never do. And when the adverb sits inside a single clause rather than between two, the semicolon has nothing to do — only the commas apply.

Addition and continuation#

additionally#

Pronunciation: /əˈdɪʃənəli/ · uh-DISH-uh-nuh-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition Pattern: [Independent clause]; additionally, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Additionally, [independent clause]. · [Subject], additionally, [predicate]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] and, additionally, [verb phrase]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle. A comma alone before it is a comma splice.

Adds one more item to a list of points, and lives mostly in reports, memos, and academic prose. It is the driest of the additive connectors: moreover claims the new point is weightier and besides claims it settles the question, while additionally simply says "and one more thing."

  1. The lease covers utilities; additionally, the landlord pays for trash pickup.
  2. Additionally, we recommend replacing the smoke detectors on the second floor.
  3. Enrollment fell six percent. Additionally, three instructors resigned mid-semester.
  4. The grant funds two positions and, additionally, covers travel to the annual conference.
  5. Applicants must submit transcripts; additionally, two letters of recommendation are required.
  6. The lease, additionally, forbids subletting for the first year.

See also: in addition, also, furthermore, moreover, and

again#

Pronunciation: /əˈɡɛn/, also /əˈɡeɪn/ · uh-GEN, also uh-GAYN Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (one more point of the same kind). Also an ordinary time adverb meaning "once more" (play it again), which sits in the predicate and takes no commas. Pattern: Again, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; again, [independent clause]. · [Subject], again, [predicate]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] again. (time adverb — "once more," no commas) Punctuation: Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are involved — a bare comma there is a comma splice. The commas are also what separate the two words: Again, the vote failed ("here is one more point") versus The vote failed again ("a second time"). Variants: once again (time only), then again (concession — a different connector), likewise

The additive again introduces one more instance of the same point rather than the repetition of an event — the "and here's another" of argument, lecture, and testimony. Position and commas do all the work, since stranded at the end of a predicate the same word is the ordinary adverb meaning "a second time."

  1. Again, the department had no written policy on any of this.
  2. The first draft ignored the budget; again, so did the second.
  3. Consider Ohio. Again, the pattern holds: turnout rose in every county that added a polling place.
  4. Her testimony, again, was never challenged by either side.
  5. Again, I want to be clear that nobody has been accused of anything.
  6. He read the paragraph again before signing it. (time adverb — "once more," not a connector)

See also: also, additionally, likewise, then again, furthermore

also#

Pronunciation: /ˈɔlsoʊ/ · AWL-soh Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition Pattern: [Independent clause]; [subject] also [verb phrase]. · [Subject] [auxiliary] also [main verb]. · [Subject] [be] also [complement]. · Also, [independent clause]. Punctuation: A semicolon or period before the clause it sits in, because also joins nothing itself — The clinic is closed Sundays, it also closes early on Fridays is a comma splice. Inside the clause it takes no commas at all: right after the auxiliary or be, or immediately before a one-word main verb. Comma after it only when it opens a sentence.

The everyday additive adverb, and the one learners most often try to use as a conjunction. And joins; also joins nothing — it simply marks the clause it sits in as an addition to the one before it. Opening a sentence with Also is standard in speech and general writing, though conservative editors prefer In addition in formal prose.

  1. She speaks Portuguese, and she also reads Latin.
  2. The clinic is closed Sundays; it also closes early on Fridays.
  3. Also, don't forget to sign the last page.
  4. We're out of eggs. Also, the milk went bad.
  5. He also mentioned that the deadline had moved to the fifteenth.
  6. The apartment is cheap. It's also the only one with a dishwasher.

See also: additionally, in addition, besides, and

besides#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈsaɪdz/ · bih-SIDES Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: informal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (clinching reason). Also a preposition meaning "in addition to" or "apart from," which takes a noun phrase instead of a clause. Pattern: [Independent clause]. Besides, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; besides, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause], and besides, [independent clause]. · Besides [noun phrase], [independent clause]. (preposition) Punctuation: Period or semicolon before it, comma after it — a bare comma between the two clauses is a comma splice. As a preposition it takes no semicolon and no comma of its own, only the comma that closes a fronted phrase: Besides Dan, nobody came. Variants: besides [noun phrase] (the prepositional form: besides the cost), anyhow, what's more

Adds the reason the speaker was saving: the clincher meant to end the discussion. It covers the same additive ground as moreover but is far more conversational, and it usually carries an undertone of "and anyway, that settles it." Beside is a different word — the preposition meaning "next to" — and not a spelling variant of this one.

  1. I don't want to drive four hours. Besides, the tickets are sold out.
  2. The couch won't fit through the door; besides, we can't afford it.
  3. "You should apologize." "For what? Besides, she started it."
  4. It's too late to call him. Besides, he never picks up.
  5. The job pays less, and besides, I'd have to move to Cleveland.
  6. Besides the two of us, nobody knew the combination. (preposition — a noun phrase follows, not a clause)

See also: moreover, anyway, in addition, after all

besides which#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈsaɪdz ˌwɪtʃ/ · bih-SIDES-wich Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: informal to neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (a clinching extra reason). Formally a relative which pointing back at the whole clause before it, which is why it can attach across a comma. Pattern: [Independent clause], besides which [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Besides which, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; besides which, [independent clause]. Punctuation: Unlike bare besides, this one tolerates a comma before it, because the which subordinates what follows instead of leaving two independent clauses face to face: The car is twenty years old, besides which it burns oil. A period or semicolon is equally correct and is the safer choice in edited prose; the comma after it is optional and usually dropped. Variants: besides, and besides, not to mention, on top of which

Piles one more reason onto the one just given, with the same "and that settles it" force as besides. It belongs to letters, columns, and dialogue rather than to reports, and its relative-pronoun grammar is what lets it hang on the previous clause where besides would demand a full stop.

  1. The apartment is out of our price range, besides which it has no parking.
  2. He never returned the deposit. Besides which, he still has my ladder.
  3. I don't have the time; besides which, I'd be terrible at it.
  4. The report came in three weeks late, besides which two of the tables were mislabeled.
  5. She had already booked the flight, besides which her sister was expecting her.

See also: besides, moreover, what's more, for that matter, in addition

for that matter#

Pronunciation: /fər ˈðæt ˌmætər/ · fur THAT mat-ur Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral to informal Clause type: Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of addition that stretches the previous claim to a further case Pattern: [Independent clause]; for that matter, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. For that matter, [independent clause]. · [noun phrase], or [noun phrase] for that matter, [continuation]. · [Independent clause], and for that matter, [independent clause]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless and carries the join — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Tacked onto a noun phrase after or, it takes only the commas that fence that phrase. Variants: come to that (British-flavored), if it comes to that, and indeed

Extends what was just said to a further case the hearer may not have considered, usually a broader or more damning one. Where besides adds a separate reason, for that matter stretches the same point until it covers more ground: He hasn't read the contract — for that matter, neither have I.

  1. I've never met the man; for that matter, I'd never heard his name until Tuesday.
  2. The elevator hasn't worked in a month. For that matter, neither has the buzzer.
  3. She doesn't speak French, or Spanish for that matter.
  4. No one on the committee is an engineer; for that matter, no one there has ever built anything.
  5. The rule was never enforced, and for that matter, it was never printed in the handbook.

See also: besides, besides which, moreover, in fact, indeed

furthermore#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɜrðərˌmɔr/ · FUR-thur-mor Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition Pattern: [Independent clause]; furthermore, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Furthermore, [independent clause]. · [Subject], furthermore, [predicate]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] and, furthermore, [verb phrase]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause. The comma after it is not optional in edited prose. Variants: further (identical function, a shade lighter: Further, the report omits three sources.)

Adds another point pushing in the same direction as the last one and signals that the argument is still being built. Where moreover presents its point as the heavier one, furthermore merely continues the accumulation.

  1. The contract was never signed; furthermore, no payment was ever made.
  2. Furthermore, the committee failed to record two of its votes.
  3. The study used a small sample. Furthermore, it excluded everyone over sixty.
  4. She argued that the policy was expensive and, furthermore, that it had never worked anywhere.
  5. Further, the audit found no documentation for eleven thousand dollars in travel.
  6. The estimate is, furthermore, three weeks out of date.

See also: moreover, additionally, in addition, besides

in addition#

Pronunciation: /ɪn əˈdɪʃən/ · in uh-DISH-un Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition. Also in addition to, a preposition taking a noun phrase. Pattern: [Independent clause]; in addition, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. In addition, [independent clause]. · [Subject], in addition, [predicate]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] and, in addition, [object]. · In addition to [noun phrase], [independent clause]. (preposition) Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Do not confuse it with the preposition in addition to, which takes a noun phrase and no semicolon: In addition to the analysts, we hired a designer. Variants: in addition to [noun phrase] (the prepositional form, which cannot take a clause)

The neutral phrasal additive: acceptable in business writing without the starch of furthermore, and safer in formal prose than a sentence-initial Also. It adds a genuinely separate item rather than an elaboration of the previous one.

  1. The apartment includes parking; in addition, the building has a gym.
  2. In addition, all employees will receive a stipend for home internet.
  3. She teaches two seminars. In addition, she advises eleven graduate students.
  4. The fee covers materials and, in addition, one private lesson a month.
  5. In addition to the salary, the offer includes equity. (preposition — a noun phrase follows, not a clause)
  6. Two of the exhibits, in addition, were never entered into evidence.

See also: additionally, also, furthermore, moreover

moreover#

Pronunciation: /mɔrˈoʊvər/ · mor-OH-vur Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (reinforcing) Pattern: [Independent clause]; moreover, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Moreover, [independent clause]. · [Subject], moreover, [predicate]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides when it sits inside the clause. Variants: what's more (the informal spoken equivalent)

Adds a point that reinforces and outweighs the one before it — the "and what's more" of formal argument. Save it for reasoning: using moreover to add a second item to a grocery list is the classic register mistake.

  1. The device is cheaper than its rivals; moreover, it uses half the power.
  2. Moreover, no one in the department was told the position existed.
  3. The translation is inaccurate. Moreover, whole paragraphs are missing.
  4. The witness had no reason to lie and, moreover, nothing to gain by it.
  5. The lease is expiring; what's more, the rent is going up forty percent.
  6. The statute, moreover, has never once been enforced.

See also: furthermore, besides, additionally, indeed

what's more#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌts ˈmɔr/ · wuts MOR Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: informal to neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (reinforcing) Pattern: [Independent clause]; what's more, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. What's more, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause], and what's more, [independent clause]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless and carries the join — and a comma after it. Front position only; it does not close a clause. Written out as what is more it is a shade more formal and identically punctuated. Variants: what is more (formal), moreover (the formal equivalent), on top of that, better still

The conversational moreover: it adds a second point and claims the second one hits harder. It belongs to speech, journalism, and sales copy — in a brief or a dissertation, moreover is the expected form.

  1. The tickets were half price; what's more, they came with parking.
  2. He missed the deadline. What's more, he never told anyone he was going to.
  3. The house needs a roof, and what's more, the furnace is thirty years old.
  4. What's more, the same company had been fined twice before.
  5. She finished the course in half the time; what is more, she did it while working nights.

See also: moreover, besides, furthermore, in addition, indeed

withal#

Pronunciation: /wɪˈðɔl/ · with-AWL Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: archaic, literary Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition ("besides, moreover"), and in later use of concession ("nevertheless"). Also an archaic postposed preposition meaning "with" (the tools he worked withal). Pattern: [Independent clause]; withal, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [verb phrase], withal. · [adjective] and withal [adjective]. · [noun phrase] [relative clause] withal. (archaic preposition — "with") Punctuation: Comma before it in final position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period where two independent clauses meet. Mid-phrase it is often printed with no commas at all — a grave and withal kindly man — which is how Shakespeare's editors set it. Variants: therewithal (rarer), moreover, nevertheless (for the concessive sense)

Elizabethan and Victorian English for "besides, and along with that," drifting toward "nevertheless" by the nineteenth century. An American meets it in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and period novels and essentially nowhere else; written today it reads as costume.

  1. "A goodly gentleman, and withal a scholar." (period diction)
  2. He was a hard master, withal a just one.
  3. The room was cold, dim, and withal strangely comfortable.
  4. She kept the accounts, managed the tenants, and withal found time to write.
  5. The wages were small; withal, the family wanted for nothing.
  6. He had nothing to defend himself withal. (the archaic preposition — "with," not a connector)

See also: moreover, besides, nevertheless, notwithstanding, howbeit

Contrast, concession, and reversal#

admittedly#

Pronunciation: /ədˈmɪtɪdli/ · ad-MIT-id-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of concession Pattern: [Independent clause]; admittedly, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Admittedly, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary], admittedly, [complement]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary] admittedly [complement]. (integrated — no commas) · [Independent clause] — admittedly — [continuation]. Punctuation: Comma after it in front position; commas or dashes on both sides in mid position. Period or semicolon before it when two independent clauses are involved — never a bare comma.

Concedes a point against your own argument before you go on to answer it. Granted does the same job conversationally and of course can sound impatient doing it; admittedly is the neutral written form, and it commits the writer to a but somewhere soon after.

  1. Admittedly, the first draft was a mess.
  2. The plan is expensive; admittedly, it is also the only one that has ever worked.
  3. She was, admittedly, the least experienced candidate on the shortlist.
  4. Admittedly, I have never actually read the whole report.
  5. The evidence is thin — admittedly so — but the pattern is hard to ignore.
  6. The design is admittedly dated, but everything still works. (integrated into the clause — no commas)

See also: granted, of course, that said, even so

at the same time#

Pronunciation: /ət ðə ˌseɪm ˈtaɪm/ · ut thuh saym TIME Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of concession (two things true at once). Also a plain time adverbial meaning "simultaneously," which belongs to the predicate. Pattern: [Independent clause]. At the same time, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; at the same time, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary], at the same time, [complement]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] and, at the same time, [verb phrase]. · [Subject] [verb phrase] at the same time. (literal simultaneity — no commas) Punctuation: Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. In the literal "simultaneously" sense it is part of the predicate and takes no commas at all: Both alarms went off at the same time. Variants: at once (literal sense only), meanwhile (simultaneity only), then again, that said, yet (the coordinating equivalent, which takes a comma)

Holds two facts in the frame together and asks the reader to keep both: "that is true, and so is this." The connector sense is concessive, not temporal — meanwhile puts two events on a clock, while at the same time puts two judgments on a scale. The literal sense survives right beside it, and only the commas tell them apart.

  1. The layoffs were necessary; at the same time, they were handled badly.
  2. I want her to take the job. At the same time, I'd hate to see her move across the country.
  3. She's the best coach we've had. She is, at the same time, impossible to work for.
  4. The report praises the program and, at the same time, recommends cutting its budget.
  5. Both alarms went off at the same time. (literal simultaneity — no commas, not a connector)

See also: meanwhile, on the other hand, even so, then again, however

contrariwise#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɑntrɛriˌwaɪz/ · KAHN-treh-ree-wize Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal, literary (dated) Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (the opposite case) Pattern: [Independent clause]; contrariwise, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Contrariwise, [independent clause]. · [Subject], contrariwise, [predicate]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Variants: conversely (the current formal word), on the contrary, contrarily (rare), per contra

The nineteenth-century ancestor of conversely: it flips the previous statement and asserts the reverse case. In American English it survives mainly in quotation and deliberate whimsy — Tweedledee's "Contrariwise, if it was so, it might be" is where most readers have met it — so conversely is the word for real formal prose.

  1. "Contrariwise," continued Tweedledee, "if it was so, it might be; and if it were so, it would be."
  2. The northern counties gained population; contrariwise, the delta lost a fifth of its residents.
  3. Contrariwise, the older statute imposed no penalty at all.
  4. Heat expands the metal; contrariwise, cold draws it tight.
  5. He believed the letters would clear him. Contrariwise, they were what convicted him.

See also: conversely, on the contrary, per contra, in contrast, however

conversely#

Pronunciation: /kənˈvɜrsli/, also /ˈkɑnvɜrsli/ · kun-VURS-lee, also KAHN-vurs-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (logical reversal) Pattern: [Independent clause]; conversely, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Conversely, [independent clause]. · [Subject], conversely, [predicate]. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause.

Introduces the exact reverse of what was just stated — the same terms with their positions swapped. Use it only when the second clause really is the mirror image; where two ideas are merely being weighed against each other, the honest choice is on the other hand.

  1. Warm weather lifts ice cream sales; conversely, a cold July kills them.
  2. Small firms adapted within months. Conversely, the largest banks took years.
  3. If the price rises, demand falls; conversely, if the price drops, demand climbs.
  4. Adding staff shortened the wait. Conversely, every cut lengthened it.
  5. He finds mornings easy and evenings impossible; his wife, conversely, comes alive after ten.

See also: in contrast, on the other hand, on the contrary, however

even so#

Pronunciation: /ˌivən ˈsoʊ/ · ee-vun SOH Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of concession Pattern: [Independent clause]. Even so, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; even so, [independent clause]. · [Subject], even so, [predicate]. · [Independent clause] and, even so, [verb phrase]. Punctuation: Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Variants: all the same, just the same (both more conversational)

The plain-English nevertheless: it grants everything just said and then holds the original position anyway. However only marks a contrast; even so explicitly concedes first, which is why it so often follows a sentence the speaker has just agreed with.

  1. The tickets were nonrefundable. Even so, the airline gave us a credit.
  2. He apologized twice; even so, she didn't return his calls.
  3. It's a nine-hour drive. Even so, I'd rather go than fly.
  4. We knew the odds going in. Even so, losing hurt.
  5. She had trained for a year and, even so, finished dead last.
  6. The house was, even so, the best thing we saw all weekend.

See also: nevertheless, nonetheless, still, that said, at the same time, anyway

granted#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡræntɪd/ · GRAN-tid Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: informal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of concession Pattern: Granted, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Granted. [Independent clause]. · Granted that [clause], [main clause]. (subordinating use) Punctuation: Comma after it in front position, and a period or semicolon before it when a full clause precedes — The team is young; granted, so was last year's squad, never a bare comma. It can also stand alone as a one-word sentence (Granted.). Add that and it becomes a subordinating conjunction taking a full clause, punctuated like although: a comma after the granted that clause when that clause comes first, and no comma when it follows the main clause. Variants: granted that (+ clause), true, fair enough (informal), to be sure (formal)

Concedes the other side's point out loud, almost always to set up a but. It is the conversational cousin of admittedly, and it is the one connector in this family that can be a complete turn in a conversation by itself.

  1. Granted, the price is steep — but you'll use it every day.
  2. He's rude. Granted. That doesn't make him wrong.
  3. Granted, I've only seen the first season.
  4. The team is young; granted, so was last year's championship squad.
  5. Granted that the data is five years old, the trend is still unmistakable.

See also: admittedly, of course, even so, that said

however#

Pronunciation: /haʊˈɛvər/ · how-EV-ur Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral (formal-leaning in front position) Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast. Also a subordinating conjunction meaning "no matter how," which does introduce a real adverbial clause. Pattern: [Independent clause]; however, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. However, [independent clause]. · [Subject], however, [predicate]. · [Independent clause]; [independent clause], however. Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is the textbook comma splice (It rained, however we went is wrong). Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. The subordinator however takes none of that: However you slice it, the numbers don't work.

The default contrast connector of written English: it marks its clause as running against the expectation the previous sentence created. But does the same work as a coordinating conjunction with only a comma. Everything that separates the two words follows from however being an adverb — the heavier punctuation, and the freedom to move.

  1. The flight was delayed four hours; however, the airline paid for our hotel.
  2. We expected a crowd. However, only nine people showed up.
  3. The proposal is thorough. The budget, however, is pure fiction.
  4. She promised she'd call. She never did, however.
  5. However you slice it, the numbers don't work. (here however is a subordinator meaning "no matter how," not this connector)

See also: nevertheless, still, on the other hand, that said, but

in contrast#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈkɑntræst/ · in KAHN-trast Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (two cases compared). Also in contrast to / in contrast with, a preposition taking a noun phrase. Pattern: [Independent clause]. In contrast, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; in contrast, [independent clause]. · [Subject], in contrast, [predicate]. · In contrast to [noun phrase], [independent clause]. (preposition) Punctuation: Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Adding to or with makes it a preposition that must be followed by a noun phrase: In contrast to last year, sales rose. Variants: by contrast; in contrast to / in contrast with [noun phrase] (prepositional)

Sets two things side by side and points at the difference between them. Unlike however, it makes no claim that the second fact is surprising — only that it differs — which makes it the workhorse of comparative analysis and data commentary.

  1. Rural districts lost population. In contrast, the suburbs grew nine percent.
  2. The first novel is spare and cold; in contrast, the second is almost sentimental.
  3. Japanese firms invested in retraining. American firms, in contrast, cut headcount.

Pronunciation: /ɪn pərˈtɪkjələr/ · in pur-TIK-yuh-lur Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of specification (singling one case out of a set already mentioned). Also a postmodifier attached directly to a noun phrase. Pattern: [Independent clause]; in particular, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. In particular, [independent clause]. · [Subject], in particular, [predicate]. · [Noun phrase] in particular [verb phrase]. (postmodifier — no commas) Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Attached to a noun phrase it takes no commas at all: one paragraph in particular worried the lawyers. Variants: particularly, especially, notably, above all (strongest), specifically (exactness rather than prominence)

Picks one member out of a group already named and says it matters most. For example offers a sample and claims nothing about it; in particular claims prominence — this one above the others — which is why it so often carries the weight of the argument.

  1. The storm damaged three schools; in particular, the roof of the high school gym came down.
  2. Costs are up across the board. In particular, insurance has nearly doubled since 2019.
  3. One paragraph in particular worried the lawyers. (attached to the noun phrase — no commas)
  4. Older drivers, in particular, benefit from the larger displays.
  5. In particular, no employee may accept a gift worth more than fifty dollars.

See also: specifically, namely, for example, that is, indeed

namely#

Pronunciation: /ˈneɪmli/ · NAYM-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: formal Clause type: No clause of its own — it introduces the exact identification of something already named in general terms Pattern: [clause], namely, [list]. · [clause] — namely, [item]. · [noun phrase]: namely, [item]. Punctuation: A comma, dash, or colon before it; the comma after it is optional (namely, the cost and namely the cost are both standard). It does not take a semicolon, because what follows is a phrase, not a clause. Variants: viz. (very formal, from Latin videlicet), to wit (legal), specifically, that is

Introduces the complete and exact identification of what was just referred to generally — all of it, not a sample. For example offers one of many; namely closes the set, which is why it is the safer word in contracts and specifications.

  1. Only one thing worried her, namely the cost.
  2. Three states border the lake, namely Ohio, Michigan, and Indiana.
  3. The committee has a single job: namely, to decide whether the site is safe.
  4. He left out the most important detail — namely, that he had already resigned.
  5. Two witnesses were called, viz., the driver and the paramedic.

See also: that is, for example, i.e., in other words, specifically

specifically#

Pronunciation: /spəˈsɪfɪkli/ · spuh-SIF-ik-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of specification. Also a plain manner adverb meaning "expressly, for that purpose," which sits in the predicate. Pattern: [Independent clause]; specifically, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Specifically, [independent clause]. · [clause], specifically, [noun phrase]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary] specifically [verb phrase]. (manner adverb — no commas) Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Before a bare phrase rather than a clause, a comma or dash is enough. As a manner adverb inside the predicate it takes no commas: the grant was specifically earmarked for equipment. Variants: namely (for a closed list), that is, to be specific, more precisely, in particular (prominence rather than exactness)

Narrows a general statement down to the exact case, and it is the one word in this family that takes either a full clause or a bare phrase after it. Namely closes a set; specifically sharpens a description — and where i.e. restates the same thing in other terms, specifically moves one step down in generality.

  1. The audit found problems; specifically, four invoices had no matching purchase order.
  2. Attendance is down. Specifically, weeknight showings have lost a third of their audience.
  3. She objected to one line, specifically, the clause about weekend hours.
  4. Specifically, the ordinance bars parking on the north side between two and six.
  5. The room was built specifically for recording. (manner adverb — "expressly," not a connector)

strictly speaking#

Pronunciation: /ˌstrɪktli ˈspikɪŋ/ · strik-tlee SPEE-king Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: No clause of its own — a comment adverbial narrowing what follows to its exact, technical truth. Grammatically a fronted participial phrase, and nothing in the sentence has to be doing the speaking. Pattern: Strictly speaking, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [be], strictly speaking, [complement]. · [Independent clause], strictly speaking. Punctuation: Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. It joins no clauses on its own, so where two independent clauses meet the usual semicolon or period is still required. Variants: technically, properly speaking, strictly, in the strict sense

Signals that what follows is true by the exact definition, and usually that ordinary usage says otherwise: Strictly speaking, a tomato is a fruit. Its opposites are generally speaking and broadly speaking, and its constant companion is a mild pedantry the listener did not ask for.

  1. Strictly speaking, the building is in the township, not the city.
  2. She is, strictly speaking, an independent contractor rather than an employee.
  3. Strictly speaking, none of this is confidential, but I'd keep it to yourself.
  4. A koala isn't a bear, strictly speaking.
  5. Strictly speaking, the deadline is Friday; in practice, nobody reads them until Monday.

See also: generally speaking, in general, specifically, in particular, that is

that is#

Pronunciation: /ˌðæt ˈɪz/ · that IZ Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of restatement and specification Pattern: [clause], that is, [restatement]. · [Independent clause]. That is, [independent clause]. · [noun phrase] — that is, [noun phrase]. Punctuation: Comma, dash, or period before it, comma after it; commas on both sides when it sits inside a clause. Never a bare comma joining two independent clauses — use a period or semicolon there. Variants: that is to say, i.e. (the written abbreviation), or used appositively (the coda, or final section)

Restates what came before in a more exact form, narrowing rather than paraphrasing. It sits between its two neighbors: in other words rewrites the whole idea in plainer language, while namely identifies the members of a set.

  1. We'll meet on the first Tuesday, that is, the seventh.
  2. The offer applies to residents — that is, anyone who has lived here six months.
  3. She's a stringer; that is, she's paid per story rather than by salary.
  4. That is, no one had checked whether the permit was still valid.
  5. He was let go for cause, that is to say, for cause the company never described.

which is to say#

Pronunciation: /ˌwɪtʃ ɪz tə ˈseɪ/ · wich iz tuh SAY Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a continuative relative) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: A continuative relative clause that restates the whole clause before it. Because the which needs an antecedent in the same sentence, it cannot open one in edited prose — though writers break that rule for effect. Pattern: [Independent clause], which is to say, [restatement]. · [Independent clause] — which is to say, [restatement]. · [noun phrase], which is to say [noun phrase], [continuation]. Punctuation: A comma or dash before it — this is one of the few restatement connectors that takes a comma rather than a semicolon, because the relative pronoun subordinates what follows. The comma after it is optional and usually dropped before a short phrase. Variants: that is to say, that is, i.e., or rather

Restates the preceding clause in sharper or franker terms, generally to expose what it really amounts to. Its relative-clause grammar is the practical difference from in other words: it attaches across a comma where in other words would demand a semicolon or a period.

  1. The position was eliminated, which is to say he was fired.
  2. The fund is self-insured, which is to say the county pays every claim out of the general budget.
  3. They asked for volunteers — which is to say, they asked us.
  4. The trial ended in a hung jury, which is to say nothing was decided at all.
  5. The house is a fixer-upper, which is to say the floors are gone.

after all#

Pronunciation: /ˌæftər ˈɔl/ · af-tur AWL Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of justification. In final position, a different sense: "contrary to expectation." Pattern: [Independent clause]; after all, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. After all, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause] after all. ("contrary to expectation") Punctuation: Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position. In the "contrary to expectation" sense it closes the clause and takes no comma before it — She came after all, not She came, after all.

Two distinct jobs, and position tells you which one you have. As a connector it supplies a reason the hearer is expected to accept already: Don't be hard on him; after all, he's twelve. At the end of a clause it reports that the expected thing failed to happen: The flight wasn't canceled after all.

  1. Let him sleep in; after all, it's Saturday.
  2. Nobody was surprised — she'd been training for a year, after all.
  3. After all, we did agree to split the cost down the middle.
  4. The flight wasn't canceled after all. (the "contrary to expectation" sense)
  5. It turned out they had a table for us after all.

as a matter of fact#

Pronunciation: /æz ə ˌmætər əv ˈfækt/ · az uh MAT-ur uv FAKT Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral to informal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of emphatic correction or confirmation Pattern: As a matter of fact, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; as a matter of fact, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary], as a matter of fact, [complement]. · [Independent clause], as a matter of fact. Punctuation: Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end; a semicolon or period where two independent clauses meet — a bare comma there is a comma splice. Variants: in fact, actually, in point of fact (formal), matter of fact (informal and comma-free: matter of fact, I did)

Introduces a fact that contradicts, corrects, or one-ups what the listener has just assumed, and it is markedly more combative than plain in fact because the phrase itself announces that a correction is coming. In American conversation it very often prefaces a small triumph: As a matter of fact, I have.

  1. As a matter of fact, I was just about to call you.
  2. "You've never been to Texas." "As a matter of fact, I lived in Austin for two years."
  3. The tenants were never notified; as a matter of fact, the notice went to the wrong address.
  4. She isn't new here, as a matter of fact — she trained everyone in the room.
  5. As a matter of fact, the same question came up at the last meeting.

as it turns out#

Pronunciation: /æz ɪt ˈtɜrnz ˌaʊt/ · az it TURNZ owt Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link introducing a fact learned after the event, usually one that upsets the expectation just described Pattern: As it turns out, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; as it turns out, [independent clause]. · [Subject], as it turns out, [predicate]. · [Independent clause], as it turns out. Punctuation: Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. Between two independent clauses a semicolon or period is required — a bare comma is a comma splice. Variants: as it happens (a coincidence rather than a discovery), it turns out, as luck would have it, sure enough (when the expectation was confirmed instead of upset)

Reports what was learned later, and almost always marks a reversal: the thing everyone assumed turns out to be wrong. As it happens is the milder cousin, flagging a lucky coincidence rather than a correction, and it turns out is the same connector with the subordinator dropped.

  1. As it turns out, the check had cleared two days before the letter went out.
  2. We packed for rain. As it turns out, it was eighty degrees all week.
  3. The building was condemned in 1998; as it turns out, three families were still living in it.
  4. Her grandfather, as it turns out, had worked in the same plant.
  5. As it happens, I have a copy in the car.

importantly#

Pronunciation: /ɪmˈpɔrtəntli/ · im-POR-tunt-lee Type: conjunctive adverb · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of emphasis marking the following statement as significant. Also a manner adverb meaning "with a self-important air" (he nodded importantly), a wholly different word. Pattern: Importantly, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; importantly, [independent clause]. · [Subject], importantly, [predicate]. · [Subject] [verb] importantly. (manner adverb — "pompously," no commas) Punctuation: Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are joined. The commas are what keep it from being read as manner. Variants: more importantly, most importantly, crucially, notably

Marks its sentence as one of the significant ones without ranking it against anything in particular, where more importantly explicitly outranks the point before it. Traditionalists once objected that the word should only mean "in an important manner," but the sentence-adverb use has long since won.

  1. Importantly, the study was funded by the state rather than by the manufacturer.
  2. The building passed inspection; importantly, the sprinkler system was tested under load.
  3. The settlement pays the back wages and, importantly, admits no wrongdoing.
  4. Importantly, none of this was disclosed to the buyers.
  5. He cleared his throat and nodded importantly. (manner adverb — "with a self-important air")

in fact#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈfækt/ · in FAKT Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of emphasis or correction Pattern: [Independent clause]; in fact, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. In fact, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause] — in fact, [independent clause]. · [Subject] [be/auxiliary], in fact, [complement]. Punctuation: Semicolon, period, or dash before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Variants: as a matter of fact, in point of fact (formal), actually (informal), in reality

Introduces something stronger, more surprising, or flatly corrective of what the listener assumed. Compare indeed, which mostly confirms and intensifies: in fact is the word you reach for when the truth turns out to be different from the expectation.

  1. He said he'd never met her; in fact, they had worked together for three years.
  2. The repair isn't difficult. In fact, you can do it with a screwdriver.
  3. She wasn't annoyed — in fact, she seemed relieved.
  4. The building is, in fact, older than the town charter.
  5. As a matter of fact, I was just about to call you.

indeed#

Confirms the previous statement and then pushes it further — "yes, and more so." Unlike in fact, it rarely corrects anything; it doubles down. It also serves as a one-word agreement token in formal speech.

Pronunciation: /ˌmɔr ɪmˈpɔrtəntli/ · mor im-POR-tunt-lee Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of addition that ranks the new point above the last Pattern: [Independent clause]; more importantly, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Adds a point and claims it outweighs the one before it — moreover with an explicit ranking. of course#

Pronunciation: /əv ˈkɔrs/, often reduced to /əˈkɔrs/ · uv KORSS, often uh-KORSS Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of concession or shared assumption. to be honest#

Pronunciation: /tə bi ˈɑnɪst/ · tuh bee AH-nist Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: informal (spoken) Clause type: No clause of its own — a comment adverbial prefacing the speaker's frank opinion. Grammatically an infinitive phrase with no subject, held in place by convention rather than by grammar. Warns that a candid and usually unflattering opinion is coming — and, by implication, that the speaker has been holding it back. To tell the truth is the same move in slightly older clothing and truth be told is the literary version; overused, all three read as filler rather than as candor.

  1. To be honest, I'd rather stay home.
  2. The second half was a slog, to be honest.
  3. To tell the truth, nobody on the committee had read the whole thing.
  4. She is, to be honest, the only reason the department still functions.
  5. To be honest, I never expected them to call back.

truth be told#

Pronunciation: /ˌtruθ bi ˈtoʊld/ · trooth bee TOHLD Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: neutral to literary (a fixed formula) Clause type: No clause of its own — a comment adverbial prefacing an admission. Introduces something the speaker has been politely leaving out — a confession delivered with a slightly literary flourish. It is more written than to be honest, and it turns up constantly in memoir, sports columns, and obituaries.

  1. Truth be told, the house had never been worth what they paid for it.
  2. He enjoyed the attention, truth be told.
  3. Truth be told, none of us thought the team would make it past October.
  4. She was, truth be told, relieved when the store finally closed.

all in all#

Pronunciation: /ˌɔl ɪn ˈɔl/ · awl in AWL Type: conjunctive adverb (phrasal) · Register: informal to neutral Clause type: Independent clause — adverbial link of summation (a net verdict) Pattern: [Independent clause]. All in all, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]; all in all, [independent clause]. Punctuation: Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Delivers the net verdict after the details have been weighed — the everyday summing-up phrase of reviews and travel stories. It reports a judgment, where in short compresses information and in conclusion announces the end of a formal text.

  1. The flights were late and it rained twice. All in all, though, a good trip.
  2. All in all, I'd rather have the older model.
  3. There were a few rough edges; all in all, the show worked.
  4. Overall, the program has done what it promised.
  5. All things considered, we came out ahead.

The formal twin of in any case, and the standard form in legal writing, where it introduces the argument that wins even if the previous one loses: In any event, the claim is time-barred. In ordinary speech it sounds stiff, and anyway does the work.

  1. The letter may never have been sent; in any event, it was never received.
  2. In any event, the university will honor the original offer.
  3. The statute does not apply to volunteers. In any event, the plaintiff was not a volunteer.
  4. We can argue about the numbers, but the plant closes in June, in any event.
  5. In any event, nobody disputes that the money was paid.
