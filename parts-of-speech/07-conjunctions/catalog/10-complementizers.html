Complementizers and noun-clause introducers#

The words that shrink a whole clause down to the size of a noun, so that one sentence can serve as another sentence's subject or object.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Every word in this file does the same structural job: it takes a clause and makes it available as a noun — the subject of a verb, the object of a verb or a preposition, the complement of an adjective, or an appositive to a noun. Some of them (that, whether, if, for) are pure markers that carry no meaning and fill no role inside the clause they introduce; the wh-words (what, who, when, how) both introduce the clause and occupy a slot in it; the -ever forms fold a head noun and a relative clause into a single word. What separates the whole category from the rest of the conjunction system is that a noun clause is an argument — delete it and the sentence collapses (I know … what?) — while an adverbial clause is a modifier the sentence can survive without.

Three rules run through everything below. Never put a comma between a verb and its noun clause: He said that he was tired, not He said, that he was tired — the comma belongs only with a direct quotation (He said, "I'm tired."). An embedded question keeps statement word order and takes no question mark: I asked where the office is, never I asked where is the office? And the however in this file is not the however that means "nevertheless" — that one is a conjunctive adverb, and between two independent clauses it requires a semicolon or a period before it and a comma after it (We left early; however, the traffic was already bad); a comma in the semicolon's place is a comma splice. Any fronted-clause comma you see below (Whatever happens, call me) belongs to a different construction — the concessive adverbial — and not to the noun clause.

The bare complementizers#

Markers that fill no slot inside their own clause and take no comma at the junction with the verb, noun, or adjective they complete — not even when the clause they head is the subject of the sentence. Most are pure grammar and contribute no meaning of their own; like, as if, and as though carry a faint hypothetical tint, which is exactly what formal writing weighs when it chooses among the three. Three of these headwords (as if, as though, whether or not) also do adverbial work elsewhere, and in that other job — never in this one — a fronted clause takes a comma.

as if#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈɪf/ · az IF — the first word almost always reduces to /əz/, and the stress sits on if Type: complementizer (compound); the same pair is also a manner-and-comparison subordinator and the standalone retort As if! · Register: neutral — the formal counterpart of complementizer like Clause type: noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (seem, look, sound, feel, appear), and of be in the fixed It's not as if frame Pattern: It [seems/looks/sounds/appears] as if [clause]. · [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] as if [clause]. · It [verb of appearance] to [object] as if [clause]. · It's not as if [clause]. · It's not as if [clause], but [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma before as if in this use: it stands between a verb and its own complement, and that junction never takes a comma. The comparison as if is the one with a comma rule, and only when it is fronted (As if nothing had happened, he sat down). Deletion tells you which one you have — It seems by itself is unfinished, so that as if clause is an argument and takes no comma; He talks by itself is a whole sentence, so that one is a modifier and can be moved. Variants: as though (interchangeable, a shade more literary); like (informal); that after seem and appear (It seems that nobody read it)

The formal substitute for complementizer like, and the form to reach for the moment the writing stops being conversational. Mood is the detail Americans split on: when the clause is contrary to fact, careful writing keeps the past subjunctive (It looked as if he were joking), and when the situation may well be true the indicative is normal and far commoner (It looks as if the flight is on time). Keep it apart from the manner as if of He talks as if he owns the place: that one modifies a verb the sentence survives without and can be fronted, while this one is an argument and cannot move anywhere.

  1. It seems as if nobody bothered to open the attachment.
  2. It's not as if I had a choice in the matter.
  3. The engine sounds as if something came loose underneath.
  4. I feel as if we skipped a step somewhere in this recipe.
  5. From up here it looks as if the whole street lost power.
  6. It appeared to the auditors as if the invoices had been backdated.

See also: as though, like, that, whether, as if (comparison)

as though#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈðoʊ/ · az THOH — the first word reduces to /əz/; the stress falls on though Type: complementizer (compound); the same pair is also a manner-and-comparison subordinator · Register: neutral to formal — a shade more literary than as if Clause type: noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (seem, look, sound, feel, appear), and of be in the It's not as though frame Pattern: It [seems/looks/sounds/appears] as though [clause]. · [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] as though [clause]. · It [verb of appearance] to [object] as though [clause]. · It's not as though [clause]. · It's not as though [clause], but [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma before as though when it completes a verb of appearance or sensation — the junction between a verb and its complement never takes one. The comparison as though is the one that takes a comma, and only when fronted: As though on cue, the lights came back on. That construction is a modifier, not an argument. Variants: as if (same meaning, commoner in speech); like (informal); that after seem and appear

The near-twin of as if, genuinely interchangeable with it in meaning and differing only in texture: as though reads slightly more literary and is mildly favored after verbs of feeling (It felt as though hours had passed). Like as if, it does double duty — a complementizer when the clause completes seem, look, sound, feel, or appear, and an adverbial of manner when it modifies an ordinary verb (He shrugged as though the whole thing bored him). Only the adverbial one can be fronted, and only the adverbial one ever takes a comma.

  1. It felt as though hours had passed in that waiting room.
  2. It seems as though every form here has to be filed twice.
  3. It's not as though nobody warned us about the fees.
  4. The house looked as though no one had lived there in years.
  5. It sounded as though the furnace kicked on and then quit.
  6. It seemed to everyone in the room as though the decision had already been made.

See also: as if, like, that, as though (comparison)

if#

Pronunciation: /ɪf/ · IF — reduced to /əf/ in connected speech (I don't know əf he's home) Type: complementizer; the same spelling is also the subordinating conjunction of condition · Register: neutral, leaning informal Clause type: noun clause (indirect yes-no question) Pattern: [subject] [verb] if [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] if [clause]. · [imperative verb] if [clause]. · [subject] [verb] if [clause] or not. Punctuation: No comma before if and none inside the clause. Conditional if is the one that takes a comma when its clause is fronted (If it rains, we'll drive); a noun-clause if can never be fronted at all, so no comma question ever arises. Variants: if … or not — the or not can only trail the clause (if he's coming or not), never sit next to if

The everyday spoken alternative to whether for indirect yes-no questions. It cannot go everywhere whether goes: no if-clause may follow a preposition, precede a to-infinitive, or serve as the subject of a sentence. It is also ambiguous between condition and question — Let me know if he calls can mean "in the event he calls" or "tell me whether he calls" — and swapping in whether is the standard fix. Keep it separate from the conditional if, which is a different word wearing the same three letters: that one heads an adverbial clause the sentence survives without, moves to the front, and takes a comma there; this one is an argument the verb cannot do without and cannot move anywhere.

  1. I don't know if the pharmacy on Third is still open.
  2. She asked if I'd ever driven a stick shift.
  3. Check if the milk's expired before you pour it.
  4. He wondered if anyone had noticed him slip out.
  5. Nobody could tell me if the flight was delayed or not.
  6. See if the hardware store carries that size.

See also: whether, whether or not, that, as to whether, if (conditional)

like#

Pronunciation: /laɪk/ · LYKE — unstressed in this use and often reduced to /lək/ (It's not lək I had a choice) Type: complementizer (informal); the same spelling is also a preposition, a comparison subordinator, a verb, a noun, a quotative (she was like, "no way"), and a discourse particle · Register: informal — universal in speech, ordinary in fiction and journalism, still corrected in academic and formal business writing Clause type: noun clause — complement of a verb of appearance or sensation (seem, look, sound, feel, act), and of be in the fixed It's not like frame Pattern: It [seems/looks/sounds] like [clause]. · [subject] [seems/looks/sounds/feels] like [clause]. · [subject] [verb of acting] like [clause]. · It's not like [clause]. · It's not like [clause], but [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma before like and none inside the clause: it stands between a verb and its own complement, and that junction never takes a comma. The discourse particle like is the one that gets commas (It was, like, three hours), and it is a different word entirely. Variants: formal substitutes as if / as though (It seems as though nobody read it); that after feel and seem (I feel that we're missing a step)

The complement-clause like, as opposed to the comparison like of She sings like her mother did. The test is whether the clause is an argument: It seems and I feel are unfinishable without what follows, so the like-clause is filling a noun slot, not modifying anything. Americans use it constantly and edited prose still resists it, so the usable rule is to keep it in speech and dialogue and swap in as if, as though, or that the moment the writing turns formal. The It's not like… frame is fixed and idiomatic, and no substitution improves it.

  1. It seems like nobody bothered to open the attachment.
  2. I feel like we skipped a step somewhere in this recipe.
  3. It's not like I had a choice in the matter.
  4. The engine sounds like something came loose underneath.
  5. She acts like she's never seen a parking meter before.
  6. Honestly, it looks like the whole thing was decided before the meeting started.

See also: as if, as though, that, zero that, not that, like (comparison)

that#

Pronunciation: /ðæt/ · THAT — as a complementizer it is nearly always reduced to /ðət/ · thut; the full vowel signals the demonstrative (I said /ðæt/) Type: complementizer; the same spelling is also a relative pronoun, a demonstrative determiner and pronoun, and a degree adverb · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (declarative content clause) — subject, object, subject complement, adjective complement, or noun appositive Pattern: [subject] [verb] that [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] that [clause]. · That [clause] [verb phrase]. · It [verb] [object] that [clause]. · [noun] that [clause]. · [adjective] that [clause]. · The [noun] is that [clause]. Punctuation: Never a comma before complementizer that — He said, that he was tired is an error. A parenthetical inserted right after it takes commas on both sides: He said that, if it rains, they'll cancel. No comma either when the that-clause is a fronted subject: That he lied surprised no one. Variants: zero that — see zero that

The workhorse of the category: a pure marker that packages a statement into a noun-sized chunk a verb, noun, or adjective can take as its complement. Four different words share the spelling, and only this one is a conjunction. Unlike relative that (the box that came yesterday), it fills no role inside its own clause and leaves no gap, which is exactly why it can usually be deleted; unlike demonstrative that (I said that), it carries no meaning and takes no stress; unlike the degree adverb that (I'm not that hungry), it modifies nothing. Where whether and if introduce a question, that introduces an assertion.

  1. She admitted that she'd forgotten the deadline entirely.
  2. That he never called back tells you everything.
  3. It's obvious that they rehearsed this.
  4. There's a rumor that the plant is closing in March.
  5. I'm just glad that you two finally met.
  6. The problem is that nobody wants to pay for the repairs.

See also: zero that, whether, if, like, the fact that, not that, but that

whether#

Pronunciation: /ˈwɛðər/ · WEH-thur — a homophone of weather for most Americans; a conservative /ˈhwɛðər/ survives in parts of the South and Midwest Type: complementizer; also the first half of the correlative pair whether … or · Register: neutral, leaning formal Clause type: noun clause (indirect yes-no question, or alternative question) Pattern: [verb] whether [clause]. · Whether [clause] [verb phrase]. · [preposition] whether [clause]. · [verb] whether to [verb]. · [verb] whether [clause] or [clause]. Punctuation: No comma before whether, none inside the clause, and none after a fronted subject clause: Whether he comes is up to him. A fronted concessive whether … or … clause is a different construction and does take a comma: Whether you like it or not, it's happening. Variants: whether or not; archaic whether or no

The complementizer of indirect yes-no questions and of stated alternatives, and the neutral choice everywhere if is barred: after a preposition (depends on whether), before an infinitive (whether to go), at the head of a subject clause, and any time if would be misread as a condition. Against that, the difference is settled versus open — I know that she's coming asserts it; I know whether she's coming only claims to have the answer.

  1. It depends entirely on whether the landlord approves the sublet.
  2. I still can't decide whether to drive or fly.
  3. Whether she was joking is anybody's guess.
  4. He asked me whether the offer was still good.
  5. The real question is whether anyone will notice.
  6. We argued about whether to tell him now or wait until Monday.

See also: if, whether or not, as to whether, that, whatever, whether to / what to / how to / where to, the question whether

whether or not#

Pronunciation: /ˌwɛðər ər ˈnɑt/ · weh-thur er NAHT — the middle syllable is almost always the reduced /ər/ Type: complementizer; also subordinating in its concessive use · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (indirect question); also adverbial clause of concessive condition Pattern: [verb] whether or not [clause]. · [verb] whether [clause] or not. · Whether or not [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause], whether or not [clause]. Punctuation: No comma in the noun-clause use. In the concessive use ("regardless of whether"), a fronted clause takes a comma after it and a trailing one normally takes a comma before it. If or not is not English in either use. Variants: split whether … or not; archaic whether or no

Two constructions sharing one shape. As a complementizer it is plain whether with the alternative spelled out, and the or not is padding most editors cut. As a concessive it means "no matter whether," and there the or not is structural and cannot be dropped — which is the quick test for telling the two apart.

  1. Let me know whether or not you can make it Saturday.
  2. I asked whether the check had cleared or not.
  3. Whether or not the weather cooperates, the ceremony starts at four.
  4. Nobody has told us whether or not the position is still open.
  5. He's showing up, whether or not anyone invited him.
  6. The board votes tonight on whether or not to renew the lease.

See also: whether, if, whatever, however

zero that#

Pronunciation: ∅ — unpronounced; the clause simply begins with its own subject Type: complementizer (null) · Register: neutral to informal Clause type: noun clause (declarative content clause), object or complement only Pattern: [subject] [verb] ∅ [clause]. · [adjective] ∅ [clause]. · The [noun] is ∅ [clause]. Punctuation: No comma, ever — and the missing word makes the wrong comma more tempting: I think, we took a wrong turn is an error, not a pause. Variants: the overt that

American English drops complementizer that freely after common reporting and mental verbs (say, think, know, hope, feel, guess, bet) and after adjectives (glad, sure, afraid), and the omission is fully standard in speech and in most edited prose. Omission is blocked in five places: when the clause is the sentence's subject (That he lied surprised me), after a noun (the rumor that…), when anything comes between the verb and the clause (He told me yesterday that he'd quit), in the second of two coordinated clauses (He said he was tired and that he wanted to leave), and in fixed frames like the fact that and not that. After heavy reporting verbs — contend, assert, maintain, note — dropping it is possible but reads badly in formal writing.

  1. I think ∅ we took a wrong turn back at the light.
  2. She said ∅ she'd call back after lunch.
  3. Everyone knows ∅ the parking around the stadium is terrible.
  4. I'm just glad ∅ it's over.
  5. He swears ∅ he mailed the check on Monday.
  6. The trouble is ∅ nobody kept the receipts.

Wh-words that head noun clauses#

These are not pure complementizers: each one introduces the clause and fills a slot inside it, which is why none of them can be deleted the way that can. Two rules govern all of them. Keep statement word order — no auxiliary inversion (I asked what he wants, never what does he want) — and never end the containing sentence with a question mark unless the whole sentence is itself a question. All but why and how come also allow a to-infinitive — what to do, how to ask, where to go, who to call, but not why to go or how come to go — and that construction has an entry of its own at whether to / what to / how to / where to.

how#

Pronunciation: /haʊ/ · HOW Type: complementizer (interrogative adverb); the same word is also an exclamative adverb (How quickly this year went!) and, in the comparison family, a manner subordinator · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause — embedded wh-question of manner, means, or degree; also a fused relative of manner, and an embedded exclamative Pattern: [subject] [verb] how [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] how [clause]. · How [clause] [verb phrase]. · [subject] [verb] how to [verb]. · [preposition] how [clause]. · [subject] [verb] how [adjective/adverb] [clause]. Punctuation: No comma anywhere — not before how, not inside its clause, and not before a fronted how-clause subject (How she found the address is still a mystery). Statement order inside the clause: I asked how it works, not I asked how does it work.

Introduces an embedded question about manner, means, or — with an adjective or adverb attached — extent (how long, how much). It also heads a fused relative meaning "the way in which," where it competes with the way that; formal writing prefers how to the redundant the way how, which is not standard. A third use embeds an exclamative rather than a question — It's amazing how quickly kids pick it up — and that clause, like the others, keeps statement order and ends the sentence with a period.

  1. Nobody ever explained how the billing cycle actually works.
  2. How she found the address is still a mystery to me.
  3. I never learned how to parallel park.
  4. It depends on how much overtime they approve.
  5. Watch how he shifts his grip on the second swing.
  6. She wanted to know how long the drive takes.
  7. It's amazing how fast that dog learned the fence line.

See also: how come, why, what, however, how that, whether to / what to / how to / where to

how come#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ ˈkʌm/ · how-KUM Type: complementizer (interrogative phrase) · Register: informal Clause type: noun clause (embedded wh-question of reason) Pattern: How come [clause]? · [subject] [verb] how come [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [object] how come [clause]. · [imperative verb] [indirect object] how come [clause]. Punctuation: No comma. Unlike why, how come never triggers inversion even in a direct question: How come he left?, never How come did he leave? A direct question with it still takes a question mark; an embedded one still takes a period (She never explained how come she quit.).

A colloquial why that takes statement word order in main clauses as well as embedded ones, which makes it the one wh-question American learners can never get inverted by mistake. Its embedded use (I don't know how come) is common in speech and avoided in edited writing, where why is the safe substitute. It also refuses the to-infinitive that most wh-words allow: how come to leave is not English.

  1. How come nobody told me the meeting moved?
  2. I have no idea how come the charge showed up twice.
  3. She never explained how come she quit so suddenly.
  4. "How come you're up so early?" "Couldn't sleep."
  5. Ask him how come the car's still in the shop.

See also: why, how

what#

Pronunciation: /wʌt/ · WUHT — also /wɑt/ · WAHT; reduced to /wət/ in connected speech Type: complementizer (interrogative pronoun and determiner; also fused relative pronoun and exclamative determiner) · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause — embedded wh-question, fused ("headless") relative, or embedded exclamative Pattern: [subject] [verb] what [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] what [clause]. · What [clause] [verb phrase]. · [subject] [verb] what to [verb]. · [preposition] what [clause]. · [subject] [verb] what [noun] [clause]. · [subject] [verb] what a [noun] [clause]. Punctuation: No comma, and none before a fronted what-clause subject: What he said surprised us. Never follow it with that: what that he wants is not English.

Three jobs in one word. As an interrogative it heads an indirect question (I asked what he wants); as a fused relative it means "the thing(s) which" and needs no antecedent (What he wants is a raise); as an exclamative determiner it embeds the same way an exclamation does, minus the exclamation point (What a mess this is! → You wouldn't believe what a mess this is). The fused-relative use is where learners overreach: what replaces an entire noun-plus-relative sequence, so the thing what he wants and everything what I own are both wrong — after an expressed noun you need that or which, and the what relative of the man what fixed the roof is dialect, not standard American English.

  1. I never found out what the second envelope contained.
  2. What really bothers me is the tone of the email.
  3. Tell me what you need and I'll grab it on the way.
  4. She has no idea what to wear to a rehearsal dinner.
  5. The final bill depends on what the inspector finds.
  6. What he said and what he meant were two very different things.
  7. You wouldn't believe what a mess the kitchen was when we got home.

See also: which, whatever, that, how, whether to / what to / how to / where to

when#

Pronunciation: /wɛn/ · WEN — reduced to /wən/; conservative /hwɛn/ in some regional speech Type: complementizer (interrogative adverb); the same word is also a subordinating conjunction of time, a concessive subordinator, and a relative adverb · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (embedded wh-question of time) — distinct from the adverbial when of the time-clause family Pattern: [subject] [verb] when [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] when [clause]. · When [clause] [verb phrase]. · [subject] [verb] when to [verb]. · [preposition] when [clause]. · [linking verb] when [clause]. Punctuation: No comma in the noun-clause use, including when the clause is a fronted subject: When they'll announce it is anyone's guess. Adverbial when is the one that takes a comma when fronted: When they announce it, we'll know.

One word, two grammars. In the noun-clause use the when-clause is an argument — substitute something and the sentence still stands — and it never takes a comma; in the adverbial use it modifies the main clause and follows the ordinary fronted-subordinator comma rule. There is also a tense test: the noun clause allows a future (I don't know when he'll arrive), while the adverbial one forbids it (when he arrives, not when he will arrive).

  1. Nobody will tell me when the results get posted.
  2. When the renovation actually finishes is anybody's guess.
  3. She's still deciding when to give notice.
  4. The whole schedule hinges on when the permit clears.
  5. Ask the dispatcher when the next bus comes through.
  6. I honestly don't know when he'll get here.
  7. The only real question now is when they'll announce it.

See also: whenever, where, why, how

where#

Pronunciation: /wɛr/ · WAIR — conservative /hwɛr/ in some regional speech Type: complementizer (interrogative adverb; also fused relative); the same word is a place subordinator, a relative adverb, and a concessive subordinator elsewhere · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (embedded wh-question of place); also a fused relative of place Pattern: [subject] [verb] where [clause]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] where [clause]. · Where [clause] [verb phrase]. · [subject] [verb] where to [verb]. · [preposition] where [clause]. · [linking verb] where [clause]. Punctuation: No comma before where, none inside the clause, and none after a fronted where-clause subject: Where the money went has never been explained. Adverbial where is the one with a comma rule when fronted (Where the sidewalk ends, the gravel starts). Statement order: I asked where it is, not I asked where is it.

Heads an embedded question about place, and also a fused relative meaning "the place at which" (This is where I grew up). Distinguish it from the adverbial where of Sit where you like, which modifies the verb rather than filling an argument slot and belongs to the place-clause family, not to this one.

  1. She wouldn't say where she'd been all afternoon.
  2. Where the money actually went has never been explained.
  3. We spent an hour arguing about where to put the couch.
  4. That bend in the road is where the fault line runs.
  5. Text me where you parked so I'm not circling the lot.
  6. It matters a great deal where the closing takes place.

whether to / what to / how to / where to#

Pronunciation: /ˈwɛðər tə/, /ˈwʌt tə/, /ˈhaʊ tə/, /ˈwɛr tə/ · WEH-thur tuh, WUHT tuh, HOW tuh, WAIR tuh — the stress stays on the wh-word and to always reduces to /tə/, flapping to /ɾə/ after a vowel (what to order comes out whaddu order) Type: complementizer + infinitive marker (infinitival wh-clause); each member is one of the wh-words in this section, or whether, followed by bare to · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (infinitival embedded question) — object of a verb, object of a preposition, subject, or subject complement Pattern: [subject] [verb] [wh-word] to [verb]. · [subject] [verb] [indirect object] [wh-word] to [verb]. · [Wh-word] to [verb] [verb phrase]. · [preposition] [wh-word] to [verb]. · [linking verb] [wh-word] to [verb]. · [subject] [verb] whether to [verb] or to [verb]. Punctuation: No comma anywhere, and no subject inside the clause — the whole point of the construction is that the subject goes unexpressed and is recovered from the main clause. Never add should on top of the to (what to do or what I should do, never what to should do), and never insert that (how to fix it, not how that to fix it). Variants: the finite paraphrase with should or can (where to park → where I should park); whether or not to (whether or not to sign); who to — the ordinary American form, where the stiffest prose writes whom to

The infinitival half of the embedded-question system: the wh-word supplies the question, to supplies a verb with no tense, and the subject is left out and read off the main clause — She's deciding whether to go can only mean she is deciding about herself. It is open to whether and to every wh-word in this section except why and how come (why to go and how come to leave are not English), and it is the one embedded question that sits comfortably after a preposition (a question of what to charge). What it buys is compression: nobody told us where to file it says in six words what nobody told us where we were supposed to file it needs ten to say.

  1. She still hasn't decided whether to take the job or stay put.
  2. Nobody told us where to drop off the paperwork.
  3. He's never learned how to back a trailer down a driveway.
  4. What to wear to the rehearsal dinner turned into a two-day argument.
  5. The real problem is who to call at eleven o'clock on a Sunday night.
  6. Half the meeting went to arguing about whether to renew the contract or to rebid it.
  7. The manual says nothing about which to replace first.

See also: whether, what, how, where, who, why, whether or not

which#

Pronunciation: /wɪtʃ/ · WICH — a homophone of witch for most Americans; conservative /hwɪtʃ/ Type: complementizer (interrogative pronoun or determiner); the same word is also a relative pronoun, restrictive and nonrestrictive · Register: neutral Clause type: noun clause (embedded wh-question, selective) Pattern: [subject] [verb] which [clause]. · [subject] [verb] which [noun] [clause]. · [subject] [verb] which of [noun phrase] [clause]. · [subject] [verb] which ([noun]) to [verb]. · Which [noun] [verb phrase] [verb phrase]. · Which [noun] [subject] [verb] [verb phrase]. · [preposition] which [clause]. Punctuation: No comma in the noun-clause use. Relative which is the one with comma rules (the car, which was new, wouldn't start); the noun-clause which never takes one, no matter how long the clause runs.

The -ever and -soever forms: fused relatives#

Each of these folds a head noun and a relative clause into one word — whoever wins means "the person who wins" — so the clause fills a noun slot with no antecedent anywhere in sight. The rule that governs the whole subgroup is that case and function are assigned from inside the clause, not from outside it, which is why Give it to whoever wants it is correct: the preposition takes the entire clause as its object, so it cannot reach in and force whomever. All of them also have a concessive adverbial use meaning "no matter what/who/where," and that use — and only that use — takes a comma when fronted; every entry below states the rule for its own word, and no noun-clause use in this section takes a comma in any position. The last two members, whomsoever and whosoever, are built on the older emphatic suffix -soever; they behave exactly like whomever and whoever and differ only in register, which is archaic.

Phrasal, formal, and archaic complementizers#

Multi-word frames and survivals. Several of them (as to, as to whether, the fact that, the idea that, the question whether, for) exist mainly to solve a structural problem — English will not let a bare that- or wh-clause sit after most prepositions and most nouns — and the rest are period pieces, dialect survivals, or narrow specialists. Almost none of them takes a comma between the frame and its clause; the two conversational be-frames that close the section (the thing is, trouble is) are the exception, and each states its own rule.
