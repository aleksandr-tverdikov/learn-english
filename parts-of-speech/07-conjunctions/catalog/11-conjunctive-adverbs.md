# Conjunctive adverbs and transitional connectors

> The words that carry a conjunction's meaning — addition, contrast, result, sequence — while behaving grammatically as adverbs, which is why they take a semicolon or a period rather than a comma.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Every word in this file expresses a relationship a real conjunction could express — *however* means roughly what *but* means, *therefore* means roughly what *so* means — but not one of them is a conjunction. They are **adverbs**, and the difference is not academic: a conjunction joins two independent clauses across a comma, while a conjunctive adverb joins nothing at all. Write *It rained, however we went anyway* and you have produced a **comma splice**, the most common punctuation error in American English. Those two clauses need a semicolon or a period between them: *It rained; however, we went anyway.* / *It rained. However, we went anyway.*

What these words get in exchange for the heavier punctuation is **mobility**, and mobility is the test that identifies them. A conjunctive adverb can slide anywhere inside its clause — *However, the results held.* / *The results, however, held.* / *The results held, however.* — while *but* is welded to the seam between the two clauses and cannot move an inch. So one three-part rule runs through this entire category: **semicolon or period before; comma after it in front position, or commas on both sides in the middle; and never a bare comma joining the clauses.** Two riders. Short adverbs (*then, thus, hence, still, also, next, instead*) routinely drop the following comma, while the multi-word phrases (*on the other hand, as a result, in other words*) essentially never do. And when the adverb sits inside a single clause rather than between two, the semicolon has nothing to do — only the commas apply.

## Addition and continuation

### additionally

**Pronunciation:** /əˈdɪʃənəli/ &middot; *uh-DISH-uh-nuh-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *additionally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Additionally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *additionally*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] and, *additionally*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses; comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle. A comma alone before it is a comma splice.

Adds one more item to a list of points, and lives mostly in reports, memos, and academic prose. It is the driest of the additive connectors: *moreover* claims the new point is weightier and *besides* claims it settles the question, while *additionally* simply says "and one more thing."

1. The lease covers utilities; *additionally*, the landlord pays for trash pickup.
2. *Additionally*, we recommend replacing the smoke detectors on the second floor.
3. Enrollment fell six percent. *Additionally*, three instructors resigned mid-semester.
4. The grant funds two positions and, *additionally*, covers travel to the annual conference.
5. Applicants must submit transcripts; *additionally*, two letters of recommendation are required.
6. The lease, *additionally*, forbids subletting for the first year.

**See also:** [in addition](#in-addition), [also](#also), [furthermore](#furthermore), [moreover](#moreover), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### again

**Pronunciation:** /əˈɡɛn/, also /əˈɡeɪn/ &middot; *uh-GEN, also uh-GAYN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (one more point of the same kind). Also an ordinary **time adverb** meaning "once more" (*play it again*), which sits in the predicate and takes no commas.
**Pattern:** *Again*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *again*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *again*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] *again*. *(time adverb — "once more," no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are involved — a bare comma there is a comma splice. The commas are also what separate the two words: *Again, the vote failed* ("here is one more point") versus *The vote failed again* ("a second time").
**Variants:** *once again* (time only), *then again* (concession — a different connector), *likewise*

The additive *again* introduces one more instance of the same point rather than the repetition of an event — the "and here's another" of argument, lecture, and testimony. Position and commas do all the work, since stranded at the end of a predicate the same word is the ordinary adverb meaning "a second time."

1. *Again*, the department had no written policy on any of this.
2. The first draft ignored the budget; *again*, so did the second.
3. Consider Ohio. *Again*, the pattern holds: turnout rose in every county that added a polling place.
4. Her testimony, *again*, was never challenged by either side.
5. *Again*, I want to be clear that nobody has been accused of anything.
6. He read the paragraph *again* before signing it. *(time adverb — "once more," not a connector)*

**See also:** [also](#also), [additionally](#additionally), [likewise](#likewise), [then again](#then-again), [furthermore](#furthermore)

### also

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔlsoʊ/ &middot; *AWL-soh*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; [subject] *also* [verb phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *also* [main verb]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *also* [complement]. &middot; *Also*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** A semicolon or period before the clause it sits in, because *also* joins nothing itself — *The clinic is closed Sundays, it also closes early on Fridays* is a comma splice. Inside the clause it takes no commas at all: right after the auxiliary or *be*, or immediately before a one-word main verb. Comma after it only when it opens a sentence.

The everyday additive adverb, and the one learners most often try to use as a conjunction. *And* joins; *also* joins nothing — it simply marks the clause it sits in as an addition to the one before it. Opening a sentence with *Also* is standard in speech and general writing, though conservative editors prefer *In addition* in formal prose.

1. She speaks Portuguese, and she *also* reads Latin.
2. The clinic is closed Sundays; it *also* closes early on Fridays.
3. *Also*, don't forget to sign the last page.
4. We're out of eggs. *Also*, the milk went bad.
5. He *also* mentioned that the deadline had moved to the fifteenth.
6. The apartment is cheap. It's *also* the only one with a dishwasher.

**See also:** [additionally](#additionally), [in addition](#in-addition), [besides](#besides), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### besides

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsaɪdz/ &middot; *bih-SIDES*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (clinching reason). Also a **preposition** meaning "in addition to" or "apart from," which takes a noun phrase instead of a clause.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Besides*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *besides*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *besides*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Besides* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it, comma after it — a bare comma between the two clauses is a comma splice. As a preposition it takes no semicolon and no comma of its own, only the comma that closes a fronted phrase: *Besides Dan, nobody came.*
**Variants:** *besides* [noun phrase] (the prepositional form: *besides the cost*), *anyhow*, *what's more*

Adds the reason the speaker was saving: the clincher meant to end the discussion. It covers the same additive ground as *moreover* but is far more conversational, and it usually carries an undertone of "and anyway, that settles it." *Beside* is a different word — the preposition meaning "next to" — and not a spelling variant of this one.

1. I don't want to drive four hours. *Besides*, the tickets are sold out.
2. The couch won't fit through the door; *besides*, we can't afford it.
3. "You should apologize." "For what? *Besides*, she started it."
4. It's too late to call him. *Besides*, he never picks up.
5. The job pays less, and *besides*, I'd have to move to Cleveland.
6. *Besides* the two of us, nobody knew the combination. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows, not a clause)*

**See also:** [moreover](#moreover), [anyway](#anyway), [in addition](#in-addition), [after all](#after-all)

### besides which

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈsaɪdz ˌwɪtʃ/ &middot; *bih-SIDES-wich*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (a clinching extra reason). Formally a **relative** *which* pointing back at the whole clause before it, which is why it can attach across a comma.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause], *besides which* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Besides which*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *besides which*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Unlike bare *besides*, this one tolerates a comma before it, because the *which* subordinates what follows instead of leaving two independent clauses face to face: *The car is twenty years old, besides which it burns oil.* A period or semicolon is equally correct and is the safer choice in edited prose; the comma after it is optional and usually dropped.
**Variants:** *besides*, *and besides*, *not to mention*, *on top of which*

Piles one more reason onto the one just given, with the same "and that settles it" force as *besides*. It belongs to letters, columns, and dialogue rather than to reports, and its relative-pronoun grammar is what lets it hang on the previous clause where *besides* would demand a full stop.

1. The apartment is out of our price range, *besides which* it has no parking.
2. He never returned the deposit. *Besides which*, he still has my ladder.
3. I don't have the time; *besides which*, I'd be terrible at it.
4. The report came in three weeks late, *besides which* two of the tables were mislabeled.
5. She had already booked the flight, *besides which* her sister was expecting her.

**See also:** [besides](#besides), [moreover](#moreover), [what's more](#whats-more), [for that matter](#for-that-matter), [in addition](#in-addition)

### for that matter

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈðæt ˌmætər/ &middot; *fur THAT mat-ur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of addition that stretches the previous claim to a further case
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *for that matter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *For that matter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [noun phrase], or [noun phrase] *for that matter*, [continuation]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *for that matter*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless *and* carries the join — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Tacked onto a noun phrase after *or*, it takes only the commas that fence that phrase.
**Variants:** *come to that* (British-flavored), *if it comes to that*, *and indeed*

Extends what was just said to a further case the hearer may not have considered, usually a broader or more damning one. Where *besides* adds a separate reason, *for that matter* stretches the same point until it covers more ground: *He hasn't read the contract — for that matter, neither have I.*

1. I've never met the man; *for that matter*, I'd never heard his name until Tuesday.
2. The elevator hasn't worked in a month. *For that matter*, neither has the buzzer.
3. She doesn't speak French, or Spanish *for that matter*.
4. No one on the committee is an engineer; *for that matter*, no one there has ever built anything.
5. The rule was never enforced, and *for that matter*, it was never printed in the handbook.

**See also:** [besides](#besides), [besides which](#besides-which), [moreover](#moreover), [in fact](#in-fact), [indeed](#indeed)

### furthermore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɜrðərˌmɔr/ &middot; *FUR-thur-mor*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *furthermore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Furthermore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *furthermore*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] and, *furthermore*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause. The comma after it is not optional in edited prose.
**Variants:** *further* (identical function, a shade lighter: *Further, the report omits three sources.*)

Adds another point pushing in the same direction as the last one and signals that the argument is still being built. Where *moreover* presents its point as the heavier one, *furthermore* merely continues the accumulation.

1. The contract was never signed; *furthermore*, no payment was ever made.
2. *Furthermore*, the committee failed to record two of its votes.
3. The study used a small sample. *Furthermore*, it excluded everyone over sixty.
4. She argued that the policy was expensive and, *furthermore*, that it had never worked anywhere.
5. *Further*, the audit found no documentation for eleven thousand dollars in travel.
6. The estimate is, *furthermore*, three weeks out of date.

**See also:** [moreover](#moreover), [additionally](#additionally), [in addition](#in-addition), [besides](#besides)

### in addition

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn əˈdɪʃən/ &middot; *in uh-DISH-un*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition. Also *in addition to*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in addition*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In addition*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in addition*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] and, *in addition*, [object]. &middot; *In addition to* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Do not confuse it with the preposition *in addition to*, which takes a noun phrase and no semicolon: *In addition to the analysts, we hired a designer.*
**Variants:** *in addition to* [noun phrase] (the prepositional form, which cannot take a clause)

The neutral phrasal additive: acceptable in business writing without the starch of *furthermore*, and safer in formal prose than a sentence-initial *Also*. It adds a genuinely separate item rather than an elaboration of the previous one.

1. The apartment includes parking; *in addition*, the building has a gym.
2. *In addition*, all employees will receive a stipend for home internet.
3. She teaches two seminars. *In addition*, she advises eleven graduate students.
4. The fee covers materials and, *in addition*, one private lesson a month.
5. *In addition to* the salary, the offer includes equity. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows, not a clause)*
6. Two of the exhibits, *in addition*, were never entered into evidence.

**See also:** [additionally](#additionally), [also](#also), [furthermore](#furthermore), [moreover](#moreover)

### moreover

**Pronunciation:** /mɔrˈoʊvər/ &middot; *mor-OH-vur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (reinforcing)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *moreover*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Moreover*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *moreover*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides when it sits inside the clause.
**Variants:** *what's more* (the informal spoken equivalent)

Adds a point that reinforces and outweighs the one before it — the "and what's more" of formal argument. Save it for reasoning: using *moreover* to add a second item to a grocery list is the classic register mistake.

1. The device is cheaper than its rivals; *moreover*, it uses half the power.
2. *Moreover*, no one in the department was told the position existed.
3. The translation is inaccurate. *Moreover*, whole paragraphs are missing.
4. The witness had no reason to lie and, *moreover*, nothing to gain by it.
5. The lease is expiring; *what's more*, the rent is going up forty percent.
6. The statute, *moreover*, has never once been enforced.

**See also:** [furthermore](#furthermore), [besides](#besides), [additionally](#additionally), [indeed](#indeed)

### what's more

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ˈmɔr/ &middot; *wuts MOR*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition (reinforcing)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *what's more*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *What's more*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *what's more*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless *and* carries the join — and a comma after it. Front position only; it does not close a clause. Written out as *what is more* it is a shade more formal and identically punctuated.
**Variants:** *what is more* (formal), *moreover* (the formal equivalent), *on top of that*, *better still*

The conversational *moreover*: it adds a second point and claims the second one hits harder. It belongs to speech, journalism, and sales copy — in a brief or a dissertation, *moreover* is the expected form.

1. The tickets were half price; *what's more*, they came with parking.
2. He missed the deadline. *What's more*, he never told anyone he was going to.
3. The house needs a roof, and *what's more*, the furnace is thirty years old.
4. *What's more*, the same company had been fined twice before.
5. She finished the course in half the time; *what is more*, she did it while working nights.

**See also:** [moreover](#moreover), [besides](#besides), [furthermore](#furthermore), [in addition](#in-addition), [indeed](#indeed)

### withal

**Pronunciation:** /wɪˈðɔl/ &middot; *with-AWL*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition ("besides, moreover"), and in later use of concession ("nevertheless"). Also an archaic **postposed preposition** meaning "with" (*the tools he worked withal*).
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *withal*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase], *withal*. &middot; [adjective] and *withal* [adjective]. &middot; [noun phrase] [relative clause] *withal*. *(archaic preposition — "with")*
**Punctuation:** Comma before it in final position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period where two independent clauses meet. Mid-phrase it is often printed with no commas at all — *a grave and withal kindly man* — which is how Shakespeare's editors set it.
**Variants:** *therewithal* (rarer), *moreover*, *nevertheless* (for the concessive sense)

Elizabethan and Victorian English for "besides, and along with that," drifting toward "nevertheless" by the nineteenth century. An American meets it in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and period novels and essentially nowhere else; written today it reads as costume.

1. "A goodly gentleman, and *withal* a scholar." *(period diction)*
2. He was a hard master, *withal* a just one.
3. The room was cold, dim, and *withal* strangely comfortable.
4. She kept the accounts, managed the tenants, and *withal* found time to write.
5. The wages were small; *withal*, the family wanted for nothing.
6. He had nothing to defend himself *withal*. *(the archaic preposition — "with," not a connector)*

**See also:** [moreover](#moreover), [besides](#besides), [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [howbeit](12-formal-archaic.md#howbeit)

## Contrast, concession, and reversal

### admittedly

**Pronunciation:** /ədˈmɪtɪdli/ &middot; *ad-MIT-id-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *admittedly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Admittedly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *admittedly*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary] *admittedly* [complement]. *(integrated — no commas)* &middot; [Independent clause] — *admittedly* — [continuation].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position; commas or dashes on both sides in mid position. Period or semicolon before it when two independent clauses are involved — never a bare comma.

Concedes a point against your own argument before you go on to answer it. *Granted* does the same job conversationally and *of course* can sound impatient doing it; *admittedly* is the neutral written form, and it commits the writer to a *but* somewhere soon after.

1. *Admittedly*, the first draft was a mess.
2. The plan is expensive; *admittedly*, it is also the only one that has ever worked.
3. She was, *admittedly*, the least experienced candidate on the shortlist.
4. *Admittedly*, I have never actually read the whole report.
5. The evidence is thin — *admittedly* so — but the pattern is hard to ignore.
6. The design is *admittedly* dated, but everything still works. *(integrated into the clause — no commas)*

**See also:** [granted](#granted), [of course](#of-course), [that said](#that-said), [even so](#even-so)

### at the same time

**Pronunciation:** /ət ðə ˌseɪm ˈtaɪm/ &middot; *ut thuh saym TIME*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession (two things true at once). Also a plain time adverbial meaning "simultaneously," which belongs to the predicate.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *At the same time*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *at the same time*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *at the same time*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] and, *at the same time*, [verb phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] *at the same time*. *(literal simultaneity — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. In the literal "simultaneously" sense it is part of the predicate and takes no commas at all: *Both alarms went off at the same time.*
**Variants:** *at once* (literal sense only), *meanwhile* (simultaneity only), *then again*, *that said*, *yet* (the coordinating equivalent, which takes a comma)

Holds two facts in the frame together and asks the reader to keep both: "that is true, and so is this." The connector sense is concessive, not temporal — *meanwhile* puts two events on a clock, while *at the same time* puts two judgments on a scale. The literal sense survives right beside it, and only the commas tell them apart.

1. The layoffs were necessary; *at the same time*, they were handled badly.
2. I want her to take the job. *At the same time*, I'd hate to see her move across the country.
3. She's the best coach we've had. She is, *at the same time*, impossible to work for.
4. The report praises the program and, *at the same time*, recommends cutting its budget.
5. Both alarms went off *at the same time*. *(literal simultaneity — no commas, not a connector)*

**See also:** [meanwhile](#meanwhile), [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [even so](#even-so), [then again](#then-again), [however](#however)

### contrariwise

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑntrɛriˌwaɪz/ &middot; *KAHN-treh-ree-wize*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, literary (dated)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (the opposite case)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *contrariwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Contrariwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *contrariwise*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *conversely* (the current formal word), *on the contrary*, *contrarily* (rare), *per contra*

The nineteenth-century ancestor of *conversely*: it flips the previous statement and asserts the reverse case. In American English it survives mainly in quotation and deliberate whimsy — Tweedledee's "Contrariwise, if it was so, it might be" is where most readers have met it — so *conversely* is the word for real formal prose.

1. "*Contrariwise*," continued Tweedledee, "if it was so, it might be; and if it were so, it would be."
2. The northern counties gained population; *contrariwise*, the delta lost a fifth of its residents.
3. *Contrariwise*, the older statute imposed no penalty at all.
4. Heat expands the metal; *contrariwise*, cold draws it tight.
5. He believed the letters would clear him. *Contrariwise*, they were what convicted him.

**See also:** [conversely](#conversely), [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [per contra](#per-contra), [in contrast](#in-contrast), [however](#however)

### conversely

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈvɜrsli/, also /ˈkɑnvɜrsli/ &middot; *kun-VURS-lee, also KAHN-vurs-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (logical reversal)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *conversely*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Conversely*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *conversely*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause.

Introduces the exact reverse of what was just stated — the same terms with their positions swapped. Use it only when the second clause really is the mirror image; where two ideas are merely being weighed against each other, the honest choice is *on the other hand*.

1. Warm weather lifts ice cream sales; *conversely*, a cold July kills them.
2. Small firms adapted within months. *Conversely*, the largest banks took years.
3. If the price rises, demand falls; *conversely*, if the price drops, demand climbs.
4. Adding staff shortened the wait. *Conversely*, every cut lengthened it.
5. He finds mornings easy and evenings impossible; his wife, *conversely*, comes alive after ten.

**See also:** [in contrast](#in-contrast), [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [however](#however)

### even so

**Pronunciation:** /ˌivən ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *ee-vun SOH*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Even so*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *even so*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *even so*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause] and, *even so*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *all the same*, *just the same* (both more conversational)

The plain-English *nevertheless*: it grants everything just said and then holds the original position anyway. *However* only marks a contrast; *even so* explicitly concedes first, which is why it so often follows a sentence the speaker has just agreed with.

1. The tickets were nonrefundable. *Even so*, the airline gave us a credit.
2. He apologized twice; *even so*, she didn't return his calls.
3. It's a nine-hour drive. *Even so*, I'd rather go than fly.
4. We knew the odds going in. *Even so*, losing hurt.
5. She had trained for a year and, *even so*, finished dead last.
6. The house was, *even so*, the best thing we saw all weekend.

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [nonetheless](#nonetheless), [still](#still), [that said](#that-said), [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [anyway](#anyway)

### granted

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd/ &middot; *GRAN-tid*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession
**Pattern:** *Granted*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Granted*. [Independent clause]. &middot; *Granted that* [clause], [main clause]. *(subordinating use)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, and a period or semicolon before it when a full clause precedes — *The team is young; granted, so was last year's squad*, never a bare comma. It can also stand alone as a one-word sentence (*Granted.*). Add *that* and it becomes a subordinating conjunction taking a full clause, punctuated like *although*: a comma after the *granted that* clause when that clause comes first, and no comma when it follows the main clause.
**Variants:** *granted that* (+ clause), *true*, *fair enough* (informal), *to be sure* (formal)

Concedes the other side's point out loud, almost always to set up a *but*. It is the conversational cousin of *admittedly*, and it is the one connector in this family that can be a complete turn in a conversation by itself.

1. *Granted*, the price is steep — but you'll use it every day.
2. He's rude. *Granted*. That doesn't make him wrong.
3. *Granted*, I've only seen the first season.
4. The team is young; *granted*, so was last year's championship squad.
5. *Granted that* the data is five years old, the trend is still unmistakable.

**See also:** [admittedly](#admittedly), [of course](#of-course), [even so](#even-so), [that said](#that-said)

### however

**Pronunciation:** /haʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *how-EV-ur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral (formal-leaning in front position)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast. Also a **subordinating** conjunction meaning "no matter how," which does introduce a real adverbial clause.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *however*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *However*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *however*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause]; [independent clause], *however*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is the textbook comma splice (*It rained, however we went* is wrong). Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. The subordinator *however* takes none of that: *However you slice it, the numbers don't work.*

The default contrast connector of written English: it marks its clause as running against the expectation the previous sentence created. *But* does the same work as a coordinating conjunction with only a comma. Everything that separates the two words follows from *however* being an adverb — the heavier punctuation, and the freedom to move.

1. The flight was delayed four hours; *however*, the airline paid for our hotel.
2. We expected a crowd. *However*, only nine people showed up.
3. The proposal is thorough. The budget, *however*, is pure fiction.
4. She promised she'd call. She never did, *however*.
5. *However* you slice it, the numbers don't work. *(here* however *is a subordinator meaning "no matter how," not this connector)*

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [still](#still), [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [that said](#that-said), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### in contrast

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑntræst/ &middot; *in KAHN-trast*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (two cases compared). Also *in contrast to* / *in contrast with*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *In contrast*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in contrast*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in contrast*, [predicate]. &middot; *In contrast to* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Adding *to* or *with* makes it a preposition that must be followed by a noun phrase: *In contrast to last year, sales rose.*
**Variants:** *by contrast*; *in contrast to* / *in contrast with* [noun phrase] (prepositional)

Sets two things side by side and points at the difference between them. Unlike *however*, it makes no claim that the second fact is surprising — only that it differs — which makes it the workhorse of comparative analysis and data commentary.

1. Rural districts lost population. *In contrast*, the suburbs grew nine percent.
2. The first novel is spare and cold; *in contrast*, the second is almost sentimental.
3. Japanese firms invested in retraining. American firms, *in contrast*, cut headcount.
4. *In contrast*, the control group showed no change at all.
5. *In contrast to* her sister, she never touched a piano again. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows)*

**See also:** [conversely](#conversely), [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [similarly](#similarly), [whereas](04-cause.md#whereas)

### instead

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈstɛd/ &middot; *in-STED*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of substitution. Also *instead of*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase or an *-ing* form.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *instead*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Instead*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; [independent clause] *instead*. &middot; *Instead of* [noun phrase / -ing form], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice. Comma after it in front position (often dropped in casual writing); **no** comma before it in final position, which is where it most often lands in speech: *We drove instead.*
**Variants:** *instead of* [noun phrase / -ing form] (prepositional)

Marks its clause as the replacement for something that did not happen — it answers "so what happened instead?" Where *rather* corrects a statement, *instead* substitutes an action, and its final-position use (*Let's walk instead*) has no equivalent among the formal contrast adverbs.

1. The meeting was canceled; *instead*, we all went to lunch.
2. He didn't apologize. *Instead*, he changed the subject.
3. We were going to fly. We drove *instead*.
4. *Instead of* driving, we took the train and got there first. *(preposition — an* -ing *form follows, not a clause)*
5. The city rejected the tax increase. *Instead*, it cut weekend bus service.

**See also:** [rather](#rather), [alternatively](#alternatively), [otherwise](#otherwise), [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary)

### nevertheless

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɛvərðəˈlɛs/ &middot; *nev-ur-thuh-LESS*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *nevertheless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Nevertheless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *nevertheless* [verb phrase]. &middot; [Subject], *nevertheless*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause]. [Independent clause] *nevertheless*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause; in final position the comma before it is usual but often dropped.
**Variants:** *never the less* (obsolete three-word spelling)

Grants the previous statement in full and then asserts the opposite of what it implied. It and *nonetheless* are interchangeable in nearly every American context; *nevertheless* is the older and slightly more frequent of the two, and both are considerably heavier than everyday *still* or *even so*.

1. The evidence was circumstantial; *nevertheless*, the jury convicted.
2. She had no formal training. *Nevertheless*, she rebuilt the engine herself.
3. The forecast was terrible. We went ahead *nevertheless*.
4. The proposal is, *nevertheless*, the best one we have received.
5. Two members objected; the motion *nevertheless* passed eleven to two.

**See also:** [nonetheless](#nonetheless), [even so](#even-so), [still](#still), [however](#however), [regardless](#regardless)

### nonetheless

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnʌnðəˈlɛs/ &middot; *nun-thuh-LESS*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *nonetheless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Nonetheless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary] *nonetheless* [complement]. &middot; [Independent clause]. [Independent clause] *nonetheless*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice. Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides mid-clause. In final position the comma before it is optional: set it off when it is an afterthought (*The offer stands, nonetheless*), run it in when it belongs to the predicate (*The conclusion holds nonetheless*).
**Variants:** *none the less* (older three-word spelling, now rare in American writing)

Functionally identical to *nevertheless* — pick either. The only real differences are frequency and rhythm: *nonetheless* is a syllable shorter, so it falls more comfortably at the end of a sentence, where American writers reach for it slightly more often than for its twin.

1. The repairs were expensive; *nonetheless*, the car is worth keeping.
2. He was outvoted. *Nonetheless*, he filed a written objection.
3. The film is uneven, but it is *nonetheless* the most ambitious thing she has made.
4. The data are incomplete. The conclusion holds *nonetheless*.
5. *Nonetheless*, the board scheduled a second vote for December.

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [even so](#even-so), [regardless](#regardless), [still](#still)

### notwithstanding

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑtwɪθˈstændɪŋ/, also /ˌnɑtwɪðˈstændɪŋ/ &middot; *naht-with-STAN-ding* — the second form has a voiced *th*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal (legal and academic)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession. Also a **preposition**, standing either before or after its noun phrase, and with *that* a formal **subordinating** conjunction.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *notwithstanding*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Notwithstanding*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Notwithstanding* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)* &middot; [Noun phrase] *notwithstanding*, [independent clause]. *(preposition, postposed)* &middot; *Notwithstanding that* [clause], [main clause]. *(subordinator)*
**Punctuation:** As a connector: semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it; but that use is rare. In the far commoner prepositional uses there is no semicolon at all, just the comma that closes the fronted phrase.
**Variants:** *notwithstanding that* (+ clause; formal subordinator)

A formal *nevertheless*, still alive in contracts, statutes, and academic prose. It is much more often a preposition than a connector, and it is the only word in this family that can follow the noun it governs: *objections notwithstanding*.

1. The evidence was thin; *notwithstanding*, the jury convicted.
2. *Notwithstanding*, the department went ahead with the hire.
3. *Notwithstanding* the storm warnings, the ferry sailed on schedule. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows)*
4. The objections of two members *notwithstanding*, the motion carried. *(preposition, placed after its noun)*
5. She had no formal training; *notwithstanding*, she outperformed every analyst on the desk.
6. *Notwithstanding that* the deadline had passed, the clerk accepted the filing. *(subordinating conjunction — a full clause follows)*

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [regardless](#regardless), [nonetheless](#nonetheless), [even so](#even-so)

### on the contrary

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈkɑntrɛri/ &middot; *on thuh KAHN-trair-ee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contradiction
**Pattern:** [Negative statement]. *On the contrary*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Negative statement]; *on the contrary*, [independent clause]. &middot; "[Claim]" — "*On the contrary*, [independent clause]."
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it, comma after it, always — a bare comma between the two clauses is a comma splice. Because it needs something to contradict, it almost never opens a paragraph.
**Variants:** *quite the contrary*, *quite the opposite*, *far from it* (informal)

Denies what was just said and replaces it with the truth. It requires a preceding negative or a claim you are rejecting — and this is the most common misuse in the whole category: *on the contrary* is not a synonym for *on the other hand*, which balances two things that can both be true at once.

1. "You must be exhausted." "*On the contrary*, I've never felt better."
2. The reform did not reduce costs; *on the contrary*, it doubled them.
3. He isn't shy. *On the contrary*, he'll talk to anyone who stands still.
4. *On the contrary*, the data show the trend running the other way.
5. Nobody was bored. *On the contrary*, the whole room was riveted.

**See also:** [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [conversely](#conversely), [in fact](#in-fact), [rather](#rather), [instead](#instead)

### on the other hand

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ði ˈʌðər ˌhænd/ &middot; *on thee UTH-ur hand*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (a balancing consideration)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *On the other hand*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *on the other hand*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *on the other hand*, [predicate]. &middot; *On the one hand*, [clause]; *on the other hand*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. When it is paired with *on the one hand*, a semicolon between the two halves is the cleanest choice.
**Variants:** *on the one hand … on the other hand* (paired); *then again* (informal)

Introduces a consideration that pulls the other way without denying the first — both sides stay true, and the reader is asked to weigh them. That is exactly what separates it from *on the contrary*, which cancels the previous statement outright.

1. The apartment is small. *On the other hand*, it's two blocks from the office.
2. Hiring now is expensive; *on the other hand*, waiting until spring may cost us the project.
3. *On the one hand*, the treatment works; *on the other hand*, almost nobody can afford it.
4. He's never managed a team. *On the other hand*, neither had you at his age.
5. The used one is half the price. The mileage, *on the other hand*, would scare me.

**See also:** [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [then again](#then-again), [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [conversely](#conversely), [in contrast](#in-contrast), [however](#however)

### per contra

**Pronunciation:** /pər ˈkɑntrə/ &middot; *pur KAHN-truh*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal, Latin) &middot; **Register:** formal, technical (accounting, law, older criticism)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (the entry on the opposite side of the account)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *per contra*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Per contra*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *per contra*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. American publishing sets it in roman type rather than italicizing it as a foreign phrase.
**Variants:** *on the contrary*, *conversely*, *contra* (in citation: *contra Smith*)

Latin for "on the contrary," literally "by the opposite side" — the phrase comes from double-entry bookkeeping, where the contra entry sits opposite. In American English it is bookish and rare, surviving in accounting (*a per contra item*), in judicial writing, and in older criticism; *conversely* or *on the other hand* is the ordinary choice.

1. The bank showed a loss for the quarter; *per contra*, the insurance subsidiary returned nine percent.
2. *Per contra*, the defense offered no witness of its own.
3. The lease is an asset to the tenant and, *per contra*, a liability to the owner.
4. Younger readers found the book cold. *Per contra*, reviewers of his own generation called it his warmest.
5. The receivable appears in one column and, *per contra*, in the other as an offsetting charge.

**See also:** [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [conversely](#conversely), [contrariwise](#contrariwise), [in contrast](#in-contrast), [however](#however)

### rather

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðər/ &middot; *RATH-ur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of correction. The same form is also a **degree adverb** (*rather cold*), half of the preference idiom *would rather*, and part of the quasi-coordinator *rather than* — none of which is this connector.
**Pattern:** [Negative clause]; *rather*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Negative clause]. *Rather*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Negative clause] — *rather*, [independent clause]. &middot; [phrase], or *rather*, [corrected phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *rather* [adjective]. *(degree adverb)* &middot; [Subject] would *rather* [verb phrase]. *(preference idiom)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon, period, or dash before it when two independent clauses are involved — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. In the *or rather* correction, commas go on both sides of the whole phrase.
**Variants:** *or rather* (self-correction inside a sentence), *more precisely*, *to be exact*

Corrects the statement just made and puts a more accurate one in its place: "not that — this." Three other *rathers* have nothing to do with this entry: the degree adverb (*rather cold*), the preference form (*I'd rather stay*), and the quasi-coordinator *rather than*.

1. It wasn't a negotiation; *rather*, it was an ultimatum.
2. The problem isn't the software. *Rather*, nobody was ever trained to use it.
3. She didn't refuse — *rather*, she simply never answered.
4. We met on Tuesday, or *rather*, Tuesday night.
5. The change was not sudden; *rather*, it took the better part of a decade.
6. It was *rather* cold, and I'd *rather* have stayed home. *(degree adverb and preference idiom — neither one is this connector)*

**See also:** [instead](#instead), [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [in fact](#in-fact), [that is](#that-is), [rather than](01-coordinating.md#rather-than)

### regardless

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈɡɑrdləs/ &middot; *rih-GARD-luss*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral (informal in final position)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession (dismissal). Also *regardless of*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *regardless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Regardless*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *regardless*. &middot; *Regardless of* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and no comma before it in final position (*He's going regardless*). *Regardless of* takes a noun phrase, and a comma when it is fronted.
**Variants:** *regardless of* [noun phrase] (prepositional), *no matter what* (informal)

Brushes everything just said aside as not changing the outcome. It is blunter than *nevertheless*: it does not really concede the point so much as decline to weigh it. *Irregardless* is nonstandard and widely condemned in American usage — avoid it.

1. The forecast calls for rain; *regardless*, the game will be played.
2. He knows the risks. He's going *regardless*.
3. *Regardless*, somebody has to tell her before Friday.
4. The budget may be cut. We'll finish the study *regardless*.
5. *Regardless of* the outcome, I'm proud of this team. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows)*

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [anyway](#anyway), [in any case](#in-any-case), [notwithstanding](#notwithstanding), [even so](#even-so)

### still

**Pronunciation:** /stɪl/ &middot; *STIL*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession. The same form is also a plain **time adverb** meaning "up to now" (*She's still asleep*) and a **degree adverb** with comparatives (*still colder*), neither of which is a connector.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Still*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *still*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], but *still*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *still* [complement]. *(time adverb — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it; the comma after it in front position is usual but freely dropped in informal writing (*Still, I liked it* / *Still I liked it*). Never a bare comma joining the clauses.

The everyday concessive: "all of that is true, and yet." It is the shortest and least formal member of the *nevertheless* family. Keep it clear of the time adverb *still* (*She's still asleep*), which sits inside the clause and takes no comma at all.

1. The reviews were brutal. *Still*, I liked it.
2. He isn't qualified; *still*, he'd learn the job in a month.
3. It's a long shot. *Still*, somebody has to try.
4. I know she meant well. *Still*, she shouldn't have said it in front of everyone.
5. The car needs a clutch and four tires. *Still*, it runs.
6. She's *still* asleep. *(time adverb — inside the clause, no commas, not a connector)*

**See also:** [nevertheless](#nevertheless), [even so](#even-so), [though](#though), [that said](#that-said), [yet](01-coordinating.md#yet)

### that said

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈsɛd/ &middot; *that SED*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession (pivot)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *That said*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *that said*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. It does not occur mid-clause or finally; it is a front-position pivot only.
**Variants:** *having said that*, *that being said*, *with all that said*

A conversational pivot: it acknowledges everything just said and then turns against it. Functionally it is *nevertheless* with a friendlier face, and it is now standard in American business writing and product reviews. It began life as an absolute phrase, which is why the fuller *having said that* still sounds a shade more careful.

1. The screen is gorgeous. *That said*, the battery is a real disappointment.
2. I trust her judgment; *that said*, I'd like a second opinion before we sign.
3. The plan is sound. *Having said that*, the timeline worries me.
4. He's the strongest candidate. *That being said*, he's asking for a lot of money.
5. *That said*, nobody is forcing you to decide today.

**See also:** [even so](#even-so), [still](#still), [however](#however), [admittedly](#admittedly), [then again](#then-again)

### then again

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛn əˈɡɛn/ &middot; *then uh-GEN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of contrast (second thought)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Then again*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *then again*, [independent clause]. &middot; But *then again*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. It frequently follows *but* (*But then again, who knows?*), which is legal because *but* is doing the joining and *then again* is only modifying.

Introduces a second thought that undercuts the speaker's own previous statement — the connector of visible hesitation. *On the other hand* weighs two options evenly; *then again* signals that you are in the act of talking yourself out of what you just said.

1. I should go to bed. *Then again*, it's Friday.
2. She'd never lie about that. *Then again*, she never mentioned the loan either.
3. The price seems high. *Then again*, so is everything downtown.
4. We could drive; *then again*, parking down there is impossible.
5. He's probably fine. But *then again*, he hasn't called in three days.

**See also:** [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [that said](#that-said), [still](#still), [anyway](#anyway)

### though

**Pronunciation:** /ðoʊ/ &middot; *THOH*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession. Also a **subordinating** conjunction, in which use it fronts a dependent clause.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. [Independent clause], *though*. &middot; [Subject] [verb], *though*, [continuation]. &middot; *Though* [clause], [main clause]. *(subordinating use — a different animal)*
**Punctuation:** As a connector it never opens its clause, so there is no semicolon to place: put a comma before it in final position and commas on both sides mid-clause. The clause it sits in still needs a period or a semicolon in front of it, not a comma. As a subordinator it takes a comma after its own clause when that clause comes first (*Though it rained, we went*); when the *though* clause follows, concession is the standard exception to the no-comma rule for trailing subordinate clauses, and the comma stays (*We went, though it rained*).
**Variants:** *tho* (informal writing and texting only)

The spoken workhorse of concession, tacked onto the end of a sentence: *It's cold. Nice view, though.* This use has no formal equivalent — *although* cannot do it, because *although* has no adverb life at all. Compare the subordinating *though*, which fronts a dependent clause and behaves like *although* in every respect.

1. The hotel was overpriced. The view was worth it, *though*.
2. I don't eat much meat. I'll take a burger, *though*.
3. She never wrote back. She did read it, *though*.
4. It's a tiny apartment. It's cheap, *though*.
5. The repair is expensive. It would, *though*, solve the problem for good.
6. *Though* the rent was high, we took the place. *(subordinating conjunction — it fronts a dependent clause)*

**See also:** [still](#still), [however](#however), [that said](#that-said), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

## Comparison and equivalence

### equally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈikwəli/ &middot; *EE-kwuh-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of parallel weight ("and just as much"). Also a **degree adverb** inside the clause (*equally difficult*) and a **manner adverb** (*divided equally*), neither of which is a connector.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *equally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Equally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *equally*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *equally* [adjective]. *(degree adverb — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position. Drop the commas and it stops being a connector: *Equally, the second study was flawed* ("and just as importantly") versus *The second study was equally flawed* ("flawed to the same degree").
**Variants:** *equally important*, *just as important*, *by the same token*, *likewise*

Presents the next point as carrying exactly the same weight as the last, rather than as merely resembling it — which is what separates it from *likewise* and *similarly*, both of which claim likeness of content. British writers front the connector more freely than Americans, who often prefer *equally important* or *just as important*.

1. The schedule is unrealistic; *equally*, the budget assumes prices that no longer exist.
2. Teachers need smaller classes. *Equally*, they need time to plan.
3. The state has a duty to prosecute; it has, *equally*, a duty to disclose.
4. *Equally*, no one on the board asked where the money had gone.
5. The second proposal was *equally* expensive. *(degree adverb — "to the same degree," not a connector)*

**See also:** [likewise](#likewise), [similarly](#similarly), [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [moreover](#moreover), [in turn](#in-turn)

### likewise

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlaɪkˌwaɪz/ &middot; *LIKE-wize*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of comparison (a parallel case)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *likewise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Likewise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] *likewise* [verb phrase]. &middot; "*Likewise*." *(as a full reply)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause, except when it sits directly before the verb (*his lawyer likewise refused*), where it takes no commas at all.

Says that what follows holds in the same way — the matching case, the matching instruction. It also survives as a one-word reply that *similarly* cannot manage ("Nice working with you." "*Likewise*."), and in the connector use it is a shade more formal than *similarly*.

1. Fold the top edge down; *likewise*, fold the bottom edge up.
2. The East Coast offices closed early. *Likewise*, the Chicago branch sent everyone home.
3. Freshmen must register by Friday; sophomores, *likewise*, face a Friday deadline.
4. He refused to comment, and his lawyer *likewise* said nothing.
5. "Great working with you." "*Likewise*."

**See also:** [similarly](#similarly), [in contrast](#in-contrast), [also](#also), [conversely](#conversely)

### similarly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪmələrli/ &middot; *SIM-uh-lur-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of comparison (a parallel pattern)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *similarly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Similarly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *similarly*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *in the same way*, *by the same token*, *in like manner* (dated)

Introduces a second case that behaves like the first, usually inside an explanation or an argument. *Likewise* points at sameness of action; *similarly* points at sameness of pattern, which is why analytical writing prefers it.

1. Copper conducts heat well; *similarly*, it conducts electricity.
2. Toddlers learn words by hearing them repeated. *Similarly*, adults pick up slang from the people they spend time with.
3. The northern route floods every spring. The southern route, *similarly*, closes after heavy rain.
4. *Similarly*, the second trial found no difference between the groups.
5. Freelancers must file quarterly; small partnerships, *by the same token*, cannot wait until April.

**See also:** [likewise](#likewise), [in contrast](#in-contrast), [conversely](#conversely), [for example](#for-example)

## Result, inference, and consequence

### accordingly

**Pronunciation:** /əˈkɔrdɪŋli/ &middot; *uh-KOR-ding-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result (an action taken in response)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *accordingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Accordingly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb] *accordingly*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position. In final position (*plan accordingly*) it is a plain manner adverb and takes no comma at all.

Says that someone acted in the way the previous fact called for — result plus compliance. *Therefore* draws a conclusion; *accordingly* reports an adjustment, so it nearly always has a human agent doing something about it.

1. Demand had doubled; *accordingly*, the plant added a second shift.
2. The regulations changed in January. *Accordingly*, all permits must be renewed.
3. The forecast worsened, and the captain *accordingly* turned back.
4. *Accordingly*, the committee has revised its recommendation.
5. She was told the meeting had moved, and she planned her day *accordingly*. *(manner adverb — "in a way that fits," not a connector)*

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [as a result](#as-a-result), [as such](#as-such), [to that end](#to-that-end), [thus](#thus)

### as a result

**Pronunciation:** /əz ə rɪˈzʌlt/, emphatic /æz ə rɪˈzʌlt/ &middot; *uz uh rih-ZULT, emphatic az uh rih-ZULT* — *as* is reduced in running speech; the full /æz/ appears only under emphasis
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result. Also *as a result of*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *as a result*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *As a result*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb], *as a result*, [complement]. &middot; *As a result of* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Add *of* and it becomes a preposition taking a noun phrase, with a comma after the whole fronted phrase and no semicolon anywhere.
**Variants:** *as a consequence* (more formal), *for this reason*, *because of that*; *as a result of* [noun phrase] (prepositional)

The plainest way to mark a consequence, and the everyday alternative to *consequently* in speech and general writing. It states cause and effect without the analytic flavor *therefore* brings.

1. The bridge closed for repairs; *as a result*, the detour added twenty minutes.
2. Two suppliers went bankrupt. *As a result*, the line shut down for a week.
3. She missed the filing deadline and, *as a result*, lost the appeal.
4. *As a result*, ticket prices have nearly doubled since June.
5. *As a result of* the storm, half the county lost power. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows)*

**See also:** [consequently](#consequently), [therefore](#therefore), [accordingly](#accordingly), [in turn](#in-turn), [to that end](#to-that-end), [so](01-coordinating.md#so)

### as such

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *uz SUCH* — *as* is reduced here; the full /æz/ appears only under contrastive stress
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of inference drawn from a category just named. Also a **prepositional phrase** meaning "in that capacity" or "considered in itself," which sits inside the predicate and takes no commas.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *as such*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *As such*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *as such*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be] [noun phrase] and *as such* [verb phrase]. *("in that capacity" — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. In the "in that capacity" sense it is part of the predicate and takes no commas at all: *She chairs the committee and as such sets the agenda.*
**Variants:** *in that capacity* (the literal sense spelled out), *on that basis*, *therefore*, *accordingly*

Two jobs pull at this phrase, and only one of them is fully settled. Strictly, *as such* means "in that capacity," and *such* has to point back to a noun already on the page: *He is the trustee, and as such he signs the checks.* American writing has also stretched it into a loose *therefore* with no noun behind it — *The vendor missed two deadlines; as such, we canceled the contract* — which is now everywhere in business prose and still flagged by careful editors. The rule of thumb: if you cannot say which noun *such* refers to, write *therefore* or *accordingly* instead.

1. She is the executor of the estate; *as such*, she is the only person who can sign.
2. The building is a registered landmark. *As such*, it cannot be demolished without a hearing.
3. The document is a draft, and *as such* it has not been fact-checked. *(integrated "in that capacity" sense — no commas)*
4. Interns are students, not staff; they are, *as such*, paid a stipend rather than a salary.
5. The vendor missed two deadlines; *as such*, we canceled the contract. *(the loose "therefore" use — common, but* therefore *is safer in edited prose)*

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [accordingly](#accordingly), [consequently](#consequently), [in turn](#in-turn), [hence](#hence)

### by definition

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˌdɛfəˈnɪʃən/ &middot; *by def-uh-NISH-un*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of inference drawn from meaning alone. Also an ordinary prepositional adjunct inside the predicate (*settled by definition rather than by evidence*).
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *by definition*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *By definition*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *by definition*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *by definition* [complement]. *(integrated — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are joined — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Sitting between *be* and its complement it normally takes no commas: *A volunteer is by definition unpaid.*
**Variants:** *by its very nature*, *necessarily*, *ipso facto* (Latin), *a priori* (loosely)

Says the conclusion follows from what the words mean, not from any evidence — the cheapest and strongest kind of inference, and for that reason a favorite of people arguing in bad faith. *Therefore* claims the conclusion follows from the facts just given; *by definition* claims no facts are needed.

1. The account was opened last week; *by definition*, it has no payment history.
2. A volunteer is *by definition* unpaid.
3. *By definition*, an emergency cannot be scheduled.
4. Every member of the committee is an employee — *by definition*, none of them is independent.
5. The policy covers preexisting conditions, which *by definition* began before the coverage did.

**See also:** [ipso facto](#ipso-facto), [by implication](#by-implication), [therefore](#therefore), [hence](#hence), [thus](#thus)

### by extension

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ɪkˈstɛnʃən/ &middot; *by ik-STEN-shun*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link carrying a conclusion over to a neighboring case
**Pattern:** [Independent clause], and *by extension*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *by extension*, [independent clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] and, *by extension*, [noun phrase]. &middot; *By extension*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides in the middle and a comma after it in front position; a semicolon or period where two independent clauses meet unless *and* carries the join. It very often attaches to a second noun phrase rather than a clause, and there the pair of commas is all that is needed: *the mayor, and by extension the city*.
**Variants:** *and so*, *and therefore*, *by the same logic*, *by implication*

Carries a conclusion already reached over to something attached to it — typically from a person or a case to the larger body standing behind it. Where *by implication* draws out what was left unsaid, *by extension* stretches a stated conclusion to a further target.

1. The ruling binds the district court and, *by extension*, every case still pending there.
2. She controls the foundation; *by extension*, she controls the museum's entire endowment.
3. The ad insulted the union and *by extension* everyone who had ever walked a picket line.
4. *By extension*, the same reasoning would void half the contracts in the industry.
5. The report faults the lab director and, *by extension*, the university that hired her.

**See also:** [by implication](#by-implication), [by definition](#by-definition), [as such](#as-such), [therefore](#therefore), [in turn](#in-turn)

### by implication

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˌɪmpləˈkeɪʃən/ &middot; *by im-pluh-KAY-shun*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of inference from what was left unsaid
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *by implication*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *By implication*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *by implication*, [predicate]. &middot; [noun phrase] and, *by implication*, [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *by inference*, *impliedly* (legal), *tacitly*, *by extension*

Draws out something the previous statement entails without saying — the standard move of legal reasoning and close reading. It is careful where *by extension* is expansive: the implication is claimed to be in the words already.

1. The statute names only employers; *by implication*, contractors fall outside it.
2. The board approved the budget and, *by implication*, the layoffs it assumes.
3. *By implication*, the letter concedes that the deadline was missed.
4. She never denied that the meeting took place — *by implication*, she confirmed it.
5. The court held that the warranty was disclaimed, *by implication*, once the buyer inspected the goods.

**See also:** [by extension](#by-extension), [by definition](#by-definition), [in effect](#in-effect), [as such](#as-such), [therefore](#therefore)

### consequently

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑnsəkwəntli/ &middot; *KAHN-suh-kwunt-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *consequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Consequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *consequently* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.

Marks its clause as the outcome the previous clause produced. Where *therefore* draws a logical conclusion from evidence, *consequently* reports an actual chain of events — and it is the word most often confused with *subsequently*, which claims only that the second thing came later.

1. The vendor missed two deliveries; *consequently*, we switched suppliers.
2. Funding was cut in half. *Consequently*, the survey was reduced to one state.
3. The pipes froze and, *consequently*, burst overnight.
4. *Consequently*, the department will not be filling the position this year.
5. He never filed the paperwork; the claim was *consequently* denied.

**See also:** [as a result](#as-a-result), [in turn](#in-turn), [therefore](#therefore), [accordingly](#accordingly), [subsequently](#subsequently), [hence](#hence)

### elsewise

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛlsˌwaɪz/ &middot; *ELS-wize*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic and dialectal (nonstandard in edited prose)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of negative condition ("if not, then")
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *elsewise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Elsewise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Imperative], *elsewise* [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position. Dialect writing routinely drops that comma, and the missing comma is part of how the voice is signaled.
**Variants:** *otherwise* (the standard form), *or else*, *elsewheres* (dialectal)

An old variant of *otherwise* that outlived standard prose in regional American speech and in dialect writing — Appalachia, the Ozarks, cowboy fiction. Reach for it only when you are rendering a voice; in your own prose it reads as an error or an affectation.

1. Best start early; *elsewise* you'll be riding home in the dark.
2. "Pay the man what you owe, *elsewise* he'll take the horse." *(dialect writing)*
3. The stove has to be banked at night. *Elsewise*, the pipes freeze.
4. He kept the receipt; *elsewise* there'd have been no proving it.
5. "*Elsewise*," she said, "we'd have lost the whole crop."

**See also:** [otherwise](#otherwise), [alternatively](#alternatively), [leastways](#leastways), [in any case](#in-any-case), [anyway](#anyway)

### hence

**Pronunciation:** /hɛns/ &middot; *HENSS*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of inference. Also used before a bare noun phrase with no verb, and separately as a time adverb meaning "from now" (*two weeks hence*).
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *hence* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Hence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *hence* [noun phrase]. &middot; [Independent clause] — *hence* [noun phrase]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *hence* [independent clause]. &middot; [Time expression] *hence*. *(time adverb — "from now")*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon, period, or dash before it — a bare comma joining the two clauses is a comma splice, and the only way to get a comma there is to add a coordinator (*The vote was tied, and hence the measure failed*). The comma after it is optional and is normally **omitted** before a bare noun phrase — *hence the delay*, never *hence, the delay*.
**Variants:** *thus*, *therefore*, *ergo* (Latin; formal or joking)

A compressed *therefore*, and the only member of this family that regularly appears with no verb after it at all: *Snow was forecast — hence the empty parking lot.* In full clauses it is terser and more literary than *therefore*. The time adverb *hence* (*two weeks hence*, "from now") is a separate word class, not a variant of this connector.

1. The bridge was out; *hence* the four-hour detour.
2. The samples were contaminated. *Hence*, the results were discarded.
3. He grew up in Naples, *hence* the accent.
4. Two servers failed at once; *hence* the outage.
5. The vote was tied, and *hence* the measure failed.
6. The report is due two weeks *hence*. *(time adverb — "from now," a different word class)*

**See also:** [thus](#thus), [therefore](#therefore), [consequently](#consequently), [accordingly](#accordingly), [as such](#as-such)

### in consequence

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈkɑnsəkwɛns/ &middot; *in KAHN-suh-kwens*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result. Also *in consequence of*, a **preposition** taking a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in consequence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In consequence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in consequence*, [predicate]. &middot; *In consequence of* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. *(preposition)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. The prepositional *in consequence of* takes no semicolon, only the comma that closes a fronted phrase.
**Variants:** *consequently*, *as a consequence*, *in consequence of* [noun phrase] (preposition), *as a result*

The heavier, older sibling of *consequently*, at home in official reports, judgments, and nineteenth-century prose. American editors generally prefer *consequently* or *as a result*; *in consequence* survives where the writing is deliberately formal.

1. The line was cut on Tuesday; *in consequence*, three shifts lost their pay.
2. The company misstated its reserves. *In consequence*, the auditors resigned.
3. She had, *in consequence*, no way of knowing that the account was overdrawn.
4. *In consequence of* the flooding, the courthouse was closed for a week. *(preposition — a noun phrase follows, not a clause)*
5. Two directors voted against the merger and, *in consequence*, were not renominated.

**See also:** [consequently](#consequently), [as a result](#as-a-result), [accordingly](#accordingly), [therefore](#therefore), [hence](#hence)

### in effect

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ɪˈfɛkt/ &middot; *in ih-FEKT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of practical equivalence ("what it amounts to"). Distinct from the predicative phrase *in effect* meaning "in force" (*the rule is still in effect*), which is not a connector.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in effect*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In effect*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb], *in effect*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *in effect*. *("in force" — not a connector)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. The "in force" sense sits bare at the end of the predicate with no commas at all.
**Variants:** *effectively*, *for all practical purposes*, *to all intents and purposes* (formal), *in essence*

Restates what was just said as what it actually amounts to once the formalities are stripped away — usually something blunter than the official version. *In essence* reduces a thing to its core; *in effect* reports the practical upshot, and it is the standard way to say that something is one thing on paper and another in practice.

1. The company stopped answering our letters; *in effect*, the contract was over.
2. The fee applies to every transaction. *In effect*, it is a tax on paying your rent.
3. She was given no work and no office — *in effect*, she had been fired.
4. The amendment, *in effect*, repeals the section it claims to clarify.
5. The ordinance is still *in effect*. *("in force" — the predicative phrase, not a connector)*

**See also:** [in essence](#in-essence), [in other words](#in-other-words), [that is](#that-is), [by implication](#by-implication), [in short](#in-short)

### in turn

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈtɜrn/ &middot; *in TURN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result one link further along a chain. Also a plain adverbial meaning "one after another" (*the judges spoke in turn*), which belongs to the predicate and takes no commas.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in turn*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In turn*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in turn*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], which *in turn* [verb phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] *in turn*. *(literal "one after another" — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Inside a relative clause (*which in turn raised prices*) it is integrated and takes no commas of its own. In the literal "one after another" sense it sits in the predicate, uncommaed: *Each witness testified in turn.*
**Variants:** *which in turn* (inside a relative clause), *in its turn* (formal and less common), *and that in turn*

Marks the next link in a chain of consequences: A causes B, and B *in turn* causes C. That is exactly what separates it from *as a result*, which reports one effect and stops; *in turn* promises that the effect has itself become a cause. It is the connector of economic, mechanical, and epidemiological explanation, and the clause it opens is usually where the paragraph was headed all along.

1. The tariff raised the cost of steel; *in turn*, appliance prices climbed.
2. Fewer flights meant fewer hotel bookings. *In turn*, the whole downtown lost its winter business.
3. Higher rates cool construction, which *in turn* softens demand for lumber.
4. Cutting the night bus stranded the hospital's overnight staff; the hospital, *in turn*, started paying for cabs.
5. Each of the six speakers was given four minutes and answered questions *in turn*. *(literal "one after another" — no commas, not a connector)*

**See also:** [as a result](#as-a-result), [consequently](#consequently), [therefore](#therefore), [accordingly](#accordingly), [thus](#thus)

### ipso facto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪpsoʊ ˈfæktoʊ/ &middot; *ip-soh FAK-toh*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal, Latin) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of inference ("by that fact alone")
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *ipso facto*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Ipso facto*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *ipso facto* [complement]. *(integrated — no commas)* &middot; [Subject] [verb], *ipso facto*, [complement].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position. Inside a predicate it takes no commas: *the sale is ipso facto void.* American legal style sets it in roman, not italics.
**Variants:** *by that very fact*, *automatically*, *by definition*, *eo ipso* (rarer)

Latin for "by the fact itself": the consequence follows from the bare fact, with no further finding required. It lives in contracts, canon law, and judicial opinions — an *ipso facto clause* ends an agreement the moment a party files for bankruptcy — and in ordinary argument it usually signals someone reaching for authority they have not earned.

1. A member who fails to pay dues for two years *ipso facto* forfeits membership.
2. The filing of a petition renders the lease, *ipso facto*, terminable at the lessor's option.
3. Excommunication in such cases is incurred *ipso facto*, without any declaration by the ordinary.
4. He assumed that because the study was published, it was *ipso facto* reliable.
5. The property was pledged as security; *ipso facto*, the bank's claim outranks the others.

**See also:** [by definition](#by-definition), [by implication](#by-implication), [therefore](#therefore), [hence](#hence), [ergo](12-formal-archaic.md#ergo)

### otherwise

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðərˌwaɪz/ &middot; *UTH-ur-wize*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of negative condition ("if not"). Also a manner adverb meaning "differently" (*I couldn't have done otherwise*) and a modifier meaning "in other respects" (*an otherwise quiet street*).
**Pattern:** [Imperative or statement]; *otherwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Otherwise*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *otherwise* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], but *otherwise* [independent clause]. *("in all other respects")* &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *otherwise* [verb phrase]. *(manner adverb — "differently")*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it, comma after it in front position — though the comma is often dropped in short warnings (*Hurry; otherwise we'll be late*). Never a bare comma between the clauses.
**Variants:** *or else* (the coordinating equivalent, which does take a comma)

States what will follow if the previous statement is not acted on: "if that doesn't happen, this will." It carries a second, unrelated connector sense — "in all other respects" (*The soup was cold; otherwise the meal was excellent*) — and a manner sense (*I couldn't have done otherwise*).

1. Leave by six; *otherwise*, you'll hit traffic on the bridge.
2. Write it down. *Otherwise*, you'll forget by Thursday.
3. The deposit is due Friday; *otherwise* the room goes back on the market.
4. She has a cough. *Otherwise*, she's fine. *(the "in all other respects" sense)*
5. We got lost twice, but *otherwise* the trip was perfect.
6. She could hardly have done *otherwise*. *(manner adverb — "differently," not a connector)*

**See also:** [instead](#instead), [alternatively](#alternatively), [then](#then), [or else](01-coordinating.md#or-else)

### therefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɛrˌfɔr/ &middot; *THAIR-for*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of logical conclusion
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *therefore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Therefore*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *therefore* [verb phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [verb] and is, *therefore*, [complement].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are joined; comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle. *It rained, therefore we stayed in* is a comma splice — the single most-tested error in this category.
**Variants:** *ergo* (Latin; formal or joking), *for that reason*

Draws a conclusion that follows logically from what came before — the connector of proofs, contracts, and arguments. *So* does the same work in speech as a coordinating conjunction with just a comma. The whole difference is grammatical class, and the punctuation follows from it.

1. The witness was out of the country; *therefore*, she could not have made the call.
2. Every flight was full. *Therefore*, we rented a car and drove.
3. The two triangles have equal sides and *therefore* equal angles.
4. The clause is unenforceable and is, *therefore*, void.
5. It rained all afternoon; *therefore*, the game was postponed to Sunday.

**See also:** [thus](#thus), [hence](#hence), [consequently](#consequently), [accordingly](#accordingly), [as such](#as-such), [so](01-coordinating.md#so)

### thus

**Pronunciation:** /ðʌs/ &middot; *THUS*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of result. Also a manner adverb meaning "in this way," and a linker to *-ing* participles.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *thus* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Thus*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] and *thus* [verb phrase]. &middot; [Independent clause], *thus* [-ing participle phrase]. &middot; *Thus* [verb] [subject]. *(literary inversion)* &middot; [Verb] [object] *thus*. *(manner adverb — "in this way")*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and the comma after it is optional and usually dropped (*thus the findings cannot be generalized*). No semicolon is needed when it introduces a participle, since no second clause exists: *…washed out, thus cutting the town off.*

Carries both "therefore" and "in this way," and the second sense is what separates it from *therefore*: *Hold the brush thus* means "like this." As a connector it is the most literary of the result adverbs, and it is the only one that comfortably heads a participial phrase.

1. The sample was too small; *thus* the findings cannot be generalized.
2. The road washed out, *thus* cutting the town off for two days.
3. *Thus* began the longest winter in the region's history.
4. Each part is machined separately and *thus* can be replaced on its own.
5. The treaty was never ratified. *Thus*, the old rules still apply.
6. Hold the brush *thus*, at a shallow angle to the paper. *(manner adverb — "in this way")*

**See also:** [therefore](#therefore), [hence](#hence), [consequently](#consequently), [accordingly](#accordingly)

### to that end

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˌðæt ˈɛnd/ &middot; *tuh that END* — *to* is reduced to /tə/, never /tu/
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of purpose: the means adopted to reach a goal the previous sentence stated
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *To that end*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *to that end*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] *to that end*. &middot; *To this end*, [independent clause]. *(same connector, pointing at a goal just stated)*
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it, comma after it in front position; no comma before it in final position (*the company hired two lobbyists to that end*). A bare comma between the clauses is a comma splice, exactly as with every other adverb in this file.
**Variants:** *to this end*, *with that in mind*, *with this goal in view* (heavier), *toward that end* (also standard American), *accordingly* (result rather than purpose)

Names the action taken in service of a goal already stated — the forward-looking mirror of *as a result*, which reports what a cause produced. It belongs to proposals, mission statements, and grant applications, and it needs a stated goal in front of it or it has nothing to point at.

1. The district wants every third grader reading at grade level. *To that end*, it has added an hour of literacy instruction a day.
2. We need the report by Friday; *to that end*, I've cleared your afternoon.
3. The foundation exists to protect the watershed. *To this end*, it has bought four thousand acres of riverfront.
4. The board resolved to cut emissions in half by 2030 and hired a consultant *to that end*.
5. *To that end*, the university will match the first million dollars raised.

**See also:** [accordingly](#accordingly), [as a result](#as-a-result), [therefore](#therefore), [so that](05-purpose-result.md#so-that)

## Time, sequence, and ordering

### afterward

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftərwərd/ &middot; *AF-tur-wurd*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *afterward*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Afterward*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *afterward*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position; no comma before it in final position (*We got coffee afterward*).
**Variants:** *afterwards* (the standard **British** form, and common in American speech; *afterward* without the *-s* is the preferred form in American edited prose), *later*, *later on*, *subsequently* (formal)

Places its clause later in time than the last one. Unlike *then*, it does not claim immediate succession — an *afterward* can be hours or years later — and unlike *subsequently* it is comfortable in conversation.

1. The ceremony ran long; *afterward*, everyone crowded into the lobby.
2. We toured the plant. *Afterward*, the manager answered questions for an hour.
3. She apologized to him privately *afterward*.
4. *Afterward*, nobody could agree on what had actually been said.
5. He was fine during the interview and fell apart *afterwards*. *(the* -s *form — British in print, ordinary in American speech)*

**See also:** [subsequently](#subsequently), [thereafter](#thereafter), [then](#then), [next](#next), [previously](#previously), [meanwhile](#meanwhile)

### finally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfaɪnəli/ &middot; *FY-nuh-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence (final item). Also a plain **time adverb** meaning "at long last," which sits inside the clause and takes no commas.
**Pattern:** *Finally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Finally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *finally*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] *finally* [verb]. *(time adverb, not a connector)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when a full clause precedes — never a bare comma — and a comma after it in front position when it closes a list. When it means "at long last" it sits inside the clause and takes **no** commas at all; that position difference is the only signal of which sense you have.
**Variants:** *lastly*, *last of all*, *last but not least* (informal)

Marks the last item in a sequence of steps or arguments. Keep it apart from the time adverb meaning "at long last" (*The bus finally came*), which reports relief rather than structure.

1. *Finally*, add the zest and stir once.
2. We considered cost, then schedule; *finally*, we looked at risk.
3. *Finally*, I'd like to thank the volunteers who stayed until midnight.
4. The bus *finally* came. *(time adverb — no comma, no connector reading)*
5. *Finally*, no proposal may exceed twelve pages.

**See also:** [lastly](#lastly), [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [next](#next), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion)

### first and foremost

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɜrst ən ˈfɔrmoʊst/ &middot; *furst und FOR-mohst*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal, enumerative) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of enumeration that also ranks: the first item and the most important one at once
**Pattern:** *First and foremost*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *first and foremost*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be], *first and foremost*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *first and foremost* [complement]. *(integrated — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, with a period or semicolon before it when a clause precedes — a bare comma there is a comma splice. Inside a predicate it is integrated and takes no commas: *she is first and foremost a trial lawyer.*
**Variants:** *first of all*, *above all*, *most importantly*, *chiefly*

Opens a list and marks its first item as the one that matters most — a double duty that plain *first* does not do. Speeches and mission statements have worn it smooth, so in tight prose *above all* or a bare *first* often reads better.

1. *First and foremost*, no one was hurt.
2. *First and foremost*, the district needs teachers; the buildings can wait.
3. She is, *first and foremost*, a trial lawyer.
4. The job is *first and foremost* about showing up on time. *(integrated — no commas)*
5. *First and foremost*, I want to thank the people who stayed late all week.

**See also:** [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [first off](#first-off), [in particular](#in-particular), [lastly](#lastly), [finally](#finally)

### first off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɜrst ˈɔf/ &middot; *furst OFF*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal, enumerative) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of enumeration opening a list, most often a list of objections
**Pattern:** *First off*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *First off*, [independent clause]. &middot; *For starters*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and a period or semicolon before it — never a bare comma joining the two clauses. Front position only: neither form sits inside a clause or at the end of one.
**Variants:** *for starters*, *first of all*, *to start with*, *for one thing*

The spoken opener of a list, and in American English it very often introduces the first item in a list of complaints: *First off, nobody asked me.* *For starters* is the same move with more edge — it implies the list could go on a while — and neither belongs in formal writing, where *first* does the work.

1. *First off*, that is not what the email said.
2. *First off*, we can't afford it; second, we don't have the space.
3. *For starters*, the permit was never filed.
4. "What's wrong with the plan?" "*First off*, it's illegal."
5. *For starters*, he showed up two hours late and without the paperwork.

**See also:** [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [first and foremost](#first-and-foremost), [next](#next), [lastly](#lastly), [anyway](#anyway)

### first, second, third

**Pronunciation:** /fɜrst/ &middot; *FURST*; /ˈsɛkənd/ &middot; *SEK-und*; /θɜrd/ &middot; *THURD*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (enumerative series) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of enumeration. The same words are ordinary **ordinal adjectives** and **nouns** elsewhere (*the first draft*, *she finished second*); only the fronted, comma-marked use is a connector.
**Pattern:** *First*, [independent clause]. *Second*, [independent clause]. *Third*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *second*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Statement]: *first*, [item]; *second*, [item]. &middot; *First* [noun phrase] [verb phrase]. *(ordinal adjective — no comma)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after each one in front position. Each ordinal introduces its own sentence, so a period or semicolon separates the items — the series never runs on commas alone, which would be a string of comma splices.
**Variants:** *firstly, secondly, thirdly* (equally correct, a shade more formal; American style guides generally prefer the short forms), *first of all*, *to begin with*, *for one thing … for another*

Numbers the steps of an argument or a set of instructions and tells the reader how many more are coming. The one rule that matters is consistency: do not mix the two series (*first … secondly*), and do not start with *firstly* unless you are prepared to finish with *fourthly*.

1. *First*, preheat the oven. *Second*, grease the pan.
2. There are two problems: *first*, the cost; *second*, the timeline.
3. *First*, the deadline has passed. *Second*, the form was never signed. *Third*, nobody told the client.
4. *Firstly*, I'd question the sample size.
5. *First of all*, that is not what I said.
6. The *first* draft ran three times too long. *(ordinal adjective — no comma, not a connector)*

**See also:** [next](#next), [finally](#finally), [lastly](#lastly), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion)

### henceforth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛnsˈfɔrθ/ &middot; *henss-FORTH*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal (legal, ceremonial)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of time (from now on)
**Pattern:** *Henceforth*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *henceforth*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *henceforth* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides mid-clause.
**Variants:** *henceforward* (rarer), *from now on* / *going forward* (the everyday equivalents)

Announces that a new rule holds for all future time. It is genuinely formal — statutes, bylaws, and proclamations — and in ordinary American speech *from now on* is what people actually say.

1. *Henceforth*, all requests must be submitted through the portal.
2. The building will *henceforth* be known as the Hartley Center.
3. The treaty was signed in May; *henceforth*, the border was closed to trade.
4. *Henceforth*, no member may vote by proxy.
5. She resigned from the board and *henceforth* refused to discuss the company at all.

**See also:** [hitherto](#hitherto), [thereafter](#thereafter), [previously](#previously), [subsequently](#subsequently), [thus](#thus)

### hitherto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪðərˈtu/ &middot; *hith-ur-TOO*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of time (up to now)
**Pattern:** *Hitherto*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *hitherto* [verb phrase]. &middot; [noun phrase] *hitherto* [past participle].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position; no commas when it sits before the verb or modifies a participle (*hitherto unknown*). Semicolon or period before it if a second independent clause precedes — a bare comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *heretofore* (legal), *until now*, *up to now* (everyday)

"Up to this point" — it names the state of affairs that held before the change you are about to describe. It is the past-facing mirror of *henceforth*, and it sounds distinctly bookish in American speech.

1. *Hitherto*, the treaty had been observed by both sides.
2. The technique revealed structures *hitherto* invisible to microscopy.
3. Sales had *hitherto* been confined to the Northeast; the new stores changed that.
4. The archive had been sealed for eighty years; *hitherto*, not one of these letters had been published.
5. She published data that had *hitherto* been held privately by the manufacturer.

**See also:** [henceforth](#henceforth), [previously](#previously), [subsequently](#subsequently)

### in the first instance

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈfɜrst ˌɪnstəns/ &middot; *in thuh FURST in-stunss*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal (legal, administrative)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence meaning "at the first stage, before anything else is tried." Distinct from the fixed legal phrase *court of first instance*, which names the court where a case is first heard.
**Pattern:** *In the first instance*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in the first instance*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [modal] [verb phrase], *in the first instance*, [continuation]. &middot; [Subject] should [verb phrase] *in the first instance*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, and a comma before it at the end; a period or semicolon where two independent clauses meet.
**Variants:** *initially*, *at first*, *to begin with*, *first*

Names the first step of a procedure, with the strong implication that further steps exist if it fails — which is why it belongs to policies, statutes, and grievance procedures rather than to narrative. It is commoner in British than American usage, and an American reader hears it as institutional language.

1. *In the first instance*, complaints should be raised with the immediate supervisor.
2. Applications are reviewed, *in the first instance*, by the department chair.
3. The matter is heard by a magistrate *in the first instance*, with an appeal to the circuit court.
4. *In the first instance*, the agency asked only for the missing schedules.
5. Funding is allocated *in the first instance* to districts with the highest poverty rates.

**See also:** [in the first place](#in-the-first-place), [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [previously](#previously), [subsequently](#subsequently), [finally](#finally)

### in the first place

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈfɜrst ˌpleɪs/ &middot; *in thuh FURST playss*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — two jobs. Fronted, it enumerates the first of several reasons; at the end of a clause it points back to the beginning of the whole business and means "before any of this ever started."
**Pattern:** *In the first place*, [independent clause]. &middot; *In the first place*, [independent clause]; *in the second place*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *in the first place*. *(retrospective — no comma before it)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, with a period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice. In the retrospective sense it closes the clause and takes **no** comma at all: *Why did you sign it in the first place?*
**Variants:** *in the second place* (the matching enumerator), *first of all*, *to begin with* (both senses), *originally*

Fronted, it opens an argument with the first of a numbered set of reasons and promises more. Stranded at the end, it makes a different point entirely — that the thing should never have been started — and it carries an audible note of complaint.

1. *In the first place*, the deadline had already passed.
2. *In the first place*, she never agreed to it; *in the second place*, she wasn't even in the country.
3. Why did you lend him the car *in the first place*? *(retrospective — no comma)*
4. *In the first place*, the road isn't ours to close.
5. The permit should never have been issued *in the first place*.

**See also:** [in the first instance](#in-the-first-instance), [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [first off](#first-off), [after all](#after-all), [anyway](#anyway)

### lastly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlæstli/ &middot; *LAST-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of enumeration (final item)
**Pattern:** *Lastly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Lastly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *lastly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [item], [item], and, *lastly*, [final item]. *(inside a list)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when a full clause precedes — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. It fronts its own clause; the one other place it appears is inside a list, where it takes commas on both sides.
**Variants:** *last of all*, *last but not least* (informal)

Closes an enumerated list. It is slightly more formal than *finally* and has one practical advantage over it: *lastly* has no "at long last" sense, so it can never be misread as a time adverb.

1. *Lastly*, please initial the bottom of each page.
2. We looked at price, then at reliability; *lastly*, we considered resale value.
3. *Lastly*, I want to say something about the budget.
4. The kit contains a wrench, two gaskets, and, *lastly*, a tube of sealant.
5. *Lastly*, no refunds will be issued after the thirtieth.

**See also:** [finally](#finally), [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion), [to sum up](#to-sum-up)

### meanwhile

**Pronunciation:** /ˈminˌwaɪl/ &middot; *MEEN-wile*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of simultaneity
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *meanwhile*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Meanwhile*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *meanwhile*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *in the meantime* (preferred for the waiting period between two events), *meantime* (informal), *at the same time*, *simultaneously* (technical)

Points at what was happening elsewhere at the same time. In journalism it often carries a dry contrast — *The mayor promised action; meanwhile, the potholes got worse* — where the simultaneity is the joke. Use *in the meantime* when you mean the interval between two events rather than a parallel scene.

1. I'll start the grill; *meanwhile*, you can chop the onions.
2. The trial dragged into its third week. *Meanwhile*, the company kept selling the product.
3. Her flight was canceled. *In the meantime*, the hotel gave away her room.
4. The council debated the wording for an hour; the building, *meanwhile*, went on flooding.
5. *Meanwhile*, back at the office, nobody had noticed she was gone.

**See also:** [at the same time](#at-the-same-time), [then](#then), [afterward](#afterward), [subsequently](#subsequently), [in contrast](#in-contrast)

### next

**Pronunciation:** /nɛkst/ &middot; *NEKST*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence. Also a determiner-like adjective (*next week*, *the next chapter*) and part of the preposition *next to*.
**Pattern:** *Next*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Next*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb] *next*. &middot; *Next* [noun phrase] [verb phrase]. *(adjective — no comma)* &middot; [clause] *next to* [noun phrase]. *(part of a preposition — not this connector)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position; each step is normally its own sentence, so a period does the separating, and a semicolon is required if you run two clauses together — a bare comma there is a comma splice. In final position (*What happens next?*) it takes no comma.
**Variants:** *then*, *after that*, *second* (in a numbered series)

The step-by-step connector of recipes, instructions, and narration. It differs from *then* only in explicitness: *next* announces that this is the following item in a list you have already promised.

1. *Next*, whisk the eggs into the warm milk.
2. We interviewed the staff. *Next*, we pulled three years of invoices.
3. *Next*, the officer asked for my registration.
4. Attach the two brackets. *Next*, tighten the bolts a quarter turn each.
5. Nobody knew what would happen *next*.
6. We're moving *next* month, into the house *next* to the school. *(adjective and preposition — neither one is this connector)*

**See also:** [then](#then), [first, second, third](#first-second-third), [finally](#finally), [afterward](#afterward)

### previously

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpriviəsli/ &middot; *PREE-vee-us-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of time (earlier point). Also a plain time adverb inside the predicate and a modifier of a participle or adjective (*previously undisclosed*), neither of which takes commas.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *previously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Previously*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *previously* [verb phrase]. &middot; *Previously* [past participle] [noun phrase] [verb phrase]. *(modifier — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position; no commas when it stands directly before the main verb (*had previously testified*).
**Variants:** *before that*, *earlier* (everyday), *formerly* (of a former state, name, or title)

Points back to a time earlier than the one under discussion. It is the reporting word: *previously* belongs to résumés, court records, and news copy, where *earlier* or *before that* would be the conversational choice.

1. The building is now a library; *previously*, it housed the county jail.
2. *Previously*, she had worked as a translator in Geneva.
3. Two witnesses had *previously* given the same account to police.
4. The rate was fixed at four percent. *Previously*, it floated with the market.
5. *Previously* undisclosed emails surfaced during discovery. *(modifying a participle — no commas, not a connector)*

**See also:** [hitherto](#hitherto), [afterward](#afterward), [subsequently](#subsequently), [henceforth](#henceforth)

### subsequently

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌbsəkwəntli/ &middot; *SUB-suh-kwunt-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *subsequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; *Subsequently*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [auxiliary] *subsequently* [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position; no commas before the main verb (*was subsequently withdrawn*).

"Afterward," in the register of a report or a court record. It means **only** that the second event came later — it makes no causal claim at all, which is precisely where it parts company with *consequently*, the word it is most often confused with.

1. The charges were *subsequently* dropped.
2. He resigned in March; *subsequently*, two of his deputies left as well.
3. *Subsequently*, the manufacturer recalled every unit sold that year.
4. The paper was published in 2019 and was *subsequently* retracted.
5. She was hired as an intern and *subsequently* promoted twice in one year.

**See also:** [consequently](#consequently), [afterward](#afterward), [thereafter](#thereafter), [then](#then), [previously](#previously)

### then

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛn/, weak /ðən/ &middot; *THEN, weak thun*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence, or of inference drawn from a condition. Also a plain time adverb meaning "at that time" (*we lived in Ohio then*) and, before a title, an attributive adjective (*the then governor*).
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *then* [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *then* [independent clause]. &middot; If [clause], *then* [main clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *then*. *(inference, informal)* &middot; the *then* [noun]. *(attributive adjective — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it joins two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless a coordinator carries the join (*and then*) — and normally **no** comma after it (*Then we drove home*, not *Then, we drove home*). In the *if … then* pattern the comma belongs after the *if* clause, not after *then*.
**Variants:** *and then* (the usual spoken form), *in that case* (for the inference sense only), *back then* (time reference)

Two connector jobs. First, next in a sequence: *Brown the onions; then add the garlic.* Second, the conclusion drawn from a condition: *If that's true, then we have a problem.* The second job pairs it with *if* so tightly that it feels like a conjunction, but it still passes the movement test — *If that's true, we have a problem, then* — which a real conjunction never would.

1. Preheat the oven; *then* mix the dry ingredients.
2. We finished the paperwork, and *then* the power went out.
3. If the flight is canceled, *then* we'll drive.
4. "She already left." "*Then* there's no point waiting."
5. First he denied it; *then* he blamed the intern.
6. We lived in Ohio *then*, and the *then* governor was a family friend. *(plain time adverb and attributive adjective — neither one is this connector)*

**See also:** [next](#next), [afterward](#afterward), [thereafter](#thereafter), [otherwise](#otherwise), [therefore](#therefore), [and then](01-coordinating.md#and-then)

### thereafter

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðɛrˈæftər/ &middot; *thair-AF-tur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal (contracts, statutes, and narrative history)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of sequence ("after that point")
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *thereafter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Thereafter*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [verb phrase] *thereafter*. &middot; [Time expression] *thereafter*, [independent clause]. *(as in* shortly thereafter*)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position. In final position it belongs to the predicate and takes no comma before it: *The rate resets annually thereafter.* In the fixed phrases *shortly thereafter* and *soon thereafter*, the comma follows the whole phrase, not the adverb.
**Variants:** *after that*, *from then on*, *subsequently* (formal), *shortly thereafter* / *soon thereafter* (the usual fixed phrases), *thenceforth* (archaic)

"After that point," anchored to a moment the text has already fixed. It is *afterward* in the register of a lease, a statute, or a chronicle, and it differs from *henceforth* in where it stands: *henceforth* counts forward from the moment of writing, *thereafter* from a reference point inside the story. It needs that reference point — with nothing before it for *there* to name, it reads as a drafting error.

1. The lease runs for three years; *thereafter*, it renews month to month.
2. She served two terms on the council. *Thereafter*, she never held public office again.
3. Interest is fixed for the first sixty months and floats *thereafter*.
4. The dam failed at 4:10 a.m.; shortly *thereafter*, the sirens sounded downstream.
5. *Thereafter*, every shipment was inspected at the border.

**See also:** [afterward](#afterward), [subsequently](#subsequently), [henceforth](#henceforth), [previously](#previously), [then](#then)

## Example, restatement, and specification

### as aforesaid

**Pronunciation:** /æz əˈfɔrˌsɛd/ &middot; *az uh-FOR-sed*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** legal boilerplate (archaic outside legal drafting)
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — an adverbial back-reference identifying something already stated in the same document
**Pattern:** [Independent clause], *as aforesaid*, [continuation]. &middot; [noun phrase] *as aforesaid*. &middot; *As aforesaid*, [independent clause]. &middot; the said [noun] *as aforesaid*.
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides when it interrupts a clause; no commas when it trails the noun phrase it points back to (*the premises as aforesaid*). Between two independent clauses a semicolon or period is still required, as with any conjunctive adverb.
**Variants:** *as hereinbefore stated*, *as previously stated*, *as set forth above*, *aforementioned* (attributive)

The drafter's back-pointer: "the thing I already identified above," so that the instrument need not describe it twice. It belongs to deeds, leases, wills, and pleadings — outside a legal document it reads as parody, and plain-language drafting guides push writers toward *as stated above* or simple repetition of the name.

1. The Seller, *as aforesaid*, shall deliver clear title on or before the closing date.
2. The rent *as aforesaid* shall be payable in twelve equal monthly installments.
3. *As aforesaid*, the parties have agreed to submit any dispute to arbitration in Cook County.
4. Witness my hand and seal the day and year *as aforesaid*.
5. The said premises *as aforesaid* are conveyed subject to all easements of record.

**See also:** [for the avoidance of doubt](#for-the-avoidance-of-doubt), [namely](#namely), [that is](#that-is), [to wit](12-formal-archaic.md#to-wit), [hereby](12-formal-archaic.md#hereby)

### as it were

**Pronunciation:** /æz ɪt ˈwɜr/, reduced /əz ɪt ˈwɜr/ &middot; *az it WUR, reduced uz it WUR*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral (bookish)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a parenthetical comment on the wording just used, marking it as figurative or approximate. The verb is a fossilized **subjunctive** (*were*), which is why it never changes tense.
**Pattern:** [clause], *as it were*, [continuation]. &middot; [phrase] — *as it were* — [continuation]. &middot; [Independent clause], *as it were*.
**Punctuation:** Always fenced by commas or dashes, wherever it sits, including the comma before it when it closes a sentence. It joins no clauses, so it never takes a semicolon of its own.
**Variants:** *so to speak*, *in a manner of speaking*, *if you will*, *sort of* (informal)

Apologizes for a metaphor by marking the words just used as not literally true. *So to speak* does the same job in plainer clothes; *as it were* carries an unmistakably donnish tone, and used more than once a page it reads as fussy.

1. He became, *as it were*, the family's accountant, though nobody ever paid him.
2. The committee had painted itself into a corner, *as it were*.
3. She was the ghost in the machine, *as it were* — everywhere and nowhere in the company's records.
4. We were, *as it were*, guests in our own house that summer.
5. The novel is a map of the city, *as it were*, drawn entirely from memory.

**See also:** [in other words](#in-other-words), [that is](#that-is), [strictly speaking](#strictly-speaking), [in effect](#in-effect), [of course](#of-course)

### case in point

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkeɪs ɪn ˈpɔɪnt/ &middot; *kayss in POYNT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a noun phrase used as a connector) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** No clause of its own in the connector use — it labels what follows as the illustration of the claim just made. It is also an ordinary **noun phrase** inside a clause (*a case in point is the 2019 audit*).
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Case in point*: [example]. &middot; [Independent clause] — *case in point*, [example]. &middot; A *case in point* is [noun phrase]. *(ordinary noun phrase)* &middot; [Noun phrase] is a *case in point*.
**Punctuation:** A period or dash before it, and a colon or comma after it — the colon is the usual American choice, because what follows is normally a fragment rather than a clause. As a full noun phrase inside a sentence it takes no punctuation at all.
**Variants:** *a case in point*, *exhibit A* (informal), *witness* (formal: *witness the 2008 collapse*), *for example*

Presents the next thing said as the proof of the point just made, and it is the one member of the exemplification family that is comfortable as a bare fragment. *For example* offers one of many and claims nothing about it; *case in point* claims the example is decisive.

1. Deferred maintenance always costs more later. *Case in point*: the roof of the middle school.
2. Small towns lose their pharmacies first. *Case in point* — Rushville, which has had none since 2021.
3. The agency approves nearly everything it reviews; the pipeline permit is a *case in point*.
4. *Case in point*: last winter's outage, which lasted six days.
5. A *case in point* is the 1994 amendment, which nobody has ever invoked.

**See also:** [for example](#for-example), [for instance](#for-instance), [in particular](#in-particular), [namely](#namely), [e.g.](#eg)

### e.g.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌi ˈdʒi/ &middot; *ee-JEE* — read aloud as "for example" in careful speech
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal abbreviation) &middot; **Register:** formal (writing only)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — it introduces one or more sample items inside a clause
**Pattern:** [clause] (*e.g.*, [item], [item]). &middot; [noun phrase] — *e.g.*, [item] — [continuation]. &middot; [clause], *e.g.*, [item].
**Punctuation:** American style keeps both periods and puts a comma after the abbreviation — *e.g.,* — every time. It normally lives inside parentheses, dashes, or commas, and it is never followed by *etc.* in the same list, since *e.g.* already announces that the list is partial. If it ends a sentence, its final period does double duty; do not type a second one.
**Variants:** *eg* / *e.g* (British and web styles; American editing keeps both periods)

From Latin *exempli gratia*, "for the sake of an example." It introduces a sample, never a complete list — and that is the entire difference between it and *i.e.*, which restates exactly what was meant. Spell it out as *for example* in running text; keep the abbreviation for parentheses and notes.

1. Bring something warm (*e.g.*, a fleece or a heavy sweater).
2. Citrus fruits — *e.g.*, lemons, limes, and grapefruit — grow well in the valley.
3. Avoid oily fish, *e.g.*, mackerel and sardines, for two days before the test.
4. Some states (*e.g.*, Nevada) have no state income tax.
5. The policy covers minor repairs, *e.g.*, a cracked windshield.

**See also:** [for example](#for-example), [i.e.](#ie), [namely](#namely), [that is](#that-is)

### for example

**Pronunciation:** /fər ɪɡˈzæmpəl/ &middot; *fur ig-ZAM-pul*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of exemplification
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *for example*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *For example*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *for example*, [predicate]. &middot; [noun phrase], *for example*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are joined — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, and a comma before it at the end.
**Variants:** *for instance* (interchangeable in every American context), *e.g.* (the written abbreviation), *say* (informal: *take a small town, say, Burlington*)

Introduces one illustration of a general point. It says nothing about the size of the whole set — for a complete list you want *namely*, and for a restatement of the same idea, *that is*.

1. Some birds don't migrate. Cardinals, *for example*, stay all winter.
2. The rules are inconsistent; *for example*, you can appeal a fine but not a suspension.
3. *For instance*, last April that same server went down twice in a week.
4. Many household costs have risen — insurance, *for example*, is up nine percent.
5. Take a small city like Burlington, *for example*.

**See also:** [e.g.](#eg), [namely](#namely), [in particular](#in-particular), [that is](#that-is), [similarly](#similarly), [in fact](#in-fact)

### for instance

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈɪnstəns/ &middot; *fur IN-stunss*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of exemplification
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *for instance*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *For instance*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *for instance*, [predicate]. &middot; [noun phrase], *for instance*.
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, and a comma before it when it trails a noun phrase.
**Variants:** *for example* (interchangeable), *e.g.* (the written abbreviation), *say*, *take [noun phrase]*

Interchangeable with *for example* in every American context; the only real difference is rhythm, and *for instance* is slightly the commoner of the two in speech and in mid-sentence position. Like *for example*, it offers one illustration and claims nothing about the rest of the set.

1. Some fees are unavoidable; the recording fee, *for instance*, is set by the county.
2. *For instance*, the Cleveland office closed three weeks before anyone was told.
3. Take the school calendar, *for instance* — it hasn't changed since 1978.
4. Older houses hide their problems. *For instance*, the wiring in this one is cloth-wrapped.
5. She has a talent for the small kindness — remembering birthdays, *for instance*.

**See also:** [for example](#for-example), [e.g.](#eg), [case in point](#case-in-point), [in particular](#in-particular), [namely](#namely)

### for the avoidance of doubt

**Pronunciation:** /fər ði əˌvɔɪdəns əv ˈdaʊt/ &middot; *fur thee uh-VOY-dunss uv DOWT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** legal boilerplate (formal contract drafting)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of clarification, announcing that what follows resolves an ambiguity rather than adding a new obligation
**Pattern:** *For the avoidance of doubt*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *For the avoidance of doubt*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *for the avoidance of doubt*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, and a period or semicolon before it — a bare comma joining the two clauses is a comma splice. Front position: drafters put it at the head of its own sentence or its own numbered subsection.
**Variants:** *to avoid doubt*, *for clarity*, *it being understood that*, *provided, however, that* (which changes the deal rather than clarifying it)

A drafter's flag meaning "this sentence only spells out what the agreement already says." Litigators treat it warily — a clause that truly adds nothing is unnecessary, and one that adds something has been mislabeled — but it is standard furniture in commercial contracts and, increasingly, in corporate email.

1. *For the avoidance of doubt*, nothing in this Section limits the Company's rights under Section 4.2.
2. *For the avoidance of doubt*, the Fee is exclusive of all applicable taxes.
3. The license is non-transferable. *For the avoidance of doubt*, a change of control constitutes a transfer.
4. *For the avoidance of doubt*, no partnership or joint venture is created by this Agreement.
5. *For the avoidance of doubt*, the parties agree that time is of the essence with respect to each delivery date.

**See also:** [as aforesaid](#as-aforesaid), [namely](#namely), [that is](#that-is), [to wit](12-formal-archaic.md#to-wit), [provided always that](12-formal-archaic.md#provided-always-that)

### I mean

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈmin/ &middot; *eye MEEN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a fixed discourse marker) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of self-correction and restatement. It is frozen in the first-person present: *I meant* is a report of what somebody said, not this connector.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause] — *I mean*, [corrected clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *I mean*, [independent clause]. &middot; [phrase], *I mean*, [corrected phrase].
**Punctuation:** A dash or period before it and a comma after it, with commas on both sides when it interrupts. A bare comma joining two independent clauses across it is still a comma splice in edited prose, though transcripts and dialogue print it that way to keep the rhythm of speech.
**Variants:** *that is* (the written equivalent), *or rather*, *well, I mean*, *I mean like* (very informal)

The spoken counterpart of *that is*: it repairs, sharpens, or defends what was just said. In fast conversation it has also become a plain turn-opener with no repair work left in it (*I mean, who wouldn't?*), which is why it grates on the page — use *that is* or *in other words* there.

1. It's expensive — *I mean*, not outrageous, but more than I wanted to spend.
2. She's my cousin. *I mean*, technically my second cousin.
3. We left around nine, *I mean*, closer to nine thirty.
4. *I mean*, what was he supposed to do, walk home?
5. He was upset. *I mean*, he was really upset.

**See also:** [that is](#that-is), [in other words](#in-other-words), [rather](#rather), [strictly speaking](#strictly-speaking), [to be honest](#to-be-honest)

### i.e.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪ ˈi/ &middot; *eye-EE* — read aloud as "that is"
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal abbreviation) &middot; **Register:** formal (writing only)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — it introduces a restatement inside a clause
**Pattern:** [clause] (*i.e.*, [restatement]). &middot; [noun phrase] — *i.e.*, [restatement] — [continuation]. &middot; [clause], *i.e.*, [restatement].
**Punctuation:** Both periods stay, and American style puts a comma after the abbreviation — *i.e.,* — every time. Like *e.g.*, it belongs inside parentheses, dashes, or commas rather than at the head of a sentence.
**Variants:** *ie* / *i.e* (British and web styles; American editing keeps both periods)

From Latin *id est*, "that is." It renames or restates the preceding phrase exactly; it never introduces a mere example. The test is substitution: if "in other words" fits, *i.e.* is right, and if "for example" fits, you wanted *e.g.*

1. The fee is due at the end of the quarter (*i.e.*, March 31).
2. Only full members — *i.e.*, those who have paid dues for two years — may vote.
3. The device runs on a single cell, *i.e.*, one AA battery.
4. Payment is due on receipt, *i.e.*, the day the invoice arrives.
5. She works in the humanities, *i.e.*, the departments hit hardest by the cuts.

**See also:** [that is](#that-is), [in other words](#in-other-words), [e.g.](#eg), [namely](#namely)

### in essence

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈɛsəns/ &middot; *in ES-unss*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of restatement that reduces something to its core
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in essence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In essence*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be], *in essence*, [complement]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *in essence* [complement]. *(integrated — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *essentially*, *basically* (informal), *at bottom*, *in substance* (legal)

Strips a complicated statement down to what it fundamentally is. *In effect* reports the practical result; *in essence* reports the underlying nature — and the two are not interchangeable, though speakers swap them constantly.

1. The proposal runs ninety pages; *in essence*, it asks the city for a tax abatement.
2. *In essence*, both sides agree about the facts and disagree about what they mean.
3. The dispute is, *in essence*, about who owns the parking lot.
4. The new rule is *in essence* the old rule with a longer form to fill out.
5. *In essence*, the drug slows the disease without stopping it.

**See also:** [in effect](#in-effect), [in short](#in-short), [in other words](#in-other-words), [that is](#that-is), [in the main](#in-the-main)

### in other words

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˌʌðər ˈwɜrdz/ &middot; *in uth-ur WURDZ*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of restatement
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *In other words*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in other words*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] — *in other words*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period, semicolon, or dash before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. It is a front-position connector; it does not sit at the end of a clause.
**Variants:** *put another way*, *to put it another way*, *what this means is* (informal)

Restates what was just said in plainer terms, or spells out what it really amounts to. Where *that is* narrows and specifies, *in other words* translates — and in conversation it very often introduces a blunt paraphrase the other person may not enjoy hearing.

1. The account is overdrawn; *in other words*, we are out of money.
2. The position was eliminated. *In other words*, he was fired.
3. She said she'd "circle back in Q3" — *in other words*, never.
4. *In other words*, nobody read the contract before signing it.
5. The results were not statistically significant; *in other words*, the drug did nothing.

**See also:** [that is](#that-is), [i.e.](#ie), [in short](#in-short), [namely](#namely)

### in particular

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn pərˈtɪkjələr/ &middot; *in pur-TIK-yuh-lur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of specification (singling one case out of a set already mentioned). Also a postmodifier attached directly to a noun phrase.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in particular*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In particular*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in particular*, [predicate]. &middot; [Noun phrase] *in particular* [verb phrase]. *(postmodifier — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Attached to a noun phrase it takes no commas at all: *one paragraph in particular worried the lawyers.*
**Variants:** *particularly*, *especially*, *notably*, *above all* (strongest), *specifically* (exactness rather than prominence)

Picks one member out of a group already named and says it matters most. *For example* offers a sample and claims nothing about it; *in particular* claims prominence — this one above the others — which is why it so often carries the weight of the argument.

1. The storm damaged three schools; *in particular*, the roof of the high school gym came down.
2. Costs are up across the board. *In particular*, insurance has nearly doubled since 2019.
3. One paragraph *in particular* worried the lawyers. *(attached to the noun phrase — no commas)*
4. Older drivers, *in particular*, benefit from the larger displays.
5. *In particular*, no employee may accept a gift worth more than fifty dollars.

**See also:** [specifically](#specifically), [namely](#namely), [for example](#for-example), [that is](#that-is), [indeed](#indeed)

### namely

**Pronunciation:** /ˈneɪmli/ &middot; *NAYM-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — it introduces the exact identification of something already named in general terms
**Pattern:** [clause], *namely*, [list]. &middot; [clause] — *namely*, [item]. &middot; [noun phrase]: *namely*, [item].
**Punctuation:** A comma, dash, or colon before it; the comma after it is optional (*namely, the cost* and *namely the cost* are both standard). It does not take a semicolon, because what follows is a phrase, not a clause.
**Variants:** *viz.* (very formal, from Latin *videlicet*), *to wit* (legal), *specifically*, *that is*

Introduces the complete and exact identification of what was just referred to generally — all of it, not a sample. *For example* offers one of many; *namely* closes the set, which is why it is the safer word in contracts and specifications.

1. Only one thing worried her, *namely* the cost.
2. Three states border the lake, *namely* Ohio, Michigan, and Indiana.
3. The committee has a single job: *namely*, to decide whether the site is safe.
4. He left out the most important detail — *namely*, that he had already resigned.
5. Two witnesses were called, *viz.*, the driver and the paramedic.

**See also:** [that is](#that-is), [for example](#for-example), [i.e.](#ie), [in other words](#in-other-words), [specifically](#specifically)

### specifically

**Pronunciation:** /spəˈsɪfɪkli/ &middot; *spuh-SIF-ik-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of specification. Also a plain manner adverb meaning "expressly, for that purpose," which sits in the predicate.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *specifically*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Specifically*, [independent clause]. &middot; [clause], *specifically*, [noun phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary] *specifically* [verb phrase]. *(manner adverb — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle. Before a bare phrase rather than a clause, a comma or dash is enough. As a manner adverb inside the predicate it takes no commas: *the grant was specifically earmarked for equipment.*
**Variants:** *namely* (for a closed list), *that is*, *to be specific*, *more precisely*, *in particular* (prominence rather than exactness)

Narrows a general statement down to the exact case, and it is the one word in this family that takes either a full clause or a bare phrase after it. *Namely* closes a set; *specifically* sharpens a description — and where *i.e.* restates the same thing in other terms, *specifically* moves one step down in generality.

1. The audit found problems; *specifically*, four invoices had no matching purchase order.
2. Attendance is down. *Specifically*, weeknight showings have lost a third of their audience.
3. She objected to one line, *specifically*, the clause about weekend hours.
4. *Specifically*, the ordinance bars parking on the north side between two and six.
5. The room was built *specifically* for recording. *(manner adverb — "expressly," not a connector)*

**See also:** [namely](#namely), [in particular](#in-particular), [that is](#that-is), [i.e.](#ie), [in other words](#in-other-words)

### strictly speaking

**Pronunciation:** /ˌstrɪktli ˈspikɪŋ/ &middot; *strik-tlee SPEE-king*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a comment adverbial narrowing what follows to its exact, technical truth. Grammatically a fronted participial phrase, and nothing in the sentence has to be doing the speaking.
**Pattern:** *Strictly speaking*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be], *strictly speaking*, [complement]. &middot; [Independent clause], *strictly speaking*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. It joins no clauses on its own, so where two independent clauses meet the usual semicolon or period is still required.
**Variants:** *technically*, *properly speaking*, *strictly*, *in the strict sense*

Signals that what follows is true by the exact definition, and usually that ordinary usage says otherwise: *Strictly speaking, a tomato is a fruit.* Its opposites are *generally speaking* and *broadly speaking*, and its constant companion is a mild pedantry the listener did not ask for.

1. *Strictly speaking*, the building is in the township, not the city.
2. She is, *strictly speaking*, an independent contractor rather than an employee.
3. *Strictly speaking*, none of this is confidential, but I'd keep it to yourself.
4. A koala isn't a bear, *strictly speaking*.
5. *Strictly speaking*, the deadline is Friday; in practice, nobody reads them until Monday.

**See also:** [generally speaking](#generally-speaking), [in general](#in-general), [specifically](#specifically), [in particular](#in-particular), [that is](#that-is)

### that is

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈɪz/ &middot; *that IZ*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause or phrase — adverbial link of restatement and specification
**Pattern:** [clause], *that is*, [restatement]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *That is*, [independent clause]. &middot; [noun phrase] — *that is*, [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma, dash, or period before it, comma after it; commas on both sides when it sits inside a clause. Never a bare comma joining two independent clauses — use a period or semicolon there.
**Variants:** *that is to say*, *i.e.* (the written abbreviation), *or* used appositively (*the coda, or final section*)

Restates what came before in a more exact form, narrowing rather than paraphrasing. It sits between its two neighbors: *in other words* rewrites the whole idea in plainer language, while *namely* identifies the members of a set.

1. We'll meet on the first Tuesday, *that is*, the seventh.
2. The offer applies to residents — *that is*, anyone who has lived here six months.
3. She's a stringer; *that is*, she's paid per story rather than by salary.
4. *That is*, no one had checked whether the permit was still valid.
5. He was let go for cause, *that is to say*, for cause the company never described.

**See also:** [i.e.](#ie), [in other words](#in-other-words), [namely](#namely), [specifically](#specifically), [in particular](#in-particular), [for example](#for-example)

### which is to say

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪtʃ ɪz tə ˈseɪ/ &middot; *wich iz tuh SAY*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a continuative relative) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** A continuative **relative clause** that restates the whole clause before it. Because the *which* needs an antecedent in the same sentence, it cannot open one in edited prose — though writers break that rule for effect.
**Pattern:** [Independent clause], *which is to say*, [restatement]. &middot; [Independent clause] — *which is to say*, [restatement]. &middot; [noun phrase], *which is to say* [noun phrase], [continuation].
**Punctuation:** A comma or dash before it — this is one of the few restatement connectors that takes a comma rather than a semicolon, because the relative pronoun subordinates what follows. The comma after it is optional and usually dropped before a short phrase.
**Variants:** *that is to say*, *that is*, *i.e.*, *or rather*

Restates the preceding clause in sharper or franker terms, generally to expose what it really amounts to. Its relative-clause grammar is the practical difference from *in other words*: it attaches across a comma where *in other words* would demand a semicolon or a period.

1. The position was eliminated, *which is to say* he was fired.
2. The fund is self-insured, *which is to say* the county pays every claim out of the general budget.
3. They asked for volunteers — *which is to say*, they asked us.
4. The trial ended in a hung jury, *which is to say* nothing was decided at all.
5. The house is a fixer-upper, *which is to say* the floors are gone.

**See also:** [that is](#that-is), [in other words](#in-other-words), [i.e.](#ie), [namely](#namely), [in effect](#in-effect)

## Emphasis, evidence, and appeal to the obvious

### after all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæftər ˈɔl/ &middot; *af-tur AWL*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of justification. In final position, a different sense: "contrary to expectation."
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *after all*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *After all*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *after all*. *("contrary to expectation")*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it in front position. In the "contrary to expectation" sense it closes the clause and takes **no** comma before it — *She came after all*, not *She came, after all*.

Two distinct jobs, and position tells you which one you have. As a connector it supplies a reason the hearer is expected to accept already: *Don't be hard on him; after all, he's twelve.* At the end of a clause it reports that the expected thing failed to happen: *The flight wasn't canceled after all.*

1. Let him sleep in; *after all*, it's Saturday.
2. Nobody was surprised — she'd been training for a year, *after all*.
3. *After all*, we did agree to split the cost down the middle.
4. The flight wasn't canceled *after all*. *(the "contrary to expectation" sense)*
5. It turned out they had a table for us *after all*.

**See also:** [of course](#of-course), [besides](#besides), [in fact](#in-fact), [indeed](#indeed)

### as a matter of fact

**Pronunciation:** /æz ə ˌmætər əv ˈfækt/ &middot; *az uh MAT-ur uv FAKT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of emphatic correction or confirmation
**Pattern:** *As a matter of fact*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *as a matter of fact*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *as a matter of fact*, [complement]. &middot; [Independent clause], *as a matter of fact*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end; a semicolon or period where two independent clauses meet — a bare comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *in fact*, *actually*, *in point of fact* (formal), *matter of fact* (informal and comma-free: *matter of fact, I did*)

Introduces a fact that contradicts, corrects, or one-ups what the listener has just assumed, and it is markedly more combative than plain *in fact* because the phrase itself announces that a correction is coming. In American conversation it very often prefaces a small triumph: *As a matter of fact, I have.*

1. *As a matter of fact*, I was just about to call you.
2. "You've never been to Texas." "*As a matter of fact*, I lived in Austin for two years."
3. The tenants were never notified; *as a matter of fact*, the notice went to the wrong address.
4. She isn't new here, *as a matter of fact* — she trained everyone in the room.
5. *As a matter of fact*, the same question came up at the last meeting.

**See also:** [in fact](#in-fact), [indeed](#indeed), [as it turns out](#as-it-turns-out), [truth be told](#truth-be-told), [to be honest](#to-be-honest)

### as it turns out

**Pronunciation:** /æz ɪt ˈtɜrnz ˌaʊt/ &middot; *az it TURNZ owt*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link introducing a fact learned after the event, usually one that upsets the expectation just described
**Pattern:** *As it turns out*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *as it turns out*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *as it turns out*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], *as it turns out*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. Between two independent clauses a semicolon or period is required — a bare comma is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *as it happens* (a coincidence rather than a discovery), *it turns out*, *as luck would have it*, *sure enough* (when the expectation was confirmed instead of upset)

Reports what was learned later, and almost always marks a reversal: the thing everyone assumed turns out to be wrong. *As it happens* is the milder cousin, flagging a lucky coincidence rather than a correction, and *it turns out* is the same connector with the subordinator dropped.

1. *As it turns out*, the check had cleared two days before the letter went out.
2. We packed for rain. *As it turns out*, it was eighty degrees all week.
3. The building was condemned in 1998; *as it turns out*, three families were still living in it.
4. Her grandfather, *as it turns out*, had worked in the same plant.
5. *As it happens*, I have a copy in the car.

**See also:** [sure enough](#sure-enough), [in fact](#in-fact), [as a matter of fact](#as-a-matter-of-fact), [after all](#after-all), [of course](#of-course)

### crucially

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkruʃəli/ &middot; *KROO-shuh-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal (academic, journalistic)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of emphasis marking the following point as the decisive one. Also an ordinary **degree adverb** inside the clause (*crucially dependent on the discount rate*), which takes no commas.
**Pattern:** *Crucially*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *crucially*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *crucially*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *crucially*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be] *crucially* [adjective]. *(degree adverb — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses. Without the commas it modifies the adjective beside it rather than the whole sentence.
**Variants:** *critically*, *importantly*, *most importantly*, *decisively*

Flags the sentence it opens as the one the argument turns on — the workhorse of research abstracts and analytical journalism. It ranks a point rather than merely adding one, which is what separates it from *moreover*.

1. *Crucially*, the second trial included patients over seventy-five.
2. The bill passed the House; *crucially*, it did so without a single amendment.
3. The lease runs another eight years and, *crucially*, cannot be assigned.
4. *Crucially*, no one has explained where the money came from.
5. The outcome is *crucially* dependent on the discount rate. *(degree adverb — not a connector)*

**See also:** [importantly](#importantly), [more importantly](#more-importantly), [in particular](#in-particular), [specifically](#specifically), [indeed](#indeed)

### importantly

**Pronunciation:** /ɪmˈpɔrtəntli/ &middot; *im-POR-tunt-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of emphasis marking the following statement as significant. Also a **manner adverb** meaning "with a self-important air" (*he nodded importantly*), a wholly different word.
**Pattern:** *Importantly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *importantly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *importantly*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb] *importantly*. *(manner adverb — "pompously," no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period before it when two independent clauses are joined. The commas are what keep it from being read as manner.
**Variants:** *more importantly*, *most importantly*, *crucially*, *notably*

Marks its sentence as one of the significant ones without ranking it against anything in particular, where *more importantly* explicitly outranks the point before it. Traditionalists once objected that the word should only mean "in an important manner," but the sentence-adverb use has long since won.

1. *Importantly*, the study was funded by the state rather than by the manufacturer.
2. The building passed inspection; *importantly*, the sprinkler system was tested under load.
3. The settlement pays the back wages and, *importantly*, admits no wrongdoing.
4. *Importantly*, none of this was disclosed to the buyers.
5. He cleared his throat and nodded *importantly*. *(manner adverb — "with a self-important air")*

**See also:** [more importantly](#more-importantly), [crucially](#crucially), [in particular](#in-particular), [in fact](#in-fact), [indeed](#indeed)

### in fact

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfækt/ &middot; *in FAKT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of emphasis or correction
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *in fact*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In fact*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] — *in fact*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [be/auxiliary], *in fact*, [complement].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon, period, or dash before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *as a matter of fact*, *in point of fact* (formal), *actually* (informal), *in reality*

Introduces something stronger, more surprising, or flatly corrective of what the listener assumed. Compare *indeed*, which mostly confirms and intensifies: *in fact* is the word you reach for when the truth turns out to be different from the expectation.

1. He said he'd never met her; *in fact*, they had worked together for three years.
2. The repair isn't difficult. *In fact*, you can do it with a screwdriver.
3. She wasn't annoyed — *in fact*, she seemed relieved.
4. The building is, *in fact*, older than the town charter.
5. *As a matter of fact*, I was just about to call you.

**See also:** [indeed](#indeed), [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [rather](#rather), [after all](#after-all)

### indeed

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈdid/ &middot; *in-DEED*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of emphatic confirmation
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *indeed*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Indeed*, [independent clause]. &middot; [adjective] — *indeed*, [stronger adjective]. &middot; [phrase], *indeed* [stronger phrase], [continuation]. &middot; *Indeed* [subject] [verb]. *(integrated emphasizer — no comma)* &middot; "*Indeed*." *(as a full reply)*
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas or dashes on both sides when it introduces an intensified restatement. As a reply token or an integrated emphasizer it takes no comma at all: *Indeed it was.*
**Variants:** *very much so*, *quite so* (both dated or British-flavored in American speech)

Confirms the previous statement and then pushes it further — "yes, and more so." Unlike *in fact*, it rarely corrects anything; it doubles down. It also serves as a one-word agreement token in formal speech.

1. The book is long; *indeed*, it runs to nine hundred pages.
2. She was qualified — *indeed*, overqualified.
3. *Indeed*, the committee had warned about exactly this back in March.
4. "That was quite a storm." "*Indeed* it was."
5. The delay was costly, *indeed* ruinous, for the smaller vendors.

**See also:** [in fact](#in-fact), [moreover](#moreover), [of course](#of-course), [after all](#after-all)

### more importantly

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɔr ɪmˈpɔrtəntli/ &middot; *mor im-POR-tunt-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of addition that ranks the new point above the last
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *more importantly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *More importantly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *more importantly*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *more importantly*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice unless *and* carries the join — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *most importantly* (for the last and greatest item in a list), *more important* / *most important* (the older adjectival forms, still preferred by some editors), *above all*, *better still*

Adds a point and claims it outweighs the one before it — *moreover* with an explicit ranking. Use *more importantly* when two things are being compared and *most importantly* when you are topping off a list of three or more; the older *more important* (short for "what is more important") is equally correct, and the quarrel between the forms is now a matter of ear.

1. The repair is cheap and, *more importantly*, it can be done today.
2. She knows the statute; *more importantly*, she knows the judge who wrote it.
3. *More importantly*, no one has asked the residents what they want.
4. The plan saves money. *Most importantly*, it keeps the clinic open.
5. *Most important*, the agency never published the data it relied on. *(the older adjectival form)*

**See also:** [importantly](#importantly), [crucially](#crucially), [moreover](#moreover), [first and foremost](#first-and-foremost), [in particular](#in-particular)

### nay

**Pronunciation:** /neɪ/ &middot; *NAY*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary (rhetorical)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — it introduces a stronger word or phrase that replaces the one just used. Also the archaic negative reply *nay* ("no"), which survives in recorded voice votes (*the ayes and the nays*).
**Pattern:** [phrase], *nay*, [stronger phrase]. &middot; [adjective] — *nay*, [stronger adjective]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *nay*, [stronger independent clause]. &middot; "*Nay*," [reported speech]. *(archaic "no")*
**Punctuation:** Commas or dashes on both sides. Between two full independent clauses a semicolon before it is the usual choice; a comma alone there is a splice, exactly as with *indeed*.
**Variants:** *indeed* (the modern equivalent), *or rather*, *not to say*, *even*

Corrects upward: it withdraws the word just used in favor of a stronger one — *difficult, nay, impossible.* In American English it survives only in deliberately elevated or jokey writing, in period fiction, and in the voice-vote formula *the nays have it*; anywhere else *indeed* or *or rather* is the living form.

1. The task would be difficult, *nay*, impossible.
2. He was a rival, *nay*, an enemy, from the day they met.
3. "*Nay*, my lord, I know not what you mean." *(period diction — the archaic "no")*
4. The proposal is unwise — *nay*, reckless — and the council should reject it out of hand.
5. The clerk called the roll, and the *nays* carried the day. *(the noun — a negative vote)*

**See also:** [indeed](#indeed), [rather](#rather), [in fact](#in-fact), [contrariwise](#contrariwise), [withal](#withal)

### of course

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈkɔrs/, often reduced to /əˈkɔrs/ &middot; *uv KORSS, often uh-KORSS*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of concession or shared assumption. Also a standalone reply token ("*Of course.*") and, uncommaed, an integrated emphasizer inside the clause.
**Pattern:** *Of course*, [independent clause], but [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *of course*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *of course*, [predicate]. &middot; [Subject] [verb], *of course*. &middot; *Of course* [subject] [verb]. *(integrated emphasizer — no comma)* &middot; "*Of course*." *(as a full reply)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. When two independent clauses are joined, a semicolon or period comes first — never a bare comma. The comma drops when *of course* is fully integrated into the clause as an emphasizer rather than set off as an aside: *Of course the store was closed.*
**Variants:** *naturally*, *obviously*, *needless to say*, *certainly*

Marks what follows as something everyone already knows or would expect — very often to concede a point before turning against it: *Of course, the data is three years old, but the pattern holds.* Watch the tone: attached to something the listener genuinely did not know, it reads as condescending.

1. *Of course*, the estimate assumes nothing goes wrong.
2. She's the most experienced person in the room, *of course*.
3. *Of course* the store was closed by the time we got there.
4. The plan will cost money; *of course*, so will doing nothing.
5. "Can I borrow your charger?" "*Of course*."

### sure enough

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃʊr ɪˈnʌf/ &middot; *shoor ih-NUFF*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link confirming that what was expected or predicted did in fact happen
**Pattern:** *Sure enough*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], and *sure enough*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Sure enough*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *sure enough*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a period or semicolon before it unless *and* carries the join. Fast narrative often drops the comma after it (*and sure enough it rained*), which is standard in fiction and dialogue.
**Variants:** *as expected*, *as predicted*, *wouldn't you know it* (informal), *of course* (drier)

Confirms a prediction, usually one that somebody made out loud a moment earlier: the storyteller's payoff word. It is the mirror image of *as it turns out*, which reports that the expectation was wrong.

1. He said the transmission would go before winter, and *sure enough*, it did.
2. *Sure enough*, the flight was delayed again.
3. She told us to bring cash. *Sure enough*, the card reader was broken.
4. I checked the attic and, *sure enough*, there was the missing box.

### to be honest

**Pronunciation:** /tə bi ˈɑnɪst/ &middot; *tuh bee AH-nist*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a comment adverbial prefacing the speaker's frank opinion. Grammatically an infinitive phrase with no subject, held in place by convention rather than by grammar.
**Pattern:** *To be honest*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], *to be honest*. &middot; [Subject], *to be honest*, [predicate]. &middot; *To tell the truth*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, comma before it at the end, commas on both sides in the middle. It never joins two independent clauses by itself; a period or semicolon does that work.
**Variants:** *to tell the truth*, *honestly*, *if I'm honest*, *to be perfectly honest*, *TBH* (texting)

Warns that a candid and usually unflattering opinion is coming — and, by implication, that the speaker has been holding it back. *To tell the truth* is the same move in slightly older clothing and *truth be told* is the literary version; overused, all three read as filler rather than as candor.

1. *To be honest*, I'd rather stay home.
2. The second half was a slog, *to be honest*.
3. *To tell the truth*, nobody on the committee had read the whole thing.
4. She is, *to be honest*, the only reason the department still functions.
5. *To be honest*, I never expected them to call back.

### truth be told

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtruθ bi ˈtoʊld/ &middot; *trooth bee TOHLD*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to literary (a fixed formula)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a comment adverbial prefacing an admission. The verb is a fossilized **subjunctive** with its subject in front of it (*truth be told*, never *the truth is told*), which is why the phrase cannot be reworded.
**Pattern:** *Truth be told*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], *truth be told*. &middot; [Subject], *truth be told*, [predicate]. &middot; *If truth be told*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, comma before it at the end, commas on both sides in the middle. A semicolon or period still does the work of joining two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *if truth be told*, *to tell the truth*, *to be honest*, *in truth* (literary)

Introduces something the speaker has been politely leaving out — a confession delivered with a slightly literary flourish. It is more written than *to be honest*, and it turns up constantly in memoir, sports columns, and obituaries.

1. *Truth be told*, the house had never been worth what they paid for it.
2. He enjoyed the attention, *truth be told*.
3. *Truth be told*, none of us thought the team would make it past October.

## Summary and conclusion

### all in all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ɪn ˈɔl/ &middot; *awl in AWL*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of summation (a net verdict)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *All in all*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *all in all*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Front position only.
**Variants:** *overall*, *on the whole*, *all things considered*, *on balance* (formal)

Delivers the net verdict after the details have been weighed — the everyday summing-up phrase of reviews and travel stories. It reports a judgment, where *in short* compresses information and *in conclusion* announces the end of a formal text.

1. The flights were late and it rained twice. *All in all*, though, a good trip.
2. *All in all*, I'd rather have the older model.
3. There were a few rough edges; *all in all*, the show worked.
4. *Overall*, the program has done what it promised.
5. *All things considered*, we came out ahead.

**See also:** [in short](#in-short), [to sum up](#to-sum-up), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion)

### all things considered

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl θɪŋz kənˈsɪdərd/ &middot; *awl thingz kun-SID-urd*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a **nominative absolute** phrase delivering a net judgment once everything on both sides has been weighed
**Pattern:** *All things considered*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], *all things considered*. &middot; [Subject] [be], *all things considered*, [complement].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, comma before it at the end, commas on both sides in the middle; a period or semicolon where two independent clauses meet.
**Variants:** *on balance*, *all in all*, *taking everything into account*, *considering everything*

Announces a verdict reached after weighing the bad against the good, and it almost always concedes that there was something on the other side of the scale. *All in all* sums an experience up; *all things considered* judges it, often with the sense "given how it could have gone."

1. *All things considered*, we came out ahead.
2. The car needs tires, but *all things considered*, it was a good buy.
3. She handled the interview well, *all things considered*.
4. *All things considered*, the district would rather settle than go to trial.
5. It was, *all things considered*, the least bad option on the table.

### at the end of the day

**Pronunciation:** /ət ði ˌɛnd əv ðə ˈdeɪ/ &middot; *ut thee END uv thuh DAY*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken; a cliché in writing)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of final judgment ("when everything else has been set aside"). Also a literal time adverbial (*at the end of the day, lock the register*), which is not a connector.
**Pattern:** *At the end of the day*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *at the end of the day*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause], *at the end of the day*. &middot; [Independent clause] *at the end of the day*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and a comma before it at the end; a period or semicolon between two independent clauses. The literal time sense takes the same commas, so only the meaning tells the two apart.
**Variants:** *when all is said and done*, *in the end*, *ultimately*, *the bottom line is*

Sweeps the details aside and states what the speaker thinks really governs — the reflex phrase of coaches, executives, and pundits, and one of the most complained-about clichés in American English. In writing, *ultimately* or *in the end* says the same thing in three fewer words.

1. *At the end of the day*, the voters decide.
2. We can argue about the design; *at the end of the day*, we have to ship something.
3. *At the end of the day*, it's a business, not a family.
4. He's difficult to work with, but *at the end of the day* he delivers.
5. *At the end of the day*, count the drawer and lock the safe. *(literal time adverbial — not a connector)*

**See also:** [when all is said and done](#when-all-is-said-and-done), [in the final analysis](#in-the-final-analysis), [all things considered](#all-things-considered), [all in all](#all-in-all), [on the whole](#on-the-whole)

### by and large

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbaɪ ən ˈlɑrdʒ/ &middot; *by und LARJ*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of generalization ("with exceptions allowed for")
**Pattern:** *By and large*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *by and large*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *by and large*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], *by and large*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end; a semicolon or period between two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *on the whole*, *for the most part*, *in general*, *generally speaking*, *in the main* (formal)

Concedes exceptions while asserting the general rule — closer to "mostly, though not always" than to "always." The phrase comes from sailing, where *by* and *large* named two ways of taking the wind, which is why its parts cannot be dropped or reordered.

1. *By and large*, the tenants have been patient about the noise.
2. The buses run on time, *by and large*.
3. *By and large*, the changes were cosmetic.
4. Rural counties, *by and large*, lost population over the decade.
5. The reviews were mixed, but *by and large* audiences liked it.

**See also:** [on the whole](#on-the-whole), [for the most part](#for-the-most-part), [in general](#in-general), [generally speaking](#generally-speaking), [in the main](#in-the-main)

### for the most part

**Pronunciation:** /fər ðə ˈmoʊst ˌpɑrt/ &middot; *fur thuh MOHST part*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of generalization stating that the claim holds for most of the cases
**Pattern:** *For the most part*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *for the most part*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *for the most part*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause] *for the most part*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; at the end of a clause it usually runs on with no comma at all (*the roads stayed clear for the most part*). A semicolon or period is still required between two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *mostly*, *largely*, *by and large*, *on the whole*, *in the main*

Says the statement covers most of the cases and quietly admits that it does not cover all of them. It is the most countable of the generalizers — it points at a proportion, where *by and large* points at an overall impression.

1. *For the most part*, the crew had worked together for years.
2. The complaints, *for the most part*, were about parking.
3. Snow held off until midnight; *for the most part*, the roads stayed clear.
4. *For the most part*, we agree about what has to happen next.
5. She writes her own material *for the most part*.

**See also:** [by and large](#by-and-large), [on the whole](#on-the-whole), [in general](#in-general), [in the main](#in-the-main), [generally speaking](#generally-speaking)

### generally speaking

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒɛnərəli ˈspikɪŋ/ &middot; *JEN-ur-uh-lee SPEE-king*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a comment adverbial announcing that what follows is a generalization. Like *strictly speaking*, it is a fronted participial phrase with nobody doing the speaking.
**Pattern:** *Generally speaking*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *generally speaking*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], *generally speaking*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end. It joins no clauses on its own; a period or semicolon does that.
**Variants:** *broadly speaking*, *in general*, *as a rule*, *by and large*

Warns the listener not to press the statement too hard, because exceptions exist. Its exact opposite is *strictly speaking*, and the two often appear a sentence apart when a speaker is drawing the line between the usual case and the technical one.

1. *Generally speaking*, the older machines are easier to repair.
2. Juries, *generally speaking*, are more skeptical than lawyers expect.
3. *Generally speaking*, you want the permit before you pour the slab.
4. The fee is refundable, *generally speaking*, up to thirty days out.
5. *Generally speaking*, that neighborhood floods twice a decade.

**See also:** [in general](#in-general), [strictly speaking](#strictly-speaking), [by and large](#by-and-large), [for the most part](#for-the-most-part), [on the whole](#on-the-whole)

### in conclusion

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˈkluʒən/ &middot; *in kun-KLOO-zhun*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of closure
**Pattern:** *In conclusion*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In conclusion*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period before it — it opens the final paragraph or the final sentence — and a comma after it. Front position only.
**Variants:** *to conclude*, *in closing*, *finally* (in a spoken list)

Announces the closing section of a speech, essay, or formal report. Use it once, at the actual end. In anything shorter than a few pages it reads as padding, and American editors routinely cut it.

1. *In conclusion*, the evidence supports the second hypothesis.
2. *In conclusion*, I want to thank the families who took part in this study.
3. The findings are mixed. *In conclusion*, the authors recommend a larger trial.
4. *In closing*, let me return to the question we started with.
5. *In conclusion*, the committee finds no basis for the complaint.

**See also:** [to sum up](#to-sum-up), [in short](#in-short), [lastly](#lastly), [finally](#finally)

### in fine

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈfaɪn/ &middot; *in FYNE*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary (formerly standard formal prose)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of summation ("in short, to conclude")
**Pattern:** *In fine*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in fine*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *In fine*, [independent clause]. &middot; [series], and, *in fine*, [last item].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, and a period or semicolon before it — a bare comma joining two independent clauses is a comma splice. Front position is the normal placement.
**Variants:** *in short*, *in sum*, *in conclusion*, *finally*

From Latin *in fine*, "at the end" — the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century way of saying *in short*. An American meets it in Johnson, Burke, the Federalist Papers, and older sermons; written today it is either a period effect or a confusion with the unrelated legal citation *in fine* ("at the end of the page cited").

1. *In fine*, the whole scheme rested on credit that no one had troubled to verify.
2. He was learned, industrious, and honest; *in fine*, a man made for the work.
3. *In fine*, we must either enlarge the union or dissolve it.
4. The evidence was circumstantial, contradictory, and, *in fine*, worthless.
5. *In fine*, there is no remedy but patience.

**See also:** [in short](#in-short), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion), [to sum up](#to-sum-up), [in the final analysis](#in-the-final-analysis), [withal](#withal)

### in general

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈdʒɛnərəl/ &middot; *in JEN-ur-ul*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of generalization. Also a **postmodifier** attached directly to a noun phrase (*readers in general*), which takes no commas.
**Pattern:** *In general*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in general*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in general*, [predicate]. &middot; [Noun phrase] *in general* [verb phrase]. *(postmodifier — no commas)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period between two independent clauses. Attached to a noun it takes none at all: *readers in general don't notice.*
**Variants:** *generally*, *generally speaking*, *as a rule*, *broadly*

States the ordinary case and leaves room for exceptions, without the folksiness of *by and large* or the proportion-counting of *for the most part*. Note the second job: pinned to a noun it means "taken as a whole," and the missing commas are the only signal.

1. *In general*, the fee is waived for first-time applicants.
2. Winters here are, *in general*, milder than people expect.
3. *In general*, we don't take appointments after four.
4. The report says little about hospitals *in general* and nothing about rural ones. *(postmodifier — no commas)*
5. *In general*, the older the roof, the higher the premium.

**See also:** [generally speaking](#generally-speaking), [by and large](#by-and-large), [for the most part](#for-the-most-part), [in the main](#in-the-main), [on the whole](#on-the-whole)

### in short

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈʃɔrt/ &middot; *in SHORT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of summation (compression)
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *In short*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in short*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period or semicolon before it — a bare comma there is a comma splice — and a comma after it. Front position only.
**Variants:** *in brief*, *in sum* (formal), *in a word*, *long story short* (informal), *bottom line* (business)

Compresses everything just laid out into a single sentence. Where *in conclusion* signals structure — this is the end — *in short* signals compression, and it can appear anywhere in a text, including in the middle of an argument.

1. The engine is shot, the frame is bent, the title is missing. *In short*, it's junk.
2. *In short*, we cannot afford both.
3. He wanted more money, fewer hours, and a new title; *in short*, a different job.
4. *Long story short*, we ended up driving back at two in the morning.
5. *In sum*, the policy achieved none of its stated goals.

**See also:** [in other words](#in-other-words), [all in all](#all-in-all), [to sum up](#to-sum-up), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion)

### in the final analysis

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˌfaɪnəl əˈnæləsɪs/ &middot; *in thuh FY-nul uh-NAL-uh-sis*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of final judgment
**Pattern:** *In the final analysis*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in the final analysis*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in the final analysis*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period between two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *in the last analysis*, *ultimately*, *when all is said and done*, *at the end of the day* (the spoken equivalent)

The formal register's version of *at the end of the day*: it sets the details aside and names what finally decides the question. Editors treat it as padding whenever it opens a sentence that would stand perfectly well without it.

1. *In the final analysis*, the case turned on a single unsigned memo.
2. The reforms were popular; *in the final analysis*, they changed almost nothing.
3. Responsibility, *in the final analysis*, rests with the board that approved the loan.
4. *In the final analysis*, the choice is between raising rates and closing branches.
5. *In the last analysis*, no treaty binds a government that refuses to be bound.

**See also:** [at the end of the day](#at-the-end-of-the-day), [when all is said and done](#when-all-is-said-and-done), [all things considered](#all-things-considered), [in conclusion](#in-conclusion), [on the whole](#on-the-whole)

### in the main

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ðə ˈmeɪn/ &middot; *in thuh MAYN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** formal (somewhat dated)
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of generalization ("for the most part")
**Pattern:** *In the main*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *in the main*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *in the main*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause] *in the main*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle; a semicolon or period between two independent clauses. At the end of a clause it often runs on without a comma.
**Variants:** *for the most part*, *on the whole*, *by and large*, *mainly*

A bookish, slightly old-fashioned *for the most part*, at home in reviews, editorials, and formal reports. American readers register it as literary; in speech, *mostly* and *by and large* have taken the job over.

1. *In the main*, the objections were procedural rather than substantive.
2. The translation is, *in the main*, faithful to the original.
3. Attendance held up *in the main*, though the Sunday matinees suffered.
4. *In the main*, the state's schools are funded by property taxes.
5. Her account agrees, *in the main*, with what the other witnesses described.

### long story short

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɔŋ ˌstɔri ˈʃɔrt/ &middot; *lawng stor-ee SHORT*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken)
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — an elliptical absolute phrase, clipped from *to make a long story short*, announcing that the speaker is skipping to the outcome
**Pattern:** *Long story short*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Long story short*, [independent clause]. &middot; *To make a long story short*, [independent clause]. *(the full form)*
**Punctuation:** Comma after it, and a period before it, since it opens a new sentence. Front position only — it cannot close a clause or sit in the middle of one.
**Variants:** *to make a long story short*, *to cut a long story short* (British), *bottom line*, *in short* (the neutral written form)

The storyteller's fast-forward: it skips the middle of the anecdote and delivers the result. It is purely conversational — a written summary uses *in short* — and it is very often deployed after the story has already gone on too long.

1. *Long story short*, we ended up driving back at two in the morning.
2. The tow truck never came. *Long story short*, we slept in the car.
3. *Long story short*, she got the job.
4. *To make a long story short*, the permit had been in the wrong name the whole time.
5. *Long story short*, don't lend him anything you want back.

### on the whole

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈhoʊl/ &middot; *ahn thuh HOHL*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of generalized summation ("taking everything together")
**Pattern:** *On the whole*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *on the whole*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject], *on the whole*, [predicate]. &middot; [Independent clause], *on the whole*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position, commas on both sides in the middle, comma before it at the end; a semicolon or period between two independent clauses.
**Variants:** *all in all*, *by and large*, *overall*, *for the most part*

Weighs the whole of something and reports where the balance falls — halfway between the generalizing *by and large* and the summing-up *all in all*. It is the mildest way in English to say the good outweighed the bad without denying that there was bad.

1. *On the whole*, the season went better than anyone expected.
2. The reviews were kind, *on the whole*.
3. *On the whole*, I'd rather deal with the county than with the city.
4. The changes have, *on the whole*, made the form harder to read.
5. It was a difficult year; *on the whole*, though, the shop is stronger than it was.

### to sum up

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˌsʌm ˈʌp/ &middot; *tuh sum UP* — *to* is always reduced to /tə/ here, never /tu/
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of summation
**Pattern:** *To sum up*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *To sum up*, [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** Period before it, comma after it. Front position only.
**Variants:** *to summarize*, *in summary*, *summing up*, *to recap* (informal, spoken)

Introduces a restatement of the main points, typically near the close of a talk or a report. Because it is an infinitive phrase, the clause that follows needs a subject that can plausibly do the summing — *To sum up, we recommend…* works; *To sum up, the results were poor* is technically a dangler, though it passes unremarked in speech.

1. *To sum up*, we recommend replacing the system rather than repairing it.

3. *To summarize*, the drug is safe but no more effective than aspirin.
4. *To recap*, we meet at eight, we leave at nine, and we're back by six.
5. *In summary*, the department met two of its four targets.

### when all is said and done

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn ˌɔl ɪz ˌsɛd ən ˈdʌn/ &middot; *wen awl iz sed und DUN*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a fixed subordinate clause) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral
**Clause type:** Formally a subordinate *when* clause, but frozen as a summing-up connective: nothing in the main clause has to be simultaneous with any saying or doing
**Pattern:** *When all is said and done*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *when all is said and done*. &middot; [Subject], *when all is said and done*, [predicate].
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position; the comma before it at the end is optional, as with any trailing adverbial clause. Because it is a subordinate clause rather than a bare adverb, it can attach with a comma where a conjunctive adverb would need a semicolon.
**Variants:** *at the end of the day*, *in the end*, *when it comes down to it*, *in the final analysis* (formal)

Sets the arguing aside and names the thing that finally counts. It is the same move as *at the end of the day* with an older and more sententious ring, and it carries the same risk of sounding like filler.

1. *When all is said and done*, the house is still standing.
2. He'll complain for a week, but *when all is said and done* he'll sign it.
3. *When all is said and done*, the strike cost both sides more than the raise was worth.
4. The awards are nice; *when all is said and done*, though, the work is what lasts.
5. *When all is said and done*, we are talking about eleven dollars a month.

## Alternatives, digression, and discourse management

### alternatively

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈtɜrnətɪvli/ &middot; *awl-TUR-nuh-tiv-lee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of alternative
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]; *alternatively*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]. *Alternatively*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Subject] [modal], *alternatively*, [verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it when it links two independent clauses — a bare comma there is a comma splice — with a comma after it in front position and commas on both sides in the middle.
**Variants:** *or* (the coordinating equivalent), *or else*, *another option is*, *failing that*

Offers a second option as a whole sentence — the adverb answering to *or*. *Or* joins two elements inside one sentence; *alternatively* starts a new one, which is why instructions and proposals lean on it so heavily.

1. You can file online; *alternatively*, you can mail the form to the county office.
2. We could rent a van. *Alternatively*, we could make two trips.
3. *Alternatively*, the committee may postpone the vote until spring.
4. Bake for forty minutes or, *alternatively*, simmer the whole thing on the stove.
5. Pay the balance in full; *alternatively*, set up a twelve-month plan.

**See also:** [otherwise](#otherwise), [instead](#instead), [on the other hand](#on-the-other-hand), [or](01-coordinating.md#or)

### anyway

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌweɪ/ &middot; *EN-ee-way*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb &middot; **Register:** informal
**Clause type:** Independent clause — adverbial link of dismissal, or a discourse marker returning to the main topic
**Pattern:** [Independent clause]. *Anyway*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause]; *anyway*, [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *anyway*. &middot; [Independent clause], but [independent clause] *anyway*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after it in front position; **no** comma before it in final position (*I worried anyway*). Period or semicolon before it when it opens a new independent clause — a bare comma there is a comma splice.
**Variants:** *anyhow* (equally standard, a little more casual), *anyways* (nonstandard but common in casual American speech), *any way* (two words = "by any method" — a different phrase entirely)

Three conversational jobs: returning to the main thread after a digression (*Anyway, where was I?*), dismissing what was just said as not changing anything (*It's expensive. I'm buying it anyway*), and closing a topic or a phone call (*Anyway, I should let you go*). Written *nevertheless* covers only the middle one.

1. *Anyway*, that's how we ended up in Tulsa.
2. He told me not to worry about it. I worried *anyway*.
3. The store was closing, but they let us in *anyway*.
4. *Anyway*, I should let you go — it's late there.
5. "*Anyhow*," she said, "the roof still leaks."
6. It's probably closed by now; *anyway*, we don't need much.

**See also:** [in any case](#in-any-case), [at any rate](#at-any-rate), [regardless](#regardless), [besides](#besides), [incidentally](#incidentally)

### as for

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈfɔr/, reduced /əz fər/ &middot; *az FOR, reduced uz fur*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — prepositional in form) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a fronted topic-shifting preposition taking a noun phrase or a gerund, which then becomes what the sentence is about
**Pattern:** *As for* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. &middot; *As for* [gerund phrase], [independent clause]. &middot; *As for* [wh- clause], [independent clause].
**Punctuation:** A comma closes the fronted phrase and separates it from the clause that follows. No semicolon is involved, because what comes after *as for* is a phrase rather than a second independent clause.
**Variants:** *as to*, *as regards*, *regarding*, *when it comes to*, *speaking of*

Turns the spotlight onto the next topic — usually one already on the table, and often with an edge: *As for Dan, he can find his own ride.* It differs from *regarding* by presupposing that the topic has come up already, and it very often prefaces a dismissal.

1. *As for* the money, we'll settle up on Friday.
2. *As for* the roof, it can wait until spring.
3. She's taking the train. *As for* me, I'm driving.
4. *As for* whether he knew, nobody has been able to establish it.
5. *As for* renewing the lease, the owner hasn't returned a single call.

**See also:** [as regards](#as-regards), [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [in terms of](#in-terms-of), [speaking of which](#speaking-of-which)

### as regards

**Pronunciation:** /æz rɪˈɡɑrdz/ &middot; *az rih-GARDZ*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — prepositional in form) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — a topic-marking preposition taking a noun phrase. Note the fixed *-s*: *as regards*, never *as regard*.
**Pattern:** *As regards* [noun phrase], [independent clause]. &middot; [Independent clause] *as regards* [noun phrase]. &middot; [Subject] [be] silent *as regards* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** A comma closes the phrase when it is fronted; no comma when it sits inside the predicate (*silent as regards damages*). No semicolon, since a noun phrase follows it.
**Variants:** *as to*, *with regard to*, *in regard to*, *regarding*, *concerning*

The bureaucratic topic-marker of reports, memoranda, and older business correspondence. American usage guides — Fowler and Garner among them — treat this whole family as flabby, and *about*, *on*, or *for* will usually replace it without any loss.

1. *As regards* the second invoice, the department has no record of receiving it.
2. The contract is silent *as regards* renewal.
3. *As regards* staffing, the clinic has been two nurses short since June.
4. Nothing has changed *as regards* our position on the easement.
5. *As regards* the timeline, we expect a decision before the end of the quarter.

**See also:** [as for](#as-for), [with regard to](#with-regard-to), [with respect to](#with-respect-to), [in respect of](#in-respect-of), [in terms of](#in-terms-of)

### as the case may be

**Pronunciation:** /æz ðə ˈkeɪs meɪ ˌbi/ &middot; *az thuh KAYSS may bee*
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (phrasal — a fixed clause-final tag) &middot; **Register:** formal, legal
**Clause type:** No clause of its own — it closes a disjunction and tells the reader that whichever of the listed alternatives actually applies is the one meant

**Punctuation:** A comma closes the fronted phrase; no commas inside the predicate. No semicolon, since what follows is a noun phrase rather than a clause.
**Variants:** *with regard to*, *as to*, *regarding*, *w/r/t* and *wrt* (informal abbreviations), *as respects* (archaic)

The standard American drafting phrase for "concerning, as applied to" — the workhorse of contracts, opinions, and technical writing, where its precision earns its length. In everyday prose it is padding; note the scientific use, though, where it names the variable of differentiation and cannot be replaced by *about*.

1. The Seller makes no warranty *with respect to* the condition of the roof.
2. *With respect to* the third count, the motion is denied.
3. The company has no obligations *with respect to* employees hired after the closing.
4. Differentiate the function *with respect to* time. *(the technical sense — not a connector)*
5. *With respect to* the remaining items, we can take them up next week.
