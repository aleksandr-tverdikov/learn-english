# Coordinating conjunctions

> The seven FANBOYS and their relatives — the words that join two things of equal grammatical rank and mark how they relate.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Coordinating conjunctions join two elements of **equal grammatical rank** — word to word, phrase to phrase, or whole sentence to whole sentence. Neither half is demoted; either one could usually stand on its own. English has exactly seven true coordinators, the **FANBOYS** (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*), plus a short list of fixed two-word coordinators (*and/or, or else, but then*) and a fringe of coordinator-like items (*plus, minus, times, versus, as well as, along with, let alone, if not*) that link equal partners without quite behaving like the seven.

What unites all of them is **position**: a coordinator sits *between* the two things it joins and can never move to the front of the first one. That single test separates this class from subordinators like *although*, which head their own clause and can front the sentence, and from conjunctive adverbs like *however*, which can slide around inside their clause.

One comma rule runs through the entire category: **when a coordinator joins two independent clauses, put a comma before it; when it joins anything smaller than a clause, don't.** *She called, and I answered* takes the comma; *bread and butter* does not. Two riders: in a list of three or more, American editing adds a serial comma before the final *and* or *or* (*red, white, and blue*), and *for* always takes its comma, because without one it reads as the preposition.

## The seven FANBOYS

The only seven words in English that are coordinating conjunctions and nothing else in that role. Learn them as a closed set: **f**or, **a**nd, **n**or, **b**ut, **o**r, **y**et, **s**o.

### and

**Pronunciation:** /ænd/, weak /ənd/, /ən/, /n̩/ &middot; *AND, weak uhnd / uhn / n*
**Русский:** и — основной соединительный союз: добавление, последовательность, результат
**Type:** coordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases of any rank
**Pattern:** [X] *and* [Y]. &middot; [X], [Y], *and* [Z]. &middot; [independent clause], *and* [independent clause]. &middot; [imperative] *and* [result]. &middot; *And* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** No comma between two joined words, phrases, or verbs sharing one subject (*bread and butter*; *She stood up and left*). Comma before *and* when it joins two independent clauses (*She called, and I answered*), droppable when both clauses are very short. In a list of three or more, American editing puts a serial comma before the final *and* (*red, white, and blue*).
**Variants:** *&* (ampersand — company names, notes, informal writing) &middot; *'n'* (*rock 'n' roll*, *mac 'n' cheese*) &middot; reduced /ən/ in ordinary speech

The default coordinator: it adds, and it will add anything to anything as long as the two halves are the same grammatical kind. Beyond plain addition it carries sequence (*She got up and left*), result (*He tripped and fell*), and, after an imperative, a condition (*Move and I'll shoot* means "if you move"). Of all the addition words it is the only one that makes a subject genuinely plural — *plus* is informal and *as well as* leaves the verb alone.

1. She ordered a salad *and* a glass of iced tea.
2. We packed the car, *and* the kids were asleep before we hit the highway.
3. Add the flour, the sugar, *and* a pinch of salt.
4. Say that one more time *and* I'm leaving.
5. He came home, dropped his bag, *and* went straight to bed.
6. *And* then, out of nowhere, the power went out.

**See also:** [and/or](#andor), [and so](#and-so), [and then](#and-then), [and yet](#and-yet), [plus](#plus), [as well as](#as-well-as), [or](#or), [both … and](02-correlative.md#both--and)

### but

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt/, weak /bət/ &middot; *BUT, weak buht*
**Русский:** но, а — второе идёт вразрез с тем, чего ждёшь после первого
**Type:** coordinating (also a preposition meaning "except", and an adverb in fixed phrases) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases
**Pattern:** [X] *but* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *but* [independent clause]. &middot; not [X] *but* [Y]. &middot; *But* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *but* when it joins two independent clauses (*I called, but nobody answered*). No comma when it joins two words or phrases (*small but tidy*). In the *not X but Y* frame the comma is optional and marks emphasis (*It's not the money, but the principle*). No comma after *But* at the head of a sentence unless what follows is itself parenthetical.
**Variants:** *but then* &middot; *but rather* &middot; *but also* (in *not only … but also*) &middot; prepositional *but* (*everyone but me*) is a different word class

Marks the second element as running against what the first led you to expect. It is the plain, neutral contrast word, and the differences around it are worth memorizing: *yet* adds a note of "in spite of that," *however* means the same but is an adverb and needs a semicolon before it, and *although* subordinates instead of coordinating, which changes which half of the sentence carries the point.

1. I called twice, *but* nobody picked up.
2. The soup was bland *but* filling.
3. It's not the money *but* the principle.
4. *But* nobody had checked the budget.
5. She said she'd think about it, *but* I could tell she had already decided.
6. We can go Friday *but* not Saturday.

**See also:** [yet](#yet), [and yet](#and-yet), [but then](#but-then), [but rather](#but-rather), [only](#only), [however](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#however), [although](07-concession.md#although)

### for

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr/, weak /fɚ/ &middot; *FOR, weak fer*
**Русский:** ибо, ведь — книжн./устар. союз причины; всегда с запятой перед ним
**Type:** coordinating (far more often a preposition — *a gift for you* — and the marker of infinitival clauses: *for her to leave now would be rude*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of reason
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *for* [independent clause]. — normally medial &middot; *For* [clause]. (sentence-initial; scripture and consciously elevated prose only, linking back to the sentence before)
**Punctuation:** Always put a comma before conjunctive *for*; without one the word reads as the preposition (*He waited for the bus* vs. *He waited, for the bus was late*). It never joins anything smaller than a clause. Sentence-initial *For* belongs to scripture and deliberately elevated writing (*For the wages of sin is death*), where it points back across the period and takes no comma after it.
**Variants:** none as a conjunction &middot; prepositional *for* (*a gift for you*) is a different word class &middot; infinitival *for* (*for her to leave now would be rude*)

Supplies a reason or a justification for what was just said — roughly *because* or *since*, but grammatically a coordinator. In American English it is close to literary now: outside scripture, older fiction, and deliberately elevated prose, people say *because*. Its behavior is unlike *because* too — its clause cannot be moved ahead of the main clause (*For she had never lied to me, I trusted her* is impossible), it cannot answer *why?*, and it cannot be the focus of a cleft.

1. I trusted her, *for* she had never lied to me.
2. We turned back, *for* the trail had washed out below the ridge.
3. Do not weep, *for* the child is not dead but sleeping.
4. He spoke slowly, *for* he wanted every word to land.
5. The town emptied out in October, *for* there was no work to be had after the harvest.
6. She kept the letters, *for* they were all she had left of him.
7. He refused the money. *For* he had given his word, and that was the end of it.

**See also:** [so](#so), [and so](#and-so), [because](04-cause.md#because), [since](04-cause.md#since), [therefore](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#therefore)

### nor

**Pronunciation:** /nɔr/, weak /nɚ/ &middot; *NOR, weak ner*
**Русский:** и не, также не — второе отрицание; требует инверсии: nor did he call
**Type:** coordinating (also the second half of the correlative *neither … nor*) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate clause, negative
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *nor* [auxiliary or *be*] [subject] [rest]. &middot; neither [X] *nor* [Y]. &middot; [negative clause], *nor* [noun phrase]. (with the verb gapped) &middot; *Nor* [auxiliary] [subject] … (sentence-initial, formal)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *nor* when it joins two independent clauses (*He didn't call, nor did he write*). No comma inside a *neither … nor* pair joining words or phrases (*neither hot nor cold*). The clause after clause-linking *nor* is inverted — auxiliary or *be* before the subject. Sentence-initial *Nor* follows a period or a semicolon, inverts the same way, and takes no comma after it.
**Variants:** *neither … nor* (correlative) &middot; *nor yet* (literary, archaic)

Adds a second negative to a negative already on the table: "and not that either." Two traps come with it. Word order inverts (*nor did he call*, never *nor he called*), and the clause takes no second *not*, because *nor* already carries the negation.

1. He didn't call, *nor* did he answer my texts.
2. The company never admitted fault, *nor* did it offer a refund.
3. *Neither* the manager *nor* the assistants were told about the merger.
4. She isn't unkind, *nor* is she especially warm.
5. I have no time for this, *nor* the patience.
6. *Nor* should we forget who paid for the road in the first place.

**See also:** [or](#or), [and](#and), [much less](#much-less), [neither … nor](02-correlative.md#neither--nor), [either … or](02-correlative.md#either--or)

### or

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr/, weak /ɚ/ &middot; *OR, weak er*
**Русский:** или — альтернатива, обычно невзаимоисключающая (можно и то и другое)
**Type:** coordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate clause; also joins words and phrases
**Pattern:** [X] *or* [Y]. &middot; [X], [Y], *or* [Z]. &middot; [independent clause], *or* [independent clause]. &middot; [imperative], *or* [consequence]. &middot; either [X] *or* [Y]. &middot; [term], *or* [other name for the same thing], …
**Punctuation:** No comma between two joined words or phrases (*coffee or tea*). Comma before *or* joining two independent clauses (*Hurry, or we'll miss it*), droppable only when both clauses are very short. Serial comma before the final *or* in a list of three or more (*by car, by train, or by bus*). A comma before *or* also signals the renaming use, and a second comma closes it (*the humerus, or upper arm bone, is …*).
**Variants:** *or else* &middot; *or rather* &middot; *and/or* &middot; *either … or* (correlative)

Offers an alternative. English *or* is normally inclusive — *coffee or tea* does not forbid both — and only context or an explicit *either … or* forces the exclusive reading. Two side jobs are worth learning: after an imperative it states the consequence of not complying, and after a comma it renames the thing just mentioned. Note also that after a negative it is *or*, not *and*, that spreads the negation: *I don't eat meat or fish*.

1. Do you want the aisle *or* the window?
2. We could drive, *or* we could fly.
3. Would you rather have coffee, tea, *or* something cold?
4. Hurry up, *or* we'll miss the last train.
5. I don't eat meat *or* fish.
6. She studies the humerus, *or* upper arm bone, in the first week of the course.
7. *Either* you tell him *or* I will.

**See also:** [and/or](#andor), [or else](#or-else), [or rather](#or-rather), [nor](#nor), [and](#and), [versus](#versus), [either … or](02-correlative.md#either--or), [whether](10-complementizers.md#whether)

### so

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ &middot; *SOH*
**Русский:** поэтому, так что — следствие: сначала причина, потом результат
**Type:** coordinating (also subordinating in *so that*, a degree adverb, and a discourse marker) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of result
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *so* [independent clause]. &middot; *So* [clause]. (conversational opener) &middot; compare subordinating [clause] *so (that)* [purpose clause]. — no comma
**Punctuation:** Comma before *so* when it joins two independent clauses (*It rained, so we stayed in*). No comma when *so* means "so that" and introduces a purpose (*I left early so I could beat traffic*) — the presence or absence of that comma is the main clue to which reading is intended. Sentence-initial discourse *So* takes a comma after it only as a conversational aside (*So, what happened?*).
**Variants:** *and so* &middot; *so that* (subordinating, purpose) &middot; *so … that* (correlative, degree)

States the result of what came before: cause first, effect after *so*. It is the mirror image of *because*, which puts the effect first and the cause second. The high-value distinction is against *so (that)*: *I left early, so I beat traffic* reports what happened, while *I left early so I could beat traffic* reports what was intended.

1. Traffic was terrible, *so* we missed the opening.
2. The store was out of basil, *so* I used parsley.
3. He never wrote back, *so* I stopped emailing.
4. I left early *so* I could beat the traffic.
5. *So*, what did the doctor say?
6. She's allergic to cats, *so* we met at my place instead.

**See also:** [and so](#and-so), [for](#for), [therefore](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#therefore), [so that](05-purpose-result.md#so-that), [because](04-cause.md#because)

### yet

**Pronunciation:** /jɛt/ &middot; *YET*
**Русский:** но всё же, и тем не менее — противопоставление с оттенком неожиданности
**Type:** coordinating (also a time adverb: *not yet*, *has she left yet?*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more formal than *but* in writing
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of concessive contrast
**Pattern:** [X] *yet* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *yet* [independent clause]. &middot; [clause] and *yet* [clause]. &middot; *Yet* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *yet* when it joins two independent clauses (*She apologized, yet nothing changed*). No comma when it joins two words or phrases (*firm yet fair*). *And yet* takes the same comma. Sentence-initial *Yet* takes no comma after it.
**Variants:** *and yet* &middot; adverbial *yet* (*I haven't finished yet*) is a different word class

Contrast plus surprise: it asserts that both facts hold even though they look incompatible. *But* merely opposes; *yet* adds "and in spite of that." It is also one of only two FANBOYS words that ordinary usage lets you put *and* in front of — *and yet* and *and so* — a clue that both are half adverb; *and but*, *and or*, and *and for* are impossible in any variety, and *and nor* is British — American editing writes *nor* alone.

1. She apologized, *yet* nothing actually changed.
2. The apartment is small *yet* surprisingly bright.
3. He had every reason to quit, *yet* he showed up every morning.
4. It was a simple question, *yet* nobody in the room could answer it.
5. *Yet* something about the story didn't add up.
6. She had barely known him and *yet* she cried through the whole service.

**See also:** [but](#but), [and yet](#and-yet), [but then](#but-then), [nevertheless](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#nevertheless), [although](07-concession.md#although)

## Compound coordinators

Fixed two-word sequences that function as a single coordinator. Each has a comma rule of its own, and none of them can be broken apart or reordered.

### and/or

**Pronunciation:** /ˌænd ˈɔr/ &middot; *and-OR*
**Русский:** и/или — юр., канц.: «одно, другое или оба сразу»
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, technical, and administrative writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase; joins words and phrases, rarely clauses
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *and/or* [noun phrase]. &middot; [subject] *and/or* [subject] [verb] … &middot; [verb phrase] *and/or* [verb phrase]. &middot; [adjective] *and/or* [adjective]. &middot; recommended rewrite: [X] or [Y] or both.
**Punctuation:** Written with a slash and no spaces around it. Punctuate the surrounding sentence exactly as you would for *or*: no comma between the two joined elements, none after the slash. Avoid it in a list of three or more, where its scope becomes genuinely ambiguous. Note too that subject–verb agreement after an *and/or* subject is unsettled (*the tenant and/or the guarantor is* — or *are* — *liable*), which is one more reason editors rewrite it.
**Variants:** *and or* (nonstandard spacing) &middot; *or/and* (rare) &middot; recommended rewrite: *X or Y or both*

A written shorthand for "either one or both," invented to close the loophole that inclusive *or* supposedly leaves open in contracts. Plain-language guides and many courts dislike it, since ordinary *or* is already inclusive and the slash breaks down past two items; the standard fix is *X or Y or both*.

1. Bring a passport *and/or* a driver's license.
2. Applicants must show two years of experience *and/or* a relevant certificate.
3. The fee may be paid by the tenant *and/or* the guarantor.
4. Damage caused by fire *and/or* flooding is excluded from coverage.
5. Please copy your manager *and/or* the project lead on the final draft.
6. Signatures may be collected in person *and/or* electronically.

**See also:** [or](#or), [and](#and), [or else](#or-else), [either … or](02-correlative.md#either--or)

### and so

**Pronunciation:** /ˌænd ˈsoʊ/, weak /ənd ˈsoʊ/, /ən ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *and-SOH, weak uhn-SOH*
**Русский:** и потому, и вот — итоговое следствие, весомее простого so
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral; narrative or slightly formal
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of result
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *and so* [independent clause]. &middot; *And so* [clause]. (sentence-initial, narrative)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *and so* when it joins two independent clauses; never a comma between *and* and *so*, and none after *so*. Sentence-initial *And so* takes no comma after it.
**Variants:** *and therefore* (formal) &middot; do not confuse with *and so* + inversion (*and so did I* = "I did too")

The same result link as bare *so*, but heavier and more deliberate — it sums up rather than merely reports, which is why it lands so often at the end of a story or an argument. Keep it apart from additive *and so* with inversion (*She was exhausted, and so was I*), where *so* means "likewise" and is an adverb.

1. The flight was canceled, *and so* we spent the night in Denver.
2. No one else volunteered, *and so* the job fell to me.
3. *And so* the summer ended, the way summers do.
4. The evidence never surfaced, *and so* the case was dropped.
5. We had promised the kids a beach day, *and so* a beach day is what they got.
6. The road washed out in April, *and so* the town was cut off for a week.

**See also:** [so](#so), [and](#and), [and then](#and-then), [for](#for), [therefore](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#therefore)

### and then

**Pronunciation:** /ˌænd ˈðɛn/, weak /ənd ˈðɛn/, /ən ˈðɛn/ &middot; *and-THEN, weak uhn-THEN*
**Русский:** а потом, и затем — закрепляет порядок событий, который and лишь подразумевает
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational in narrative
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of sequence
**Pattern:** [X] *and then* [Y]. &middot; [subject] [verb phrase] *and then* [verb phrase]. &middot; [independent clause], *and then* [independent clause]. &middot; [X], [Y], *and then* [Z]. &middot; *And then* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** No comma when *and then* joins two verb phrases sharing one subject (*We had dinner and then walked down to the water*). Comma before *and then* when it joins two independent clauses (*First the check bounced, and then the landlord called*) — the ordinary *and* rule, though fast narrative drops it between short clauses. Never a comma between *and* and *then*, and none after *then*; sentence-initial *And then* takes no comma either.
**Variants:** *and then some* (idiom: "and more besides") &middot; *and afterward* (formal)

Fixes the order of events, which plain *and* only implies. It has a second, rhetorical life in complaint and storytelling, where each *and then* stacks one new development on the last.

1. We had dinner *and then* walked down to the water.
2. She read the letter twice *and then* put it back in the drawer.
3. First the check bounced, *and then* the landlord called.
4. *And then* he tells me the whole thing was my idea.
5. Boil the pasta, drain it, *and then* toss it with the sauce.
6. The power flickered twice *and then* went out for good.

**See also:** [and](#and), [and so](#and-so), [or else](#or-else), [after](03-time.md#after), [then](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#then)

### and yet

**Pronunciation:** /ˌænd ˈjɛt/, weak /ənd ˈjɛt/, /ən ˈjɛt/ &middot; *and-YET, weak uhn-YET*
**Русский:** и всё же, и тем не менее — усиленное yet: признаёт первое и дивится второму
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral; common in writing
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of concessive contrast
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *and yet* [independent clause]. &middot; [X] *and yet* [Y]. &middot; *And yet* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *and yet* between two independent clauses. Sentence-initial *And yet* normally takes no comma after it, though writers add one for a beat of hesitation.
**Variants:** *and still* &middot; *but still* (looser, more conversational) &middot; *yet* alone

Intensifies the surprise already in *yet*: it concedes the first fact and then registers the second as genuinely hard to square with it. English tolerates a doubled coordinator here only because *yet* is halfway to an adverb — the same license that produces *and so* — which is why *and but* and *and or* are impossible.

1. He had every advantage, *and yet* he threw it away.
2. The book is barely two hundred pages *and yet* took him nine years to write.
3. She's been asked a hundred times, *and yet* she still answers as if it were new.
4. *And yet* nobody thought to check the basement.
5. The town has no industry left, *and yet* the population keeps growing.
6. It's the most obvious explanation, *and yet* it's wrong.

**See also:** [yet](#yet), [but](#but), [but then](#but-then), [and](#and), [nevertheless](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#nevertheless)

### but rather

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbʌt ˈræðɚ/, weak /bət ˈræðɚ/ &middot; *but-RA-ther, weak buht-RA-ther*
**Русский:** а (не X, а Y) — отменяет отвергнутое и подставляет верное; нужно отрицание впереди
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase or clause, corrective
**Pattern:** not [X] *but rather* [Y]. &middot; [negative clause], *but rather* [corrected element]. &middot; It is not that [clause] *but rather* that [clause].
**Punctuation:** In the *not X but rather Y* frame a comma before *but rather* is optional and adds emphasis; never a comma after it. When the first element is a full clause, use the comma.
**Variants:** *but instead* &middot; *but rather that* &middot; bare *but* &middot; *rather* alone (*not carelessness, rather fatigue* — formal)

Replaces a rejected item with the correct one, so it needs a negative in front of it to do its work. Where plain *but* contrasts two things that can both be true, *but rather* cancels the first outright and substitutes the second.

1. The problem wasn't the code *but rather* the data it was fed.
2. She didn't refuse, *but rather* asked for more time.
3. It's not that he's rude, *but rather* that he's completely unaware of other people.
4. We're not raising prices, *but rather* trimming the menu.
5. The delay wasn't caused by weather *but rather* by a paperwork error.
6. He isn't lazy *but rather* exhausted.

**See also:** [but](#but), [rather than](#rather-than), [or rather](#or-rather), [instead](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#instead)

### but then

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbʌt ˈðɛn/, weak /bət ˈðɛn/ &middot; *but-THEN, weak buht-THEN*
**Русский:** но с другой стороны, впрочем — вторая мысль, смягчающая сказанное; разг.
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational
**Clause type:** coordinate clause, concessive afterthought
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *but then* [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *but then again*, [independent clause]. &middot; *But then* [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *but then*. When *but then again* interrupts, fence it with a comma on each side. No comma after sentence-initial *But then*; a dash before it is a common alternative to the comma.
**Variants:** *but then again* &middot; *then again* (on its own) &middot; *but on the other hand*

Introduces a second thought that softens or half-cancels what was just said — "on the other hand, this is also true." It differs from the purely temporal *but then* of narrative ("and after that"); the concessive one can always be replaced by *but then again*.

1. The hotel was overpriced, *but then* everything downtown is.
2. She's never on time, *but then* neither am I.
3. I'd like to move somewhere warm — *but then*, I said that last year too.
4. *But then again*, he did warn us.
5. The sequel wasn't very good, *but then* the first one wasn't either.
6. He offered to pay, *but then* he always does.

**See also:** [but](#but), [yet](#yet), [and then](#and-then), [only](#only), [however](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#however)

### nor yet

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɔr ˈjɛt/, weak /nɚ ˈjɛt/ &middot; *nor-YET, weak ner-YET*
**Русский:** и уж тем более не — устар., книжн.: усиленное nor
**Type:** coordinating (compound; the reinforced form of *nor*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary; archaic in flavor
**Clause type:** coordinate clause or phrase, negative
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *nor yet* [noun phrase]. &middot; [negative clause], *nor yet* [auxiliary or *be*] [subject] [rest]. &middot; neither [X], nor [Y], *nor yet* [Z]. &middot; not [X], *nor yet* [Y].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *nor yet*, never one between the two words or after them. When a full clause follows, it inverts exactly as it does after bare *nor* (*nor yet did he write*). In a three-part negative list it marks the last item and takes the same comma the other items take.
**Variants:** *nor* (the plain modern form) &middot; *nor even* &middot; *neither … nor* &middot; *and not … either* (plain-English rewrite)

A reinforced *nor*: it adds one more negative and marks it as the least expected item of the set, the one a reader would have thought least likely. Hymns, scripture translations, and nineteenth-century prose use it freely; present-day American writing has all but dropped it, and bare *nor* carries the same load.

1. He had no money, no prospects, *nor yet* any wish to acquire either.
2. She was not beautiful, *nor yet* plain.
3. The committee has no budget for this, *nor yet* the authority to ask for one.
4. I never learned my father's trade, *nor yet* wanted to.
5. The treaty settled neither the border, nor the fishing rights, *nor yet* the question of who would pay.
6. There was no answer at the door, *nor yet* a light in any of the windows.

**See also:** [nor](#nor), [and yet](#and-yet), [still less](#still-less), [yea](#yea), [neither … nor](02-correlative.md#neither--nor)

### or else

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔr ˈɛls/ &middot; *or-ELS*
**Русский:** а то, иначе — последствие или угроза при невыполнении первого
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral to informal
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of alternative consequence
**Pattern:** [imperative], *or else* [consequence]. &middot; [independent clause], *or else* [independent clause]. &middot; [warning] … *or else.* (elliptical threat)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *or else* when it joins two clauses; the comma is often dropped when the first clause is a short imperative. The bare threat *Or else.* stands as its own sentence and takes a period, and a dash before it is common.
**Variants:** bare *else* (literary) &middot; *otherwise* (conjunctive adverb — takes a semicolon or a new sentence)

States what will happen if the first clause is not carried out: a more explicitly consequential *or*. It is also the one coordinator in English that can survive with nothing after it at all, as an open-ended threat.

1. Leave a note, *or else* they'll worry.
2. We need to leave by six, *or else* we'll hit rush hour.
3. Write it down, *or else* you'll forget by morning.
4. Pay the ticket by Friday *or else* it doubles.
5. Have the car back by ten — *or else*.
6. Book it now, *or else* the fare goes up.

**See also:** [or](#or), [else](#else), [and/or](#andor), [otherwise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#otherwise), [unless](06-condition.md#unless)

### or rather

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔr ˈræðɚ/ &middot; *or-RA-ther*
**Русский:** вернее, точнее — поправка к слову, только что сказанному самим говорящим
**Type:** coordinating (compound) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase or clause, self-correcting
**Pattern:** [X], *or rather*, [more accurate X]. &middot; [clause], *or rather* [restated clause]. &middot; [clause] — *or rather*, [restated clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *or rather*, and a comma after it as well when a full clause follows. Dashes are common in place of the first comma, since the correction is parenthetical.
**Variants:** *or, more precisely* &middot; *or better* &middot; *that is* (formal) &middot; *I mean* (spoken)

Withdraws the word just used and substitutes a better one in mid-sentence. Unlike *but rather*, it needs no negative in front of it — it is correcting a statement the speaker has only just made.

1. He's a colleague — *or rather*, he was, until last month.
2. We inherited a house, *or rather* a pile of lumber with a roof on it.
3. She agreed to help, *or rather* she didn't say no.
4. It's a diner, *or rather*, it was a diner in 1974 and nothing has been touched since.
5. I read the report, *or rather* I read the first two pages of it.
6. The meeting is Tuesday, *or rather* Wednesday now.

**See also:** [or](#or), [but rather](#but-rather), [rather than](#rather-than), [namely](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#namely)

## Quasi-coordinators and coordinator-like items

Words that join equal partners but fail at least one test for true coordination — they may not link finite clauses, may not make a subject plural, or may be classed as prepositions by strict grammars. They are grouped here because learners meet them in coordinator slots and need the same comma rules.

### along with

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlɔŋ wɪð/, also /wɪθ/ &middot; *uh-LONG-with*
**Русский:** вместе с, наряду с — добавляет второго участника, не меняя числа подлежащего
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a two-word preposition by strict analysis; *along* by itself is a preposition or a particle — *along the river*, *bring it along*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on), not a true clause link
**Pattern:** [X] *along with* [Y]. &middot; [subject], *along with* [add-on], [verb agreeing with the subject alone] … &middot; [independent clause] *along with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [verb phrase] *along with* [*-ing* verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** Fence a subject add-on with commas on both sides (*The pitcher, along with two coaches, was ejected*); never a comma after *along with* itself. Those commas are punctuation only — they do not change agreement, and the verb matches the first noun with or without them. No comma when the phrase simply trails a complete clause as part of it (*She brought the contract along with a check*).
**Variants:** *together with* &middot; *in addition to* &middot; *as well as* &middot; *and* (the true coordinator — the one addition word that does make the subject plural)

Adds a second party to the sentence without letting it into the subject, which is the whole reason to reach for it instead of *and*: *The coach and two players were fined* puts three people on the hook grammatically, while *The coach, along with two players, was fined* keeps the coach alone in the subject. It behaves exactly like *as well as* and *together with* for agreement, and unlike *as well as* it has no comparative twin to be confused with.

1. The pitcher, *along with* two of the coaches, was ejected in the fourth inning.
2. She brought the signed contract *along with* a check for the deposit.
3. Bring a photo ID *along with* proof of address.
4. The fire took the barn *along with* everything stored in it.
5. My grandmother's recipes, *along with* her cast-iron skillet, went to my sister.
6. He lost the election *along with* whatever credibility he had left.

**See also:** [as well as](#as-well-as), [and](#and), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [both … and](02-correlative.md#both--and)

### and so forth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌænd soʊ ˈfɔrθ/, weak /ən soʊ ˈfɔrθ/ &middot; *and-soh-FORTH, weak uhn-soh-FORTH*
**Русский:** и так далее, и тому подобное — нейтральный письменный способ оставить список открытым
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a general extender — the coordinator *and* plus a fixed placeholder for the members not named) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; the written counterpart of *and stuff*
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — closes a list with a vague final member; never joins finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X], [Y], *and so forth*. &middot; [X], [Y], [Z], *and so forth*. &middot; [list], *and so forth*, [rest of the clause]. &middot; [list] *and so on and so forth* (emphatic doubling)
**Punctuation:** Take the comma the list would take before its final item — American editing keeps the serial comma in front of it (*forms, permits, and so forth*). A second comma closes it when the sentence runs on. It ends a list and never introduces one, so nothing else follows it inside the list.
**Variants:** *and so on* &middot; *et cetera* / *etc.* &middot; *and the like* &middot; *and so on and so forth* (emphatic) &middot; *and suchlike* (dated)

Closes an open-ended list by pointing at the members not named: "more items of the same kind." It is the neutral written extender — plainer than *et cetera*, more respectable than *and stuff* — and it works only when the pattern is clear enough for a reader to continue it unaided.

1. Bring the deed, the survey, the insurance binder, *and so forth*.
2. She handles payroll, scheduling, *and so forth*, while I deal with the customers.
3. The course covers tenses, participles, irregular verbs, *and so forth*.
4. We spent the morning on permits, inspections, *and so forth*, and never got to the design.
5. There was the usual talk about the weather, the roads, the price of feed, *and so forth*.
6. He listed every objection — cost, timing, staffing *and so on and so forth* — without offering a single fix.

**See also:** [et cetera / etc.](#et-cetera--etc), [and the like / or the like](#and-the-like--or-the-like), [and stuff / and everything / and all that](#and-stuff--and-everything--and-all-that), [or something](#or-something), [and](#and)

### and stuff / and everything / and all that

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈstʌf/, /ən ˈɛvriθɪŋ/, /ən ˌɔl ˈðæt/ &middot; *uhn-STUHF, uhn-EV-ree-thing, uhn-awl-THAT*
**Русский:** и всё такое, и всё прочее — разг., устное незавершённое перечисление
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (general extenders — a reduced *and* plus a vague placeholder) &middot; **Register:** informal; spoken, rare in edited prose except in quoted speech
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — closes a list, a noun phrase, or a whole clause with a vague final member
**Pattern:** [X] *and stuff*. &middot; [X], [Y], *and everything*. &middot; [clause] *and all that*. &middot; [X] *and stuff like that*. &middot; [X] *and all that jazz*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before it in speech-like writing, where it runs on as part of the last item (*chips and salsa and stuff*); use one only if the list already carries serial commas. Never a comma after it. Outside dialogue, editing usually cuts it or swaps in *and so on*.
**Variants:** *and stuff like that* &middot; *and things* &middot; *and all* &middot; *and whatnot* &middot; *and all that jazz* &middot; *and so on* (neutral) &middot; *and so forth* (formal)

The spoken general extenders: they close a list by gesturing at more of the same and signal that the speaker assumes you can fill in the rest. The three are not quite interchangeable — *and everything* leans toward completeness ("the whole business"), *and all that* toward dismissal, and *and stuff* is the plain all-purpose one.

1. We got chips, salsa, *and stuff* for the game.
2. He kept apologizing, saying he'd been swamped at work *and everything*.
3. She's pleasant *and all that*, but I wouldn't want to work for her.
4. The doctor asked about diet, exercise, sleep, *and all that*.
5. I still have to pack, print the tickets, *and stuff* like that.
6. They redid the floors, repainted the kitchen, *and everything* — you wouldn't know it was the same house.

**See also:** [and so forth](#and-so-forth), [or something](#or-something), [and the like / or the like](#and-the-like--or-the-like), [et cetera / etc.](#et-cetera--etc), [and](#and)

### and the like / or the like

**Pronunciation:** /ən ðə ˈlaɪk/, /ɔr ðə ˈlaɪk/ &middot; *uhn-thuh-LYK, or-thuh-LYK*
**Русский:** и тому подобное — вещи того же рода; книжн., техн. и юр. проза
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a general extender) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; at home in technical, legal, and administrative prose
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — closes a list with a vague final member; never joins finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X], [Y], *and the like*. &middot; [X] *or the like*. &middot; [list], *and the like*, [rest of the clause]. &middot; [single example] *or the like*.
**Punctuation:** Comma before it in a serial list — the same comma the final item would take — and a second comma when the sentence continues. No comma after it. Pick *and the like* after a positive inventory and *or the like* after a single example or a negative.
**Variants:** *and suchlike* (dated) &middot; *and similar* &middot; *or similar* &middot; *and so forth* &middot; *et cetera* &middot; *and the rest* (rhetorical)

Extends a list to cover things of the same kind, and it is the extender a contract or a manual reaches for, because "of the same kind" is a slightly tighter promise than *et cetera* makes. Use the *or* form when only one example has been given, or when the list offers alternatives rather than an inventory.

1. Bring a passport, a birth certificate, *or the like*.
2. The lease forbids grills, space heaters, *and the like* on the balcony.
3. Damage from earthquake, flood, *and the like* is excluded from the policy.
4. He collects stamps, coins, old survey maps, *and the like*.
5. Use a level, a plumb bob, *or the like* to check that the frame is true.
6. The store sells tents, sleeping bags, camp stoves, *and the like*, but nothing for climbing.

**See also:** [and so forth](#and-so-forth), [et cetera / etc.](#et-cetera--etc), [and stuff / and everything / and all that](#and-stuff--and-everything--and-all-that), [as well as](#as-well-as), [or](#or)

### and then some

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈðɛn sʌm/, also /ən ðɛn ˈsʌm/ &middot; *uhn-THEN-suhm*
**Русский:** и даже больше, и с лихвой — разг.: названная величина ещё занижена
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a fixed additive tag; distinct from the ordinary sequence *and then* + *some*) &middot; **Register:** informal; conversational, and common in sports and feature writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — a clause-final tag that outbids the amount just stated
**Pattern:** [clause naming a quantity], *and then some*. &middot; [X], *and then some*. &middot; *And then some.* (standing alone as a reply)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *and then some* when it caps a full clause; no comma inside it, and none after it, since it ends the sentence. As a standalone reply it takes a period of its own.
**Variants:** *and more* &middot; *and some* (regional) &middot; *if not more* &middot; *and change* (with money and time: *two hours and change*)

Says that the figure just named is, if anything, an understatement — "that much, plus an unspecified extra." It always follows the quantity it inflates and can never introduce anything, which is what separates it from the sequential *and then* it grew out of.

1. The repair cost us the whole deposit, *and then some*.
2. She works forty hours a week, *and then some*.
3. That truck will haul a ton, *and then some*.
4. "Was it worth the drive?" "*And then some*."
5. He paid back every dollar he owed me, *and then some*.
6. The renovation ate up our summer, *and then some*.

**See also:** [and then](#and-then), [if not](#if-not), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [and](#and)

### as also

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈɔlsoʊ/ &middot; *uhz-AWL-soh*
**Русский:** а также — устар., книжн.: старинный аналог as well as
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (archaic; a fossilized additive, close kin to *as well as*) &middot; **Register:** archaic, formal; scholarly editions, old-style legal recitals, and devotional prose
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on) — joins noun phrases and prepositional phrases; not a finite-clause link
**Pattern:** [X], *as also* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *as also* [noun phrase]. &middot; [list], *as also* [final add-on]. &middot; [prepositional phrase], *as also* [matching prepositional phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *as also*, none after it. Treat it as period costume in present-day American prose: outside legal recitals, editions of older texts, and deliberate pastiche, write *as well as* or *and also*.
**Variants:** *as well as* (the living equivalent) &middot; *and also* &middot; *as likewise* (rarer) &middot; *together with*

An older additive that tacks a second item onto the first without disturbing the subject, exactly as *as well as* does today. Editors of nineteenth-century texts and drafters of old-style deeds still meet it; nobody says it.

1. The estate passed to his widow, *as also* the lands in Cheshire.
2. The volume prints the sermons, *as also* the letters of 1842.
3. We are bound by the treaty, *as also* by the custom of the port.
4. The catalogue lists the coins, *as also* the medals struck for the coronation.
5. She had charge of the household accounts, *as also* of the tenants' rents.
6. The rule binds the executor, *as also* any successor in the office.

**See also:** [as well as](#as-well-as), [along with](#along-with), [in conjunction with](#in-conjunction-with), [yea](#yea), [likewise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#likewise)

### as opposed to

**Pronunciation:** /əz əˈpoʊzd tə/ &middot; *uhz-uh-POHZD-tuh*
**Русский:** в отличие от, а не — прямо называет отвергаемую альтернативу
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a three-word preposition by strict analysis; elsewhere *opposed* is the past participle of the verb *oppose* — *she opposed the merger*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase of contrast — joins noun phrases, *-ing* clauses, and adjective phrases; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *as opposed to* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *as opposed to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [*-ing* form] *as opposed to* [*-ing* form]. &middot; [subject], *as opposed to* [rejected alternative], [verb] … &middot; *As opposed to* [X], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it joins two parallel words or phrases (*a loan as opposed to a grant*). Comma before it when the contrast is tacked onto a finished clause, and a comma on each side when it interrupts. A fronted *As opposed to* phrase takes a comma after the phrase. Never a comma after *to*.
**Variants:** *rather than* &middot; *instead of* &middot; *and not* &middot; *versus* (shorter, and the usual choice in headings and tables)

Spells out the alternative being ruled out, and spells it out deliberately — it is what a writer reaches for when the rejected option has to go on the record. Two limits separate it from *rather than*: it cannot take a bare infinitive (*I'd walk rather than pay*, never *as opposed to pay*), and it never joins finite clauses. Where *versus* frames a contest between two things, *as opposed to* heads off a misreading of one of them.

1. We're talking about a loan, *as opposed to* a grant.
2. The study measured hours actually worked *as opposed to* hours billed.
3. *As opposed to* the old model, this one holds a charge for a full day.
4. Renting, *as opposed to* buying, keeps our options open for another year.
5. I asked for a direct flight *as opposed to* whatever the site found cheapest.
6. The complaint names the parent company *as opposed to* the local franchise.

**See also:** [rather than](#rather-than), [versus](#versus), [but rather](#but-rather), [or](#or), [whereas](08-comparison.md#whereas)

### as well

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈwɛl/, full /æz ˈwɛl/ &middot; *uhz-WEL, full az-WEL*
**Русский:** тоже, также — в конце фразы; в амер. речи обычно говорят too
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (an additive adverb by strict analysis — the clause-final counterpart of *also* and *too*; distinct from the coordinator *as well as* and from the manner adverbial *as well*, "equally skillfully") &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more formal than *too* in American speech, and the ordinary everyday form in British and Canadian English
**Clause type:** coordinate tag — adds the phrase or clause it ends to what came before; never links two clauses itself
**Pattern:** [clause] *as well*. &middot; [subject] [verb] [X] *as well*. &middot; [clause], and [second item] *as well*. &middot; *As well*, [clause]. (sentence-initial; chiefly Canadian and business writing, and disliked by American editors)
**Punctuation:** No comma before clause-final *as well* — it belongs to the clause it ends (*She's coming as well*); add one only to mark it as an afterthought (*and the garden, as well*). Nothing follows it, since it closes the sentence. It cannot join two independent clauses on its own: *He was tired, as well he was hungry* is impossible — write *and he was hungry as well*, or start a new sentence.
**Variants:** *too* (the ordinary American clause-final form) &middot; *also* (usually before the verb) &middot; *besides* &middot; *to boot* (wry) &middot; *as well as* (the two-word coordinator — a different item) &middot; *as well … as* (correlative)

Tacks one more item onto what has just been said and marks it as more of the same: "that too." American English mostly says *too* in this slot and keeps *as well* for writing and for a slightly more measured tone; *also* does the same work but sits before the verb rather than at the end. Do not confuse it with the coordinator *as well as*, which stands between the two items it joins instead of trailing them.

1. The ticket covers the museum, and the sculpture garden *as well*.
2. She speaks Portuguese, and a little Italian *as well*.
3. We'll need a permit, and probably an inspection *as well*.
4. If you're going up to the counter, get me a coffee *as well*.
5. He lost the house and, in the end, the business *as well*.
6. The clinic is open Saturdays, and two evenings a week *as well*.

**See also:** [as well as](#as-well-as), [to boot](#to-boot), [either](#either), [and](#and), [also](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#also), [likewise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#likewise)

### as well as

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈwɛl əz/ &middot; *uhz-WEL-uhz*
**Русский:** а также, равно как и — добавляет второе, не делая подлежащее множественным
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (identical in form to the comparative *as … as* wrapped around the adverb *well*: *She plays as well as her brother*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on), not a true clause link
**Pattern:** [X] *as well as* [Y]. &middot; [subject], *as well as* [add-on], [singular verb] … &middot; [verb phrase] *as well as* [*-ing* verb phrase].
**Punctuation:** When the phrase is a parenthetical add-on to the subject, fence it with commas on both sides (*The manager, as well as the interns, was invited*). Without the commas it is open to being read as the comparative *as well as* — but the commas change nothing grammatically: the verb agrees with the first noun either way. Never a comma after *as well as* itself.
**Variants:** *along with* &middot; *together with* &middot; *in addition to* — all three behave the same way for agreement

Adds a second item while leaving the first one grammatically in charge, and that is the whole difference from *and*. A subject joined by *as well as* stays singular (*The coach, as well as the players, was fined*), and a verb after it takes the *-ing* form (*She writes the newsletter as well as running the website*). Watch the ambiguity built into the phrase: *He speaks Portuguese as well as Spanish* can mean "in addition to Spanish" (the conjunction) or "as fluently as he speaks Spanish" (the comparative). Only context, a comma, or a rewrite settles it.

1. The manager, *as well as* the interns, was invited to the retirement lunch.
2. The tuition covers books *as well as* lab fees.
3. She writes the newsletter *as well as* running the website.
4. The storm knocked out power in Dayton *as well as* three counties to the south.
5. The clinic is open Saturdays *as well as* weekday evenings.
6. The report criticizes the contractor, *as well as* the agency that hired him.

**See also:** [along with](#along-with), [and](#and), [plus](#plus), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [both … and](02-correlative.md#both--and)

### better yet

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɛtɚ ˈjɛt/; *worse yet* /ˌwɚs ˈjɛt/ &middot; *bet-er-YET, wurs-YET*
**Русский:** а ещё лучше — второй вариант, превосходящий первый по той же шкале
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a fixed comparative escalator, reduced from *and what is better yet*; the negative twin *worse yet* behaves identically) &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational, and common in journalism, reviews, and sales copy
**Clause type:** discourse connective — heads a clause that outbids the one before it on the same scale
**Pattern:** [clause]; *better yet*, [stronger clause]. &middot; [clause]. *Better yet*, [clause]. &middot; [X], or *better yet*, [better X]. &middot; [clause], and *worse yet*, [worse clause]. &middot; *Worse yet*, [clause]. (sentence-initial)
**Punctuation:** It is an adverb, not a coordinator, so between two independent clauses it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it (*The place is cheap; better yet, it's two blocks from the train*) — a comma alone there is a comma splice. A comma follows it in every position. When *and* or *or* does the joining, the phrase turns parenthetical and takes a comma on each side (*and, better yet, the parking is free*).
**Variants:** *worse yet* (the negative twin) &middot; *better still* / *worse still* (a shade more formal) &middot; *even better* / *even worse* &middot; *or better yet* (offering a replacement rather than an addition) &middot; *what's better* / *what's worse*

Adds a second item and claims it outranks the first on the very scale the first was measured by: *better yet* upgrades, *worse yet* piles on. That ranking is what separates it from the neutral add-ons — *not to mention* adds without ordering, and *and then some* inflates a quantity rather than naming a new item. After *or* it withdraws the first option instead of adding to it (*Email it, or better yet, bring a copy*).

1. The apartment is cheap; *better yet*, it's two blocks from the train.
2. Email me the file, or *better yet*, bring a printed copy.
3. We could meet Thursday — *better yet*, let's just do it over lunch.
4. *Better yet*, the whole thing runs on batteries you already own.
5. The flight was four hours late; *worse yet*, they lost both our bags.
6. He forgot the tickets, and *worse yet*, he'd left his wallet on the counter.
7. The roof leaks and the furnace is shot; *worse yet*, the seller won't come down a dollar.

**See also:** [on top of that](#on-top-of-that), [not only that](#not-only-that), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [and then some](#and-then-some), [if not](#if-not), [moreover](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#moreover)

### coupled with

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkʌpəld wɪð/, also /wɪθ/ &middot; *KUH-puhld-with*
**Русский:** в сочетании с, вкупе с — книжн.: два фактора вместе дают один результат
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a participial preposition by strict analysis, from the verb *couple*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; business, medical, and news writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on) — joins noun phrases and *-ing* clauses; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *coupled with* [Y] [verb agreeing with X alone] … &middot; [subject], *coupled with* [add-on], [verb] … &middot; *Coupled with* [X], [main clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *coupled with* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Fence it with commas on both sides when it interrupts the subject (*The drought, coupled with the tariffs, ruined him*); a fronted *Coupled with* phrase takes a comma after the phrase. Never a comma after *with*. Like *along with* and *as well as*, it leaves agreement with the first noun, and the commas change nothing about that.
**Variants:** *combined with* &middot; *together with* &middot; *along with* &middot; *paired with* &middot; *in combination with* (formal)

Adds a second factor and presents the two as working together to produce one effect, which is what sets it apart from the neutral add-ons: *coupled with* is nearly always causal. The subject stays singular — *Poor pay, coupled with long hours, drives people out*.

1. The drought, *coupled with* the new tariffs, wiped out the family's margin.
2. Poor pay *coupled with* long hours drives young teachers out of the profession.
3. *Coupled with* a mild winter, the early thaw brought the river up two weeks ahead of schedule.
4. Her years abroad, *coupled with* fluent Portuguese, made her the obvious hire.
5. The medication, *coupled with* physical therapy, restored most of his range of motion.
6. Slow sales *coupled with* rising rent forced the shop to close in March.

**See also:** [along with](#along-with), [in conjunction with](#in-conjunction-with), [in tandem with](#in-tandem-with), [as well as](#as-well-as), [plus](#plus)

### cum

**Pronunciation:** /kʌm/, also /kʊm/ &middot; *KUHM*
**Русский:** он же, совмещённый с — книжн./шутл.: kitchen-cum-dining room — кухня-столовая
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (the Latin preposition *cum* "with", used in English to fuse two nouns into one compound) &middot; **Register:** formal or jocular; chiefly British, and uncommon in American writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — joins two nouns naming one thing; never clauses
**Pattern:** [noun]-*cum*-[noun] &middot; [role]-*cum*-[second role] &middot; [noun] *cum* [noun] (older, unhyphenated)
**Punctuation:** Hyphenate it to both nouns and put no spaces or commas around it (*a kitchen-cum-dining room*). The whole compound behaves as a single noun and takes a singular verb. Older and more formal writing sometimes drops the hyphens; American editing usually rewrites with *and* or with a slash.
**Variants:** unhyphenated *cum* &middot; *and* &middot; */* (*writer/director*) &middot; *slash* (*a bar slash bookshop* — informal spoken)

Fuses two descriptions of one and the same thing, so that neither label is complete on its own: a *kitchen-cum-dining room* is one room doing two jobs, not two rooms. It stays bookish or faintly jokey in American English, where *writer/director* and plain *and* do the work; the spelling also coincides with an unrelated vulgar slang noun, which is one more reason American writers avoid it.

1. They rent a kitchen-*cum*-dining room at the back of the flat.
2. He works as a driver-*cum*-bodyguard for the family.
3. The place is a bar-*cum*-bookshop that closes whenever the owner feels like it.
4. She is the founder-*cum*-chief-engineer of the whole operation.
5. The building started life as a chapel-*cum*-schoolhouse for the mill workers.
6. What we got was a lecture-*cum*-sales-pitch that ran ninety minutes.

**See also:** [and](#and), [and/or](#andor), [plus](#plus), [versus](#versus), [as well as](#as-well-as)

### either

**Pronunciation:** /ˈiðɚ/, also /ˈaɪðɚ/ &middot; *EE-ther, also EYE-ther*
**Русский:** тоже не — отрицательный двойник too: I don't like it either «мне тоже не нравится»
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (an additive adverb by strict analysis — the negative-polarity counterpart of *too* and *as well*; distinct from the correlative *either … or* and from the determiner and pronoun *either*, as in *either road will get you there*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate tag — attaches one more negative to a negative already on the table; never links two clauses itself
**Pattern:** [negative clause] *either*. &middot; [subject] [negative verb] [X] *either*. &middot; [negative clause], and [second negative clause] *either*. &middot; not [X] *either* &middot; compare positive [clause] *too*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before clause-final *either* in ordinary use (*I don't like it either*); a comma marks it as an afterthought (*and she never called, either*), and American editing allows both. Nothing follows it. It cannot join two clauses by itself — *He didn't call, either he wrote* is impossible; use *and … either*, or *nor* with inversion.
**Variants:** *too* / *as well* (the positive-polarity forms) &middot; *neither* with inversion (*neither do I*) &middot; *me either* (spoken) &middot; correlative *either … or* and determiner *either* (*either road*) are different words

The negative twin of *too*: it says that a negative already stated holds for one more person or case. Swap in *too* and the sentence breaks (*I don't like it too*), which is the quickest test for it. Where *nor* carries its own negation and forces inversion, bare *either* leaves the second clause's own *not* in place and adds nothing but "likewise."

1. I don't like horror movies, and my sister doesn't *either*.
2. He never wrote back, and he didn't call *either*.
3. "I can't make Thursday." "I can't *either*."
4. The store doesn't take checks, and it doesn't take cards *either*.
5. She wasn't rude, exactly, but she wasn't friendly *either*.
6. If you're not going, I'm not going *either*.

**See also:** [neither](#neither), [nor](#nor), [as well](#as-well), [either … or](02-correlative.md#either--or), [also](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#also)

### else

**Pronunciation:** /ɛls/ &middot; *ELS*
**Русский:** иначе, а не то — устар. как самостоятельный союз; живёт в основном в or else
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (bare use); adverb when attached to an indefinite word &middot; **Register:** literary, archaic on its own; neutral inside *or else*
**Clause type:** coordinate clause of alternative consequence
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *else* [independent clause]. (literary) &middot; [imperative], *else* [consequence]. &middot; [clause], *else* why [auxiliary] [subject] [rest]? (surviving idiom) — modern American prose writes *or else* in all three slots
**Punctuation:** Comma before conjunctive *else*. In present-day American prose, write *or else* instead; bare *else* in this slot reads as period costume.
**Variants:** *or else* &middot; *otherwise* &middot; adverbial *else* after an indefinite (*something else*, *who else*) is a different word class

The bare conjunction *else* meaning "otherwise" survives in proverbs, hymn and scripture texts, and deliberately old-sounding prose; everyday American English requires *or* in front of it. The living, ordinary *else* is an adverb attached to an indefinite word (*somewhere else*, *what else*), not a conjunction at all.

1. Speak now, *else* forever hold your peace.
2. We must act tonight, *else* the chance is gone.
3. Take the earlier flight, *else* you'll be stuck in that airport until midnight.
4. He must have known, *else* why keep the letters all these years?
5. Write the address down, *else* you'll be circling that block for an hour.
6. Say nothing of this to your brother, *else* the whole family hears about it by Sunday.

**See also:** [or else](#or-else), [or](#or), [otherwise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#otherwise), [unless](06-condition.md#unless)

### et cetera / etc.

**Pronunciation:** /ɛt ˈsɛtərə/, often /ɛt ˈsɛtrə/; /ɛk ˈsɛtərə/ is widely heard and widely condemned &middot; *et-SET-er-uh, et-SET-ruh*
**Русский:** и так далее, и прочее — лат. сокращение, оставляющее список открытым
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a general extender borrowed whole from Latin *et cetera* "and the rest"; *etc.* is its abbreviation) &middot; **Register:** neutral; *etc.* belongs to writing, the spelled-out form to formal or ironic use
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — closes a list with a vague final member; never joins clauses
**Pattern:** [X], [Y], *etc.* &middot; [X], [Y], *etc.*, [rest of the clause]. &middot; [list], *et cetera*. &middot; [list], *etc., etc.* (emphatic doubling)
**Punctuation:** Keep the period on *etc.*, and take a comma before it in a serial list (*pens, paper, etc.*). When the sentence continues, a comma follows the abbreviation's period; when the sentence ends there, that one period does both jobs — never type two. Never write *and etc.*, since *et* already means "and," and never pair it with *such as* or *for example*, which have already marked the list as partial.
**Variants:** *etc.* &middot; *&c.* (older) &middot; *et al.* (of people, not things) &middot; *and so forth* &middot; *and so on* &middot; *and the like*

The Latin general extender: "and the other things of this sort." It is the standard written way to leave a list open, but it earns its keep only when the reader can actually continue the pattern — after two vague items it says nothing, which is why editors often cut it and name one more item instead.

1. Bring pens, paper, tape, *etc.*
2. The kit holds bandages, antiseptic, tweezers, *etc.*, but no scissors.
3. The form asks for name, address, date of birth, *et cetera*.
4. He gave me the whole speech about deadlines and professionalism, *etc., etc.*
5. Charges for parking, cleaning, late checkout, *etc.* are listed on the back of the folio.
6. We covered the usual ground — budget, staffing, timelines, *etc.* — in about an hour.

**See also:** [and so forth](#and-so-forth), [and the like / or the like](#and-the-like--or-the-like), [and stuff / and everything / and all that](#and-stuff--and-everything--and-all-that), [and](#and), [inter alia](12-formal-archaic.md#inter-alia)

### if not

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈnɑt/ &middot; *if-NOT*
**Русский:** если не сказать, а то и — осторожный шаг вверх: «X, а возможно, и больше»
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (scalar; distinct from the conditional *if not* that stands alone — *If not, we'll reschedule* — and from *if* + *not* inside a clause: *if he's not there by six*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, scalar — the second element is a fragment that outbids the first
**Pattern:** [X], *if not* [stronger X]. &middot; [adjective], *if not* [stronger adjective]. &middot; [quantity], *if not* [larger quantity]. &middot; [determiner] [adjective], *if not* [stronger adjective], [noun] (interrupting) &middot; [independent clause], *if not* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *if not*, no comma after it. When it interrupts instead of closing the sentence, fence it with a comma on each side (*a difficult, if not impossible, fix*). What follows is a fragment parallel to the element it outbids — never a clause with its own subject and tensed verb. Keep it apart from the conditional *if not* that stands as its own clause (*If not, call me*), which takes its comma after, not before.
**Variants:** *not to say* (formal) &middot; *or even* &middot; *perhaps even* &middot; conditional *if not* (*If not, we'll try Thursday*) is a different construction

Reaches past the item just named to a stronger one and floats it without quite asserting it — "X, and possibly more than X." It is the upward mirror of *let alone* and *much less*: those two run down a scale from a stated negative, while *if not* runs up from a stated positive and needs no negative in front of it at all. The hedge is real and worth respecting — a reporter who writes *hundreds, if not thousands* has committed to hundreds and merely raised the possibility of thousands.

1. The repair will take weeks, *if not* months.
2. It's a difficult, *if not* impossible, thing to prove.
3. Hundreds of homes, *if not* thousands, lost power overnight.
4. He's the best shortstop in the division, *if not* the league.
5. She was annoyed, *if not* furious, by the time we finally showed up.
6. The policy is unwise, *if not* actually illegal.

**See also:** [let alone](#let-alone), [much less](#much-less), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [rather than](#rather-than), [or](#or)

### in conjunction with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn kənˈdʒʌŋkʃən wɪð/, also /wɪθ/ &middot; *in-kuhn-JUNGK-shuhn-with*
**Русский:** совместно с, в сочетании с — офиц.: двое действуют заодно
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a complex preposition built on the noun *conjunction*) &middot; **Register:** formal; administrative, technical, and institutional writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on) — joins noun phrases and *-ing* clauses; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *in conjunction with* [Y]. &middot; [subject], *in conjunction with* [add-on], [verb agreeing with the subject alone] … &middot; [verb phrase] *in conjunction with* [noun phrase]. &middot; *In conjunction with* [X], [main clause].
**Punctuation:** Fence a subject add-on with commas on both sides, and put a comma after a fronted *In conjunction with* phrase. Never a comma after *with*. Agreement stays with the first noun: *The university, in conjunction with two hospitals, is running the trial*.
**Variants:** *in collaboration with* &middot; *in partnership with* &middot; *jointly with* &middot; *together with* &middot; *along with* &middot; *with* (the plain rewrite)

Adds a second party or a second instrument and says the two are meant to act together — the difference between it and the merely additive *along with*. It is institutional language, at home in press releases, protocols, and instructions for use; in ordinary prose plain *with* says the same thing in one word.

1. The university, *in conjunction with* two teaching hospitals, is running the trial.
2. This medication should be taken *in conjunction with* food.
3. The exhibit was organized *in conjunction with* the state historical society.
4. Read the manual *in conjunction with* the wiring diagram on the last page.
5. The city, *in conjunction with* the county, will pay for the new interchange.
6. The scholarship is awarded *in conjunction with* a summer research placement.

### in tandem with

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈtændəm wɪð/, also /wɪθ/ &middot; *in-TAN-duhm-with*
**Русский:** в связке с, параллельно с — двое движутся одновременно и согласованно
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a complex preposition; *tandem* is a hitch with one horse behind the other) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; business, engineering, and news writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on) — joins noun phrases and *-ing* clauses; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *in tandem with* [Y]. &middot; [subject], *in tandem with* [add-on], [verb agreeing with the subject alone] … &middot; [verb phrase] *in tandem with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [plural subject] [verb] *in tandem*. (bare adverbial, no *with*)
**Punctuation:** Commas on both sides when it interrupts the subject, none after *with*; agreement stays with the first noun. The bare adverbial *in tandem* takes no punctuation at all (*The two systems run in tandem*).
**Variants:** *in tandem* (bare adverbial) &middot; *in step with* &middot; *in parallel with* &middot; *side by side with* &middot; *in conjunction with* &middot; *together with*

Joins two parties or two processes and insists that they move together — at the same time, at the same rate, or as a matched pair. Where *along with* merely adds a second item, *in tandem with* claims coordination in the everyday sense of the word.

1. The fire department worked *in tandem with* the sheriff's office all night.
2. Rents have climbed *in tandem with* home prices for three straight years.
3. The new software runs *in tandem with* the old system until the spring.
4. She develops the curriculum *in tandem with* the teachers who will actually use it.
5. The city's own plan, *in tandem with* a federal grant, finally paid for the levee.
6. Two pumps operate *in tandem*, so one can be serviced without shutting the plant down.

### let alone

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛt əˈloʊn/ &middot; *let-uh-LOHN*
**Русский:** не говоря уже о, тем более — после отрицания: первое невозможно, второе и подавно
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (distinct from the literal verb phrase *let alone* = "leave undisturbed") &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, scalar — the second element is a fragment
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *let alone* [noun phrase]. &middot; [negative clause], *let alone* [verb phrase matching the one before it]. &middot; [clause with *barely*, *hardly*, or *too*], *let alone* [stronger element]. &middot; [question], *let alone* [harder case]?
**Punctuation:** Comma before *let alone*, no comma after it; a dash in place of the comma marks a bigger jump. What follows is a fragment parallel to the constituent it echoes — a noun phrase (*let alone Japan*), a verb phrase (*let alone bought him anything*), a prepositional phrase — never a clause with a subject and a tensed verb of its own.
**Variants:** *much less* &middot; *never mind* &middot; *to say nothing of* (formal) &middot; *still less* (formal)

Adds a second, more extreme item and says that if the first is already out of reach, the second is far more so. It needs a negative, a question, or some other downward-entailing context in front of it — *He can cook, let alone boil water* is ungrammatical, because the scale runs backward.

1. He can't boil water, *let alone* cook a turkey.
2. I've never been to Canada, *let alone* Japan.
3. We couldn't afford the rent, *let alone* a second car.
4. She barely has time to eat, *let alone* train for a marathon.
5. Nobody remembered his birthday, *let alone* bought him anything.
6. There isn't room for a table in here — *let alone* a couch.

### minus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmaɪnəs/ &middot; *MY-nuhs*
**Русский:** минус; без — арифметическое вычитание; разг. «без чего-то»: явился без бороды
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a preposition by strict analysis in the "without" sense; also a noun and an adjective — *a minus sign*, *a B minus*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic; informal and wry in the "without" sense
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — joins noun phrases only, never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [number] *minus* [number] [singular verb] [number]. &middot; [noun phrase] *minus* [noun phrase]. &middot; [independent clause] *minus* [noun phrase]. &middot; [subject], *minus* [noun phrase], [verb] … (parenthetical)
**Punctuation:** No commas in arithmetic (*Nine minus four is five*). In the "without" sense the comma before *minus* is optional and depends on how parenthetical the phrase feels; never a comma after it.
**Variants:** *less* (*the price less tax*) &middot; *without* &middot; *sans* (jocular)

Subtracts the second item from the first, and the whole phrase stays singular — *Ten minus three is seven*, not *are*. In casual use it means "and without," usually with a wry note: *He showed up minus the beard*. It is grouped with the coordinators here because it patterns exactly against *plus*, though grammars class the "without" use as a preposition.

1. Nine *minus* four is five.
2. The payment is your salary *minus* taxes and insurance.
3. He came back from the trip *minus* one suitcase.
4. We got the whole crew, *minus* the two who had a wedding that weekend.
5. That's the same recipe *minus* the anchovies.
6. The final total, *minus* shipping, came to eighty dollars.

### much less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmʌtʃ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *much-LES*
**Русский:** тем более не, не говоря уже о — то же, что let alone, но чуть книжнее
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (distinct from the literal comparative *much less*: *much less money*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; a shade more formal than *let alone*
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, scalar — the second element is a fragment
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *much less* [noun phrase]. &middot; [negative clause], *much less* [verb phrase matching the one before it]. &middot; [negative clause ending in a content noun], *much less* that [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *much less*, no comma after it. What follows is a fragment parallel to the constituent it echoes — a noun phrase (*much less a car*), a verb phrase (*much less offered to fix it*), or a *that*-clause when the first half already ends in one (*no proof he was there, much less that he took anything*) — but never a free-standing clause with its own subject.
**Variants:** *let alone* &middot; *still less* (formal) &middot; *never mind* (informal)

Does the same job as *let alone* — naming a bigger case that follows automatically from a negative already stated — but reads a shade more formal and turns up more often in writing than in speech. Like *let alone*, it is ungrammatical after a positive clause.

1. She doesn't have a bicycle, *much less* a car.
2. Most of us can't name our senators, *much less* our state representatives.
3. He never apologized, *much less* offered to fix it.
4. The town couldn't fund a library, *much less* a new school.
5. I wouldn't lend him a pen, *much less* five hundred dollars.
6. There's no proof he was there, *much less* that he took anything.

**See also:** [let alone](#let-alone), [never mind](#never-mind), [still less](#still-less), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [nor](#nor)

### neither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈniðɚ/, also /ˈnaɪðɚ/ &middot; *NEE-ther, also NY-ther*
**Русский:** и я тоже нет — подхватывает отрицание; требует инверсии: Neither do I
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (an additive negative adverb by strict analysis, taking subject–auxiliary inversion; distinct from the correlative *neither … nor* and from the determiner and pronoun *neither*, as in *neither road was plowed*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in the inverted form (*Neither do I*) and in spoken *me neither*; nonstandard clause-final (*I don't like it neither*), where it belongs to Southern, Appalachian, and other regional speech and to stage dialect in fiction
**Clause type:** inverted echo clause — repeats a negative already stated and applies it to someone or something else
**Pattern:** [negative clause]. *Neither* [auxiliary or *be*] [subject]. &middot; [negative clause], and *neither* [auxiliary] [subject]. &middot; [negative clause]; *neither* [auxiliary] [subject] [rest]. &middot; Me *neither*. (spoken short answer) &middot; nonstandard [negative clause] *neither*. (clause-final, dialectal)
**Punctuation:** Comma before *neither* when *and* joins the two clauses (*I couldn't sleep, and neither could she*). Standing on its own it needs a period or a semicolon in front of it and never a bare comma: *I didn't know, neither did anyone else* is a comma splice — write *I didn't know. Neither did anyone else.* The inversion is obligatory (*neither did she*, never *neither she did*), and *Me neither* takes no internal punctuation.
**Variants:** *either* (the standard uninverted, clause-final form) &middot; *nor* (same inversion, but it joins rather than answers) &middot; *me neither* / *me either* (spoken) &middot; *neither … nor* (correlative) &middot; clause-final *neither* (*I don't neither*) is dialectal, not standard

The negative echo: it picks up a negative just stated and extends it to a second party. Standard American English requires subject–auxiliary inversion after it, and that is exactly what separates it from *either* — *Neither did I* and *I didn't either* say the same thing, but neither pattern tolerates the other's word order. An American meets the uninverted, clause-final *neither* in regional speech and in novelists writing dialect, not in edited prose, where *either* fills that slot.

1. I couldn't sleep, and *neither* could she.
2. "I've never seen it." "*Neither* have I."
3. The heat didn't work. *Neither* did the hot water.
4. He wasn't invited, and *neither* was his brother.
5. "I don't want to go." "Me *neither*."
6. The lease says nothing about pets; *neither* does it mention the parking space.
7. "I ain't got no money, and she ain't got none *neither*," he told the deputy. (dialect — edited English writes *either* here)

**See also:** [either](#either), [nor](#nor), [nor yet](#nor-yet), [neither … nor](02-correlative.md#neither--nor), [likewise](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#likewise)

### never mind

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɛvɚ ˈmaɪnd/ &middot; *nev-er-MYND*
**Русский:** уж куда там, не говоря уже о — разг.: самый просторечный из ряда let alone
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (also an ordinary imperative verb phrase: *Never mind the mess*) &middot; **Register:** informal; conversational, rare in edited prose
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, scalar — the second element is a fragment
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *never mind* [noun phrase]. &middot; [negative clause], *never mind* [verb phrase matching the one before it]. &middot; [clause with *barely* or *hardly*], *never mind* [harder case].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *never mind*, no comma after it; a dash in place of the comma marks a bigger jump. What follows is a fragment parallel to the constituent it echoes, never a clause with its own subject.
**Variants:** *let alone* (neutral) &middot; *much less* &middot; *still less* (formal) &middot; *to say nothing of* (formal) &middot; imperative *never mind* (*Never mind, I'll do it myself*) is a different construction

The most casual member of the *let alone* family: it names a bigger case and points out that the first one already rules it out. Like *let alone* and *much less* it needs a negative or some other downward-entailing context ahead of it, and unlike them it almost never survives into edited writing. Keep it apart from the imperative *never mind* meaning "forget it," which takes an object and stands on its own.

1. He can't manage a two-week trip, *never mind* a year abroad.
2. I haven't finished the first chapter, *never mind* the whole book.
3. The kids can barely sit through a cartoon, *never mind* a three-hour opera.
4. We couldn't get a table on a Tuesday, *never mind* a Saturday night.
5. She's never driven a stick shift, *never mind* hauled a trailer.
6. There's no money for raises this year — *never mind* bonuses.

**See also:** [let alone](#let-alone), [much less](#much-less), [still less](#still-less), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of)

### not only that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈoʊnli ˌðæt/ &middot; *not-OHN-lee-THAT*
**Русский:** мало того, и это ещё не всё — отсылает назад, через точку, к сказанному
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a fixed clausal connective, the free-standing descendant of the correlative *not only … but also*; distinct from *not only* heading a *that*-clause, as in *not only that he lied, but that he kept lying*) &middot; **Register:** neutral; conversational, and common in opinion writing and complaint
**Clause type:** discourse connective — heads a new clause that adds a further, usually stronger item
**Pattern:** [clause]. *Not only that*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *not only that*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]. *Not only that*, but [clause]. &middot; [clause] — *not only that*, [clause]. &middot; *Not only that*, but [clause] as well.
**Punctuation:** Put a period or a semicolon in front of it when two independent clauses are involved (*He was late; not only that, he was rude*) — a comma there is a comma splice, since the phrase is an adverbial and not a coordinator. A comma always follows it. The *but* that so often trails it is optional and takes no comma of its own.
**Variants:** *not only that, but …* &middot; *and not only that* &middot; *what's more* &middot; *on top of that* &middot; *moreover* (formal) &middot; correlative *not only … but also*

Announces that what was just said is not the whole of it and that something further, usually worse, is coming. It differs from the correlative it grew out of in needing no second half in the same sentence: *not only … but also* balances two elements inside one clause, while *not only that* points backward across a full stop.

1. He showed up two hours late. *Not only that*, he brought three friends.
2. The bill doubled; *not only that*, they charged us for a room we never used.
3. She runs the shop alone. *Not only that*, she keeps the books herself.
4. The road is closed all summer — *not only that*, the detour adds forty minutes.
5. *Not only that*, but the warranty had expired the week before it broke.
6. They never apologized. *Not only that*, they sent a second invoice.

**See also:** [on top of that](#on-top-of-that), [better yet](#better-yet), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [to boot](#to-boot), [not only … but also](02-correlative.md#not-only--but-also), [moreover](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#moreover)

### not to mention

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt tə ˈmɛnʃən/ &middot; *not-tuh-MEN-shuhn*
**Русский:** не говоря уже о — добавляет пункт под видом умолчания; отрицание не нужно
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (distinct from the literal infinitive *not to mention*: *He promised not to mention the money*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on)
**Pattern:** [X], *not to mention* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *not to mention* [noun phrase]. &middot; [independent clause], *not to mention* [adjective phrase or verb phrase matching the one before it].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *not to mention*, no comma after it. Dashes or parentheses work better when the add-on runs long.
**Variants:** *to say nothing of* (formal) &middot; *never mind* &middot; *plus* (informal) &middot; *let alone* (only after a negative)

Adds a further item while pretending to leave it out — a rhetorical move that presents the addition as too obvious to need saying. Unlike *let alone* and *much less*, it needs no negative in front of it and does not rank the two items on a scale.

1. The trip cost us two weeks of vacation, *not to mention* four thousand dollars.
2. He's late every morning, *not to mention* rude about it.
3. Rewiring the house would take a month, *not to mention* an electrician we can't afford.
4. The noise was unbearable, *not to mention* the dust.
5. She runs the whole department, *not to mention* two committees.
6. That neighborhood has better schools, *not to mention* a grocery store you can walk to.

**See also:** [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of), [let alone](#let-alone), [much less](#much-less), [never mind](#never-mind), [plus](#plus), [as well as](#as-well-as)

### on top of that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑn ˈtɑp əv ˌðæt/, weak /ˌɑn ˈtɑp ə ˌðæt/ &middot; *on-TOP-uhv-that, weak on-TOP-uh-that*
**Русский:** вдобавок ко всему, и в довершение — разг., почти всегда о новой неприятности
**Type:** conjunctive adverb (a fixed prepositional connective; distinct from the literal spatial *on top of that* — *the box on top of that one* — and from additive *on top of* + noun phrase, *a surcharge on top of the fare*) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; conversational, and at home in feature writing and complaint rather than in contracts, where *over and above* does the same work
**Clause type:** discourse connective — heads a clause that adds one more item to a list already felt to be heavy
**Pattern:** [clause]. *On top of that*, [clause]. &middot; [clause]; *on top of that*, [clause]. &middot; [clause], and *on top of that*, [clause]. &middot; *On top of everything else*, [clause]. &middot; compare prepositional *on top of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Semicolon or period before it between two independent clauses (*Tuition went up; on top of that, they cut the shuttle*) — a comma there is a comma splice, unless *and* is doing the joining, in which case the phrase turns parenthetical and takes a comma on each side (*and, on top of that, the car wouldn't start*). A comma follows it. The prepositional use needs no comma at all.
**Variants:** *on top of everything else* &middot; *on top of it all* &middot; *besides that* &middot; *what's more* &middot; *in addition* (formal) &middot; *over and above* (formal, and of amounts rather than grievances) &middot; prepositional *on top of* + noun phrase

Piles one more item onto a load the reader is already meant to feel, which is why it leans negative: *on top of that* almost always adds a further cost, delay, or insult. *In addition* and *besides* carry no such freight, and *over and above* — the same metaphor in formal dress — stacks amounts rather than grievances.

1. The flight was canceled. *On top of that*, the hotel gave away our room.
2. Tuition went up nine percent; *on top of that*, they cut the shuttle to campus.
3. She works two jobs, and *on top of that*, she's finishing her degree at night.
4. The repair ran four hundred dollars — *on top of that*, we lost a day of work.
5. *On top of everything else*, the dog got into the trash.
6. He never returned the ladder, and *on top of that*, he broke it.

**See also:** [over and above](#over-and-above), [not only that](#not-only-that), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [to boot](#to-boot), [plus](#plus), [besides](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#besides), [in addition](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#in-addition)

### only

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊnli/ &middot; *OHN-lee*
**Русский:** только вот, вот только — разг.: оговорка, портящая всё только что сказанное
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (far more often a focusing adverb or an adjective) &middot; **Register:** informal; conversational
**Clause type:** coordinate clause, adversative — equivalent to *but* or *except that*
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *only* [independent clause]. &middot; *Only* [clause]. (as a following sentence, informal)
**Punctuation:** Comma before conjunctive *only*. Without that comma the word is read as the focusing adverb, which attaches to whatever follows it and changes the meaning of the sentence.
**Variants:** *except that* &middot; *but* (the neutral equivalents) &middot; adverbial *only* (*I only asked*) is a different word class

Introduces the single thing that spoils or qualifies what was just said — "but there's a catch." It is squarely conversational; in edited prose the same job goes to *but* or *except that*.

1. I'd love to come, *only* I have a deadline Friday.
2. It's a great apartment, *only* the radiators bang all night.
3. We'd have made it on time, *only* the tunnel was closed.
4. She'd tell you herself, *only* she's not speaking to either of us.
5. I would have called. *Only* it was two in the morning.
6. The car runs fine, *only* it burns oil.

**See also:** [but](#but), [but then](#but-then), [except that](07-concession.md#except-that), [however](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#however)

### or something

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr ˈsʌmθɪŋ/, weak /ɚ ˈsʌmθɪŋ/ &middot; *or-SUHM-thing, weak er-SUHM-thing*
**Русский:** или что-то в этом роде — разг.: говорящий не ручается за точность
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a general extender — *or* plus a vague placeholder) &middot; **Register:** informal; spoken
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — a hedging tag that closes a noun phrase, a clause, or a guess
**Pattern:** [X] *or something*. &middot; [clause] *or something*. &middot; [X] *or something like that*. &middot; [negative clause] *or anything*. &middot; Are you [adjective] *or something*? (challenge)
**Punctuation:** No comma before it — it belongs to the phrase it trails. A comma follows only when the sentence keeps going (*He's a cousin or something, I think*). Never a comma between the two words.
**Variants:** *or something like that* &middot; *or whatever* &middot; *or anything* (after a negative) &middot; *or so* (with quantities) &middot; *or some such* (formal)

Marks the item just named as approximate: the speaker is not sure of it and does not want to be held to it. After a negative it switches to *or anything* (*He didn't apologize or anything*), and in a question it turns aggressive — *Are you deaf or something?*

1. He's a second cousin *or something* — I've never been clear on it.
2. She said she'd be here at seven *or something* like that.
3. Grab a sandwich *or something* on the way over.
4. Are you trying to get us both fired *or something*?
5. They never called, never wrote, never sent a card *or anything*.
6. It cost eighty dollars *or something*, which seemed like a lot at the time.

**See also:** [or what](#or-what), [and stuff / and everything / and all that](#and-stuff--and-everything--and-all-that), [and so forth](#and-so-forth), [et cetera / etc.](#et-cetera--etc), [or](#or)

### or what

**Pronunciation:** /ɔr ˈwʌt/, also /ɔr ˈhwʌt/ &middot; *or-WHUT*
**Русский:** или как? ну разве не...? — разг. концовка вопроса: требует согласия или решения
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a fixed tag — *or* plus the interrogative *what*) &middot; **Register:** informal; spoken
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — a clause-final tag, and only in questions
**Pattern:** Is [subject] [adjective], *or what*? (rhetorical — "obviously yes") &middot; Are [subject] [verb]-ing, *or what*? (impatient — "decide") &middot; [yes-or-no question], *or what*?
**Punctuation:** Comma before *or what* in writing, and always a question mark after it: it exists only in questions. No comma between the two words, and nothing follows it in the sentence.
**Variants:** *or not?* (the neutral alternative-question form) &middot; *or anything?* &middot; *or something?* &middot; *huh?* (spoken)

Closes a question with a shrug at the alternatives it declines to name, and it does one of two jobs. After a description it demands agreement (*Is that a view, or what?*); after a plain yes-or-no question it demands a decision (*Are we ordering, or what?*). Both are conversational, and neither survives into edited prose.

1. Is that a view, *or what*?
2. Are we ordering, *or what*?
3. Was that the best game you've seen all year, *or what*?
4. So is he coming to the wedding, *or what*?
5. Did they forget about us entirely, *or what*?
6. Is this kid a natural, *or what*?

### other than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌðɚ ðən/, also /ˈʌðɚ ðæn/ &middot; *UH-ther-thuhn*
**Русский:** кроме, помимо — единственное исключение; нужны отрицание, вопрос или «все»
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (exceptive; a two-word preposition by strict analysis, built on comparative *than*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, exceptive — subtracts one member from the set just named; also fronts the sentence
**Pattern:** [negative or universal clause] *other than* [noun phrase]. &middot; no one / nothing / nobody *other than* [X] &middot; *Other than* [X], [main clause]. &middot; [noun] *other than* [noun] (*any option other than surrender*) &middot; *Other than that*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when it attaches straight to the noun it excepts (*no one other than the owner*). Comma before it when it trails a finished clause, and a comma after a fronted *Other than* phrase (*Other than the rain, the trip was perfect*). Never a comma after *than*.
**Variants:** *apart from* &middot; *aside from* &middot; *except* / *except for* &middot; *besides* &middot; *outside of* (informal) &middot; prepositional *but* (*no one but the owner*)

Names the one member a statement does not cover, so it needs a negative, a universal, or a question in front of it to have something to subtract from. *Other than that* has hardened into a discourse formula for waving away everything already mentioned.

1. No one *other than* the owner has a key.
2. *Other than* the rain on Tuesday, the whole trip went perfectly.
3. There's nothing on the menu *other than* fish.
4. She never discussed the case with anyone *other than* her attorney.
5. The flight sat on the tarmac for two hours; *other than* that, it was fine.
6. Any solution *other than* raising taxes is going to take years.

**See also:** [outside of](#outside-of), [rather than](#rather-than), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [but](#but), [save](12-formal-archaic.md#save)

### outside of

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd əv/, weak /ˌaʊtˈsaɪd ə/ &middot; *out-SYD-uhv*
**Русский:** кроме, если не считать — разг., амер.; на письме предпочитают apart from
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (exceptive; distinct from the literal spatial *outside of* — *outside of town*) &middot; **Register:** informal; American, and avoided in edited prose
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, exceptive — subtracts one member from the set just named; also fronts the sentence
**Pattern:** *Outside of* [X], [main clause]. &middot; [clause], *outside of* [noun phrase]. &middot; nobody / nothing *outside of* [X] &middot; *Outside of that*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *Outside of* phrase; comma before it when it trails a finished clause. Never a comma after *of*. In the spatial sense the *of* is optional and usually dropped (*outside the building*); in this exceptive sense it is fixed.
**Variants:** *apart from* &middot; *aside from* &middot; *other than* &middot; *except for* &middot; *besides* &middot; spatial *outside (of)* (*outside of town*) is a different use

The colloquial American *except for*: it lifts one item out of an otherwise sweeping statement. Editors object that *outside* is properly spatial and prefer *apart from* or *other than* in writing; in speech it is completely ordinary and completely clear.

1. *Outside of* a couple of typos, the draft is clean.
2. Nobody knew about the offer *outside of* the two of us.
3. *Outside of* one summer in Maine, she has never left the state.
4. She hasn't taken a day off, *outside of* the week her mother was sick.
5. There's nothing to do out here *outside of* fishing.
6. The car needs tires; *outside of* that, it's in good shape.

### over and above

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚ ənd əˈbʌv/ &middot; *oh-ver-uhnd-uh-BUHV*
**Русский:** сверх, помимо — офиц.: вторая сумма именно добавочная, а не включённая
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (additive; a fixed three-word preposition) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; contracts, pay talk, and official writing
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on) — joins noun phrases; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *over and above* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *over and above* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Over and above* [X], [main clause]. &middot; *over and above* what / whatever [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when the phrase attaches directly to what it adds to (*a bonus over and above her salary*). Comma before it when it trails a finished clause, and a comma after a fronted phrase. Never a comma inside the phrase itself.
**Variants:** *in addition to* &middot; *on top of* &middot; *besides* &middot; *above and beyond* (a different idiom — "more than duty required")

Adds one amount to another and insists the second is extra rather than included — the phrase a contract uses so that nobody can later claim the two figures were one. Keep it apart from *above and beyond*, which praises effort instead of stacking quantities.

1. The bonus is paid *over and above* her base salary.
2. *Over and above* the repair bill, we lost a full week of work.
3. Tenants pay for utilities *over and above* the monthly rent.
4. The grant covers travel *over and above* the tuition award.
5. He wants a share of the profits *over and above* what he has already been paid.
6. The storm did structural damage *over and above* the flooding in the basement.

**See also:** [plus](#plus), [as well as](#as-well-as), [along with](#along-with), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [in addition](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#in-addition)

### plus

**Pronunciation:** /plʌs/ &middot; *PLUHS*
**Русский:** плюс; да и ещё — арифметическое сложение; разг. союз «и» с добавочным доводом
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (also a noun and an adjective — *that's a plus*, *a B plus*, *a plus sign*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic; informal as a clause connector
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase; also a coordinate clause in informal use
**Pattern:** [number] *plus* [number] [singular verb] … &middot; [X] *plus* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause], *plus* [independent clause]. (informal) &middot; *Plus*, [clause]. (sentence-initial, informal)
**Punctuation:** No comma when *plus* joins two noun phrases or numbers (*rent plus utilities*). Comma before clause-joining *plus*. Sentence-initial *Plus* normally takes a comma after it.
**Variants:** *and* (the neutral equivalent) &middot; *plus that* (nonstandard) &middot; *besides* (conjunctive adverb)

In arithmetic and pricing it adds one quantity to another and leaves the subject singular (*Two plus two is four*). In speech it doubles as an informal *and* that tacks on one more reason, usually the clincher — *It's closer, plus the parking is free*. That clause-joining use is at home in conversation and email and out of place in formal writing.

1. Two *plus* two is four.
2. The rate is ninety dollars a night, *plus* tax.
3. It's closer to the office, *plus* the parking is free.
4. *Plus*, we already paid for the tickets.
5. I don't have the tools for it, *plus* I'd probably break something.
6. The job comes with health insurance *plus* three weeks of vacation.

### rather than

**Pronunciation:** /ˈræðɚ ðən/ &middot; *RA-ther-thuhn*
**Русский:** а не, вместо того чтобы — называет невыбранный вариант; формы по обе стороны совпадают
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (prepositional before a noun phrase; *rather* on its own is an adverb of degree or preference, and *than* elsewhere is a comparative subordinator) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase of preference; also fronts the sentence as an adverbial
**Pattern:** [X] *rather than* [Y]. &middot; *Rather than* [X], [main clause]. &middot; [modal + bare verb] *rather than* [bare verb]. &middot; [*-ing* form] *rather than* [*-ing* form].
**Punctuation:** No comma when *rather than* joins two parallel words or phrases (*Call rather than email*). Comma after a fronted *Rather than* phrase (*Rather than argue, she paid the fee*), and commas on both sides when it interrupts the sentence.
**Variants:** *instead of* (takes a noun or an *-ing* form, never a bare infinitive) &middot; *as opposed to* &middot; *sooner than* (older)

Names the option that was not chosen. The two halves have to be the same grammatical shape, and the form after *rather than* copies the form before it: *I'd walk rather than pay for parking* (bare verb after a bare verb), but *He spends evenings reading rather than watching television* (*-ing* after *-ing*). Note that the American vowel in *rather* is the one in *cat*; the British /ˈrɑːðə/ is a different sound entirely.

1. I'd walk *rather than* pay for parking.
2. *Rather than* argue about it, she just paid the fee.
3. We took the train *rather than* the bus.
4. He spends his evenings reading *rather than* watching television.
5. The change was cosmetic *rather than* structural.
6. She'd resign *rather than* sign off on numbers she didn't believe.

### still less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌstɪl ˈlɛs/ &middot; *stil-LES*
**Русский:** и тем более не — книжн., самый формальный из ряда let alone / much less
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (distinct from the literal comparative *still less*: *still less time than before*) &middot; **Register:** formal; chiefly written
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase, scalar — the second element is a fragment
**Pattern:** [negative clause], *still less* [noun phrase]. &middot; [negative clause], *still less* [verb phrase matching the one before it]. &middot; [negative clause ending in a content noun], *still less* that [clause].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *still less*, no comma after it. What follows is a fragment parallel to the constituent it echoes; it never carries a subject and a tensed verb of its own.
**Variants:** *much less* &middot; *let alone* (neutral) &middot; *never mind* (informal) &middot; *to say nothing of* &middot; literal comparative *still less* (*still less patience*) is a different word class

The most formal of the scalar add-ons — the one a judge, a philosopher, or an obituarist reaches for where speech would use *let alone*. It behaves exactly like *much less*: it demands a negative or other downward-entailing context in front of it, and the item it introduces is the harder case that the stated negative already covers. In American writing it is noticeably rarer than *much less* and reads as deliberately elevated.

1. The report does not establish motive, *still less* intent.
2. He had no authority to reopen the file, *still less* to destroy it.
3. Nothing in the record suggests she knew, *still less* that she approved.
4. The treaty was never ratified, *still less* enforced.
5. We cannot explain consciousness, *still less* reproduce it.
6. She rarely spoke of her childhood, *still less* of the years just after the war.

**See also:** [much less](#much-less), [let alone](#let-alone), [never mind](#never-mind), [to say nothing of](#to-say-nothing-of), [nor](#nor)

### times

**Pronunciation:** /taɪmz/ &middot; *TYMZ*
**Русский:** умножить на — арифметическое умножение: three times four «трижды четыре»
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (arithmetic; also the plural noun *times* and, informally, a verb: *times it by four*) &middot; **Register:** neutral in arithmetic; the verb use is schoolroom and informal
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — joins numerals and quantity expressions only, never clauses
**Pattern:** [number] *times* [number] [singular verb] [number]. &middot; [number] *times* [noun phrase]. &middot; [indefinite pronoun] *times* [number] [singular verb] [number]. &middot; What's [number] *times* [number]? (question)
**Punctuation:** No commas anywhere around it, and the whole phrase takes a singular verb (*Six times seven is forty-two*). Spell it out in running prose and use × between figures. Do not carry it over to dimensions: × between two measurements is read *by*, not *times* (*an eight-by-ten print*).
**Variants:** *×* (multiplication sign) &middot; *multiplied by* (formal) &middot; *by* — the word for dimensions, where *times* is wrong (*eight by ten*) &middot; verbal *times* (*Times that by three* — informal)

Multiplies one quantity by another and, like *plus* and *minus*, leaves the resulting subject singular. It is the third member of the arithmetic set and the only one of the three with no extension into ordinary prose: *plus* became an informal *and* and *minus* became an informal *without*, but *times* stayed with the numbers, surviving outside them only in multiplier phrases such as *three times the price*.

1. Six *times* seven is forty-two.
2. Quick — what's eight *times* twelve?
3. Anything *times* zero is zero.
4. Rent downtown runs about three *times* what we pay now.
5. Two *times* the recipe should feed the whole crew.
6. Twelve *times* twelve equals one hundred forty-four.

**See also:** [plus](#plus), [minus](#minus), [versus](#versus), [and](#and)

### to boot

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˈbut/, full /tu ˈbut/ &middot; *tuh-BOOT*
**Русский:** в придачу, вдобавок — разг., слегка старомодно и с иронией; только в конце фразы
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a clause-final additive tag; the *boot* in it is the obsolete noun *bōt* "advantage, something extra", unrelated to footwear and surviving elsewhere only in *bootless* "useless") &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; old-fashioned in flavor and faintly wry, at home in speech, reviews, and feature writing, and out of place in business or technical prose
**Clause type:** coordinate tag — closes a clause by throwing in one last item
**Pattern:** [clause], and [final item] *to boot*. &middot; [X], [Y], and [Z] *to boot*. &middot; [adjective], and [second adjective] *to boot*. &middot; [noun phrase] *to boot*.
**Punctuation:** Comma before the added item, no comma between that item and *to boot*, and nothing after it — the tag ends the sentence (*He was late, and rude to boot*). It never joins two clauses by itself: put *and* in front of the second element, or start a new sentence.
**Variants:** *besides* &middot; *as well* &middot; *in the bargain* (American) &middot; *into the bargain* (chiefly British) &middot; *for good measure* &middot; *and then some* (of quantities rather than items)

Adds one final item and presents it as a bonus thrown in on top of the rest. It means roughly what *as well* and *besides* mean, but carries a wry, faintly antique tone that neither of them has, and unlike them it is locked to the end of the sentence.

1. The car was cheap, and it came with new tires *to boot*.
2. He's a fine cook, and a patient teacher *to boot*.
3. The house needs a roof, a furnace, and rewiring *to boot*.
4. She won the election, and carried two neighboring counties *to boot*.
5. The place was noisy, cramped, and overpriced *to boot*.
6. They refunded the tickets, and threw in a voucher for next season *to boot*.

**See also:** [as well](#as-well), [and then some](#and-then-some), [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [on top of that](#on-top-of-that), [over and above](#over-and-above), [besides](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#besides)

### to say nothing of

**Pronunciation:** /tə ˌseɪ ˈnʌθɪŋ əv/ &middot; *tuh-say-NUH-thing-uhv*
**Русский:** не говоря уже о — книжн. вариант not to mention
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (distinct from the literal infinitive *to say nothing of*: *She chose to say nothing of the argument*) &middot; **Register:** formal to neutral; chiefly written
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase (an add-on)
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *to say nothing of* [noun phrase]. &middot; [X], *to say nothing of* [Y]. &middot; [independent clause] — *to say nothing of* [noun phrase].
**Punctuation:** Comma before *to say nothing of*, no comma after it. A dash or a pair of parentheses reads better when the add-on runs long or lands as an aside.
**Variants:** *not to mention* (neutral) &middot; *never mind* (informal) &middot; *let alone* (only after a negative) &middot; *to say nothing about* (rare)

The written, faintly rhetorical cousin of *not to mention*: it adds one more item under cover of declining to raise it. Like *not to mention* it needs no negative ahead of it and does not rank the two items on a scale, which is exactly what separates it from *let alone*, *much less*, and *still less*.

1. The renovation swallowed our savings, *to say nothing of* two summers.
2. He lost the account, *to say nothing of* the goodwill that came with it.
3. The plan would take a decade, *to say nothing of* the money.
4. She managed the move alone, *to say nothing of* the paperwork it involved.
5. The heat was punishing, *to say nothing of* the mosquitoes.
6. The bridge closure cost the county millions — *to say nothing of* the two years drivers spent sitting in traffic.

**See also:** [not to mention](#not-to-mention), [never mind](#never-mind), [let alone](#let-alone), [much less](#much-less), [as well as](#as-well-as)

### versus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɚsəs/, often /ˈvɚsəz/ &middot; *VUR-suhs*
**Русский:** против, в сравнении с — сталкивает две стороны; юр. и спорт.
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (a preposition by strict analysis) &middot; **Register:** neutral; fixed and formal in legal and sports citation
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase — joins noun phrases and, informally, *-ing* clauses; never finite clauses
**Pattern:** [X] *versus* [Y]. &middot; [X] *vs.* [Y]. &middot; [Party] *v.* [Party]. (case names)
**Punctuation:** No commas around it. Abbreviate as *vs.* in general American prose and *v.* in the names of legal cases (*Roe v. Wade*); American style keeps the period on both, where British style often drops it.
**Variants:** *vs.* &middot; *v.* (legal) &middot; *versus* spelled out; the spelling *verses* is a different word entirely

Sets two things against each other as opposed alternatives — a real contest (*Yankees versus Red Sox*) or a choice being argued about (*speed versus accuracy*). Unlike *or*, it does not offer a choice so much as frame a comparison, and it cannot join two finite clauses.

1. Tonight it's the Yankees *versus* the Red Sox.
2. The whole debate comes down to speed *versus* accuracy.
3. We compared the cost of repairing the roof *versus* replacing it.
4. Ohio State *vs.* Michigan is the only game he cares about.
5. Every first-year law student reads Brown *v.* Board of Education.
6. It's a question of quantity *versus* quality.

**See also:** [or](#or), [rather than](#rather-than), [as opposed to](#as-opposed-to), [minus](#minus), [times](#times), [whereas](08-comparison.md#whereas)

### yea

**Pronunciation:** /jeɪ/ &middot; *YAY*
**Русский:** более того, да что там — устар., книжн.: усиливает сказанное, не отменяя его
**Type:** quasi-coordinator (archaic, intensifying; distinct from the *yea* of voice votes — *the yeas and nays* — and from informal *yeah* /ˈjɛə/) &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary; scripture, older oratory, and deliberate pastiche
**Clause type:** coordinate phrase or clause — introduces a stronger restatement of what was just said
**Pattern:** [X], *yea*, [stronger X]. &middot; [clause], *yea*, [stronger clause]. &middot; [adjective], *yea*, [stronger adjective]. &middot; compare corrective [X], *nay*, [replacement X].
**Punctuation:** Fence it with a comma on each side: it interrupts rather than linking two halves that could stand alone. Sentence-initial *Yea* in older texts takes its own comma after it.
**Variants:** *nay* (the corrective twin — it replaces the first word instead of raising it) &middot; *indeed* &middot; *even* &middot; *and more than that*

Climbs from the word just used to a stronger one and keeps both: *a generous offer, yea, an extravagant one* asserts generosity and then outbids it with extravagance. Its twin *nay* withdraws the first word instead of keeping it (*a good, nay, a great man*). Both belong to scripture, older oratory, and writing that means to sound like them.

1. It was a generous offer, *yea*, an extravagant one.
2. He served the town for thirty years, *yea*, for the better part of his life.
3. The work is difficult, *yea*, all but impossible for one person.
4. She was a friend to him, *yea*, the only friend he had.
5. They gave what they could spare, *yea*, more than they could spare.
6. The road was long, *yea*, longer than any of us had reckoned.

**See also:** [nor yet](#nor-yet), [as also](#as-also), [if not](#if-not), [and](#and), [indeed](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#indeed)
