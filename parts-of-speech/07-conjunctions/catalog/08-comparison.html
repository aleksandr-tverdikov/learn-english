Subordinators of comparison and manner#

The conjunctions that measure one thing against another — more, less, or equal — and the ones that say in what manner something is done.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Every conjunction in this file answers one of two questions: how much compared to what, or in what way. What unites them grammatically is that they almost never work alone — the subordinator is the second half of a frame whose first half sits earlier in the sentence, in a comparative word (taller, more slowly, fewer) or a correlative marker (as, so, the same, no sooner). Learn the pairs, not the single words: than is meaningless without a comparative in front of it, and a lone as is a different conjunction from as … as.

The clause they introduce is normally elliptical — English deletes from the comparative clause everything the main clause already supplied, which is why She's taller than I am shrinks to than I and then, in speech, to than me. Both are current American English: than I am and as tall as he is are the safe written forms, the bare objective (than me, as tall as him) is the normal spoken one, and than whom is the single place where the objective case is fixed.

The punctuation rule that runs through the whole category: comparative clauses are integral to the sentence, so they take no comma. Write She finished faster than anyone expected, never faster, than anyone expected. There are four exceptions, and they are worth memorizing. (1) The paired frames whose halves are two full clauses — the more … the more and as … so — take a comma at the seam. (2) Any clause in this file that is moved to the front of the main clause takes a comma after it: As far as I know, the office is closed. (3) The supplementary markers — whereas, contrastive while, as compared with, in comparison with, in contrast to, unlike, as opposed to, as against, as distinct from, as distinguished from, as with, and often compared to — are set off with commas, because they comment on the main clause rather than complete it. The everyday compared to is the loose one: fronted it always takes a comma (Compared to last winter, this one has been mild), but a short trailing phrase usually drops it (The rent is reasonable compared to what friends pay downtown). (4) Than whom takes commas on both sides, because it heads a supplementary relative clause instead of finishing a comparison.

One rule from outside this file belongs here, because it is where these commas actually go wrong. However leads two lives. The subordinator catalogued below — the one meaning "in whatever way" — takes a comma only when its clause is fronted: However you slice it, the numbers don't work. The conjunctive adverb however, the one that means "but," cannot join two independent clauses with a comma. That is a comma splice: ✗ We were late, however they held our table. It requires a semicolon or a period — We were late; however, they held our table or We were late. However, they held our table. The same holds for every conjunctive adverb of comparison and contrast: likewise, similarly, conversely, by contrast, instead, on the other hand, in comparison. A comma alone will not carry any of them. Only the true coordinating conjunctions — and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet — join two independent clauses with a comma before them (The northern route is faster, but the coastal road is prettier), and none of the seven live in this file.

Comparison of inequality: the than family#

These attach to a comparative form earlier in the sentence. The comparative clause itself never takes a comma before than: faster than anyone expected, not faster, than anyone expected. Four entries in this section are exceptions, and each is an exception because it is not a plain comparison — than whom heads a supplementary relative clause and takes commas on both sides, rather than takes commas when it is a parenthetical afterthought rather than a linked alternative, the rhetorical how much more / how much less is punctuated off from the clause that sets it up, and the Latin a fortiori is not a subordinator at all but a conjunctive adverb, which is why it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it between two independent clauses.

a fortiori#

Pronunciation: /ˌeɪ ˌfɔrʃiˈɔraɪ/, also /ˌeɪ ˌfɔrtiˈɔri/ · ay for-shee-OR-eye, also ay for-tee-OR-ee Type: conjunctive adverb (Latin) of comparative inference; also attributive, as an adjective before a noun (an a fortiori argument) · Register: formal; Latin — the register of judicial opinions, briefs, law reviews, and analytic philosophy Clause type: sentence adverbial marking its clause as following with even greater force from what the previous clause already granted Pattern: [Independent clause]; a fortiori, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. A fortiori, [independent clause]. · If [weaker case], then a fortiori [stronger case]. · [main clause], and a fortiori [phrase]. · an a fortiori [argument / case / reason] (attributive). Punctuation: Comma after it when it opens a clause. Between two independent clauses it is an adverb, not a conjunction, so it takes a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a comma splice: ✗ The rule bars a dinner, a fortiori it bars a car. ✓ The rule bars a dinner; a fortiori, it bars a car. Only and can carry it across with a comma (…, and a fortiori…). No commas in the attributive use. Set it in roman in American legal writing; it has been naturalized long enough not to need italics. Variants: how much more (the English rhetorical frame), all the more so, even more so, a maiore ad minus and a minore ad maius (the two directions, in legal Latin)

Latin for "from the stronger [argument]": it says that a point already conceded in a weaker case must hold with greater force in a stronger one. It is the Latin name of the how much more frame, and the difference is what each one does with the move — how much more asks a rhetorical question, while a fortiori asserts a conclusion, which is why briefs and philosophy papers use it where a sermon uses the English. An American reader meets it in court opinions, appellate briefs, and academic argument, and essentially nowhere else; in ordinary prose all the more so carries the same idea without the Latin.

  1. If a warrant is required to search a car, then a fortiori one is required to search a home.
  2. The statute bars a legislator from accepting a fifty-dollar dinner; a fortiori, it bars accepting a car.
  3. Congress may regulate the sale of the drug; a fortiori, it may regulate its manufacture.
  4. She had no duty to volunteer the information, and a fortiori no duty to repeat it.
  5. The court found the weaker record in Ramirez insufficient, and this record is thinner still — an a fortiori case if there ever was one.
  6. If a grown adult could not have understood the waiver, a fortiori a fourteen-year-old could not.

any more than#

Pronunciation: /ˌɛni ˈmɔr ðən/, /ˌɛni ˈmɔr ðæn/ · EN-ee MOR thuhn Type: correlative comparative frame (subordinating), negative-polarity — it needs a negative, a question, or another non-affirmative context earlier in the sentence · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison (paired denial) Pattern: [negative clause] any more than [clause]. · [negative clause] any more than [noun phrase] (elliptical). · Not [X] any more than [Y]. · [question] any more than [clause]? Punctuation: No comma before any more than, and none inside the frame. Variants: no more … than (the affirmative twin), not … any more than, no more than

The negative-polarity form of no more … than: it denies the main clause and the compared clause in one stroke, so I can't rebuild a transmission any more than I can land a plane says that both are beyond me. It cannot stand in a plain positive sentence — something non-affirmative (not, never, hardly, or a question) has to license it. Keep the two words apart: any more here is a degree phrase, not the adverb anymore meaning "these days."

  1. I can't rebuild a transmission any more than I can land a plane.
  2. She doesn't set the schedule any more than the rest of us do.
  3. A new logo won't fix the company any more than fresh paint fixes a cracked foundation.
  4. He never asked for this any more than you did.
  5. Nobody enjoys the paperwork any more than the inspectors do.
  6. Does a title make him an expert any more than a uniform makes me a pilot?

how much more / how much less#

Pronunciation: /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈmɔr/, /ˌhaʊ mʌtʃ ˈlɛs/ · how much MOR, how much LESS Type: rhetorical comparative frame (a fortiori) — a wh- degree phrase heading an elliptical clause, not a plain subordinator · Register: formal, rhetorical, literary Clause type: exclamatory or interrogative comparative clause, usually elliptical, hanging off a clause that supplies the standard Pattern: If [clause], how much more [phrase]? · [clause]; how much less [phrase]. · How much more [adj/adv] [noun phrase] [verb phrase]! · [clause] — how much more so [phrase]. Punctuation: A comma or a semicolon at the seam, because the second half is a separate rhetorical move rather than a completion of the first. A question mark or an exclamation point at the end, depending on whether it is put as a question or an exclamation. No comma inside the frame. Variants: how much the more (archaic), how much more so, all the more, a fortiori (the Latin name for the move)

The a-fortiori frame: it takes a case the reader has already granted and argues that the point holds with even greater force in the second case. How much more pushes the conclusion up from a small case to a large one, and how much less pushes it down after a negative. The second half is almost always elliptical, and it is a rhetorical question — no answer is expected or wanted.

  1. If one missed payment dents your credit, how much more a foreclosure?
  2. They wouldn't listen to their own auditor; how much less to a stranger with a spreadsheet.
  3. If the draft was too long, how much more the version with the appendices.
  4. Children notice when adults are tired — how much more when adults are angry.
  5. If we can't keep one office running, how much less a national network of them.
  6. How much more difficult the climb becomes once the weather turns.

no less than#

Pronunciation: /noʊ ˈlɛs ðən/, /noʊ ˈlɛs ðæn/ · noh LESS thuhn Type: correlative comparative frame (subordinating) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison Pattern: [X] is no less [adj] than [Y] (is). · [clause] [verb] no less than [clause]. · No less than [quantity / name] [verb phrase]. · [X] is no less a [noun] than [Y]. Punctuation: No comma before than. No comma inside the frame. Variants: none the less than (archaic), no less a … than (with a noun: "no less a figure than")

Asserts that the first term measures up to the second, usually with the rhetorical suggestion that people underrate it. Where no more … than denies the quality of both terms at once, no less … than affirms it of both, and the bare no less than before a number or a name means "and that is a lot" rather than "at most."

  1. The sequel is no less ambitious than the original.
  2. Her second novel was no less well received than her first.
  3. He was no less surprised than I was to see her name on the list.
  4. No less than the mayor herself showed up to cut the ribbon.
  5. The repairs will cost no less than they did last spring.
  6. Two summers on a job site taught him no less than four years of classes had.
  7. Losing the grant was no less a setback than losing the lease had been.

no more than#

Pronunciation: /noʊ ˈmɔr ðən/, /noʊ ˈmɔr ðæn/ · noh MOR thuhn Type: correlative comparative frame (subordinating) · Register: neutral; the paired-denial frame is formal and rhetorical Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison Pattern: [X] is no more [adj] than [Y] (is). · [X] is no more a [noun] than [Y] (is). · [clause] [verb] no more than [clause]. · [clause] [verb] no more than [quantity]. Punctuation: No comma before than. Variants: not any more … than (informal), no more a … than (with a noun: "no more a lawyer than")

In its argument frame this denies the property of both terms together: He is no more a scientist than I am says neither of us is one. Learners regularly read it as an ordinary "less than" comparison; it is actually a paired denial, and the second term is chosen precisely because it is obviously false. Before a bare quantity it means simply "at most."

  1. A rented tux makes him no more a groom than a costume makes me a pilot.
  2. The committee has no more authority here than the audience does.
  3. She's no more to blame than you are.
  4. It took no more than twenty minutes to change the tire.
  5. He earns no more than he did five years ago.
  6. That was no more an accident than the fire last spring.

no sooner … than#

Pronunciation: /noʊ ˈsunər … ðən/ · noh SOO-nur … thuhn Type: correlative · Register: formal to neutral; the fronted form is literary Clause type: adverbial clause of time built on a comparative frame Pattern: No sooner [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] than [clause]. · [subject] had no sooner [past participle] than [clause]. Punctuation: No comma at the seam — than joins the halves directly. Fronting No sooner forces subject–auxiliary inversion: No sooner had we sat down than…, never No sooner we had sat down. Variants: no sooner had … than, hardly … when and scarcely … when (same meaning, different second half)

Formally a comparison, semantically pure sequence: it says the second event followed the first immediately. Because the frame is comparative, the second half must be than; no sooner … when is the commonest error in this construction, and it comes from confusing it with hardly … when and scarcely … when, which do take when.

  1. No sooner had we sat down than the fire alarm went off.
  2. No sooner did the rain stop than the mosquitoes came out.
  3. She had no sooner hung up than the phone rang again.
  4. No sooner was the ink dry than they wanted changes.
  5. We had no sooner unpacked than the landlord called about a leak.
  6. No sooner had the news broken than the stock dropped nine points.

rather than#

Pronunciation: /ˈræðər ðən/ · RA-thur thuhn Type: subordinating; also works as a quasi-coordinator · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of preference and substitution; also links two parallel phrases Pattern: Rather than [bare infinitive / -ing], [main clause]. · [main clause] rather than [bare infinitive]. · [X] rather than [Y] (two matching phrases). · [subject] would rather [bare infinitive] than [bare infinitive]. Punctuation: Comma after a fronted rather than phrase. No comma when it links two parallel elements inside a clause. Commas on both sides only when it is a parenthetical afterthought. Variants: rather … than, would rather … than

Marks the rejected alternative. Its nearest neighbor is instead of, and they take different complements: rather than takes a bare infinitive when the main clause has one (Rather than pay, we walked out), while instead of requires -ing (Instead of paying, we walked out). Whatever sits on the two sides of rather than must be the same kind of phrase.

  1. Rather than argue about it, I just paid the fee.
  2. We took the train rather than fly.
  3. She sees it as a delay rather than a defeat.
  4. Rather than wait for approval, the team shipped a draft.
  5. He wants honest feedback rather than praise.
  6. The problem is structural rather than personal.

See also: sooner than, than, not so much … as, or

sooner than#

Pronunciation: /ˈsunər ðən/ · SOO-nur thuhn Type: subordinating · Register: neutral, emphatic Clause type: adverbial clause of preference; also a plain comparison of time Pattern: [subject] would [bare infinitive] sooner than [bare infinitive] (preference). · [subject] 'd sooner [bare infinitive] than [bare infinitive]. · Sooner than [bare infinitive], [main clause]. · [main clause] sooner than [clause] (literal time). Punctuation: No comma before sooner than. Comma after a fronted Sooner than phrase. Variants: would sooner … than, 'd sooner … than

The emphatic sibling of rather than: it states a preference the speaker treats as obvious, usually a refusal dressed up as a choice. It also has a literal time reading (She got here sooner than we expected), and the bare infinitive after it is the signal that you are in the preference sense rather than the time sense.

  1. I'd quit sooner than work another holiday weekend.
  2. He'd sell the house sooner than ask his brother for money.
  3. Sooner than sit through that meeting again, I'll take the early flight.
  4. The package arrived sooner than the tracking page promised.
  5. She'd walk home in the rain sooner than call a cab.
  6. They finished sooner than anyone thought possible.

than#

Pronunciation: /ðæn/, weak /ðən/, /ðn̩/ · THAN, thuhn Type: subordinating (comparative); prepositional in the than me usage · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison, attached to a comparative word earlier in the sentence Pattern: [clause + comparative adj/adv] than [clause]. · [clause + more / less / fewer + noun] than [clause]. · [clause + comparative] than [noun phrase] (elliptical: than me, than last year's). · [clause + other / rather / sooner / else / different] than [phrase]. Punctuation: No comma before than in a comparison. A comma appears only when the than phrase is a deliberate afterthought set off from the rest of the sentence. Variants: than what (nonstandard, as in "bigger than what I expected"), than whom (formal; see its own entry)

The workhorse of unequal comparison. It cannot start a comparison by itself — something earlier must license it, either a comparative form (taller, more slowly, fewer) or one of a short list of trigger words (other, rather, sooner, else, different). The clause after it is almost always elliptical, which is where the case argument lives: taller than I (am) is the conservative written form and taller than me the ordinary spoken one, and both are defensible in American English. One last thing it is not: then. Than compares; then tells time or draws a consequence. They sound alike in unstressed speech — both reduce to /ðən/ — and that is exactly why the misspelling is the single commonest error in the category. Test it by stressing the word: if the sentence still means "at that time," you want then.

  1. She finished the course faster than anyone expected.
  2. This apartment costs more than our last one did.
  3. There were fewer people at the hearing than the organizers had hoped.
  4. He knows the neighborhood better than I do.
  5. I'd rather drive than sit in an airport for six hours.
  6. The recipe calls for something other than butter.
  7. That's a bigger crowd than last year's.

See also: than whom, rather than, as … as, the same as, but

than whom#

Pronunciation: /ðæn ˈhum/ · than HOOM Type: subordinating conjunction + relative pronoun · Register: formal, literary Clause type: nonrestrictive relative clause built on a comparative Pattern: [noun phrase], than whom [no one / few / nobody] [verb phrase], [rest of main clause]. · [noun phrase], than which [nothing / few / no + noun] [verb phrase], [rest of main clause]. · [noun phrase], than whom [subject] [verb] [no one / nothing] more, [rest of main clause]. Punctuation: Commas on both sides, always — the clause is supplementary, never restrictive. This is the one place in the than family where a comma stands directly before than. Variants: than which (for things)

A fossilized formal construction, and the one place in modern English where than is unmistakably followed by the objective case — the historical argument for calling than a preposition. It reads as deliberately elevated and nearly always pairs with a negative that gives it superlative force (than whom no one, than which nothing).

  1. Dr. Reyes, than whom no one is better qualified, will chair the panel.
  2. She studied under Kowalski, than whom the department has known no stricter grader.
  3. The Chrysler Building, than which few American towers have aged better, still stops traffic.
  4. He named his old editor, than whom he trusted no one more, as his literary executor.
  5. The award went to Ellis, than whom the league has produced no steadier defender.

Comparison of equality: the as … as family#

Equatives come in two halves that straddle the compared word: the first half is a degree word sitting inside the main clause — the adverb as or so, or the determiner the same — and only the second half is the actual subordinator. Nothing inside the frame takes a comma. The one thing that attracts one is fronting — move a whole as clause to the head of the sentence and it takes a comma after it: As much as I'd like to help, I'm booked all week.

as … as#

Pronunciation: /æz … əz/ — the first as carries the stress and stays full, the second reduces to /əz/ · AZ … uhz Type: correlative (equative comparison) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison Pattern: [clause] as [adj/adv] as [clause/phrase]. · [clause] not as [adj/adv] as [clause/phrase]. · as [adj] a(n) [noun] as [phrase]. · [clause] as much / as many [noun] as [clause]. · [clause] [twice / half / three times] as [adj/adv] as [phrase]. Punctuation: No commas anywhere inside the frame, in any of those orders. Variants: not as … as (ordinary negative), not so … as (formal, older), just as … as (emphatic)

States that two things match on some scale. The trap is word order in the noun pattern: when a singular count noun is compared, the article jumps to the far side of the adjective — as fine a meal as I've had, not as a fine meal as I've had. No other English frame moves the article that way.

  1. The drive is as long as the flight once you count security.
  2. She's as good a manager as the company has ever had.
  3. Bring as many chairs as you can fit in the car.
  4. It's not as cold today as it was yesterday.
  5. He explained it as clearly as anyone could have.
  6. The copy reads as well as the original did.

See also: so … as, as much as, as many as, as well as, the same as, than

as … so#

Pronunciation: /æz … ˈsoʊ/ · az … SOH Type: correlative · Register: formal, literary Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison with a resumptive main clause Pattern: As [clause], so [clause]. · Just as [clause], so (too) [clause]. Punctuation: Comma at the seam between the two halves. So opens the second half and often triggers inversion (so grows the tree, so too should writers rest). Variants: just as … so, just as … so too, as … so also

Sets up an analogy: as the first proposition goes, so goes the second. This is the elevated, written-out version of the comparison that ordinary speech makes with just like, and it belongs in sermons, editorials, and formal argument rather than conversation.

  1. As the twig is bent, so grows the tree.
  2. Just as the coasts warmed early, so the interior followed a decade later.
  3. As the harvest failed, so the price of bread climbed.
  4. Just as athletes taper before a race, so too should writers rest before a deadline.
  5. As a city treats its parks, so it treats its people.

as far as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈfɑr əz/, weak /əz ˈfɑr əz/ · az FAR uhz Type: subordinating (compound) with a clause; prepositional with a noun phrase in the distance sense · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison and extent; also literal distance Pattern: As far as [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] as far as [clause]. · [main clause] as far as [noun phrase] (literal distance; prepositional). · As far as [noun phrase] is concerned / goes, [main clause]. · [main clause] as far as [noun phrase] is concerned / goes. Punctuation: Comma after the clause whenever it is fronted (As far as I know, …). No comma when it follows the main clause — in the extent sense (He'll help as far as he's able) and in the literal-distance sense (The trail runs as far as the old fire tower) alike. Variants: so far as (formal), as far as … is concerned, as far as I'm concerned, as far as … goes

Limits a claim to the reach of somebody's knowledge, authority, or physical distance. In edited American prose it should keep its verb: write as far as the budget is concerned, not the clipped as far as the budget, which is common in speech but still corrected in print. Its formal twin is insofar as.

  1. As far as I know, the office closes at four on Fridays.
  2. The trail runs as far as the old fire tower.
  3. As far as the budget is concerned, nothing has changed.
  4. He'll help as far as he's able.
  5. As far as anyone could tell, the machine was working fine.
  6. You can see as far as the state line from up here.

See also: insofar as, as … as, so … as, according as

as lief … as#

Pronunciation: /əz ˈlif … əz/ · az LEEF … uhz Type: correlative (equative of preference) · Register: archaic, literary Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison expressing preference Pattern: [subject] had as lief [bare infinitive] as [bare infinitive]. · [subject] would as lief [bare infinitive] as [bare infinitive]. · [subject] 'd as lief [bare infinitive] as not. · As lief [bare infinitive] as [bare infinitive] — [main clause]. Punctuation: No commas inside the frame. Variants: had as lief, would as lief, 'd as lief, as lief … as not, as leave (a respelling from after the word went obsolete)

The Elizabethan ancestor of would just as soon. Lief is a dead adverb meaning "willingly" (the root survives in German lieb), and the frame claims equal willingness to do either thing — which in practice is a courteous refusal of the first. Note the verb the old idiom takes: had as lief, not would as lief, is what the printed texts have, and anything written this century should say I'd just as soon.

  1. I had as lief walk as wait an hour for the next bus.
  2. He'd as lief pay the fine as argue with a clerk about it.
  3. "I had as lief not be as live to be in awe of such a thing as I myself." — Julius Caesar
  4. She would as lief have stayed home, and said so twice.
  5. They'd as lief see the building torn down as watch it rot another decade.
  6. I'd as lief not, if it's all the same to you.

as many as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈmɛni əz/, weak /əz ˈmɛni əz/ · az MEN-ee uhz Type: correlative (equative, count) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison Pattern: [clause] as many [plural noun] as [clause/phrase]. · [clause] as many of [noun phrase] as [clause]. · [clause] [twice / half / three times] as many [plural noun] as [phrase]. · As many as [number] [verb phrase]. · [clause] as many as [number]. Punctuation: No commas inside the frame. Variants: not as many as, half as many as, twice as many as

The countable half of the equative pair: as many as takes plural count nouns and as much as takes mass nouns, and mixing them (as much chairs) is one of the most visible learner errors in English. Standing alone before a number it means "up to — and that is a surprising number."

  1. Take as many forms as you need.
  2. She's read as many of his books as I have.
  3. As many as two hundred people were turned away at the door.
  4. We didn't sell as many tickets as we did last spring.
  5. Bring as many helpers as will fit in the van.
  6. There were as many questions as there were answers.

as much as#

Pronunciation: /æz ˈmʌtʃ əz/, weak /əz ˈmʌtʃ əz/ · az MUCH uhz Type: correlative (equative, degree) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of comparison; also an adverbial clause of concession Pattern: [clause] as much as [clause/phrase]. · [clause] as much [mass noun] as [clause]. · [clause] [twice / half] as much [mass noun] as [phrase]. · As much as [clause], [main clause] (concessive). · [clause] as much as [quantity]. Punctuation: No comma in the degree sense. In the concessive sense the clause comes first and takes a comma after it: As much as I'd like to, I can't. Variants: as much … as, not as much as, twice as much as

Measures degree, or quantity of a mass noun. It leads a second life at the front of a sentence, where As much as I like her means "although I like her very much" — identical words, completely different job, and only position and the comma tell you which one you are reading. The formal equivalent of that concessive use is much as.

  1. He worries about the deadline as much as you do.
  2. Pour in as much stock as the pan will hold.
  3. As much as I'd like to help, I'm booked all week.
  4. The repair cost as much as the car was worth.
  5. She travels as much as her job allows.
  6. As much as it hurt, quitting was the right call.

insofar as#

Restricts a statement to the degree that something holds — the legal-and-academic register of as far as. Manner and resemblance#

These say how, not how much. The whole like-versus-as argument lives in this section, and the safe way through it is the plain, unobjectionable the way.

analogously#

Pronunciation: /əˈnælədʒəsli/ · uh-NAL-uh-jus-lee Type: conjunctive adverb of resemblance; prepositional in the heavier analogously to · Register: formal; academic, mathematical, scientific, and legal Clause type: sentence adverbial introducing a parallel case that runs the same way as the one just described Pattern: [Independent clause]; analogously, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Analogously, [independent clause]. · [main clause], and analogously [clause]. · [subject], analogously, [verb phrase]. · [main clause] analogously to [noun phrase] (prepositional, heavy). Punctuation: Comma after it when it opens a sentence or a clause, and commas on both sides when it sits inside one. Between two independent clauses it is an adverb, so it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice: ✗ The odd case runs by induction, analogously the even case does too. ✓ The odd case runs by induction; analogously, the even case does too. No comma in the prepositional analogously to. Variants: similarly, likewise, by analogy, in an analogous way, correspondingly

The technical register's similarly: it claims that the second case is built the same way as the first, point for point, rather than merely resembling it. That is what separates it from its everyday neighbors — similarly asserts a likeness and likewise only adds a second item, while analogously says the structure carries over, which is why it belongs to proofs, protocols, and doctrinal argument. It sounds pretentious the moment all you mean is "also."

  1. The proof for the odd case runs by induction; analogously, the even case follows from the same lemma.
  2. Sodium reacts violently with water. Analogously, potassium does, only faster.
  3. Federal courts read the exemption narrowly; analogously, most state courts have read their own versions the same way.
  4. Wages track productivity in manufacturing, and analogously in freight.
  5. A vaccine trains the immune system before there is an infection. Analogously, a fire drill trains a building's occupants before there is smoke.
  6. The second experiment was designed analogously to the first, with temperature held constant instead of pressure.

Pronunciation: /laɪk/ · LYKE Type: subordinating (informal) before a clause; preposition before a noun phrase; and outside the conjunction system a verb (I like it), a noun (the likes of), a suffix (businesslike), and — in speech only — a quotative (she was like, no) and a filler · Register: informal as a conjunction; neutral as a preposition Clause type: adverbial clause of manner and comparison Pattern: [main clause] like [noun phrase] (prepositional; standard everywhere). · [main clause] like [clause] (manner, = as; informal). · [look/sound/seem/feel] like [clause] (= as if; informal). · Like [clause], [main clause] (fronted comment: Like I said, …). · Like [noun phrase], [main clause]. Like before a noun phrase — She sings like her father — has never been controversial. Like before a full clause with its own verb — She sings like her father did, It looks like it's going to rain — is what draws objections, and it is one of the longest-running arguments in American usage.

On the like / as question, the facts are these. Clause-introducing like is universal in speech, standard in journalism and fiction, and still corrected in academic and formal business writing. When you need it to pass anywhere, substitute as for the manner sense (as her father did) and as if or as though for the appearance sense (as if it's going to rain). The substitution does not run the other way: with a bare noun phrase, as changes the meaning, because She works like her father compares her to him while She works as her father would mean in the role of her father.

  5. The engine sounds like something's loose.
  6. Like I said, the store opens at ten.

The King James Bible's way of opening a simile: Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. mutatis mutandis#

Pronunciation: /mjuˌteɪtɪs mjuˈtændɪs/, also /muˌtɑtɪs muˈtɑndɪs/ · myoo-TAY-tis myoo-TAN-dis, also moo-TAH-tis moo-TAHN-dis Type: adverbial phrase (Latin) of qualified analogy — a manner adverbial in the predicate, a sentence adverbial when set off; never a clause-taking conjunction · Register: formal; Latin — contract, treaty, and statutory boilerplate, and academic argument Clause type: adverbial phrase of manner and comparison, carrying an earlier rule or argument over to a new case Pattern: [provision] shall apply mutatis mutandis to [noun phrase]. · [main clause], mutatis mutandis. · Mutatis mutandis, [main clause]. · [main clause], mutatis mutandis, [continuation]. · [main clause] mutatis mutandis (bare, trailing). Variants: with the necessary changes, with the appropriate modifications, with suitable changes made, mutatis mutandis (invariable)

Latin for "with those things changed that need to be changed": it imports a rule or an argument whole into a second case while conceding that the names, dates, and parties will differ. An American meets it in contracts, treaties, and statutes, where it saves a drafter from setting out the same clause twice, and in academic writing, where it saves an argument from being rehearsed twice — it is boilerplate rather than prose, and outside those settings with the necessary changes says the same thing to a reader who has not studied law. Its near neighbor a fortiori moves an argument to a stronger case; this one moves it sideways to a parallel one.

  1. The provisions of Article 12 shall apply mutatis mutandis to any renewal term.

  3. What the court said about the county's ordinance holds, mutatis mutandis, for the city's.
  4. Mutatis mutandis, the same objection can be raised against the second version of the argument.
  5. The parties agree that the arbitration rules shall govern any appeal mutatis mutandis.
  6. His account of the nineteenth-century railroads applies mutatis mutandis to broadband today.

Proportion and correspondence#

Five entries that say the halves of a sentence vary together, or that pair their members off one for one. They are punctuated differently, so do not generalize from one to the other. The more … the more joins two full clauses of equal weight and takes a comma at the seam. According as and in proportion as are ordinary subordinators: comma after the clause when it is fronted, no comma when it trails. The other two are not conjunctions at all but adverbs, and that changes everything about the commas — correspondingly standing between two independent clauses needs a semicolon or a period in front of it, exactly like however, and respectively sits at the end of its own clause behind a single comma and joins nothing to anything.

according as#

Pronunciation: /əˈkɔrdɪŋ əz/ · uh-KOR-ding uhz Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: formal, dated Clause type: adverbial clause of proportion and correspondence Pattern: [main clause] according as [clause]. · According as [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] according as [clause] or [clause] (the paired-outcome form). · [main clause] according to whether [clause] (the modern paraphrase). · [main clause] according to [noun phrase] (prepositional — a different word class). Punctuation: Comma after the fronted clause; no comma when it follows the main clause. No comma before the internal or that pairs the two outcomes. Variants: according to whether (ordinary modern paraphrase), according to which

Means "in proportion to whether" or "depending on which of these turns out to be true" — the outcome varies with the condition. It survives in legal, mathematical, and older literary prose. Note that it is a conjunction taking a clause, unlike the everyday preposition according to, which takes a noun phrase; everyday American English says depending on whether instead.

  1. The rate rises or falls according as the index moves.
  2. According as the weather allows, the ceremony will be held outdoors or in the hall.
  3. Each parcel is taxed according as it is classified residential or commercial.
  4. The sign of the result changes according as n is even or odd.
  5. They will be judged according as they have acted.

See also: according to, insofar as, as far as, in proportion as, the more … the more

correspondingly#

Pronunciation: /ˌkɔrəˈspɑndɪŋli/, also /ˌkɑrəˈspɑndɪŋli/ · kor-uh-SPON-ding-lee Type: conjunctive adverb of correspondence; also an ordinary degree adverb inside the clause (a correspondingly larger bill) · Register: neutral to formal; reporting, policy, and technical prose Clause type: sentence adverbial saying its clause moves in step with the one before it; degree modifier in the adjectival use Pattern: [Independent clause]; correspondingly, [independent clause]. · [Independent clause]. Correspondingly, [independent clause]. · [main clause], and [subject] [verb phrase] correspondingly. · [subject], correspondingly, [verb phrase]. · [main clause] a correspondingly [comparative adjective] [noun] (degree use). Punctuation: Comma after it when it opens a clause; commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb. Between two independent clauses it needs a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a splice: ✗ The levy rose, correspondingly receipts fell. ✓ The levy rose; correspondingly, receipts fell. No comma at all in the degree use, where it simply modifies an adjective: a correspondingly smaller refund. Variants: proportionately, in proportion, to a corresponding degree, equally, by the same token

The adverb of matched movement: whatever the first clause reports going up or down, the second clause is said to follow it. That makes it narrower than similarly, which claims only a resemblance — correspondingly claims a linkage between two quantities, and it rings false when nothing is actually varying. Its clause-taking equivalents are in proportion as and the more … the more, which say the same thing with a subordinator instead of an adverb, and a comma instead of a semicolon.

  1. The district enrolled two hundred more students; correspondingly, it hired six more teachers.
  2. Freight volumes fell all spring. Correspondingly, rail revenue came in under forecast.
  3. Premiums went up nine percent, and coverage narrowed correspondingly.
  4. As the deductible rises, the monthly payment drops correspondingly.
  5. The new building is twice the size of the old one and carries a correspondingly larger tax bill.
  6. Testing slowed over the holiday; correspondingly, the reported case counts dipped and then caught up.

in proportion as#

Pronunciation: /ɪn prəˈpɔrʃən əz/ · in pruh-POR-shun uhz Type: subordinating (compound); the shortened in proportion to is a preposition taking a noun phrase · Register: formal, dated Clause type: adverbial clause of proportion and correspondence Pattern: [main clause] in proportion as [clause]. · In proportion as [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] in proportion to [noun phrase] (prepositional — a different word class). · In proportion as [clause], so [clause] (with a resumptive so opening the second half). Says that two quantities move together — as one rises or falls, so does the other. It is the clause-taking twin of the everyday preposition in proportion to, and the difference is only what follows: the conjunction takes a full clause (in proportion as the debt grows), the preposition takes a noun phrase (in proportion to the debt). In American English the conjunction is confined to formal argument, older prose, mathematics, and physical law; ordinary writing reaches for the more … the more or as … so, which say the same thing and sound like this century.

  1. Wages rose in proportion as the cost of housing climbed.
  2. In proportion as the light fades, the colors go flat.
  3. A tax is fair in proportion as it tracks the ability to pay.
  4. The pressure increases in proportion as the volume shrinks.
  5. In proportion as the town grew, the old rivalries lost their heat.
  6. He grew more careful in proportion as the stakes rose.
  7. In proportion as the population aged, so the tax base narrowed.

  3. Gold, silver, and bronze went to Norway, Japan, and Canada, respectively.

  5. Her first and second novels sold nine hundred and ninety thousand copies, respectively.

Pronunciation: /ðə ˈmɔr … ðə ˈmɔr/ · thuh MOR … thuh MOR Type: correlative (proportional comparative) · Register: neutral Clause type: paired comparative clauses — the first is subordinate, the second is the main clause Pattern: The [comparative], the [comparative]. · The more [clause], the more [clause]. · The [comparative] [noun], the [comparative] [noun] (verbless). Says that two quantities rise or fall together.   1. The more he explained, the less I understood.
  2. The sooner you file, the sooner you get the refund.
  3. The colder it gets, the more the pipes rattle.
  4. The harder she practiced, the better she played.
  5. The bigger the crowd, the longer the wait.
  6. The sooner, the better.

Phrasal comparison markers#

Reduced, participial, and prepositional phrases that name the baseline of a comparison, plus the two clause-taking contrast subordinators — whereas and while — that punctuate the same way. The contrast markers among them comment on the main clause instead of completing it, which is why they are normally set off with commas: always after a fronted one, and normally before a trailing one. The loose member of that set is the everyday compared to, which drops the comma when a short phrase trails. The conformity markers — according to, consistent with, in conformity with, in keeping with, in line with, relative to, when it comes to — split the difference: fronted they take a comma like any other long adverbial, but trailing they belong to the predicate and take none (Seats were assigned according to rank).

Watch the boundary with the conjunctive adverbs, because it is where the commas in this section go wrong. Compared to, in comparison with, and in contrast to keep their to or with, take a phrase, and are set off with commas. Strip the to and the same words become adverbs — by comparison, in contrast, on the other hand — and an adverb standing between two independent clauses needs a semicolon or a period in front of it: Rural counties shrank; in contrast, the suburbs grew, never ✗ Rural counties shrank, in contrast the suburbs grew.

according to#

Pronunciation: /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tu/, weak /əˈkɔrdɪŋ tə/ · uh-KOR-ding too Type: preposition (complex) — it takes a noun phrase or a nominal wh- clause, never a finite clause; the clause-taking form is the conjunction according as · Register: neutral Clause type: prepositional phrase of source, standard, or correspondence; supplementary when fronted Pattern: According to [noun phrase], [main clause] (attribution). · [main clause], according to [noun phrase] (attribution, trailing). · [main clause] according to [noun phrase] (standard or correspondence). · [main clause] according to whether [clause]. · [main clause] according to [wh-clause]. Punctuation: Comma after the fronted phrase, always. In the attribution sense a trailing phrase takes a comma before it (The flight is on time, according to the airline); in the correspondence sense a trailing phrase is integral to the predicate and takes none (Seats were assigned according to rank). Variants: according as (the conjunction), according to whether, in accordance with (formal, conformity only), per (business shorthand)

Two jobs share one phrase. In the attribution sense it names whose account you are relaying (according to the police), and it carries a faint note of distance — you are reporting the claim, not vouching for it, which is why According to me sounds wrong and in my opinion is the phrase for your own view. In the correspondence sense it names the standard something varies with (paid according to experience), and that is the sense whose clause-taking twin is according as.

  1. According to the airline, the delay is weather.
  2. Seats were assigned according to seniority.
  3. The bill will be scored according to the committee's own formula.
  4. According to the label, the paint needs two hours between coats.
  5. Attendance rose and fell according to the weather.
  6. The fee changes according to whether you file online or by mail.
  7. It rained all weekend in the mountains, according to my sister.

The statistician's contrast: it sets one measured value beside another and lets the gap speak for itself.   1. Turnout was thirty-one percent, as against nineteen in the last off-year election.

  5. Revenue grew four percent, as against an industry average of one.
  6. As against what the district spends per student, the neighboring county looks generous.

  1. The dollar has weakened relative to the euro.
  2. Relative to its population, the state sends an outsized delegation to Washington.
  3. Housing costs are high here relative to wages.
  4. The fund underperformed relative to its benchmark for a third straight quarter.
  5. Relative to last quarter, complaints are down by a third.
  6. Measure the shadow relative to the height of the pole.

unlike#

Pronunciation: /ˌʌnˈlaɪk/ · un-LYKE Type: preposition of contrast — the negative of prepositional like; also an adjective in It's unlike her to be late · Register: neutral Clause type: supplementary comparative phrase; it has no clause-taking form at all Pattern: Unlike [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [main clause], unlike [noun phrase]. · [X], unlike [Y], [verb phrase]. · It is unlike [person] to [verb phrase] (adjective). Punctuation: Comma after the fronted phrase, always. Comma before it when it trails as a supplement. Commas on both sides when it interrupts a subject and its verb: My sister, unlike me, reads the manual. Variants: not like (informal), in contrast to, as opposed to, unlike with (loose, in the "in the case of" sense)

The negative counterpart of prepositional like, and the one member of this family with no conjunction use whatsoever — there is no unlike he does, only unlike him. Because it is a bare phrase, it dangles easily: Unlike most cities, the streets here are numbered compares cities to streets. Whatever follows unlike has to be the same kind of thing as the subject of the main clause. It carries a second, idiomatic "out of character" sense (It's unlike her to be late), where it is an adjective rather than a preposition.

  1. Unlike her brother, she never learned to drive.
  2. The new lease, unlike the old one, lets us sublet.
  3. Unlike most of the coast, this stretch has no public access.
  4. He took the criticism well, unlike the last person in the job.
  5. It's unlike Marco to leave without saying goodbye.
  6. Unlike the earlier models, this one runs quietly enough to sit next to.

vis-à-vis#

Pronunciation: /ˌviz ə ˈvi/ · veez-uh-VEE Type: preposition (from French) — "in relation to," "as against"; it takes a noun phrase, never a clause, and it is also a noun meaning "counterpart" · Register: formal; diplomatic, business, and academic Clause type: adverbial phrase of relation and comparison Pattern: [main clause] vis-à-vis [noun phrase]. · [X]'s position vis-à-vis [Y]. · Vis-à-vis [noun phrase], [main clause]. · [X] is [comparative] vis-à-vis [Y]. Punctuation: Comma after the fronted phrase; no comma when it trails. Keep both hyphens, and keep the grave accent — vis-à-vis is the dictionary spelling in American English, and the bare vis-a-vis is a common second best. Variants: vis-a-vis (unaccented), in relation to, with respect to, as against, face to face with (the literal French sense)

Literally "face to face," and the surviving sense keeps that picture: two parties lined up opposite each other, with one measured against the other. Analysts and diplomats use it for standing and leverage — the union's position vis-à-vis management — where compared to would sound merely arithmetical. In loose American usage it has drifted toward a vague "regarding," and that drift draws complaints; keep it where two parties genuinely face each other.

  1. The bank's position vis-à-vis its European competitors has improved.
  2. Vis-à-vis the state, a city has only the powers the legislature grants it.
  3. She wanted to know where the department stood vis-à-vis the new funding rules.
  4. The treaty leaves the smaller countries weaker vis-à-vis their neighbors.
  5. Our costs are unremarkable vis-à-vis the rest of the industry.
  6. He was hired to handle the company's dealings vis-à-vis the regulators.

when it comes to#

Pronunciation: /wɛn ɪt ˈkʌmz tu/, also /hwɛn/ · wen it KUMZ too Type: fixed topic-limiting phrase — prepositional in effect, taking a noun phrase or an -ing form; the clause is frozen, and when it came to is the only variation it allows · Register: neutral to informal Clause type: adverbial clause of respect, narrowing the main clause to one topic Pattern: When it comes to [noun phrase], [main clause]. · When it comes to [-ing], [main clause]. · [main clause] when it comes to [noun phrase]. · When it came to [noun phrase], [main clause] (past narrative). Punctuation: Comma after the fronted phrase, always. No comma when it trails the main clause. Variants: when it came to, as far as … is concerned, where … is concerned, as for, in the matter of (formal)

Narrows the sentence to a single subject before the claim arrives — "on the topic of." It reads as speech written down: useful in a column or an email, and usually deletable in formal prose, where As for or a plain preposition does the same work in fewer words. Note that it is not a time clause at all, despite the when: nothing in it points to a moment.

  1. When it comes to paperwork, she is the most patient person I know.
  2. When it comes to raising rates, the board has been cautious.
  3. He's fearless on the water and useless when it comes to spiders.
  4. When it came to naming a successor, the founder stalled for two years.
  5. When it comes to barbecue, everyone in this town has a theory and a cousin.
  6. The company is generous with equipment and stingy when it comes to time off.

whereas#

Pronunciation: /wɛrˈæz/, /hwɛrˈæz/ · wair-AZ Type: subordinating · Register: formal to neutral; the "given that" sense is legal Clause type: adverbial clause of contrast, comparing two parallel facts Pattern: [main clause], whereas [clause]. · Whereas [clause], [main clause]. · [X] [verb phrase], whereas [Y] [verb phrase] (the balanced pair). · Whereas [clause]; whereas [clause]; now, therefore, [main clause] (legal preamble). Punctuation: Comma before whereas even when it follows the main clause — unlike most subordinators it is always supplementary, never integral. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. In legal preambles, where each Whereas clause is a separate recital, the recitals are closed with semicolons rather than commas. Note that whereas is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb: the comma before it is correct and no semicolon is needed. Write however the other way — We were late; however, they held our table — because that one is an adverb, not a conjunction. Variants: where as (misspelling), whereas … whereas (stacked, in legal preambles)

Sets two facts side by side to point out that they differ, so the contrast is the entire content of the comparison. Compare its neighbors: although concedes, meaning the main clause wins the argument, while whereas balances, asserting both halves equally; and while is ambiguous between "at the same time" and "by contrast," where whereas can only mean the contrast. Note that whilst is British — it is a synonym of while, not of whereas, and it has no place in American prose.

  1. My brother loves horror movies, whereas I can't get through the trailer.
  2. The northern route is faster, whereas the coastal road is prettier.
  3. Whereas the first draft ran long, the second barely filled ten pages.
  4. She was trained as a chemist, whereas her partner came up through sales.
  5. Enrollment rose in the suburbs, whereas the city schools lost students.
  6. Whereas the parties wish to settle all outstanding claims, the following terms are agreed.

See also: while, much as, compared to, in contrast to, in comparison with, but

while#

Pronunciation: /waɪl/, also /hwaɪl/ · WYLE Type: subordinating — contrast here, but the same word also subordinates time and concession, and outside the conjunction system it is a noun (stay a while) and a verb (while away an afternoon) · Register: neutral; a step below whereas in formality and far commoner in speech Clause type: adverbial clause of contrast, setting two parallel facts side by side Pattern: [main clause], while [clause]. · While [clause], [main clause]. · [X] [verb phrase], while [Y] [verb phrase] (the balanced pair). · [subject], while [reduced phrase], [predicate] (concessive reduction: The plan, while ambitious, is affordable — see while (concession)). Punctuation: Comma before a trailing contrastive while clause and after a fronted one. That comma is doing real work, because a trailing temporal while clause takes no comma: He waited while she made the call means "during," and He waited, while she made the call means "she, by contrast, made the call." Do not reach for a semicolon here — while is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb, so a comma is the correct mark; it is simply not optional in the contrast sense. Variants: whereas (the formal equivalent, contrast only), whilst (British — dated or affected in American prose), where (contrastive, more clipped)

Three jobs, one word: time ("during"), concession ("although"), and contrast ("whereas"). Only context and that comma sort them out, which is why careful American writers switch to whereas whenever a sentence could be read two ways. In the contrast sense while balances two facts and leaves both standing, exactly as whereas does, but at a conversational level. Note that the British whilst is a synonym of while in all three senses, not of whereas, and it has no place in American prose; the same flag applies to amongst and amidst.

  1. The morning session ran long, while the afternoon finished early.
  2. While the coasts get the coverage, the growth is happening inland.
  3. Her first album was all guitars, while this one is mostly synths.
  4. While federal law caps the fee, the state sets no limit at all.
  5. Deliveries are up, while the number of drivers has barely moved.
  6. He handles the money, while she does everything else.
