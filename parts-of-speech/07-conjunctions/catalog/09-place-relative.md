# Subordinators of place, and relative connectors

> The words that pin a clause to a location, and the *wh-* words that hook a whole clause onto a noun already standing in the sentence.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Two families share one shelf here. The first is small and genuinely adverbial: *where, wherever, everywhere, anywhere* and their archaic cousins *whence* and *whither* build clauses that answer **where?** about the main verb. The second is the relative system — *who, whom, whose, which, that, when, where, why* — which does not modify the verb at all, but reaches back and fastens a clause onto a noun. The two families overlap because *where* does both jobs, and they share a shape because every word in this file descends from the same Old English *hw-* stem.

One rule runs through the entire category, and it is a comma rule. A clause that **narrows** which thing you mean is **restrictive** and takes **no commas**: *the house where I grew up*. A clause that merely **adds** information about something already identified is **nonrestrictive** and is **fenced off by commas on both sides**: *our old house, where I grew up, is finally for sale*. That one distinction decides three separate things at once — whether you write commas, whether you may use *that*, and whether you may drop the relative word entirely. Restrictive clauses allow *that* and allow the zero relative (*the book I read*); nonrestrictive clauses allow neither and require a *wh-* word. In edited American prose the nonrestrictive relative for things is *which*, never *that*.

Word order is fixed and worth stating once: every word in this file goes at the **front of its own clause**, no matter what job the word does inside that clause. *The people with whom he shared the house* moves the preposition to the front along with it; *the people he shared the house with* leaves the preposition stranded at the end. Both are correct American English, and stranding is the normal spoken option.

## Subordinators of place

These build adverbial clauses that answer *where?* about the main verb. Most of them can be fronted for emphasis, and every one that can follows the same comma rule: **comma after a fronted place clause, no comma when the place clause trails the main clause.**

### anyplace

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌpleɪs/ &middot; *EN-ee-playss*
**Русский:** где угодно; куда угодно — разг., амер. вариант anywhere; редактор правит на anywhere
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*we didn't go anyplace*) and a pronoun (*anyplace is fine*) &middot; **Register:** informal, chiefly American — ordinary in speech and casual writing, changed to *anywhere* by copy editors
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (free choice)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *anyplace* [that] [clause]. &middot; *Anyplace* [that] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [negated main clause] *anyplace* [clause] — the negative-polarity use. &middot; [main clause] *anyplace* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *anyplace the dog can't get to*.
**Punctuation:** Exactly the *anywhere* rule: no comma when the clause follows the main clause, comma after it when the clause is fronted, and never a comma on either side of the optional *that*.
**Variants:** *anywhere* (the standard equivalent, available in every position *anyplace* occupies); *any place* as two words is the literal noun phrase, not this pro-form

The informal American twin of *anywhere*, built on *place* instead of the *-where* stem. It is a real Americanism — British English does not have it — and it is unremarkable in conversation and in fiction dialogue, but it is a **register** choice and not a meaning choice, so edited prose puts *anywhere* back. Note the spelling: closed up for the pro-form, two words for the ordinary noun (*any place you can name*).

1. Sit *anyplace* you want — nobody saved seats.
2. We couldn't find *anyplace* that was still open past nine.
3. *Anyplace* the road narrows to one lane, expect a wait.
4. He'd have followed that team *anyplace* they played.
5. You can't smoke *anyplace* there's a kid within fifty feet.
6. She'll eat *anyplace* they keep the pie under glass.

**See also:** [anywhere](#anywhere), [someplace](#someplace), [everyplace](#everyplace), [no place](#no-place), [anywheres](#anywheres)

### anywhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌwɛr/ &middot; *EN-ee-wair*
**Русский:** где угодно; куда угодно; при отрицании — нигде, никуда — придаточное места
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*I looked, but I couldn't find it anywhere*) and a pronoun (*anywhere is fine with me*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (free choice)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *anywhere* [that] [clause]. &middot; *Anywhere* [that] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [negated main clause] *anywhere* [clause] — the negative-polarity use, as in *you can't park anywhere the curb is red*. &middot; [main clause] *anywhere* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *anywhere the dog can't get to*.
**Punctuation:** No comma when the *anywhere* clause follows the main clause; comma after it when it is fronted. The optional *that* never takes a comma before or after it.
**Variants:** *anyplace* (informal, chiefly American)

Marks every location as equally acceptable — the choice is open and no place is excluded. Against its neighbors: *everywhere* sweeps across all locations at once, *wherever* leaves open which single one turns out to apply, and *anywhere* says the pick simply does not matter.

1. Sit *anywhere* you like — the seats aren't assigned.
2. You can't park *anywhere* the curb is painted red.
3. *Anywhere* the trail crosses water, expect mud for the next half mile.
4. She would have followed that band *anywhere* they toured.
5. We couldn't find a motel *anywhere that* wasn't already booked.
6. He'll take a job *anywhere* he can bike to.
7. He's happy *anyplace* there's a decent taco truck.

**See also:** [everywhere](#everywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere), [somewhere](#somewhere), [wherever](#wherever), [where](#where)

### anywheres

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌwɛrz/ &middot; *EN-ee-wairz*
**Русский:** где угодно — диал., прост.: нестандартная форма anywhere с -s
**Type:** subordinating; more often a plain place adverb &middot; **Register:** dialectal and nonstandard — regional American speech and the dialect writing that records it; never correct in edited prose
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (free choice); relative clause hosted by a place pro-form
**Pattern:** [negated main clause] *anywheres* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *anywheres* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *anywheres* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded. &middot; *not … anywheres near* [noun] — the frame in which it is heard most often.
**Punctuation:** The *anywhere* rule unchanged: no comma when the clause trails, comma after a fronted clause, no comma around the optional *that*. The *-s* affects nothing but the register.
**Variants:** *anywhere* (the standard form); *somewheres*, [*nowheres*](#nowheres), [*everywheres*](#everywheres) (the same *-s* on the rest of the set)

The *-s* is the old adverbial genitive that standard English froze in *always*, *besides*, *nowadays*, and *unawares* and then stopped adding; regional American speech kept adding it. So *anywheres* is a survival rather than a blunder — but it is firmly nonstandard now, and an American meets it in Twain, in dialogue by Faulkner and Caldwell, and in rural speech from northern New England through the Ozarks, essentially nowhere else in print.

1. There wasn't *anywheres* we could tie up the raft before dark.
2. He never went *anywheres* his mother didn't know about.
3. You won't cut hay like that *anywheres* the ground stays this wet.
4. I don't reckon there's *anywheres* around here a body can get a hot meal.
5. She won't shop *anywheres* they don't take a check.
6. It ain't *anywheres* near as far as the map makes it look.

**See also:** [anywhere](#anywhere), [nowheres](#nowheres), [everywheres](#everywheres), [anyplace](#anyplace)

### everyplace

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriˌpleɪs/ &middot; *EV-ree-playss*
**Русский:** везде, где; повсюду — разг., амер. вариант everywhere
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*we looked everyplace*) &middot; **Register:** informal, chiefly American; the least-used member of the *-place* set and the one an editor is quickest to change
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (universal)
**Pattern:** *Everyplace* [that] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everyplace* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everyplace* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded.
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *everyplace* clause, none when it trails, and no comma on either side of the optional *that* — the *everywhere* rule exactly.
**Variants:** *everywhere* (the standard equivalent, always available); *every place* as two words is the ordinary noun phrase, not this pro-form

The informal American counterpart of *everywhere*. It is the weakest member of its family: *anyplace* and *someplace* are unremarkable in educated American speech, while *everyplace* reads as distinctly more casual and more rural. Nothing about the meaning changes — this is register only.

1. *Everyplace* we stopped, the same three songs were playing.
2. Kudzu came up *everyplace* the county had cleared brush.
3. She left a note *everyplace* he was bound to look.
4. The chain has a store *everyplace* there's an interstate exit.
5. *Everyplace* the roof had been patched, it leaked worse than before.
6. They'll ship *everyplace* the mail truck goes.

**See also:** [everywhere](#everywhere), [anyplace](#anyplace), [someplace](#someplace), [no place](#no-place), [everywheres](#everywheres)

### everywhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriˌwɛr/ &middot; *EV-ree-wair*
**Русский:** везде, где; повсюду, где — придаточное места со значением «во всех местах»
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*I looked everywhere*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (universal)
**Pattern:** *Everywhere* [that] [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everywhere* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everywhere* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *everywhere the light gets to*.
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *everywhere* clause; none when it follows the main clause. The optional *that* never takes a comma on either side.
**Variants:** *everyplace* (informal, chiefly American)

Says the main clause holds true at every location without exception. Where *anywhere* offers a free pick from many places, *everywhere* covers all of them simultaneously, which is why it so often opens the sentence as a sweeping scene-setter.

1. *Everywhere* I go, somebody asks about the accent.
2. Bluebonnets came up *everywhere* the county had reseeded.
3. *Everywhere* the fire had passed, the ground was still warm two days later.
4. She left sticky notes *everywhere* he was likely to look.
5. *Everywhere that* the map showed a paved road, we found a dirt track.
6. The company ships *everywhere* the postal service delivers to.
7. The same chain restaurant turns up *everywhere* the interstate has an exit.

**See also:** [anywhere](#anywhere), [wherever](#wherever), [where](#where), [wheresoever](#wheresoever)

### everywheres

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛvriˌwɛrz/ &middot; *EV-ree-wairz*
**Русский:** везде — диал., прост.: нестандартная форма everywhere с -s
**Type:** subordinating; more often a plain place adverb &middot; **Register:** dialectal and nonstandard, and the rarest of the *-s* forms — regional American speech and the dialect fiction that records it
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (universal)
**Pattern:** *Everywheres* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everywheres* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *everywheres* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded.
**Punctuation:** The *everywhere* rule: comma after a fronted clause, none when it trails. The *-s* changes nothing about the pointing.
**Variants:** *everywhere* (the standard form); [*anywheres*](#anywheres), *somewheres*, [*nowheres*](#nowheres) (the same *-s* on the rest of the set)

Carries the same old adverbial *-s* as *anywheres* and *nowheres* — the ending standard English kept in *always* and *besides* and stopped extending. Of the three, *everywheres* is the least often heard and the least often written down; dialect novelists reach for *anywheres* and *nowheres* far more freely. Treat it as a marker of a speaker's region and generation, never as a stylistic option.

1. *Everywheres* we went that summer, somebody wanted to talk about the flood.
2. He'd been *everywheres* the railroad ran, and then some.
3. There was ice *everywheres* the sun hadn't reached by noon.
4. *Everywheres* that creek gets out of its banks, the fences go with it.
5. She'd looked *everywheres* she could think of and still come up empty.
6. They sell that syrup *everywheres* the trucks stop.

**See also:** [everywhere](#everywhere), [anywheres](#anywheres), [nowheres](#nowheres), [everyplace](#everyplace)

### no place

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈpleɪs/ &middot; *noh-PLAYSS*
**Русский:** негде, некуда; нет места, где — разг., амер. вариант nowhere; вперёд не выносится
**Type:** subordinating (negative); also a place adverb and a noun phrase (*forty miles of no place*) &middot; **Register:** informal, chiefly American
**Clause type:** relative clause hosted by a negative place pro-form
**Pattern:** [there is/was] *no place* [that] [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *no place* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *no place* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *no place I'd want to eat at*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before the clause — it always narrows, so it is restrictive. And note the one syntactic difference from *nowhere*: **no place does not front for inversion.** *Nowhere does the lease say you can sublet* is idiomatic; *No place does the lease say* is not. Write it as two words — the one-word *noplace* is a further informal step that most American editors reject.
**Variants:** *nowhere* (the standard equivalent, and the only one of the two that can be fronted); *noplace* (one word, more informal still)

The informal American negative of the *-place* set, matching *anyplace*, *someplace*, and *everyplace*. It substitutes for *nowhere* in the ordinary post-verbal slot and nowhere else: the emphatic fronted construction, the noun (*the middle of nowhere*), and the fixed idioms all belong to *nowhere* alone.

1. There was *no place* we could park within six blocks of the ballpark.
2. By ten o'clock there was *no place* in town still serving food.
3. The dog had *no place* to run that wasn't fenced.
4. He had *no place* he could go once the shift ended.
5. We looked for an hour and found *no place* the kids could swim.
6. Off that exit there's *no place* I'd want to spend the night.

**See also:** [nowhere](#nowhere), [anyplace](#anyplace), [someplace](#someplace), [everyplace](#everyplace), [nowheres](#nowheres)

### nowhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊˌwɛr/ &middot; *NOH-wair*
**Русский:** нигде, никуда; негде, некуда — отрицательное место; при выносе вперёд требует инверсии
**Type:** subordinating (negative); also a place adverb and a noun (*the middle of nowhere*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause hosted by a negative place pro-form; with fronting, a negative adverbial
**Pattern:** [there is/was] *nowhere* [that] [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *nowhere* [that] [clause]. &middot; *Nowhere* [auxiliary or *be*] [subject] [rest of clause] — fronted, with subject–auxiliary inversion. &middot; [main clause] *nowhere* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *nowhere I'd want to eat at*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before the clause that follows *nowhere* — it always narrows, so it is restrictive. Fronted *nowhere* takes no comma either, but it **forces inversion**: *Nowhere does the lease say…*, never *Nowhere the lease says…*.
**Variants:** *no place* (informal, chiefly American)

The negative member of the *somewhere / anywhere / everywhere* set: it denies that any location satisfies the clause. Note that the clause after it is a relative clause with the relative word usually dropped, not a true adverbial clause — which is why *that* can be inserted (*nowhere that still served food*) but a comma cannot.

1. There was *nowhere* we could park within six blocks of the stadium.
2. By ten o'clock there was *nowhere* that still served hot food.
3. *Nowhere* does the lease say you can sublet.
4. The kid had *nowhere* he could go after school, so the library became it.
5. *Nowhere* is the difference clearer than in the third-quarter numbers.
6. Off that exit there's *nowhere* I'd want to eat at.
7. We drove around for an hour and found *no place* the dog could run off leash.

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [anywhere](#anywhere), [everywhere](#everywhere), [where](#where)

### nowheres

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnoʊˌwɛrz/ &middot; *NOH-wairz*
**Русский:** нигде — диал., прост.: форма nowhere с -s, обычна в двойном отрицании
**Type:** subordinating (negative); more often a plain place adverb &middot; **Register:** dialectal and nonstandard — regional American speech and the dialect fiction that records it, Mark Twain's above all
**Clause type:** relative clause hosted by a negative place pro-form
**Pattern:** [there is/was] *nowheres* [clause]. &middot; [subject] [verb] *nowheres* [that] [clause]. &middot; *not* … *nowheres* — the double negative, which is ordinary grammar in the dialects that have this word. &middot; *nowheres near* [noun] — the frame that has spread furthest beyond the dialect.
**Punctuation:** No comma before the clause. Note that *nowheres*, unlike standard *nowhere*, does **not** front with subject–auxiliary inversion: the dialects that use it build emphasis with a doubled negative (*there warn't nowheres to go*), not with *Nowheres did…*.
**Variants:** *nowhere* (the standard form); [*no place*](#no-place) (informal but with standard syntax); [*anywheres*](#anywheres), *somewheres*, [*everywheres*](#everywheres)

Standard *nowhere* with the old adverbial genitive *-s* that survives in *always* and *besides*. It belongs to the same regional grammar as *ain't* and the double negative, and it reaches print almost only as recorded speech: Huck Finn uses it constantly, and it is still live in rural speech from northern New England through the Ozarks. It is never a formal option — outside dialogue it is an error, not a choice.

1. There warn't *nowheres* we could tie up till after dark.
2. He had *nowheres* to sleep and no money for a room.
3. There's *nowheres* around here a man can get his boots fixed anymore.
4. She wouldn't go *nowheres* her sister hadn't been first.
5. That's *nowheres* near enough lumber to finish the porch.
6. They hunted all afternoon and got *nowheres* the sheriff hadn't already looked.

**See also:** [nowhere](#nowhere), [no place](#no-place), [anywheres](#anywheres), [everywheres](#everywheres)

### someplace

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmˌpleɪs/ &middot; *SUHM-playss*
**Русский:** где-нибудь, куда-нибудь — разг., амер. вариант somewhere
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*I left it someplace*) and a pronoun &middot; **Register:** informal, chiefly American — common in educated American speech and in fiction dialogue, replaced by *somewhere* in formal prose
**Clause type:** relative clause hosted by an indefinite place pro-form
**Pattern:** [main clause] *someplace* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *someplace* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded. &middot; *Someplace* [that] [clause], [main clause] — the whole phrase fronted as a topic and resumed by *that's* or *there*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before the clause — it says what sort of place is wanted, so it is restrictive. A comma appears only when the whole *someplace* phrase is fronted as a topic and the main clause picks it up again.
**Variants:** *somewhere* (the standard equivalent); *some place* as two words is the literal noun phrase, not this pro-form

The informal American twin of *somewhere*, and the most natural-sounding of the *-place* set — plenty of careful American speakers use it without noticing. It carries the spacing distinction that the *-where* forms never have to make: *someplace quiet* is the pro-form, *some place I've never heard of* is an ordinary noun plus a relative clause, and only the closed-up spelling belongs to this entry.

1. We need *someplace* the whole crew can sit down at once.
2. He's looking for *someplace* he can leave the boat over the winter.
3. Let's eat *someplace* that doesn't have a drive-through.
4. She stashed the deed *someplace* even the lawyer couldn't find.
5. *Someplace* the kids can burn off an afternoon, that's what this town lacks.
6. Get off at the next exit and stop *someplace* they sell coffee.

**See also:** [somewhere](#somewhere), [anyplace](#anyplace), [everyplace](#everyplace), [no place](#no-place)

### somewhere

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsʌmˌwɛr/ &middot; *SUHM-wair*
**Русский:** где-нибудь, куда-нибудь; там, где — неопределённое место с придаточным
**Type:** subordinating; also a place adverb (*I left it somewhere*) and a pronoun (*somewhere in Ohio is far enough*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause hosted by an indefinite place pro-form
**Pattern:** [main clause] *somewhere* [that] [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *somewhere* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *somewhere only his sister knows about*. &middot; *Somewhere* [that] [clause], [main clause] — the whole phrase fronted as a topic and picked up again by *that's* or *there*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before the clause — it identifies which sort of place is wanted, so it is restrictive. A comma appears only when the whole *somewhere* phrase is fronted as a topic and the main clause resumes it.
**Variants:** *someplace* (informal, chiefly American)

Names an unspecified location and then describes it with a clause. Strictly this is a relative construction rather than a place conjunction — *somewhere* is the head and the clause modifies it — but learners meet it in exactly the same slot as *anywhere* and *everywhere*, and it takes the same optional *that*.

1. I need *somewhere* I can spread out all these papers.
2. They want *somewhere* the kids can run around without getting yelled at.
3. Let's find *someplace* that isn't a chain restaurant.
4. He keeps a spare key *somewhere* only his sister knows about.
5. She's looking for *somewhere* she can practice without the neighbors calling the police.
6. *Somewhere* the kids can run off some energy, that's what this trip is missing.
7. Park *someplace* the tow trucks won't bother checking.

**See also:** [anywhere](#anywhere), [nowhere](#nowhere), [where](#where), [wherever](#wherever)

### whence

**Pronunciation:** /wɛns/ (older and Southern /ʍɛns/) &middot; *WENS*
**Русский:** откуда — устар., книжн.: место-источник
**Type:** subordinating; also a relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (source); relative clause of origin; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *whence* [clause]. &middot; [place noun], *whence* [clause]. &middot; [place noun] *whence* [clause] — restrictive, no comma. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *whence* [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; [main clause] *from whence* [clause] — the long-established redundant form.
**Punctuation:** Comma before *whence* when the clause merely adds information about a place already named; no comma when the clause completes the main clause or answers an implied question.
**Variants:** *from whence* (redundant but long established); *whencesoever* (archaic)

Means "from what place" or "from which place." The *from* is already baked into the word, which makes *from whence* strictly redundant — but it is in the King James Bible and in Shakespeare, so it has four centuries of cover. Set against its partners: *whence* is the source, *whither* the destination, *where* the location.

1. Return the stone *whence* it came.
2. He never said *whence* the money had come.
3. Investigators traced the outbreak to a single farm, *whence* the shipments had gone out to nine states.
4. "I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills, *from whence* cometh my help."
5. The company moved its charter back to Delaware, *whence* it had come twenty years earlier.
6. The village *whence* his family came no longer appears on any map.
7. Nobody could establish *whence* the rumor had started.

**See also:** [whither](#whither), [where](#where), [whereof](#whereof), [wherefore](#wherefore)

### whencesoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛnsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; carefully /ˌwɛnssoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *wens-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** откуда бы ни — устар., юр.: из любого источника, откуда бы ни поступило
**Type:** subordinating; also a relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in scriptural cadence and in old customs and revenue drafting
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (source, universal); relative clause of origin
**Pattern:** [main clause] *whencesoever* [clause]. &middot; *Whencesoever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [noun] *whencesoever* [past participle] — the reduced statutory formula, as in *goods whencesoever imported*. &middot; [noun] *whencesoever* [present participle] — as in *income whencesoever arising*.
**Punctuation:** The *wheresoever* rule: comma after a fronted clause, none when it trails. The reduced participial formula takes no comma at all — *income whencesoever arising* is one noun phrase.
**Variants:** *whence* (the plain form); *whencesoe'er* (poetic); *from whatever source*, *from wherever* (the living paraphrases)

The *-soever* intensification of *whence*, meaning "from absolutely any place that." Its whole purpose is the same as *wheresoever*'s: to close loopholes by sounding exhaustive, which is why revenue and estate drafters kept it long after the word died in prose. Set against its partners, *whencesoever* is the source, [*whithersoever*](#whithersoever) the destination, *wheresoever* the location.

1. The duty is charged on goods *whencesoever* imported into the territory.
2. The trustee shall gather in the income of the estate, *whencesoever* arising.
3. *Whencesoever* the report had come, it was on the governor's desk by noon.
4. He would take help *whencesoever* it was offered, and ask no questions after.
5. The parish was bound to relieve any traveler in want, *whencesoever* he came.
6. Rumor, *whencesoever* it begins, is not easily called back.

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [whithersoever](#whithersoever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [whither](#whither)

### where

**Pronunciation:** /wɛr/ (older and Southern /ʍɛr/) &middot; *WAIR* — unstressed as a conjunction but not reduced; it never falls together with *were* /wər/
**Русский:** где; куда; там, где — место; также относительное «в котором» и косвенный вопрос «где»
**Type:** subordinating; also a relative adverb and an interrogative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place; relative clause modifying a place noun; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [main clause] *where* [clause]. &middot; *Where* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [place noun] *where* [clause]. &middot; [place noun], *where* [clause], [rest of sentence]. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *where* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma when the place clause follows the main clause. Comma after a fronted *where* clause. As a relative adverb the general rule applies: **no commas** if the clause narrows which place is meant (*the town where I was born*), **commas on both sides** if the place is already identified (*Tulsa, where I was born, has changed*). Never a comma before an indirect question (*ask where it is*, not *ask, where it is*).
**Variants:** none in standard American English; *at which / in which* is the formal relative equivalent

The workhorse of the category, and the one word that belongs to both families in this file. As an adverbial subordinator it locates the whole event (*put it where I can see it*). As a relative adverb it modifies a place noun and can be swapped for *in which* (*the room where we met* = *the room in which we met*). It also drifts into abstract territory, meaning "in cases in which" (*where the law is silent, custom governs*), which is standard in legal and academic prose.

1. Put the boxes back *where* you found them.
2. *Where* the sidewalk ends, the gravel road starts.
3. This is the diner *where* we had breakfast every Sunday for six years.
4. We spent August in Bar Harbor, *where* my grandmother still keeps a house.
5. Nobody could tell me *where* the registration desk was.
6. *Where* the contract is silent, state law fills the gap.
7. He'll argue *where* most people would just walk away.

**See also:** [wherever](#wherever), [wherein](#wherein), [when](#when), [which](#which), [whence](#whence), [whither](#whither)

### where all

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛr ˌɔl/ &middot; also /ˌwɛr ˈɔl/ &middot; *WAIR-awl*
**Русский:** где именно, во всех каких местах — диал. (юг США): вопрос требует перечислить все места
**Type:** subordinating (interrogative); also an interrogative adverb in direct questions &middot; **Register:** dialectal — Southern and South Midland American; unremarkable in speech across that region and absent from edited prose everywhere
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question, exhaustive)
**Pattern:** *Where all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — direct question, with inversion. &middot; [verb of asking/knowing] *where all* [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; *Where all* [subject] [verb] [preposition]? — stranded. &middot; *Where* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *all*? — the *all* floated to the end of the clause.
**Punctuation:** No comma between *where* and *all*, and none before the embedded clause. The direct question takes a question mark; the embedded clause takes none.
**Variants:** [*who all*](#who-all), [*what all*](#what-all) (the same *all* on the other *wh-* words); *all the places (that)* (the standard paraphrase)

The *all* here is not the ordinary quantifier but an exhaustiveness marker welded to the *wh-* word: *Where did you go?* invites one answer, *Where all did you go?* asks for the whole itinerary. Standard English has no single word for the distinction and has to paraphrase, which is why the construction is so stable in the regions that have it — and why it is worth recognizing rather than correcting when you meet it.

1. *Where all* did you go on that trip?
2. I don't know *where all* she's lived since college.
3. *Where all* have they put these stores now?
4. He couldn't remember *where all* he'd left his tools.
5. Ask her *where all* the family is coming in from.
6. *Where all* did y'all look before you called me?

**See also:** [where](#where), [who all](#who-all), [what all](#what-all), [whereabouts](#whereabouts)

### where at

**Pronunciation:** /wɛr … æt/ &middot; the *at* is unstressed and normally reduced to /ət/ &middot; *WAIR … at*
**Русский:** где — прост.: избыточное at в конце придаточного, в русском не отражается
**Type:** subordinating — plain *where* with a pleonastic stranded *at* &middot; **Register:** nonstandard; extremely widespread in casual American speech in every region, and one of the most heavily corrected features in American schools
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question of place); adverbial clause of place
**Pattern:** [verb of asking/knowing] *where* [subject] [verb] *at*. &middot; *Where* [auxiliary] [subject] *at*? — the direct question. &middot; [main clause] *where* [subject] [verb] *at*. &middot; *where it's at* — the frozen idiom, which is a different animal.
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere in it; it points exactly like plain *where*. Note that the *at* attaches to the **end of its own clause**, not to *where* — which is why the headword is *where … at* and not a single word, and why it can be separated from *where* by an entire clause (*where you said the key was at*).
**Variants:** plain *where* (the standard form — the *at* contributes nothing); *whereat* as one word is an unrelated archaic relative meaning "at which," documented in [12-formal-archaic](12-formal-archaic.md#whereat)

*Where* already means "at what place," so the trailing *at* is redundant, and that redundancy is the whole basis of the schoolroom joke (*"Where are you at?" "Between the A and the T"*). It is nonetheless one of the most common features of casual American speech, cutting across region, race, and class — treat it as informal rather than illiterate, but keep it out of writing. One offshoot escaped the stigma completely: the frozen idiom *where it's at*, meaning "the thing that actually matters," is standard colloquial English and no editor blinks at it.

1. Do you know *where* the extra chairs are *at*?
2. "*Where* you *at*?" is the entire text message in this family.
3. Nobody could tell me *where* the trailhead was *at*.
4. He asked *where* I was *at* on the quarterly report.
5. She never says *where* she's *at*, only when she'll be back.
6. Comfortable shoes and a ten-minute commute — that's *where it's at*.

**See also:** [where](#where), [whereabouts](#whereabouts), [where all](#where-all), [wherein](#wherein)

### whereabouts

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛrəˌbaʊts/ &middot; also /ˌwɛrəˈbaʊts/ &middot; *WAIR-uh-bowts*
**Русский:** где примерно, в каком районе — разг.: спрашивает о приблизительном месте
**Type:** subordinating (interrogative); also an interrogative adverb and a plural noun (*his whereabouts are unknown*) &middot; **Register:** informal; the embedded use is chiefly British and Irish rather than American
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question of approximate place)
**Pattern:** *Whereabouts* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — direct question, with inversion. &middot; *Whereabouts* [prepositional phrase] [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — as in *Whereabouts in Ohio did you grow up?* &middot; [verb of asking/knowing] *whereabouts* [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; [verb of asking/knowing] *whereabouts* [prepositional phrase] [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma before it in either use. The direct question takes a question mark; the embedded clause takes none.
**Variants:** *whereabout* (rare singular); the identical noun *whereabouts* (*his whereabouts are unknown*)

Asks for a rough location where *where* asks for an exact one — "in what general area." Americans use the direct question freely (*Whereabouts are you from?*) but rarely embed it as a conjunction; *She asked whereabouts I lived* reads as British. The homograph noun, *his whereabouts*, is fully standard American and takes either a singular or a plural verb.

1. *Whereabouts* in Ohio did you grow up?
2. She asked *whereabouts* I'd left the car.
3. I couldn't tell the ranger *whereabouts* on the trail we'd seen the bear.
4. *Whereabouts* are you staying — downtown or out by the airport?
5. Nobody is quite sure *whereabouts* the property line actually runs.

**See also:** [where](#where), [wherever](#wherever), [why](#why)

### whereas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæz/ &middot; *wair-AZ*
**Русский:** тогда как — противопоставление; в преамбуле договора (юр.) «принимая во внимание, что»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal; the preamble use is legal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of contrast; in legal drafting, a recital
**Pattern:** *Whereas* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *whereas* [clause]. &middot; WHEREAS, [clause]; WHEREAS, [clause]; NOW, THEREFORE, [operative clause].
**Punctuation:** **Always a comma before *whereas*** when it follows the main clause — the contrast it draws is never restrictive. Comma after it when fronted. In contract recitals, successive *whereas* clauses are separated by **semicolons**, and the last one ends with *; NOW, THEREFORE,*.
**Variants:** none; note that British *whilst* is not an American substitute for anything in this entry

Built from *where* + *as*, and originally meaning "at which place" — which is why it sits in this file. Modern American English keeps two descendants of that original sense. The living one is contrast: X holds, and by contrast Y holds. Unlike coordinating *but*, *whereas* subordinates, so its clause can be moved to the front; unlike *while*, it carries no possible time reading. The fossil one is the legal preamble, where *whereas* means "given the fact that" and introduces the background recitals of a contract.

1. Her brother took the corporate job, *whereas* she went into teaching.
2. *Whereas* the 2019 model had a physical dial, the new one is all touchscreen.
3. Federal law caps the fee at fifty dollars, *whereas* state law leaves it open.
4. *WHEREAS*, the Seller is the record owner of the property described in Exhibit A; and *WHEREAS*, the Buyer desires to purchase that property; NOW, THEREFORE, the parties agree as follows.
5. He remembers every phone number he has ever had, *whereas* I can't reliably recall my own zip code.

**See also:** [where](#where), [wherein](#wherein), [whereof](#whereof), [but](01-coordinating.md#but)

### wheresoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *wair-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** где бы ни; повсюду, где — устар., юр.: «где бы ни находилось»
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in legal and ceremonial writing
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (universal, emphatic)
**Pattern:** *Wheresoever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *wheresoever* [clause]. &middot; [noun] *wheresoever* [past participle] — the legal formula, as in *assets wheresoever situated*.
**Punctuation:** The same rule as *wherever*: comma after a fronted clause, none when it trails. The reduced participial formula takes no comma at all.
**Variants:** *wheresoe'er* (poetic), *whereso* (archaic)

The emphatic form of *wherever*, built with the old intensifying suffix *-soever* that also gives *whatsoever* and *whomsoever*. It means "in absolutely any place that," and its whole modern function is to sound total and unloopholed, which is why estate and tax drafters keep it alive.

1. The bearer shall have safe passage *wheresoever* he may travel within the realm.
2. *Wheresoever* the Company does business, these standards apply without exception.
3. He swore he would follow her *wheresoever* she went.
4. The duty is charged on the decedent's assets *wheresoever* situated.
5. *Wheresoever* two or three are gathered together, there is a committee meeting waiting to happen.

**See also:** [wherever](#wherever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whoever](#whoever), [everywhere](#everywhere)

### wherever

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɛvər/ &middot; reduced /wərˈɛvər/ &middot; *wair-EV-er*
**Русский:** где бы ни; куда бы ни; везде, где — обобщающее и уступительное место
**Type:** subordinating; also a fused relative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (universal); concessive clause of place
**Pattern:** *Wherever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *wherever* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *wherever* [clause] — afterthought, with a comma. &middot; *Wherever it is* [that] [clause] — the emphatic cleft. &middot; *Where ever* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — two words, and only in an emphatic question.
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *wherever* clause. No comma when it follows the main clause, unless it is tacked on as an afterthought (*I'll find it, wherever it ended up*).
**Variants:** *where'er*, *whereso'er* (poetic); *no matter where* (exact phrasal equivalent in the concessive sense)

Means "in any place that," "in every place that," or — the concessive reading — "it doesn't matter where." Where plain *where* points at one identifiable place, *wherever* leaves the place open or sweeps across all of them. It is the place member of the *-ever* fused-relative family along with *whatever*, *whoever*, and *whenever*.

1. *Wherever* she goes, people recognize her.
2. Set the tripod down *wherever* the ground is level.
3. *Wherever* you end up living, keep the heavy coat.
4. The company will match the offer, *wherever* it comes from.
5. He falls asleep *wherever* he sits down for more than ten minutes.
6. *Wherever* the money went, it certainly didn't go into the roof.

**See also:** [where](#where), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [whatever](#whatever), [whoever](#whoever), [anywhere](#anywhere)

### whither

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɪðər/ (older /ˈʍɪðər/) &middot; *WITH-er* — a homophone of *wither* for most Americans
**Русский:** куда — устар., книжн.: место-назначение
**Type:** subordinating; also a relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, literary
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (destination); relative clause of destination; noun clause
**Pattern:** [main clause] *whither* [clause]. &middot; [place noun] *whither* [clause] — restrictive, no comma. &middot; [place noun], *whither* [clause] — nonrestrictive, comma. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *whither* [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; *Whither* [noun phrase]? — the verbless headline formula.
**Punctuation:** Comma before it when the clause adds information about a place already named; none when the clause completes the main clause. The headline formula takes a question mark and no verb at all.
**Variants:** *whithersoever* (archaic, emphatic)

Means "to what place" or "to which place" — the destination counterpart of *whence* (source) and *where* (location). It is dead in American speech and alive in exactly two registers: the King James cadence (*whither thou goest*) and the op-ed headline (*Whither the middle class?*), where it means "where is this heading?"

1. "*Whither* thou goest, I will go."
2. Nobody asked *whither* the money was being wired.
3. They boarded a ship bound for a colony *whither* very few had gone willingly.
4. *Whither* American manufacturing?
5. He wandered off, *whither* nobody could say.
6. The families were moved to a second camp, *whither* the aid trucks rarely made it.

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [where](#where), [wherein](#wherein), [wherefore](#wherefore)

### whithersoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪðərsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *with-er-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** куда бы ни — устар., библ.: в любое место назначения
**Type:** subordinating; also a relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic; scriptural and ceremonial — an American meets it in the King James Bible, in hymn text, and in period pastiche, and nowhere else
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of place (destination, universal); relative clause of destination
**Pattern:** *Whithersoever* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *whithersoever* [clause]. &middot; [place noun] *whithersoever* [clause] — restrictive, no comma. &middot; [noun] *whithersoever* [past participle] — the reduced formula, as in *goods whithersoever consigned*.
**Punctuation:** The *wheresoever* rule: comma after a fronted clause, none when it trails. The reduced participial formula takes no comma.
**Variants:** *whither* (the plain form); *whithersoe'er* (poetic); *wherever*, *no matter where* (the living equivalents)

The *-soever* intensification of *whither*, meaning "to absolutely any place that." It completes the emphatic set: [*whencesoever*](#whencesoever) is the source, *whithersoever* the destination, [*wheresoever*](#wheresoever) the location. Like the rest of that set it does not intensify so much as change costume — the register jumps from neutral to scriptural, and nothing about the meaning is stronger than plain *wherever*.

1. And I will follow thee *whithersoever* thou goest.
2. *Whithersoever* the ark was carried, the people followed after it.
3. The knight swore to attend his lord *whithersoever* he might ride.
4. He resolved to go *whithersoever* the work took him, and it took him to Ohio.
5. *Whithersoever* the current bore them, they had no oars to argue with it.
6. The commissioners may direct the relief fund *whithersoever* the need is greatest.

**See also:** [whither](#whither), [whencesoever](#whencesoever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [whence](#whence)

## The *where-* compounds

These are connectors built from *where* plus a preposition, and they mean exactly what the parts say: *whereby* = "by which," *wherein* = "in which," *whereof* = "of which," *whereupon* = "upon which," *wherefore* = "for which reason." All of them are formal, and — this is the trap — **none of them has anything to do with place**. They inherited the shape of *where* and the meaning of the preposition. The same pattern once produced *whereat*, *whereon*, *whereto*, and *wherewith*, which are now fully archaic; two more, *wherefrom* and *whereunto*, are documented below because they still turn up in old deeds and in scriptural prose.

All of them but one are true relative connectors: they subordinate, they cannot begin a sentence, and a comma is the most punctuation they ever take. *Wherefore* is the odd one out — it behaves like the conjunctive adverb *therefore*, which means it needs a **semicolon or a period** in front of it and it *can* open a sentence in legal drafting. Sorting *whereupon* (comma) from *wherefore* (semicolon) is the single thing to get right in this section.

### whereby

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈbaɪ/ &middot; *wair-BY*
**Русский:** посредством которого; согласно которому — «by which» при существительном способа
**Type:** relative adverb &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause modifying a noun of method (*system, arrangement, process, rule, mechanism*)
**Pattern:** [noun of method] *whereby* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [preposition] [noun of method] *whereby* [clause] — as in *under a system whereby claims are audited quarterly*. &middot; [verb] [determiner] [noun of method] *whereby* [clause].
**Punctuation:** **No comma before *whereby*** — the clause defines the arrangement it follows, so it is restrictive by nature. A nonrestrictive *whereby* is possible but so rare it reads as an error; use *by which* with commas instead. *Whereby* never begins a sentence, and it never takes a semicolon.
**Variants:** *by which* (the plain equivalent, always available)

Means "by means of which" and attaches a clause to an abstract noun that names a mechanism. Despite the spelling it never means "where" — *the town whereby I live* is not English. It also has a narrow antecedent range: if the noun in front of it is not a method, procedure, or arrangement, *whereby* is the wrong word.

1. They set up a system *whereby* every request gets a tracking number.
2. The union negotiated an arrangement *whereby* senior staff pick their shifts first.
3. There's a provision *whereby* the filing fee is waived for first-time applicants.
4. He described a process *whereby* the plastic is broken back down into its original monomers.
5. The bylaws create a mechanism *whereby* any two board members can call a special meeting.
6. The tenants stayed on under an arrangement *whereby* the landlord covered heat through March.

**See also:** [wherein](#wherein), [whereupon](#whereupon), [which](#which), [whereof](#whereof)

### wherefore

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛrˌfɔr/ &middot; *WAIR-for*
**Русский:** вследствие чего, посему; устар. «почему» — юр.; в whys and wherefores — «причины»
**Type:** conjunctive adverb ("for which reason"); also an archaic interrogative adverb and, in the fixed plural, a noun &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in legal drafting and one fixed noun phrase
**Clause type:** independent clause of result or consequence; interrogative clause of reason
**Pattern:** [independent clause]; *wherefore* [independent clause]. &middot; [independent clause]. *Wherefore*, [independent clause]. &middot; *WHEREFORE*, [subject] [verb] — the prayer for relief that closes a legal pleading. &middot; *Wherefore* [auxiliary or verb] [subject] …? — the archaic interrogative meaning "why." &middot; *the whys and wherefores* — fixed noun phrase.
**Punctuation:** *Wherefore* connects two independent clauses the way *therefore* does, so it needs a **semicolon or a period before it** — *he defaulted, wherefore the lease terminated* is a comma splice, and *he defaulted; wherefore the lease terminated* is the repair. Do not confuse it with *whereupon*, which is a subordinator and takes a comma. When *wherefore* opens its own sentence it takes a comma **after** it (*WHEREFORE, Plaintiff prays…*). The interrogative use inverts the subject and ends in a question mark.
**Variants:** *whys and wherefores* (fixed plural noun); *therefore*, *hence*, *thus* (the living equivalents, all punctuated the same way)

Means "for which reason" or, in its interrogative use, simply "why." Juliet's *Wherefore art thou Romeo?* asks **why** he has to be a Romeo, not where he is — the most consistently misread line in English. Modern American English keeps it in two places: the prayer for relief at the end of a legal pleading, and the frozen phrase *the whys and wherefores*. It is the one *where-* compound in this section that is not a subordinator, which is exactly why it takes a semicolon where *whereupon* takes a comma.

1. "*Wherefore* art thou Romeo?"
2. The defendant failed to appear; *wherefore* the court entered judgment by default.
3. She wanted to know all the whys and *wherefores* before she signed anything.
4. *WHEREFORE*, Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court enter judgment in her favor and award costs.
5. The notice went to the wrong address. *Wherefore*, the thirty-day clock never started running.
6. *Wherefore* should any of us care what that committee thinks?

**See also:** [why](#why), [whereupon](#whereupon), [whereof](#whereof), [whence](#whence), [therefore](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#therefore)

### wherefrom

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈfrʌm/ &middot; also /wɛrˈfrɑm/ &middot; *wair-FRUM*
**Русский:** откуда, из которого — устар.: «from which», редчайшее в семье where-
**Type:** relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic, and the rarest of the whole *where-* family — old deeds, old wills, and period prose
**Clause type:** relative clause of source, restrictive or nonrestrictive
**Pattern:** [noun] *wherefrom* [clause] — restrictive, no comma. &middot; [noun], *wherefrom* [clause], [rest of sentence] — nonrestrictive. &middot; [noun of origin] *wherefrom* [subject] [verb].
**Punctuation:** No comma when the clause defines the noun in front of it; a comma when the noun is already identified and the clause merely adds. Like every *where-* relative except *wherefore*, it **cannot begin a sentence** and never takes a semicolon.
**Variants:** *from which* (the plain equivalent, and the one a modern reader expects); *whence* (the older single word for the same idea); *whereof*, *whereat*, *whereon*, *wherewith* (the same family)

Means "from which." It is the least successful of the *where-* compounds, and the reason is easy to see: English already owned *whence*, which says the same thing in one syllable and had four centuries of scriptural backing. *Wherefrom* was left as the drafter's word — you find it in title chains and probate files, where the whole point is to name a source without ambiguity — and even there it now looks like a slip of the pen to most readers.

1. The corporation shall disclose the account *wherefrom* the payment was drawn.
2. He would never name the source *wherefrom* the letters came.
3. The spring *wherefrom* the whole town drew its water went dry in August.
4. The deed recites the earlier grant *wherefrom* the plaintiff's title derives.
5. She returned at last to the same small parish *wherefrom* she had been sent out.
6. The record nowhere shows the fund *wherefrom* the disbursement was made.

**See also:** [whence](#whence), [whereof](#whereof), [whereunto](#whereunto), [wherein](#wherein), [in which](#in-which)

### wherein

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈɪn/ &middot; *wair-IN*
**Русский:** в котором; в чём — книжн.: «in which»
**Type:** relative adverb &middot; **Register:** formal, literary
**Clause type:** relative clause of location or of respect — restrictive or nonrestrictive
**Pattern:** [noun] *wherein* [clause]. &middot; [noun], *wherein* [clause], [rest of sentence]. &middot; *wherein lies* [noun phrase] — fixed inversion.
**Punctuation:** No comma when the clause narrows the noun (*a clause wherein the deadline is defined*); comma before it when the noun is already fully identified. The fixed *wherein lies the problem* is normally preceded by a comma or a dash.
**Variants:** *in which* (the plain equivalent); *therein* is the demonstrative counterpart, not a connector

Means "in which," covering both literal containment (*a country wherein no one pays for medicine*) and the abstract sense "in which respect" (*wherein it differs from the standard form*). Its everyday substitutes are *in which* and, for physical places, plain *where*.

1. He described a country *wherein* no one pays out of pocket for medicine.
2. The statute has a clause *wherein* "business day" is defined as excluding federal holidays.
3. Both candidates promised reform — *wherein* lies the problem, since neither would define it.
4. She published a memoir *wherein* she named half the department by name.
5. The contract is silent as to remedies, *wherein* it differs sharply from the standard form.

**See also:** [where](#where), [whereby](#whereby), [which](#which), [whereof](#whereof)

### whereof

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈʌv/ &middot; also /wɛrˈɑv/ &middot; *wair-UV*
**Русский:** о котором; которого — устар., юр.: «of which»; IN WITNESS WHEREOF — «в удостоверение чего»
**Type:** relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in one legal formula
**Clause type:** relative clause meaning "of which" or "of what"
**Pattern:** [noun] *whereof* [clause]. &middot; [noun], the [attribute] *whereof* [verb phrase]. &middot; IN WITNESS *WHEREOF*, [clause].
**Punctuation:** In the witness formula, a comma follows the whole phrase (*IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the parties have executed…*). Elsewhere, no comma when the clause defines the noun before it; a comma when it merely adds.
**Variants:** the same pattern once produced *whereto* ("to which"), *wherewith* ("with which"), *whereat* ("at which"), and *whereon* ("on which") — all now archaic

Means "of which" or "of what." In modern American English it survives in essentially one place: the closing attestation line of a contract. Everywhere else, *of which* has replaced it entirely, and *the value whereof* now reads as deliberate legalese.

1. IN WITNESS *WHEREOF*, the parties have executed this Agreement as of the date first written above.
2. "*Whereof* one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."
3. He spoke at length of matters *whereof* he had no direct knowledge.
4. She produced a receipt, the authenticity *whereof* was immediately disputed.
5. The estate, the value *whereof* exceeds two million dollars, passes into the trust.

**See also:** [whose](#whose), [which](#which), [wherein](#wherein), [whereby](#whereby)

### whereunto

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrənˈtu/ &middot; also /wɛrˈʌntu/ &middot; *wair-un-TOO*
**Русский:** к которому; для чего — устар., библ.: «to which»
**Type:** relative adverb &middot; **Register:** archaic; scriptural, and a fossil in older legal drafting
**Clause type:** relative clause of destination or purpose, restrictive or nonrestrictive
**Pattern:** [noun] *whereunto* [clause] — restrictive, no comma. &middot; [noun], *whereunto* [clause], [rest of sentence] — nonrestrictive. &middot; [noun of office or purpose] *whereunto* [subject] [verb] — as in *the office whereunto he was called*.
**Punctuation:** No comma when the clause defines the noun before it; a comma when it merely adds. It cannot begin a sentence and never takes a semicolon. Do not confuse it with the demonstrative *hereunto*, which is not a connector at all and which sits in a signature block (*have hereunto set their hands*) with no clause after it.
**Variants:** *whereto* (the shorter form, equally archaic); *to which* (the plain modern equivalent); *hereunto*, *thereunto* (the demonstrative counterparts, still typed into American signature blocks every working day)

Means "to which." It is the *-unto* member of the family, and its ordinary habitat is the Authorized Version and the prose written in imitation of it — a purpose, an office, or a calling that something is directed toward. Modern English says *to which*, and in the one place the *-unto* shape is still alive it is not this word but its demonstrative cousin: *the parties have hereunto set their hands* closes contracts that were signed this morning.

1. He proved faithful in the office *whereunto* he had been called.
2. The instrument creates an easement *whereunto* the adjoining owner is entitled in perpetuity.
3. She had no notion of the end *whereunto* all this labor was tending.
4. The witness could not name the fund *whereunto* the money had been transferred.
5. There is a purpose *whereunto* every one of these trials is bent.
6. The charter recites the uses *whereunto* the endowment shall be applied.

**See also:** [whereof](#whereof), [wherefrom](#wherefrom), [wherein](#wherein), [whither](#whither), [in which](#in-which)

### whereupon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛrəˈpɑn/ &middot; also /ˌwɛrəˈpɔn/ &middot; *wair-uh-PON*
**Русский:** после чего; вслед за чем — присоединяет следствие ко всему предыдущему высказыванию
**Type:** relative adverb (continuative) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** nonrestrictive relative clause whose antecedent is the entire preceding clause
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *whereupon* [subject] [verb]. &middot; [independent clause], *whereupon* [clause], [continuation]. &middot; [noun of occasion] *whereupon* [clause] — rare and formal, as in *the moment whereupon everything turned*.
**Punctuation:** In the ordinary use — where the antecedent is the whole preceding clause — **always a comma before it, never a semicolon and never nothing.** This is the one that trips people up: *whereupon* looks like *therefore* and *consequently*, but it is a subordinator, not a conjunctive adverb, so it takes a comma where those take a semicolon. Its own neighbor *wherefore* goes the other way — semicolon — so the two cannot be punctuated alike. The rare use with a noun antecedent (*the moment whereupon everything turned*) is an ordinary restrictive relative and takes **no** comma at all. *Whereupon* also cannot begin a sentence, and the clause after it cannot stand alone.
**Variants:** *upon which*, *at which point* (both less formal, same meaning)

Means "and immediately after that," chaining an event to the reaction it triggered. Compared with *then*, which is an adverb and therefore needs its own *and* or a semicolon, *whereupon* does the joining itself. Compared with *whereby* (method), *whereupon* is pure sequence.

1. The fire alarm went off at 3 a.m., *whereupon* two hundred guests filed into the parking lot in bathrobes.
2. He asked for a raise, *whereupon* his manager changed the subject to parking.
3. The bill cleared the Senate, *whereupon* the House refused to take it up at all.
4. She set the pie on the windowsill, *whereupon* the dog took an immediate professional interest.
5. The pitcher argued the call, *whereupon* the umpire ejected him.

**See also:** [whereby](#whereby), [wherein](#wherein), [which](#which), [when](#when)

## Relative pronouns

A relative pronoun does two jobs at once: it links its clause to a noun in the main sentence, and it fills a grammatical slot **inside** its own clause — subject, object, or object of a preposition. That double duty is why there is always a "gap" after a relative pronoun (*the book that I read* ∅) and why case matters (*who* vs *whom*).

Three rules govern the whole group. **First, the comma rule:** restrictive (identifying) clauses take no commas; nonrestrictive (adding) clauses take commas on both sides. **Second, the *that/which* rule:** in edited American prose, restrictive clauses about things take *that*, nonrestrictive clauses about things take *which* — and *that* can never follow a comma. British and older American writing uses restrictive *which* freely, so you will meet it. **Third, the zero relative:** in a restrictive clause you may delete the relative pronoun when it is the **object** (*the email I sent*), but not when it is the **subject** (*the email that arrived* — you cannot drop *that*). Nonrestrictive clauses never allow deletion. That deletion is common enough to be its own headword, [zero relative](#zero-relative), at the end of this section.

### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/ &middot; weak /əz/ &middot; *AZ*
**Русский:** который — относительное только после such, the same, as much/many; либо «как» обо всей фразе
**Type:** relative pronoun (only after *such*, *same*, and *as many/much*); elsewhere a subordinating conjunction of time, cause, manner, or concession, a preposition (*as a rule*, *hired as a temp*), an adverb of degree (*as tall*), and half of the correlative *as … as* &middot; **Register:** neutral in the fixed frames; formal in the sentential use; nonstandard in the bare relative use
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause; sentential relative clause
**Pattern:** *such* [noun] *as* [clause]. &middot; *the same* [noun] *as* [clause]. &middot; *as many/much* [noun] *as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as* [clause] — sentential, trailing. &middot; *As* [clause], [main clause] — sentential, fronted. &middot; [subject], *as* [clause], [predicate] — sentential, interrupting.
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere in the *such / same / as…as* frames. A sentential *as* clause is parenthetical: comma before it when it closes the sentence, comma after it when it opens, commas on both sides when it interrupts.
**Variants:** *such as*, *the same as* (the fixed frames)

*As* is a relative pronoun in only three environments: after *such*, after *the same*, and in a sentential relative like *as I mentioned*, where its antecedent is a whole clause. Everywhere else it is a subordinating conjunction of time, cause, or manner — a different word entirely. Regional American speech extends relative *as* to ordinary nouns (*the man as sold me the truck*), which is nonstandard and marked.

1. He isn't the same person *as* he was ten years ago.
2. Bring only such tools *as* you'll actually need on the roof.
3. The dough will take as much flour *as* the humidity demands.
4. The flight was delayed, *as* everyone at the gate had predicted.
5. *As* the minutes show, the motion failed on a tie vote.
6. She got the same grade *as* I did, which still annoys me.
7. The budget, *as* you'll see on page four, was blown by March.

**See also:** [that](#that), [which](#which), [what](#what), [and](01-coordinating.md#and)

### but

**Pronunciation:** /bʌt/ &middot; weak /bət/ &middot; *BUT* &middot; *buht*
**Русский:** который не — устар.: относительное со встроенным отрицанием, требует отрицания в главном
**Type:** relative pronoun (negative) — a relative with its own negative built in; elsewhere a coordinating conjunction, a preposition (*everyone but me*), and a focusing adverb (*but a child*) &middot; **Register:** archaic and literary; alive in a handful of proverbs and otherwise a period form
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause, licensed only by a negative, an interrogative, or *only* in the clause above it
**Pattern:** [negated existential] *but* [verb phrase] — as in *there is no one but knows it*. &middot; *no* / *not a* / *scarcely a* [noun] *but* [verb phrase]. &middot; *Who is there but* [verb phrase]? — the rhetorical question. &middot; *never so* [adjective] *but* [clause] — the concessive cousin, treated at [*but that*](12-formal-archaic.md#but-that).
**Punctuation:** **No comma before it** — the clause narrows the negated noun, so it is restrictive by nature. It cannot be fronted, cannot open a sentence, and never takes a semicolon. A comma there turns it into coordinating *but* and changes the sentence entirely: *there is no rule, but it has exceptions* says something different from *there is no rule but has exceptions*.
**Variants:** *that … not*, *who … not*, *which … not* (the modern paraphrases); [*but that*](12-formal-archaic.md#but-that), [*but what*](10-complementizers.md#but-what) (the same negative *but* with an extra complementizer); [*but for*](12-formal-archaic.md#but-for) (the conditional preposition, unrelated to this entry)

A relative pronoun that swallows a negative: *there is no rule but has an exception* means "no rule **that does not** have an exception." Because the negative is already inside it, the word needs a negative or a question above it to be licensed at all — strip the *no* and the sentence collapses, since *there is a rule but has an exception* is not English. That dependence is what makes it a genuinely different word from the *but* of *slow but steady*. Americans meet it in proverbs, in the King James Bible, and in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose; nobody produces it fresh.

1. There is no rule *but* has its exception.
2. There is not a man among them *but* would swear to it in open court.
3. Who is there *but* has wished, at least once, to begin the whole thing over?
4. Not a day passed *but* she thought of that letter.
5. There breathes no sailor on this coast *but* knows that light.
6. Scarcely a family in the county *but* has lost somebody to the mill.

**See also:** [that](#that), [which](#which), [who](#who), [zero relative](#zero-relative), [but](01-coordinating.md#but), [but that](12-formal-archaic.md#but-that)

### for which reason

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr ˌwɪtʃ ˈrizən/ &middot; weak /fər ˌwɪtʃ ˈrizən/ &middot; *for wich REE-zun*
**Русский:** по каковой причине; вследствие чего — книжн., юр.: развёрнутый эквивалент wherefore
**Type:** relative connective — pied-piped preposition + relative determiner + noun; functionally a continuative relative &middot; **Register:** formal; judicial opinions, contract recitals, and old-fashioned expository prose
**Clause type:** nonrestrictive relative clause whose antecedent is the whole preceding clause
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *for which reason* [clause]. &middot; [noun of cause], *for which reason* [clause]. &middot; *For which reason*, [clause] — a fresh sentence, which only legal drafting does. &middot; *for which purpose* / *for which cause* — the same frame with the noun changed.
**Punctuation:** **A comma before it** — it opens a nonrestrictive relative clause that cannot stand on its own. This is the trap: it means the same thing as *therefore*, so writers reach for the *therefore* pointing and produce the wrong mark. *Therefore* is a conjunctive adverb and needs a **semicolon or a period**; *for which reason* is a relative and takes a **comma**, exactly like *whereupon*. When legal drafting does start a sentence with it, a comma follows it as well (*For which reason, the motion is denied.*).
**Variants:** [*wherefore*](#wherefore) (the one-word archaic equivalent, and punctuated the opposite way); *for which purpose*, *for which cause*, *on which account*; *therefore*, *hence*, *which is why* (the living substitutes)

The transparent, unpacked ancestor of *wherefore* — every *where-* compound is exactly "preposition + which," and this is what that one looks like spelled out. It has outlived the compound in the places where a writer needs to hang a consequence on a whole clause without starting a new sentence: opinions, recitals, and careful academic prose. Against *which is why*, which does the same job in ordinary English, *for which reason* is heavier by design.

1. The notice was mailed to an address the company had abandoned two years earlier, *for which reason* the thirty-day period never began to run.
2. Two of the three appraisals were prepared by the seller's own broker, *for which reason* the court gave them little weight.
3. He had signed the waiver before reading a word of it, *for which reason* his objection came far too late.
4. The samples were stored well above the specified temperature, *for which reason* the results are reported here only as provisional.
5. The statute contains no severability clause, *for which reason* the whole section falls with the invalid subsection.
6. The witness had been in the room the entire time, *for which reason* her account was preferred to his.

**See also:** [wherefore](#wherefore), [which](#which), [whereupon](#whereupon), [why](#why), [in which](#in-which)

### he that

**Pronunciation:** /hi ˈðæt/ &middot; weak /hi ðət/ &middot; *hee-THAT*
**Русский:** тот, кто — устар., библ.: предшественник whoever, без родовой нейтральности
**Type:** fused-relative frame — a bare pronoun head plus relative *that*; equivalent to the modern one-word fused relative *whoever* &middot; **Register:** archaic; scripture, hymnody, proverb, and legal maxim — an American meets it in the King James Bible, in Shakespeare, in Franklin, and in the older sayings of the law
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause with a pronoun head; the whole phrase functions as a noun phrase
**Pattern:** *He that* [verb phrase] + [main verb] — the phrase as subject. &middot; *He that* [verb phrase], [imperative or resumptive clause] — the older pointing, licensed by a resumptive *him* or *let him*. &middot; *They that* [verb phrase] + [main verb] — plural. &middot; [verb/preposition] *him that* [verb phrase] — oblique case on the head. &middot; *He who* [verb phrase] — the same frame with *who*, and the only version still written today.
**Punctuation:** **Never a comma between the head pronoun and *that*** — they are one phrase. The comma that period texts put after a long subject clause (*He that hath ears to hear, let him hear*) is licensed there by the resumptive *let him*; without a resumptive it is an error, so write *He that hesitates is lost* with no comma at all.
**Variants:** *they that*, *she that*, *those that*, *him that*, *them that* (the same frame inflected for number and case); *he who*, *those who* (the *who* version, still current); *whoever*, *anyone who* (the living equivalents); nonstandard *them that* survives in dialect (*them that has, gets*)

Before *whoever* took the job over, English said *he that*: a bare pronoun head with a relative clause hung on it, the whole thing meaning "any person who." Three habitats keep it alive — scripture and hymn (*he that hath ears*), proverb (*he that goes a-borrowing goes a-sorrowing*), and the maxims of equity (*he that seeks equity must do equity*). It has no gender-neutral form, which is one reason modern prose moved on to *whoever* and *anyone who*.

1. "*He that* is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her."
2. *He that* goes a-borrowing goes a-sorrowing.
3. "*They that* wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength."
4. *He that* comes into equity must come with clean hands.
5. Give the notice to *him that* holds the lease, not to the occupant.
6. *They that* sow the wind shall reap the whirlwind.

**See also:** [that](#that), [whoever](#whoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [who](#who), [the which](#the-which)

### in which

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; weak /ən ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *in-WICH*
**Русский:** в котором — книжн.: предлог вынесен вперёд; в разговорной речи он уходит в конец
**Type:** relative pronoun with a pied-piped preposition &middot; **Register:** formal; the stranded alternative (*the box it came in*) is the neutral spoken form and is equally correct
**Clause type:** relative clause — restrictive after a bare noun, nonrestrictive after a comma
**Pattern:** [noun] *in which* [subject] [verb] — restrictive. &middot; [noun], *in which* [clause], [rest of sentence] — nonrestrictive. &middot; [noun] [that / ∅] [subject] [verb] *in* — the stranded equivalent. &middot; the identical frame with any preposition: *of which*, *to which*, *for which*, *on which*, *with which*, *at which*, *by which*, *from which*, *under which*, *through which*, *upon which*.
**Punctuation:** ✗ *Never a comma between the preposition and *which*** — the two move as one unit and belong to the same phrase. The clause itself follows the ordinary rule: no commas when it narrows which thing is meant (*the cabinet in which the contracts were stored*), commas on both sides when the thing is already identified (*the north cabinet, in which the contracts were stored, was never locked*). And note what the fronted preposition forbids: it ✗ *blocks *that*** and it **blocks the zero relative**, so *the box in that it came* and *the box in it came* are both impossible.
**Variants:** *of which*, *to which*, *for which*, *on which*, *with which*, *at which*, *by which*, *from which*, *under which* (the same construction with the preposition changed); the stranded *that … in* / ∅ *… in* (the ordinary spoken form); *wherein*, *whereof*, *whereto*, *whereon*, *wherewith* (the archaic one-word compounds for the same thing); *of which* doubles as the stiff possessive alternative to *whose*

Fronting the preposition along with *which* is the formal option; leaving it at the end of the clause is the normal spoken one. Both are correct American English — the old rule against ending a sentence with a preposition was never a rule of the language — so the choice is register, not grammar. What is real is the constraint the fronting creates: once a preposition stands in front, only *which* or *whom* can follow it, which means choosing the formal word order also forces the formal relative pronoun. *Of which* is worth a separate look, since it does double duty as the inanimate possessive (*a house the roof of which leaks*), where most writers now prefer *whose*.

1. This is the cabinet *in which* the old contracts were stored.
2. The year *in which* the plant closed is still a sore subject in this town.
3. She described the conditions *under which* the samples had been held.
4. The account *to which* the wire was sent had been opened that same morning.
5. He kept a list of every town *through which* the line had once run.
6. There were four bidders, *of which* only two submitted complete packages.
7. The tools *with which* the repair was made were not the ones the manual specifies.

**See also:** [which](#which), [whom](#whom), [wherein](#wherein), [whereof](#whereof), [the manner in which](#the-manner-in-which), [zero relative](#zero-relative)

### that

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt/ &middot; weak and usual /ðət/ &middot; *THAT* &middot; *thuht*
**Русский:** который, что — относительное только в ограничительных придаточных, без запятой
**Type:** relative pronoun; also a complementizer, a demonstrative determiner, and a degree adverb &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** **restrictive** relative clause only; as a complementizer, a noun clause
**Pattern:** [noun] *that* [verb phrase] — subject relative, and *that* cannot be deleted. &middot; [noun] *that* [subject] [verb] — object relative, and *that* may be deleted. &middot; [noun] *that* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded preposition, as in *the chair that she sat on*. &middot; [noun] ∅ [subject] [verb] — the zero relative, object position only.
**Punctuation:** **Never a comma before relative *that*.** A comma marks a clause as nonrestrictive, and *that* cannot introduce a nonrestrictive clause; if the sentence wants a comma there, the word must be *which* (things) or *who* (people). *That* also cannot follow a preposition — *the chair on that she sat* is impossible; use *on which* or strand the preposition.
**Variants:** the [zero relative](#zero-relative) ∅ in object position (*the book I read*)

The default restrictive relative, usable for people, things, and animals alike. Against *which*: American editors keep *that* for clauses that narrow the reference and *which* for clauses that add — the comma and the word choice move together. Against *who*: both are correct for people restrictively, but *who* is preferred in formal writing. And note that the relative *that* is a different word from the complementizer *that* in *I know that he left*: the complementizer fills no gap and has no antecedent.

1. The apartment *that* we looked at on Tuesday is already rented.
2. Anything *that* can go wrong on a Friday afternoon will.
3. She's the only person *that* still remembers how the old phone system worked.
4. This is the road *that* floods every single spring.
5. Nothing *that* the committee proposed made it into the final budget.
6. The one *that* got away was a twelve-pound striper.
7. This is the shelf *that* everything eventually gets dumped on.

**See also:** [which](#which), [who](#who), [what](#what), [whose](#whose), [as](#as), [zero relative](#zero-relative)

### the conditions under which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə kənˈdɪʃənz ˌʌndər ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh kun-DISH-unz UN-der WICH*
**Русский:** условия, при которых — книжн., научн.: предлог вынесен вперёд
**Type:** complex relative frame — head noun + pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal; scientific, technical, regulatory, and legal prose
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause modifying *conditions*; the whole phrase serves as the subject or the object of the main clause
**Pattern:** *the conditions under which* [subject] [verb] + [main verb] — the phrase as subject. &middot; [verb of specifying/describing] *the conditions under which* [clause] — as object. &middot; [preposition] *the conditions under which* [clause]. &middot; *the circumstances under which* / *the terms under which* / *the rules under which* — the same frame with the head noun changed.
**Punctuation:** No comma anywhere inside the frame, and — the one that gets missed — **no comma between the frame and the main verb** when the whole phrase is the subject: *The conditions under which the test was run are not stated* takes nothing before *are*, however long the clause has grown. The relative clause is restrictive, so it does not open with a comma either.
**Variants:** *the circumstances under which*, *the terms under which*, *the conditions in which*; *the conditions whereunder* (archaic legal); *when* or *how* (the lean substitutes an editor will suggest)

A fixed technical frame rather than a single connector: *under which* is the relative proper and *the conditions* is the head noun it modifies. It earns a place because it does something no single *wh-* word does — *specify the conditions under which the warranty applies* asks for the entire set of qualifying situations, where *specify when the warranty applies* asks only for an occasion. That is a real distinction, and it is why the phrase resists the editing that its bulk invites.

1. The paper never states *the conditions under which* the samples were stored.
2. Section 4 sets out *the conditions under which* the deposit becomes nonrefundable.
3. *The conditions under which* the experiment was run bear no resemblance to a working kitchen.
4. We should settle in advance *the conditions under which* either side may walk away.
5. The manual is silent as to *the conditions under which* the alarm resets itself.
6. She wanted *the conditions under which* she would be released put in writing.

**See also:** [in which](#in-which), [the extent to which](#the-extent-to-which), [the manner in which](#the-manner-in-which), [which](#which), [where](#where)

### the degree to which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə dɪˈɡri tə ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh dih-GREE tuh WICH*
**Русский:** степень, в какой; насколько — книжн.: мера, поддающаяся оценке
**Type:** complex relative frame — noun of measure + pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal; academic, analytical, and evaluative prose
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause modifying *degree*; the whole phrase behaves as a noun phrase, usually the subject or the object of a verb of measuring or judging
**Pattern:** *The degree to which* [subject] [verb] + [main verb] — the phrase as subject. &middot; [verb of measuring/assessing] *the degree to which* [clause] — as object. &middot; [preposition] *the degree to which* [clause] — as in *depends on the degree to which*.
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the frame, none before *to which*, and none between the whole phrase and the main verb when the phrase is the subject. The clause is restrictive throughout.
**Variants:** [*the extent to which*](#the-extent-to-which) (the commoner near-twin); *the degree of* + noun (the nominal version); *how far*, *how much*, *how well* (the plain substitutes)

Names an amount and then defines it with a clause: *the degree to which the model fits* is "how well the model fits," packaged so it can sit in a subject slot. Against *the extent to which*, its nearest neighbor: *degree* leans toward intensity on a graded scale and *extent* toward reach or coverage, but in most sentences either will serve, and *extent* is the more common of the two in American academic writing.

1. *The degree to which* the two accounts agree is itself the surprising part.
2. Nobody has measured *the degree to which* the subsidy actually changed behavior.
3. The finding depends entirely on *the degree to which* respondents understood the question.
4. *The degree to which* she had prepared for that hearing was obvious within five minutes.
5. The report says nothing about *the degree to which* the estimate is sensitive to the discount rate.
6. Reviewers disagreed about *the degree to which* the method was genuinely novel.

**See also:** [the extent to which](#the-extent-to-which), [in which](#in-which), [the manner in which](#the-manner-in-which), [which](#which)

### the extent to which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ɪkˈstɛnt tə ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh ik-STENT tuh WICH*
**Русский:** то, насколько; мера, в какой — книжн.: стандартная осторожная формула
**Type:** complex relative frame — noun of measure + pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal; the standard hedge of academic, scientific, and official American prose
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause modifying *extent*; the whole phrase behaves as a noun phrase
**Pattern:** *The extent to which* [subject] [verb] + [main verb] — the phrase as subject. &middot; [verb of measuring/assessing] *the extent to which* [clause] — as object. &middot; [preposition] *the extent to which* [clause]. &middot; *to the extent that* [clause] — the related conditional frame, which is a different construction and lives with the [comparison words](08-comparison.md#to-the-degree-that).
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the frame and none between it and the main verb when the phrase is the subject — *The extent to which the two versions differ is a matter of one clause* takes nothing before *is*. Never a comma before *to which*.
**Variants:** [*the degree to which*](#the-degree-to-which) (the near-twin); *how far*, *how much* (the plain substitutes); *the extent of* + noun (the nominal version)

The workhorse of the *which* frames, and the phrase American academic prose reaches for when it wants to ask *how much* without committing to an answer. It is a genuine hedge rather than mere padding: *the extent to which the ruling still controls* concedes in advance that the answer is partial, where *whether the ruling still controls* forces a yes or no. That is why editors who cut it to *how far* sometimes change the claim.

1. *The extent to which* the contamination had spread was not known for weeks.
2. The study measures *the extent to which* households actually changed their driving.
3. *The extent to which* the two versions differ comes down to a single clause.
4. The auditors asked about *the extent to which* the estimates relied on management's own projections.
5. It is hard to overstate *the extent to which* that one decision shaped the department.
6. The brief argues about *the extent to which* the earlier ruling still controls.

**See also:** [the degree to which](#the-degree-to-which), [in which](#in-which), [the conditions under which](#the-conditions-under-which), [which](#which)

### the manner in which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈmænər ɪn ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh MAN-er in WICH*
**Русский:** то, как; способ, которым — книжн., юр.; стилисты советуют заменять на how
**Type:** complex relative frame — noun of manner + pied-piped preposition + relative pronoun &middot; **Register:** formal; legal, official, and old-fashioned expository prose — and the standing example, in every American style guide, of a phrase that ought to be *how*
**Clause type:** restrictive relative clause modifying *manner*; the whole phrase behaves as a noun phrase
**Pattern:** *The manner in which* [subject] [verb] + [main verb] — the phrase as subject. &middot; [verb of describing/objecting to] *the manner in which* [clause] — as object. &middot; [preposition] *the manner in which* [clause] — as in *as to the manner in which*. &middot; *in the manner in which* [clause] — the doubled form, best avoided. &middot; *the manner whereby* / *the manner wherein* — archaic.
**Punctuation:** No comma inside the frame; none between the frame and the main verb when the phrase is the subject. The clause is restrictive, so it never opens with a comma.
**Variants:** *the way in which* (a shade lighter, same construction); *the way (that)* (informal); *how* (the one-word equivalent, available in nearly every sentence either of the others appears in); *the manner whereby*, *the manner wherein* (archaic)

The bulkiest of the *which* frames: *the manner in which he answered* is four words doing the work of *how he answered*. It survives for two honest reasons — it is genuinely more formal, and it lets a complaint, an opinion, or a personnel file describe conduct at arm's length without seeming to characterize it. Everywhere else it is padding, and *how* is the fix.

1. The complaint objects to *the manner in which* the search was conducted.
2. *The manner in which* the layoffs were announced did more damage than the layoffs.
3. The statute prescribes *the manner in which* notice must be given to a tenant.
4. She testified at length about *the manner in which* the samples had been handled.
5. *The manner in which* he answered that one question told the panel everything.
6. The bylaws are silent as to *the manner in which* a tie vote is to be broken.

**See also:** [in which](#in-which), [the conditions under which](#the-conditions-under-which), [wherein](#wherein), [which](#which), [the way](08-comparison.md#the-way)

### the which

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *thuh WICH*
**Русский:** каковой, который — устар. (Чосер, Шекспир): по смыслу ничем не отличается от which
**Type:** relative pronoun carrying a definite article &middot; **Register:** archaic; Middle and Early Modern English, and after about 1700 a deliberate costume — an American meets it in Chaucer, Malory, the 1611 Bible, and Shakespeare
**Clause type:** relative clause, restrictive or nonrestrictive; also sentential, with a whole clause as antecedent
**Pattern:** [noun], *the which* [verb phrase] — nonrestrictive. &middot; [noun] *the which* [subject] [verb] — restrictive. &middot; [preposition] *the which* [clause] — as in *of the which*, *in the which*. &middot; [clause], *the which* [clause] — sentential, the whole preceding clause as antecedent.
**Punctuation:** Pointed exactly like plain *which*: commas around a clause that merely adds, none around one that narrows. Be warned that Early Modern printing does not follow that rule reliably, so a period text will not confirm it — apply the modern rule when you write the form yourself.
**Variants:** plain *which* (the form that won and the only living one); *the whiche* (the Middle English spelling); *of the which*, *in the which* (the pied-piped versions)

English once had a definite relative *the which* running alongside bare *which*, on the model of French *lequel* and Latin *qui*. It was ordinary for Chaucer, still available to Shakespeare, and gone from living prose by the eighteenth century. Nothing separates it from plain *which* in meaning — the article marked a definiteness that English relatives no longer mark at all — so a modern writer who reaches for it is quoting a period rather than drawing a distinction.

1. He showed me the deed, *the which* I read twice before signing anything.
2. There followed a great silence, *the which* no man cared to break.
3. The king sent letters, in *the which* he named his successor.
4. She took a fever, *the which* held her a fortnight and more.
5. They came at last to a river, *the which* they could by no means cross.
6. He spoke much of his debts, of *the which* he had a great many.

**See also:** [which](#which), [that](#that), [he that](#he-that), [in which](#in-which), [whereof](#whereof)

### what

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/ &middot; also /wɑt/ &middot; weak /wət/ &middot; *WUT*
**Русский:** то, что — слитное относительное: само содержит свой антецедент («the thing which»)
**Type:** fused relative pronoun; also an interrogative and an exclamative determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative (free relative) clause — a clause that functions as a noun phrase
**Pattern:** *What* [clause] + [main verb] — the clause as subject. &middot; [verb/preposition] *what* [clause] — the clause as object. &middot; *What* [clause] *is* [complement] — the pseudo-cleft. &middot; *what* + noun (*what money we had left*).
**Punctuation:** **No comma** between a *what* clause and the verb that goes with it — the clause is the subject or the object, and neither is ever cut off from its verb by a single comma. A fronted pseudo-cleft (*What I need is a nap*) likewise takes no comma.
**Variants:** *whatever* (the emphatic free relative); *that which* (the stiff formal equivalent)

*What* is the relative with no antecedent: it swallows its own head, so *what he said* means "the thing that he said" in a single word. Contrast *which* and *that*, which both require a noun standing in front of them. Nonstandard American dialects use *what* as an ordinary relative (*the man what fixed the roof*), which is strongly marked.

1. *What* he said in the meeting is not what he told me afterward.
2. I'll have *what* she's having.
3. *What* worries me is the timeline, not the budget.
4. She gave the movers *what* was left in the garage.
5. Tell me *what* the doctor actually said, not what you wish he'd said.
6. *What* the storm didn't take, the insurance company argued about for a year.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [which](#which), [that](#that), [whoever](#whoever)

### what all

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt ˌɔl/ &middot; also /ˈwɑt ˌɔl/ &middot; casually /ˈwʌɾ ˌɔl/, with the *t* flapped &middot; *WUT-awl*
**Русский:** что именно; всё то, что — диал. (юг США): вопрос требует полного перечня
**Type:** fused relative and interrogative pronoun carrying exhaustive *all* &middot; **Register:** dialectal — Southern and South Midland American, and heard well beyond that in casual speech; absent from edited prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question, exhaustive); nominal relative clause
**Pattern:** *What all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — direct question. &middot; [verb of asking/knowing/telling] *what all* [subject] [verb] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; *What all* [verb phrase] + [main verb] — the clause as subject. &middot; *what all* + noun + [clause] — as in *what all paperwork they need*. &middot; *What* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *all*? — the *all* floated to the end.
**Punctuation:** No comma between *what* and *all*, and none before the embedded clause. A direct question takes a question mark; the embedded clause takes none.
**Variants:** [*who all*](#who-all), [*where all*](#where-all), *how all* (the same *all* on the other *wh-* words); *everything that*, *all the things (that)* (the standard paraphrases)

The *all* is not the ordinary quantifier floating loose but an exhaustiveness marker fixed to the *wh-* word: *What did he say?* invites one answer, *What all did he say?* asks for the complete list. Standard English has to paraphrase that distinction with *everything that*, which is exactly why the construction stays so stable in the regions that have it. Note that it can also head a free relative (*what all he did with the money*), where standard English would need *everything he did*.

1. *What all* did the mechanic say was wrong with it?
2. I don't know *what all* she put in that casserole.
3. Tell me *what all* I need to bring on Saturday.
4. *What all* he did with the money never came out at trial.
5. He'll tell you *what all* is wrong with this county in about four minutes.
6. Nobody had written down *what all* was promised at that meeting.

**See also:** [what](#what), [who all](#who-all), [where all](#where-all), [whatever](#whatever)

### which

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃ/ (older and Southern /ʍɪtʃ/) &middot; *WICH*
**Русский:** который; что — о предметах; в амер. норме неограничительное, с запятыми
**Type:** relative pronoun; also an interrogative determiner &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause — nonrestrictive in edited American prose, restrictive after a preposition
**Pattern:** [noun], *which* [clause], [rest of sentence]. &middot; [noun] [preposition] *which* [clause]. &middot; [independent clause], *which* [clause] — sentential relative. &middot; *which* + noun (*by which time*, *at which point*).
**Punctuation:** A nonrestrictive *which* clause is **fenced by commas on both sides** — one before it, and one after it if the sentence continues. Dropping that closing comma is the most common punctuation error with this word: *Her novel, which took eleven years to write sold four thousand copies* is wrong. When *which* follows a preposition (*the box in which it came*), the clause is restrictive and takes **no** commas.
**Variants:** *that* (restrictive equivalent, no commas); *of which* (possessive alternative to *whose*)

Refers to things, animals, and ideas — never to people, who take *who*. The American *that/which* convention: use *that* for a clause that narrows which thing you mean, and *which* with commas for a clause that adds a detail about a thing already identified. *Which* has one power *that* lacks: it can take an entire preceding clause as its antecedent (*He was late, which surprised no one*).

1. Her first novel, *which* took eleven years to write, sold four thousand copies.
2. The bridge, *which* was rebuilt in 2004, is closed for repairs again.
3. He missed the filing deadline, *which* surprised absolutely no one.
4. This is the cabinet in *which* the old contracts were stored.
5. They moved in June, by *which* time the roof was already leaking.
6. The recipe calls for pancetta, *which* you can replace with bacon.

**See also:** [that](#that), [who](#who), [whose](#whose), [wherein](#wherein), [whichever](#whichever)

### who

**Pronunciation:** /hu/ &middot; weak /hʊ/ &middot; *HOO*
**Русский:** кто; который — о людях, форма подлежащего
**Type:** relative pronoun (subject case); also an interrogative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause, restrictive or nonrestrictive; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [person noun] *who* [verb phrase] — subject relative, restrictive. &middot; [person noun], *who* [verb phrase], [rest of sentence] — subject relative, nonrestrictive. &middot; [person noun] *who* [subject] [verb] — object relative, normal in speech where formal writing takes *whom*. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *who* [clause] — indirect question.
**Punctuation:** No commas when the clause identifies which person is meant (*the woman who called*). Commas on both sides when the person is already identified (*my sister, who called Tuesday, is coming*). Because a proper name is already fully identified, a relative clause after a name is nearly always nonrestrictive and therefore takes commas.
**Variants:** *that* (acceptable restrictive alternative for people, slightly less formal); *whom* (object case); *whose* (possessive)

The people relative. In everyday American English *who* has annexed most of *whom*'s territory, so *the guy who I met* is normal speech and *the guy whom I met* is formal writing. The one place *whom* is still required is directly after a preposition.

1. The woman *who* called about the sofa never showed up.
2. My neighbor, *who* teaches at the middle school, waters our plants in August.
3. Anyone *who* wants seconds should speak up now.
4. I have no idea *who* left the back door open.
5. She's the one *who* actually fixed the sprinkler system.
6. Kids *who* eat breakfast test better — that's the entire finding.

**See also:** [whom](#whom), [whose](#whose), [that](#that), [whoever](#whoever), [which](#which)

### who all

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhu ˌɔl/ &middot; casually /ˌhuˈwɔl/, with a linking *w* &middot; *HOO-awl*
**Русский:** кто именно; все, кто — диал. (юг США): вопрос требует перечислить всех
**Type:** interrogative and relative pronoun carrying exhaustive *all* &middot; **Register:** dialectal — Southern and South Midland American; ordinary in speech across that region and absent from edited prose
**Clause type:** noun clause (indirect question, exhaustive); relative clause
**Pattern:** *Who all* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb]? — direct question. &middot; *Who all* [verb phrase]? — as subject of its own question. &middot; [verb of asking/knowing] *who all* [verb phrase] — embedded, statement word order. &middot; *who all's* + noun — the possessive, as in *who all's car is that*. &middot; *Who* [auxiliary] [subject] [verb] *all*? — the *all* floated to the end.
**Punctuation:** No comma between *who* and *all*, and none before the embedded clause. Direct questions take a question mark. Note the contracted possessive: it is written *who all's*, with the apostrophe on *all*, never *whose all*.
**Variants:** [*what all*](#what-all), [*where all*](#where-all) (the same construction on other *wh-* words); *y'all* (the same *all* fused onto *you*); *everyone who*, *which of them* (the standard paraphrases)

Asks for a full roster rather than a single name: *Who came?* can be answered with one person, *Who all came?* asks for everybody. The construction has a grammar of its own — the *all* can float away from *who* (*Who did you invite all?*), and it takes a possessive (*who all's truck is in the driveway?*) — which is why it is best analyzed as a plural interrogative pronoun rather than as a loose quantifier that happens to be standing nearby.

1. *Who all* is coming to the reunion this year?
2. I never found out *who all* signed that letter.
3. *Who all's* truck is parked behind the shed?
4. She wanted to know *who all* he'd talked to before he called her.
5. *Who all* did they end up laying off?
6. There is no record of *who all* was in the room that afternoon.

**See also:** [who](#who), [what all](#what-all), [where all](#where-all), [whoever](#whoever), [whom](#whom)

### whom

**Pronunciation:** /hum/ &middot; *HOOM*
**Русский:** кого, кому — объектная форма who; книжн., обязательна после предлога
**Type:** relative pronoun (object case); also an interrogative &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** relative clause, restrictive or nonrestrictive; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [noun] *whom* [subject] [verb] — object relative. &middot; [noun] [preposition] *whom* [clause] — pied-piped, as in *the people with whom he lived*. &middot; [noun], *whom* [clause], [rest of sentence] — nonrestrictive.
**Punctuation:** The same restrictive/nonrestrictive comma rule as *who*. Never a comma between a preposition and *whom*.
**Variants:** *who* (the normal informal substitute in every position except immediately after a preposition); *whomever*

The object form of *who*. The reliable test: rebuild the relative clause as a plain sentence and try *he* versus *him* — if *him* fits, *whom* is correct (*the committee selected him* → *the candidate whom the committee selected*). In practice *whom* is obligatory only right after a preposition; elsewhere American speech uses *who*, and overcorrecting to *whom* in subject position (*the man whom called*) is worse than using *who*.

1. To *whom* it may concern.
2. The candidate *whom* the committee selected withdrew a week later.
3. My grandmother, *whom* I saw maybe twice a year, sent a card every single week.
4. The people with *whom* he shared the house never learned his last name.
5. I have no idea *whom* they'll appoint to the vacancy.
6. She's the attorney for *whom* I clerked right out of law school.

**See also:** [who](#who), [whomever](#whomever), [whose](#whose), [that](#that)

### whose

**Pronunciation:** /huz/ &middot; *HOOZ*
**Русский:** чей; которого — притяжательное относительное, о людях и (реже) о предметах
**Type:** relative determiner (possessive); also an interrogative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause, restrictive or nonrestrictive; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [noun] *whose* [noun] [verb] — the possessed noun always follows immediately. &middot; [noun], *whose* [noun] [clause], [rest of sentence]. &middot; [preposition] *whose* [noun] [clause]. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *whose* [noun] [clause].
**Punctuation:** The standard restrictive/nonrestrictive comma rule. Never a comma between *whose* and the noun it possesses — they form one phrase.
**Variants:** *of which* (the stiff alternative for inanimate antecedents); *whosever* (rare); note that *who's* is a contraction of *who is* and is never the possessive

The possessive relative, and the only relative word that is a determiner rather than a pronoun — it must be followed by a noun. It is **not** restricted to people, despite a stubborn myth: *a house whose roof leaks* is standard American English, and the alternative *a house the roof of which leaks* is stiff enough that even careful editors avoid it.

1. The family *whose* dog got out lives two doors down.
2. My cousin, *whose* flight was canceled, ended up driving nine hours overnight.
3. It's an old building *whose* wiring nobody wants to touch.
4. Students *whose* names appear on this list should report to the front office.
5. No one could figure out *whose* car was blocking the alley.
6. A company *whose* only real asset is one patent is a risky bet.

**See also:** [who](#who), [whom](#whom), [which](#which), [whereof](#whereof)

### zero relative

**Pronunciation:** ∅ — unpronounced; the clause simply begins with its own subject
**Русский:** нулевое относительное — пропуск which/that/whom-дополнения; в русском так опустить нельзя
**Type:** relative pronoun (null) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the ordinary spoken choice
**Clause type:** **restrictive** relative clause only, and only when the missing word is the object
**Pattern:** [noun] ∅ [subject] [verb] — object relative, as in *the email I sent*. &middot; [noun] ∅ [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded preposition, as in *the chair she sat on*. &middot; [time noun] ∅ [clause] — *the day we met*. &middot; [place noun] ∅ [clause] — *the town I grew up in*. &middot; *the reason* ∅ [clause] — *the reason I left*.
**Punctuation:** **No comma, ever.** A comma would mark the clause as nonrestrictive, and a nonrestrictive clause cannot drop its relative word — *my brother, I met at the airport, was late* is not English. The gap is also the reason no comma may separate the antecedent from the clause: *the email, I sent yesterday* is an error, not a pause.
**Variants:** the overt [that](#that), [which](#which), [who](#who), [whom](#whom), [when](#when), [where](#where), [why](#why) — any of which can be restored

The commonest relative in American speech is no word at all. Deletion is licensed in exactly one environment: a restrictive clause in which the relative word would have been the **object** of the verb or of a preposition. It is blocked when the relative word would be the **subject** (*the email that arrived* — you cannot say *the email arrived* and mean the same thing), blocked in every nonrestrictive clause, and blocked after a pied-piped preposition (*the people with whom he lived*, never *the people with he lived*). The three relative adverbs delete on the same terms, which is why *the day we met* and *the town I grew up in* need no connector either.

1. The email ∅ I sent Tuesday never got a reply.
2. That's the chair ∅ she always sits in.
3. The guy ∅ we hired in March already quit.
4. It's the one thing ∅ nobody wants to talk about.
5. Remember the summer ∅ the power went out for nine days?
6. The reason ∅ I called is that the check never cleared.
7. That's not the town ∅ I grew up in.

**See also:** [that](#that), [which](#which), [whom](#whom), [when](#when), [where](#where), [why](#why), [zero *that*](10-complementizers.md#zero-that)

## Relative adverbs

A relative adverb replaces a preposition-plus-*which*: *the day when we met* = *the day on which we met*; *the town where I grew up* = *the town in which I grew up*; *the reason why he left* = *the reason for which he left*. There are exactly three, and each is locked to one kind of antecedent — *when* to a time noun, *where* to a place noun, *why* to the noun *reason*. All three follow the standard comma rule, and all three can be dropped in a restrictive clause (*the day we met*), which is what makes them feel so much lighter than *on which*.

*Where* is documented in full among the [subordinators of place](#where) above, since that is where learners meet it first.

### when

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn/ &middot; weak /wən/ (older and Southern /ʍɛn/) &middot; *WEN*
**Русский:** когда; в который — относительное наречие при существительном времени
**Type:** relative adverb; also a subordinating conjunction of time and an interrogative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause modifying a time noun; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** [time noun] *when* [clause] — restrictive. &middot; [time noun], *when* [clause], [rest of sentence] — nonrestrictive. &middot; [time noun] ∅ [clause] — relative *when* deleted. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *when* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No commas when the clause specifies which time is meant (*the day when we met*). Commas on both sides when the time is already fixed (*last Tuesday, when we met, was miserable*). Never a comma before an indirect question.
**Variants:** *that*, or nothing at all (*the day we met*); *on which / in which / at which* (formal equivalents)

This entry covers *when* as a **relative adverb** — the word that hooks a clause onto a time noun. Its far more common life as an adverbial conjunction (*Call me when you land*) belongs with the time subordinators, and the test between them is simple: a relative *when* has a time noun sitting in front of it that the clause modifies; an adverbial *when* modifies the verb and has no antecedent at all.

1. That was the summer *when* everything changed.
2. There are days *when* nothing in this building works.
3. August, *when* half the staff is on vacation, is the worst possible month to launch.
4. I remember the exact moment *when* the power came back on.
5. Nobody told us *when* the inspection was scheduled.
6. He's holding out for a time *when* interest rates finally come down.

**See also:** [where](#where), [why](#why), [which](#which), [whereupon](#whereupon)

### why

**Pronunciation:** /waɪ/ (older and Southern /ʍaɪ/) &middot; *WY*
**Русский:** почему; по которой — относительное наречие только при слове reason
**Type:** relative adverb; also an interrogative &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** relative clause modifying *reason*; noun clause (indirect question)
**Pattern:** *the reason* [*why*] [clause] — the *why* is optional. &middot; [verb of knowing/asking] *why* [clause]. &middot; *Why* [clause] + [main verb] — the clause as subject, as in *Why he left is still a mystery*. &middot; *That's why* [clause].
**Punctuation:** No comma before relative *why*, and none before an indirect question. When a *why* clause is the subject of the sentence, no comma separates it from its verb.
**Variants:** the zero relative (*the reason I left*); *that* (*the reason that I left*); *for which* (formal)

The most restricted of the three relative adverbs: its antecedent is essentially always the noun *reason*. *The reason why* is often attacked as redundant; it is not ungrammatical, and it has been in continuous use for centuries, but *the reason I left* and *why I left* are both leaner and neither loses anything.

1. The reason *why* the bridge failed is still under investigation.
2. Nobody can explain *why* the printer only jams on Mondays.
3. That is exactly *why* I keep a spare key in the garage.
4. *Why* the company waited three weeks to disclose it is the real question.
5. There's no reason *why* we can't start an hour early.
6. She never told anyone *why* she quit.

**See also:** [when](#when), [where](#where), [wherefore](#wherefore), [that](#that)

## Fused relatives (the *-ever* words)

A fused relative packs the antecedent and the relative word into one word: *whoever* means "the person who," *whatever* means "the thing that," *wherever* means "the place where." Because the head is fused in, the whole clause behaves as a **noun phrase** — it can be the subject of the main verb, the object of a verb, or the object of a preposition.

That produces the one punctuation rule worth memorizing for this group. When the *-ever* clause **fills a slot** in the main clause, there is **no comma**: *Whoever parked here is getting towed*. When the same word instead introduces a **concessive** clause meaning "no matter who/what/which," it is an adverbial, it is fronted, and it **does** take a comma: *Whatever you decide, I'll back you*. Ask whether the clause is doing the job of a noun or commenting on the sentence, and the comma follows.

Case is decided **inside** the clause, not outside it: in *Give it to whoever answers*, the word is the subject of *answers*, so *whoever* is correct despite following the preposition *to*. The place member of this family, *wherever*, is documented [among the place subordinators](#wherever) above.

The group also has an older, heavier wing built on *-soever* rather than *-ever* — *whatsoever*, *whichsoever*, *whosoever*, *whomsoever*, and the *wheresoever* filed with the place words. These are not intensified versions of the modern forms so much as period spellings of them: they carry the same grammar and the same commas, but the register jumps from neutral to scriptural or statutory, and a writer who reaches for one in ordinary prose has changed costume, not emphasis. A thinner wing still is the poetic one, in which *-ever* is elided to *-e'er* to save the line a syllable; the whole set of those is gathered under [whate'er](#whateer).

### whate'er

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛr/ &middot; also /wɑtˈɛr/ &middot; *wut-AIR* — two syllables where *whatever* has three, which is the entire reason the spelling exists
**Русский:** что бы ни — устар., поэт.: стяжённое whatever
**Type:** fused relative pronoun and determiner; also a concessive subordinator &middot; **Register:** archaic and poetic — hymn text, eighteenth- and nineteenth-century verse, and Shakespeare; in modern prose it is period costume and nothing else
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** *Whate'er* [clause] + [main verb] — clause as subject. &middot; [verb/preposition] *whate'er* [clause] — clause as object. &middot; *Whate'er* [clause], [main clause] — concessive. &middot; the same elision on the rest of the family: *whene'er* (= *whenever*), *where'er* (= *wherever*), *howe'er* (= *however*), *whoe'er* (= *whoever*).
**Punctuation:** Identical to the uncontracted forms — no comma when the clause fills a noun slot, comma after a fronted concessive clause. The apostrophe replaces the *v* of *-ever* and nothing else: there is no second apostrophe, no space, and no hyphen.
**Variants:** *whene'er*, *where'er*, *howe'er*, *whoe'er*, *whatso'er*, *whereso'er* (the rest of the elided family); *ne'er*, *e'er*, *o'er* (the same elision on ordinary words); the full forms [*whatever*](#whatever), [*wherever*](#wherever), [*whoever*](#whoever), *whenever*, *however*

Not a separate word but a metrical spelling: *-ever* collapses to *-e'er* so the line scans one syllable shorter. Each member of the set contracts a **different** base word — *whate'er* is *whatever*, *where'er* is *wherever*, *whoe'er* is *whoever*, *whene'er* is *whenever*, *howe'er* is *however* — and every one of them keeps the grammar, the clause type, and the commas of the word it stands for. They are collected under one heading here because the elision, not the individual word, is the thing to recognize: it announces verse, hymnody, or deliberate pastiche, and never anything else.

1. *Whate'er* betide, I shall not leave thee.
2. *Whene'er* he passed the churchyard gate, he lowered his eyes.
3. *Where'er* the pilgrim turned, the road ran uphill.
4. *Howe'er* the world may judge, my conscience holds.
5. *Whoe'er* thou art that readest this, pray for the mason.
6. Take *whate'er* thou wilt, and leave me but the ring.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [wherever](#wherever), [whoever](#whoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvər/ &middot; also /wɑtˈɛvər/ &middot; casually /wəˈɾɛvər/, with the *t* flapped &middot; *wut-EV-er*
**Русский:** что бы ни; всё, что — слитное относительное и уступительное
**Type:** fused relative pronoun and determiner; also a concessive subordinator &middot; **Register:** neutral; the standalone dismissive *Whatever.* is very informal
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause (acting as a noun phrase); concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** *Whatever* [clause] + [main verb] — clause as subject. &middot; [verb/preposition] *whatever* [clause] — clause as object. &middot; *Whatever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive. &middot; *whatever* + noun + [clause] (*whatever time you get in*).
**Punctuation:** **No comma** when the *whatever* clause is the subject or object of the main verb (*Whatever you decide is fine with me*). **Comma** when it is a fronted concessive adverbial (*Whatever you decide, I'll back you*). Same word, two structures, and the comma is what tells them apart.
**Variants:** *whatsoever* (emphatic, formal); *what ever* as two words only in an emphatic question (*What ever were you thinking?*)

Means "anything that" in the nominal use and "no matter what" in the concessive one. Against plain *what*: *what* is neutral and specific ("the thing that"), while *whatever* is free-choice and waves away the alternatives. Against *whichever*: *whatever* draws from an open field, *whichever* from a known set.

1. *Whatever* you decide, I'll back you all the way.
2. *Whatever* is left in the fridge on Sunday is fair game.
3. Take *whatever* you need from the supply closet.
4. *Whatever* time you get in tonight, text me.
5. He'll argue about *whatever* you put in front of him.
6. *Whatever* the survey said, the customers are obviously unhappy.

**See also:** [what](#what), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whichever](#whichever), [whoever](#whoever), [wherever](#wherever)

### whatsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌtsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; also /ˌwɑtsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *wut-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** какой бы то ни было; совершенно никакой — усилитель после отрицаемого существительного
**Type:** emphatic fused relative; in modern use chiefly a negative-polarity intensifier &middot; **Register:** formal; the negative-tag use is neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause (archaic); in modern use, no clause at all — a postmodifier attached to a negated noun
**Pattern:** *no* [noun] *whatsoever*. &middot; *none whatsoever*. &middot; *not … any* [noun] *whatsoever*. &middot; *Whatsoever* [clause], [main clause] — archaic.
**Punctuation:** No comma before it in the negative-intensifier use — it sits immediately after the noun it strengthens and belongs to the same phrase. The archaic clausal use follows the *wheresoever* rule: comma after a fronted clause.
**Variants:** *whatever* (equally correct in the negative-tag slot: *no reason whatever*); *whatso* (archaic)

Historically the emphatic form of *whatever*, built with the intensifying suffix *-soever*. In modern American English it has almost stopped being a relative pronoun: nearly all of its uses are the tag after a negated noun, where it means "at all." It must **follow** the noun — *no whatsoever reason* is not English — and it needs a negative to attach to, which is why *there is evidence whatsoever* sounds broken.

1. There is no evidence *whatsoever* that the supplement does anything.
2. She had no interest *whatsoever* in going back to that job.
3. "Any objection?" "None *whatsoever*."
4. The lease gives the tenant no right *whatsoever* to sublet the unit.
5. He offered no explanation *whatsoever* for the missing three hours.
6. "*Whatsoever* thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might."

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whoever](#whoever), [what](#what)

### whichever

**Pronunciation:** /wɪtʃˈɛvər/ &middot; *wich-EV-er*
**Русский:** какой бы ни; тот из них, который — выбор из известного набора
**Type:** fused relative pronoun and determiner; also a concessive subordinator (*Whichever way you go, allow an hour*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** [verb] *whichever* [clause] — clause as object. &middot; *Whichever* [clause] + [main verb] — clause as subject. &middot; *whichever* + noun + [clause] (*whichever route you pick*). &middot; *Whichever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive. &middot; …, *whichever* [comes first / is greater] — fixed contractual tag.
**Punctuation:** No comma when the clause is the subject or object of the main verb. Comma after a fronted concessive *whichever* clause. The contractual tag always takes a comma before it: *three years or 36,000 miles, whichever comes first*.
**Variants:** *whichsoever* (archaic, legal)

The selective free relative. *Whatever* leaves the field wide open; *whichever* picks from a known, closed set — two flavors on the counter get *whichever*, an unlimited menu gets *whatever*. Its fixed tag *whichever comes first* is standard in warranties, leases, and statutes.

1. Take *whichever* seat is open.
2. *Whichever* route you pick, you'll hit construction somewhere.
3. The warranty runs three years or 36,000 miles, *whichever* comes first.
4. He'll happily eat *whichever* one you don't want.
5. *Whichever* of the two bids comes in lower will get the contract.
6. Call the office or email us, *whichever* is easier for you.

**See also:** [whatever](#whatever), [which](#which), [whoever](#whoever), [wherever](#wherever)

### whichsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɪtʃsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *wich-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** какой бы ни — устар., юр.: усиленное whichever, в старых полисах и завещаниях
**Type:** emphatic fused relative pronoun and determiner; also a concessive subordinator &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives only in older policies, leases, and wills, and even there *whichever* has all but replaced it
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** [verb] *whichsoever* [clause] — clause as object. &middot; *whichsoever* + noun + [clause] — as in *whichsoever remedy the buyer elects*. &middot; *Whichsoever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive. &middot; …, *whichsoever* [shall first occur / is the lesser] — the contractual tag.
**Punctuation:** The *whichever* rules exactly: no comma when the clause fills a noun slot, comma after a fronted concessive clause, and **always a comma before the contractual tag** — *ten years or the death of the survivor, whichsoever shall first occur*.
**Variants:** [*whichever*](#whichever) (the living equivalent in every sense); [*whatsoever*](#whatsoever), [*whosoever*](#whosoever), [*whomsoever*](#whomsoever), [*wheresoever*](#wheresoever) (the rest of the *-soever* family)

The *-soever* partner of *whichever*, and functionally identical to it: the suffix adds period weight, not meaning or emphasis. One habitat is left to it — the closed-set tag in older insurance policies, leases, and wills (*whichsoever shall first occur*), which drafters preserve because that exact wording has been litigated for two centuries while *whichever* has not been litigated in that particular document. Outside drafting it reads as costume, exactly like *whosoever*.

1. The term ends in ten years or upon the death of the survivor, *whichsoever* shall first occur.
2. The insurer shall pay the cost of repair or the actual cash value, *whichsoever* is the lesser.
3. *Whichsoever* of the two remedies the buyer elects, the seller's liability is capped at the purchase price.
4. The executor may sell or retain the shares, *whichsoever* course he judges best for the estate.
5. Notice may be given by mail or by hand, *whichsoever* method the parties have last agreed upon.
6. *Whichsoever* road the commissioners choose, the mill will object to it.

**See also:** [whichever](#whichever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [which](#which)

### whoever

**Pronunciation:** /huˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-EV-er*
**Русский:** кто бы ни; тот, кто — слитное относительное, форма подлежащего
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (subject case); also a concessive subordinator (*Whoever calls, I'm out*) &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** *Whoever* [verb phrase] + [main verb] — clause as subject. &middot; [verb/preposition] *whoever* [verb phrase] — clause as object of the outer verb, but *whoever* is still the subject inside. &middot; *Whoever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive.
**Punctuation:** No comma between a subject *whoever* clause and the main verb, and none between a preposition and *whoever*. Comma after a fronted concessive *whoever* clause (*Whoever you talk to, get it in writing*).
**Variants:** [whosoever](#whosoever) (archaic, biblical); *whoso* (archaic); *whomever* (object case inside the clause); *whosever* (rare possessive)

Means "anyone who," "the person who," or, concessively, "no matter who." The case trap is the thing to learn: *whoever* takes its case from its job **inside its own clause**, not from the outer sentence. *Give it to whoever answers the door* is correct — *whoever* is the subject of *answers*, and the whole clause, not the single word, is the object of *to*.

1. *Whoever* parked in my spot is getting towed.
2. Give the extra ticket to *whoever* shows up first.
3. *Whoever* wrote this manual has clearly never used the machine.
4. *Whoever* you talk to over there, get it in writing.
5. She'll beat *whoever* runs against her in November.
6. *Whoever* left the milk out on the counter, please deal with it.

**See also:** [whomever](#whomever), [who](#who), [whatever](#whatever), [wherever](#wherever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever)

### whomever

**Pronunciation:** /humˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoom-EV-er*
**Русский:** кого бы ни; того, кого — книжн.: объектная форма whoever
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (object case) &middot; **Register:** formal
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomever* [subject] [verb] — object inside its own clause. &middot; [main clause] [preposition] *whomever* [subject] [verb] — as in *to whomever the client designates*, where the preposition governs the whole clause, not the pronoun. &middot; [verb] *whomever* [subject] [verb] [preposition] — stranded, as in *whomever you spoke with*.
**Punctuation:** No comma before it, and none between a preposition and *whomever*.
**Variants:** *whoever* (the ordinary spoken substitute); [*whomsoever*](#whomsoever) (archaic, legal)

Correct only when the word is the **object inside its own clause**: *She'll hire whomever the committee recommends* (object of *recommends*) versus *She'll hire whoever applies* (subject of *applies*). Because writers reach for *whomever* by reflex whenever a preposition precedes it, the commonest error in American prose is *send it to whomever answers the phone*, which should be *whoever* — the preposition governs the whole clause, not the pronoun.

1. Send the invoice to *whomever* the client designates.
2. She'll hire *whomever* the committee recommends.
3. He was willing to sell the truck to *whomever* he could find.
4. Address the letter to *whomever* you spoke with last week.
5. The award goes to *whomever* the judges pick, and there's no appeal.
6. Bring *whomever* you like to the reception — there's plenty of food.
7. The senior partner will assign the case to *whomever* she trusts most.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [whom](#whom), [who](#who), [whichever](#whichever)

### whomsoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhumsoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoom-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** кого бы ни — устар., юр.: объектная форма whosoever, в завещаниях и трастах
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (object case) &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in wills, trusts, and set formulas — never neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause
**Pattern:** [verb] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] — object inside its own clause. &middot; [preposition] *whomsoever* [subject] [verb] — as in *to whomsoever the trustee shall designate*. &middot; *Whomsoever* [subject] [verb], [main clause] — fronted concessive. &middot; *to whomsoever it may concern* — the fixed salutation.
**Punctuation:** No comma before it, and none between a preposition and *whomsoever*. A comma appears only when the clause is fronted as a concessive (*Whomsoever the committee names, the term begins in January*).
**Variants:** *whomever* (the living equivalent); *whoever* (the ordinary spoken substitute); *whosoever* (subject case)

The *-soever* partner of *whomever*, and correct on exactly the same terms: only when the word is the **object inside its own clause**. *The estate passes to whomsoever the executor names* is right, because *whomsoever* is the object of *names*; *the estate passes to whomsoever survives him* is a hypercorrection, because there the word is the subject of *survives* and *whosoever* is wanted. Outside estate drafting and deliberate period pastiche it reads as costume, not formality.

1. The residue shall be distributed to *whomsoever* the trustee may designate in writing.
2. She addressed the letter, half seriously, to *whomsoever* it might concern.
3. *Whomsoever* the committee names, the appointment takes effect in January.
4. The king granted the charter to *whomsoever* his council recommended.
5. He promised the parcel to *whomsoever* he found first.
6. The bounty is payable to *whomsoever* the sheriff shall approve.

**See also:** [whomever](#whomever), [whosoever](#whosoever), [whom](#whom), [whoever](#whoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever)

### whosoever

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhusoʊˈɛvər/ &middot; *hoo-soh-EV-er*
**Русский:** кто бы ни; всякий, кто — устар., библ.: усиленное whoever
**Type:** fused relative pronoun (subject case); also a concessive subordinator &middot; **Register:** archaic; survives in scripture, hymn text, and legal drafting — never neutral
**Clause type:** nominal relative clause; concessive adverbial clause
**Pattern:** *Whosoever* [verb phrase] + [main verb] — clause as subject. &middot; [verb/preposition] *whosoever* [verb phrase] — clause as object of the outer verb, with *whosoever* still the subject inside. &middot; *Whosoever* [clause], [main clause] — concessive. &middot; *to whomsoever* [clause] — the object partner, used after a preposition that governs the pronoun itself.
**Punctuation:** No comma between a subject *whosoever* clause and the main verb, and none between a preposition and *whosoever*. Comma after a fronted concessive clause, exactly as with *whoever*. The King James comma after the subject clause (*Whosoever believeth in him, should not perish*) is seventeenth-century practice and is an error today.
**Variants:** *whoever* (the living equivalent in every sense); [*whomsoever*](#whomsoever) (object case); *whoso* (older still); *whosesoever* (rare possessive)

The *-soever* member of the *whoever* family, built with the same intensifying suffix as *wheresoever* and *whatsoever*, and meaning "any person at all who." It is not a stylistic upgrade on *whoever* — it is a period costume, and outside scripture, hymnody, and statutory drafting it reads as parody. Its object form *whomsoever* follows the same rule as *whomever*: it is correct only when the pronoun is the object inside its own clause, so *to whomsoever shall apply* is a hypercorrection for *to whosoever shall apply*.

1. "*Whosoever* shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father."
2. The trust shall pay the balance to *whosoever* survives the settlor.
3. *Whosoever* drafted this indemnity clause did not read the rest of the contract.
4. *Whosoever* the next tenant turns out to be, the easement runs with the land.
5. The reward is offered to *whosoever* returns the manuscript intact.
6. She addressed the letter, half seriously, to *whomsoever* it might concern.

**See also:** [whoever](#whoever), [whomever](#whomever), [whomsoever](#whomsoever), [wheresoever](#wheresoever), [whatsoever](#whatsoever)
