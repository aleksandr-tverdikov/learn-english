# Subordinators of cause and reason

> The words that hook a reason onto a statement — everything in English that answers *why*.

[← The grammar of conjunctions](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

This category holds the words that take a full clause and demote it into the reason for something else. They range from the three plain workhorses (*because*, *since*, *as*) through a large family of phrasal subordinators built on *that* (*given that*, *seeing that*, *on the grounds that*) and the ordinary conjunctions with a focusing adverb welded on the front (*not least because*, *especially since*) to the spoken frames that carry a reason without joining anything (*that's because*, *you see*) and the legal and archaic survivors (*inasmuch as*, *forasmuch as*, *for why*, the *WHEREAS* of contract recitals). What separates them is not meaning so much as **strength and stance**: *because* asserts the reason as news, *since* and *as* treat it as already agreed, *considering that* asks you to weigh it, and *on the grounds that* merely reports the reason somebody gave.

Two rules run through the whole set. **Position and comma:** a reason clause placed *before* the main clause always takes a comma after it; placed *after*, it usually takes none — except with *since*, *as*, and the parenthetical phrasals, where a comma is normal because the clause is background rather than the point. A handful of members are locked to one position and cannot be fronted at all: the coordinator *for*, and the subordinators *in that*, *for the reason that*, and *by reason that*; *on the grounds that* and *on the basis that* front only rarely, in legal and academic prose. **Clause vs. noun phrase:** every conjunction here needs a subject and a verb after it, and many of them have a prepositional twin that needs a noun instead — *because* / *because of*, *due to the fact that* / *due to*, *given that* / *given*. Mixing them (`because of it snowed`) is the most common error in the category, so the prepositions are catalogued in the last section, clearly marked as not conjunctions.

One rule that governs the neighboring categories barely applies here. The comma before the coordinator *for* is obligatory, exactly as it is before *and* or *but* joining two independent clauses — but no headword in this file is a conjunctive adverb, so the semicolon rule that governs *therefore*, *consequently*, and *as a result* comes up only once, in the warning attached to the preposition *as a result of*. Those words point at an effect, not a reason, and they live in the [purpose and result](05-purpose-result.md) file. Where one of them stands between two independent clauses it requires a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a comma splice: `The pass was closed, therefore we drove around` has to be *The pass was closed; therefore, we drove around* or *The pass was closed. Therefore, we drove around.*


## The everyday reason subordinators

The three plain words, the two that add a time element, and the one that turns a situation somebody is already in into grounds for a request — these six cover the great majority of reason clauses spoken in a day.


### as

**Pronunciation:** /æz/, reduced /əz/ &middot; *AZ, uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating (also a preposition — *works as a nurse* — a degree adverb in the frame *as … as*, and a relative pronoun after *such* and *the same*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** *As* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *as* clause. A comma is normal when it follows too, because the *as* clause supplies background rather than the point being made.

The weakest and most easily misread of the three plain reason words: it presents the reason as something the listener already knows. *As* is the hardest-working word in this file across the parts of speech — besides cause it marks time (*as I was leaving*), manner (*do as I do*), and comparison (*as tall as you*), and outside the conjunctions it is a preposition of role or capacity (*she serves as treasurer*), the degree adverb of the *as … as* frame, and a relative pronoun after *such* and *the same* (*the same story as I heard*). A causal *as* clause can therefore be genuinely ambiguous, and Americans use it for cause far less than the British do, so *because* or *since* is usually the safer choice.

1. *As* it was already dark, we decided to camp where we were.
2. I brought an extra chair, *as* I figured we'd be short.
3. *As* neither of us had eaten, we stopped at a diner outside Reno.
4. She was excused from the meeting, *as* she had a class at four.
5. *As* the roads hadn't been plowed, the district canceled school.
6. He didn't bother knocking, *as* the door was already open.

**See also:** [because](#because), [since](#since), [for](#for), [now that](#now-that), [as long as](#as-long-as), [as (time sense)](03-time.md#as)

### as long as

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈlɔŋ əz/, reduced /əz ˈlɔŋ əz/ &middot; *az-LAWNG-uhz, uhz-LAWNG-uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal; the same three words also build clauses of time and of condition) &middot; **Register:** neutral, conversational  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause ("since," "given that")  
**Pattern:** *As long as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as long as* [clause]. &middot; *So long as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *so long as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *long as* [clause]. (informal speech)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *as long as* clause. In this causal sense a comma is normal when the clause follows as well, because it states a situation both speakers already take as given rather than the point of the sentence. That comma is one of the things that separates the causal use from the conditional one, which takes none: *I'll lend you the truck as long as you fill the tank* (condition) versus *I'll lend you the truck, as long as you're headed that way anyway* (cause).  
**Variants:** *so long as* (interchangeable here, a shade more formal), *long as* (the *as* dropped — informal speech only)

Takes a situation the listener is already in and turns it into grounds for a request or a decision: "since you're there anyway." Three different constructions share the same three words — duration (*as long as the music played*), condition (*as long as you pay on time*), and cause (*As long as you're up, get the door*) — and only the causal one can be swapped for *since* without changing the sentence. Look at what the clause attaches to: the causal reading is the one that heads for an imperative, an offer, or a suggestion, and it is common enough in conversation that Americans rarely register it as a separate sense at all.

1. *As long as* you're going to the store anyway, would you pick up milk?
2. We might as well repaint the whole hallway, *as long as* the furniture is already out.
3. *So long as* the ladder's up there, let's do the gutters too.
4. I'll knock out the taxes tonight, *as long as* I've got the folder open.
5. *As long as* we're being honest, I never liked that couch either.
6. Grab me one too, *long as* you're already in line.

**See also:** [since](#since), [seeing that](#seeing-that), [now that](#now-that), [as long as (time sense)](03-time.md#as-long-as), [as long as (conditional sense)](06-condition.md#as-long-as)

### because

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkɔz/, /bɪˈkɑz/, /bɪˈkʌz/, reduced /bəˈkəz/, /kəz/ &middot; *bih-KAWZ, bih-KAHZ, bih-KUZ, reduced buh-KUZ, kuz*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *because* [clause]. &middot; *Because* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; *It is because* [clause] *that* [clause]. (cleft) &middot; [main clause] not *because* [clause] but *because* [clause]. &middot; (as a whole answer) "Why?" "*Because* [clause]."  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *because* clause. No comma when it follows — with two exceptions. Use a comma when the main clause is negative and the reason attaches to the whole negative (*He didn't quit, because the money was good* = he stayed; *He didn't quit because the money was good* = he quit, for some other reason), and use one when the clause explains why you are *saying* it rather than why it happened (*It must have frozen, because the birdbath is solid*).  
**Variants:** *'cause*, *cuz*, *coz*, *cos* (British spelling)

The default, unambiguous reason word: it answers *why* directly and presents the reason as the new, important information. *Since* and *as* offer a reason as already accepted; *because* puts the weight on it, which is why it alone can stand as a complete answer to a *why* question, and why it is the only reason conjunction here that a cleft can focus (*It's because the pass is closed that we're going around*; the prepositions do it too, but *since*, *as*, and *for* cannot). In formal writing avoid *the reason is because* — write *the reason is that*.

1. We canceled the picnic *because* it rained all morning.
2. *Because* the bridge was out, the detour added an hour.
3. "Why are you up so early?" "*Because* the dog decided it was time."
4. It must have frozen overnight, *because* the birdbath is solid.
5. They didn't complain, *because* the manager comped the whole meal.
6. *Because* you asked so nicely, I'll think about it.

**See also:** ['cause](#cause), [because of](#because-of), [since](#since), [as](#as), [for](#for), [due to the fact that](#due-to-the-fact-that)

### 'cause

**Pronunciation:** /kəz/, /kʌz/ &middot; *kuz*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *'cause* [clause]. &middot; *'Cause* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Same as *because*: comma after a fronted clause, none when it follows. The apostrophe spelling *'cause* is the form used in edited dialogue and lyrics; *cuz* and *coz* are texting spellings, and *cos* is British.  
**Variants:** *cause* (no apostrophe), *cuz*, *coz*, *'cos* / *cos* (British)

The ordinary spoken reduction of *because*, so routine in American speech that most people do not hear it as slang — but writing it marks the text as casual, and it has no place in formal prose. Note that *cuz* is also an unrelated noun meaning "cousin" or "buddy"; only context tells them apart.

1. I'm heading out early *'cause* traffic on the 5 is brutal after four.
2. *'Cause* I said so, that's why.
3. Sorry, can't make it — *cuz* I'm on call this weekend.
4. We turned around *'cause* the trail was still under snow.
5. He's not answering, *'cause* his phone's been dead since noon.
6. She only stayed *'cause* her ride was late.

**See also:** [because](#because), [on account of](#on-account-of), [being that](#being-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that)

### now that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnaʊ ðət/, full /ˈnaʊ ðæt/ &middot; *NOW-thut, NOW-THAT*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal; bare *now* is otherwise the ordinary time adverb) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause with a time element  
**Pattern:** *Now that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *now that* [clause]. &middot; *Now* [clause], [main clause]. (with *that* dropped)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *now that* clause; normally no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *now* (with *that* dropped — informal but very common)

Gives a reason that has only just become true — "because, and this is a new situation." *Because* is neutral about timing; *now that* insists that something changed and that the main clause follows from the change, which is why it cannot introduce a permanent fact (`Now that water boils at 212 degrees...` is wrong).

1. *Now that* the semester is over, I can finally sleep in.
2. The commute is easy *now that* they opened the second lane.
3. *Now that* you mention it, I did hear something last night.
4. She can drive herself to practice *now that* she has her license.
5. *Now that* the kids are both in school, the house is unnervingly quiet.
6. *Now* you're here, we can actually start.

**See also:** [since](#since), [because](#because), [seeing that](#seeing-that), [given that](#given-that), [once](03-time.md#once)

### since

**Pronunciation:** /sɪns/ &middot; *SINS*  
**Type:** subordinating (also a preposition — *since Monday* — and an adverb — *he has since retired*) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (also of time — see the time category)  
**Pattern:** *Since* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *since* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *since* clause. A comma is usual when it follows as well — the *since* clause is background, not the news — and that is exactly where it differs from *because*, which normally takes no comma in that position.  
**Variants:** *ever since* (time sense only — it can never mean "because")

Offers a reason the listener is expected to accept already, leaving the news in the main clause. It is the same word as temporal *since*, and with a past-tense clause the two readings collide: *Since you took over, things have gone smoothly* can mean "from the time you took over" or "because you took over." *Ever since* forces the time reading; *because* forces the reason reading. The word works in three classes and only the conjunction is at issue here: it is also a preposition taking a noun (*since Monday*, *since the merger*) and an adverb standing alone (*She left in March and has not called since*; *he has since retired*) — neither of which ever carries the causal sense.

1. *Since* you're already up, could you get the door?
2. We may as well split a cab, *since* we're both headed downtown.
3. *Since* nobody objected, the motion carried.
4. I'll take the middle seat, *since* I'm the shortest.
5. *Since* the recipe calls for buttermilk and we have none, I'm improvising.
6. He handled the introductions, *since* he was the only one who knew everybody.

**See also:** [because](#because), [as](#as), [now that](#now-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that), [since (time sense)](03-time.md#since)

## The one coordinator of reason

Every other word in this file subordinates. This one joins two clauses as equals, and it behaves accordingly.


### for

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr/, weak /fər/ &middot; *FOR, fer*  
**Type:** coordinating (the same spelling is far more often a preposition — *a gift for you*, *fined for speeding* — and it is also the subordinator that introduces infinitive clauses — *for her to leave now would be rude*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary  
**Clause type:** coordinate clause giving a reason  
**Pattern:** [independent clause], *for* [independent clause]. — medial position only, and it joins nothing smaller than a clause.  
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma — or by a semicolon if either half already contains commas. The comma is not optional the way it can be before a short *and*: without it the word reads as the preposition (*He waited for the bus* versus *He waited, for the bus was late*). It can never be fronted: `For it was late, we left` is ungrammatical, and opening a sentence with *For* in this sense reads as biblical.  
**Variants:** None as a conjunction. The far more common *for* is a preposition (*a gift for you*), and *for* also introduces infinitive clauses (*for her to leave now would be rude*).

The one FANBOYS coordinator that gives a reason. It means roughly *because*, but it joins two clauses as equals instead of demoting one, and it always trails the clause it explains. In American speech it is rare and distinctly bookish — *because* or *since* is the ordinary choice — and it must not be confused with the preposition *for* (*this is for you*).

1. I trusted her, *for* she had never lied to me.
2. We turned back, *for* the light was failing and the trail was unmarked.
3. He said nothing, *for* there was nothing left to say.
4. The town emptied out every August, *for* the mills shut down for maintenance.
5. Bring a coat, *for* it will be cold by the time we come back.
6. She read the letter twice, *for* she could not believe the first reading.

**See also:** [because](#because), [since](#since), [as](#as), [for (as one of the FANBOYS)](01-coordinating.md#for), [and](01-coordinating.md#and), [so](01-coordinating.md#so)

## Reason subordinators with a focusing adverb

Not new conjunctions but *because*, *since*, and *as* with an adverb welded onto the front, and the adverb does real work: it says how this reason ranks against the others the sentence has not mentioned. *Not least because* picks out the biggest one, *if only because* concedes the smallest one that still settles the matter, *especially since* raises the weight of a reason the listener already grants, and *the more so as* turns up the degree of the main clause itself. All four are supplementary rather than integral, which is why every one of them takes a comma in front of it even where the bare conjunction inside it would take none, and why three of the four cannot be fronted at all.


### especially since

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈspɛʃəli sɪns/, reduced /ɪˈspɛʃli sɪns/ &middot; *ih-SPESH-uh-lee-SINS, ih-SPESH-lee-SINS*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal — the focusing adverb *especially* plus the reason subordinator *since* or *as*) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (a reason singled out as the weightiest)  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *especially since* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *especially as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *particularly since* [clause]. &middot; (as a follow-up fragment) *Especially since* [clause]. — following position only; there is no fronted `Especially since [clause], [main clause]`.  
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, since it hangs a reason off a clause that is already complete. It cannot be fronted, but it can stand alone as a fragment in conversation, tacked onto something the other speaker just said.  
**Variants:** *especially as* (a shade more formal, and commoner in British English), *particularly since*, *particularly as*, *all the more since*

Marks its reason as the one that matters most among several — "and this above all." The *since* or *as* inside it keeps the "you already accept this" flavor of those words, so the fact it points at should be one the listener will grant without argument; a reason that is genuinely news wants *not least because* instead.

1. We should leave now, *especially since* the last train is at eleven.
2. I wouldn't repaint the hallway, *especially as* that wall is coming down in the spring.
3. The fee seems steep, *especially since* the first consultation used to be free.
4. She deserves the credit, *especially since* nobody else would touch the project.
5. Skipping the meeting is a bad idea, *especially since* your name is on the agenda.
6. "We could just walk." "Sure — *especially since* parking down there is impossible."

**See also:** [since](#since), [as](#as), [not least because](#not-least-because), [the more so as](#the-more-so-as), [considering that](#considering-that)

### if only because

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ˈoʊnli bɪˈkɔz/, /ɪf ˈoʊnli bɪˈkʌz/ &middot; *if-OHN-lee-bih-KAWZ*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal — the focusing *if only* plus *because*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal, chiefly written  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (a minimal or conceded reason)  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *if only because* [clause]. &middot; *If only because* [clause], [main clause]. (less common) &middot; [main clause], *if only for* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *if only to* [infinitive]. (purpose, not reason)  
**Punctuation:** A comma before it when it follows, because it is an afterthought rather than the point; a comma after the clause on the rare occasions it is fronted. Do not read it as the wish construction *if only* (*If only we'd left earlier*), which introduces no reason at all — here *if only* is a unit meaning "for this reason at minimum."  
**Variants:** *if only for* (+ noun phrase), *if only to* (+ infinitive — a purpose, not a reason), *if for no other reason than that*

Concedes that the reason it gives is a small one and insists it is enough by itself: "for this reason at least, whatever else is or isn't true." It is the mirror image of *not least because* — that phrase names the largest reason among several, this one names the least reason that still settles the question.

1. The report is worth reading, *if only because* nobody else has bothered to add up the numbers.
2. She'll be at the hearing, *if only because* her name is on the complaint.
3. We keep the landline, *if only because* the alarm system needs it.
4. *If only because* the deadline is Friday, somebody has to make a decision today.
5. The old road is worth the detour, *if only for* the view from the top.
6. He answered every message that week, *if only because* silence would have looked worse.

**See also:** [not least because](#not-least-because), [especially since](#especially-since), [because](#because), [in that](#in-that), [if only](06-condition.md#if-only)

### not least because

**Pronunciation:** /nɑt ˈlist bɪˈkɔz/, /nɑt ˈlist bɪˈkʌz/ &middot; *not-LEEST-bih-KAWZ*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal — the focusing *not least* plus *because*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal; at home in journalism and commentary  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (one reason picked out of several)  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *not least because* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *and not least because* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *not least* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *not least on account of* [noun phrase]. — following position only.  
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma: it adds a reason to a sentence that has already finished its business. It is not fronted — `Not least because the funding lapsed, the study stalled` is not idiomatic English.  
**Variants:** *not least* (+ noun phrase), *not least on account of* (+ noun phrase), *not least in that*, *if only because* (the opposite end of the scale)

Litotes: "not the smallest reason is that." The double negative is doing understatement rather than hedging, so the reason it introduces is normally the real one — the phrase concedes that others exist and then quietly ranks this one first.

1. The bill stalled, *not least because* two committee chairs opposed it.
2. Attendance has fallen, *not least because* the shuttle stopped running in March.
3. It was a strange season, *not least because* the team's best player sat out half of it.
4. The house sold in a week, *not least because* the roof was only two years old.
5. The whole plan collapsed, *not least because* nobody had asked the tenants first.
6. Everyone was relieved when the audit finally ended, *not least* the bookkeeper.

**See also:** [especially since](#especially-since), [if only because](#if-only-because), [because](#because), [the more so as](#the-more-so-as), [in that](#in-that)

### the more so as

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈmɔr soʊ æz/, reduced /ðə ˈmɔr soʊ əz/ &middot; *thuh-MOR-soh-az, thuh-MOR-soh-uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal — the degree phrase *the more so* plus *as*, *since*, or *because*) &middot; **Register:** formal, literary  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (a reason that raises the degree of the main clause)  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *the more so as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *the more so because* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *all the more so as* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *the more so* [participial or prepositional phrase]. — following position only.  
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, and by a semicolon or a dash where the main clause already carries commas of its own. It is never fronted, and nothing separates *the more so* from the conjunction that follows it.  
**Variants:** *the more so because*, *the more so since*, *all the more so as / because*, *so much the more as* (archaic)

Says that the main clause holds, and holds to a greater degree, on account of what follows: the reason turns the dial up instead of merely explaining. It attaches almost always to a judgment rather than to a report of events, which is what keeps it in essayistic and formal prose and out of conversation.

1. The verdict was a surprise, *the more so as* the same judge had ruled the other way in March.
2. Her silence was strange, *the more so because* she had written twice that same week.
3. The delay hurt the small suppliers, *the more so as* most of them had already been paid late twice.
4. The house feels empty now, *all the more so as* the dog is gone too.
5. The oversight is hard to excuse, *the more so as* two people were paid to catch exactly this.
6. He took the criticism badly, *the more so* coming from a friend.

**See also:** [not least because](#not-least-because), [especially since](#especially-since), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that), [as](#as)

## Phrasal subordinators built on *that*

Compound conjunctions ending in *that*, most of which can drop the *that* or swap it for a noun phrase. They differ from plain *because* in what they ask you to do with the reason: weigh it, grant it, or merely note who claimed it.


### being that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbiɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *BEE-ing-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** informal, regional  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** *Being that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *being that* [clause]. &middot; *Being as (how)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *being as (how)* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; a comma is usual when it follows as well, since the clause is offered as background the listener is expected to accept, exactly as with *since*. Keep the whole family out of formal writing — editors treat it as nonstandard.  
**Variants:** *being as*, *being as how*, *bein' as how* (eye dialect)

A conversational reason marker heard across the South and Midwest and in older or rural speech generally, meaning plain *because*. *Seeing that* is its slightly more respectable cousin; *since* is the safe substitute in writing.

1. *Being that* it was a holiday, the bank was closed.
2. We took my truck, *being that* the road washes out every spring.
3. *Being as how* you're family, I'll give you the wholesale price.
4. *Being that* nobody else volunteered, the job fell to me.
5. She got the corner office, *being as* she'd been there longest.
6. *Being that* I'd never made a pie crust before, I used the store-bought one.

**See also:** [seeing that](#seeing-that), ['cause](#cause), [on account of](#on-account-of), [since](#since), [because](#because)

### considering that

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈsɪdərɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *kuhn-SID-uh-ring-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating (bare *considering* is also a preposition and a sentence-final adverb; *considering* is likewise the ordinary participle of the verb *consider*) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (weighed reason)  
**Pattern:** *Considering that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *considering that* [clause]. &middot; *Considering* [clause / noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *considering*. (clause omitted)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause, and a comma is usual when it follows, because the clause comments on the main clause rather than completing it. *That* drops freely, and bare *considering* can close a sentence with nothing after it.  
**Variants:** *considering*, *considering the fact that*

Introduces a fact that has to be weighed in judging the main clause — "in light of the fact that." Unlike *because*, it does not claim the fact caused anything; it asks the listener to grade the main clause against it, which is why it so often attaches to an assessment (*not bad, considering*).

1. *Considering that* she'd never seen the script, her reading was remarkable.
2. The car runs well, *considering that* it has 230,000 miles on it.
3. *Considering* how late we started, we made good time.
4. *Considering that* the budget was frozen in March, finishing at all was an achievement.
5. He took the news calmly, *considering*.
6. *Considering the fact that* two inspectors signed off on it, the failure is hard to explain.

**See also:** [given that](#given-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that), [in light of the fact that](#in-light-of-the-fact-that), [in view of the fact that](#in-view-of-the-fact-that), [insofar as](#insofar-as)

### for the reason that

**Pronunciation:** /fər ðə ˈrizən ðət/ &middot; *fer-thuh-REE-zuhn-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (stated grounds)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *for the reason that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for the reasons that* [clause] and [clause]. — following position only; there is no fronted `*For the reason that* [clause], [main clause]`.  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause, and it is not normally fronted. The plural *for the reasons that* introduces a list of grounds.  
**Variants:** *for the reasons that*, *for the reason(s) set forth below*

A heavy, explicit *because*, used where a decision is required to point at its grounds — denials, findings, rulings, objections. Ordinary prose has no use for it, since *because* says the same thing in one word.

1. The application was denied *for the reason that* the deadline had passed.
2. The court declined to hear the appeal *for the reason that* no federal question was presented.
3. We object *for the reason that* the question assumes facts not in evidence.
4. Coverage was rescinded *for the reason that* the applicant misstated her age.
5. The board rejected the bid *for the reasons that* it exceeded the cap and omitted the bond.
6. Her request was granted *for the reason that* no other examiner was available.

**See also:** [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [by reason that](#by-reason-that), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [because](#because)

### given that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪvən ðət/ &middot; *GIV-uhn-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating (bare *given* is a preposition taking a noun phrase, and *given* is also the past participle of *give*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (premise)  
**Pattern:** *Given that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *given that* [clause]. (comma optional) &middot; *Given* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *given* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Given the fact that* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause. When it follows, the comma is optional — use one if the clause is an afterthought or if the main clause is an assessment, as it is in most real examples. *Given* plus a noun phrase punctuates the same way.  
**Variants:** *given* (+ noun phrase), *given the fact that*

Presents a fact as an accepted premise and draws the main clause out of it; standard in argument, analysis, and mathematics. Where *because* asserts a cause, *given that* offers a starting point and invites you to reason forward, which is why it sits so naturally in front of conclusions and predictions.

1. *Given that* the vote is next week, we need the report by Friday.
2. Attendance is going to be low, *given that* it's the first warm Saturday of the year.
3. *Given* the forecast, I'd move the ceremony indoors.
4. *Given that* no two witnesses agree, the timeline is guesswork.
5. The price seems fair, *given that* the roof was replaced last year.
6. *Given that* n is even, n squared must be even as well.

**See also:** [considering that](#considering-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that), [since](#since), [in view of the fact that](#in-view-of-the-fact-that), [on the basis that](#on-the-basis-that), [given that (conditional sense)](06-condition.md#given-that)

### in that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈðæt/ &middot; *in-THAT*  
**Type:** subordinating (the identical two words are also the everyday preposition plus demonstrative — *in that box*, *in that case* — which is a sequence of two words, not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause and respect  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in that* [clause]. — and no other position.  
**Punctuation:** Normally no comma when it follows; a comma is acceptable if the clause is a parenthetical afterthought. There is no fronted position: `In that it assumes funding, the plan is risky` is not idiomatic English. Note that *that* keeps its full vowel here and often takes the stress.

Means "because" and "in the respect that" at the same time: it names the exact particular in which the main clause holds. Where *because* gives the cause of an event, *in that* explains the sense of a claim, which is why it follows evaluations and comparisons rather than reports of things happening.

1. The plan is risky *in that* it assumes the grant will renew.
2. Her position is unusual *in that* she reports to both directors.
3. Whales differ from fish *in that* they breathe air.
4. The evening was a success *in that* nobody left early.
5. The two proposals are alike *in that* both push the cost onto the tenant.
6. He was fortunate *in that* the damage stopped at the drywall.

**See also:** [insofar as](#insofar-as), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [considering that](#considering-that), [on the basis that](#on-the-basis-that), [because](#because)

### on the basis that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈbeɪsɪs ðət/ &middot; *on-thuh-BAY-sis-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (the premise an action rests on)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the basis that* [clause]. &middot; *On the basis that* [clause], [main clause]. (rare, and chiefly legal or academic) &middot; [main clause] *on the basis of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows the main clause — it completes the verb of deciding, approving, or arguing rather than commenting on it. Comma after the clause on the rare occasions it is fronted.  
**Variants:** *on the basis of* (+ noun phrase)

Names the premise a decision or a plan was built on. It shades into *on the grounds that*, but the two are not interchangeable: *grounds* points at a justification somebody offered for refusing or objecting, while *basis* points at the assumption the whole action rests on — which is why *on the basis that* attaches as readily to forecasts, purchases, and hires as it does to rulings.

1. The forecast was built *on the basis that* fuel prices would hold steady.
2. She took the offer *on the basis that* the start date could slide to September.
3. *On the basis that* nobody had objected in writing, the clerk recorded the vote as unanimous.
4. The bank approved the loan *on the basis that* the second appraisal came in higher.
5. He was hired *on the basis of* a single phone interview.
6. The appeal was dismissed *on the basis that* it raised nothing the trial court had not already heard.

**See also:** [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [given that](#given-that), [for the reason that](#for-the-reason-that), [by virtue of the fact that](#by-virtue-of-the-fact-that), [insofar as](#insofar-as)

### on the grounds that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈɡraʊndz ðət/ &middot; *on-thuh-GROWNDZ-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (reported justification)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the grounds that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *on the ground that* [clause]. (legal singular) &middot; *On the grounds that* [clause], [main clause]. (rare) &middot; [main clause] *on the grounds of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it — it attaches tightly to the verb of refusing, objecting, arguing, or deciding. It is rarely fronted; when it is, a comma follows the clause.  
**Variants:** *on the ground that* (standard in legal writing), *on grounds that* (journalistic), *on the grounds of* (+ noun phrase)

Introduces the reason somebody *gave* for an action, not necessarily the reason that is true. That reporting distance is the whole difference from *because*: *He refused on the grounds that he was sick* leaves room for doubt in a way *He refused because he was sick* does not.

1. The city rejected the permit *on the grounds that* the lot is zoned residential.
2. She declined to answer *on the grounds that* the question was irrelevant.
3. The union struck *on the grounds that* the contract had been violated.
4. He challenged the ticket *on the ground that* the sign was obscured by a branch.
5. The paper was retracted *on grounds that* the data could not be reproduced.
6. Their insurer denied the claim *on the grounds that* flooding was excluded.
7. *On the grounds that* the ordinance was too vague to enforce, the judge threw out the citation.

**See also:** [for the reason that](#for-the-reason-that), [by reason that](#by-reason-that), [given that](#given-that), [on the basis that](#on-the-basis-that), [on account of the fact that](#on-account-of-the-fact-that), [because](#because)

### on the premise that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈprɛmɪs ðət/ &middot; *on-thuh-PREM-iss-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (the assumption an action rests on)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the premise that* [clause]. &middot; *On the premise that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *on the false premise that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *on the premise of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when it follows — it completes the verb of planning, agreeing, or arguing rather than commenting on it. Comma after the clause when it is fronted.  
**Variants:** *on the premise of* (+ noun phrase), *on the false premise that*, *on the mistaken premise that*, *on the assumption that*

Names the assumption a decision was built on, and leaves that assumption open to challenge — which is why *false*, *mistaken*, and *dubious* slide in front of *premise* so easily. That is the whole difference from *on the basis that*, where the writer is not inviting you to doubt the premise at all.

1. The budget was drawn up *on the premise that* enrollment would hold steady.
2. *On the premise that* the tenant would renew, the owner put off the repairs.
3. They bought the second van *on the premise that* the contract would run three more years.
4. The entire argument rests *on the false premise that* the two funds are interchangeable.
5. She agreed to chair the panel *on the premise that* somebody else would do the scheduling.
6. *On the premise that* the river would not crest until Tuesday, the town held off sandbagging.

**See also:** [on the basis that](#on-the-basis-that), [on the theory that](#on-the-theory-that), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [given that](#given-that), [assuming that](06-condition.md#assuming-that)

### on the theory that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn ðə ˈθiəri ðət/, /ɑn ðə ˈθɪri ðət/ &middot; *on-thuh-THEE-uh-ree-thut, on-thuh-THEER-ee-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (a working guess acted on)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on the theory that* [clause]. &middot; *On the theory that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *on the theory that* [clause]. (as an afterthought)  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it when the clause completes the verb of acting, buying, or deciding; a comma when the whole phrase is tacked on as an afterthought. Comma after a fronted clause.  
**Variants:** *on the principle that* (a rule rather than a guess), *on the assumption that*, *working on the theory that*

Gives the guess somebody acted on rather than a fact they knew: "working on the assumption that." The noun *theory* carries a shrug — the writer reports the reasoning without endorsing it, which is why the phrase so often comes just before an account of how the reasoning went wrong.

1. We took the sedan *on the theory that* the road would be plowed by noon.
2. *On the theory that* the leak was upstairs, the plumber opened the bathroom wall first.
3. He bought two of them, *on the theory that* one would break before spring.
4. The clinic stays open until eight *on the theory that* people will come after work.
5. She filed anyway, *on the theory that* a late claim beats no claim at all.
6. *On the theory that* nobody reads the second page, they buried the fee there.

**See also:** [on the premise that](#on-the-premise-that), [on the basis that](#on-the-basis-that), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [given that](#given-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that)

### seeing that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsiɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *SEE-ing-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal; bare *seeing* is otherwise the participle and gerund of the verb *see*) &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** *Seeing that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *seeing that* [clause]. &middot; *Seeing as (how)* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *seeing as (how)* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; a comma is usual when it follows, since the clause points at something already obvious rather than delivering news. *Seeing as how* is the most colloquial member of the set and should stay out of formal writing.  
**Variants:** *seeing as*, *seeing as how*, *seeing how*, *seeing* (bare)

A conversational *since*: it points at a fact both speakers can see and treats it as obvious grounds for what follows. *Seeing as how* is the folksiest member of the family, *being that* the least standard, and *given that* the formal equivalent.

1. *Seeing that* you're already in line, would you grab me one too?
2. We might as well eat, *seeing that* the movie doesn't start till nine.
3. *Seeing as* nobody's using the conference room, let's take it.
4. *Seeing as how* you've done this before, you should go first.
5. I figured I'd drive, *seeing that* my car's the only one with snow tires.
6. *Seeing that* it's your birthday, dessert is on the house.

**See also:** [being that](#being-that), [given that](#given-that), [considering that](#considering-that), [since](#since), ['cause](#cause)

### the reason being that

**Pronunciation:** /ðə ˈrizən ˈbiɪŋ ðət/ &middot; *thuh-REE-zuhn-BEE-ing-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating (phrasal; strictly an absolute construction — the noun *reason* plus the participle *being* — rather than a true conjunction) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral in speech and email; avoided in edited prose  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause hung on a clause that has already finished  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *the reason being that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause], *the reason being* [clause]. (with *that* dropped) &middot; [main clause] — *reason being,* [clause]. (informal, article dropped) &middot; [main clause], *the reason being* [noun phrase]. — following position only; it is never fronted.  
**Punctuation:** Always preceded by a comma, a dash, or a period in speech-like writing. *That* drops freely, and in casual speech the whole thing reduces to *reason being* with a comma after it. In formal writing use a finite verb — *the reason is that* — or simply *because*; note that *the reason is because* is the version editors strike.  
**Variants:** *the reason being*, *reason being* (informal — the article dropped), *the reason for this being that*, *reason is*

Hangs a reason off the end of a finished clause in the shape of a participial absolute rather than a subordinate clause. Editors dislike it because it spends four words on *because* and leaves the reason without a finite verb, but the pause in front of it does real work in speech, which is why it thrives there.

1. We moved the meeting to Thursday, *the reason being that* half the team is out Monday.
2. The trail is closed above the falls, *the reason being* the footbridge washed out in June.
3. I never learned to drive a stick — *reason being,* nobody in my family owned one.
4. The application came back, *the reason being that* page three had never been signed.
5. She keeps two calendars, *the reason being that* the work one syncs to her phone and the other doesn't.
6. They stopped stocking it, *the reason being* low turnover.

**See also:** [for the reason that](#for-the-reason-that), [because](#because), [that's because](#thats-because), [on account of the fact that](#on-account-of-the-fact-that), [in that](#in-that)

## The *fact that* frames

A family of prepositions propped up with *the fact that* so they can take a clause. All of them mean *because*, all of them are long, and every American style guide tells you to cut them — but they are everywhere in institutional prose, so they have to be read even if they are never written.


### by virtue of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈvɜrtʃu əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *by-VUR-choo-uv-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *by virtue of the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *By virtue of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *by virtue of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *By virtue of* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *by virtue of* (+ noun phrase)

Says the main clause holds *automatically* because of the stated fact — the fact confers the status rather than merely bringing it about. That entailment is what separates it from *because*, and it is why the phrase lives in law, eligibility rules, and definitions.

1. She qualifies for in-state tuition *by virtue of the fact that* her father is stationed here.
2. *By virtue of the fact that* the deed was recorded first, their claim takes priority.
3. Members vote *by virtue of the fact that* they pay dues, not because the board invites them.
4. The child is a citizen *by virtue of the fact that* she was born in Ohio.
5. He chairs the committee *by virtue of* his office.
6. The covenant binds the buyer *by virtue of the fact that* it runs with the land.

**See also:** [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that), [due to the fact that](#due-to-the-fact-that)

### due to the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu tə ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *doo-tuh-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *due to the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *Due to the fact that* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; normally no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *due to* (+ noun phrase), *due to the fact of* (+ noun phrase — worse than either)

A five-word way of saying *because*, and the single most-flagged phrase in American style guides. Use it only when the sentence genuinely needs the noun *fact*; otherwise every editor since Strunk will cut it back to *because*.

1. The flight was canceled *due to the fact that* the crew had timed out.
2. *Due to the fact that* enrollment fell, two sections were dropped.
3. Delivery is delayed *due to the fact that* the warehouse is closed for inventory.
4. *Due to the fact that* the samples were mislabeled, the whole run had to be repeated.
5. Rates rose *due to the fact that* claims exceeded projections for a third year.
6. We rescheduled *due to the fact that* half the committee was traveling.

**See also:** [because](#because), [due to](#due-to), [owing to the fact that](#owing-to-the-fact-that), [on account of the fact that](#on-account-of-the-fact-that), [in view of the fact that](#in-view-of-the-fact-that)

### in light of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈlaɪt əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *in-LYTE-uv-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (weighed reason)  
**Pattern:** *In light of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in light of the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *In light of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in light of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; the comma is optional when it follows.  
**Variants:** *in light of* (+ noun phrase), *in the light of* (British)

Presents a fact as something the decision in the main clause had to take into account — nearer to *considering that* than to *because*, since it weighs rather than causes. It belongs to announcements, memos, and rulings.

1. *In light of the fact that* the vendor missed two deadlines, we're rebidding the contract.
2. The sentence was reduced *in light of the fact that* he had no prior record.
3. *In light of* the storm warning, the district closed all campuses.
4. *In light of the fact that* the sample was small, the authors hedge their conclusion.
5. The board revisited the policy *in light of the fact that* three other states had changed theirs.
6. *In light of the fact that* nobody had been notified, the vote was voided.

**See also:** [in view of the fact that](#in-view-of-the-fact-that), [considering that](#considering-that), [given that](#given-that), [on account of the fact that](#on-account-of-the-fact-that)

### in view of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈvju əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *in-VYOO-uv-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** *In view of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in view of the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *In view of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in view of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; the comma is optional when it follows.  
**Variants:** *in view of* (+ noun phrase)

The bureaucratic twin of *in light of the fact that*, with the same "taking this into account" sense and the same wordiness problem. Fronting is its natural position, since it sets up the decision that follows.

1. *In view of the fact that* the contractor has filed for bankruptcy, the city will rebid the work.
2. The committee adjourned early *in view of the fact that* no quorum was present.
3. *In view of* her length of service, the penalty was suspended.
4. *In view of the fact that* two witnesses have recanted, the charges were dropped.
5. Premiums were held flat *in view of the fact that* claims fell for a second year.
6. *In view of the fact that* the road remains closed, deliveries are suspended until further notice.

**See also:** [in light of the fact that](#in-light-of-the-fact-that), [considering that](#considering-that), [given that](#given-that), [owing to the fact that](#owing-to-the-fact-that)

### on account of the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn əˈkaʊnt əv ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *on-uh-KOWNT-uv-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on account of the fact that* [clause]. &middot; *On account of the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *on account of* [clause]. (spoken, nonstandard)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; no comma when it follows. In casual speech *the fact* drops out and *on account of* takes a clause directly — common across much of the country in conversation, nonstandard in every kind of writing.  
**Variants:** *on account of* (+ clause, spoken), *on accounta* (eye dialect)

A conversational *because* that turns clunky the moment it is written out in full. Unlike its formal neighbors *owing to* and *due to*, this one sounds folksy in speech and padded on the page; plain *because* fixes both.

1. They closed the pool *on account of the fact that* the filter failed.
2. *On account of the fact that* the tickets were nonrefundable, we went anyway.
3. Practice moved indoors *on account of the fact that* the field was underwater.
4. He walks to work *on account of the fact that* parking downtown runs three hundred a month.
5. *On account of the fact that* nobody read the memo, we had the same meeting twice.
6. We're eating late *on account of* the roast took longer than the recipe said.

**See also:** [on account of](#on-account-of), [because](#because), [due to the fact that](#due-to-the-fact-that), [owing to the fact that](#owing-to-the-fact-that), [being that](#being-that)

### owing to the fact that

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊɪŋ tə ðə ˈfækt ðət/ &middot; *OH-ing-tuh-thuh-FAKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** *Owing to the fact that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *owing to the fact that* [clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause; normally no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *owing to* (+ noun phrase)

The most formal and the least common of the *fact that* frames in American English; to an American ear it reads as British or as institutional prose. *Because* is the plain replacement, *owing to* plus a noun phrase the compact one.

1. *Owing to the fact that* the ferry was out of service, the island went a week without mail.
2. The exhibit closed early *owing to the fact that* the loan agreement expired.
3. *Owing to the fact that* records from that period were destroyed, the claim cannot be verified.
4. Construction stalled *owing to the fact that* the permits were never filed.
5. *Owing to* a scheduling conflict, the hearing was moved to March.
6. Attendance fell *owing to the fact that* the parking garage was closed all summer.

**See also:** [owing to](#owing-to), [due to the fact that](#due-to-the-fact-that), [in view of the fact that](#in-view-of-the-fact-that), [because](#because)

## Spoken frames that carry the reason

Three things people say that do a reason clause's work without being conjunctions at all: two of them are complete sentences and the third is a parenthetical aside. They are collected here because in conversation they fill the slot a *because* clause fills on the page, and because none of them can be dropped into edited prose in a conjunction's place — *that's because* starts a new sentence rather than joining one, *you see* joins nothing whatever, and *as it is* only means anything when a hypothetical has already been set up for it to knock down. Their punctuation follows from that: each is fenced off, and the comma splice is the standing danger.


### as it is

**Pronunciation:** /əz ɪt ˈɪz/, full /æz ɪt ˈɪz/ &middot; *uhz-it-IZ, az-it-IZ*  
**Type:** fixed adverbial phrase (not a conjunction — the subordinator *as* plus a clause, frozen into an idiom, and it takes no clause of its own) &middot; **Register:** neutral, very common in speech  
**Clause type:** none — it stands as a sentence adverbial; the reason is the real state of affairs it points at  
**Pattern:** [hypothetical clause], but *as it is*, [main clause]. &middot; *As it is*, [main clause]. &middot; [first clause]; *as it is*, [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *as it is*. (a different sense — "already")  
**Punctuation:** Set off by a comma in front position and fenced with commas in the middle. Keep it apart from the unpunctuated *as it is* meaning "already" or "in its present state" (*Leave the table as it is*; *The rent is high enough as it is*) — that one takes no comma and gives no reason. Because the phrase does not join clauses, a comma between the hypothetical and it is a splice unless *but* or a semicolon carries the join.  
**Variants:** *as things are*, *as things stand*, *as it stands*, *as matters stand* (formal), *as it was* (past)

Sets the actual situation against a hypothetical one and lets the difference do the explaining: "that would have been the plan — here is what is really true." It almost always answers a *would*, an *if*, or a wish in the sentence before it, and with no such setup in front of it the phrase says nothing.

1. I'd drive you, but *as it is*, my car is in the shop until Thursday.
2. We meant to hike the whole loop; *as it is*, we barely made the first overlook.
3. If the grant had come through we'd have hired two people. *As it is*, we hired nobody.
4. She would have finished the degree, but *as it is*, the program closed in her second year.
5. The plan called for four sessions; *as it is*, there was time for two.
6. I'd love to argue the point, but *as it is*, I have a train to catch.

**See also:** [that's because](#thats-because), [you see](#you-see), [since](#since), [with](#with), [as](#as)

### that's because

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðæts bɪˈkɔz/, /ˈðæts bɪˈkʌz/ &middot; *THATS-bih-KAWZ, THATS-bih-KUZ*  
**Type:** clause frame (not a conjunction — the full main clause *that is* with a *because* clause as its complement) &middot; **Register:** neutral; the workhorse of spoken explanation and of explanatory writing  
**Clause type:** the *because* clause is the complement of *is*, not an adverbial modifier  
**Pattern:** [statement]. *That's because* [clause]. &middot; [statement]. *That is because* [clause]. (formal) &middot; [statement]. *This is because* [clause]. (in writing, pointing back at a paragraph) &middot; [statement], and *that's because* [clause]. &middot; [statement]. *That's why* [clause]. (the mirror — what follows is the result, not the reason)  
**Punctuation:** It opens a sentence of its own; that is the entire point of the frame. Joining it to the statement in front of it with a comma is a comma splice — `The pass is closed, that's because it snowed` needs a period, a semicolon, or an added *and*. No comma after *because* itself.  
**Variants:** *that is because* (formal), *this is because*, *that's why* (the mirror — result, not reason), *which is why* (the joining version, catalogued under result)

Takes a statement that has already been made and gives its reason a whole sentence, which is why explanatory writing leans on it so heavily: it lets a paragraph slow down and take one cause at a time. Its mirror image *that's why* runs the other direction — what follows *that's because* is the reason, what follows *that's why* is the result.

1. The bread never rose. *That's because* the yeast was two years old.
2. Half the class failed the unit — *that's because* nobody told them the chapter had changed.
3. You'll see the meter run backward on sunny afternoons. *That's because* the panels are pushing power back onto the grid.
4. She never answers the phone before ten. *That is because* she works nights.
5. The photographs all look yellow. *This is because* the room was lit with tungsten bulbs.
6. The lot was full and the trail was empty, and *that's why* we turned around.

**See also:** [because](#because), [the reason being that](#the-reason-being-that), [you see](#you-see), [which is why](05-purpose-result.md#which-is-why), [why](09-place-relative.md#why)

### you see

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈsi/, reduced /jə ˈsi/ &middot; *yoo-SEE, yuh-SEE*  
**Type:** discourse marker (not a conjunction — a parenthetical main clause) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral; in writing it marks the voice as conversational  
**Clause type:** none — it attaches to a clause and flags that clause as the explanation  
**Pattern:** *You see,* [clause]. &middot; [clause], *you see*. &middot; [subject], *you see*, [predicate]. &middot; *See,* [clause]. (informal, chiefly American) &middot; *You see,* [clause] — *that's* the trouble.  
**Punctuation:** Always fenced off: a comma after it in front position, a comma before it at the end, commas on both sides in the middle. It never takes *that* — `You see that the phone was dead` reports what somebody saw and explains nothing. Since it joins no clauses, the two clauses around it stay separate sentences or need a real conjunction between them.  
**Variants:** *see* (bare — informal American), *y'see* (eye dialect), *the thing is*, *you know*

Flags the clause it sits with as the explanation the listener has been waiting for, and presumes the listener will accept it once it is said. It is close to *because* in job and nothing like it in grammar: it joins nothing, subordinates nothing, and can be deleted without touching the sense of either clause.

1. I couldn't call. *You see,* the phone had been dead since noon.
2. He turned the job down — the commute, *you see*, would have been two hours each way.
3. *You see,* the house had stood empty for a decade before they bought it.
4. She won't eat the crust. *See,* somebody told her once that it was burnt.
5. We had to reroute the whole tour; the tunnel, *you see*, floods every spring.
6. *You see,* nobody had actually read the contract before signing it.

**See also:** [that's because](#thats-because), [as it is](#as-it-is), [because](#because), [after all](11-conjunctive-adverbs.md#after-all)

## Formal, legal, and archaic

The reason subordinators that survive in statutes, contracts, rulings, and scripture. Recognizing them matters more than using them.


### because that

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkɔz ðət/, /bɪˈkʌz ðət/ &middot; *bih-KAWZ-thut, bih-KUZ-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating (archaic — *because* with the old complementizer *that* still attached) &middot; **Register:** archaic  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *because that* [clause]. &middot; *Because that* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Punctuated exactly like modern *because*: comma after a fronted clause, none when it follows. Modern editing does not repunctuate the phrase, it deletes the *that*.  
**Variants:** *for that* (the same construction built on *for* — see the formal and archaic category), *because as* (dialect), *for because* (archaic, emphatic)

The Early Modern shape of *because*, carrying the *that* that once had to follow a preposition or adverb before it could take a clause — the same *that* still fossilized inside *now that*, *seeing that*, and *so that*. It is dead outside the King James Bible, Shakespeare, and writing that imitates them; English kept the phrase and dropped the *that*.

1. *Because that* the day was far spent, they lodged in the village.
2. He was put out of the synagogue *because that* he had confessed him openly.
3. *Because that* the emperor had commanded all strangers to depart the city, they came into Corinth.
4. The lease was held void *because that* the rent had not been paid at the appointed day.
5. *Because that* no man was found worthy, the book remained sealed.
6. The narrator slips into scripture whenever he is nervous: "and I answered nothing, *because that* I was afraid."

**See also:** [for that](12-formal-archaic.md#for-that), [because](#because), [for why](#for-why), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [now that](#now-that)

### by reason that

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈrizən ðət/ &middot; *by-REE-zuhn-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *by reason that* [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *by reason of* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows, and it is not fronted in modern usage.  
**Variants:** *by reason of* (+ noun phrase — still current in legal writing), *by reason whereof*

An old formal *because* that survives only in legal boilerplate and older statutes. The clause form is effectively dead, while *by reason of* plus a noun phrase is alive and well in fixed phrases like *not guilty by reason of insanity*; for anything new, write *because* or *on the grounds that*.

1. The lease terminated *by reason that* the premises were destroyed.
2. The plaintiff was barred *by reason that* the statute of limitations had run.
3. He was found not guilty *by reason of* insanity.
4. Payment was withheld *by reason that* the work never passed inspection.
5. The office fell vacant *by reason that* no successor had been named.
6. Any loss suffered *by reason of* the delay shall be borne by the seller.

**See also:** [for the reason that](#for-the-reason-that), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as)

### for why

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈwaɪ/, /fɔr ˈwaɪ/, older /fər ˈʍaɪ/ &middot; *fer-WHY, for-WHY, fer-HWY*  
**Type:** subordinating (archaic; the same two words are also the interrogative *for why did he come?*) &middot; **Register:** archaic; it survives only as dialect and as a fixed rhetorical tag  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause  
**Pattern:** [main clause]; *for why*, [clause]. &middot; [main clause] *for why* [clause]. &middot; *Forwhy* [clause], [main clause]. (the older solid spelling) &middot; "*For why?*" [answer]. (the rhetorical question)  
**Punctuation:** In older texts a comma or a semicolon comes before it and a comma often follows the phrase itself, since it works as an announced turn: *and he would not go; for why, he had given his word*. The one form still alive is punctuated as a question of its own, with its answer in a separate sentence.  
**Variants:** *forwhy* (one word — the Middle English and Spenserian spelling), *for why?* (the rhetorical question, still heard), *for-why* (hyphenated in some editions)

An old *because*, built out of a preposition and a *wh*-word exactly as *wherefore* is. It was gone from standard English by the eighteenth century, leaving behind the rhetorical *For why?* that a speaker uses to ask his own question before answering it, plus scattered dialect use in Britain and Ireland.

1. He would not sign the paper; *for why*, he could not read a word of it.
2. *Forwhy* the night was dark, the company tarried at the inn.
3. The rest of them turned back, *for why* the ford was running high.
4. She'll not be at the market Sunday — *for why*, the stall's been let to somebody else.
5. The preacher paused and answered himself: "And *for why?* Because none of you were listening."
6. In Spenser the word is printed solid: *forwhy* he loved her more than his own life.

**See also:** [because that](#because-that), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [by reason that](#by-reason-that), [wherefore](09-place-relative.md#wherefore), [why](09-place-relative.md#why)

### forasmuch as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɔrəzˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *for-uhz-MUCH-uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (preamble)  
**Pattern:** *Forasmuch as* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *forasmuch as* [clause]. (rare, and only in scriptural pastiche)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after the clause, which is nearly always fronted — the word exists to open a sentence. On the rare occasions it follows, no comma precedes it.  
**Variants:** *for as much as*

A preamble word out of the King James Bible and early statutes, meaning "seeing that" or "because." Nobody writes it now except for period effect or in ceremonial texts; *inasmuch as* is its surviving relative, and *whereas* took over its job in legal recitals.

1. *Forasmuch as* many have taken in hand to set forth a declaration, it seemed good to write unto thee.
2. *Forasmuch as* the said lands lie within the county, the tax shall be levied there.
3. *Forasmuch as* it hath pleased Almighty God to take unto himself the soul of our departed brother, we commit his body to the ground.
4. *Forasmuch as* the parties are agreed, let the record so reflect.
5. *Forasmuch as* no heir survives, the estate escheats to the Commonwealth.
6. The narrator opens in mock scripture: "*Forasmuch as* the town had no mayor, the barber presided."
7. He was sore afraid *forasmuch as* he had not kept the covenant.

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [whereas](#whereas), [by reason that](#by-reason-that), [seeing that](#seeing-that)

### in respect that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn rɪˈspɛkt ðət/ &middot; *in-rih-SPEKT-thut*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** archaic in general English; still live in Scottish legal writing  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause and respect  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *in respect that* [clause]. &middot; *In respect that* [clause], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *in respect of* [noun phrase]. (the surviving prepositional form, and no longer causal)  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows; comma after a fronted clause. Do not read it as the living *in respect of* or *with respect to*, which mean "as regards" and give no reason at all — the causal sense went out of the prepositional form centuries ago and stayed only in the clause.  
**Variants:** *in respect whereof*, *in respect of the fact that*, *in respect of* (+ noun phrase — no longer causal)

An old formal *because* that keeps a trace of "in the particular that," exactly as *in that* still does. Scots law never let it go: interlocutors and pleadings are still written *in respect that the pursuer has failed to appear*, and outside that setting the phrase is period costume.

1. Decree was granted *in respect that* the defender lodged no defences.
2. The appeal was refused *in respect that* it raised no point of law.
3. *In respect that* the deceased left no will, the estate falls to be divided by law.
4. The tenant was removed *in respect that* the rent had been three terms in arrears.
5. He was held liable *in respect that* he had undertaken the repair himself.
6. *In respect that* the parties are agreed on the figures, no proof is necessary.

**See also:** [in that](#in-that), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [by reason that](#by-reason-that), [for the reason that](#for-the-reason-that), [insomuch as](12-formal-archaic.md#insomuch-as)

### inasmuch as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnəzˈmʌtʃ əz/ &middot; *in-uhz-MUCH-uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause and extent  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *inasmuch as* [clause]. &middot; *Inasmuch as* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted clause. When it follows, no comma is the norm — the clause names the operative reason and belongs to the sentence — though writers do set it off when it reads as a parenthetical aside.  
**Variants:** *in as much as* (older spelling)

Formal *because*, carrying a residual sense of "to the degree that" left over from its parts. It differs from *insofar as*, which is purely about extent and limits a claim rather than explaining it — the test is substitution: if *since* preserves the meaning, *inasmuch as* is the word you want.

1. The ruling matters *inasmuch as* it binds every district in the circuit.
2. *Inasmuch as* the defendant has already served the maximum, the appeal is moot.
3. Her testimony is relevant *inasmuch as* she was the last person in the building.
4. *Inasmuch as* no objection was filed within thirty days, the plan stands approved.
5. The company is liable *inasmuch as* it controlled the site on the day of the accident.
6. *Inasmuch as* you were not present, you cannot be asked about the conversation.

**See also:** [insofar as](#insofar-as), [in that](#in-that), [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [because](#because)

### insofar as

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɪnsəˈfɑr əz/ &middot; *in-suh-FAR-uhz*  
**Type:** subordinating &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of extent (cause only by implication)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *insofar as* [clause]. &middot; *Insofar as* [clause], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Normally no comma when it follows — the clause restricts the main claim rather than commenting on it, so setting it off with a comma changes the meaning. Comma after a fronted clause.  
**Variants:** *in so far as* (three words — older, and chiefly British today), *so far as* (chiefly British except in the fixed American hedge *so far as I know*)

Means "to the extent that": it limits the main clause instead of causing it, which is exactly why it gets written where *inasmuch as* was meant. Keep the pair apart — *insofar as* answers "how far is this true?" and *inasmuch as* answers "why?"

1. The warranty applies *insofar as* the damage was not caused by misuse.
2. *Insofar as* the state law conflicts with the federal rule, it is preempted.
3. He is responsible *insofar as* he signed off on the design.
4. *Insofar as* I can tell from the photographs, the frame is straight.
5. The memo is useful *insofar as* it dates the decision, but it names nobody.
6. We'll cooperate *insofar as* the confidentiality agreement allows.

**See also:** [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [in that](#in-that), [considering that](#considering-that), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that)

### whereas

**Pronunciation:** /wɛrˈæz/, /hwɛrˈæz/ &middot; *wair-AZ, hwair-AZ*  
**Type:** subordinating (the identical word is also the everyday subordinator of contrast — see the concession category) &middot; **Register:** formal, and specifically legal — outside recitals and resolutions the causal sense does not occur  
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of cause (legal recital)  
**Pattern:** *WHEREAS,* [clause]; *WHEREAS,* [clause]; NOW, THEREFORE, [operative clause]. &middot; *Whereas* [clause], [main clause]. (outside the recital format — resolutions, older statutes, preambles)  
**Punctuation:** Two conventions, and they must not be mixed. In recitals the word is capitalized, followed by a comma, and each recital closes with a semicolon — the last one usually with "; and". The operative text then opens with "NOW, THEREFORE". Outside that format the word behaves like any other fronted subordinator: nothing after *whereas* itself, one comma after the clause. Writing `WHEREAS, the Employee has skills, the Company wishes to employ her` splices the two conventions together and is wrong under both.  
**Variants:** *whereas* (contrast sense — a different conjunction entirely; see the concession category), *WHEREAS* (small caps or full caps in drafting)

In contracts, resolutions, and statutes, *whereas* opens each background fact the agreement rests on: "given that", "because". This is not the everyday *whereas*, which marks contrast (*She writes, whereas he edits*) — the two senses share a spelling and nothing else, and only the legal one belongs in this category.

1. *WHEREAS,* the Seller is the record owner of the property described in Exhibit A; and
2. *WHEREAS,* the Parties desire to set forth the terms of their agreement; NOW, THEREFORE, in consideration of the mutual promises below, the Parties agree as follows.
3. *WHEREAS,* the Council finds that traffic on Elm Street has increased forty percent since 2019;
4. *Whereas* the town has no full-time engineer, the Board hereby authorizes the hiring of a consultant.
5. *WHEREAS,* the Employee possesses skills valuable to the Company; and *WHEREAS,* the Company wishes to employ her on the terms set forth below;
6. The resolution runs to four *whereas* clauses before it says anything at all.

**See also:** [forasmuch as](#forasmuch-as), [inasmuch as](#inasmuch-as), [given that](#given-that), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [whereas (contrast sense)](07-concession.md#whereas)

## Reason markers that take a noun phrase, not a clause

These are prepositions, not conjunctions, and they are here only because learners reach for them in a conjunction's slot. Each takes a noun phrase — *with* and *what with* also take a verbless clause of the *with the roads closed* type, and *from* takes an *-ing* phrase (*hoarse from shouting*) — but not one of them can be followed by a subject and a finite verb. Four of them shade off into neighboring meanings and are easy to reach for wrongly: *out of* names a motive and needs a human decision behind it, *over* names the issue a fight is about, *through* names the channel and usually assigns fault, and *from* names the source a physical result came out of. Four more sit at the edges of the set: *by dint of* names the effort that produced the result, *courtesy of* names who supplied it, *consequent upon* runs from effect back to cause the way *as a result of* does, and *as evidenced by* is not causal at all — it points at the evidence for a claim rather than at the cause of an event.


### as a result of

**Pronunciation:** /əz ə rɪˈzʌlt əv/, full /æz ə rɪˈzʌlt əv/ &middot; *uhz-uh-rih-ZULT-uv, az-uh-rih-ZULT-uv*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, not a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *as a result of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As a result of* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows. Keep it apart from the bare conjunctive adverb *as a result*, which stands between two independent clauses and demands a semicolon or a period in front of it — a comma there is a comma splice (*The line went down; as a result, shipments slipped a week*).  
**Variants:** *as a consequence of*, *in consequence of* (formal, chiefly British), *as a result* (no object — a conjunctive adverb, catalogued under result)

The cause-and-effect preposition of news writing and official notices: it names a prior event and presents the main clause as the outcome. It is heavier than *because of* and more sequential — it suits accidents, rulings, and policy changes better than everyday reasons, and nobody says it about wanting a sandwich. It cannot take a clause: `as a result of the line went down` is not English; write *because the line went down*.

1. Two hundred homes lost power *as a result of* the storm.
2. *As a result of* the recall, the plant idled a full shift.
3. She missed the deadline *as a result of* a filing error at the clerk's office.
4. *As a result of* last year's audit, every purchase over five thousand dollars now needs a second signature.
5. Enrollment dropped nine percent *as a result of* the tuition increase.
6. The bridge was closed *as a result of* damage found during a routine inspection.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [as a result](05-purpose-result.md#as-a-result)

### as evidenced by

**Pronunciation:** /əz ˈɛvɪdənst baɪ/ &middot; *uhz-EV-ih-duhnst-by*  
**Type:** participial preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal; academic, scientific, and legal prose  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase; to reach a clause it needs *as evidenced by the fact that*  
**Pattern:** [main clause], *as evidenced by* [noun phrase]. &middot; *As evidenced by* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *as evidenced by the fact that* [clause]. &middot; [noun], *as evidenced in* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Always set off by a comma — it comments on the whole clause in front of it instead of completing any verb in it. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted. Note that *as* here is not the reason conjunction *as*; the phrase is a reduced relative, and nothing may be inserted between *as* and *evidenced*.  
**Variants:** *as evidenced by the fact that* (+ clause), *as shown by*, *as demonstrated by*, *as is evidenced by*, *as evidenced in*

Points at the evidence for a claim, not at the cause of an event — which is the trap that lands it in this file. *The soil is acidic because of the pines* names a cause; *the soil is acidic, as evidenced by the pines* names the grounds for believing the claim, the same relation the epistemic *because* carries in *It must have frozen, because the birdbath is solid*.

1. The building settled unevenly, *as evidenced by* the cracks above every doorframe.
2. Demand has not recovered, *as evidenced by* three straight quarters of flat bookings.
3. The species once ranged much farther north, *as evidenced by* bones found in the Yukon.
4. He knew about the defect before the sale, *as evidenced by* his own emails.
5. *As evidenced by* the attendance figures, the new schedule is working.
6. The two manuscripts share a source, *as evidenced by the fact that* both repeat the same copying error.

**See also:** [because](#because), [in that](#in-that), [given that](#given-that), [insofar as](#insofar-as), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of)

### because of

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈkɔz əv/, reduced /bɪˈkʌzə/ &middot; *bih-KAWZ-uv, bih-KUZ-uh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, not a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *because of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Because of* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *because of* phrase; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *because of the fact that* (+ clause — wordy), *because* + bare noun (*because science* — an internet joke construction, never formal)

The prepositional partner of *because*: *because* takes a clause with a subject and a verb, *because of* takes a noun phrase. *We stayed home because it snowed* and *We stayed home because of the snow* are the same sentence in two shapes, and crossing them — `because of it snowed` — is one of the most common learner errors in English.

1. The game was called *because of* lightning.
2. *Because of* the detour, we missed the first ten minutes.
3. She got the job *because of* her fieldwork, not her degree.
4. *Because of* what happened last year, the policy now requires two signatures.
5. Half the crop failed *because of* a late frost.
6. He's on crutches *because of* a pickup game that got out of hand.

**See also:** [because](#because), [due to](#due-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [owing to](#owing-to), [from](#from), [over](#over)

### by dint of

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˈdɪnt əv/, reduced /baɪ ˈdɪntə/ &middot; *by-DINT-uv, by-DIN-tuh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction — a fixed phrase; the noun *dint*, "a blow, force," survives in nothing else) &middot; **Register:** formal to literary  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* phrase, never a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *by dint of* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *by dint of* [*-ing* phrase]. &middot; *By dint of* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows. Nothing may be inserted inside the phrase — `by the dint of` and `by dint of the hard work` both break the idiom, which wants a bare or lightly modified noun.  
**Variants:** *by means of* (the plain equivalent), *by virtue of* (status conferred rather than effort spent), *by sheer dint of*

Gives the cause as the effort or force that produced the result: "by sheer application of." It wants a noun of exertion or persistence after it — *hard work*, *practice*, *sheer stubbornness* — and that is what keeps it apart from *by virtue of*, where the noun confers a status and nobody has to work at anything.

1. She made the team *by dint of* showing up to every practice for two years.
2. *By dint of* hard bargaining, the union kept the pension intact.
3. The village kept its school open *by dint of* fundraising that never let up.
4. He learned the whole score *by dint of* repetition rather than talent.
5. *By dint of* sheer stubbornness, they finished the wall before the first frost.
6. The paper survived the decade *by dint of* a printing contract nobody else wanted.

**See also:** [by virtue of the fact that](#by-virtue-of-the-fact-that), [through](#through), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [out of](#out-of), [because of](#because-of)

### consequent upon

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɑnsəkwənt əˈpɑn/, /ˈkɑnsəkwɛnt əˈpɑn/ &middot; *KON-suh-kwuhnt-uh-PON, KON-suh-kwent-uh-PON*  
**Type:** adjective phrase working as a preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal; legal and administrative, and rather more British than American  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, never a clause  
**Pattern:** [noun phrase] *consequent upon* [noun phrase]. (post-modifying a noun — its commonest shape) &middot; [main clause] *consequent upon* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Consequent upon* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it post-modifies a noun, because it belongs inside that noun phrase; a comma after the phrase when the whole thing is fronted. It is not *consequently*, which is a conjunctive adverb standing between two independent clauses and demanding a semicolon or a period in front of it.  
**Variants:** *consequent on*, *consequential upon* (legal), *consequent to* (disputed), *consequent* (bare, post-modifying — *the losses consequent*)

Runs from effect back to cause: whatever stands in front of it happened as a result of whatever follows. It modifies a noun far more often than a verb — *the redundancies consequent upon the merger* is the shape it almost always takes — and in American prose *as a result of* or *following* is the ordinary substitute.

1. The layoffs *consequent upon* the merger were announced in October.
2. Any loss *consequent upon* the delay is to be borne by the carrier.
3. *Consequent upon* the closure of the branch line, three villages lost their only bus.
4. The council is reviewing the parking changes *consequent on* the new one-way system.
5. Costs *consequent upon* an adjournment are usually awarded against the party who sought it.
6. The illness, and the debts *consequent upon* it, ran the family out of the county.

**See also:** [as a result of](#as-a-result-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [due to](#due-to), [because of](#because-of), [by reason that](#by-reason-that)

### courtesy of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɜrtəsi əv/, reduced /ˈkɜrtəsiə/ &middot; *KUR-tuh-see-uv, KUR-tuh-see-uh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral; heavily used in journalism, sportswriting, and credit lines  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, never a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *courtesy of* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *courtesy of* [noun phrase]. (afterthought) &middot; *Courtesy of* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; (credit line) *Photograph courtesy of* [name].  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it completes the clause; a comma when it is tacked on at the end, which is where sportswriting normally puts it. Comma after a fronted phrase. In a credit line it takes no punctuation at all.  
**Variants:** *by courtesy of* (older and more formal), *thanks to* (the everyday equivalent), *courtesy* (bare, in captions — *photo courtesy the Newberry Library*)

*Thanks to*, with the credit spelled out: it names the person or thing that supplied whatever the main clause reports. It leads two lives — the literal credit line of captions and acknowledgments, and the extended causal use of news and sports writing, which is as often sardonic as grateful.

1. The Rangers won it in overtime, *courtesy of* a deflection off a defenseman's skate.
2. We had front-row seats, *courtesy of* her brother's season tickets.
3. *Courtesy of* a burst main on Sixth Street, half of downtown was dry by noon.
4. The map is reproduced *courtesy of* the state archives.
5. Everybody got a second helping, *courtesy of* a neighbor who had over-ordered.
6. He spent the afternoon in the emergency room, *courtesy of* a ladder he should have replaced years ago.

**See also:** [thanks to](#thanks-to), [because of](#because-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [by dint of](#by-dint-of), [through](#through)

### due to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdu tu/, reduced /ˈdu tə/ &middot; *DOO-too, DOO-tuh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, not a clause  
**Pattern:** [noun] *is due to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *due to* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Due to* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *due to the fact that* (+ clause)

Traditionally *due to* was an adjectival phrase modifying a noun after a form of *be* (*The delay was due to fog*), while *because of* modified verbs (*We were delayed because of fog*). Educated American usage now allows *due to* in both slots, but conservative editors and some standardized tests still enforce the old split, so a fronted *Due to fog, we were delayed* remains the version most likely to be marked.

1. The delay was *due to* fog on the runway.
2. Her recovery is *due to* a change in medication, not to rest.
3. *Due to* budget cuts, the branch library now closes at five.
4. The outage was *due to* a squirrel in a transformer.
5. Most of the increase is *due to* higher fuel costs.
6. *Due to* circumstances beyond our control, tonight's performance is canceled.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to the fact that](#due-to-the-fact-that), [owing to](#owing-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [from](#from)

### from

**Pronunciation:** /frʌm/, /frɑm/, reduced /frəm/ &middot; *FRUM, FRAHM, reduced fruhm*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction — the same word is the everyday preposition of source and starting point: *a letter from Ohio*, *open from nine*) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase or an *-ing* phrase, never a finite clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *from* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause] *from* [*-ing* phrase]. &middot; [adjective] *from* [noun phrase]. &middot; *From* [noun phrase], [main clause]. (rare, and mostly with *sheer*)  
**Punctuation:** No comma when it follows, which is where it almost always sits. On the rare fronted use, a comma after the phrase.  
**Variants:** *from sheer* (+ noun), and the pair *die of* / *die from* — *of* for diseases and conditions, *from* for injuries and external causes, though the two have largely merged in speech

Names the source a state or a change came out of: cause read as an origin rather than as a reason anybody had. It attaches to things that happen *to* a person or an object — *sore from the hike*, *warped from the damp*, *hoarse from shouting* — and not to decisions, which is why *He resigned from frustration* goes wrong where *He resigned out of frustration* is fine. It cannot take a clause: `from it rained all night` is not English; write *because it rained all night*.

1. Her hands were raw *from* scrubbing the deck all afternoon.
2. Three of the horses got sick *from* the moldy hay.
3. He lost the hearing in one ear *from* years on the flight line.
4. The pavement buckled *from* the heat.
5. *From* sheer exhaustion, she slept through the alarm twice.
6. Half the sign is unreadable *from* the salt spray.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [out of](#out-of), [through](#through), [due to](#due-to), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of)

### on account of

**Pronunciation:** /ɑn əˈkaʊnt əv/, reduced /ɑn əˈkaʊntə/ &middot; *on-uh-KOWNT-uv, on-uh-KOWNT-uh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** informal to neutral  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase (a bare clause after it is nonstandard)  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *on account of* [noun phrase]. &middot; *On account of* [noun phrase], [main clause].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *on accounta* (eye dialect), *on account of the fact that* (+ clause)

A homey American *because of*, at home in speech and dialogue and slightly out of place in formal writing. In many varieties it also takes a bare clause (*on account of it was raining*), which passes unnoticed in conversation and is nonstandard everywhere in print.

1. School let out early *on account of* the ice storm.
2. *On account of* his back, he hasn't shoveled a driveway in years.
3. They moved to Tucson *on account of* her asthma.
4. The parade was rerouted *on account of* construction on Main.
5. *On account of* the holiday, there's no mail delivery Monday.
6. He missed the ceremony *on account of* a flat tire outside Amarillo.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [on account of the fact that](#on-account-of-the-fact-that), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [out of](#out-of), [owing to](#owing-to)

### out of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈaʊt əv/, reduced /ˈaʊtə/ &middot; *OWT-uv, OW-duh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** none — takes a bare abstract noun of feeling or motive, never a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *out of* [bare abstract noun]. &middot; *Out of* [bare abstract noun], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *out of* [bare abstract noun] *for* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows, except where the phrase is tacked on as an afterthought (*Nobody said anything, out of fear of making it worse*).  
**Variants:** none in the causal sense — the spatial *out of* (*out of the house*) and the quantitative one (*four out of five*) are different prepositions

Gives the motive somebody acted from: the feeling inside the actor rather than the event outside them. It takes a bare noun with no article — *out of curiosity*, *out of spite*, *out of respect*, *out of habit* — and inserting one breaks the idiom (`out of a curiosity`). Because it names a motive, it only works with a deliberate human choice: a bridge closes *because of* corrosion, never *out of* it.

1. I asked *out of* curiosity, not because I doubted her.
2. *Out of* respect for the family, the paper withheld the name.
3. He still saves the receipts *out of* habit.
4. *Out of* sheer stubbornness, she walked the last four miles.
5. They sat through the second act *out of* politeness.
6. Nobody said anything, *out of* fear of making it worse.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [for](#for), [what with](#what-with), [from](#from)

### over

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊvər/, reduced /ˈoʊvɚ/ &middot; *OH-ver*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction — the same word is the ordinary preposition of position and motion, *over the fence*, and the adverb of *it's over*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, and the standard choice in news writing  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, never a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *over* [noun phrase]. &middot; [noun of dispute] *over* [noun phrase]. (*a lawsuit over the fee*, *a walkout over pay*) — following position only; it is not fronted.  
**Punctuation:** No comma before it. It attaches tightly to the verb or noun in front of it, so setting it off breaks the phrase.  
**Variants:** none in the causal sense

The cause preposition of conflict: it names the issue a quarrel, resignation, protest, firing, or lawsuit is *about*. That is a narrower job than *because of* — it wants a word of dispute or reaction in front of it, so *They fell out over money* works and `They were delighted over money` does not. Because it never fronts and never takes a clause, it is the one causal preposition that cannot be swapped into a fronted *because of* slot: `Over the fee, they sued` is not English.

1. The union walked out *over* a change to the overtime rules.
2. Two neighbors have been in court for a year *over* a fence.
3. She resigned *over* the board's decision to close the clinic.
4. Parents packed the meeting *over* the redistricting plan.
5. The paper is being sued *over* a photograph it never ran.
6. They stopped speaking *over* something neither of them can remember now.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [on the grounds that](#on-the-grounds-that), [out of](#out-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of)

### owing to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊɪŋ tu/, reduced /ˈoʊɪŋ tə/ &middot; *OH-ing-too, OH-ing-tuh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, not a clause  
**Pattern:** *Owing to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *owing to* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *owing to the fact that* (+ clause)

A formal, faintly British *because of*. It has one practical advantage over *due to*: nobody objects to fronting it, so *Owing to fog, the flight was delayed* is safe in places where *Due to fog...* still draws editorial fire.

1. *Owing to* heavy snow, the pass is closed until spring.
2. The concert was postponed *owing to* the conductor's illness.
3. *Owing to* an error in the tally, the results were reissued.
4. Yields were down *owing to* a dry June.
5. *Owing to* the size of the estate, probate took two years.
6. The bridge is posted for ten tons *owing to* corrosion in the deck.

**See also:** [due to](#due-to), [because of](#because-of), [owing to the fact that](#owing-to-the-fact-that), [on account of](#on-account-of)

### thanks to

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθæŋks tu/, reduced /ˈθæŋks tə/ &middot; *THANKS-too, THANKS-tuh*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, not a clause  
**Pattern:** *Thanks to* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *thanks to* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *no thanks to* [noun phrase].  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows — except that the sarcastic *no thanks to* is usually set off by a comma as an afterthought.  
**Variants:** *no thanks to* (+ noun phrase — the sarcastic negative)

*Because of*, with credit or blame attached. It began as gratitude and is now used just as readily in irony (*thanks to the geniuses in accounting*), while *no thanks to* reverses it and withholds the credit outright.

1. *Thanks to* a tailwind, we landed twenty minutes early.
2. We found the place *thanks to* a neighbor who walked us over.
3. *Thanks to* whoever left the gate open, the dog spent the night in the woods.
4. The team made the playoffs *thanks to* a rookie nobody had heard of in March.
5. I got there on time, *no thanks to* the detour signs.
6. *Thanks to* the new hire, the backlog is finally under a week.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [due to](#due-to), [on account of](#on-account-of), [owing to](#owing-to), [through](#through)

### through

**Pronunciation:** /θru/ &middot; *THROO*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction — the everyday *through* is spatial, *through the tunnel*, and temporal, *Monday through Friday*) &middot; **Register:** neutral to formal  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, normally an abstract one; never a clause  
**Pattern:** [main clause] *through* [noun phrase]. &middot; *Through* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause] *through no fault of* [possessive]. (fixed phrase)  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted phrase; no comma when it follows.  
**Variants:** *through no fault of one's own* (fixed), *thru* (informal spelling, never in edited prose)

Gives the cause as the channel a result came about by — close to "by way of," and used mostly with abstract nouns naming conduct or circumstance: *through carelessness*, *through an oversight*, *through sheer luck*. It leans toward assigning responsibility, which is why the negative fixed phrase *through no fault of his own* is by far its most common outing in American English. Like every preposition here it refuses a clause: `through the clerk mislabeled it` has to be *because the clerk mislabeled it*.

1. The file was deleted *through* an oversight at the vendor.
2. She got the part *through* nothing but persistence.
3. *Through* a clerical error, the same refund went out twice.
4. Thousands lost coverage *through* no fault of their own.
5. He came by the land *through* his grandmother.
6. *Through* sheer luck, nobody was standing under it.

**See also:** [because of](#because-of), [from](#from), [owing to](#owing-to), [out of](#out-of), [as a result of](#as-a-result-of)

### what with

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ˈwɪð/, /ˌwʌt ˈwɪθ/ &middot; *wut-WITH*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction) &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase or an -ing phrase, never a finite clause  
**Pattern:** *What with* [noun phrase] and [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *what with* [noun phrase]. &middot; [main clause], *what with* [noun phrase] *-ing*. (verbless clause) &middot; [main clause], *what with* [noun phrase] and all.  
**Punctuation:** The *what with* phrase is set off by a comma in both positions.  
**Variants:** *what with ... and all*

Introduces a pile of contributing circumstances rather than one cause, which is why it so often runs a list joined by *and* and trails off in *and all*. It cannot take a finite clause: `what with the baby was sick` is not English — use *because* there.

1. *What with* the rain and the detour, we got there an hour late.
2. I haven't cooked all week, *what with* the move.
3. *What with* three kids under five, they don't get out much.
4. Nobody's sleeping, *what with* the neighbors renovating and the dog next door.
5. *What with* gas at five dollars and the toll going up, I started taking the train.
6. She's had a rough month, *what with* the surgery and all.

**See also:** [with](#with), [because of](#because-of), [on account of](#on-account-of), [thanks to](#thanks-to), [given that](#given-that)

### with

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð/, /wɪθ/, reduced /wəð/ &middot; *WITH, wuhth*  
**Type:** preposition (not a conjunction — the same word is the everyday preposition of accompaniment, *coffee with milk*, and of instrument, *cut it with a knife*) &middot; **Register:** neutral, common in speech and in edited prose alike  
**Clause type:** none — takes a noun phrase, or a verbless (absolute) clause made of a noun phrase plus an adjective, participle, or prepositional phrase; never a finite clause  
**Pattern:** *With* [noun phrase], [main clause]. &middot; *With* [noun phrase] + [adjective / *-ing* / *-ed* / prepositional phrase], [main clause]. &middot; [main clause], *with* [noun phrase] + [adjective / participle]. &middot; [main clause], *with* [noun phrase] *-ing*.  
**Punctuation:** Comma after a fronted *with* phrase. A comma is normal when it follows as well, because the phrase adds a circumstance instead of completing the verb — without one, *with* is read as accompaniment or instrument (*He left with the dog barking* versus *He left, with the dog barking the whole time*).  
**Variants:** *what with* (the emphatic informal version), *with … and all*

Sets a circumstance beside the main clause and lets the reader draw the causal line: *With the bridge out, everything routes through town.* The cause is in the pairing rather than in the word, which makes *with* the weakest and most deniable of the reason markers — and the most useful, because it takes a verbless clause where *because* would need a full one. That is also the trap: `With the bridge was out, everything routes through town` is not English. Drop the verb (*with the bridge out*) or switch to the conjunction (*because the bridge was out*).

1. *With* the furnace out, we all slept in the living room.
2. *With* three exams this week, I'm not going anywhere Saturday.
3. He's been doing the shopping, *with* his wife still on crutches.
4. *With* the lot repaved, the store finally looks open for business.
5. *With* both kids in college, they've started renting out the top floor.
6. Nobody wanted to bring it up, *with* her sister sitting right there.

**See also:** [what with](#what-with), [because of](#because-of), [now that](#now-that), [given that](#given-that), [as](#as)
