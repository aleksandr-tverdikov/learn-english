The American English Conjunction Dictionary#

Every conjunction, subordinator, correlative pair, complementizer, and transitional connector in American English — 816 entries across 12 categories.

Conjunctions are a closed class: unlike nouns or interjections, the full list is finite, so this dictionary aims to be genuinely complete rather than merely broad.

Each entry carries an IPA pronunciation (with the reduced form where speakers use one), a type and register label, the clause type it creates, a substitutable pattern, its punctuation rule, a description, and at least five example sentences.

2Correlative conjunctions (paired)The fixed two-part joiners — both … and, either … or, no sooner … than — where one half marks each of the two things being joined and neither half can stand alone.44
3Subordinators of timeThe connectors that attach one clause to another and say when — before it, after it, during it, from it, or up to it.70
4Subordinators of cause and reasonThe words that hook a reason onto a statement — everything in English that answers why.55
5Subordinators of purpose and resultWords that attach a clause naming the goal an action aims at, or the outcome that action produced.50
6Subordinators of conditionThe words that hang one clause on another as a condition — saying what has to be true, what would have to have been true, or what will happen if it isn't.67
7Subordinators of concession and contrastThe conjunctions that hand the other side a point and then take the sentence back anyway.75
8Subordinators of comparison and mannerThe conjunctions that measure one thing against another — more, less, or equal — and the ones that say in what manner something is done.72
9Subordinators of place, and relative connectorsThe words that pin a clause to a location, and the wh- words that hook a whole clause onto a noun already standing in the sentence.59
10Complementizers and noun-clause introducersThe words that shrink a whole clause down to the size of a noun, so that one sentence can serve as another sentence's subject or object.44

How to read an entry#

### although

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈðoʊ/ · *awl-THOH*        ← IPA, then STRESS in caps
**Type:** subordinating · **Register:** neutral  ← which class, and how formal
**Clause type:** adverbial clause of concession  ← what it builds
**Pattern:** Although [clause], [main clause].   ← substitute your own words in
**Punctuation:** Comma when it comes first.      ← the comma rule for THIS word

Description, then five or more example sentences, then See also.

Pattern is the line to read first. It shows every position the word can legally occupy, so you can substitute your own clauses directly into the skeleton.

Punctuation is the line that prevents the most common written errors. Note especially that conjunctive adverbs — however, therefore, moreover — are not true conjunctions: joining two independent clauses with one and a comma produces a comma splice. They need a semicolon or a period.
