Subordinators of purpose and result#

Words that attach a clause naming the goal an action aims at, or the outcome that action produced.

← The grammar of conjunctions · All categories

Purpose answers what for; result answers what happened. The same forms often serve both — so that is the clearest case — and English separates the two senses not with different words but with a comma and a modal verb: a purpose clause looks forward and almost always carries can, could, will, would, or may (I left early so that I could beat traffic), while a result clause reports something that actually occurred (I left early, so that I beat traffic). Purpose also comes in a non-finite form built on to, in order to, and so as to, and those forms require the main clause and the purpose phrase to share a subject; when the subjects differ you have to switch to a finite so that clause or to in order for X to Y.

One punctuation rule runs through the whole category and splits it down the middle. The subordinators — so that, in order that, lest, such that, with the result that — attach with a comma or with nothing at all, and never with a semicolon, because the clause they introduce is not independent. The result connectives at the end of this file — therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly, as a result — are conjunctive adverbs rather than conjunctions, so joining two independent clauses with one of them takes a semicolon or a period: It rained; therefore, we canceled is correct, and It rained, therefore we canceled is a comma splice. One member of that group behaves differently: thereby normally heads an -ing phrase rather than a second independent clause, so it takes a plain comma and a semicolon in front of it would be wrong.

Purpose: finite clauses#

Subordinators that take a full clause with its own subject and verb. The verb in the purpose clause is normally a modal — can, could, will, would, may, might — because the goal has not happened yet. Fronting the clause always puts a comma after it. In the ordinary order the comma is normally absent, and that absence carries meaning: with so and so that a comma in front of the clause flips the reading from purpose to result, and to the end that picks one up in legal drafting when the aim is an add-on. None of them ever takes a semicolon, because what follows is not an independent clause.

for fear that#

Pronunciation: /fɚ ˈfɪr ðæt/, reduced /fɚ ˈfɪr ðət/ · fer-FEER-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: adverbial clause of negative purpose Pattern: [main clause] for fear that [clause]. · For fear that [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] for fear of [gerund/noun]. Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted, which is rare. Variants: for fear (that-less), for fear of + gerund

Negative purpose: the clause names the thing the subject is acting to avoid. It covers the same ground as lest but stays inside ordinary modern American prose, and unlike lest it takes a normal indicative or modal verb rather than the subjunctive — for fear that he would find out, not for fear that he find out.

  1. She kept the receipt for fear that the store would refuse the return.
  2. He never mentioned the offer for fear his boss would hear about it.
  3. They moved the ceremony indoors for fear that the storm would arrive early.
  4. Nobody said a word for fear that the wrong person was listening.
  5. I checked the lock twice for fear of another break-in.
  6. Parents pulled their kids out of the pool for fear that lightning was coming.
  7. For fear that the news would leak overnight, the company briefed its staff and the press the same morning.

See also: lest, in case, in the hope that, so that, in order that

in case#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈkeɪs/ · in-KAYSS Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of precautionary purpose Pattern: [main clause] in case [clause]. · In case [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause], just in case. · In case of [noun], [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma before in case in the normal order. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. The tag just in case at the end of a sentence does take a comma before it. Variants: just in case, in case of + noun (prepositional)

Precautionary purpose — you do the main action now so that you are ready for a possibility. That is exactly what separates it from if: Take an umbrella in case it rains means take it regardless, while Take an umbrella if it rains means take it only once the rain has started. American English uses the present tense for the future contingency (in case it rains, never in case it will rain).

  1. Bring a jacket in case the theater is cold.
  2. I wrote the address on my hand in case my phone died.
  3. In case the power goes out, there are candles in the drawer.
  4. We booked the second room in case her parents decided to come after all.
  5. Save the confirmation email, just in case.
  6. In case of fire, use the stairs.

See also: for fear that, in the hope that, lest, so that

in order that#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ ðæt/, reduced /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ ðət/ · in-OR-der-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: formal Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] in order that [clause with modal]. · In order that [clause with modal], [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted.

The formal written twin of so that, and almost always followed by a modal — may, might, can, could, will, would, shall. Americans say so that or bare so in speech; in order that survives mainly in legal, academic, and ceremonial prose. Use in order to instead when both clauses share a subject and an infinitive will do.

  1. The records are sealed in order that the witnesses may be protected.
  2. In order that everyone can attend, the meeting was moved to Friday afternoon.
  3. Funds were set aside in order that the district might hire a second counselor.
  4. The statute was amended in order that small businesses would not be penalized.
  5. He repeated the instructions slowly in order that no one would misunderstand them.
  6. In order that the record may be complete, the clerk will now read the motion.

See also: so that, in order to, to the end that, that, lest

in the hope that#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp ðæt/, reduced /ɪn ðə ˈhoʊp ðət/ · in-thuh-HOHP-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of tentative purpose Pattern: [main clause] in the hope that [clause]. · In the hope that [clause], [main clause]. · [main clause] in hopes that [clause]. · [main clause] in the hope of [gerund/noun]. Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate, not independent. Variants: in hopes that, in the hopes that, in the hope of + gerund, in hopes of + gerund

Purpose with the confidence taken out of it. So that presents the goal as reachable; in the hope that concedes it may not work, which is why it takes an ordinary indicative verb (in the hope that someone would answer) instead of the modal-heavy clause in order that demands. American English uses the plural in hopes that as freely as the singular.

  1. She left a note on the windshield in the hope that the driver would call.
  2. They cut the price twice in hopes that somebody would bid.
  3. In the hope that a deal could still be reached, the union delayed the strike vote.
  4. He mailed the manuscript to twelve agents in the hope of a single reply.
  5. The city planted two hundred maples in the hope that the block would be shaded by 2035.
  6. We left the porch light on all night in hopes that the cat would find her way back.
  7. He drove past the house twice in hopes of catching her at home.

See also: so that, in order that, for fear that, in case, with the aim of

lest#

Pronunciation: /lɛst/ · LEST Type: subordinating · Register: formal, literary Clause type: adverbial clause of negative purpose Pattern: [main clause] lest [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. · [main clause] lest [subject] should [verb]. · Lest [clause], [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the clause when it is fronted, as in the fixed phrase Lest we forget, ...

Negative purpose, equal to so that ... not or for fear that. It takes the present subjunctive — a bare verb with no -s (lest he be late, not lest he is late) — or should. The negation already sits inside the word, which is the classic trap: lest anyone hear means "so that nobody hears," so adding not reverses the sense. Everyday American speech uses so that ... doesn't or in case.

  1. He whispered the address lest anyone overhear.
  2. She hid the letter lest her sister find it.
  3. Lest we forget, the same promise was made five years ago.
  4. The committee moved carefully lest it be accused of favoritism.
  5. Keep the receipt lest there should be a dispute later.
  6. I said nothing lest I make the argument worse.

See also: for fear that, in case, so that, in order that

so#

Pronunciation: /soʊ/, reduced /sə/ in rapid connected speech · SOH Type: subordinating (reduced so that); also coordinating in the result sense · Register: neutral to informal Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose; coordinate clause in the result sense Pattern: [main clause] so [clause with modal]. — purpose · [independent clause], so [independent clause]. — result · [subject] [verb] so [adjective] that [clause]. — degree Punctuation: One comma decides the meaning. No comma = purpose (I left early so I could beat traffic). Comma = result (I left early, so I beat traffic), which is the ordinary rule for a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses. A semicolon before result so is defensible only when both clauses are long and already full of commas; in an ordinary sentence it is wrong, and it is always wrong before purpose so. Variants: so that (full form), so as to (infinitival), so ... that (degree)

In casual American English bare so stands in for so that in the purpose sense, and the dropped that is completely standard in speech. The same two letters do at least four other jobs, which is why that comma has to work so hard: the coordinating conjunction of result, one of the FANBOYS (The bus never came, so we walked); the degree adverb of so ... that and of bare intensifying so (I'm so tired); the pro-form standing in for a whole clause (I think so, If so, call me); and the discourse opener that starts a turn in conversation (So, where were we?).

  1. Text me when you land so I know you got there.
  2. She sat in the front row so she could hear the questions.
  3. We left the porch light on so the sitter wouldn't have to hunt for the keyhole.
  4. Write it on the calendar so you don't forget again.
  5. The bus never came, so we walked the last mile.
  6. Move the couch over so there's room for the tree.

See also: so that, so ... that, so as to, so (coordinating)

so that#

Pronunciation: /ˈsoʊ ðæt/, reduced /ˈsoʊ ðət/ · SOH-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: neutral Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose, or of result when preceded by a comma Pattern: [main clause] so that [clause with modal]. — purpose · [main clause], so that [clause]. — result · So that [clause], [main clause]. — fronted, formal Punctuation: The central rule of this category. No comma before so that = purpose: He spoke slowly so that everyone would understand (that was the goal). Comma before so that = result: He spoke slowly, so that everyone understood (that is what happened). A semicolon is always wrong here. Variants: so (that-dropped, informal)

The default purpose subordinator of American English, and the one to reach for when the two clauses have different subjects. The purpose clause normally carries can, could, will, would, or may; drop the modal and the sentence tips toward the result reading. Compare in order that (formal), bare so (casual), and in order to (same subject, infinitive).

  1. Label the boxes so that the movers know which room each one goes in.
  2. I set two alarms so that I wouldn't oversleep on the first day.
  3. The city widened the sidewalk so that strollers and wheelchairs could pass each other.
  4. She froze half the soup so that there would be something ready on Thursday.
  5. The window was left open all night, so that the whole apartment smelled like rain.
  6. So that nobody would be left out, they made the party a potluck.

See also: so, in order that, in order to, so as to, so ... that, lest

that#

Pronunciation: /ðæt/, reduced /ðət/ · THAT Type: subordinating (purpose use of the complementizer) · Register: literary, archaic Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] that [subject] may/might [verb]. · [imperative], that [subject] may/might [verb]. · [main clause] that [subject] [bare subjunctive verb]. Punctuation: No comma is required, and none appears when the purpose clause is the point of the sentence (He gave up his seat that she might sit down). A comma is usual after an imperative or a short main clause, where the purpose clause reads as an added statement of aim (Speak plainly, that all may understand). Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate. Modern edited prose simply rewrites the whole thing as so that. Variants: so that and in order that are the modern equivalents

Bare that as a purpose subordinator survives in scripture, hymns, oratory, poetry, and deliberate archaism: Come, that we may see. Only the purpose that pairs with may or might, which is the test for spotting it, because the same word does four commoner jobs: the complementizer that opens a noun clause (I know that he left), the relative pronoun (the book that I read), the demonstrative determiner and pronoun (that book, give me that), and the degree adverb of informal speech (It wasn't that cold).

  1. Speak plainly, that all may understand.
  2. He gave up his seat that the older woman might sit down.
  3. Come closer, that we may see your face.
  4. They fought that their children might live free.
  5. Write these things down, that the record be preserved.
  6. She worked all her life that her name might outlast her.

See also: so that, in order that, to the end that, lest

to the end that#

Pronunciation: /tə ði ˈɛnd ðæt/, reduced /tə ði ˈɛnd ðət/ · tuh-thee-END-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: formal, legal Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] to the end that [clause with modal]. · [main clause], to the end that [clause with modal]. · To the end that [clause with modal], [main clause]. — rare Punctuation: A comma before it is common in legal drafting, where the clause states an added aim; leave the comma out when the purpose clause is essential to the sentence. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate. Variants: to the intent that (legal, rarer)

A ceremonial in order that, at home in statutes, contracts, charters, and older sermons and nowhere else. End here means "purpose, goal," not "finish," which is what makes the phrase opaque to learners. In any ordinary sentence, write so that.

  1. The board publishes its minutes, to the end that the public may follow its work.
  2. Records shall be retained for seven years to the end that an audit may be conducted.
  3. The treaty was drafted to the end that neither party could later claim surprise.
  4. He endowed the chair to the end that the subject might never be dropped from the curriculum.
  5. All parties shall cooperate, to the end that the transfer may close on schedule.
  6. The rules are circulated in advance to the end that no member is taken unawares.
  7. To the end that the fund may continue in perpetuity, the trustees shall reinvest all income.

See also: in order that, to the intent that, so that, that, to which end, whereby

to the intent that#

Pronunciation: /tə ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðæt/, reduced /tə ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðət/ · tuh-thee-in-TENT-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: archaic legal — conveyancing and drafting boilerplate Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause], to the intent that [clause with may/might/shall]. · [main clause] to the intent that [clause with may/might/shall]. · [main clause] to the intent and purpose that [clause]. — the doubled drafting formula · To the intent that [clause], [main clause]. — rare Punctuation: A comma before it is the drafting norm, because the purpose clause is hung on a provision that is already complete; leave the comma out when the purpose restricts the main clause rather than commenting on it. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate, not independent. Variants: to the intent and purpose that, to the end that (the commoner twin), with the intent that (the modern American drafting equivalent)

An American meets this one in old deeds, wills, and trust instruments, in English conveyancing precedents, and in nineteenth-century statutes and charters — never in modern American drafting, which writes with the intent that or plain so that. It differs from its twin to the end that only in what the noun names: end names the goal aimed at, while intent names what was in the drafter's mind, which is why this version outlived the other in instruments where somebody's intention is itself the operative fact. The clause takes may, might, or shall, or occasionally the bare present subjunctive.

  1. The lands are conveyed to the trustees, to the intent that the same may be sold and the proceeds divided among my children.
  2. This release is given to the intent that no further claim shall be made upon the estate.
  3. I give my library to the parish, to the intent that it may be kept together and lent to any inhabitant.
  4. The Act was passed to the intent that the ancient rights of common might be preserved to the freeholders.
  5. The shares were transferred into her name alone, to the intent that she should take the whole beneficially.
  6. Notice shall be published for three weeks, to the intent that no creditor may afterwards plead ignorance of the proceeding.
  7. The parties have set their hands hereunto, to the intent and purpose that the covenants herein may bind their heirs and assigns.

See also: to the end that, with the intent that, in order that, so that, to which end

to which end#

Pronunciation: /tə ˌwɪtʃ ˈɛnd/, reduced /tə wɪtʃ ˈɛnd/ · tuh-wich-END Type: relative connective of purpose (subordinating) · Register: formal, legal Clause type: sentential relative clause of purpose Pattern: [independent clause], to which end [clause]. · [independent clause]. To which end, [clause]. · [independent clause], to which end [subject] shall/may [verb]. Punctuation: Comma before it, never a semicolon and never nothing — it is a supplementary relative, so it takes the punctuation of which is why rather than the punctuation of therefore. A comma after it is optional and turns up mainly when the phrase opens a new sentence. Variants: to that end, to this end (the demonstrative versions, which start a fresh sentence)

The relative twin of to that end: it reaches back, treats the whole preceding clause as the goal, and then names the step taken to reach it. End here means "purpose," exactly as in to the end that, and not "finish." It belongs to charters, trusts, and contracts; in ordinary prose start a new sentence with to that end.

  1. The trust must be preserved in perpetuity, to which end the trustees shall reinvest all income.
  2. The parties wish to close by March 1, to which end each shall deliver its schedules within ten days.
  3. The society was founded to keep the language alive, to which end it runs a summer school every July.
  4. He meant the farm to pass to his grandchildren intact, to which end he broke none of it up in the will.
  5. The council resolved to clear the backlog by fall, to which end two additional clerks were hired in April.
  6. The charter directs the board to publish every vote, to which end the minutes go up within a week of each meeting.

See also: to the end that, in order that, whereby, which is why, to that end

with the intent that#

Pronunciation: /wɪð ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðæt/, reduced /wɪð ði ɪnˈtɛnt ðət/ · with-thee-in-TENT-thut Type: subordinating (compound) · Register: formal, legal Clause type: adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] with the intent that [clause]. · [main clause] with the intention that [clause]. · [main clause] with intent to [base verb]. — the criminal-law formula · With the intent that [clause], [main clause]. — rare Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause, which is where it almost always sits. Comma after the clause in the rare fronted order. Never a semicolon — the clause is subordinate. Variants: with the intention that, with intent to + base verb, with the object that (legalistic)

States the actor's purpose as a matter of record, which is why it lives in statutes, wills, and pleadings: what is being asserted is what was in somebody's mind, not what actually came about. The clause takes a modal — would, should, might — or, in drafting, the present subjunctive: with the intent that the fund be divided equally. Its everyday relatives with the aim of and with the intention of take an -ing form and cannot govern a clause of their own.

  1. She signed the deed over in March with the intent that her sister would keep the house.
  2. The clause was inserted with the intent that neither side could walk away without notice.
  3. He transferred the shares with the intention that the dividends should fund the scholarship.
  4. The photographs were released with the intent that they be read as evidence, not as art.
  5. Congress wrote the exemption narrowly with the intent that only family farms would qualify.
  6. He was charged with entering the building with intent to commit theft.
  7. The memo went to every department with the intention that nobody could later plead ignorance.

Purpose: infinitives and phrases#

Non-finite purpose markers. These take a bare infinitive or an -ing form instead of a clause, and because the purpose phrase has no subject of its own it borrows the subject of the main clause — He left early to catch the train can only mean that he catches it. When the subjects differ, switch to so that or to in order for X to Y. Most of them take a comma when fronted and none when they follow. Two members sit outside that description: for to, the archaic doubled marker, does not front at all, and with this in mind takes no complement of any kind, because it points back at a goal already stated instead of naming a new one.

for the purpose of#

Pronunciation: /fɚ ðə ˈpɝpəs əv/ · fer-thuh-PUR-puss-uv Type: quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a gerund) · Register: formal Clause type: non-finite purpose adjunct (gerund phrase) Pattern: [main clause] for the purpose of [gerund phrase]. · For the purpose of [gerund phrase], [main clause]. · For purposes of [noun], [main clause]. Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted. Variants: for purposes of, for the purposes of

The purpose marker of statutes, contracts, and policy documents. It takes an -ing form and never an infinitive — for the purpose of collecting data, not for the purpose of to collect data. The for purposes of X variant does something slightly different: it limits a definition to one document or context.

  1. The data is retained for the purpose of verifying eligibility.
  2. For the purpose of this agreement, "employee" includes independent contractors.
  3. She entered the building for the purpose of serving the summons.
  4. The fund was created for the purpose of rebuilding the pier.
  5. Cameras were installed for the purpose of deterring theft, not monitoring staff.
  6. For purposes of the tax code, the two entities are treated as one.

See also: in order to, so as to, to, with a view to, with the aim of

for the sake of#

Pronunciation: /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk əv/, reduced /fɚ ðə ˈseɪk ə/ · fer-thuh-SAYK-uv Type: quasi-subordinator (prepositional, takes a noun or a gerund) · Register: neutral Clause type: non-finite purpose adjunct (noun or gerund phrase) Pattern: [main clause] for the sake of [noun/gerund]. · For the sake of [noun/gerund], [main clause]. · [main clause] for [possessive] sake. · For the sake of argument, [main clause]. — frozen hedge Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted, which is common. Never a semicolon — a phrase follows, not an independent clause. Variants: for [possessive] sake (for the children's sake), for the sake of argument, for goodness' sake (interjection, not purpose)

Purpose named through the person or thing that benefits — for the sake of the kids — or through the quality being served, as in for the sake of clarity. It takes a noun or an -ing form and never an infinitive. Two things sit next to it and are not it: the possessive shortcut for her own sake, which is the same construction turned inside out, and the exasperated interjection for heaven's sake, which names no goal at all.

  1. They stayed in the same city for the sake of the kids.
  2. For the sake of clarity, the report uses the 2019 boundaries throughout.
  3. She learned the software for the sake of keeping the job, not because she liked it.
  4. Both sides agreed to a short recess for the sake of letting tempers cool.
  5. Don't rewrite the whole chapter for the sake of one sentence.
  6. For the sake of argument, suppose the early numbers are right.
  7. He gave up the corner office for his partner's sake.

See also: in the interest of, for the purpose of, with the aim of, in order to, so that

for to#

Pronunciation: /fɚ ˈtu/, usually reduced /fɚ tə/ · fer-TOO Type: subordinating (infinitival), nonstandard · Register: archaic, dialectal, balladry Clause type: non-finite adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] for to [base verb]. · [main clause] for to [base verb] [object]. · [verb of motion] for to [base verb]. — the commonest frame Punctuation: No comma before it. It does not front, and it never takes a semicolon. Variants: plain to and in order to are the standard equivalents; for [subject] to [verb] is a different and fully standard pattern

A doubled purpose marker — the preposition for stacked on the infinitive to — that was ordinary in Middle and Early Modern English and survives now only in ballads, the King James Bible, Appalachian and Southern regional speech, and Irish and Scots English. The trap is the standard pattern that looks like it: in He waited for the rain to stop a subject sits between the two words and the sentence is unremarkable, while He waited for to see her is dialect. In edited American prose, write plain to.

  1. We come for to sing you a song about the river.
  2. He went down to the courthouse for to pay the fine himself.
  3. A sower went out for to sow his seed.
  4. She saved all winter for to buy the ticket home.
  5. They gathered on the bank for to watch the boats come in.
  6. I only stopped by for to say goodbye before the train.
  7. The old ballad has him riding out for to seek his fortune.

See also: to, in order to, so as to, that, to the end that

in an effort to#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ən ˈɛfɚt tu/, reduced /ɪn ən ˈɛfɚt tə/ · in-un-EFF-ert-too Type: quasi-subordinator (prepositional phrase plus infinitive) · Register: neutral; the house style of American news writing Clause type: non-finite purpose adjunct (to-infinitive) Pattern: [main clause] in an effort to [base verb]. · In an effort to [base verb], [main clause]. · [main clause] in an attempt to [base verb]. · [main clause] in a bid to [base verb]. — headline register Punctuation: No comma when it follows the main clause. Comma after the phrase when it is fronted, which is common in reporting. Never a semicolon — an infinitive phrase follows, not an independent clause. Variants: in an attempt to, in a bid to (headlinese), in hopes of + gerund

Purpose with a hedge built in. In order to and so as to simply name the aim; in an effort to reports that somebody was trying, and quietly declines to say it worked, which is why reporters reach for it when the outcome is still open or has already failed. Like every infinitival purpose marker it borrows the subject of the main clause, so the person making the effort has to be the person in the main clause. In a bid to is the compressed newspaper version and sounds odd outside a headline or a lede.

  1. The city cut fares in half in an effort to get riders back on the buses.
  2. In an effort to clear the backlog, the clinic added Saturday hours.
  3. Growers flooded the fields in an attempt to keep the frost off the buds.
  4. He rewrote his resume three times in an effort to get past the screening software.
  5. The league changed the overtime rule in an effort to cut down on head injuries.
  6. In an attempt to settle before trial, both sides agreed to one more day of mediation.

See also: in order to, so as to, with the aim of, to, for the purpose of

in order to#

Pronunciation: /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ tu/, reduced /ɪn ˈɔrdɚ tə/ · in-OR-der-too Type: subordinating (infinitival) · Register: neutral to formal Clause type: non-finite adverbial clause of purpose Pattern: [main clause] in order to [base verb]. · In order to [base verb], [main clause]. · [main clause] in order not to [base verb]. · [main clause] in order for [subject] to [base verb]. Punctuation: Comma after the fronted in order to phrase. No comma when it follows the main clause. Variants: in order not to, in order for ... to

The explicit, slightly formal version of the plain purpose infinitive to. Its real value is disambiguation: He wrote to the senator is unclear, He wrote in order to reach the senator is not. The negative is in order not to — careful writing puts not before to, though in order to not is common in speech. Use in order for X to Y when the two clauses have different subjects.

  1. He took a second job in order to pay off the loan faster.
  2. In order to qualify, applicants must have lived in the county for a year.
  3. She left the office at four in order not to hit rush hour.

Result: degree correlatives#

Paired constructions in which the first half sets a degree and the second half reports what that degree caused. Both halves have to appear — so without that, or too without to, leaves the sentence unfinished. No comma belongs inside the construction, with one exception: in speech and informal writing a comma can stand in for a dropped that after so (It was so loud, I left).

Subordinators, infinitivals, and relatives that attach a clause reporting an outcome. Because the clause they introduce is dependent, every one of them takes a comma or nothing — a semicolon in front of any word in this section is an error. That is exactly what separates them from the conjunctive adverbs in the last section.

Labels what followed, in so many words. It belongs to reports, academic writing, and legal prose; in speech Americans say so. Unlike so that, it can never be read as purpose — it states an outcome and nothing else, which makes it useful precisely where so that would be ambiguous.

  2. Rainfall was half the average, with the result that the reservoirs never refilled.
  3. The form was redesigned last year, with the result that error rates fell by a third.

  6. The rule was never enforced, with the consequence that everyone ignored it.

Result: conjunctive adverbs#

These express result but are adverbs, not conjunctions, and they cannot join two independent clauses on a comma. Between two independent clauses each one needs a semicolon or a period before it and normally a comma after it: The signature was forged; therefore, the contract is void. They also move around inside their clause, which a real conjunction cannot do — that mobility is the test for spotting them.

The semicolon rule is a rule about what follows, not about the word itself. When the adverb heads something less than an independent clause — a verbless tag after hence, an -ing phrase after thus or thereby — there is no second clause to join, so a plain comma or a dash is correct and a semicolon is wrong: The printer jammed twice, hence the delay.

Pronunciation: /ðɛrˈbaɪ/, also /ˈðɛrbaɪ/ · thair-BY Type: conjunctive adverb (result by means of what was just said) · Register: formal Clause type: heads an -ing participial phrase; rarely a full independent clause Pattern: [independent clause], thereby [-ing participle phrase]. · [subject] [verb] and thereby [verb] ... · [subject] [auxiliary] thereby [verb] ... · [independent clause]; thereby, [independent clause]. — rare Punctuation: A plain comma before it in the ordinary participial pattern, because what follows is a phrase and not an independent clause — The city rezoned the block, thereby allowing apartments must not take a semicolon. "By that means, and as a result of it." It is the adverb to whereby's relative: whereby has to hang on a noun that names a mechanism, while thereby points back at the whole preceding clause.
