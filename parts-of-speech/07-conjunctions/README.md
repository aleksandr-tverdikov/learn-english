# Conjunctions

> A conjunction is a word (or fixed pair of words) that joins other words, phrases, or clauses and shows the logical relationship between them.

> **📖 Looking for a specific conjunction?** This file covers the *grammar*. The *lexicon* — **[816 connectors](catalog/README.md)** across 12 categories, each with IPA pronunciation, the clause type it builds, a substitutable pattern, its punctuation rule, and five or more example sentences — lives in the **[Conjunction Dictionary](catalog/README.md)**. There is also an **[audio browser](catalog/browse.html)** with filters by type, register, and category.

## 1. What it does

A conjunction is a connector. It takes two or more grammatical units — two nouns, two verb phrases, two whole sentences — and welds them into one larger unit, while telling you *how* the pieces relate: addition, contrast, cause, condition, time, choice.

Without conjunctions you can only write lists of separate short sentences. With them you can build a single sentence that carries several ideas and ranks them against each other:

> I studied for the exam, **but** I still failed it.
>
> (**but** joins two independent clauses and marks the second as a contrast to what you'd expect from the first)

> We canceled the picnic **because** it rained all morning.
>
> (**because** attaches a reason clause to the main clause; the reason clause can no longer stand alone)

The two examples above show the fundamental split in the whole word class. **But** joins two things of *equal* grammatical rank — either half could be its own sentence. **Because** does something different: it takes a full sentence, `it rained all morning`, and demotes it into a dependent piece that must lean on a main clause. This is the difference between **coordination** (equal partners) and **subordination** (one clause serves another), and almost every rule in this document follows from it.

A third group of words — **however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless** — *feels* like conjunctions because they express the same relationships, but grammatically they are adverbs. They cannot legally join two independent clauses with only a comma. That distinction is one of the highest-value things an intermediate learner can master, and Section 2 and Section 8 return to it repeatedly.

> Traffic was terrible, **so** we missed the opening. *(so = coordinating conjunction; comma is correct)*
>
> Traffic was terrible; **therefore**, we missed the opening. *(therefore = conjunctive adverb; needs a semicolon or a period)*

## 2. Types

### Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)

Coordinating conjunctions join two elements of **equal grammatical rank**: word + word, phrase + phrase, or independent clause + independent clause. English has exactly seven, remembered by the acronym **FANBOYS**.

| Letter | Word | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| F | for | reason (formal, literary; means "because") |
| A | and | addition |
| N | nor | negative addition |
| B | but | contrast |
| O | or | alternative |
| Y | yet | contrast (with a sense of "despite that") |
| S | so | result |

Examples:

> She ordered a salad **and** a glass of iced tea. *(joins two noun phrases)*
>
> The apartment is small **yet** surprisingly bright. *(joins two adjectives)*
>
> He didn't call, **nor** did he answer my texts. *(joins two clauses; note the inverted word order after* nor*)*
>
> Hurry up, **or** we'll miss the last train. *(alternative / warning)*
>
> I trusted her, **for** she had never lied to me. *(formal reason; roughly "because")*

Notes on the two odd members: **for** as a conjunction is formal and somewhat old-fashioned in speech — most Americans say *because*. **Nor** normally follows a negative clause and triggers subject–auxiliary inversion (*nor did he*, not *nor he did*).

### Correlative conjunctions

Correlative conjunctions are **fixed pairs** that work together, one part before each of the joined elements. They coordinate, so the two joined elements must be grammatically parallel.

Common pairs:

| Pair | Meaning |
|---|---|
| both … and | joint addition |
| either … or | choice between two |
| neither … nor | exclusion of both |
| not only … but (also) | additive emphasis |
| whether … or | alternative (often in indirect questions) |
| just as … so | comparison (formal) |
| no sooner … than | immediate sequence |
| hardly/scarcely … when | immediate sequence |
| such … that / so … that | degree and result |
| the more … the more | proportional correlation |

Examples:

> She speaks **both** Spanish **and** Portuguese fluently.
>
> **Neither** the manager **nor** the assistants were told about the merger.
>
> He wants **not only** a raise **but also** a corner office.
>
> **No sooner** had we sat down **than** the fire alarm went off.
>
> **The more** you practice, **the easier** it gets.

### Subordinating conjunctions

Subordinating conjunctions introduce a **dependent (subordinate) clause** and mark its relationship to the main clause. The class is large; it is best learned by meaning category.

#### Time

**when, whenever, while, before, after, since, until, till, as, as soon as, as long as, once, by the time, no sooner … than, every time, the moment**

> **After** the movie ended, we walked to the diner.
>
> I'll call you **as soon as** I land.
>
> She has lived in Chicago **since** she graduated.

#### Cause and reason

**because, since, as, now that, inasmuch as, seeing that, in that, considering that**

> We left early **because** the baby was fussy.
>
> **Now that** the semester is over, I can finally sleep in.
>
> **Since** you're already up, could you get the door?

#### Purpose

**so that, in order that, lest, for fear that**

> Write it down **so that** you don't forget.
>
> He spoke quietly **lest** the children overhear. *(formal; takes the subjunctive)*

#### Result

**so … that, such … that, so that**

> The line was **so** long **that** we gave up.
>
> It was **such** a hot day **that** the asphalt softened.

#### Condition

**if, unless, provided (that), providing (that), as long as, in case, on condition that, supposing (that), whether, only if, even if**

> **If** the flight is delayed, text me.
>
> You can borrow the car **as long as** you fill the tank.
>
> Take an umbrella **in case** it rains.
>
> I'm going **even if** nobody else does.

#### Contrast and concession

**although, though, even though, while, whereas, whilst (BrE), much as, even if**

> **Although** she was exhausted, she finished the shift.
>
> My brother loves horror movies, **whereas** I can't watch them.
>
> **Much as** I admire him, I wouldn't work for him.

Note that *while* does double duty: it marks contrast here (*While I agree in principle, I have doubts*) and simultaneity in the time category (*She read while I cooked*). Only context decides. *Since* is split the same way between time and cause — see Section 8.

#### Concessive condition

**even if, whether … or, no matter how/what/where, whatever, however (= "no matter how"), regardless of whether**

These say that the outcome holds across every alternative, not just one.

> **No matter how** hard I try, I can't parallel park.
>
> **Whether** you agree **or** not, the vote stands.
>
> **However** you slice it, the numbers don't work. *(here* however *is a subordinator, not the conjunctive adverb)*

#### Preference and substitution

**rather than, sooner than, instead of (+ -ing), as opposed to**

> **Rather than** wait in line, we ordered delivery. *(bare infinitive after* rather than*)*
>
> I'd quit **sooner than** work for him again.

#### Manner and comparison

**as, as if, as though, like (informal), the way, than, as … as**

> He acted **as if** nothing had happened.
>
> Do it **the way** I showed you.
>
> She runs faster **than** I do.

#### Place

**where, wherever, everywhere, anywhere**

> Put the boxes **where** you found them.
>
> **Wherever** she goes, people recognize her.

#### Exception and proportion

**except that, save that, but that, insofar as, according as, the more … the less**

> The trip was perfect, **except that** it rained on the last day.
>
> **The more** he explained, **the less** I understood. *(proportion — the two comparatives rise or fall together)*

#### Complementizers (noun-clause introducers)

**that, whether, if** introduce clauses that function as nouns (subject, object, complement). Many grammars class these separately as *complementizers*, but they are traditionally listed under subordinating conjunctions.

> I know **that** you're busy. *(object noun clause)*
>
> **That** he lied surprised no one. *(subject noun clause)*
>
> I don't know **whether** she's coming.

### Conjunctive adverbs (transitional adverbs)

Conjunctive adverbs express conjunction-like meanings but are **adverbs**, not conjunctions. They modify a whole clause and can move around inside it. Crucially, they **cannot** join two independent clauses with only a comma — that produces a comma splice.

Grouped by meaning:

| Meaning | Members |
|---|---|
| Addition | also, besides, furthermore, moreover, in addition, additionally |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, nonetheless, conversely, on the other hand, instead, still, rather |
| Result | therefore, thus, hence, consequently, accordingly, as a result |
| Time/sequence | then, next, meanwhile, afterward, subsequently, finally, earlier |
| Emphasis | indeed, in fact, certainly, undoubtedly |
| Example | for example, for instance, namely, that is |
| Comparison | similarly, likewise |
| Concession | admittedly, granted, of course |
| Summary | in short, in conclusion, overall, all in all |

> The proposal was expensive; **however**, the board approved it.
>
> The proposal was expensive. **However**, the board approved it.
>
> The proposal was expensive; the board, **however**, approved it. *(note the mobility — a real conjunction could never move here)*

### Compound / phrasal conjunctions

Multi-word units that behave as a single subordinating conjunction. They are learned as fixed chunks.

**as soon as, as long as, as far as, as much as, in order that, so that, provided that, on condition that, in case, in the event that, given that, now that, so long as, rather than, sooner than, in spite of the fact that, despite the fact that, for fear that, on the grounds that, to the extent that**

> **In the event that** the server fails, the backup takes over.
>
> **Given that** you've never driven a stick shift, maybe I should drive.

### Quasi-coordinators

A small set of words that coordinate loosely but do not behave exactly like FANBOYS: **as well as, rather than, as much as, let alone, not to mention, plus (informal), only (informal "but")**. Note that a subject joined by *as well as* does **not** change verb agreement — only *and* creates a plural subject.

> The manager, **as well as** the interns, **was** invited. *(singular verb — as well as is not* and*)*
>
> I'd walk **rather than** pay for parking.
>
> He can't boil water, **let alone** cook a turkey.

### Discourse markers used conjunctively

In informal speech, words like **so, and, but, plus, anyway, still, only, except** often open a sentence as a connector rather than a joiner. This is standard in conversation and increasingly accepted in writing.

> **So**, what did the doctor say?
>
> I'd love to come. **Only** I have a deadline Friday.

## 3. Forms and inflection

**Conjunctions do not inflect.** They are a closed, invariable class. This means:

- No plural: there is no `*ands`, `*buts` (except when a conjunction is deliberately used as a noun: *no ifs, ands, or buts*).
- No tense, person, or number agreement: *and* is *and* regardless of what surrounds it.
- No comparative or superlative: no `*more because`, `*most although`.
- No case marking.

The only shape changes you will see are **fixed alternations and reductions**, not inflection:

| Base form | Variant | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| although | though | *though* is more informal and can also end a sentence as an adverb |
| until | till / 'til | *till* is standard, not a clipping of *until*; *'til* is nonstandard |
| while | whilst | *whilst* is British; Americans use *while* |
| provided that | provided | *that* is optional |
| so that | so | *that* is optional in informal use |
| in order that | so that | *in order that* is formal |
| whether or not | whether | *or not* is optional except when it means "regardless" |
| because | 'cause / cuz | spoken reduction only; never write in formal prose |

**Optional deletion of `that`.** The complementizer *that* is frequently omitted after common reporting and mental verbs:

> She said **(that)** she'd be late.
>
> I think **(that)** you're right.

Keep *that* when the clause is long, when the subordinate subject could be misread as an object, or in formal writing:

> The report showed **that** the new procedure reduced errors. *(without* that*, "the new procedure" briefly reads as the object of "showed")*

**Ellipsis after coordinators.** Repeated material is often dropped after *and, but, or*:

> She can sing and **[she can]** dance.
>
> I'll take the blue one, and my sister **[will take]** the red.

## 4. Position and word order

### Coordinating conjunctions

They sit **between** the elements they join and nowhere else. They cannot move, and they cannot be stacked freely (`*but so`, `*and but`, `*or and`). A few fixed combinations are idiomatic — *and yet*, *and so*, *but then*, *or else* — but these are learned pairs, not a general license to stack coordinators.

> Right: We could drive **or** we could fly.
>
> Wrong: We could drive, we could fly **or**.

Starting a sentence with **and, but, so, or, yet** is grammatical and common in American publishing, though some conservative style guides discourage it in formal academic writing:

> The plan looked flawless. **But** nobody had checked the budget.

### Subordinating conjunctions

The subordinate clause can come **before** or **after** the main clause, and the conjunction always stands at the front of *its own* clause.

> **Because** it was snowing, we stayed home. *(subordinate first — comma required)*
>
> We stayed home **because** it was snowing. *(main first — usually no comma)*

Fronting the subordinate clause emphasizes the circumstance and creates a smoother link to the previous sentence. Ending with it emphasizes the reason itself.

A subordinating conjunction never allows a coordinator in front of the main clause in the same sentence:

> Wrong: **Although** he apologized, **but** she stayed angry.
>
> Right: **Although** he apologized, she stayed angry.

### Correlative conjunctions

Each half sits immediately before the element it marks. Placement determines what is being contrasted — misplacement is the most common correlative error.

> Right: She **not only** wrote the script **but also** directed the film. *(two verb phrases)*
>
> Wrong: She **not only** wrote the script **but also** the film. *(unbalanced)*

Move both halves inward or outward together:

> He wants **either** coffee **or** tea. *(two nouns)*
>
> He **either** wants coffee **or** needs a nap. *(two verb phrases)*

### Conjunctive adverbs

These are the mobile ones. They can appear at the start, in the middle, or at the end of their clause:

> **However**, the results were inconclusive.
>
> The results, **however**, were inconclusive.
>
> The results were inconclusive, **however**.

Mobility is the diagnostic test: if the word can move, it is an adverb, not a conjunction.

### Ordering with other elements

When several connectors converge, the standard order is: coordinator → conjunctive adverb → subject.

> **But** nevertheless, the committee voted yes. *(grammatical but redundant — both words mark contrast, so usually pick one)*
>
> **But** then again, she's always been unpredictable. *(natural)*

Within a series joined by *and* or *or*, the conjunction appears only before the final item unless you use polysyndeton for effect:

> We bought bread, cheese, **and** olives. *(standard)*
>
> We bought bread **and** cheese **and** olives. *(polysyndeton — deliberate emphasis)*

## 5. Rules and patterns

### Punctuation: the core table

| Structure | Punctuation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Independent + coordinating conj. + independent | comma **before** the conjunction | `She called, but nobody answered.` |
| Two short independent clauses | comma optional | `He tried and he failed.` |
| Long independent clauses that already contain commas | **semicolon** before the coordinator (traditional; a period also works) | `We booked the flights, the hotel, and the car; but the trip fell through.` |
| Coordinating conj. joining words/phrases (not clauses) | **no** comma | `bread and butter` |
| Compound predicate (one subject, two verbs) | **no** comma | `She stood up and left the room.` |
| Series of three or more | commas, incl. serial comma before *and* | `red, white, and blue` |
| Series whose items contain commas | **semicolons** between items | `We invited Ann, the CEO; Bob, the CFO; and Carla.` |
| Correlative pair joining two independent clauses | comma before the second half | `Not only did he lie, but he also covered it up.` |
| Correlative pair joining words or phrases | **no** comma | `She speaks both Spanish and Portuguese.` |
| Subordinate clause **first** | comma **after** it | `If you're ready, let's go.` |
| Subordinate clause **second** | usually **no** comma | `Let's go if you're ready.` |
| Second clause is a nonessential contrast | comma allowed | `She agreed, although she had doubts.` |
| Two independents + conjunctive adverb | **semicolon** before, comma after | `It rained; therefore, we stayed in.` |
| Conjunctive adverb starting a new sentence | period before, comma after | `It rained. Therefore, we stayed in.` |
| Conjunctive adverb mid-clause | commas on both sides | `We, however, stayed in.` |
| Two independents, no connector | **semicolon** or period, never a comma | `It rained; we stayed in.` |

### The serial (Oxford) comma

American style guides split: the *Chicago Manual of Style*, MLA, and APA require the serial comma; AP style (journalism) omits it. Most American academic and book publishing uses it, and it prevents genuine ambiguity:

> I'd like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand, and God. *(three separate items)*
>
> I'd like to thank my parents, Ayn Rand and God. *(reads as if the parents are Ayn Rand and God)*

Use it consistently within any one document.

### Parallel structure

Elements joined by a coordinating or correlative conjunction must be **the same grammatical kind**: noun with noun, gerund with gerund, infinitive with infinitive, clause with clause.

> Wrong: She likes **hiking**, **swimming**, and **to bike**.
>
> Right: She likes **hiking**, **swimming**, and **biking**.

> Wrong: The job requires **patience**, **creativity**, and **you must work hard**.
>
> Right: The job requires **patience**, **creativity**, and **hard work**.

> Wrong: He is **neither** interested **nor** does he have time.
>
> Right: He is **neither** interested **nor** available.

Parallelism applies to prepositions too — repeat the preposition or drop it consistently:

> Right: She is interested **in** and committed **to** the project. *(different prepositions must both appear)*
>
> Wrong: She is interested and committed to the project.

### Subject–verb agreement with conjunctions

| Joiner | Agreement rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| and | plural | `Tom and Jerry **are** here.` |
| and (single entity) | singular | `Macaroni and cheese **is** my comfort food.` |
| either … or | verb agrees with the **nearer** subject | `Either the twins or Maria **is** driving.` |
| neither … nor | verb agrees with the **nearer** subject | `Neither Maria nor the twins **are** driving.` |
| or | nearer subject | `The keys or the wallet **is** missing.` |
| as well as / along with / together with | ignore the add-on; agree with the head | `The coach, as well as the players, **was** fined.` |
| both … and | plural | `Both the coach and the players **were** fined.` |
| not only … but also | nearer subject | `Not only the players but also the coach **was** fined.` |

Style advice: when the *nearer subject* rule produces an awkward sentence, reorder so the plural comes last, or rewrite.

### Required vs. optional elements

- **Both halves of a correlative are obligatory.** `*Either coffee, tea` is ungrammatical; you need `either coffee or tea`.
- **`also` in `not only … but also` is optional**: `Not only did he lie, but he bragged about it.`
- **`that` after `so`** is optional: `Speak up so (that) they can hear you.`
- **`that` as complementizer** is often optional (see Section 3), but obligatory after a noun, in a subject clause, and when other material separates the verb from the clause: `the fact **that** she left`, `**That** she left surprised no one`, `He argued at the hearing **that** the data was flawed.`
- **Negative + `nor` vs. `or`**: after a single negative, use *or* unless you want emphatic parallel negation: `He doesn't smoke **or** drink.` / `He neither smokes **nor** drinks.`

### Combining restrictions

- Do not pair a subordinator with a coordinator across the same two clauses: `Wrong: Because it rained, so we left.`
- Do not use *although* and *but* together: `Wrong: Although he tried, but he failed.`
- Do not use double negatives with *neither/nor*: `Wrong: I don't have neither.` → `I have neither.`
- Only one subordinator per clause: `Wrong: Even though although she agreed…`

### Clause types conjunctions create

| Conjunction type | Clause created | Can it stand alone? | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coordinating | independent clause | yes | equal partner |
| Correlative | matched equal elements | depends on element | equal partners |
| Subordinating (adverbial) | adverbial dependent clause | no | modifies the main clause |
| Complementizer (*that, whether, if*) | noun clause | no | subject, object, or complement |
| Relative pronoun (*who, which, that*) | relative clause | no | modifies a noun — **not** a conjunction |
| Conjunctive adverb | none — it's a modifier | n/a | signals a relationship between sentences |

### Sentence types conjunctions build

Which conjunction you choose determines what kind of sentence you end up with. This is the standard four-way classification:

| Sentence type | Structure | Conjunction used | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple | one independent clause | none (or a coordinator joining words/phrases) | `The bus was late.` |
| Compound | two or more independent clauses | coordinating or correlative; also a semicolon + conjunctive adverb | `The bus was late, so I walked.` |
| Complex | one independent + one or more dependent | subordinating | `Because the bus was late, I walked.` |
| Compound-complex | two or more independent + at least one dependent | both types together | `Because the bus was late, I walked, and I got there first.` |

Note that a conjunctive adverb never changes the count: `The bus was late; therefore, I walked.` is still a compound sentence, punctuated with a semicolon precisely because *therefore* is not a conjunction.

## 6. Common mistakes

- ✗ *It was late, however we kept working.* → ✓ *It was late; however, we kept working.* — **However** is a conjunctive adverb, not a conjunction; a comma alone creates a comma splice.

- ✗ *Although she was tired, but she finished the report.* → ✓ *Although she was tired, she finished the report.* — English uses either the subordinator or the coordinator, never both for the same contrast.

- ✗ *Because the road was closed, so we took the highway.* → ✓ *Because the road was closed, we took the highway.* — Same doubling error with cause/result.

- ✗ *She is not only smart but also she works hard.* → ✓ *She is not only smart but also hardworking.* — Correlatives must join parallel elements: adjective with adjective, not adjective with clause.

- ✗ *Neither the students or the teacher was ready.* → ✓ *Neither the students nor the teacher was ready.* — *Neither* pairs with *nor*; *either* pairs with *or*.

- ✗ *I called him, he didn't pick up.* → ✓ *I called him, but he didn't pick up.* / *I called him; he didn't pick up.* — Two independent clauses need a conjunction, a semicolon, or a period — never a bare comma.

- ✗ *The manager as well as the employees were notified.* → ✓ *The manager, as well as the employees, was notified.* — *As well as* is not *and*; it does not make the subject plural.

- ✗ *He enjoys to read, writing, and to travel.* → ✓ *He enjoys reading, writing, and traveling.* — Items in a coordinated series must share one form.

- ✗ *Despite of the rain, the game continued.* → ✓ *Despite the rain, the game continued.* / *In spite of the rain, the game continued.* — *Despite* takes no *of*; and note both are prepositions, so they take a noun phrase, not a clause.

- ✗ *Although of his experience, he was rejected.* → ✓ *Although he had experience, he was rejected.* — *Although* is a conjunction and requires a full clause with subject and verb.

- ✗ *I don't know that she is coming.* → ✓ *I don't know whether she is coming.* — Use *whether/if* for yes-no uncertainty; *that* states a fact.

- ✗ *I asked when will the meeting start.* → ✓ *I asked when the meeting would start.* — In an indirect question, the clause after the connector takes statement word order, not question inversion.

- ✗ *Unless you don't hurry, we'll be late.* → ✓ *Unless you hurry, we'll be late.* — *Unless* already means "if not"; adding another negative reverses the meaning.

- ✗ *He said me that he was busy.* → ✓ *He told me that he was busy.* / *He said that he was busy.* — Not a conjunction error itself, but *that*-clauses expose the *say/tell* pattern difference.

- ✗ *The reason is because the flight was canceled.* → ✓ *The reason is that the flight was canceled.* — *The reason is because* is redundant in formal writing; use *that*.

- ✗ *I will call you when I will arrive.* → ✓ *I will call you when I arrive.* — After time conjunctions (*when, before, after, as soon as, until*), use the present tense for future meaning.

## 7. American English notes

Conjunctions are one of the most uniform areas of English; differences between American and British usage are genuinely small. The real ones:

**Spelling and word choice**

| American | British | Note |
|---|---|---|
| while | whilst / while | *Whilst* is essentially nonexistent in American English and sounds affected. |
| toward | towards | Not a conjunction, but the same *-s* pattern shows up in *amid/amidst*, *among/amongst*. |
| till / until | till / until | Same in both; *'til* is nonstandard everywhere. |

**Punctuation**

- The **serial (Oxford) comma** is standard in most American academic and book publishing (Chicago, MLA, APA) and omitted in American journalism (AP). British usage more often omits it outside Oxford University Press. This is a house-style difference, not a national rule, but Americans encounter the serial comma more often.
- Americans place commas and periods **inside** closing quotation marks regardless of logic, which affects sentences where a conjunction follows a quotation: *She said "yes," but she looked unsure.*

**Collective-noun agreement**

Not a conjunction rule, but it interacts with coordination: American English treats collective nouns as singular (*The team is winning*), British English often as plural (*The team are winning*). So *The team and the coach* is plural in both, but *The team, as well as the coach,* takes a singular verb in American English more consistently.

**Register**

- *So* as a sentence opener (*So, here's the thing…*) is extremely common in American speech.
- *Like* as a conjunction (*It looks like it's going to rain*) is fully normal in American speech and increasingly in writing, though formal style still prefers *as if / as though*. British usage is somewhat more resistant.
- *Different than* before a clause is common and accepted in American English (*The result was different than we expected*); British English prefers *different from* or *different to*. *Different from* is safe in both varieties.
- *Nor* and *for* as coordinators are equally formal/literary in both varieties.

Beyond these, American and British conjunction inventories, meanings, and punctuation conventions are the same.

## 8. Tricky cases

### Words that belong to more than one class

Many connectors switch class depending on what follows them. The test: **conjunctions take a clause (subject + verb); prepositions take a noun phrase; adverbs stand alone.**

| Word | As conjunction | As preposition | As adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| before | *Call me **before** you leave.* | *Call me **before** noon.* | *I've been here **before**.* |
| after | *We ate **after** the show ended.* | *We ate **after** the show.* | *She arrived soon **after**.* |
| since | *I've known him **since** we were kids.* | *I've known him **since** 2010.* | *He left and hasn't called **since**.* |
| until | *Wait **until** I call.* | *Wait **until** noon.* | — |
| as | *She left **as** the bell rang.* | *He works **as** a nurse.* | *He's **as** tall as I am.* (degree) |
| than | *He runs faster **than** I do.* | *He runs faster **than** me.* | — |
| but | *I tried, **but** I failed.* | *Everyone **but** Sam came.* (= except) | — |
| for | *I left, **for** it was late.* | *This is **for** you.* | — |
| so | *I trained hard, **so** I won.* | — | *It's **so** cold.* |
| that | *I know **that** he left.* (conj.) | — | *It's not **that** cold.* (adv.) — also a determiner and relative pronoun |
| while | *She read **while** I cooked.* | — | not an adverb — in *Stay a **while*** it is a noun |
| like | *It looks **like** it'll rain.* (informal conj.) | *He sings **like** his father.* | — |
| yet | *It's small, **yet** comfortable.* | — | *She hasn't left **yet**.* |
| though | *Though it's late, I'll stay.* | — | *I'll stay, **though**.* (sentence-final adverb) |

### *Because* vs. *since* vs. *as*

All three give reasons, but they differ in strength and ambiguity risk. **Because** is the strongest and answers *why* directly. **Since** and **as** are weaker and can be misread as time markers:

> **Since** you took over the project, things have gone smoothly. *(genuinely ambiguous: "from the time you took over" or "because you took over"?)*
>
> **Because** you took over the project, things have gone smoothly. *(unambiguously reason)*
>
> **Ever since** you took over the project, things have gone smoothly. *(unambiguously time —* ever *forces the time reading)*

Prefer *because* when the reason is the new information.

### *If* vs. *whether*

Both introduce indirect yes-no questions, but *whether* is required in several environments:

| Environment | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| After a preposition | whether | *It depends on **whether** she agrees.* |
| Before an infinitive | whether | *I can't decide **whether** to go.* |
| As a subject clause | whether | ***Whether** he comes is up to him.* |
| With *or not* immediately after | whether | ***Whether or not** you like it…* |
| General indirect question | either | *I don't know **if/whether** it's open.* |

Also note that *if* is ambiguous between condition and indirect question: *Let me know if he calls* could mean "tell me in the event he calls" or "tell me whether he calls." *Whether* removes the ambiguity.

### *That* — four different jobs

> **That** book is mine. *(determiner)*
>
> I want **that**. *(demonstrative pronoun)*
>
> The book **that** I read was long. *(relative pronoun)*
>
> I know **that** you read it. *(subordinating conjunction / complementizer)*

Relative pronouns are **not** conjunctions, even though they connect clauses — they also fill a grammatical role (subject or object) inside their own clause. A complementizer *that* fills no such role, which is why it can be deleted.

### *Neither … nor* with more than two items

Standard usage restricts *either/neither* to two alternatives. For three or more, most style guides accept extension but suggest rewriting:

> **Neither** rain **nor** sleet **nor** snow stops the mail. *(accepted, rhetorical)*
>
> Rain, sleet, and snow do not stop the mail. *(cleaner)*

### *No sooner … than*, not *… when*

> Right: **No sooner** had she left **than** the phone rang.
>
> Wrong: **No sooner** had she left **when** the phone rang.

But *hardly/scarcely* pair with *when*:

> Right: **Hardly** had she left **when** the phone rang.

Both require inversion after the fronted negative adverb.

### *Not only* at the start triggers inversion

> **Not only** did he apologize, **but** he also sent flowers. *(inverted after fronted* not only*)*
>
> He **not only** apologized **but** also sent flowers. *(no inversion, mid-sentence)*

### Conjunction vs. conjunctive adverb: the movement test

If you can slide the word into the middle or end of its clause, it's an adverb and needs a semicolon or period before it:

> The price rose; **however**, sales held steady. → *The price rose; sales, **however**, held steady.* **movable = adverb**
>
> The price rose, **but** sales held steady. → Wrong: *The price rose, sales **but** held steady.* **immovable = conjunction**

### *So* vs. *so that*

> I saved money, **so** I could buy a laptop. *(result: I saved, and as a result I could buy it)*
>
> I saved money **so that** I could buy a laptop. *(purpose: buying was the goal)*

The comma plus bare *so* signals result; *so that* without a comma signals purpose.

### Fragments created by subordinators

A subordinate clause standing alone is a fragment. This is the single most common consequence of misusing subordinators:

> Wrong: **Because the store was closed.** *(fragment)*
>
> Right: We went home **because the store was closed.**

Deliberate fragments appear in fiction and advertising for effect, but avoid them in academic writing.

### *And/or*

Common in legal and technical writing, disliked in prose. Rewrite when possible:

> Bring a passport **and/or** a driver's license. → *Bring a passport, a driver's license, or both.*

## 9. Quick reference

| Type | Members (core) | Joins | Punctuation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Coordinating (FANBOYS)** | for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so | equal units | comma before, when joining two independent clauses | `She sang, **and** he played.` |
| **Correlative** | both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, whether…or | equal, parallel units | no comma between the pair unless clauses | `**Either** stay **or** go.` |
| **Subordinating — time** | when, while, before, after, since, until, as soon as, once | dep. clause to main | comma if the dependent clause comes first | `**After** it stopped, we left.` |
| **Subordinating — cause** | because, since, as, now that, seeing that | dep. clause to main | same | `We left **because** it ended.` |
| **Subordinating — purpose** | so that, in order that, lest | dep. clause to main | usually no comma | `Speak up **so that** they hear.` |
| **Subordinating — result** | so…that, such…that | dep. clause to main | no comma | `It was **so** loud **that** I left.` |
| **Subordinating — condition** | if, unless, provided that, in case, as long as, even if | dep. clause to main | comma if first | `**If** it rains, we'll drive.` |
| **Subordinating — concession** | although, though, even though, whereas, while | dep. clause to main | comma if first; often comma if second | `**Although** it's late, I'll stay.` |
| **Subordinating — manner** | as, as if, as though, than, the way | dep. clause to main | no comma | `He talks **as if** he owns it.` |
| **Subordinating — place** | where, wherever | dep. clause to main | no comma | `Sit **where** you like.` |
| **Subordinating — concessive condition** | even if, no matter how/what, whatever, whether…or | dep. clause to main | comma if first | `**Even if** it rains, we'll go.` |
| **Subordinating — preference** | rather than, sooner than | dep. element to main | comma if first | `**Rather than** argue, I left.` |
| **Complementizer** | that, whether, if | noun clause to verb/noun | no comma | `I hope **that** you're well.` |
| **Conjunctive adverb** | however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, thus, consequently, meanwhile | sentences (as a modifier) | semicolon or period before; comma after | `It's late; **therefore**, I'll go.` |
| **Compound/phrasal** | as soon as, in order that, given that, in the event that | dep. clause to main | as for subordinators | `**Given that** it's late, let's stop.` |
| **Quasi-coordinator** | as well as, rather than, let alone | loosely equal units | commas around the add-on | `The dog, **as well as** the cats, was fed.` |

**Three-second punctuation rule:**
`INDEPENDENT , FANBOYS INDEPENDENT` · `SUBORDINATE , INDEPENDENT` · `INDEPENDENT SUBORDINATE` · `INDEPENDENT ; ADVERB , INDEPENDENT`

## 10. Practice

**A. Fill in the blank** with the best conjunction.

1. ______ she had studied all night, she still felt unprepared.
2. Neither the printer ______ the scanner is working.
3. I'll wait here ______ you get back.
4. The restaurant was crowded; ______, we found a table by the window.
5. Let me know ______ you can make it on Friday. *(indirect question — be precise)*

**B. Identify the type** of the bolded connector (coordinating / correlative / subordinating / conjunctive adverb).

6. The bridge was closed, **so** we took the ferry.
7. **Not only** was the food cold, **but** the service was rude.
8. She kept working **even though** her contract had expired.
9. The data was incomplete; **nevertheless**, the team drew conclusions.

**C. Correct the error.**

10. Although the weather was bad, but the flight departed on time. Also fix: *The supervisor as well as the technicians were praised.*

**D. Fix the parallelism or the punctuation.**

11. The internship taught me patience, teamwork, and how to manage a budget.
12. It was raining hard, however, the game continued.
13. We can either take the train or we can drive.
14. She stood up, and walked out of the room.
15. Not only the interns but also the supervisor were late.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Although** (or *Even though* / *Though*) — a concession subordinator; the second clause contradicts the expectation set by the first. *But* cannot fill this slot: a coordinator has to sit *between* the two clauses, never in front of the first one.

2. **nor** — *neither* obligatorily pairs with *nor*. Note the verb *is* agrees with the nearer subject, *the scanner*.

3. **until** (or *till*) — a time subordinator meaning "up to the point when." *When* would change the meaning to a single point rather than a duration.

4. **however** (or *nevertheless* / *nonetheless*) — the semicolon before the blank plus the comma after it is the signature of a conjunctive adverb. A FANBOYS word would not fit: *but* takes a comma before it, not a semicolon, and takes no comma after it.

5. **whether** — this is an indirect yes-no question, so *whether* is precise. *If* is acceptable in speech but ambiguous: it could be read as a condition ("in the event that you can make it").

6. **Coordinating** — *so* is one of the FANBOYS, joining two independent clauses with a comma before it.

7. **Correlative** — *not only … but* is a fixed pair. Note the subject–auxiliary inversion (*was the food*) triggered by fronted *not only*.

8. **Subordinating** (concession) — *even though* introduces a dependent clause that cannot stand alone. Because the dependent clause follows the main clause, no comma is needed.

9. **Conjunctive adverb** — *nevertheless* is an adverb, which is why the semicolon before it and the comma after it are required. Proof: it could move — *the team, nevertheless, drew conclusions.*

10. Two fixes.
    - *Although the weather was bad, **but** the flight departed on time.* → **Although the weather was bad, the flight departed on time.** (or *The weather was bad, but the flight departed on time.*) — English marks the contrast once: use the subordinator or the coordinator, never both.
    - *The supervisor as well as the technicians **were** praised.* → **The supervisor, as well as the technicians, was praised.** — *As well as* is not *and*; it does not create a plural subject, so the verb agrees with *the supervisor*. The commas set off the parenthetical add-on.

11. **The internship taught me patience, teamwork, and budget management.** — Parallelism: the first two items are noun phrases, so the third must be one too, not a *how*-clause. (Alternatively make all three clauses: *how to be patient, how to work on a team, and how to manage a budget*.)

12. **It was raining hard; however, the game continued.** (or *It was raining hard. However, the game continued.*) — *However* is a conjunctive adverb, so a comma in front of it produces a comma splice. It needs a semicolon or a period.

13. **We can either take the train or drive.** — *Either … or* must bracket two parallel elements; here they are two bare verb phrases sharing *We can*. If you keep both full clauses, move the first half out: *Either we can take the train, or we can drive.*

14. **She stood up and walked out of the room.** — *Stood up* and *walked out* are a compound predicate under one subject, not two independent clauses, so no comma goes before *and*.

15. **Not only the interns but also the supervisor was late.** — With *not only … but also*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, *the supervisor*, which is singular. (Reversing the order to *Not only the supervisor but also the interns were late* also works and reads more naturally.)
