Interjections#

An interjection is a word or fixed phrase — wow, ouch, hey, well, um, oh my God — that expresses a reaction, manages a conversation, or gets someone's attention, and that stands outside the grammar of the sentence around it.

📖 Looking for a specific interjection? This file covers the grammar. The lexicon — 1,602 interjections across 20 categories, each with IPA pronunciation, a register label, and five or more example sentences — lives in the Interjection Dictionary. There is also an audio browser where you can search all of them and hear any one spoken.

1. What it does#

An interjection does not act as a subject, an object, a modifier, or a predicate. It attaches to a sentence the way a sticker attaches to a suitcase: it travels with the sentence and colors it, but it is not part of the structure. Remove it and the rest of the sentence is still complete and grammatical.

Ouch! I burned my hand. — Ouch expresses sudden pain. The clause I burned my hand is complete without it.

Well, I guess we should get going. — Well signals a shift or a mild reluctance. It links the speaker to the conversation, not to the clause.

Because an interjection is grammatically detached, it takes no agreement, no tense, and no case. It never changes to match a subject, and nothing in the sentence has to change to accommodate it. Compare a verb, which must agree (she runs / they run), with an interjection, which never does (Wow, she runs fast / Wow, they run fast).

Interjections do three broad jobs. They express something the speaker feels (Yuck! Yay! Ugh!). They manage the conversation — opening it, closing it, holding the floor, showing you are still listening (Hi. Um… Uh-huh. Anyway. Bye.). And they act on the listener, telling someone to be quiet, to stop, or to look this way (Shh! Whoa! Hey! Psst!).

Hey, um, could you pass the salt? Thanks. — Three interjections in one turn: hey gets attention, um buys planning time, thanks closes the exchange politely. None of them is part of the clause could you pass the salt.

Interjections are the most conversational word class in English. They are extremely common in speech, in dialogue, in text messages, and in social media, and they are rare in academic, legal, and technical prose. Knowing which ones to use — and, just as importantly, when not to use any — is a major part of sounding natural in American English.

2. Types#

Grammarians classify interjections along two independent dimensions: where the word comes from (primary vs. secondary) and what the word does (emotive, cognitive, volitive, phatic). A single interjection has one value on each dimension. Wow is primary and emotive; Nonsense! is secondary and emotive; Shh! is primary and volitive; Thanks is secondary and phatic. The subcategories below cover both dimensions, plus the functional families that sit alongside them.

Primary interjections#

Words that exist only as interjections. They are not derived from nouns, verbs, or any other class, they often contain sounds or sound sequences unusual for English (shh, psst, tsk, brr, ugh), and they cannot normally be used anywhere else in the grammar.

Common members: ah, aha, ahem, argh, aw, brr, duh, eek, eh, er, eww, gah, ha, hey, hm/hmm, huh, mm-hmm, oh, oof, ooh, oops, ouch, ow, oy, phew, psst, shh, tsk, ugh, uh, uh-huh, uh-oh, um, whee, whew, whoa, whoops, wow, yay, yikes, yippee, yuck, yum.

Psst! Over here. Brr, it's freezing in this office. Yikes, that's a lot more expensive than I expected.

Secondary interjections#

Words and phrases borrowed from other word classes and pressed into interjection duty. They keep their original meaning only loosely — Shoot! has nothing to do with firing a gun, and Boy! is not about a child.

Common members: Boy! Man! Dude! God! Goodness! Gosh! Heavens! Hell! Jeez! Nuts! Please! Right! Shoot! Sure! Well! Nonsense! Awesome! Bummer! Sweet! Perfect! Great! Congratulations! Welcome! Fine!

Man, that game went long. Nonsense! You're perfectly qualified for the job. Sweet! I've been waiting for this update for months.

Note the tell: the source word still exists in its original class. Man is a noun in the man left, but an interjection in Man, I'm tired — where it has no article, no plural, and no role in the clause.

Emotive interjections#

Interjections whose job is to express the speaker's feeling or bodily sensation. They are directed inward, not at the listener, and they can be uttered when no one else is in the room.

FeelingMembers
Painouch, ow, oof, yowch
Surprise / shockwow, whoa, whoa there, my goodness, holy cow, geez, yikes
Disgustugh, yuck, eww, blech, gross
Pleasure / delightyay, yippee, whee, mmm, yum, awesome, sweet, nice
Reliefwhew, phew, thank goodness, oh good
Feareek, yikes, help
Sympathy / tendernessaw, aww, oh no, poor thing
Frustration / annoyanceargh, ugh, come on, oh brother, great (sarcastic)
Boredom / indifferencemeh, whatever, eh
Regret / minor erroroops, whoops, oh shoot, my bad

Aw, look at that puppy. Whew, I thought I'd left my passport at home. Meh. The sequel wasn't nearly as good as the original.

Cognitive interjections#

Interjections that report a mental state or a change in the speaker's knowledge: understanding something, remembering something, doubting, thinking, or hesitating over a conclusion. They are sometimes called epistemic interjections.

Common members: aha, ah (of realization), oh (of new information), oh yeah, huh (of puzzlement), hmm, uh-huh (understanding), I see, right, ohhh, wait, hang on.

Aha! So that's why the file wouldn't open. Hmm, I'm not sure that number is right. Oh, you meant this Friday. Got it.

A useful contrast: oh used for surprise is emotive (Oh! You scared me.); oh used to register that you have just learned something is cognitive (Oh, the meeting moved to three.).

Volitive (conative) interjections#

Interjections aimed at the listener, demanding an action, a stop, or attention. They function much like imperatives but have no verb.

Common members: shh, psst, hey, yo, ahem, whoa, hold on, hush, come on, boo, hup, heave-ho, here (calling a dog), giddyup, shoo, sic 'em, fore! (golf), timber! (logging), heads up.

Shh! The baby just fell asleep. Ahem. I believe that's my seat. Whoa, slow down — you're going forty in a school zone.

Phatic interjections#

Interjections that oil the social machinery of talk: greetings, farewells, thanks, apologies, toasts, and blessings. Their content matters less than the fact that you said them at the right moment.

Common members: hello, hi, hey, howdy, good morning, welcome, bye, bye-bye, goodbye, so long, see ya, later, thanks, thank you, you're welcome, no problem, please, sorry, excuse me, pardon me, my bad, congratulations, cheers (as a toast), bless you, gesundheit, good luck, take care, no worries.

Hi! Long time no see. Excuse me, is this seat taken? Bless you. — Thanks.

Response particles and backchannels#

Short words that answer a yes/no question or that show the speaker is still listening. Backchannels do not take the floor; they hand it back.

Common members (answering): yes, yeah, yep, yup, uh-huh, mm-hmm, sure, absolutely, definitely, no, nope, nah, nuh-uh, not really, no way. Common members (backchanneling): mm-hmm, uh-huh, right, yeah, okay, I see, gotcha, sure, wow, really, huh.

"Did you send the invoice?" — "Yep." "So then he says he's quitting —" "Uh-huh." "— and moving to Denver." "Wow." "You don't have to come." "No, no, I want to."

Discourse markers used as interjections#

Words and short phrases that mark a turn in the discourse: starting, changing topic, softening, correcting, concluding. They are drawn from adverbs, verbs, and clauses, and they behave like interjections in that they are syntactically detached and set off by commas.

Common members: well, so, now, anyway, anyhow, look, listen, see, I mean, you know, actually, okay, alright, right, by the way, then, still, besides, first of all, in any case.

Well, that didn't go the way I planned. Anyway, where were we? Look, I'm not trying to start an argument. It's not that I dislike it — I mean, it's fine.

Fillers and hesitation markers#

Sounds and phrases used to hold the floor while planning what to say. They carry no propositional meaning at all.

Common members: uh, um, er, erm, hmm, ah, like, you know, sort of, kind of, I guess, well, so yeah.

The deadline is, um, next Tuesday, I think. He was like, you know, really upset about it. It's sort of a hybrid of the two designs.

Fillers are normal and universal in speech. They are edited out of formal writing entirely, and they are only written down to represent speech — in dialogue, transcripts, or to signal hesitation in a text message.

Onomatopoeic and imitative interjections#

Words that imitate a sound and are used as standalone exclamations. When they appear in a clause with a role (the loud bang woke me), they are nouns; when they stand alone, they function as interjections.

Common members: achoo, ahem, bam, bang, boing, boom, clang, crash, ding, ha-ha, hee-hee, honk, meow, moo, oink, plop, pop, splash, splat, thud, thump, vroom, whoosh, woof, zap, zoom.

Boom! The whole shelf came down. Achoo! — Bless you. Ha-ha, very funny.

Expletive interjections and minced oaths#

Interjections whose force comes from taboo or religious content. English has a graded ladder from strong profanity down through euphemistic substitutes (minced oaths) that are safe in almost any setting.

StrengthMembers
Strong profanity (avoid in any professional setting)the four-letter words, God damn it, Jesus Christ
Mild profanity and blasphemy (fine among friends, risky with strangers, clients, or children)damn, hell, crap, God! Jesus! Christ!
Minced oath (safe almost anywhere)gosh, golly, gee, geez/jeez, jeepers, darn, dang, doggone, shoot, shucks, heck, crud, gee whiz, for Pete's sake, my goodness, good grief, holy cow, holy moly, land sakes

Every minced oath is a deliberate distortion of a taboo word: gosh and golly from God, gee, geez, and jeepers from Jesus, darn and dang from damn, heck from hell, shoot from shit. That history explains why they cluster in the same slots as the words they replace.

Darn, I forgot to set an alarm. Good grief, this line is long. For Pete's sake, just call her.

Minced oaths are the safe register for classrooms, workplaces, and conversations with people you have just met. Gosh, golly, and shucks also sound old-fashioned or deliberately folksy to many Americans — fine for humor, less common in neutral speech.

Interjectional phrases (multi-word interjections)#

Fixed multi-word units that behave as a single interjection. They cannot be reordered, and their words usually cannot be changed or expanded.

Common members: oh my God, oh my gosh, oh my word, my goodness, good heavens, no way, get out of here, you're kidding, you've got to be kidding me, for crying out loud, what on earth, holy smokes, big deal, so what, there you go, here we go, way to go, no kidding, dear me, thank goodness, at last, of course, all right, that's a shame, tough luck.

Oh my God, I completely forgot about the appointment. No way! You met her in person? For crying out loud, would you two stop arguing? Way to go! That's a personal best.

Animal-directed, work, and warning calls#

A small, specialized set used to direct animals, coordinate physical work, or warn of danger. Most speakers know them passively even if they never use them.

Common members: whoa (to a horse), giddyup, gee (turn right), haw (turn left), shoo, scat, here boy, sic 'em, hup, heave-ho, timber, fore, heads up, look out, man overboard, all aboard, mush (to a dog team).

Whoa, girl. Easy. Heads up! There's a bike coming. Fore!

Written-only and internet interjections#

Forms that originated in typing. Most are initialisms, and they are read as whole words or spelled out only jokingly (ell-oh-ell, oh-em-gee). They are fully standard in casual digital writing and out of place in formal writing.

Common members: lol, lmao, omg, wtf, brb, ikr, smh, tbh, ngl, imo, rip, f (as in "press F"), haha, hehe, heh, woo, woot.

Omg, that's hilarious. Brb, someone's at the door. Smh. He did it again.

Do not confuse these with ordinary spoken interjections that simply happen to be typed a lot. Ugh, meh, oof, yikes, welp, aww, hmm, and yay are all said out loud and were in speech long before they were in chat windows; they are informal, but they are not internet-only.

3. Forms and inflection#

Interjections do not inflect. They have no plural, no possessive, no tense, no person, no number, no comparative, and no participle. Ouch is ouch whether one person or ten people are hurt, whether it happened yesterday or is happening now.

Ouch, I stubbed my toe. / Ouch, we all stubbed our toes. Wow, she's fast. / Wow, they were fast.

What interjections do instead is vary expressively — by repetition, by stretching sounds, and by punctuation. These are not inflections (they carry no grammatical information such as tense or number), but they are systematic enough to be worth learning.

Conversion: interjections that become other word classes#

An interjection can be converted into a noun or verb; once converted, it inflects normally. This is a change of class, not an inflection of the interjection.

✗ The audience wowed at the reveal. → ✓ The reveal wowed the audience. — Converted wow is a transitive verb: the thing that impresses is the subject, and the people impressed are the object.

4. Position and word order#

Initial position (the default)#

Most interjections come first, before the clause they attach to. This is the unmarked, most natural position for nearly every interjection in the language.

Wow, that's a great score. Hey, do you have a minute? Oh, I had no idea.

Standing alone#

An interjection can be a complete utterance with no clause at all. This is fully grammatical, and in dialogue it is extremely common.

"The flight's delayed four hours." "Ugh." Ouch! Bye!

Medial position (parenthetical)#

Interjections can interrupt a clause. They are then enclosed by two commas (or dashes), not one, and they are lowercase.

The whole thing was, well, a little embarrassing. She got the job — wow — on the first try. It costs, um, about two hundred dollars?

Medial fillers cluster at natural planning points: before a difficult word, after a conjunction, at a clause boundary.

I called him and, uh, left a message.

Final position#

Some interjections work at the end, usually to soften, to confirm, or to add an afterthought.

That was fun, huh? Let's just go, okay? I'll see you Thursday, then. Sorry about that, man.

Note that a final tag-like huh? or right? takes a question mark, because it turns the whole utterance into a question.

Order when interjections stack#

Multiple interjections in a row tend to follow one order in natural American speech:

reaction (oh, wow, ugh) → attention-getter (hey, yo) → discourse marker (well, so, look) → filler (um, uh) → clause

Oh is the strongest case: it comes first in nearly every combination it enters (oh hey, oh wow, oh no, oh yeah, oh well, oh boy), and reversing it sounds wrong.

Oh, hey, sorry — I didn't see you there. Oh, wow, um, I don't know what to say. Hey, so, um, are you two related? Well, um, I'm not sure how to answer that.

Within that, a few fixed pairings are effectively frozen and cannot be reversed:

Fixed orderNot said
Oh no!✗ No oh!
Oh yeah!✗ Yeah oh!
Oh boy!✗ Boy oh! — but the set phrase boy oh boy is fixed and fine
Well now✗ Now well
Uh-oh✗ Oh-uh
Oh my God✗ My oh God

Position relative to vocatives (names and titles)#

The interjection comes before the name, and each element is separated by a comma.

Hey, Maria, can you look at this? Well, Dr. Chen, that's an interesting theory. Oh, sir, you dropped your badge.

An interjection can also come before a yes/no particle, and the particle before the clause:

Oh, yes, I remember him. Well, no, that's not quite what happened.

Position in written dialogue#

In American punctuation, commas and periods go inside the closing quotation mark. An exclamation point stays inside if it belongs to the quoted speech.

"Wow," she said, "that's incredible." "Ouch!" he yelled. Hey, wait — did you say "yikes"?

5. Rules and patterns#

Rule 1 — No grammatical connection#

An interjection has no subject, no object, and no agreement with anything. It cannot be the subject or object of a verb, and it cannot take a determiner or an adjective while functioning as an interjection.

✓ Wow, the view is amazing. ✗ The wow was amazing. (Only valid if wow has been converted to a noun: the wow factor.)

Rule 2 — Punctuation depends on force, not on the word#

The same interjection takes different punctuation depending on how strongly it is said.

PunctuationForceExample
Commamild, run-on into the clauseOh, I didn't know that.
Exclamation pointstrong emotion, standaloneOh! You startled me.
Periodflat, deadpan, resignedOh. Okay then.
Question markpuzzlement, request to repeatHuh?
Ellipsistrailing, unfinishedWell… if you say so.
Two commasinterrupting a clauseIt was, oh, maybe ten years ago.

Rule 3 — Capitalization#

Capitalize an interjection when it begins a sentence; keep it lowercase in the middle of one. The interjection O (poetic vocative) is always capitalized and is never followed directly by a comma.

Oh, I see. / It was, oh, about four o'clock. O Captain! my Captain! (archaic/poetic; O addresses, oh exclaims) Ugh, my back. / Everything hurts and, ugh, I need coffee.

When God refers to the deity, it is capitalized in edited writing (Oh my God!). In casual writing many Americans write oh my god or omg without capitals; both are common, but the capitalized form is the one to use in anything formal enough to be edited.

Rule 4 — One exclamation point#

Standard American writing uses a single exclamation point. Multiple exclamation points (Wow!!!) belong to texting and social media only.

✓ Congratulations! / ✗ Congratulations!!! (in a business email)

Rule 5 — Interjections do not join clauses#

An interjection is not a conjunction. It cannot glue two independent clauses together; you still need a period, a semicolon, or a coordinator.

✗ I was tired, ugh, I went home. ✓ I was tired. Ugh. I went home. ✓ I was tired — ugh — so I went home.

Rule 9 — What interjections combine with#

  • ✓ A following full clause: Wow, this works.
  • ✓ A following vocative: Hey, Sam.
  • ✓ Another interjection: Oh, hey!
  • ✓ A following exclamative: Wow, what a mess!
  • ✓ An intensifier inside a fixed phrase: Oh my goodness; boy oh boy.
  • ✗ A subject or object slot: I said wow is fine only as a quotation of the word.
  • ✗ Modification by an adverb: ✗ very wow, ✗ extremely ouch (jokes aside).
  • ✗ Negation: ✗ not ouch.
  • ✗ Complementation: ✗ ouch that it hurt.

6. Common mistakes#

  • ✗ Oh! I forgot my keys, ugh!, I have to go back. → ✓ Oh, I forgot my keys. Ugh — I have to go back. — An interjection cannot join two clauses; use a period or a dash, and don't stack a comma after an exclamation point.
  • ✗ That was, Wow, unexpected. → ✓ That was, wow, unexpected. — Interjections in the middle of a sentence are lowercase; only capitalize at the start of a sentence.
  • ✗ Woah, that's expensive. → ✓ Whoa, that's expensive. — The standard spelling is whoa; woah is an informal misspelling.
  • ✗ Awe, that's so cute. → ✓ Aw, that's so cute. — Awe is a noun meaning reverent wonder; the interjection is aw (or aww).
  • ✗ Congratulations!!! Please find the report attached. → ✓ Congratulations! Please find the report attached. — One exclamation point in professional writing; multiples read as texting.
  • ✗ The research shows, um, three main effects. (in an essay) → ✓ The research shows three main effects. — Fillers belong to speech and are edited out of writing entirely.
  • ✗ Ouch, I broke it yesterday, ouched a lot. → ✓ Ouch! I broke it yesterday, and it hurt a lot. — Interjections do not inflect and cannot become verbs on the fly.
  • ✗ Hey Professor Alvarez, gimme an extension? → ✓ Dear Professor Alvarez, Could I request an extension? — Hey is too casual for an email to a professor or a client; use Dear or Hello.
  • ✗ Yay! for the team. → ✓ Yay for the team! — Don't put terminal punctuation inside the phrase; the exclamation point closes the whole utterance.
  • ✗ "Wow", she said. → ✓ "Wow," she said. — In American English the comma goes inside the closing quotation mark.
  • ✗ Cheers, thanks for the help. (American workplace) → ✓ Thanks for the help. — In American English cheers is mainly a drinking toast, not a way to say thanks or goodbye.
  • ✗ Sorry, do you know where the elevator is? → ✓ Excuse me, do you know where the elevator is? — Excuse me is the American default for approaching a stranger. Opening with sorry is the British pattern; in American English it more often reads as an apology for something you did.
  • ✗ I said oops to my boss when the server crashed. → ✓ I apologized to my boss when the server crashed. — Oops is for trivial slips; a serious error needs a real apology.
  • ✗ Yeah, I would be delighted to accept the position. → ✓ Yes, I would be delighted to accept the position. — Yeah is casual; formal contexts take yes.
  • ✗ Uh-huh, thank you for the promotion. → ✓ Thank you very much for the promotion. — Uh-huh is a backchannel, not a response to something important.
  • ✗ Bless you! (said after someone coughs) → ✓ Bless you! (said only after a sneeze) — The blessing is tied specifically to sneezing; after a cough, say nothing or Are you okay?

7. American English notes#

Interjections differ between American and British English more than most word classes do, because they are learned socially rather than taught. The differences below are real and well documented; the two varieties share the large core (oh, ah, wow, ouch, hey, well, um, hmm, yes, no, hello, thanks, sorry).

Hesitation sounds are spelled differently#

AmericanBritishSame sound?
uherYes — the spelling reflects r-dropping in British English, not a different sound
umermYes

American transcript: I was, uh, thinking we'd start Monday. British transcript: I was, er, thinking we'd start Monday.

Interjections that are distinctly American#

Each of these is either American in origin or far more common in the United States than in Britain. A few have traveled: meh, my bad, and duh now turn up in British speech, and gosh and golly are older British forms that survive much more vigorously in American English.

Gee, gee whiz, golly, gosh, jeez/geez, jeepers, dang, darn, doggone, shucks, gosh darn it, holy cow, holy moly, holy smokes, for Pete's sake, good grief, uh-oh, yikes, duh, meh, welp, oof, yay, yippee, yee-haw, howdy, yo, sup, dude, my bad, gesundheit, no problem, you bet, you betcha, atta boy / atta girl, way to go, bless your heart, uff da, ope, oy vey, aloha.

You bet I'll be there. Howdy! (Texas and the Southwest; friendly, slightly folksy elsewhere) Uff da, what a day. (Upper Midwest, from Norwegian) Ope, sorry, let me squeeze past ya. (Midwestern, when bumping into someone) Oy vey. (Yiddish-American; widely understood in the Northeast and in urban centers) Gesundheit! (German-American; an alternative to bless you after a sneeze)

Interjections that are distinctly British#

Recognize them, but leave them out if you are aiming for a neutral American voice: blimey, crikey, cor, oi, bloody hell, bollocks, ta (= thanks), cheers (= thanks / goodbye), cheerio (now dated), ta-ra, righto, right-o, whoops-a-daisy, hiya, alright? (as a greeting), innit, oi oi, aye (= yes; Scotland and northern England).

Spelling#

There are no systematic American/British spelling differences in interjections comparable to color/colour. The spelling of interjections is unsettled in both varieties (hmm/hm, aw/aww, ha-ha/haha), and the variation is stylistic rather than national. The one consistent national pattern is the uh/er and um/erm split described above.

8. Tricky cases#

The same word in several classes#

Many interjections are homonyms of other word classes. Test: if the word can be deleted with no damage to the clause, and it has no role in the clause, it is an interjection.

Diagnostic: She sings well. — delete well and the meaning changes, so it is an adverb. Well, she sings. — delete well and the clause is untouched, so it is an interjection.

Oh vs. O#

Oh is the ordinary interjection and is followed by a comma or an exclamation point. O is an archaic vocative particle used before a name in poetry and liturgy, is always capitalized, and takes no punctuation after it.

Oh, I didn't realize. O Lord, hear our prayer.

Ouch vs. Ow#

Both express pain. Ow is more often an involuntary cry at the moment of pain; ouch is common both at the moment and as a comment on something painful or costly, including figuratively.

Ow! (touching a hot pan) Ouch — a $600 repair bill? (figurative; ow would be odd here)

Huh has three distinct uses#

Huh? — I didn't hear you. (rising intonation, request to repeat) Huh. — Interesting, I didn't know that. (falling intonation, mild surprise) That's weird, huh? — Tag seeking agreement.

Yeah vs. Yea vs. Yay#

Three different words that learners collapse.

WordMeaningExample
yeahcasual yesYeah, I'll come.
yeaarchaic/formal yes; a vote in favorThe motion passed, twelve yeas to three.
yaycheer of delightYay! We won!

Yea and yay are pronounced the same way, which is why they get swapped in writing. Yeah is a separate word with a different vowel — it starts like yes, not like yay.

Answering negative questions#

American English answers the fact, not the wording of the question, which creates a well-known trap.

"You didn't call her?" — "No." = I did not call her. "You didn't call her?" — "Yes, I did." = I called her. (The bare yes is ambiguous; add the clause.) "Don't you want dessert?" — "No, thanks." = I don't want it.

Things that look like interjections but are not#

  • Exclamative clauses: What a mess! and How lovely! are full exclamative constructions with internal grammar, not interjections. But an interjection can precede one: Wow, what a mess!
  • Vocatives: Sarah! and Ladies and gentlemen are noun phrases used to address someone. Hey, Sarah! contains one interjection (hey) and one vocative (Sarah).
  • Imperatives: Stop!, Look out!, and Watch it! have verbs and are commands. Borderline items like Look and Listen have drifted into interjection use when they no longer mean "use your eyes/ears": Look, I'm not going to argue.
  • Sentence adverbs: Frankly, Honestly, Obviously, Unfortunately modify the whole clause and can be expanded (Frankly speaking…). They are the formal alternative to interjections and are acceptable in academic writing.
  • Conjunctive adverbs: However, Therefore, Moreover connect ideas logically. They are set off by commas like interjections but carry propositional meaning.
  • Onomatopoeia inside a clause: In the balloon went pop, pop is functioning as a noun-like complement, not an interjection. Standing alone — Pop! — it is an interjection.

Interjections that are older than they sound#

Alas, lo, lo and behold, hark, fie, alack, zounds, gadzooks, and egad are archaic. Modern Americans use them only for humor or period flavor. Using Alas sincerely in conversation will sound theatrical.

Alas, the printer is out of toner again. (jokey, and understood as jokey)

Sarcastic interjections#

Several positive interjections invert in meaning with flat intonation. Learners often miss this and take them literally.

Great. The car won't start. (= terrible) Wonderful. Another meeting. (= not wonderful) Thanks a lot. Now I have to redo it. (= you made things worse) Nice. (genuine with rising intonation; sarcastic when flat)

Sorry has three jobs#

Sorry, I broke your mug. (apology) Sorry? (I didn't hear you — rising intonation) I'm sorry for your loss. (sympathy; here sorry is an adjective in a clause, not an interjection)

10. Practice#

A. Fill in the blank with a natural interjection.

  1. ______, that's the third time the printer has jammed today.
  2. "Achoo!" "______ you." "Thanks."
  3. ______! I almost dropped the whole tray.
  4. ______, could you tell me where Gate 14 is?

B. Identify the type (primary/secondary and emotive/cognitive/volitive/phatic, or filler/discourse marker).

  1. Psst! Come here for a second.
  2. Aha! So the password was in the email all along.
  3. Nonsense! You did a wonderful job.

C. Correct the error.

  1. That was, Wow, the best meal I've had all year.
  2. "Wow", he said, "I didn't expect that".
  3. Hey Professor Nakamura, ugh, I totally forgot the assignment, my bad!!!

D. Choose the better option for the setting.

  1. (First email to a new client) Hey Dana, / Hi Dana,
  2. (Spoken in a job interview, reacting to a hard question) Um, wow, that's tough. / Well, that's a good question.
  3. (A novel, punctuating a flat, resigned reaction) "Oh! Fine." / "Oh. Fine."
  4. (Thanking a coworker in an American office) Cheers. / Thanks.
Answers
  1. Ugh (or Argh, Oh, come on) — Repeated mechanical failure calls for a frustration interjection. Ugh is the most neutral and workplace-safe.
  2. Bless you (or Gesundheit) — The blessing after a sneeze is a fixed phatic routine in American English; Gesundheit is the German-American alternative.
  3. Whew / Phew (or Whoa) — Relief after a near-accident. Both spellings are standard; whew is the more common American form. Never write few.
  4. Excuse me — The American default for opening a request to a stranger. Opening with Sorry is the British pattern; in American English it tends to sound like an apology for something you did.
  5. Primary, volitive. Psst exists only as an interjection (primary) and is aimed at getting the listener to act (volitive).
  6. Primary, cognitive. Aha is interjection-only and marks a change in the speaker's knowledge — a realization, not an emotion.
  7. Secondary, emotive. Nonsense is a noun pressed into interjection service (secondary), expressing the speaker's dismissive reaction (emotive).
  8. That was, wow, the best meal I've had all year. — A mid-sentence interjection stays lowercase and is enclosed by two commas.
  9. "Wow," he said, "I didn't expect that." — In American punctuation, the comma and the final period go inside the closing quotation marks.
  10. Dear Professor Nakamura, I'm very sorry — I forgot to submit the assignment. — Three fixes: Hey is too casual for a professor; ugh, totally, and my bad are conversational and trivialize a real mistake; and multiple exclamation points do not belong in academic or professional writing.
  11. Hi Dana, — Hey reads as too familiar for a first contact with a client. Hi is the American business-email default; Dear Dana is the safest choice for a formal matter.
  12. Well, that's a good question. — Well is an interview-safe discourse marker that buys you a moment. Um and wow both signal that the question caught you unprepared.
  13. "Oh. Fine." — A period gives the flat, resigned reading. The exclamation point would make the speaker sound startled or angry instead. Punctuation, not the word, carries the force.
  14. Thanks. — In American English cheers is a drinking toast. Using it to mean thanks or goodbye is British.
