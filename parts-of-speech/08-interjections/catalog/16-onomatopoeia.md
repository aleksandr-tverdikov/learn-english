# Onomatopoeia and sound effects used as interjections

> The words that *are* the noise — bang, splash, meow — dropped into a sentence to play a sound back instead of describing it.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What unites this group is that the word does not name a sound so much as perform one, which is why a speaker can drop it into a story with no verb, no article, and no apology. Because they are imitations, their spelling is elastic: vowels stretch to show how long the noise lasted (*vroooom*), the whole thing repeats to show it happened more than once (*beep beep beep*), and many take a comic *ka-* or *ker-* prefix that packs the fall or the blast into the word itself (*kaboom*, *kerplop*). Most of them work three ways at once — as a bare sound effect, as a stage direction dropped into a story, and as narration of what your own body or face is doing (*gulp*, *snort*, *mmm*). Punctuation follows the noise rather than the grammar: an exclamation point for impact, periods or ellipses for something slow or fading, and commas only where a speaker would really pause.

## Impacts, hits, and crashes

### bang

**Pronunciation:** /bæŋ/ &middot; *BANG*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bang bang*, *baaang*, *ka-bang*

The sound of a gunshot, a slammed door, or anything hard meeting anything hard. As an interjection it is almost always narration — you say it to replay the moment for your listener — and doubled up it is how Americans of every age imitate gunfire.

1. He came around the corner and — *bang* — right into a parked car.
2. "*Bang bang*," the kid said, pointing two fingers at his brother.
3. I heard a *bang* from the garage and my heart stopped.
4. You slam that trunk, *bang*, and the alarm goes off every time.
5. *Bang!* The screen door shut behind her before I could answer.

**See also:** [boom](#boom), [crash](#crash), [pow](#pow)

### bonk

**Pronunciation:** /bɑŋk/ &middot; *BONK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bonk bonk*, *bonk!*, *bop*

A light, hollow knock, usually on somebody's head. It is inherently comic: a bonk never really hurts and never breaks anything, so the word carries a little affection with it.

1. He stood up too fast and — *bonk* — right into the cabinet door.
2. *Bonk.* She tapped him on the head with the rolled-up newspaper.
3. The ball went *bonk* off the rim and out of bounds.
4. I go *bonk* into that low doorway about twice a week.
5. "*Bonk*," my daughter said, knocking gently on my forehead. "Anybody home?"

**See also:** [bang](#bang), [thud](#thud), [whack](#whack)

### boom

**Pronunciation:** /bum/ &middot; *BOOM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *kaboom*, *ka-boom*, *boom boom*, *badaboom*

A deep explosion or a rolling, distant crash — thunder, fireworks, a demolition. It is also the all-purpose sound effect for something landing with finality, which is why people say it the moment a thing is finished or proven.

1. There was a *boom*, and then every car alarm on the block went off.
2. You mix it, you pour it, *boom* — dinner's done.
3. *Boom!* The whole sky lit up green over the lake.
4. The transformer blew with a *boom* we felt through the floor.
5. He circled the total on the whiteboard. "And *boom*, there's your answer."

**See also:** [bang](#bang), [crash](#crash), [boom](01-joy.md#boom)

### clang

**Pronunciation:** /klæŋ/ &middot; *CLANG*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *clang clang*, *clank*, *clunk*, *clangity-clang*

Metal hitting metal — a dropped wrench, a cell door, a bell. The three forms are not interchangeable: a clang rings, a clank rattles, and a clunk just stops dead.

1. The gate shut behind us with a *clang*.
2. *Clang!* He dropped an entire tray of pans.
3. Something went *clunk* under the hood and the car died.
4. You hear that *clank* every time the radiator kicks on.
5. The bell went *clang clang clang* and we all filed back inside.

**See also:** [crash](#crash), [ding](#ding), [click](#click)

### crash

**Pronunciation:** /kræʃ/ &middot; *KRASH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *craaash*, *crash bang*, *crashhh*

Something big breaking or falling all at once — dishes, a shelf, two cars. Dropped into a story it replays the exact instant of impact, and it is usually followed by the silence that comes right after.

1. There was a *crash* in the kitchen and then total silence.
2. He tipped the ladder and — *crash* — down came the whole display.
3. *Crash!* The wind took the patio umbrella straight into the glass door.
4. I heard a *crash*, then footsteps, then nothing at all.
5. Every time the cat gets on the counter it's *crash*, *crash*, *crash* until morning.

**See also:** [bang](#bang), [boom](#boom), [clang](#clang)

### knock knock

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɑk ˌnɑk/ &middot; *NOK-nok*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *knock-knock*, *knock knock knock*

The sound of rapping on a door, said out loud instead of actually knocking — usually because the door is open or there is no door at all. It is also the fixed opening of the knock-knock joke, where it obliges the listener to answer "Who's there?"

1. "*Knock knock*," he said, leaning into the doorway. "Got a second?"
2. *Knock knock!* Anybody home?
3. She tapped the edge of the cubicle. "*Knock knock* — sorry to interrupt."
4. "*Knock knock*." "Who's there?" "Boo." "Boo who?" "Don't cry, it's just a joke."
5. *Knock knock knock* on the window, and the dog lost his mind.

**See also:** [tap tap](#tap-tap), [ding-dong](#ding-dong), [psst](10-attention.md#psst)

### pow

**Pronunciation:** /paʊ/ &middot; *POW*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *kapow*, *ka-pow*, *blam*, *ka-blam*, *powie*

A comic-book punch — the sound of a fist landing, printed in big letters. Americans use it for any sudden forceful hit and, by extension, for anything that arrives with a jolt.

1. He swung and — *pow* — right in the jaw.
2. You're driving along, and *pow*, a deer comes out of nowhere.
3. *Kapow!* That's exactly the reaction she was going for.
4. One punch, *pow*, and the whole fight was over.
5. She hit the last note and *pow*, the entire room stood up.

**See also:** [bang](#bang), [smack](#smack), [wham](#wham)

### smack

**Pronunciation:** /smæk/ &middot; *SMAK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *smack smack*, *smaack*

A flat, open-handed slap — or the wet sound of lips, whether kissing or chewing. Context settles which one you mean, and it always does.

1. *Smack!* She got the mosquito on the first try.
2. The book landed on the desk with a *smack*.
3. He kissed her on the cheek — *smack* — and ran for the bus.
4. Please quit going *smack smack* with that gum.
5. The ball hit the wall with a *smack* and came right back at me.

**See also:** [whack](#whack), [mwah](#mwah), [thud](#thud)

### splat

**Pronunciation:** /splæt/ &middot; *SPLAT*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *splatt*, *ker-splat*, *splat!*

Something soft and wet hitting a flat surface — a dropped tomato, a bug on the windshield, a snowball on a wall. It is the messy cousin of *thud*.

1. The egg hit the floor with a *splat*.
2. *Splat!* Right on the windshield, of course.
3. He dropped the whole scoop of ice cream — *splat* — on his own shoe.
4. The paintball caught him in the chest, *splat*, and the game was over.
5. She threw the wet towel at the wall and it landed with a *splat*.

**See also:** [splash](#splash), [plop](#plop), [thud](#thud)

### tap tap

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtæp ˌtæp/ &middot; *TAP-tap*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *tap-tap*, *tap tap tap*, *rap rap*, *rat-a-tat*, *rat-a-tat-tat*

A light, quick knocking — on a door, a shoulder, a microphone. Said out loud, it warns someone gently that you are about to interrupt them.

1. *Tap tap.* "Is this thing on?"
2. She went *tap tap* on my shoulder and I nearly came out of my chair.
3. "*Tap tap* — mind if I steal you for a minute?"
4. There was a *rat-a-tat* on the door at six in the morning.
5. He kept up a steady *tap tap tap* with his pen until I said something.

**See also:** [knock knock](#knock-knock), [click](#click), [ahem](#ahem)

### thud

**Pronunciation:** /θʌd/ &middot; *THUD*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *thump*, *thud thud*, *whump*, *ker-thud*

A heavy, dull impact with no ring to it — a body hitting the floor, a book dropped flat, a bass drum. *Thump* is the same sound with a little more bounce, and it is the standard word for a heartbeat.

1. Something hit the roof with a *thud*.
2. He fell out of the top bunk — *thud* — and got right back up.
3. I could hear my own heart going *thump thump thump*.
4. The suitcase came off the belt and landed with a *thud*.
5. *Thud.* The dog jumped off the couch and the whole house shook.

**See also:** [bonk](#bonk), [boom](#boom), [whomp](#whomp)

### whack

**Pronunciation:** /wæk/ &middot; *WAK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *thwack*, *whap*, *whop*, *whack!*

A hard swat with something long or flat — a bat, a stick, a rolled-up magazine. *Thwack* adds a little sting and a little comedy; *whap* is the same hit landing on something softer.

1. *Whack!* He caught the ball right on the sweet spot.
2. She hit the branch — *thwack* — and the snow came down on both of us.
3. The screen door swung back and got me, *whap*, right in the shoulder.
4. He gave the vending machine a *whack* and the chips came loose.
5. *Thwack.* The arrow was dead center.

**See also:** [smack](#smack), [wham](#wham), [pow](#pow)

### wham

**Pronunciation:** /wæm/ &middot; *WAM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *whammo*, *wham-o*, *whamm*

A sudden violent collision, almost always one nobody saw coming. It is the favorite word for telling car-accident stories and for turns of events that hit just as hard.

1. I was stopped at the light and — *wham* — somebody rear-ended me.
2. *Wham!* The wind slammed the door so hard the pictures came off the wall.
3. Everything's fine for a year, and then *wham*, the rent goes up four hundred dollars.
4. He never even braked. Just *wham*, straight into the guardrail.
5. You take one bite and *whammo*, the heat hits you.

**See also:** [bang](#bang), [pow](#pow), [whack](#whack)

### whomp

**Pronunciation:** /wɑmp/ or /wʌmp/ &middot; *WAHMP* or *WUHMP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *whump*, *ka-whomp*, *whomp!*

A big, soft-edged impact with real weight behind it — a mattress dropped, a rotor blade, a wave against a hull. Do not confuse it with *womp womp*, which is a sad trombone rather than a hit.

1. The snow slid off the roof and landed with a *whomp*.
2. *Whomp!* He belly-flopped straight into the deep end.
3. You could feel each *whomp* of the rotors before the helicopter cleared the trees.
4. She dropped the duffel bag on the bed — *whomp* — and started unpacking.
5. The waves hit the side of the boat with a slow *whomp*, *whomp*, *whomp*.

**See also:** [thud](#thud), [boom](#boom), [womp womp](#womp-womp)

## Water, liquid, and cooking

### drip

**Pronunciation:** /drɪp/ &middot; *DRIP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *drip drip*, *drip-drip-drip*, *drip, drip*

Water falling one drop at a time. Repeated, it is the sound of a leak you cannot ignore, and it doubles as a figure of speech for anything that arrives slowly and maddeningly.

1. *Drip. Drip. Drip.* I could not sleep through it.
2. The faucet went *drip drip* all night long.
3. There's a *drip* somewhere behind that wall and I can't find it.
4. It wasn't a flood — it was *drip, drip, drip* until the ceiling gave.
5. The story came out *drip*, *drip*, one email at a time.

**See also:** [plop](#plop), [splash](#splash), [plink](#plink)

### fizz

**Pronunciation:** /fɪz/ &middot; *FIZ*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *fizzz*, *fsss*, *fizzle*

The hiss of carbonation — a can opening, soda over ice, a tablet dissolving. *Fizzle* is the same sound running out of energy, which is why it also names a disappointing ending.

1. He cracked the can and it went *fizz* all over the counter.
2. *Fizzz* — that first pour over ice is the best sound in the world.
3. The tablet hit the water with a *fizz* and disappeared.
4. You could hear the *fizz* die down as it went flat.
5. The big finale went *fizzle* instead, and everybody just went home.

**See also:** [hiss](#hiss), [sizzle](#sizzle), [pop](#pop)

### glug

**Pronunciation:** /ɡlʌɡ/ &middot; *GLUG*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *glug glug*, *glug-glug-glug*, *gluglug*

Liquid coming out of a narrow opening in gulps — a jug emptying, a bottle being chugged. Said out loud it usually means somebody is drinking fast and you are teasing them about it.

1. He tipped the jug back — *glug glug glug*.
2. The bottle emptied into the sink with a slow *glug*.
3. "*Glug glug*," she said, miming a beer. "That's his entire weekend."
4. You hear that *glug* every time the water cooler refills.
5. He poured the milk too fast and it came out in one long *glug*.

**See also:** [gulp](#gulp), [slurp](#slurp), [drip](#drip)

### plop

**Pronunciation:** /plɑp/ &middot; *PLOP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *kerplop*, *kerplunk*, *plop plop*, *ploop*

A small round thing dropping into liquid, or, more loosely, anything landing without ceremony. The *ker-* forms are longer and funnier and put the whole fall into the word.

1. The ice cube went *plop* into the glass.
2. He dropped his keys off the dock — *kerplunk* — and just stared at the water.
3. *Plop.* The frog was off the rock before anyone got a picture.
4. One raindrop, *plop*, right on the back of my neck.
5. The dumplings go in the pot — *plop*, *plop*, *plop* — and you're done.

**See also:** [splash](#splash), [splat](#splat), [drip](#drip)

### sizzle

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪzəl/ &middot; *SIZ-uhl*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *sizzzle*, *tssss*, *ssss*

Fat hitting a hot pan. It is the sound of cooking going right, and Americans borrow it for anything with heat behind it.

1. Butter in the pan — *sizzle* — and the whole house smells like Sunday.
2. *Sssss.* The steak hit the grill and nobody said another word.
3. You want that *sizzle* before you add the onions.
4. The rain hit the hot pavement with a *sizzle* and came right back up as steam.
5. He touched the iron and it went *sizzle*, and yes, it was as bad as it sounds.

**See also:** [fizz](#fizz), [hiss](#hiss), [crunch](#crunch)

### splash

**Pronunciation:** /splæʃ/ &middot; *SPLASH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *sploosh*, *kersplash*, *splish-splash*, *splish splash*

Something hitting water hard enough to throw it. *Sploosh* is bigger and sillier, and *splish-splash* is the light repeated version you use with a kid in a bathtub.

1. *Splash!* He jumped in with all his clothes on.
2. The bucket went over the side with a *sploosh*.
3. You hear a *splash* out on the lake and you know somebody caught something.
4. *Splish-splash* — she smacked the bathwater with both hands.
5. A truck went by and — *splash* — I was soaked to the knees.

**See also:** [splat](#splat), [plop](#plop), [glug](#glug)

## Eating, drinking, and mouth sounds

### chomp

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɑmp/ &middot; *CHOMP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *chomp chomp*, *chomp-chomp*, *chompchomp*

A big, deliberate bite with the jaw closing hard. It is the sound of cartoon eating and of a dog taking food straight out of your hand.

1. *Chomp!* The dog took the whole hot dog in one bite.
2. He held it up to his mouth and went "*chomp chomp*" like a shark.
3. The horse leaned over the fence and — *chomp* — there went my sandwich.
4. She took one *chomp* out of the apple and handed it back.
5. *Chomp chomp chomp.* That's the sound of a toddler discovering crackers.

**See also:** [crunch](#crunch), [munch](#munch), [nom](#nom)

### crunch

**Pronunciation:** /krʌntʃ/ &middot; *KRUNCH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *crunch crunch*, *kruntch*, *scrunch*

The sound of something brittle giving way — chips, gravel underfoot, frozen snow, a fender.

1. *Crunch.* You could hear him eating chips over the phone.
2. Every step on the frozen driveway went *crunch*.
3. The bumper hit the pole with a *crunch* I'm still hearing.
4. She bit into the celery — *crunch* — right in the middle of the meeting.
5. *Crunch crunch crunch* through the leaves the whole way home.

**See also:** [chomp](#chomp), [munch](#munch), [crash](#crash)

### gulp

**Pronunciation:** /ɡʌlp/ &middot; *GULP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *gulp gulp*, *gullp*, *gulp!*

Swallowing hard — either a big drink or the dry swallow of somebody who has just realized they are in trouble. Standing alone in writing, a single *gulp* almost always means fear.

1. *Gulp.* I hadn't read a single page of the assignment.
2. "Your mother's on the phone." "*Gulp*."
3. She looked up at the top of the ladder and said, "*Gulp*."
4. *Gulp gulp gulp* — he finished the whole bottle without breathing.
5. He saw the bill, *gulp*, and reached for his card anyway.

**See also:** [glug](#glug), [slurp](#slurp), [gulp](05-fear.md#gulp)

### mmm

**Pronunciation:** /m̩ː/ (a long hum with the lips closed — no vowel in it) &middot; *MMM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *mmmm*, *mm*, *mmmmm*, *mm-mmm*

A closed-lip hum of pleasure, nearly always about taste or smell. Stretched out, it is a compliment to whoever cooked; the whole meaning rides on the length and the falling pitch, since there is no vowel to carry it.

1. *Mmm.* That's the best thing I've eaten all year.
2. She took one bite and went, "*Mmmmm*."
3. *Mmm* — smell that. Somebody down the street is grilling.
4. "How is it?" "*Mmm*," he said with his mouth full, and gave a thumbs up.
5. *Mmm*, this coffee is exactly what I needed.

**See also:** [yum](#yum), [nom](#nom), [mmm](03-pain.md#mmm)

### munch

**Pronunciation:** /mʌntʃ/ &middot; *MUNCH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *munch munch*, *munch-munch*, *munchmunch*

Steady, unhurried chewing — snacking rather than eating a meal. Doubled, it is what people caption a picture of themselves with a bowl of something.

1. *Munch munch.* Don't mind me, I found the pretzels.
2. He sat there going *munch, munch* through an entire bag of carrots.
3. "*Munch*," she texted, with a picture of the popcorn.
4. The rabbit worked through the lettuce — *munch munch munch* — without ever looking up.
5. I could hear you *munch* through that entire phone call.

**See also:** [chomp](#chomp), [crunch](#crunch), [nom](#nom)

### nom

**Pronunciation:** /nɑm/ &middot; *NOM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text  
**Variants:** *nom nom*, *nom nom nom*, *om nom nom*, *omnomnom*

Happy, greedy eating, borrowed from Cookie Monster and now standard online. It is playful and a little childish on purpose: adults use it about food they are enjoying too much to be dignified about.

1. *Om nom nom.* This pizza is unbelievable.
2. She captioned the picture of the cake "*nom nom nom*."
3. "How's the sandwich?" "*Nom*."
4. Leftover birthday cake for breakfast — *nom*.
5. *Nom nom* — I regret nothing.

**See also:** [yum](#yum), [mmm](#mmm), [munch](#munch)

### slurp

**Pronunciation:** /slɝp/ &middot; *SLURP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *slurp slurp*, *slurrrp*, *sluuurp*

Drinking or eating noisily through pursed lips — soup, noodles, the last inch of a milkshake through a straw. At an American table it reads as bad manners, which is exactly why the word is funny everywhere else.

1. *Slurp.* He got the last of it out of the straw and the whole table looked over.
2. She ate the ramen the right way — *slurrrp* — and I was jealous.
3. That *slurp* at the bottom of a milkshake is the best part of the milkshake.
4. The dog drank out of the toilet again — *slurp slurp slurp*.
5. *Sluuurp*, and the noodles were gone in about nine seconds.

**See also:** [glug](#glug), [gulp](#gulp), [smack](#smack)

### yum

**Pronunciation:** /jʌm/ &middot; *YUM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *yum yum*, *yummy*, *yum-yum*, *yummers*

Said about food that looks or tastes good — before the first bite as often as after it. *Yummy* is the adjective form and sounds noticeably more childish coming out of an adult.

1. *Yum.* Is that garlic bread?
2. "Chocolate chip." "*Yum yum.*"
3. She looked at the menu and said, "*Yum*, they still have the short rib."
4. *Yum* — save me a piece of that.
5. "Try it." "*Yummy*," he said, in a voice that meant the opposite.

**See also:** [mmm](#mmm), [nom](#nom), [ew](04-disgust.md#ew)

## Involuntary body sounds

### achoo

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtʃu/ &middot; *uh-CHOO*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *ah-choo*, *atchoo*, *kerchoo*, *hachoo*

A sneeze, written down — and also the word Americans say out loud to imitate one. A real sneeze in earshot obliges everyone present to answer with *bless you*.

1. *Achoo!* "Bless you." "Thanks — allergies."
2. He went *ah-choo* three times in a row and gave up on the meeting.
3. Every spring it's *achoo, achoo, achoo* from March to June.
4. The cat sneezed — one tiny *kerchoo* — and looked personally offended.
5. She turned away from the table. "*Achoo.* Sorry, don't shake my hand."

**See also:** [cough](#cough), [sniff](03-pain.md#sniff), [bless you](17-ritual.md#bless-you)

### ahem

**Pronunciation:** /əˈhɛm/ &middot; *uh-HEM*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *a-hem*, *ahem-ahem*, *hem*, *hem-hem*

A deliberate throat-clearing used as a signal rather than a real cough. It asks for attention, marks that you are about to speak, or delivers a discreet correction without anyone having to say the rude part out loud.

1. "*Ahem.* I believe you're in my seat."
2. *Ahem* — if anyone's counting, I finished the report an hour ago.
3. She gave a small *ahem* and the room went quiet.
4. "*Ahem!*" The teacher didn't have to say anything else.
5. *Ahem.* Some of us have been standing in this line for twenty minutes.

**See also:** [cough](#cough), [psst](10-attention.md#psst), [ahem](10-attention.md#ahem)

### burp

**Pronunciation:** /bɝp/ &middot; *BURP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *urp*, *buuurp*, *berp*, *belch*

The word said in place of the noise, usually by or about the person who just made it. Doing the real thing in public is rude in the United States; saying the word about it is a joke.

1. *Burp.* "Excuse me."
2. He drank the whole soda and let out a *burp* you could hear in the parking lot.
3. "*Burp*," she said flatly, patting the baby's back. "There it is."
4. One sip of seltzer and it's *urp* for the next ten minutes.
5. *Burp* — sorry, that's just the carbonation talking.

**See also:** [hiccup](#hiccup), [gulp](#gulp), [achoo](#achoo)

### cough

**Pronunciation:** /kɔf/ or /kɑf/ &middot; *KAWF* or *KOFF*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *cough cough*, *koff koff*, *ahem*

A fake cough that badly and deliberately hides a word you are pretending not to say. Written between asterisks online, it is one of the most reliable ways to be sarcastic in text; spoken, it comes out as a stage cough that fools nobody.

1. Somebody around here — *cough* — never returns the stapler.
2. "It was a group project." "*Cough cough.*"
3. He said the project was right on schedule. *Cough.*
4. The best team in the division — *cough*, not this year — plays Sunday.
5. *Cough.* I believe the word you're looking for is "sorry."

**See also:** [ahem](#ahem), [snort](#snort), [heh](#heh)

### hiccup

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɪkʌp/ or /ˈhɪkəp/ &middot; *HIK-up*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hic*, *hiccough*, *hic!*, *hiccup hiccup*

The word for the spasm, said out loud in place of it. The short form *hic* is the standard way American comedy shows that a character has had too much to drink.

1. *Hic.* "Excuse me." *Hic.* "This happens every time I eat too fast."
2. He wobbled in, went "*hic*," and everybody knew exactly where he'd been.
3. *Hiccup.* Try holding your breath and counting to ten.
4. "*Hic* — sorry, I can't stop." "Drink it upside down."
5. Every few seconds, *hic*, right in the middle of her toast.

**See also:** [burp](#burp), [gulp](#gulp), [hic](03-pain.md#hic)

### snort

**Pronunciation:** /snɔrt/ &middot; *SNORT*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *snrk*, *snerk*, *snort laugh*

A sharp laugh out through the nose, written as a stage direction. It signals amused contempt — the thing was funny and dumb at the same time — and online it has largely taken over the job *lol* used to do for that reaction.

1. *Snort.* Sure he did.
2. She read his excuse and let out a *snort*.
3. "He told them he's 'basically a doctor.'" "*Snort.*"
4. *Snrk* — I'm sorry, I shouldn't laugh at that.
5. He tried to keep a straight face and it came out as a *snort*.

**See also:** [heh](#heh), [har har](#har-har), [lmao](18-internet.md#lmao)

## Laughs and kisses

### bwahaha

**Pronunciation:** /bwɑhɑˈhɑ/ &middot; *bwah-hah-HAH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *mwahaha*, *muahaha*, *bwahahaha*, *mwah-ha-ha*

The villain laugh — big, theatrical, and always at least partly a joke about being evil. Nobody has ever laughed this way by accident.

1. "I scheduled the meeting for 4:55 on a Friday. *Bwahaha*."
2. *Mwahaha!* My plan is working perfectly.
3. He rubbed his hands together and went "*bwahahaha*" like a cartoon.
4. I ate the last of the good ice cream. *Bwahaha.*
5. "*Muahaha*," she typed, right after taking the last open parking spot.

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [heh](#heh), [bwahaha](18-internet.md#bwahaha)

### ha

**Pronunciation:** /hɑ/ &middot; *HAH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hah*, *ha!*

A single hard laugh — which, on its own, rarely means something was funny. Depending on the tone it means "I was right," "I caught you," or "that is absurd."

1. *Ha!* I knew you'd say that.
2. "*Ha.* Very funny." She wasn't smiling.
3. *Ha* — you actually found it.
4. He read the note and just said, "*Ha*."
5. "You'll get used to it." "*Ha!* We'll see."

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [har har](#har-har), [heh](#heh)

### haha

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɑhɑ/ &middot; *HAH-hah*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ha ha*, *hahaha*, *ha-ha*, *hahahaha*

Written laughter, where the length is the message: *haha* is polite acknowledgment, *hahahaha* is the real thing, and a flat *ha ha* with a space in the middle usually reads as sarcasm.

1. *Haha*, that's a good one.
2. "*Hahaha* — okay, you got me."
3. She wrote back "*ha ha*" and I could feel the eye roll from here.
4. He was laughing so hard it was just *ha ha ha ha* into the phone.
5. *Haha*, no. Absolutely not.

**See also:** [heh](#heh), [hee-hee](#hee-hee), [lol](18-internet.md#lol)

### har har

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɑr ˌhɑr/ &middot; *HAR-har*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *har-har*, *har har har*, *har de har har*

A fake, flat laugh that means the joke was not funny. It is always sarcastic — no one has ever genuinely laughed like this.

1. "*Har har.* Very original."
2. He made the same short joke about my height again. *Har har.*
3. *Har de har har.* You should take that act on the road.
4. "Get it? Because you're always late." "*Har har har*."
5. She typed "*har har*" and then, a second later, "seriously though."

**See also:** [ha](#ha), [haha](#haha), [heh](#heh)

### hee-hee

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhihi/ &middot; *HEE-hee*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *heehee*, *hee hee*, *tee-hee*, *teehee*

A high, giggly laugh — mischief rather than comedy. *Tee-hee* is the same laugh with more self-awareness, usually written by an adult being deliberately cute about something small and bad.

1. *Hee-hee.* I put the whole thing on his card.
2. She covered her mouth. "*Tee-hee*, sorry, I shouldn't laugh."
3. The kids went *hee hee hee* behind the couch until we found them.
4. "*Teehee*," he wrote, which is a lot coming from a grown man.
5. *Hee-hee* — wait until she sees what we did to his desk.

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [heh](#heh), [teehee](18-internet.md#teehee)

### heh

**Pronunciation:** /hɛ/ &middot; *HEH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *heh heh*, *hehe*, *hehehe*, *heh.*

A small, low, one-breath laugh. It means mild amusement at most, and it is very often the sound of somebody laughing at their own joke.

1. *Heh.* That's actually not bad.
2. "*Heh heh* — I've done that more times than I'll admit."
3. He read it, said "*heh*," and kept scrolling.
4. *Hehe*, okay, that's funnier than it has any right to be.
5. "*Heh.*" That was the entire review.

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [snort](#snort), [hehe](18-internet.md#hehe)

### ho ho ho

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhoʊ hoʊ ˌhoʊ/ &middot; *HOH-hoh-hoh*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ho ho*, *ho-ho-ho*, *hohoho*

The deep belly laugh, now almost entirely the property of Santa Claus. Any other time of year it reads as a deliberately hammy, old-fashioned chuckle.

1. "*Ho ho ho!* Merry Christmas!"
2. He put on the red suit and practiced his *ho ho ho* in the mirror.
3. *Ho ho* — and what do we have here?
4. The mall Santa's *ho ho ho* carried all the way to the food court.
5. "*Ho ho ho*," my uncle said, handing out presents in his regular clothes.

**See also:** [ha](#ha), [haha](#haha), [bwahaha](#bwahaha)

### mwah

**Pronunciation:** /mwɑ/ &middot; *MWAH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *muah*, *mwuah*, *mwah mwah*, *muah!*

The sound of a kiss, usually one blown rather than given. It ends phone calls between family and close friends, and at the end of a text it does the work a kiss on the cheek does at the end of a visit.

1. "Love you, honey. *Mwah*."
2. She kissed her fingers and threw it toward the stage. "*Mwah!*"
3. Thank you for the birthday flowers — *mwah*.
4. *Mwah mwah* — one on each cheek, and then she was gone.
5. "Drive safe. *Muah*."

**See also:** [smooch](#smooch), [smack](#smack), [mmm](#mmm)

### smooch

**Pronunciation:** /smutʃ/ &middot; *SMOOCH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *smooch smooch*, *smoochies*, *smoocharoo*

A loud, playful kiss — the word said instead of the kiss, or written where the kiss would go. It is warmer and sillier than *mwah*, and it stays inside the family and the friend group.

1. "*Smooch!*" she said, and kissed the top of his head.
2. Thanks again for everything — *smooch*.
3. He leaned in for a *smooch* and the dog got there first.
4. *Smooch smooch.* Goodnight, sweetie.
5. "Give Grandma a *smooch* before we go."

**See also:** [mwah](#mwah), [smack](#smack), [hee-hee](#hee-hee)

## Machines, alarms, and everyday noises

### beep

**Pronunciation:** /bip/ &middot; *BEEP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *beep beep*, *beeep*, *bip*, *beep boop*

A short electronic tone — a microwave, a truck backing up, a car horn. Doubled, it is also how you say "move, please" from behind a steering wheel, and *beep boop* is the standard joke sound for a robot.

1. The microwave went *beep* and nobody moved.
2. *Beep beep!* Coming through with hot coffee.
3. Leave a message after the *beep*.
4. The truck backed up — *beep, beep, beep* — for a solid two minutes.
5. "*Beep boop*, I am a robot," he said in the flattest voice he had.

**See also:** [honk](#honk), [ding](#ding), [buzz](#buzz)

### buzz

**Pronunciation:** /bʌz/ &middot; *BUZ*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *bzzz*, *bzzt*, *buzz buzz*, *bzz*

A low vibrating hum — a bee, a phone face-down on a table, a bad fluorescent light — or the harsh *bzzt* of a wrong-answer buzzer.

1. My phone went *buzz* in the middle of the ceremony.
2. *Bzzz.* There is a bee in this car. There is a bee in this car.
3. "The capital of Australia is Sydney." "*Bzzt.* Canberra."
4. The light over the sink has a *buzz* I cannot get rid of.
5. *Buzz buzz* — that's the third text in under a minute.

**See also:** [beep](#beep), [hiss](#hiss), [zap](#zap)

### click

**Pronunciation:** /klɪk/ &middot; *KLIK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *click click*, *clack*, *clickety-clack*, *click-click*

The small hard snap of a mechanism — a latch, a pen, a mouse button, a seatbelt. *Clack* is the harder, flatter version, and *clickety-clack* is a train on rails or long fingernails on a keyboard.

1. *Click.* The door locked behind me and my keys were on the counter.
2. You'll hear a *click* when the car seat is in right.
3. He kept going *click click click* with that pen until somebody said something.
4. *Clickety-clack* down the hall — you could hear her heels coming.
5. The seatbelt went *clack* against the door frame.

**See also:** [tick-tock](#tick-tock), [snap](#snap), [tap tap](#tap-tap)

### creak

**Pronunciation:** /krik/ &middot; *KREEK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *creeeak*, *creak creak*, *crrreak*

The long dry complaint of old wood or an unoiled hinge. It is the standard sound effect of a haunted house, and people apply it cheerfully to their own knees.

1. The stairs went *creak* and we both froze.
2. *Creeeak.* The door opened by itself, which was the last thing I needed.
3. Every board on that porch has its own *creak*.
4. He got up off the floor — *creak*, *creak* — and made a joke about turning forty.
5. There's a *creak* in the hallway right where the cat likes to sleep.

**See also:** [squeak](#squeak), [click](#click), [thud](#thud)

### ding

**Pronunciation:** /dɪŋ/ &middot; *DING*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *ding ding*, *ding ding ding*, *ting*, *diiing*

A single clear bell tone — an elevator, a kitchen timer, a notification. Three of them in a row means somebody has just given the right answer.

1. *Ding.* The elevator opened on the wrong floor.
2. The oven went *ding* and the whole house came running.
3. "*Ding ding ding* — we have a winner."
4. Every time an email lands, *ding*, and there goes my train of thought.
5. The bell over the shop door went *ting* as she walked out.

**See also:** [ding-dong](#ding-dong), [ring](#ring), [ding ding ding](14-realization.md#ding-ding-ding)

### ding-dong

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪŋ ˌdɔŋ/ &middot; *DING-dawng*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ding dong*, *dingdong*, *ding-dong ding-dong*

A doorbell, in its two tones. Said out loud it means "someone is at the door," or it announces that the someone is you.

1. *Ding-dong.* "I'll get it."
2. "*Ding dong!* Pizza's here."
3. The bell went *ding-dong* twice and the dog lost his mind.
4. She knocked and then said "*ding-dong*" out loud, since the bell was broken.
5. *Ding-dong* — and of course there was nobody there.

**See also:** [ding](#ding), [knock knock](#knock-knock), [ring](#ring)

### hiss

**Pronunciation:** /hɪs/ &middot; *HISS*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hissss*, *ssss*, *sss*

A long *s* — a snake, a leaking tire, a radiator, or a cat that has had enough. A crowd can hiss too, which is a public and old-fashioned way of booing.

1. *Hisssss.* The tire was flat before we got to the highway.
2. The cat gave one warning *hiss* and left the room.
3. You could hear the radiator *hiss* all night long.
4. *Ssss* — careful, that pan is still hot.
5. The crowd let out a *hiss* when the ref made the call.

**See also:** [sizzle](#sizzle), [fizz](#fizz), [hiss](04-disgust.md#hiss)

### honk

**Pronunciation:** /hɑŋk/ &middot; *HONK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *honk honk*, *hooonk*, *hoooonk*

A car horn — and also a goose. Said out loud, it usually reports that somebody honked at you, or that you would very much like to honk at somebody.

1. *Honk!* The guy behind me had been waiting a full half second.
2. *Honk honk* — that's your ride out front.
3. The geese went over the field, *honk*, *honk*, all morning.
4. He gave one friendly little *honk* and waved.
5. One more *honk* out of that guy and I'm getting out of the car.

**See also:** [beep](#beep), [vroom](#vroom), [quack](#quack)

### ka-ching

**Pronunciation:** /kəˈtʃɪŋ/ &middot; *kuh-CHING*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *cha-ching*, *ker-ching*, *ka-ching ka-ching*

An old cash register drawer flying open — the sound of money being made. Nobody uses that machine anymore, but everyone still says the sound when a deal closes or a payday lands.

1. He signed the contract and I heard *ka-ching* in my head.
2. Two hundred a ticket, six hundred seats — *cha-ching*.
3. *Ka-ching!* The deposit finally cleared.
4. Every time they add another fee, *ka-ching*, straight to the bottom line.
5. She sold the entire set in one afternoon. *Cha-ching.*

**See also:** [ding](#ding), [ta-da](#ta-da), [cha-ching](01-joy.md#cha-ching)

### plink

**Pronunciation:** /plɪŋk/ &middot; *PLINK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *plunk*, *plink plink*, *plinka-plink*

A small, thin, high sound — one piano key, a pebble on a windshield, a drop landing in an empty sink. *Plunk* is the same noise a size heavier.

1. *Plink.* A single drop hit the bottom of the empty sink.
2. He sat at the piano going *plink, plink* with one finger.
3. The hail started with a *plink* on the roof of the car.
4. She dropped a quarter in the jar — *plunk* — and walked off.
5. *Plink plink plink* — the sleet was coming down on the skylight.

### pop

**Pronunciation:** /pɑp/ &middot; *POP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *pop pop*, *pop!*, *popopop*, *poppop*

A small sharp burst — a balloon, a cork, a knuckle, a bubble. Repeated fast, it is how Americans describe gunfire heard from a distance.

1. *Pop!* The cork hit the ceiling.
2. The balloon went *pop* and the baby burst into tears.
3. We heard three *pops* and everybody hit the ground.
4. My knee goes *pop* every single time I stand up.
5. *Pop, pop, pop* — the popcorn finally got going.

### ring

**Pronunciation:** /rɪŋ/ &middot; *RING*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *ring ring*, *riiing*, *brring*, *ring-a-ling*

A telephone or a hand bell, said out loud where the sound would go. *Ring ring* is now faintly retro, since hardly anybody's phone actually rings anymore.

1. *Ring ring.* "That's probably them now."
2. She held her hand up to her ear. "*Ring ring* — hello, reality calling."
3. The old rotary phone in the hall had a *ring* you could hear from the yard.
4. *Brring!* Nobody has ever picked up a phone faster.
5. Two *rings* and it went to voicemail, which means he declined it.

**See also:** [ding](#ding), [ding-dong](#ding-dong), [buzz](#buzz)

### snap

**Pronunciation:** /snæp/ &middot; *SNAP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *snap snap*, *snapp*, *snap!*

A quick clean break, or a snap of the fingers. As a sound effect it marks the exact instant something gave way; as *snap snap* with the fingers it means "hurry up, look alive."

1. *Snap.* The pencil broke right in half.
2. He went *snap snap* with his fingers and the dog sat down.
3. The branch went *snap* under my foot and every deer in the county left.
4. One *snap* of the reins and the horse took off.
5. *Snap* — that is the sound of a phone screen you cannot afford to replace.

**See also:** [pop](#pop), [click](#click), [crunch](#crunch)

### squeak

**Pronunciation:** /skwik/ &middot; *SKWEEK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *squeek*, *squeak squeak*, *skweek*

A high thin noise out of something small or unlubricated — a mouse, a sneaker on a gym floor, a hinge, a dog toy.

1. The floorboard let out a *squeak* and the baby woke up.
2. *Squeak, squeak, squeak* — I got the shopping cart with the bad wheel, naturally.
3. Somebody's sneakers went *squeak* the entire second half.
4. *Squeak!* The mouse was more startled than I was.
5. The dog squeezed that toy — *squeak squeak squeak* — for forty-five minutes.

**See also:** [creak](#creak), [chirp](#chirp), [plink](#plink)

### tick-tock

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtɪk ˌtɑk/ &middot; *TIK-tok*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *tick tock*, *tick-tock tick-tock*, *tick tick tick*

A clock. Said out loud it almost never describes an actual sound — it is a way of telling somebody that time is running out on them.

1. *Tick-tock.* The offer expires at midnight.
2. You could hear the kitchen clock going *tick-tock* in all that silence.
3. "*Tick tock*, people. The bus leaves in five."
4. *Tick, tick, tick* — that's the sound of your deadline.
5. He tapped his wrist. "*Tick-tock*."

### twang

A plucked string letting go — a guitar, a rubber band, a bowstring. The same word names the nasal edge in a Southern or country accent.

1. *Twang.* He snapped the rubber band clear across the room.
2. The low string went *twang* and the whole song fell apart.
3. She let the arrow go — *twang* — and hit the target dead center.

## Speed, motion, and energy

### boing

**Pronunciation:** /bɔɪŋ/ &middot; *BOYNG*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *sproing*, *boing boing*, *boink*, *boi-oi-oing*

A spring, or something bouncing off one — a mattress, a diving board, a coiled toy. It is a cartoon sound before it is a real one, so it always brings a little comedy with it.

1. *Boing!* He hit the trampoline and went straight over the fence.
2. The screen door spring went *sproing* and came off in my hand.
3. *Boing boing boing* — the kids found the air mattress.
4. Her curls came loose — *boing* — the second she took the hat off.
5. The mousetrap went *boing* and launched itself across the counter.

**See also:** [twang](#twang), [pop](#pop), [whoosh](#whoosh)

### poof

**Pronunciation:** /pʊf/ or /puf/ &middot; *PUUF* or *POOF*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *poof!*, *pouf*, *puff*

The sound of something vanishing in a small cloud — a magic trick, a struck match, a plan. In most real uses nothing makes any noise at all: *poof* is the sound of the disappearance itself.

1. *Poof.* Gone. Thirty years of savings.
2. He snapped his fingers. "*Poof* — you're free to go."
3. The magician lifted the cloth and — *poof* — the bird was gone.
4. One rainstorm and *poof*, there went the entire weekend.
5. She said the money would just go *poof*, and she was right.

**See also:** [pop](#pop), [puff](03-pain.md#puff), [presto](01-joy.md#presto)

### vroom

**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *vroom vroom*, *vrooom*, *brrm*, *brum brum*, *vroom-vroom*

An engine revving. Children say it while pushing a toy car; adults say it about anything moving fast, and *vroom vroom* is the standard tease for somebody showing off a loud vehicle.

1. *Vroom vroom* — my son has been driving that couch cushion for an hour.
2. He revved it at the light. *Vroom.* Real impressive.
3. The bikes went by, *vroooom*, and set off every car alarm on the street.
4. "*Brrm brrm*," she said, running the little truck up my arm.
5. New exhaust, huh? *Vroom vroom.*

**See also:** [whoosh](#whoosh), [whiz](#whiz), [honk](#honk)

### whiz

**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *whizz*, *whizzz*, *whiz-bang*

Something small going past fast enough to be heard and not seen — an arrow, a bullet, a bike on the sidewalk.

1. The ball went *whiz* past my ear.
2. *Whizzz* — a cyclist came out of nowhere and I nearly wore him.
3. You can hear them *whiz* by on the highway all night.
4. A wasp went *whiz* right between us and we both ducked.
5. *Whiz*, and the frisbee was in the neighbor's yard.

**See also:** [whoosh](#whoosh), [zing](#zing), [vroom](#vroom)

### whoosh

**Pronunciation:** /wʊʃ/ or /wuʃ/ &middot; *WUUSH* or *WOOSH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *woosh*, *whooosh*, *swoosh*, *swish*

A rush of air or a fast pass — a door opening, a bus going by, a basketball dropping through the net. *Swoosh* and *swish* are the softer, cloth-and-net versions of the same sound.

1. The subway doors closed and — *whoosh* — off it went.

**See also:** [whiz](#whiz), [vroom](#vroom), [zing](#zing)

### zap

**Pronunciation:** /zæp/ &middot; *ZAP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *zzap*, *zap zap*, *zzzap*

Electricity — a shock off a doorknob, a laser, a bug light. It also covers anything a machine does instantly, which is why Americans *zap* leftovers in the microwave.

1. I touched the doorknob and — *zap*.
2. *Zap!* The bug light on the porch went off all night.
3. The fence gave the dog one little *zap* and he never went near it again.
4. Just *zap* it for thirty seconds and it's fine.
5. *Zap, zap, zap* — the whole laser tag arena sounded like a bad sci-fi movie.

**See also:** [buzz](#buzz), [zing](#zing), [pop](#pop)

### zing

**Pronunciation:** /zɪŋ/ &middot; *ZING*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *zinnng*, *zing!*, *zing zing*

A bright singing sound — something small flying past, a struck wire — and, far more often these days, the tag somebody puts on a perfectly landed insult.

1. "You look great. Did you finally get some sleep?" "*Zing.*"
2. The arrow went *zing* off the metal post.
3. *Zing!* In front of his whole team, too.
4. She fired one right back at him and the table went, "Ooh, *zing*."
5. There was a *zing* in the wire every time the wind picked up.

**See also:** [whiz](#whiz), [zap](#zap), [ba-dum-tss](#ba-dum-tss)

## Musical stings and comic timing

### ba-dum-tss

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɑ dəm ˈts/ &middot; *bah-dum-TSS* (the last beat is a hissed cymbal, not a real syllable)  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ba-dum-tish*, *badum-tss*, *ba-dum-ching*, *ba dum tss*

The drum rimshot that lands after a bad pun. It is said or typed either by the person who made the joke or by somebody else pointing out how corny it was.

1. "I used to be a banker, but I lost interest. *Ba-dum-tss*."
2. He delivered the pun, waited a beat, and added, "*Ba-dum-ching*."
3. Somebody in the back said "*ba-dum-tss*" and the whole room groaned.
4. *Badum-tss.* I'll be here all week, folks.
5. She answered his dad joke with nothing but "*ba dum tss*."

**See also:** [dun dun dun](#dun-dun-dun), [womp womp](#womp-womp), [zing](#zing)

### dun dun dun

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʌn dʌn ˈdʌn/ &middot; *dun-dun-DUN*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *dun dun duuun*, *duhn duhn duhn*, *dun dun DUNNN*, *dun-dun-dun*

The three dramatic orchestra hits that follow a shocking revelation. It is nearly always ironic: you attach it to ordinary news to make it sound like a soap opera cliffhanger.

1. "And guess who was already there? *Dun dun dunnn*."
2. It turned out the charge came from her own account. *Dun dun dun.*
3. He opened the envelope and somebody at the table went, "*Dun dun duuun*."
4. *Dun dun dun* — the printer had been unplugged the entire time.
5. She ended the story with "*duhn duhn duhn*" and everybody lost it.

**See also:** [ba-dum-tss](#ba-dum-tss), [ta-da](#ta-da), [womp womp](#womp-womp)

### ta-da

**Pronunciation:** /tɑˈdɑ/ &middot; *tah-DAH*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *tada*, *ta-daa*, *ta-dah*, *ta-daaa*

The little fanfare you sing when you reveal something you are proud of. It can be perfectly sincere or completely deadpan, and the deadpan version is funnier.

1. She pulled the cake out of the box. "*Ta-da!*"
2. *Ta-da.* Two hours of work and it still looks like a pancake.
3. He fixed the sink, wiped his hands, and said, "*Ta-daa*."
4. *Ta-da* — one working printer, exactly as promised.
5. "Close your eyes. Okay, open them. *Ta-da!*"

**See also:** [ka-ching](#ka-ching), [dun dun dun](#dun-dun-dun), [ta-da](01-joy.md#ta-da)

### womp womp

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɑmp ˌwɑmp/ &middot; *WOMP-womp*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wah-wah*, *wah wah waaah*, *wamp wamp*, *whomp whomp*

The sad trombone — the sound of failure, played over somebody's disappointment. Aimed at yourself it is self-deprecating; aimed at someone else it is a small and very cheerful cruelty.

1. "They gave the promotion to Dave." "*Womp womp*."
2. Sold out. *Wah-wah.*
3. I drove all the way out there and they'd closed at four. *Womp womp.*
4. He missed the shot and the whole bench went "*wah wah waaah*."
5. "*Womp womp*," she said, and somehow I felt worse.

**See also:** [dun dun dun](#dun-dun-dun), [ba-dum-tss](#ba-dum-tss), [womp womp](06-sadness.md#womp-womp)

## Animal noises

### baa

**Pronunciation:** /bɑ/ or /bæ/ &middot; *BAH* or *BA*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *baaa*, *ba-a-a*, *bah*

A sheep. It lives in picture books and petting zoos, and it gets borrowed for people who follow the crowd without thinking about it.

1. "What does the sheep say?" "*Baa!*"
2. The lambs went *baa* every time we walked past the fence.
3. *Baaa* — one of them answered him right back.
4. He held up the toy sheep and said, "*Baa*, baa, black sheep."
5. Everybody just repeats whatever the boss said. *Baa.*

**See also:** [moo](#moo), [neigh](#neigh), [oink](#oink)

### chirp

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɝp/ &middot; *CHURP*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *tweet*, *cheep*, *chirp chirp*, *tweet tweet*, *chirp-chirp*

A small bird, a cricket, or a smoke detector with a dying battery. Said into a silence after your own joke, *chirp chirp* is the cricket gag: nobody laughed and you know it.

1. *Chirp, chirp* — the birds started before the sun did.
2. I made the joke and the room went dead quiet. *Chirp. Chirp.*
3. The smoke alarm gave one *chirp* every ninety seconds all night.
4. "What does the bird say?" "*Tweet tweet!*"
5. Out past the porch light it was nothing but *cheep, cheep, cheep*.

**See also:** [squeak](#squeak), [hoot](#hoot), [beep](#beep)

### cock-a-doodle-doo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɑk ə ˌdudəl ˈdu/ &middot; *kok-uh-DOO-dul-DOO*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *cockadoodledoo*, *cock a doodle doo*, *cock-a-doodle-dooo*

A rooster. It is the standard American version of the crow, learned in early childhood, and adults use it to mean "it is unreasonably early" or "get up."

1. "And what does the rooster say?" "*Cock-a-doodle-doo!*"
2. *Cock-a-doodle-doo!* Everybody up — we leave in ten minutes.
3. The rooster next door starts his *cock-a-doodle-doo* at 4:45 sharp.
4. He threw open the curtains and yelled "*cockadoodledoo*" at his sleeping teenager.
5. *Cock-a-doodle-doo* — out there, that's the only alarm clock anybody has.

**See also:** [hoot](#hoot), [quack](#quack), [moo](#moo)

### grr

**Pronunciation:** /ɡr̩/ (a growled *r* with no vowel in it) &middot; *GRR*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *grrr*, *grrrr*, *grrrrrr*

A growl. Out of a dog it is a warning; out of a person it is a small, half-joking noise of frustration, and it is much milder than actually saying you are angry.

1. *Grrr.* The website logged me out again.
2. The dog gave a low *grr* and I put the treat back in the bag.
3. "How'd the meeting go?" "*Grr*."
4. *Grr* — I missed that train by about ten seconds.
5. She typed "*grrrr*" and then, a second later, "okay, I'm over it."

**See also:** [rawr](#rawr), [woof](#woof), [grr](07-anger.md#grr)

### hoot

**Pronunciation:** /hut/ &middot; *HOOT*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hoo*, *hoo-hoo*, *who-who*, *hoot hoot*

An owl. American children learn it as *hoo, hoo*, and the sound is shorthand for night, woods, and everything being very quiet.

1. *Hoo, hoo.* There's an owl in that tree every single night.
2. "What does the owl say?" "*Hoot hoot!*"
3. We heard one long *hoot* and then nothing for an hour.
4. *Who-who* — he did the owl call and one actually answered him.
5. The barn owl's *hoot* carried all the way across the field.

**See also:** [chirp](#chirp), [cock-a-doodle-doo](#cock-a-doodle-doo), [ribbit](#ribbit)

### meow

**Pronunciation:** /miˈaʊ/ &middot; *mee-OW*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *mew*, *miaow*, *meow meow*, *mrow*

A cat. Said by one person about another, it flags that somebody just made a catty, claws-out remark.

1. The cat sat by the empty bowl going *meow, meow, meow*.
2. "She called his new place 'charming for the price.'" "*Meow.*"
3. *Meow!* Somebody's in a mood this morning.
4. "What does the kitty say?" "*Meow!*"
5. A tiny *mew* came from under the porch, and that is how we got Charlie.

**See also:** [purr](#purr), [hiss](#hiss), [woof](#woof)

### moo

**Pronunciation:** /mu/ &middot; *MOO*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *mooo*, *moo moo*, *mooooo*

A cow. It is a barnyard-and-toddler word through and through, and one of the first sounds American children are taught to make.

1. "What does the cow say?" "*Moo!*"
2. One *moo* from the far end of the field and the whole herd answered.
3. *Mooooo* — he did the cow voice and the baby laughed for ten minutes.
4. We drove past the dairy and the kids yelled "*moo*" out the window.
5. *Moo, moo.* The toy makes the sound every time you tip it over.

**See also:** [baa](#baa), [oink](#oink), [neigh](#neigh)

### neigh

**Pronunciation:** /neɪ/ &middot; *NAY*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *neighhh*, *neigh neigh*, *nay*

A horse. Children say it; adults imitating a horse usually skip the word and do the whinny instead.

1. "What does the horse say?" "*Neigh!*"
2. She galloped around the yard going "*neigh, neigh*."
3. *Neighhh* — he did the horse voice and the pony ignored him completely.
4. The horse let out a long *neigh* the second the feed bucket came out.
5. Every toy in that barn set goes *neigh* when you press it.

**See also:** [moo](#moo), [baa](#baa), [oink](#oink)

### oink

**Pronunciation:** /ɔɪŋk/ &middot; *OYNK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *oink oink*, *oinkoink*

A pig. Outside the barnyard, saying *oink oink* at somebody is a small insult about greed or table manners.

1. "What does the pig say?" "*Oink oink!*"
2. The pigs at the fair went *oink* the second they saw the bucket.
3. He took the last three slices. "*Oink.*"
4. *Oink oink* — that's the sound of a man double-dipping.
5. She squeezed the toy pig and it went *oink* about four hundred times.

**See also:** [moo](#moo), [baa](#baa), [chomp](#chomp)

### purr

**Pronunciation:** /pɝ/ &middot; *PUR*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *purrr*, *prrr*, *purr purr*

The rumble of a contented cat. Said of an engine it means the thing is running beautifully; said of a person it means they are being openly pleased with themselves.

1. One scratch behind the ears and it was *purrrr* for the rest of the movie.
2. *Purrr.* That is the sound of a cat who got exactly what she wanted.
3. New timing belt, and now the engine just goes *purr*.
4. "*Purr*," he said, admiring his own parking job.
5. You can feel the *purr* before you can hear it.

**See also:** [meow](#meow), [mmm](#mmm), [vroom](#vroom)

### quack

**Pronunciation:** /kwæk/ &middot; *KWAK*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *quack quack*, *quackquack*

A duck. It mostly stays at the pond and in picture books, though *quack quack* also does duty for somebody talking on and on about nothing.

1. "What does the duck say?" "*Quack quack!*"
2. The ducks came right up to the bench — *quack, quack, quack* — expecting bread.
3. *Quack!* His duck call sounded almost real.
4. He held up the rubber duck and made it go *quack*.
5. Twenty minutes of *quack quack quack* and he still hadn't gotten to the point.

**See also:** [honk](#honk), [chirp](#chirp), [moo](#moo)

### rawr

**Pronunciation:** /rɔr/ &middot; *RAWR*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *roar*, *rawrr*, *raar*, *rawr!*

A big animal roaring — a lion, a dinosaur, a monster in a game of pretend. Spelled *rawr* it is playful and slightly flirty; spelled *roar* it is the actual animal.

1. *Rawr!* He came around the corner as a dinosaur and the toddler screamed with joy.
2. "What does the lion say?" "*Roar!*"
3. She sent back a "*rawr*" and I still don't know how to read it.
4. *Rawr* — that's my monster voice and it is terrifying.
5. The crowd let out a *roar* when he came out of the tunnel.

**See also:** [grr](#grr), [meow](#meow), [woof](#woof)

### ribbit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɪbɪt/ &middot; *RIB-it*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *ribbit ribbit*, *ribbet*, *rivet*

A frog. It is the standard American frog sound, even though only some frogs actually make anything like it.

1. "What does the frog say?" "*Ribbit!*"
2. *Ribbit, ribbit* — the pond behind the house is loud all summer.
3. He hopped across the room going "*ribbit*" and the kids followed him.
4. One *ribbit* out of the reeds and then total silence.
5. *Ribbit ribbit.* The sound machine has an entire swamp setting.

**See also:** [hoot](#hoot), [chirp](#chirp), [quack](#quack)

### woof

**Pronunciation:** /wʊf/ &middot; *WUUF*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *arf*, *bow-wow*, *ruff*, *woof woof*

A dog. *Woof* is the deep bark, *arf* and *ruff* are the small ones, and *bow-wow* is the storybook version children meet first.

1. "What does the doggy say?" "*Woof woof!*"
2. *Arf!* The little one barks at absolutely everything.
3. He knocked on the door and got exactly one *woof* in reply.
4. *Bow-wow* — that's the entire first page of the book.
5. She barked back at him. "*Ruff*." The dog was not impressed.

**See also:** [grr](#grr), [meow](#meow), [rawr](#rawr)
