# Profanity used as interjections

> Taboo words thrown out as pure reaction — pain, shock, anger, disbelief, delight — plus the softened substitutes Americans reach for when the real word would cost them something.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

## Register warning — read this first

**Every word in this file is offensive to someone.** They are documented here because a learner who does not know them cannot tell how hard they are landing when an American says one — and cannot tell that *shoot* and *shit* are the same slot with very different consequences. Learn to recognize all of them. Produce only the ones you have heard people in your own situation use.

Each entry carries an **Offensiveness** rating on this four-step scale:

| Rating | What it costs you | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **mild** | Almost nothing. A stranger may raise an eyebrow; nobody complains to HR. | *crap, darn, heck, gosh, shoot* |
| **moderate** | Fine among friends, risky with strangers, clients, children, or religious people. | *damn, hell, Jesus, screw you* |
| **strong** | Marks the speech as coarse. In a workplace it gets noticed; repeated, it gets reported. | *shit, bullshit, asshole, son of a bitch, goddammit* |
| **very strong** | The heaviest words in the language. In writing or in public they are a genuine incident. | *fuck* and everything built from it |

Two ratings that are not on the scale, because they are worse than anything here: **slurs** aimed at race, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexual orientation, or disability. Those are not interjections and they are not in this file. There is no context in which a learner should use one.

This file also covers **minced oaths** — deliberate distortions of taboo words that keep the rhythm and drop the offense (*gosh* from *God*, *darn* from *damn*, *heck* from *hell*, *shoot* from *shit*, *frick* from *fuck*). They occupy exactly the same slot in the sentence as the words they replace, which is why they are grouped here rather than scattered through the other categories. Punctuation is simple throughout: these words take an exclamation point when shouted, a period when muttered, and a comma when they lead into a clause — and Americans mutter them far more often than they shout them.

## Core profane exclamations

### asshole

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæsˌhoʊl/ &middot; *ASS-hohl*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** venting to friends; alone in a car, which is where most of them are said
**Never in:** work, school, anything written, or within earshot of the person it is aimed at
**Variants:** *assholes, A-hole (softened), you asshole*

The standard American shout at somebody whose behavior has just been selfish or reckless, and the single most common thing Americans say in traffic. Unlike most of this file it names a person rather than a situation, which is why it starts arguments that [shit](#shit) and [damn](#damn) do not.

1. "*Asshole!*" she yelled at the car that ran the red light.
2. Somebody had parked across two spaces. *Asshole.*
3. "He fired her by text message." "What an *asshole.*"
4. "*Asshole,*" he muttered, and let the elevator doors close on the guy running for it.
5. *Asshole* — he knows that's my seat.

**See also:** [bastard](#bastard), [son of a bitch](#son-of-a-bitch), [fuck you](#fuck-you)

### bastard

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbæstərd/ &middot; *BAS-terd*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate to strong — moderate thrown at an object, strong aimed at a person
**Safe in:** venting among close friends; swearing at an object or a situation when nobody is around
**Never in:** work, school, anything written with your name on it, or within earshot of the person it is about
**Variants:** *bastards*

An outburst aimed at someone who has just done something cruel or underhanded — and, just as often in American speech, at an object or a situation that has beaten you. As an interjection it is an explosion, not a description, and it is far more common in Britain and Ireland than in the United States.

1. "*Bastard!*" he shouted at the truck that had cut him off.
2. The bolt sheared clean off in his hand. *Bastard.*
3. He took the last parking spot right in front of me — *bastard.*
4. "*Bastards.*" She read the layoff email a second time.
5. The lock finally gave, and he muttered, "*Bastard,*" as he shoved the door open.

**See also:** [son of a bitch](#son-of-a-bitch), [motherfucker](#motherfucker), [son of a gun](#son-of-a-gun)

### bollocks

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɑləks/ &middot; *BOL-uks*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate in the US, strong in Britain
**Safe in:** casual British and Irish speech among friends
**Never in:** any American workplace; any formal setting anywhere
**Variants:** *bollocks!, complete bollocks*

British and Irish, familiar to Americans mainly from television. It does two jobs: an exclamation of annoyance when something goes wrong, and a flat verdict of "that is nonsense" on something you have just been told. Americans should recognize it and leave it to the British — it sounds like costume jewelry in an American mouth.

1. "*Bollocks!*" he said when the screen went black in the middle of the call.
2. Our train's canceled? *Bollocks.*
3. "*Bollocks.* You never told me any of that."
4. "He says he ran it in under four minutes." "*Bollocks.* I've seen him jog."
5. *Bollocks* — I've left my keys at the office.

**See also:** [bugger](#bugger), [bullshit](#bullshit), [bloody hell](#bloody-hell)

### bugger

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʌɡər/ &middot; *BUG-er*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** mild in the US, where most people do not register it as a swear word; moderate in Britain
**Safe in:** British and Irish casual speech; American speech among friends, where it reads as quaint
**Never in:** British professional settings; American writing meant to sound native
**Variants:** *bugger it, bugger me, buggeration*

A British and Irish exclamation of mild disaster — the thing you say when you have just missed a train or dropped something. Its literal sense is obscene, but the interjection has drifted far from it, and in the United States it barely registers as profanity at all.

1. "*Bugger!*" She had locked her keys in the car again.
2. *Bugger* — the shop closed ten minutes ago.
3. He dropped the entire tray. "*Bugger* it."
4. *Bugger.* I've been on hold for forty minutes.
5. They'd missed the last ferry by about a minute. "*Bugger.*"

**See also:** [bollocks](#bollocks), [damn](#damn), [crap](#crap)

### bullshit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʊlˌʃɪt/ &middot; *BULL-shit*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** arguing with friends, family, or teammates; sports crowds
**Never in:** meetings, classrooms, customer conversations, or any written complaint
**Variants:** *horseshit, bull, BS*

Called out when you believe what you have just heard is a lie, an excuse, or an unfair ruling. It is a verdict, not a feeling — you are telling the room that a specific claim is false or that a specific decision was unjust.

1. "*Bullshit.* You were nowhere near the office at seven."
2. The ref called a foul and half the bench yelled "*Bullshit!*"
3. They raised the rent again? *Bullshit.*
4. *Bullshit* — he promised me that shift three weeks ago.
5. "*Bullshit!*" came a voice from the back of the room.

**See also:** [my ass](#my-ass), [no shit](#no-shit), [shit](#shit)

### Christ

**Pronunciation:** /kraɪst/ &middot; *KRYST*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate — but strong to anyone religious, who hears it as blasphemy rather than as swearing
**Safe in:** casual speech among friends who are not devout
**Never in:** church, religious households, work, or conversation with someone whose beliefs you do not know
**Variants:** *oh Christ, Christ almighty, Christ on a cracker*

A one-word release of exasperation, shock, or weariness. Note that its offensiveness works on a completely different axis from *shit* or *fuck*: many Americans who swear freely will not say this one, and many who never swear will say it without thinking.

1. "*Christ,* it's cold out here."
2. *Christ* — you scared me half to death.
3. He looked at the estimate and said, "*Christ.* That can't be right."
4. *Christ,* how long is this line?
5. "Oh, *Christ,*" she said, remembering the oven.

**See also:** [Jesus](#jesus), [Jesus Christ](#jesus-christ), [God](#god), [cripes](#cripes)

### crap

**Pronunciation:** /kræp/ &middot; *KRAP*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** most American workplaces among peers, casual conversation, family life
**Never in:** a presentation to clients, a job interview, an elementary school classroom, formal writing
**Variants:** *craaap, oh crap*

The everyday American reaction to a small setback — a forgotten item, a spilled drink, a jammed printer. It is the softest of the genuinely profane words and the one most likely to slip out at work without consequence.

1. "*Crap.* I left my badge at home."
2. *Crap* — is that the time?
3. He spilled coffee down his shirt five minutes before the interview. "*Crap!*"
4. Oh, *crap,* I never answered her email.
5. "*Crap,*" she muttered as the printer jammed again.

**See also:** [shit](#shit), [crud](#crud), [holy crap](#holy-crap), [shoot](#shoot)

### damn

**Pronunciation:** /dæm/ &middot; *DAM*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate — mild as frustration, moderate to religious listeners, who hear the original curse in it
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers, sports
**Never in:** formal writing, a first meeting with a client, a religious household, school
**Variants:** *damn!, daaamn, damn it (see separate entry)*

Two distinct jobs. As frustration it marks a small defeat; drawn out and stressed, it is admiration — surprise at how good, how big, or how expensive something is. The tone tells you which one is happening.

1. "*Damn,* that's a nice car."
2. *Damn* — I meant to call her back yesterday.
3. He missed the shot at the buzzer. "*Damn!*"
4. *Damn,* these prices have gone up since last year.
5. "You finished the whole thing in a weekend? *Damn.*"

**See also:** [damn it](#damn-it), [goddammit](#goddammit), [dang](#dang), [darn](#darn)

### fuck

**Pronunciation:** /fʌk/ &middot; *FUK*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear, and almost nowhere else; alone in a car
**Never in:** work, school, any writing, any public place, any conversation with someone you have just met, anywhere children can hear
**Variants:** *fuuuck*

The heaviest single word in American English, and as an interjection it is a pure pressure release — pain, panic, disaster, or the moment a mistake becomes irreversible. Learners should treat it as a word to understand rather than a word to use: nothing else in this file will damage a professional relationship as fast.

1. "*Fuck!*" He had slammed his thumb in the car door.
2. *Fuck* — I just sent that email to the wrong person.
3. She read the letter twice and whispered, "*Fuck.*"
4. "*Fuck,*" he said quietly, watching the tow truck pull away with his car.
5. The whole shelf came down. *Fuck.*
6. "*Fuuuck,*" she groaned when the alarm went off at four in the morning.

**See also:** [fuck it](#fuck-it), [what the fuck](#what-the-fuck), [shit](#shit), [frick](#frick)

### God

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɑd/ &middot; *GOD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild as swearing, strong to devout listeners, who hear a commandment being broken
**Safe in:** casual conversation among friends
**Never in:** church, religious families, formal writing; be careful with strangers
**Variants:** *oh God, my God, dear God, good God, God almighty*

Exasperation, dread, or weariness in one syllable. It is grammatically an appeal and functionally a groan, and it is the most common blasphemous exclamation in American English by a wide margin.

1. "*God,* I'm tired."
2. *God* — that music is loud.
3. She saw the line at security and said, "Oh *God.*"
4. "*God,* you're stubborn."
5. *God,* I hope this works.

**See also:** [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [Jesus](#jesus), [gosh](#gosh), [for God's sake](#for-gods-sake)

### hell

**Pronunciation:** /hɛl/ &middot; *HEL*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers
**Never in:** formal writing, religious settings, conversation with someone's grandmother
**Variants:** *aw hell, oh hell, hell's bells (dated)*

Sentence-initial *hell* is a shrug that introduces an opinion — a way of saying "look, honestly." Standing alone with *oh* or *aw* in front of it, it is dismay at something that has just gone wrong.

1. "*Hell,* I don't know. Ask your brother."
2. *Hell* — we've been driving for six hours.
3. "*Hell,* if it were up to me I'd say yes."
4. Oh, *hell.* I think I left the stove on.
5. It's not far — *hell,* you could walk it in ten minutes.

**See also:** [what the hell](#what-the-hell), [hell no](#hell-no), [hell yeah](#hell-yeah), [heck](#heck)

### Jesus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʒizəs/ &middot; *JEE-zuhs*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate — strong to religious listeners
**Safe in:** casual speech among friends who are not devout
**Never in:** church, religious households, work, school, anything written
**Variants:** *sweet Jesus, oh Jesus, Jesus wept*

Shock, exhaustion, or disbelief — usually at something startling, expensive, or absurd. Like [Christ](#christ), it offends on religious grounds rather than on grounds of coarseness, so the people it upsets are not the same people who mind [shit](#shit).

1. "*Jesus,* you gave me a heart attack."
2. *Jesus* — look at that line.
3. He opened the repair estimate and said, "*Jesus.*"
4. *Jesus,* it's hot in here.
5. "*Jesus,*" she said, watching the truck back into the mailbox.

**See also:** [Jesus Christ](#jesus-christ), [Christ](#christ), [jeez](#jeez), [sheesh](#sheesh)

### shit

**Pronunciation:** /ʃɪt/ &middot; *SHIT*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears, sports, driving alone
**Never in:** work, school, customer-facing anything, email, presentations, around children
**Variants:** *shiiit, shite (British and Irish)*

The workhorse American swear word for a bad moment: the dropped glass, the missed exit, the realization that the deadline was yesterday. Drawn out — *shiiit* — it becomes weary agreement or amazement instead of alarm.

1. "*Shit.* I locked myself out again."
2. *Shit* — the meeting started ten minutes ago.
3. He looked at the flat tire and said, "*Shit.*"
4. Oh, *shit,* that's my train pulling out.
5. "*Shit,*" she said as the glass slipped out of her hand.
6. *Shiiit,* that's a nine-hour drive.

**See also:** [oh shit](#oh-shit), [holy shit](#holy-shit), [crap](#crap), [shoot](#shoot)

## Compound and phrasal profane exclamations

### bloody hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌblʌdi ˈhɛl/ &middot; *bluh-dee HEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate (strong in Britain, where *bloody* still carries weight)
**Safe in:** British and Irish casual speech
**Never in:** American speech if you want to sound native; British workplaces; broadcast before evening
**Variants:** *bloody hell!, oh bloody hell*

The standard British exclamation of shock or exasperation, known to Americans entirely through television and film. Americans understand it perfectly and almost never say it.

1. "*Bloody hell,* it's freezing."
2. *Bloody hell* — that's the third delay this week.
3. He saw the price of the tickets and said, "*Bloody hell.*"
4. "*Bloody hell,* would you look at that."
5. *Bloody hell,* I've been on this call for an hour.

**See also:** [hell](#hell), [bollocks](#bollocks), [crikey](#crikey)

### damn it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdæmɪt/ &middot; *DAM-it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers, driving
**Never in:** formal writing, client meetings, religious settings, school
**Variants:** *dammit, damnit, damn it all*

Frustration with a specific, immediate failure — the file that did not save, the exit you just missed. It is sharper and more directed than plain [damn](#damn), because the *it* points at the thing that went wrong.

1. "*Damn it,* the file didn't save."
2. *Damn it* — I dropped my phone in the sink.
3. "*Dammit!*" He had missed the exit again.
4. *Damn it,* I promised I'd be there by six.
5. She hit the steering wheel. "*Damn it,* not again."

**See also:** [goddammit](#goddammit), [damn](#damn), [doggone it](#doggone-it), [dang](#dang)

### damn straight

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdæm ˈstreɪt/ &middot; *dam STRAYT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild — but it is still *damn*, and a listener who objects to that word objects here
**Safe in:** casual conversation, friendly workplaces, sports talk
**Never in:** formal writing, a job interview, anything with a client
**Variants:** *damn right*

An emphatic yes. It agrees with what was just said and adds that there was never any doubt about it, and it can stand alone or run straight into a clause (*Damn straight I am*).

1. "You're going to ask for a raise?" "*Damn straight* I am."
2. *Damn straight.* That's exactly what I told him.
3. "Was it worth the wait?" "*Damn straight* it was."
4. "*Damn straight,*" he said, and shook my hand.
5. Am I proud of her? *Damn straight* I am.

**See also:** [hell yeah](#hell-yeah), [no shit](#no-shit), [hell no](#hell-no)

### for fuck's sake

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈfʌks ˌseɪk/ &middot; *fer FUKS sayk*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear freely; alone
**Never in:** work, school, public places, any writing, any conversation with a stranger
**Variants:** *FFS (written), for fuck sake*

Exasperation at the third repetition of the same problem. It is the impatient end of the *for X's sake* family, and everything in that family — *for God's sake*, *for Pete's sake*, *for crying out loud* — is a softer version of this.

1. "*For fuck's sake,* the elevator's broken again."
2. *For fuck's sake* — how many forms do they need?
3. He read the message twice. "*For fuck's sake.*"
4. *For fuck's sake,* just tell me what time you're coming.
5. "*For fuck's sake,*" she muttered, restarting the computer for the third time.

**See also:** [for God's sake](#for-gods-sake), [for Pete's sake](#for-petes-sake), [for crying out loud](#for-crying-out-loud)

### for God's sake

**Pronunciation:** /fər ˈɡɑdz ˌseɪk/ &middot; *fer GODZ sayk*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate — moderate for religious listeners
**Safe in:** family, friends, casual workplaces
**Never in:** church, formal writing, conversation with someone devout
**Variants:** *for Christ's sake, for the love of God, for God's sake!*

An appeal made out of impatience: hurry up, be reasonable, do the obvious thing. It usually wraps around an imperative, either in front of it or trailing behind it.

1. "*For God's sake,* somebody answer the phone."
2. *For God's sake* — take an umbrella.
3. "Hurry up, *for God's sake,* we're going to miss it."
4. *For God's sake,* it's eleven at night.
5. "*For God's sake,*" he said, "just ask her out."

**See also:** [for Pete's sake](#for-petes-sake), [for crying out loud](#for-crying-out-loud), [God](#god)

### fuck it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfʌk ɪt/ &middot; *FUK it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends; talking to yourself
**Never in:** work, school, writing, anywhere in public
**Variants:** *fuck this, fuck that, ah fuck it*

The sound of a decision. It announces that you are done weighing the options and are about to do the easy, reckless, or long-postponed thing anyway.

1. "*Fuck it.* I'm going home."
2. We couldn't get a table anywhere, so — *fuck it* — we ordered a pizza.
3. "*Fuck it,*" she said, and hit send.
4. I stared at the spreadsheet for an hour and thought, *fuck it,* I'll finish it Monday.
5. *Fuck it,* let's just drive.

**See also:** [screw it](#screw-it), [what the hell](#what-the-hell), [fuck](#fuck)

### fuck me

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʌk ˈmi/ &middot; *fuk MEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear
**Never in:** any public or professional setting; anywhere it could be misheard as an invitation
**Variants:** *fuck me sideways, well fuck me*

Astonishment or dismay at something the speaker has just seen or learned. Despite the literal words, it is not sexual at all — it is the same slot as *wow* or *oh no*, at maximum volume.

1. "*Fuck me,* look at that view."
2. *Fuck me* — that's the second flat tire this month.
3. He opened the electric bill and said, "*Fuck me.*"
4. "*Fuck me,*" she said as the wind took the tent.
5. *Fuck me,* it's already Thursday.

**See also:** [holy shit](#holy-shit), [fuck](#fuck), [son of a bitch](#son-of-a-bitch)

### fuck off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʌk ˈɔf/ &middot; *fuk AWF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** joking with a close friend who is certain you are joking
**Never in:** work, school, public, or any argument you want to survive
**Variants:** *fuck right off, eff off (softened)*

Go away — said in real anger in American English, and often in mock disbelief between friends in British and Irish English. That transatlantic gap catches learners out: what sounds playful in a British sitcom starts a fight in an American parking lot.

1. "*Fuck off.* You did not meet him."
2. "*Fuck off,*" he said, laughing. "You're making that up."
3. *Fuck off* — I'm trying to sleep.
4. "Want to help me move on Saturday?" "*Fuck off.*"
5. She glared at him and said, "*Fuck off,* Danny."

**See also:** [fuck you](#fuck-you), [piss off](#piss-off), [screw you](#screw-you)

### fuck yeah

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʌk ˈjɛə/ &middot; *fuk YEH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear; celebrating with people who already swear around you
**Never in:** work, school, any writing, public places, anywhere children can hear
**Variants:** *fuck yes, fuck no (the refusal), hell yeah (softened)*

Enthusiastic agreement or triumph at full volume — the profane top of the [hell yeah](#hell-yeah) family. It is pure delight rather than anger, but the word in the middle is still the heaviest one in the language and costs the speaker exactly as much as any other use of it.

1. "They said yes." "*Fuck yeah* they did."
2. "*Fuck yeah!*" he shouted when the ball dropped through the net.
3. The last bolt came loose and he sat back on his heels. "*Fuck yeah.*"
4. Her scan came back clean and she just kept saying it — "*Fuck yeah.*"
5. "Want to see them play in October?" "*Fuck yeah.*"

**See also:** [hell yeah](#hell-yeah), [damn straight](#damn-straight), [fuck](#fuck)

### fuck you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʌk ˈju/ &middot; *fuk YOO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** essentially nowhere; among very close friends it can be affectionate, and that is a gamble
**Never in:** work, school, writing, public argument, anywhere a stranger can hear it
**Variants:** *f you, F-U*

A direct attack on the listener — contempt, defiance, refusal, all at once. It is the most confrontational item in this file, and unlike most entries here it is aimed at a person rather than at the universe.

1. "*Fuck you,*" he said, and walked out.
2. She read the message, said "*Fuck you,*" and blocked the number.
3. "*Fuck you!*" somebody yelled from a passing car.
4. He mouthed *fuck you* at his brother across the room.
5. "*Fuck you* and your reserved parking spot," she muttered.

**See also:** [fuck off](#fuck-off), [screw you](#screw-you), [my ass](#my-ass)

### fucking hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfʌkɪŋ ˈhɛl/ &middot; *FUK-ing HEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear; alone
**Never in:** work, school, any writing, public places, anywhere children can hear
**Variants:** *fuckin' hell, oh fucking hell, bloody hell (British and far milder)*

Shock, exhaustion, or the end of the speaker's patience, and heavier than either word by itself. It is more common in Britain and Ireland than in the United States, where an American reaching for the same weight is likelier to say [holy shit](#holy-shit).

1. "*Fucking hell,* it's four in the morning."
2. "*Fucking hell,* would you two give it a rest?"
3. "*Fucking hell,*" he said, looking at what the storm had done to the fence.
4. *Fucking hell* — how is it still snowing?
5. She got to the front of the line and was told she needed a different form. "*Fucking hell.*"

**See also:** [bloody hell](#bloody-hell), [for fuck's sake](#for-fucks-sake), [holy shit](#holy-shit)

### go to hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ tə ˈhɛl/ &middot; *goh tuh HEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** arguments among friends, siblings, and family who know each other's limits
**Never in:** work, school, writing, or any argument with someone you will have to deal with again
**Variants:** *go to hell!, oh go to hell, go straight to hell*

A dismissal aimed straight at the listener: refusal and contempt in four short words. It is milder and older-sounding than [fuck you](#fuck-you), and between friends it can be nearly affectionate — from a stranger it is a real insult.

1. "*Go to hell,*" she said, and hung up.
2. "You could always apologize." "*Go to hell.*"
3. He told the whole committee to *go to hell* and walked out.
4. "*Go to hell,*" his brother said, laughing, and threw a towel at him.
5. *Go to hell* — I'm not driving out there again.

**See also:** [fuck you](#fuck-you), [screw you](#screw-you), [piss off](#piss-off)

### goddammit

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɑˈdæmɪt/ &middot; *god-DAM-it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong — and stronger still for religious listeners, who object twice over
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears, working alone on something that will not cooperate
**Never in:** work, school, any writing, any religious setting
**Variants:** *God damn it, goddamn it, goddamnit*

Full-throated frustration, heavier than [damn it](#damn-it) because it drags the deity in. It is the classic American exclamation for the moment a tool, a machine, or a person fails you one time too many.

1. "*Goddammit,* the printer's out of toner again."
2. *Goddammit* — I told you to lock the gate.
3. He dropped the ladder on his foot. "*Goddammit!*"
4. *Goddammit,* who moved my chair?
5. "*God damn it,*" she said slowly, "that was the last one."

**See also:** [damn it](#damn-it), [damn](#damn), [gosh](#gosh)

### hell no

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛl ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *hel NOH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate
**Safe in:** casual conversation, friendly workplaces, family
**Never in:** formal writing, an interview, a customer conversation
**Variants:** *hell to the no, aw hell no*

A refusal with the door slammed on it. It answers a yes-or-no question and adds that the question was never really open.

1. "Do you want to work Saturday?" "*Hell no.*"
2. *Hell no* — I'm not driving in this weather.
3. "*Hell no,*" he said. "I've seen what that repair costs."
4. Would I do it again? *Hell no.*
5. "*Hell no,* not after last time."

**See also:** [hell yeah](#hell-yeah), [hell](#hell), [heck](#heck)

### hell yeah

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛl ˈjɛə/ &middot; *hel YEH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate — the same *hell* as everywhere else, and the people who mind it mind it here too
**Safe in:** friends, teammates, casual workplaces, celebrations
**Never in:** formal writing, an interview, a client call
**Variants:** *hell yes, hell yeah!*

Enthusiastic agreement — the answer to an invitation you have been waiting for. It is louder and warmer than plain *yes* and carries no anger at all despite the *hell*.

1. "Pizza?" "*Hell yeah.*"
2. *Hell yeah* — I've been waiting all year for this.
3. "Did you get the job?" "*Hell yeah* I did."
4. "*Hell yeah!*" he shouted when the ball went in.
5. Do I want to come? *Hell yeah,* I do.

**See also:** [hell no](#hell-no), [damn straight](#damn-straight), [hell](#hell)

### holy crap

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhoʊli ˈkræp/ &middot; *HOH-lee KRAP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers, family
**Never in:** formal writing, a client presentation, a classroom of small children
**Variants:** *holy cow (softer), holy moly (softer)*

Astonishment at size, price, speed, or sheer unexpectedness. It is the safe member of the *holy* family, one step down from [holy hell](#holy-hell) and two steps down from [holy shit](#holy-shit).

1. "*Holy crap,* look at the size of that dog."
2. *Holy crap* — the tickets sold out in four minutes.
3. He saw the repair bill. "*Holy crap.*"
4. *Holy crap,* you cut your hair.
5. "*Holy crap,*" she said, "that was close."

**See also:** [holy shit](#holy-shit), [holy hell](#holy-hell), [crap](#crap)

### holy hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhoʊli ˈhɛl/ &middot; *HOH-lee HEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** friends, family, casual workplaces
**Never in:** formal writing, religious settings, customer conversations
**Variants:** *holy hell!*

Amazement, usually mixed with a little alarm. The phrase is a contradiction on its face, which is exactly why it works as an intensifier.

1. "*Holy hell,* it's pouring out there."
2. *Holy hell* — that's a long line.
3. He opened the closet and said, "*Holy hell.* When did we buy all this?"
4. *Holy hell,* she actually did it.
5. "*Holy hell,*" he whispered, watching the storm come across the field.

**See also:** [holy shit](#holy-shit), [holy crap](#holy-crap), [hell](#hell)

### holy shit

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhoʊli ˈʃɪt/ &middot; *HOH-lee SHIT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears, sports crowds
**Never in:** work, school, writing, around children or strangers
**Variants:** *holy fuck (much stronger), holy crap (softer)*

The standard American exclamation of genuine shock — at a near miss, a huge number, or something you did not think was possible. It is the loudest thing most Americans say that is not [fuck](#fuck).

1. "*Holy shit,* did you see that?"
2. *Holy shit* — the whole tree came down.
3. She checked the account balance and said, "*Holy shit.*"
4. *Holy shit,* he's actually going to make it.
5. "*Holy shit,*" the kid said, and his mother told him to watch his mouth.

**See also:** [holy hell](#holy-hell), [holy crap](#holy-crap), [shit](#shit), [fuck me](#fuck-me)

### Jesus Christ

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒizəs ˈkraɪst/ &middot; *jee-zuhs KRYST*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate as swearing, strong as blasphemy
**Safe in:** casual speech among friends who are not religious
**Never in:** church, religious households, work, school, anything written
**Variants:** *Jesus H. Christ, Jesus Christ almighty, Christ almighty*

The full form, and heavier than [Jesus](#jesus) alone: impatience, disbelief, or the end of the speaker's patience. The joking expansion *Jesus H. Christ* is older Americans' way of stretching it out for emphasis.

1. "*Jesus Christ,* would you slow down?"
2. *Jesus Christ* — I've been on hold for an hour.
3. He looked at the flooded basement. "*Jesus Christ.*"
4. *Jesus Christ,* it's only Tuesday.
5. "*Jesus Christ,*" she said, "you scared me."

**See also:** [Jesus](#jesus), [Christ](#christ), [God](#god), [jeez](#jeez)

### kiss my ass

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɪs maɪ ˈæs/ &middot; *kis my ASS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate to strong
**Safe in:** friends and siblings; complaining about somebody who is not in the room
**Never in:** work, school, writing, or to the face of anyone you need on your side
**Variants:** *kiss my butt (softened), you can kiss my ass, kiss my grits (dated and jokey)*

A refusal with an insult welded to it — not only no, but you had no business asking. Americans aim it at an absent third party at least as often as at the person in front of them, which is what keeps it below [fuck you](#fuck-you) in real-world damage.

1. "They want it done by Friday." "They can *kiss my ass.*"
2. "*Kiss my ass,*" he said, and went back to his sandwich.
3. Reimburse me in ninety days? *Kiss my ass.*
4. She smiled at him across the table and mouthed *kiss my ass.*
5. The landlord raised the rent again and called it standard. *Kiss my ass,* she thought.

**See also:** [my ass](#my-ass), [up yours](#up-yours), [screw you](#screw-you)

### motherfucker

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmʌðərˌfʌkər/ &middot; *MUTH-er-fuk-er*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear; alone, at an object that has just injured you
**Never in:** work, school, public, writing, anywhere near children
**Variants:** *motherf---er, mutha, MF*

An explosion of pain or rage, usually at an object rather than a person — the cabinet door, the frozen bolt, the machine that just ate your money. Said with a laugh it can be admiration, but a learner should not attempt that reading.

1. "*Motherfucker!*" He had hit his head on the open cabinet door.
2. The engine died halfway up the hill. *Motherfucker.*
3. "*Motherfucker,*" she said, staring at the cracked screen.
4. *Motherfucker* — that's the second time this week.
5. He finally got the splinter out and muttered, "*Motherfucker.*"

**See also:** [son of a bitch](#son-of-a-bitch), [fuck](#fuck), [bastard](#bastard)

### my ass

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈæs/ &middot; *my ASS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate to strong
**Safe in:** friends, family, arguing about something unimportant
**Never in:** work, school, writing, any conversation with someone you are trying to persuade
**Variants:** *my ass!, my foot (softened), my eye (dated)*

A flat denial that attaches to the words you are rejecting: repeat the claim, then add *my ass*. It says the claim is not merely wrong but insulting to your intelligence.

1. "He's been working all weekend." "Working *my ass.* He was at the lake."
2. "It's a ten-minute walk." "Ten minutes *my ass.*"
3. He's been telling everyone he paid for dinner. Paid for dinner *my ass* — I saw the check.
4. "The repair is covered under warranty." "Covered *my ass.*"
5. Free shipping *my ass* — they charged me nine dollars.

**See also:** [bullshit](#bullshit), [no shit](#no-shit), [kiss my ass](#kiss-my-ass)

### no shit

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈʃɪt/ &middot; *noh SHIT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears
**Never in:** work, school, writing, conversation with strangers
**Variants:** *no shit, Sherlock*

Two opposite readings, separated entirely by intonation. Flat and falling, it is sarcasm — "obviously." Rising, as a question, it is genuine surprise — "really?" Get the tone wrong and you insult someone by accident.

1. "It's cold in Minnesota in January." "*No shit.*"
2. "*No shit?* She's moving to Tokyo?"
3. He actually finished the marathon? *No shit.*
4. "*No shit,*" she said flatly. "I worked that out an hour ago."
5. "They're closing the store." "*No shit.* Since when?"

**See also:** [bullshit](#bullshit), [my ass](#my-ass), [shit](#shit)

### oh fuck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈfʌk/ &middot; *oh FUK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear; alone, in the second after something goes wrong
**Never in:** work, school, any writing, public places, around children
**Variants:** *ah fuck, aw fuck, oh fuck no*

The realization that something has gone badly wrong — one full step above [oh shit](#oh-shit) in dread and in cost. The *oh* is the moment of understanding; the second word is what the understanding feels like.

1. "*Oh fuck.*" The car in front had stopped dead.
2. *Oh fuck* — I sent that to the whole company.
3. He patted his pockets on the platform. "*Oh fuck,* my passport."
4. "*Oh fuck no,*" she said when she saw whose name was on the schedule.
5. The tent poles went into the river one after another. *Oh fuck.*

**See also:** [oh shit](#oh-shit), [fuck](#fuck), [what the fuck](#what-the-fuck)

### oh my God

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ maɪ ˈɡɑd/ &middot; *oh my GOD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Offensiveness:** mild as swearing, strong to devout listeners
**Safe in:** casual conversation, texting, most workplaces among peers
**Never in:** church, religious households, formal writing, conversation with someone who has told you they mind
**Variants:** *OMG, oh my god, oh my gosh (softened), oh God, omigod*

The all-purpose American exclamation of shock, delight, horror, or excitement — by far the most frequent item in this file and the mildest of the religious ones. In writing it is usually *OMG*, which reads as lighter still.

1. "*Oh my God,* I love this song."
2. *Oh my God* — are you okay?
3. She opened the door and said, "*Oh my God,* you're here!"
4. *Oh my God,* that was the longest meeting of my life.
5. "*Oh my god,*" he texted back. "I completely forgot."

**See also:** [God](#god), [gosh](#gosh), [Jesus](#jesus), [good grief](#good-grief)

### oh shit

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈʃɪt/ &middot; *oh SHIT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears, driving
**Never in:** work, school, writing, around children
**Variants:** *ah shit, aw shit*

The moment you realize something has gone wrong — a beat of dread rather than the flat annoyance of plain [shit](#shit). The *oh* is the realization; the second word is the reaction to it.

1. "*Oh shit,* I forgot my wallet."
2. *Oh shit* — the light's changing.
3. He heard the crash from the kitchen. "*Oh shit.*"
4. *Oh shit,* is that today?
5. "*Oh shit,*" she said, catching the vase just in time.

**See also:** [shit](#shit), [holy shit](#holy-shit), [oh my God](#oh-my-god)

### piss off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpɪs ˈɔf/ &middot; *pis AWF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** friends who are joking; British and Irish casual speech
**Never in:** work, school, writing, any real argument
**Variants:** *piss off!, sod off (British)*

Go away, or stop talking nonsense. It is the milder cousin of [fuck off](#fuck-off) and is much more common in Britain and Ireland than in the United States, where it can sound slightly theatrical.

1. "*Piss off.* I'm not lending you the car again."
2. "*Piss off,*" he said, but he was smiling.
3. *Piss off* — you know that's not true.
4. She'd had enough of the guy following her down the block. "*Piss off.*"
5. "Give me a hand?" "*Piss off,* I just sat down."

**See also:** [fuck off](#fuck-off), [screw you](#screw-you), [bugger](#bugger)

### screw it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈskru ɪt/ &middot; *SKROO it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers, family
**Never in:** formal writing, a client meeting, a job interview
**Variants:** *screw this, screw that, screw it all*

The socially acceptable version of [fuck it](#fuck-it): the point where you stop deliberating and do the easy thing. Most Americans will use this one at work without a second thought.

1. "*Screw it.* I'm ordering takeout."
2. We were already an hour behind, so — *screw it* — we turned around.
3. "*Screw it,*" she said, and bought the plane ticket.

### screw you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌskru ˈju/ &middot; *skroo YOO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** friends who know you are joking; siblings
**Never in:** work, school, writing, an argument with a stranger
**Variants:** *screw off, screw them*

A softened [fuck you](#fuck-you) — still an insult, but one that can be affectionate between friends. Tone carries all the weight: the same two words end a friendship or land as a joke.

1. "*Screw you,* I got here first."
2. "*Screw you,*" he said, laughing, when she beat him again.
3. *Screw you* — that's my sandwich.
4. She said "*Screw you*" under her breath and kept typing.

### son of a bitch

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌn əv ə ˈbɪtʃ/ &middot; *sun-uv-uh-BICH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** strong
**Safe in:** friends, family that swears, alone with a stubborn machine
**Never in:** work, school, writing, around children
**Variants:** *sonuvabitch, son of a gun (softened), S.O.B.*

Classic American frustration — the shout that follows a stubbed toe or a towed car. Said slowly and with a smile it flips into admiration or amazement, the way *well, I'll be damned* does.

1. "*Son of a bitch!*" He had caught his toe on the bed frame.
2. *Son of a bitch* — it actually worked.
3. She looked at the empty space where she had parked. "*Son of a bitch,* they towed it."
4. "Well, *son of a bitch,*" he said when the old engine turned over.

### up yours

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʌp ˈjʊrz/ &middot; *up YOORZ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** moderate
**Safe in:** joking with friends and siblings
**Never in:** work, school, writing, or any argument you intend to win
**Variants:** *up yours!, up your ass (stronger), stick it (related)*

A short dismissal of the listener, usually delivered with a raised middle finger. It sits between [screw you](#screw-you) and [fuck you](#fuck-you) in force but well below both in seriousness, because it now sounds like something out of a 1970s sitcom.

1. "*Up yours,*" he said, without looking up from his phone.
2. "You're going to lose, you know." "*Up yours.*"
3. She watched the bus pull away without her. "*Up yours.*"
4. "*Up yours* and the horse you rode in on," his grandfather liked to say.
5. He paid the bet, muttered *up yours,* and left.

### what the fuck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ðə ˈfʌk/ &middot; *wut thuh FUK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Offensiveness:** very strong
**Safe in:** close friends who swear; texting between friends as *WTF*
**Never in:** work, school, any writing you sign, public places, around children
**Variants:** *WTF, the fuck, what the actual fuck*

Blank incomprehension at something absurd, unfair, or inexplicable. It can stand alone as a whole question or open a real one (*what the fuck is this?*), and the clipped modern form is just *the fuck*.

1. "*What the fuck?* Who parked me in?"
2. *What the fuck* — my card just got declined.
3. He opened the box and said, "*What the fuck* is this?"
4. "*What the fuck,*" she said quietly, reading the letter again.
5. *What the fuck* are they doing to the road out there?

**See also:** [what the hell](#what-the-hell), [what the h-e-double-hockey-sticks](#what-the-h-e-double-hockey-sticks), [fuck](#fuck)

### what the hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ðə ˈhɛl/ &middot; *wut thuh HEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Offensiveness:** mild to moderate
**Safe in:** casual conversation, most workplaces among peers
**Never in:** formal writing, religious settings, a first meeting with a client
**Variants:** *what the heck (softened), what in the hell, what the hell?!*

Two senses. As a question it is confusion or protest at something that makes no sense. Flat and falling, in front of a suggestion, it means "why not" — a shrug that accepts a small indulgence.

1. "*What the hell?* The door was locked five minutes ago."
2. *What the hell* — I'll have another slice.
3. "*What the hell* is that noise?"
4. She shrugged. "*What the hell,* let's do it."
5. *What the hell* was I thinking?

**See also:** [what the fuck](#what-the-fuck), [heck](#heck), [hell](#hell), [screw it](#screw-it)

## Minced oaths and euphemistic softenings

Every word in this section is a deliberate distortion of a taboo word — *gosh* and *golly* from *God*, *jeez* and *jeepers* and *cripes* from *Jesus*, *darn* and *dang* from *damn*, *heck* from *hell*, *shoot* and *shucks* and *sugar* from *shit*, *frick* and *fudge* and *eff* from *fuck*, *tarnation* from *damnation*. They fill exactly the same slot in the sentence, they carry the same emotion, and they cost you nothing. Many of them sound old-fashioned, rural, or childish, which is part of their charm and their protection: nobody thinks you are being coarse, they think you are being careful.

### crikey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkraɪki/ &middot; *KRY-kee*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere, in any company — it is safe even in front of grandparents and children
**Never in:** American speech if you want to sound native; formal writing
**Variants:** *crikey Moses, blimey (related British form)*

An Australian and British exclamation of surprise, softened from *Christ*. Americans know it from wildlife documentaries and Australian friends and understand it perfectly, but almost never say it themselves.

1. "*Crikey,* that's a big spider."
2. *Crikey* — look how much they've grown.
3. He whistled. "*Crikey.* That's steep."
4. *Crikey,* it's cold this morning.
5. "*Crikey,*" she said, "I haven't been back here in twenty years."

**See also:** [cripes](#cripes), [criminy](#criminy), [bloody hell](#bloody-hell)

### criminy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkrɪməni/ &middot; *KRIM-uh-nee*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere, including work, school, and church
**Never in:** formal writing — and be aware that anywhere else it will make you sound seventy years old
**Variants:** *criminy sakes, jiminy, jiminy Christmas*

A mild, old-fashioned exclamation of exasperation or surprise, softened from *Christ*. You will hear it from older Americans and in family films, and it signals that the speaker does not swear.

1. "*Criminy,* what a mess."
2. *Criminy* — you nearly knocked me over.
3. "*Criminy,*" he said, "that's the fourth time today."
4. *Criminy,* it's dark in here.
5. She looked at the receipt. "*Criminy.* Twelve dollars for a sandwich."

**See also:** [cripes](#cripes), [jeepers](#jeepers), [crikey](#crikey)

### cripes

**Pronunciation:** /kraɪps/ &middot; *KRYPS*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere — work, school, church, around children
**Never in:** formal writing; it is speech only
**Variants:** *for cripes' sake, criminy (related)*

A softened *Christ*, used for annoyance or mild astonishment. Like most of this section it sounds mid-century American, which is exactly why people reach for it when children are in the room.

1. "*Cripes,* it's hot."
2. *Cripes* — I nearly forgot your birthday.
3. "*Cripes,*" he said, "there must be two hundred people in that line."
4. *Cripes,* the dog's gotten into the trash again.
5. Oh, *cripes,* I left the car window open.

**See also:** [criminy](#criminy), [jeez](#jeez), [Christ](#christ)

### crud

**Pronunciation:** /krʌd/ &middot; *KRUD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere — work, school, family, in front of children
**Never in:** formal writing or a formal speech
**Variants:** *oh crud, cruddy*

A soft substitute for [crap](#crap), used for small setbacks. It is the sort of thing said by people who have decided not to swear in front of their kids and have kept the habit.

1. "*Crud.* I forgot to set my alarm."
2. *Crud* — we're out of milk.
3. "*Crud,*" he said when the pen leaked in his shirt pocket.
4. Oh, *crud,* the game starts in ten minutes.
5. *Crud,* I think I left it at the office.

**See also:** [crap](#crap), [shoot](#shoot), [fudge](#fudge)

### dagnabbit

**Pronunciation:** /dæɡˈnæbɪt/ &middot; *dag-NAB-it*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere; it is almost always meant to be funny
**Never in:** anywhere you want to be taken seriously
**Variants:** *dagnabit, dad-gum it, consarn it*

A scrambled *God damn it*, now attached firmly to cartoon prospectors and cranky grandfathers. Real people do say it, almost always as a joke about their own irritation.

1. "*Dagnabbit,* where did I put my glasses?"
2. *Dagnabbit* — the mower won't start again.
3. "*Dagnabbit!*" the old man shouted at the raccoons on his porch.
4. *Dagnabbit,* I've told you kids to stay off the grass.
5. He kicked the fence post. "*Dagnabbit.*"

**See also:** [doggone it](#doggone-it), [goddammit](#goddammit), [dang](#dang)

### dang

**Pronunciation:** /dæŋ/ &middot; *DANG*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere — work, school, family, church
**Never in:** formal writing
**Variants:** *dang it, dadgum, dagnab*

A softened *damn*, and like *damn* it swings between frustration and admiration. It carries a Southern and Midwestern coloring that many Americans use deliberately for warmth.

1. "*Dang,* that's a nice truck."
2. *Dang* — I missed the turn.
3. "*Dang it,*" she said when the zipper stuck.
4. *Dang,* these boots are comfortable.
5. He looked at the final score. "*Dang.*"

**See also:** [darn](#darn), [damn](#damn), [doggone it](#doggone-it)

### darn

**Pronunciation:** /dɑrn/ &middot; *DARN*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere — work, school, church, in front of anyone
**Never in:** formal writing, where you would simply drop it
**Variants:** *darn it, darn it all, durn (regional), dagnabbit (stronger and funnier)*

The most standard softening of *damn*, and the safest word in this file. It expresses disappointment about something small and signals, clearly and instantly, that the speaker chooses not to swear.

1. "*Darn.* The store closes at six."
2. *Darn it* — I forgot the tickets.
3. "*Darn,*" she said, "I was one number off."
4. *Darn,* that's disappointing news.
5. He shook his head. "*Darn it all,* I really wanted that job."

**See also:** [dang](#dang), [damn](#damn), [shoot](#shoot), [heck](#heck)

### doggone it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɔɡˈɡɔn ɪt/ or /ˌdɑɡˈɡɑn ɪt/ &middot; *dog-GAWN it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere, in any company
**Never in:** formal writing; anywhere you are trying to sound young
**Variants:** *doggone, dad-gum it, dog-gone-it*

A softened *God damn it*, warm, old-fashioned, and Southern or rural in flavor. It handles frustration, and *doggone* on its own can be simple admiration.

1. "*Doggone it,* the truck won't start."
2. *Doggone it* — I left my glasses at the restaurant.
3. "*Doggone it,*" he said, "that's the third time this week."
4. *Doggone,* she's fast.
5. Well, *doggone it,* I think you're right.

### eff

**Pronunciation:** /ɛf/ &middot; *EF*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** casual workplaces, friends, texting — anywhere the real word would be too much
**Never in:** formal writing; a very formal meeting, where even the substitute draws attention
**Variants:** *effing, eff off, eff it, eff this*

The spoken name of the letter F, standing in for [fuck](#fuck). Everyone knows exactly which word you mean, which is the point: it acknowledges the feeling while declining to say the word out loud.

1. "*Eff!*" She had dropped her phone in the parking lot.
2. *Eff* — I sent it to the wrong address.
3. "*Eff* this," he said, and closed the laptop.
4. "*Eff off,*" she told him, but she was grinning.
5. *Eff* it, let's take the long way home.

**See also:** [frick](#frick), [fudge](#fudge), [fuck](#fuck)

### fiddlesticks

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɪdəlˌstɪks/ &middot; *FID-uhl-stiks*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Offensiveness:** mild
**Safe in:** anywhere at all — work, school, church, in front of children
**Never in:** formal writing; anywhere you need to sound under sixty
**Variants:** *oh fiddlesticks, fiddlesticks!, fiddle-dee-dee (older still)*

Annoyance at a small setback, or a flat "nonsense" verdict on something you have just been told. It fills the [bullshit](#bullshit) slot with every trace of the offense removed, and today it reads as either genuinely elderly or deliberately comic.

1. "*Fiddlesticks.* I've dropped a stitch."
2. "You're too old to be up a ladder." "*Fiddlesticks.*"
3. *Fiddlesticks* — the recipe wanted two eggs, not one.
4. "Oh, *fiddlesticks,*" she said, "the cat's gotten out again."
5. He looked at the flat tire and said, entirely seriously, "*Fiddlesticks.*"

**See also:** [good grief](#good-grief), [bullshit](#bullshit), [shucks](#shucks)

A softened *for Christ's sake* — Pete stands in for St. Peter. It is impatience with the edges filed off, and it usually introduces or follows an imperative.

### frick

A softened [fuck](#fuck), and currently the most common one among American teenagers and twenty-somethings. It slots in everywhere the real word does — *frick*, *what the frick*, *frick it* — with none of the damage.

### fudge

A softened [fuck](#fuck) that borrows the candy. It is used for small domestic disasters and sounds slightly comic, which takes the heat out of the moment.

1. "*Fudge.* I burned the toast."

### gee

A softened *Jesus*, used for mild surprise, sympathy, or hesitation. Modern Americans often use it with a hint of irony, and *gee, thanks* is almost always sarcastic.

### golly

**Variants:** *by golly, golly gee, gollee*

A softened *God*, expressing wonder or mild astonishment. It is thoroughly old-fashioned now, and younger Americans use it mostly in a jokey, folksy voice.

The standard softening of *God*, used for surprise, sympathy, or polite hesitation. *Oh my gosh* is the safe version of [oh my God](#oh-my-god) and is extremely common among Americans who avoid religious swearing.
