Disagreement, refusal, and skepticism#

The words Americans reach for to say no, to shut a request down, and to signal that they do not believe a word of what they just heard.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

This category runs from a bare grunt (uh-uh) through the polite decline (no thanks) all the way to a whole museum of words that mean "nonsense" (poppycock, malarkey, horsefeathers). Two things run through all of it. First, several of the sharpest items here are words of agreement — sure, right, okay, uh-huh — turned inside out by intonation alone; said flat, slow, and falling they mean the opposite of what they say, which is why writers stretch the spelling (riiight, suuure) or drop in a cold period (Okay.) to make the sarcasm visible on the page. Second, the "that's nonsense" family is largely built from minced substitutes for barnyard vulgarity (horsefeathers, bull hockey, baloney), so the register ladder is unusually long: pick the wrong rung and you either sound like a cartoon grandfather or get a call from HR. That family is not purely a museum, either — cap does the same job for speakers under thirty that baloney did for their great-grandparents.

Saying no — plain negation and refusal#

hardly#

Pronunciation
/ˈhɑrdli/ · HARD-lee
Русский
отнюдь / куда там — «вовсе нет», сухое возражение на явное преувеличение
Type
secondary
Register
neutral
Variants
oh, hardly

Standing alone, hardly rejects a claim as far off the mark — it means "not remotely," not "barely." It is a dry, slightly bookish way to disagree, and it usually answers something the speaker considers an overstatement.

  1. "Was the movie scary?" "Hardly. I fell asleep."
  2. "You must have been furious." "Hardly. I thought it was funny."
  3. "So he's an expert?" "Hardly. He read one article on a plane."
  4. "That's got to be the hardest class in the department." "Hardly — the final is open-book."
  5. "That must have cost a fortune." "Hardly — forty bucks at an estate sale."

I don't think so#

Pronunciation
/ˌaɪ doʊnt ˈθɪŋk soʊ/ · eye dohnt THINGK soh
Русский
вряд ли / не думаю — мягкое «нет»; сказанное веско — твёрдый отказ с предупреждением
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
I think not, I really don't think so, uh, I don't think so

Two different jobs depending on delivery. Said lightly it is a hedged no — polite, provisional, easy to argue with. Said flat and slow with the weight dropped on think, it is a hard refusal with a warning inside it: the speaker has caught you trying something. I think not is the stiff, jokey-formal cousin.

  1. "Is he coming with us?" "I don't think so, he's got class."
  2. "Do we need to bring anything?" "I don't think so."
  3. He reached for the last of the fries. "I don't think so."
  4. "I'll just add the charge to your account." "I don't think so. We agreed on a flat rate."
  5. "You'll let this one slide, right?" "I don't think so."

I'm good#

Pronunciation
/aɪm ˈɡʊd/ · eyem GUUD
Русский
мне не надо, спасибо — мягкий отказ от предложенного: «у меня всё есть»
Type
phrase
Register
informal
Variants
nah, I'm good, I'm all set, I'm fine

A soft, friendly refusal of something offered — food, a drink, a ride, help with the boxes. It declines without ever using the word no, and it implies that the speaker already has what they need, so it lands as easygoing rather than blunt.

  1. "Want another slice?" "I'm good, thanks."
  2. "I can drive you to the airport." "I'm good — I already booked a car."
  3. "You need a hand with those boxes?" "Nah, I'm good."
  4. "Should I order you a coffee?" "I'm good. I've had three already."
  5. "Can I get you anything else?" "I'm all set, thank you."

mm-mm#

Pronunciation
no settled IPA — a two-beat hum with the lips closed and a glottal catch between the beats, roughly /ˈm̩ʔm̩/, pitch falling from high to low · MM-mm
Русский
м-м / не-а — «нет» с закрытым ртом; тон высокий-низкий (низкий-высокий mm-hmm = «да»)
Type
primary
Register
informal
Variants
mm-mmm, m-m, hm-mm

The closed-mouth version of uh-uh: a nasal grunt that means no. The pitch pattern is what carries the meaning — high-then-low is no, while the low-then-high mm-hmm is yes, so the two are opposites that differ only in melody.

  1. "Did you touch the thermostat?" "Mm-mm."
  2. She shook her head. "Mm-mm, that's not what he said."
  3. "Anybody want the last one?" "Mm-mm." "Mm-mm."
  4. "Mm-mm," he managed, mouth full of sandwich, waving the check away.
  5. "Is this the right building?" "Mm-mm — it's the one across the street."

nah#

Pronunciation
/næ/ or /nɑ/ · NA (rhyming with cat) or NAH; the spelling naw is /nɔ/, NAW
Русский
не-а / да не — расслабленное разговорное «нет», мягче и менее окончательное, чем no
Type
primary
Register
informal
Variants
naw, nahh, nahhh

A relaxed, casual no — the everyday spoken negative among friends. It is softer and less final than no, and it usually trails a clause behind it that explains or cushions the refusal.

  1. "You coming out tonight?" "Nah, I'm beat."
  2. "Naw, man, I already ate."
  3. "Do you want me to wait for you?" "Nah, go ahead."
  4. "Is it a big deal?" "Nah, it happens all the time."
  5. "Nah, I don't think that's how the billing works."

nay#

Pronunciation
/neɪ/ · NAY
Русский

Type

Register
dated (neutral in a roll-call vote)
Variants

The Old English no, alive today in exactly two places: the recorded vote (the nays have it) and deliberately archaic joking. As an everyday refusal it dropped out of American speech centuries ago, so anyone who says it straight is either presiding over a meeting or doing a bit.

  1. "All those opposed?" "Nay."
  2. The motion failed, nineteen nays to twelve ayes.
  3. "Nay, good sir, I shall not be moved from this couch."
  4. "Does the senator yield?" "Nay, Mr. Chairman."
  5. "One more round?" "Nay — I'm driving."

negative#

Pronunciation
/ˈnɛɡətɪv/ · NEG-uh-tiv
Русский

Type
secondary
Register

Variants

The radio-procedure word for no, used where clarity matters more than tone: aviation, the military, police work, dispatch. Outside those settings Americans use it as a deadpan joke, and the hammed-up negatory is always a joke.

  1. "Do you have visual on the vehicle?" "Negative."
  2. "Are you carrying any hazardous materials?" "Negative, tower."
  3. "Negative — the shipment never left the warehouse."
  4. "Did you remember the tickets?" "Negative." "You're kidding."
  5. "You want anchovies on that?" "Negatory."

never#

Pronunciation
/ˈnɛvɚ/ · NEV-er
Русский
никогда / ни за что — отказ навсегда, а не только на сейчас
Type
secondary
Register
neutral
Variants
never ever, never again

As a one-word answer, never refuses for all time rather than just for now. It is emphatic by nature, so it tends to answer a question about the future or a proposal the speaker finds permanently unacceptable.

  1. "Would you ever move back?" "Never."
  2. "Never. I'm not signing that."
  3. "You'll thank me for this one day." "Never."
  4. "Have you tried durian?" "No — and never."
  5. "Come on, just one more game." "Never again. Last time took four hours."

no#

Pronunciation
/noʊ/ · NOH
Русский
нет — базовое отрицание; no no no — тревожное «стой-стой»; nooo — «не-ет» с досадой
Type
primary
Register
neutral
Variants
nooo, no no no, NO

The base negative of English and the plainest refusal available in any register. Doubled or tripled (no no no) it turns urgent rather than merely negative; stretched out (nooo) it expresses dismay at something already happening rather than refusal of something proposed.

  1. "Is this seat taken?" "No, go ahead."
  2. "No. I am not lending him the car again."
  3. "No, no, no — you have to click Save first."
  4. "Did anyone call while I was out?" "No, it's been quiet all morning."
  5. "So you're fine with the change?" "No, and I said so in the meeting."

no thanks#

Pronunciation
/ˌnoʊ ˈθæŋks/ · noh THANKS
Русский
нет, спасибо — стандартный вежливый отказ от предложения (no thank you — полнее и официальнее)
Type

Register
neutral
Variants

The standard polite refusal of an offer. No thank you is the fuller and more formal version, no thanks is what most Americans actually say, and either one is a complete answer on its own.

  1. "More wine?" "No thanks."
  2. "Would you like a bag?" "No thank you, I brought one."
  3. "Can I interest you in the extended warranty?" "No thanks."
  4. "We could split an appetizer." "No thanks — I want to save room."
  5. "No thanks," she said, handing the flyer back.

nope#

Pronunciation
/noʊp/ · NOHP
Русский

Type

Register

Variants

A crisp, casual no, closed off with a /p/ that makes it sound final and a little breezy. It suits flat factual answers and cheerful refusals to engage; tripled in a text (nope nope nope) it means "absolutely not, and I am backing away slowly."

  1. "Did the package come?" "Nope."
  2. "Are you going to tell me what she said?" "Nope."
  3. "Nope, that's not my department."
  4. "Any chance they'll reschedule?" "Nope, the deadline's fixed."
  5. She looked at the spider in the sink and typed nope nope nope into the group chat.

not really#

Pronunciation
/ˌnɑt ˈriəli/, often /ˌnɑt ˈrɪli/ · not REE-uh-lee
Русский
да не особо / не совсем — самое мягкое несогласие; вежливый способ сказать «не понравилось»
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
nah, not really, not really, no, no, not really

The softest disagreement in ordinary American use. It answers in the negative while pulling most of the force out first, so it can decline, doubt, or contradict without closing anything off. It is also the standard polite way to say a thing was not good without ever saying it was bad.

  1. "Did you like the movie?" "Not really."
  2. "Are you busy?" "Not really — what's up?"
  3. "So that's how the tax works?" "Not really. It's messier than that."
  4. "You must be thrilled." "Not really, to be honest."
  5. "Have you two met?" "Not really — we've been on the same call a few times."

nuh-uh#

Pronunciation
/ˈnʌʔʌ/ · NUH-uh (first syllable stressed, pitch falling; the catch in the middle is the glottal stop of uh-oh)
Русский
не-а, неправда — детское возражение на только что сказанное; у взрослых — в шутку
Type
primary
Register
childish
Variants
nuh-unh, nunh-uh

The playground contradiction: "that is not true." Where uh-uh simply answers no, nuh-uh denies a statement somebody has just made, and its natural sparring partner is uh-huh meaning "it is too." Adults use it jokingly, or when they are arguing like children on purpose.

  1. "You started it." "Nuh-uh, you did."
  2. "Nuh-uh! I saw you take the last cookie."
  3. "Dad said we could." "Nuh-uh, he said maybe."
  4. "You've never even been to Texas." "Nuh-uh — I lived in Austin for a year."
  5. "Nuh-uh," he said, grinning. "That is not what the rules say."

pass#

Pronunciation
/pæs/ · PASS
Русский

Borrowed from card games and quiz shows, pass declines a turn, an invitation, or an offer. Hard pass is the emphatic version and carries a note of distaste — you are not merely declining, you want it on the record that you would never.

  1. "There's a karaoke thing after work." "Pass."
  2. "Want to try the pickle soda?" "Hard pass."
  3. "I'll pass on the Monday meeting — just send me the notes."
  4. "Anyone want to present first?" "Pass."
  5. "They want us in the office five days a week." "Yeah, that's a hard pass for me."

uh-uh#

Pronunciation
/ˈʌʔʌ/ · UH-uh (first syllable stressed, pitch falling)
Русский
не-а / а-а — гортанное «нет», тон высокий-низкий (uh-huh «да» — низкий-высокий)
Type

Register
informal
Variants
uh uh, unh-uh

The grunted no: two vowels with a catch in the throat between them, said high-then-low. It is the exact mirror of uh-huh ("yes"), which runs low-then-high, and melody is the only thing separating them — which is why learners get misheard on this one more than on almost any other word.

  1. "Did you finish the dishes?" "Uh-uh."
  2. "Uh-uh, don't even think about it."
  3. "Is that your car getting towed?" "Uh-uh, mine's the blue one."
  4. "You want me to keep going?" "Uh-uh, that's plenty."
  5. He held out the plate and the toddler clamped her mouth shut. "Uh-uh."

Emphatic refusal — "absolutely not"#

absolutely not#

Pronunciation
/ˌæbsəˈlutli ˈnɑt/ · ab-suh-LOOT-lee NOT

Type

Variants

The strongest refusal that is still safe in a meeting or an email. It closes the door completely without slang or profanity, which makes it the default emphatic no in professional settings.

  1. "Should I cc the whole team?" "Absolutely not."
  2. "Are you suggesting we falsified the numbers?" "Absolutely not."
  3. "Certainly not at that price."
  4. "Can I put you down as a maybe?" "Absolutely not — I'll be out of the country."
  5. "Definitely not," the doctor said. "Not while you're on this medication."

forget it#

Pronunciation
/fɚˈɡɛt ɪt/ · fer-GET it
Русский
забудь / и не думай — резкий отказ; также «неважно, проехали»
Type

Register
informal
Variants
forget about it, fuhgeddaboudit, forget that

Two jobs, both dismissive: it refuses a proposal flatly ("that is never happening"), and it waves away something that no longer matters ("never mind, drop it"). The eye-dialect spelling fuhgeddaboudit /fəˌɡɛdəˈbaʊdɪt/ covers both senses plus a third — "obviously, no question about it" — but be aware of what you are doing with it: it is an imitation of a working-class Italian-American New York accent, and outside New York it reads as a mob-movie impression.

  1. "Can I borrow the truck for the move?" "Forget it."
  2. "What were you going to say?" "Forget it, it wasn't important."
  3. "Forget about it — I'm not driving into the city on a holiday weekend."
  4. "How much do I owe you?" "Forget it, it's on me."
  5. "Is the pizza there any good?" "Fuhgeddaboudit. Best in Brooklyn."

  2. "On the contrary, the numbers have been consistent for six quarters."
  3. "I take it you object." "Quite the contrary — I'm in favor."
  4. "The witness was uncooperative, then." "On the contrary, Your Honor."
  5. "So the rain ruined the whole weekend." "On the contrary, it was the best part."

The written form of a dismissive puff of air — not a true raspberry, which is made with the tongue between the lips and is ruder. This one just leaks out and means "that is not worth taking seriously." It waves off a claim, a threat, or a worry with almost no effort at all, and the effortlessness is part of the insult.

  1. "Pfft. That's not even a real rule."
  2. "He said he could beat you at chess." "Pfft."
  3. "Pfft, I've fixed worse than this with duct tape."
  4. "Aren't you nervous about the presentation?" "Pfft. No."
  5. "They think a two-hour drive is far." "Pfft."

Challenges a claim that something is a rule, a habit, or a settled fact: the speaker never agreed to it and would like to know where it came from. It is close kin to says who, and it lands as a shove even when the tone is light — which is why it is a bad answer to give a police officer or a boss.

  1. "We don't allow outside food." "Since when?"
  2. "You hate cilantro." "Since when? I put it on everything."
  3. "Since when do we need approval for a fifty-dollar order?"
  4. "He's in charge of the schedule now." "Since when?"
  5. "It's a black-tie thing." "Since when? I wore jeans last year."
