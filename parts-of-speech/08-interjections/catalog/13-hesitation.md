# Hesitation, fillers, and discourse markers

> The stalling sounds, hedges, and traffic signals of American conversation — what speakers say while they think, and how they steer a talk from one thing to the next.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These are the words Americans use to buy time, hold the floor, and steer a conversation — sounds and short phrases that carry almost no dictionary meaning of their own but tell the listener what is happening: *I am still thinking*, *I am changing the subject*, *don't take that too literally*. Many of them are ordinary words (*well, so, look, right*) doing a second job outside the grammar of the clause, which is why they are set off by commas, dashes, or ellipses rather than folded into the sentence. The raw hesitation sounds barely have vowels to transcribe: *hmm* and *mm* are hummed on a closed mouth with no vowel at all, and *uh* and *um* use the shortest, most neutral vowel in English. Almost everything in this file belongs to speech — apart from *actually*, *and so on*, *anyway*, *by the way*, *granted*, *in other words*, *more or less*, *so to speak*, and *that said*, writing these into an essay or a report is a mistake.

## Vocalized pauses

*The raw hesitation sounds: no dictionary meaning at all, just a noise that says "I am still talking."*

### ah

**Pronunciation:** /ɑ/ &middot; *AH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral (speech only)
**Variants:** *ahh*, *ahhh*

A drawn-out open vowel used to stall for a beat while you retrieve a word or soften a hesitant answer. In this hesitation use it is flatter and quieter than the *ah* of relief or discovery, and Americans reach for it less often than for *uh*.

1. I was, *ah*, hoping we could push the meeting to Thursday.
2. His name is — *ah* — it's right on the tip of my tongue.
3. The total comes to, *ah*, three hundred and forty dollars.
4. *Ah*, how do I say this without sounding rude?
5. We're, *ah*, still waiting on legal to sign off.
6. The witness paused, said *ah* twice, and then changed his answer.

**See also:** [uh](#uh), [um](#um), [er](#er), [hmm](#hmm)

### er

**Pronunciation:** /ɝ/ (British /ɜː/, with no r-sound in it) &middot; *UR*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional (chiefly British; in American use mostly a written spelling)
**Variants:** *err*, *uhr*

The British spelling of the basic hesitation sound, written *er* because non-rhotic British English pronounces it as a long vowel with no r in it. An American reading the page aloud puts the r back in and says /ɝ/, which is not the sound the spelling was invented for. Americans say *uh* and write *uh*; when an American writes *er*, it is usually in print, in a joking-formal tone, or to mark a small verbal stumble.

1. The report is due — *er* — I think it was Friday.
2. He claims he was at the office all night, which, *er*, seems unlikely.
3. So the meeting is at nine, *er*, nine thirty.
4. I'd like to introduce our, *er*, guest of honor.
5. "*Er*, I don't think that's quite what she meant," he said.
6. The transcript is one long string of *er* and *um*.

**See also:** [uh](#uh), [um](#um), [erm](#erm), [ah](#ah)

### erm

**Pronunciation:** /ɝm/ (British /ɜːm/, with no r-sound in it) &middot; *URM*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional (British)
**Variants:** *ermm*

The British counterpart of American *um* — the same hesitation hum, spelled to match a non-rhotic accent. Americans recognize it in writing but do not normally produce it, and using it out loud in the United States reads as an affectation or as an imitation of a British speaker.

1. *Erm*, I'm not sure that's how it works over here.
2. He wrote back, "*Erm*, we never actually agreed to that."
3. The British witness kept saying *erm* where an American would say *um*.
4. *Erm* — sorry, could you repeat the question?
5. Her text just said "*erm*" and nothing else, which was worse than a no.

**See also:** [um](#um), [er](#er), [uh](#uh)

### hmm

**Pronunciation:** /hm̩/ &middot; *HMM — a hummed m with the lips closed, no vowel in it*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *hm*, *hmmm*, *hmmmm*

A closed-lip hum meaning that you are thinking, weighing, or not quite convinced. Length carries the meaning: a clipped *hm* is mild interest or skepticism, while a long *hmmm* is real deliberation.

1. *Hmm.* Let me look at the numbers again before I answer.
2. "How does eight o'clock work?" "*Hmm*, could we do eight thirty?"
3. *Hmm*, that's strange — the file was here this morning.
4. She read the offer, said *hmm*, and slid it back across the table.
5. *Hmmm.* I'm not sure I agree with that at all.
6. He answered every question with a noncommittal *hm*.

**See also:** [mm](#mm), [let me think](#let-me-think), [well](#well), [uh](#uh)

### mm

**Pronunciation:** /m̩/ &middot; *MM — a hum on a closed m, no vowel*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mmm*

A short level hum that fills a pause or signals lukewarm thinking. Note that this is not the pleasure sound *mmm* (as in *mmm, that smells good*) and not the agreement sound *mm-hmm*; the hesitation *mm* is flat in pitch and goes nowhere.

1. *Mm*, I'd have to check my calendar for that week.
2. "Is the new place any good?" "*Mm*. It's fine."
3. He gave a low *mm* and kept reading.
4. *Mm* — maybe. Ask me again after lunch.
5. "*Mm*, I'm not sure about the blue one," she said, holding it up to the light.

**See also:** [hmm](#hmm), [uh](#uh), [um](#um)

### uh

**Pronunciation:** /ə/ (also /ʌ/) &middot; *UH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral (speech only)
**Variants:** *uhh*, *uhhh*, *er (British spelling)*

The default American hesitation sound — a short neutral vowel that holds the floor for the half second it takes to plan the next word. It is normal and near-universal in American speech, and it is edited out of writing entirely except when the writing represents speech.

1. It's, *uh*, about a twenty-minute drive from here.
2. *Uh*, I think you may have the wrong number.
3. We're looking at, *uh*, maybe three or four candidates.
4. "What's the password?" "*Uh* … hang on, I wrote it down somewhere."
5. He said *uh* eleven times in a two-minute answer.
6. I was going to — *uh* — sorry, what was the question again?

**See also:** [um](#um), [er](#er), [ah](#ah), [hmm](#hmm), [like](#like)

### um

**Pronunciation:** /əm/ (also /ʌm/) &middot; *UM*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral (speech only)
**Variants:** *umm*, *ummm*, *erm (British spelling)*

The other core American filler: a hum that ends with the lips closing, used like *uh* but typically before a longer or more delicate stretch of speech. It often carries a note of awkwardness, and a written *um* in a text message almost always means "I have a problem with what you just said."

1. The deadline is, *um*, next Tuesday, I think.
2. *Um*, is this seat taken?
3. *Um*, you do know she's his sister, right?
4. She started every answer with *um* and then finished it confidently.
5. Can I, *um*, ask you something kind of personal?
6. He texted back a single "*um*" and I knew I had messed up.

**See also:** [uh](#uh), [erm](#erm), [hmm](#hmm), [well](#well), [like](#like)

## Stalling and floor-holding phrases

*Full phrases that ask the listener to wait while you look something up, work something out, or find your place again.*

### as I was saying

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˌaɪ wəz ˈseɪɪŋ/ &middot; *az eye wuz SAY-ing*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (can sound pointed after an interruption)
**Variants:** *like I was saying*, *as I said*, *anyway, as I was saying*

Takes the floor back after an interruption and signals that you are picking up the exact thread you were on. Said mildly it is just bookkeeping; said flatly to the person who cut you off, it is a rebuke with no rude word in it.

1. *As I was saying*, the permits won't clear until March.
2. Sorry, the dog got out — *as I was saying*, we'd need two more people to pull this off.
3. *Like I was saying*, nobody on that call had actually read the contract.
4. He waited for the laughter to die down. "*As I was saying* …"
5. *As I was saying* before the fire drill, there are three options on the table.

**See also:** [where was I](#where-was-i), [anyway](#anyway), [so anyway](#so-anyway), [hold on](#hold-on)

### bear with me

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɛr wɪð mi/ &middot; *BAIR with mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *bear with me a second*, *bear with me here*

A request for the listener's patience while you find a file, work something out, or talk your way through a half-formed idea. It is polite enough for a client call and casual enough for a friend.

1. *Bear with me* — I know that invoice is in here somewhere.
2. *Bear with me a second* while I pull up the slides.
3. This is going to sound crazy, so *bear with me*.
4. "*Bear with me*, folks, we're having a technical issue."
5. *Bear with me here*, the math takes a minute to explain.

**See also:** [hold on](#hold-on), [just a second](#just-a-second), [hang on](#hang-on), [hear me out](#hear-me-out)

### hang on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhæŋ ɑn/ &middot; *HANG-on*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hang on a sec*, *hang on a minute*

A casual "stop and wait" — used to buy yourself a moment, to interrupt someone mid-flow, or to flag that something they just said does not add up.

1. *Hang on*, let me grab a pen.
2. *Hang on a sec* — I think that's my phone.
3. *Hang on*, you said the store closes at six?
4. "Ready to go?" "*Hang on*, I'm still tying my shoes."
5. *Hang on*, I know exactly who you mean now.

**See also:** [hold on](#hold-on), [wait](#wait), [just a second](#just-a-second), [bear with me](#bear-with-me)

### hear me out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪr mi ˈaʊt/ &middot; *heer mee OUT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *just hear me out*, *hear me out on this*

Asks the listener to hold their objection until you have finished, and admits up front that the idea is going to sound bad at first. It is a floor-holding move rather than a stall: it buys the whole rest of your turn instead of a single beat.

1. *Hear me out* — what if we drove instead of flying?
2. I know how this sounds, but *hear me out*.
3. "Absolutely not." "*Just hear me out* for thirty seconds."
4. *Hear me out on this*: we cancel the party and take the money to the beach.
5. He held up a hand and said, "*Hear me out* before you say no."

**See also:** [bear with me](#bear-with-me), [hold on](#hold-on), [look](#look), [listen](#listen)

### hold on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhoʊld ɑn/ &middot; *HOHLD-on*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *hold on a second*, *hold up*, *hold on there*

A request to pause. It works both as a stall while you look something up and as a mild objection that stops the other person so you can catch up.

1. *Hold on*, I'll transfer you to someone who can help with that.
2. *Hold on* — did you just say she quit?
3. *Hold on a second*, I want to write this down.
4. "*Hold on*, I've got another call coming in."
5. *Hold up*, that's not what the email said at all.

**See also:** [hang on](#hang-on), [wait](#wait), [just a second](#just-a-second), [bear with me](#bear-with-me)

### how do I put this

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ du aɪ ˈpʊt ðɪs/ &middot; *how do I PUT this*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *how should I put this*, *how do I say this*, *how do I put it*

A stall announcing that you are choosing your words because what comes next is awkward, critical, or delicate. It warns the listener that a blunt opinion or some bad news is on the way.

1. *How do I put this* — you're not really a morning person, are you?
2. The apartment is, *how do I put this*, cozy.
3. *How should I put this*? He's not the strongest writer on the team.
4. She's, *how do I say this*, very direct with clients.
5. *How do I put this* delicately? Your car is not worth fixing.

**See also:** [what's the word](#whats-the-word), [let me think](#let-me-think), [I mean](#i-mean), [well](#well)

### just a second

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒʌst ə ˈsɛkənd/ &middot; *just a SEK-uhnd*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *just a sec*, *one sec*, *one second*, *give me a second*, *gimme a sec*

A short promise that the pause will be brief, used when you have to finish something before you can answer or move on.

1. *Just a second* — I'm almost done with this email.
2. "Can you come here?" "*One sec*!"
3. *Just a sec*, let me put you on speaker.
4. *Give me a second* to think about how to answer that.
5. *One second* and I'll have the total for you.

**See also:** [hold on](#hold-on), [hang on](#hang-on), [bear with me](#bear-with-me), [let's see](#lets-see)

### let me think

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛt mi ˈθɪŋk/ &middot; *let me THINK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *let me think for a second*, *let me think about it*

An explicit announcement that you are working out an answer rather than dodging the question. Said briskly it simply buys time; said slowly, it often signals that the answer is going to be no.

1. *Let me think* — I want to say it was 2019?
2. "What's the fastest route from here?" "*Let me think for a second*."
3. *Let me think* about it and I'll get back to you tomorrow.
4. *Let me think.* There were four of us at dinner, maybe five.
5. Hmm, *let me think* how to explain this without the jargon.

**See also:** [let's see](#lets-see), [hmm](#hmm), [hold on](#hold-on), [that's a good question](#thats-a-good-question), [where was I](#where-was-i)

### let's see

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɛts ˈsi/ &middot; *lets SEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *let me see*, *let's see here*, *let's see now*

A stall used while you scan, count, or search — the verbal equivalent of running a finger down a list. It is the standard thing Americans say out loud while looking something up.

1. *Let's see* — I've got Tuesday at ten or Thursday at two.
2. *Let me see*, where on earth did I park?
3. "How many people are coming?" "*Let's see* … eleven, counting the kids."
4. *Let's see here*, your account was opened back in March.
5. So we need milk, bread, and, *let's see*, coffee.

**See also:** [let me think](#let-me-think), [hmm](#hmm), [what's the word](#whats-the-word), [just a second](#just-a-second)

### that's a good question

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæts ə ˈɡʊd ˌkwɛstʃən/ &middot; *thats uh GOOD kwes-chun*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *good question*, *that's a great question*, *that's a fair question*

Buys several seconds while you assemble an answer, and flatters the asker on the way in. Everyone recognizes what it is doing, which is why it carries a faint smell of the press conference and the job interview — and why a bare *good question* sometimes means "I have no idea."

1. "How much would that cost us?" "*That's a good question* — let me run the numbers."
2. *Good question.* I honestly don't know.
3. She asked why the policy exists at all, and all I had was "*that's a good question*."
4. "Why is it spelled that way?" "*That's a great question*, and the answer is that nobody knows."
5. *That's a fair question*, and I'd rather answer it once I've seen the report.

**See also:** [let me think](#let-me-think), [let's see](#lets-see), [how do I put this](#how-do-i-put-this), [bear with me](#bear-with-me)

### wait

**Pronunciation:** /weɪt/ &middot; *WAYT*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (blunt when it cuts someone off mid-sentence)
**Variants:** *wait a second*, *wait a minute*, *wait wait wait*

A one-word brake on the conversation that stalls the other speaker while you catch up. In American speech it very often introduces a realization or a correction — *wait* rarely means "wait quietly," it means "something just registered."

1. *Wait* — I think I left my keys in the car.
2. *Wait a second*, weren't you supposed to be in Denver today?
3. "It's the third one on the left." "*Wait*, coming from which direction?"
4. *Wait wait wait*, start over from the part about the dog.
5. *Wait*, so you two already knew each other?

**See also:** [hold on](#hold-on), [hang on](#hang-on), [hmm](#hmm), [actually](#actually)

### what's the word

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ðə ˈwɝd/ &middot; *whuts thuh WURD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *what's the word for it*, *what do you call it*, *what's it called*

The standard American way to flag that you are hunting for a specific word and would welcome help finding it. It is usually dropped into the middle of a sentence, which then resumes once the word arrives.

1. It's one of those — *what's the word* — collapsible chairs.
2. He was being deliberately, *what's the word*, evasive.
3. *What do you call it*, the thing that holds the door open?
4. She had a look of, *what's the word*, resignation.
5. It's a kind of pasta, *what's it called*, orecchiette.

**See also:** [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [thingy](#thingy), [whatsit](#whatsit), [how do I put this](#how-do-i-put-this)

### where was I

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛr wəz ˈaɪ/ &middot; *wair wuz EYE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *where were we*, *now where was I*

Said after an interruption to find your place again in your own story or explanation. It quietly asks the listener to hand the thread back to you.

1. Sorry — *where was I*? Oh, right, the flight.
2. *Now where was I*? Ah yes, the third-quarter numbers.
3. "*Where were we*?" "You were telling us about your neighbor."
4. Give me a second, I lost my train of thought. *Where was I*?
5. Okay, *where was I* — the part where the car wouldn't start.

**See also:** [as I was saying](#as-i-was-saying), [anyway](#anyway), [so anyway](#so-anyway), [let me think](#let-me-think)

## Openers, shifters, and closers

*Ordinary words doing a second job: starting a turn, changing the subject, checking that you are still being followed, and winding things down.*

### actually

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæktʃuəli/ (also /ˈæktʃəli/) &middot; *AK-choo-uh-lee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral

Flags that what follows corrects, contradicts, or gently surprises — either fixing what was just said or introducing a fact the listener would not expect. At the front of a sentence it can sound corrective, so Americans often cushion it with *well* or a smile.

1. *Actually*, the meeting got moved to Thursday.
2. "You've never been to Chicago, right?" "*Actually*, I lived there for two years."
3. I thought it would be a nightmare, but it was *actually* pretty easy.
4. *Actually*, on second thought, I'll have the salmon.
5. That's not a bad idea, *actually*.

**See also:** [I mean](#i-mean), [well](#well), [right](#right), [basically](#basically), [technically](#technically)

### alright

**Pronunciation:** /ɔlˈraɪt/ &middot; *awl-RITE*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (the two-word spelling *all right* is the one for formal prose)
**Variants:** *all right*, *aight*, *a'ight*

A resetting marker that closes off what came before and starts the next thing — the sound of a teacher beginning class or a driver putting the car in gear. With rising pitch at the end of a sentence it becomes a check: *alright?* means "are we good so far?"

1. *Alright*, let's get started.
2. *Alright*, so the first thing you'll need is a screwdriver.
3. "*Alright*, everybody up — we're leaving in ten minutes."
4. We'll meet at the north entrance at six, *alright*?
5. *Alright*, I'll let you go — say hi to your mom for me.

**See also:** [okay](#okay), [right](#right), [so](#so), [now](#now)

### anyhow

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌhaʊ/ &middot; *EN-ee-how*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (slightly dated; most common in the South and Midwest)
**Variants:** *anyhoo*, *anywho*, *any old how*

An older, folksier twin of *anyway*, used to drop a digression and get back to the point or to wrap a story up. Younger speakers use it far less often than *anyway*.

1. *Anyhow*, that's how we ended up with three cats.
2. It rained all weekend. *Anyhow*, how was your trip?
3. I don't know what he was thinking. *Anyhow*, it's done now.
4. *Anyhow*, I should let you go.
5. She never did call back. *Anyhow*, no harm done.

**See also:** [anyway](#anyway), [so anyway](#so-anyway), [well](#well)

### anyway

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌweɪ/ &middot; *EN-ee-way*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (*anyways* is very informal and widely treated as nonstandard)
**Variants:** *anyways*

The workhorse of topic management: it ends a digression and returns to the main thread, or signals that you are winding the conversation down. Left hanging at the end of a turn, it means roughly "so that's that."

1. *Anyway*, back to what I was saying about the budget.
2. *Anyway*, it's getting late — I should head out.
3. The food was mediocre and the service was slow. *Anyway*.
4. *Anyways*, we ended up driving instead of flying.
5. He may never forgive me. *Anyway*, I did what I had to do.

**See also:** [anyhow](#anyhow), [so anyway](#so-anyway), [well](#well), [where was I](#where-was-i)

### basically

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbeɪsɪkli/ &middot; *BAY-sik-lee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral in its "here is the short version" sense; informal as a filler

Announces a simplified version — the short answer, with the details filed off. In casual speech it has drifted into a near-filler that adds little beyond "here comes my summary."

1. *Basically*, the engine needs to be replaced.
2. "How does the refund work?" "*Basically*, you have thirty days to change your mind."
3. *Basically* what happened is she got a better offer.
4. We spent *basically* the whole weekend cleaning.
5. He's *basically* running the department at this point.

**See also:** [I mean](#i-mean), [kind of](#kind-of), [pretty much](#pretty-much), [literally](#literally), [so](#so)

### by the way

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbaɪ ðə ˈweɪ/ &middot; *by thuh WAY*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (the abbreviation *BTW* is informal, for email and texts only)
**Variants:** *BTW*, *btw*, *by the by*

Introduces a side remark that is not part of the current topic, often a small piece of news the speaker does not want to make a production of. The written abbreviation *BTW* is standard in email and texts.

1. *By the way*, your package came while you were out.
2. I ran into Marcus yesterday — *by the way*, he says hi.
3. *By the way*, do you still have my ladder?
4. The rent is going up in January, *by the way*.
5. *BTW*, I moved our call to three.

**See also:** [speaking of which](#speaking-of-which), [that reminds me](#that-reminds-me), [anyway](#anyway), [now](#now), [come to think of it](#come-to-think-of-it)

### come to think of it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌm tə ˈθɪŋk əv ɪt/ &middot; *kum tuh THINK uhv it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *now that I think about it*, *come to think about it*, *thinking about it*

Marks something that has surfaced in your head while you were saying something else — usually a detail that quietly changes the picture. Unlike *that reminds me*, what surfaces is still attached to the point at hand instead of opening a new topic.

1. *Come to think of it*, I haven't seen him since the wedding.
2. She never did answer that email, *come to think of it*.
3. *Come to think of it*, the car was making that same noise last week.
4. "Was anyone else in the building?" "*Now that I think about it*, the cleaning crew was still there."
5. You two would probably get along, *come to think of it*.

**See also:** [that reminds me](#that-reminds-me), [speaking of which](#speaking-of-which), [by the way](#by-the-way), [actually](#actually)

### granted

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡræntɪd/ &middot; *GRAN-tid*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *granted, though*, *admittedly*, *I'll grant you that*

Hands the other side one point before you argue against it, so that the rest of your position looks even-handed. It is nearly always followed, sooner or later, by a *but*, and standing alone as a full turn it is a grudging half-concession.

1. *Granted*, the first hour is slow, but it's worth staying for the ending.
2. He's the youngest person on the staff, *granted*, and he still outworks everyone.
3. *Granted*, I only heard one side of it.
4. "It's cheaper to fly out of the other airport." "*Granted.* It's also two hours away."
5. *Granted*, we've blown a deadline before — that's exactly why I'm asking now.

**See also:** [that said](#that-said), [to be fair](#to-be-fair), [actually](#actually), [well](#well)

### here's the thing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrz ðə ˈθɪŋ/ &middot; *heerz thuh THING*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *the thing is*, *here's the thing though*, *the thing about it is*

Announces the complication — the fact that explains why the obvious answer will not work. It promises the listener the real story, which makes it a favorite of people who are about to deliver bad news gently.

1. *Here's the thing*: we already paid for the tickets.
2. I'd love to help, but *here's the thing* — I'm out of town all week.
3. *The thing is*, nobody ever asked the customers.
4. "Just call and cancel." "*Here's the thing*, the deposit isn't refundable."
5. *Here's the thing though* — she was right about the timeline.

**See also:** [look](#look), [see](#see), [I mean](#i-mean), [that said](#that-said), [well](#well)

### in other words

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn ˈʌðɚ ˌwɝdz/ &middot; *in UTH-er WURDZ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (at home in writing as well as speech)
**Variants:** *or rather*, *put another way*, *which is to say*

Restates what was just said in plainer or blunter terms, either to make it clearer or to drag the real meaning into the open. It is the neutral, printable cousin of *I mean* — and turned flatly on someone else's explanation, it becomes an accusation.

1. *In other words*, we're starting over.
2. The lease runs through August — *in other words*, you have four more months.
3. "The role is being restructured." "*In other words*, I'm fired?"
4. *Put another way*, every dollar we save here costs us two next year.
5. She wants the schedule and the budget; *in other words*, the whole project.

**See also:** [I mean](#i-mean), [basically](#basically), [so to speak](#so-to-speak), [that said](#that-said)

### listen

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪsən/ &middot; *LIS-uhn*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (can sound stern or confrontational)
**Variants:** *listen up*, *now listen*

Demands the listener's full attention before something important, blunt, or final. It carries more edge than *look*, so tone does the work: a warm *listen* is confiding, a flat one is a warning.

1. *Listen*, I'm not going to pretend this is easy.
2. *Listen*, you've got nothing to apologize for.
3. "*Listen up*, everybody — buses leave at eight sharp."
4. *Listen*, if you don't want to go, just say so.
5. *Now listen*, I've told you three times already.

**See also:** [look](#look), [see](#see), [okay](#okay)

### long story short

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɔŋ ˌstɔri ˈʃɔrt/ &middot; *long story SHORT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *to make a long story short*, *long story*, *to cut a long story short (chiefly British)*

Announces that you are skipping the middle of a story and going straight to how it came out. Speakers often reach for it only after they have already talked for five minutes, which is half the joke of it.

1. *Long story short*, we missed the flight and drove all night.
2. He talked for ten minutes and then said, "*Long story short*, we're out of money."
3. *To make a long story short*, the roof is fine and the basement isn't.
4. "How'd the meeting go?" "*Long story short*, they want a redesign."
5. *Long story short*: don't lend him your car.

**See also:** [anyway](#anyway), [so anyway](#so-anyway), [basically](#basically), [yada yada](#yada-yada)

### look

**Pronunciation:** /lʊk/ &middot; *LOOK*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (can sound impatient)
**Variants:** *now look*, *look here (dated)*

Marks that you are cutting through the back-and-forth to state your real position. Politicians and managers lean on it constantly; used too often, it reads as impatience.

1. *Look*, we've all made mistakes.
2. *Look*, the numbers just don't support it.
3. "*Look*, I'll be honest with you — we can't match that price."
4. *Look*, can we talk about this after dinner?
5. *Now look*, nobody is blaming you.

**See also:** [listen](#listen), [see](#see), [here's the thing](#heres-the-thing), [I mean](#i-mean), [well](#well)

### now

**Pronunciation:** /naʊ/ &middot; *NOW*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *now then*, *and now*

Moves the conversation on to the next step or the next item, with no reference to time at all. It also softens a coming correction, as in *Now, that's not quite what I said*.

1. *Now*, where would you like to start?
2. We've covered the budget. *Now*, about the schedule.
3. *Now*, I'm not saying she was wrong.
4. *Now then*, who wants dessert?
5. Add the eggs one at a time. *Now*, slowly fold in the flour.

**See also:** [so](#so), [then](#then), [okay](#okay), [alright](#alright)

### okay

**Pronunciation:** /oʊˈkeɪ/ &middot; *oh-KAY*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *OK*, *O.K.*, *'kay*, *mkay*, *okey-dokey*

The all-purpose transition: it closes the previous topic and opens the next one, and with rising pitch it checks that the listener is still with you. Note that a drawn-out *okaaay* signals doubt, not agreement.

1. *Okay*, let's take it from the top.
2. *Okay*, so what you want to do is press and hold the button.
3. Turn left at the light, *okay*? Then it's the second driveway.
4. "*Okay*, everyone, eyes up here."
5. *Okay* … that's not what I expected to hear.
6. *'Kay*, see you at six.

**See also:** [alright](#alright), [so](#so), [right](#right), [now](#now)

### right

**Pronunciation:** /raɪt/ &middot; *RITE*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *right?*, *right then*, *all right*

Two jobs. (1) As an opener it marks a fresh start or a return to business. (2) Tacked onto the end of a clause with rising pitch, it checks that the listener agrees or is still following — though repeated *right?* tags can sound pushy in an argument.

1. *Right*, let's figure out who's driving.
2. So we're meeting at the office first, *right*?
3. You've done this before, *right*?
4. *Right* — first question: who's the audience?
5. It was raining, *right*, and none of us had an umbrella.
6. "The blue one?" "*Right*."

**See also:** [okay](#okay), [alright](#alright), [you know](#you-know), [so](#so)

### see

**Pronunciation:** /si/ &middot; *SEE*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *see, the thing is*, *y'see*

Introduces the explanation the listener has been waiting for, often a little defensively — *see* frames what follows as the reason things are the way they are.

1. *See*, the problem is that nobody told the client.
2. I would have called, *y'see*, but my phone was dead.
3. *See*, that's exactly what I was afraid of.
4. It's not that I don't want to go. *See*, I already promised my sister.
5. *See*, this is why we keep a spare key under the mat.

**See also:** [you see](#you-see), [look](#look), [I mean](#i-mean), [listen](#listen)

### so

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ/ &middot; *SOH*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *sooo*

The most common way Americans open a turn: it introduces a topic, launches a question, or draws a conclusion from what came before. A stretched *sooo* at the start of a sentence usually means awkward news is coming.

1. *So*, how did the interview go?
2. *So* I'm standing in line at the bank, and my phone rings.
3. The store closes at six, *so* — we should probably head out.
4. *Sooo*, slight change of plans.
5. *So*, to summarize: we need two more weeks and one more engineer.
6. "*So*? What did she say?"

**See also:** [so anyway](#so-anyway), [well](#well), [okay](#okay), [anyway](#anyway)

### so anyway

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ ˈɛniˌweɪ/ &middot; *so EN-ee-way*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *so anyways*, *anyway so*

A doubled marker used to abandon a side story and get back on track — the *so* pushes forward while the *anyway* dismisses the digression, which is exactly what a rambling storyteller needs.

1. *So anyway*, we finally got the car started around midnight.
2. *So anyway*, that's why I don't eat at that place anymore.
3. *So anyways*, she calls me the next morning like nothing happened.
4. Long story. *So anyway*, how's your mom doing?
5. *So anyway*, where do you want to eat tonight?

**See also:** [anyway](#anyway), [so](#so), [anyhow](#anyhow), [where was I](#where-was-i)

### so yeah

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈjɛə/ &middot; *soh YEH-uh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *so yeah, anyway*, *and yeah*, *so, yeah*

Closes out a turn that has run out of steam: the point has been made, there is no tidy ending available, and *so yeah* hands the floor back. It is the spoken equivalent of a shrug at the end of a paragraph, and it is everywhere in the speech of Americans under about forty.

1. It's a long commute, but the pay is better. *So yeah*.
2. We tried it, it didn't work, *so yeah* — back to the drawing board.
3. That's pretty much the whole plan, *so yeah*, let me know what you think.
4. She hasn't called me since. *So yeah*, I'd say we're done.
5. *So yeah* — that was my Tuesday.

**See also:** [anyway](#anyway), [so](#so), [you know](#you-know), [I guess](#i-guess)

### speaking of which

**Pronunciation:** /ˌspikɪŋ əv ˈwɪtʃ/ &middot; *SPEE-king uhv WICH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *speaking of*, *speaking of that*, *which reminds me*

Hooks a new topic onto the one just mentioned, so that changing the subject looks like a natural step instead of an interruption. Americans use it to raise something they have been waiting all conversation for an opening to bring up.

1. *Speaking of which*, did you ever hear back about the apartment?
2. We're driving up Friday. *Speaking of which*, do you still need a ride?
3. "Traffic was awful this morning." "*Speaking of*, I think your registration expired."
4. *Speaking of which*, I still owe you twenty dollars.
5. The budget's due Monday — *speaking of which*, I need your numbers by Thursday.

**See also:** [that reminds me](#that-reminds-me), [by the way](#by-the-way), [anyway](#anyway), [now](#now)

### that reminds me

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt rɪˈmaɪndz mi/ &middot; *that ruh-MYNDZ mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *which reminds me*, *oh, that reminds me*, *before I forget*

Credits the other person's remark with knocking loose a subject of your own. Like *speaking of which* it softens a change of topic, but it points at something you had genuinely forgotten until that second.

1. *That reminds me* — your mother called while you were out.
2. Oh, *that reminds me*, I need to renew my passport before June.
3. "The Hendersons are moving." "*That reminds me*, we never sent them a card."
4. He mentioned the storm, *which reminds me* — is your power back on?
5. *That reminds me*, we're almost out of coffee filters.

**See also:** [speaking of which](#speaking-of-which), [by the way](#by-the-way), [anyway](#anyway), [now](#now)

### that said

**Pronunciation:** /ðæt ˈsɛd/ &middot; *that SED*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *that being said*, *having said that*, *all that said*

Concedes the point you have just made and then pushes back against it. It is the courteous way to say *but*, and unlike most of this file it is at home in writing as well as speech.

1. The price is high. *That said*, you're not going to find another one like it.
2. I'm not thrilled about the hours. *That being said*, I'd take the job.
3. She's new here. *That said*, she should have caught this.
4. It rained the entire week — *that said*, we'd go back tomorrow.
5. *Having said that*, nobody is forcing you to sign anything today.

**See also:** [granted](#granted), [actually](#actually), [anyway](#anyway), [well](#well), [I mean](#i-mean)

### then

**Pronunciation:** /ðɛn/ &middot; *THEN — voiced th, as in this*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *well then*, *okay then*, *right then*

Tacked onto the end of a clause, it draws a conclusion from what has just been established: "given all that." At the front, paired with *well* or *okay*, it closes a decision out.

1. You're all set, *then*.
2. "I can't make Friday." "Monday it is, *then*."
3. *Well then*, I guess we're driving.
4. So it isn't broken, *then*?
5. *Okay then*, let's call it a night.

**See also:** [so](#so), [now](#now), [okay](#okay), [well](#well), [alright](#alright)

### well

**Pronunciation:** /wɛl/ &middot; *WELL*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *welll*, *well now*, *well then*

The classic hesitation opener. It buys a beat before an answer and warns that the answer will not be a simple yes — a reservation, a qualification, or some bad news is coming. Stretched out, *welllll* is almost a polite no all by itself.

1. *Well*, that's one way to look at it.
2. "Did you like it?" "*Well* … it wasn't really my thing."
3. *Well*, I suppose we could try again next month.
4. *Well*, look who finally showed up.
5. *Well*, to be honest, I'd rather stay home.
6. *Welll*, technically the deadline was yesterday.

**See also:** [um](#um), [so](#so), [actually](#actually), [hmm](#hmm), [I mean](#i-mean)

### yeah no

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjɛə ˌnoʊ/ &middot; *YEH-noh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *no yeah*, *yeah no for sure*, *nah yeah*

A distinctly American pair in which the first word acknowledges what you just said and the second delivers the actual answer: *yeah no* means "I hear you, but no," and *no yeah* means "I hear your doubt, but yes." Speakers are usually unaware they are doing it.

1. "Want to come to the thing on Saturday?" "*Yeah no*, I'm gonna pass."
2. *No yeah*, it's a great movie — you should definitely see it.
3. *Yeah no*, I hear you, but the schedule isn't moving.
4. *Yeah no for sure*, I'll send it over tonight.
5. "You didn't hate it?" "*No yeah*, I actually liked it a lot."

**See also:** [right](#right), [okay](#okay), [I mean](#i-mean), [well](#well)

### you know what

**Pronunciation:** /jə ˈnoʊ ˌwʌt/ &middot; *yuh NOH wut*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *y'know what*, *you know what, never mind*, *tell you what*

Announces a decision or an opinion the speaker has arrived at just this second, usually with a change of direction behind it. Despite the shared words it is not the filler *you know* — it is a small drumroll, and what follows is the whole point of the sentence.

1. *You know what*? Let's just take my car.
2. *You know what*, forget I said anything.
3. *You know what* — I think she was right the whole time.
4. "We could push it to next week." "*You know what*? That would help a lot."
5. *You know what*, I'm going to bed.

**See also:** [you know](#you-know), [actually](#actually), [okay](#okay), [alright](#alright)

### you see

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈsi/ &middot; *yoo SEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (can sound condescending)
**Variants:** *ya see*, *don't you see*

Marks the explanation of something the listener did not know, presenting it as the key that makes everything make sense. Overused it turns into lecturing, which is why it is so common in teaching and in period fiction.

1. The bridge was out, *you see*, so we had to go the long way around.
2. *You see*, the two accounts were never actually linked.
3. He'd already sold the house, *you see*. Nobody had told her.
4. *Ya see*, that's where most people get it wrong.
5. It isn't the cost, *you see* — it's the timing.

**See also:** [see](#see), [look](#look), [I mean](#i-mean), [listen](#listen)

## Hedges, softeners, and vagueness tags

*Words that lower the temperature of a claim, blur a number, or hand the rest of the sentence to the listener to finish.*

### and so on

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈsoʊ ɑn/ &middot; *uhn SOH on*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (the one list-closer in this group that survives in writing)
**Variants:** *and so forth*, *and so on and so forth*, *etc.*

Closes a list on the understanding that the pattern keeps going. It is the plain, printable version of *and stuff* and *and whatnot*; doubling it into *and so on and so forth* usually signals impatience with the list rather than a longer one.

1. Bring a sleeping bag, a flashlight, bug spray, *and so on*.
2. The form wants your address, your previous address, *and so forth*.
3. We went through the whole routine — deep breaths, count to ten, *and so on*.
4. He had an excuse for everything: traffic, the weather, his alarm, *and so on and so forth*.
5. It's the usual argument about who pays and who drives *and so on*.

**See also:** [and stuff](#and-stuff), [and whatnot](#and-whatnot), [or whatever](#or-whatever), [yada yada](#yada-yada), [blah blah blah](#blah-blah-blah)

### and stuff

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈstʌf/ &middot; *uhn STUFF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *and stuff like that*, *and all that*, *and everything*, *and all*

A tag that closes a list without finishing it, implying more of the same. It is casual to the point of sloppiness — fine with friends, weak in a job interview.

1. We just hung out, watched a movie *and stuff*.
2. She's into hiking and camping *and all that*.
3. I have to do laundry *and stuff* before we leave.
4. They gave us pens and notebooks *and stuff like that*.
5. He was talking about interest rates *and stuff* — I tuned out.

**See also:** [or something](#or-something), [or whatever](#or-whatever), [and whatnot](#and-whatnot), [and so on](#and-so-on), [like](#like), [you know](#you-know)

### and whatnot

**Pronunciation:** /ən ˈwʌtnɑt/ &middot; *uhn WUT-not*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (folksy; reads as older or small-town)
**Variants:** *and what have you*, *and such*, *and whatnot else*

Closes an unfinished list the way *and stuff* does, but with a faintly old-fashioned flavor. It is common in the speech of older Americans, in the trades, and anywhere a list is too tedious to finish.

1. She brought paper plates, napkins, *and whatnot*.
2. We spent the whole morning filling out forms *and whatnot*.
3. He does drywall, tile, *and whatnot* — whatever the job needs.
4. There's a box of cables *and what have you* out in the garage.
5. They talked about the weather, the neighbors, *and whatnot*, for a solid hour before anyone brought up the money.

**See also:** [and stuff](#and-stuff), [and so on](#and-so-on), [or whatever](#or-whatever), [or something](#or-something), [yada yada](#yada-yada)

### I dunno

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ dəˈnoʊ/ &middot; *eye duh-NO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *dunno*, *iunno*, *I donno*

The reduced spoken form of "I don't know," used less as a real admission of ignorance than as a hedge that softens an opinion you are about to give anyway. Written out, it deliberately marks casual speech.

1. *I dunno*, it just doesn't feel right to me.
2. "Where should we eat?" "*I dunno*, you pick."
3. *I dunno*, maybe he was just having a bad day.
4. It's, *I dunno*, forty minutes north of the city?
5. "*Dunno*," she said, without looking up.

**See also:** [I guess](#i-guess), [I feel like](#i-feel-like), [kind of](#kind-of), [or something](#or-something)

### I feel like

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈfil ˌlaɪk/ &middot; *eye FEEL like*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I feel like maybe*, *I kind of feel like*

Frames an opinion as a personal impression rather than a claim about the world, which makes it harder to argue with and easier to walk back. In casual American speech it has largely taken over the ground that *I think* used to hold.

1. *I feel like* we've had this exact conversation before.
2. *I feel like* she wasn't really listening to a word of it.
3. Honestly, *I feel like* the first draft was better.
4. "Should we wait for him?" "*I feel like* we should just go."
5. *I feel like* nobody actually reads these emails.

**See also:** [I guess](#i-guess), [I mean](#i-mean), [kind of](#kind-of), [to be honest](#to-be-honest)

### I guess

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈɡɛs/ &middot; *eye GESS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I guess so*, *guess so*, *I suppose*

Marks reluctant or low-confidence agreement — you accept the conclusion without any enthusiasm for it. At the end of a sentence it drains the certainty out of whatever came before.

1. *I guess* we could leave a little earlier.
2. "Is that everything?" "*I guess so*."
3. It's fine, *I guess*. I just expected more.
4. *I guess* I should have read the fine print.
5. She's the most qualified, *I guess*, but I'd still interview the others.

**See also:** [I dunno](#i-dunno), [I feel like](#i-feel-like), [I want to say](#i-want-to-say), [kind of](#kind-of), [sort of](#sort-of)

### I mean

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈmin/ &middot; *eye MEEN*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I mean like*

Repairs, restates, or braces what you just said: you are rewording for accuracy, or you are about to defend an opinion. In fast speech it has also become a plain turn-opening filler with no repair work to do.

1. It's expensive — *I mean*, not outrageous, but more than I wanted to spend.
2. *I mean*, what was he supposed to do?
3. She's my cousin. *I mean*, technically my second cousin.
4. *I mean*, I'd help, but I'll be out of town that week.
5. He was upset. *I mean*, he was really upset.

**See also:** [like](#like), [you know](#you-know), [actually](#actually), [well](#well), [see](#see), [in other words](#in-other-words)

### I want to say

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈwɑnt tə ˌseɪ/ (casually /aɪ ˈwɑnə ˌseɪ/) &middot; *eye WAHNT tuh say, casually eye WAH-nuh say*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I wanna say*, *I want to say something like*

Introduces a half-remembered fact — a year, a price, a name — and marks it as the speaker's best guess rather than a claim. It is usually delivered with rising, question-like intonation, and it openly invites correction.

1. *I want to say* it was 2015, but don't quote me.
2. "How far is it?" "*I want to say* forty miles?"
3. Her sister's name is — *I wanna say* Diane?
4. *I want to say* there were about a dozen of us at that dinner.
5. Rent was, *I want to say*, eleven hundred a month back then.

**See also:** [I guess](#i-guess), [I dunno](#i-dunno), [or so](#or-so), [let me think](#let-me-think), [-ish](#-ish)

### if that makes sense

**Pronunciation:** /ɪf ðæt ˌmeɪks ˈsɛns/ &middot; *if that makes SENSE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *if that makes any sense*, *does that make sense*

A closing hedge that admits your explanation may have come out muddled and invites the listener to say so. It is polite self-deprecation rather than a real question.

1. It's more of a feeling than an argument, *if that makes sense*.
2. I want something structured but not rigid, *if that makes sense*.
3. She's not unfriendly, just very private — *if that makes any sense*.
4. We're rebuilding it while it's still running, *if that makes sense*.
5. So it's a hobby that pays for itself, *if that makes sense*.

**See also:** [you know](#you-know), [I mean](#i-mean), [kind of](#kind-of), [sort of](#sort-of)

### -ish

**Pronunciation:** /ɪʃ/ &middot; *ISH*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal

A suffix that has broken loose and become a standalone hedge meaning "approximately" or "not exactly." As a one-word answer it softens a time, a number, or a lukewarm yes.

1. "Are you free around seven?" "*Ish.* Make it seven fifteen."
2. Dinner's at eight, *ish* — don't stress about the traffic.
3. There were fifty people there, *ish*.
4. The house is Victorian, *ish* — somebody tacked a wing on it in the seventies.
5. "Is the draft done?" "*Ish*. I still have to proofread it."

**See also:** [kind of](#kind-of), [sort of](#sort-of), [or so](#or-so), [I guess](#i-guess)

### kind of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkaɪnd əv/ (also /ˈkaɪndə/) &middot; *KINE-duhv, casually KINE-duh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *kinda*, *kind of like*

Softens a claim to "somewhat" or "not entirely," lowering the temperature of an adjective, a verb, or a whole statement. It is one of the most common American hedges and is unmistakably casual on the page.

1. I *kind of* wanted to stay home tonight.
2. It's *kinda* cold in here — do you mind if I close the window?
3. "Do you like it?" "*Kind of.* Not as much as the first one."
4. He *kind of* implied that the job was already mine.
5. That's *kinda* the whole point.

**See also:** [sort of](#sort-of), [-ish](#-ish), [pretty much](#pretty-much), [like](#like), [I guess](#i-guess)

### like

**Pronunciation:** /laɪk/ &middot; *LIKE*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal

Three filler jobs, all separate from the verb *like*. (1) A hesitation marker that holds the floor. (2) An approximator meaning "about." (3) A quotative with *be*, introducing speech or thought, as in *he was like, "no way."* All three are extremely common in American speech and all three are out of place in formal settings.

1. It was, *like*, the best day of the whole trip.
2. There were *like* forty people crammed into that apartment.
3. And she was *like*, "You're seriously not coming?"
4. It's not far — *like*, twenty minutes?
5. He just, *like*, walked out in the middle of the meeting.
6. I'm *like* ninety percent sure I locked the door.

**See also:** [you know](#you-know), [I mean](#i-mean), [um](#um), [or something](#or-something), [kind of](#kind-of)

### literally

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪtɚəli/ (also /ˈlɪtrəli/) &middot; *LIT-uh-ruh-lee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal as an intensifier; neutral in its original "to the letter" sense

Once strictly "in the exact sense," now used mostly as an intensifier for something figurative, and in the loosest speech as a near-empty emphasis filler. The intensifier use is ordinary in casual American speech and still draws complaints in formal writing.

1. I *literally* just walked in the door — let me call you back.
2. My phone *literally* died in the middle of the call.
3. There were *literally* a thousand emails waiting for me.
4. She *literally* cannot say no to anyone.
5. We showed up *literally* wearing the same jacket.
6. He took the instructions *literally* and painted the whole wall. (the original sense)

**See also:** [basically](#basically), [actually](#actually), [like](#like), [so to speak](#so-to-speak), [technically](#technically)

### more or less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɔr ɚ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *mor er LESS — the middle word reduces to a plain r-colored vowel*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

Marks a statement as true in the main but not in every detail — "approximately," "in effect." It hedges numbers, descriptions, and whole conclusions, and unlike most of this file it is respectable enough for a written report.

1. The job is done, *more or less*.
2. We agreed on the terms, *more or less*.
3. It's *more or less* the same recipe my grandmother used.
4. "Is that everyone?" "*More or less* — two people are calling in."
5. He runs the place, *more or less*, whatever his title says.

**See also:** [pretty much](#pretty-much), [kind of](#kind-of), [sort of](#sort-of), [or so](#or-so)

### or so

**Pronunciation:** /ɚ ˈsoʊ/ &middot; *er SOH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *or thereabouts*, *or so ago*

Follows a number, a time, or a distance to mark it as an estimate. It is tighter than *or something*: *or so* hedges the quantity and nothing else, which is why it survives in careful writing.

1. It's a mile *or so* from here.
2. Give it twenty minutes *or so* and check it again.
3. There were thirty people *or so* in the room.
4. She's been gone a week *or so*.
5. The whole repair came to four hundred dollars *or so*.

**See also:** [-ish](#-ish), [more or less](#more-or-less), [or something](#or-something), [kind of](#kind-of)

### or something

**Pronunciation:** /ɚ ˈsʌmθɪŋ/ &middot; *er SUM-thing*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *or something like that*, *or somethin'*

Tacked onto an uncertain detail to mark it as approximate — you are giving the gist, not the fact. It also softens a suggestion into something the listener can easily turn down.

1. He's a consultant *or something* — I've never really understood his job.
2. We could grab coffee *or something* this week.
3. It cost two hundred dollars *or something like that*.
4. She said she was going to Portland *or something*.
5. Did you leave it in the car *or something*?

**See also:** [or whatever](#or-whatever), [and stuff](#and-stuff), [kind of](#kind-of), [I dunno](#i-dunno)

### or whatever

**Pronunciation:** /ɚ wəˈtɛvɚ/ &middot; *er wuht-EV-er*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (can sound dismissive)
**Variants:** *or whatever it is*, *or whatever you call it*

Closes an option or a list with a shrug, signaling that the exact item does not matter to you. Depending on tone it is either easygoing or openly indifferent, so it is risky when the details matter to the person you are talking to.

1. Bring chips, soda, *or whatever* — it's not a fancy party.
2. We can meet Tuesday, Wednesday, *or whatever* works for you.
3. He's studying economics *or whatever* — some business thing.
4. Just leave it on the porch *or whatever*.
5. She wanted the corner office, the title, *or whatever* it is people want.

**See also:** [or something](#or-something), [and stuff](#and-stuff), [and whatnot](#and-whatnot), [blah blah blah](#blah-blah-blah)

### pretty much

**Pronunciation:** /ˌprɪti ˈmʌtʃ/ &middot; *PRIT-ee MUCH — the t is a quick flap, like the d in ladder*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *just about*, *pretty much it*

Means "almost entirely," and works as a hedge that keeps a strong statement from being an absolute one. Americans use it constantly, often where a British speaker would reach for *more or less*.

1. That's *pretty much* everything I know about it.
2. The season is *pretty much* over at this point.
3. "Are you packed?" "*Pretty much*."
4. He *pretty much* admitted he'd forgotten the whole thing.
5. We eat the same dinner *pretty much* every night.

**See also:** [more or less](#more-or-less), [basically](#basically), [kind of](#kind-of), [-ish](#-ish)

### so to speak

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ tə ˈspik/ &middot; *soh tuh SPEEK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *as it were*, *in a manner of speaking*

Flags the words just used as figurative, borrowed, or not quite the right ones, so the listener does not take them at face value. It is the exact opposite of *literally*, and it is comfortable in writing as well as speech.

1. He jumped ship, *so to speak*, about a month before the merger.
2. We're flying blind, *so to speak*, until the survey comes back.
3. She's the mayor of that whole neighborhood, *so to speak*.
4. The account has been in a coma, *so to speak*, since March.
5. I'm the referee, *so to speak*, whenever those two get going.

**See also:** [literally](#literally), [kind of](#kind-of), [more or less](#more-or-less), [if that makes sense](#if-that-makes-sense)

### sort of

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɔrt əv/ (also /ˈsɔrtə/) &middot; *SORT-uhv, casually SORT-uh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *sorta*, *sort of like*

The near-twin of *kind of*: it hedges a description down to "partly" or "in a way." The difference is slight — *sort of* feels marginally more bookish, *kind of* more all-purpose — and either one can hedge a single word or a whole sentence.

1. I *sort of* knew this was coming.
2. It's *sorta* like tennis, but on a smaller court.
3. "Are you two dating?" "*Sort of.* It's complicated."
4. The whole trip was *sort of* a disaster.
5. He *sort of* apologized, if you can call it that.

**See also:** [kind of](#kind-of), [-ish](#-ish), [I guess](#i-guess), [like](#like)

### technically

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtɛknɪkli/ &middot; *TEK-nik-lee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (as a lone objection it sounds pedantic)
**Variants:** *technically speaking*, *well, technically*, *technically, yes*

Marks a statement as true by the strict letter of the rule while hinting that the spirit of the rule points somewhere else. It hedges and corrects at the same time, and aimed at someone else's sentence it is a small act of pedantry that Americans find funny and irritating in about equal measure.

1. *Technically*, the store closed five minutes ago.
2. She's *technically* my stepsister, but we grew up in the same house.
3. "You're late." "*Technically* I'm on time — the meeting starts at ten after."
4. *Technically speaking*, none of this is in my job description.
5. It's *technically* legal, which is not the same thing as a good idea.

**See also:** [actually](#actually), [literally](#literally), [kind of](#kind-of), [well](#well)

### to be fair

**Pronunciation:** /tə bi ˈfɛr/ &middot; *tuh bee FAIR*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *in fairness*, *to be fair to him*, *TBF*, *tbf*

Introduces the point that cuts against what you have just said, usually in defense of somebody who is not in the room. It advertises that you are being even-handed on purpose — and used constantly, especially online, it starts to read as reflexive contrarianism.

1. *To be fair*, nobody told him the deadline had moved.
2. The food took forever, but *to be fair*, they were packed.
3. *In fairness*, she did offer to pay for half.
4. "He never proofreads anything." "*To be fair*, neither do you."
5. I complained the entire drive — *to be fair*, it was six hours.

**See also:** [to be honest](#to-be-honest), [granted](#granted), [that said](#that-said), [I mean](#i-mean)

### to be honest

**Pronunciation:** /tə bi ˈɑnəst/ &middot; *tuh bee ON-ist — the h is silent*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (the abbreviation *TBH* is for texting only)
**Variants:** *honestly*, *to be perfectly honest*, *if I'm being honest*, *TBH*, *tbh*

Braces the listener for a blunt or unflattering opinion and asks in advance to be forgiven for it. It does not really claim that everything before it was dishonest, though a listener who wants to take it that way can.

1. *To be honest*, I'd rather not go.
2. The first album was better, *to be honest*.
3. *To be perfectly honest*, I forgot you were coming.
4. "What did you think?" "*Honestly*? It needs work."
5. *TBH* I never finished the book.

**See also:** [to be fair](#to-be-fair), [I mean](#i-mean), [I feel like](#i-feel-like), [look](#look), [well](#well)

### you know

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈnoʊ/ (also /jəˈnoʊ/) &middot; *yoo NOH, casually yuh-NO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *y'know*, *ya know*, *know what I mean*, *you know what I'm saying*

Invites the listener to fill in the rest, either because the point is obvious or because you would rather not spell it out. In heavy use it becomes a pure filler that shows up every few words, and that is the version people get criticized for.

1. It's just been one of those weeks, *you know*?
2. He's, *y'know*, not exactly a people person.
3. *You know*, I was thinking the exact same thing.
4. She wants to travel more while she still can, *you know*?
5. It's not that I'm mad, it's just — *you know*.
6. *Know what I mean*? Nobody wants to be the one to bring it up.

**See also:** [I mean](#i-mean), [like](#like), [see](#see), [you see](#you-see), [and stuff](#and-stuff)

## Word-search and placeholder terms

*What Americans say when the actual word will not come: stand-ins for an object, a person, or a stretch of talk not worth repeating.*

### blah blah blah

**Pronunciation:** /ˌblɑ blɑ ˈblɑ/ &middot; *blah blah BLAH*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *blah blah*, *blah-blah-blah*

Stands in for talk the speaker considers predictable, tedious, or not worth repeating. It dismisses the content rather than the listener — unless you use it while someone is still talking.

1. The email was all "we value your feedback," *blah blah blah*.
2. He gave the usual speech about teamwork, *blah blah blah*.
3. Terms and conditions, privacy policy, *blah blah blah* — just click accept.
4. She started in on how busy she's been, *blah blah blah*.
5. *Blah blah blah* — get to the part where somebody fixes it.

**See also:** [yada yada](#yada-yada), [or whatever](#or-whatever), [and stuff](#and-stuff)

### doohickey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈduˌhɪki/ &middot; *DOO-hik-ee*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal (folksy)
**Variants:** *doohicky*, *dohickey*, *doodad*

A placeholder for a small gadget or part whose name you do not know, typically something mechanical or electronic that you can point at. It sounds homey and a little old-fashioned.

1. Hand me that *doohickey* with the red button on it.
2. There's a little *doohickey* on the back that adjusts the height.
3. "What's this thing for?" "That *doohickey* keeps the door from slamming."
4. I lost the *doohickey* that came with the blender.
5. You need some kind of *doohickey* to open the battery compartment.

**See also:** [gizmo](#gizmo), [thingamajig](#thingamajig), [thingy](#thingy), [whatsit](#whatsit), [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit)

### gizmo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪzmoʊ/ &middot; *GIZ-moh*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *gismo*, *gizmos*

A placeholder for a gadget, usually an electronic one, carrying a hint that the speaker thinks it is clever, unnecessary, or both. Where *doohickey* sounds folksy and mechanical, *gizmo* sounds modern and slightly skeptical.

1. His car is full of *gizmos* he has never once used.
2. There's a *gizmo* on the dashboard that tells you the tire pressure.
3. She bought some *gizmo* that's supposed to sharpen knives.
4. "What's that?" "Some *gizmo* my brother sent me for my birthday."
5. The kitchen drawer is nothing but *gizmos* and dead batteries.

**See also:** [doohickey](#doohickey), [thingamajig](#thingamajig), [thingy](#thingy), [whatsit](#whatsit)

### so-and-so

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsoʊ ən ˌsoʊ/ &middot; *SOH-uhn-soh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (as an insult it is a euphemism — mild on the page, but it stands in for a much stronger word)
**Variants:** *Mr. So-and-so*, *so and so*, *such-and-such a person*

A stand-in for a person whose name does not matter or is not being supplied — useful when you are describing a pattern rather than a case. Keep the separate insult sense in mind: *that old so-and-so* is a polite substitute for a much stronger word, and it is affectionate about as often as it is not.

1. They'll announce that *so-and-so* is retiring and everyone will act surprised.
2. You can't tell the client that *so-and-so* dropped the ball and leave it there.
3. Every single year *so-and-so* forgets to book the room.
4. "Who signs off on it?" "*So-and-so* in legal — I'd have to look up the name."
5. He called me a stubborn old *so-and-so* and then bought me a beer.

**See also:** [whatshisname](#whatshisname), [such and such](#such-and-such), [thingy](#thingy), [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit)

### such and such

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌtʃ ən ˈsʌtʃ/ &middot; *such uhn SUCH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *such-and-such*, *such and such a thing*

A stand-in for an unspecified detail — a date, an amount, a name — used when you are sketching how something generally goes rather than reporting a particular case. It almost always turns up inside a hypothetical.

1. The letter says you owe *such and such* an amount by *such and such* a date.
2. They want a form saying you worked at *such-and-such* a company for five years.
3. You call, you sit on hold, and they tell you to press *such and such* a number.
4. He'll swear the meeting was on *such-and-such* a day and nobody can prove otherwise.
5. "Just fill in the blanks — name, address, *such and such*."

**See also:** [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [and whatnot](#and-whatnot), [yada yada](#yada-yada)

### thingamajig

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθɪŋəməˌdʒɪɡ/ &middot; *THING-uh-muh-jig*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *thingamabob*, *thingumajig*, *thingummy*

A nonsense elaboration of *thing*, used for an object whose name has escaped you at that moment. It is longer and more playful than *thingy*, and it almost always comes with a gesture.

1. Where's that *thingamajig* for opening wine bottles?
2. He's got a *thingamajig* on his keychain that measures the temperature.
3. Push the little *thingamabob* on the side to reset it.
4. The *thingamajig* under the sink started leaking again.
5. I need one of those *thingamajigs* that keeps the cord from tangling.

**See also:** [doohickey](#doohickey), [thingy](#thingy), [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [whatsit](#whatsit)

### thingy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθɪŋi/ &middot; *THING-ee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *thingie*

The shortest and most common placeholder noun, used for an object — and sometimes for a person, an event, or a quirk — when the real word will not come. Very frequent in casual speech and easy to overuse.

1. Can you pass me that *thingy* on the counter?
2. There's a *thingy* in the app that shows how much data you've used.
3. She has a *thingy* about people wearing shoes in the house.
4. The plastic *thingy* on my charger broke off.
5. We're doing the *thingy* at Dave's on Saturday, right?

**See also:** [thingamajig](#thingamajig), [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [whatsit](#whatsit), [doohickey](#doohickey)

### whatchamacallit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtʃəməˌkɔlɪt/ (also /ˌwɑtʃəməˈkɔlɪt/) &middot; *WUCH-uh-muh-kawl-it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *whatchacallit*, *whaddayacallit*, *whatchamacallem*

A compressed "what you may call it," dropped into a sentence in place of an object or a place whose name you have lost. Saying it usually invites the listener to supply the missing word.

1. Grab the — *whatchamacallit* — the spatula, the wide one.
2. He works at that *whatchamacallit* downtown, the co-working place.
3. I need a *whatchamacallit* to get the lid off.
4. She used the *whatchacallit*, the paddle attachment.
5. It's right next to the *whaddayacallit*, the old post office.

**See also:** [whatsit](#whatsit), [thingy](#thingy), [thingamajig](#thingamajig), [what's the word](#whats-the-word)

### whatshisname

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtsɪzˌneɪm/ &middot; *WUTS-iz-name*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (dismissive about the person named — not something to say to their face; the *-face* forms are ruder still)
**Variants:** *whatshername*, *whatsisname*, *what's-his-face*, *what's-her-face*

A placeholder for a person whose name you cannot recall or cannot be bothered to use. The *-face* versions are blunter and usually carry a little contempt.

1. *Whatshisname* from accounting stopped by looking for you.
2. Wasn't she married to *whatshisname*, the guy with the boat?
3. I ran into *whatshername* from your old office at the airport.
4. *What's-his-face* still hasn't returned my email.
5. It was directed by *whatshisname* — he did that space movie.

**See also:** [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [so-and-so](#so-and-so), [thingy](#thingy), [what's the word](#whats-the-word)

### whatsit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌtsɪt/ &middot; *WUT-sit*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (chiefly British; an American is far likelier to reach for *thingy* or *whatchamacallit*)
**Variants:** *whatsits*, *whatsis*, *whatsitsname*

A short placeholder built out of "what is it," used for a small object or, in the *-name* form, for a thing or person whose name is gone. Americans understand it and use it occasionally, but it is a British habit at heart, and in an American mouth it can sound slightly put on.

1. Hand me the *whatsit*, the little metal clip.
2. It's in the *whatsit* — the drawer next to the fridge.
3. He fixed it with a screwdriver and some kind of rubber *whatsit*.
4. We stayed at the *whatsitsname*, the place with the red awning.
5. There's a *whatsit* missing from the box.

**See also:** [whatchamacallit](#whatchamacallit), [doohickey](#doohickey), [thingy](#thingy)

### yada yada

**Pronunciation:** /ˌjɑdə ˈjɑdə/ &middot; *yah-duh YAH-duh*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yada yada yada*, *yadda yadda*, *yaddayadda*

Skips over a stretch of a story that is boring, obvious, or awkward to spell out: "and so on, you can fill in the rest." Made famous nationally by a 1997 episode of Seinfeld, it now often implies that the speaker is glossing over the interesting part on purpose.

1. We argued, we made up, *yada yada*, now we're married.
2. He gave me the whole speech about responsibility, *yada yada yada*.
3. So I go in, fill out the forms, *yada yada*, and they still say I'm not in the system.
4. Dinner, drinks, *yada yada* — it was a long night.
5. She said she was sorry, *yada yada*, but nothing actually changed.

**See also:** [blah blah blah](#blah-blah-blah), [and stuff](#and-stuff), [or whatever](#or-whatever)
