# Surprise, astonishment, and disbelief

> The words Americans reach for in the first half-second after something unexpected lands — from a quiet *huh* to a full *oh my God*.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

This is the largest family of interjections in English, and the one where register matters most. The group runs from perfectly safe (*wow*, *gosh*, *holy cow*) through the religious (*oh my God*, *Jesus*) to the frankly vulgar (*holy crap*, *holy shit*), and a huge share of it consists of **minced oaths** — softened swaps for *God*, *Jesus*, and *Christ* invented so people could exclaim without swearing, which is why *gosh*, *golly*, *jeez*, *criminy*, *cripes*, *jiminy*, and *sheesh* all still echo the words they replaced. Many of these words stretch for emphasis in writing (*whoaaa*, *sheeesh*, *nooo way*) and take an exclamation point far more often than a period. Almost all of them sit at the very front of the sentence, set off by a comma, and the whole sentence after them is optional — *Wow.* on its own is a complete turn in a conversation.

## Core exclamations of surprise

### boy oh boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɔɪ oʊ ˈbɔɪ/ &middot; *BOY oh BOY*  
**Русский:** ну и ну — устар., добродушное изумление перед чем-то большим или хлопотным
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *boy-oh-boy*, *boy howdy*

An old-fashioned, good-natured exclamation of amazement at something big, unexpected, or about to cause trouble. It sounds folksy and a little theatrical, and it works for good news and bad news alike.

1. *Boy oh boy*, that was a long flight.
2. *Boy oh boy*, wait until you see the size of their new dog.
3. She opened the electric bill and said, "*Boy oh boy*."
4. *Boy oh boy*, is your mother going to have something to say about this.
5. We finally got the roof fixed, and *boy oh boy*, was it expensive.

**See also:** [oh boy](#oh-boy), [hoo boy](#hoo-boy), [man](#man)

### dude

**Pronunciation:** /dud/, drawn out /duːd/ &middot; *DOOD, drawn out DOOOOD*  
**Русский:** чувак — разг., целая реакция сама по себе: ровно — «ну ты даёшь», протяжно — «вау»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *duuude*, *dude!*, *dude.*

An address term that stopped addressing anybody decades ago and now works as a complete reaction on its own. Said flat with a period after it, *dude.* is deadpan disbelief; stretched into *duuude* it flips to awe. It started as California surfer and stoner slang and is now nationwide, and Americans use it to and about people of any gender.

1. *Dude*. You drove all the way there and forgot the tickets?
2. *Duuude*, look at that sunset.
3. *Dude*, I've been calling you for two hours.
4. He read the whole group thread and wrote back one word: *dude*.
5. *Dude* — that's my car she just backed into.

**See also:** [bruh](#bruh), [man](#man), [whoa](#whoa)

### gasp

**Pronunciation:** not a word — a fast, sharp intake of breath through an open mouth, the involuntary sound of shock; only when Americans say the spelling out loud as a joke does it become /ɡæsp/ &middot; *GASP*  
**Русский:** ах! — резкий вдох от потрясения; на письме ремарка, вслух — только в шутку
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal (chiefly written)  
**Variants:** *gaaasp*, *gasp!*

Strictly speaking this is a breath, not a word: the real thing is an audible inhalation, and the spelling is a stage direction that lets writers put that breath on the page. Americans do also pronounce the four letters aloud, but only as a joke, to mark mock scandal at something they are not actually shocked by.

1. She reached the last line of the letter and there was an audible *gasp* from the back of the room.
2. *Gasp* — you mean he knew the whole time?
3. The lights came up and the whole theater let out one *gasp*.
4. Her text was just *gasp* and a string of question marks.
5. *Gasp*. A politician who kept a promise.

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [wow](#wow)

### hoo boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhu ˈbɔɪ/ &middot; *hoo-BOY*  
**Русский:** ох-хо-хо / ну всё — разг., предвещает что-то большое, неприятное или запутанное
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hoo-boy*, *hoo boy oh boy*

A drawn-out exclamation that braces the listener for something big, messy, or hard to explain. It signals that what follows is going to be a lot: bad news, a long story, or a complication nobody wants.

1. *Hoo boy*, you're not going to believe what happened at the meeting.
2. I looked at the repair estimate and thought, *hoo boy*.
3. *Hoo boy*, they want all of this filled out before Friday?
4. *Hoo boy*, the line at the DMV goes out the door and around the corner.
5. When she mentioned her ex, my brother just said, "*Hoo boy*."

**See also:** [oh boy](#oh-boy), [boy oh boy](#boy-oh-boy), [man](#man)

### huh

**Pronunciation:** /hʌ/, usually nasalized /hʌ̃/ &middot; *HUH*  
**Русский:** хм — лёгкое удивление или недоумение; с вопросительной интонацией — «а?»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hunh*, *huh?*

A short, often nasalized grunt of mild surprise or puzzlement, used when something does not match what you expected. Said flat and short it simply registers a small new fact; said with a rising tone it asks the speaker to confirm what you just heard.

1. *Huh*. I could have sworn I locked the door.
2. *Huh*, the hardware store is closed on Mondays now.
3. *Huh*? You already finished the whole season?
4. She read the label and said, "*Huh*, it's actually made in Ohio."
5. *Huh*, that's not what the map showed at all.

**See also:** [oh](#oh), [really](#really), [what](#what)

### man

**Pronunciation:** /mæn/ &middot; *MAN*  
**Русский:** ну блин / надо же — разг., сильное чувство; о людях любого пола
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *oh man*, *man oh man*, *aw man*

A general-purpose exclamation of strong feeling that, in this category, marks something as impressive, shocking, or hard to take in. It sits at the front of the sentence far more often than anywhere else, and Americans use it to and about people of any gender.

1. *Man*, that storm came out of nowhere.
2. *Man*, I didn't expect the tickets to sell out in four minutes.
3. *Oh man*, look at that view.
4. He caught it one-handed. *Man*.
5. *Man*, you scared me. I thought the house was empty.

**See also:** [dude](#dude), [oh boy](#oh-boy), [boy oh boy](#boy-oh-boy), [wow](#wow)

### my

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ/ &middot; *MY*  
**Русский:** ишь ты / надо же — устар., мягкое удивление, часто удвоенное, с ноткой укоризны
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *my my*, *my, my*, *my oh my*

A gentle, old-fashioned exclamation of surprise, most often doubled as my, my. It sounds polite and slightly grandmotherly, and it can carry a hint of amused disapproval underneath the good manners.

1. *My, my*, look who finally showed up.
2. *My*, you've grown a foot since Christmas.
3. *My, my*. Somebody has been busy.
4. *My*, what a beautiful garden you have back here.
5. *My oh my*, aren't we fancy tonight.

**See also:** [oh my](#oh-my), [my goodness](#my-goodness), [my word](#my-word)

### oh

**Pronunciation:** /oʊ/ &middot; *OH*  
**Русский:** о! / а! — базовый сигнал того, что дошла новая информация
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *ohh*, *ohhh*, *o*

The most basic surprise marker in English: a single vowel signaling that new information has just arrived. On its own it registers mild surprise; stretched out or combined with another word it scales all the way up to real astonishment.

1. *Oh*! I didn't hear you come in.
2. *Oh*, you're already here. I thought traffic would hold you up.
3. Wait — *oh*, that's not my suitcase.
4. *Oh*. So you two already know each other.
5. *Oh*, wow, they repainted the entire lobby.

**See also:** [oh my](#oh-my), [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [wow](#wow), [huh](#huh)

### oh boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈbɔɪ/ &middot; *oh-BOY*  
**Русский:** ох ты / ну вот — разг.; тон решает: восторг или «опять двадцать пять»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *oh boy oh boy*

An exclamation that greets something unexpected with a mix of surprise and bracing. Tone does all the work: bright and rising it means delight, flat and falling it means here we go again.

1. *Oh boy*, they're giving out free samples at the entrance.
2. *Oh boy*. The check engine light just came on again.
3. *Oh boy*, is this going to be a long week.
4. The kids saw the pool and yelled, "*Oh boy*!"
5. *Oh boy*, I didn't think that door would come off in my hand.

**See also:** [boy oh boy](#boy-oh-boy), [hoo boy](#hoo-boy), [man](#man)

### oh my

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈmaɪ/ &middot; *oh-MY*  
**Русский:** ой / боже мой — мягкая, вежливая замена oh my God
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *oh my oh my*, *oh my, oh my*

A soft, slightly formal exclamation of surprise at something startling, sweet, or overwhelming. It is the polite alternative to oh my God and reads as gentle rather than dramatic.

1. *Oh my*, there's enough food here for a dozen people.
2. *Oh my*, did you make this yourself?
3. She opened the door, saw the flowers, and said, "*Oh my*."
4. *Oh my*, look how much snow came down overnight.
5. *Oh my*, I had no idea it was already midnight.

**See also:** [my](#my), [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [oh my gosh](#oh-my-gosh), [my goodness](#my-goodness)

### oh snap

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈsnæp/ &middot; *oh-SNAP*  
**Русский:** оба-на / ух ты — разг., восторг от неожиданного поворота; из афроам. сленга
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *oh snaps*, *snap*

A delighted reaction to a sudden turn: a comeback that landed, a twist nobody saw coming, or a small disaster you are enjoying from a safe distance. It came into general slang out of Black English in the 1990s. It is completely clean, which is a large part of why it traveled so far.

1. *Oh snap*, she said that to his face?
2. *Oh snap* — I left my badge in the car.
3. "And then he read the whole email out loud." "*Oh snap*."
4. *Oh snap*, the sequel comes out this Friday?
5. He watched the replay twice and said, "*Oh snap*."

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [no way](#no-way), [wow](#wow), [bruh](#bruh)

### ope

**Pronunciation:** /oʊp/ &middot; *OHP*  
**Русский:** опа / ой — диал. (Средний Запад), при лёгком столкновении или мелкой оплошности
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional (Midwestern)  
**Variants:** *ope!*, *oop*

A tiny, clipped exclamation of surprise used when you bump into someone, drop something, or catch a small mistake. It is strongly associated with the Midwest, where it usually runs straight into an apology or a request to squeeze past.

1. *Ope*, sorry, I didn't see you there.
2. *Ope*, let me just squeeze past you real quick.
3. *Ope*, that's my phone.
4. *Ope*, I almost knocked the whole stack over.
5. *Ope*, wrong turn. I'll flip around at the light.

**See also:** [oh](#oh), [whoa](#whoa), [huh](#huh)

### whoa

**Pronunciation:** /woʊ/, also /hwoʊ/ &middot; *WOH, also HWOH*  
**Русский:** воу / ого — резкое изумление; то же слово значит «стой, погоди»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *woah*, *whoah*, *whooaa*

A sharp exclamation of astonishment at something big, sudden, or impressive. The same word also means slow down or hold on, and the surprise use keeps a trace of that: whoa marks the thing that stopped you in your tracks. Note that woah is a very common misspelling.

1. *Whoa*, that's a huge bill for one month of electricity.
2. *Whoa* — did you see how fast that car went by?
3. *Whoa*, you cut your hair!
4. He opened the box and just said, "*Whoa*."
5. *Whoa*, back up a second. She said what?

**See also:** [wow](#wow), [holy cow](#holy-cow), [oh snap](#oh-snap), [wait, what](#wait-what)

### wow

**Pronunciation:** /waʊ/ &middot; *WOW*  
**Русский:** ух ты / вау — универсальное изумление, и от хорошего, и от плохого
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *oh wow*, *woww*, *wowww*

The default American expression of amazement, good or bad. It covers genuine awe, polite admiration, and, said flat with a pause in front of it, stunned disapproval.

1. *Wow*, this view is unbelievable.
2. *Wow*, you finished the whole thing in one night?
3. *Wow*. That's a really rude thing to say to someone.
4. I actually said *wow* out loud when I saw the price.
5. *Wow*, they have completely redone the lobby since I was here.

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [holy cow](#holy-cow), [oh my God](#oh-my-god)

### wowzers

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwaʊzɚz/ &middot; *WOW-zurz*  
**Русский:** ого-го / ух ты ж — разг., нарочито мультяшное усиление wow
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wowza*, *wowzer*, *wowie zowie*

A playful, cartoonish intensifier of wow, used when you want to sound amazed without sounding serious. The goofiness is the point.

1. *Wowzers*, there's a solid pound of cheese on that pizza.
2. *Wowzers*, three job offers in one week?
3. She looked at the price tag and said, "*Wowzers*."
4. *Wowzers*, the whole backyard is full of fireflies.
5. I got my results back and, *wowzers*, they were better than I hoped.

**See also:** [wow](#wow), [yowza](#yowza), [holy moly](#holy-moly)

### yowza

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjaʊzə/ &middot; *YOW-zuh*  
**Русский:** ух ты! / ого — разг. (слегка устар.), о чём-то впечатляющем или дорогом
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal (somewhat dated)  
**Variants:** *yowzah*, *yowza yowza yowza*

An exclamation of amazement, usually at something impressive, expensive, or good-looking. It comes out of mid-century show business, so it always sounds a little like a bandleader is saying it.

1. *Yowza*, look at the size of that steak.
2. Three hundred dollars for one night in a motel? *Yowza*.
3. *Yowza*, that band can play.
4. *Yowza*, that thing goes zero to sixty in three seconds.
5. He whistled and said, "*Yowza*."

**See also:** [wowzers](#wowzers), [wow](#wow), [holy cow](#holy-cow)

## Goodness, heavens, and the religious exclamations

### good grief

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈɡrif/ &middot; *good GREEF*  
**Русский:** батюшки / ну что ты будешь делать — смягчение good God: удивление с усталостью
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *good grief!*, *oh good grief*

A softened *good God*: surprise shading into weariness at something excessive, absurd, or one thing too many. Charlie Brown made it a fixture of American speech and it still carries his particular blend of patience and despair. It is completely safe in any company.

1. *Good grief*, how long has that been in the refrigerator?
2. *Good grief* — they've raised the parking rate again.
3. "He's rewriting the whole thing from scratch." "*Good grief*."
4. *Good grief*, it's only Tuesday.
5. She looked at the twelve-page form and said, "*Good grief*."

**See also:** [good heavens](#good-heavens), [my goodness](#my-goodness), [gosh](#gosh), [sheesh](#sheesh)

### good heavens

**Pronunciation:** /ɡʊd ˈhɛvənz/ &middot; *good HEV-uhnz*  
**Русский:** боже правый / силы небесные — устар., мягкое удивление или испуг
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *heavens*, *heavens above*, *good heavens above*

A mild, old-fashioned exclamation of surprise or dismay. It sounds proper and slightly theatrical, and today it turns up more in writing and from older speakers than in everyday conversation.

1. *Good heavens*, is that the time already?
2. *Good heavens*, what happened to your arm?
3. *Good heavens* — I had no idea the ceiling was leaking.
4. She read the letter twice and said, "*Good heavens*."
5. *Good heavens*, there must be two hundred people out there.

**See also:** [good grief](#good-grief), [my goodness](#my-goodness), [goodness gracious](#goodness-gracious), [my word](#my-word), [heavens to Betsy](#heavens-to-betsy)

### goodness gracious

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊdnəs ˈɡreɪʃəs/ &middot; *GOOD-nis GRAY-shuhs*  
**Русский:** батюшки светы / боже ты мой — совершенно безобидное удивление
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (somewhat dated)  
**Variants:** *gracious*, *gracious me*, *goodness gracious me*

A soft, wholesome exclamation of surprise, and one of the safest in the whole family: nobody is offended by it. It usually introduces a comment on something excessive or unexpected.

1. *Goodness gracious*, look at all this mail.
2. *Goodness gracious*, you scared me half to death.
3. *Goodness gracious*, that child can run.
4. *Goodness gracious*, it's cold out there this morning.
5. Grandma looked at the pile of gifts and said, "*Goodness gracious*."

**See also:** [my goodness](#my-goodness), [good heavens](#good-heavens), [gosh](#gosh)

### Jesus

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʒizəs/ &middot; *JEE-zuhs*  
**Русский:** господи Иисусе — груб., божба; верующие слышат в этом кощунство
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar  
**Variants:** *Jesus Christ*, *sweet Jesus*, *Jesus H. Christ*

The strongest word in this file after *holy shit*, and the one most likely to cost you something. Plenty of Americans use it as an all-purpose reaction to shock; to observant Christians it is blasphemy — taking the Lord's name in vain — and they hear it that way whether or not you meant it. This is swearing, not emphasis. Keep it out of work, school, church, and any company you do not already know well.

1. *Jesus*, that car came out of nowhere.
2. *Jesus Christ*, look at this traffic.
3. *Jesus*, are you okay? I heard the crash from inside the house.
4. He saw the damage to the fender and muttered, "*Jesus*."
5. *Jesus*, I had no idea it was that serious.

**See also:** [jeez](#jeez), [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [holy shit](#holy-shit), [holy crap](#holy-crap)

### lordy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɔrdi/ &middot; *LOR-dee*  
**Русский:** ох ты господи — диал. (южн.), протяжное удивление, часто удвоенное
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** regional (Southern)  
**Variants:** *lawdy*, *lawd*, *lordy lordy*, *Lord have mercy*

A drawled exclamation of surprise or amazement with a strong Southern flavor, often doubled. Depending on the tone it can be warm, weary, or scandalized.

1. *Lordy*, it's hot out there today.
2. *Lordy, lordy*, look who's forty.
3. *Lordy*, that boy has grown since summer.
4. She fanned herself and said, "*Lordy*, what a day."
5. *Lordy*, I've never seen so many cars at one funeral.

**See also:** [mercy](#mercy), [land sakes](#land-sakes), [my stars](#my-stars)

### mercy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɝsi/ &middot; *MUR-see*  
**Русский:** батюшки / господи помилуй — диал. (южн.), устар., перед чем-то чрезмерным
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** regional (Southern)  
**Variants:** *mercy me*, *have mercy*, *mercy sakes*, *Lord have mercy*

An exclamation of surprise at something extreme, striking, or overwhelming. It is old-fashioned and warm, and it survives best in Southern and rural speech, often expanded to mercy me.

1. *Mercy*, it's really coming down out there.
2. *Mercy me*, you gave me a fright.
3. *Mercy*, those steps are steeper than they look.
4. *Mercy*, what a beautiful baby.
5. He tasted the chili, wiped his forehead, and said, "*Mercy*."

**See also:** [lordy](#lordy), [land sakes](#land-sakes), [goodness gracious](#goodness-gracious)

### mother of God

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmʌðɚ əv ˈɡɑd/ &middot; *MUTH-ur uhv GOD*  
**Русский:** матерь божья / святые угодники — сильный шок; верующим режет слух
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *sweet mother of God*, *holy mother of God*, *mother of mercy*

A heavy exclamation of shock lifted out of Catholic prayer and now used well outside it. It sits a step above *oh my God* and lands closest to real horror or real awe — nobody says it about a parking space. Devout listeners, Catholics in particular, may hear it as irreverent, so it is a poor choice in mixed company.

1. *Mother of God*, look at the size of that wave.
2. He came around the corner, saw the wreck, and said, "*Mother of God*."
3. *Sweet mother of God*, it's cold.
4. *Mother of God* — how fast was he going?
5. *Mother of God*, they want forty thousand dollars to fix it.

**See also:** [oh my God](#oh-my-god), [Jesus](#jesus), [holy shit](#holy-shit), [good heavens](#good-heavens)

### my goodness

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈɡʊdnəs/ &middot; *my GOOD-nis*  
**Русский:** боже мой / надо же — мягкое удивление, приличное в любом обществе
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *goodness*, *goodness me*, *oh my goodness*

A gentle exclamation of surprise that nobody could object to. It suits polite company, older listeners, and any moment where you want to register astonishment without swearing.

1. *My goodness*, that's a big dog.
2. *My goodness*, you've done all this since this morning?
3. *My goodness*, I didn't recognize you with the beard.
4. *My goodness*, the prices in this neighborhood have gone crazy.
5. "*My goodness*," she said, "you two look exactly alike."

**See also:** [goodness gracious](#goodness-gracious), [my word](#my-word), [good heavens](#good-heavens), [oh my gosh](#oh-my-gosh)

### my word

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈwɝd/ &middot; *my WURD*  
**Русский:** однако / вот это да — устар., книжн., сдержанное удивление
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *upon my word*, *my word!*

A restrained, slightly formal exclamation of surprise. It sounds bookish and old-fashioned, the sort of thing a professor or a grandparent says when something impresses them.

1. *My word*, this house is enormous.
2. *My word*, I haven't seen you in twenty years.
3. *My word*, that's a great deal of luggage for one weekend.
4. He looked at the final score and said, "*My word*."
5. *My word*, the garden has really taken off this summer.

**See also:** [my goodness](#my-goodness), [good heavens](#good-heavens), [well, I never](#well-i-never)

### oh my God

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ maɪ ˈɡɑd/ &middot; *oh my GOD*  
**Русский:** о боже мой / господи — самое ходовое восклицание шока; верующим режет слух
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *omigod*, *ohmigod*, *oh God*, *oh my Lord*

The strongest and most common all-purpose expression of shock in American English, used for horror, delight, and everything in between. It is everywhere in casual speech, but religious listeners may hear it as disrespectful and it is out of place in formal settings.

1. *Oh my God*, you're engaged!
2. *Oh my God*, I completely forgot about the meeting.
3. *Oh my God* — is everyone okay?
4. She opened the door and screamed, "*Oh my God*!"
5. *Oh my God*, this is the best cake I've ever had.

**See also:** [OMG](#omg), [oh my gosh](#oh-my-gosh), [Jesus](#jesus), [mother of God](#mother-of-god), [holy shit](#holy-shit)

### oh my gosh

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ maɪ ˈɡɑʃ/ &middot; *oh my GOSH*  
**Русский:** ой боже мой / ой-ёй — вежливая замена oh my God, без религиозного риска
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *omigosh*, *ohmigosh*

The polite substitute for oh my God, and nearly as common. It carries the same shock with none of the religious risk, which makes it the safe choice at work, at school, and around children.

1. *Oh my gosh*, look how much he has grown!
2. *Oh my gosh*, I'm so sorry — I thought the meeting was at three.
3. *Oh my gosh*, that puppy is tiny.
4. *Oh my gosh* — you got the job?

4. *Bruh*, it's four in the morning.
5. *Bruh* — you can't show up to an interview in flip-flops.

**See also:** [dude](#dude), [for real](#for-real), [shut up](#shut-up), [seriously](#seriously)

### do tell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdu ˈtɛl/ &middot; *doo TELL*  
**Русский:** да ну / рассказывай — разг., устар.; тепло — «ну-ка подробнее», сухо — сарказм
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (somewhat dated)  
**Variants:** *do tell!*, *well, do tell*

An invitation to keep talking, delivered with the eyebrows up. Warm, it means *go on, I want all of this*; flat, it is dry sarcasm and does the same job as *you don't say*. It sounds a little arch either way, which is the fun of it.

1. "There's a reason she left the party early." "*Do tell*."
2. *Do tell* — I want every single detail.
3. He caught the words "at the wedding" and said, "*Do tell*."
4. So the two of them have been talking all summer. *Do tell*.
5. "I have some news." "*Well, do tell*."

**See also:** [you don't say](#you-dont-say), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [really](#really)

### for real

**Pronunciation:** /fɚ ˈril/ &middot; *fer REEL*  
**Русский:** серьёзно? / правда? — с вопросом просит подтвердить, ровно — горячо соглашается
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *fr*, *frfr*, *for realsies*

A short demand for confirmation: is that actually true? Said with a rising tone it asks; said flat it agrees emphatically with what someone else just said.

1. *For real*? They canceled the whole season?
2. Wait, *for real*, you're moving to Portland?
3. She got the promotion? *For real*?
4. *For real*, that's the best sandwich in the city.
5. "I quit today." "*For real*?"

**See also:** [seriously](#seriously), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [really](#really), [you're kidding](#youre-kidding)

### get out

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɛt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *get OWT*  
**Русский:** да ладно / иди ты — разг., недоверие к новости, а не приказ уйти
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *get outta here*, *get out of here*, *get outta town*

A disbelieving reaction to surprising news, meaning you cannot be serious rather than leave the room. It is friendly and casual, and the tone makes the difference obvious.

1. *Get out* — you two are related?
2. She won the raffle twice? *Get out*.
3. *Get outta here*, that's my old high school teacher.
4. "I met him once, years ago." "*Get out*!"
5. *Get out of here*, that hat looks great on you.

**See also:** [shut up](#shut-up), [no way](#no-way), [you're kidding](#youre-kidding)

### no kidding

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ ˈkɪdɪŋ/ &middot; *noh KID-ing*  
**Русский:** да ты что / серьёзно — или сухое «да неужели» о чём-то очевидном
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no kiddin'*

Either a genuine expression of surprise, meaning really, I had no idea, or a dry confirmation that something painfully obvious is true. Tone and context tell you which one you are hearing.

1. *No kidding*, she has been working here twelve years?
2. *No kidding*, the store gave me a full refund with no receipt.
3. "It's cold out." "*No kidding*."
4. He fixed the whole thing himself? *No kidding*.
5. *No kidding*, that's exactly what happened to me last year.

**See also:** [seriously](#seriously), [for real](#for-real), [you don't say](#you-dont-say), [really](#really)

### no shot

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈʃɑt/ &middot; *noh SHOT*  
**Русский:** да ну на / не может быть — разг., молодёжная замена no way
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *no shot!*, *there's no shot*

The current young way of saying *no way* — flat disbelief that something happened or ever could. It is roughly a decade old in wide use and strongly marked for age: under thirty it is ordinary, and over forty it sounds like a costume.

1. *No shot* — he made that from half court?
2. "They gave everybody the day off." "*No shot*."
3. *No shot* you finished the whole series in one sitting.
4. *No shot*, the tickets can't be gone already.
5. She said *no shot* about four times before she believed me.

**See also:** [no way](#no-way), [as if](#as-if), [get out](#get-out), [bruh](#bruh)

### no way

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈweɪ/ &middot; *noh WAY*  
**Русский:** да ладно / не может быть — разг., прямое недоверие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no way!*, *no freaking way*, *no way, José*

A flat statement of disbelief: that cannot possibly be true. The same phrase also works as a hard refusal, but in surprise use it comes with wide eyes and a rising pitch.

1. *No way* — you got front-row seats?
2. *No way*, I just saw him an hour ago.
3. *No way*! They're finally opening the bridge?
4. "The whole thing was free." "*No way*."
5. *No way* that fit in the overhead bin.

**See also:** [get out](#get-out), [no shot](#no-shot), [shut up](#shut-up), [you're kidding](#youre-kidding), [as if](#as-if)

### really

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrili/, also /ˈrɪli/ &middot; *REE-lee, also RIL-ee*  
**Русский:** правда? / серьёзно? — с вопросом удивление, ровно и протяжно — скепсис и упрёк
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *oh really*, *rly*

A one-word check on something surprising: is that true? With a rising tone it is genuine surprise; flat and drawn out it turns into skepticism or open disapproval.

1. *Really*? I thought the meeting was canceled.
2. She said that to your face? *Really*?
3. *Really*. You're going to leave the dishes there all night.
4. "He's already back from Europe." "*Really*?"
5. *Oh really*, and when exactly were you planning to tell me?

**See also:** [seriously](#seriously), [for real](#for-real), [no kidding](#no-kidding), [huh](#huh)

### say what

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ ˈwʌt/ &middot; *say WUT*  
**Русский:** чего-чего? / как-как? — разг., шутливая просьба повторить неожиданное
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *say what now*, *say whaaat*

A casual, playful demand that someone repeat something surprising. It is relaxed and a little comic, and it works only among people who are already comfortable with each other.

1. *Say what*? You're the one who wrote that?
2. *Say what*? They're giving everyone Friday off?
3. "I sold the truck." "*Say what*?"
4. *Say what now*? Run that by me one more time.
5. *Say what*, you actually met her in person?

**See also:** [wait, what](#wait-what), [what](#what), [huh](#huh)

### seriously

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪriəsli/ &middot; *SEER-ee-uhs-lee*  
**Русский:** серьёзно? / да ты что — разг., требование подтвердить или возмущение
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *srsly*, *for serious*, *are you serious*

A demand that the speaker confirm something surprising, or an exclamation at something outrageous. It is one of the most common modern reactions to unexpected news.

1. *Seriously*? They lost the reservation again?
2. Wait, *seriously*, you've never seen the ocean?
3. *Seriously*, that's the third time this week.
4. "I ran a marathon last month." "*Seriously*?"
5. *Seriously*? You're wearing that to the wedding?

**See also:** [for real](#for-real), [really](#really), [you're kidding](#youre-kidding), [no way](#no-way)

### shut up

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʌt ˈʌp/ &middot; *shut UP*  
**Русский:** да ладно! / да ты гонишь — разг.; здесь не «замолчи», а радостное недоверие
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *shut up!*, *shut the front door*, *shut your mouth*

Here shut up does not mean be quiet: it means I cannot believe that, said with delight. Tone carries everything, and a listener who does not know the idiom can badly misread it, so save it for friends.

1. *Shut up*! You're pregnant?
2. *Shut up*, that didn't happen.
3. "I got us upgraded to first class." "*Shut up*!"
4. *Shut the front door*, they're getting back together?
5. *Shut up*, you didn't actually meet him in person.

**See also:** [get out](#get-out), [no way](#no-way), [you're kidding](#youre-kidding)

### since when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɪns ˈwɛn/ &middot; *sins WEN*  
**Русский:** с каких это пор — вопрос-упрёк: удивление плюс намёк, что должны были сказать
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *since when?!*, *oh, since when*

A challenge wearing the clothes of a question. It registers surprise at a fact by implying that the speaker ought to have known about it already, and it almost never wants an actual date in reply. Tone runs from friendly astonishment to open suspicion.

1. *Since when* do you drink your coffee black?
2. *Since when*? Nobody told me the deadline moved.
3. "I run in the mornings now." "*Since when*?"
4. *Since when* is the library closed on Saturdays?
5. He plays banjo? *Since when*.

**See also:** [really](#really), [seriously](#seriously), [wait, what](#wait-what), [what](#what)

### wait, what

**Pronunciation:** /ˌweɪt ˈwʌt/ &middot; *wayt WUT*  
**Русский:** стоп, что? / погоди, чего? — факт не уложился в голове
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wait what*, *wait, what?!*

A two-part reaction showing that a fact has just failed to fit. Wait stops the conversation and what asks for it again; together they are the standard modern signal of a small mental collision.

1. *Wait, what*? You already left?
2. He mentioned it in passing and I just went, *wait, what*.
3. She told me she was quitting, and I said, "*Wait, what*?"
4. *Wait, what* — there's a test today?
5. *Wait, what*? Say that part about the refund again.

**See also:** [say what](#say-what), [since when](#since-when), [what](#what), [huh](#huh), [whoa](#whoa)

### what

**Pronunciation:** /wʌt/, also /hwʌt/ &middot; *WUT, also HWUT*  
**Русский:** что?! / чего?! — протяжный возглас недоверия, а не обычное вопросительное «что»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *whaaat*, *whut*, *wut*

A one-word cry of disbelief, usually stretched out and rising. Standing alone as a reaction it means I cannot believe what I just heard, which is different from the ordinary question word.

1. *What*?! He's leaving in the middle of the season?
2. *What*, you drove all night?
3. Hold on, they sold the house? *What*?
4. "We're out of coffee." "*What*?"
5. *What*? I never agreed to any of that.

**See also:** [wait, what](#wait-what), [say what](#say-what), [huh](#huh), [what the](#what-the)

### what the

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwʌt ðə/ &middot; *WUT thuh*  
**Русский:** что за… / какого… — оборванное восклицание: крепкое слово не договаривают
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *what the heck*, *what the hell*, *what the...*

A truncated exclamation of bewilderment in which the speaker stops just before the word that would finish it. Left hanging it is safe in most company; completed with heck it stays mild, and with stronger words it gets progressively cruder.

1. *What the* — who parked in my spot?
2. *What the heck* is that noise coming from the basement?
3. I opened the fridge and just said, "*What the*..."
4. *What the* — did the whole file just disappear?
5. *What the heck*, it's snowing in April.

**See also:** [what in the world](#what-in-the-world), [what](#what), [huh](#huh), [holy crap](#holy-crap)

### yeah right

**Pronunciation:** /ˌjɛə ˈraɪt/ &middot; *yeh-RITE*  
**Русский:** ага, конечно / ну да, как же — саркастическое отрицание услышанного
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *yeah, right*, *yeah sure*

A sarcastic rejection of what you have just been told. The words say yes twice; the tone says absolutely not.

1. *Yeah right*, he's going to be on time for once.
2. She says the check is in the mail. *Yeah, right*.
3. *Yeah right*, that's the same excuse he gave last week.
4. "I'll have it done by noon." "*Yeah, right*."
5. *Yeah right*, and I'm the Queen of England.

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [no way](#no-way), [you don't say](#you-dont-say)

### you don't say

**Pronunciation:** /ju doʊnt ˈseɪ/ &middot; *yoo dohnt SAY*  
**Русский:** да что вы говорите / неужели — от искреннего интереса до сухого сарказма
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *you don't say so*

A response to surprising news that ranges from genuine interest to flat sarcasm. Said warmly it invites the speaker to go on; said dryly it means that was completely obvious.

1. *You don't say*. He actually apologized?
2. "It turns out the road is closed." "*You don't say*."
3. *You don't say*, she has been there since the very beginning?
4. The mechanic told me it needs a new transmission. *You don't say*.
5. *You don't say*! I had no idea you two were neighbors.

### you're kidding

**Pronunciation:** /jɚ ˈkɪdɪŋ/, carefully /jʊr ˈkɪdɪŋ/ &middot; *yer KID-ing*  
**Русский:** да ты шутишь / да брось — дружелюбный вызов слишком удивительной новости
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *you're kidding me*, *you've got to be kidding me*, *you gotta be kidding*

A direct challenge to something that sounds too surprising to be true. It is friendly rather than hostile: the speaker usually does believe it and is simply registering shock.

1. *You're kidding* — they gave you the whole week off?
2. *You're kidding me*. I just talked to her yesterday.
3. "The concert has been moved to Tuesday." "*You're kidding*."
4. *You've got to be kidding me*, the line goes around the block.
5. *You're kidding*, right? Please tell me you're kidding.

## Reacting to the unexpected

### go figure

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ ˈfɪɡjɚ/ &middot; *goh FIG-yur*  
**Русский:** поди пойми / вот и пойми — разг., усмешка над тем, что вышло наперекор ожиданиям
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *go figure that*, *figure that*

A wry comment on something that turned out contrary to expectation. It shrugs at the world's inconsistency instead of expressing real shock, and it almost always comes at the end of the story.

1. I studied all week and got a B; he crammed for an hour and aced it. *Go figure*.
2. The cheap tires lasted longer than the expensive ones. *Go figure*.
3. *Go figure* — the one day I bring an umbrella, it's sunny.
4. She hated that job and stayed nine years. *Go figure*.
5. We drove two hours for the restaurant and it was closed. *Go figure*.

**See also:** [who knew](#who-knew), [wouldn't you know it](#wouldnt-you-know-it), [imagine that](#imagine-that), [will wonders never cease](#will-wonders-never-cease)

### how about that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ əˌbaʊt ˈðæt/ &middot; *how uh-bowt THAT*  
**Русский:** надо же / каково? — разг., приятное удивление задним числом
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *how 'bout that*, *how do you like that*

A mild exclamation of pleased surprise, said after the fact rather than in the first shocked second. It usually invites the listener to be impressed too.

1. They named a scholarship after her. *How about that*.
2. *How about that*, the old radio still works.
3. We got the last two seats on the flight. *How about that*!
4. *How about that*, the package actually arrived on time.
5. "He finished the whole marathon." "Well, *how about that*."

### I'll be darned

**Pronunciation:** /aɪl bi ˈdɑrnd/ &middot; *ile bee DARND*  
**Русский:** ну надо же / чтоб мне провалиться — устар.; darned — приличная замена damned
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *I'll be damned*, *well I'll be darned*, *I'll be dogged*

A folksy expression of amazement that something turned out to be true. Darned is the polite swap for damned, which is what older or saltier speakers actually say.

1. *Well, I'll be darned*, it started on the first try.
2. *I'll be darned* if that isn't my old bicycle.
3. *I'll be darned*, you were right about the shortcut.
4. He looked at the photo a long time and said, "*I'll be darned*."
5. *I'll be darned*, they kept the place exactly the same.

**See also:** [well, I'll be](#well-ill-be), [son of a gun](#son-of-a-gun), [what do you know](#what-do-you-know)

### imagine that

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈmædʒən ˌðæt/ &middot; *ih-MAJ-in that*  
**Русский:** подумать только / надо же — разг.; сухо — «а то как же» о предсказуемом
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *just imagine that*, *imagine*

A dry or gently amused response to something surprising, or to something that was entirely predictable. Tone decides whether it means how remarkable or of course it did.

1. They found the missing keys in the couch. *Imagine that*.
2. *Imagine that*, a politician changing his mind.
3. She has been playing since she was four years old. *Imagine that*.
4. The store was out of the one thing I came in for. *Imagine that*.
5. *Imagine that*, the two of them ended up at the same college.

### lo and behold

**Pronunciation:** /ˌloʊ ən bɪˈhoʊld/ &middot; *loh uhn bih-HOHLD*  
**Русский:** и вот, извольте / и что бы вы думали — книжн., вводит неожиданный поворот
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *lo and behold!*

A set phrase that introduces the surprising turn in a story, meaning and then, sure enough. It is a little literary and is usually delivered with a touch of humor.

1. I checked the garage one more time and, *lo and behold*, there was the wallet.
2. We gave up looking, and *lo and behold*, the cat strolled in at midnight.
3. *Lo and behold*, the store had exactly one left in my size.
4. She swore she would never fly again, and *lo and behold*, she booked a ticket in June.
5. I opened the drawer and, *lo and behold*, every single battery was dead.

### son of a gun

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌn əv ə ˈɡʌn/ &middot; *sun uhv uh GUN*  
**Русский:** вот те на / ёлки-палки — устар., мягкое удивление, особенно когда всё же вышло
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *sonofagun*, *son of a biscuit*

A mild exclamation of surprise at something unexpected, especially something that finally worked. It is dated and friendly, and far softer than the phrase it replaced.

1. Well, *son of a gun*, the engine turned over.
2. *Son of a gun*, I found the receipt after all.
3. He hit the shot from half court. *Son of a gun*.
4. *Son of a gun*, that's the same model my dad had.
5. "I passed." "*Son of a gun*, good for you."

### speak of the devil

**Pronunciation:** /ˌspik əv ðə ˈdɛvəl/ &middot; *speek uhv thuh DEV-uhl*  
**Русский:** лёгок на помине — о том, кто вошёл ровно тогда, когда о нём говорили
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *speak of the devil and he shall appear*, *well, speak of the devil*

Said the moment the person you were just discussing walks in. It is friendly, not an accusation — the *devil* is a fossil from a much older superstition and no American hears it as an insult now. It has to land on the arrival; said thirty seconds later it falls flat.

1. *Speak of the devil* — we were just talking about you.
2. The door opened and my sister said, "*Speak of the devil*."
3. *Speak of the devil*, here comes the man with the keys.
4. We had just gotten to the part about her promotion when — *speak of the devil* — she walked in.
5. "*Speak of the devil*," he said. "Your ears must have been burning."

### well, I'll be

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛl aɪl ˈbi/ &middot; *wel ile BEE*  
**Русский:** ну надо же / вот те на — диал. (сельск.), божба обрывается на полуслове
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** regional (rural)  
**Variants:** *well I'll be*, *well I'll be a monkey's uncle*, *well I'll be danged*

A trailing-off expression of astonishment in which the speaker leaves the rest of the oath unsaid. It sounds rural, warm, and pleasantly old-fashioned.

1. *Well, I'll be*. Look who just walked through the door.
2. *Well, I'll be*, the check actually cleared.
3. She fixed the whole thing with a paperclip. *Well, I'll be*.
4. *Well, I'll be a monkey's uncle*, it worked.
5. "Same last name, same hometown." "*Well, I'll be*."

### well, I never

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɛl aɪ ˈnɛvɚ/ &middot; *wel eye NEV-ur*  
**Русский:** ну знаете ли / такого я ещё не видывала — устар., возмущение дурными манерами
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *well I never did*, *I never*

An indignant, old-fashioned exclamation of shock, almost always at bad manners. The unspoken ending is I never heard of such a thing in all my life.

1. *Well, I never*, he took the last piece without asking anyone.
2. She hung up on me. *Well, I never*.
3. "*Well, I never*," my aunt said, "in all my born days."

### what do you know

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt də jə ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *wut duh yuh NOH*  
**Русский:** надо же / вот тебе и раз — разг., довольное удивление постфактум; не вопрос
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *whaddya know*, *what d'you know*, *well what do you know*

A pleased, slightly surprised remark that something turned out well or turned out to be true. It comes after the discovery, not during it, and it is not a real question.

1. *What do you know*, the printer is working again.
2. I looked in the last box and, *what do you know*, there was the charger.
3. *Well, what do you know*. She showed up after all.
4. *What do you know*, the shortcut actually saved us time.
5. "It fits." "*Whaddya know*."

**See also:** [how about that](#how-about-that), [speak of the devil](#speak-of-the-devil), [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold), [son of a gun](#son-of-a-gun)

### what in the world

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ɪn ðə ˈwɝld/ &middot; *wut in thuh WURLD*  
**Русский:** что же это такое / да что за — недоумение; in the world — безобидный усилитель
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *what on earth*, *what in the heck*, *what in tarnation*, *what in blazes*

A bewildered question-exclamation aimed at something that makes no sense at all. In the world is the harmless intensifier; on earth, in the heck, and blunter options swap in as the feeling gets stronger.

1. *What in the world* is that smell?
2. *What in the world* happened to the front door?
3. *What on earth* were you thinking?
4. *What in the world* — who left the freezer open all night?
5. *What in the world* is she wearing to a job interview?

**See also:** [what the](#what-the), [what](#what), [huh](#huh)

### who knew

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhu ˈnu/ &middot; *hoo NOO*  
**Русский:** кто бы мог подумать — разг., лёгкое приятное удивление вопреки ожиданиям
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *who knew?*, *who would have thought*

A light exclamation that something turned out to be true against all expectation. It is a shrug of pleasant surprise, and it usually lands at the end of the sentence.

1. Turns out he can cook. *Who knew*.
2. *Who knew* a folded paper towel could fix a wobbly table?
3. The cheap seats had the best view in the house. *Who knew*.
4. *Who knew*, the library has a whole recording studio in the basement.
5. She's fluent in Portuguese. *Who knew*?

**See also:** [go figure](#go-figure), [imagine that](#imagine-that), [how about that](#how-about-that)

### will wonders never cease

**Pronunciation:** /wɪl ˌwʌndɚz ˌnɛvɚ ˈsis/ &middot; *wil WUN-durz nev-ur SEES*  
**Русский:** чудеса, да и только — разг., почти всегда с иронией
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wonders never cease*

A wry exclamation at something you had long since given up expecting. It is almost always a little sarcastic, even when the surprise is genuinely welcome.

1. He did the dishes without being asked. *Will wonders never cease*.
2. *Will wonders never cease*, the train is actually on time.
3. The city finally filled the pothole. *Will wonders never cease*.
4. *Will wonders never cease*, she answered on the first ring.
5. "He apologized." "*Will wonders never cease*."

**See also:** [go figure](#go-figure), [who knew](#who-knew), [how about that](#how-about-that)

### wouldn't you know it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʊdənt jə ˈnoʊ ɪt/ &middot; *WOOD-nt yuh NOH it*  
**Русский:** как назло / и надо же — разг., о предсказуемо неудачном повороте
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wouldn't you know*, *and wouldn't you know it*

The marker for a twist you should have seen coming, and it is nearly always the unlucky one. It sets up the punchline of a small misfortune and asks the listener to agree that this is simply how the world operates.

1. I finally got the car washed and, *wouldn't you know it*, it rained that afternoon.
2. *Wouldn't you know it*, the one meeting I skip is the one where they take attendance.
3. We waited all week for the delivery and, *wouldn't you know*, it came while we were out.
4. *Wouldn't you know it* — the spare was flat too.
5. She got to the front of the line and, *wouldn't you know it*, they'd just closed.

**See also:** [go figure](#go-figure), [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold), [will wonders never cease](#will-wonders-never-cease)

### would you look at that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʊdʒə ˈlʊk ət ˌðæt/ &middot; *WOOD-juh LOOK uht that*  
**Русский:** нет, ты посмотри / вы только гляньте — зовёт разделить удивление; не вопрос
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *well, would you look at that*, *look at that*, *would you look at this*

An invitation to share your surprise at something that is right there in front of both of you. It is warm and unhurried, it usually comes with a nod toward whatever it is, and it is not a real question — nobody answers it.

1. *Would you look at that*, the tomatoes finally came in.
2. Well, *would you look at that* — somebody swept the whole porch.
3. He held up the old photograph. "*Would you look at that*."
4. *Would you look at that*, there's a hawk sitting on the mailbox.
5. She turned the corner, saw the line down the block, and said, "*Would you look at that*."

**See also:** [how about that](#how-about-that), [what do you know](#what-do-you-know), [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold)
