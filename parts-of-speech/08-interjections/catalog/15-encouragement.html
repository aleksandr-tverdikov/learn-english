Encouragement, praise, and cheering#

The words you shout at somebody who is trying — before they start, while they are struggling, and after they have pulled it off.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

What unites this group is direction. Most interjections report on the speaker's own state; these are aimed outward, at a player, a performer, or a friend walking into a hard room, and their job is to change how that person feels about what is in front of them. Two mechanical things run through the whole category: the apostrophe, because so many of these are frozen contractions (go get 'em, knock 'em dead, sic 'em, c'mon) where it stands for a swallowed them or come and dropping it reads as an error; and sarcasm, because American English routinely says the praise words flat and means the opposite. Nice going, way to go, nailed it, and good luck with that are as common as complaints as they are as compliments, and nothing but intonation separates the two — so a learner should assume that any short praise formula here can be flipped, and listen for the falling, unenthusiastic delivery that signals it. One member of the category is not made of speech at all: where a Pronunciation line describes a physical action instead of giving ordinary phonemes — as it does for whistle — that is deliberate, because transcribing that sound into vowels and consonants would be a lie.

Spurring someone into action#

charge#

Pronunciation: /tʃɑrdʒ/ · CHARJ Type: secondary · Register: informal

A cavalry command turned playful rallying cry. Americans use it to launch a group into motion — a game, a chore, a run to the car — with a deliberate whiff of the battlefield about it, and it is the four-note fanfare every stadium organ plays.

  1. "Charge!" he yelled, and forty second-graders ran for the playground.
  2. Everybody grab a box off the truck. Charge!
  3. The organ played the little fanfare and the whole ballpark yelled charge.
  4. Charge! Last one to the water buys lunch.
  5. She raised her coffee cup like a sword and said, "Charge."

See also: come on, let's go, go get 'em

come on#

Pronunciation: /ˌkʌm ˈɑn/, also /kəˈmɑn/ · kum-AHN, also kuh-MAHN Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: c'mon, come on now, cmon

The workhorse of American encouragement: it urges someone to hurry, to follow, or to keep trying, and it is what fans shout at a struggling team. Said flat with a falling tone it flips into protest or disbelief — oh, come on means you cannot be serious — so tone does all the work.

  1. Come on, we're going to miss the previews.
  2. "C'mon, c'mon, c'mon," he muttered, watching the free throw.
  3. Come on, buddy, you've got one more lap in you.
  4. Come on over — the grill's already hot.
  5. Oh, come on, that was clearly a foul.
  6. Come on now, you know I'd never say that about you.

See also: let's go, go on, keep going

get after it#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡɛɾ ˈæftɚ ɪt/ · get-AF-ter-it Type: phrase · Register: very informal

A locker-room and job-site push meaning go do the work, hard, starting now. Coaches, trainers, and foremen use it constantly; in an office it sounds like you are quoting a coach on purpose.

  1. All right, boys — get after it.
  2. Weights are racked and the clock's running. Get after it.
  3. He clapped once, said "get after it," and the whole team hit the field.
  4. You've got four hours and a full truck to unload. Get after it.
  5. Get after it today, because tomorrow's forecast is ugly.

See also: let's get it, go get 'em, charge

giddyup#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡɪdiˌʌp/ · GID-ee-up Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: giddy-up, giddyap, giddap

The command that tells a horse to start moving, borrowed for people. Off a horse it is jokey — a way to say let's move that makes the speaker sound like a cowboy on purpose.

  1. Giddyup! she said, and the pony started down the trail.
  2. Coats on, shoes on, giddyup — the bus is at the corner.
  3. He signed the last page, slapped the folder shut, and said, "Giddyup."
  4. Giddy-up, little man. Bath time.
  5. The light turned green and the guy behind us leaned out and hollered, "Giddyup!"

See also: let's go, come on, yee-haw

go for it#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊ ˈfɔr ɪt/ · goh-FOR-it Type: phrase · Register: informal

Permission and encouragement in one: take the chance, I'm behind you. It answers a hesitation, and on fourth down it is what the whole stadium yells at the coach.

  1. If you want to apply, go for it — worst case they say no.
  2. "Can I have the last slice?" "Go for it."
  3. Fourth and one on the forty. Go for it!
  4. She's been talking about that tattoo for a year, so I told her to go for it.
  5. Go for it, man. You'll regret not trying a lot more than you'll regret failing.

See also: have at it, you got this, go get 'em

go get 'em#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊ ˈɡɛɾ əm/ · goh-GET-em Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: get 'em, go get 'em, tiger, go git 'em

A send-off for someone walking into a contest, an interview, or a stage. It casts whatever they are about to face as an opponent, which is exactly why it lands as a boost.

  1. Straighten your tie. Go get 'em.
  2. "Go get 'em, tiger," his mom said, and shut the car door.
  3. She's got the closing argument this morning — go get 'em.
  4. Get 'em, Rosie! Two more points!
  5. He texted one line before the audition: "Go get 'em."

See also: knock 'em dead, break a leg, sic 'em

go on#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊ ˈɑn/ · goh-AHN Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: go on now, go ahead

Two jobs: it invites a speaker to keep talking, and it nudges a hesitating person to go ahead and act. Said with a smile and a wave of the hand it also works as mock-modest deflection — oh, go on means you're flattering me, keep going.

  1. Go on — I want to hear what she said next.
  2. Go on, ask her. She's not going to bite.
  3. "You should hear him play." "Oh, go on."
  4. He stopped talking, and the detective just nodded and said, "Go on."
  5. Go on, take the last one. Nobody else is going to eat it.

See also: keep going, come on, there you go

have at it#

Pronunciation: /ˌhæv ˈæɾ ɪt/ · hav-AT-it Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: have at 'em, have at thee (jocular, archaic)

Cheerful permission to go ahead and start — usually with something the speaker is handing over: a plate of food, a broken appliance, a fight they are staying out of. It grants the floor rather than praising anything.

  1. Tools are in the garage. Have at it.
  2. "Mind if I take a crack at the crossword?" "Have at it."
  3. There's a whole tray of leftovers in the fridge — have at it.
  4. He pushed the keyboard across the desk and said, "Have at it."
  5. If you two want to argue about the thermostat all night, have at it.

See also: go for it, go on, charge

let's do this#

Pronunciation: /ˌlɛts ˈdu ðɪs/ · lets-DOO-this Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: let's do it, all right, let's do this

The psych-up said at the threshold: everybody is assembled, the thing is about to start, and this is the sentence that starts it. Unlike let's go, it never means leave — it points at one specific job both speakers already have in mind, which is why it works just as well muttered to yourself as shouted at a group.

  1. Gloves on, bags out. Let's do this.
  2. "Let's do this," she told the mirror, and walked out to the podium.
  3. Four people, one couch, three flights of stairs — let's do this.
  4. The anesthesiologist asked if she had any questions. "Nope. Let's do it."
  5. "We could keep talking about it for another month." "No. Let's do this."

See also: let's go, let's get it, here we go

let's get it#

Pronunciation: /ˌlɛts ˈɡɛɾ ɪt/ · lets-GET-it Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: let's get it in, let's gettit

A hype phrase out of sports and hip-hop meaning let's start, and let's dominate. It lives in locker rooms and group chats; in a staff meeting it reads as trying too hard.

  1. Bags are packed, van's loaded. Let's get it.
  2. He posted a picture of the empty field with the caption "Let's get it."
  3. First day of camp — let's get it, fellas.
  4. "You ready?" "Let's get it."
  5. Let's get it, we're only down six with a whole half to play.

See also: let's go, get after it, boom

let's go#

Pronunciation: /ˌlɛts ˈɡoʊ/ · lets-GOH Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: let's gooo, let's goooo, lezgo

Literally an invitation to leave, but shouted after a big play or good news it means yes — that is exactly what I wanted. The lengthened spelling let's gooo is how the celebratory sense gets written online, because the plain spelling looks like you are just telling somebody to get in the car.

  1. Let's go! He made the putt!
  2. Grab your jacket, let's go.
  3. "We got the apartment." "LET'S GO."
  4. Three seconds left and we're up one — let's go, defense!
  5. She read the acceptance email twice and whispered, "Let's goooo."

See also: let's get it, come on, go

onward#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑnwɚd/ · AHN-werd Type: secondary · Register: formal Variants: onward and upward, onwards

A literary keep moving forward, used to close a speech, an email, or a bad day. It concedes that the setback happened and announces that the group is proceeding anyway.

  1. The quarter was rough, but the pipeline looks strong. Onward.
  2. She ended every newsletter the same way: "Onward and upward."
  3. Well, that meeting was a disaster. Onward.
  4. Onward, friends — there's a lot of work ahead and not much daylight.
  5. He signed the note "Onward," which was as sentimental as he ever got.

See also: keep going, chin up, charge

sic 'em#

Pronunciation: /ˈsɪk əm/ · SIK-em Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: sic him, sick 'em (common misspelling)

The command that sets a dog on a target, used with people as rowdy encouragement to go after an opponent, an argument, or a rival team. It is aggressive by design — friendly among fans and friends, hostile anywhere it could be taken literally.

  1. The mailman came up the walk and Grandpa laughed and said, "Sic 'em, Duke."
  2. She's about to cross-examine the witness. Sic 'em.
  3. "Sic 'em, Bears!" is the entire chant at Baylor.
  4. He rolled up his sleeves and I said, "Sic 'em, tiger."
  5. Somebody finally posted the receipts in the comments — sic 'em.

See also: go get 'em, give 'em hell, charge

Cheers and rallying cries#

bravo#

Pronunciation: /ˈbrɑˌvoʊ/, also /brɑˈvoʊ/ · BRAH-voh, also brah-VOH Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: brava (to a woman), bravi (to a group), bravo!!!

The shout of approval at the end of a performance. The Italian gender endings survive in opera houses — brava for a soprano, bravi for the whole cast — but most Americans use bravo for everybody, and away from a stage it is often faintly sarcastic.

  1. The aria ended and half the balcony was on its feet yelling bravo.
  2. Brava! She held that last note forever.
  3. Bravo to whoever finally fixed the printer.
  4. He read the closing paragraph out loud and said, "Bravo. That's the piece."
  5. "I ate the entire pizza by myself." "Bravo."

See also: encore, hear hear, well done

cheers#

Pronunciation: /tʃɪrz/ · CHEERZ Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: cheers to —, three cheers for —

Raised-glass approval. As a toast it salutes whoever is named; in three cheers for Dana it introduces an actual organized cheer; and cheers used alone as a sign-off or a casual thanks is British, which Americans notice immediately.

  1. Cheers — to twenty-five years and counting.
  2. Eight glasses came up over the middle of the table and everybody said cheers at slightly different times.
  3. Three cheers for the groundskeepers! Hip hip —
  4. She lifted her glass and said, "Cheers. You did it."
  5. My coworker in London ends every email with "Cheers," and it still reads as foreign to me.

See also: hip hip hooray, hooray, hear hear

encore#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑnˌkɔr/, also /ɑnˈkɔr/ · AHN-kor, also ahn-KOR Type: secondary · Register: neutral

The shout that demands one more song. Americans chant it as much as they mean it as a request, and off the stage it survives as a joking do that again.

  1. The house lights stayed down and the crowd started chanting "Encore! Encore!"
  2. Encore! Play the one about the river!
  3. He nailed the impression and his sisters all yelled encore.
  4. Encore, Dad! Do the coin thing again.
  5. "Encore!" my grandmother called, clapping over her head, and the pianist sat back down.

See also: bravo, take a bow, hooray

give it up for#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡɪv ɪɾ ˈʌp fɚ/ · giv-it-UP-fer Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: let's hear it for —, put your hands together for —, round of applause for —

The emcee's call for applause, always followed by the name of whoever is walking out or being honored. It is an instruction to the room rather than a compliment to the person — the praise is the noise it produces — and away from a microphone it works as mock fanfare for something ordinary.

  1. Give it up for the band. They drove nine hours to be here.
  2. "Let's hear it for the graduating class!"
  3. Give it up for Marcus, everybody — twenty years on this crew.
  4. He came back through the door with the pizzas and somebody yelled, "Give it up for the delivery guy."
  5. Put your hands together for our next reader.

See also: encore, take a bow, bravo

go#

Pronunciation: /ɡoʊ/ · GOH Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: go go go, go, team

The bare stadium cheer, almost always followed by the name of the team or person you are backing. Repeated fast — go go go — it stops being encouragement and becomes urgency, the word you shout at somebody who has to move right now.

  1. Go, Wildcats!
  2. Go, Maya! You're almost at the finish line!
  3. "Go go go!" the coach yelled as the runner took the baton.
  4. He forwarded the mandatory-fun email to me with two words on top: "Go, team."
  5. Go, Dad — you've got this hill.

See also: let's go, come on, you got this

here we go#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪr wi ˈɡoʊ/ · heer-wee-GOH Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: here we go now, here we go again (resigned, not a cheer)

Two things at once: the stadium chant that carries a team through a drive — here we go, Steelers, here we go — and the breath somebody takes alone, out loud, right before starting the hard thing. Add one word and it inverts: here we go again is weariness, and everybody hears the difference.

  1. Here we go, Steelers, here we go!
  2. Seat belts on, coffee in the holder. Here we go.
  3. The house lights went down and she whispered, "Here we go."
  4. Here we go — third and goal from the two.
  5. His mother started in about the wedding again, and my husband muttered, "Here we go."

See also: let's go, let's do this, go

hip hip hooray#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪp ˌhɪp həˈreɪ/ · hip-hip-huh-RAY Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hip hip hurray, hip-hip-hooray

A call-and-response cheer: one person shouts hip hip and everybody else answers hooray, traditionally three times. It is now mostly a birthday-party and retirement-party thing, and among adults it is a little corny on purpose.

  1. Hip hip — "Hooray!"
  2. Let's hear it for the birthday girl: hip hip hooray!
  3. The whole office did an awkward hip hip hooray around the sheet cake.
  4. "Hip hip hooray!" the campers shouted three times, exactly as instructed.
  5. Thirty years at the same company — hip hip hooray, and somebody hand him the plaque.

See also: hooray, huzzah, cheers

hooah#

Pronunciation: /ˈhuˌɑ/ · HOO-ah Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: hua, huah

The U.S. Army's all-purpose shout of agreement, readiness, and morale — roughly understood, and I'm fired up about it. It is branch-specific: Marines say oorah, sailors and Coast Guardsmen say hooyah, and mixing them up is the kind of mistake veterans catch instantly.

  1. "Everybody clear on the timeline?" "Hooah, Sergeant."
  2. Hooah! Let's move.
  3. He answered every question that morning with a flat hooah.
  4. The general finished his remarks and four hundred soldiers answered hooah.
  5. "Chow's in ten." "Hooah."

See also: oorah, rah, charge

hooray#

Pronunciation: /həˈreɪ/; hurrah: /həˈrɑ/ · huh-RAY, huh-RAH Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: hurray, hurrah, hoorah, hooray!!!

The standard English shout of joy and approval. Hooray and hurray are the everyday spellings; hurrah is older and more literary, and it sounds like a history book or a sea story.

  1. Hooray! The power's back on.
  2. Everyone yelled hooray when the principal announced the snow day.
  3. Hurray for whoever brought donuts.
  4. "Hurrah!" the crew shouted as the sails filled.
  5. Well, hooray — the plumber says it's only the flapper valve.

See also: hip hip hooray, huzzah, yay

hooyah#

Pronunciation: /ˈhuˌjɑ/ · HOO-yah Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: hoo-yah, hooyah!

The Navy and Coast Guard answer to the Army's hooah — acknowledgment, readiness, and morale in two syllables, loudest in SEAL and rescue-swimmer training, where it is shouted back at an instructor dozens of times a day. The three service shouts are not interchangeable: hooah is Army, oorah is Marine Corps, hooyah is Navy and Coast Guard, and using the wrong one is the giveaway that a movie had no veterans on set.

  1. "Do you understand the standard?" "Hooyah, Instructor!"
  2. The whole boat crew answered hooyah and picked the log back up.
  3. Hooyah — surf zone in five minutes.
  4. Twenty-six years in the Navy, and he still answers the phone on his birthday with a hooyah.
  5. She finished the swim, coughed up half the Pacific, and got out one hooyah.

See also: hooah, oorah, rah

huzzah#

Pronunciation: /həˈzɑ/ · huh-ZAH Type: primary · Register: dated Variants: huzza, huzzah!!!

An antique hooray, alive today mainly at Renaissance fairs, in historical fiction, and in the mouths of people being theatrically pleased. Nobody says it plainly anymore.

  1. Huzzah! The turkey leg cart has been located.
  2. The whole tavern stood up and shouted huzzah three times.
  3. She got the last spot in the garage and yelled, "Huzzah!"
  4. Huzzah — the build finally passed.
  5. "Huzzah," he said flatly, holding up the new stapler.

See also: hooray, bravo, yay

olé#

Pronunciation: /oʊˈleɪ/ · oh-LAY Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: ole, olé olé olé, oléééé

A Spanish shout of approval, borrowed whole and never translated. In bullfighting and flamenco it marks the exact instant of a clean pass or a hard turn, and it reached most Americans through soccer, where olé, olé, olé is what a crowd sings when its team is stringing passes together and running out the clock. Written without the accent it looks like the Scandinavian name Ole, which is why the accent tends to survive even in casual typing.

  1. The cape came around and half the plaza shouted olé.
  2. The dancer's heels hit the last beat and somebody in the front row called out olé.
  3. Four minutes left, up two goals, and the whole away end started in with "Olé! Olé! Olé!"
  4. He got the car into that spot on the first try and his sister said, "Olé."
  5. The waiter set down the paella, lifted the empty pan over his head, and the table gave him an olé.

See also: bravo, hooray, yeah

oorah#

Pronunciation: /uˈrɑ/ · oo-RAH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: ooh-rah, hoorah

The U.S. Marine Corps shout — agreement, enthusiasm, and identity in two syllables. Like hooah, it belongs to one branch, and Marines use it with each other far more than they use it with civilians.

  1. "Feeling motivated this morning?" "Oorah, Staff Sergeant."
  2. Oorah! Semper Fi.
  3. The recruiter grinned, shook my hand, and said, "Oorah."
  4. Every birthday text from his old platoon just said "Oorah."
  5. He finished the story with a quiet oorah and left it there.

See also: hooah, rah, charge

rah#

Pronunciation: /rɑ/ · RAH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: rah rah, rah-rah-rah, sis boom bah

The bare syllable of an old cheerleading chant. Doubled as rah-rah it usually describes enthusiasm instead of expressing it — a rah-rah speech is one that is all energy and no content.

  1. Rah! Rah! Go, Panthers!
  2. The squad came onto the track chanting "Rah, rah, sis boom bah."
  3. Rah, team. That's about all the enthusiasm I have left today.
  4. Somebody in the back yelled rah and got exactly nobody to join in.
  5. "Rah!" she said, pumping her fist about two inches.

See also: hooah, oorah, go

whistle#

Pronunciation: Not speech, and no phonemic transcription of it is honest. It is a tone, not a syllable: air forced through pursed lips, or through two fingers hooked back against the tongue, at a pitch high enough to cut through a crowd. The cheering pattern is one long shriek or a run of short rising blasts. In writing it is described (he whistled*) rather than spelled, though comics use* wheet and fweet.

Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal

The loudest applause one person can produce without help, and in an American crowd it means straightforward approval — it goes up at concerts, graduations, rodeos, and school assemblies, always stacked on top of clapping rather than instead of it. Two things set it apart from everything else in this category. It has no agreed spelling, because it has no speech sounds in it. And one specific pattern, the two-note falling wolf whistle aimed at a person's body, is not encouragement in any current American reading: it is street harassment, it is heard that way regardless of what the whistler intended, and the friendly crowd whistle does not protect it.

  1. Her name was called and her whole row stood up and whistled.
  2. Somebody two seats back put two fingers in his mouth and let out a whistle that made my ear ring.
  3. The band came back out for the encore and the whistling went on for a solid minute.
  4. He whistled through his teeth when the tow truck finally pulled the pickup out of the ditch.
  5. "Don't whistle at people," her mother said. "Clap, like a human being."

See also: whoop, give it up for, woo-hoo

whoop#

Pronunciation: /wup/, also /wʊp/ · WOOP Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal Variants: whoop whoop, woop woop, whoo

A loud, wordless holler of excitement, spelled out. Doubled as whoop whoop it works as a written cheer, and because it also imitates a siren it shows up in songs and jokes about police.

  1. Whoop! That's my kid up on that stage.
  2. Whoop whoop! Friday at last.
  3. "Whoop!" he yelled, and threw his cap in the air.
  4. Whoop whoop — pizza's here, everybody.
  5. She read the score off her phone and let out a whoop that startled the dog.

See also: woo-hoo, yee-haw, yay

woo-hoo#

Pronunciation: /ˌwuˈhu/ · woo-HOO Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: woohoo, whoo-hoo, woo, wooooo

The standard American whoop of delight, said as often as it is shouted. Clipped to woo it is what people yell from an audience; stretched out in writing it is pure excitement with the volume left in. Said perfectly flat, it is one of the most reliable sarcasm markers in the language.

  1. Woo-hoo! No school tomorrow!
  2. "Woo!" somebody yelled from the back of the bar.
  3. We got the loan — woohoo!
  4. She typed "wooooo" in the group chat and then about twelve exclamation points.
  5. He said "woo-hoo" in the flattest voice imaginable when they announced the mandatory training.

See also: whoop, yay, woot

woot#

Pronunciation: /wut/ · WOOT Type: primary · Register: internet/text Variants: w00t, woot woot

A written cheer out of early-2000s gaming chat, spelled with zeros in its heyday. It still turns up in group chats and commit messages, where it now reads as either affectionate or slightly dated.

  1. Woot! Build's green.
  2. "Tickets are in my cart." "w00t"
  3. Woot woot — first weekend off since March.
  4. She replied to the entire thread with a single "woot."
  5. Got the last two seats on the aisle. Woot.

See also: woo-hoo, yay, yas

yas#

Pronunciation: /jæs/, also /jɑs/ · YASS, also YAHSS Type: primary · Register: very informal Variants: yaas, yaaas, yasss, yas queen

An emphatic, drawn-out yes used to hype somebody up — approval with a lot of energy behind it. It is shouted out loud at least as often as it is typed, so calling it an internet word undersells it. It comes out of Black and Latino ballroom culture and spread through drag performance and social media, and it stays heavily marked: in a straight white mouth, or in a work channel, it reads as an impression of somebody else's speech rather than as enthusiasm.

  1. Yas! That dress is unbelievable on you.
  2. "I quit this morning." "YAAAS. Finally."
  3. She commented "yas queen" on every single photo.
  4. Yasss, book the trip. Do it.
  5. The whole chat went yas when the tour dates dropped.

See also: yes, yay, woo-hoo

yay#

Pronunciation: /jeɪ/ · YAY Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: yayyy, yaaay, yay!!!

The small, warm cheer — happiness at good news rather than triumph over anybody. It is gentle enough for a text to a friend and extremely common in speech to and from children, which is why a shouted yay from a grown man usually reads as a joke.

  1. Yay! You made it.
  2. "The results came back clean." "Yay!"
  3. Yay, cake.
  4. She texted back "yayyy" and about six exclamation points.
  5. Yay for whoever finally fixed the coffee machine.

See also: hooray, woo-hoo, yes

yeah#

Pronunciation: /jɛə/ · YEH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: yeahhh, yeah!!, yeah, buddy

Shouted rather than spoken, yeah stops being agreement and becomes a cheer — the noise a crowd makes when the band starts the song everybody came for. Drawn out, it also works as loud approval of somebody else's decision.

  1. Yeah! That's what I'm talking about!
  2. The first chord hit and ten thousand people yelled yeah.
  3. "I told him no." "Yeahhh. Good."
  4. Yeah, buddy! Look at that catch!
  5. She jumped off the last rock, landed clean, and shouted "Yeah!"

See also: yes, whoop, let's go

yee-haw#

Pronunciation: /ˈjiˌhɔ/ · YEE-haw Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: yeehaw, yee-hah, yahoo

The cowboy holler of delight. Ranch country still uses it straight; everywhere else it is playful — a way of saying this is great while doing a Western accent.

  1. Yee-haw! We're going to Texas.
  2. He hit the jump, landed it, and let out a yee-haw.

yes#

Pronunciation: /jɛs/ · YESS Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: yesss, yessss, yes!!!

Said sharply with a fist pump, yes is not an answer to anything — it is the sound of a win landing. It is the most common American exclamation of triumph, and in writing the extra s's carry the volume.

  1. Yes! I got the job!
  2. "YES!" he screamed at the television.
  3. The ball dropped in and she just said, quietly, "Yes."
  4. Yesss, they're bringing back the breakfast menu.
  5. He read the text, closed his eyes, and whispered yes.

Praise and approval#

attaboy#

Pronunciation: /ˈæɾəˌbɔɪ/ · AT-uh-boy Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: atta boy, attagirl, atta girl, attaway, that-a-boy

A worn-down that's the boy — quick praise delivered in the moment rather than afterward. It is affectionate but hierarchical: it fits a coach to a player, a handler to a dog, an older person to a younger one, and between equals it can land as condescending, especially attagirl said to a grown woman.

  1. Attaboy! That's how you block.
  2. "Atta girl," her father said as she backed the trailer into the driveway.
  3. He scratched the dog's ears and said, "Attaboy, Cooper."
  4. Attaway, Marcus — right down the middle.
  5. She landed the account, and all he said was "attagirl," which somehow made it worse.

boom#

Pronunciation: /bum/ · BOOM Type: onomatopoeic · Register: very informal Variants: boom!, boom goes the dynamite, boom shakalaka

The sound of an explosion used to punctuate a success, usually your own. It marks the exact moment something lands: the shot goes in, the code compiles, the argument ends.

  1. Two clicks, one password reset, and boom — you're back in.
  2. Boom! Nothing but net.
  3. He flipped the last card over. "Boom. Full house."
  4. You put the pan in at 400 for twenty minutes and boom, dinner.
  5. She dropped the last folder on his desk. Boom. A day early.

good job#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡʊd ˈdʒɑb/ · guud-JOB Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: great job, nice job, good job!

The plainest praise in American English, safe in every setting. It is also the default thing adults say to small children, so between adult peers it can sound faintly like a gold star — nice work is the safer version at the office.

  1. Good job, everybody. That was a clean launch.
  2. "Good job!" the coach called as she came off the field.
  3. Great job on the deck — it looks brand new.
  4. He studied the drawing for a long moment and said, "Good job, bud."
  5. Nice job getting all of that done before the holiday.

hats off#

Pronunciation: /ˌhæts ˈɔf/ · hats-AWF Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: hats off to —, my hat's off to you

Public credit where credit is due, from the old gesture of removing your hat in respect. It is slightly formal and shows up most in speeches, toasts, and thank-you notes.

  1. Hats off to the volunteers who ran registration all weekend.
  2. My hat's off to you — I could not have kept my temper like that.
  3. Hats off to the kitchen. That was the best meal we've had all year.
  4. He raised his glass and said, "Hats off to thirty years of this nonsense."
  5. Hats off, honestly. You made a hard thing look easy.

hear hear#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪr ˈhɪr/ · heer-HEER Type: phrase · Register: formal Variants: hear, hear, here here (common misspelling)

A shout of agreement with something just said, out of parliamentary practice, where members could not applaud and so called out hear him. Americans use it at toasts, board meetings, and dinner-table speeches, and the spelling here here is wrong in a way people notice.

  1. "Hear, hear!" someone called from the end of the table.
  2. He quoted the whole paragraph into the group chat and added, "Hear, hear."
  3. He finished the toast and half the room said hear hear.
  4. "Nobody should have to work two jobs to make rent." "Hear, hear."
  5. Hear hear to that, and to the people who did the actual work.

Pronunciation: /ˈkuˌdoʊz/, also /ˈkjuˌdoʊz/ · KOO-dohz, also KYOO-dohz Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: kudos to —

Credit and praise, from a Greek word for glory. It is a singular noun that looks plural, so careful writers avoid a kudo; in practice Americans use it as a ready-made compliment, especially in work email and internal announcements.

  1. Kudos to the whole team for turning that around in two days.
  2. Kudos — that was a genuinely hard call and you made it.
  3. She sent a company-wide kudos to the night shift.
  4. Kudos to Ellen for catching the error before it went to print.
  5. The review gave kudos to the lighting designer and barely mentioned the cast.

more power to you#

Pronunciation: /ˌmɔr ˈpaʊɚ tə ju/ · mor-POW-er-tuh-yoo Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: more power to her, more power to 'em

Approval of somebody else's choice, especially one you would not make yourself. Meant sincerely it is generous; delivered with a shrug it means that's your business, not mine, which is how it usually lands when the choice sounds unwise.

  1. She's moving to Alaska to fly bush planes. More power to her.
  2. If you can eat like that and still run marathons, more power to you.
  3. "I'm doing the whole remodel without a contractor." "More power to you."
  4. More power to 'em — somebody has to be willing to do that job.
  5. He wants to try stand-up at forty-five, and honestly, more power to him.

nailed it#

Pronunciation: /ˈneɪld ɪt/ · NAILD-it Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: you nailed it, crushed it, killed it

Praise for something that came out exactly right. Online it is also the standard sarcastic caption for a spectacular failure — a collapsed cake beside the photo it was copied from — so context decides which one is meant.

  1. Nailed it. That presentation could not have gone better.
  2. "How was the audition?" "Nailed it."
  3. You nailed it on the color — that's the exact green she wanted.
  4. He landed the dismount clean and his teammates all yelled "Nailed it!"
  5. The cake collapsed into a puddle of frosting. Nailed it.

nice going#

Pronunciation: /ˌnaɪs ˈɡoʊɪŋ/ · nice-GOH-ing Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: real nice going

Literally praise, but in American speech it is sarcastic more often than not — the thing you say to whoever just knocked the drink over. Said warmly, with the stress falling on nice, it is genuine; said flat, it is a complaint.

  1. Nice going! That's three strikeouts in a row for our guy.
  2. Nice going — now we have to start the whole thing over.
  3. "I locked the keys in the car." "Nice going."
  4. She fixed the leak herself in under an hour. Nice going, honestly.
  5. Real nice going, buddy. The neighbors definitely heard that.

nice one#

Pronunciation: /ˌnaɪs ˈwʌn/ · nice-WUN Type: phrase · Register: informal

Quick praise for one specific thing — a shot, a joke, a catch, a save. It compliments the act rather than the person, which makes it lighter and easier to say than good job. Like nice going, it flips sarcastic when the "one" was a mistake.

  1. Nice one! That pass was perfect.
  2. She told the joke, and from the back somebody said "nice one."
  3. Nice one on the parking spot — I've circled this block twice already.
  4. You caught the typo in the headline? Nice one.
  5. He spilled coffee down his own shirt. "Nice one," his brother said.

nice work#

Pronunciation: /ˌnaɪs ˈwɝk/ · nice-WERK Type: phrase · Register: neutral

The American workplace default for praise between adults — warmer than a nod, less parental than good job, and safe in either direction between a junior and a senior person. It compliments the output rather than the effort.

  1. Nice work on the quarterly numbers.
  2. Nice work, both of you. That was not an easy client.
  3. She read it twice and wrote back two words: "Nice work."
  4. Nice work getting the permit through in a week.
  5. "Nice work out there today," the foreman said, handing back the clipboard.

now you're talking#

Pronunciation: /ˌnaʊ jɚ ˈtɔkɪŋ/ · now-yer-TAW-king Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: now we're talking, now you're talkin'

Approval of a suggestion that has finally gotten good. It rewards a change of direction — the first three ideas were fine, this one is right — and now we're talking says the same thing about a situation improving rather than about a person.

  1. "What if we just rent a van and all drive out together?" "Now you're talking."
  2. There's brisket? Now we're talking.
  3. Now you're talking — that's the version I would actually pay for.
  4. He turned the heat up and the onions finally started to color. Now we're talking.

Pronunciation: /prɑps/ · PRAHPS Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: mad props, props to —

Short for proper respect, out of hip-hop and now general American slang. You give props: publicly acknowledging that somebody did something worth acknowledging, often something the speaker could not have pulled off.

  1. Props to Dani for driving all night to get here.

  3. Mad props to the kitchen for keeping up with that rush.

respect#

Pronunciation: /rɪˈspɛkt/ · rih-SPEKT Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: respect to —, big respect, nothing but respect

A one-word salute: what you did was admirable and I acknowledge it. Said alone, usually with a nod, it carries more weight than a longer compliment, because it costs the speaker something to concede.

  1. You walked away from a job that was making you miserable? Respect.
  2. Respect to everybody who showed up in that rain.
  3. He finished the marathon at fifty-eight. Big respect.
  4. "I told my boss exactly what I thought." "Respect."

take a bow#

Pronunciation: /ˌteɪk ə ˈbaʊ/ · tayk-uh-BOW (rhyming with now*)* Type: phrase · Register: informal

An instruction to accept the credit you have earned, borrowed from the stage; the vowel is the one in now, not the one in low. It is what you say to somebody who is deflecting praise, and to a whole group whose work went well.

  1. Take a bow, chef — that was extraordinary.
  2. Whoever wrote that headline, take a bow.
  3. Take a bow, everybody. Sold out three nights running.

Pronunciation: /ˌðæts ðə ˈspɪrɪt/ · thats-thuh-SPEER-it Type: phrase · Register: neutral

Approval of an attitude rather than a result. You say it when somebody who was reluctant, discouraged, or grumbling suddenly shows a little willingness — which is also why it works as gentle mockery of forced cheer.

  1. "Fine, I'll try one more time." "That's the spirit."
  2. That's the spirit! Get back on the horse.

Praise for somebody who has just gotten it right after struggling — the verbal pat on the back a parent gives a kid learning to ride a bike. It also means here is the thing you asked for when you hand something over, and see, that's exactly my point in an argument, so tone does the sorting.

  1. There you go! You've got the rhythm now.

  3. "There ya go," the barista said, sliding the cup across the counter.

Pronunciation: /ˌweɪ tə ˈɡoʊ/ · way-tuh-GOH Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: way to be, way to hustle, way to go!

Enthusiastic praise for something somebody just did, standard from the sidelines and the bleachers. The pattern extends freely — way to hustle, way to hang in there — and, like nice going, the flat sarcastic version is common enough that intonation decides the meaning.

  1. Way to go! Two goals in one half.
  2. Way to go, honey — straight A's again.
  3. "Way to hustle out there," the coach said, slapping his shoulder pad.

Complete, slightly formal praise for a finished piece of work. It is the phrase for writing — a note at the top of a paper, a line in a review — and in American speech it can sound stiff or faintly British next to nice work. The steak sense is unrelated.

  1. Well done. That report answered every question the board had.

Encouragement to keep your composure through disappointment, from the posture of not looking down. It is kind but light: right for a small setback, and capable of sounding dismissive after a real loss.

The standard American don't quit for somebody in the middle of something hard and long — an illness, a bad stretch at work, a slow recovery. It promises nothing except that you are watching and you think they can outlast it.

Straightforward: do not stop now. It is what you say to a runner with a hill left, to a writer in the middle of a draft, and to anybody who has paused to check whether they should keep talking.

  1. Keep going — you're closer than you think.

  3. Keep going, three more reps.
  4. He wanted to quit at mile nineteen and somebody on the curb yelled, "Keep going!"

Praise and instruction at once: what you are doing is working, so do more of it. It requires an established pattern — you cannot say it about a one-time success — and it is standard in performance reviews and report-card comments.

  1. Grades are up across the board. Keep it up.
  2. "Keep up the good work," the note on her essay said.
  3. Three straight months under budget — keep it up.

A flat statement of belief used as a push. It is aimed at somebody who doubts their own ability rather than somebody who is merely tired, and it is one of the few encouragements that works equally well shouted from a sideline and said quietly in a hallway.

The modern American confidence-booster, said right before somebody does the hard thing. It is shorter and less earnest than you can do it — the missing have is casual on purpose — and it works in a text as well as it works out loud.

  3. She has her recital at four, so text her break a leg.
  4. Break a leg at the interview. Call me the second you're out.
  5. He hates being told good luck, so we all just say break a leg.
