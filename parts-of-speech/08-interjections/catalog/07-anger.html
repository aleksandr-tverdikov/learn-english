Anger, frustration, and minced oaths#

The noises, curses, and polite substitutes Americans reach for when patience runs out.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

What unites this group is pressure escaping. Some entries are raw noises (argh, grr), some are real profanity (damn, hell), and a large share are minced oaths — deliberate distortions that keep the rhythm and the opening sound of a taboo word while dropping the offense: gosh for God, darn for damn, heck for hell, shoot for shit. Register is the whole risk here, so the labels below are blunt about which forms are safe at work and which are not, and every minced oath names the word it is standing in for — a learner who cannot see the original has no way to judge the substitute. The full inventory of taboo words, with offensiveness ratings for each, lives on the profanity page; the register ladder on the main interjections page places them against everything else. One punctuation quirk runs through the last section: the sarcastic entries (great, perfect, nice, fine) are marked by a flat, falling tone, which in writing usually means a period — an exclamation point would make them sincere again.

Raw sounds of frustration#

These are not words so much as noises with a spelling. They carry no taboo at all, so they are safe everywhere, and their length on the page is a volume dial: argh is a grumble, aaargh is a howl.

argh#

Pronunciation: /ɑrɡ/, /ɑr/ · AHRG, AHR Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: aargh, arrgh, aaargh, arg

A groan of frustration or defeat, aimed at a situation that has gone wrong rather than at a person. Extra letters in writing mean a longer, louder groan.

  1. "Argh! I just lost an hour of work."
  2. Argh — the store closes in five minutes and I'm still ten minutes away.
  3. He looked at the flat tire and let out a long argh.
  4. "Aaargh. Why does this printer hate me?"
  5. Argh, I knew I should have left earlier.

See also: ugh, gah, grr

gah#

Pronunciation: /ɡɑ/ · GAH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: gaah, gahh

A short, sharp bark of irritation, quicker and more sudden than ugh. It usually escapes before you have found words for whatever is annoying you.

  1. "Gah! This zipper is stuck again."
  2. Gah, I hate group projects.
  3. She opened an inbox full of spam and muttered a quiet gah.
  4. "Gah! I said the wrong name in the meeting."
  5. Gah — do not talk to me before coffee.

See also: argh, ugh, grr

grr#

Pronunciation: no standard IPA — a growl with no vowel of its own: the r is held and carries the whole syllable, roughly /ɡr̩ː/ · GRR (a low animal growl, teeth together, r drawn out) Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal Variants: grrr, grrrr

An imitation of an animal growl used to show annoyance, most often in writing or in a half-joking tone. Said out loud it is usually playful rather than genuinely angry.

  1. "Grr. The website logged me out again."
  2. She texted back a single grr and a photo of the parking ticket.
  3. Grrr — somebody took my lunch out of the fridge.
  4. "Grr!" he said, wrestling with the jar lid.
  5. Grr, I have to redo the whole slide deck.

See also: argh, hmph, ugh

hmph#

Pronunciation: no standard IPA — a hum with the lips shut, pushed out through the nose and cut off, roughly /hm̩f/ · HMF (mouth never opens; the sound is all nose and breath) Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal Variants: humph, harrumph, hmpf

A short snort through the nose that signals offended disapproval or grudging skepticism. It tells everyone you are unimpressed and a little insulted without committing you to words. The spelled-out harrumph is the one form that has a real vowel: /həˈrʌmf/.

  1. "Hmph. I wasn't even invited."
  2. She crossed her arms and gave a loud hmph.
  3. Hmph — that is not what he told me yesterday.
  4. "Humph! Fine, do it your way."
  5. He listened to the excuse, said hmph, and walked out.

See also: grr, oh brother, honestly

ugh#

Pronunciation: /ʌɡ/ · UHG Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: uggh, ughhh, ugghh

The all-purpose groan of disgust, weariness, or annoyance. It covers a bad smell, a tedious chore, and news you did not want to hear, and it is one of the most common interjections in American speech.

  1. "Ugh, Monday already."
  2. Ugh — the milk went bad.
  3. "Traffic on the bridge is stopped." "Ugh."
  4. She read the group chat and typed ugh before she typed anything else.
  5. Ughhh, I have three more hours of this.

See also: argh, gah, sheesh

Profanity and blasphemy#

The real thing rather than a substitute. Everything in this section is fine among friends and wrong in a job interview, a client meeting, or a classroom; the religious ones (God, Jesus, Christ) also give genuine offense to many believers, which is exactly why the minced oaths in the next section exist.

Christ#

Pronunciation: /kraɪst/ · KRYST Type: secondary · Register: vulgar Variants: oh Christ, Christ almighty

A blasphemous exclamation of exasperation, said when a person or a situation has pushed you past patience. This is real profanity, not a soft oath: it is a step stronger than jeez, which is its minced form, and a religious listener hears it as breaking a commandment rather than as ordinary swearing.

  1. "Christ, would you slow down?"
  2. Christ — how many times do I have to explain this?
  3. He looked at the repair bill and said, "Oh, Christ."
  4. "Christ almighty, the meeting is three hours long."
  5. Christ, I just mopped that floor.

See also: Jesus, jeez, for Christ's sake

crap#

Pronunciation: /kræp/ · KRAP Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: oh crap, aw crap

A mild swear for something that has just gone wrong, and a family-safe stand-in for shit. Most Americans hear it as mild, but it is still a swear word: keep it away from clients, strangers, and other people's children.

  1. "Crap, I left my badge at home."
  2. Crap — the game went to overtime and I have to leave.
  3. He dropped the tray and said crap under his breath.
  4. "Crap! That's the second flat tire this month."
  5. Crap, I forgot to hit send.

See also: oh crap, crud, shoot

damn#

Pronunciation: /dæm/ · DAM Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: damn!

The most common mild curse in American speech, used when something disappoints or irritates you. Said flat and clipped it is pure frustration; drawn out with a rising tone it flips to admiration, as in "Damn, that's good." It is mild to most ears and still a genuine curse to religious ones, who hear the original wish for damnation in it.

  1. "Damn, the flight got canceled."
  2. Damn — I meant to pick that up on the way home.
  3. He read the rejection letter and muttered damn.
  4. "Damn! I hit reply-all again."
  5. Damn, this line hasn't moved in ten minutes.

See also: damn it, dang, darn

damn it#

Pronunciation: /ˈdæmɪt/ · DAM-it Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: dammit, damnit, damn it all

A sharper, more directed damn, aimed at a situation that keeps going wrong. It is said as one word with no pause, which is why dammit is the ordinary written spelling.

  1. "Damn it, the file didn't save."
  2. Dammit — I told you to check the oil.
  3. He slammed the drawer shut. "Damn it all."
  4. "Answer the phone, damn it."
  5. Dammit, I was two minutes from finishing.

See also: damn, damn you, goddamn it

damn you#

Pronunciation: /ˈdæm ju/ · DAM-yoo Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: damn him, damn her, damn them all

A curse aimed at a person rather than at a situation — literally a wish for their damnation, which is why it still lands harder than damn it even when it is half a joke. In the third person (damn him, damn them) it is a complaint about somebody who is not in the room.

  1. "Damn you, you knew and you let me walk in there anyway."
  2. Damn him — he borrowed the good ladder again and didn't bring it back.
  3. "You were right." "Damn you. I hate it when you're right."
  4. She read the letter a second time and said it quietly. "Damn you."
  5. Damn them all, I am not paying a forty-dollar change fee.

See also: damn it, go to hell, screw you

go to hell#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡoʊ tə ˈhɛl/ · goh-tuh-HEL Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: go straight to hell, the hell with you, tell him to go to hell

A hostile dismissal telling someone they are finished as far as you are concerned. Unlike screw you, which friends trade as a joke, this one is almost always meant, and it is the line people remember years later.

  1. "Go to hell. I am not apologizing for something I didn't do."
  2. "He wants us to eat the shipping cost." "Tell him to go to hell."
  3. Go straight to hell — you promised me that was over.
  4. She hung up, looked at the phone, and said, "Go to hell."
  5. The hell with you, then. I'll move it myself.

See also: screw you, damn you, to hell with it

God#

Pronunciation: /ɡɑd/ · GAHD Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: oh God, my God, God almighty

An exasperated appeal used when someone is wearing your patience thin. In this angry sense it is flat and clipped, unlike the drawn-out oh God of shock or dread. Most Americans treat it as mild; devout listeners do not, and to them it is the commandment about taking the name in vain.

  1. "God, you are impossible."
  2. God — just pick a restaurant already.
  3. "Oh God, not this argument again."
  4. She rubbed her eyes. "God almighty, it's only Tuesday."
  5. God, I wish this elevator would hurry up.

See also: Jesus, gosh, for God's sake

goddamn it#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡɑdˈdæmɪt/ · gahd-DAM-it Type: phrase · Register: vulgar Variants: goddammit, god damn it, goddamnit

The strongest member of the damn family, adding blasphemy to the curse. It signals real anger, it is fully taboo at work, in front of children, and around anyone you have just met, and religious listeners object to it twice over.

  1. "Goddamn it, I asked for this yesterday."
  2. Goddammit — the power went out right before I saved.
  3. He kicked the bumper. "God damn it."
  4. "Would you listen to me, goddamn it?"
  5. Goddamn it, that's the third time this week.

See also: damn it, gosh darn it, dagnabbit

hell#

Pronunciation: /hɛl/ · HEL Type: secondary · Register: very informal Variants: oh hell, hell no

A mild swear used alone for resigned annoyance, and used inside questions as an intensifier that sharpens the whole sentence (where the hell, why the hell). Alone it is milder than damn it; inside a question it is noticeably hotter. It is common enough to pass unnoticed among friends and still counts as swearing in a classroom or a client meeting.

  1. "Hell, I don't know why the server keeps crashing."
  2. Where the hell did I put my keys?
  3. "Hell — I might as well start over."
  4. Why the hell would he say that to her?
  5. Hell, at this point just cancel the whole thing.

See also: what the hell, heck, oh hell

Jesus#

Pronunciation: /ˈdʒizəs/ · JEE-zuhs Type: secondary · Register: vulgar Variants: Jesus Christ, Jesus H. Christ, oh Jesus

An exasperated blasphemous exclamation, usually aimed at a person who has just done something careless or maddening. Jesus Christ is the fuller and angrier form. Both are moderate as swearing and strong as blasphemy, so the room, not the word, decides how badly it lands.

  1. "Jesus, watch where you're going."
  2. Jesus Christ — who parked like that?
  3. He saw the estimate and said, "Jesus."
  4. "Jesus H. Christ, would you two stop?"
  5. Jesus, I've told him four times.

See also: Christ, jeez, jiminy Christmas

oh crap#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈkræp/ · oh-KRAP Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: aw crap, oh crud

Crap with the sinking realization built into it: you have just noticed the problem, and it is yours. It leans more toward dismay than toward anger.

  1. "Oh crap, I double-booked myself."
  2. Oh crap — the dog got into the trash.
  3. She checked the clock. "Oh, crap. I'm late."
  4. "Oh crap! I sent that to the wrong Sarah."
  5. Oh crap, the tickets were for last night.

See also: crap, oh hell, shoot

oh hell#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈhɛl/ · oh-HEL Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: aw hell, oh hell no

A groan of dismayed frustration at bad news or an unwelcome sight. The longer oh hell no is a separate and very emphatic refusal.

  1. "Oh hell, the basement is flooded."
  2. Oh hell — he brought his brother with him.
  3. "Aw hell, I left the tickets on the counter."
  4. She saw the check engine light come on. "Oh hell."
  5. "They want us there at five in the morning." "Oh hell no."

See also: oh crap, hell, to hell with it

screw it#

Pronunciation: /ˈskru ɪt/ · SKROO-it Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: screw this, screw that

A declaration that you are giving up on something out of frustration and moving on — the printable form of fuck it. Screw this aims the same feeling at the situation you are stuck in right now.

  1. "Screw it, I'm ordering pizza."
  2. Screw this — I've been on hold for forty minutes.
  3. He looked at the instructions, then at the pile of parts. "Screw it."
  4. "Screw it, we'll fix it in the next release."
  5. Screw that, I'm not driving four hours for a one-hour meeting.

See also: to hell with it, whatever, fine

screw you#

Pronunciation: /skru ˈju/ · skroo-YOO Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: screw him, screw them, screw off

A hostile insult aimed straight at a person, and the softened form of fuck you. Softened is relative: it still ends conversations and starts arguments. Close friends sometimes use it jokingly, but never assume that.

  1. "Screw you, I did all the work on that."
  2. Screw you — you knew and you didn't say anything.
  3. "You're being dramatic." "Screw you."
  4. He told his brother screw you and hung up.
  5. "Screw him, we'll find another contractor."

See also: go to hell, shut up, whatever

shit#

Pronunciation: /ʃɪt/ · SHIT Type: secondary · Register: vulgar Variants: oh shit, aw shit

The strongest of the everyday one-word swears of frustration, and genuinely taboo in public, professional, and family settings. Shoot, sugar, and crap are the polite substitutes built from it.

  1. "Shit, I locked my keys in the car."
  2. Shit — that's a police car behind us.
  3. He read the number on the screen and said shit very quietly.
  4. "Oh shit, the deadline was yesterday."
  5. Shit, I think I deleted the wrong folder.

See also: shoot, sugar, crap

to hell with it#

Pronunciation: /tə ˈhɛl wɪð ɪt/ · tuh-HEL-with-it Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: the hell with it, to hell with this

A more emphatic screw it: you are abandoning the effort out of anger or exhaustion. It can also be pointed at a person or a rule you have decided to stop respecting.

  1. "To hell with it, I'm going home."
  2. The hell with it — I'll just buy a new one.
  3. She stared at the spreadsheet another minute, then said, "To hell with it."
  4. "To hell with this, the line isn't moving."
  5. To hell with it, let's just tell them the truth.

See also: screw it, fine, that does it

what the hell#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt ðə ˈhɛl/ · wut-thuh-HEL Type: phrase · Register: very informal Variants: what the hell?, the hell

Two distinct uses. 1. An angry or bewildered demand for an explanation, with the stress on hell. 2. Said flat, with a shrug, a decision to take the risk anyway — roughly "why not."

  1. "What the hell were you thinking?"
  2. What the hell — my car was right here an hour ago.
  3. "What the hell is that noise?"
  4. He opened the box. "What the hell? These are the wrong size."
  5. "Should we split the last piece?" "Ah, what the hell — take it."
  6. It's my birthday, so what the hell, I'm getting the expensive one.

See also: what the heck, hell, are you kidding me

Minced oaths#

Every item here is a taboo word with the offense filed off, usually by keeping the first sound and changing everything after it. They are the safe register for classrooms, workplaces, and grandparents, and a few of them now sound so old-fashioned that Americans use them mainly for comic effect.

cheese and crackers#

Pronunciation: /ˌtʃiz ən ˈkrækərz/ · cheez-uhn-KRAK-erz Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: cheese and rice, oh cheese and crackers

A deliberately silly stand-in for Jesus Christ, built to match its rhythm and opening sounds. It is used to be funny about being annoyed rather than to hide real anger.

  1. "Cheese and crackers, look at this mess."
  2. Cheese and crackers — who let the dog on the couch?
  3. "Cheese and rice, that's expensive."
  4. Grandma says cheese and crackers so she doesn't swear in front of the kids.
  5. Cheese and crackers, I forgot the tickets again.

See also: jiminy Christmas, jeez Louise, mother of pearl

criminy#

Pronunciation: /ˈkrɪməni/ · KRIM-uh-nee Type: primary · Register: dated Variants: crimeny, criminey, criminy sakes

An old-fashioned minced form of Christ, used for mild exasperation. It sounds gently folksy today and comes mostly from older speakers or from people reaching for a laugh.

  1. "Criminy, how much did they charge you?"
  2. Criminy — that dog next door barks all night long.
  3. "Criminy sakes, slow down."
  4. He came in stamping the snow off his boots. "Criminy, it is cold out there."
  5. Criminy, these instructions make no sense at all.

See also: cripes, jiminy Christmas, drat

cripes#

Pronunciation: /kraɪps/ · KRYPS Type: primary · Register: dated Variants: oh cripes, for cripes' sake, cripes almighty

A minced form of Christ, used for mild exasperation or startled annoyance. It peaked in mid-century American speech and now sounds like dialogue out of an old comic strip, which is mostly why people reach for it.

  1. "Cripes, would you look at this traffic."
  2. Cripes — the dog just ate an entire stick of butter.
  3. "Oh cripes, I left the water running upstairs."
  4. He got the estimate back from the mechanic. "Cripes."
  5. Hold still, for cripes' sake, I am almost done.

See also: criminy, jeez, jiminy Christmas

crud#

Pronunciation: /krʌd/ · KRUHD Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: oh crud, aw crud

A soft substitute for crap, safe around children and coworkers. It marks small annoyances rather than real anger.

  1. "Crud, I forgot my lunch."
  2. Crud — the pen leaked all over my bag.
  3. "Oh, crud. The store already closed."
  4. She caught a typo in the last line and said crud.
  5. Crud, I think I left the light on in the garage.

See also: crap, darn, shoot

dagnabbit#

Pronunciation: /dæɡˈnæbɪt/ · dag-NAB-it Type: phrase · Register: dated Variants: dagnab it, dad gum it, dadgummit

A rural, old-fashioned scrambling of goddamn it, now heard mainly in cartoons, Westerns, and self-aware humor. Almost nobody says it with a straight face anymore.

  1. "Dagnabbit, the fence is down again."
  2. Dagnabbit — those kids cut through my yard every morning.
  3. "Dad gum it, where did I put my hat?"
  4. He kicked the tire and hollered dagnabbit.
  5. Dagnabbit, I can never get this lid off.

See also: doggone, gosh darn it, dang

dang#

Pronunciation: /dæŋ/ · DANG Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: dang it, dang it all

A soft, slightly Southern-flavored substitute for damn. Like the word it replaces, it works as a complaint when it is clipped and as admiration when it is drawn out.

  1. "Dang, that storm knocked the power out."
  2. Dang it — I dropped my phone in the parking lot.
  3. "Dang, this line is long."
  4. He looked at the dent in the door. "Dang it all."
  5. Dang, I meant to record that game.

See also: darn, damn, doggone

darn#

Pronunciation: /dɑrn/ · DAHRN Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: darn it, darn it all, dern

The standard polite replacement for damn, usable in front of anyone at all. Darn it is the full frustration form, while darn before a noun is just an intensifier for something that is annoying you.

  1. "Darn it, the batteries are dead."
  2. Darn — I missed the turn.
  3. "This darn printer jams every single time."
  4. She dropped a stitch and said, "Darn it all."
  5. Darn, I was hoping they'd still have my size.

See also: dang, damn it, gosh darn it

doggone#

Pronunciation: /ˌdɔɡˈɔn/, /ˌdɑɡˈɑn/ · dawg-GAWN, dahg-GAHN Type: secondary · Register: dated Variants: doggone it, doggonit, doggoned

A folksy minced oath for goddamn, used both as an exclamation and as a mild intensifier in front of a noun. It reads as small-town and gently old-fashioned.

  1. "Doggone it, the mower won't start."
  2. Doggone — the deer got into the tomatoes again.
  3. "Where is my doggone wrench?"
  4. He shook his head slowly. "Doggone it all anyway."
  5. Doggone it, I forgot to mail the check.

See also: dagnabbit, darn, tarnation

drat#

Pronunciation: /dræt/ · DRAT Type: primary · Register: dated Variants: drat it, drat it all

A quaint, mild curse of annoyance, historically a squeezed-down "God rot it." It survives mostly in children's books and in playful speech.

  1. "Drat, the cookies burned."
  2. Drat — I forgot my umbrella again.
  3. "Drat it all, the cat got out."
  4. She missed the bus by ten seconds. "Drat."
  5. Drat, there goes my whole afternoon.

See also: rats, phooey, fiddlesticks

eff#

Pronunciation: /ɛf/ · EF Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: eff it, effing, what the eff, eff off

The letter f said out loud in place of fuck — the most transparent minced oath in the language, since it names the first letter of the word it is dodging. It fills every slot the original does: eff it, effing, what the eff, and the hostile eff off.

  1. "Eff it, I'm taking the train."
  2. What the eff — somebody parked across two spaces again.
  3. This effing printer has been broken since March.
  4. She caught herself in front of her mother-in-law and said eff instead.
  5. "He told me to eff off," she said, still furious about it a week later.

See also: freaking, frick, fudge

fiddlesticks#

Pronunciation: /ˈfɪdəlstɪks/ · FID-uhl-stiks Type: secondary · Register: dated Variants: oh fiddlesticks

A gentle exclamation of annoyance or dismissal, meaning roughly "how irritating" or "nonsense." It is strongly old-fashioned and usually said with a wink.

  1. "Fiddlesticks, I've lost my reading glasses again."
  2. Fiddlesticks — the recipe calls for buttermilk and I have none.
  3. "You'll never finish in time." "Fiddlesticks. Watch me."
  4. She looked at the broken zipper and said fiddlesticks.
  5. Fiddlesticks, the bank closed ten minutes ago.

See also: phooey, drat, nuts

freaking#

Pronunciation: /ˈfrikɪŋ/ · FREE-king Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: freakin', frigging, friggin', flipping

Not a standalone interjection but the intensifier that carries the anger inside other expressions — the minced substitute for fucking. It slots in before adjectives and nouns and inside fixed phrases like are you freaking kidding me.

  1. "Are you freaking kidding me?"
  2. This freaking door sticks every time it rains.
  3. "Freakin' unbelievable," he said, staring at the scoreboard.
  4. I have been on hold for two freaking hours.
  5. "Would you just open the frigging window?"

See also: eff, frick, are you kidding me

frick#

Pronunciation: /frɪk/ · FRIK Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: frick it, what the frick, frickin'

A minced form of fuck, common with teenagers and with anyone trying to keep it clean. What the frick and frick it mirror the phrases it replaces, sound for sound.

  1. "Frick, I forgot my homework."
  2. What the frick — who ate the last slice?
  3. "Frick it, I'll just walk."
  4. He stubbed his toe on the bed frame and yelled frick.
  5. Are you frickin' serious right now?

See also: freaking, eff, fudge

fudge#

Pronunciation: /fʌdʒ/ · FUHJ Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: oh fudge, fudge it

A candy-flavored substitute for fuck, chosen because it starts the same way. Saying it out loud usually announces that you are consciously not swearing, which is half the point of using it.

  1. "Fudge, I spilled coffee on my shirt."
  2. Oh fudge — the recipe needed two eggs, not one.
  3. "Fudge!" she said, catching herself in front of the kids.
  4. He hit his thumb with the hammer. "Fudge."
  5. Fudge, the meeting started five minutes ago.

See also: frick, shoot, sugar

good grief#

Pronunciation: /ɡʊd ˈɡrif/ · gud-GREEF Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: oh good grief

An exasperated reaction to something excessive, ridiculous, or exhausting. It is weary rather than hostile and is completely safe in mixed company.

  1. "Good grief, how much luggage did you bring?"
  2. Good grief — this form is eleven pages long.
  3. "Good grief. They raised the price again."
  4. She saw the line stretching out the door. "Good grief, forget it."
  5. Good grief, just let the man finish a sentence.

See also: oh brother, sheesh, for crying out loud

gosh#

Pronunciation: /ɡɑʃ/ · GAHSH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: oh gosh, my gosh, gosh no

The everyday minced form of God, and the syllable that gosh darn it and for gosh sakes are built on. In this category it carries mild exasperation rather than shock, and it is safe in front of absolutely anyone.

  1. "Gosh, would you two give it a rest."
  2. Gosh — that is the fourth time he has rescheduled on me.
  3. "Oh gosh, I completely forgot to call the vet back."
  4. She went through the receipts twice. "My gosh, where is it all going?"
  5. Slow down, gosh, nobody is chasing you.

See also: gosh darn it, jeez, for God's sake

gosh darn it#

Pronunciation: /ˌɡɑʃ ˈdɑrn ɪt/ · gahsh-DAHRN-it Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: gosh darn, goshdarnit, gosh dang it, gol dang it

A doubly minced goddamn it — gosh for God and darn for damn. It is the safest way to sound genuinely fed up in front of anyone.

  1. "Gosh darn it, the mower's out of gas."
  2. Gosh darn it — I asked for no onions.
  3. "Gosh dang it, that's the wrong screw again."
  4. He fumbled the keys twice. "Gosh darn it all."
  5. Gosh darn it, I left my wallet at the restaurant.

See also: darn, goddamn it, dagnabbit

H-E-double-hockey-sticks#

Oath phrases: for ___'s sake#

A single fixed frame with a swappable holy name. The strength of the phrase depends entirely on which name goes in the slot: Christ and God are the real oaths, while Pete, heaven, and crying out loud are the substitutions that make the same exasperation printable.

for Christ's sake#

Pronunciation: /fər ˈkraɪsts ˌseɪk/ · fer-KRYSTS-sayk Type: phrase · Register: vulgar Variants: for Christ sake, for Christ's sakes

A blasphemous plea for someone to stop being unreasonable. It is the sharpest member of the for ...'s sake family, and to a religious listener it is not exasperation but profanity.

  1. "For Christ's sake, just answer the question."
  2. For Christ's sake — the meeting was supposed to start an hour ago.
  3. "Pick up the phone, for Christ's sake."
  4. He threw up his hands. "For Christ's sake, I already told you."
  5. For Christ's sake, it isn't that complicated.

A wholesome expression of exasperation that stands in for the religious oaths it imitates. It is the safest way to announce to a room that you have had enough.

Telling someone to stop#

Volitive interjections: short commands whose job is to end a behavior. They are ordered here roughly from a protest (come on) to a genuine warning (that does it), and all of them get their force from a falling, clipped delivery rather than from volume.

back off#

Pronunciation: /ˌbæk ˈɔf/ · bak-AWF Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: back off!, back the heck off, back way off

A warning to stop pushing — physically, or more often with questions, pressure, or criticism. It is more confrontational than cut it out because it treats the other person as a threat rather than as a nuisance.

  1. "Back off. I said I would handle it."
  2. Back off — she has had a rough enough week already.
  3. He was standing about six inches away, so I told him to back off.
  4. The two of them squared up until the bartender said, "Back off, both of you."
  5. Back off a little and let the kid figure it out himself.

See also: knock it off, cut it out, give it a rest

come on#

Pronunciation: /kəˈmɑn/ · kuh-MAHN Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: c'mon, oh come on, aw come on

A protest that something is unfair, unreasonable, or obviously wrong. Stretched out with a falling pitch it means "you cannot be serious"; short and clipped it simply means "hurry up."

  1. "Come on, that was clearly a foul."
  2. Oh come on — you promised you'd be there.
  3. "C'mon, we're going to miss the previews."
  4. He watched a third car cut him off. "Come on!"
  5. Come on, nobody actually believes that.

See also: give me a break, oh please, seriously

cut it out#

Pronunciation: /ˌkʌt ɪt ˈaʊt/ · kut-it-OWT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: cut that out, cut it

A direct order to stop an annoying behavior, most often aimed at children, siblings, or friends. It is firm without being hostile.

  1. "Cut it out, both of you."
  2. Cut it out — I'm trying to listen.
  3. "You keep kicking my seat. Cut it out."
  4. She heard the giggling start up again. "Cut that out."
  5. Cut it out, that stopped being funny an hour ago.

An order to stop talking about one particular thing. Where cut it out is about behavior, drop it is about a topic, and the sharpness usually means the topic hurts.

  1. "Drop it, Dad. I don't want to talk about it."

A flat command to stop, said when patience has run out. It carries real authority and works from a parent, a teacher, or a manager without sounding coarse.

  3. "That's enough, we are not discussing it any further."

Exasperated disbelief#

Questions and adverbs that have stopped asking anything. Every one of these is a complaint dressed as a question, so the answer is never expected, and the pitch usually falls at the end even when there is a question mark on the page.

An outraged question that expects no answer — you are protesting that something is absurd or unfair. The stress lands hard on kidding.

  1. "Are you kidding me? They canceled it again?"
  2. Are you kidding — forty-five dollars for parking?
  3. "The game's rained out." "Are you kidding me?"
  4. She stared at the empty pan. "Are you serious? I left for one minute."
  5. Are you kidding me, the deadline moved up a week?

honestly#

Pronunciation: /ˈɑnəstli/ · AHN-ist-lee Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: oh honestly

Said alone with a sigh, a small expression of despairing exasperation at someone's behavior — roughly "I cannot believe you." It is mild enough for almost any setting, including the office.

  1. "Honestly. You'd lose your head if it weren't attached."
  2. Honestly — I turn my back for one minute.
  3. "He did it again." "Honestly!"
  4. She picked the wet towel up off the bed. "Honestly, every single day."
  5. Honestly, I don't know why I bother.

oh brother#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈbrʌðər/ · oh-BRUHTH-er Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: brother

A weary eye-roll put into words, used when someone is being dramatic, ridiculous, or entirely predictable. It is dismissive but harmless.

  1. "Oh brother, here we go again."
  2. Oh brother — he's telling the fishing story.
  3. "She says the whole thing is my fault." "Oh brother."
  4. He watched the fine print scroll past. "Oh, brother."
  5. Oh brother, not another software update.

oh please#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈpliz/ · oh-PLEEZ Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: please, oh, please

A scornful rejection of what someone just said: you find it exaggerated, insincere, or ridiculous. The tone is flat and falling, nothing like the pleading please.

  1. "Oh please, you were nowhere near the finish line."
  2. Oh please — he's been saying that for years.
  3. "I would have won if the wind hadn't picked up." "Oh, please."
  4. She rolled her eyes at the commercial. "Please. Nothing works that fast."
  5. Oh please, like anybody's going to notice.

really#

Pronunciation: /ˈrili/, /ˈrɪli/ · REE-lee, RIL-ee Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: really?, oh really

Said flat, a rebuke: you are pointing out that someone has just done something thoughtless. Said with a rising pitch the same word is ordinary surprise instead, so the tone carries the entire meaning.

  1. "Really. You parked across two spaces."
  2. Really — you're going to eat that over my keyboard?
  3. "I told her you said it." "Really? Thanks a lot."
  4. He watched his roommate leave the dish in the sink. "Really, man?"
  5. Really, after everything I did for him?

seriously#

Pronunciation: /ˈsɪriəsli/ · SEER-ee-uhs-lee Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: seriously?, are you serious

An exasperated challenge meaning "you cannot possibly be doing this." Flat and falling it is a complaint; rising, it is disbelief.

  1. "Seriously? You ate the last one?"
  2. Seriously — it has been down all morning.
  3. "The meeting moved to five o'clock." "Seriously."
  4. She found the printer jammed for the third time. "Seriously?"
  5. Seriously, who leaves a shopping cart in the middle of the lot?

the nerve#

Pronunciation: /ðə ˈnɜrv/ · thuh-NURV Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: the nerve of him, the nerve of some people, of all the nerve

An outraged verdict on somebody's gall. It is said about the offender rather than to them, and it usually arrives about ten seconds after they have left the room.

  1. "The nerve. He asked me to cover his shift on my day off."
  2. The nerve of some people — she took the spot I was clearly waiting for.
  3. "And then he asked for a refund." "The nerve of him."
  4. She read the email twice, just to be sure she had it right. "Of all the nerve."
  5. Charging eight dollars for a bottle of water — the nerve.

unbelievable#

Pronunciation: /ˌʌnbɪˈlivəbəl/ · un-bih-LEE-vuh-buhl Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: absolutely unbelievable, un-be-lievable

A one-word verdict on something outrageous. In the right context it can be admiring, but said flatly about a problem it is pure anger.

  1. "Unbelievable. They towed my car."
  2. Unbelievable — three hours late and not one phone call.
  3. "He denied the whole thing." "Unbelievable."
  4. She looked at the bill. "Absolutely unbelievable."
  5. Unbelievable, I fixed that yesterday and it's already broken.

what is wrong with you#

Pronunciation: /ˌwʌt ɪz ˈrɔŋ wɪð ju/ · wut-iz-RAWNG-with-yoo Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: what's wrong with you, what is wrong with people, what the hell is wrong with you

An angry question about somebody's judgment, not their health: you are saying that what they did was so thoughtless it needs explaining. Aimed at a stranger it is close to a challenge; aimed at a friend it is a scolding.

  1. "What is wrong with you? That's a fire exit."
  2. What's wrong with you — you cannot say that to a customer.
  3. "He posted the whole conversation online." "What is wrong with people?"
  4. She found the crayon mural in the hallway. "What is wrong with you two?"
  5. Honestly, what is wrong with you, letting the dog drink out of the pool.

Pronunciation: /ju ˌɡɑtə bi ˈkɪdɪŋ mi/ · yoo-gah-tuh-bee-KID-ing-mee Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: you've got to be kidding me, you have got to be kidding me, you gotta be kidding

A stronger, more theatrical are you kidding me, aimed at the universe as much as at any person. It marks the moment something goes wrong on top of something that was already wrong.

  1. "You gotta be kidding me — a second flat tire?"
  2. You've got to be kidding me. The reservation was for tomorrow?
  3. "They lost the paperwork." "You have got to be kidding me."
  4. He watched the elevator doors close in his face. "You gotta be kidding me."
  5. You gotta be kidding me, I just paid that off.

Sarcasm and grudging surrender#

Ordinary positive words that mean their exact opposite when the pitch falls flat at the end. This is the single most common place learners take an American literally, and in writing the tell is the punctuation: a period keeps the sarcasm, an exclamation point would make the praise sincere.

big whoop#

Pronunciation: /ˌbɪɡ ˈwup/ · big-WOOP Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: big whoop-de-doo, big deal, whoop-de-doo

A flat, sarcastic refusal to be impressed by whatever somebody has just announced. The whoop is a mock cheer, and delivering it without any energy at all is the entire joke.

  1. "Big whoop. I've been doing that job for six years."
  2. "He got a certificate." "Big whoop."
  3. Big whoop — a ten-dollar gift card for working every weekend in December.
  4. She read the employee-of-the-month plaque and muttered, "big whoop."
  5. So he apologized. Big deal. He should never have said it in the first place.

See also: whatever, oh joy, thanks a lot

fine#

Pronunciation: /faɪn/ · FYN Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: fine!, oh fine, well fine

A clipped surrender that means the opposite of fine: you are giving in while making sure everyone knows you are unhappy about it. The short, falling fine is one of the most passive-aggressive words in American English.

  1. "Fine. Do it your way."
  2. Fine — I'll drive.
  3. "You have to be there at eight." "Fine."
  4. She grabbed her coat off the hook. "Fine, I'm going."
  5. Fine, don't listen to me.

See also: whatever, screw it, great

great#

Pronunciation: /ɡreɪt/ · GRAYT Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: oh great, that's great

Said flat and falling, the sarcastic opposite of its literal meaning: something has just gone wrong. Learners regularly take this one at face value.

  1. "Great. The car won't start."
  2. Oh great — now it's raining.
  3. "Your flight is delayed four hours." "Great."
  4. He found the milk carton empty in the fridge. "Great."
  5. Great, now I have to do the whole thing over.

just great#

Pronunciation: /ˌdʒʌst ˈɡreɪt/ · just-GRAYT Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: that's just great, oh just great

Great with the sarcasm made unmistakable by the just. It is almost never sincere.

  1. "Just great. The Wi-Fi is out again."
  2. Oh, just great — I left the tickets at home.
  3. "That's just great, now we're both stuck here."
  4. She read the cancellation notice on the door. "Just great."
  5. Just great, the one day I forget my umbrella.

Pronunciation: /naɪs/ · NYS Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: real nice, nice going, nice one

With flat intonation, a sarcastic jab at something clumsy or inconsiderate that somebody just did. With rising intonation the same word is a real compliment, so tone is everything.

  1. "Nice. You just spilled it on the rug."
  2. Nice going — you woke the baby.
  3. "I told her about the party." "Real nice."
  4. He watched the driver take up two spots. "Nice."
  5. Nice one, now the whole file is gone.

oh joy#

Pronunciation: /oʊ ˈdʒɔɪ/ · oh-JOY Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: oh joy of joys, what joy

Sarcastic delight at something you are dreading. It is drier and more theatrical than great, and it usually introduces the bad news instead of reacting to it.

  1. "Oh joy. Another all-hands meeting."
  2. Oh joy — my in-laws are staying for two weeks.
  3. "You're on the schedule for New Year's Eve." "Oh joy."
  4. He opened the envelope and found a jury summons. "Oh, joy."
  5. Three connecting flights, oh joy, and not one of them on the same airline.

Pronunciation: /ˈpɜrfɪkt/ · PUR-fikt Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: oh perfect, just perfect

A sarcastic verdict on a new problem arriving at the worst possible moment. Flat delivery is the only thing marking it as sarcasm.

  1. "Perfect. The printer is out of toner."
  2. Oh perfect — a flat tire on the way to the airport.
  3. "They moved the meeting to Friday at five." "Perfect."
  4. She looked at the coffee stain on her white shirt. "Just perfect."
  5. Perfect, now the neighbors are mowing.

  2. Oh swell — they gave away our table.

  5. So now I'm paying for both of them. Swell.
