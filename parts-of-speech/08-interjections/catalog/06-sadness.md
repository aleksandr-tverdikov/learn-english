# Sadness, sympathy, disappointment, and resignation

> The interjections Americans reach for when something goes wrong — to feel bad about it, to feel bad *for* someone else, or to shrug and move on.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

This category runs from real grief (*alas*, *my condolences*) through the enormous middle ground of everyday letdown (*dang*, *shoot*, *bummer*, *oh no*) and out the other side into acceptance (*oh well*, *welp*, *it is what it is*). Two things run through almost all of it. First, **tone carries the meaning**: *too bad*, *poor baby*, and *boo-hoo* are sincere sympathy with a falling, gentle delivery and pure sarcasm with a flat one, and nothing in the spelling tells you which. Second, **a large share of these words are minced oaths** — *dang*, *darn*, *shoot*, *fudge*, *heck*, *doggone*, *tarnation* — polite disguises for stronger words, which is why they are safe at work and around children while their originals are not. In writing, most of these take an exclamation point when sharp (*Rats!*) and a period or comma when resigned (*Oh well.*), and lengthening the vowel in print (*awww*, *oh nooo*) lengthens it in the reader's ear. A few members of this group are not really words at all — a *sigh*, a *groan*, the descending *womp womp* — and their entries give the pronunciation of the word you type, then describe the noise separately, because the noise itself has no speech sounds in it.

## Sympathy and comfort

### aw

**Pronunciation:** /ɔ/ &middot; *AW* (with the vowel of *saw*; many Americans merge this with /ɑ/, the vowel of *father*)
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *aww*, *awww*, *awwww*

A soft, falling sound of tenderness or sympathy — the noise you make at a puppy, a baby picture, or bad news a friend has just shared. Extra *w*'s in writing signal a longer, warmer coo; delivered flat and short instead, the same sound turns into disappointment.

1. "*Aw*, look at his little face."
2. *Aww*, you really didn't have to get me anything.
3. "I didn't make the team." — "*Aw*, I'm sorry."
4. She sent a picture of the kitten and I texted back *awww* with about six *w*'s.
5. *Aw*, that was so sweet of her to remember.
6. "*Aw*, come on — the movie was only half over."

**See also:** [poor thing](#poor-thing), [aw man](#aw-man), [too bad](#too-bad)

### bless your heart

**Pronunciation:** /ˌblɛs jɚ ˈhɑrt/ &middot; *bless yer HART*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** regional (Southern) &mdash; frequently a polite insult
**Variants:** *bless her heart*, *bless his heart*, *bless it*

A Southern formula that wraps sympathy around someone's misfortune or shortcoming. Said warmly it is genuine pity; attached to a criticism, or delivered with a certain sweetness, it is a socially acceptable way to call someone foolish — and Americans outside the South often hear only that second meaning.

1. "She drove two hours in the rain to bring my wallet back, *bless her heart*."
2. *Bless your heart*, you've had a week, haven't you?
3. "He tried to fix the sink himself and flooded the kitchen, *bless him*."
4. Grandma looked at my sunburn and said, "*Bless your heart*, did you not bring a hat?"
5. "*Bless her heart*, she means well, but that woman cannot cook."
6. He thought that was a compliment. *Bless his heart.*

**See also:** [poor thing](#poor-thing), [aw](#aw), [there there](#there-there)

### my condolences

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ kənˈdoʊlənsəz/ &middot; *my kun-DOH-lun-siz*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *my deepest condolences*, *our condolences*, *condolences*

The set formula for expressing sympathy after a death. Its formality is the point: it works for coworkers, clients, and acquaintances, where a warmer "I'm so sorry" might feel too intimate. Among close friends and family it can sound stiff.

1. "*My condolences* on the loss of your father."
2. Please pass along *my condolences* to the whole family.
3. "*My deepest condolences*," the director said, shaking his hand at the door.
4. I wrote a short note — just *condolences* and an offer to help with the kids.
5. *Our condolences* to everyone at the firm; he will be badly missed.

**See also:** [poor thing](#poor-thing), [too bad](#too-bad), [what a shame](#what-a-shame)

### poor thing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpʊr ˈθɪŋ/ &middot; *poor THING* (the first word is also commonly /pɔr/, rhyming with *pour*)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (condescending, and often sarcastic, when aimed at another adult)
**Variants:** *poor baby*, *poor you*, *poor guy*, *you poor thing*

A quick expression of pity for someone — or an animal — who is suffering or unlucky. It is warm toward children, pets, and close friends, but aimed at another adult it can land as condescending, and *poor baby* in particular is very often sarcastic.

1. "He's been throwing up since midnight." — "*Poor thing*."
2. *You poor thing* — sit down, I'll get you some water.
3. The dog hid under the bed through the whole fireworks show, *poor guy*.
4. "*Poor baby*," she said flatly. "You had to wait ten whole minutes."
5. She worked a double and then her car wouldn't start, *poor thing*.

**See also:** [aw](#aw), [there there](#there-there), [bless your heart](#bless-your-heart), [too bad](#too-bad)

### sorry for your loss

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɑri fɚ jɚ ˈlɔs/ &middot; *SAR-ee fer yer LAWSS* (many Americans say /ˈsɔri/ for the first word, and the merged /ˈlɑs/ for the last)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *I'm sorry for your loss*, *so sorry for your loss*, *sorry about your loss*

The ordinary American condolence, spoken at a wake or written in a card. It is warmer and far more common than *my condolences* and fits almost any degree of closeness — though heard forty times in one afternoon it starts to sound like a script, which is exactly why some people reach for something plainer instead.

1. "*I'm sorry for your loss*," the nurse said, and left us alone with him.
2. *So sorry for your loss.* Tell me if the kids need rides this week.
3. He stood at the door of the funeral home and said *sorry for your loss* to two hundred people.
4. "*Sorry about your loss* — I had no idea your dad was sick."
5. She signed the card with her name and nothing else, because *sorry for your loss* felt too small.
6. "My grandmother passed on Sunday." — "Oh, honey. *I'm so sorry for your loss*."

**See also:** [my condolences](#my-condolences), [poor thing](#poor-thing), [what a shame](#what-a-shame), [rip](#rip)

### that sucks

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæt ˈsʌks/ &middot; *that SUKS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal &mdash; mildly crude; the verb's obscene origin is faded but not gone, and older or stricter listeners still hear it
**Variants:** *this sucks*, *that really sucks*, *sucks*, *that sucks, man*

The default sympathetic reply to a friend's bad news for most Americans under about sixty. In casual speech it has largely displaced *too bad*, but it is genuinely out of place in a condolence card, a message to a client, or a classroom, and saying it to someone's grandmother will land badly.

1. "They cut my hours again." — "*That sucks*."
2. *This sucks.* We drove four hours and the museum was closed.
3. "*That really sucks*, man. Do you want me to come get you?"
4. She lost the deposit and the apartment in the same afternoon, which *sucks*.
5. "I got put on the Thanksgiving shift." — "Ugh, *that sucks*."
6. He said "*sucks*" and nothing else, which from him counted as sympathy.

**See also:** [too bad](#too-bad), [bummer](#bummer), [tough luck](#tough-luck), [oof](#oof)

### there there

**Pronunciation:** /ˈðɛr ˌðɛr/ &middot; *THAIR thair*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *there, there*, *there now*

Soothing words said while comforting someone who is crying or upset, usually along with a pat on the back or shoulder. It is gentle but faintly old-fashioned and parental, so between adults it often comes out half-joking.

1. "*There, there.* It's just a scraped knee."
2. He patted her shoulder and said *there there* until the crying slowed down.
3. "*There, there*, you'll find another apartment."
4. *There now*, nobody is mad at you.
5. I told him about the layoff and he said "*there there*" in the least helpful voice imaginable.

**See also:** [aw](#aw), [poor thing](#poor-thing), [boo-hoo](#boo-hoo)

### too bad

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtu ˈbæd/ &middot; *too BAD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *that's too bad*, *aw, too bad*

A short response to bad news or a missed opportunity, ranging from real sympathy to a flat brush-off. Tone does all the work: warm and falling, it means "I'm sorry to hear that"; clipped and level, it means "that is not my problem."

1. "They gave the job to someone else." — "*Aw, too bad*."
2. *Too bad* about the rain — that picnic would have been perfect.
3. "*That's too bad.* She was really looking forward to it."
4. "I don't want to go." — "*Too bad.* You're going."
5. It's *too bad* we didn't find out until Friday afternoon.

**See also:** [what a shame](#what-a-shame), [bummer](#bummer), [that sucks](#that-sucks), [poor thing](#poor-thing)

### tough luck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʌf ˈlʌk/ &middot; *tuff LUK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (bare *tough luck* is usually a brush-off, not sympathy)
**Variants:** *tough break*, *that's tough*, *tough*, *tough one*, *tough noogies*

A remark on somebody's bad fortune whose meaning flips with the wording. *Tough break* and *that's tough* are nearly always sincere; bare *tough luck* almost always is not — it is a flat refusal to care, one step from "not my problem." *Tough noogies* is the playground version and stays joking.

1. "He blew out his knee two weeks before the meet." — "*Tough break*."
2. "I lost the receipt." — "*Tough luck.* No receipt, no refund."
3. *Tough break* — she missed qualifying by a tenth of a second.
4. "That's a *tough* one. I honestly don't know what I'd do either."
5. "But I don't want to sit in the middle seat." — "*Tough noogies*."
6. He got laid off three weeks into the job; *tough luck* doesn't begin to cover it.

**See also:** [too bad](#too-bad), [that sucks](#that-sucks), [bummer](#bummer), [that's life](#thats-life)

### what a shame

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt ə ˈʃeɪm/ &middot; *wut uh SHAYM*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *such a shame*, *a shame*, *shame*

A regretful comment on a situation that turned out badly, especially one where something good was wasted or lost. It points at circumstances rather than at anybody's fault, which is what separates it from blame.

1. "They tore down the old theater." — "*What a shame*."
2. *Such a shame* — she trained for that race all year.
3. "It's *a shame* you couldn't stay longer."
4. All that food went straight into the trash. *What a shame.*
5. *What a shame* the weather turned; the view is beautiful on a clear day.

**See also:** [too bad](#too-bad), [bummer](#bummer), [my condolences](#my-condolences)

## Grief, lament, and crying

### alack

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlæk/ &middot; *uh-LAK*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** archaic (surviving only as a joke)
**Variants:** *alack-a-day*, *alas and alack*

An archaic cry of grief or regret, the old companion to *alas*. No American says it straight today; it survives almost entirely inside the fixed pair *alas and alack* and in deliberate mock-Shakespearean joking.

1. "*Alas and alack*, the coffee maker is broken again."
2. The stage manager read the cancellation notice out loud and added, "*Alack*."
3. She threw a hand across her forehead: "*Alack!* I am undone!"
4. The email ended with "*alas and alack*," which told me the answer was no.
5. *Alack-a-day* — the only hardware store in town is closed on Sundays.

**See also:** [alas](#alas), [woe is me](#woe-is-me)

### alas

**Pronunciation:** /əˈlæs/ &middot; *uh-LASS*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *alas and alack*

A word of regret introducing something unfortunate. In modern American English it is literary or ironic — in speech it usually just means "sadly, no," delivered with a small smile.

1. "*Alas*, we're out of the chocolate ones."
2. I checked the schedule; *alas*, the last train leaves at ten.
3. "Did you get the promotion?" — "*Alas*, no."
4. *Alas*, the recipe calls for a pan I do not own.
5. He wanted to come, but *alas*, his flight was canceled.

**See also:** [alack](#alack), [woe is me](#woe-is-me), [oh dear](#oh-dear)

### boo-hoo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbu ˈhu/ &middot; *boo-HOO* — a conventional spelling of crying rather than a transcription of it; real sobbing has no fixed consonants
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal (openly mocking when aimed at another person)
**Variants:** *boohoo*, *boo hoo hoo*

An imitation of loud crying. Occasionally it describes real sobbing, but far more often an American says it sarcastically, to dismiss someone's complaint as self-pity — which is why it stings.

1. "I had to wake up at nine." — "*Boo-hoo.* Some of us started at five."
2. The toddler sat down in the driveway and went *boo-hoo-hoo* until somebody picked him up.
3. *Boo hoo*, the millionaire has to fly commercial.
4. He rubbed both eyes with his fists and went "*boo-hoo*" until I finally laughed.
5. He kept saying *boo-hoo* whenever I mentioned my knee, which did not help.

**See also:** [waah](#waah), [sob](#sob), [sad face](#sad-face)

### groan

**Pronunciation:** The word is /ɡroʊn/ &middot; *GROHN*. The sound it names is not made of speech sounds at all: it is a low, voiced exhalation on a falling pitch with the mouth barely open, no consonants and no settled vowel. Written attempts at the noise itself come out as *ughhh* or *unnnh*.
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (also internet/text)
**Variants:** *big groan*, *audible groan*; often typed between asterisks

Like *sigh* and *sob*, the word stands in for the noise instead of making it — usually a stage direction in text marking that a pun, a chore, or a piece of scheduling news has landed badly. Said out loud, it is a joke about the very reaction it names.

1. "*Groan.* They moved the meeting to seven in the morning."
2. She read the pun off the popsicle stick and the whole table went *groan*.
3. \*groan\* — two more forms, and then a third form about the forms.
4. He typed *groan* and then, a second later, "fine, I'll do it."
5. My *audible groan* apparently carried into the next cubicle.
6. "The dentist wants to see you again Thursday." — "*Groan.*"

**See also:** [sigh](#sigh), [sob](#sob), [oh brother](#oh-brother), [oof](#oof)

### oy vey

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔɪ ˈveɪ/ &middot; *oy VAY*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (regional — Yiddish-American, strongly associated with New York, but widely understood)
**Variants:** *oy*, *oy gevalt*, *oy vey iz mir*

A Yiddish cry of dismay, weariness, or "what a mess." It has spread far beyond Jewish American speech and reacts to trouble that is exhausting rather than tragic.

1. "*Oy vey*, the printer is jammed again."
2. She looked at the repair estimate and just said, "*Oy*."
3. *Oy vey* — three flights of stairs with all this luggage?
4. "My mother invited forty people." — "*Oy gevalt*."
5. *Oy*, what a day.

**See also:** [oh brother](#oh-brother), [sigh](#sigh), [woe is me](#woe-is-me)

### sigh

**Pronunciation:** The word is /saɪ/ &middot; *SIGH* (rhymes with *my*). An actual sigh is not speech at all — a long, audible out-breath, mostly voiceless, with no consonants or vowels in it to transcribe.
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (also internet/text)
**Variants:** *heavy sigh*, *big sigh*; often typed between asterisks

The word for the breath itself, spoken or typed instead of actually sighing, to mark weariness, mild disappointment, or resignation. In writing it usually appears between asterisks as a kind of stage direction.

1. "*Sigh.* Another meeting that could have been an email."
2. She texted back a single *sigh* and then "fine, I'll drive."
3. *Heavy sigh* — I guess I'm redoing the whole slide deck.
4. He said "*sigh*" out loud instead of sighing, which somehow made it funnier.
5. *Sigh.* Two more hours of this.

**See also:** [oh well](#oh-well), [welp](#welp), [ah well](#ah-well), [groan](#groan)

### sob

**Pronunciation:** /sɑb/ &middot; *SAHB*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (also internet/text)
**Variants:** *sob sob*, *sniff*

A typed or spoken stand-in for crying, used the way *sigh* is used — as a stage direction rather than a real sound. Online it is almost always playful, marking exaggerated distress over something small.

1. "They discontinued my shampoo. *Sob*."
2. He held up the cracked screen and said, "*Sob*."
3. *Sob* — that season finale destroyed me.
4. "Last day of vacation." — "*Sob sob*."
5. She wrote *sniff* under the photo of her empty dorm room.

**See also:** [boo-hoo](#boo-hoo), [sad face](#sad-face), [waah](#waah), [groan](#groan)

### waah

**Pronunciation:** /wɑː/ &middot; *WAAH*, held long — a stylized spelling; a real infant cry is a strained, high-pitched wail with no consonant at the front
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal (imitating a small child; mocking, and often insulting, when aimed at an adult)
**Variants:** *wah*, *waaah*, *waaaah*

The written sound of a baby or small child crying at full volume. Adults use it either to imitate a child or, sarcastically, to accuse someone of whining.

1. The baby went *waaah* the second the car stopped moving.
2. "*Waah*, I don't wanna go to bed," he said, imitating his little brother.
3. *Wah*, poor you, you have to work on a Saturday.
4. She typed "*waaaah*" in the group chat when the concert sold out.
5. Somewhere down the hall a kid was going *waah waah waah*.

**See also:** [boo-hoo](#boo-hoo), [sob](#sob)

### woe is me

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwoʊ ɪz ˈmi/ &middot; *woh iz MEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated (now almost always jokey)
**Variants:** *oh, woe is me*

An old-fashioned lament, used today almost entirely in mockery — poking fun at your own self-pity or at someone else's. Said straight, it would sound theatrical.

1. "*Woe is me*, I have to go to the DMV."
2. He flopped onto the couch: "*Woe is me!*"
3. She's been doing the whole *woe is me* routine since the breakup.
4. *Woe is me* — my package is arriving Thursday instead of Wednesday.
5. "I burned the toast." — "*Woe is me.*"

**See also:** [alas](#alas), [boo-hoo](#boo-hoo), [poor thing](#poor-thing)

## Disappointment and dismay

### aw man

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔ ˈmæn/ &middot; *aw MAN*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *aw, man*, *oh man*, *man*

A falling groan of disappointment at news you did not want — a canceled plan, a sold-out show, a lost game. It is one of the most common spoken reactions to small bad news in American English.

1. "*Aw man*, they're out of the good donuts."
2. *Oh man*, I left my charger at the hotel.
3. "The game got rained out." — "*Aw, man.*"
4. *Man*, I really thought we had that one.
5. "*Aw man*, do we have to?"

**See also:** [aw](#aw), [bummer](#bummer), [oh no](#oh-no), [dang](#dang)

### blast

**Pronunciation:** /blæst/ &middot; *BLAST*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated (chiefly British; in American mouths, theatrical)
**Variants:** *blast it*, *blast it all*

A mild, old-fashioned oath of annoyance and disappointment — more common in British English and in older American writing than in current American speech. An American who says it today is usually being playfully theatrical.

1. "*Blast!* I left the tickets on the kitchen counter."
2. *Blast it*, the store closed ten minutes ago.
3. He looked at the flat tire and muttered, "*Blast*."
4. "*Blast it all*, where did I put my glasses?"
5. *Blast* — the file saved right over the old version.

**See also:** [drat](#drat), [curses](#curses), [dang](#dang)

### bummer

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʌmɚ/ &middot; *BUM-er*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *what a bummer*, *major bummer*, *bummer, dude*

A one-word verdict that something is disappointing. It works both as sympathy for someone else's bad luck and as a comment on your own, and it still carries a faint 1970s surfer flavor that has never quite worn off.

1. "My flight got pushed to tomorrow." — "*Bummer*."
2. *What a bummer* — I was really looking forward to that show.
3. "*Bummer*, dude. Next time."
4. The whole trip fell apart two days out, which was a major *bummer*.
5. "*Bummer*," he said, and handed the broken racket back.

**See also:** [too bad](#too-bad), [what a shame](#what-a-shame), [that sucks](#that-sucks), [oof](#oof), [aw man](#aw-man)

### crud

**Pronunciation:** /krʌd/ &middot; *KRUD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh crud*, *aw crud*

A very mild, family-safe substitute for a stronger word, said when something goes wrong. It sounds slightly goofy, which is a good part of its appeal.

1. "*Crud*, I forgot to move the laundry."
2. *Oh crud* — is that due today?
3. He hit save, saw the error message, and said "*crud*" under his breath.
4. "*Crud.* The milk's expired."
5. *Crud*, we are going to be so late.

**See also:** [shoot](#shoot), [dang](#dang), [darn](#darn), [heck](#heck)

### curses

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkɝsəz/ &middot; *KURS-iz*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated (jokey)
**Variants:** *curses, foiled again*

A mock-villain exclamation of defeat, borrowed from old movies and cartoons. Nobody says it seriously; it is a way of admitting you lost while making a joke of the loss.

1. "*Curses!* Foiled again."
2. She read the final score and said, "*Curses*."
3. *Curses* — the one time I don't bring an umbrella.
4. "I already ate the last slice." — "*Curses.*"
5. He shook his fist at the vending machine. "*Curses!*"

**See also:** [drat](#drat), [blast](#blast), [dagnabbit](#dagnabbit)

### dagnabbit

**Pronunciation:** /dæɡˈnæbɪt/ &middot; *dag-NAB-it*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated (regional and cartoonish)
**Variants:** *dagnabit*, *dag nab it*, *dad-gum it*

A heavily disguised version of a much stronger oath, associated with rural old-timers and cartoon prospectors. It comes across as affectionate and comic rather than genuinely angry.

1. "*Dagnabbit*, the fence is down again."
2. He kicked the tire and hollered, "*Dagnabbit!*"
3. *Dagnabbit* — I told you to latch that gate.
4. "*Dag nab it*, where'd that dog get to?"
5. Grandpa still says *dagnabbit* every time the remote goes missing.

**See also:** [tarnation](#tarnation), [doggone](#doggone), [dang](#dang)

### dang

**Pronunciation:** /dæŋ/ &middot; *DANG*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *dang it*, *dangit*, *well dang*

A softened form of *damn*, safe in almost any casual American setting. Bare *dang* often expresses surprise or grudging admiration; *dang it* is nearly always plain disappointment at something that just went wrong.

1. "*Dang it*, I missed the exit."
2. *Dang*, that is a long drive.
3. "The pool's closed today." — "*Dang.*"
4. He dropped the screw into the engine bay and said "*dang it*" about four times.
5. *Dang it*, the batteries are dead.
6. "*Dang*, I really wanted that jacket."

**See also:** [darn](#darn), [doggone](#doggone), [shoot](#shoot), [dagnabbit](#dagnabbit)

### darn

**Pronunciation:** /dɑrn/ &middot; *DARN*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *darn it*, *darnit*, *gosh darn it*, *durn*

The most common polite substitute for *damn* — safe in front of children, coworkers, and grandparents. On its own it registers disappointment; *darn it* is the standard form when something has just gone wrong.

1. "*Darn it*, the pen leaked all over my bag."
2. *Darn* — I thought I had one more coupon.
3. "Sold out." — "*Well, darn.*"
4. She stubbed her toe and said "*gosh darn it*" instead of what she meant.
5. *Darn it*, now I have to start the whole thing over.

**See also:** [dang](#dang), [shoot](#shoot), [shucks](#shucks), [heck](#heck)

### dear me

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪr ˈmi/ &middot; *deer MEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *oh dear me*, *dear, dear*

A gentle, old-fashioned expression of dismay — the kind of thing a polite older person says on walking into a mess. Younger Americans use it, if at all, to sound quaint on purpose.

1. "*Dear me*, look at this kitchen."
2. She peered at the bill. "*Oh dear me.*"
3. *Dear me* — you've been standing out there in the cold this whole time?
4. "*Dear, dear*, what a lot of trouble over nothing."
5. *Dear me*, I've gone and lost my place in the book again.

**See also:** [oh dear](#oh-dear), [oh no](#oh-no), [alas](#alas)

### doggone

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɔɡˈɔn/ &middot; *dawg-GAWN* (for speakers who merge the vowels, /ˌdɑɡˈɑn/ &middot; *dahg-GAHN*)
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (older speakers; Southern and Midwestern flavor)
**Variants:** *doggone it*, *doggonit*, *dadgum*

A homespun euphemism for *damn*, used as a standalone exclamation or as an adjective slapped onto whatever annoyed you. It sounds warm and a little country rather than harsh.

1. "*Doggone it*, the truck won't start."
2. *Doggone* — I was one number off.
3. "Now where is that *doggone* wrench?"
4. He laughed and said, "*Doggone it*, you got me."
5. *Doggone it*, I meant to call her back yesterday.
6. "The dog ate the whole pie." — "*Well, doggone.*"

### drat

**Variants:** *drat it*, *oh drat*

A small, prim exclamation of annoyance at something that has gone wrong. It is old-fashioned and mild enough to sound almost cute.

1. "*Drat*, I forgot the milk."
2. *Oh drat* — the ribbon tore.
3. "*Drat it*, that's the second time this week."

A quaint exclamation of annoyance, heard now mostly from older speakers or from people being deliberately old-timey. It can also mean "nonsense," dismissing what somebody just said.

### fudge

A family-friendly stand-in for the strongest four-letter word, chosen because it starts the same way. It shows up constantly around children and in workplaces where the real word would not fly.

### just my luck

A wry complaint that a misfortune has landed on you specifically, as though the universe were keeping score. It is self-deprecating rather than genuinely bitter.

### nuts

A short bark of disappointment, most familiar from mid-century American speech and from a famous one-word refusal by an American general at Bastogne. It sounds mildly retro today but is still perfectly clear.

**Variants:** *oh dear, oh dear*, *oh dear me*

A soft, slightly formal reaction to something that has gone wrong or is about to. It covers other people's trouble as well as your own and is gentle enough for any company.

1. "*Oh dear*, that stain is not coming out."

**Variants:** *oh, great*, *just great*, *that's just great*

Pure sarcasm: the words say "great," the flat or falling tone says the opposite. It marks a new problem landing on top of the problems you already had.

1. "*Oh great*, now it's raining."

### oh no

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *oh no no no*, *oh nooo*, *oh no!*

The default American reaction to bad news, small disasters, and dawning realizations. It stretches from mild ("oh no, we're out of coffee") to genuine alarm depending entirely on length and pitch.

1. "*Oh no*, I think I left the oven on."

4. *Oh no no no*, that is the wrong file.

### oof

**Variants:** *big oof*, *ooof*

Originally the grunt of getting hit in the stomach, now a general reaction to something painful, awkward, or unfortunate — most often something that happened to someone else. *Big oof* is the online intensifier.

### rats

A clean, kid-safe exclamation of disappointment when a small plan falls through. It is common with children and in polite company and sounds gentler than most of the alternatives.

The standard polite substitute for a much stronger word that begins the same way. It is what Americans say the moment they realize they have forgotten something or made a small mistake.

### shucks

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʌks/ &middot; *SHUKS*

**Variants:** *aw shucks*, *oh shucks*

Two related uses. **1.** A mild, countrified expression of disappointment. **2.** In the fixed form *aw shucks*, a bashful deflection of praise — now its most common use, and often used to describe a manner rather than said out loud.

1. "*Shucks*, and I'd been saving that coupon all month."
2. *Oh shucks* — the pond froze over too late for skating.

### story of my life

**Pronunciation:** /ˌstɔri əv maɪ ˈlaɪf/ &middot; *stor-ee uv my LIFE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *that's the story of my life*

A rueful comment that a disappointment is entirely typical of your existence. It is meant lightly and invites a sympathetic laugh, not actual pity.

1. "So close and yet so far." — "*Story of my life*."
2. He got there right as they flipped the sign to CLOSED. *Story of my life.*
3. "*That's the story of my life* — always the backup, never the pick."

### tarnation

**Pronunciation:** /tɑrˈneɪʃən/ &middot; *tar-NAY-shun*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated (regional and comic)
**Variants:** *what in tarnation*, *what in tarnation's name*

An old euphemism for *damnation*, surviving now almost entirely in the question *what in tarnation*, which conveys baffled dismay. It reads as a joke about country speech far more often than as real dialect.

1. "*What in tarnation* happened to my garden?"
2. *Tarnation!* That fence took me all weekend to build.

### womp womp

**Variants:** *womp*, *wah wah*, *sad trombone*

A vocal imitation of the descending "sad trombone" sound effect, marking a letdown. Aimed at yourself it is self-mocking; aimed at someone else it is mock sympathy that plainly means "too bad, and I don't care."

1. "The store was out of them." — "*Womp womp*."
2. *Womp womp* — no bonus this year.
3. He missed the shot and the whole bench went "*wah wah*."
4. "I studied all night and still failed." — "*Womp.*"
5. She complained about her tiny raise and somebody in the back said *womp womp*.

## Online and texting shorthand

### F

**Pronunciation:** /ɛf/ &middot; *EFF*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text
**Variants:** *press F*, *F in the chat*, *F's in the chat*

A single letter posted to pay respects to something lost, from the video-game prompt "press F to pay respects." It is a compact, half-joking condolence for anything from a dead phone battery to a canceled show.

1. "My laptop died with the paper unsaved." — "*F*."
2. *F* in the chat for everybody who has to work this weekend.
3. He posted a photo of the shattered screen with a single *F* underneath.
4. "*Press F*," somebody typed, and about forty people did.
5. The good coffee shop on Fifth closed for good. *F.*

**See also:** [rip](#rip), [sad face](#sad-face), [oof](#oof), [womp womp](#womp-womp)

### rip

**Pronunciation:** usually spelled out, /ˌɑr ˌaɪ ˈpi/ &middot; *ar-eye-PEE*; increasingly said as one word, /rɪp/ &middot; *RIP*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text
**Variants:** *RIP*, *rest in peace*, *r.i.p.*

Short for *rest in peace*, extended online to anything that has ended, broken, or gone badly — a phone, a plan, a winning streak, an evening. Used about an actual death it is sincere; used about a spilled coffee it is a joke.

1. "*RIP* my phone — it went screen-down on concrete."
2. *Rip*, I just deleted the entire folder.
3. "He dropped his ice cream in the parking lot." — "*RIP*."
4. *RIP* to my weekend; I'm covering both shifts.
5. "*RIP* to the best diner in town — they're closing Sunday."

**See also:** [F](#f), [oof](#oof), [womp womp](#womp-womp), [sad face](#sad-face)

### sad face

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsæd ˈfeɪs/ &middot; *sad FACE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text
**Variants:** *sadface*, *sad face emoji*

The words for the typed frown, used when you want to register disappointment without making a scene about it. Said out loud, it is a deliberate joke about the way people text.

1. "Can't make it tonight. *Sad face*."
2. She texted *sad face* and nothing else, so I called her.
3. "*Sad face*," he said out loud, which is somehow worse than actually frowning.
4. No tickets left. *Sadface.*
5. He signed off the email with *sad face*, which felt unprofessional but honest.

**See also:** [rip](#rip), [F](#f), [womp womp](#womp-womp), [boo-hoo](#boo-hoo)

## Resignation and acceptance

### ah well

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑ ˈwɛl/ &middot; *ah WELL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *ah, well*

A soft sigh of acceptance, close to *oh well* but a shade gentler and more philosophical. It closes the subject and moves on.

1. "*Ah well*, there's always next year."
2. *Ah, well* — we tried.
3. "They sold the house to somebody else." — "*Ah well.*"
4. He looked at the empty pan. "*Ah well*, cereal it is."
5. *Ah well*, it probably wasn't going to work out anyway.

**See also:** [oh well](#oh-well), [welp](#welp), [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie), [that's life](#thats-life)

### can't win 'em all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkænt ˈwɪn əm ˌɔl/ &middot; *kant WIN em awl*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *you can't win 'em all*, *can't win them all*, *win some, lose some*

A stock consolation after a loss, aimed at yourself or handed to somebody else. It concedes the defeat but files it as ordinary — one result in a long series rather than a verdict on anybody.

1. "*Can't win 'em all*," he said, and started loading the cooler back into the trunk.
2. *Win some, lose some.* The second interview went a lot better anyway.
3. "Three rejections in a row." — "*You can't win 'em all*."
4. She missed the shot, shrugged, and jogged back on defense. *Can't win 'em all.*
5. *Can't win them all* — though I sure would have liked to win that one.

**See also:** [oh well](#oh-well), [that's life](#thats-life), [ah well](#ah-well), [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie)

### c'est la vie

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪ lɑ ˈvi/ &middot; *say lah VEE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *say la vee* (common misspelling)

French for "that's life," fully naturalized in American English as a shrug at something you cannot change. Depending on the company it sounds either a touch worldly or a touch flippant.

1. "*C'est la vie* — the good ones always move away."
2. He shrugged. "*C'est la vie*."
3. *C'est la vie*, I guess. The deposit is gone either way.
4. "You lost the bet." — "*C'est la vie.*"
5. We missed the ferry by four minutes. *C'est la vie.*

**See also:** [that's life](#thats-life), [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [oh well](#oh-well), [so be it](#so-be-it)

### it is what it is

**Pronunciation:** /ɪt ˌɪz wʌt ɪt ˈɪz/ &middot; *it IZ wut it IZ* (both forms of *is* are stressed; the second carries the main beat)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *is what it is*

The modern American shrug for an unchangeable, unwelcome situation. It has become so common in offices and locker rooms that many people now hear it as a way of ending a conversation rather than as real acceptance.

1. "*It is what it is.* We'll play the hand we were dealt."
2. The budget got cut in half. *Is what it is.*
3. "Are you upset about the new schedule?" — "*It is what it is.*"
4. *It is what it is* — nobody's happy, but the deadline moved.
5. He said "*it is what it is*" so many times that I stopped hearing it.

**See also:** [that's life](#thats-life), [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie), [so be it](#so-be-it), [oh well](#oh-well)

### oh well

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ ˈwɛl/ &middot; *oh WELL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *oh, well*, *ah well*

The everyday marker of letting go of a small disappointment. It concedes that things did not go your way and signals that you have finished thinking about it.

1. "They're out of the blue ones." — "*Oh well*, red is fine."
2. *Oh well* — I'll catch the next one.
3. She missed the deadline by a day. *Oh well.*
4. "*Oh, well.* Can't win them all."
5. *Oh well*, at least we got the afternoon off.

### que sera sera

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkeɪ səˈrɑ səˈrɑ/ &middot; *kay suh-RAH suh-RAH*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated (jokey)
**Variants:** *que será, será*, *whatever will be, will be*, *che sarà, sarà*

A shrug at an outcome nobody can influence. It reached American English through the 1956 Doris Day song rather than from Spanish or Italian speakers — the phrase is not really grammatical in either language — and Americans use it lightly, usually with a wave of the hand.

1. "*Que sera sera* — the movers show up at eight whether we've packed or not."
2. She shrugged at the forecast. "*Whatever will be, will be*."
3. "Do you think you got in?" — "*Que sera sera*. I find out Friday."
4. He stuck the lottery ticket under a magnet on the fridge and said *que sera sera*.
5. *Que sera sera*, I guess. There's nothing left for me to do about it.

**See also:** [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie), [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [that's life](#thats-life), [oh well](#oh-well)

### so be it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ ˈbi ɪt/ &middot; *so BEE it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral (slightly formal)
**Variants:** *then so be it*

A firm acceptance of an outcome you did not want, usually said when you have decided to hold your position and take the consequences. It is more resolute than *oh well* and carries a hint of defiance.

1. "If that means we end up in court, *so be it*."
2. *So be it* — I'd rather lose the account than lie to a client.
3. "They'll think you're difficult." — "*So be it.*"
4. If this trip costs us the deposit, then *so be it*.
5. He said *so be it* and signed the resignation letter.

**See also:** [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [whatever](#whatever), [that's life](#thats-life)

### that's life

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðæts ˈlaɪf/ &middot; *thats LIFE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *such is life*, *that's the way it goes*, *that's how it goes*

A general-purpose consolation that setbacks are simply part of living. It can comfort someone else or excuse your own bad luck.

1. "*That's life* — some years the garden just doesn't take."
2. *Such is life.* We'll try again in the spring.
3. "I did everything right and still didn't get it." — "*That's the way it goes* sometimes."
4. He shrugged. "*That's life* in this business."
5. You save for a year and then the transmission goes. *That's how it goes.*

**See also:** [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie), [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [oh well](#oh-well), [story of my life](#story-of-my-life)

### well

**Pronunciation:** /wɛl/ &middot; *WEL*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *well then*, *welp*

Standing alone, with a falling tone and a pause after it, *well* concedes a disappointing fact and pushes the conversation forward — "there is nothing more to say about that." (The word has many other jobs; this entry covers only the resigned one.)

1. "*Well.* That's that, then."
2. She closed the laptop. "*Well.* I guess we're not doing that."
3. "*Well*, we knew it was a long shot."
4. *Well* — nothing to do now but wait.
5. "They said no." — "*Well.* Thanks for trying."

**See also:** [oh well](#oh-well), [welp](#welp), [ah well](#ah-well)

### welp

**Pronunciation:** /wɛlp/ &middot; *WELP*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *whelp* (common misspelling)

A clipped, casual *well*, marking the moment you accept a bad outcome and get up to deal with it. It is very common in speech and online, and it almost always opens a sentence or stands completely alone.

1. "*Welp*, there goes the weekend."
2. *Welp* — guess I'm walking.
3. "The tickets sold out in four minutes." — "*Welp.*"
4. He read the email, said "*welp*," and closed his laptop.
5. *Welp*, that's the last time I trust a weather app.

### what can you do

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌt kən ju ˈdu/ &middot; *wut kin yoo DOO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *what are you gonna do*, *whatcha gonna do*, *what can ya do*, *what else can you do*

A rhetorical question that answers itself: nothing. It closes out a complaint by conceding the situation is past changing, and it is almost never delivered with a rising, question-like tone — in print it often takes a period instead of a question mark.

1. "*What can you do*," he said, and got back in line.
2. The rent went up again. *What are you gonna do.*
3. "Doesn't that drive you crazy?" — "Sure it does. But *what can you do*."
4. *Whatcha gonna do* — you can't argue with the weather.
5. She spread her hands. "*What can ya do*."
6. Two hours on hold and then they hung up on me. *What can you do.*

**See also:** [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [that's life](#thats-life), [oh well](#oh-well), [c'est la vie](#cest-la-vie)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvɚ/ &middot; *wut-EV-er*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal &mdash; frequently rude; to a parent, partner, or boss it reads as a deliberate provocation
**Variants:** *whatevs*, *yeah, whatever*

A dismissive surrender that ends the conversation without conceding the point. Said to a friend it is mild resignation; said to a parent, a partner, or a boss it is close to an insult.

1. "*Whatever.* Do it your way."
2. He shrugged and said "*whatever*," which made her angrier than arguing would have.
3. "You're going to have to redo it." — "*Whatever*, fine."
4. *Whatever* — I'm not going to fight about a parking space.
5. She said "*whatevs*" and put her headphones back in.

**See also:** [so be it](#so-be-it), [it is what it is](#it-is-what-it-is), [well](#well)
