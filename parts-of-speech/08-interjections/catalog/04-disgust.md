# Disgust, contempt, and disapproval

> The noises and set phrases Americans use to push something away — a smell, a claim, a person's behavior — as revolting, worthless, or not worth a serious reply.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

This is the loudest and least polite corner of the interjection system, and it splits into two halves that behave differently. The visceral half — *ew, yuck, blech, ugh* — reacts to something the body rejects, and several of its members are not really made of speech sounds at all: the rasping /x/ that ends *blech* and *yech*, the vowel-less puff of *pfft*, the closed-mouth nasal grunt spelled *humph*, the tongue click written *tsk* and *tut*. Where an entry's Pronunciation line describes a physical action instead of giving ordinary phonemes, that is deliberate — the transcription would otherwise be a lie. The scornful half — *as if, whatever, give me a break, yeah right* — is built from ordinary words and puts all of the meaning in a flat, falling tone, which is why a learner who copies the words without the sarcasm will simply sound agreeable. Two writing conventions run through the whole category: repeated letters mean a longer, stronger reaction (*ewww, ughhh, booo*), and a period instead of an exclamation point marks the dry, deadpan delivery that most of the dismissals depend on.

Entries are alphabetical within each section.

## Physical disgust and revulsion

Reactions to something the senses reject — a taste, a smell, a texture — and to behavior that produces the same recoil.

### barf

**Pronunciation:** /bɑrf/ &middot; *BARF*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal

A blunt "that's revolting," borrowed from the verb meaning to vomit. As an interjection it answers something genuinely nauseating or, more often, something merely tacky or sappy; it peaked in 1980s teen slang, so today it lands as jokey or slightly dated.

1. "*Barf.* Who microwaved fish in the break room?"
2. He proposed on the stadium scoreboard. *Barf.*
3. "*Barf!*" my sister said, pushing the plate of liver away.
4. His whole profile is quotes about his own "journey." *Barf.*
5. *Barf*, that cologne is strong enough to strip paint.

**See also:** [gross](#gross), [yuck](#yuck), [gag me](#gag-me), [blech](#blech)

### blech

**Pronunciation:** /blɛx/, also /blɛk/ &middot; *BLEKH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *blecch*, *blegh*, *bleah*

A drawn-out noise of nausea — the sound of someone tasting something foul. It answers bad flavors, bad smells, and by extension anything sickening. The ending is usually a raspy /x/, like the *ch* in *Bach*, a consonant English does not otherwise use.

1. "*Blech!* This coffee has been sitting on the burner since morning."
2. I tried the licorice gelato. *Blech.*
3. *Blech* — cough syrup tastes exactly the way it did when I was six.
4. "Black olives?" "*Blech.* Pick them off mine."
5. She took one sip of the green smoothie and went "*blech*."

**See also:** [yech](#yech), [yuck](#yuck), [ew](#ew), [ugh](#ugh)

### bleh

**Pronunciation:** /blɛ/ &middot; *BLEH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *blah*

A limp, unenthusiastic noise: not real disgust so much as "I don't like it and I can't be bothered to explain why." It also describes how the speaker feels — flat, tired, mildly unwell. Keep it separate from *blech*, which is actively nauseated.

1. "How was the movie?" "*Bleh.* Two hours I'm not getting back."
2. *Bleh.* I've had a headache since lunch.
3. The new logo is just... *bleh*.
4. "Want to come out tonight?" "*Bleh*, I think I'm staying in."
5. Monday morning, gray sky, cold coffee — *bleh*.

**See also:** [meh](#meh), [ugh](#ugh), [whatever](#whatever)

### ew

**Pronunciation:** /iu/, also /ju/ &middot; *EE-oo — one gliding syllable, not two; or simply yoo*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *eww*, *ewww*, *ewwww*, *euw*

The default American expression of disgust, especially at something dirty, slimy, or physically off-putting. Extra w's in writing mean a longer and more emphatic reaction. It is very common among younger speakers and in text, where it also carries social distaste — "that's creepy" — and not only physical revulsion.

1. "*Ew!* There's hair in the drain."
2. *Ew*, don't touch that — it's been on the floor.
3. "He's forty-five and she's nineteen." "*Ewww.*"
4. She opened the container, said "*ew*," and put it straight in the trash.
5. *Ew, ew, ew* — something just ran across my foot.

**See also:** [gross](#gross), [ick](#ick), [yuck](#yuck), [cringe](#cringe)

### gag me

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡæɡ mi/ &middot; *GAG-mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *gag me with a spoon*

A theatrical "that makes me want to throw up," originally California Valley-girl slang from around 1980. Almost nobody uses it straight anymore; when Americans say it now, they are quoting the era on purpose.

1. "They named the baby after both grandmothers *and* the dog. *Gag me.*"
2. *Gag me with a spoon* — they're playing this song again?
3. He showed up to brunch in a velvet tuxedo. *Gag me.*
4. "It's a three-hour documentary about his own career." "*Gag me.*"
5. My mother still says "*gag me with a spoon*," which tells you exactly when she was in high school.

**See also:** [barf](#barf), [gross](#gross), [yuck](#yuck)

### gah

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɑ/, also /ɡæ/ &middot; *GAH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *gahh*, *ggah*

A short, strangled grunt of exasperated revulsion — the sound of patience running out in the presence of something unpleasant. It sits on the border between disgust and frustration: you say it at moldy leftovers and at the third error message in a row.

1. "*Gah!* There's mold all the way through the bread."
2. *Gah*, I hate the sound of styrofoam.
3. He read the email, muttered "*gah*," and shut the laptop.
4. *Gah* — who leaves a wet towel on the couch?
5. *Gah* — she chews ice. All day. Two feet from my desk.

**See also:** [ugh](#ugh), [blech](#blech), [bleh](#bleh)

### grody

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡroʊdi/ &middot; *GROH-dee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *groady*, *grody to the max*

Southern California teen slang of about 1980 for "disgusting," probably a chewed-up *grotesque*. It belongs to the same moment as *gag me* and survives the same way — as a period costume. Said straight by anyone under fifty it sounds like a quotation, and the full *grody to the max* is never anything but a joke now.

1. "*Grody!* There's something floating in the punch."
2. "They rent the shoes out. To everybody." "*Grody.*"
3. *Grody to the max* — he found a sandwich in his backpack from last October.
4. She peeled the sticker off and made a face: "*Grody*."
5. My mother still says "*grody*," and my kids are convinced she invented it.

**See also:** [gross](#gross), [gag me](#gag-me), [yuck](#yuck), [icky](#icky)

### gross

**Pronunciation:** /ɡroʊs/ &middot; *GROHSS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal

An adjective used as a one-word verdict of disgust. It covers physical revulsion — spoiled food, bodily fluids — and social or moral distaste, such as a creepy comment or a tacky move, and it is the most common everyday American word for both.

1. "*Gross!* Don't put your feet on the table where we eat."
2. There's something growing in the back of the fridge. *Gross.*
3. "He tried to kiss her at the office party." "*Gross.*"
4. *Gross* — the pool water is actually green.
5. She held up the kitchen sponge: "*Gross*, when did anyone last replace this?"

**See also:** [ew](#ew), [yuck](#yuck), [ick](#ick), [cringe](#cringe)

### ick

**Pronunciation:** /ɪk/ &middot; *IK*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ickk*, *ik*

A short, clipped sound of distaste — smaller and more contained than *ew*. In recent dating slang it is also a noun: *the ick* is the sudden loss of attraction to someone over some trivial thing they did.

1. "*Ick.* There's something sticky on this doorknob."
2. *Ick* — I hate reaching into a bag without knowing what I'm touching.
3. "He calls his mother twice during every date." "*Ick.*"
4. She gave a little "*ick*" and wiped her hands on her jeans.
5. *Ick, ick, ick.* Please take the spider outside.

**See also:** [ew](#ew), [icky](#icky), [yuck](#yuck), [cringe](#cringe)

### icky

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪki/ &middot; *IK-ee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** childish

The adjective built on *ick*, used as an exclamation mostly by and to small children, or by adults being deliberately cute. It covers sticky, slimy, unpleasantly messy things, and also a vague feeling of unease.

1. "*Icky!* Don't put that in your mouth."
2. *Icky* — the sunscreen makes my hands feel weird.
3. "Is the medicine icky?" "*Icky*," she agreed solemnly.
4. He held up a fistful of wet leaves and announced, "*Icky*."
5. *Icky!* Get the frosting off the car seat.

**See also:** [ick](#ick), [yucky](#yucky), [gross](#gross)

### nasty

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnæsti/ &middot; *NAS-tee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *nassty*, *straight-up nasty*

An adjective thrown down as a verdict: filthy, or foul-tasting, or both. It is more physical than *gross* — *gross* can be about behavior, *nasty* is usually about something you could touch or swallow — and it is especially strong in Southern and Black American English, where a drawn-out *naaasty* is the standard reaction to bad food or a dirty room. Two other senses of the same word ("cruel" and, in sports, "impressively skillful") are sorted out entirely by context.

1. "*Nasty.* Somebody left tuna in the microwave again."
2. *Nasty* — that milk has been sitting out all night.
3. "Try it with the pickle juice." "That is *nasty*."
4. She looked at the shower drain and said, "Ooh, *nasty*."
5. He put ketchup on his eggs, which the entire table agreed was *nasty*.

**See also:** [gross](#gross), [yuck](#yuck), [ew](#ew), [barf](#barf)

### P.U.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpi ˈju/ &middot; *pee-YOO*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pee-yew*, *pee-you*, *peeyew*, *PU*, *pew*

Said about a bad smell and nothing else. Despite the spelling it is not an abbreviation of anything; it is a stylized version of a disgusted *pew*. Adults use it playfully, usually while holding their nose. A long *phew* is sometimes used the same way, although *phew* on its own normally means relief.

1. "*P.U.!* Somebody needs a diaper change."
2. *Pee-yew* — did a skunk get under the porch?
3. He opened the gym bag, said "*P.U.*," and zipped it right back up.
4. "*Pee-yew!* What died in this car?"
5. *P.U.*, that cheese is a biological weapon.

**See also:** [ptooey](#ptooey), [blech](#blech), [ew](#ew), [ugh](#ugh)

### ptooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtui/, also /pəˈtui/ &middot; *TOO-ee, or puh-TOO-ee*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ptui*, *ptoo*, *ptooie*

The written sound of spitting something out — used literally for a bad taste and figuratively as a gesture of contempt or a mock-superstitious charm. It is rarely spoken as a word; it lives in comics, jokes, and narration.

1. He bit into the unripe persimmon and went, "*Ptooey!*"
2. *Ptooey* — that mouthwash tastes like gasoline.
3. "Ninety dollars to park for an hour? *Ptooey.*"
4. The old man said the name and spat: "*Ptooey.*"
5. She rinsed, spat — *ptooey* — and told the dentist she was ready.

**See also:** [P.U.](#pu), [yech](#yech), [blech](#blech)

### ugh

**Pronunciation:** /ʌɡ/, also /ʌx/ &middot; *UHG*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ughh*, *ughhh*, *uggh*

A heavy groan of disgust, weariness, or distaste, and the workhorse of the whole category. Americans use it for spoiled milk, for bad news, for their own inbox, and for people they find exhausting. Lengthening it into *ughhh* turns the sound into a full complaint.

1. "*Ugh.* The milk turned."
2. *Ugh*, another meeting that could have been an email.
3. "He's bringing his new girlfriend." "*Ugh.*"
4. She read the parking ticket and let out a long "*ughhh*."
5. *Ugh* — I just stepped in something.

**See also:** [bleh](#bleh), [meh](#meh), [gah](#gah), [blech](#blech)

### yech

**Pronunciation:** /jɛx/, also /jɛk/ &middot; *YEKH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *yecch*, *yeccch*, *yechh*

A gargled noise of disgust, close to *yuck* but ending in the same scraping /x/ as *blech*. It is strongly associated with mid-century comics and *Mad* magazine, which is why it now reads as old-fashioned rather than neutral.

1. "*Yech!* This gravy has skin on it."
2. *Yech* — the sponge smells sour.
3. He looked at the cafeteria meatloaf and said "*yech*" under his breath.
4. "Grape cough drops?" "*Yecch.*"
5. *Yech*, there's a whole colony of ants under the sink.

**See also:** [yuck](#yuck), [blech](#blech), [ick](#ick)

### yuck

**Pronunciation:** /jʌk/ &middot; *YUK*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yuk*, *yukk*, *yucch*

A plain, forceful "that is disgusting," used freely by children and adults alike. It is a shade more childlike than *gross* and a shade less squeamish than *ew*, and it attaches to taste, smell, and texture more often than to behavior.

1. "*Yuck!* This yogurt expired in March."
2. *Yuck*, why is the floor of this bathroom wet?
3. "Try one bite." "*Yuck.* No."
4. He made a *yuck* face and handed the fork back.
5. *Yuck* — I just found a slug in the lettuce.

**See also:** [yucky](#yucky), [ew](#ew), [gross](#gross), [blech](#blech)

### yucky

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjʌki/ &middot; *YUK-ee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** childish

The adjective built on *yuck*, used as an exclamation mainly with small children. From an adult in an adult setting it sounds deliberately babyish.

1. "*Yucky!* We don't eat things off the sidewalk."
2. *Yucky* — the bathwater has bits floating in it.
3. "Broccoli?" "*Yucky*," he said, and slid off his chair.
4. She pointed at the puddle and announced, "*Yucky.*"
5. *Yucky!* Get the worm off your brother.

**See also:** [yuck](#yuck), [icky](#icky), [ick](#ick)

## Scorn and dismissal

Aimed at an idea, a claim, or a person's self-importance. Nearly all of these live or die by tone: said sincerely, most of them mean the opposite.

### as if

**Pronunciation:** /æz ˈɪf/ &middot; *az-IF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A flat refusal to grant that something could ever happen — "you must be joking." It exploded into general use with the 1995 film *Clueless* and is still current, though it usually carries a knowing 1990s flavor.

1. "He said he'd pay you back." "*As if.*"
2. *As if* I'd give up a Saturday for that.
3. "Maybe they'll upgrade us to first class." "*As if!*"
4. She rolled her eyes and mouthed, "*as if*."
5. They want the whole report by Monday? *As if.*

**See also:** [yeah right](#yeah-right), [oh please](#oh-please), [get real](#get-real), [whatever](#whatever)

### bah

**Pronunciation:** /bɑ/, also /bæ/ &middot; *BAH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *bahh*

A curt noise of contempt or refusal, thrown at an idea the speaker considers worthless. It survives mostly in writing and in jokey speech; a real modern grump is more likely to say *pfft* or *whatever*.

1. "*Bah.* Another committee, another year of nothing."
2. *Bah* — nine dollars to check a bag I could carry myself.
3. "The forecast says snow." "*Bah*, they're always wrong."
4. He waved the brochure away with a *bah*.
5. *Bah!* Let them figure it out themselves.

**See also:** [bah humbug](#bah-humbug), [pshaw](#pshaw), [phooey](#phooey), [feh](#feh)

### bah humbug

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɑ ˈhʌmˌbʌɡ/ &middot; *bah-HUM-bug*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *humbug*

A quotation from Scrooge in Dickens's *A Christmas Carol*, used almost entirely to announce that you are sick of Christmas cheer — or, by extension, of any organized enthusiasm. It is nearly always half-joking: the person saying it is admitting to being a grump.

1. "*Bah humbug.* They started playing carols before Halloween."
2. He refuses to do Secret Santa. *Bah humbug*, apparently.
3. "Aren't you coming to the office party?" "*Bah humbug.*"
4. *Bah humbug* — twelve dollars for a peppermint latte.
5. She hung a wreath on her door that read "*bah humbug*."

**See also:** [bah](#bah), [phooey](#phooey), [meh](#meh)

### big deal

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɪɡ ˈdil/ &middot; *big-DEEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *big whoop*, *big fat deal*

Sarcastic dismissal of something the other person clearly considers impressive or serious. Said flat, it means the exact opposite of what the words say, and it is usually introduced by *oh* or *so*.

1. "He got the corner office." "*Big deal.* He's there until nine every night."
2. *Big deal* — I've been doing that job for years without the title.
3. "It's a limited edition." "Oh, *big deal*."
4. So they finished a day early. *Big whoop.*
5. "*Big deal*," she said. "Anyone can buy a trophy."

**See also:** [whoop-de-doo](#whoop-de-doo), [so what](#so-what), [meh](#meh), [whatever](#whatever)

### come off it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌm ˈɔf ɪt/ &middot; *kum-AWF-it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh, come off it*

Said to someone who is exaggerating, posturing, or pretending: drop the act. It is more common in British English, but Americans use it too, usually with a note of affectionate impatience rather than real anger.

1. "*Come off it.* You've never run a marathon in your life."
2. Oh, *come off it* — you knew about this weeks ago.
3. "I'm the only one who does any work around here." "*Come off it.*"
4. *Come off it*, nobody reads the terms and conditions.
5. "He's just being modest." "*Come off it*, he brings it up every single day."

**See also:** [get real](#get-real), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break), [oh please](#oh-please), [spare me](#spare-me)

### feh

**Pronunciation:** /fɛ/ &middot; *FEH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional

A dismissive Yiddish exhalation meaning roughly "worthless, not worth my breath." It entered American English through New York speech and is most at home there; it carries a shrug of disdain rather than heat.

1. "How was the restaurant?" "*Feh.* Overpriced and undersalted."
2. *Feh* — I've seen better work from an intern.
3. "They gave him an award." "*Feh*, they give everybody an award."
4. He looked at the offer, said "*feh*," and went back to his crossword.
5. *Feh.* You call that a bagel?

**See also:** [bah](#bah), [meh](#meh), [pfft](#pfft), [phooey](#phooey)

### get real

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɛt ˈril/ &middot; *get-REEL*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

An order to stop kidding yourself, aimed at someone whose claim or plan strikes the speaker as delusional. It is blunter and more confrontational than *come on*.

1. "I'll have the whole thing finished tonight." "*Get real.*"
2. *Get real* — nobody is paying that for a car with 200,000 miles on it.
3. "He says he's quitting smoking on New Year's." "*Get real.*"
4. "*Get real*, Dad. Nobody wears those anymore."
5. They want two years of experience for an entry-level job? *Get real.*

**See also:** [come off it](#come-off-it), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break), [as if](#as-if), [oh please](#oh-please)

### give me a break

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɪv mi ə ˈbreɪk/ &middot; *giv-mee-uh-BRAYK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *gimme a break*, *oh, give me a break*

A protest at something the speaker finds absurd, unfair, or transparently false. Depending on the tone it means either "stop insulting my intelligence" or "stop piling on."

1. "He says the dog ate the contract." "*Give me a break.*"
2. *Gimme a break* — I've apologized three times already.
3. "There's a four-dollar fee to pay your bill online." "Oh, *give me a break*."
4. "*Give me a break*," he muttered at the television.
5. They cut the budget and moved the deadline up. *Give me a break.*

**See also:** [come off it](#come-off-it), [spare me](#spare-me), [oh please](#oh-please), [get real](#get-real)

### la-di-da

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɑ diˈdɑ/ &middot; *lah-dee-DAH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *la-de-da*, *la-di-dah*, *well, la-di-da*

Mock-fancy singsong aimed at somebody putting on airs. The syllables mean nothing at all — they are a snatch of tune — and the whole insult lives in the rising-then-falling melody, which imitates a person being terribly refined. It is nearly always introduced by *ooh* or *well*, and it stings without being obscene, which is why it survives in families.

1. "He only drinks single-origin now." "Ooh, *la-di-da*."
2. *La-di-da* — a separate fork for the salad.
3. "She flew business class." "Well, *la-di-da*."
4. "*La-di-da*," my brother sang, when I mentioned that I have a guy who does my taxes.
5. They served the cheese after dinner instead of before. *La-de-da.*

**See also:** [whoop-de-doo](#whoop-de-doo), [big deal](#big-deal), [oh please](#oh-please), [pshaw](#pshaw)

### meh

**Pronunciation:** /mɛ/ &middot; *MEH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mehh*, *meh.*

A verdict of complete indifference: not bad enough to complain about, not good enough to care about. It spread through *The Simpsons* and the early internet and is now the standard American way to say "unimpressed."

1. "How was the new place?" "*Meh.* I wouldn't go back."
2. "Thai or Italian?" "*Meh*, either one."
3. The reviews were ecstatic; my own reaction was *meh*.
4. "*Meh*," he said, and kept scrolling.
5. "It's the biggest game of the season." "*Meh.* I don't really follow baseball."

**See also:** [bleh](#bleh), [whatever](#whatever), [so what](#so-what), [big deal](#big-deal)

### oh please

**Pronunciation:** /oʊ ˈpliz/ &middot; *oh-PLEEZ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *puh-lease*, *puh-leeze*, *please*

Sarcastic disbelief with a whiff of superiority — "you cannot expect me to swallow that." The stretched spelling *puh-lease* marks an especially theatrical delivery.

1. "He said the traffic was bad." "*Oh, please.* He's late every single day."
2. *Oh please* — that painting looks like it took ten minutes.
3. "She's the most humble person I know." "*Puh-lease.*"
4. "*Oh, please*," my grandmother said. "I've buried tougher men than him."
5. They're calling that a scandal? *Oh, please.*

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [yeah right](#yeah-right), [spare me](#spare-me), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break)

### pfft

**Pronunciation:** /pft/ &middot; *pfft* — not speech: no vowel at all, just lips pressed together and a short puff of air released through them; the *psh* spelling is /pʃ/, *pshh*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pff*, *pft*, *psh*, *pssh*, *phht*

The sound of air pushed dismissively through the lips: "that's nothing," "who cares," "not a chance." It is written far more often than it is spoken, and it turns any statement into a shrug. There is no vowel in it — you simply release a short puff of air, with the tongue involved for the *psh* variant.

1. "You'd need at least a week." "*Pfft.* Two days, tops."
2. *Pfft* — he won't even remember saying that.
3. "It's supposed to be the best restaurant in town." "*Pfft*, it's fine."
4. She made a *pfft* noise and turned back to her screen.
5. "*Psh.* I've driven that route a hundred times."

**See also:** [meh](#meh), [feh](#feh), [whatever](#whatever), [bah](#bah)

### phooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfui/ &middot; *FOO-ee*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *phooie*, *fooey*

An old-fashioned noise of disgust or rejection, originally a spitting sound turned into a word. It can mean "nonsense" or simply "darn it," and it now sounds mild and cartoonish, which is exactly why parents reach for it in front of children.

1. "*Phooey.* The store's already closed."
2. *Phooey* — I forgot to record the game.
3. "They say it cures colds." "*Phooey.* It's sugar water."
4. He crumpled the losing ticket and said, "*Phooey*."
5. *Phooey* on the whole idea.

**See also:** [pshaw](#pshaw), [bah](#bah), [ptooey](#ptooey), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### pshaw

**Pronunciation:** /ʃɔ/, also /pʃɔ/ &middot; *SHAW*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated

A scornful puff dismissing something as foolish or beneath notice. It belongs to nineteenth- and early twentieth-century speech; Americans today use it only in period writing or as a deliberate joke.

1. "*Pshaw!* You'll believe anything you read."
2. *Pshaw* — a gentleman does not discuss his losses.
3. "You must be exhausted." "*Pshaw.* I'm perfectly fine."
4. She waved him off with a *pshaw* and went back to her knitting.
5. "*Pshaw*," he said, sounding like someone in a black-and-white movie.

**See also:** [phooey](#phooey), [bah](#bah), [poppycock](#poppycock), [pfft](#pfft)

### so what

**Pronunciation:** /soʊ ˈwʌt/ &middot; *soh-WUT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

A challenge denying that the other person's point carries any weight. It can be defensive — "yes, I did, and?" — or contemptuous. Do not mistake it for a neutral request for relevance: said to a manager, a customer, or anyone senior to you it reads as open insolence, and it is one of the fastest ways to turn a disagreement into a fight.

1. "You're two hours late." "*So what?* Nobody was waiting."
2. *So what* if it's raining — we have umbrellas.
3. "The CEO reads every one of these." "*So what.* It's still a bad idea."
4. He just shrugged: "*So what.*"
5. "She has ten years on you." "*So what?* I've done the work."

**See also:** [big deal](#big-deal), [whatever](#whatever), [whoop-de-doo](#whoop-de-doo), [meh](#meh)

### spare me

**Pronunciation:** /ˈspɛr mi/ &middot; *SPAIR-mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh, spare me*, *spare me the details*

"I don't want to hear it" — used to cut off an excuse, a lecture, or a display of feeling the speaker considers phony.

1. "I only did it because I care about you." "*Spare me.*"
2. Oh, *spare me* the speech about teamwork.
3. "He wrote a whole apology letter." "*Spare me.*"
4. "*Spare me*," she said. "I've heard this one before."
5. *Spare me* the details — just tell me whether it's fixed.

**See also:** [give me a break](#give-me-a-break), [oh please](#oh-please), [come off it](#come-off-it), [boo hoo](#boo-hoo)

### whatever

**Pronunciation:** /wʌtˈɛvər/ &middot; *wut-EV-er*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *whatevs*, *whatev*, *whateverrr*

A dismissal that ends the conversation: I am no longer engaging with this, and I don't respect the point. Delivered flat, it is one of the most contemptuous words in casual American English — teenagers get in trouble for it on a regular basis.

1. "You're the one who forgot to call." "*Whatever.*"
2. *Whatever* — do it your way.
3. "I told you it wouldn't work." "Yeah, *whatever*."
4. She said "*whatever*" and put her headphones back on.
5. "*Whatevs.* I'm not going to argue about a parking spot."

**See also:** [meh](#meh), [so what](#so-what), [big deal](#big-deal), [pfft](#pfft)

### who cares

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhu ˈkɛrz/ &middot; *hoo-KAIRZ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *who cares?*, *like anyone cares*, *nobody cares*

A flat denial that the subject matters to anyone. Written with a question mark it is still not a question, and the delivery is falling and bored rather than curious. It is broader than *so what*: *so what* rejects your argument, *who cares* rejects the entire topic, which is why it shuts conversations down so completely — and why it lands as contemptuous of the person who raised it.

1. "They changed the font on the whole website." "*Who cares.*"
2. *Who cares* what he posts. You are never going to meet the man.
3. "Their coach said we only won because of the weather." "*Who cares?* We won."
4. "*Who cares*," she said, and the design review was effectively over.
5. He spent forty minutes on the seating chart. *Who cares.*

**See also:** [so what](#so-what), [big deal](#big-deal), [whatever](#whatever), [meh](#meh)

### whoop-de-doo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʊpdiˈdu/, also /ˌhʊpdiˈdu/ &middot; *wuup-dee-DOO*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *whoop-de-do*, *whoopty-doo*, *whoop-dee-doo*

Mock celebration. You say it in a deadpan voice to show that whatever was just announced does not impress you in the slightest.

1. "They're giving everyone a five-dollar gift card." "*Whoop-de-doo.*"
2. *Whoop-de-doo* — a certificate. That'll pay the rent.
3. "He finally answered my email." "*Whoopty-doo.*"
4. She twirled one finger in the air: "*Whoop-de-doo*."
5. So the meeting got moved up an hour. *Whoop-de-doo.*

**See also:** [big deal](#big-deal), [so what](#so-what), [meh](#meh), [pfft](#pfft)

### yeah right

**Pronunciation:** /jɛə ˈraɪt/ &middot; *yeah-RITE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yeah, right*, *riiight*, *oh, right*

Flat sarcastic disbelief. The words say agreement; the drawn-out, falling delivery says "I don't believe you for a second." Tone carries the entire meaning — said sincerely, the same two words mean actual agreement.

1. "I'll be there at seven sharp." "*Yeah, right.*"
2. *Yeah right* — and I'm the Queen of England.
3. "He says he's never seen the show." "*Yeah, right.*"
4. She laughed. "*Yeah, riiight.* Try again."
5. A free upgrade with no strings attached? *Yeah, right.*

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [oh please](#oh-please), [get real](#get-real), [pfft](#pfft)

## Disapproval, booing, and tutting

Public and semi-public censure — the sounds you make at a performance, a rule-breaker, or a child.

### boo

**Pronunciation:** /bu/ &middot; *BOO*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *booo*, *boooo*, *boo-hiss*

The standard American noise of public disapproval, shouted at a performer, a referee, or a politician. In smaller settings it registers mock protest at bad news. Do not confuse it with the *boo* used to startle someone, which is a different word entirely.

1. The comedian tried the same joke a second time and the back of the room started to *boo*.
2. When the referee waved off the goal, forty thousand people made the same sound: "*Boooo.*"
3. The mayor got as far as "modest rate adjustment" before somebody in the third row said it out loud: "*Boo.*"
4. "The meeting has been moved to eight a.m." "*Boo.*"
5. *Boo* — bring back the old logo.

**See also:** [hiss](#hiss), [harrumph](#harrumph), [tsk](#tsk), [shame on you](#shame-on-you)

### harrumph

**Pronunciation:** /həˈrʌmf/ &middot; *huh-RUMF*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *harumph*, *hurrumph*

A loud throat-clearing grunt of pompous disapproval — the noise of a self-important person who objects but will not say so plainly. It is written far more than spoken, and it is almost always affectionate mockery of the harrumpher.

1. "*Harrumph.* In my day we wrote thank-you notes."
2. He gave a small *harrumph* and shook out his newspaper.
3. *Harrumph* — nobody asked the finance department, of course.
4. "The dress code has been relaxed." "*Harrumph.*"
5. She answered my suggestion with a *harrumph* and one raised eyebrow.

**See also:** [humph](#humph), [tsk](#tsk), [boo](#boo), [bah](#bah)

### hiss

**Pronunciation:** /hɪs/ &middot; *HISS*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hisss*, *boo-hiss*

The theatrical sound of disapproval, aimed especially at a villain. In modern American use it is nearly always written or performed as a joke — real audiences boo far more often than they hiss.

1. "And then he raised the rent." "*Hiss.*"
2. *Boo, hiss* — the sequel ruined the ending.
3. The audience knew the routine: cheer for the hero, "*hiss*" for the landlord.
4. "*Hisssss*," my roommate said, crossing two fingers at me like I was a vampire.
5. She typed "*hiss*" under his post about waking up at four a.m.

**See also:** [boo](#boo), [harrumph](#harrumph), [tsk](#tsk)

### humph

**Pronunciation:** /hm̩f/ &middot; *HMF* — usually not a spoken word at all but a two-part nasal grunt with the lips shut, released at the end; read off the page it becomes /hʌmf/, *HUMF*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hmph*, *hmmph*, *humpf*

A short nasal grunt of skepticism, offense, or sulky disapproval — the sound of not being satisfied by what you just heard. It usually comes with folded arms, and it is often produced with the mouth completely closed.

1. "*Humph.* That's not what you said yesterday."
2. She said "*hmph*" and turned back to the stove.
3. *Humph* — I suppose that's one way to do it.
4. "You'll thank me later." "*Hmph.*"
5. He listened to the excuse, gave a *humph*, and walked out.

**See also:** [harrumph](#harrumph), [tsk](#tsk), [pfft](#pfft), [bah](#bah)

### shame on you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʃeɪm ɔn ju/ &middot; *SHAYM-on-yoo*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *for shame*, *shame*

A direct moral rebuke: your behavior was wrong and you should feel bad about it. Adults say it to children in earnest; between adults it is either quite serious or clearly teasing, with very little in between. *For shame* is the dated literary version.

1. "You took the last one and didn't tell your sister? *Shame on you.*"
2. *Shame on you* for letting them take the blame.
3. "I told everyone your secret." "*Shame on you!*"
4. Her grandmother used the older form: "*For shame*, Michael."
5. Charging a family that much for a hotel room during an evacuation — *shame on them*.

**See also:** [tsk](#tsk), [boo](#boo), [harrumph](#harrumph)

### tsk

**Pronunciation:** /ǀ/ — a dental click, made by sucking the tongue off the front teeth; there is no vowel. Read aloud from the page it becomes /tɪsk/ &middot; *TISK*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *tsk-tsk*, *tsk tsk*, *tsktsk*

The little clicking sound of disapproval — schoolteacherly, faintly smug, and usually aimed at someone else's small moral failing. Doubling it makes it unmistakably judgmental; a single click can simply mean regret. The sound itself is not a spoken word but a click of the tongue against the teeth; *tisk* is what people say when reading it off a page.

1. "*Tsk, tsk.* Cheating on a quiz you would have passed anyway."
2. She clicked her tongue — *tsk* — and kept reading.
3. *Tsk tsk*, someone parked across two spaces again.
4. "He never even called to apologize." "*Tsk.*"
5. My aunt has a *tsk* that can end an argument from across the room.

**See also:** [tut](#tut), [humph](#humph), [harrumph](#harrumph), [shame on you](#shame-on-you)

### tut

**Pronunciation:** /ǀ/ — the same dental click as *tsk*, not a spoken syllable; read aloud from the page it becomes /tʌt/ &middot; *TUT*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** regional (chiefly British; in American use mostly a written spelling)
**Variants:** *tut-tut*, *tut tut*

The British cousin of *tsk*: a clicking noise of mild reproof, written *tut* or *tut-tut*. Americans recognize it and write it, but rarely say it — an American disapproving out loud makes the click and spells it *tsk*.

1. "*Tut-tut.* Elbows off the table."
2. *Tut* — leaving without saying goodbye again.
3. "He's late." "*Tut-tut*, and on his first day."
4. The headmaster gave a *tut* and made a note.
5. "*Tut, tut.* And you call yourself a professional."

**See also:** [tsk](#tsk), [humph](#humph), [shame on you](#shame-on-you)

## Calling something nonsense

One-word verdicts that a statement is false or empty. They run from grandfatherly to profane, and the register gap between them is enormous.

### balderdash

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɔldərˌdæʃ/ &middot; *BAWL-der-dash*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

Nonsense, announced in the grandest register the language offers. The word is nineteenth-century and sounds it; an American who says it out loud today is performing indignation rather than feeling it, usually with an eyebrow up. Its one modern home outside of jokes is the board game named after it.

1. "*Balderdash.* Nobody was ever cured by drinking vinegar."
2. *Balderdash* — the man has never set foot in this building.
3. "He says he wrote the whole score in one night." "*Balderdash.*"
4. "*Balderdash!*" the old judge said, and half the courtroom laughed.
5. The pamphlet was *balderdash* from the title down.

**See also:** [poppycock](#poppycock), [hogwash](#hogwash), [horsefeathers](#horsefeathers), [malarkey](#malarkey)

### baloney

**Pronunciation:** /bəˈloʊni/ &middot; *buh-LOH-nee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *boloney*, *bologna*

"That's a lie," or "that's worthless talk," from the sausage. It is the family-friendly stand-in for much stronger words, and it sounds a little like somebody's grandfather.

1. "*Baloney.* Nobody loses forty pounds in a month."
2. That whole story about the lost invoice is *baloney*.
3. "He says the market always recovers by spring." "*Baloney.*"
4. "*Baloney!*" the old man shouted at the television.
5. *Baloney* — I was standing right there when it happened.

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [hogwash](#hogwash), [malarkey](#malarkey), [poppycock](#poppycock)

### bull

**Pronunciation:** /bʊl/ &middot; *BUUL*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *bullcrap*, *B.S.*

A short, blunt "that isn't true," clipped from *bullshit*. It is milder than the full word but still not something to say to a client.

1. "*Bull.* You never sent that email."
2. He called *bull* on the entire explanation.
3. "They said the part was on back order." "*Bull*, I checked the site myself."
4. "*Bullcrap*," my brother said. "You made that up."
5. *Bull* — that game was rigged from the first quarter.

**See also:** [bullshit](#bullshit), [baloney](#baloney), [hogwash](#hogwash), [malarkey](#malarkey)

### bullshit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʊlˌʃɪt/ &middot; *BUUL-shit*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Variants:** *bullshit!*, *B.S.*

The strongest everyday way to reject a claim as a lie or as empty talk. It is genuinely profane: fine among friends, career-limiting in a meeting, and bleeped on broadcast television.

1. "*Bullshit.* You knew about the layoffs in March."
2. *Bullshit* — I watched him do it.
3. "The fee is non-negotiable." "That's *bullshit* and you know it."
4. "*Bullshit!*" someone yelled from the back of the room.
5. He listened to the excuse and said exactly one word: "*Bullshit.*"

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [baloney](#baloney), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### bunk

**Pronunciation:** /bʌŋk/ &middot; *BUNK*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *bunkum*, *a lot of bunk*, *pure bunk*

A short, dry "that is not true." It is clipped from *bunkum*, which came from a congressman's famously pointless speech about Buncombe County, North Carolina. Of everything in this group it is the flattest: no theater, no folksiness, no profanity, just a verdict — which is why it still turns up in print where *hogwash* would be too colorful. The verb *debunk* is built on it.

1. "*Bunk.* The study never said anything close to that."
2. Most of what you read about that diet is *bunk*.
3. "They say the wiring in old houses is safer." "*Bunk.*"
4. "*Bunk*," he said, without looking up from the page.
5. The whole "we're like a family here" speech turned out to be *bunk* by October.

**See also:** [hogwash](#hogwash), [baloney](#baloney), [hooey](#hooey), [malarkey](#malarkey)

### garbage

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɑrbɪdʒ/ &middot; *GAR-bij*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hot garbage*, *pure garbage*, *trash*

"That is worthless" — used both for a claim that is false and for work that is simply bad. It is the most current-sounding word in this group and the least funny: where *poppycock* and *horsefeathers* are costumes, *garbage* carries real contempt, and people take it personally when it is aimed at something they made. *Hot garbage* is the emphatic form.

1. "*Garbage.* He never filed the paperwork and he knows it."
2. That call was *garbage* and the whole stadium knew it.
3. "The reviews say it's a masterpiece." "It's *garbage*."
4. Two hundred dollars for a jacket that came apart in a month — *hot garbage*.
5. He called the study *garbage* in front of the two people who wrote it.

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [bullshit](#bullshit), [baloney](#baloney), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### hogwash

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɔɡˌwɔʃ/, also /ˈhɑɡˌwɑʃ/ &middot; *HAWG-wosh*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

Nonsense — literally the slop fed to pigs. It is emphatic, old-fashioned, and completely clean, which makes it a favorite of people who don't swear.

1. "*Hogwash.* The report says the opposite."
2. *Hogwash* — nobody has ever proved that.
3. "They claim it's an ancient family recipe." "*Hogwash.* It's on the back of the box."
4. "*Hogwash!*" the senator said, and the room went quiet.
5. Most of what he told you was *hogwash*.

**See also:** [baloney](#baloney), [malarkey](#malarkey), [poppycock](#poppycock), [bull](#bull)

### hooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhui/ &middot; *HOO-ee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

Nonsense, in a folksy American register that has not really moved since the 1920s. It is almost always preceded by *a bunch of* or *a lot of* rather than standing alone, and it is mild enough to say in front of absolutely anyone. Note that it rhymes with *phooey* and is one letter away from it, but the two do different jobs: *phooey* rejects a situation, *hooey* rejects a claim.

1. "*Hooey.* You cannot tell the winter from a caterpillar."
2. That is a bunch of *hooey* and you know it.
3. "The label says it boosts your immune system." "*Hooey.*"
4. "*Hooey!*" my grandfather said, every single time the anchor started with "experts agree."
5. He sat through a lot of *hooey* before anyone in that room told him a plain fact.

**See also:** [baloney](#baloney), [bunk](#bunk), [malarkey](#malarkey), [phooey](#phooey)

### horsefeathers

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɔrsˌfɛðərz/ &middot; *HORSE-feth-erz*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

A 1920s minced substitute for *horseshit*, coined so the sentiment could be expressed in company and preserved by the 1932 Marx Brothers film of the same name. Nobody rejects anything with it seriously now; it is a joke about a particular kind of old-fashioned bluster, and it is completely clean.

1. "*Horsefeathers.* He has been telling that story for thirty years."
2. *Horsefeathers* — you cannot patent a color.
3. "The manual says the whole thing takes fifteen minutes." "*Horsefeathers.*"
4. "*Horsefeathers!*" she said, mostly to make the children laugh.
5. He wrote "*horsefeathers*" in the margin next to the cost estimate and initialed it.

**See also:** [balderdash](#balderdash), [poppycock](#poppycock), [hogwash](#hogwash), [bull](#bull)

### malarkey

**Pronunciation:** /məˈlɑrki/ &middot; *muh-LAR-kee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *malarky*

Foolish talk, exaggeration, or nonsense — dismissive but affectionate, the word an older American uses to wave away a tall tale.

1. "*Malarkey.* He's never been within a mile of a submarine."
2. That's a lot of *malarkey* for a two-page memo.
3. "They say the new system saves four hours a day." "*Malarkey.*"
4. "*Malarkey!*" she said. "You'll believe anything."
5. Cut the *malarkey* and tell me what it costs.

**See also:** [baloney](#baloney), [hogwash](#hogwash), [poppycock](#poppycock), [bull](#bull)

### nonsense

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɑnˌsɛns/ &middot; *NON-sens*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral

Two uses. (1) A flat rejection of what was just said as untrue or unreasonable — unlike the rest of this group, it is perfectly usable in a formal setting. (2) A warm brushing-away of someone's self-criticism, worry, or excessive politeness, where it means "don't be silly, of course not."

1. "*Nonsense.* The figures were audited twice."
2. "I'm probably not qualified." "*Nonsense* — you've done this job for six years."
3. *Nonsense*, it's no trouble at all. Stay for dinner.
4. "*Nonsense!*" the professor said. "Read the footnote."
5. "We should pay you back for the gas." "*Nonsense.*"
6. He dismissed the rumor as *nonsense* before anyone could ask a second question.

**See also:** [poppycock](#poppycock), [baloney](#baloney), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### poppycock

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑpiˌkɑk/ &middot; *POP-ee-kok*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

Nonsense, delivered with a theatrical, old-money flourish. It comes from a Dutch phrase for soft dung, though no American who says it knows that. Today it is used mainly for comic effect.

1. "*Poppycock.* There is no such thing as a lucky pen."
2. *Poppycock* — the whole theory falls apart in the first paragraph.
3. "It's bad luck to move into a house on a Friday." "*Poppycock.*"
4. "*Poppycock!*" he boomed, entirely delighted with himself.
5. She called the diet plan *poppycock* and ordered dessert.

**See also:** [hogwash](#hogwash), [malarkey](#malarkey), [baloney](#baloney), [pshaw](#pshaw)

## Cringe, mock sympathy, and secondhand embarrassment

Reactions to somebody else's misstep, and refusals of sympathy dressed up as sympathy.

### awkward

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔkwərd/ &middot; *AWK-werd*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal

The one-word label for a moment that has just gone wrong socially, said by someone watching rather than by the person suffering. Stretching the first syllable (*aaawkward*) is standard and turns it into a joke everyone is in on; said flat and quietly, it is a small cruelty, because it tells the room that the moment was bad enough to name.

1. He introduced his wife by his ex-wife's name. *Awkward.*
2. "She asked when the baby was due. He isn't pregnant." "*Awkward.*"
3. *Awkward* — we both reached for the check and we both meant it.
4. Nobody clapped, and then somebody in the back said "*aaawkward*," which was worse.
5. "*Awkward*," my nephew announced, in case anyone at the table had missed it.

**See also:** [cringe](#cringe), [oof](#oof), [yikes](#yikes), [big yikes](#big-yikes)

### big yikes

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɪɡ ˈjaɪks/ &middot; *big-YIKES*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text

An intensified *yikes*: not merely awkward but genuinely bad, and almost always about somebody else's embarrassing or offensive behavior. It is young and online, and spoken aloud by anyone much over thirty-five it sounds borrowed.

1. "He replied all to complain about his own boss." "*Big yikes.*"
2. *Big yikes* — she posted the entire argument, screenshots and all.
3. "They used a stock photo of the wrong country." "*Big yikes.*"
4. I read three sentences of that comment section. *Big yikes.*
5. "*Big yikes*," she typed, and closed the tab.

**See also:** [yikes](#yikes), [cringe](#cringe), [oof](#oof), [ew](#ew)

### boo hoo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbu ˈhu/ &middot; *boo-HOO*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *boo-hoo*, *boohoo*

Written crying, used almost entirely as sarcasm: your complaint does not move me. Said gently to a small child it can be sympathetic, but between adults it is mockery.

1. "I had to wake up at nine." "*Boo hoo.*"
2. *Boo hoo* — poor guy only got two weeks in Hawaii.
3. "They gave him the smaller office." "*Boo-hoo.*"
4. She pretended to rub her eyes. "*Boo hoo.*"
5. So the free lunch was cold. *Boo hoo.*

**See also:** [cry me a river](#cry-me-a-river), [wah](#wah), [sad](#sad), [spare me](#spare-me)

### cringe

**Pronunciation:** /krɪndʒ/ &middot; *KRINJ*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text
**Variants:** *cringey*, *cringy*

A one-word verdict that something is embarrassing to witness. It began online as a noun and an adjective and is now used as a standalone reaction; it points at the awkwardness of the person being described, not at the speaker's own feelings.

1. "He did a whole rap about the quarterly numbers." "*Cringe.*"
2. *Cringe* — nobody asked him to make a speech.
3. "She commented on her own photo pretending to be a fan." "*Cringe.*"
4. I found my old blog last night. *Cringe*, start to finish.
5. "*Cringe*," my daughter said, which is apparently a complete sentence now.

**See also:** [oof](#oof), [yikes](#yikes), [big yikes](#big-yikes), [ew](#ew)

### cry me a river

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkraɪ mi ə ˈrɪvər/ &middot; *kry-mee-uh-RIV-er*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

An elaborate refusal of sympathy, from the 1953 song title: keep crying, I still don't care. It is sharper and more openly hostile than *boo hoo* — a deliberate insult, not a tease, and it ends the conversation it is aimed at.

1. "I only made six figures this year." "*Cry me a river.*"
2. *Cry me a river* — you had the same deadline as everyone else.
3. "He says the commute is killing him." "He works from home. *Cry me a river.*"
4. "*Cry me a river*," she said, and went back to her spreadsheet.
5. Two hours of overtime once a month. *Cry me a river.*

### oof

**Pronunciation:** /ʊf/, also /uf/ &middot; *UUF*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ooof*, *oooof*

Originally the sound of taking a blow to the stomach; online it has become the standard reaction to something painful, awkward, or badly misjudged that happened to someone else. It carries a wince, and it can be either sympathetic or quietly judgmental.

1. "He proposed and she said she needed time to think." "*Oof.*"
2. *Oof* — that's a rough way to find out you've been replaced.
3. "The email went to the entire company." "*Oof.*"
4. She looked at the repair estimate: "*Oof.*"
5. *Oof*, forty-two degrees and raining on move-in day.

**See also:** [yikes](#yikes), [cringe](#cringe), [big yikes](#big-yikes), [ugh](#ugh)

### sad

**Pronunciation:** /sæd/ &middot; *SAD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text

A clipped one-word judgment meaning "pathetic," tacked onto a description of somebody's behavior. It has the shape of sympathy but is nearly always a put-down, and the terse delivery is strongly associated with social media posts.

1. "He still brings up a high school football game." "*Sad.*"
2. Grown adults arguing about a parking spot in the group chat. *Sad.*
3. "They bought their followers." "*Sad*, honestly."
4. "*Sad!*" was the entire reply, which somehow made it worse.
5. Three thousand dollars on a jacket to impress people who don't like him. *Sad.*

**See also:** [cringe](#cringe), [boo hoo](#boo-hoo), [cry me a river](#cry-me-a-river), [yikes](#yikes)

### sucks to be you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsʌks tə ˈbi ju/ &middot; *suks-tuh-BEE-yoo*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *sucks to suck*, *sucks for you*

Sympathy withheld on purpose: the problem is real, and it is entirely yours. Between friends it is teasing and often affectionate; to anyone else it is simply mean, and it is not something to say to a person who is actually upset. *Sucks to suck* is the younger, blunter form.

1. "I have to work the whole holiday weekend." "*Sucks to be you.*"
2. *Sucks to be you* — I finished mine last week.
3. "They towed my car." "Out of the fire lane you parked in? *Sucks to be you.*"
4. "*Sucks to suck*," he said, and took the last slice.
5. He told me the flight was overbooked and then added, cheerfully, "*Sucks to be you*."

**See also:** [cry me a river](#cry-me-a-river), [boo hoo](#boo-hoo), [wah](#wah), [sad](#sad)

### wah

**Pronunciation:** /wɑ/ &middot; *WAH*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *waah*, *waaah*

A mocking imitation of a baby's cry, used to dismiss someone's complaint as whining. It is written more often than spoken, and it is flatly insulting — it calls the other person a baby. Teasing among friends is about the only safe place for it.

1. "I had to park three blocks away." "*Wah.*"
2. *Waah* — somebody got a B on one quiz.
3. "He's upset that nobody thanked him." "*Wah, wah.*"
4. She held two fists to her eyes and went "*waaah*."
5. "*Wah*," my brother said. "That's the world's smallest violin."

**See also:** [boo hoo](#boo-hoo), [cry me a river](#cry-me-a-river), [sad](#sad)

### womp womp

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɑmp ˈwɑmp/ &middot; *womp-WOMP*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *womp*, *wamp wamp*, *womp womp womp*

The written form of the descending "sad trombone" sting from old television, typed or hummed to file somebody's misfortune under comedy rather than sympathy. About yourself it is self-deprecating and harmless. About someone else it is a small act of contempt, and about someone else's real suffering it is a large one — a televised *womp womp* aimed at a genuinely hurt person has more than once become a national argument.

1. "They ran out of the good doughnuts." "*Womp womp.*"
2. *Womp womp* — I studied the wrong chapter.
3. "He didn't get the promotion." "*Womp womp*," somebody typed, and the chat went quiet.
4. She missed the shot at the buzzer and the whole bench went "*womp womp*."
5. I sent a six-paragraph complaint and got two words back: "*womp womp*."

**See also:** [boo hoo](#boo-hoo), [wah](#wah), [sad](#sad), [cry me a river](#cry-me-a-river)

### yeesh

**Pronunciation:** /jiʃ/ &middot; *YEESH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal

A wince with a little disapproval folded into it — somewhere between *yikes* and *sheesh*. It answers something excessive, unpleasant, or clumsily handled, and it is the mildest thing in this section: it registers that something was bad without accusing anyone of much.

1. "*Yeesh.* Forty-one emails since lunch."
2. *Yeesh* — you could have just said no.
3. "He told her that in front of the whole team." "*Yeesh.*"
4. She looked at the bill, said "*yeesh*," and split it anyway.
5. Ninety-eight degrees at nine in the morning. *Yeesh.*

**See also:** [yikes](#yikes), [oof](#oof), [ugh](#ugh), [cringe](#cringe)

### yikes

**Pronunciation:** /jaɪks/ &middot; *YIKES*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yipes*

A wince in one word. Its older sense is alarm, but its dominant modern use is distaste at what somebody said or did — "that was a bad look." Tone tells you which one you are hearing.

1. "He asked how much she paid for the house — at the funeral." "*Yikes.*"
2. *Yikes* — that's a lot of exclamation points for a work email.
3. "They fired him by text message." "*Yikes.*"
4. She read the first paragraph out loud and stopped. "*Yikes.*"
5. Six hundred dollars for one night in that motel? *Yikes.*
