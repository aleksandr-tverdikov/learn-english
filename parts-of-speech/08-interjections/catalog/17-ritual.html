Ritual formulas, toasts, blessings, and politeness#

The fixed phrases a situation demands — the words you say because the moment calls for words, not because you have anything new to report.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

These are scripts, not sentences. A sneeze requires bless you, a raised glass requires cheers, a favor requires thanks and then requires an answer to the thanks, and saying nothing at those moments is itself a message. Because they are frozen wholes, they resist grammar: many happy returns has no verb, bon appétit and mazel tov are not English at all, and good luck with that means almost the opposite of its parts. Two things to watch throughout — register, which is where the real danger is (my bad and my apologies cover the same mistake and belong in completely different rooms), and tone, since a startling number of these formulas flip to sarcasm on delivery alone: thanks a lot, congratulations, be my guest, and bless your heart are all sincere or cutting depending entirely on how you say them.

Variants

A formal apology, a formal request for a repeat, or — with a rising, indignant tone — an objection meaning how dare you. That third sense is the one you will actually hear most often in American English.

  2. The biopsy came back clean. Alhamdulillah.
  3. He pushed back from the table. "Alhamdulillah. I can't eat another bite."
  4. Alhamdulillah — everybody got out of the building.
  5. She says hamdulillah where her coworkers say thank God, and it means the same thing.
