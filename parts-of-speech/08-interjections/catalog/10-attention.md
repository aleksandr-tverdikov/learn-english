# Attention, silencing, and directives

> The interjections that act on the listener: they point a head in your direction, shut a room up, stop something mid-motion, clear a path, or hand somebody the floor.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

Everything in this file is **volitive**: it is aimed outward at another person and asks them to do something — look, listen, be quiet, stop, move, or go ahead. That makes register the whole ballgame. *Excuse me* and *knock it off* both mean "stop that," but one is what you say to a stranger on a train and the other is what you say to your kid; *zip it* and *shut up* will end a conversation with a coworker permanently. Most of these words are shouted or snapped, so they take exclamation points far more often than periods, and two of them — *shh* and *psst* — are not spellings of words at all but written-down noises, which is why their pronunciations are described rather than transcribed. *Ahem* sits in between: spelled out and read aloud it really is /əˈhɛm/, but what a speaker actually produces is a deliberate cough.

## Getting someone's attention

### ahem

**Pronunciation:** /əˈhɛm/ &middot; *uh-HEM*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *ahem-ahem*

A deliberate throat-clearing used as a signal rather than a real cough. It asks for attention, marks that you are about to speak, or delivers a discreet correction or disapproval without saying anything explicit.

1. "*Ahem.* I believe you're sitting in my seat."
2. *Ahem* — if anyone's interested, I finished the report an hour ago.
3. She gave a small *ahem* and the room went quiet.
4. "*Ahem!*" The teacher didn't have to say anything else.
5. *Ahem.* Some of us have been waiting in line for twenty minutes.

**See also:** [psst](#psst), [hey](#hey), [excuse me](#excuse-me)

### attention

**Pronunciation:** /əˈtɛnʃən/ &middot; *uh-TEN-shun*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *attention please*, *may I have your attention*

A formal call for a whole group to stop what they are doing and listen. It is the standard opener for public-address announcements, and barked as a single word it is the military command to stand up straight.

1. "*Attention*, passengers: the six-fifteen to Boston is now boarding on track nine."
2. "*Attention*, shoppers — the store closes in fifteen minutes."
3. May I have your *attention*, please? We're about to get started.
4. "*Attention*, everyone," the coordinator called over the noise. "We're starting in two minutes."
5. The sergeant barked "*Attention*!" and forty recruits snapped upright.

**See also:** [listen up](#listen-up), [hear ye](#hear-ye), [order](#order), [quiet](#quiet)

### behold

**Pronunciation:** /bɪˈhoʊld/ &middot; *bih-HOHLD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

An archaic command to look. It survives in scripture, ceremony, and — far more often today — in deliberate self-mockery when someone unveils something unimpressive.

1. "*Behold*!" he said, pulling the sheet off the finished table.
2. And *behold*, a star appeared in the east.
3. She opened the fridge, waved a hand, and said, "*Behold* — dinner."
4. *Behold*, the mighty three-page report I spent all weekend on.
5. The magician raised both arms and shouted, "*Behold*!"

**See also:** [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold), [look](#look), [check it out](#check-it-out), [hark](#hark)

### excuse me

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskjuz ˌmi/ &middot; *ik-SKYOOZ mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *'scuse me*, *excuse me?*

The all-purpose American politeness formula, doing four jobs at once: getting a stranger's attention, asking someone to let you pass, apologizing for a small offense such as a bump or a burp, and — with a sharp rise at the end — signaling that you did not hear something or cannot believe what you just heard.

1. "*Excuse me*, do you know if this bus goes downtown?"
2. *Excuse me* — could I squeeze past? My stop is next.
3. He bumped her cart and said, "Oh, *excuse me*."
4. "*Excuse me*?" she said, leaning in. "Would you mind repeating that?"
5. *Excuse me*, but I was in line before you were.
6. "*Excuse me*! I don't remember asking for your opinion."

**See also:** [pardon me](#pardon-me), [coming through](#coming-through), [ahem](#ahem), [hey](#hey)

### hark

**Pronunciation:** /hɑrk/ &middot; *HARK*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *hearken*

An archaic "listen!" No American says it straight anymore outside of hymns and old poetry; it survives as a joke, delivered with mock solemnity and usually a hand cupped to the ear.

1. *Hark*! The herald angels sing.
2. "*Hark*," he said, cupping a hand to his ear. "Is that the pizza guy?"
3. *Hark*, I hear the sweet sound of the coffee maker finishing.
4. The narrator began, "*Hark*, travelers, and hear my tale."
5. "*Hark*! Who goes there?" the kid yelled down from the treehouse.

**See also:** [listen](#listen), [behold](#behold), [hear ye](#hear-ye), [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold)

### hear ye

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɪr ˌji/ &middot; *HEER yee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *hear ye, hear ye*, *hear this*

The town crier's opener, almost always doubled: "Hear ye, hear ye!" Modern Americans use it only in period settings, in courtrooms, or when clowning around before an announcement at home.

1. "*Hear ye, hear ye*! The court is now in session."
2. *Hear ye, hear ye* — the annual bake sale begins at noon!
3. He stood on a chair, banged a spoon against a glass, and said, "*Hear ye, hear ye*."
4. The crier's voice carried down the street: "*Hear ye*! A royal proclamation!"
5. "*Hear ye, hear ye*, the kitchen is officially closed," my dad announced at nine.

**See also:** [oyez](#oyez), [attention](#attention), [order](#order), [hark](#hark)

### hello?

**Pronunciation:** /həˈloʊ/ &middot; *huh-LOH (also /hɛˈloʊ/, heh-LOH)*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hellooo*, *helloooo*

Not the greeting. With a rising, drawn-out contour, "Hello?" means "is anyone there?" — into a phone, an empty house, or at a person who has clearly stopped listening. Said flat and clipped, it means "isn't this completely obvious?"

1. "*Hello*? Is anybody home?" she called through the screen door.
2. *Hello*? Can you hear me? I think the call is breaking up.
3. I waved a hand in front of his face. "*Hello*? Earth to Jason."
4. "*Hello*? You're the one who said you didn't want it," she snapped.
5. He knocked twice and pushed the door open. "*Hello*? Dr. Reyes?"

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [yoo-hoo](#yoo-hoo), [look alive](#look-alive), [psst](#psst)

### hey

**Pronunciation:** /heɪ/ &middot; *HAY*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *heyyy*, *hey there*, *ay*

The default American attention-getter, and a casual greeting besides. Said sharply it is a protest — "Hey! That's mine" — and drawn out it is warm and friendly. It is fine with friends, coworkers, and strangers on the street, but it is too casual for addressing a stranger in a formal setting, where *excuse me* is the safe choice.

1. "*Hey*, do you have a second?"
2. *Hey*! That's my parking spot — I was waiting for it.
3. "*Hey*, over here!" she yelled, waving both arms.
4. *Hey*, thanks for covering my shift last night.
5. He tapped on the driver's window. "*Hey*, your lights are still on."

**See also:** [yo](#yo), [psst](#psst), [look](#look), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [hello?](#hello)

### listen

**Pronunciation:** /ˈlɪsən/ &middot; *LIS-uhn*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *listen here*, *now listen*

A verb used as a conversational flag: what follows is important, or blunt, or both. It often carries a note of "stop arguing and take this in," and it is the softest way in English to announce that you are about to be direct.

1. "*Listen*, I'm going to be straight with you: we can't afford it."
2. *Listen*, if you need to leave early, just say so.
3. "*Listen* to me. You did nothing wrong."
4. *Listen*, I know you're upset, but yelling isn't going to help.
5. "Okay, *listen*," the coach said. "Here's the plan for the second half."

**See also:** [look](#look), [listen up](#listen-up), [look here](#look-here), [hey](#hey)

### listen up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɪsən ˈʌp/ &middot; *lis-uhn UP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *listen up, everybody*

A command to a whole group to stop talking and pay attention. Coaches, sergeants, and shift managers use it constantly; from a peer with no authority it sounds bossy.

1. "*Listen up*, everybody — the bus leaves at seven sharp."
2. *Listen up*! I'm only going to explain this once.
3. The coach clapped twice. "*Listen up*. We're changing the lineup."
4. "All right, *listen up*," the nurse said to the room. "Visiting hours end at eight."
5. *Listen up*, folks, because this part is going to be on the test.

**See also:** [listen](#listen), [attention](#attention), [pay attention](#pay-attention), [heads up](#heads-up), [quiet](#quiet)

### lo and behold

**Pronunciation:** /ˌloʊ ən bɪˈhoʊld/ &middot; *loh-uhn-bih-HOHLD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *lo*

A set phrase, always at least slightly tongue-in-cheek, introducing a result that was predictable, ironic, or suspiciously convenient. The bare *lo* survives only in scripture and in parody of it.

1. I checked the fridge again and, *lo and behold*, the leftovers were gone.
2. He swore he'd never come back, and then, *lo and behold*, there he was.
3. We unplugged the router, plugged it back in, and *lo and behold*, the internet worked.
4. *Lo and behold*, the one day I forget my umbrella is the day it pours.
5. She dug through the couch cushions and — *lo and behold* — there were her keys.

**See also:** [behold](#behold), [get this](#get-this), [guess what](#guess-what), [hark](#hark)

### look

**Pronunciation:** /lʊk/ &middot; *LUUK*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *look now*, *lookit*

Like *listen*, a verb repurposed as a flag before a blunt or final statement. It usually carries a trace of impatience: the speaker is done going in circles and is about to say the plain thing.

1. "*Look*, I don't have time to argue about this."
2. *Look*, if it doesn't work out, we'll figure something else out.
3. "*Look*, I'm sorry. I should have called."
4. *Look*, all I'm saying is that we should read the contract first.
5. "*Look*, either you want the job or you don't."

**See also:** [listen](#listen), [look here](#look-here), [now see here](#now-see-here), [check it out](#check-it-out)

### look alive

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlʊk əˈlaɪv/ &middot; *luuk uh-LYV*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *look sharp*

A shout telling someone to snap out of a daze and get ready, usually because something is about to come at them. It lives on ball fields, loading docks, and busy restaurant lines.

1. "*Look alive*, Martinez!" the coach yelled as the ball came off the bat.
2. *Look alive*, people — the health inspector just pulled into the lot.
3. He snapped his fingers. "*Look alive*. Customers."
4. "*Look alive* up there!" the foreman called to the crew on the scaffolding.
5. "*Look alive*," the lifeguard said to the guard on the next chair. "Kid in the deep end."

**See also:** [heads up](#heads-up), [hello?](#hello), [listen up](#listen-up), [chop chop](#chop-chop)

### look here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlʊk ˈhɪr/ &middot; *luuk HEER*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *see here*, *look-a-here*

An old-fashioned opener for a scolding or a challenge. It is confrontational, and to modern ears it sounds like a man in a black-and-white movie about to lose his temper.

1. "*Look here*, young man, that is no way to speak to your mother."
2. *Look here* — I've been a customer for twenty years and I expect better.
3. "Now *look here*, nobody said anything about paying extra."
4. He jabbed a finger at the survey stake. "*Look here*, that fence is six feet onto my property."
5. "*Look here*," the old man said, "you'll do it my way or not at all."

**See also:** [now see here](#now-see-here), [look](#look), [listen](#listen)

### now see here

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaʊ ˌsi ˈhɪr/ &middot; *now see HEER*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *see here*

The same indignant opener as *look here*, one degree stuffier. It announces that the speaker is about to lay down the law, and in current American speech it is almost always a parody of an outraged older man.

1. "*Now see here*, I will not be spoken to that way."
2. *Now see here*, sir — this is a private club.
3. "*Now see here*, young lady, you are not going out dressed like that."
4. He stood up, red-faced. "*Now see here*! That's my chair."
5. "*Now see here*," the principal said, "we take these things very seriously."

**See also:** [look here](#look-here), [look](#look), [listen](#listen)

### oyez

**Pronunciation:** /oʊˈjeɪ/ &middot; *oh-YAY (also /oʊˈji/, oh-YEE, and /oʊˈjɛz/, oh-YEZ)*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *oyes*

The bailiff's ceremonial cry that opens a court session, from Anglo-French for "hear!" In American life it appears almost only at the opening of the Supreme Court and at Renaissance fairs, and it is traditionally called three times.

1. "*Oyez*! *Oyez*! *Oyez*! All persons having business before the Honorable Court are admonished to draw near."
2. The marshal's *oyez* is nearly word for word the one used two hundred years ago.
3. "*Oyez*, *oyez*!" the costumed crier called across the fairgrounds.
4. Court opened with the traditional *oyez* and everyone rose.
5. The fourth graders opened their mock trial with a shaky "*Oyez*, *oyez*."

**See also:** [hear ye](#hear-ye), [order](#order), [silence](#silence), [attention](#attention)

### pardon me

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑrdən ˌmi/ &middot; *PAR-duhn mee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *pardon*, *pardon?*, *I beg your pardon*

A more formal cousin of *excuse me*: it apologizes for a small intrusion, asks permission to squeeze past, or — as a bare "Pardon?" — asks someone to repeat what they said. "I beg your pardon," delivered with a falling, icy stress, is not an apology at all but a rebuke.

1. "*Pardon me*, is this seat taken?"
2. *Pardon me* — I need to get to the aisle.
3. "*Pardon*?" she said, leaning in. "The music's too loud."
4. He stepped on my foot and murmured, "*Pardon me*."
5. "I beg your *pardon*," my grandmother said icily. "I was speaking."
6. *Pardon me* for asking, but how much did that thing cost?

**See also:** [excuse me](#excuse-me), [coming through](#coming-through), [after you](#after-you), [one moment](#one-moment)

### pay attention

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpeɪ əˈtɛnʃən/ &middot; *pay uh-TEN-shun*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *pay attention now*

A direct order to focus, aimed at somebody whose mind has visibly wandered. Teachers, coaches, and driving instructors say it a hundred times a day; between adult peers it is condescending, because it announces out loud that you think the other person was not listening.

1. "*Pay attention*. This part is going to be on the test."
2. *Pay attention* — our exit is right after the second light.
3. "Eyes up here, *pay attention*," the instructor said, tapping the whiteboard.
4. She snapped her fingers in front of the screen. "*Pay attention*."
5. *Pay attention* to how she does it, because you're up next.

**See also:** [listen](#listen), [listen up](#listen-up), [look alive](#look-alive), [hey](#hey)

### psst

**Pronunciation:** /pst/ &middot; *pssst — no vowel at all, just p-s-s-t hissed through the teeth*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pst*, *psssst*

A low hiss aimed at exactly one person so that nobody else notices. It always carries a whiff of secrecy: come here, look at this, or you have spinach in your teeth.

1. "*Psst*! Over here — I saved you a seat."
2. *Psst* — your fly is down.
3. She leaned across the aisle. "*Psst*, what page are we on?"
4. "*Psst*!" he whispered from the doorway. "Is she gone yet?"
5. *Psst*, don't tell anybody, but there's cake in the break room.

**See also:** [ahem](#ahem), [hey](#hey), [yoo-hoo](#yoo-hoo), [shh](#shh)

### say

**Pronunciation:** /seɪ/ &middot; *SAY*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *say there*, *I say*

A mid-century opener that flags down a stranger or floats a thought that just occurred to you: "Say, don't I know you from somewhere?" Nobody under sixty uses it straight anymore, so from a younger speaker it reads as a period impression. *I say* is the British cousin and sounds it.

1. "*Say*, aren't you Marjorie's brother?"
2. *Say*, that reminds me — did you ever hear back about the house?
3. "*Say*, buddy, you dropped your glove."
4. He looked up from the paper. "*Say*, what time does the game start?"
5. *Say*, that's a good-looking bike.

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [hark](#hark), [now see here](#now-see-here)

### yo

**Pronunciation:** /joʊ/ &middot; *YOH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *ayo*, *yo yo*

A blunt call for attention and a casual greeting, strongly associated with urban American speech, hip-hop, and young men. It is friendly among peers and abrasive aimed at a stranger, a customer, or a boss.

1. "*Yo*! Wait up!"
2. *Yo*, is this your backpack?
3. He stuck his head in the door. "*Yo*, we're leaving in five."
4. "*Yo*, Marcus — check your phone."
5. *Yo*, that was the best game I've seen all season.

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [psst](#psst), [hello?](#hello)

### yoo-hoo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈju ˌhu/ &middot; *YOO-hoo*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yoohoo*, *yoo hoo*

A sing-song, two-note call used to reach someone at a distance or to snap someone out of a daydream. It sounds friendly and a little old-fashioned; aimed at an adult who is ignoring you, it is openly mocking.

1. "*Yoo-hoo*! Over here, honey!" she called across the parking lot.
2. *Yoo-hoo* — anybody home?
3. "*Yoo-hoo*, up here!" she called down from the second-floor window.
4. "*Yoo-hoo*, Mrs. Petrelli!" the neighbor called over the fence.
5. He knocked on the open door. "*Yoo-hoo*. Room service."

**See also:** [hello?](#hello), [psst](#psst), [hey](#hey)

## Flagging what comes next

### check it out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʃɛk ɪt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *chek it OWT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *check this out*, *check that out*

An invitation to look at or try something the speaker thinks is genuinely cool. It points at a thing you can see or hold, not at an idea.

1. "*Check it out* — I found this record at a garage sale for a dollar."
2. *Check it out*, they finally opened that taco place on Fifth.
3. "*Check this out*," he said, holding up his phone.
4. *Check it out*: the whole thing folds down into a backpack.
5. She spun around in the dress. "*Check it out*. Twelve bucks."

**See also:** [get a load of this](#get-a-load-of-this), [get this](#get-this), [look](#look), [behold](#behold)

### get a load of this

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɛt ə ˈloʊd əv ˌðɪs/ &middot; *get uh LOHD uhv this*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *get a load of that*, *get a load of this guy*

A nudge to look at something ridiculous, outrageous, or hard to believe. Unlike *check it out*, it almost always carries mockery or indignation.

1. "*Get a load of this* — they're charging nine dollars for a cup of coffee."
2. *Get a load of this guy*, parked across two spaces.
3. He waved the letter. "*Get a load of this*. They denied the claim again."
4. *Get a load of that* sunset.
5. "*Get a load of this*," she said, reading the email out loud in a fake executive voice.

**See also:** [check it out](#check-it-out), [get this](#get-this), [guess what](#guess-what)

### get this

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɛt ðɪs/ &middot; *GET this*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *and get this*

A storyteller's flag dropped into the middle of an anecdote, right before the surprising part. It means "wait until you hear what comes next."

1. So I called the bank, and — *get this* — they'd closed the wrong account.
2. "He showed up two hours late, and *get this*, he asked me to pay for parking."
3. *Get this*: the flight was delayed, rebooked, and then delayed again.
4. They fixed the whole thing in one day, and *get this*, they didn't charge us.
5. And *get this* — she'd known the entire time and never said a word.

**See also:** [guess what](#guess-what), [get a load of this](#get-a-load-of-this), [lo and behold](#lo-and-behold)

### guess what

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɛs ˈwʌt/ &middot; *ges WUT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *guess what?*, *you'll never guess*

An opener designed to make the listener say "what?" before you deliver your news. Children use it literally; adults use it as a wind-up, and sometimes sarcastically, right before an "I told you so."

1. "*Guess what*! I got the apartment!"
2. *Guess what* — we're going to Chicago for Thanksgiving.
3. "Mom, *guess what*, I lost a tooth!"
4. You told him not to touch it, and *guess what*, he touched it.
5. *Guess what*? The dryer broke again.

**See also:** [get this](#get-this), [get a load of this](#get-a-load-of-this), [heads up](#heads-up)

### heads up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛdz ˈʌp/ &middot; *hedz UP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *heads-up*, *head's up*

Two jobs. Shouted, it warns that something is flying at you and you should look now. In conversation and email it means advance notice — flagging something before it lands on someone.

1. "*Heads up*!" someone yelled, and a foul ball landed two rows behind us.
2. Just a *heads up* — the meeting moved to two o'clock.
3. "*Heads up*, the boss is walking this way."
4. *Heads up*, everybody, there's construction on Route 9 this morning.
5. Thanks for the *heads up* about the parking situation.

**See also:** [look alive](#look-alive), [listen up](#listen-up), [gangway](#gangway), [guess what](#guess-what)

### wait for it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌweɪt fər ˈɪt/ &middot; *wayt fer IT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A comic's setup line, dropped in one beat before the punchline or the reveal so the listener holds still for it. It is deliberately theatrical, and it only works if the payoff actually lands.

1. "They fixed it in an hour and charged us — *wait for it* — nine hundred dollars."
2. *Wait for it*. Any second now the dog is going to notice the cat.
3. "And the winner is — *wait for it* — the intern."
4. He held up one finger. "*Wait for it*."
5. She got the job, the apartment, and, *wait for it*, a company car.

**See also:** [get this](#get-this), [get a load of this](#get-a-load-of-this), [guess what](#guess-what), [check it out](#check-it-out)

### watch this

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɑtʃ ˈðɪs/ &middot; *wotch THIS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *watch*, *watch me*

The announcement before a stunt, a trick, or a demonstration — the speaker is about to do something and wants an audience for it. Coming from a kid on a diving board or a cousin holding a lighter, it is famously the last thing said before something goes wrong.

1. "*Watch this*!" he yelled from the end of the diving board.
2. *Watch this* — if you hold the button down, the whole thing resets.
3. She lined up the shot. "*Watch this*."
4. "*Watch this*," my nephew said, and rode straight into the hedge.
5. *Watch this*, and then tell me you can't hear the difference.

**See also:** [check it out](#check-it-out), [get a load of this](#get-a-load-of-this), [look](#look), [behold](#behold)

## Calling for silence

### button it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʌtən ɪt/ &middot; *BUT-uhn it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *button your lip*, *button up*

A rude order to stop talking — milder and more old-fashioned than *shut up*, but still an insult unless you are clearly joking with someone you know well.

1. "*Button it*, both of you. I'm trying to drive."
2. *Button it* — here she comes.
3. "You want to keep this job? *Button it*."
4. I opened my mouth and my sister mouthed "*button it*" from across the table.
5. "*Button your lip*," his brother hissed, "or Dad's going to hear."

**See also:** [zip it](#zip-it), [can it](#can-it), [put a sock in it](#put-a-sock-in-it), [shut up](#shut-up)

### can it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkæn ɪt/ &middot; *KAN it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

A brusque "stop talking" or "stop doing that," with the flavor of an old movie or an irritated uncle. It is dismissive, and it stings coming from anyone who outranks you.

1. "*Can it*, Wilson. Nobody asked."
2. *Can it*, you two — I can hear you from the kitchen.
3. "*Can it* with the sound effects," she said.
4. "*Can it* and eat," my father said, and that was the end of the discussion.
5. "*Can it*!" he snapped, and the laughing stopped.

### hold your tongue

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhoʊld jər ˈtʌŋ/ &middot; *hohld yer TUNG*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *watch your tongue*, *hold your peace*

A stern, old-fashioned order to stop speaking, with real authority behind it — a parent, a judge, a commanding officer. Said today it lands as either genuinely severe or costume-drama, with very little in between. Do not confuse it with *bite your tongue*, which means "don't jinx it."

1. "*Hold your tongue* until you're spoken to."
2. *Hold your tongue*, sir. You are addressing the court.
3. She wanted to argue the point, but she held her tongue.
4. "You will *hold your tongue* in this house," his father said.
5. I held my tongue for about four seconds and then said it anyway.

### hush

**Pronunciation:** /hʌʃ/ &middot; *HUSH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hush up*, *hush now*, *hushabye*

A gentle "be quiet," used with children, with someone who is crying, or when you need to hear something. *Hush up* is the sharper, Southern-flavored version and is a genuine reprimand.

1. "*Hush*, baby, it's just thunder."
2. *Hush* now and let Grandpa finish his story.
3. "*Hush*!" she whispered. "Somebody's in the hallway."

### keep it down

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *keep the noise down*

Not "be silent" but "be quieter." It is what you say through a wall, in a movie theater, or to kids who are supposed to be asleep.

1. "Hey, *keep it down* in there! Some of us are trying to sleep."
2. Could you guys *keep it down*? I'm on a call.
3. "*Keep it down*," the librarian said without looking up.

### mum's the word

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mum*

A promise — or a request — that something stays secret. It is most often the reply when someone has just told you not to tell anybody.

1. "Don't say anything to Dana about the party." — "*Mum's the word*."

### not a word

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ə ˈwɜrd/ &middot; *not uh WURD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *not one word*, *not another word*

An order to say nothing at all — either right this second, or to anyone, ever. Said quietly it is far more menacing than shouting.

1. "*Not a word* to your mother about this, understand?"

### order

**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *order in the court*, *order, please*

The presiding officer's call for a noisy room to fall silent and get back to business. Outside courtrooms and formal meetings, Americans use it only as a joke about a chaotic dinner table.

1. "*Order*! *Order* in the court!" the judge said, banging the gavel.

### pipe down

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Tells a noisy person or group to quiet down, in a cranky, old-school tone. It is what an adult says to rowdy kids, or a coach to a loud bench — never what you say to your boss.

1. "*Pipe down* back there! I can't hear the radio."

The plain command for silence. On its own it is sharp; softened with *please* or *down* it becomes the everyday version that teachers, ushers, and parents use all day long.

### save it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈseɪv ɪt/ &middot; *SAVE it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *save your breath*, *save it for someone who cares*

Stop talking, because whatever is coming — an excuse, an apology, a sales pitch — is not going to work on me. It is dismissive by design and it stings, but it contains nothing profane, which is why it survives in offices where *shut up* would end a career.

1. "*Save it*. I already talked to your sister."

**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *sh*, *shhh*, *ssh*

The universal hush sound, usually delivered with a finger against the lips. It asks for silence, and said softly and repeatedly it also comforts someone who is upset.

1. "*Shh*, they can hear us through the wall."

### shush

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʌʃ/ &middot; *SHUSH (also /ʃʊʃ/, SHUUSH)*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *shush up*

A word-shaped version of *shh*, used both as the command and as a regular verb ("she shushed him"). It sounds softer and more familiar than a bare *shh*, and between friends it can be almost affectionate.

1. "*Shush*, I'm trying to hear the announcement."

### shut up

**Variants:** *shut it*, *shut your mouth*, *shaddap*

The blunt, hostile order to stop talking. It is genuinely offensive from a stranger and enough to start a fight; learners should treat it as off-limits at work. Among close friends it has a second, harmless life as an expression of delighted disbelief, roughly "no way!"

1. "*Shut up* and let her talk."

A one-word command with institutional authority behind it — courtroom, church, concert hall, parade ground. From a peer it sounds theatrical, and that is usually the joke.

A curt "stop talking," often paired with a zipping motion across the lips. Playful with kids and close friends; hostile with anyone else.

## Stopping, waiting, and holding off

### back off

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbæk ˈɔf/ &middot; *bak AWF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *back up*, *back away*

Tells someone to move away physically, or to stop pressuring you. Both senses are confrontational, and the physical one is a warning that something is about to happen.

1. "*Back off*! I'm warning you."
2. *Back off*, man, I'm not looking for trouble.
3. "Tell your lawyer to *back off*," she said.
4. *Back off* — he's had a rough week and doesn't need a lecture.
5. He put both hands up. "*Back off*, I'm going."

**See also:** [lay off](#lay-off), [give it a rest](#give-it-a-rest), [easy](#easy), [step aside](#step-aside)

### cut it out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌt ɪt ˈaʊt/ &middot; *kut it OWT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *cut that out*, *cut it*

Tells somebody to stop doing something annoying. It is a shade more familiar than *knock it off* and is the standard American parent-to-kid line at the dinner table.

1. "*Cut it out*! That hurts."
2. *Cut it out*, you two, or we're turning this car around.
3. "*Cut that out*," she said, pulling the cat off the counter.
4. He kept poking me until I told him to *cut it out*.
5. "*Cut it out* — I'm being serious."

**See also:** [knock it off](#knock-it-off), [quit it](#quit-it), [stop](#stop), [enough](#enough)

### drop it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdrɑp ɪt/ &middot; *DROP it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *let it drop*, *drop the subject*

Stop bringing this subject up. With a hard edge it means the conversation is over and pushing further will start a fight. It is also, literally, what you say to a dog with your shoe in its mouth.

1. "*Drop it*, Ellen. I don't want to talk about it."
2. Just *drop it* — you're not going to change his mind.
3. "*Drop it*!" she yelled at the dog, who had one of my slippers.
4. I brought it up one more time and he said, "*Drop it*."
5. Can we *drop it* and just enjoy dinner?

**See also:** [give it a rest](#give-it-a-rest), [enough](#enough), [lay off](#lay-off), [that'll do](#thatll-do)

### enough

**Pronunciation:** /ɪˈnʌf/ &middot; *ih-NUF*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *that's enough*, *enough already*, *enough is enough*

Calls a halt because a limit has been reached. Snapped by a parent, teacher, or manager it ends the behavior on the spot; *enough already* is the exasperated, New York-flavored version.

1. "*Enough*! Both of you, go to your rooms."
2. *That's enough* — I've heard everything I need to hear.
3. "*Enough already* with the complaining."
4. *Enough*. We are not doing this again tonight.
5. "That's *enough*, thank you," the judge told the witness.

**See also:** [that'll do](#thatll-do), [stop](#stop), [cut it out](#cut-it-out), [knock it off](#knock-it-off), [give it a rest](#give-it-a-rest)

### freeze

**Pronunciation:** /friz/ &middot; *FREEZ*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *freeze right there*

Stop moving this instant. It is the police command of a thousand American movies, and it is equally at home in a game of tag or aimed at a kid about to step on broken glass.

1. "*Freeze*! Police! Hands where I can see them."
2. "*Freeze*," I said. "There's glass all over the floor."
3. The kids played freeze tag, shrieking "*Freeze*!" every few seconds.
4. *Freeze* — don't move, there's a wasp on your shoulder.
5. "*Freeze* right there," the guard said, stepping out of the booth.

### give it a rest

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɪv ɪt ə ˈrɛst/ &middot; *giv it uh REST*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *give it a break*

An exasperated "stop going on about it." It says the topic, the joke, or the nagging has thoroughly worn out its welcome.

1. "*Give it a rest*, would you? We've been over this twice."
2. *Give it a rest* — nobody cares about your fantasy team.
3. "Oh, *give it a rest*," he muttered.
4. She's been complaining since Tuesday and she will not *give it a rest*.
5. *Give it a rest*, Dad. I'll do it after dinner.

### halt

**Pronunciation:** /hɔlt/ &middot; *HAWLT*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *halt right there*

A formal command to stop moving, at home in the military, at a checkpoint, and in the old sentry's challenge "Halt! Who goes there?" Civilians use it only for comic effect.

1. "*Halt*! Who goes there?"
2. The guard raised a hand. "*Halt*. Identification, please."
3. "Company, *halt*!" the drill sergeant barked.
4. *Halt* — this is a restricted area.

### hands off

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hands off it*, *hands to yourself*

Don't touch that. It guards an object — a dessert, a tool, somebody else's phone — rather than a person's space, which is what separates it from *back off*. Snapped at an adult it stops being a request.

1. "*Hands off*! That's for the party."

**Variants:** *hang on a sec*, *hang on a minute*

Wait a moment — the everyday version of *hold on*, used for short delays and also for "wait, that doesn't add up."

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *knock that off*

A firm "stop that," aimed at behavior that is annoying or about to get someone hurt. It is the classic American parent-to-kid and coach-to-player line, and it carries real irritation.

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *lay off it*, *lay off him*

### not so fast

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt soʊ ˈfæst/ &middot; *not soh FAST*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Hold on — you are not getting away with that. It is aimed at somebody who is leaving, claiming a win, or trying to slip one past you, and it almost always comes with a raised eyebrow rather than a raised voice.

1. "*Not so fast*. You still owe me twenty bucks."

### one moment

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌn ˈmoʊmənt/ &middot; *wun MOH-muhnt*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *one moment please*, *just a moment*

The polite, service-industry way to ask someone to wait. Receptionists, hosts, and phone agents say it all day; friends say *just a sec* instead.

1. "*One moment*, please. I'll see if she's available."
2. *One moment*, sir — your table is almost ready.
3. "*Just a moment*," the clerk said, typing. "I'm pulling up your account."

### sit tight

## Calming people down and breaking things up

### break it up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbreɪk ɪt ˈʌp/ &middot; *brayk it UP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *break it up, you two*

What you shout at people who are fighting or arguing, telling them to separate. It is standard from police officers, teachers, coaches, and bartenders, and it assumes you have the standing to say it.

1. "*Break it up*, you two!" the teacher shouted across the cafeteria.

3. "All right, *break it up*. Nothing to see here."
4. *Break it up*, guys — save it for the game.

### calm down

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkɑm ˈdaʊn/ &middot; *kahm DOWN*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *just calm down*, *calm down now*

The plainest way to tell an upset person to get hold of themselves. It is also famously counterproductive: aimed at somebody who is genuinely angry or frightened, it reads as an accusation of overreacting and reliably makes things worse.

1. "*Calm down*. Start at the beginning and tell me what happened."
2. *Calm down* — nobody is getting fired over a spreadsheet.
3. "Don't tell me to *calm down*," she said, which is the whole problem with telling people to calm down.
4. He walked a lap around the parking lot to *calm down* before going back in.
5. "Everybody *calm down*. The alarm was a test."

### chill

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɪl/ &middot; *CHIL*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *chill out*, *chillax*

Tells an agitated person to calm down. It is friendly enough among peers, and infuriating when the other person is genuinely upset — telling someone to chill is often what escalates the argument.

1. "*Chill*, dude. It's not that big a deal."
2. *Chill out* — we still have an hour before the movie.
3. "Would you please just *chill*? You're making me nervous."
4. He told her to *chill* and she got twice as angry.
5. *Chill*, I already paid the bill.

### cool it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈkul ɪt/ &middot; *KOOL it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *cool your jets*

Stop what you are doing and calm down, said to somebody who is getting loud, angry, or carried away. *Cool your jets* is the teasing version.

1. "*Cool it*, you two, before somebody gets hurt."
2. *Cool it* with the jokes — his mother is standing right there.
3. "You need to *cool it*," the coach said. "One more foul and you're out."
4. *Cool it*, I was only asking.
5. She told them to *cool it*, and to my surprise they actually did.

**See also:** [chill](#chill), [easy](#easy), [simmer down](#simmer-down), [knock it off](#knock-it-off)

### easy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈizi/ &middot; *EE-zee*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *easy there*, *easy now*, *easy does it*, *take it easy*

A soothing "slow down" — used with a spooked animal, an angry friend, or somebody carrying something heavy and breakable. The tone is calm and low, never sharp.

1. "*Easy*, boy," he said, patting the horse's neck.

### now now

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaʊ ˈnaʊ/ &middot; *now NOW*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *now, now*

A soothing two-beat used to head off tears or a squabble before it grows. It is gentle, but because it is fundamentally what adults say to small children, aimed at a grown-up it can land as deeply patronizing.

1. "*Now now*, there's no need for any of that."

### relax

**Pronunciation:** /rɪˈlæks/ &middot; *rih-LAKS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *just relax*, *relax, relax*

Ease up — you are more wound up than the situation calls for. Between friends it is reassuring; aimed at somebody who is genuinely upset it is dismissive, and it earns exactly the same glare as *calm down*.

1. "*Relax*, I already sent it."
2. *Relax* — we've got a full hour before we have to leave.
3. "Would you *relax*? You're going to wear a hole in the carpet."
4. He told me to *relax*, which used up the last of my patience.

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛtəl ˈdaʊn/ &middot; *set-uhl DOWN*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *settle*

Calm down and get quiet — the standard teacher's phrase for a restless room and a parent's for an over-excited kid. It is firm without being rude.

1. "All right, everyone, *settle down* and take your seats."

Calm down, said to somebody who is worked up. It is folksier and more teasing than *settle down*, and it can come across as condescending.

Go away, and go now. It is hostile — the line a shopkeeper yells at kids loitering out front — though among friends it can be mock-hostile.

**Variants:** *clear the area*, *clear the room*

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *comin' through*

Announced rather than requested: you are moving through a crowd and people need to make room. Standard for servers, movers, and anybody carrying something hot or bulky.

1. "*Coming through*! Hot plates!"

Clear a path, from the old nautical call for the ship's passageway. Today it sounds either shipboard, cartoonish, or like something shouted in a 1940s newsroom — which is usually the point.

1. "*Gangway*!" the busser called, backing out of the kitchen with a full tray.
2. *Gangway*, everybody — she's carrying the baby.

### get lost

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

Go away — sharper than *beat it* and unmistakably an insult. It is also how you refuse an offer or a person outright.

### step aside

**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *step back*, *stand aside*

1. "*Step aside*, please. Let them through."
2. *Step aside*, sir. We need to check your bag.
3. "Everybody *step aside* — ambulance coming."
4. She asked the crowd to *step aside* so the family could pass.
5. "*Step aside*," the officer repeated, more firmly this time.

**See also:** [make way](#make-way), [move](#move), [coming through](#coming-through), [stand back](#stand-back), [after you](#after-you)

### take a hike

**Pronunciation:** /ˌteɪk ə ˈhaɪk/ &middot; *tayk uh HYK*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *take a walk*

Go away — a dismissive, half-joking brush-off that is nonetheless an insult. It also works for rejecting an offer flat out.

1. "*Take a hike*, we're not buying anything."
2. *Take a hike* — this is a members-only lot.
3. "I told him to *take a hike* and he finally left."
4. "*Take a hike*, pal," the bartender said, pointing at the door.
5. "*Take a hike*," I said, and closed the door on both of them.

**See also:** [get lost](#get-lost), [beat it](#beat-it), [buzz off](#buzz-off), [scram](#scram)

### vamoose

**Pronunciation:** /væˈmus/ &middot; *va-MOOS (also /vəˈmus/, vuh-MOOS)*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *vamos*

Get out of here, from Spanish *vamos*. It is Old West-flavored and almost always used lightly, with a grin behind it.

1. "*Vamoose*, before I change my mind."
2. Let's *vamoose* — this place is dead.
3. "*Vamoose*, you varmints!" he said in his best cowboy voice.
4. By the time I found my coat, everybody else had already *vamoosed*.
5. *Vamoose*, kid. The grown-ups are talking.

**See also:** [skedaddle](#skedaddle), [scram](#scram), [beat it](#beat-it), [hit the road](#hit-the-road)

## Hurrying somebody along

### chop chop

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʃɑp ˈtʃɑp/ &middot; *chop CHOP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *chop-chop*

Hurry up, usually delivered with two sharp claps. It is bossy by design: playful from a parent, condescending from a boss, and best avoided with anyone who outranks you.

1. "*Chop chop*, we're leaving in five minutes!"
2. *Chop chop* — those dishes aren't going to wash themselves.
3. She clapped twice. "*Chop chop*, everybody."
4. "Shoes on, coats on, *chop chop*."
5. *Chop chop*, the reservation is at seven.

**See also:** [hurry up](#hurry-up), [shake a leg](#shake-a-leg), [step on it](#step-on-it), [move](#move), [look alive](#look-alive)

### get a move on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɛt ə ˈmuv ɑn/ &middot; *get uh MOOV on*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *get moving*, *get a move on it*

Start moving and go faster — an everyday prod that assumes the other person is dawdling. It sits between the clap-along *chop chop* and a genuine bark, and it works up and down a family but not up a chain of command.

1. "*Get a move on*, the movie starts at seven."
2. *Get a move on* — that line is only getting longer.
3. He was still eating breakfast at eight fifteen. "*Get a move on*."
4. If we don't *get a move on*, we're going to hit rush hour.
5. "*Get a move on*, Sanchez!" the coach hollered from the sideline.

**See also:** [hurry up](#hurry-up), [chop chop](#chop-chop), [shake a leg](#shake-a-leg), [move](#move)

### hurry up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɜri ˈʌp/ &middot; *hur-ee UP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *hurry*, *hurry it up*, *hurry along*

The plainest member of this group: go faster. It is neutral enough for strangers, children, and coworkers alike, though repeated it turns naggy fast, and *hurry it up* is noticeably sharper than a bare *hurry up*.

1. "*Hurry up*, the bus is already at the corner."
2. *Hurry up* and pick one — there are people behind us.
3. "Can you *hurry up* in there? I need the bathroom too."
4. He kept telling everyone to *hurry up* and then spent twenty minutes finding his keys.
5. "*Hurry it up*, folks. We're closing in ten."

**See also:** [chop chop](#chop-chop), [get a move on](#get-a-move-on), [shake a leg](#shake-a-leg), [step on it](#step-on-it)

### make it snappy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmeɪk ɪt ˈsnæpi/ &middot; *mayk it SNAP-ee*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Be quick about it — a wisecracking order straight out of a 1940s detective picture. Nine times out of ten a modern American says it with a wink, because said flat it sounds like a caricature of a boss.

1. "Two coffees, and *make it snappy*."
2. *Make it snappy* — I've got a meeting in four minutes.
3. "You get one question. *Make it snappy*."
4. He said he needed a favor, so I told him to *make it snappy*.
5. "*Make it snappy*, kid," she said, doing her worst Bogart.

**See also:** [step on it](#step-on-it), [chop chop](#chop-chop), [hurry up](#hurry-up), [on the double](#on-the-double)

### on the double

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑn ðə ˈdʌbəl/ &middot; *on thuh DUB-uhl*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Immediately, at a run — from the military *double time*. Out of uniform it is deliberately jokey-officious, the tone of a parent doing a drill-sergeant voice about the laundry.

1. "Get me that file, *on the double*."
2. *On the double*, gentlemen. We're already behind.
3. "Upstairs, teeth brushed, *on the double*," my mother said.
4. The medic was there *on the double*.
5. "*On the double*!" he barked, and then ruined it by laughing.

**See also:** [chop chop](#chop-chop), [hurry up](#hurry-up), [step on it](#step-on-it), [look alive](#look-alive)

### shake a leg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃeɪk ə ˈlɛɡ/ &middot; *shayk uh LEG*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Hurry up. Folksy and a little old-fashioned — the kind of thing a parent yells up the stairs at somebody who is still not dressed.

1. "*Shake a leg*! The bus is at the corner."
2. *Shake a leg*, we're already running late.
3. "Come on, *shake a leg* — the game starts in ten minutes."
4. She banged on the bathroom door. "*Shake a leg* in there!"
5. *Shake a leg* — Grandma's been sitting out in the car for ten minutes.

**See also:** [chop chop](#chop-chop), [hurry up](#hurry-up), [step on it](#step-on-it), [move](#move), [look alive](#look-alive)

### snap to it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsnæp ˈtu ɪt/ &middot; *snap TOO it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Get on it right now — a brisk, faintly military prod that normally comes from somebody who outranks you and knows it. It is bossier than *hurry up* and lands badly among peers.

1. "Boxes on the truck by noon. *Snap to it*."
2. *Snap to it* — the truck is here and nothing's loaded.
3. "Yes, sir," he said, and snapped to it.
4. She pointed at the sink full of dishes. "*Snap to it*."
5. *Snap to it*, people. The bus leaves whether you're on it or not.

**See also:** [chop chop](#chop-chop), [hurry up](#hurry-up), [look alive](#look-alive), [move](#move)

### step on it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈstɛp ɑn ɪt/ &middot; *STEP on it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *step on the gas*, *put your foot down*

Drive faster — literally, step on the gas pedal. It stretches beyond cars to mean "hurry up," and it always sounds a little like a line from a movie.

1. "*Step on it*! We've got twelve minutes."
2. *Step on it*, the flight boards at four.
3. "Airport, and *step on it*," he told the driver.
4. If we want to catch the last ferry, you'd better *step on it*.
5. "*Step on it*," she said, "before that light changes."

**See also:** [chop chop](#chop-chop), [shake a leg](#shake-a-leg), [hurry up](#hurry-up), [make it snappy](#make-it-snappy), [move](#move)

## Yielding, inviting, and giving permission

### after you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftər ju/ &middot; *AF-ter yoo*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *after you, sir*, *you first*

A polite yielding of the right of way at a door, an elevator, or a buffet line, almost always with a small hand gesture. It is one of the few interjections in this category that makes you sound more courteous, not less.

1. "*After you*," he said, holding the door.
2. *After you* — I'm in no hurry.
3. She stepped back from the elevator. "*After you*."
4. "No, no, *after you*. Please."
5. *After you*, ma'am. I'll catch the next one.

**See also:** [go ahead](#go-ahead), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [pardon me](#pardon-me), [step aside](#step-aside)

### be my guest

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbi maɪ ˈɡɛst/ &middot; *bee my GEST*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

Permission with a shrug: go right ahead, help yourself. Warm hospitality in one tone of voice and a dry "suit yourself, it won't help" in another.

1. "Mind if I take the last slice?" — "*Be my guest*."
2. *Be my guest* — the coffee's fresh.
3. "You want to try explaining it to him? *Be my guest*."
4. If you think you can fix it, *be my guest*.
5. "Can I borrow your desk for an hour?" — "*Be my guest*."

**See also:** [go ahead](#go-ahead), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [knock yourself out](#knock-yourself-out), [have at it](#have-at-it)

### by all means

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˌɔl ˈminz/ &middot; *by awl MEENZ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal

An emphatic, courteous "yes, of course — go ahead." It grants permission with a touch of ceremony, and delivered flatly it curdles into sarcasm.

1. "May I ask a question?" — "*By all means*."
2. *By all means*, take as much time as you need.
3. "*By all means*, sit down. Can I get you anything?"
4. If you'd like to see the contract, *by all means*.
5. "*By all means*," he said, "tell us what you would have done differently."

**See also:** [go ahead](#go-ahead), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [fire away](#fire-away)

### carry on

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkæri ˈɑn/ &middot; *ka-ree ON*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *carry on then*, *as you were*

Go back to what you were doing — the standard release after an interruption, from an officer walking a room or a manager who stuck their head in and saw nothing wrong. Delivered dryly to friends caught mid-nonsense, it is a small joke.

1. "Don't mind me. *Carry on*."
2. *Carry on* — I was only looking for a stapler.
3. The colonel returned the salute. "*Carry on*."
4. She walked in on the two of them arguing about pizza toppings, said "*Carry on*," and walked back out.
5. "*Carry on*, everybody. False alarm."

**See also:** [go ahead](#go-ahead), [by all means](#by-all-means), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [have at it](#have-at-it)

### feel free

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfil ˈfri/ &middot; *feel FREE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *feel free to*

Permission granted in advance: nobody will mind if you do this. It is the office and hospitality standard, warm without gushing, and flattened out it carries a shrug — *feel free, but I wouldn't*.

1. "Can I move this chair?" — "*Feel free*."
2. *Feel free* to grab a coffee while you wait.
3. "*Feel free* to email me if anything else comes up."
4. If you want to try it your way, *feel free*.
5. She left the pen on the counter and said, "*Feel free*."

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [go ahead](#go-ahead), [by all means](#by-all-means)

### fire away

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfaɪər əˈweɪ/ &middot; *fyr uh-WAY*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Go ahead and ask — the standard invitation when somebody says they have questions for you.

1. "Can I ask you something?" — "*Fire away*."
2. *Fire away* — I've got about ten minutes before my next call.
3. "You said you had a list of questions. *Fire away*."
4. He put down his pen. "All right, *fire away*."
5. "*Fire away*, but I may not know the answer."

**See also:** [shoot](#shoot), [go ahead](#go-ahead), [by all means](#by-all-means), [have at it](#have-at-it)

### go ahead

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ əˈhɛd/ &middot; *goh uh-HED*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *go on*, *go right ahead*

Permission to proceed — start talking, start eating, take the parking spot. Said with a hard edge and a stare, the same words become a dare: "go ahead, try it."

1. "*Go ahead*, I'm listening."
2. *Go ahead* and start without me — I'll be twenty minutes late.
3. "Is this seat free?" — "*Go right ahead*."
4. *Go ahead*, take the last one. I've already had three.
5. "*Go ahead*," he said. "Say it one more time and see what happens."

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [by all means](#by-all-means), [after you](#after-you), [shoot](#shoot)

### go for it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊ fər ˈɪt/ &middot; *goh fer IT*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Encouraging permission: do it, take the shot, don't hesitate. It is a green light with enthusiasm attached, which is what separates it from a flat *go ahead*.

1. "Should I apply?" — "*Go for it*!"
2. *Go for it* — the worst they can say is no.
3. "Mind if I try?" — "*Go for it*."
4. She's been talking about that trip for years, so I told her to *go for it*.
5. "*Go for it*, man. You've got nothing to lose."

**See also:** [have at it](#have-at-it), [knock yourself out](#knock-yourself-out), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [go ahead](#go-ahead)

### have at it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæv ˈæt ɪt/ &middot; *hav AT it*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *have at*

Go ahead and dig in, take over, or take your best shot. It usually comes with a handoff — of a plate, a tool, or a problem you are tired of.

1. "There's pizza in the kitchen — *have at it*."
2. *Have at it*. I've been staring at this code for six hours and I'm done.
3. "You think you can do better? *Have at it*."
4. He handed me the wrench. "*Have at it*."
5. The kids saw the presents and we said, "*Have at it*."

**See also:** [go for it](#go-for-it), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [knock yourself out](#knock-yourself-out)

### help yourself

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛlp jərˈsɛlf/ &middot; *help yer-SELF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *help yourselves*

Take what you want without asking first — the host's standard line about food, drinks, and supplies.

1. "*Help yourself*, there's plenty."
2. *Help yourself* to anything in the fridge.
3. "Coffee's over there — *help yourselves*."
4. "Can I grab a pen?" — "*Help yourself*."
5. *Help yourself* to the leftovers; otherwise they're going in the trash.

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [have at it](#have-at-it), [go ahead](#go-ahead)

### knock yourself out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑk jərˈsɛlf ˌaʊt/ &middot; *nok yer-SELF owt*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Go right ahead — usually with a shrug that means "it's your time to waste" or "I'm not going to stop you." It is rarely warm, and it is never something to say to a customer.

1. "Can I reorganize the whole spreadsheet?" — "*Knock yourself out*."
2. *Knock yourself out* — I already tried it and it didn't work.
3. "You want to argue with him about it? *Knock yourself out*."
4. "*Knock yourself out*," he said, sliding the whole box of files across the desk.
5. If you want to stand in that line for three hours, *knock yourself out*.

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [have at it](#have-at-it), [go for it](#go-for-it)

### shoot

**Pronunciation:** /ʃut/ &middot; *SHOOT*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal

In this use, "go ahead and ask" — a casual green light for somebody's question. The identical word is also a mild substitute swear for frustration, which is a completely separate use.

1. "You got a minute? I need your read on something." — "*Shoot*."
2. *Shoot*. What's on your mind?
3. He leaned back in his chair. "All right, *shoot*."
4. "I have two questions for you." — "*Shoot*."
5. *Shoot* — but keep it short, I've got a call in five minutes.

**See also:** [fire away](#fire-away), [go ahead](#go-ahead), [by all means](#by-all-means)

### take five

**Pronunciation:** /ˌteɪk ˈfaɪv/ &middot; *tayk FYV*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *take ten*, *take a breather*

Stop and rest for a few minutes — from the five-minute break called on film sets, in rehearsal rooms, and on job sites. It grants a pause rather than permission to act, which is what separates it from *go ahead*.

1. "All right, everybody, *take five*."
2. *Take five* and we'll run it again from the top.
3. The foreman waved at the crew. "*Take ten*. It's too hot for this."
4. We took five on the tailgate and split a bag of chips.
5. "*Take five*," she said. "You've been staring at that screen since seven."

**See also:** [time out](#time-out), [one moment](#one-moment), [hold on](#hold-on), [go ahead](#go-ahead)
