Agreement, affirmation, and backchanneling#

The words Americans use to say yes, to signal that they agree with what was just said, and to prove out loud that they are still listening.

This is the largest and most everyday family of interjections in American English, and it splits into three jobs that shade into one another. Some of these words answer a question (yes, yep, sure, absolutely); some endorse a statement someone else made (exactly, totally, word, true); and some are backchannels — the small noises and receipts a listener drops into the gaps (uh-huh, mm-hmm, oh, really) that mean nothing more than "I'm here, keep going." The last group is the one learners most often skip, and silence where an American expects a backchannel reads as boredom, disagreement, or a bad phone connection.

Two quirks run through the whole category. First, register spread is enormous: indeed and word mean nearly the same thing and could not be less interchangeable. Second, several of the most common items are not really spellings of words at all. Mm, mm-hmm, and hm are hums made with the lips closed; uh-huh and aha are open-mouthed grunts. None of them have a fixed vowel to spell, their meaning lives almost entirely in the pitch contour, and a flat mm-hmm can mean the opposite of an enthusiastic one. Punctuation is light: these words take a comma when they lead into a sentence (Yeah, I saw that) and a period or exclamation point when they stand alone.

The standard way to endorse someone else's point rather than answer their question.   4. "She wasn't angry, she was embarrassed." "Exactly. That's what I've been trying to say."
  5. "Exactly!" three people said at once, and the meeting finally moved on.

Backchannels and continuers#

These are the noises a listener makes to prove they are still there. Most of them carry no content at all — their entire meaning is in the pitch. Flat means "go on," rising means "really?", and falling means "hm, I'm not so sure." American speakers expect one every few seconds on the phone, and the silence of a listener who makes none reads as anger or a dropped call.

  2. "Oh, I didn't realize you two knew each other."
  3. "She's not coming — she's got the flu." "Oh, that's too bad."
  4. "Ohhh, you meant the other Main Street."
  5. "Oh," he said quietly, and you could hear him rearranging his whole afternoon.

The workhorse backchannel of surprise and interest. 1. Rising, it asks the speaker to confirm and keep going. 2. Falling and flat, it is skeptical or unimpressed, and oh, really with a drawl is nearly always sarcastic.

Русский

Type

Variants
uh huh, unh-huh, uh-huh?

The open-mouthed cousin of mm-hmm: an affirmative grunt, made with the jaw loose and the second syllable stressed and higher, that answers yes-or-no questions and keeps a conversation moving. It is not a synonym for aha, which announces a discovery rather than acknowledging one. A rapid, flat string of uh-huhs often signals impatience rather than attention.

  1. "Did you remember to lock up?" "Uh-huh."
  2. "So you take 95 north—" "Uh-huh." "—for about ten miles."
  3. "Uh-huh, uh-huh, and then what?"
  4. "Are you even listening?" "Uh-huh." "Then what did I just say?"
  5. "You'll be there by seven?" "Uh-huh, maybe earlier."

Usually filed under surprise, but as a backchannel it does a specific job: it tells the speaker their news deserves a reaction. It is the listener's way of scoring a story as impressive, shocking, or bad — and a flat, delayed wow is a well-known way of expressing disapproval.

  1. "They drove the whole thing in one day — nineteen hours." "Wow."
  2. "Wow, that's a lot of money for a used car."
  3. "And then he just walked out in the middle of the meeting." "Oh, wow."
  4. "Wow." She set down the letter. "I don't even know what to say."
  5. "I told her the truth and she thanked me for it." "Wow, that's rare."
