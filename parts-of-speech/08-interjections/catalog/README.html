2Surprise, astonishment, and disbeliefThe words Americans reach for in the first half-second after something unexpected lands — from a quiet huh to a full oh my God.88
3Pain, effort, and physical sensationThe interjections Americans use when the body speaks first — when something hurts, when something is heavy, when something is too hot, too cold, too delicious, or about to come out of the nose.49
4Disgust, contempt, and disapprovalThe noises and set phrases Americans use to push something away — a smell, a claim, a person's behavior — as revolting, worthless, or not worth a serious reply.68
5Fear, alarm, and warningThe words Americans use when something is wrong — the cries that escape you when you are frightened, and the shouts that make other people move.61
6Sadness, sympathy, disappointment, and resignationThe interjections Americans reach for when something goes wrong — to feel bad about it, to feel bad for someone else, or to shrug and move on.63
7Anger, frustration, and minced oathsThe noises, curses, and polite substitutes Americans reach for when patience runs out.97
8Profanity used as interjectionsTaboo words thrown out as pure reaction — pain, shock, anger, disbelief, delight — plus the softened substitutes Americans reach for when the real word would cost them something.77
9Greetings, farewells, and social formulasThe interjections that open and close a conversation — the words you say not because they carry information, but because saying nothing would be rude.91
10Attention, silencing, and directivesThe interjections that act on the listener: they point a head in your direction, shut a room up, stop something mid-motion, clear a path, or hand somebody the floor.123
11Agreement, affirmation, and backchannelingThe words Americans use to say yes, to signal that they agree with what was just said, and to prove out loud that they are still listening.91
12Disagreement, refusal, and skepticismThe words Americans reach for to say no, to shut a request down, and to signal that they do not believe a word of what they just heard.88
13Hesitation, fillers, and discourse markersThe stalling sounds, hedges, and traffic signals of American conversation — what speakers say while they think, and how they steer a talk from one thing to the next.83
14Realization, understanding, and requests for repetitionThe noises Americans make when a fact finally lands — and the ones they make when it doesn't.59
15Encouragement, praise, and cheeringThe words you shout at somebody who is trying — before they start, while they are struggling, and after they have pulled it off.66
16Onomatopoeia and sound effects used as interjectionsThe words that are the noise — bang, splash, meow — dropped into a sentence to play a sound back instead of describing it.84
17Ritual formulas, toasts, blessings, and politenessThe fixed phrases a situation demands — the words you say because the moment calls for words, not because you have anything new to report.102
18Internet and text-native interjectionsThe reactions that were born on a screen — acronyms promoted to words, spelled-out noises, and one-word verdicts that Americans type first and only later learn to say out loud.86
19Regional, dated, and folksy AmericanismsThe exclamations that come with a place, a decade, or a grandparent attached — words that tell you where a speaker is from or which era they are borrowing from.79
20Animal-directed, child-directed, and work callsThe words Americans shout at creatures and crews — animals, babies, and whole rooms full of people who need to move at the same instant.80
