# Ritual formulas, toasts, blessings, and politeness

> The fixed phrases a situation demands — the words you say because the moment calls for words, not because you have anything new to report.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These are scripts, not sentences. A sneeze requires *bless you*, a raised glass requires *cheers*, a favor requires *thanks* and then requires an answer to the thanks, and saying nothing at those moments is itself a message. Because they are frozen wholes, they resist grammar: *many happy returns* has no verb, *bon appétit* and *mazel tov* are not English at all, and *good luck with that* means almost the opposite of its parts. Two things to watch throughout — register, which is where the real danger is (*my bad* and *my apologies* cover the same mistake and belong in completely different rooms), and tone, since a startling number of these formulas flip to sarcasm on delivery alone: *thanks a lot*, *congratulations*, *be my guest*, and *bless your heart* are all sincere or cutting depending entirely on how you say them.

## Thanks and gratitude

### appreciate it

**Pronunciation:** /əˈpriʃiˌeɪt ɪt/ &middot; *uh-PREE-shee-ayt-it*  
**Русский:** спасибо, ценю — разг., тёплое сдержанное спасибо за услугу, а не за подарок
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *I appreciate it*, *'preciate it*, *much appreciated*

A warm, understated thank-you, said for a favor rather than for a gift. Dropping the *I* makes it sound casual and a little gruff, which is exactly why so many Americans reach for it instead of *thank you*.

1. "Here's your coffee." "Hey, *appreciate it*."
2. Thanks for covering my shift — really *appreciate it*.
3. *Appreciate it*, man. I'll get you back next week.
4. He held the door and she nodded. "*'Preciate it*."
5. If you could send that over by Friday, *much appreciated*.

**See also:** [thanks](#thanks), [thank you](#thank-you), [I owe you one](#i-owe-you-one)

### I owe you one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪ ˌoʊ ju ˈwʌn/ &middot; *eye-oh-yoo-WUN*  
**Русский:** с меня причитается / буду должен — разг., спасибо с обещанием ответной услуги
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *I owe you*, *I owe you big time*, *I owe you big*

Thanks that promises a favor back. It is said when someone has gone genuinely out of their way, and it implies a friendly running tally rather than an actual debt.

1. Thanks for picking me up at the airport — *I owe you one*.
2. "*I owe you one*," he said, sliding the signed form back across the desk.
3. You really didn't have to stay late for this. *I owe you big time*.
4. *I owe you one* — next round's on me.
5. If you can get us that reservation, *I owe you*.

**See also:** [appreciate it](#appreciate-it), [thanks a million](#thanks-a-million), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### many thanks

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛni ˈθæŋks/ &middot; *men-ee-THANKS*  
**Русский:** большое спасибо / благодарю — книжн., письменное: концовка письма или записки
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *many thanks in advance*, *many thanks again*

A tidy written thank-you, at home at the end of an email, a note, or an acknowledgments page. Spoken aloud by an American it sounds a little bookish or a little British; on paper it is completely ordinary.

1. *Many thanks* for turning this around so quickly.
2. He signed for the package. "*Many thanks*."
3. *Many thanks* to the volunteers who set up the chairs this morning.
4. She ended every message the same way: "*Many thanks*, Ellen."
5. *Many thanks* in advance for your help with these forms.

**See also:** [thanks](#thanks), [thank you](#thank-you), [appreciate it](#appreciate-it)

### much obliged

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmʌtʃ əˈblaɪdʒd/ &middot; *much uh-BLYJD*  
**Русский:** премного благодарен — устар., старомодно-учтивое, с ковбойским налётом
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *much obliged to you*, *obliged*

An old-fashioned thank-you with a courtly, Western flavor. Americans still say it, but mostly older men, Southerners, and anyone deliberately putting on a cowboy voice.

1. "*Much obliged*, ma'am," he said, tipping his hat.
2. *Much obliged* for the ride into town.
3. My grandfather never said thanks — it was always "*much obliged*."
4. She handed him the wrench and he grunted, "*Obliged*."
5. *Much obliged*, folks. That'll get us through the winter.

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [thanks](#thanks), [appreciate it](#appreciate-it)

### thank you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈθæŋk ju/ &middot; *THANK-yoo*  
**Русский:** спасибо / благодарю вас — полная нейтральная форма, уместна везде; сухо — «вот именно»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *thank you very much*, *thank you so much*, *thank you kindly*, *thank ya*

The full, standard expression of gratitude — correct absolutely everywhere, from a cashier to a eulogy, and more sincere-sounding than *thanks* simply because it takes longer to say. Said flatly with a falling tone it has a second life meaning *that is exactly my point*.

1. *Thank you* — this is the nicest thing anyone's done for me all year.
2. "*Thank you so much* for coming," she said at the door.
3. *Thank you*, Your Honor.
4. And that, *thank you very much*, is why I don't lend him my car.
5. "Your fries." "*Thank you*."
6. See? I was right all along. *Thank you*.

**See also:** [thanks](#thanks), [appreciate it](#appreciate-it), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

### thank you for your service

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθæŋk ju fər jər ˈsɜrvɪs/ &middot; *thank-yoo-fer-yer-SUR-vis*  
**Русский:** спасибо за вашу службу — формула благодарности ветерану или военному
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *thank you for your service to our country*, *thanks for your service*

The formula an American says to someone identified as a veteran or an active-duty service member — at an airport gate, at a register offering a military discount, at a Veterans Day assembly. It is barely thirty years old and now close to automatic, and it is worth knowing that a substantial number of veterans find it hollow, embarrassing, or a conversation-ender, and would rather be asked an actual question.

1. The gate agent saw the cap. "*Thank you for your service*."
2. "*Thank you for your service*, sir. The coffee's on us."
3. Somebody says *thank you for your service* to my uncle about once a week and he still never knows what to say back.
4. The principal shook each of them by the hand: "*Thank you for your service*."
5. "*Thank you for your service*." "I fixed radios in Germany, but thanks."

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [appreciate it](#appreciate-it), [God bless](#god-bless)

### thanks

**Pronunciation:** /θæŋks/ &middot; *THANKS*  
**Русский:** спасибо — короткое повседневное, на любой мелкий повод; сухо брошенное звучит небрежно
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *thanx*, *thx*, *thnx*, *tks*

The short everyday thank-you — one syllable, low effort, right for anything from a held door to a delivered package. Alone at the bottom of a work email it is perfectly normal; said clipped and flat to someone's face it can read as dismissive.

1. *Thanks!* I'll take it from here.
2. "Two sugars?" "Just one, *thanks*."
3. *Thanks* for the heads-up about the road closure.
4. She typed "*thx*" and hit send.
5. No *thanks* — I'm driving tonight.
6. Yeah. *Thanks* for nothing.

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [thanks a lot](#thanks-a-lot), [cheers](#cheers)

### thanks a lot

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθæŋks ə ˈlɑt/ &middot; *thanks-uh-LOT*  
**Русский:** большое спасибо — тон решает всё: искренне или язвительное «ну спасибо тебе»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

Literally an intensified *thanks*, but Americans use it sarcastically about as often as sincerely, and tone does all the work. Bright and quick it is real gratitude; flat, with the weight on *lot*, it means *you have just made my life worse*.

1. *Thanks a lot* for staying late to help us finish.
2. "*Thanks a lot*, you guys. I couldn't have done it without you."
3. You told her it was my idea? *Thanks a lot*.
4. Well, *thanks a lot* — now I have to start the whole thing over.
5. He read the review, muttered "*thanks a lot*," and shut his laptop.

**See also:** [thanks](#thanks), [thanks a million](#thanks-a-million), [thank you](#thank-you)

### thanks a million

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθæŋks ə ˈmɪljən/ &middot; *thanks-uh-MIL-yun*  
**Русский:** огромное спасибо / спасибо преогромное — разг., почти всегда искренне
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *thanks a bunch*, *thanks a ton*, *thanks a heap*

A friendly, exaggerated thank-you for a real favor. *Thanks a million* is nearly always sincere, while *thanks a bunch* tips into sarcasm about as easily as *thanks a lot* does.

1. *Thanks a million*, Rosa — you saved the whole presentation.
2. "*Thanks a ton* for the recommendation. I got the job."
3. Got the tickets. *Thanks a million!*
4. *Thanks a bunch* for warning me the floor was wet.
5. She waved from the car — "*Thanks a million!*" — and pulled out of the driveway.

**See also:** [thanks a lot](#thanks-a-lot), [thanks](#thanks), [appreciate it](#appreciate-it)

### you shouldn't have

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈʃʊdənt ˌhæv/ &middot; *yoo-SHOOD-unt-hav*  
**Русский:** ну зачем же вы / не стоило — ритуальный протест при вручении подарка
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *oh, you shouldn't have*, *you really shouldn't have*

The scripted thing to say while unwrapping a gift — a modest protest that functions as thanks. Nobody says it literally, and nobody is expected to take the gift back.

1. "*You shouldn't have!*" she said, already tearing the paper off.
2. Flowers? *You really shouldn't have*.
3. He handed her the little box. "*Oh, you shouldn't have*."
4. *You shouldn't have* — but I'm very glad you did.
5. They gave him a retirement plaque with his name misspelled. *You shouldn't have*.

**See also:** [you're too kind](#youre-too-kind), [thank you](#thank-you), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### you're too kind

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr ˌtu ˈkaɪnd/ &middot; *yer-too-KYND*  
**Русский:** вы слишком добры / вы мне льстите — учтивое отклонение комплимента
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *too kind*, *you are too kind*, *far too kind*

A gracious deflection of a compliment or a generous gesture, meaning roughly *that is more than I deserve*. It is sincere in courtly mouths and dryly amused when the praise is obviously over the top.

1. "That was the best talk of the whole conference." "*You're too kind*."
2. *You're too kind* — I really just showed up and read the slides.
3. He gave a small bow. "*Too kind*, ladies and gentlemen."
4. She sent lunch for the entire office. *You're too kind*.
5. "*You're too kind*," he said, pocketing the check anyway.

**See also:** [you shouldn't have](#you-shouldnt-have), [thank you](#thank-you), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

## Answering thanks

### anytime

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌtaɪm/ &middot; *EN-ee-time*  
**Русский:** всегда пожалуйста / обращайся в любое время — разг., теплее, чем no problem
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *any time*

A relaxed reply to thanks that means *ask me again whenever you want*. It is warmer than *no problem* and works with friends, neighbors, and coworkers, but it sounds too familiar coming from someone serving a stranger.

1. "Thanks for the jump start." "*Anytime*."
2. *Anytime*, man. Just text me.
3. "You're a lifesaver." "*Anytime* — seriously."
4. "I hate that I keep asking you for this." "*Anytime*. It's five minutes of my day."
5. *Anytime* you need another set of eyes on it, say the word.

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [sure thing](#sure-thing), [you bet](#you-bet)

### at your service

**Pronunciation:** /ət jər ˈsɜrvɪs/ &middot; *at-yer-SUR-vis*  
**Русский:** к вашим услугам — книжн., учтивое предложение помощи и ответ на спасибо
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *yours truly, at your service*, *always at your service*

An offer of help delivered with a flourish, and a courtly way of answering thanks. It is straight-faced in hotels and fine restaurants; everywhere else it is friendly theater, usually tacked onto your own name.

1. "Are you the one who fixed the projector?" "*At your service*."
2. "Dave Ramirez, *at your service*."
3. The concierge nodded. "*At your service*, ma'am."
4. Need a ride to the airport at five in the morning? *At your service*.
5. He swept off an imaginary hat. "*At your service*."

**See also:** [happy to help](#happy-to-help), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure), [be my guest](#be-my-guest)

### don't mention it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdoʊnt ˈmɛnʃən ɪt/ &middot; *dohnt-MEN-shun-it*  
**Русский:** не стоит благодарности / пустяки — чуть старомодный учтивый ответ на спасибо
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

A modest answer to thanks, meaning the favor was too small to be worth thanking anyone for. It is slightly old-fashioned and faintly gallant, and it can carry a joking undertone of *let us never speak of this again*.

1. "Thank you for dinner." "*Don't mention it*."
2. *Don't mention it* — you'd have done the same for me.
3. He waved her off. "*Don't mention it*. Really."
4. "I owe you." "*Don't mention it*. Ever."
5. *Don't mention it*; it took me all of five minutes.

**See also:** [think nothing of it](#think-nothing-of-it), [no problem](#no-problem), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### don't worry about it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdoʊnt ˈwɜri əˌbaʊt ɪt/ &middot; *dohnt-WUR-ee-uh-bowt-it*  
**Русский:** да брось / не переживай, ерунда — разг., ответ и на спасибо, и на извинение
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *don't worry*, *don't even worry about it*, *don't worry about it, seriously*

A wave of the hand that answers a thank-you, an apology, or an offer to pay you back. It is generous when warm and conversation-ending when flat, and it is one of the most common things Americans say instead of *you're welcome*.

1. "I'm so sorry I'm late." "*Don't worry about it*, we just sat down."
2. *Don't worry about it* — I'll cover the check this time.
3. "I still owe you twenty bucks." "*Don't even worry about it*."
4. He broke a glass and the bartender said, "*Don't worry about it*, happens every night."
5. "Should I call and explain?" "*Don't worry about it*. It's handled."

**See also:** [no worries](#no-worries), [no problem](#no-problem), [think nothing of it](#think-nothing-of-it)

### happy to help

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi tə ˈhɛlp/ &middot; *hap-ee-tuh-HELP*  
**Русский:** рад помочь / всегда рад помочь — вежливое рабочее «пожалуйста»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *glad to help*, *happy to*, *always happy to help*

The workplace and service-industry standard reply to thanks — friendly, professional, and safe with customers, where *no problem* can sound too loose. It also works before the fact, as an offer.

1. "Thanks for walking me through it." "*Happy to help!*"
2. *Glad to help* — call back if it starts doing that again.
3. Let me know if you get stuck; *happy to help*.
4. "*Happy to*," she said, already reaching for the box.
5. No trouble at all — *happy to help*.

**See also:** [my pleasure](#my-pleasure), [no problem](#no-problem), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

### my pleasure

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈplɛʒər/ &middot; *my-PLEZH-er*  
**Русский:** мне только в радость / всегда рад — книжн., учтивый ответ на спасибо
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *the pleasure is mine*, *pleasure's all mine*, *it was my pleasure*

A gracious reply to thanks, standard in hospitality and upscale service and common in polite professional email. Between close friends it lands as either very courtly or lightly sarcastic.

1. "Thank you for a wonderful evening." "*My pleasure*."
2. "*My pleasure*, sir. Enjoy your stay with us."
3. Thanks again for the introduction. — *My pleasure*; I think you two will get along.
4. "It was *my pleasure*," she said, and clearly meant it.
5. "Thanks for the ride." "*Pleasure's all mine*."

**See also:** [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [happy to help](#happy-to-help), [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it)

### no problem

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈprɑbləm/ &middot; *noh-PRAH-blum*  
**Русский:** без проблем / не за что — разг., самый ходовой ответ на спасибо у молодых
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no prob*, *no probs*, *np*

The most common casual American reply to thanks, and also a way to wave off an apology or agree to a request. Mind the generation gap: to anyone under about fifty it simply means *you're welcome*, but some older speakers hear it as implying the favor might have been a problem.

1. "Thanks for grabbing that." "*No problem*."
2. Sorry I'm late. — *No problem*, we just started.
3. Can you get it to me by five? — *No problem*.
4. He shrugged. "*No problem*, happens all the time."
5. She texted back a single "*np*."

**See also:** [no worries](#no-worries), [no sweat](#no-sweat), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [sure thing](#sure-thing)

### no sweat

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈswɛt/ &middot; *noh-SWET*  
**Русский:** да без проблем / раз плюнуть — разг., чуть устар. (1970–80-е), с бравадой
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no sweat at all*

A breezy *no problem* that insists the favor cost you no effort whatsoever. It peaked in the 1970s and 80s and now sounds a shade older and a shade more swaggering than *no problem*, and it answers a request as readily as it answers thanks.

1. "Thanks for hauling all that up three flights." "*No sweat*."
2. Can you swing by and grab the keys? — *No sweat*.
3. "Sorry, I know it's short notice." "*No sweat*. I wasn't doing anything anyway."
4. He wiped his hands on his jeans. "*No sweat*. Call me if it leaks again."
5. *No sweat* — I'll have the whole thing rewired by Friday.

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [don't worry about it](#dont-worry-about-it), [sure thing](#sure-thing)

### no worries

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈwɜriz/ &middot; *noh-WUR-eez*  
**Русский:** не парься / всё нормально — разг., из австралийского; и на спасибо, и на извинение
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no worries at all*, *no worries, mate* (Australian flavor)

Australian in origin and now thoroughly at home in American English, especially with younger speakers. It answers both *thank you* and *sorry*, and it works on its own as reassurance that nothing is wrong.

1. "Sorry, I didn't see you there." "*No worries*."
2. *No worries* — I'll just catch the next train.
3. "Thanks for waiting." "*No worries at all*."
4. If you can't make it tonight, *no worries*.
5. He replied "*no worries*" and put his phone face down.

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it), [sorry](#sorry)

### not at all

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ət ˈɔl/ &middot; *naht-uht-AWL*  
**Русский:** не за что / ну что вы — книжн., чуть чопорно, у американцев звучит по-британски
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal

A polite, slightly formal reply to thanks or to an apology, meaning the trouble was nonexistent. In American mouths it sounds faintly British and rather courtly; younger Americans say *no problem* instead almost every time.

1. "Thank you so much for making room for us." "*Not at all*."
2. Am I keeping you? — *Not at all*, please sit down.
3. "*Not at all*," the professor said. "I'm glad you asked."
4. I hope this isn't an imposition. — *Not at all*.
5. "Sorry to bother you." "*Not at all* — what can I do for you?"

**See also:** [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure), [think nothing of it](#think-nothing-of-it)

### of course

**Pronunciation:** /əv ˈkɔrs/ &middot; *uv-KORSS*  
**Русский:** ну конечно / о чём речь — как ответ на спасибо: «само собой, о чём тут говорить»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *but of course*, *of course, of course*

As an answer to thanks it means *there was never any question I would do it*, and it has quietly become the default for younger Americans who find *you're welcome* stiff and *no problem* careless. It is warm, it is safe with customers and strangers, and it does ordinary duty as an emphatic *yes* besides.

1. "Thanks for holding my spot in line." "*Of course*."
2. "*Of course*," the nurse said. "That's what we're here for."
3. Could you send me that file again? — *Of course*, one second.
4. "It meant a lot that you came." "*Of course* I came."
5. She says *of course* where her mother would have said *you're welcome*.

**See also:** [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [happy to help](#happy-to-help), [anytime](#anytime)

### sure thing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃʊr ˈθɪŋ/ &middot; *shoor-THING*  
**Русский:** конечно / не вопрос — разг., и «пожалуйста», и согласие на просьбу
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *sure*, *sure enough thing* (rare)

A breezy formula that answers either a thank-you or a request: after *thanks* it means *you're welcome*, and after a favor asked it means *yes, I'll do it*. It is friendly and very American, and it belongs in speech rather than writing.

1. "Thanks for locking up." "*Sure thing*."
2. Could you grab the mail while we're gone? — *Sure thing*.
3. "*Sure thing*, boss. Give me ten minutes."
4. "Two coffees?" "*Sure thing*, coming right up."
5. He said *sure thing* and hung up before I could explain the rest.

**See also:** [you bet](#you-bet), [no problem](#no-problem), [anytime](#anytime)

### think nothing of it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθɪŋk ˈnʌθɪŋ əv ɪt/ &middot; *think-NUTH-ing-uv-it*  
**Русский:** полно, не стоит благодарности — устар., подчёркнуто галантный ответ
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated

A courtly dismissal of thanks, meaning the favor cost you nothing at all. It is genuinely old-fashioned now — you hear it from older speakers, in period pieces, and from people being grandly and deliberately gracious.

1. "I can't thank you enough." "*Think nothing of it*."
2. *Think nothing of it*, my dear — it was on my way.
3. He gave a small bow. "*Think nothing of it*."
4. "You paid for all of us?" "*Think nothing of it*."
5. *Think nothing of it*. I had the whole afternoon free anyway.

**See also:** [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it), [not at all](#not-at-all), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### you bet

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈbɛt/ &middot; *yoo-BET*  
**Русский:** а как же / само собой — разг., среднезападное: и «пожалуйста», и решительное «да»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *you betcha*, *yeah, you bet*

A hearty Midwestern and Western reply that answers *thank you* with *of course* and answers a question with an emphatic *yes*. *You betcha* is the folksier form, and outside the Upper Midwest it can sound like a put-on.

1. "Thanks for the help with the fence." "*You bet*."
2. Are you coming to the game? — *You bet* I am.
3. "*You betcha*," she said. "Wouldn't miss it for anything."
4. Can you hold the elevator? — *You bet*.
5. "Appreciate it." "*You bet*. Anytime."

**See also:** [sure thing](#sure-thing), [you got it](#you-got-it), [no problem](#no-problem), [anytime](#anytime)

### you got it

**Pronunciation:** /ju ˈɡɑt ɪt/ &middot; *yoo-GOT-it*  
**Русский:** будет сделано / понял, сделаю — разг., и согласие на просьбу, и ответ на спасибо
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ya got it*, *you got it, boss*, *you got it, chief*

A quick, cheerful *consider it done* that doubles as an answer to thanks. It is diner-counter and hardware-store American — the server taking your order and the coworker you just asked for a favor both reach for it — and it lives in speech, essentially never in writing.

1. "Can I get a refill over here?" "*You got it*."
2. "Thanks for covering the phones." "*You got it*."
3. *You got it*, boss. Eight two-by-fours, cut to six feet.
4. "Don't mention I told you." "*You got it*."
5. He gave a thumbs-up through the kitchen window. "*You got it!*"

**See also:** [sure thing](#sure-thing), [you bet](#you-bet), [no problem](#no-problem)

### you're welcome

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr ˈwɛlkəm/, also /jɔr ˈwɛlkəm/ &middot; *yer-WEL-kum, also yor-WEL-kum*  
**Русский:** пожалуйста / не за что — стандартный ответ на спасибо, уместен всюду
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *you're very welcome*, *you're quite welcome*, *ur welcome*

The standard, unmarked answer to *thank you* — never wrong anywhere, and the safest thing a learner can memorize. Said pointedly to someone who did not thank you, it becomes a sharp little reproach. Do not confuse it with plain *welcome*, which greets an arrival.

1. "Thanks for the ride." "*You're welcome*."
2. *You're very welcome* — I'm glad it worked out.
3. She said *you're welcome* to every single person coming through the line.
4. I fixed your printer, by the way. *You're welcome*.
5. "You saved me an entire afternoon." "*You're welcome*. It was nothing."

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [no problem](#no-problem), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure), [welcome](09-greetings.md#welcome)

## Please, pardon, and apology

### after you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæftər ju/ &middot; *AF-ter-yoo*  
**Русский:** после вас / проходите первым — уступая дорогу, очередь или лифт
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *after you, please*

Said while yielding a doorway, an elevator, a buffet line, or a turn — an offer to let the other person go first. It is one of the few politeness formulas that essentially always comes with a gesture.

1. He held the door and stepped aside. "*After you*."
2. "*After you*," she said, waving him toward the elevator.
3. Please, *after you* — I insist.
4. "Who's up next?" "*After you*. I'm still stretching."
5. *After you*, ladies.

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [excuse me](#excuse-me)

### be my guest

**Pronunciation:** /bi maɪ ˈɡɛst/ &middot; *bee-my-GEST*  
**Русский:** сделай одолжение / пожалуйста — разрешение; часто с иронией «ну попробуй»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Permission granted with a shrug: *go ahead, it costs me nothing*. It can be genuinely hospitable, but very often it carries a dry undertone of *if you think you can do better, try it*.

1. "Mind if I take the last slice?" "*Be my guest*."
2. You want to tell him yourself? *Be my guest*.
3. "Can I use your charger?" "*Be my guest* — it's on the counter."
4. "I'm going to look that up right now." "*Be my guest*."
5. *Be my guest*, but I'd wear boots if I were you.

**See also:** [help yourself](#help-yourself), [by all means](#by-all-means), [after you](#after-you)

### beg your pardon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɛɡ jər ˈpɑrdən/ &middot; *beg-yer-PAR-dun*  
**Русский:** прошу прощения — книжн.: извинение или переспрос; возмущённо — «да как вы смеете»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *I beg your pardon*, *begging your pardon*

A formal apology, a formal request for a repeat, or — with a rising, indignant tone — an objection meaning *how dare you*. That third sense is the one you will actually hear most often in American English.

1. *I beg your pardon* — I didn't mean to interrupt.
2. "*Beg your pardon?* I didn't catch that last part."
3. *I beg your pardon!* That is absolutely not what I said.
4. "*Begging your pardon*, sir, but the car's out front."
5. He stepped on her foot and mumbled, "*Beg your pardon*."

**See also:** [pardon](#pardon), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [my apologies](#my-apologies)

### by all means

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ ˌɔl ˈminz/ &middot; *by-awl-MEENZ*  
**Русский:** разумеется / сделайте одолжение — книжн., охотно данное разрешение
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal

Enthusiastic permission — *yes, absolutely, go ahead*. It is more gracious than *sure*, and it turns up in meetings, in email, and wherever a host wants to sound generous rather than merely willing.

1. "May I ask a question?" "*By all means*."
2. *By all means*, take as much time as you need.
3. "Should I bring my brother?" "*By all means* — the more the merrier."
4. If you'd like to see the garden, *by all means*, go on out.
5. "*By all means*, sit," she said, pulling out a chair.

**See also:** [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [please](#please), [help yourself](#help-yourself)

### excuse me

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskjuz mi/ &middot; *ik-SKYOOZ-mee*  
**Русский:** извините / простите — привлечь внимание, извиниться за помеху, переспросить
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *'scuse me*, *excuse me?*, *excuse us*

The workhorse of American politeness: it gets a stranger's attention, apologizes for a small physical intrusion, asks someone to repeat themselves, covers a burp or a sneeze, and — with a sharp rising tone — challenges something rude.

1. *Excuse me*, do you know what time the museum closes?
2. *Excuse me* — I just need to squeeze past you.
3. "*Excuse me?* Could you say that one more time?"
4. *Excuse me!* That's the second time you've cut in line.
5. He burped, then said "*Excuse me*" to nobody in particular.
6. *Excuse us*, we're trying to get through with a stroller.

**See also:** [pardon](#pardon), [beg your pardon](#beg-your-pardon), [sorry](#sorry)

### excuse my French

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˌskjuz maɪ ˈfrɛntʃ/ &middot; *ik-skyooz-my-FRENCH*  
**Русский:** простите за выражение — шутливое извинение за мат, до или сразу после него
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *pardon my French*, *excuse the French*, *'scuse my French*

A joking apology for swearing, dropped in just before or just after the word itself. The joke is very old and everybody knows it, and it does not actually make the profanity acceptable in a room where profanity is not.

1. That was, *excuse my French*, a complete disaster.
2. "*Pardon my French*, but the man is an idiot."
3. He glanced at his mother. "*Excuse my French*, Ma."
4. The whole thing is screwed — *excuse my French* — from top to bottom.
5. "*Excuse my French*." "You said that after, not before."

**See also:** [pardon](#pardon), [no offense](#no-offense), [damn](08-profanity.md#damn)

### my apologies

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ əˈpɑlədʒiz/ &middot; *my-uh-PAH-luh-jeez*  
**Русский:** приношу свои извинения — книжн., деловое извинение, суше и холоднее I'm sorry
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *apologies*, *my sincere apologies*, *apologies for the delay*

A crisp, businesslike apology, most at home in email and professional speech. It sits a notch cooler than *I'm sorry*, which makes it useful when you want to own a mistake without sounding wounded about it.

1. *My apologies* for the delayed reply — I was out of the office all week.
2. *Apologies*, everyone; the projector is being difficult again.
3. "*My apologies*," the manager said. "That should never have happened."
4. *My sincere apologies* to anyone who was kept waiting.
5. I sent the wrong version of the file. *My apologies*.

**See also:** [sorry](#sorry), [beg your pardon](#beg-your-pardon), [my bad](#my-bad)

### my bad

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈbæd/ &middot; *my-BAD*  
**Русский:** мой косяк / виноват — очень разг., лёгкое признание оплошности; для серьёзного не годится
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

A casual admission of fault that came out of pickup basketball in the 1980s and spread everywhere. It is friendly and low-stakes; using it for anything genuinely serious makes it sound like you are not taking it seriously.

1. "You took my charger." "Oh — *my bad*."
2. *My bad*, I thought the meeting was at three.
3. He knocked over the cup and said "*my bad*" without looking up.
4. *My bad* on the typo in that last email.
5. "Wrong turn again?" "*My bad*."

**See also:** [sorry](#sorry), [my apologies](#my-apologies), [excuse me](#excuse-me)

### no offense

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ əˈfɛns/, also /ˌnoʊ ˈɔfɛns/ &middot; *noh-uh-FENSS, also noh-AW-fenss*  
**Русский:** без обид — разг., оговорка при грубости; ответная реплика — none taken
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no offense meant*, *no offense to anyone*, *none taken* (the standard reply)

A disclaimer bolted onto something rude, either just before or just after saying it. Everyone knows it does not actually neutralize the insult, and the expected answer is *none taken*.

1. *No offense*, but that shirt is a lot.
2. "Your handwriting is completely unreadable. *No offense*."
3. *No offense* to anyone in this room, but this meeting could have been an email.
4. "*No offense*." "*None taken*."
5. She said *no offense* three times in one sentence, which is never a good sign.

**See also:** [sorry](#sorry), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [none taken](#none-taken), [my apologies](#my-apologies)

### none taken

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnʌn ˈteɪkən/ &middot; *nun-TAY-kun*  
**Русский:** да я и не обиделся — разг., ритуальный ответ на no offense, часто из вежливости
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *none taken at all*, *and none taken*

The scripted answer to *no offense*, granting that you are not insulted. It is often a polite fiction, and said flatly or after a pause it signals the exact opposite.

1. "No offense." "*None taken*."
2. *None taken* — you're not wrong about the shirt.
3. "No offense to anyone in accounting." "*None taken*," said the accountant.
4. He shrugged. "*None taken*. I've heard a lot worse."
5. "I hope that didn't come out badly." "*None taken*, honestly."

**See also:** [no offense](#no-offense), [no worries](#no-worries), [don't worry about it](#dont-worry-about-it)

### pardon

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑrdən/ &middot; *PAR-dun*  
**Русский:** простите / виноват — книжн.; с вопросительной интонацией — «что-что?»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *pardon me*, *pardon?*, *pardon me for asking*

A short, slightly formal *excuse me*. With rising intonation it asks for a repeat; with falling intonation it apologizes for brushing past someone or for some small breach of manners.

1. "*Pardon?* I didn't hear you."
2. *Pardon me* — is this seat taken?
3. He squeezed down the row whispering "*Pardon*, *pardon*."
4. *Pardon me for asking*, but how much did a place like this run you?
5. "*Pardon me*, ma'am, you dropped your glove."
6. *Pardon?* He did what?

**See also:** [excuse me](#excuse-me), [beg your pardon](#beg-your-pardon), [excuse my French](#excuse-my-french), [sorry](#sorry)

### please

**Pronunciation:** /pliz/ &middot; *PLEEZ*  
**Русский:** пожалуйста — превращает приказ в просьбу; отдельно, с падением тона — «да брось, перестань»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *pretty please*, *please please please*, *if you please* (dated), *plz*, *pls*

The word that turns an order into a request; leaving it out is the fastest way for a learner to sound rude in English. Said alone with a falling tone it has a second sense entirely — *stop it*, or *I don't believe you for a second*.

1. Two coffees, *please*.
2. Could you *please* turn that down?
3. "*Please*," she said. "Just hear me out."
4. *Please*. He has never once been on time in his life.
5. *Pretty please*? With a cherry on top?
6. "Would you like a bag?" "Yes, *please*."

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [by all means](#by-all-means), [excuse me](#excuse-me)

### sorry

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɑri/, also /ˈsɔri/ &middot; *SAH-ree, also SAW-ree*  
**Русский:** извини / простите — извинение; sorry? — переспрос; I'm sorry, but... — вежливое несогласие
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *I'm sorry*, *so sorry*, *sorry about that*, *soz* (internet)

The default apology, stretching from a bumped shopping cart to a death in the family. Two of its uses are not apologies at all: *sorry?* with a rising tone asks for a repeat, and *I'm sorry, but...* introduces a flat disagreement with no regret in it whatsoever.

1. *Sorry!* I didn't see you standing there.
2. *I'm so sorry* about your dad. He was a good man.
3. "*Sorry?* Say that one more time."
4. *I'm sorry*, but that is simply not what the contract says.
5. *Sorry about that* — the line was longer than I expected.
6. "*Sorry*, wrong number."

**See also:** [my bad](#my-bad), [my apologies](#my-apologies), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [condolences](#condolences)

### with all due respect

**Pronunciation:** /wɪð ˌɔl du rɪˈspɛkt/ &middot; *with-awl-doo-ri-SPEKT*  
**Русский:** при всём уважении — книжн., предупреждение: сейчас последует возражение
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *with respect*, *with all respect*, *no disrespect*

A warning label bolted to the front of a disagreement, usually aimed at someone senior to you. It sounds deferential and functions as the opposite: every American who hears it braces for the criticism coming right behind it.

1. *With all due respect*, sir, that timeline isn't realistic.
2. "*With all due respect*, Senator, you didn't answer the question."
3. *With all due respect* to the previous speaker, the numbers say otherwise.
4. "*With all due respect* —" "Here we go."
5. *No disrespect*, but I've been doing this job for twenty years.

**See also:** [no offense](#no-offense), [beg your pardon](#beg-your-pardon), [my apologies](#my-apologies)

## Hospitality and the table

### bon appetit

**Pronunciation:** /ˌboʊn ɑpəˈti/ &middot; *bohn-ah-puh-TEE*  
**Русский:** приятного аппетита — из французского: своей формулы английский так и не завёл
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *bon appétit*

Borrowed from French and said to people about to eat — American English never developed a native equivalent, which is why this one stuck. It carries a touch of ceremony, so at a backyard cookout most Americans say *dig in* instead.

1. She set the plates down and said, "*Bon appétit*."
2. *Bon appétit*, everybody — don't wait for me.
3. The waiter poured the wine, wished us *bon appétit*, and disappeared.
4. "Here you go. *Bon appetit*." "It's a hot dog, Kevin."
5. *Bon appétit!* Save room for dessert.

**See also:** [dig in](#dig-in), [enjoy](#enjoy), [help yourself](#help-yourself)

### come in

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌm ˈɪn/ &middot; *kum-IN*  
**Русский:** войдите! / заходи — ответ на стук и первое слово хозяина гостю
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *come on in*, *come in, come in*

The formula for admitting someone through a door — the answer to a knock, and the first thing a host says to a guest. Doubling it is warmer and hurries the person in out of the cold.

1. There was a knock, and she called, "*Come in!*"
2. *Come on in* — the door's unlocked.
3. "*Come in, come in*. You must be freezing out there."
4. *Come in* and shut the door behind you, please.
5. He knocked twice on the doorframe. "*Come in*," said the doctor without looking up.

**See also:** [make yourself at home](#make-yourself-at-home), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [welcome](09-greetings.md#welcome)

### dig in

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdɪɡ ˈɪn/ &middot; *dig-IN*  
**Русский:** налетай! / приступайте — разг., хозяин разрешает начинать есть
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

The plain American *start eating*, said by a host once the food is on the table. It is casual and hearty, and its real function is to release everyone from politely waiting.

1. All right, everybody — *dig in*.
2. "*Dig in* before it gets cold."
3. She set the platter in the middle of the table. "*Dig in*."
4. Don't stand on ceremony. *Dig in*.
5. Nobody touched a fork until Grandpa finally said *dig in*.

**See also:** [help yourself](#help-yourself), [bon appetit](#bon-appetit), [enjoy](#enjoy)

### eat up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌit ˈʌp/ &middot; *eet-UP*  
**Русский:** ешь как следует / кушайте — разг., настойчивее dig in, по-бабушкински
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *eat up, now*, *everybody eat up*

A push to start eating and to keep going — warmer and more insistent than *dig in*, and strongly associated with grandmothers, church basements, and anyone who thinks you look thin. It is an order in form only.

1. "*Eat up*, there's a whole other pan in the oven."
2. *Eat up* — we're leaving in twenty minutes.
3. She kept refilling his plate. "*Eat up*, you're skin and bones."
4. *Eat up* — you've got a four-hour drive ahead of you.
5. "*Eat up*, now," my grandmother said, and it was not a suggestion.

**See also:** [dig in](#dig-in), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [bon appetit](#bon-appetit)

### enjoy

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈdʒɔɪ/ &middot; *in-JOY*  
**Русский:** приятного аппетита! / наслаждайтесь — подавая блюдо или вручая что-то приятное
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *enjoy!*, *enjoy your meal*, *enjoy your evening*

What an American server says while setting down a plate, and what anyone says handing over something pleasant. It is used with no object at all, which is what makes it a formula rather than an ordinary verb.

1. "One veggie burger, no onions. *Enjoy!*"
2. Here's your ticket — *enjoy the show*.
3. *Enjoy*, and let me know what you think of the ending.
4. She handed him the paperback. "*Enjoy*."
5. "*Enjoy your meal*." "You too — I mean, thanks."

**See also:** [bon appetit](#bon-appetit), [dig in](#dig-in), [help yourself](#help-yourself)

### help yourself

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛlp jərˈsɛlf/ &middot; *help-yer-SELF*  
**Русский:** угощайтесь / берите сами — разрешение брать еду и прочее без лишних просьб
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *help yourselves*, *help yourself to whatever*

A host's standing permission to take food, drink, or anything else on offer without asking again. It is the usual American way to open a buffet, a fridge, or a snack table to a guest.

1. "*Help yourself* — there's more in the kitchen."
2. Coffee's on the counter; *help yourselves*.
3. *Help yourself to* whatever's in the fridge.
4. "Can I have one?" "*Help yourself*."
5. There's a whole box of them by the door — *help yourself*.

**See also:** [dig in](#dig-in), [be my guest](#be-my-guest), [make yourself at home](#make-yourself-at-home)

### make yourself at home

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmeɪk jərˌsɛlf ət ˈhoʊm/ &middot; *mayk-yer-self-at-HOHM*  
**Русский:** чувствуй себя как дома — приглашение гостю располагаться
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *make yourselves at home*

A host's blanket invitation to relax and treat the place as your own. It is warm, completely standard, and usually said within the first minute of somebody walking in.

1. "Come in, *make yourself at home*. I'll be right back."
2. *Make yourselves at home* — bathroom's down the hall on the left.
3. "*Make yourself at home*," he said, clearing a pile of laundry off the couch.
4. *Make yourself at home*, but please don't touch the thermostat.
5. "*Make yourself at home*." "Careful what you offer — I'm a fridge person."

**See also:** [come in](#come-in), [help yourself](#help-yourself), [be my guest](#be-my-guest)

### say when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪ ˈwɛn/ &middot; *say-WEN*  
**Русский:** скажешь, когда хватит — наливая; по сценарию отвечают одним словом «when»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *tell me when*, *when!* (the expected reply)

Said while pouring or serving, asking the other person to call out at the moment they have enough. The scripted answer is the single word *when*, which is what makes it a ritual rather than a real question.

1. He tipped the bottle over her glass. "*Say when*."
2. "*Say when*." "When! That's plenty."
3. *Say when* — I have no idea how much cheese you want.
4. She kept pouring, waiting for him to say *when*.
5. "*Say when*," the bartender said, and then just kept going.

**See also:** [help yourself](#help-yourself), [dig in](#dig-in), [bottoms up](#bottoms-up)

## Toasts

### bottoms up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɑtəmz ˈʌp/ &middot; *bot-umz-UP*  
**Русский:** до дна! — разг., тост допить залпом; в шутку и о лекарстве
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bottoms-up*

A toast that tells everyone to drain the glass — the bottom of the glass goes up as you tip it back. It is jovial and a little rowdy, and it does joking duty for medicine and anything else you would rather swallow fast.

1. "*Bottoms up!*" he said, and the whole table drank.
2. All right — *bottoms up*, and then we're switching to water.
3. She held up the shot glass. "*Bottoms up*."
4. He looked at the cough syrup, sighed, and said, "*Bottoms up*."
5. *Bottoms up*, everyone. Last call was ten minutes ago.

**See also:** [down the hatch](#down-the-hatch), [cheers](#cheers), [here's to you](#heres-to-you)

### cheers

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɪrz/ &middot; *CHEERZ*  
**Русский:** будем! / за здоровье — стандартный тост; ещё и лёгкое «спасибо», и подпись в письме
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral

The standard American toast, said as glasses are raised and clinked. Under British influence it has picked up two more jobs here — a light *thanks*, and a casual email sign-off — both of which sound faintly Anglophile coming from an American.

1. "*Cheers!*" Glasses clinked all around the table.
2. *Cheers* to the happy couple.
3. "Here's your change." "*Cheers*."
4. Let me know if you need anything else. *Cheers*, — Dan
5. He raised his beer an inch off the bar. "*Cheers*, man."

**See also:** [cheers to that](#cheers-to-that), [here's to you](#heres-to-you), [to your health](#to-your-health), [thanks](#thanks)

### cheers to that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʃɪrz tə ˈðæt/ &middot; *cheerz-tuh-THAT*  
**Русский:** вот за это выпьем / полностью поддерживаю — согласие в форме тоста
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *I'll drink to that*, *I'll cheers to that*

Agreement dressed up as a toast — you are endorsing what somebody just said strongly enough to raise a glass to it. It works with or without an actual drink in your hand.

1. "To never working another weekend." "*Cheers to that!*"
2. Whoever invented the four-day week deserves a statue. — *Cheers to that*.
3. *Cheers to that*, brother.
4. "May this be the last time we ever move apartments." "*I'll drink to that*."
5. She lifted her coffee mug. "*Cheers to that*."

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [here's to you](#heres-to-you), [amen](#amen)

### down the hatch

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdaʊn ðə ˈhætʃ/ &middot; *down-thuh-HATCH*  
**Русский:** поехали! / за воротник — разг., шутливый тост перед тем как выпить залпом
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A jokey toast said right before swallowing something in one go — the hatch being your throat, in old nautical humor. Like *bottoms up*, it moonlights as what you say to unpleasant medicine.

1. "*Down the hatch!*" he said, tossing back the shot.
2. She plugged her nose. *Down the hatch*.
3. One more spoonful — *down the hatch*.
4. *Down the hatch*, and let's never order that again.
5. He raised the glass to the whole room. "*Down the hatch*."

**See also:** [bottoms up](#bottoms-up), [cheers](#cheers), [here's mud in your eye](#heres-mud-in-your-eye)

### here's looking at you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrz ˈlʊkɪŋ ət ju/ &middot; *heerz-LOOK-ing-at-yoo*  
**Русский:** за тебя! — устар., цитата из «Касабланки», говорят с понимающей усмешкой
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *here's looking at you, kid*

A toast made famous by *Casablanca* in 1942 and now essentially a quotation. Americans say it over a raised glass with a knowing smile, usually as a small piece of movie cosplay.

1. He raised his glass across the table. "*Here's looking at you, kid*."
2. *Here's looking at you* — thirty years and still standing.
3. "*Here's looking at you, kid*," she said, doing a terrible Bogart.
4. *Here's looking at you*, old friend.
5. They clinked their paper cups. "*Here's looking at you*."

**See also:** [here's to you](#heres-to-you), [cheers](#cheers), [here's mud in your eye](#heres-mud-in-your-eye)

### here's mud in your eye

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrz ˈmʌd ɪn jər ˌaɪ/ &middot; *heerz-MUD-in-yer-eye*  
**Русский:** ну, будем! — устар., нарочито старомодный застольный тост
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated

An old drinking toast whose origin nobody agrees on — trench mud, racetrack mud, or farm mud, depending on who is telling it. It survives mainly as a deliberately old-timey thing to say when glasses come together.

1. "*Here's mud in your eye!*" my grandfather said before every first sip.
2. The reenactors touched their tin cups together. "*Here's mud in your eye*."
3. *Here's mud in your eye*, and many more.
4. "What kind of toast is that?" "An old one. *Here's mud in your eye*."
5. *Here's mud in your eye*, boys — we made it through another season.

### here's to you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪrz tə ˈju/ &middot; *heerz-tuh-YOO*  
**Русский:** за тебя / за вас — универсальная рамка тоста: here's to + кто или что
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *here's to*, *here's to us*, *here's to that*

The all-purpose American toast frame: *here's to* plus whoever or whatever you are honoring. Said on its own as *here's to you*, it salutes the person standing in front of you.

1. *Here's to you*, Coach. We wouldn't have made it without you.
2. "*Here's to* twenty-five years," he said, raising his glass.
3. *Here's to* the ones who couldn't be here tonight.
4. *Here's to us* — nobody else would put up with us.
5. She stood, glass in hand. "*Here's to* new beginnings."

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [to your health](#to-your-health), [congratulations](#congratulations)

### l'chaim

**Pronunciation:** /ləˈxaɪm/, commonly Anglicized to /ləˈhaɪm/ &middot; *luh-KHYME (the* kh *is a throat-clearing sound), also luh-HYME*  
**Русский:** лехаим! — еврейский тост, на иврите «за жизнь»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *l'chayim*, *lechaim*, *le chaim*

Hebrew for *to life* — the standard Jewish toast, widely recognized in American English through Jewish communities and *Fiddler on the Roof*. Many American speakers simplify the guttural opening of the second syllable to a plain *h*.

1. "*L'chaim!*" the whole table answered, glasses up.
2. *L'chaim* — to life, and to the bride and groom.
3. He poured two fingers of slivovitz and said, "*L'chaim*."
4. *L'chaim*, Bubbe. Ninety-five years.
5. The rabbi raised his cup. "*L'chaim*."

**See also:** [mazel tov](#mazel-tov), [cheers](#cheers), [to your health](#to-your-health)

### prost

**Pronunciation:** /proʊst/ &middot; *PROHST*  
**Русский:** прост! — немецкий тост, примерно «на здоровье»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *prosit*

The German toast, used in American English at Oktoberfest, in German restaurants, and by anyone holding a very large beer and enjoying themselves. It means roughly *may it do you good*.

1. "*Prost!*" The steins came together with a thud.
2. *Prost*, everybody — happy Oktoberfest.
3. He hoisted the liter mug. "*Prost!*"
4. *Prost!* And now somebody please help me carry this thing.
5. She learned exactly two German words in Munich: *danke* and *prost*.

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [skol](#skol), [salud](#salud)

### salud

**Pronunciation:** /səˈlud/ &middot; *suh-LOOD*  
**Русский:** салуд! — испанский тост «за здоровье»; по-испански ещё и ответ на чихание
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *salut*, *¡salud!*

Spanish for *health*, used as a toast wherever Spanish and English mix in the United States, which is nearly everywhere. In Spanish it also answers a sneeze, though speaking English most Americans stick with *bless you*.

1. "*Salud!*" they said, touching bottles.
2. *Salud*, amigos — to a good harvest.
3. He raised his margarita. "*Salud*."
4. *Salud*, and thank you all for coming out tonight.
5. Her grandmother says *salud* every time anybody sneezes.

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [prost](#prost), [to your health](#to-your-health), [bless you](#bless-you)

### skol

**Pronunciation:** /skoʊl/ &middot; *SKOHL*  
**Русский:** сколь! — скандинавский тост, от старого слова «чаша»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *skål*, *skoal*

The Scandinavian toast, from an old word for *bowl* or *drinking cup*. In America you hear it at Scandinavian-heritage gatherings across the Upper Midwest — and, in a completely separate life, as the chant of Minnesota Vikings fans.

1. "*Skol!*" everyone shouted, and drank.
2. *Skål*, and God jul.
3. He lifted the aquavit. "*Skol*, cousin."
4. Fifty thousand people chanting "*Skol!*" in unison is a strange thing to hear.
5. *Skol* to Grandma Ingrid, who made every single one of these cookies.

**See also:** [prost](#prost), [cheers](#cheers), [salud](#salud)

### sláinte

**Pronunciation:** /ˈslɑntʃə/ &middot; *SLAHN-chuh*  
**Русский:** сланте (произносится «слáнче») — ирландский тост «за здоровье»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *slainte*, *slàinte*, *sláinte mhaith*

Irish (and Scottish Gaelic) for *health*, and the standard Irish toast — heard in American bars every March and wherever Irish Americans raise a glass. The spelling defeats nearly everyone, which is why bartenders say it out loud first.

1. "*Sláinte!*" they said, and the pints came together.
2. *Sláinte* — to Grandpa Sean, who would have loved every minute of this.
3. He raised the whiskey. "*Sláinte*."
4. The bartender made the whole table say *sláinte* before anybody drank.
5. *Sláinte*, and happy St. Patrick's Day to you.

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [to your health](#to-your-health), [salud](#salud), [skol](#skol)

### to your health

**Pronunciation:** /tə jər ˈhɛlθ/ &middot; *tuh-yer-HELTH*  
**Русский:** за ваше здоровье — книжн., полный английский тост: свадьбы, юбилеи, проводы
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *your health*, *to your good health*, *your very good health*

The full, formal English toast that all the borrowed ones — *salud*, *prost*, *skål* — translate to. It sounds slightly grand in American English, which is why it shows up at weddings and retirement dinners rather than in bars.

1. "*To your health*," he said, and everyone drank.
2. *To your health*, and to a long and happy retirement.
3. She raised her glass to the guest of honor. "*Your very good health*, sir."
4. *To your health* — and to whoever's driving us home tonight.
5. "What am I supposed to say back?" "Just '*to your health*.'"

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [here's to you](#heres-to-you), [salud](#salud), [prost](#prost)

## Blessings and good wishes

### all the best

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ðə ˈbɛst/ &middot; *awl-thuh-BEST*  
**Русский:** всего наилучшего / всего доброго — тёплое пожелание, часто подпись в письме
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *all my best*, *all the best to you*, *all the best to you and yours*

A broad, warm good wish, most common as a written sign-off and as the last thing you say to someone who is leaving. Because it sounds like a send-off, it can read as a goodbye even when you did not mean one.

1. *All the best* in the new job — they're lucky to have you.
2. "*All the best*," she wrote, and signed her name.
3. *All the best* to you and the family this holiday season.
4. He shook my hand at the door. "*All the best*."
5. *All my best* — and don't be a stranger.

**See also:** [best of luck](#best-of-luck), [God bless](#god-bless), [take care](09-greetings.md#take-care)

### best of luck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɛst əv ˈlʌk/ &middot; *best-uv-LUK*  
**Русский:** удачи вам / успехов — чуть официальнее good luck, обычно на прощание
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *the best of luck*, *best of luck to you*

A more finished, slightly more formal *good luck*, common in email sign-offs and in send-offs where you may not see the person again. Delivered flatly about something doomed, it turns sarcastic like everything else in this category.

1. *Best of luck* on Thursday — you're going to do great.
2. *Best of luck* in the new role, and please keep in touch.
3. "*Best of luck to you*," the interviewer said, shaking his hand.
4. You're going to ask him to reschedule the whole quarter? *Best of luck*.
5. *Best of luck*, everyone. Results go up at noon.

**See also:** [good luck](#good-luck), [break a leg](#break-a-leg), [godspeed](09-greetings.md#godspeed)

### bless you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈblɛs ju/ &middot; *BLESS-yoo*  
**Русский:** будь здоров! — в ответ на чихание; о сделавшем добро — «ты просто ангел»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *God bless you*, *bless ya*

The standard American answer to a sneeze — automatic, expected, and only faintly religious anymore. A second sense, said to someone who has just done you a kindness, means roughly *you are an angel*.

1. She sneezed twice. "*Bless you*."
2. "*Bless you*." "Thanks — allergies."
3. He brought me coffee at six in the morning. *Bless you*.
4. *Bless you*, whoever labeled this box.
5. "*Bless you*," said three separate strangers on the subway.

**See also:** [gesundheit](#gesundheit), [God bless](#god-bless), [bless your heart](#bless-your-heart)

### bless your heart

**Pronunciation:** /ˌblɛs jər ˈhɑrt/ &middot; *bless-yer-HART*  
**Русский:** дай тебе Бог здоровья / ах ты бедняжка — диал. (Юг); о третьем лице — вежливая насмешка
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *bless her heart*, *bless his heart*, *bless it*

A Southern formula with two faces. Said warmly and to your face it is genuine affection or sympathy; said in the third person about someone who has just done something foolish, it is a polite insult — and every Southerner hears the difference instantly.

1. "You drove all this way for me? *Bless your heart*."
2. He tried to fix the sink himself. *Bless his heart*.
3. *Bless your heart*, honey, you must be exhausted.
4. She's been telling that same story for thirty years, *bless her*.
5. My aunt can say *bless your heart* in a tone that strips paint.

**See also:** [bless you](#bless-you), [God bless](#god-bless), [congratulations](#congratulations)

### break a leg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbreɪk ə ˈlɛɡ/ &middot; *brayk-uh-LEG*  
**Русский:** ни пуха ни пера! — театральное: пожелать «удачи» перед спектаклем — дурная примета
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Theater's superstitious way of wishing good luck, since saying *good luck* in a theater is thought to jinx the show. It has spread well past the stage to auditions, interviews, and any nerve-racking appearance.

1. "*Break a leg* out there!" the stage manager whispered.
2. *Break a leg* at the audition tomorrow.
3. "*Break a leg*," she texted him ten minutes before his interview.
4. He hugged his daughter backstage. "*Break a leg*, kid."
5. You never say good luck in a theater — you say *break a leg*.

**See also:** [good luck](#good-luck), [best of luck](#best-of-luck), [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood)

### fingers crossed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɪŋɡərz ˈkrɔst/ &middot; *fing-gerz-KRAWST*  
**Русский:** скрестим пальцы / тьфу-тьфу, чтоб получилось — надежда на удачный исход
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *keeping my fingers crossed*, *fingers crossed for you*

A wish for luck named after the old gesture of crossing your index and middle fingers. It can be a wish aimed at someone else or a statement of your own hope about something still up in the air.

1. *Fingers crossed* — we hear back about the house tomorrow.
2. "*Fingers crossed* for you on Monday."
3. The first test came back clean, and the second one, *fingers crossed*, will too.
4. *Fingers crossed* this flight actually leaves on time.
5. She held up both hands. "*Fingers crossed*."

**See also:** [good luck](#good-luck), [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood), [break a leg](#break-a-leg)

### gesundheit

**Pronunciation:** /ɡəˈzʊnthaɪt/ &middot; *guh-ZOONT-hyte*  
**Русский:** будь здоров! — из немецкого, светская замена bless you после чихания
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral

German for *health*, and the standard secular alternative to *bless you* after a sneeze. It is fully naturalized in American English and carries no religious weight at all, which is exactly why some people prefer it.

1. "Achoo!" "*Gesundheit*."
2. *Gesundheit* — that was a big one.
3. He sneezed into his elbow and somebody in the back said "*Gesundheit*."
4. "Bless you." "*Gesundheit*." "Thank you both."
5. My mother says *gesundheit*, my father says bless you, and nobody knows why.

**See also:** [bless you](#bless-you), [God bless](#god-bless), [excuse me](#excuse-me)

### get well soon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɛt wɛl ˈsun/ &middot; *get-wel-SOON*  
**Русский:** выздоравливай поскорее — пожелание больному; в живой речи чаще feel better
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *get well*, *feel better*, *feel better soon*, *hope you feel better*

The standard American wish for someone who is sick — printed on cards, written on a group signature, said at the door of a sickroom. In actual speech *feel better* is far more common; *get well soon* sounds like the card it came from.

1. *Get well soon!* The whole team signed the card.
2. "*Feel better*," he said, leaving the soup on the porch.
3. *Get well soon* — the office is chaos without you.
4. She texted "*feel better soon*" with a picture of the dog.
5. "*Get well*, and don't come back in here until you're not contagious."

**See also:** [bless you](#bless-you), [God bless](#god-bless), [all the best](#all-the-best)

### God bless

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɑd ˈblɛs/ &middot; *gahd-BLESS*  
**Русский:** храни тебя Бог / дай Бог здоровья — прощание, благодарность или умилённая досада
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *God bless you*, *God bless 'em*, *God bless America*

A short blessing used as a farewell, a thank-you, or an expression of fond exasperation about somebody. Its religious force varies enormously from speaker to speaker — for many Americans it now lands closer to *take care*.

1. "Thank you for everything. *God bless*."
2. *God bless* — drive safe, now.
3. He's eighty-four and still shovels his own driveway, *God bless him*.
4. "*God bless you*," she said, pressing the money into his hand.
5. Good night, everybody, and *God bless*.

**See also:** [bless you](#bless-you), [amen](#amen), [godspeed](09-greetings.md#godspeed)

### God rest his soul

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɑd ˌrɛst ɪz ˈsoʊl/ &middot; *gahd-rest-iz-SOHL*  
**Русский:** царствие ему небесное — устар., приписка сразу после имени покойного
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *God rest her soul*, *God rest him*, *God rest their souls*

A small blessing tucked in right after naming someone who has died. It is nearly automatic in older, Catholic, and Southern speech, and it marks respect rather than making any real theological claim.

1. My father, *God rest his soul*, never trusted a bank in his life.
2. "Aunt Rita — *God rest her soul* — made this every single Easter."
3. He learned the trade from old Mr. Kowalski, *God rest him*.
4. *God rest his soul*, the man would have hated all this fuss.
5. She mentions her husband, *God rest his soul*, in almost every conversation.

**See also:** [rest in peace](#rest-in-peace), [condolences](#condolences), [God bless](#god-bless)

### good luck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈlʌk/ &middot; *good-LUK*  
**Русский:** удачи — универсально; но good luck with that — «ну-ну, флаг тебе в руки»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *good luck to you*, *good luck with that*, *gl* (gaming)

The default American wish for a good outcome — safe in every register, spoken or written. Watch the fixed phrase *good luck with that*, which almost always means *that will never work and I want no part of it*.

1. *Good luck* on your exam!
2. "*Good luck*," the nurse said. "You'll do just fine."
3. *Good luck* to both teams tonight.
4. You're asking him for a raise this week? *Good luck with that*.
5. *Good luck*, and let us know how it goes.

**See also:** [best of luck](#best-of-luck), [break a leg](#break-a-leg), [fingers crossed](#fingers-crossed)

### knock on wood

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑk ɑn ˈwʊd/ &middot; *nahk-on-WOOD*  
**Русский:** тьфу-тьфу-тьфу, чтоб не сглазить — сказав о своей удаче, стучат по дереву
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *knock wood*, *touch wood* (British)

Said immediately after mentioning your own good fortune, to keep from jinxing it — usually while actually rapping your knuckles on something wooden. It is the most-used superstition formula in American speech, and even confirmed skeptics say it.

1. I haven't been sick all winter — *knock on wood*.
2. "*Knock on wood*," he said, tapping the table twice.
3. The car's been running fine, *knock wood*.
4. We've never had a flood on this street. *Knock on wood*.
5. *Knock on wood*, but I think we might actually finish early.

**See also:** [fingers crossed](#fingers-crossed), [good luck](#good-luck), [break a leg](#break-a-leg)

### same to you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪm tə ˈju/ &middot; *saym-tuh-YOO*  
**Русский:** и тебе того же / взаимно — ответ на пожелание; после оскорбления — «сам такой»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *and the same to you*, *same to you and yours*, *you too*, *likewise*

The reflexive answer that hands a good wish straight back, and the safe response to any holiday greeting you were not expecting. Said sharply after an insult, it hands that back too.

1. "Happy new year!" "*Same to you!*"
2. "Have a good one." "*Same to you*."
3. *Same to you and yours* — merry Christmas.
4. She wished him luck on the exam, and he said *same to you*.
5. "Drive safe." "*Same to you*, and text me when you're home."
6. "Go jump in a lake." "*Same to you*, pal."

**See also:** [happy new year](#happy-new-year), [happy holidays](#happy-holidays), [good luck](#good-luck)

## Congratulation, celebration, and condolence

### condolences

**Pronunciation:** /kənˈdoʊlənsɪz/ &middot; *kun-DOH-lun-sez*  
**Русский:** соболезнования — книжн., формальное сочувствие после чьей-то смерти
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *my condolences*, *my deepest condolences*, *our condolences*

The formal expression of sympathy after a death — standard in cards, at funeral homes, and in professional messages. In casual speech most Americans say *I'm so sorry* instead, and using *condolences* with a close friend can sound stiff.

1. "*My condolences*," he said, shaking her hand at the door.
2. Please accept our *deepest condolences* on the loss of your mother.
3. *Condolences* to the whole family.
4. She wrote a short note — just *my condolences* and a memory of him at the lake.
5. The entire department sends its *condolences*.

### congrats

**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *congratz*, *grats*, *gratz* (gaming)

The clipped, casual *congratulations* — right for texts, comments, and hallway conversation, and too breezy for a card, a speech, or anything printed. The gaming forms *grats* and *gratz* stay online.

1. *Congrats* on the new job!
2. "*Congrats*, you two!" she wrote under the photo.
3. Just heard about the baby — *congrats*, man.
4. *Congrats* to everybody who finished the race today.

### congratulations

**Pronunciation:** /kənˌɡrætʃəˈleɪʃənz/, casually /kənˌɡrædʒəˈleɪʃənz/ &middot; *kun-grach-uh-LAY-shunz, casually kun-graj-uh-LAY-shunz*  
**Русский:** поздравляю! — с достижением или доброй новостью; сухо сказанное — колкость хвастуну
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *congrats*, *congratulations to you*, *congradulations* (common misspelling)

The full formula for marking somebody else's achievement or good news — a degree, a promotion, an engagement, a baby. Said flatly to someone who is bragging, it becomes a dry insult.

1. *Congratulations!* We're so happy for you both.
2. *Congratulations* on twenty-five years with the company.
3. "*Congratulations*, Doctor," he said, handing her the diploma.
4. *Congratulations* are in order — she passed the bar.
5. So you finally did the dishes. *Congratulations*.

**See also:** [congrats](#congrats), [mazel tov](#mazel-tov), [best of luck](#best-of-luck)

### happy anniversary

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi ˌænəˈvɜrsəri/ &middot; *hap-ee-an-uh-VUR-suh-ree*  
**Русский:** с годовщиной! — свадьбы, а также рабочей и любой другой; обычно паре сразу
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *happy anniversary to you both*, *happy tenth*, *happy anniversary, babe*

The fixed wish for a wedding anniversary, extended freely to work anniversaries and to the anniversary of anything worth marking. Like *happy birthday* it takes *happy* and nothing else, and it is normally addressed to the couple together rather than to one half of it.

1. *Happy anniversary!* Forty years is no small thing.
2. "*Happy anniversary*, you two," he said, setting the champagne on the table.
3. She left a card propped against the coffee maker: *Happy anniversary*.
4. *Happy anniversary* to Denise — ten years with the firm this month.
5. "It's the fifteenth." "Oh. *Happy anniversary*." "You forgot again."

**See also:** [happy birthday](#happy-birthday), [congratulations](#congratulations), [here's to you](#heres-to-you)

### happy birthday

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi ˈbɜrθdeɪ/ &middot; *hap-ee-BURTH-day*  
**Русский:** с днём рождения! — единственно возможная формула, других вариантов нет
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *happy birthday to you*, *happy bday*, *HBD*

The fixed American birthday formula — said, sung, written on cake, and posted by the hundred on social media. There is no alternative: *congratulations on your birthday* is not something Americans say.

1. *Happy birthday!* Make a wish.
2. "*Happy birthday*, Grandpa," the kids shouted into the phone.
3. *Happy birthday* to my favorite person — twenty-nine again, I hear.
4. He posted "*HBD*" on her wall and figured that covered it.
5. *Happy birthday*, and many more.

**See also:** [many happy returns](#many-happy-returns), [congratulations](#congratulations), [happy new year](#happy-new-year)

### happy holidays

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi ˈhɑlədeɪz/ &middot; *hap-ee-HAH-luh-dayz*  
**Русский:** с праздниками! — нейтральное зимнее поздравление, не привязанное к религии
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

The inclusive American winter greeting, covering Christmas, Hanukkah, Kwanzaa, and New Year's in one phrase. It is the default in retail, in offices, and in mail to people whose religion you do not know.

1. *Happy holidays!* Thanks for shopping with us.
2. "*Happy holidays*," the mail carrier said, handing over a stack of cards.
3. *Happy holidays* from all of us at Bergstrom Dental.
4. She signs every December email "*Happy holidays*."
5. *Happy holidays* — see you in the new year.

**See also:** [merry Christmas](#merry-christmas), [happy new year](#happy-new-year), [cheers](#cheers)

### happy new year

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi nu ˈjɪr/ &middot; *hap-ee-noo-YEER*  
**Русский:** с Новым годом! — в полночь 31 декабря и потом первые недели января
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *Happy New Year*, *happy New Year's*

Said at midnight on December 31st and then for the first couple of weeks of January, to anybody you have not seen yet that year. Capital *N* and *Y* are standard when the phrase names the holiday itself.

1. "*Happy new year!*" everyone shouted at midnight.
2. *Happy New Year* — hope this one treats you better than the last.
3. She called her parents at 12:01. "*Happy new year*, Mom."
4. *Happy new year* to you and yours.
5. It's the eighteenth of January and he's still saying *happy new year* to everybody.

**See also:** [happy holidays](#happy-holidays), [merry Christmas](#merry-christmas), [cheers](#cheers)

### happy Thanksgiving

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi ˌθæŋksˈɡɪvɪŋ/ &middot; *hap-ee-thanks-GIV-ing*  
**Русский:** с Днём благодарения! — поздравление с праздником, без религиозного оттенка
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *Happy Thanksgiving*, *happy Turkey Day*

The greeting for the fourth Thursday in November, said on the day and for a few days on either side of it. It is the one big American holiday formula with no religious edge at all, which is why offices and cashiers use it freely where they would hedge with *happy holidays* in December.

1. "*Happy Thanksgiving!*" she called through the screen door, holding a pie.
2. *Happy Thanksgiving* — drive safe, and call when you get in.
3. He said *happy Thanksgiving* to the whole crew and sent everybody home at noon.
4. "*Happy Turkey Day*," her brother texted, with a photo of the fryer.
5. *Happy Thanksgiving* from all of us here at the station.

**See also:** [happy holidays](#happy-holidays), [merry Christmas](#merry-christmas), [same to you](#same-to-you)

### many happy returns

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛni ˌhæpi rɪˈtɜrnz/ &middot; *men-ee-hap-ee-ri-TURNZ*  
**Русский:** многая лета / долгих лет — устар. в США, британское пожелание на день рождения
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *many happy returns of the day*

An old birthday wish — that the day may come back many more times — still ordinary in Britain and distinctly antique in the United States. An American who says it is usually being courtly on purpose or quoting a grandparent.

1. "*Many happy returns*," he wrote inside the card.
2. *Many happy returns of the day*, my dear.
3. Great-Aunt Ruth still sends a note every year wishing me *many happy returns*.
4. "Happy birthday!" "And *many happy returns*," her husband added.
5. *Many happy returns*, Professor. We hope there are eighty more.

**See also:** [happy birthday](#happy-birthday), [congratulations](#congratulations), [best of luck](#best-of-luck)

### mazel tov

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɑzəl ˌtɔv/, also /ˈmɑzəl ˌtɑv/ &middot; *MAH-zul-tawv, also MAH-zul-tahv*  
**Русский:** мазл тов — поздравляю! ивр. и идиш «доброй удачи», об уже свершившемся событии
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *mazal tov*, *mazeltov*

Hebrew and Yiddish for *good fortune*, said as congratulations at weddings, bar and bat mitzvahs, births, and graduations. It is understood well beyond Jewish American communities, and it congratulates an accomplished fact rather than wishing future luck.

1. "*Mazel tov!*" the guests shouted as the glass broke.
2. *Mazel tov* on the engagement — we're thrilled for you.
3. He hugged his son. "*Mazel tov*, kiddo."
4. *Mazel tov* to the graduate and to the parents who paid for it.
5. "She got in early decision." "*Mazel tov!*"

**See also:** [congratulations](#congratulations), [l'chaim](#lchaim), [congrats](#congrats)

### merry Christmas

**Pronunciation:** /ˌmɛri ˈkrɪsməs/ &middot; *mer-ee-KRIS-mus*  
**Русский:** с Рождеством! — именно merry, а не happy: это просто надо запомнить
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *Merry Christmas*, *merry Xmas*, *merry Christmas to you*

The standard Christmas greeting in American English. Note *merry*, not *happy* — this is the one holiday that takes a different adjective, and it simply has to be memorized.

1. *Merry Christmas!* Come in, come in.
2. "*Merry Christmas*, Mrs. Delgado," the kids called from the sidewalk.
3. *Merry Christmas* and a happy new year.
4. He said *merry Christmas* to every single person in the checkout line.
5. *Merry Christmas*, everybody — and don't peek at the presents.

**See also:** [happy holidays](#happy-holidays), [happy new year](#happy-new-year), [God bless](#god-bless)

### rest in peace

**Pronunciation:** /ˌrɛst ɪn ˈpis/ &middot; *rest-in-PEESS*  
**Русский:** покойся с миром — на надгробии и как стандартный отклик на смерть в интернете
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *RIP*, *R.I.P.*, *rest easy*, *may she rest in peace*

A blessing on the dead, carved on headstones and now the standard way Americans mark a death online. The abbreviation *RIP* is normally spelled out letter by letter in speech, occasionally read as the word *rip*, and it has spread jokingly to broken phones and canceled shows.

1. *Rest in peace*, Grandma. We'll miss you every single day.
2. "*RIP* to a legend," the post read.
3. He was a good dog. *Rest easy*, buddy.
4. *RIP* my laptop, 2014 to 2026.
5. She was ninety-two, and she went in her sleep. May she *rest in peace*.

**See also:** [condolences](#condolences), [amen](#amen), [God bless](#god-bless)

### sorry for your loss

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɑri fər jər ˈlɔs/, also /ˌsɔri fər jər ˈlɔs/ &middot; *sah-ree-fer-yer-LAWSS, also saw-ree-fer-yer-LAWSS*  
**Русский:** соболезную вашей утрате — стандартная, чуть безличная формула для скорбящего
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *I'm sorry for your loss*, *so sorry for your loss*, *sorry for your loss, ma'am*

The default American condolence to someone who has been bereaved — expected, safe, and slightly impersonal, which is exactly why police officers, coworkers, and anyone who did not know the deceased reach for it. Nobody will fault you for saying it, and nobody will remember that you did.

1. "*I'm so sorry for your loss*," the officer said quietly.
2. *Sorry for your loss*, man. Anything you need, you call me.
3. Every card in the stack said some version of *sorry for your loss*.
4. "I heard about your mom. *I'm sorry for your loss*."
5. He shook every hand in the receiving line: "*Sorry for your loss*. *Sorry for your loss*."

**See also:** [condolences](#condolences), [rest in peace](#rest-in-peace), [sorry](#sorry), [thoughts and prayers](#thoughts-and-prayers)

### thoughts and prayers

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθɔts ən ˈprɛrz/ &middot; *thawts-un-PRAIRZ*  
**Русский:** мыслями и молитвами с вами — публично после трагедии звучит как пустая отписка
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *my thoughts and prayers*, *our thoughts and prayers are with you*, *thoughts and prayers to the family*

An offer of sympathy after a death, an illness, or a disaster. Said to one grieving person it is sincere and ordinary; issued publicly after a mass shooting it has been mocked so relentlessly as a substitute for action that many Americans now hear it as empty by default.

1. "You and your family are in our *thoughts and prayers*."
2. *Thoughts and prayers* to everyone still without power tonight.
3. She sent flowers and a note: *thoughts and prayers*.
4. Another press conference, another round of *thoughts and prayers*, and nothing changes.
5. "*Thoughts and prayers*," he said, and you could hear the quotation marks around it.

**See also:** [sorry for your loss](#sorry-for-your-loss), [condolences](#condolences), [God bless](#god-bless)

## Devotional formulas

### alhamdulillah

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑlhɑmdʊˈlɪlɑ/ &middot; *ahl-hahm-doo-LIL-lah*  
**Русский:** альхамдулиллях — араб. «хвала Богу»: на добрую весть, на «как дела», после еды
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *alhamdulilah*, *al-hamdu lillah*, *hamdulillah*

Arabic for *praise be to God*, and the everyday Muslim response to good news, to *how are you?*, and to a finished meal. In American English it does exactly the work that *thank God* and *praise the Lord* do, and among Arab and Muslim Americans it is just as frequent and just as unremarkable.

1. "How've you been?" "Good, *alhamdulillah*."
2. The biopsy came back clean. *Alhamdulillah*.
3. He pushed back from the table. "*Alhamdulillah*. I can't eat another bite."
4. *Alhamdulillah* — everybody got out of the building.
5. She says *hamdulillah* where her coworkers say *thank God*, and it means the same thing.

**See also:** [inshallah](#inshallah), [mashallah](#mashallah), [praise the Lord](#praise-the-lord), [amen](#amen)

### amen

**Pronunciation:** /ɑˈmɛn/, also /eɪˈmɛn/ &middot; *ah-MEN, also ay-MEN*  
**Русский:** аминь — «да будет так» в конце молитвы; в речи — «вот именно, полностью согласен»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *amen to that*, *amen, brother*, *A-men*

Hebrew for *so be it*, said at the close of a prayer to affirm it. Outside church it is one of the most common ways an American endorses a statement — *amen to that* means *I could not possibly agree more*. Both pronunciations are standard; *ay-MEN* is more common in Protestant and gospel settings, *ah-MEN* in Catholic liturgy and in secular agreement.

1. "...in Jesus' name we pray. *Amen*."
2. *Amen*, and pass the potatoes.
3. "This week needs to be over." "*Amen to that*."
4. *Amen*, brother. Preach.
5. The whole congregation answered *amen*.
6. Nobody should have to work on their own birthday. — *Amen*.

**See also:** [hallelujah](#hallelujah), [praise be](#praise-be), [cheers to that](#cheers-to-that)

### glory be

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡlɔri ˈbi/ &middot; *glor-ee-BEE*  
**Русский:** слава тебе Господи / батюшки-светы — устар., деревенское, бабушкино «ух ты»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *glory be to God*, *glory*, *glory hallelujah*

An old devotional exclamation of wonder or thanks, and also the name of a short Catholic prayer. In everyday American speech it is antique, rural, and often faintly comic — a grandmother's version of *wow*.

1. "*Glory be*, would you look at that."
2. *Glory be to God*, the boy finally cleaned his room.
3. She stepped out onto the porch. "*Glory be*, it's snowing."
4. *Glory be* — the truck started on the first try.
5. My grandmother says *glory be* about eleven times a day.

**See also:** [praise be](#praise-be), [hallelujah](#hallelujah), [amen](#amen)

### God forbid

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɑd fərˈbɪd/ &middot; *gahd-fer-BID*  
**Русский:** не дай Бог / упаси Господь — оберег сразу после упоминания беды
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *heaven forbid*, *God forbid it*, *perish the thought*

A ward set against a bad possibility, dropped in immediately after naming it — the dark twin of *knock on wood*. Most Americans who say it are asking God for no more than they are when they say *bless you*. Said with the weight on *forbid*, it flips into sarcasm and mocks somebody else's overreaction.

1. If anything happens to me, *God forbid*, the will is in the safe.
2. *God forbid* the man should ever admit he took a wrong turn.
3. We'd need a bigger generator if the power went out for a week, *God forbid*.
4. *Heaven forbid* we start a meeting on time around here.
5. "What if she says no?" "*God forbid*. Then we're back to square one."

**See also:** [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood), [inshallah](#inshallah), [God bless](#god-bless)

### hallelujah

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæləˈlujə/ &middot; *hal-uh-LOO-yuh*  
**Русский:** аллилуйя — в церкви хвала Богу; в речи — «ну наконец-то!», возглас облегчения
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *alleluia*, *hallelujer* (dialect)

Hebrew for *praise the Lord*, used in worship and, far more often in daily speech, as an exclamation of relief that something has finally happened. The spelling *alleluia* is the Latin-derived church form.

1. "*Hallelujah!*" the choir sang.
2. The heat's back on. *Hallelujah*.
3. *Hallelujah* — she finally answered her phone.
4. "The results came back negative." "*Hallelujah*."
5. He threw up both hands. "*Hallelujah*, it's Friday."

**See also:** [amen](#amen), [praise be](#praise-be), [praise the Lord](#praise-the-lord)

### inshallah

**Pronunciation:** /ɪnˈʃɑlə/, also /ˌɪnʃəˈlɑ/ &middot; *in-SHAH-luh, also in-shuh-LAH*  
**Русский:** иншаллах — араб. «если Богу будет угодно»; в шутку — «этого не случится никогда»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *insha'Allah*, *in sha Allah*, *inshaAllah*, *God willing* (the English equivalent)

Arabic for *if God wills it*, attached to any statement about the future. It is ordinary, sincere, everyday speech for Muslim and Arab Americans — closer to *hopefully* than to anything ceremonial. Outside those communities it has picked up a joking second life meaning *that is never happening on schedule*, and that joke reads as mockery in front of anyone who uses the word for real.

1. "See you at the wedding in June." "*Inshallah*."
2. We close on the house next month, *inshallah*.
3. *God willing*, she'll be home from the hospital by Friday.
4. "Is the plumber actually coming today?" "*Inshallah*."
5. He ends every plan with *inshallah*, and he means every word of it.

**See also:** [alhamdulillah](#alhamdulillah), [mashallah](#mashallah), [God bless](#god-bless), [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood)

### mashallah

**Pronunciation:** /mɑˈʃɑlə/, also /ˌmɑʃɑˈlɑ/ &middot; *mah-SHAH-luh, also mah-shah-LAH*  
**Русский:** машаллах — араб. «на то воля Божья», говорят, любуясь, чтобы не сглазить
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *masha'Allah*, *ma sha Allah*, *mashaAllah*

Arabic for *what God has willed*, said while admiring something — a baby, a new house, somebody's good news. It gives the credit to God, and in long practice it also keeps the compliment from drawing the evil eye. Leaving it out when you praise a child can genuinely sting in Arab and Muslim American families, which makes it one of the few formulas here where the omission is the message.

1. "Look how big he's gotten!" "*Mashallah*."
2. *Mashallah*, the house came out beautiful.
3. She showed her aunt the acceptance letter and got "*mashallah*" four separate times.
4. Three languages at six years old, *mashallah*.
5. He complimented the baby without saying *mashallah*, and his mother-in-law noticed.

**See also:** [alhamdulillah](#alhamdulillah), [inshallah](#inshallah), [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood)

### peace be with you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpis bi wɪð ˈju/ &middot; *peess-bee-with-YOO*  
**Русский:** мир вам — литургическое приветствие; вне церкви нарочито торжественно или иронично
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *peace be with you all*, *peace*, *and with your spirit* (the liturgical response)

The blessing exchanged during the sign of peace at Mass and in many Protestant services, said while shaking hands with the people around you. Outside a church it is deliberately solemn, and among friends it is gently ironic.

1. He turned to the pew behind him. "*Peace be with you*."
2. "*Peace be with you*." "And with your spirit."
3. The priest raised his hand: "*Peace be with you all*."
4. She ended the eulogy with "*peace be with you*" and stepped down from the lectern.
5. "*Peace be with you*, brother," he said, handing the wrench back.

**See also:** [God bless](#god-bless), [amen](#amen), [shalom](09-greetings.md#shalom)

### praise be

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpreɪz ˈbi/ &middot; *prayz-BEE*  
**Русский:** слава Богу — устар., у пожилых и верующих; у светских людей иронично
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *praise be to God*

A short devotional thanks to God, now heard mostly from older and more religious speakers and in historical settings — and, since *The Handmaid's Tale*, with an ominous edge for younger listeners. Secular Americans use it ironically.

1. "*Praise be*," she said when the rain finally came.
2. Everybody got home safe, *praise be*.
3. *Praise be to God* for another harvest.
4. The printer works again. *Praise be*.
5. "*Praise be*," he muttered, watching the tow truck finally pull in.

**See also:** [praise the Lord](#praise-the-lord), [hallelujah](#hallelujah), [amen](#amen)

### praise the Lord

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpreɪz ðə ˈlɔrd/ &middot; *prayz-thuh-LORD*  
**Русский:** слава Господу! — обычное в церкви; в светской речи — сильное облегчение
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *praise God*, *praise Jesus*, *praise the Lord and pass the ammunition*

A devotional exclamation of thanks and joy, standard in American church speech and especially in Black and evangelical churches, where it works as call and response. In secular mouths it is relief laid on thick.

1. "*Praise the Lord!*" somebody shouted from the back pew.
2. She came through the surgery just fine. *Praise God*.
3. *Praise the Lord* — the check finally cleared.
4. "*Praise the Lord*," said the pastor, "and amen."
5. He got the job. *Praise the Lord*.

**See also:** [hallelujah](#hallelujah), [amen](#amen), [praise be](#praise-be)

## Other set-piece rituals

### jinx

**Pronunciation:** /dʒɪŋks/ &middot; *JINKS*  
**Русский:** чур, я первый! — детский возглас, когда двое сказали одно и то же разом
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *jinx, you owe me a Coke*, *double jinx*, *jinx, personal jinx*

Called out by whoever is faster when two people say the same thing at the same instant. The playground rules — the loser owes a soda and cannot speak until somebody says their name — are known to every American child and get invoked, half-seriously, by adults forever after.

1. They both said "pizza" in the same breath. "*Jinx!*"
2. "*Jinx*, you owe me a Coke."
3. "That's exactly what I was about to say." "*Jinx*. You can't talk now."
4. Two of us called *jinx* at the same moment, which the rules do not cover.
5. He mouthed *jinx* across the conference table and went back to his notes.

**See also:** [say cheese](#say-cheese), [knock on wood](#knock-on-wood), [trick or treat](#trick-or-treat)

### say cheese

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪ ˈtʃiz/ &middot; *say-CHEEZ*  
**Русский:** скажи «сыр»! — команда фотографа за полсекунды до снимка
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *cheese!*, *everybody say cheese*, *smile*

What the person holding the camera says a half second before the shutter, because the word pulls the mouth into a smile. The scripted answer is the word *cheese*, stretched out until the picture is taken.

1. "Everybody squeeze in. *Say cheese!*"
2. *Say cheese* — and Grandpa, open your eyes this time.
3. "*Say cheese*." "*Cheeeeese*."
4. He counted to three, said *say cheese*, and took the picture a beat too early anyway.
5. Twenty relatives on a staircase, saying *cheese* for the fourth time.

**See also:** [say when](#say-when), [trick or treat](#trick-or-treat), [enjoy](#enjoy)

### trick or treat

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtrɪk ər ˈtrit/ &middot; *trik-er-TREET*  
**Русский:** кошелёк или жизнь! — что кричат дети на Хеллоуин, выпрашивая конфеты
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *trick-or-treat*, *trick or treat, smell my feet* (children's rhyme)

What American children shout at the door on Halloween to ask for candy; the threatened "trick" is entirely theoretical. Adults say it jokingly when they turn up anywhere hoping to be handed something.

1. "*Trick or treat!*" three small ghosts shouted through the screen door.
2. She opened the door with the bowl ready. "*Trick or treat*, huh?"
3. *Trick or treat* — and please, not raisins again this year.
4. He knocked on his neighbor's door holding an empty casserole dish. "*Trick or treat*."
5. The kids practiced saying *trick or treat* the whole way down the block.

**See also:** [say cheese](#say-cheese), [happy holidays](#happy-holidays), [please](#please)
