# Animal-directed, child-directed, and work calls

> The words Americans shout at creatures and crews — animals, babies, and whole rooms full of people who need to move at the same instant.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

What ties this category together is that none of these words are said to an equal. They go down the chain — to a dog, a horse, a toddler, a line of customers, a work crew — and they expect obedience rather than an answer. Because of that, almost all of them are shouted, stretched, or repeated: *soooo-ee*, *staaay*, *heave... ho*, *going once, going twice*. Two warnings run through the whole list. First, several of these words are genuinely rude when aimed at an adult — *good boy*, *sit*, and *scram* are fine for a retriever and insulting for a coworker. Second, some are regional working vocabulary (*gee*, *haw*, *sooey*, *mush*) that a learner should recognize but will almost never need to say.

## Dogs, cats, and other pets

### atta boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈætə ˌbɔɪ/ &middot; *AT-uh-boy*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *attaboy*, *atta girl*, *attagirl*, *that's a boy*

Praise called out to a dog — or to a person you are cheering on — at the exact moment they do the thing you wanted. It is warm and a little rough, and aimed at an adult by someone who outranks them it can sound condescending.

1. "*Atta boy!*" he said, scratching the retriever behind the ears.
2. She dropped the ball at his feet and he laughed. "*Atta girl*."
3. *Atta boy* — you sat the whole time without moving.
4. The old dog finally climbed into the truck bed. "*Attaboy*, Duke."
5. He hit the free throw and the whole bench yelled, "*Atta boy!*"

**See also:** [good boy](#good-boy), [bad dog](#bad-dog), [attaboy](15-encouragement.md#attaboy)

### bad dog

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbæd ˈdɔɡ/ &middot; *bad-DAWG*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bad boy*, *bad girl*, *bad kitty*, *no bad dog*

The standard scolding for a dog caught doing something forbidden. It is delivered low and flat rather than shouted, and it usually follows a sharp "No!"

1. "*Bad dog!* Get off the couch."
2. He came home to shredded mail all down the hallway. "Oh, *bad dog*."
3. *Bad girl* — we do not dig in the flower beds.
4. "No! *Bad dog*," she said, pulling the sock out of his mouth.
5. Trainers will tell you that a "*bad dog*" three hours after the fact means nothing to the animal.

**See also:** [good boy](#good-boy), [no-no](#no-no), [leave it](#leave-it)

### come

**Pronunciation:** /kʌm/ &middot; *KUM*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *come here*, *c'mere*, *come on*

The basic recall command, and the first thing most American dogs are taught. It is said once, firmly, with the dog's name in front of it, and it is an order rather than an invitation.

1. "Bella, *come!*"
2. He whistled twice and shouted, "*Come!*"
3. *Come here*, buddy — you're getting a bath whether you like it or not.
4. She practiced "*come*" with a treat in her fist for twenty minutes a day.
5. "*C'mere*," he said, patting his knee, and the puppy skidded across the kitchen floor.

**See also:** [here boy](#here-boy), [heel](#heel), [sit](#sit), [come on](15-encouragement.md#come-on)

### down

**Pronunciation:** /daʊn/ &middot; *DOWN*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *lie down*, *down boy*, *get down*

Two commands in one syllable: get all the way to the floor, or get off me. "*Down, boy*" is also said jokingly to a person who is getting overexcited about something.

1. "*Down*. Good. Stay."
2. The Lab put both paws on her chest and her husband yelled, "*Down!*"
3. *Down*, boy — she isn't going to want a muddy hello.
4. He gets "sit" every time, but "*down*" takes him a second.
5. "*Down*, boy," she teased when he started in about the new truck.

**See also:** [sit](#sit), [stay](#stay), [heel](#heel)

### drop it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdrɑp ɪt/ &middot; *DROP-it*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *drop*, *give*, *drop it now*

Told to a dog with something in its mouth — a ball, a shoe, a dead bird. Said to a person, the same two words mean stop talking about this, which is a much sharper thing.

1. "*Drop it*. *Drop it!* Good boy."
2. He had half a bagel in his jaws before I could get out "*drop it*."
3. *Drop it*, Rex. That is not yours.
4. She holds the tug rope until he hears "*drop it*," and then she throws it again.
5. "*Drop it*," the officer told the shepherd, and the padded sleeve hit the mat.

**See also:** [leave it](#leave-it), [fetch](#fetch), [no-no](#no-no)

### easy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈizi/ &middot; *EE-zee*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *easy boy*, *easy now*, *easy there*, *whoa easy*

A calming word said in a long, low voice to a spooked horse or an overexcited dog. The vowel gets stretched — the sound is doing as much work as the word.

1. "*Easy*, girl. *Easy*." He kept one hand flat on the mare's neck.
2. *Easy* now. Nobody's going to hurt you.
3. The dog's hackles went up and she said, quietly, "*Easy*."
4. He talked the whole time he was saddling her: "*Easy*, *easy*, there we go."
5. *Easy*, boy, it's just the vacuum.

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [there there](#there-there), [steady](05-fear.md#steady)

### fetch

**Pronunciation:** /fɛtʃ/ &middot; *FECH*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *go fetch*, *fetch it*, *fetch it up*

Go get the thing I just threw and bring it back. It doubles as the name of the game, so Americans say "playing fetch" at least as often as they say the command.

1. He threw the stick across the yard and yelled, "*Fetch!*"
2. "*Fetch*, girl!"
3. *Fetch* — go on, get it.
4. She has never once understood *fetch*; she just watches the ball go by.
5. "*Go fetch*," he said, and the spaniel was already in the water.

**See also:** [drop it](#drop-it), [come](#come), [good boy](#good-boy)

### good boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈbɔɪ/ &middot; *good-BOY*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *good girl*, *good dog*, *who's a good boy*, *goooood boy*

The all-purpose reward phrase for a dog, said in a high sing-song voice that matters more than the words do. Said to an adult it is patronizing, and said to a child it lands as either sweet or sarcastic depending on the child's age.

1. "*Good boy!* Yes you are!"
2. She scratched his ears. "*Who's a good girl?* You are."
3. *Good dog* — that is exactly what I wanted.
4. He gets a treat and a "*good boy*" every time he rings the bell by the door.
5. "*Good boy*," his brother said flatly when he finally took the trash out.

**See also:** [atta boy](#atta-boy), [bad dog](#bad-dog), [sit](#sit)

### heel

**Pronunciation:** /hil/ &middot; *HEEL*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *heel up*, *to heel*

Walk at my side and stay there. It is the command that separates a trained dog from one that drags its owner down the sidewalk.

1. "*Heel*," she said, and the shepherd tucked in beside her knee.
2. He can't hold a *heel* for more than a block before he starts pulling.
3. *Heel!* We are not chasing that squirrel.
4. The instructor had us walk the whole ring saying "*heel*" at every corner.
5. "*Heel*, buddy — there's a car coming."

**See also:** [come](#come), [sit](#sit), [stay](#stay)

### here boy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪr ˈbɔɪ/ &middot; *heer-BOY*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *here girl*, *here boy here boy*, *c'mere boy*

A friendly summons to a dog, softer than the trained command "come." Strangers use it constantly, because they do not know the dog's name.

1. He crouched down and slapped his thigh. "*Here, boy!*"
2. *Here, girl* — come see what I've got.
3. "*Here boy, here boy*," the kid called from the end of the driveway.
4. She whistled, then tried "*here, boy*," and finally went for the treat bag.
5. *Here, boy!* Come on. That's it.

**See also:** [come](#come), [here kitty kitty](#here-kitty-kitty), [atta boy](#atta-boy)

### here kitty kitty

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɪr ˈkɪti ˈkɪti/ &middot; *heer-KIT-ee-KIT-ee*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *kitty kitty*, *kitty kitty kitty*, *here kitty*

The standard American way to call a cat: the word *kitty* repeated in a high, coaxing voice, usually mixed with clicks or kissing noises. Cats generally ignore it, which everyone knows and everyone does anyway.

1. "*Here, kitty kitty kitty!*" she called into the crawl space.
2. He shook the food bag and went, "*Kitty kitty kitty?*"
3. *Here kitty* — come on, it's freezing out here.
4. They walked the whole block after dark calling, "*Here kitty kitty*."
5. The vet tech opened the carrier door. "*Kitty kitty*. Nobody's going to hurt you."

**See also:** [puss puss](#puss-puss), [here boy](#here-boy), [psst](10-attention.md#psst)

### leave it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈliv ɪt/ &middot; *LEEV-it*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *leave*, *leave it alone*

Do not touch that in the first place — the command that comes before "drop it." Dog owners use it constantly on walks, mostly about chicken bones and other dogs.

1. "*Leave it*," he said as they passed the half-eaten burrito on the curb.
2. *Leave it!* *Leave it.* Good.
3. She teaches "*leave it*" with a treat under her shoe.
4. "*Leave it*, Moose," and to my amazement, he left it.
5. *Leave it* — that's a skunk, not a cat.

**See also:** [drop it](#drop-it), [no-no](#no-no), [bad dog](#bad-dog)

### puss puss

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpʊs ˈpʊs/ &middot; *PUSS-puss* (the vowel of *put*, not of *bus*)  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *puss puss puss*, *here puss*, *pussy pussy*

An older way of calling a cat, heard now mostly from grandparents and in old books. Be careful with the longer form: *pussy* is vulgar slang in modern American English, so *here kitty kitty* is the safe choice.

1. "*Puss puss puss*," my grandmother used to call from the back porch.
2. He set the saucer down and said softly, "*Here, puss*."
3. *Puss puss* — where have you been all night?
4. Nobody under sixty says "*puss puss*" to a cat anymore.
5. She still calls him "*puss*," and he answers to it about half the time.

**See also:** [here kitty kitty](#here-kitty-kitty), [here boy](#here-boy), [scat](#scat)

### sic 'em

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɪk əm/ &middot; *SIK-um*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *sic him*, *sic her*, *sic 'em boy*, *sick 'em*

Set the dog on someone — attack. In real life it is rare and serious; in ordinary speech it is nearly always a joke about turning somebody loose on an opponent, a customer service line, or a rival team.

1. "*Sic 'em*, boy!" the farmer yelled, and the dog took off after the coyote.
2. He pointed at the mailman and said, "*Sic 'em*," and the beagle wagged her tail.
3. Send the lawyers in. *Sic 'em*.
4. "*Sic 'em!*" is a Baylor thing — you'll hear the whole stadium do it.
5. She forwarded the email to her boss with two words: *sic 'em*.

**See also:** [fetch](#fetch), [bad dog](#bad-dog), [go get 'em](15-encouragement.md#go-get-em)

### sit

**Pronunciation:** /sɪt/ &middot; *SIT*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *sit down*, *sit boy*, *siddown*

The first command almost every American dog learns. Said to a person, a bare "*Sit.*" is an order from someone with authority — a coach, a cop, a parent — and it lands hard.

1. "*Sit.* Good boy."
2. He holds the treat over the dog's nose and says "*sit*," and the tail hits the floor.
3. *Sit!* *Sit.* We are not opening this door until you sit.
4. She can do *sit* and shake, but stay is still a work in progress.
5. "*Sit*," the coach said, and forty kids dropped onto the gym floor.

**See also:** [stay](#stay), [down](#down), [heel](#heel)

### speak

**Pronunciation:** /spik/ &middot; *SPEEK*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *speak boy*, *say please*

A trick command telling a dog to bark once. It is taught for fun and turns up in the same breath as "shake" and "roll over."

1. "*Speak!*" The terrier let out one sharp bark and got a biscuit.
2. He'll do sit, stay, and *speak*, but he will not fetch.
3. *Speak*, boy. Come on — *speak*.
4. My uncle taught his dog to *speak*, and now the dog barks at him about everything.
5. "*Speak*," she said, and the dog just stared at her.

**See also:** [sit](#sit), [fetch](#fetch), [good boy](#good-boy)

### stay

**Pronunciation:** /steɪ/ &middot; *STAY*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *staaay*, *stay there*, *stay put*

Hold that position until I release you. Owners stretch the vowel out and hold a flat palm forward, and the word usually gets repeated while they walk backwards away from the dog.

1. "Sit. *Staaay*. Good."
2. He put the bowl down and made her *stay* for three whole seconds.
3. *Stay* — I'll be right back.
4. She can hold a *stay* across the entire yard now.
5. "*Stay*," he told the dog outside the store, and hooked the leash to the bike rack.

**See also:** [sit](#sit), [come](#come), [heel](#heel)

## Horses, livestock, and working animals

### chick chick

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtʃɪk ˈtʃɪk/ &middot; *CHIK-chik*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *chick chick chick*, *here chick chick*, *chickie chickie*

What you say to bring chickens running, usually while scattering feed. It is said fast and high, and the birds learn the sound rather than the word.

1. "*Chick chick chick!*" she called, swinging the coffee can of scratch.
2. *Here chick chick* — breakfast.
3. He goes out at dawn and the whole flock comes at the first "*chick chick*."
4. *Chick chick chick chick* — get out of the road, you idiots.
5. My grandmother could empty the barn with one "*chick chick chick*."

**See also:** [sooey](#sooey), [cluck cluck](#cluck-cluck), [here kitty kitty](#here-kitty-kitty)

### cluck cluck

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklʌk ˈklʌk/ &middot; *KLUK-kluk*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *cluck*, *cluck-cluck*

An imitation of a hen rather than a real call — said to chickens the way people talk to any animal, and said about people to mean fussy, disapproving chatter. If you actually want the birds to come, the call is *chick chick*.

1. She leaned over the fence going, "*Cluck cluck*, ladies."
2. The kid ran through the coop yelling "*cluck cluck*" and scattered every hen in it.
3. *Cluck cluck* — anybody laid me an egg today?
4. The neighbors have been *cluck-clucking* about our fence for a month.
5. He made a little *cluck cluck* noise and the hens ignored him completely.

**See also:** [chick chick](#chick-chick), [coo](#coo), [tsk](#tsk)

### coo

**Pronunciation:** /ku/ &middot; *KOO*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *coo coo*, *coo-coo*

The soft rolling sound a pigeon or a dove makes, and by extension the noise adults make over a baby. As an interjection it is more written than shouted — you are likelier to meet the verb, as in "she cooed at him."

1. "*Coo, coo*," the boy said to the pigeons, holding out a crust.
2. *Coo* — *coo* — came from the eaves all morning.
3. Everybody leaned over the bassinet going *coo, coo*.
4. She *cooed* at the baby until he finally smiled.
5. A dove landed on the rail and gave one long *coo*.

**See also:** [coochie-coo](#coochie-coo), [cluck cluck](#cluck-cluck), [chick chick](#chick-chick)

### gee

**Pronunciation:** /dʒi/ &middot; *JEE*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *gee up*, *gee haw*, *gee now*

Turn right — the teamster's word, used for horses, mules, oxen, and sled dogs. Its opposite is *haw*, and a person who "doesn't know gee from haw" is hopeless.

1. "*Gee!*" he called, and the mule swung the plow to the right.
2. *Gee* — *gee*, now — take the fork by the barn.
3. The musher shouted "*Gee!*" and the whole team leaned into the turn.
4. My grandfather drove a team on nothing but *gee*, *haw*, and *whoa*.
5. He can't tell *gee* from *haw*, and he sure can't back a trailer.

**See also:** [haw](#haw), [whoa](#whoa), [giddyup](#giddyup), [mush](#mush)

### giddyup

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡɪdiˌʌp/ &middot; *GID-ee-up*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *giddy up*, *giddyap*, *giddy-up*, *git up*

Get moving, said to a horse — a worn-down version of "get up." Americans use it constantly as a joke to hurry a person or a car along, and children shout it while riding a grown-up's back.

1. He shook the reins. "*Giddyup*."
2. *Giddy up*, horsey! Faster!
3. The light turned green and the guy behind us leaned on the horn. *Giddyup*.
4. "*Giddyap*," she said, and the old gelding started walking.
5. We have a plane to catch, people. *Giddyup*.

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [hyah](#hyah), [gee](#gee), [hup](#hup)

### haw

**Pronunciation:** /hɔ/ &middot; *HAW*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *haw there*, *gee haw*, *haw now*

Turn left — the mirror image of *gee*, used with draft animals and dog teams. It is a working word; nobody says it outside a field, a trail, or a joke about not knowing your left from your right.

1. "*Haw!*" and the team cut left around the stump.
2. *Gee* for right, *haw* for left — that's the whole vocabulary.
3. He called *haw* a beat too late and put the sled in a snowbank.
4. *Haw*, *haw* now, easy.
5. The old man drove oxen with *gee* and *haw* until the day he died.

**See also:** [gee](#gee), [whoa](#whoa), [mush](#mush), [giddyup](#giddyup)

### ho

**Pronunciation:** /hoʊ/ &middot; *HOH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *ho there*, *whoa ho*, *land ho*, *westward ho*

An old driver's call meaning stop or hold, now almost entirely replaced by *whoa*. It survives in fixed phrases like *land ho* and *westward ho*, and inside *heave ho*. Warning: standing alone in modern American slang, *ho* is an insulting word for a woman, so do not use it loose.

1. "*Ho!* *Ho there*," the teamster called, and the wagon rolled to a stop.
2. *Land ho!* — the oldest shout in the language of sailing.
3. *Westward ho*, everybody. Get in the van.
4. He raised a hand. "*Ho*, now. Let's not get ahead of ourselves."
5. In old novels the driver says "*ho*" where a modern rider would say "whoa."

**See also:** [whoa](#whoa), [heave ho](#heave-ho), [gee](#gee)

### hup

**Pronunciation:** /hʌp/ &middot; *HUP*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *hup two*, *hup two three four*, *hup hup*

A short barked syllable that marks a beat — the marching cadence, a jump cue for a horse or a dog, and a coach's word for "move." It carries rhythm rather than meaning.

1. "*Hup*, two, three, four," the drill sergeant called.
2. She tapped the horse's flank at the fence: "*Hup!*"
3. *Hup!* *Hup!* Let's go, let's go, everybody up.
4. He held the hoop out and said "*hup*," and the dog sailed through it.
5. The squad went by chanting *hup*-two-three-four the whole length of the block.

**See also:** [ten-hut](#ten-hut), [hut](#hut), [giddyup](#giddyup)

### hyah

**Pronunciation:** /haɪˈɑ/ or /jɑ/ &middot; *hy-AH* or *YAH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *yah*, *ya*, *heeyah*, *hee-yah*

The shouted syllable a rider uses to drive a horse into a run, usually with a slap of the reins. It has no settled spelling — you will see it written a dozen ways in Westerns.

1. "*Hyah!*" he shouted, and the horse broke into a gallop.
2. *Hyah!* *Hyah!* Get up there!
3. She slapped the reins across his neck and yelled, "*Yah!*"
4. In every Western the hero says exactly one thing to his horse: *hyah*.
5. *Hyah* — come on, girl, we're losing daylight.

**See also:** [giddyup](#giddyup), [whoa](#whoa), [mush](#mush)

### mush

**Pronunciation:** /mʌʃ/ &middot; *MUSH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *mush on*, *mush you huskies*, *hike*

The word everyone thinks sled-dog drivers shout to start the team. Real mushers mostly say *hike* or *all right*, but *mush* gave English the words *musher* and *mushing*, so it is the one the public knows.

1. "*Mush!*" he yelled, and the sled jerked forward.
2. Every kid who has seen one movie about Alaska yells "*mush*" at the dogs.
3. *Mush on!* We've got twelve miles to the checkpoint.
4. She says "*hike*," not "*mush*" — nobody out on the trail says mush.
5. He gave the runners a push and called, "All right — *mush!*"

**See also:** [gee](#gee), [haw](#haw), [giddyup](#giddyup), [whoa](#whoa)

### sooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsuˌi/ &middot; *SOO-ee*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *soo-ee*, *sooie*, *pig pig pig*, *woo pig sooie*

The hog call: a long "sooo" pulled up into "-ee," used to bring pigs to the trough and, in Arkansas, to bring a stadium to its feet. It is shouted at full volume with the first syllable held for several seconds.

1. "*Soooo-ee!* *Pig pig pig!*" he hollered from the fence line.
2. *Sooey!* Come on — dinner.
3. *Woo, pig, sooie* — you'll hear sixty thousand people do it in Fayetteville.
4. My uncle could call hogs two fields away with one *sooey*.
5. She let out a *sooey* at the family reunion and three cousins answered.

**See also:** [chick chick](#chick-chick), [come and get it](#come-and-get-it), [here boy](#here-boy)

### tsk

**Pronunciation:** /ǀ/ or /ǁ/ — a tongue click rather than an ordinary English word &middot; *tisk* /tɪsk/ when the spelling is read out loud  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *tsk tsk*, *tch*, *tut*, *tut-tut*

A click of the tongue. Doubled and aimed at a person it means disapproval; made once or twice at the side of the mouth and aimed at a horse it means walk on. They are two different clicks, and horse people rarely bother spelling theirs at all.

1. She clicked twice — *tsk, tsk* — and the mare moved off at a walk.
2. He looked at the report card and went, "*Tsk. Tsk.*"
3. Two quick clicks, a squeeze of the legs, and he was trotting.
4. *Tsk tsk* — and here I thought you were the responsible one.
5. Riders click at their horses the way drivers honk: constantly, and without thinking about it.

**See also:** [giddyup](#giddyup), [easy](#easy), [cluck cluck](#cluck-cluck)

### whoa

**Pronunciation:** /woʊ/ or /hwoʊ/ &middot; *WOH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *woah*, *whoa there*, *whoa Nelly*, *whoa boy*

Stop — the universal American call to a horse, said low and drawn out. Off the horse it has become one of the most common interjections in the language, meaning "slow down," "back up a second," or plain astonishment.

1. "*Whoa*. *Whoa*, girl." He pulled back gently on the reins.
2. *Whoa there* — nobody said anything about paying for it today.
3. He hit the brakes. "*Whoa!*"
4. *Whoa, Nelly.* Let's start that sentence over.
5. The horse ignored "*whoa*" completely and went straight for the water trough.

**See also:** [giddyup](#giddyup), [ho](#ho), [easy](#easy), [steady](05-fear.md#steady)

## Shooing and driving off

### git

**Pronunciation:** /ɡɪt/ &middot; *GIT*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *git on*, *git out*, *git along*, *go on git*

A rural, drawled "get" used to drive an animal — or an unwanted person — away. It is Southern and Western in flavor, and it sounds deliberately country when city people use it.

1. "*Git!*" he said, stamping his boot, and the raccoon went over the fence.
2. Go on, *git* — shoo.
3. *Git on out* of my garden, you fool dog.
4. He waved his hat at the steers. "*Git along*, now."
5. She told the guy selling windows to *git*, and he got.

**See also:** [shoo](#shoo), [scat](#scat), [scram](#scram)

### scat

**Pronunciation:** /skæt/ &middot; *SKAT*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *scat now*, *go on scat*

Go away — said mostly to cats, and to children in a mock-stern voice. It is short and sharp and almost never actually angry.

1. "*Scat!*" she said, and the cat jumped off the counter.
2. *Scat*, you two — I'm trying to get dinner made.
3. He flapped a dish towel at the tabby. "Go on. *Scat*."
4. *Scat!* That's the third time you've been on that table.
5. The old tom did not *scat*; he just looked at her.

### scram

**Pronunciation:** /skræm/ &middot; *SKRAM*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *scram outta here*, *beat it*

Get out of here, said to people rather than animals. It has a 1940s gangster-movie flavor now, so Americans use it half in fun — at kids underfoot, at a friend who is hanging around too long.

1. "*Scram*, kid. The grown-ups are talking."
2. He heard the sirens and told everybody to *scram*.
3. *Scram!* Before I call your mother.
4. She jerked a thumb at the door. "All right, you two — *scram*."
5. Nobody says *scram* with a straight face anymore, which is exactly why it's funny.

**See also:** [git](#git), [shoo](#shoo), [scat](#scat)

### shoo

**Pronunciation:** /ʃu/ &middot; *SHOO*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *shoo shoo*, *shoo fly*, *shoo now*

The standard word for waving an animal away — flies, birds, a cat on the counter, a dog under the table. Used on people it is playful and slightly bossy, the way an aunt clears kids out of a kitchen.

1. "*Shoo!* *Shoo!*" She waved both arms at the pigeons.
2. *Shoo*, fly — I'm eating here.
3. He *shooed* the cat off the keyboard for the fourth time that morning.
4. *Shoo*, you two. Go play outside.
5. The mare swished her tail to *shoo* the flies off her back.

Said to a baby or toddler at the end of a bottle, a snack, or a game, usually with both palms turned up. It is the standard American way of announcing that something has finished to someone too young to be told.

Baby talk for food, or for how good the food is, said while feeding a small child. Adults use *nom nom* the same way about their own dinner, mostly online.

A child's word for a small cut or bruise, and by extension for a small mistake. Adults say "my boo-boo" about an error to make it sound minor and forgivable.

**Variants:** *and action*, *rolling... action*

The director's call that starts a take. On a real set it comes after "roll sound" and "roll camera," and everyone off a set says it as a joke to start anything at all.

**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *cut cut cut*, *and cut*, *cut it out*

Stop filming — the director's word for the end of a take, good or bad. Off the set, "cut" means stop doing that right now, and "cut it out" is the everyday version.

**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *make a hole*, *make way*, *coming through*

Get out of the way — shouted by somebody carrying something heavy or moving fast. It comes off a ship, where the gangway is the passage, and it sounds slightly old-fashioned now outside the military.

1. "*Gangway!*" he yelled, coming around the corner with a stack of trays.
2. *Gangway* — hot pan, hot pan, hot pan.
3. *Make a hole!* Medic coming through.
4. Somebody hollered *gangway* and the whole hallway flattened against the wall.
5. *Gangway*, folks. We've got a piano on this dolly.

**See also:** [heads up](05-fear.md#heads-up), [look out](05-fear.md#look-out), [fire in the hole](#fire-in-the-hole)

### heave ho

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhiv ˈhoʊ/ &middot; *heev-HOH*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *yo-heave-ho*, *heave-ho*, *heave*

A sailor's pulling chant, said on the beat when several people haul on the same rope. As a noun, "the old heave-ho" means being thrown out or fired.

1. "*Heave... ho!* *Heave... ho!*" The rope came in a foot at a time.
2. *Yo-heave-ho* — one more good pull and she's up.
3. All right, on my count. *Heave!*
4. They gave him the old *heave-ho* after the third missed deadline.
5. *Heave ho*, everybody. This stump is coming out today.

**See also:** [one two three](#one-two-three), [hup](#hup), [timber](#timber)

### next

**Pronunciation:** /nɛkst/ &middot; *NEKST*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *next please*, *next in line*, *next customer*

The single word that moves a line forward, called by a cashier, a clerk, a barber, or a nurse. Said flat, it is brisk and impersonal, which is exactly what a long line needs.

1. "*Next!*" The line shuffled forward one step.
2. *Next*, please. Window four.
3. He called *next* before the last guy had his wallet back in his pocket.
4. *Next in line* for the fitting room?
5. She waited forty minutes to hear one word: *next*.

**See also:** [step right up](#step-right-up), [last call](#last-call), [all aboard](#all-aboard)

### step right up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌstɛp ˌraɪt ˈʌp/ &middot; *step-rite-UP*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *step right up folks*, *step up*, *hurry hurry hurry*

The carnival barker's call, pulling a crowd toward a booth or a show. Nobody says it seriously anymore, so it now signals "here comes a sales pitch," usually with a wink.

1. "*Step right up!* Three balls for a dollar!"
2. *Step right up*, folks — the show starts in five minutes.
3. He spread his hands like a barker. "*Step right up* and behold the miracle of modern plumbing."
4. *Step right up* if you want to be the one who tells the boss.
5. The whole midway was *step-right-ups* and flashing lights.

**See also:** [next](#next), [going once, going twice](#going-once-going-twice), [last call](#last-call)

### timber

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtɪmbər/ &middot; *TIM-bur*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *timberrr*, *tim-BER*

The logger's shout warning everyone that a tree is falling. Off the job, Americans yell it whenever anything tall goes over — a bookshelf, a stack of boxes, a friend tipping out of a chair.

1. "*Timber!*" The pine came down across the clearing.
2. He made the last cut and yelled *timberrr*.
3. The whole stack of pallets went over. *Timber*.
4. *Timber* — heads up, the ladder's going.
5. My uncle shouts "*timber*" every time he stands up out of the recliner too fast.

**See also:** [fore](#fore), [fire in the hole](#fire-in-the-hole), [look out](05-fear.md#look-out)

## Sports and the ring

### batter up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbætər ˈʌp/ &middot; *bat-ur-UP*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *next batter up*, *batter batter*

The call that brings the next hitter to the plate in baseball. Off the field it means "you're next," usually with a little dread attached.

1. "*Batter up!*" the umpire called, and the game started.
2. *Batter up* — Rodriguez is on deck.
3. The interviewer opened the door and said, "*Batter up*."
4. He's got three kids in Little League and he yells "*batter up*" in his sleep.
5. *Batter up*, *batter up* — let's move it along, we're losing the light.

**See also:** [play ball](#play-ball), [next](#next), [hut](#hut)

### break

**Pronunciation:** /breɪk/ &middot; *BRAYK*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *break it up*, *break clean*, *one two three break*

The referee's order to two fighters tangled up in a clinch: let go and step back. It is also what a football huddle shouts before the team claps and lines up, and what a cop says to a scuffle — "break it up."

1. "*Break!* *Break*, I said." The referee shoved in between them.
2. On three — one, two, three, *break!*
3. The huddle clapped once and yelled, "*Break!*"
4. He wouldn't *break clean*, and the ref took a point away.
5. Two bouncers came over yelling *break it up*.

**See also:** [seconds out](#seconds-out), [ding ding](#ding-ding), [cut](#cut)

### ding ding

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪŋ ˈdɪŋ/ &middot; *DING-ding*  
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ding ding ding*, *ding*

The sound of the bell that starts and ends a round in boxing. In ordinary talk, "ding ding ding" is what people say when somebody finally guesses right — the game-show bell.

1. *Ding ding* — round three.
2. "*Ding ding ding!* We have a winner."
3. He was saved by the bell — *ding ding*, and the round was over.
4. *Ding ding ding*, you got it in one.
5. The announcer does his own sound effects: "And there's the *ding ding*."

**See also:** [seconds out](#seconds-out), [break](#break), [play ball](#play-ball)

### fore

**Pronunciation:** /fɔr/ &middot; *FOR*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *fore right*, *fore left*, *foooore*

The golfer's shout warning anyone downrange that a ball is coming their way. It is called immediately and loudly, and the polite version adds the direction.

1. "*Fore!*" he shouted, watching the ball curve toward the next fairway.
2. *Fore right!* *Fore right!*
3. Somebody yelled *fore* and everyone on the tee box ducked.
4. If you don't call *fore*, you deserve the lecture you're about to get.
5. *Fore* — sorry, folks, that one got away from me.

**See also:** [timber](#timber), [gangway](#gangway), [heads up](05-fear.md#heads-up)

### play ball

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpleɪ ˈbɔl/ &middot; *play-BAWL*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *let's play ball*

The umpire's call that starts a baseball game or restarts play after a stoppage. Outside the park it means "let's get on with it," or, in business talk, "cooperate."

1. "*Play ball!*" The crowd roared and the first pitch went in.
2. The rain finally stopped around four and the umpire called *play ball*.
3. All right, everybody's here — let's *play ball*.
4. The other side wouldn't *play ball* on the price, so we walked.
5. *Play ball!* Two outs, nobody on.

**See also:** [batter up](#batter-up), [break](#break), [hut](#hut)

### pull

**Pronunciation:** /pʊl/ &middot; *PULL* (the vowel of *full*)  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *ready... pull*

The shooter's call in trap and skeet that releases the clay target. It is said sharply, and the machine throws on the word.

1. "*Pull!*" The clay flew out and he broke it clean.
2. He settled the gun on his shoulder, took a breath, and said, "*Pull*."
3. *Pull* — miss. *Pull* — got it.
4. Call *pull* whenever you're ready; there's no rush.
5. She shot twenty-five straight, one "*pull*" at a time.

**See also:** [fore](#fore), [ready set go](#ready-set-go), [one two three](#one-two-three)

### seconds out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛkəndz ˈaʊt/ &middot; *SEK-undz-OUT*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *seconds away*, *seconds out round three*

The ring announcer's order for the cornermen to clear out before a round begins. It is chiefly British; American announcers reach for it only when they want the old-fashioned formality, and otherwise just shout the round number.

1. "*Seconds out* — round four!"
2. The stools came out of the ring the moment he called *seconds out*.
3. *Seconds away*, gentlemen.
4. You still hear "*seconds out*" from British announcers on the late broadcasts.
5. *Seconds out.* And there's the bell.

**See also:** [ding ding](#ding-ding), [break](#break), [batter up](#batter-up)

## Auction, court, and closing calls

### all rise

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɔl ˈraɪz/ &middot; *awl-RIZE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *all rise please*, *please rise*

The bailiff's command that everyone in a courtroom stand as the judge comes in or goes out. It is one of the few American phrases that still gets an entire room to obey instantly.

1. "*All rise.* The Honorable Judge Alvarez presiding."
2. The bailiff said *all rise* and a hundred people stood up at once.
3. *All rise* — court is now in session.
4. Everybody knows "*all rise*" from television, but it's just as flat and fast in person.
5. *All rise.* ... You may be seated.

**See also:** [order](#order), [oyez](#oyez), [ten-hut](#ten-hut)

### going once, going twice

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈwʌns ˌɡoʊɪŋ ˈtwaɪs/ &middot; *goh-ing-WUNS goh-ing-TWICE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *going once going twice sold*, *going going gone*

The auctioneer's warning that the bidding is about to close. Anywhere else it is a friendly ultimatum — last chance to speak up before the decision is final.

1. "Two hundred. *Going once, going twice*..."
2. *Going once, going twice* — sold to the man in the green hat.
3. Last slice of pizza. *Going once. Going twice.*
4. He held the gavel up. "*Going, going, gone*."
5. *Going twice* — anybody? All right, we're moving on.

**See also:** [sold](#sold), [last call](#last-call), [step right up](#step-right-up)

### last call

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlæst ˈkɔl/ &middot; *last-KAWL*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *last call for alcohol*, *last call folks*, *closing time*

The bartender's announcement that this is your final chance to order before the bar stops serving. It has spread everywhere — airports, offices, potlucks, anything about to close.

1. "*Last call!*" The bartender flipped the lights twice.
2. *Last call for alcohol*, everybody.
3. *Last call* on the sign-up sheet — I'm sending it in at noon.
4. They gave *last call* at 1:30 and had everyone out by two.
5. *Last call*, folks. Tab, or one more?

**See also:** [all aboard](#all-aboard), [going once, going twice](#going-once-going-twice), [next](#next)

### order

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɔrdər/ &middot; *OR-dur*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *order in the court*, *order order*, *come to order*

A judge's or a chairperson's call for a room to stop talking and behave. It is nearly always doubled or extended — "Order! Order in the court!" — and it usually arrives with a gavel.

1. "*Order!* *Order in the court!*" The gavel came down three times.
2. The chair called the meeting to *order* at seven o'clock.
3. *Order* — I will clear this room if I have to.
4. He banged the gavel and shouted for *order* over the noise.
5. *Order*, please. We have one more item on the agenda.

**See also:** [all rise](#all-rise), [oyez](#oyez), [hush](10-attention.md#hush)

### oyez

**Pronunciation:** /oʊˈjeɪ/ or /oʊˈjɛz/ &middot; *oh-YAY* or *oh-YEZ*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *oyez oyez oyez*, *o yes*

A court crier's ancient "hear ye," said three times to open a session and still used by the Supreme Court and a few state courts. It comes from Anglo-French, and even the people who say it professionally disagree about how to pronounce it.

1. "*Oyez! Oyez! Oyez!* All persons having business before the Honorable Supreme Court..."
2. The marshal's *oyez* is the first thing you hear in that courtroom.
3. *Oyez, oyez* — the town crier still opens the fair that way.
4. Half the lawyers there say *oh-YEZ* and the other half say *oh-YAY*.
5. *Oyez!* Draw near and give your attention.

**See also:** [all rise](#all-rise), [order](#order), [attention](10-attention.md#attention)

### sold

**Pronunciation:** /soʊld/ &middot; *SOHLD*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *sold to the lady in red*, *sold American*

The auctioneer's word ending the bidding, said as the gavel comes down. In everyday speech, "sold" means you have convinced me — the fastest way in English to say yes.

1. "*Sold!* To the bidder in the back row."
2. *Sold*, for eight hundred and fifty dollars.
3. "There's a pool, and it's ten minutes from work." "*Sold*."
4. He said the word *sold* about four hundred times that afternoon.
5. Free food? *Sold*. I'm in.

**See also:** [going once, going twice](#going-once-going-twice), [last call](#last-call), [next](#next)
