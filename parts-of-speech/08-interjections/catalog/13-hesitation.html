Hesitation, fillers, and discourse markers#

The stalling sounds, hedges, and traffic signals of American conversation — what speakers say while they think, and how they steer a talk from one thing to the next.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

These are the words Americans use to buy time, hold the floor, and steer a conversation — sounds and short phrases that carry almost no dictionary meaning of their own but tell the listener what is happening: I am still thinking, I am changing the subject, don't take that too literally. Many of them are ordinary words (well, so, look, right) doing a second job outside the grammar of the clause, which is why they are set off by commas, dashes, or ellipses rather than folded into the sentence. The raw hesitation sounds barely have vowels to transcribe: hmm and mm are hummed on a closed mouth with no vowel at all, and uh and um use the shortest, most neutral vowel in English. Almost everything in this file belongs to speech — apart from actually, and so on, anyway, by the way, granted, in other words, more or less, so to speak, and that said, writing these into an essay or a report is a mistake.

Vocalized pauses#

The raw hesitation sounds: no dictionary meaning at all, just a noise that says "I am still talking."

ah#

Pronunciation
/ɑ/ · AH
Русский
а-а… / э-э… — протяжный звук раздумья, пока подбираешь слово
Type
primary
Register
neutral (speech only)
Variants
ahh, ahhh

A drawn-out open vowel used to stall for a beat while you retrieve a word or soften a hesitant answer. In this hesitation use it is flatter and quieter than the ah of relief or discovery, and Americans reach for it less often than for uh.

  1. I was, ah, hoping we could push the meeting to Thursday.
  2. His name is — ah — it's right on the tip of my tongue.
  3. The total comes to, ah, three hundred and forty dollars.
  4. Ah, how do I say this without sounding rude?
  5. We're, ah, still waiting on legal to sign off.
  6. The witness paused, said ah twice, and then changed his answer.
See also
uh, um, er, hmm

er#

Pronunciation
/ɝ/ (British /ɜː/, with no r-sound in it) · UR
Русский
э-э — британское написание звука колебания; американцы говорят и пишут uh
Type
primary
Register
regional (chiefly British; in American use mostly a written spelling)
Variants
err, uhr

The British spelling of the basic hesitation sound, written er because non-rhotic British English pronounces it as a long vowel with no r in it. An American reading the page aloud puts the r back in and says /ɝ/, which is not the sound the spelling was invented for. Americans say uh and write uh; when an American writes er, it is usually in print, in a joking-formal tone, or to mark a small verbal stumble.

  1. The report is due — er — I think it was Friday.
  2. He claims he was at the office all night, which, er, seems unlikely.
  3. So the meeting is at nine, er, nine thirty.
  4. I'd like to introduce our, er, guest of honor.
  5. "Er, I don't think that's quite what she meant," he said.
  6. The transcript is one long string of er and um.

uh, um, erm, ah

erm#

Pronunciation
/ɝm/ (British /ɜːm/, with no r-sound in it) · URM
Русский
э-м — брит. аналог американского um
Type
primary
Register
regional (British)
Variants
ermm

The British counterpart of American um — the same hesitation hum, spelled to match a non-rhotic accent. Americans recognize it in writing but do not normally produce it, and using it out loud in the United States reads as an affectation or as an imitation of a British speaker.

  1. Erm, I'm not sure that's how it works over here.
  2. He wrote back, "Erm, we never actually agreed to that."
  3. The British witness kept saying erm where an American would say um.
  4. Erm — sorry, could you repeat the question?
  5. Her text just said "erm" and nothing else, which was worse than a no.

hmm#

Pronunciation
/hm̩/ · HMM — a hummed m with the lips closed, no vowel in it
Русский
хм / хмм — гудение с закрытым ртом: раздумье, сомнение; чем длиннее, тем серьёзнее
Type
primary
Register
neutral
Variants
hm, hmmm, hmmmm

A closed-lip hum meaning that you are thinking, weighing, or not quite convinced. Length carries the meaning: a clipped hm is mild interest or skepticism, while a long hmmm is real deliberation.

  1. Hmm. Let me look at the numbers again before I answer.
  2. "How does eight o'clock work?" "Hmm, could we do eight thirty?"
  3. Hmm, that's strange — the file was here this morning.
  4. She read the offer, said hmm, and slid it back across the table.
  5. Hmmm. I'm not sure I agree with that at all.
  6. He answered every question with a noncommittal hm.
See also
mm, let me think, well, uh

mm#

Pronunciation
/m̩/ · MM — a hum on a closed m, no vowel
Русский
м-м — ровное мычание, заполняющее паузу; не звук удовольствия и не согласие мм-хмм
Type
primary
Register
informal
Variants
mmm

A short level hum that fills a pause or signals lukewarm thinking. Note that this is not the pleasure sound mmm (as in mmm, that smells good) and not the agreement sound mm-hmm; the hesitation mm is flat in pitch and goes nowhere.

  1. Mm, I'd have to check my calendar for that week.
  2. "Is the new place any good?" "Mm. It's fine."
  3. He gave a low mm and kept reading.
  4. Mm — maybe. Ask me again after lunch.
  5. "Mm, I'm not sure about the blue one," she said, holding it up to the light.

hmm, uh, um

uh#

Pronunciation
/ə/ (also /ʌ/) · UH
Русский
э-э — основной американский звук колебания; только в устной речи
Type
primary
Register
neutral (speech only)
Variants
uhh, uhhh, er (British spelling)

The default American hesitation sound — a short neutral vowel that holds the floor for the half second it takes to plan the next word. It is normal and near-universal in American speech, and it is edited out of writing entirely except when the writing represents speech.

  1. It's, uh, about a twenty-minute drive from here.
  2. Uh, I think you may have the wrong number.
  3. We're looking at, uh, maybe three or four candidates.
  4. "What's the password?" "Uh … hang on, I wrote it down somewhere."
  5. He said uh eleven times in a two-minute answer.
  6. I was going to — uh — sorry, what was the question again?
See also
um, er, ah, hmm, like

um#

Pronunciation
/əm/ (also /ʌm/) · UM
Русский
э-м / м-м — второй основной филлер, часто с оттенком неловкости
Type
primary
Register
neutral (speech only)
Variants
umm, ummm, erm (British spelling)

The other core American filler: a hum that ends with the lips closing, used like uh but typically before a longer or more delicate stretch of speech. It often carries a note of awkwardness, and a written um in a text message almost always means "I have a problem with what you just said."

  1. The deadline is, um, next Tuesday, I think.
  2. Um, is this seat taken?
  3. Um, you do know she's his sister, right?
  4. She started every answer with um and then finished it confidently.
  5. Can I, um, ask you something kind of personal?
  6. He texted back a single "um" and I knew I had messed up.
See also
uh, erm, hmm, well, like

Stalling and floor-holding phrases#

Full phrases that ask the listener to wait while you look something up, work something out, or find your place again.

as I was saying#

Pronunciation
/æz ˌaɪ wəz ˈseɪɪŋ/ · az eye wuz SAY-ing
Русский
как я уже говорил — возврат к прерванной мысли; может звучать как упрёк перебившему
Type
phrase
Register
neutral (can sound pointed after an interruption)
Variants
like I was saying, as I said, anyway, as I was saying

Takes the floor back after an interruption and signals that you are picking up the exact thread you were on. Said mildly it is just bookkeeping; said flatly to the person who cut you off, it is a rebuke with no rude word in it.

  1. As I was saying, the permits won't clear until March.
  2. Sorry, the dog got out — as I was saying, we'd need two more people to pull this off.
  3. Like I was saying, nobody on that call had actually read the contract.
  4. He waited for the laughter to die down. "As I was saying …"
  5. As I was saying before the fire drill, there are three options on the table.
See also
where was I, anyway, so anyway, hold on

bear with me#

Pronunciation
/ˈbɛr wɪð mi/ · BAIR with mee
Русский
потерпите немного / подождите чуть-чуть — просьба дать время разобраться
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
bear with me a second, bear with me here

A request for the listener's patience while you find a file, work something out, or talk your way through a half-formed idea. It is polite enough for a client call and casual enough for a friend.

  1. Bear with me — I know that invoice is in here somewhere.
  2. Bear with me a second while I pull up the slides.
  3. This is going to sound crazy, so bear with me.
  4. "Bear with me, folks, we're having a technical issue."
  5. Bear with me here, the math takes a minute to explain.
See also
hold on, just a second, hang on, hear me out

hang on#

Pronunciation
/ˈhæŋ ɑn/ · HANG-on
Русский
погоди / секунду — разг. «стой и подожди»
Type
phrase
Register
informal
Variants
hang on a sec, hang on a minute

A casual "stop and wait" — used to buy yourself a moment, to interrupt someone mid-flow, or to flag that something they just said does not add up.

  1. Hang on, let me grab a pen.
  2. Hang on a sec — I think that's my phone.
  3. Hang on, you said the store closes at six?
  4. "Ready to go?" "Hang on, I'm still tying my shoes."
  5. Hang on, I know exactly who you mean now.

hear me out#

Pronunciation
/ˌhɪr mi ˈaʊt/ · heer mee OUT
Русский
выслушай меня до конца — не перебивай, пока не договорю
Type
phrase
Register
informal
Variants
just hear me out, hear me out on this

Asks the listener to hold their objection until you have finished, and admits up front that the idea is going to sound bad at first. It is a floor-holding move rather than a stall: it buys the whole rest of your turn instead of a single beat.

  1. Hear me out — what if we drove instead of flying?
  2. I know how this sounds, but hear me out.
  3. "Absolutely not." "Just hear me out for thirty seconds."
  4. Hear me out on this: we cancel the party and take the money to the beach.
  5. He held up a hand and said, "Hear me out before you say no."
See also
bear with me, hold on, look, listen

hold on#

Pronunciation
/ˈhoʊld ɑn/ · HOHLD-on
Русский

Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
hold on a second, hold up, hold on there

A request to pause. It works both as a stall while you look something up and as a mild objection that stops the other person so you can catch up.

  1. Hold on, I'll transfer you to someone who can help with that.
  2. Hold on — did you just say she quit?
  3. Hold on a second, I want to write this down.
  4. "Hold on, I've got another call coming in."

how do I put this#

Pronunciation
/ˌhaʊ du aɪ ˈpʊt ðɪs/ · how do I PUT this
Русский
как бы это сказать — подбираю слова перед чем-то неловким или неприятным
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
how should I put this, how do I say this, how do I put it

A stall announcing that you are choosing your words because what comes next is awkward, critical, or delicate. It warns the listener that a blunt opinion or some bad news is on the way.

  1. How do I put this — you're not really a morning person, are you?
  2. The apartment is, how do I put this, cozy.
  3. How should I put this? He's not the strongest writer on the team.
  4. She's, how do I say this, very direct with clients.
  5. How do I put this delicately? Your car is not worth fixing.

just a second#

Pronunciation
/ˌdʒʌst ə ˈsɛkənd/ · just a SEK-uhnd

Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
just a sec, one sec, one second, give me a second, gimme a sec

A short promise that the pause will be brief, used when you have to finish something before you can answer or move on.

  1. Just a second — I'm almost done with this email.
  2. "Can you come here?" "One sec!"
  3. Just a sec, let me put you on speaker.
  4. Give me a second to think about how to answer that.

let me think#

Pronunciation
/ˌlɛt mi ˈθɪŋk/ · let me THINK
Русский
дай(те) подумать — я обдумываю ответ, а не увиливаю
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
let me think for a second, let me think about it

An explicit announcement that you are working out an answer rather than dodging the question. Said briskly it simply buys time; said slowly, it often signals that the answer is going to be no.

  1. Let me think — I want to say it was 2019?
  2. "What's the fastest route from here?" "Let me think for a second."
  3. Let me think about it and I'll get back to you tomorrow.
  4. Let me think. There were four of us at dinner, maybe five.
  5. Hmm, let me think how to explain this without the jargon.

let's see#

Pronunciation
/ˌlɛts ˈsi/ · lets SEE
Русский
так, посмотрим / сейчас — пока ищешь, считаешь или просматриваешь список
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
let me see, let's see here, let's see now

A stall used while you scan, count, or search — the verbal equivalent of running a finger down a list. It is the standard thing Americans say out loud while looking something up.

  1. Let's see — I've got Tuesday at ten or Thursday at two.
  2. Let me see, where on earth did I park?
  3. "How many people are coming?" "Let's see … eleven, counting the kids."

  5. So we need milk, bread, and, let's see, coffee.

that's a good question#

Pronunciation
/ˌðæts ə ˈɡʊd ˌkwɛstʃən/ · thats uh GOOD kwes-chun
Русский
хороший вопрос — тянет время и льстит спросившему; часто значит «понятия не имею»
Type
phrase
Register
neutral
Variants
good question, that's a great question, that's a fair question

Buys several seconds while you assemble an answer, and flatters the asker on the way in. Everyone recognizes what it is doing, which is why it carries a faint smell of the press conference and the job interview — and why a bare good question sometimes means "I have no idea."

  1. "How much would that cost us?" "That's a good question — let me run the numbers."
  2. Good question. I honestly don't know.
  3. She asked why the policy exists at all, and all I had was "that's a good question."
  4. "Why is it spelled that way?" "That's a great question, and the answer is that nobody knows."
  5. That's a fair question, and I'd rather answer it once I've seen the report.

let me think, let's see, how do I put this, bear with me

wait#

Pronunciation
/weɪt/ · WAYT
Русский
стой / погоди — резкая остановка разговора, обычно перед «до меня дошло»
Type
secondary
Register
informal (blunt when it cuts someone off mid-sentence)
Variants
wait a second, wait a minute, wait wait wait

A one-word brake on the conversation that stalls the other speaker while you catch up. In American speech it very often introduces a realization or a correction — wait rarely means "wait quietly," it means "something just registered."

  1. Wait — I think I left my keys in the car.
  2. Wait a second, weren't you supposed to be in Denver today?
  3. "It's the third one on the left." "Wait, coming from which direction?"
  4. Wait wait wait, start over from the part about the dog.
  5. Wait, so you two already knew each other?

  1. It's one of those — what's the word — collapsible chairs.

Openers, shifters, and closers#

Ordinary words doing a second job: starting a turn, changing the subject, checking that you are still being followed, and winding things down.

actually#

Pronunciation
/ˈæktʃuəli/ (also /ˈæktʃəli/) · AK-choo-uh-lee
Русский
на самом деле / вообще-то — поправка или неожиданный для собеседника факт
Type
secondary
Register
neutral

Flags that what follows corrects, contradicts, or gently surprises — either fixing what was just said or introducing a fact the listener would not expect. At the front of a sentence it can sound corrective, so Americans often cushion it with well or a smile.

  1. Actually, the meeting got moved to Thursday.
  2. "You've never been to Chicago, right?" "Actually, I lived there for two years."
  3. I thought it would be a nightmare, but it was actually pretty easy.
  4. Actually, on second thought, I'll have the salmon.
  5. That's not a bad idea, actually.
See also
I mean, well, right, basically, technically

alright#

Pronunciation
/ɔlˈraɪt/ · awl-RITE
Русский
ладно / так — закрывает предыдущее и открывает следующее; в конце фразы — «договорились?»
Type
secondary
Register
informal (the two-word spelling all right is the one for formal prose)
Variants
all right, aight, a'ight

A resetting marker that closes off what came before and starts the next thing — the sound of a teacher beginning class or a driver putting the car in gear. With rising pitch at the end of a sentence it becomes a check: alright? means "are we good so far?"

  1. Alright, let's get started.
  2. Alright, so the first thing you'll need is a screwdriver.
  3. "Alright, everybody up — we're leaving in ten minutes."
  4. We'll meet at the north entrance at six, alright?
  5. Alright, I'll let you go — say hi to your mom for me.
See also
okay, right, so, now

anyhow#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛniˌhaʊ/ · EN-ee-how
Русский

An older, folksier twin of anyway, used to drop a digression and get back to the point or to wrap a story up. Younger speakers use it far less often than anyway.

  1. Anyhow, that's how we ended up with three cats.
  2. It rained all weekend. Anyhow, how was your trip?
  3. I don't know what he was thinking. Anyhow, it's done now.
  4. Anyhow, I should let you go.

anyway#

Pronunciation
/ˈɛniˌweɪ/ · EN-ee-way

anyways

The workhorse of topic management: it ends a digression and returns to the main thread, or signals that you are winding the conversation down. Left hanging at the end of a turn, it means roughly "so that's that."

  1. Anyway, back to what I was saying about the budget.
  2. Anyway, it's getting late — I should head out.
  3. The food was mediocre and the service was slow. Anyway.
  4. Anyways, we ended up driving instead of flying.
  5. He may never forgive me. Anyway, I did what I had to do.

Pronunciation
/ˌkʌm tə ˈθɪŋk əv ɪt/ · kum tuh THINK uhv it

granted#

Pronunciation
/ˈɡræntɪd/ · GRAN-tid
Русский
допустим / надо признать — уступка перед возражением; дальше почти всегда «но»
Type
secondary
Register
neutral
Variants
granted, though, admittedly, I'll grant you that

Hands the other side one point before you argue against it, so that the rest of your position looks even-handed. It is nearly always followed, sooner or later, by a but, and standing alone as a full turn it is a grudging half-concession.

  1. Granted, the first hour is slow, but it's worth staying for the ending.
  2. He's the youngest person on the staff, granted, and he still outworks everyone.
  3. Granted, I only heard one side of it.
  4. "It's cheaper to fly out of the other airport." "Granted. It's also two hours away."
  5. Granted, we've blown a deadline before — that's exactly why I'm asking now.

Three filler jobs, all separate from the verb like. (1) A hesitation marker that holds the floor. (2) An approximator meaning "about."   3. And she was like, "You're seriously not coming?"

  5. He just, like, walked out in the middle of the meeting.
  6. I'm like ninety percent sure I locked the door.

Marks a statement as true by the strict letter of the rule while hinting that the spirit of the rule points somewhere else. It hedges and corrects at the same time, and aimed at someone else's sentence it is a small act of pedantry that Americans find funny and irritating in about equal measure.
