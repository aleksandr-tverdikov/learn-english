# Internet and text-native interjections

> The reactions that were born on a screen — acronyms promoted to words, spelled-out noises, and one-word verdicts that Americans type first and only later learn to say out loud.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These are the interjections whose native habitat is a keyboard. Some are abbreviations that hardened into words (*lol*, *smh*, *ngl*), some are attempts to spell a sound no dictionary had covered (*sksksk*, *uwu*, *bwahaha*), and some are ordinary English words that a game, a platform, or a viral clip reassigned to a brand-new job (*based*, *sus*, *mood*). Two quirks run through the whole set. First, length is meaning and capitals are volume: *lol* and *loooool* are effectively different words, and *YAS* is not the same message as *yas*. Second, pronunciation is genuinely unsettled — a single item may be read silently, spelled out as letters, said as a word, or expanded back into the phrase it stands for, and all four can be correct, so the pronunciation lines below often give more than one. Almost everything here is very informal, several items are profanity in disguise, and a few now carry political or generational baggage; the register line on each entry is the part to read twice.

## Laughter and amusement

*Written laughter is a spectrum, not a word: the spelling, the length, and the punctuation carry almost all of the meaning.*

### asdfghjkl

**Pronunciation:** *No settled pronunciation. It is a keysmash — the home row of a QWERTY keyboard struck all at once — and it is meant to be seen, not said. Read aloud it is a wordless flailing noise rather than a sequence of syllables.*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *asdfghjkl;*, *asjdkfhaskjdf*, *kjhsdfkjshdf*, *asdfghjkl* (any random string of letters works)

The typed equivalent of flapping your hands: a fistful of keyboard used to show that you are too delighted, overwhelmed, or mortified to produce actual words. It reads as affectionate hysteria and it never crosses into speech.

1. *asdfghjkl* he actually remembered my birthday
2. I just reread what I sent my boss *asdfghjkl*
3. "How was the concert?" "*ASDFGHJKL* I'm still shaking."
4. She replied with nothing but *asdfghjkl* and I knew she'd gotten the job.
5. *asjdkfhaskjdf* the puppy fell asleep inside my shoe
6. He sent a whole line of *asdfghjkl* instead of answering the question.

**See also:** [sksksk](#sksksk), [dead](#dead), [lmao](#lmao)

### bwahaha

**Pronunciation:** /bwɑˈhɑhɑ/ &middot; *bwah-HAH-hah*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal; jokey)
**Variants:** *mwahaha*, *muahaha*, *bwahahaha*, *mwahahaha*

A written villain's cackle — the laugh of someone who has just gotten away with something. It is almost always self-aware and comic; genuine gloating would use plain *haha*.

1. *Bwahaha*, I got the last parking spot right in front of the door.
2. I scheduled the meeting for 4:55 on a Friday. *Mwahaha.*
3. "I replaced all his ringtones with the Barney theme." "*Bwahahaha.*"
4. *Muahaha* — my sourdough starter has officially taken over the fridge.
5. He signed the email "your future landlord, *bwahaha*."
6. She typed *mwahahaha* right after telling me she'd already bought the tickets.

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [hehe](#hehe), [kek](#kek)

### dead

**Pronunciation:** /dɛd/ &middot; *DED*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *ded*, *deceased*, *I'm dead*, *dying*, *im deceased*

A one-word claim that something was so funny it killed you. It is a full reply on its own and it outranks *lol* by a wide margin; the deliberate misspelling *ded* softens it into something goofier and less dramatic.

1. *Dead.* I cannot believe he said that to her face.
2. The video of the cat fighting the printer — I'm *dead*.
3. "*Ded*," she wrote, and nothing else.
4. Autocorrect turned "thanks so much" into "tanks so mush" and I am *deceased*.
5. My brother's Halloween costume was a traffic cone. I'm still *dying*.
6. *Dead* at the fact that nobody told him the meeting was canceled.

**See also:** [lmao](#lmao), [rofl](#rofl), [ijbol](#ijbol), [asdfghjkl](#asdfghjkl)

### haha

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɑhɑ/, also /hɑˈhɑ/ &middot; *HAH-hah*, or *hah-HAH*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (neutral to informal)
**Variants:** *ha*, *hah*, *ha ha*, *hahaha*, *hahahaha*, *ahaha*, *bahaha*

Laughter, spelled out. Length is the entire message: a single *ha* is dry or sarcastic, *haha* is polite acknowledgment, and *hahahaha* is real amusement. Putting a space between the syllables (*ha ha*) almost always reads as sarcastic or tired.

1. *Haha*, that's actually a good point.
2. "I'll pay you back next week." "*Ha.*"
3. *Hahahaha* I can't believe you kept that receipt for eleven years.
4. He answered every one of her jokes with a flat *ha ha* and she stopped telling them.
5. *Ahaha* okay, you win, I was wrong about the pineapple thing.
6. Her text said only "*hahahahahaha*," which told me the interview had gone well.

**See also:** [hehe](#hehe), [heh](#heh), [lol](#lol), [lmao](#lmao)

### heh

**Pronunciation:** /hɛ/ &middot; *HEH*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *hehh*, *heheh*

A single dry laugh: acknowledgment that something was mildly amusing, with very little warmth behind it. Online it often means "I see what you did there," or marks amusement at your own expense.

1. *Heh.* I walked right into that one.
2. "You're the third person to make that joke today." "*Heh*, sorry."
3. He read the headline, said *heh*, and kept scrolling.
4. *Heh* — I'd completely forgotten I wrote that.
5. Her only response to the entire thread was a solitary *heh*.
6. *Heh*, yeah, I've made that same mistake more than once.

**See also:** [hehe](#hehe), [haha](#haha), [lol](#lol)

### hehe

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhihi/, also /ˈhɛhɛ/ &middot; *HEE-hee*, or *HEH-heh*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal; slightly childish or flirty)
**Variants:** *hehehe*, *heheh*, *hehey*

A small, mischievous giggle in writing. Unlike *haha* it carries a note of being pleased with yourself, being cute, or being a little bit naughty, which is why it lands so strangely in a work message.

1. *Hehe*, I already ate the last one.
2. I may have bought a third plant. *Hehe.*
3. "Guess who got us upgraded?" "*Hehehe*, you didn't."
4. She added a *hehe* to the end of the text and I couldn't tell if she was joking.
5. *Hehe* sorry, that was me who moved your chair.
6. He signs off every message with *hehe*, which took some getting used to.

**See also:** [haha](#haha), [teehee](#teehee), [heh](#heh), [uwu](#uwu)

### ijbol

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɪdʒboʊl/, also spelled out /ˌaɪˌdʒeɪˌbiˌoʊˈɛl/ &middot; *IJ-bohl*, or *eye-jay-bee-oh-EL*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; Gen Z, 2023 onward)
**Variants:** *IJBOL*, *ijbolll*, *I just burst out laughing*

"I just burst out laughing," coined by people who felt *lol* had stopped meaning anything at all. Its whole point is the claim that a laugh actually happened, out loud, without permission. It reads as very young, and outside a Gen Z group chat you will probably have to explain it.

1. *ijbol* he named the cat after his ex
2. "Did you see her face when the cake came out?" "*IJBOL*"
3. *ijbol* — my dad tried to pay for parking with his library card.
4. She typed *ijbol* and then, unprompted, explained what it stood for.
5. My roommate hung an oil portrait of himself over the couch. *ijbol.*
6. He replied *ijbol* to a message I had not meant to be funny in any way.

**See also:** [lol](#lol), [lmao](#lmao), [dead](#dead), [sksksk](#sksksk)

### kek

**Pronunciation:** /kɛk/ &middot; *KEK*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; gaming and imageboard slang, with far-right associations in some corners)
**Variants:** *topkek*, *lel*, *kekw*

A laugh inherited from online gaming, where one faction typing *lol* came out on the other faction's screen as *kek*. It means roughly what *lol* means, but it marks you as coming out of gaming or imageboard culture — and in some communities it carries political baggage worth knowing about before you type it.

1. *Kek*, he tried to solo the boss again.
2. "They banned him within the hour." "*Kek.*"
3. *Topkek* — the entire raid wiped on the tutorial.
4. He replied *kek* to the patch notes and logged off.
5. The chat filled with *kek* the second the streamer fell off the map.

**See also:** [lol](#lol), [lmao](#lmao), [pog](#pog), [gg](#gg)

### lmao

**Pronunciation:** /ləˈmaʊ/, also spelled out /ˌɛlˌɛmˌeɪˈoʊ/ &middot; *luh-MOW* (rhyming with *cow*), or *el-em-ay-OH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; mildly vulgar because of what it stands for)
**Variants:** *lmfao*, *lmaoo*, *lmaooo*, *LMAO*

A stronger *lol*, standing for "laughing my ass off." It signals genuine amusement rather than mere politeness, and because of the word hiding inside it, it does not belong in a message to a client. Extra o's mean more laughing.

1. *Lmao* he showed up to the potluck with a bag of ice.
2. "Did you see what she posted?" "*Lmao*, yes, immediately."
3. *Lmaooo* my mom just called the router "the internet box."
4. He texted *lmfao* and then, ten seconds later, "wait, are you serious?"
5. I said *lmao* out loud in a quiet office and had to explain myself.
6. Her entire review of the movie was one word: *lmao*.

**See also:** [lol](#lol), [rofl](#rofl), [dead](#dead), [omfg](#omfg)

### lol

**Pronunciation:** /lɑl/, also spelled out /ˌɛlˌoʊˈɛl/ &middot; *LOL* (rhyming with *doll*), or *el-oh-EL*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *LOL*, *lolol*, *lolz*, *lul*, *lolll*

Originally "laughing out loud," now the most worn-down word on the internet: in most messages it means nothing stronger than "this is light" or "I'm not mad." It softens a request, defuses a complaint, and takes the sting out of bad news. Written with a period after it (*lol.*) it turns cold and sarcastic.

1. *Lol*, no worries, take your time.
2. I got on the wrong train again *lol*.
3. "You're an hour late." "*Lol* sorry, traffic."
4. She ended a two-page argument with a single *lol*, which was the meanest part of it.
5. *Lolol* he's been asleep in that hammock since noon.
6. My grandmother thinks *lol* means "lots of love," so her condolence texts are alarming.

**See also:** [lmao](#lmao), [haha](#haha), [rofl](#rofl), [ijbol](#ijbol), [kek](#kek)

### rofl

**Pronunciation:** /ˈrɑfəl/ &middot; *ROFF-ul*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dated)
**Variants:** *ROFL*, *roflmao*, *rotfl*, *roflcopter*

"Rolling on the floor laughing" — an early-internet intensifier almost entirely replaced by *lmao*. Typing it now reads as either nostalgic or deliberately corny, and those are mostly the reasons people still do.

1. *Rofl*, my uncle captions every photo he sends with the word "photo."
2. "That's the worst pun you've ever made." "*Roflmao.*"
3. He said *rofl* without irony and the group chat went quiet.
4. *ROFL* — I haven't seen that word since middle school.
5. Somebody in the thread typed *roflcopter* and I felt about a hundred years old.

**See also:** [lmao](#lmao), [lol](#lol), [dead](#dead)

### sksksk

**Pronunciation:** *There is no vowel in it, so it has no IPA transcription as a word. It is a fast, voiceless clatter: a hissed s made with the tongue tip at the ridge behind the teeth, snapped off by a k closure at the back of the mouth, repeated three or four times without opening the jaw. It is typed far more often than it is ever said aloud.*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; associated with teenage girls, 2019 onward)
**Variants:** *sksksksk*, *ksksks*, *sksk*

A written stand-in for a stifled, embarrassed giggle — the sound of somebody slapping the table while laughing. It spread from TikTok and reads as young; on anyone past their mid-twenties it can sound like an impression of a teenager.

1. *sksksk* he waved back at someone who wasn't waving at him
2. "Did you actually send it?" "*sksksksk* yes"
3. She typed *sksksk* and then "I'm so embarrassed."
4. My niece answers everything with *sksksk* and I've stopped asking what it means.
5. *ksksks* the dog is wearing his little raincoat again

**See also:** [asdfghjkl](#asdfghjkl), [hehe](#hehe), [and I oop](#and-i-oop)

### teehee

**Pronunciation:** /ˈtiˌhi/ &middot; *TEE-hee*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal; deliberately cutesy, usually ironic)
**Variants:** *tee hee*, *teeheehee*, *teehee!*

A dainty little giggle, spelled out. It is far older than the internet — nineteenth-century novels are full of it — but online it is used almost entirely with an ironic wink, right after admitting to something mildly bad.

1. I ate the whole sleeve of cookies. *Teehee.*
2. *Teehee*, I might have told him you liked him.
3. "You spent how much?" "*Tee hee.*"
4. She admitted she'd been reading my drafts and then typed *teeheehee*.
5. He canceled the meeting and added a *teehee*, which did not help.

**See also:** [hehe](#hehe), [uwu](#uwu), [haha](#haha)

### xd

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛksˈdi/ &middot; *eks-DEE*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dated)
**Variants:** *XD*, *xD*, *xDD*, *xDDD*

A sideways face — X for eyes squeezed shut, D for a wide-open mouth — marking hard laughter. Once it was everywhere; now it reads as early 2010s, and most people who type it are doing so on purpose for exactly that reason.

1. He fell off the treadmill *xD*
2. "Nice save." "*XD* barely."
3. *xDD* the cat knocked the entire glass over while looking straight at me
4. She still ends her messages with *xD* and I find it weirdly comforting.
5. Typing *xD* these days is a choice, and he makes it daily.

**See also:** [lol](#lol), [uwu](#uwu), [owo](#owo)

## Shock, disbelief, and exasperation

*The initialisms in this group are read three different ways — as letters, as the phrase they stand for, or silently — and several of them are profanity wearing a disguise.*

### and I oop

**Pronunciation:** /ənd aɪ ˈup/ &middot; *and eye OOP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from drag and Black queer speech, 2019 onward)
**Variants:** *and I oop-*, *and I oop—*, *and i oop*

A phrase for the half-second in which something startling, awkward, or scandalous stops you mid-sentence. It comes from a viral video clip and is written with a trailing dash to show the thought never finishing. It is playful and never serious.

1. I said his ex was awful and then she walked in. *And I oop—*
2. "He brought his new girlfriend to the wedding." "*And I oop.*"
3. *And I oop*, I just sent that to the wrong group chat.
4. She started to defend him, saw the screenshots, and typed "*and I oop—*"
5. The waiter dropped an entire tray of glasses. *And I oop.*

**See also:** [oop](#oop), [whew chile](#whew-chile), [big yikes](#big-yikes)

### ffs

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛfˌɛfˈɛs/ &middot; *ef-ef-ESS*; more often read out as the phrase it stands for, *for fuck's sake*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *FFS*, *ffs.*

The typed form of "for fuck's sake" — flat, worn-out exasperation at something that should not still be happening. Three letters make it feel milder than the spoken phrase, but it is still profanity and it does not belong in a work channel.

1. *ffs* the printer is jammed again
2. "They rescheduled it for the third time." "*Ffs.*"
3. I locked my keys in the car. *FFS.*
4. He replied *ffs* and then spent an hour fixing my mistake anyway.
5. *ffs* — I just spent forty minutes on hold to be told to call back tomorrow.
6. She types *ffs* into the team chat roughly once per deploy.

**See also:** [fml](#fml), [jfc](#jfc), [smh](#smh), [for fuck's sake](08-profanity.md#for-fucks-sake)

### fml

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛfˌɛmˈɛl/ &middot; *ef-em-EL*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *FML*

"Fuck my life," tacked onto a message about a small personal disaster. It is self-pitying on purpose and almost always comic; attached to something genuinely tragic it reads as flippant.

1. Spilled coffee on my laptop ten minutes before the demo. *fml*
2. *FML*, I studied the wrong chapter.
3. "How's the move going?" "The truck won't start. *fml*"
4. He texted *fml* with a photo of his flat tire in the rain.
5. I told the story at dinner and everyone laughed, which — *fml* — was fair.
6. Locked out of my own account on my own birthday. *Fml.*

**See also:** [ffs](#ffs), [rip](#rip), [big oof](#big-oof), [welp](#welp)

### istg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪˌɛsˌtiˈdʒi/ &middot; *eye-es-tee-JEE*; usually read out as *I swear to God*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; swears on God, which some readers do mind)
**Variants:** *ISTG*, *stg*, *i stg*

"I swear to God," typed as a warning shot. It marks that your patience has run out — or, pointed the other way, that what you are about to say is true no matter how unlikely it sounds.

1. *istg* if he cancels one more time I'm not rescheduling
2. *ISTG* I put the keys on the hook. I watched myself do it.
3. "You're making this up." "*istg* I'm not."
4. She sent *stg* followed by a screenshot, which settled the argument.
5. *Istg* this dog understands English and is simply choosing to ignore me.

**See also:** [ffs](#ffs), [smh](#smh), [deadass](#deadass), [ong](#ong)

### jfc

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒeɪˌɛfˈsi/ &middot; *jay-ef-SEE*; usually read out as *Jesus fucking Christ*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar; also blasphemous to many readers)
**Variants:** *JFC*

"Jesus fucking Christ" compressed into three letters — stunned exasperation at something indefensible: a price, a headline, a decision. Two separate audiences take offense at it, the people who mind the swearing and the people who mind the name, so it is doubly unsafe at work.

1. *jfc* they want two hundred dollars for a checked bag
2. "He forwarded the whole thread to the client." "*JFC.*"
3. *jfc* — I just read the terms and conditions all the way through.
4. She looked at the repair estimate and typed exactly one thing: *jfc*.
5. *JFC*, we're forty minutes into this meeting and nobody has opened the agenda.

**See also:** [omfg](#omfg), [ffs](#ffs), [wtf](#wtf), [Jesus Christ](08-profanity.md#jesus-christ)

### omfg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊˌɛmˌɛfˈdʒi/ &middot; *oh-em-ef-JEE*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *OMFG*, *omfgg*

*Omg* with an expletive wedged into the middle: "oh my fucking God." It is for genuine shock, good or bad, and that one extra letter is doing real work — it lands several steps louder than *omg*.

1. *OMFG* she said yes
2. *omfg* the flight's canceled and the next one isn't until Tuesday
3. "They gave you the corner office?" "*OMFG*, right?"
4. He opened the box, typed *omfg*, and then called me.
5. *Omfg*, I have been looking for that book for six years.

**See also:** [omg](#omg), [jfc](#jfc), [wtf](#wtf)

### omg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊˌɛmˈdʒi/ &middot; *oh-em-JEE*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal, but widely accepted; mildly blasphemous to some religious readers)
**Variants:** *OMG*, *omgg*, *omgggg*, *omigod*, *ohmygod*

The workhorse of typed surprise: "oh my God" reduced to three letters. Unlike most of this list it has crossed back into speech, where Americans say *oh-em-JEE* out loud, and it stretches to cover delight, horror, and mild sympathy alike.

1. *OMG* I love this song
2. *omg* are you okay?
3. "They're having twins." "*OMG!*"
4. She said *oh-em-gee* out loud, which somehow made it funnier.
5. *Omgggg* I completely forgot we had tickets tonight.
6. My aunt puts *OMG* in every message, including sympathy cards.

**See also:** [omfg](#omfg), [oh my God](02-surprise.md#oh-my-god), [wtf](#wtf), [istg](#istg)

### oop

**Pronunciation:** /up/ &middot; *OOP*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *oop-*, *oop—*, *ooop*

The clipped cousin of *oops*, used when you have said too much, spotted something awkward, or want to bail out of a sentence you have already started. Written with a dash after it, it means the thought is being abandoned on purpose.

1. *Oop*—wrong chat, ignore that.
2. "Is that his wife?" "*Oop.*"
3. *Oop*, I think I just insulted the person who wrote it.
4. She started to say the number out loud and then went "*oop*."
5. *Oop*—never mind, I found it in my bag.

**See also:** [and I oop](#and-i-oop), [oops](05-fear.md#oops), [welp](#welp)

### smh

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛsˌɛmˈeɪtʃ/ &middot; *es-em-AITCH*; sometimes read out as *shaking my head*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *SMH*, *smdh* (shaking my damn head), *smfh* (vulgar), *smh my head* (a self-aware joke)

"Shaking my head" — disappointment rather than anger. It is the typed version of the silent head-shake you give when someone has done something foolish, and it is often aimed at the world in general rather than at the person you are writing to.

1. He forgot her birthday again. *smh*
2. *SMH* — four dollars for a bottle of water at the airport.
3. "He hit reply-all." "*smh*"
4. She read the group's excuses, typed *smdh*, and went to bed.
5. I asked for extra napkins and got exactly one. *Smh.*
6. *Smh my head*, he wrote, and I decided to let it go.

**See also:** [ffs](#ffs), [ok boomer](#ok-boomer), [cringe](#cringe), [big yikes](#big-yikes)

### tf

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtiˈɛf/ &middot; *tee-EF*; usually read out as *the fuck*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *TF*, *tf?*, *da fuq*

The tail end of *wtf*, cut loose and used on its own. Standing alone it is a blunt "what?"; slotted into a sentence it works as a rude intensifier in front of a question word.

1. *tf* was that noise
2. "He quit on a Tuesday with no notice." "*Tf?*"
3. *Tf* do you mean the deposit isn't refundable?
4. She read the invoice, typed *tf*, and forwarded it straight to accounting.
5. Who *tf* schedules a seven a.m. all-hands?

**See also:** [wtf](#wtf), [wth](#wth), [jfc](#jfc), [what the fuck](08-profanity.md#what-the-fuck)

### wtf

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʌbəljuˌtiˈɛf/, often clipped to /ˌdʌbtiˈɛf/ &middot; *dub-ul-yoo-tee-EF*, or *dub-tee-EF*; also read out as *what the fuck*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *WTF*, *wtff*, *wtfff*, *wtaf* (what the actual fuck)

"What the fuck," typed. It stretches across real bewilderment, outrage, and mild comic confusion, and it is common enough in casual writing that people forget what the middle letter stands for — which is precisely how it still gets someone in trouble at work.

1. *WTF* — my rent went up four hundred dollars.
2. *wtf* is this charge on my card
3. "He named the baby after his fantasy team." "*WTF.*"
4. She wrote *wtaf* in the margin of the contract, in pen.
5. I got home and the couch was gone. *Wtf.*
6. He typed *wtf* into the company-wide channel and had to apologize twice.

**See also:** [wth](#wth), [tf](#tf), [omfg](#omfg), [what the fuck](08-profanity.md#what-the-fuck)

### wth

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʌbəljuˌtiˈeɪtʃ/ &middot; *dub-ul-yoo-tee-AITCH*; also read out as *what the heck* or *what the hell*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *WTH*

The clean version of *wtf*, standing in for "what the heck" or "what the hell." People reach for it when they want the reaction without the word — writing to a coworker, a parent, or a group where children are reading along.

1. *WTH* — the store closed at four on a Saturday?
2. *wth* is going on with the weather this week
3. "She showed up with a llama." "*WTH?*"
4. He put *wth* in the family group chat instead of what he was actually thinking.
5. *Wth*, I paid that bill twice.

**See also:** [wtf](#wtf), [what the hell](08-profanity.md#what-the-hell), [tf](#tf)

## Wincing, sympathy, and defeat

*A cluster of comic condolences. Most of them are exaggerated on purpose — they are for secondhand embarrassment and small disasters, not for real grief. One of them, [womp womp](#womp-womp), is not sympathy at all and is meant to sting.*

### big oof

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɪɡ ˈʊf/ &middot; *big OOF*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *BIG oof*, *big ooooof*

An intensified [oof](03-pain.md#oof) — a wince on someone else's behalf at something that went badly wrong. The *big* does comic work, making the sympathy slightly theatrical, which is why it suits secondhand embarrassment far better than actual bad news.

1. He proposed at her sister's wedding. *Big oof.*
2. "I called my professor 'Mom.'" "*Big oof.*"
3. *Big oof* — the whole quarter's data was on that drive.
4. She watched him go in for a handshake while the other guy went in for a hug. *Big oof.*
5. They lost in the last four seconds. *Big oof.*

**See also:** [oof](03-pain.md#oof), [oof size large](#oof-size-large), [oofa doofa](#oofa-doofa), [big yikes](#big-yikes), [rip](#rip)

### big yikes

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɪɡ ˈjaɪks/ &middot; *big YIKES*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *BIG yikes*, *big yikers*

An intensified *yikes* for something that is not merely unfortunate but embarrassing to witness — a bad opinion said out loud, a decision nobody can walk back. It judges as much as it winces, and that is what separates it from [big oof](#big-oof).

1. He put that in a company-wide email. *Big yikes.*
2. "She brought up politics at the rehearsal dinner." "*Big yikes.*"
3. *Big yikes* — somebody dug up his posts from 2011.
4. I read the first paragraph of the review and thought, *big yikes*.
5. They spelled the guest of honor's name wrong on the cake. *Big yikes.*

**See also:** [yikes on bikes](#yikes-on-bikes), [yikes](04-disgust.md#yikes), [cringe](#cringe), [big oof](#big-oof)

### F

**Pronunciation:** /ɛf/ &middot; *EFF*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *f*, *press F*, *F in the chat*, *Fs in the chat*

A single letter used to pay respects, from a video game that instructed players to "press F to pay respects" at a funeral. Online it is almost always ironic — a one-character condolence for something trivial, like a dead phone battery or a canceled show.

1. *F.* My headphones just died on the first song.
2. "The vending machine ate my dollar." "*F*"
3. *Press F* for the office plant nobody watered.
4. The stream crashed at the final boss and the chat filled up with *F*.
5. He announced he'd been given the six a.m. shift and got twelve *F*s in reply.

**See also:** [rip](#rip), [big oof](#big-oof), [L](#l), [gg](#gg)

### L

**Pronunciation:** /ɛl/ &middot; *ELL*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; a straight insult when aimed at a person)
**Variants:** *big L*, *massive L*, *L + ratio*

A single-letter verdict meaning "loss" — a ruling that something or someone has failed. Aimed at a person it is an insult; said about yourself it is a rueful admission and nothing worse.

1. *L.* I studied for the wrong exam.
2. "They're getting rid of the free coffee." "Huge *L*."
3. He took an *L* on that trade and everybody knew it.
4. *Big L* for whoever decided the parking lot needed exactly one exit.
5. Someone answered his entire argument with a single *L* and left the thread.

**See also:** [W](#w), [F](#f), [ratio](#ratio), [rip](#rip), [big oof](#big-oof)

### oof size large

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʊf saɪz ˈlɑrdʒ/ &middot; *oof size LARJ*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; deliberately silly, dating to about 2018)
**Variants:** *oof size: large*, *oof size massive*

A joke escalation of *oof*, as though wincing came in shirt sizes. It is for a mishap worse than an ordinary *oof* but still funny, and the deliberately broken grammar is the entire point.

1. He hit reply-all on the email complaining about reply-all. *Oof size large.*
2. "She found the group chat." "*Oof size large.*"
3. *Oof size large* — they scheduled the layoffs for the day of the holiday party.
4. I locked myself out while taking the trash out in a bathrobe. *Oof size large.*
5. The typo made it onto the printed programs. All four thousand of them. *Oof size large.*

**See also:** [big oof](#big-oof), [oof](03-pain.md#oof), [oofa doofa](#oofa-doofa)

### oofa doofa

**Pronunciation:** /ˌufə ˈdufə/ &middot; *OO-fuh DOO-fuh*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; niche and jokey)
**Variants:** *oofa-doofa*, *ooffa doofa*

A nonsense elaboration of *oof*, in the same family as *oof size large*. It is small-circle slang rather than general usage, and it exists mainly to make wincing sound as ridiculous as possible.

1. *Oofa doofa*, that presentation did not go the way he planned.
2. "He asked her out in front of her boyfriend." "*Oofa doofa.*"
3. *Oofa doofa* — I just found the leftovers from three weeks ago.
4. She looked at the final score, said *oofa doofa*, and turned off the TV.
5. The bill came to two hundred dollars for four people. *Oofa doofa.*

**See also:** [oof size large](#oof-size-large), [big oof](#big-oof), [oof](03-pain.md#oof)

### rip

**Pronunciation:** /rɪp/, also spelled out /ˌɑrˌaɪˈpi/ &middot; *RIP* (rhyming with *lip*), or *ar-eye-PEE*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *RIP*, *ripp*, *rip bozo* (mocking)

The gravestone abbreviation "rest in peace," reused as a shrug for anything that has ended, broken, or gone wrong. Saying it as a word rather than as three letters is the tell that it is being used this way — actual deaths still get the letters, said plainly.

1. *RIP* my phone battery, we had a good run.
2. "They discontinued it." "*Rip.*"
3. *Rip* to everyone who has to drive home in this.
4. He spilled a full glass of water on the keyboard. *RIP.*
5. *Rip*, the coffee shop on the corner closed for good.
6. My weekend plans, and also my knee: *rip*.

**See also:** [F](#f), [big oof](#big-oof), [L](#l), [welp](#welp), [womp womp](#womp-womp)

### welp

**Pronunciation:** /wɛlp/ &middot; *WELP*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal (spoken and internet/text)
**Variants:** *wellp*, *welp.*

A clipped, resigned *well* — the sound of accepting a bad outcome and moving on. It closes a discussion instead of opening one, and online it usually stands alone as a complete message.

1. *Welp.* There goes the security deposit.
2. "The last train left twenty minutes ago." "*Welp.*"
3. *Welp*, I guess we're doing this the hard way.
4. He looked at the flooded basement, said *welp*, and went to get the shop vac.
5. *Welp*, that's the third time this month.
6. She read the rejection, typed *welp*, and opened the next application.

**See also:** [rip](#rip), [oop](#oop), [womp womp](#womp-womp), [meh](04-disgust.md#meh), [well](13-hesitation.md#well)

### womp womp

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwɑmp ˈwɑmp/ &middot; *wahmp WAHMP*
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken (very informal; mocking — it is an insult dressed as sympathy)
**Variants:** *womp*, *wamp wamp*, *womp womp womp*

The two descending notes of a sad trombone, spelled out. Typed at somebody else's misfortune it is not sympathy in any form: it means "that's too bad and I do not care," and it is one of the most dismissive things in this file. Turned on yourself it softens into a shrug at your own bad luck.

1. "They're out of the good donuts." "*Womp womp.*"
2. *Womp womp* — the sale ended at midnight and I checked at 12:40.
3. He complained that the tickets he decided not to buy had gone up in price. *Womp womp.*
4. I asked for an extension and got *womp womp* back, which I am choosing to read as a no.
5. My fantasy team lost by half a point. *Womp womp.*
6. Somebody posted *womp womp* under the layoff announcement and it did not go well for them.

**See also:** [rip](#rip), [big oof](#big-oof), [F](#f), [ratio](#ratio), [meh](04-disgust.md#meh)

### yikes on bikes

**Pronunciation:** /ˌjaɪks ɑn ˈbaɪks/ &middot; *yikes on BIKES*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; jokey)
**Variants:** *yikes on a bike*, *yikes on bikes with spikes*

A rhyming escalation of [big yikes](#big-yikes), for when something is so ill-advised that plain alarm will not cover it. The silliness of the rhyme keeps it light — it is for cringing, never for real danger.

1. He put that opinion in writing, with his name on it. *Yikes on bikes.*
2. "She's getting back together with him." "*Yikes on bikes.*"
3. *Yikes on bikes* — that's the third recall this year.
4. I read the comments section for about nine seconds. *Yikes on bikes.*
5. They named the new mascot without asking a single person. *Yikes on bikes.*

**See also:** [big yikes](#big-yikes), [yikes](04-disgust.md#yikes), [big oof](#big-oof), [cringe](#cringe)

## Agreement, verdicts, and call-outs

*One-word rulings. Each of these is a complete reply on its own, and several arrived in mainstream American English through African American English, drag culture, or online gaming — worth knowing when you decide whether to use them.*

### based

**Pronunciation:** /beɪst/ &middot; *BAYST*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; carries political baggage in some communities)
**Variants:** *based.*, *based af*, *based and redpilled* (far-right in origin)

A one-word compliment for saying something true regardless of who it annoys — praise for conviction rather than for correctness. It began as rapper slang meaning "being unapologetically yourself," was picked up by online political forums, and still smells faintly of that; between friends it is simply high praise for a bold opinion.

1. *Based.* Somebody finally said it.
2. "I don't answer emails after six." "*Based.*"
3. He refuses to own a smartphone, which is either *based* or exhausting.
4. *Based* — she stood up and left the meeting the second it ran past the hour.
5. The librarian who put up a sign about phone calls is deeply *based*.

**See also:** [facts](#facts), [W](#w), [cap](#cap), [cringe](#cringe)

### bet

**Pronunciation:** /bɛt/ &middot; *BET*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *aight bet*, *ight bet*, *ard bet*, *bet.*

"Agreed" or "you're on" — a one-word yes that also closes the conversation. Delivered flat it can mean the opposite: a skeptical "sure you will," daring the other person to actually follow through.

1. "Meet me there at seven?" "*Bet.*"
2. *Aight bet*, I'll bring the drinks.
3. "I'm going to beat your time." "*Bet.*"
4. He said he'd have it done by Friday and I said *bet*, and here we are.
5. "Don't tell her." "*Bet.*"
6. *Bet* — see you Saturday.

**See also:** [say less](#say-less), [facts](#facts), [ong](#ong), [deadass](#deadass)

### big mood

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɪɡ ˈmud/ &middot; *big MOOD*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *whole mood*, *entire mood*, *BIG mood*

An intensified [mood](#mood): "that is exactly, overwhelmingly how I feel." It is usually posted under an image or a complaint, and the *big* signals that the recognition is total rather than partial.

1. A photo of a dog refusing to get out of bed. *Big mood.*
2. "I've stared at this spreadsheet for an hour and changed nothing." "*Big mood.*"
3. *Big mood* — I have also eaten cereal for dinner twice this week.
4. She captioned the picture of the empty coffee pot "*whole mood*."
5. Somebody called October "the Sunday of months" and honestly, *big mood*.

**See also:** [mood](#mood), [same](#same), [this](#this), [facts](#facts)

### cap

**Pronunciation:** /kæp/ &middot; *KAP*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *capping*, *that's cap*, *stop capping*

A call-out meaning "that's a lie." It comes from African American English, where *capping* is boasting or exaggerating, and it is thrown at a claim that sounds too good to be true — usually as a friendly challenge rather than a real accusation.

1. "I ran a five-minute mile in high school." "*Cap.*"
2. That's *cap* and you know it.
3. He says he's never had a single cavity. *Cap.*
4. "I finished the whole book last night." "*Cap*, you were asleep by nine."
5. Stop *capping* — you did not talk to the manager.

**See also:** [no cap](#no-cap), [sus](#sus), [deadass](#deadass), [fr](#fr)

### cringe

**Pronunciation:** /krɪndʒ/ &middot; *KRINJ*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; a judgment on the person as much as the thing — genuinely unkind said to someone's face)
**Variants:** *cringey*, *so cringe*, *cringe af*, *cringe compilation*

A verb turned into a one-word verdict: "that is painfully embarrassing to look at." Online it is the exact opposite of [based](#based) — where *based* rewards someone for not caring what people think, *cringe* punishes them for it.

1. *Cringe.* Nobody asked for a forty-slide deck about synergy.
2. "He made a rap about the quarterly numbers." "*Cringe.*"
3. *Cringe* — the mascot has a catchphrase now.
4. She watched three seconds of the video, said *cringe*, and closed the tab.
5. Reading my own posts from high school is pure *cringe*.

**See also:** [based](#based), [big yikes](#big-yikes), [smh](#smh), [ok boomer](#ok-boomer)

### facts

**Pronunciation:** /fækts/ &middot; *FAKTS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal)
**Variants:** *facts.*, *big facts*, *straight facts*

A one-word "you're completely right," used as an entire reply. It is stronger than *same* — it endorses the claim rather than merely relating to it — and it is common in both texting and speech among younger Americans.

1. "Nobody should have to work before eight in the morning." "*Facts.*"
2. *Facts* — the second season was better.
3. He said the parking downtown is the real reason nobody goes. *Big facts.*
4. "You're only tired because you never sleep." "…*facts*."
5. *Facts.* That place has the best fries in the city and it isn't close.

**See also:** [based](#based), [no cap](#no-cap), [fr](#fr), [same](#same)

### ikr

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪˌkeɪˈɑr/ &middot; *eye-kay-AR*; usually read out as *I know, right?*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *IKR*, *ikrr*, *ikr!*

"I know, right?" — enthusiastic agreement with something the other person has just noticed, especially a shared complaint or a shared enthusiasm. It always responds to something; it can never open a conversation.

1. "This line hasn't moved in fifteen minutes." "*ikr*"
2. *IKR* — the trailer gave away the entire plot.
3. "He's somehow even taller in person." "*ikr!*"
4. She wrote *ikr* and then immediately started listing more examples.
5. "Why is everything scented like vanilla now?" "*ikr*, I've been saying this for years."

**See also:** [same](#same), [facts](#facts), [mood](#mood), [this](#this)

### mid

**Pronunciation:** /mɪd/ &middot; *MID*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dismissive — it is a put-down, not a neutral rating)
**Variants:** *mid.*, *that's mid*, *straight up mid*

A one-word verdict meaning thoroughly average and therefore not worth anybody's time. It stings more than "okay" does, because calling something *mid* denies it even the dignity of being bad.

1. *Mid.* I don't know why everyone was posting about it.
2. "How was the new place?" "Honestly? *Mid*."
3. The trailer looked incredible and the movie was *mid*.
4. He called my playlist *mid* and I have not recovered.
5. *Mid* — forty dollars for a sandwich and a handful of chips.
6. Everyone swears it's the best season. It's *mid*.

**See also:** [cringe](#cringe), [based](#based), [L](#l), [meh](04-disgust.md#meh)

### mood

**Pronunciation:** /mud/ &middot; *MOOD*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *mood.*, *me rn*

A one-word way of saying "that captures how I feel right now." It works as a caption or as a reply, and unlike *same* it usually points at a picture or a scene rather than at a claim.

1. A raccoon asleep in a trash can. *Mood.*
2. "I've reheated this same coffee three times." "*Mood.*"
3. *Mood* — the cat is lying face-down on the rug and so am I.
4. She posted a photo of a wilting plant with the caption "*mood*."
5. The dog watching the front door for someone who left an hour ago is a *mood*.

**See also:** [big mood](#big-mood), [same](#same), [this](#this)

### no cap

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈkæp/ &middot; *no KAP*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *no capp*, *fr no cap*, *on god no cap*

"I'm not lying" — an honesty tag attached to a claim that sounds exaggerated. It can lead the sentence or close it, and it can stand alone as agreement with somebody else.

1. That was the best sandwich I've had all year, *no cap*.
2. *No cap*, I studied for eleven hours straight.
3. "You actually liked it?" "*No cap*, it was great."
4. He's the fastest guy on the team, *no cap*.
5. *No cap* — I have never once used the fax machine.

**See also:** [cap](#cap), [fr](#fr), [ong](#ong), [deadass](#deadass)

### noice

**Pronunciation:** /nɔɪs/ &middot; *NOYSS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; jokey)
**Variants:** *noiiice*, *noice.*, *nice*

*Nice* respelled to catch an exaggerated cartoon accent — approval delivered with a wink. It marks small wins and lucky breaks, and the joke spelling keeps it from sounding sincere enough to be embarrassing.

1. Two for one on the tickets? *Noice.*
2. "I finished the whole thing before lunch." "*Noice.*"
3. *Noiiice* — the garage turned out to be free after seven.
4. He typed *noice* and gave the plan a thumbs up.
5. They upgraded our room for nothing. *Noice.*
6. *Noice*, that's the last form I had to sign.

**See also:** [pog](#pog), [W](#w), [gg](#gg), [based](#based)

### ok boomer

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊˈkeɪ ˈbumɚ/ &middot; *oh-KAY BOO-mer*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dismissive, and by now somewhat dated)
**Variants:** *OK boomer*, *okay boomer*, *ok zoomer* (the return volley)

A two-word dismissal of an opinion as hopelessly out of touch, aimed at older people or at anything that sounds like a lecture from one. It refuses to engage on purpose — that refusal is its whole force — which makes it genuinely rude rather than merely cheeky.

1. "Nobody wants to work anymore." "*Ok boomer.*"
2. He explained that we could all afford houses if we just cooked at home. *Ok boomer.*
3. *OK boomer* — the fax machine is not coming back.
4. She said *okay boomer* to her father and dinner ended early.
5. "In my day we simply called the office." "*Ok boomer.*"

**See also:** [cringe](#cringe), [smh](#smh), [L](#l), [based](#based)

### ratio

**Pronunciation:** /ˈreɪʃoʊ/ &middot; *RAY-shoh*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; hostile — it calls for a pile-on)
**Variants:** *ratioed*, *L + ratio*, *ratio + you fell off*, *get ratioed*

A one-word verdict that a post has drawn more replies than likes — meaning that nearly everyone who saw it stopped to disagree. Typed as a reply it is both a ruling and an invitation for strangers to join in, and that second half is what makes it an aggressive thing to say rather than a joke.

1. *Ratio.* Not one person in that thread agreed with him.
2. He posted a take about tipping and got *ratioed* inside of nine minutes.
3. "*Ratio + L*," somebody wrote, and four hundred people liked it.
4. *Ratio* — the replies are running about twenty to one against.
5. She said the sequel was better and the entire timeline *ratioed* her for it.
6. The brand account tried to argue with a sixteen-year-old and lost the *ratio* badly.

**See also:** [L](#l), [rekt](#rekt), [cringe](#cringe), [ok boomer](#ok-boomer), [womp womp](#womp-womp)

### rekt

**Pronunciation:** /rɛkt/ &middot; *REKT*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; gaming in origin; a taunt)
**Variants:** *get rekt*, *REKT*, *rekt lol*, *owned*, *pwned*

*Wrecked* respelled, called out at somebody who has just been beaten, corrected, or comprehensively answered. It came out of gaming trash talk and it still sounds like it: *get rekt*, aimed at the loser rather than said about them, is the rudest form and the most common one.

1. *Rekt.* He demanded a source and she posted three of them.
2. "I finished twenty minutes ahead of him." "*Get rekt.*"
3. The whole raid typed *rekt* in chat the second the boss went down.
4. *REKT* — that one screenshot ended the argument permanently.
5. He tried to correct the professor on her own paper and got *rekt* in front of the lecture hall.
6. Somebody answered his ten-paragraph post with a single receipt. *Rekt.*

### same

**Pronunciation:** /seɪm/ &middot; *SAME*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *same.*, *SAME*, *big same*, *sameee*

A one-word "me too," used as a complete reply. It is the mildest of the agreement words — it says you relate, not that you approve — and it fits a confession, a complaint, or a photo of somebody lying on the floor equally well.

1. "I've been putting off that email for three days." "*Same.*"
2. *Same* — I only came for the food.
3. "I can't hear a thing in movie theaters anymore." "*SAME.*"
4. She said she needs a nap after grocery shopping and I typed *same*.
5. *Same*, honestly. I've read that first chapter four times.

**See also:** [mood](#mood), [big mood](#big-mood), [ikr](#ikr), [this](#this)

### say less

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪ ˈlɛs/ &middot; *say LESS*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *say less fam*, *say no more*

"Stop explaining — I'm in." It agrees and ends the conversation in the same breath, and it flatters the other person: the idea was so obviously good that no further pitch is needed.

1. "There's a taco place two blocks from the hotel." "*Say less.*"
2. *Say less* — I'll be there at eight.
3. "I have an extra ticket if you—" "*Say less.*"
4. He heard "free food" and said *say less* before I could finish the sentence.
5. "It's the same director as the last one." "*Say less*, I'm going."

**See also:** [bet](#bet), [facts](#facts), [ong](#ong)

### sus

**Pronunciation:** /sʌs/ &middot; *SUSS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *suss*, *sussy*, *sus af*

Short for "suspicious." The clipping is old British slang, but the current American use comes straight from the game *Among Us*, where players accuse each other of being the impostor. It flags something that does not add up, almost always in a joking tone.

1. He says he was at the gym at two in the morning. *Sus.*
2. That is a very *sus* explanation for a missing cake.
3. "Why is the door already unlocked?" "*Sus.*"
4. *Sus* — the reviews were all posted on the same day.
5. She's been suspiciously quiet about the party, which is *sus* in itself.

**See also:** [cap](#cap), [smh](#smh), [oop](#oop)

### this

**Pronunciation:** /ðɪs/ &middot; *THIS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *^this*, *THIS*, *this.*, *all of this*

An ordinary pronoun used as a pointing finger: posted under someone else's comment, it means "everything above me is correct and I have nothing to add." The older form put a caret in front of it (*^this*) to point up the page.

1. "The problem isn't the meetings, it's the meetings about the meetings." "*This.*"
2. *THIS.* Somebody give this person a raise.

### W

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdʌbəlju/, often clipped to /dʌb/ &middot; *DUB-ul-yoo*, or *DUB*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *w*, *big W*, *dub*, *W take*

A single-letter verdict meaning "win" — approval of a decision, a purchase, or a person. It is the mirror image of [L](#l), and it can be assigned to somebody: a *W* for the coach means the coach did well.

1. Free parking downtown all December? *W.*
2. "They brought back the old menu." "Huge *W*."
3. That's a *W* for whoever finally fixed the elevator.
4. *Big W* — the library stays open until midnight during finals.
5. He took the *dub* and never let any of us forget it.

**See also:** [L](#l), [based](#based), [gg](#gg), [facts](#facts)

## Hype, emphasis, and swagger

*Words for insisting, celebrating, and turning the volume up. Several are oath-tags — ways of swearing you are telling the truth — and several came into general use from African American English or from streaming culture.*

### ayo

**Pronunciation:** /aɪˈjoʊ/, also /eɪˈjoʊ/ &middot; *eye-YOH*, or *ay-YOH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *ayoo*, *ayooo*, *ay yo*, *AYO*

An attention-grabbing *yo* with an extra beat on the front. Online it does one particular job: it stops the conversation because something has just gotten weird, inappropriate, or too good to let pass.

1. *Ayo*, that is not what I meant and you know it.
2. *AYO* — did anyone else see that price?
3. "So then I told his mother—" "*Ayo.*"
4. He typed *ayooo* the second the trailer dropped.
5. *Ayo*, whose car is parked across two spaces?

**See also:** [ayy](#ayy), [bruh](#bruh), [yo](10-attention.md#yo), [oop](#oop)

### ayy

**Pronunciation:** /eɪ/ &middot; *AY*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *ayyy*, *ayyyy*, *eyy*, *ey*, *ayy lmao*

A cheerful, drawn-out shout of recognition or triumph — the sound of pointing at somebody across a room. The more y's, the more pleased you are, and it doubles as a knowing nudge after a mild innuendo.

1. *Ayyy*, look who finally showed up.
2. "I got the promotion." "*AYYY!*"
3. *Ayy* — first one here, as usual.
4. He mentioned he had a date on Friday and the whole chat went *ayyyy*.
5. *Ayy lmao*, the pizza got here before the tracker even updated.

**See also:** [ayo](#ayo), [yas](#yas), [pog](#pog), [gg](#gg)

### damn son

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdæm ˈsʌn/ &middot; *dam SUN*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; mildly profane)
**Variants:** *dayum son*, *damn son where'd you find this*

An admiring or startled "wow," lifted from a viral video and used as a whole reaction. It carries exaggerated swagger — a compliment delivered like a punch on the shoulder — and despite the *son* it gets said to men and women alike.

1. *Damn son*, that is a serious sound system.
2. "I did the whole kitchen myself." "*Damn son.*"
3. *Damn son* — she ran it in under twenty minutes.
4. He walked in wearing the suit and somebody in the back said *damn son*.
5. Four hundred dollars for a set of tires? *Damn son.*

**See also:** [sheesh](08-profanity.md#sheesh), [ayy](#ayy), [pog](#pog), [damn](08-profanity.md#damn)

### deadass

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɛdˌæs/ &middot; *DED-ass*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; New York City in origin; mildly vulgar)
**Variants:** *dead ass*, *deadass?*, *deadazz*

"Seriously" — an honesty tag out of New York English, used to swear that you are not joking or, with a question mark, to ask whether the other person is. It can lead a sentence, close one, or stand entirely alone.

1. *Deadass*, I waited an hour and a half for a table.
2. "He quit on the spot." "*Deadass?*"
3. I'm *deadass* about the raccoon. It got into the car.
4. She said *deadass* three separate times before I believed her.
5. *Deadass* — that is the last time I trust a weather app.

**See also:** [ong](#ong), [fr](#fr), [no cap](#no-cap), [istg](#istg)

### fr

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛfˈɑr/ &middot; *ef-AR*; usually read out as *for real*, /fɚ ˈril/
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *frfr*, *fr fr*, *FR*, *fr?*

"For real" — either a tag confirming that you mean what you just said, or a one-word agreement with what somebody else said. Doubled to *frfr* it is more emphatic; with a question mark it asks whether the other person is serious.

1. *fr*, that's the best deal I've seen all year.
2. "This week has been eight days long." "*Fr.*"
3. *frfr* I have not sat down once since six this morning.
4. "He's moving to Alaska." "*Fr?*"
5. That test was harder than the practice one, *fr fr*.

**See also:** [ong](#ong), [no cap](#no-cap), [deadass](#deadass), [facts](#facts)

### gg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒiˈdʒi/ &middot; *jee-JEE*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; gaming in origin)
**Variants:** *GG*, *ggwp* (good game, well played), *gg ez* (a taunt), *gg go next*

"Good game," typed in chat at the end of a match. It leads two lives: the sincere one, a handshake between players, and the sarcastic one, where *gg* alone after a disaster means "well, that's over." The variant *gg ez* — "good game, easy" — is a deliberate insult to the losing side and gets people reported for it.

1. *gg* everyone, good match.
2. *ggwp* — that last round was close.
3. The server crashed with two minutes left. *GG.*
4. He typed *gg ez* and all five of us reported him.
5. I burned the second batch of cookies too. *gg.*
6. "We're down thirty with a minute to play." "*gg go next.*"

**See also:** [pog](#pog), [F](#f), [L](#l), [W](#w), [rekt](#rekt), [rip](#rip)

### lfg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛlˌɛfˈdʒi/ &middot; *el-ef-JEE*; usually read out as the phrase it stands for, *let's fucking go*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (vulgar)
**Variants:** *LFG*, *LFGGG*, *lfgo*, *let's go*

"Let's fucking go" — undiluted hype, typed at good news or in the last seconds before something starts. It is the loudest approval on this list, and the extra *g*s are the volume knob. Be careful in gaming spaces, where the same three letters have meant "looking for group" since long before this sense existed.

1. *LFG* the tickets just went on sale
2. "We got the apartment." "*LFGGG*"
3. *LFG* — three-day weekend and it isn't even supposed to rain.
4. He watched the last out, typed *lfg*, and threw his phone onto the couch.
5. The group chat went *LFG* over a rescheduled dentist appointment, which tells you what kind of week it had been.
6. She put *lfg* in a client channel and spent the rest of the afternoon apologizing for it.

**See also:** [pog](#pog), [ayy](#ayy), [yas](#yas), [W](#w), [gg](#gg)

### ong

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊˌɛnˈdʒi/ &middot; *oh-en-JEE*; usually read out as *on God*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *ONG*, *on god*, *on gd*

"On God" — a sworn-oath tag meaning you are telling the truth, and by extension a strong "absolutely." It makes the same move as *deadass* and *no cap*, and it can either open a claim of your own or agree with somebody else's.

1. *ong* that was the loudest concert I have ever been to
2. "You really walked the whole way?" "*Ong.*"
3. *On God*, I put the money in your account this morning.
4. He swore *ong* that he'd never seen the show and then quoted three lines from it.
5. "This is the best chicken in the city." "*ong*."

**See also:** [deadass](#deadass), [no cap](#no-cap), [fr](#fr), [istg](#istg)

### periodt

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɪriət/ &middot; *PEER-ee-ut* — there is no /dt/ cluster in it; the *d* of *period* is simply replaced by a hard, fully released /t/
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from Black and drag culture, and it can read as caricature in a mouth that is neither)
**Variants:** *period*, *perioduh*, *and that's on periodt*, *pointe*

*Period* with an extra *t* stuck on the end to make the full stop audible. It closes a statement and refuses any further discussion: what was just said is final and correct.

1. She was the best part of the entire movie, *periodt*.
2. Nobody should have to work on Christmas Eve. *Periodt.*
3. "The blue one is better." "*Periodt.*"
4. My grandmother makes the best sweet potato pie in this state, *and that's on periodt*.
5. *Periodt* — that meeting could have been an email.

**See also:** [facts](#facts), [say less](#say-less), [sis](#sis), [chile](#chile)

### pog

**Pronunciation:** /pɑɡ/ &middot; *POG*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; Twitch and gaming)
**Variants:** *poggers*, *PogChamp*, *pog champ*, *pogu*, *poggies*

An exclamation of hype out of Twitch chat, named for an emote of a streamer pulling a shocked face. It means "that was awesome" and floods a chat the instant something impressive happens; outside gaming spaces it sounds like a foreign language.

1. *Pog*, he hit that shot from half court.
2. "They just announced the sequel." "*POGGERS.*"
3. *Pog* — free shipping on everything this weekend.
4. The chat scrolled past a thousand *poggers* in about four seconds.
5. She got the very last ticket. *Pog.*

**See also:** [gg](#gg), [hmmge](#hmmge), [ayy](#ayy), [W](#w)

### slay

**Pronunciation:** /sleɪ/ &middot; *SLAY*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from Black and drag culture)
**Variants:** *slayyy*, *slay queen*, *you slayed*, *slayed*

A shout of praise for someone who has done something exceptionally well or looked exceptionally good. It came out of Black and drag ballroom culture, moved through pop music and then TikTok, and today the extra *y*s or a deadpan delivery do most of the work of separating real praise from a joke.

1. That outfit? *Slay.*
2. "I got the promotion." "*Slayyy.*"
3. She sent the presentation at midnight and it was flawless — *slay*.
4. *Slay* — you actually made the reservation for once.
5. He captioned a photo of his own lasagna *slay*, and honestly, fair.
6. *Slay*, I could never have parallel parked into that spot.

**See also:** [yas](#yas), [periodt](#periodt), [pog](#pog), [W](#w)

### yas

**Pronunciation:** /jæs/ &middot; *YASS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from Black and Latino ballroom and drag culture)
**Variants:** *yaas*, *yasss*, *yaaas*, *yassss*, *yas queen*

An extended, delighted *yes* — not an answer to a question but a cheer at somebody doing something well. It came out of ballroom and drag culture, went mainstream through television, and the extra letters are the enthusiasm.

1. *Yasss*, that jacket looks incredible on you.
2. "I finally quit." "*YAS.*"
3. *Yas queen* — go get your money.
4. She posted the graduation photo and the replies were nothing but *yaaas*.
5. *Yas*, they're playing the good stuff tonight.

**See also:** [ayy](#ayy), [periodt](#periodt), [sis](#sis), [pog](#pog)

### yeet

**Pronunciation:** /jit/ &middot; *YEET*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; jokey)
**Variants:** *YEET*, *yeeet*, *yeetus*

A shout that accompanies throwing something hard, or any sudden, committed, faintly reckless action. It started as a dance and a basketball celebration and has since become a verb as well; as an interjection it is pure comic energy.

1. *Yeet!* He threw the crumpled paper across the room and missed the trash can.
2. I found the old textbooks and — *yeet* — they went straight into the recycling.
3. "Just send the email." "*Yeet.*" And he sent it.
4. *YEET* — that's the last box on the truck.
5. She tossed the keys over the fence, yelled *yeet*, and immediately regretted it.

**See also:** [ayy](#ayy), [pog](#pog), [bet](#bet)

### yolo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjoʊloʊ/ &middot; *YOH-loh*; occasionally spelled out as *why-oh-el-oh*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dated 2012)
**Variants:** *YOLO*, *yolo!*, *you only live once*

"You only live once" — offered as the justification for a decision the speaker already knows is unwise. It exploded in 2012, was worn out inside a year, and now shows up almost entirely as a joke about its own era.

1. I bought the expensive one. *Yolo.*
2. "You're getting dessert too?" "*YOLO.*"
3. He booked the flight for tomorrow morning — *yolo*, I guess.
4. *Yolo* — I said yes before I checked the price.
5. She wrote *yolo* in the group chat and everyone groaned at once.
6. We took the long way home for no reason at all. *Yolo.*

**See also:** [bet](#bet), [yeet](#yeet), [welp](#welp), [based](#based)

## Address terms used as interjections

*Words that used to mean "brother," "sister," "child," or "family" and now mean nothing of the kind. Each has hardened into a pure reaction, and none of them cares about the gender or the age of whoever it is aimed at.*

### bro

**Pronunciation:** /broʊ/ &middot; *BROH*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *broo*, *brooo*, *bro.*, *my brother in Christ*

A term of address that has become a reaction all by itself. Standing alone with a period after it, *bro.* is flat disbelief or disappointment at what somebody has just said or done, and it is now used regardless of who is on the other end.

1. *Bro.* You told her I said that?
2. "I ate the leftovers you labeled." "*Bro.*"
3. *Broo* it's forty degrees and he's wearing shorts
4. He read the group's plan, typed *bro*, and left the chat.
5. *Bro*, that is the fourth time this week.

**See also:** [bruh](#bruh), [sis](#sis), [ayo](#ayo), [smh](#smh)

### bruh

**Pronunciation:** /brʌ/ &middot; *BRUH*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *bruhh*, *bruuh*, *bruhhh*, *brah*

*Brother* worn down to one syllable and emptied of any meaning as a form of address. It is a complete reaction on its own — flat, deadpan disbelief at something stupid — and it is aimed at men, women, animals, and inanimate objects with equal indifference.

1. *Bruh.* The meeting was canceled two hours ago.
2. "I locked the keys in the running car." "*Bruh.*"
3. *Bruh* why is there a line at the self-checkout
4. She sent one single *bruh* and I understood the entire situation.
5. *Bruhhh* — they scheduled the exam for the day after the holiday.
6. He said *bruh* so many times during his own story that nobody could follow it.

**See also:** [bro](#bro), [smh](#smh), [tf](#tf), [sis](#sis)

### chile

**Pronunciation:** /tʃaɪl/, often drawn out as /ˈtʃaɪəl/ &middot; *CHYLE*, or *CHY-uhl*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from African American English and drag culture, and it reads as caricature when a speaker outside those communities puts it on)
**Variants:** *chi-ile*, *chiiile*, *chile anyways*

*Child* spelled without the *d* to catch a particular Southern and African American pronunciation, used as an exclamation of exasperated commiseration — the equivalent of "oh, honey." It typically introduces gossip or a resigned change of subject (*chile, anyways*).

1. *Chile*, do not get me started on that man.
2. "She's redoing the kitchen again." "*Chiiile.*"
3. *Chile* — I have been on hold since nine o'clock this morning.
4. Anyway, *chile*, the wedding was a disaster from the very first minute.
5. She looked at the bill, said *chile*, and put her card back in her wallet.

**See also:** [whew chile](#whew-chile), [sis](#sis), [periodt](#periodt), [and I oop](#and-i-oop)

### dude

**Pronunciation:** /dud/ &middot; *DOOD*; drawn out as *duuude* /duːd/
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; also spoken)
**Variants:** *duuude*, *dude.*, *dood*, *dude!*

An address term emptied of meaning decades ago and now a complete reaction on its own. Standing alone with a period, *dude.* is flat disbelief or disappointment; stretched into *duuude* it flips to awe or delight. It is older and more spoken than *bruh*, but it does exactly the same job in a text thread.

1. *Dude.* You cannot park there.
2. "I got us front-row seats." "*Duuude.*"
3. *Dude* — it took me nine hours to get home from the airport.
4. He answered the entire paragraph with one *dude* and went to bed.
5. *Dude*, I have told you about the smoke alarm three times.
6. She looked at the dent, said "*dude*," and handed the keys back.

**See also:** [bruh](#bruh), [bro](#bro), [sis](#sis), [smh](#smh)

### fam

**Pronunciation:** /fæm/ &middot; *FAM*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text and spoken slang (very informal; from African American English)
**Variants:** *fam.*, *famalam*, *my fam*

*Family* clipped to one syllable and drained of any family meaning at all. It brackets a sentence aimed at a friend — front or back — and standing alone with a period it does what *bruh* does: flat disbelief that this needed saying out loud.

1. *Fam.* You cannot wear that to an interview.
2. "I've been up since four." "*Fam.*"
3. *Fam*, the line is already around the block.
4. He ended every message with *fam* until somebody finally asked him not to.
5. Listen, *fam*, I am not driving out there twice in one day.
6. *Fam* — who ate the container with my name written on it?

**See also:** [bruh](#bruh), [bro](#bro), [sis](#sis), [dude](#dude), [say less](#say-less)

### sis

**Pronunciation:** /sɪs/ &middot; *SISS*
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from Black and drag culture)
**Variants:** *sissss*, *sis.*, *not the sis*

The counterpart to *bruh*: an address term that has hardened into a reaction. Standing alone, *sis.* is gentle disbelief, a warning, or a way of saying "you and I both know better," and like *bruh* it is now pointed at anyone at all.

1. *Sis.* He is not going to text you back.
2. "I'm thinking about calling him." "*Sis.*"
3. *Sis* — check the receipt before you leave the counter.
4. She replied with one word, *sis*, and I reconsidered the whole plan.
5. *Sis*, that is a lot of money for a candle.

**See also:** [bruh](#bruh), [bro](#bro), [chile](#chile), [periodt](#periodt)

### whew chile

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhwju ˈtʃaɪl/, also /ˌfju ˈtʃaɪl/ &middot; *hwyoo-CHYLE*, or *fyoo-CHYLE*
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; from Black and drag culture, and it reads as caricature when a speaker outside those communities puts it on)
**Variants:** *whew chile the ghetto*, *whew chile!*, *woo chile*

A sigh and a commiseration rolled together, used to introduce a mess — a long story, a bad situation, or somebody else's drama. The longer form, *whew chile the ghetto*, is a stock line from a viral clip and means roughly "what an absolute disaster."

1. *Whew chile*, you should have seen the state of that apartment.
2. "How was Thanksgiving?" "*Whew chile.*"
3. *Whew chile the ghetto* — they lost my luggage on a direct flight.
4. She opened the story with *whew chile* and I knew it would run an hour.
5. *Whew chile*, this week has been something else entirely.

**See also:** [chile](#chile), [whew](03-pain.md#whew), [and I oop](#and-i-oop), [sis](#sis)

## Cutesy, roleplay, and stream chat

*Typed faces and chat-only coinages. These are read with the eyes rather than the mouth, and outside their home communities they are either meaningless or a joke about themselves.*

### hmmge

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhʌmdʒ/ &middot; *HUMJ* — but it is almost never spoken; it is an emote name, typed and read
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; niche, Twitch chat)
**Variants:** *Hmmge*, *HMMGE*

A Twitch emote name built from *hmm* plus the *-ge* ending that chat attaches to emote names, as in *Sadge* and *Pepege*. It signals exaggerated, performative thinking — "let me consider that very hard" — usually with a note of sarcasm. Outside a stream chat it will not be understood at all.

1. *Hmmge* — so the fix is to turn it off and then on again.
2. He asked chat whether he should buy the expensive sword and got a wall of *Hmmge*.
3. "What if we just skipped the tutorial?" "*Hmmge.*"
4. The stream went quiet and somebody typed *hmmge* into the void.
5. *Hmmge*, that is certainly one way to solve the puzzle.

**See also:** [pog](#pog), [hmm](13-hesitation.md#hmm), [gg](#gg)

### owo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊwoʊ/ &middot; *OH-woh*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; furry and anime fandom; usually ironic, and the stock phrase *owo what's this* is a sexual joke in origin)
**Variants:** *OwO*, *ovo*, *owo whats this*

A text face — two wide eyes and a small round mouth — showing surprise, curiosity, or exaggerated innocence at having noticed something. It is the alert cousin of *uwu*, and it is typed ironically far more often than sincerely. The set phrase *owo what's this* came out of a furry-fandom joke about noticing an arousal, so it carries innuendo even when the person typing it has no idea.

1. *owo* what's in the box
2. "There's an extra slice." "*OwO*"
3. He noticed the new hire's mechanical keyboard and typed *owo*.
4. *OwO* — somebody left an entire cake in the break room.
5. She sent a photo of the dessert menu and I answered with a single *owo*.

**See also:** [uwu](#uwu), [xd](#xd), [hehe](#hehe)

### rawr

**Pronunciation:** /rɔr/, also /rɑr/ &middot; *RAWR* (one syllable)
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; dated 2000s, now ironic)
**Variants:** *rawr xd*, *rawrr*, *RAWR*

A written cartoon roar, used flirtatiously or playfully rather than as any kind of threat. It belongs to the mid-2000s scene and emo internet — the era of "rawr means I love you in dinosaur" — so anyone typing it now is being ironic, nostalgic, or talking to a small child.

1. *Rawr*, I'm a dinosaur, apparently.
2. She signed the message *rawr xd* and I felt eleven years old again.
3. "*Rawr*," he texted, which I chose not to respond to.
4. My old screen name had *rawr* in it and I would like that scrubbed from the record.
5. He put on the costume, said *rawr*, and the toddler shrieked with joy.
6. *RAWR* — the cat has decided she owns the couch now.

**See also:** [uwu](#uwu), [owo](#owo), [xd](#xd), [teehee](#teehee)

### uwu

**Pronunciation:** /ˈuwu/ &middot; *OO-woo*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; anime and furry fandom; often ironic)
**Variants:** *UwU*, *uwuu*, *uwu owo*

A text face showing a small, blissful, closed-eyed smile — cuteness, contentment, or affection. It began in anime fandom and is now used at least half the time as a joke about being cutesy rather than as actual cuteness.

1. *uwu* he made me a lunch and put a note in it
2. "The puppy fell asleep on my foot." "*uwu*"
3. He answered a stern email with *uwu* and had to explain himself in person.
4. *UwU* — thank you for remembering.
5. *uwu* my roommate left the porch light on so I wouldn't come home to a dark house

**See also:** [owo](#owo), [teehee](#teehee), [hehe](#hehe)

## Hedges used interjectionally

*Two-, three-, and four-letter opinion flags. Grammatically they are stripped-down clauses, but they behave like interjections: they attach to a sentence without joining it, and they can stand at either end. These are also the only items in this file that survive in professional writing — several are ordinary in a work channel.*

### fwiw

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛfˌdʌbəljuˌaɪˈdʌbəlju/ &middot; *ef-dub-ul-yoo-eye-DUB-ul-yoo*; almost always read out as the phrase instead, *for what it's worth*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal; ordinary in professional chat and email)
**Variants:** *FWIW*, *for what it's worth*

"For what it's worth" — a hedge that discounts the opinion attached to it before anyone else gets the chance. It is the most office-safe item in this file, and in email it usually introduces a correction the writer would rather not turn into an argument.

1. *fwiw*, the same thing happened to us last quarter.
2. The second vendor came in cheaper, *fwiw*.
3. "Did anybody actually test this?" "*FWIW*, I ran it twice this morning."
4. She put *fwiw* in front of the correction and nobody's feelings got hurt.
5. *Fwiw* — I would not book that hotel a second time.
6. He writes *fwiw* before opinions he then defends for a solid hour.

**See also:** [imo](#imo), [tbh](#tbh), [iirc](#iirc), [ngl](#ngl)

### iirc

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪˌaɪˌɑrˈsi/ &middot; *eye-eye-ar-SEE*; usually read out as *if I recall correctly*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal; ordinary in professional chat)
**Variants:** *IIRC*, *if I recall correctly*, *if I remember right*

"If I recall correctly" — a memory hedge that flags the fact behind it as believed rather than checked. It protects the writer, but in a technical thread it works as a courtesy too: it tells everybody else which claim to go verify.

1. *iirc* the deadline moved to the eleventh.
2. They stopped making that model around 2019, *iirc*.
3. "Who signed off on it?" "*IIRC* it was Dana, but don't quote me."
4. He said *iirc* and then, as usual, turned out to be exactly right.
5. *Iirc* — and it has been a few years — the garage is free after six.
6. She wrote *iirc* in front of a number she had already looked up twice.

**See also:** [fwiw](#fwiw), [imo](#imo), [tbh](#tbh), [ngl](#ngl)

### imo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪˌɛmˈoʊ/ &middot; *eye-em-OH*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal, but common in professional chat)
**Variants:** *IMO*, *imho* (in my humble opinion), *imnsho* (in my not so humble opinion)

"In my opinion," used as a tag that marks the sentence around it as a personal view rather than a fact. Dropped at the end of a message it behaves like an interjection — a small step back from the claim you have just made.

1. The second album is better, *imo*.
2. *IMO* we should ship it now and fix the copy later.
3. "Is it worth the money?" "Not really, *imo*."
4. He adds *imho* to statements that are not humble in any way.
5. *Imo* — and I could be wrong here — the deadline was never realistic.

**See also:** [tbh](#tbh), [ngl](#ngl), [fwiw](#fwiw), [iirc](#iirc), [I mean](13-hesitation.md#i-mean)

### jk

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdʒeɪˈkeɪ/ &middot; *jay-KAY*; also read out as *just kidding*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *JK*, *j/k*, *jkjk*, *jk unless*, *just kidding*

"Just kidding," tacked onto the end of a message to pull the sting back out of it. It is a retraction far more often than a real joke marker: doubled to *jkjk* it usually means the writer meant every word, and *jk unless…* means they meant it and are hoping you say yes.

1. You're buying, right? *jk*
2. I'm quitting and moving into the woods. *jk.* Mostly.
3. "Bring me a coffee on your way in — *jk*, don't go out of your way."
4. He asked if she wanted to get dinner sometime, *jk unless*, and she said yes.
5. She added *jkjk* at the end, which is exactly how I knew she was serious.
6. *j/k* — the file is fine, I had opened the wrong one.

**See also:** [lol](#lol), [ngl](#ngl), [tbh](#tbh), [imo](#imo)

### ngl

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɛnˌdʒiˈɛl/ &middot; *en-jee-EL*; usually read out as *not gonna lie*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal)
**Variants:** *NGL*, *ngl tho*, *not gonna lie*

"Not gonna lie" — a warning that a small, slightly awkward confession is on its way. It softens an admission you suspect the other person will find surprising or unflattering, and it almost always sits at the front of the sentence.

1. *ngl*, I forgot we had a meeting today.
2. *NGL* that was better than I expected.
3. "How was the party?" "*Ngl*, I left after twenty minutes."
4. He wrote *ngl tho* and then admitted he had never read the book.
5. *Ngl*, I've been putting this off since March.

**See also:** [tbh](#tbh), [imo](#imo), [jk](#jk), [deadass](#deadass), [fr](#fr)

### tbh

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtiˌbiˈeɪtʃ/ &middot; *tee-bee-AITCH*; usually read out as *to be honest*
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** internet/text (informal)
**Variants:** *TBH*, *tbhh*, *to be honest*

"To be honest" — a flag that an unvarnished opinion is coming, usually one a little less enthusiastic than the other person is hoping for. It can open the sentence or close it, and it almost never means the speaker was lying beforehand.

1. *tbh* I don't think anyone actually read the attachment.
2. It wasn't that good, *tbh*.
3. "What did you think of the house?" "*Tbh*, the kitchen sold me."
4. She said *tbh* and I braced myself.
5. *TBH* — I have never once used the second monitor.

**See also:** [ngl](#ngl), [imo](#imo), [fwiw](#fwiw), [iirc](#iirc), [fr](#fr)
