# Disagreement, refusal, and skepticism

> The words Americans reach for to say no, to shut a request down, and to signal that they do not believe a word of what they just heard.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

This category runs from a bare grunt (*uh-uh*) through the polite decline (*no thanks*) all the way to a whole museum of words that mean "nonsense" (*poppycock, malarkey, horsefeathers*). Two things run through all of it. First, several of the sharpest items here are words of **agreement** — *sure, right, okay, uh-huh* — turned inside out by intonation alone; said flat, slow, and falling they mean the opposite of what they say, which is why writers stretch the spelling (*riiight, suuure*) or drop in a cold period (*Okay.*) to make the sarcasm visible on the page. Second, the "that's nonsense" family is largely built from minced substitutes for barnyard vulgarity (*horsefeathers, bull hockey, baloney*), so the register ladder is unusually long: pick the wrong rung and you either sound like a cartoon grandfather or get a call from HR. That family is not purely a museum, either — *cap* does the same job for speakers under thirty that *baloney* did for their great-grandparents.

## Saying no — plain negation and refusal

### hardly

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɑrdli/ &middot; *HARD-lee*
**Русский:** отнюдь / куда там — «вовсе нет», сухое возражение на явное преувеличение
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *oh, hardly*

Standing alone, *hardly* rejects a claim as far off the mark — it means "not remotely," not "barely." It is a dry, slightly bookish way to disagree, and it usually answers something the speaker considers an overstatement.

1. "Was the movie scary?" "*Hardly.* I fell asleep."
2. "You must have been furious." "*Hardly.* I thought it was funny."
3. "So he's an expert?" "*Hardly.* He read one article on a plane."
4. "That's got to be the hardest class in the department." "*Hardly* — the final is open-book."
5. "That must have cost a fortune." "*Hardly* — forty bucks at an estate sale."

**See also:** [doubtful](#doubtful), [I doubt it](#i-doubt-it), [no](#no)

### I don't think so

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪ doʊnt ˈθɪŋk soʊ/ &middot; *eye dohnt THINGK soh*
**Русский:** вряд ли / не думаю — мягкое «нет»; сказанное веско — твёрдый отказ с предупреждением
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *I think not*, *I really don't think so*, *uh, I don't think so*

Two different jobs depending on delivery. Said lightly it is a hedged *no* — polite, provisional, easy to argue with. Said flat and slow with the weight dropped on *think*, it is a hard refusal with a warning inside it: the speaker has caught you trying something. *I think not* is the stiff, jokey-formal cousin.

1. "Is he coming with us?" "*I don't think so*, he's got class."
2. "Do we need to bring anything?" "*I don't think so.*"
3. He reached for the last of the fries. "*I don't think so.*"
4. "I'll just add the charge to your account." "*I don't think so.* We agreed on a flat rate."
5. "You'll let this one slide, right?" "*I don't think so.*"

**See also:** [not really](#not-really), [I doubt it](#i-doubt-it), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### I'm good

**Pronunciation:** /aɪm ˈɡʊd/ &middot; *eyem GUUD*
**Русский:** мне не надо, спасибо — мягкий отказ от предложенного: «у меня всё есть»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *nah, I'm good*, *I'm all set*, *I'm fine*

A soft, friendly refusal of something offered — food, a drink, a ride, help with the boxes. It declines without ever using the word *no*, and it implies that the speaker already has what they need, so it lands as easygoing rather than blunt.

1. "Want another slice?" "*I'm good*, thanks."
2. "I can drive you to the airport." "*I'm good* — I already booked a car."
3. "You need a hand with those boxes?" "*Nah, I'm good.*"
4. "Should I order you a coffee?" "*I'm good.* I've had three already."
5. "Can I get you anything else?" "*I'm all set*, thank you."

**See also:** [no thanks](#no-thanks), [nah](#nah), [pass](#pass)

### mm-mm

**Pronunciation:** no settled IPA — a two-beat hum with the lips closed and a glottal catch between the beats, roughly /ˈm̩ʔm̩/, pitch falling from high to low &middot; *MM-mm*
**Русский:** м-м / не-а — «нет» с закрытым ртом; тон высокий-низкий (низкий-высокий mm-hmm = «да»)
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *mm-mmm*, *m-m*, *hm-mm*

The closed-mouth version of *uh-uh*: a nasal grunt that means no. The pitch pattern is what carries the meaning — high-then-low is *no*, while the low-then-high *mm-hmm* is *yes*, so the two are opposites that differ only in melody.

1. "Did you touch the thermostat?" "*Mm-mm.*"
2. She shook her head. "*Mm-mm*, that's not what he said."
3. "Anybody want the last one?" "*Mm-mm.*" "*Mm-mm.*"
4. "*Mm-mm*," he managed, mouth full of sandwich, waving the check away.
5. "Is this the right building?" "*Mm-mm* — it's the one across the street."

**See also:** [uh-uh](#uh-uh), [nuh-uh](#nuh-uh), [nah](#nah)

### nah

**Pronunciation:** /næ/ or /nɑ/ &middot; *NA* (rhyming with *cat*) or *NAH*; the spelling *naw* is /nɔ/, *NAW*
**Русский:** не-а / да не — расслабленное разговорное «нет», мягче и менее окончательное, чем no
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *naw*, *nahh*, *nahhh*

A relaxed, casual *no* — the everyday spoken negative among friends. It is softer and less final than *no*, and it usually trails a clause behind it that explains or cushions the refusal.

1. "You coming out tonight?" "*Nah*, I'm beat."
2. "*Naw*, man, I already ate."
3. "Do you want me to wait for you?" "*Nah*, go ahead."
4. "Is it a big deal?" "*Nah*, it happens all the time."
5. "*Nah*, I don't think that's how the billing works."

**See also:** [nope](#nope), [no](#no), [I'm good](#im-good), [uh-uh](#uh-uh)

### nay

**Pronunciation:** /neɪ/ &middot; *NAY*
**Русский:** нет (устар.) — живо только в голосовании («голоса против») и в шутливой стилизации под старину
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated (neutral in a roll-call vote)
**Variants:** *the nays*, *nay, verily*

The Old English *no*, alive today in exactly two places: the recorded vote (*the nays have it*) and deliberately archaic joking. As an everyday refusal it dropped out of American speech centuries ago, so anyone who says it straight is either presiding over a meeting or doing a bit.

1. "All those opposed?" "*Nay.*"
2. The motion failed, nineteen *nays* to twelve ayes.
3. "*Nay*, good sir, I shall not be moved from this couch."
4. "Does the senator yield?" "*Nay*, Mr. Chairman."
5. "One more round?" "*Nay* — I'm driving."

**See also:** [no](#no), [negative](#negative), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### negative

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛɡətɪv/ &middot; *NEG-uh-tiv*
**Русский:** никак нет / отрицательно — радиообменное «нет»; вне службы — деланно сухая шутка
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (radio, aviation, military, and dispatch procedure); informal and jocular everywhere else
**Variants:** *that's a negative*, *negatory*

The radio-procedure word for *no*, used where clarity matters more than tone: aviation, the military, police work, dispatch. Outside those settings Americans use it as a deadpan joke, and the hammed-up *negatory* is always a joke.

1. "Do you have visual on the vehicle?" "*Negative.*"
2. "Are you carrying any hazardous materials?" "*Negative*, tower."
3. "*Negative* — the shipment never left the warehouse."
4. "Did you remember the tickets?" "*Negative.*" "You're kidding."
5. "You want anchovies on that?" "*Negatory.*"

**See also:** [no](#no), [nope](#nope), [no can do](#no-can-do)

### never

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɛvɚ/ &middot; *NEV-er*
**Русский:** никогда / ни за что — отказ навсегда, а не только на сейчас
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *never ever*, *never again*

As a one-word answer, *never* refuses for all time rather than just for now. It is emphatic by nature, so it tends to answer a question about the future or a proposal the speaker finds permanently unacceptable.

1. "Would you ever move back?" "*Never.*"
2. "*Never.* I'm not signing that."
3. "You'll thank me for this one day." "*Never.*"
4. "Have you tried durian?" "No — and *never.*"
5. "Come on, just one more game." "*Never again.* Last time took four hours."

**See also:** [not on your life](#not-on-your-life), [over my dead body](#over-my-dead-body), [no way](#no-way)

### no

**Pronunciation:** /noʊ/ &middot; *NOH*
**Русский:** нет — базовое отрицание; no no no — тревожное «стой-стой»; nooo — «не-ет» с досадой
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *nooo*, *no no no*, *NO*

The base negative of English and the plainest refusal available in any register. Doubled or tripled (*no no no*) it turns urgent rather than merely negative; stretched out (*nooo*) it expresses dismay at something already happening rather than refusal of something proposed.

1. "Is this seat taken?" "*No*, go ahead."
2. "*No.* I am not lending him the car again."
3. "*No, no, no* — you have to click Save first."
4. "Did anyone call while I was out?" "*No*, it's been quiet all morning."
5. "So you're fine with the change?" "*No*, and I said so in the meeting."

**See also:** [nope](#nope), [nah](#nah), [uh-uh](#uh-uh), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### no thanks

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈθæŋks/ &middot; *noh THANKS*
**Русский:** нет, спасибо — стандартный вежливый отказ от предложения (no thank you — полнее и официальнее)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *no thank you*, *no thanks, I'm good*

The standard polite refusal of an offer. *No thank you* is the fuller and more formal version, *no thanks* is what most Americans actually say, and either one is a complete answer on its own.

1. "More wine?" "*No thanks.*"
2. "Would you like a bag?" "*No thank you*, I brought one."
3. "Can I interest you in the extended warranty?" "*No thanks.*"
4. "We could split an appetizer." "*No thanks* — I want to save room."
5. "*No thanks*," she said, handing the flyer back.

**See also:** [I'm good](#im-good), [pass](#pass), [no](#no)

### nope

**Pronunciation:** /noʊp/ &middot; *NOHP*
**Русский:** не-а — короткое бодрое разговорное «нет» с чётким финальным «п»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *nope nope nope*, *nopity nope*

A crisp, casual *no*, closed off with a /p/ that makes it sound final and a little breezy. It suits flat factual answers and cheerful refusals to engage; tripled in a text (*nope nope nope*) it means "absolutely not, and I am backing away slowly."

1. "Did the package come?" "*Nope.*"
2. "Are you going to tell me what she said?" "*Nope.*"
3. "*Nope*, that's not my department."
4. "Any chance they'll reschedule?" "*Nope*, the deadline's fixed."
5. She looked at the spider in the sink and typed *nope nope nope* into the group chat.

**See also:** [nah](#nah), [no](#no), [uh-uh](#uh-uh)

### not really

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ˈriəli/, often /ˌnɑt ˈrɪli/ &middot; *not REE-uh-lee*
**Русский:** да не особо / не совсем — самое мягкое несогласие; вежливый способ сказать «не понравилось»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *nah, not really*, *not really, no*, *no, not really*

The softest disagreement in ordinary American use. It answers in the negative while pulling most of the force out first, so it can decline, doubt, or contradict without closing anything off. It is also the standard polite way to say a thing was not good without ever saying it was bad.

1. "Did you like the movie?" "*Not really.*"
2. "Are you busy?" "*Not really* — what's up?"
3. "So that's how the tax works?" "*Not really.* It's messier than that."
4. "You must be thrilled." "*Not really*, to be honest."
5. "Have you two met?" "*Not really* — we've been on the same call a few times."

**See also:** [nah](#nah), [I don't think so](#i-dont-think-so), [hardly](#hardly)

### nuh-uh

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnʌʔʌ/ &middot; *NUH-uh* (first syllable stressed, pitch falling; the catch in the middle is the glottal stop of *uh-oh*)
**Русский:** не-а, неправда — детское возражение на только что сказанное; у взрослых — в шутку
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** childish
**Variants:** *nuh-unh*, *nunh-uh*

The playground contradiction: "that is not true." Where *uh-uh* simply answers no, *nuh-uh* denies a statement somebody has just made, and its natural sparring partner is *uh-huh* meaning "it is too." Adults use it jokingly, or when they are arguing like children on purpose.

1. "You started it." "*Nuh-uh*, you did."
2. "*Nuh-uh!* I saw you take the last cookie."
3. "Dad said we could." "*Nuh-uh*, he said maybe."
4. "You've never even been to Texas." "*Nuh-uh* — I lived in Austin for a year."
5. "*Nuh-uh*," he said, grinning. "That is not what the rules say."

**See also:** [uh-uh](#uh-uh), [mm-mm](#mm-mm), [says who](#says-who)

### pass

**Pronunciation:** /pæs/ &middot; *PASS*
**Русский:** пас / воздержусь — отказ от предложения или очереди; hard pass — «категорически нет»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *hard pass*, *I'll pass*, *gonna pass on that*

Borrowed from card games and quiz shows, *pass* declines a turn, an invitation, or an offer. *Hard pass* is the emphatic version and carries a note of distaste — you are not merely declining, you want it on the record that you would never.

1. "There's a karaoke thing after work." "*Pass.*"
2. "Want to try the pickle soda?" "*Hard pass.*"
3. "I'll *pass* on the Monday meeting — just send me the notes."
4. "Anyone want to present first?" "*Pass.*"
5. "They want us in the office five days a week." "Yeah, that's a *hard pass* for me."

**See also:** [I'm good](#im-good), [no thanks](#no-thanks), [forget it](#forget-it)

### uh-uh

**Pronunciation:** /ˈʌʔʌ/ &middot; *UH-uh* (first syllable stressed, pitch falling)
**Русский:** не-а / а-а — гортанное «нет», тон высокий-низкий (uh-huh «да» — низкий-высокий)
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *uh uh*, *unh-uh*

The grunted *no*: two vowels with a catch in the throat between them, said high-then-low. It is the exact mirror of *uh-huh* ("yes"), which runs low-then-high, and melody is the only thing separating them — which is why learners get misheard on this one more than on almost any other word.

1. "Did you finish the dishes?" "*Uh-uh.*"
2. "*Uh-uh*, don't even think about it."
3. "Is that your car getting towed?" "*Uh-uh*, mine's the blue one."
4. "You want me to keep going?" "*Uh-uh*, that's plenty."
5. He held out the plate and the toddler clamped her mouth shut. "*Uh-uh.*"

**See also:** [mm-mm](#mm-mm), [nuh-uh](#nuh-uh), [nope](#nope), [uh-huh](#uh-huh)

## Emphatic refusal — "absolutely not"

### absolutely not

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæbsəˈlutli ˈnɑt/ &middot; *ab-suh-LOOT-lee NOT*
**Русский:** категорически нет / ни в коем случае — сильнейший отказ, всё ещё уместный на работе
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *certainly not*, *definitely not*, *most certainly not*

The strongest refusal that is still safe in a meeting or an email. It closes the door completely without slang or profanity, which makes it the default emphatic *no* in professional settings.

1. "Should I cc the whole team?" "*Absolutely not.*"
2. "Are you suggesting we falsified the numbers?" "*Absolutely not.*"
3. "*Certainly not* at that price."
4. "Can I put you down as a maybe?" "*Absolutely not* — I'll be out of the country."
5. "*Definitely not*," the doctor said. "Not while you're on this medication."

**See also:** [out of the question](#out-of-the-question), [no way](#no-way), [no](#no)

### don't hold your breath

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdoʊnt ˌhoʊld jɚ ˈbrɛθ/ &middot; *dohnt hohld yer BRETH*
**Русский:** не жди / держи карман шире — язвительное «этого не будет, во всяком случае не скоро»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *I wouldn't hold my breath*

A sardonic warning that the thing being hoped for is not going to happen, or at least not soon. It is skepticism aimed at somebody else's optimism rather than a refusal of a request.

1. "They said they'd have it fixed by Friday." "*Don't hold your breath.*"
2. "Maybe he'll finally apologize." "*Don't hold your breath.*"
3. "The refund should post in three to five days." "*Don't hold your breath* — mine took a month."
4. "I'm sure somebody will call back." "*I wouldn't hold my breath*, honestly."
5. "Do you think the city will actually fix the potholes?" "*Don't hold your breath.*"

**See also:** [dream on](#dream-on), [fat chance](#fat-chance), [when pigs fly](#when-pigs-fly)

### dream on

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdrim ˌɑn/ &middot; *DREEM on*
**Русский:** мечтай / размечтался — насмешка над чужим ожиданием как над чистой фантазией
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *keep dreaming*

Said to somebody whose expectation you consider pure fantasy. Between friends it is teasing rather than cruel, and it can either refuse a request outright or mock a hope the other person has just voiced.

1. "You're going to let me drive it, right?" "*Dream on.*"
2. "I figure I'll be a manager by thirty." "*Dream on.*"
3. "*Keep dreaming* — those tickets sold out in four minutes."
4. "Think they'll give us Friday off too?" "*Dream on.*"
5. "So you'll cover my shift?" "*Dream on*, buddy."

**See also:** [in your dreams](#in-your-dreams), [fat chance](#fat-chance), [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath)

### fat chance

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfæt ˈtʃæns/ &middot; *fat CHANSS*
**Русский:** как же / держи карман шире — «никаких шансов»; ирония встроена в саму фразу
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Means the opposite of what it looks like: a *fat chance* is no chance at all. The sarcasm is baked into the phrase itself, so it does not need any special tone of voice to be understood.

1. "Maybe they'll upgrade us." "*Fat chance.*"
2. "*Fat chance* of getting a table on a Saturday without a reservation."
3. "He said he'd pay you back this week." "*Fat chance.*"
4. "Think the boss will even notice?" "*Fat chance* — he hasn't read an email in years."
5. "You could always ask for a raise." "*Fat chance* in this economy."

**See also:** [no chance](#no-chance), [dream on](#dream-on), [when pigs fly](#when-pigs-fly)

### forget it

**Pronunciation:** /fɚˈɡɛt ɪt/ &middot; *fer-GET it*
**Русский:** забудь / и не думай — резкий отказ; также «неважно, проехали»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *forget about it*, *fuhgeddaboudit*, *forget that*

Two jobs, both dismissive: it refuses a proposal flatly ("that is never happening"), and it waves away something that no longer matters ("never mind, drop it"). The eye-dialect spelling *fuhgeddaboudit* /fəˌɡɛdəˈbaʊdɪt/ covers both senses plus a third — "obviously, no question about it" — but be aware of what you are doing with it: it is an imitation of a working-class Italian-American New York accent, and outside New York it reads as a mob-movie impression.

1. "Can I borrow the truck for the move?" "*Forget it.*"
2. "What were you going to say?" "*Forget it*, it wasn't important."
3. "*Forget about it* — I'm not driving into the city on a holiday weekend."
4. "How much do I owe you?" "*Forget it*, it's on me."
5. "Is the pizza there any good?" "*Fuhgeddaboudit.* Best in Brooklyn."

**See also:** [pass](#pass), [no way](#no-way), [nothing doing](#nothing-doing)

### good luck with that

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈlʌk wɪð ˌðæt/ &middot; *guud LUK with that*
**Русский:** ну удачи — саркастическое пожелание: план провалится, и помогать я не собираюсь
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *good luck*, *yeah, good luck*, *good luck with all that*

Sarcastic well-wishing. The speaker expects the plan to fail and is announcing, politely, that they will not be helping. The sincere version and this one are the same four words; what marks the sarcastic one is the flat delivery and the conspicuous absence of any offer to pitch in.

1. "I'm going to ask him to reimburse me for the flight." "*Good luck with that.*"
2. "We're driving to Boston the Wednesday before Thanksgiving." "*Good luck with that.*"
3. "*Good luck with that*," she said, and went back to her own screen.
4. "I told them I need an answer by Monday." "*Good luck.*"
5. "Apparently all four systems are supposed to talk to each other by the demo." "Ha. *Good luck with that.*"

**See also:** [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath), [dream on](#dream-on), [when pigs fly](#when-pigs-fly)

### hell no

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhɛl ˈnoʊ/ &middot; *hel NOH*
**Русский:** чёрта с два / да ни за что — грубоватый резкий отказ (heck no — цензурная замена)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *hell to the no*, *aw hell no*, *heck no*

A blunt, emphatic refusal carrying mild profanity. It is completely normal among friends and completely out of place in a client meeting; *heck no* is the safe substitute that keeps the shape and drops the edge.

1. "Are you going to tell her yourself?" "*Hell no.*"
2. "*Hell no*, I'm not paying eighteen dollars for parking."
3. "Would you do it again?" "*Heck no.*"
4. "They want us to work Saturday." "*Aw hell no.*"
5. "Did you sign anything?" "*Hell no* — I called a lawyer first."

**See also:** [no way](#no-way), [like hell](#like-hell), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### in your dreams

**Pronunciation:** /ɪn jɚ ˈdrimz/ &middot; *in yer DREEMZ*
**Русский:** и не мечтай / во сне разве что — отказ с издёвкой над самой надеждой
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

Refuses a request while mocking the asker for having hoped at all. It is a taunt as much as a refusal, so it presumes enough closeness to tease.

1. "So you'll do my laundry too?" "*In your dreams.*"
2. "I'm getting the corner office." "*In your dreams.*"
3. "Give me a five-minute head start." "*In your dreams* — go."
4. "You'd let me win, right?" "*In your dreams.*"
5. "*In your dreams*, pal. That seat's taken."

**See also:** [dream on](#dream-on), [fat chance](#fat-chance), [not on your life](#not-on-your-life)

### no can do

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ kən ˈdu/ &middot; *noh kn DOO*
**Русский:** не выйдет / никак — отказ по невозможности или по правилам, а не по нежеланию
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A breezy "I can't do that," refusing on grounds of ability or rules rather than willingness. The clipped grammar is deliberate and frozen — it is never *no can does* and never takes a subject.

1. "Any chance you can squeeze me in today?" "*No can do* — I'm booked solid."
2. "*No can do*, boss. The system locks after midnight."
3. "Can you hold the elevator?" "*No can do*, it's already going."
4. "Just override it." "*No can do* without a manager's code."
5. "Swap shifts with me?" "*No can do* this week. Next week, sure."

**See also:** [negative](#negative), [nothing doing](#nothing-doing), [no dice](#no-dice)

### no chance

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈtʃæns/ &middot; *noh CHANSS*
**Русский:** без шансов / ни малейшего шанса (not a chance in hell — грубее и резче)
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *not a chance*, *not a chance in hell*, *no chance in hell*

A flat statement that the odds are zero. *Not a chance* is the more common conversational form; adding *in hell* makes it emphatic and mildly profane.

1. "Do we still have time to make the 6:40?" "*No chance.*"
2. "Would you ever go skydiving?" "*Not a chance.*"
3. "*Not a chance in hell* he finishes that by Monday."
4. "Can you talk them down on the price?" "*No chance* — they've got three other offers."
5. "Think she'll say yes?" "*Not a chance*, but ask her anyway."

**See also:** [fat chance](#fat-chance), [no way](#no-way), [not on your life](#not-on-your-life)

### no dice

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈdaɪs/ &middot; *noh DYCE*
**Русский:** не выгорело / номер не пройдёт — отказ или сообщение о неудаче, слегка старомодно и с юморком
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

From gambling: the roll doesn't count. It refuses a request outright, or reports that an attempt failed, and it carries a friendly, wisecracking, slightly old-fashioned tone.

1. "Did they give you the refund?" "*No dice.*"
2. "Can I get an extension on this?" "*No dice.*"
3. "I tried both passwords — *no dice.*"
4. "*No dice* on the reservation. They're booked through October."
5. "Ask him one more time." "I did. *No dice.*"

**See also:** [nothing doing](#nothing-doing), [no can do](#no-can-do), [forget it](#forget-it)

### no siree

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ sɚˈi/ &middot; *noh sur-EE*
**Русский:** нет уж, увольте — эмфатическое простецкое «нет» (устар.), теперь только ради комизма
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *no sirree*, *no siree Bob*, *no sir*

An emphatic, folksy *no* with an old-fashioned American twang. Nobody says it neutrally anymore; it turns up for comic emphasis or from speakers putting on a deliberate down-home voice. *Bob* is not anybody in particular.

1. "Are you ever going back there?" "*No siree.*"
2. "*No siree Bob*, I have learned my lesson."
3. "Did you touch a drop?" "*No sir*, not one."
4. "Are we lost?" "*No siree* — I know exactly where we are."
5. "You planning to retire?" "*No siree.* They'll have to carry me out."

**See also:** [nope](#nope), [no way](#no-way), [nothing doing](#nothing-doing)

### no way

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈweɪ/ &middot; *noh WAY*
**Русский:** ни за что / да ладно! — сильный отказ; с быстрым восходящим тоном — «не может быть»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *no way José*, *no way in hell*, *no freaking way*

The workhorse emphatic refusal. It also does double duty as an expression of disbelief — *No way!* said fast and rising means "I can't believe it" — so tone and context decide which one a listener hears. *No way José* (/hoʊˈzeɪ/, *hoh-ZAY*) is a rhyming intensifier that sounds playful, even childish; it is borrowing a Spanish name purely for the rhyme, which is harmless among friends and a bad bet in front of strangers who may not read it that way.

1. "Can you cover my shift Saturday?" "*No way*, I've got a wedding."
2. "*No way* am I paying that much for a haircut."
3. "Let me guess — you told him yes?" "*No way.*"
4. "Can I have a bite?" "*No way José.*"
5. "They want the whole thing redone by tomorrow." "*No way in hell* that happens."

**See also:** [no chance](#no-chance), [hell no](#hell-no), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not), [forget it](#forget-it)

### not on your life

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnɑt ɑn jɚ ˈlaɪf/ &middot; *not on yer LIFE*
**Русский:** ни за что на свете / ни за какие коврижки — театральный, слегка устаревший абсолютный отказ
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *not in a million years*, *not for anything*

An absolute refusal, staked on the other person's life for emphasis. It is a little theatrical and a little dated, which makes it handy when you want the refusal to be final but good-humored.

1. "Are you going to let him borrow the boat?" "*Not on your life.*"
2. "Would you eat that?" "*Not on your life.*"
3. "*Not on your life* — I've seen how he drives."
4. He wanted the keys to the cabin for the whole month. *Not on your life.*
5. "Everybody has a price." "*Not in a million years.*"

**See also:** [over my dead body](#over-my-dead-body), [never](#never), [no way](#no-way)

### nothing doing

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnʌθɪŋ ˈduɪŋ/ &middot; *NUTH-ing DOO-ing*
**Русский:** и не подумаю / ничего не выйдет (устар.) — «этому не бывать», с флёром 1940-х
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *nothin' doin'*, *no soap*

An old-fashioned refusal meaning "that is not going to happen." It has a 1940s newsroom flavor and now reads as deliberately vintage, though plenty of older Americans still use it perfectly straight. *No soap* is the even more antique synonym, and a listener under fifty may need it explained.

1. "Can we stay up till midnight?" "*Nothing doing.*"
2. "I asked about a discount — *nothing doing.*"
3. "*Nothing doing*, kid. Back of the line."
4. "Will they take a check?" "*Nothing doing.* Cash only."
5. "I tried to talk him out of it, but *nothing doing.*"

**See also:** [no dice](#no-dice), [no siree](#no-siree), [forget it](#forget-it)

### out of the question

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaʊt əv ðə ˈkwɛstʃən/ &middot; *owt uv thuh KWES-chun*
**Русский:** об этом не может быть и речи (книжн.) — тема закрыта, обсуждению не подлежит
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *that's out of the question*, *completely out of the question*

A refusal that declares the proposal not even eligible for discussion. It is the formal register's strongest *no*, and it comes naturally from parents, officials, and anybody with the authority to close a topic.

1. "Could we push the launch to June?" "*Out of the question.*"
2. "A refund at this point is *out of the question.*"
3. "Can I go alone?" "*Absolutely out of the question.*"
4. "*That's out of the question*," the judge said. "Next matter."
5. "What about hiring two more people?" "*Out of the question* — the budget's frozen."

**See also:** [absolutely not](#absolutely-not), [no way](#no-way), [over my dead body](#over-my-dead-body)

### over my dead body

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚ maɪ ˌdɛd ˈbɑdi/ &middot; *oh-ver my ded BOD-ee*
**Русский:** только через мой труп — самый театральный отказ, почти всегда гипербола
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

The most theatrical refusal in ordinary use: the thing can only happen once the speaker is dead. It is almost always hyperbole, and it usually defends something the speaker feels protective of.

1. "They want to tear down the old library." "*Over my dead body.*"
2. "He's planning to name the baby Ragnar." "*Over my dead body.*"
3. "*Over my dead body* are they putting a parking lot there."
4. "Can I get a tattoo?" "*Over my dead body.*"
5. "They think they can take the case away from me." "*Over my dead body.*"

**See also:** [not on your life](#not-on-your-life), [never](#never), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### that'll be the day

**Pronunciation:** /ˌðætəl bi ðə ˈdeɪ/ &middot; *THAT-l bee thuh DAY*
**Русский:** как же, дождёшься — «этому не бывать», с наигранным предвкушением
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (slightly dated)
**Variants:** *that will be the day*, *that'd be the day*

Says the day in question is never coming. It answers a prediction or a promise by pretending to look forward to it, and the fake eagerness is the whole joke — which is why it is usually delivered with a little laugh in front of it.

1. "He says he's going to start doing his own laundry." "*That'll be the day.*"
2. "Maybe the trains will run on time this winter." "*That'll be the day.*"
3. "*That'll be the day* — she hasn't apologized to anybody since I've known her."
4. "One of these years they'll fix the roof." "Ha. *That'll be the day.*"
5. "You might even end up liking it." "*That'll be the day.*"

**See also:** [when pigs fly](#when-pigs-fly), [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath), [fat chance](#fat-chance)

### when pigs fly

**Pronunciation:** /wɛn ˈpɪɡz ˌflaɪ/ &middot; *when PIGZ fly*
**Русский:** когда рак на горе свистнет — невозможный срок, то есть никогда
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *when hell freezes over*, *when pigs have wings*

Answers "when?" with an impossible date, meaning never. It is the standard American way to refuse or to dismiss a prediction while turning the whole thing into a joke.

1. "When is he going to clean out the garage?" "*When pigs fly.*"
2. "They'll fix it eventually." "Yeah, *when pigs fly.*"
3. "*When hell freezes over* — that's when I'll set foot in that place again."
4. "Are you ever going to learn to swim?" "*When pigs fly.*"
5. "She said she'd think about it." "Sure, and she'll get back to you *when pigs fly.*"

**See also:** [fat chance](#fat-chance), [dream on](#dream-on), [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath)

### you wish

**Pronunciation:** /ˌju ˈwɪʃ/ &middot; *yoo WISH*
**Русский:** размечтался / щас — насмешливый отказ: «тебе бы этого хотелось, да?»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh, you wish*, *don't you wish*

A taunting refusal aimed at the wanting rather than at the request: not merely no, but *you'd love that, wouldn't you.* It needs enough closeness to tease — said to a stranger or across a power gap it stops being funny immediately.

1. "I figured you'd give me the window seat." "*You wish.*"
2. "Admit it, you missed me." "*You wish.*"
3. "So I'm off dish duty tonight?" "*You wish.*"
4. "*You wish*," he said, and slid the last slice out of reach.
5. "She's going to say yes, I can feel it." "*You wish.*"

**See also:** [in your dreams](#in-your-dreams), [dream on](#dream-on), [nice try](#nice-try)

## Sarcastic agreement — words that mean the opposite

### as if

**Pronunciation:** /ˌæz ˈɪf/ &middot; *az IF*
**Русский:** ага, конечно / щас прям — «как будто такое возможно», с привкусом 1990-х
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (1990s flavor; now almost always knowing or ironic)
**Variants:** *as iiif*

Rejects a suggestion as laughably unlikely — it is short for "as if that would ever happen." The phrase carries a strong 1990s Valley-girl flavor from the movie *Clueless*, and Americans who use it now are usually winking at that history.

1. "He probably thinks you're going to apologize." "*As if.*"
2. "You could just ask her out." "*As if.*"
3. "*As if* I'd let you drive after that."
4. "They said the wait is only ten minutes." "*As if.*"
5. "I heard you two are getting back together." "*As if!*"

**See also:** [yeah right](#yeah-right), [in your dreams](#in-your-dreams), [oh please](#oh-please)

### likely story

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlaɪkli ˈstɔri/ &middot; *like-lee STOR-ee*
**Русский:** так я и поверил / складно врёшь — саркастическое принятие объяснения, которому не верят
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *a likely story*

Sarcastic acceptance of an explanation the speaker does not believe for a second. The word *likely* is doing exactly the opposite of its literal work, and the phrase almost always comes with a raised eyebrow.

1. "The dog ate it." "*Likely story.*"
2. "Traffic was terrible." "*A likely story.*"
3. "*Likely story* — you told me the same thing last month."
4. "I was just passing by and thought I'd stop in." "*Likely story.*"
5. "He says the transfer never went through." "*Likely story.*"

**See also:** [yeah right](#yeah-right), [says who](#says-who), [I doubt it](#i-doubt-it)

### nice try

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaɪs ˈtraɪ/ &middot; *nice TRY*
**Русский:** хорошая попытка / не прокатит — насмешливая похвала неудавшейся уловке или отговорке
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *good try*, *good one*, *nice try, buddy*

Mock praise for an attempt that did not work — a lie you saw through, an excuse you are not accepting, an offer that was never going to fly. The sincere version, said to somebody who genuinely tried, rises at the end and comes with warmth; the dismissive one is flat and leaves a small pause after it. *Good one* does the same job for a claim so unlikely that the speaker treats it as a joke.

1. "I sent it days ago — must have gone to your spam." "*Nice try.*"
2. "*Nice try.* Bedtime is still eight o'clock."
3. "I thought the deadline was next week." "*Nice try*, it's been on the calendar since June."
4. "Would you take four hundred for it?" "*Nice try.*"
5. "The dog unplugged the router." "*Good one.*"

**See also:** [likely story](#likely-story), [yeah right](#yeah-right), [you wish](#you-wish)

### oh please

**Pronunciation:** /oʊ ˈpliz/ &middot; *oh PLEEZ*
**Русский:** ой, да ладно / да брось — пренебрежительное «не смеши, это несерьёзно»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *please*, *oh, please*, *puh-leeze*

Scoffing dismissal of a claim, an excuse, or a bit of theater: "you cannot expect me to take that seriously." Note that polite *please* and dismissive *please* are the same word — the sarcastic one is lower, longer, and usually has *oh* in front of it. The joke spelling *puh-leeze* (/pəˈliz/) exists to make that drawl visible in writing.

1. "He said he was too busy to call." "*Oh please.*"
2. "I'm probably going to fail this class." "*Please.* You have an A."
3. "*Oh please*, you love the attention."
4. "This is the worst day of my life." "*Puh-leeze.* You spilled a coffee."
5. "She's basically a professional." "*Oh please*, she took two lessons."

**See also:** [come on](#come-on), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break), [pfft](#pfft)

### okay

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊˈkeɪ/ &middot; *oh-KAY*
**Русский:** ну ладно / ага, конечно — саркастическое согласие: не верю ни слову и делать ничего не буду
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *ok*, *okayyy*, *k*, *kk*

Sarcastic *okay* pretends to accept what was just said while signaling that the speaker believes none of it and intends to do nothing about it. In writing the flatness has to be spelled out: a period after *Okay.* or a bare *k* in a text both read as cold and dismissive, which is why they start arguments.

1. "I'll have it to you by Friday, I promise." "*Okay.*"
2. "*Okayyy*, sure, whatever you say."
3. "I'm the most organized person in this house." "*Okay.*"
4. She texted back a single *k* and he knew he was in trouble.
5. "You're going to run a marathon in June." "*Okay.* Let's see."

**See also:** [sure](#sure), [right](#right), [uh-huh](#uh-huh)

### right

**Pronunciation:** /raɪt/ &middot; *RITE*
**Русский:** ну да, конечно / ага — растянутое riiight означает «не верю ни единому слову»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *riiight*, *riiiight*, *oh, right*

Drawn out and falling in pitch, *right* means the speaker does not believe you at all. The stretched spelling *riiight* exists precisely because ordinary *right* — which means sincere agreement — would be read the wrong way on the page.

1. "He's quitting to become a professional poker player." "*Riiight.*"
2. "I never touched it." "*Right.*"
3. "*Riiight*, and I'm the Queen of England."
4. "She says the check is in the mail." "*Right.* Sure it is."
5. "This will only take five minutes." "*Riiight.*"

**See also:** [yeah right](#yeah-right), [sure](#sure), [okay](#okay)

### says who

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɛz ˈhu/ &middot; *sez HOO*
**Русский:** кто сказал? / это кто решил? — вызов: назови источник или отступи
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *sez who*, *who says*

A challenge to the authority behind a claim: "who exactly decided that?" It is combative — halfway between a question and a shove — and it dares the other person to produce a source or back down.

1. "You can't park here." "*Says who?*"
2. "Nobody wears those anymore." "*Says who?*"
3. "*Says who?* Show me where it says that in the lease."
4. "You're too old for that." "*Says who?*"
5. "The deal's off." "*Sez who?*"

**See also:** [nuh-uh](#nuh-uh), [like hell](#like-hell), [my foot](#my-foot)

### sure

**Pronunciation:** /ʃʊr/, also /ʃɝ/ &middot; *SHOOR* or *SHUR*
**Русский:** ну конечно / ага, как же — недоверие в маске согласия, произносится ровно или врастяжку
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *suuure*, *sure, sure*, *yeah, sure*

Said flat or drawn out, *sure* is disbelief wearing the costume of agreement. The sincere *sure* is quick and rises slightly; the sarcastic one is slow, level or falling, and often followed by a pointed little pause.

1. "I was going to call you." "*Suuure* you were."
2. "It's completely safe." "*Sure* it is."
3. "He says he read the whole contract." "*Sure.*"
4. "I'll be there at six sharp." "*Sure, sure.*"
5. "This is the last time, I swear." "*Sure*, that's what you said in March."

**See also:** [yeah right](#yeah-right), [right](#right), [uh-huh](#uh-huh), [okay](#okay)

### sure, Jan

**Pronunciation:** /ˌʃʊr ˈdʒæn/ &middot; *shoor JAN*
**Русский:** ну конечно, конечно — интернет-мем: снисходительное «верим-верим» очевидному вранью
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text (very informal; now also spoken, and openly mocking)
**Variants:** *sure Jan*, *ok Jan*

An internet-born way of humoring somebody whose claim is obviously untrue, from a line in a *Brady Bunch* movie. It is playful and at home in group chats and comment threads; it can sting badly if aimed at somebody being sincere, and it simply confuses anyone who does not know the reference.

1. "I only had one slice." "*Sure, Jan.*"
2. "He says the traffic app was wrong." "*Sure, Jan.*"
3. "I'm going to start waking up at five every day." "*Sure, Jan.*"
4. Somebody in the comments replied with a flat *sure, Jan* and the thread died right there.
5. "I could've gone pro if I hadn't hurt my knee." "*Sure, Jan.*"

**See also:** [sure](#sure), [okay](#okay), [yeah right](#yeah-right)

### uh-huh

**Pronunciation:** /ʌˈhʌ/ &middot; *uh-HUH* (the sarcastic version is flat and level, *UH-huh*)
**Русский:** ага / угу — здесь саркастическое: «слушаю и не верю ни слову»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *uh huh*, *mm-hmm*

*Uh-huh* normally means yes, or "I'm still listening." Said flat and slow with no rise at the end, it means the exact opposite: "I hear you, and I don't buy a word of it." Nothing but intonation separates the two, which makes it one of the easiest interjections in English to be misread on.

1. "I'll get to it first thing tomorrow." "*Uh-huh.*"
2. "It was already broken when I found it." "*Uh-huh.*"
3. "*Uh-huh.* And where exactly were you until two in the morning?"
4. "I read the entire thing." "*Uh-huh*, sure you did."
5. "We're just friends." "*Mm-hmm.*"

**See also:** [sure](#sure), [right](#right), [okay](#okay), [uh-uh](#uh-uh)

### yeah right

**Pronunciation:** /ˌjɛə ˈraɪt/ &middot; *yeh RITE*
**Русский:** ага, конечно / ну да, как же — стандартное американское саркастическое «не верю»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *yeah, right*, *yeah sure*, *yeah okay*

The standard American sarcastic dismissal: two words of agreement that together mean "I don't believe that for a second." The stress lands hard on *right* and the pitch falls; said with a rise the same words would be genuine agreement, which is why the run-together *yeah right* is the usual spelling for the sarcastic one.

1. "He says he's going to be here on time." "*Yeah right.*"
2. "The repair should be covered under warranty." "*Yeah right.*"
3. "*Yeah right*, like they're going to refund the whole thing."
4. "I'm quitting sugar for a year." "*Yeah, right.*"
5. "She told me she's never once been late." "*Yeah right*, I've worked with her."

**See also:** [as if](#as-if), [sure](#sure), [right](#right), [likely story](#likely-story)

## "That's nonsense" — the balderdash family

### applesauce

**Pronunciation:** /ˈæpəlˌsɔs/ &middot; *AP-l-sawss*
**Русский:** чепуха / вздор (устар., слэнг 1920-х) — особенно о лести; сейчас только стилизация под эпоху
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated (1920s slang; now purely a period costume)
**Variants:** *pure applesauce*

Jazz-age slang for nonsense, and especially for flattery — from the boardinghouse trick of filling out a cheap meal with a dish of applesauce. It is dead in ordinary speech and survives only where somebody is deliberately doing a 1920s voice.

1. "*Applesauce.* He has never set foot in a courtroom."
2. "He told her she was the most beautiful girl in Chicago." "*Applesauce.*"
3. "*Applesauce!* You'll believe anything a man in a good hat tells you."
4. "The ad says you'll double your money inside a year." "*Applesauce.*"
5. "*Applesauce*," she said, and snapped her compact shut.

**See also:** [horsefeathers](#horsefeathers), [baloney](#baloney), [bunk](#bunk)

### balderdash

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbɔldɚˌdæʃ/ &middot; *BAWL-der-dash*
**Русский:** вздор! / чушь! (устар., книжн.) — нарочито старомодное негодующее восклицание
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

"That is complete nonsense," delivered in a deliberately old-fashioned voice. Almost no American under sixty says it straight; it survives as a comic, blustery exclamation and as the name of a party word game.

1. "*Balderdash!* I have never heard such a ridiculous theory."
2. "The seller swears the painting is a genuine Homer." "*Balderdash.*"
3. "*Balderdash*, sir. You have no evidence whatsoever."
4. "He says the property line runs right through the oak tree." "*Balderdash.*"
5. "*Balderdash!*" my grandfather said, and switched off the television.

**See also:** [poppycock](#poppycock), [nonsense](#nonsense), [tommyrot](#tommyrot)

### baloney

**Pronunciation:** /bəˈloʊni/ &middot; *buh-LOH-nee*
**Русский:** чепуха / ерунда — беззлобное «неправда» без единого грубого слова, годится и для эфира
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *boloney*, *full of baloney*

The friendly American word for nonsense, spelled off the sausage. It calls a claim false without any vulgarity, which is why it shows up in politics and on the evening news in places where *bull* would never make it to air.

1. "*Baloney.* That is not what the contract says."
2. "They say prices went up because of shipping costs." "*Baloney.*"
3. "*Baloney!* You've been telling that story for years and it gets better every time."
4. "He claims he never got the message." "That's *baloney* — I watched him read it."
5. "You're too old to start over." "*Baloney.*"

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [malarkey](#malarkey), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### bosh

**Pronunciation:** /bɑʃ/ &middot; *BAHSH*
**Русский:** вздор! (устар., книжн.) — викторианское восклицание, в американской речи практически вымерло
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

A clipped Victorian exclamation meaning nonsense, borrowed from Turkish *boş*, "empty." It is essentially extinct in American speech and lives on in old novels and period dialogue.

1. "*Bosh!* The man is a fraud and everybody knows it."
2. "She says the house is haunted." "*Bosh.*"
3. "*Bosh* and nonsense. Sit down and eat your dinner."
4. "They warned us the bridge was unsafe." "*Bosh* — it's stood a hundred years."
5. "*Bosh!*" he snorted, folding up the newspaper.

**See also:** [piffle](#piffle), [tommyrot](#tommyrot), [balderdash](#balderdash)

### bull

**Pronunciation:** /bʊl/ &middot; *BUUL*
**Русский:** враньё / брехня (грубовато) — усечённое bullshit: цензурно, но не нейтрально
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (mildly coarse — it is an audible amputation of *bullshit*, and stricter listeners hear the whole word)
**Variants:** *B.S.*, *bullcrap*, *bull hockey*, *a bunch of bull*

The clipped, sayable-in-company form of *bullshit*: it flatly calls a statement a lie. It is not neutral — nobody says it in a job interview — but it clears most workplaces and all family dinners. *B.S.* is the initialed version and is milder still, while *bull hockey* and *bullcrap* are jokey minced forms that keep the shape and lose the profanity.

1. "*Bull.* You have never run a five-minute mile in your life."
2. "They said the fee is non-negotiable." "That's *bull.*"
3. "*B.S.*, and you know it."
4. "He told everyone the whole thing was his idea." "What a bunch of *bull.*"
5. "*Bull hockey*," my uncle said. "That fish was ten pounds, tops."

**See also:** [bullshit](#bullshit), [baloney](#baloney), [my foot](#my-foot)

### bullshit

**Pronunciation:** /ˈbʊlˌʃɪt/ &middot; *BUUL-shit*
**Русский:** херня / полная брехня (груб.) — «враньё»; второе значение — «это несправедливо»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Variants:** *that's bullshit*, *complete bullshit*

The strongest common way to call something a lie or a sham. This is genuine profanity — fine among friends, a real problem in a workplace, a classroom, or anything on the record. It has a second use worth knowing: it protests unfairness ("that's not right") as well as falsehood.

1. "*Bullshit.* I was standing right there."
2. "They're charging a fifty-dollar cancellation fee." "That's *bullshit.*"
3. "*Bullshit* — nobody told us the deadline moved."
4. "He says the numbers were audited." "*Bullshit* they were."
5. "You had to have known about this." "*Bullshit*, I found out this morning like everyone else."

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [my ass](#my-ass), [like hell](#like-hell)

### bunk

**Pronunciation:** /bʌŋk/ &middot; *BUNK*
**Русский:** туфта / пустая болтовня, выдаваемая за факты (отсюда debunk «разоблачать»)
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *bunkum*, *buncombe*, *a load of bunk*

Nonsense, especially empty talk dressed up as fact. It shortens *buncombe*, named for a North Carolina congressman's famously windy speech, and it is the root of *debunk* — the form most Americans now meet first.

1. "*Bunk.* There's no study that says any such thing."
2. "The whole detox thing is *bunk.*"
3. "They claim it cures a cold in a day." "*Bunk.*"
4. "*Pure bunkum*," the professor said, "and expensive bunkum at that."
5. "He gave us the usual speech about family values." "*Bunk*, all of it."

**See also:** [hooey](#hooey), [hogwash](#hogwash), [hokum](#hokum)

### cap

**Pronunciation:** /kæp/ &middot; *KAP*
**Русский:** гонишь / врёшь (молодёжн., из афроамериканского английского) — «это ложь»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal (from African American English; spoken and internet/text)
**Variants:** *that's cap*, *capping*, *stop capping*, *no cap* (the opposite — "I'm not lying")

The one young, living member of the nonsense family. *Cap* is a lie, and calling *cap* on a claim says you do not believe it. It comes from African American English, where *capping* is boasting or exaggerating. Among friends it is a challenge rather than a real accusation — but it belongs to speakers under about thirty, and in an older mouth it sounds borrowed.

1. "I ran a five-minute mile in high school." "*Cap.*"
2. "That's *cap* and you know it."
3. "He says he's never been late to anything in his life." "*Cap.*"
4. "Stop *capping* — you did not talk to the manager."
5. "I paid forty bucks for these." "*Cap.* They're two hundred online."

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [baloney](#baloney), [yeah right](#yeah-right)

### claptrap

**Pronunciation:** /ˈklæpˌtræp/ &middot; *KLAP-trap*
**Русский:** трескотня / пустозвонство (книжн.) — эффектные пустые слова ради реакции публики
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral (bookish, and somewhat dated)
**Variants:** *utter claptrap*, *sentimental claptrap*

Showy, empty talk designed to draw a reaction — the word began as theater slang for a line written to *trap* applause. It is aimed at speeches, ad copy, and editorials rather than at one false statement, and it accuses the speaker of pandering as much as of lying.

1. "*Claptrap.* Not one of those numbers is real."
2. "The closing argument was pure *claptrap.*"
3. "*Utter claptrap*, and the room ate it up."
4. "He finished with something about how we're all family here." "*Claptrap.*"
5. "*Claptrap*," she wrote in the margin, and underlined it twice.

**See also:** [hokum](#hokum), [twaddle](#twaddle), [bunk](#bunk)

### crap

**Pronunciation:** /kræp/ &middot; *KRAP*
**Русский:** фигня / брехня (грубовато) — средняя ступень между bull и bullshit
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal (mildly vulgar)
**Variants:** *that's crap*, *a load of crap*, *a bunch of crap*, *total crap*

The middle rung between *bull* and *bullshit*: it calls a claim a lie or a piece of junk with real coarseness, but stops short of full profanity. Most Americans will say it in front of friends and coworkers and not in front of a client, a classroom, or their grandmother. It has a separate life as a standalone curse at bad news, which is a different job entirely.

1. "*Crap.* That is not what the policy says."
2. "They're calling it a manufacturing defect." "That's *crap* — the thing's been recalled twice."
3. "He fed them *a load of crap* about a family emergency."
4. "So you pay the fee twice?" "Yep. It's *crap.*"
5. "The report blames the weather for the delay." "*Crap.* It sat on somebody's desk for three weeks."

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [bullshit](#bullshit), [crock](#crock)

### crock

**Pronunciation:** /krɑk/ &middot; *KROK*
**Русский:** туфта / полная лажа — усечённое a crock of shit, почти всегда с артиклем a
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal (the full form *a crock of shit* is vulgar)
**Variants:** *what a crock*, *that's a crock*, *a crock of shit*

Almost always used with *a*: *a crock* is a claim with nothing in it. It is the front half of *a crock of shit*, and everybody who hears the short version knows the long one, which gives it more bite than *baloney* and less than *bull*.

1. "*What a crock.* They knew about the leak in March."
2. "They're calling it a voluntary contribution." "That's *a crock.*"
3. "*A crock*, start to finish — read the fine print."
4. "He says the decision came down from corporate." "*What a crock.* He made the call himself."
5. "Everybody gets an equal shot at the promotion." "Oh, that's *a crock.*"

**See also:** [bull](#bull), [crap](#crap), [hogwash](#hogwash)

### fiddlesticks

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɪdəlˌstɪks/ &middot; *FID-l-stiks*
**Русский:** вздор! / вот незадача! (устар., «бабушкино») — и «чепуха», и мягкая замена ругательству
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *fiddle-faddle*, *fiddle-dee-dee*

A gentle, old-fashioned exclamation that does two jobs: it dismisses a claim as nonsense, and it works as a very mild substitute for a curse when something goes wrong. It sounds like somebody's grandmother, which is exactly why younger speakers reach for it as a joke.

1. "*Fiddlesticks!* You are not one bit too old to dance."
2. "The doctor said I should rest for a week." "*Fiddlesticks.*"
3. "*Fiddlesticks*, I dropped the whole tray."
4. "It's supposed to rain all weekend." "*Fiddlesticks.*"
5. "She insists she can't cook." "*Fiddle-faddle* — she made this pie."

**See also:** [phooey](#phooey), [piffle](#piffle), [poppycock](#poppycock)

### flapdoodle

**Pronunciation:** /ˈflæpˌdudəl/ &middot; *FLAP-doo-dl*
**Русский:** чепуха / галиматья (устар.) — держится в языке только за счёт смешного звучания
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *pure flapdoodle*

Nineteenth-century American nonsense, and one of the silliest-sounding words in the whole family — which is the only reason it is still around. Nobody picks it to be taken seriously; it is picked for the noise it makes.

1. "*Flapdoodle.* The man has never balanced a checkbook in his life."
2. "He gave a whole speech about destiny." "*Flapdoodle.*"
3. "*Pure flapdoodle*, dressed up with a chart."
4. "The label says it 'supports healthy energy.'" "*Flapdoodle.*"
5. "*Flapdoodle!*" she said, and went right on rolling out the dough.

**See also:** [poppycock](#poppycock), [balderdash](#balderdash), [fiddlesticks](#fiddlesticks)

### hogwash

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɔɡˌwɑʃ/ &middot; *HAWG-wahsh*
**Русский:** чушь собачья — резче и презрительнее, чем baloney, но вполне цензурно
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pure hogwash*, *a bunch of hogwash*

Nonsense, named for the slop fed to pigs. It is stronger and more contemptuous than *baloney* but still perfectly clean, which makes it a favorite in op-eds, congressional hearings, and letters to the editor.

1. "*Hogwash.* The report says the exact opposite."
2. "They're calling it a safety measure." "*Hogwash* — it's about money."
3. "*Pure hogwash*, and everybody in that room knew it."
4. "He says the delay was completely out of their control." "*Hogwash.*"
5. "*Hogwash!*" she said. "I've been doing this for thirty years."

**See also:** [baloney](#baloney), [bunk](#bunk), [malarkey](#malarkey)

### hokum

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhoʊkəm/ &middot; *HOH-kum*
**Русский:** дешёвая показуха / слащавая чушь (устар.) — красивые пустые слова из мира эстрады
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated

Nonsense of the crowd-pleasing kind — showy, sentimental claims that sound wonderful and mean nothing. It started as show-business slang for cheap emotional tricks and still carries a whiff of the carnival tent.

1. "*Hokum.* Nobody loses thirty pounds in a week."
2. "The whole speech was *hokum.*"
3. "They dressed it up with charts, but it's *hokum.*"
4. "He says the machine realigns your energy field." "*Hokum*, start to finish."
5. "*Hokum*," the old critic wrote, "beautifully performed."

**See also:** [bunk](#bunk), [hooey](#hooey), [poppycock](#poppycock)

### hooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhui/ &middot; *HOO-ee*
**Русский:** ерунда / чепуха — мягкое и добродушно-старомодное, обычно с a bunch of
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *a bunch of hooey*, *pure hooey*

Nonsense — an American coinage from the 1920s that still sounds cheerfully old-timey. It is mild enough for any company, and it usually arrives with *a bunch of* in front of it.

1. "*Hooey.* You can't catch a cold from being cold."
2. "That's a bunch of *hooey* and you know it."
3. "They say the new formula works twice as fast." "*Hooey.*"
4. "*Pure hooey*, if you ask me."
5. "He's been feeding you *hooey* since the day you met him."

### horsefeathers

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhɔrsˌfɛðɚz/ &middot; *HORSS-feth-erz*
**Русский:** чушь собачья (устар.) — эвфемизм 1920-х вместо скабрёзности, сейчас комическая стилизация
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *horse feathers*, *horse hockey*, *horsepucky*

A 1920s minced substitute for a barnyard vulgarity, meaning nonsense. It survives as a period joke — a Marx Brothers movie title, the sort of thing a cartoon grandfather says — and *horse hockey* pulls the same trick with the same intent.

1. "*Horsefeathers!* That's the oldest excuse in the book."
2. "He says he built the whole deck himself." "*Horsefeathers.*"
3. "*Horse hockey.* I was in the room when they voted."

### humbug

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhʌmˌbʌɡ/ &middot; *HUM-bug*
**Русский:** надувательство / вздор! — для американцев неотделимо от скруджевского «Bah, humbug!»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *bah, humbug*

A fraud or a piece of empty pretense, and as an exclamation a scornful "nonsense." For Americans it is now almost inseparable from Scrooge's *Bah, humbug!*, so it reads as Christmas-grumpy more than anything else.

1. "*Bah, humbug.* I'm not putting up lights this year."
2. "*Humbug!* The man is a salesman, not a healer."
3. "They call it a tradition. I call it *humbug.*"
4. "The whole ceremony struck him as *humbug.*"

### malarkey

**Pronunciation:** /məˈlɑrki/ &middot; *muh-LAR-kee*
**Русский:** болтовня / брехня — беззлобно-простецкое «враньё», давний фаворит американской политики
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *malarky*, *a bunch of malarkey*, *pure malarkey*

Insincere talk, exaggeration, nonsense. It is warm and folksy rather than harsh, and it has had a long second life in American politics as a clean way to call an opponent a liar on live television.

1. "*Malarkey.* That is not how the tax works."
2. "A bunch of *malarkey*, if you ask me."
3. "He promised everybody a raise by summer." "*Malarkey.*"
4. "*Pure malarkey* — I looked it up while he was still talking."
5. "Don't give me that *malarkey* about the traffic."

### my eye

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈaɪ/ &middot; *my EYE*
**Русский:** как же! / так я и поверил (устар.) — довесок к процитированным чужим словам
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *in a pig's eye*

A tag hung on the end of somebody else's words to throw them back as nonsense: repeat the claim, add *my eye*, and the claim is dead. It works exactly like *my foot* and is a shade more old-fashioned.

1. "Antique, *my eye.* They're making these in Ohio."
2. "Free upgrade *my eye* — they charged us for the bags."
3. "*My eye* he did. He wasn't even in town that week."
4. "Doctor's orders, *my eye.* He just wants the afternoon off."

### my foot

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈfʊt/ &middot; *my FUUT*
**Русский:** как же! / ага, конечно — приставляется к чужим словам и объявляет их чушью; цензурный вариант
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *my Aunt Fanny*, *my hat*

Tacked onto the end of a phrase you are quoting back, *my foot* rejects it as nonsense. It is the clean member of the family — *my eye* is older, *my ass* is vulgar — and it is a staple in the mouths of parents and grandparents.

1. "Too sick for school, *my foot.* You were fine ten minutes ago."
2. "Sold out, *my foot.* I can see three empty tables from here."
3. "*My foot* it's the last one. There's a whole case in the back."
4. "Vintage, *my foot.* That's from last season."
5. "He said it was an honest mistake." "Honest mistake, *my foot.*"

### nonsense

**Русский:** чепуха / глупости — универсальное «неправда» в любом стиле; также тёплое «да бросьте!»
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *utter nonsense*, *complete nonsense*, *what nonsense*

The plain, all-purpose word for "that is not true," usable in any register from a boardroom to a kitchen table. It has a second, warmer use: brushing aside somebody's self-criticism or their polite refusal of a favor, where it means "don't be silly."

1. "*Nonsense.* The numbers have been public for a month."
2. "I'm probably imposing." "*Nonsense*, stay for dinner."
3. "*Utter nonsense*, and the study says so on page one."

### phooey

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfui/ &middot; *FOO-ee*

**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated, but still in live use as a deliberately mild exclamation
**Variants:** *pfui*, *phooey on that*

Two jobs: a scornful "nonsense," and a mild expression of disgust or disappointment. It comes from Yiddish and German *pfui*, and it is cartoonish and gentle enough to say in front of children.

1. "*Phooey.* I don't believe a word of it."
2. "The game got rained out." "*Phooey.*"

### piffle

**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *what piffle*

Trivial nonsense — not a dangerous lie, just empty, silly talk. It is prim and bookish, and an American who reaches for it is almost always being funny on purpose.

1. "*Piffle.* Nobody was ever harmed by a little rain."

### poppycock

A blustery "nonsense," from a Dutch dialect phrase considerably less polite than it sounds in English. Americans keep it alive mostly for its comic sound, and it pairs naturally with an indignant older speaker.

### rubbish

Utter nonsense, from British schoolboy slang in which *tommy* meant foolish. It is thoroughly archaic in American speech and now shows up mainly in historical fiction or as a deliberate flourish.

**Variants:** *utter twaddle*, *sentimental twaddle*

Silly, empty talk — nonsense that is more tiresome than dishonest. It is bookish, and it is usually aimed at a piece of writing or a speech rather than at a single false statement.

## Scoffing and skepticism

### bah

**Pronunciation:** /bɑ/ &middot; *BAH*
**Русский:** тьфу / ба! (устар.) — брюзгливое пренебрежение; у американцев прежде всего «Bah, humbug!»
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated
**Variants:** *bah, humbug*

A grumpy grunt of dismissal — contempt for an idea, an offer, or the general state of the world. It sounds theatrical and old to American ears, and it is now heard mostly in its Scrooge collocation.

1. "*Bah.* I've got no use for any of it."
2. "The concert's sold out." "*Bah.*"
3. "*Bah, humbug*," he said, and went back to his crossword.
4. "Aren't you going to congratulate him?" "*Bah.*"
5. "*Bah!* Let the young ones worry about it."

**See also:** [humbug](#humbug), [pfft](#pfft), [psh](#psh)

### come off it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkʌm ˈɔf ɪt/ &middot; *kum AWF it*
**Русский:** да брось / кончай придуриваться — обвинение в притворстве, не просто в ошибке
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *oh, come off it*

Tells somebody to drop a pretense: stop exaggerating, stop pretending, stop the act. It accuses more directly than *come on* — the other person is not merely wrong, they are putting it on.

1. "*Come off it.* You've known about this for weeks."
2. "I had no idea it would cost that much." "*Oh, come off it.*"
3. "*Come off it*, you're not seriously offended."
4. "He says he's never had a drink in his life." "*Come off it.*"
5. "I'm the only one who does anything around here." "*Come off it*, Dave."

**See also:** [come on](#come-on), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break), [oh please](#oh-please)

### come on

**Pronunciation:** /kəˈmɑn/ &middot; *kuh-MON*
**Русский:** да ладно / да брось — протест: «не может быть», «это несерьёзно»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *c'mon*, *oh, come on*, *aw, come on*

Protests something as unreasonable or unbelievable: "you cannot be serious." *Come on* also lives elsewhere as encouragement (*come on, you've got this*) and as impatience (*come on, we're late*), so the skeptical sense depends entirely on a falling, exasperated delivery.

1. "The fee is one hundred dollars." "*Oh, come on.*"
2. "*Come on*, nobody actually believes that."
3. "He says he did it all himself." "*C'mon.* He had six people helping."
4. "You have to admit she had a point." "*Come on*, she was making it up as she went."
5. "They're calling that a foul?" "*Come on!*"

**See also:** [come off it](#come-off-it), [oh please](#oh-please), [give me a break](#give-me-a-break)

### doubtful

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdaʊtfəl/ &middot; *DOWT-ful*
**Русский:** сомнительно / вряд ли — спокойный однословный вердикт, годится и в переписке, и на работе
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *highly doubtful*, *very doubtful*

A one-word verdict that something probably will not happen or probably is not true. It is cooler and more measured than the sarcastic options, which makes it one of the few items here that works in writing and at work.

1. "Will the shipment make it before the holiday?" "*Doubtful.*"
2. "Do you think they'll appeal?" "*Highly doubtful.*"
3. "*Doubtful* — the forecast has it raining all weekend."
4. "Any chance he still remembers me?" "*Doubtful*, it's been fifteen years."
5. "Is that going to come in under budget?" "*Very doubtful.*"

**See also:** [hardly](#hardly), [I doubt it](#i-doubt-it), [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath)

### far from it

**Pronunciation:** /ˈfɑr frəm ɪt/ &middot; *FAR frum it*
**Русский:** отнюдь / совсем наоборот — не просто «нет», а «нет, и скорее наоборот»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *far from it, actually*, *quite the opposite*

Corrects a wrong impression by putting the truth at the other end of the scale: not merely "no," but "no, and the reverse." It usually follows a bare negative and it stays calm, which makes it one of the few items in this category that is safe in writing and at work.

1. "So you're unhappy with the numbers?" "*Far from it* — they beat the projection."
2. "Was he rude about it?" "No. *Far from it.*"
3. "I assume that settles the matter." "*Far from it.*"
4. "It sounds like the trip was a disaster." "*Far from it*, actually. We had a great time."
5. "Is the job getting any easier?" "*Far from it.*"

**See also:** [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [hardly](#hardly), [not really](#not-really)

### give me a break

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɪv mi ə ˈbreɪk/ &middot; *giv mee uh BRAKE*
**Русский:** да ладно тебе / хватит уже — раздражённое отвержение отговорки; также «отстань от меня»
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *gimme a break*, *oh, give me a break*

Exasperated rejection of a claim, an excuse, or an unreasonable demand. Depending on the tone it also means "stop criticizing me" — the same words, aimed at the other person's pressure rather than at their honesty.

1. "He says he was too busy to send one text." "*Give me a break.*"
2. "*Gimme a break* — that's a two-hundred-dollar cable."
3. "You should have caught that typo." "*Give me a break*, I was proofreading at midnight."
4. "They're calling it a luxury studio." "*Oh, give me a break.* It's a closet."
5. "She claims she's never seen the show." "*Give me a break*, she quotes it constantly."

**See also:** [come on](#come-on), [oh please](#oh-please), [come off it](#come-off-it)

### I beg to differ

**Pronunciation:** /ˌaɪ ˌbɛɡ tə ˈdɪfɚ/ &middot; *eye beg tuh DIF-er*
**Русский:** позвольте с вами не согласиться (книжн., устар.) — сейчас чаще шутливо-чопорно
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal — but so old-fashioned that most Americans now use it jokingly
**Variants:** *beg to differ*, *I'd beg to differ*, *I respectfully disagree*

A stiffly courteous way to contradict somebody. The politeness is genuine and so is the disagreement, but the formality has aged to the point where the phrase mostly arrives with a raised eyebrow — the stuffiness is the joke. In a setting that really is formal, *I respectfully disagree* is what people actually say.

1. "Nobody reads the manual." "*I beg to differ.* I read it twice."
2. "That's the best pizza in the state." "*I beg to differ.*"
3. "*I beg to differ*, counselor. The statute says otherwise."
4. "Everyone came out of that meeting happy." "*I'd beg to differ.*"
5. "You have never once done the dishes." "*I beg to differ*," he said, holding up a sponge.

**See also:** [on the contrary](#on-the-contrary), [far from it](#far-from-it), [I doubt it](#i-doubt-it)

### I doubt it

**Pronunciation:** /aɪ ˈdaʊt ɪt/ &middot; *eye DOWT it*
**Русский:** сомневаюсь / вряд ли — самый безопасный способ выразить недоверие, без сарказма и слэнга
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral
**Variants:** *I doubt that*, *I seriously doubt it*, *I highly doubt it*

A plain, polite way to say you do not believe something will happen or is true. It is the safest skeptical response in this entire category: no sarcasm, no slang, nothing that could be heard as rude.

1. "Do you think they'll even notice?" "*I doubt it.*"
2. "He'll probably call tonight." "*I doubt it.*"
3. "*I seriously doubt it* — that model was discontinued years ago."
4. "Is there any parking left?" "*I doubt it*, but let's drive by."
5. "They say the price will come down after the holidays." "*I highly doubt it.*"

**See also:** [doubtful](#doubtful), [hardly](#hardly), [don't hold your breath](#dont-hold-your-breath)

### like hell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlaɪk ˈhɛl/ &middot; *like HEL*
**Русский:** чёрта с два / как бы не так (грубовато) — резкое опровержение или отказ подчиниться приказу
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal
**Variants:** *like hell I will*, *like heck*

Contradicts a statement with force: "that is absolutely not so." It usually echoes the claim it is denying (*like hell he did*), and it can also throw an order straight back (*like hell I will*). Mild profanity — keep it out of the office.

1. "He paid for everything." "*Like hell* he did."
2. "You're going to apologize to her." "*Like hell I am.*"
3. "*Like hell* it was an accident."
4. "They said the store closes at nine." "*Like hell* — the sign says eight."
5. "You're staying home tonight." "*Like hell.*"

**See also:** [my ass](#my-ass), [hell no](#hell-no), [my foot](#my-foot)

### my ass

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈæs/ &middot; *my ASS*
**Русский:** чёрта с два / хрена с два (груб.) — вульгарный собрат my foot и my eye
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** vulgar
**Variants:** *my sweet ass*

The vulgar member of the *my foot* / *my eye* family: repeat the claim, add *my ass*, and you have called it a lie in the strongest everyday terms available. Genuinely profane — friends and bars, not offices and classrooms.

1. "Best deal in town, *my ass.*"
2. "Emergency meeting, *my ass.* He wanted an audience."
3. "*My ass* it's under warranty."
4. "He says he's retiring." "Retiring, *my ass.*"
5. "It's a nonprofit, *my ass* — look at those salaries."

**See also:** [my foot](#my-foot), [bullshit](#bullshit), [like hell](#like-hell)

### on the contrary

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑn ðə ˈkɑnˌtrɛri/ &middot; *on thuh KON-trer-ee*
**Русский:** напротив / наоборот (книжн.) — отвергает утверждение и сразу ставит на его место обратное
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal
**Variants:** *quite the contrary*, *on the contrary, though*

Rejects a statement and installs its opposite in one move. It belongs to written and prepared English — a brief, a letter, a debate, a witness who has thought about the answer — and in casual conversation it comes off as either stuffy or deliberately arch.

1. "You seem to think the policy failed." "*On the contrary.* It did exactly what it was built to do."
2. "*On the contrary*, the numbers have been consistent for six quarters."
3. "I take it you object." "*Quite the contrary* — I'm in favor."
4. "The witness was uncooperative, then." "*On the contrary*, Your Honor."
5. "So the rain ruined the whole weekend." "*On the contrary*, it was the best part."

**See also:** [far from it](#far-from-it), [I beg to differ](#i-beg-to-differ), [absolutely not](#absolutely-not)

### pfft

**Pronunciation:** not a speech sound and it has no real IPA — a short puff of air pushed out through slack, nearly closed lips, sometimes with a *t*-like cutoff at the end; written roughly /pfː(t)/, with no vowel anywhere in it &middot; *pfft*
**Русский:** пф / пфф — пренебрежительный выдох: «не стоит внимания»
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pff*, *pft*, *phhht*

The written form of a dismissive puff of air — not a true raspberry, which is made with the tongue between the lips and is ruder. This one just leaks out and means "that is not worth taking seriously." It waves off a claim, a threat, or a worry with almost no effort at all, and the effortlessness is part of the insult.

1. "*Pfft.* That's not even a real rule."
2. "He said he could beat you at chess." "*Pfft.*"
3. "*Pfft*, I've fixed worse than this with duct tape."
4. "Aren't you nervous about the presentation?" "*Pfft.* No."
5. "They think a two-hour drive is far." "*Pfft.*"

**See also:** [psh](#psh), [bah](#bah), [oh please](#oh-please)

### psh

**Pronunciation:** no settled IPA, because there is no vowel in it — a lip release running straight into a long *sh*, roughly /pʃː/, held as long as the scorn requires &middot; *psh*. The spelled-out older form *pshaw* is an ordinary word with an ordinary vowel: /ʃɔ/, *SHAW*
**Русский:** пф / ой, да ну — презрительное шипение, резче pfft и чаще в адрес человека
**Type:** onomatopoeic &middot; **Register:** informal
**Variants:** *pssh*, *pshh*, *psshh*, *pshaw*

A scoffing hiss of dismissal, close to *pfft* but a shade more contemptuous and more often aimed at a person than at an idea. *Pshaw* is the nineteenth-century spelling and now sounds theatrical or rural.

1. "*Psh.* He's not going to say anything."
2. "You'll need a reservation." "*Psh*, on a Tuesday?"
3. "*Pssh*, that's nothing. Wait till you see mine."
4. "She thinks she's the best player here." "*Psh.*"
5. "*Pshaw*," said the old man. "In my day we walked."

**See also:** [pfft](#pfft), [bah](#bah), [oh please](#oh-please)

### since when

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsɪns ˈwɛn/ &middot; *sinss WEN*
**Русский:** это с каких пор? — вызов: кто и когда решил, что это правило
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal (confrontational)
**Variants:** *since when?*, *oh, since when*

Challenges a claim that something is a rule, a habit, or a settled fact: the speaker never agreed to it and would like to know where it came from. It is close kin to *says who*, and it lands as a shove even when the tone is light — which is why it is a bad answer to give a police officer or a boss.

1. "We don't allow outside food." "*Since when?*"
2. "You hate cilantro." "*Since when?* I put it on everything."
3. "*Since when* do we need approval for a fifty-dollar order?"
4. "He's in charge of the schedule now." "*Since when?*"
5. "It's a black-tie thing." "*Since when?* I wore jeans last year."

**See also:** [says who](#says-who), [nuh-uh](#nuh-uh), [come off it](#come-off-it)
