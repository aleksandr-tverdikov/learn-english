Fear, alarm, and warning#

The words Americans use when something is wrong — the cries that escape you when you are frightened, and the shouts that make other people move.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

This category has two halves that work as one. Some of these words point inward and simply leak the feeling — eek, yikes, uh-oh, oh no — while the rest point outward and are really commands with the panic left in: look out, duck, freeze, heads up. That second half is why so many entries here are ordinary nouns and verbs doing interjection duty; in an emergency English grabs the shortest word available. Two habits run through the whole group: these take exclamation points more freely than any other kind of interjection, and they get repeated in threes when the danger is immediate (whoa, whoa, whoa; brace, brace, brace), because a shout repeated three times cannot be mistaken for anything else.

Cries of fear, alarm, and dismay#

aaah#

Pronunciation: /ɑː/ — the vowel of father, held; each extra letter in the spelling means a longer hold · AHH Type: primary · Register: informal (chiefly written) Variants: aah, ahhh, aaaah

The written form of a scream. Extra letters make the scream longer and more frantic, so the spelling does the work that volume does in life — terror, panic, or the sound of somebody falling. Note that a short ah with one letter is a different word entirely, expressing realization or relief.

  1. "Aaah! There's a wasp in the car!"
  2. He hit the water yelling aaah the whole way down.
  3. "Aaaaah!" she screamed, and the whole theater screamed with her.
  4. The text was just aaaah and a picture of a spider the size of my hand.
  5. Aaah — get it off me, get it off me!
  6. Somewhere down the hall a kid went aaah and then started laughing.

See also: agh, aiee, eek

ack#

Pronunciation: /æk/ · AK Type: primary · Register: informal (chiefly written; comics and internet) Variants: aack, ackk

A short, strangled noise of dismay or mild alarm, popularized by American newspaper comic strips. It marks a small shock — a bad email, a forgotten deadline — and it is almost always typed rather than spoken.

  1. "Ack! I sent that to the whole department."
  2. Ack — I forgot we have people coming over at six.
  3. She read the tuition bill and let out a quiet ack.
  4. "Ack, my coffee." He grabbed a fistful of napkins.
  5. Ack! Do not scroll down, the photo is horrifying.

See also: agh, eek, yikes

agh#

Pronunciation: /ɑː/ (with a strained, throaty quality) · AHH Type: primary · Register: informal (chiefly written) Variants: aagh, aaagh, augh

A choked cry of fright, revulsion, or panic. Spoken, it is hard to tell from a plain scream; in writing, the g signals something more strangled and less musical than aaah.

  1. "Agh! The lid wasn't on."
  2. Agh, there's something moving down in the basement.
  3. He jerked his hand back with an agh and knocked the pan off the burner.
  4. "Aaagh, don't jump out at me like that!"
  5. Agh — I really thought that branch was going to hold.

See also: aaah, ack, eek

aiee#

Pronunciation: /aɪˈiː/ · eye-EE Type: primary · Register: informal (chiefly written; comics and genre fiction) Variants: aiiee, aieee, aiyee

A shriek of terror or sudden pain, used almost entirely in print — comic books, adventure novels, video-game subtitles. Real people rarely say it out loud, and saying it in conversation reads as a joke.

  1. "Aiee!" cried the villager as the roof gave way.
  2. The whole page was one long aiee in jagged red letters.
  3. Aiee — my ankle, my ankle!
  4. He read the line "Aiee!" out loud and the whole table lost it.
  5. My brother yells aiee every time he stubs a toe, like he thinks he's in a comic book.

See also: aaah, eek, yipes

boo#

Pronunciation: /buː/ · BOO Type: primary · Register: informal

Shouted to startle someone on purpose, usually from hiding and usually in fun. It is the one word in this group that causes fear instead of expressing it. The drawn-out boo that a crowd aims at a bad performance is a separate word meaning disapproval.

  1. He jumped out from behind the door and yelled "Boo!"
  2. "Boo! Did I get you?"
  3. She hid under the blanket and said boo every time the dog walked past.
  4. "Boo!" The twins had been crouched behind the couch for ten minutes.
  5. I said boo and my sister dropped an entire bowl of popcorn.
  6. He got me with a boo from behind the shower curtain and I have not forgiven him.

See also: eek, yikes

eek#

Pronunciation: /iːk/ · EEK Type: primary · Register: informal (often playful) Variants: eeek, eeeek

A high, short squeal of fright or squeamishness — mice, spiders, cold water, an unexpected bill. Americans use it more often as a light, half-joking reaction than as a real scream, and it is very common in texting.

  1. "Eek!" She yanked both feet up onto the chair.
  2. Eek, the invoice is due tomorrow and I haven't started it.
  3. There was a mouse behind the fridge and my mother let out a tiny eek.
  4. Eeek — I just realized I've been on mute this entire time.
  5. He put one toe in the lake and went eek, which was not a good look for a grown man.
  6. "Eek, sorry, wrong chat."

See also: eep, yikes, aaah

eep#

Pronunciation: /iːp/ · EEP Type: primary · Register: very informal (chiefly internet/text) Variants: eeep

A tiny, mouse-like squeak of alarm or embarrassment — smaller and cuter than eek. It lives almost entirely in chat, texting, and social media, where it softens bad news you are delivering about yourself.

  1. Eep! I did not mean to send that.
  2. "Eep, is he standing right behind me?"
  3. She typed eep and then deleted the whole message.
  4. Eep — the meeting started five minutes ago.
  5. He made a small eep sound when the cat landed on his chest.

See also: eek, ack

gasp#

Pronunciation: /ɡæsp/ when the written word is read aloud — but the sound it stands for is not speech at all: it is a sharp intake of breath through an open mouth · GASP Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal (chiefly written; said aloud as a word it is almost always sarcastic)

The written stand-in for the breath people suck in when they are shocked or frightened. On the page it marks alarm the way gulp marks dread. Said out loud as an actual word — flat, unhurried, no real breath behind it — it means the opposite: the speaker is only pretending to be shocked.

  1. She got to the last line of the letter and I heard one sharp gasp from the other room.
  2. "Gasp! The butler did it after all."
  3. "He actually paid for dinner." "Gasp. Who are you and what have you done with my brother?"
  4. There was an audible gasp in the theater when the lights came up on the second act.
  5. He read the group text, typed gasp, and then three question marks.
  6. My mother's gasp when she saw the tattoo was louder than anything she said afterward.

See also: gulp, oh no, yikes

gulp#

Pronunciation: /ɡʌlp/ when the written word is read aloud — the thing it names is a hard swallow, a squeeze of the throat with no speech sound in it · GULP Type: onomatopoeic · Register: informal (chiefly written)

The sound of swallowing hard, written down to show nervous dread. It is a printed stage direction more than a spoken word: you write gulp to admit you are afraid of whatever comes next.

  1. The dentist said I'd need three of them. Gulp.
  2. Gulp — my in-laws just pulled into the driveway.
  3. Gulp — the new boss turns out to be the guy I argued with at the conference.
  4. "Your presentation got moved to first." Gulp.
  5. He posted a photo of the electric bill with one word under it: gulp.

See also: gasp, hoo boy, uh-oh

hoo boy#

Pronunciation: /ˌhuː ˈbɔɪ/ · hoo-BOY Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hoo-boy, hooboy

Said while bracing for something big, bad, or exhausting that you can already see coming. It carries weary, half-amused dread rather than sharp fear.

  1. Hoo boy. This is going to be a long meeting.
  2. "You should read the comments." "Hoo boy."
  3. Hoo boy, wait until she finds out who broke it.
  4. He looked at the stack of paperwork and said hoo boy to nobody in particular.
  5. Hoo boy — three connecting flights with a toddler.

See also: oh no, uh-oh, gulp

oh dear#

Pronunciation: /ˌoʊ ˈdɪr/ · oh-DEER Type: phrase · Register: neutral; distinctly old-fashioned in American English Variants: oh dear me, oh dear, oh dear

A mild, restrained expression of dismay at something that has gone wrong. In the U.S. it sounds gentle and a little grandmotherly; most Americans under fifty reach for oh no instead.

  1. "Oh dear, I think I've mixed up the dates."
  2. Oh dear. That stain is never coming out.
  3. She looked at the broken teacup and said, "Oh dear, oh dear."
  4. Oh dear — he is not going to take that well.
  5. "The flight's canceled." "Oh dear. Now what do we do?"

See also: oh no, uh-oh

oh no#

Pronunciation: /ˌoʊ ˈnoʊ/ · oh-NOH Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: oh no no no, oh nooo

The all-purpose American reaction to bad news or an unfolding disaster, from a dropped phone to a call from the hospital. Length carries the weight: a flat oh no is sympathy, a stretched oh nooo is real dread.

  1. "Oh no. Did I leave the oven on?"
  2. Oh no — that's my car they're towing.
  3. She read the text and whispered oh no.
  4. "Oh no no no, don't touch that wire."
  5. Oh no, I'm so sorry. Is he going to be all right?
  6. He watched the glass tip over the edge and had time for exactly one oh no.

See also: oh dear, uh-oh, yikes

oops#

Pronunciation: /uːps/, also /ʊps/ · OOPS Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: whoops, oopsie, whoopsie, oops-a-daisy

Said the instant you notice you have made a small mistake — a spill, a typo, a wrong turn. It is an alarm and an apology at once, and it keeps the mistake small, so saying oops about something serious sounds callous. Whoops /wʊps/ is a slightly more playful spelling, and oopsie is baby-ish or teasing.

  1. "Oops. Wrong file — let me resend it."
  2. Whoops, I think I just deleted the entire column.
  3. She caught the cup with her elbow and said oops before it even landed.
  4. Oops — sorry, I thought this was the other conference room.
  5. "Oopsie," he said, wiping applesauce off the baby's chin.
  6. He hit reply-all, saw what he had done, and typed one word: oops.

See also: uh-oh, eek

uh-oh#

Pronunciation: /ˈʌʔoʊ/ · UH-oh Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: uh oh, oh-oh, uhoh

A two-note signal that something has just gone wrong or is about to. The syllables are separated by a glottal stop — a small catch in the throat, not a consonant — and the pitch falls from high to low. Americans learn it as toddlers and use it for everything from a dropped fork to a warning light.

  1. "Uh-oh. The check engine light just came on."
  2. Uh-oh — she's using my full name.
  3. The baby dropped his spoon and said uh-oh, delighted with himself.
  4. Uh-oh, I'm pretty sure we took the wrong exit.
  5. "Uh-oh." "What?" "The Wi-Fi's down and the deadline is at noon."
  6. He heard the smoke alarm go off downstairs and thought uh-oh before he thought anything else.

See also: oh no, oops, yikes

yikes#

Pronunciation: /jaɪks/ · YIKES Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: yiiikes

A reaction to something alarming, awkward, or expensive — low-grade real fear plus a strong dose of I want no part of that. Online it has also become the standard American response to something socially embarrassing, where it means a quiet, judgmental that was a bad thing to say.

  1. "Yikes. Have you seen what they're charging for rent over there?"
  2. Yikes — that is a lot of blood for a paper cut.
  3. She saw the dent in the bumper and said yikes very quietly.
  4. "He said that out loud? In the meeting?" "Yikes."
  5. Yikes, the road is completely iced over.
  6. The forecast says nine inches tonight. Yikes.

See also: yipes, oh no, eek

yipes#

Pronunciation: /jaɪps/ · YIPES Type: primary · Register: informal, dated (cartoon flavor) Variants: yipe

An older, more cartoonish sibling of yikes, expressing sudden fright. Americans mostly know it from mid-century comics and Saturday-morning cartoons, so using it now sounds deliberately goofy.

  1. "Yipes! A ghost!" He ran straight through the wall and kept going.
  2. Yipes — I nearly stepped on the sprinkler head.
  3. My grandfather still says yipes when the total comes up at the gas pump.
  4. She let out a theatrical yipes and hid behind a couch cushion.
  5. He looked at the receipt, said yipes, and put half the groceries back.

See also: yikes, zoinks, eek

zoinks#

Pronunciation: /zɔɪŋks/ · ZOYNKS Type: primary · Register: informal, jocular (cartoon flavor)

A yelp of fright borrowed straight from Shaggy on Scooby-Doo. No American says it in real fear; saying it announces that you are playing the coward on purpose, which is the same trick yipes pulls.

  1. "Zoinks!" He backed out of the doorway and pointed down the basement stairs.
  2. Zoinks — there is definitely something living in the crawlspace.
  3. My roommate says zoinks every time the smoke detector chirps at three in the morning.
  4. She opened the hall closet, said zoinks, and shut it again without explaining.
  5. The whole group chat was one zoinks and a screenshot of the repair estimate.

See also: yipes, yikes, eek

Calls for help and emergency signals#

abandon ship#

Pronunciation: /əˌbændən ˈʃɪp/ · uh-BAN-duhn SHIP Type: phrase · Register: neutral in nautical use; informal and jocular elsewhere

The order to get off a sinking vessel, shouted as a warning that the ship can no longer be saved. On land, Americans use it jokingly about any project, party, or job that has obviously failed.

  1. "Abandon ship!" The captain was the last one over the rail.
  2. Abandon ship — the whole lower deck is filling with water.
  3. When the third founder quit, it was pretty much abandon ship around here.
  4. "Abandon ship!" he yelled, and all four kids jumped off the raft at once.
  5. Somebody below decks was screaming abandon ship before the alarm even sounded.

See also: man overboard, mayday, run

fire#

Pronunciation: /ˈfaɪər/, also /faɪr/ · FYE-er Type: secondary · Register: neutral

Shouted to warn everyone in earshot that there is a fire and they need to get out. It is the one alarm word Americans are taught never to shout falsely, which is where the phrase about yelling fire in a crowded theater comes from. The order to shoot a weapon is a separate use of the same word.

  1. "Fire! Everybody out — take the stairs, not the elevator!"
  2. Someone shouted fire and the entire restaurant stood up at once.
  3. Fire — call 911, the garage is going up.
  4. You can't yell fire in a crowded theater just to see what happens.
  5. She pounded on the neighbors' door yelling fire until the lights came on.

See also: help, run, clear

help#

Pronunciation: /hɛlp/ · HELP Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: help me, somebody help

The basic English cry for assistance in danger. Shouted on its own it means you are in trouble right now; said flatly in conversation or typed online, it can also mean overwhelmed rather than endangered.

  1. "Help! Somebody call an ambulance!"
  2. She was out past the buoy yelling help and waving both arms.
  3. Help — I can't get the door open and there's smoke in the hallway.
  4. A small voice from the top of the tree said, "Help, I'm stuck."
  5. My group project partner disappeared two weeks ago. Help.
  6. He mouthed the word help at me across the dinner table.

See also: mayday, SOS, fire

man down#

Pronunciation: /ˌmæn ˈdaʊn/ · man-DOWN Type: phrase · Register: neutral in military, police, and emergency-services use; informal and jocular elsewhere Variants: officer down

Called out when someone has been hurt and is on the ground, so that help comes to that spot immediately. Civilians borrow it half-jokingly for anyone taken out by a cold, a hangover, or a double shift.

  1. "Man down!" the sergeant shouted into his radio.
  2. Man down — we need a medic at the fifty-yard line.
  3. Somebody yelled man down and three people came running with the first-aid kit.
  4. Man down: Kevin ate the gas station sushi.
  5. The dispatcher heard man down and had units rolling in under a minute.

See also: help, medic, man overboard

man overboard#

Pronunciation: /ˌmæn ˈoʊvərˌbɔrd/ · man-OH-ver-bord Type: phrase · Register: neutral (nautical)

Shouted the moment someone falls off a boat, so the crew can cut the engines, mark the spot, and turn back. Ashore it gets used jokingly whenever anyone falls into water — or drops out of a group.

  1. "Man overboard! Point at him and do not take your eyes off him."
  2. Somebody screamed man overboard and the engines cut immediately.
  3. Man overboard — starboard side, about thirty yards back!
  4. My cousin went off the dock and my uncle hollered man overboard before he even surfaced.
  5. The drill starts when the instructor calls man overboard without any warning.

See also: abandon ship, help, mayday

mayday#

Pronunciation: /ˈmeɪˌdeɪ/ · MAY-day Type: primary · Register: neutral (radio and aviation procedure) Variants: mayday, mayday, mayday

The international radio distress call, used only for life-threatening emergencies and spoken three times in a row so it cannot be mistaken for anything else. It comes from the French venez m'aider, "come help me," and has nothing to do with the month of May. Making a false mayday call is a federal crime in the United States, and the Coast Guard prosecutes it. Away from a radio, Americans use the word jokingly to mean I am in over my head.

  1. "Mayday, mayday, mayday. Cessna four-two-alpha, engine failure, going down."
  2. The Coast Guard picked up a mayday from a fishing boat forty miles out.
  3. Mayday — I've got two sick kids and a deadline at noon.
  4. You do not say mayday on the radio unless somebody's life is on the line.
  5. He was so calm you would never have guessed he was calling in a mayday.

See also: SOS, pan-pan, help

medic#

Pronunciation: /ˈmɛdɪk/ · MED-ik Type: secondary · Register: neutral in military and emergency-services use; informal and jocular elsewhere Variants: corpsman

Shouted for someone who is hurt, to pull the nearest person with a first-aid bag to that spot. In the Army it is literal and deadly serious; the Navy and Marine Corps equivalent is corpsman. Civilians borrow it on ball fields and, as a joke, for anything from a stubbed toe to a hangover.

  1. "Medic!" Two of them were already dragging him behind the wall.
  2. Somebody yelled medic and the trainer came off the bench at a dead run.
  3. Medic — we've got a player down along the third-base line.
  4. He caught his shin on the coffee table and hollered medic at nobody in particular.
  5. In the Marines you call for a corpsman, not a medic, and the difference matters to them.

See also: help, man down, mayday

pan-pan#

Pronunciation: /ˈpɑn ˈpɑn/, often Americanized to /ˈpæn ˈpæn/ · PAHN-PAHN Type: primary · Register: neutral (radio procedure); almost never heard outside a cockpit or a marine radio Variants: pan-pan-pan, pan pan pan

The urgency call one step below mayday: something is wrong and you need help, but nobody's life is in immediate danger — an engine out on a boat that is still floating, a sick passenger, a pilot lost and low on fuel. Like mayday it is repeated three times so it cannot be mistaken for anything else, and like mayday it is French: panne, "breakdown."

  1. "Pan-pan, pan-pan, pan-pan. Cessna two-one-tango, low on fuel, requesting vectors to the nearest field."
  2. He put out a pan-pan when the alternator quit, then upgraded it to a mayday an hour later.
  3. The Coast Guard logged a pan-pan from a sailboat with a line wrapped around its propeller.
  4. If nobody is dying, it's a pan-pan, not a mayday.
  5. Our instructor made us practice the pan-pan call until we stopped stumbling over the words.

See also: mayday, SOS, help

SOS#

Pronunciation: /ˌɛs oʊ ˈɛs/ · ess-oh-ESS Type: primary · Register: neutral Variants: S.O.S.

The Morse distress signal — three dots, three dashes, three dots — used as a written or flashed call for help. The letters do not stand for anything; the pattern was chosen because it is easy to tap and hard to mistake. In everyday American use, an SOS is any urgent plea for rescue, including a text to a friend.

  1. They stamped a giant SOS into the snow so the helicopter would see it.
  2. "I got your SOS. What's going on?"
  3. She sent up an SOS to the group chat at two in the morning.
  4. The keeper flashed SOS from the lighthouse until a ship turned toward shore.
  5. SOS — my ride bailed and the last bus leaves in ten minutes.

See also: mayday, help

Warnings of danger, impact, and things in motion#

behind you#

Pronunciation: /bɪˈhaɪnd juː/ · bih-HYND YOO Type: phrase · Register: informal (standard in restaurant kitchens and busy workspaces) Variants: behind, behind ya

Called out to tell someone you are passing close behind them, so they do not turn around into you — usually while carrying something hot, sharp, or heavy. In kitchens it is barked as a single word, behind.

  1. "Behind you!" He slid past with a full sheet pan in both hands.
  2. Behind — hot pan, hot pan!
  3. In that kitchen you say behind you or you end up wearing the soup.
  4. She called behind you twice and he still stepped right into her.
  5. "Behind ya," the mover said, backing a dresser down the hallway.

See also: coming through, heads up, watch out

bombs away#

Pronunciation: /ˌbɑmz əˈweɪ/ · bomz uh-WAY Type: phrase · Register: informal, usually jocular

Originally the bombardier's call that the payload has been released; now used almost entirely in fun, to announce that you are about to drop, pour, or launch something and that anyone underneath should move.

  1. "Bombs away!" He cannonballed into the deep end.
  2. Bombs away — I'm dumping the cooler over the side of the deck.
  3. The kids yelled bombs away and let all the water balloons go at once.
  4. She tipped the entire bag of chocolate chips into the bowl. "Bombs away."
  5. He held the piñata rope, shouted bombs away, and let it drop.

See also: fore, heads up, timber

brace#

Pronunciation: /breɪs/ · BRAYSS Type: secondary · Register: neutral (aviation and emergency procedure); informal when figurative Variants: brace, brace, brace, brace for impact, brace yourself

The command to get into a protective position because an impact is coming. Flight crews shout it in threes — brace, brace, brace — and Americans use brace yourself conversationally to warn that bad news is about to land.

  1. "Brace, brace, brace!" the flight attendant shouted down the aisle.
  2. Brace for impact — we're going in hard.
  3. He saw the truck coming through the intersection and had one second to yell brace.
  4. Brace yourself: the estimate came back at eleven thousand dollars.
  5. "Brace!" somebody yelled, and everyone grabbed the seat in front of them.

See also: incoming, take cover, duck

clear#

Pronunciation: /klɪr/ · KLEER Type: secondary · Register: neutral (medical, firearms, and construction procedure) Variants: all clear, stand clear

Two senses. 1. Shouted before something dangerous happens, to make everyone get back: a defibrillator shock, a swinging load, a controlled blast. 2. All clear is the opposite signal — the danger has passed and it is safe to come out.

  1. "Clear!" She waited until every hand was off the patient, then delivered the shock.
  2. Clear — nobody stands under that beam while it's moving.
  3. The range officer shouted clear before anyone walked downrange.
  4. They gave the all clear about twenty minutes after the tornado passed.
  5. "All clear," the firefighter called from the doorway. "You can go back inside."
  6. We sat in the hallway until the all clear came over the intercom.

See also: fire in the hole, stand back, heads up

coming through#

Pronunciation: /ˌkʌmɪŋ ˈθru/ · kum-ing THROO Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: comin' through

Announces that you are moving through a crowd or a doorway right now and that people need to make room. It is a warning and a request at the same time, and it is normal in busy hallways, kitchens, and crowded bars.

  1. "Coming through!" He had a keg on one shoulder and no free hands.
  2. Coming through — hot coffee, hot coffee.
  3. The paramedics shouted coming through and the crowd split down the middle.
  4. She said comin' through and rolled the cart straight at us.
  5. Coming through, folks — wheelchair on your left.

See also: gangway, behind you, on your left

duck#

Pronunciation: /dʌk/ · DUK Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: duck down, get down

Shouted to tell someone to drop their head fast, because something is about to hit it — a branch, a ball, a low beam. It is the shortest warning there is, and it works because saying it takes about as long as doing it.

  1. "Duck!" The line drive went straight over the dugout.
  2. Duck — the garage door is still coming down.
  3. He yelled duck and I dropped without even looking up.
  4. "Duck down, they're pointing the camera this way."
  5. Somebody shouted duck a half second too late, and the frisbee caught him in the ear.

See also: heads up, hit the deck, look out

fire in the hole#

Pronunciation: /ˌfaɪər ɪn ðə ˈhoʊl/ · FYE-er in the HOHL Type: phrase · Register: neutral in demolition and military use; informal and jocular elsewhere

The standard warning that an explosive is about to go off and everyone should take cover. It comes from mining, where the hole was the bore drilled for the charge. Video games have made it familiar to Americans who have never been near dynamite, and it is now used as a joke before anything loud or messy.

  1. "Fire in the hole!" Three seconds later the stump was gone.
  2. The blaster called fire in the hole twice before he pressed the switch.
  3. Fire in the hole — everybody get behind the truck.
  4. He tossed the grenade and yelled fire in the hole out of pure habit.
  5. "Fire in the hole," my dad announced, and lowered the turkey into the fryer.

See also: clear, bombs away, incoming

fore#

Pronunciation: /fɔr/ · FOR Type: primary · Register: neutral (golf)

The golfer's shout warning anyone downrange that a ball is heading toward them and they should cover up. It sounds exactly like four and for, so on a course the situation does the disambiguating. It has no use off the golf course except as a joke.

  1. "Fore!" Everyone on the next tee ducked behind their bags.
  2. He sliced it into the parking lot and yelled fore about a second too late.
  3. You're supposed to shout fore the instant you see it heading toward people.
  4. Fore — heads up on the left side of the fairway!
  5. Somebody hollered fore, and a ball bounced off the cart path right next to us.

See also: heads up, duck, look out

gangway#

Pronunciation: /ˈɡæŋˌweɪ/ · GANG-way Type: secondary · Register: informal; dated outside docks, warehouses, and stage crews

A shout for people to clear a path, from the nautical word for the passage on and off a ship. It has an old-movie flavor now — most Americans say coming through instead — though it survives on working waterfronts, on loading docks, and backstage.

  1. "Gangway!" The stretcher came around the corner at a dead run.
  2. Gangway — this thing is a lot heavier than it looks.
  3. He barreled down the aisle shouting gangway like a cartoon character.
  4. "Gangway, boys," the old stagehand said, and the flat swung past our heads.
  5. Somebody yelled gangway and the whole crowd on the pier stepped back.

See also: coming through, behind you, look out

geronimo#

Pronunciation: /dʒəˈrɑnəˌmoʊ/ · juh-RON-uh-moh Type: secondary · Register: informal (jocular); the borrowed name makes it a poor choice in some company

Shouted while jumping from a height — off a diving board, out of an airplane, into a pile of leaves. It started with American paratroopers in World War II, who took it from the name of the Chiricahua Apache leader Geronimo. It warns whoever is below as much as it announces the jumper's nerve. Worth knowing before you use it: Geronimo was a real man who died a prisoner of war, and plenty of Native Americans hear the shout as a thoughtless borrowing rather than a tribute.

  1. "Geronimo!" He went off the high dive with his knees tucked to his chest.
  2. The kid yelled geronimo and cannonballed into the deep end.
  3. Geronimo — I'm jumping on three, so move!
  4. My uncle still shouts geronimo every time he wades into a cold lake.
  5. The whole stick of paratroopers went out the door yelling geronimo.

See also: bombs away, timber

heads up#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɛdz ˈʌp/ · hedz-UP Type: phrase · Register: informal (very common; fine at work) Variants: heads-up, head's up

Shouted, it means something is flying toward you and you should look. Said calmly, it means here is some advance warning, and hyphenated as a noun, a heads-up is that warning itself. The literal shout is oddly backwards — what you actually want to do is duck.

  1. "Heads up!" The ball came down two feet from the picnic table.
  2. Heads up — they're painting the stairwell, so use the elevator today.
  3. Just a heads up: the client moved the call to eight in the morning.
  4. He shouted heads up and the entire sideline scattered.
  5. Heads up, there's black ice on the bridge.
  6. Thanks for the heads-up about the road closure.

See also: look out, watch out, duck

hit the deck#

Pronunciation: /ˌhɪt ðə ˈdɛk/ · hit thuh DEK Type: phrase · Register: informal (military and nautical in origin) Variants: hit the dirt, hit the floor

Get flat on the ground this second, because something is coming over your head — gunfire, a swinging board, a thrown bottle. Deck is the floor of a ship; the Army version is hit the dirt. It asks for more than duck does: your whole body down, not just your head.

  1. "Hit the deck!" Everybody was flat on the floor before the second shot.
  2. Hit the deck — he's swinging that ladder around like nobody else is in the room.
  3. Somebody yelled hit the deck and the entire tailgate went face-down in the grass.
  4. "Hit the dirt!" he shouted, half a second before the branch came down through the tent.
  5. The catcher screamed hit the deck when the bat got away from the batter and went into the stands.

See also: duck, take cover, brace

incoming#

Pronunciation: /ˈɪnˌkʌmɪŋ/ · IN-kum-ing Type: secondary · Register: neutral in military use; informal and jocular elsewhere

A warning that something is arriving fast and you should take cover — originally artillery or mortar fire. In civilian American speech it announces anything unwelcome heading your way, from a toddler to an email from Legal.

  1. "Incoming!" Everybody flattened out behind the wall.
  2. Incoming — she's walking over here with the complaint form.
  3. He heard the whistle and screamed incoming before he hit the dirt.
  4. Incoming! Nine-year-olds with water balloons, two o'clock.
  5. Somebody in accounting yelled incoming the second the audit email went out.

See also: duck, heads up, brace

look alive#

Pronunciation: /ˌlʊk əˈlaɪv/ · look uh-LYVE Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: look sharp

A sharp order to wake up and pay attention, because something is about to happen or has already started. It is half warning and half rebuke, and it is common in sports, kitchens, and any job with a supervisor walking the floor.

  1. "Look alive! Ball's in play!"
  2. Look alive — the health inspector just pulled into the lot.
  3. The coach clapped twice and yelled look alive at the whole bench.
  4. "Look alive, gentlemen. Doors open in five minutes."
  5. He was half asleep at the register until his manager said look alive.

See also: heads up, hey, watch it

look out#

Pronunciation: /ˌlʊk ˈaʊt/ · look-OWT Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: look out below

The general-purpose American warning shout: the danger is right there, react now. It is interchangeable with watch out in almost every situation and sounds a shade more urgent. Written as one word, lookout is a noun and not an interjection.

  1. "Look out!" She grabbed his sleeve and pulled him back onto the curb.
  2. Look out — that step is completely rotted through.
  3. He shouted look out and everybody scattered off the ice.
  4. "Look out below!" The bucket came down fast on a rope.
  5. Look out for the dog; he bolts the second the door opens.
  6. Somebody yelled look out, and the whole line of skaters swerved at once.

See also: watch out, heads up, careful

on your left#

Pronunciation: /ˌɑn jər ˈlɛft/ · on yer LEFT Type: phrase · Register: neutral (standard on bike paths, running trails, and in warehouses) Variants: on your right, on the left

Called by someone overtaking you, to say which side they are passing on so you do not drift into them. American cyclists pass on the left by default, so on your left is the usual form; on your right is the exception and is worth saying twice.

  1. "On your left!" The cyclist was past us before we finished turning around.
  2. On your left — thanks, folks!
  3. She called on your left from about thirty feet back, which is exactly right.
  4. "On your right, sorry — the path is blocked on the other side."
  5. If you pass a walker without saying on your left, you're the problem.

See also: behind you, coming through, heads up

run#

Pronunciation: /rʌn/ · RUN Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: run for it

The bluntest warning in the language: the danger is too big to face, so leave now. Shouted on its own it is genuinely frightening; used lightly, it is a joke about an unpleasant person or chore heading your way.

  1. "Run!" The whole hillside was coming down behind them.
  2. Run — don't stop, don't look back, just go.
  3. He screamed run and shoved his sister toward the door.
  4. "Run for it!" The rain came down like somebody had turned on a faucet.
  5. "Run," my sister said, only half joking, when I told her which department had offered me the job.

See also: help, look out, abandon ship

stand back#

Pronunciation: /ˌstænd ˈbæk/ · stand BAK Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: stay back, get back, everybody back

Move away and give the dangerous thing room. Unlike look out, it does not say where the danger is or what it is about to do; it just opens up space, which is why it is the shout police officers, firefighters, and paramedics use on a crowd.

  1. "Stand back!" The whole crowd moved at once when they saw the wire down on the pavement.
  2. Stand back — that cable is under a lot more tension than it looks.
  3. The firefighter waved us off the porch and told us to stay back until the truck got there.
  4. "Everybody back!" A deputy was already dragging cones across both lanes.
  5. Somebody hollered get back, and every kid on the pond skated for the bank.

See also: clear, look out, watch out

take cover#

Pronunciation: /ˌteɪk ˈkʌvər/ · tayk KUH-ver Type: phrase · Register: neutral (military, police, and severe-weather use); informal and jocular elsewhere Variants: take shelter, get to cover

Put something solid between yourself and whatever is coming — a wall, a truck, a basement. It is the standard American wording in tornado and hail warnings and under gunfire, and it survives as a joke for hiding from a person you would rather not talk to.

  1. "Take cover!" Half the squad was behind the berm before the first round landed.
  2. The siren went off and the radio kept saying take cover in a basement or an interior room.
  3. Take cover — that hail is the size of golf balls.
  4. Somebody yelled take cover as the wind lifted the whole tent off the concession stand.
  5. She saw her ex come through the door and muttered take cover into her coffee.

See also: hit the deck, brace, duck

timber#

Pronunciation: /ˈtɪmbər/ · TIM-ber Type: secondary · Register: neutral in logging; informal and jocular elsewhere

The logger's call warning everyone that a tree is coming down and to get clear. Off the job site, Americans shout it whenever anything tall goes over — a bookshelf, a Christmas tree, a friend tipping out of a lawn chair.

  1. "Timber!" The pine came down exactly where he said it would.
  2. He gave the trunk one more cut and yelled timber.
  3. Timber — everybody get away from the fence line!
  4. The ladder started to go and somebody in the back hollered timber.
  5. She pushed the last block and said timber as the whole tower folded over.

See also: heads up, look out, bombs away

watch it#

Pronunciation: /ˈwɑtʃ ɪt/ · WOTCH it Type: phrase · Register: informal; sense 2 is confrontational and lands as a threat Variants: watch yourself

Two senses. 1. A quick warning about a physical hazard, the same as watch out but curter. 2. A warning to a person about their behavior — closer to a threat, and genuinely rude if the tone is sharp.

  1. "Watch it — that railing isn't attached to anything."
  2. He backed into me on the escalator and I said watch it louder than I meant to.
  3. Watch it, the floor is soaking wet right there.
  4. "Watch it, buddy. That's my sister you're talking about."
  5. She told the kids to watch it or the game was going back in the box.
  6. Watch yourself around him — he repeats everything he hears.

See also: watch out, careful, hey

watch out#

Pronunciation: /ˌwɑtʃ ˈaʊt/ · wotch-OWT Type: phrase · Register: neutral

The everyday American warning that something dangerous is right there. It is the safest choice of all the warning shouts — it works with strangers, at work, with children, and in writing — and it takes for when you name the danger.

  1. "Watch out!" The bike came through the crosswalk without slowing down at all.
  2. Watch out for the second step; it's shorter than the rest of them.
  3. She yelled watch out and he stopped with his foot an inch from the glass.
  4. Watch out — that pan handle has been sitting over the burner this whole time.
  5. "Watch out, honey. Hot."
  6. Everyone in the office will tell you to watch out for parking enforcement on Third.

See also: look out, watch it, careful

watch your step#

Pronunciation: /ˌwɑtʃ jər ˈstɛp/ · wotch yer STEP Type: phrase · Register: neutral Variants: watch your footing

Two senses. 1. A literal warning about uneven, slick, or dark ground. 2. A figurative warning to be careful how you behave, usually from someone with power over you. Americans also see it on signs at curbs and thresholds, where the British equivalent is mind the step.

  1. "Watch your step — nobody salted the ice out front."
  2. Watch your step getting off; it's a big drop down to the platform.
  3. The usher said watch your step and pointed his flashlight at the stairs.
  4. "Watch your step around the new director. She takes notes."
  5. Watch your step, son. One more warning and you're out of the program.
  6. There's a yellow sign at the door that just says watch your step.

Calls to stop, hold, or slow down#

careful#

Pronunciation: /ˈkɛrfəl/ · KAIR-ful Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: be careful, careful now, careful there

A gentler warning than look out — it tells someone to slow down and pay attention rather than to react instantly. Used as an interjection it drops the be, and it is what Americans say to someone carrying something heavy, holding a knife, or standing too close to an edge.

  1. "Careful — that plate just came out of the oven."
  2. Careful, buddy. Both hands on the railing.
  3. She handed me the box and said careful, there's glass in there.
  4. "Careful now," he said, easing the ladder off the back of the truck.
  5. Careful — you're about to promise her something you can't actually deliver.
  6. He was going too fast on the gravel and his mom yelled careful from the porch.

See also: easy, watch out, watch your step

easy#

Pronunciation: /ˈizi/ · EE-zee Type: secondary · Register: informal Variants: easy now, easy does it, easy there, take it easy

Said to calm someone or something down — a spooked horse, an angry friend, a nervous dog, a person about to drop their end of the couch. It means slow, gentle, no sudden moves, and it counts as a warning because sudden moves are exactly what would cause the accident.

  1. "Easy, girl." He kept one hand flat on the horse's neck.
  2. Easy — let me get my grip before you lift your end.
  3. "Easy now, nobody is accusing you of anything."
  4. She poured the last of it in slowly, saying easy, easy the whole time.
  5. Easy does it. Lower it about an inch at a time.
  6. He came at me hot and I put both hands up and said easy.

See also: whoa, steady, careful

freeze#

Pronunciation: /friz/ · FREEZ Type: secondary · Register: neutral (police command); informal in games and play

An order to stop moving entirely and immediately, most familiar from American police work and from movies about it. Off duty, it is what you shout when someone is about to step in something, walk through wet paint, or wreck a photo.

  1. "Freeze! Police! Let me see your hands!"
  2. Freeze — don't move, there's a wasp on your shoulder.
  3. He yelled freeze and the kids all locked in place mid-dance.
  4. "Freeze!" she said. "You have paint on your shoes and that is a white rug."
  5. Somebody shouted freeze and every single person in the room actually stopped.

See also: halt, stop, hold on

halt#

Pronunciation: /hɔlt/ · HAWLT Type: secondary · Register: formal (military and police); dated or jocular in everyday speech

A formal command to stop where you are, used by sentries, drill instructors, and officers directing traffic. In ordinary American conversation it sounds stiff or theatrical — stop is the normal word.

  1. "Halt! Who goes there?"
  2. The guard raised a hand and said halt before we got anywhere near the gate.
  3. Halt — this area is restricted past the fence line.
  4. He shouted halt in his best drill-sergeant voice and the kids ignored him completely.
  5. "Company, halt!" Forty pairs of boots stopped on the same beat.

See also: freeze, stop, hold up

hey#

Pronunciation: /heɪ/ · HAY Type: primary · Register: informal; the warning sense is sharp enough to sound like a rebuke, especially aimed at a stranger Variants: hey, hey, hey

As a warning, a sharp hey means stop that or that's dangerous — it is the sound of catching someone in the act or heading off an accident. In a friendly tone the same word is just a greeting or a call for attention; the warning sense lives entirely in the volume and the falling pitch.

  1. "Hey! Get away from the edge!"
  2. Hey, hey, hey — put that down before somebody gets hurt.
  3. He yelled hey and the guy dropped the bike and ran.
  4. "Hey!" The lifeguard was on his feet before the whistle reached his mouth.
  5. Hey — watch where you're swinging that thing.
  6. A sharp hey from the next room is usually enough to stop the dog.

See also: watch it, look alive, whoa

hold on#

Pronunciation: /ˌhoʊld ˈɑn/ · hohld-ON Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hold on a second, hold on tight

Two senses. 1. Wait — stop what you are doing, because something is wrong or needs checking. 2. A literal warning to grab something and grip it, because a jolt is coming.

  1. "Hold on — you signed this without reading it?"
  2. Hold on, don't put any weight on that leg until we look at it.
  3. "Hold on tight!" The boat hit the wake and everybody left their seats.
  4. Hold on — I hear something moving inside the wall.
  5. She said hold on and pulled the fuse before he touched the panel.
  6. "Hold on, hold on. Nobody is going anywhere until we count heads."

See also: hold up, wait, whoa

hold up#

Pronunciation: /ˌhoʊld ˈʌp/ · hohld-UP Type: phrase · Register: informal Variants: hold up a second

Stop moving or stop talking for a moment, usually because something is not right. It is the more casual of the pair with hold on, and it is extremely common in American speech as a way of pumping the brakes on somebody's plan.

  1. "Hold up. Something is leaking under the sink."
  2. Hold up — nobody drives anywhere until that tire gets checked.
  3. He was halfway out the door when I said hold up.
  4. "Hold up, you're telling me the gas was on all night?"
  5. Hold up a second, let me make sure the trailer is actually hitched.

See also: hold on, wait, whoa

steady#

Pronunciation: /ˈstɛdi/ · STED-ee Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: steady now, steady there

Said to someone who is wobbling, physically or emotionally, to keep them from going over. It is most at home around horses, ladders, boats, and people who have just stood up too fast. Steady on is British and sounds foreign in an American mouth.

  1. "Steady — I've got you. One step at a time."
  2. He held the bottom of the ladder and said steady, steady while she climbed down.
  3. Steady, boy. Nobody here is going to hurt you.
  4. He went red reading the email, and his wife said steady before he could start typing a reply.
  5. "Steady," the coxswain called as the shell rocked in the chop.

See also: easy, whoa, careful

stop#

Pronunciation: /stɑp/ · STOP Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: stop right there

The plainest command in the language, and the one Americans actually shout when someone is about to walk into traffic. Repeated fast — stop, stop, stop — it means the danger is immediate.

  1. "Stop!" She caught his backpack a foot from the curb.
  2. Stop, stop, stop — you're about to back into the mailbox.
  3. He screamed stop and the whole crew froze on the scaffold.
  4. "Stop right there. That is a live wire."
  5. Stop — don't drink that, it isn't water.

See also: freeze, halt, whoa

uh-uh#

Pronunciation: /ˈʌʔʌ/ — stress on the first syllable, pitch falling from high to low; often produced with the lips closed, which turns it into a nasal hum · UH-uh Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: unh-unh, nuh-uh, mm-mm

Two low grunts with a glottal catch between them and the stress on the first, meaning no — and, as a warning, don't you dare. It is what an American parent says across a room to a child reaching for something off limits. Do not confuse it with uh-huh, which means yes: the vowels are identical in the two words, and what separates them is the h and the melody. Uh-huh has a real h, takes its stress on the second syllable, and rises low-to-high; uh-uh has a glottal catch instead of the h, stresses the first syllable, and falls high-to-low.

  1. "Uh-uh. Not that drawer."
  2. Uh-uh — hands off, that pan is still hot.
  3. She saw him reaching for the cake and said uh-uh without even looking up.
  4. "Can I take the car?" "Uh-uh. Not after last time."
  5. He started toward the fence and his mother's uh-uh stopped him cold.

See also: hey, stop, watch it

wait#

Pronunciation: /weɪt/ · WAYT Type: secondary · Register: neutral Variants: wait up, wait a second, wait, wait, wait

Stop and do nothing for a moment — the most common American way to head off a mistake already in progress. Tripled, wait, wait, wait means something is wrong and everyone needs to stop right now; wait up means stop so I can catch up to you.

  1. "Wait! Don't plug that in yet."
  2. Wait, wait, wait — that is not the right cable.
  3. He was already dialing when I said wait.
  4. "Wait up!" She was thirty yards behind us on the trail.
  5. Wait — did anybody actually turn off the propane?
  6. She yelled wait and the elevator doors stopped an inch apart.

See also: hold on, hold up, whoa

whoa#

Pronunciation: /woʊ/, also /hwoʊ/ · WOH Type: primary · Register: informal Variants: woah, whoa there, whoa, whoa, whoa

Originally the call that tells a horse to stop, now the everyday American brake pedal for people: slow down, back up, something just went too far. Repeated — whoa, whoa, whoa — it means stop immediately. The dictionary spelling is whoa; woah is common online but still reads as a misspelling in edited writing.

  1. "Whoa!" He caught the shelf before it went all the way over.
  2. Whoa, whoa, whoa — that is not what I agreed to.
  3. "Whoa, girl." She pulled back gently on the reins.
  4. Whoa — you are way too close to the edge of that dock.
  5. He came flying around the corner and I put a hand up and said whoa.
  6. "Whoa there. Let's back this up and start over."

See also: easy, steady, hold on
