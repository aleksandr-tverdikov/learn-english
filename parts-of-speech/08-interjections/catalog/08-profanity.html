Profanity used as interjections#

Taboo words thrown out as pure reaction — pain, shock, anger, disbelief, delight — plus the softened substitutes Americans reach for when the real word would cost them something.

← Back to the interjections index · All categories

Register warning — read this first#

Every word in this file is offensive to someone. They are documented here because a learner who does not know them cannot tell how hard they are landing when an American says one — and cannot tell that shoot and shit are the same slot with very different consequences. Learn to recognize all of them. Produce only the ones you have heard people in your own situation use.

Each entry carries an Offensiveness rating on this four-step scale:

RatingWhat it costs youExamples
mildAlmost nothing. A stranger may raise an eyebrow; nobody complains to HR.crap, darn, heck, gosh, shoot
moderateFine among friends, risky with strangers, clients, children, or religious people.damn, hell, Jesus, screw you
strongMarks the speech as coarse. In a workplace it gets noticed; repeated, it gets reported.shit, bullshit, asshole, son of a bitch, goddammit
very strongThe heaviest words in the language. In writing or in public they are a genuine incident.fuck and everything built from it

Two ratings that are not on the scale, because they are worse than anything here: slurs aimed at race, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexual orientation, or disability. Those are not interjections and they are not in this file. There is no context in which a learner should use one.

This file also covers minced oaths — deliberate distortions of taboo words that keep the rhythm and drop the offense (gosh from God, darn from damn, heck from hell, shoot from shit, frick from fuck). They occupy exactly the same slot in the sentence as the words they replace, which is why they are grouped here rather than scattered through the other categories. Punctuation is simple throughout: these words take an exclamation point when shouted, a period when muttered, and a comma when they lead into a clause — and Americans mutter them far more often than they shout them.

Never in

The standard American shout at somebody whose behavior has just been selfish or reckless, and the single most common thing Americans say in traffic. Unlike most of this file it names a person rather than a situation, which is why it starts arguments that shit and damn do not.

  1. "Asshole!" she yelled at the car that ran the red light.
  2. Somebody had parked across two spaces. Asshole.
  3. "He fired her by text message." "What an asshole."
  4. "Asshole," he muttered, and let the elevator doors close on the guy running for it.
  5. Asshole — he knows that's my seat.

bastard#

Pronunciation
/ˈbæstərd/ · BAS-terd
Русский

Type

Safe in
venting among close friends; swearing at an object or a situation when nobody is around
Never in
work, school, anything written with your name on it, or within earshot of the person it is about
Variants

An outburst aimed at someone who has just done something cruel or underhanded — and, just as often in American speech, at an object or a situation that has beaten you. As an interjection it is an explosion, not a description, and it is far more common in Britain and Ireland than in the United States.

  1. "Bastard!" he shouted at the truck that had cut him off.
  2. The bolt sheared clean off in his hand. Bastard.
  3. He took the last parking spot right in front of me — bastard.
  4. "Bastards." She read the layoff email a second time.
  5. The lock finally gave, and he muttered, "Bastard," as he shoved the door open.

British and Irish, familiar to Americans mainly from television. fuck#

Pronunciation
/fʌk/ · FUK

The heaviest single word in American English, and as an interjection it is a pure pressure release — pain, panic, disaster, or the moment a mistake becomes irreversible.   5. The whole shelf came down. Fuck.
