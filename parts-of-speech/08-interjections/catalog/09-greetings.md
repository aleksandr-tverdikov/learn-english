# Greetings, farewells, and social formulas

> The interjections that open and close a conversation — the words you say not because they carry information, but because saying nothing would be rude.

[← Back to the interjections index](../README.md) &middot; [All categories](README.md)

These are the most formulaic interjections in English, and the most socially loaded. Almost none of them mean what their parts say — *how are you* is not a question about your health, *long time no see* is ungrammatical on purpose, and *goodbye* has not been *God be with ye* for four hundred years — so they have to be learned whole, as chunks. The risk for a learner is never grammar; it is register: *hey* and *hi* look interchangeable until *hey* lands in an email to a client, and *good day* sounds courteous until an American hears it as a door being shut. Two mechanical notes that run through the whole group: a greeting takes a comma before a name (*Hi, Dana* — though American business email usually drops it: *Hi Dana,*), and time-of-day formulas split cleanly into greetings (*good morning, good afternoon, good evening*) and one farewell (*good night*), which is the single most common mistake in this category.

The category does not stop at the door. The same social machinery runs through the middle of a conversation too — *thanks* and the four or five things Americans say back to it, the reflex *sorry* you produce when you brush past a stranger, the *bless you* a sneeze obliges everyone within earshot to supply, and the toasts and congratulations that mark somebody else's good news. Those formulas fill the last three sections, and they obey the same rule as the greetings: what matters is not what the words mean but that you produced one at the moment it was owed. Two of them carry real social freight in the United States. *No problem* versus *you're welcome* splits roughly along generational lines, and older listeners sometimes hear the first as grudging; and *cheers* is a drinking toast here, not a way to say thanks or goodbye, however British television may have taught you otherwise.

## General greetings

### ahoy

**Pronunciation:** /əˈhɔɪ/ &middot; *uh-HOY*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ahoy there*, *ship ahoy*

A nautical hail used to get a boat's or a person's attention. On land Americans reach for it only playfully — to sound piratical, or to make an ordinary greeting deliberately silly.

1. "*Ahoy!*" the captain called across the water. "Do you need a tow?"
2. *Ahoy* there, matey — the pizza's here.
3. He answers his phone with "*Ahoy*" because he read that Alexander Graham Bell wanted it that way.
4. *Ahoy!* Anybody home?
5. My uncle greets every kid at the marina with a big *ahoy*.

**See also:** [hello](#hello), [yo](#yo), [hey](#hey)

### aloha

**Pronunciation:** /əˈloʊhɑ/ &middot; *uh-LOH-hah*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional

The Hawaiian word for love and goodwill, used in Hawaii as both *hello* and *goodbye*. On the mainland it shows up mostly in tourist and luau settings, where it can sound like part of a costume.

1. *Aloha!* Welcome to the Big Island.
2. The flight attendant said *aloha* to every passenger stepping off the plane.
3. *Aloha*, and thank you for spending the evening with us.
4. She ended the call with a soft "*Aloha*, see you in September."
5. Every sign in the lobby said *Aloha* in big painted letters.

**See also:** [hello](#hello), [goodbye](#goodbye), [ciao](#ciao)

### as-salamu alaykum

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑs səˈlɑmu əˈleɪkʊm/ &middot; *ahs-suh-LAH-moo uh-LAY-koom*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *salaam*, *salaam alaikum*, *as-salaam alaykum*

Arabic for *peace be upon you*, the standard greeting among Muslims in the United States and one a non-Muslim is welcome to return. The fixed reply is *wa alaykumu s-salam* — "and upon you, peace." Shortened to a bare *salaam*, it does the ordinary work of *hi*.

1. "*As-salamu alaykum*," he said, stepping out of his shoes at the door.
2. *As-salamu alaykum*, and thank you all for coming out on a cold night.
3. She answered the phone with *salaam* and switched to English when she heard my voice.
4. "*As-salamu alaykum*." "*Wa alaykumu s-salam.* Come in, come in."
5. The imam greeted every family in the parking lot with a handshake and a quiet *salaam*.

**See also:** [shalom](#shalom), [namaste](#namaste), [hello](#hello)

### greetings

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɡritɪŋz/ &middot; *GREE-tingz*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *greetings and salutations*

A stiff, ceremonial hello. It is still normal at the top of a formal announcement or a postcard, but in conversation Americans use it as a joke — the way a robot, an alien, or a cartoon villain would say hello.

1. *Greetings*, and welcome to the annual shareholders' meeting.
2. *Greetings*, earthlings. Take me to your leader.
3. *Greetings* from Yellowstone! Wish you were here.
4. "*Greetings*, humans," he said, shuffling into the kitchen in his bathrobe.
5. His draft notice opened with a single word: *Greetings*.

**See also:** [salutations](#salutations), [hello](#hello), [good day](#good-day)

### hello

**Pronunciation:** /həˈloʊ/, also /hɛˈloʊ/ &middot; *huh-LOH, also heh-LOH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *hullo*, *hallo*, *hello there*

The all-purpose English greeting — safe with strangers, bosses, and friends alike, and the standard way to answer the telephone. With rising intonation it asks *is anyone there?*, and said flatly with an eye roll it means *isn't that obvious?*

1. "*Hello*, thanks for calling Riverside Dental. How can I help you?"
2. *Hello*, everyone — let's go ahead and get started.
3. She waved and said *hello* to the neighbors on her way to the mailbox.
4. "*Hello?* Is this thing on?"
5. *Hello?* Dad? Are you still there?
6. *Hello?* Of course I told her — she's my sister.

**See also:** [hi](#hi), [hey](#hey), [good morning](#good-morning)

### hey

**Pronunciation:** /heɪ/ &middot; *HAY*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *heyyy*

The most common casual American greeting between people who already know each other. It doubles as an attention-getter and as an objection (*Hey! That's mine!*), and written to a stranger, a professor, or a client it reads as too familiar.

1. *Hey*, good to see you — how was the drive?
2. "*Hey!*" she yelled. "That's my bike!"
3. *Hey*, do you have a second to look at this?
4. He texted just "*hey*" at eleven o'clock at night.
5. Oh, *hey* — I didn't know you were coming.

**See also:** [hi](#hi), [hey there](#hey-there), [yo](#yo)

### hey there

**Pronunciation:** /ˌheɪ ˈðɛr/ &middot; *hay-THAIR*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hi there*, *hello there*

A warmer, softer version of *hey*, used when you are pleased to see someone or when you are addressing someone whose name you do not have. It can sound flirtatious, so it is handled carefully at work.

1. *Hey there!* I was hoping I'd run into you.
2. *Hi there* — are you two ready to order?
3. *Hello there*, little guy. Where did you come from?
4. He opened the message to his coworker with "*Hey there*," then deleted it and typed "Hi Jen" instead.
5. *Hi there*, I'm calling about the apartment listing on Grant Street.

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [hi](#hi), [hello](#hello)

### hi

**Pronunciation:** /haɪ/ &middot; *HIGH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *hi there*

The default friendly greeting in American English — a shade less formal than *hello*, a shade more polite than *hey*. It is also the standard opening of a business email.

1. *Hi*, I'm Dana. We spoke on the phone last week.
2. *Hi* Marcus — following up on the invoice from Tuesday.
3. She said *hi* to everyone in the office before she even took her coat off.
4. "*Hi!*" the kids shouted from the top of the slide.
5. *Hi* — sorry I'm late, the train was a mess.

**See also:** [hello](#hello), [hey](#hey), [hiya](#hiya)

### hiya

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhaɪjə/ &middot; *HY-yuh*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *heya*, *hey ya*

A chipper squeezing-together of *hi you* or *hey you*, used between people on easy terms. Americans do say it, though it is more common in Britain and here can sound folksy or a little old-fashioned.

1. *Hiya!* Long time.
2. "*Heya*," she typed back, "just got home."
3. *Hiya*, kiddo — how was school?
4. He stuck his head in the door with a quick *hiya* and kept walking.
5. *Heya*, can you grab the door for me?

**See also:** [hi](#hi), [hey](#hey), [howdy](#howdy)

### hola

**Pronunciation:** /ˈoʊlɑ/ &middot; *OH-lah*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal

Spanish for *hi*, widely understood in the United States and used as a light, friendly greeting by plenty of people who speak no other Spanish. Note that the *h* is silent.

1. *Hola!* Come in, come in.
2. She answered the door with a cheerful "*Hola*, neighbor."
3. *Hola*, everybody — sorry, I'm still on mute.
4. My daughter has started greeting the dog with *hola*.
5. The barista said *hola* and slid the drink across the counter.

**See also:** [hello](#hello), [hi](#hi), [adios](#adios)

### howdy

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhaʊdi/ &middot; *HOW-dee*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** regional  
**Variants:** *howdy do*, *how-do*, *howdy y'all*

A friendly greeting from Texas and the rural West, worn down over centuries from *how do ye*. Inside that region it is ordinary; outside it, Americans use it to sound folksy, cowboyish, or deliberately corny.

1. *Howdy!* What can I get started for you?
2. He tipped his hat and said *howdy* to everyone on the porch.
3. *Howdy*, folks — welcome to the rodeo.
4. She answered the video call with a big "*Howdy*, y'all" from Austin.
5. *Howdy*, stranger. Haven't seen you around in a while.

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [hiya](#hiya), [hi](#hi)

### namaste

**Pronunciation:** /ˈnɑməˌsteɪ/, also /ˌnɑməˈsteɪ/ &middot; *NAH-muh-stay, also nah-muh-STAY*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral

A Hindi greeting of respect, said with the palms pressed together, that most Americans meet at the end of a yoga class. Used outside that setting by speakers with no connection to it, it can come across as affected, so it is worth handling with care.

1. "*Namaste*," the instructor said, and the whole class bowed.
2. She greeted her grandmother with a quiet *namaste*.
3. *Namaste* — thank you all for practicing with me this morning.
4. The sign above the studio door just said *Namaste* in gold letters.
5. He pressed his palms together and said *namaste* to the family who had hosted him.

**See also:** [hello](#hello), [shalom](#shalom), [aloha](#aloha)

### salutations

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsæljəˈteɪʃənz/ &middot; *sal-yuh-TAY-shunz*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *greetings and salutations*

An ornate, bookish hello that no American uses with a straight face in conversation. It survives as a joke and inside the set phrase *greetings and salutations*.

1. *Salutations*, fellow nerds. The tournament begins at noon.
2. He walked into the meeting and announced, "*Salutations*, everyone."
3. *Greetings and salutations* — I hope this finds you well.
4. Her email opened with *Salutations*, which made me laugh out loud.
5. *Salutations*, my friends. It has been far too long.

**See also:** [greetings](#greetings), [good day](#good-day), [hello](#hello)

### shalom

**Pronunciation:** /ʃəˈloʊm/, also /ʃɑˈloʊm/ &middot; *shuh-LOHM, also shah-LOHM*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral

Hebrew for *peace*, used as both a greeting and a farewell in Jewish American communities and understood well beyond them. It is warm rather than formal, and it works on arrival and on departure alike.

1. *Shalom!* Come in — dinner's almost ready.
2. The rabbi greeted each family at the door with a handshake and a *shalom*.
3. *Shalom*, and thank you all for coming tonight.
4. She signed every letter to her cousins in Tel Aviv with *shalom*.
5. "*Shalom*," he said. "See you next Friday."

**See also:** [aloha](#aloha), [namaste](#namaste), [peace out](#peace-out)

### yo

**Pronunciation:** /joʊ/ &middot; *YOH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** very informal

A blunt, high-energy greeting or attention-getter, strongly associated with young men, hip-hop, and the urban Northeast. It works between friends and sounds rude to a stranger, a customer, or a boss.

1. *Yo!* Over here!
2. *Yo*, did you see the game last night?
3. He answers the phone "*Yo*" no matter who is calling.
4. *Yo*, somebody left their lights on in the parking lot.
5. "*Yo*, my bad," he said, picking up the papers he had knocked over.

**See also:** [hey](#hey), [sup](#sup), [what's up](#whats-up)

### yoo-hoo

**Pronunciation:** /ˈjuˌhu/ &middot; *YOO-hoo*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *yoohoo*, *yoo-hoo there*

A sing-song call used to reach someone who has not noticed you yet — across a parking lot, through a screen door, down a hallway. The two syllables are sung rather than spoken, high then low. It is warm and faintly comic, associated with older women and with cartoons, and it is a summons rather than a plain face-to-face hello.

1. *Yoo-hoo!* Over here, by the flagpole!
2. She rapped on the screen door. "*Yoo-hoo*, anybody home?"
3. *Yoo-hoo* — you left your headlights on.
4. He waved the menu over his head and stage-whispered "*Yoo-hoo*" at the waiter.
5. My neighbor calls *yoo-hoo* over the fence whenever she wants to talk about the mailbox again.

**See also:** [ahoy](#ahoy), [hey](#hey), [hello](#hello)

## Time-of-day greetings

### good afternoon

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˌæftɚˈnun/ &middot; *good-af-ter-NOON*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *afternoon*

A polite greeting for roughly noon until five or six in the evening. It is more formal than *hi*, and it is the standard opening for a presentation, a customer-service call, or an approach to someone you do not know.

1. *Good afternoon*, everyone. Thanks for staying through lunch.
2. "*Good afternoon*, Wexler and Associates. How may I direct your call?"
3. *Good afternoon*, ma'am — can I help you find anything?
4. He nodded at the mail carrier. "*Afternoon*."
5. *Good afternoon*, and welcome aboard Amtrak service to Chicago.

**See also:** [good morning](#good-morning), [good evening](#good-evening), [good day](#good-day)

### good day

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈdeɪ/ &middot; *good-DAY*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *g'day*

A formal greeting or dismissal that has largely left American speech; Americans hear it as British, Australian, or old-fashioned. When it does appear, it is often a curt way of ending a conversation rather than starting one.

1. "*Good day*, sir," the butler said, and closed the door.
2. I have nothing further to say about it. *Good day*.
3. *Good day*, and thank you for your business.
4. He tipped his hat, said *good day*, and walked off down the platform.
5. Her letter ended with "I wish you a *good day*" — very formal, and very final.

**See also:** [good morning](#good-morning), [good afternoon](#good-afternoon), [farewell](#farewell)

### good evening

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈivnɪŋ/ &middot; *good-EEV-ning*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *evening*, *'evening*

A polite greeting used from roughly six in the evening onward. It is a greeting only: when you *leave* at night you say *good night*, never *good evening*.

1. *Good evening*, and welcome to the eleven o'clock news.
2. "*Good evening.* Do you have a reservation?"
3. *Good evening*, folks — the kitchen closes at ten, just so you know.
4. He touched the brim of his cap. "*Evening*, officer."
5. *Good evening*, ladies and gentlemen. Please take your seats.

**See also:** [good night](#good-night), [good afternoon](#good-afternoon), [good morning](#good-morning)

### good morning

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈmɔrnɪŋ/ &middot; *good-MOR-ning*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *g'morning*, *mornin'*

The standard greeting from waking until about noon, safe in every setting from a boardroom to a kitchen table. Americans use it as a greeting only — you do not say it on your way out the door.

1. *Good morning!* The coffee's already made.
2. *Good morning*, everyone — let's start with last quarter's numbers.
3. "*Good morning*, sunshine," she said, pulling the curtains open.
4. He mumbled a *good morning* and went straight for the cereal.
5. *Good morning*, this is Elena from Dr. Patel's office, calling to confirm your appointment.

**See also:** [morning](#morning), [good afternoon](#good-afternoon), [good night](#good-night)

### morning

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɔrnɪŋ/ &middot; *MOR-ning*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *mornin'*, *'morning*

The clipped everyday form of *good morning*, used between people who see each other regularly. It is friendly and a little gruff — exactly what you say to a coworker at the coffee machine.

1. *Morning.* Sleep okay?
2. "*Mornin'*," the guy behind the counter said without looking up.
3. *Morning*, Bill. Cold one out there.
4. She walked past my desk with a quick *morning* and kept going.
5. *Morning*, all — the bagels are in the kitchen.

**See also:** [good morning](#good-morning), [hey](#hey), [hi](#hi)

### top of the morning

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtɑp əv ðə ˈmɔrnɪŋ/ &middot; *tahp-uv-thuh-MOR-ning*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *top o' the morning*, *top of the morning to you*

A stage-Irish greeting that Irish people do not actually use and Americans deploy only as a joke, usually around St. Patrick's Day. The traditional storybook reply is *and the rest of the day to you*.

1. "*Top of the morning to you!*" he said, in an accent that fooled nobody.
2. My boss says *top of the morning* every March seventeenth without fail.
3. "*Top o' the morning*," she said, handing me a green bagel.
4. *Top of the morning*, and welcome to the parade route.
5. He greeted the whole class with a *top of the morning* and got exactly zero laughs.

**See also:** [good morning](#good-morning), [morning](#morning), [howdy](#howdy)

## Greeting questions

### how are you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ ɚ ˈju/ &middot; *how-er-YOO*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *how are ya*, *how are you doing*, *how ya doin'*, *how you doing*

The standard polite greeting question, and a formula rather than a real inquiry. The expected answer is short and positive — *Good, thanks, and you?* — and an honest medical update will startle whoever asked.

1. "*How are you?*" "Good, thanks — how are you?"
2. *How are you doing* today? Did you find everything all right?
3. *How ya doin'*? I haven't seen you since the wedding.
4. She hugged him and said, "*How are you*, really?"
5. *How are you*, Mrs. Alvarez? I'll let the doctor know you're here.

**See also:** [how's it going](#hows-it-going), [how have you been](#how-have-you-been), [how do you do](#how-do-you-do), [what's up](#whats-up)

### how do you do

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ də jə ˈdu/ &middot; *how-duh-yuh-DOO*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *how d'you do*

A formal greeting said on being introduced to someone. It is not really a question and takes no real answer — the traditional reply is to say it straight back. Most Americans now find it dated and say *nice to meet you* instead.

1. "Ambassador, this is Dr. Reyes." "*How do you do.*"
2. *How do you do*, Mr. Chen. I've heard a great deal about you.
3. He bowed slightly and said, "*How do you do*," like someone in a period drama.
4. "*How do you do?*" "*How do you do.*"
5. My grandmother still answers her front door with "*How do you do?*"

**See also:** [nice to meet you](#nice-to-meet-you), [how are you](#how-are-you), [good day](#good-day)

### how have you been

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊ həv ju ˈbɪn/ &middot; *how-huv-yoo-BIN*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *how've you been*, *how you been*, *how've ya been*

The greeting question for someone you have not seen in a while. The past tense is doing real work: it asks about the whole gap rather than about this particular minute, so it opens the door to a fuller answer than *how are you* — though *good, busy* still closes it out politely.

1. *How have you been?* It has to be two years.
2. "*How've you been*, man?" "Busy. Good busy."
3. She sat down across from me. "So. *How have you been*, really?"
4. *How you been*? I heard you moved out to Denver.
5. *How've you been* since the surgery — are you back at work yet?

**See also:** [how are you](#how-are-you), [long time no see](#long-time-no-see), [what's new](#whats-new)

### how's it going

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhaʊz ɪt ˈɡoʊɪŋ/ &middot; *howz-it-GOH-ing*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *how's it goin'*, *how goes it*

A casual greeting question among friends, coworkers, and service staff. Like *how are you*, it wants a short answer — *Good, you?* — and not an actual status report.

1. *How's it going?* Long week?
2. "*How's it goin'*, man? It's been a while."
3. He leaned into the office. "*How's it going* in here?"
4. *How's it going* with the new apartment — are you all moved in?
5. "*How goes it?*" "Can't complain."

**See also:** [how are you](#how-are-you), [what's up](#whats-up), [sup](#sup)

### sup

**Pronunciation:** /sʌp/ &middot; *SUP*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *'sup*, *whaddup*

What is left of *what's up* after everything but the last syllable falls off. It is a greeting between young friends, most often young men, and it is close to meaningless as an actual question.

1. *Sup*, man. You going tonight?
2. He nodded at me across the gym. "*'Sup.*"
3. *Sup*, dude — did you finish the lab writeup?
4. My brother's entire text message was "*sup*."
5. "*Sup*, y'all," she said, dropping her bag by the door.

**See also:** [what's up](#whats-up), [yo](#yo), [what's good](#whats-good)

### what's good

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ˈɡʊd/ &middot; *wuts-GOOD*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal

A greeting out of African American English that has spread through youth slang, meaning roughly *what's up*. Tone decides everything: the same two words said hard are a challenge, closer to *what's your problem*.

1. *What's good*, fam? Where've you been?
2. He came in with a "*What's good*, everybody" and started unpacking his gear.
3. *What's good* — you eat yet?
4. "*What's good* with you and Jordan? You two okay?"
5. She squared up to him. "*What's good?*"

**See also:** [what's up](#whats-up), [sup](#sup), [what's happening](#whats-happening)

### what's happening

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ˈhæpənɪŋ/ &middot; *wuts-HAP-uh-ning*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *what's happenin'*, *what's poppin'*

A casual greeting question that can also, in the right tone, genuinely ask what is going on. It carries a mild 1970s flavor; *what's poppin'* is the current young version.

1. *What's happening*, guys? Am I late?
2. "*What's happenin'*?" he asked, dropping into the chair beside me.
3. *What's happening* tonight — anything I should know about?
4. He answered the door in a bathrobe. "*What's happening*?"
5. *What's poppin'*? Are you coming to the game or not?

**See also:** [what's up](#whats-up), [what's new](#whats-new), [what's good](#whats-good)

### what's new

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ˈnu/ &middot; *wuts-NOO*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *what's new with you*

A greeting for someone you have not seen in a while, sitting halfway between a hello and a real question about their life. The stock answer is *not much*.

1. *What's new?* It feels like I haven't seen you in a year.
2. "*What's new* with you?" "Same old, same old."
3. He sat down at the bar and said, "*What's new*, Sal?"
4. *What's new* at the shop — did they ever hire anybody?
5. "*What's new?*" "Nothing. That's the problem."

**See also:** [what's up](#whats-up), [how are you](#how-are-you), [long time no see](#long-time-no-see)

### what's up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌwʌts ˈʌp/ &middot; *wuts-UP*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *wassup*, *whassup*, *whazzup*, *what up*

The most common casual American greeting question among friends. It usually needs no answer beyond *not much* or another *what's up* — but said with a worried tone it becomes a real question meaning *what's wrong?*

1. *What's up*, man? Good to see you.
2. "*What's up?*" "Not much. You?"
3. *Wassup!* I didn't know you'd be here.
4. Hey, *what's up* — you look upset.
5. She called at two in the morning, so I answered with "*What's up?* Is everything okay?"

**See also:** [sup](#sup), [what's good](#whats-good), [how's it going](#hows-it-going)

## Meeting and welcoming formulas

### good to see you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd tə ˈsi ju/ &middot; *good-tuh-SEE-yoo*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *nice to see you*, *great to see you*, *so good to see you*

What you say to someone you already know, either on meeting or on parting. The distinction that matters for learners: *good to see you* is for people you have met before, and *nice to meet you* is only for the first time.

1. *Good to see you!* It's been way too long.
2. "*Nice to see you* again, Mr. Ford. Same table?"
3. *Great to see you*, buddy — thanks for driving all this way.
4. She shook his hand at the door. "*Good to see you*, and get home safe."
5. *Good to see you* both. Give my love to the kids.

**See also:** [nice to meet you](#nice-to-meet-you), [long time no see](#long-time-no-see), [welcome](#welcome)

### long time no see

**Pronunciation:** /ˌlɔŋ taɪm noʊ ˈsi/ &middot; *long-time-no-SEE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *long time*

A fixed, deliberately ungrammatical greeting for someone you have not seen in months or years. The broken grammar is part of the phrase and must not be repaired.

1. *Long time no see!* You look great.
2. "Well, well — *long time no see*."
3. *Long time no see*, stranger. Where have you been hiding?
4. He clapped me on the shoulder. "*Long time no see*, huh?"
5. *Long time no see* — are you still working over on Fifth?

**See also:** [good to see you](#good-to-see-you), [what's new](#whats-new), [hey](#hey)

### nice to meet you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaɪs tə ˈmit ju/ &middot; *nice-tuh-MEET-yoo*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *pleased to meet you*, *good to meet you*, *nice meeting you*

The standard American formula on being introduced to someone for the first time. Use *nice meeting you* on the way out of that first conversation — and never use either one on someone you have met before.

1. "Sam, this is my sister Priya." "*Nice to meet you!*"
2. *Pleased to meet you*, Ms. Okafor. Thank you for making the time.
3. *Nice to meet you* — I've heard a lot about you.
4. "*Nice meeting you*," she said, handing him her card.
5. It was *nice to meet you* at the conference; here's the article I mentioned.

**See also:** [how do you do](#how-do-you-do), [good to see you](#good-to-see-you), [welcome](#welcome)

### welcome

**Pronunciation:** /ˈwɛlkəm/ &middot; *WEL-kum*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *welcome back*, *welcome aboard*, *welcome home*

A greeting that offers hospitality, said to someone arriving at your home, your event, your city, or your company. Do not confuse it with *you're welcome*, which answers *thank you*.

1. *Welcome!* Come in — let me take your coat.
2. *Welcome* to Denver, where the local time is 4:15.
3. *Welcome aboard*, Nina. We're glad to have you on the team.
4. "*Welcome back*," her manager said. "How was Portugal?"
5. *Welcome*, everyone, to the twenty-third annual chili cook-off.

**See also:** [good to see you](#good-to-see-you), [hello](#hello), [nice to meet you](#nice-to-meet-you), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

## Farewells

### adios

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑdiˈoʊs/ &middot; *ah-dee-OHS*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *adiós*, *adios amigo*

Spanish for goodbye, used by English speakers as a breezy, slightly jokey sign-off. Aimed at a thing rather than a person, it carries a clear note of good riddance.

1. *Adios!* See you Monday.
2. He grabbed his keys, yelled "*Adios*," and was gone.
3. *Adios*, winter — I have never been so happy to see March.
4. "*Adios*, amigos," she said, ducking out of the meeting early.
5. One more mistake like that and it's *adios* for the whole project.

**See also:** [sayonara](#sayonara), [hola](#hola), [ciao](#ciao)

### au revoir

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊ rəˈvwɑr/ &middot; *oh-ruh-VWAHR*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

French for *until we see each other again*. Americans use it sparingly and usually with a wink, to sound worldly, theatrical, or a bit grand.

1. *Au revoir!* Don't forget your umbrella.
2. She kissed both my cheeks and said "*Au revoir*" like we were in a movie.
3. *Au revoir*, Paris. Same time next year.
4. He ended the postcard with "*Au revoir* — see you at Christmas."
5. "Goodbye?" "*Au revoir.* It's not the same thing."

**See also:** [adios](#adios), [ciao](#ciao), [farewell](#farewell)

### bye

**Pronunciation:** /baɪ/ &middot; *BYE*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *bye now*, *byeee*

The everyday American goodbye, clipped from *goodbye* and usable with almost anyone — friends, coworkers, cashiers, your boss. Stretched out in writing (*byeee*) it turns playful or pointedly dismissive.

1. *Bye!* Text me when you land.
2. "Okay, *bye*." She hung up before I could answer.
3. He waved at the kids through the window and mouthed *bye*.
4. *Bye* — thanks again for dinner.
5. All right, I've got to run. *Bye* now.

**See also:** [goodbye](#goodbye), [bye-bye](#bye-bye), [see you](#see-you)

### bye-bye

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbaɪˈbaɪ/ &middot; *bye-BYE*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bye bye*, *buh-bye*, *bye-bye now*

A doubled, sing-song *bye* used with small children and pets, and affectionately between adults. The clipped *buh-bye* is a flight-attendant staple and, said flatly to an adult, a dismissal.

1. Say *bye-bye* to Grandma!
2. "*Bye-bye*, sweetie. Have a good day at school."
3. She wiggled her fingers at the baby. "*Bye-bye!*"
4. "*Buh-bye*, thanks for flying with us. *Buh-bye*."
5. He didn't even argue — just "*buh-bye*" and hung up.

**See also:** [bye](#bye), [nighty-night](#nighty-night), [goodbye](#goodbye)

### catch you later

**Pronunciation:** /ˌkætʃ ju ˈleɪtɚ/ &middot; *kach-yoo-LAY-ter*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *catch ya later*, *catch you around*, *catch you on the flip side*

A relaxed goodbye between friends that implies you expect to run into each other again without committing to when.

1. *Catch you later*, then — say hi to your mom for me.
2. "*Catch ya later*, man." He fist-bumped me and headed for the elevator.
3. *Catch you later* — I'll be around after six if you need me.
4. She shouted "*Catch you on the flip side*!" out the car window.
5. *Catch you around*, Rodriguez.

**See also:** [see you](#see-you), [later](#later), [take it easy](#take-it-easy)

### cheerio

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtʃɪriˈoʊ/ &middot; *cheer-ee-OH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *cheerio then*

A British goodbye that is dated even in Britain. An American who says it is doing an impression, and for most listeners it calls up the breakfast cereal first.

1. "*Cheerio!*" he called, in the worst English accent I have ever heard.
2. *Cheerio* — I'm off to the airport.
3. My grandfather ended every phone call with a brisk *cheerio*.
4. She signed the postcard "*Cheerio* for now — love, Aunt Deb."
5. I answered "*Cheerio*" without thinking, and my coworker just stared at me.

**See also:** [ta-ta](#ta-ta), [toodle-oo](#toodle-oo), [so long](#so-long)

### ciao

**Pronunciation:** /tʃaʊ/ &middot; *CHOW*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *ciao ciao*, *ciao for now*

Italian for both hello and goodbye; in American English it is almost always a goodbye. It strikes some ears as stylish and others as pretentious, and it is spelled *ciao* — never *chow*.

1. *Ciao!* See you at the gallery.
2. She blew a kiss at the camera and said "*Ciao*."
3. *Ciao*, everybody — great rehearsal tonight.
4. He signs off every email with a single word: *Ciao*.
5. "*Ciao ciao*," the waiter said as we left.

**See also:** [adios](#adios), [bye](#bye), [au revoir](#au-revoir)

### farewell

**Pronunciation:** /ˌfɛrˈwɛl/ &middot; *fair-WELL*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *fare thee well*

A grand, literary goodbye that implies a long or permanent parting. In everyday American speech it turns up mainly as deliberate drama or inside the fixed phrase *farewell party*.

1. *Farewell*, my friends. May we meet again in better times.
2. He raised his glass. "*Farewell*, and good luck in Seattle."
3. "*Farewell!*" she said, marching out with a suitcase in each hand.
4. The captain's last words to the crew were a simple *farewell*.
5. *Farewell*, and thank you for forty remarkable years.

**See also:** [godspeed](#godspeed), [goodbye](#goodbye), [so long](#so-long)

### goodbye

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊdˈbaɪ/ &middot; *good-BYE*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *good-bye*, *good bye*, *goodbye now*

The full, weightier form of *bye*, worn down over centuries from *God be with ye*. Because it can sound final, Americans usually reach for *bye*, *see you*, or *take care* in ordinary situations and save *goodbye* for real partings.

1. *Goodbye*, and thank you for everything.
2. "*Goodbye*, Mr. Hartley." The door clicked shut behind her.
3. He never says *goodbye* on the phone — he just hangs up.
4. *Goodbye* for now; I'll write as soon as I get settled.
5. She hugged each of them and said a quiet *goodbye*.

**See also:** [bye](#bye), [farewell](#farewell), [so long](#so-long)

### hasta la vista

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɑstə lə ˈvistə/ &middot; *ah-stuh-luh-VEE-stuh*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *hasta luego*, *hasta*, *hasta la vista, baby*

Spanish for *until the seeing*, borrowed into English as a jokey, faintly theatrical goodbye. For most Americans it arrives with Arnold Schwarzenegger attached to it, which makes it nearly impossible to say straight; *hasta luego* is the version people who actually speak Spanish use.

1. *Hasta la vista!* I'm off to the airport.
2. He dropped the dead toaster in the dumpster. "*Hasta la vista*, baby."
3. "*Hasta luego*," she said. "See you at practice."
4. One missed payment and it's *hasta la vista*, subscription.
5. My roommate has said *hasta* instead of goodbye for three years and has never once finished the phrase.

**See also:** [adios](#adios), [ciao](#ciao), [sayonara](#sayonara)

### later

**Pronunciation:** /ˈleɪtɚ/ &middot; *LAY-ter*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *laters*, *later on*, *later, dude*

A one-word goodbye clipped out of *see you later* and usually thrown over your shoulder on the way out. *Laters* is the jokier, slightly dated extension.

1. All right, *later*.
2. "*Later*, dude." He was out the door before I could answer.
3. She texted back a single word: "*laters*."
4. *Later*, everybody — good practice today.
5. I'm heading out. *Later*, man.

**See also:** [see you](#see-you), [catch you later](#catch-you-later), [peace out](#peace-out)

### over and out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌoʊvɚ ən ˈaʊt/ &middot; *oh-ver-and-OWT*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal

A joking sign-off borrowed from radio procedure. Real operators never say it — *over* means "your turn to talk" and *out* means "I'm finished," so the two cancel each other — but Americans say it anyway to end a call or a message with a flourish.

1. Message received. *Over and out*.
2. "I'm going to bed. *Over and out*," he said, saluting.
3. That's everything from me for the week — *over and out*.
4. She ended the group chat with "Good night, all. *Over and out*."
5. Dad closed every walkie-talkie conversation on that camping trip with *over and out*.

**See also:** [ttyl](#ttyl), [goodbye](#goodbye), [so long](#so-long)

### peace out

**Pronunciation:** /ˌpis ˈaʊt/ &middot; *pees-OWT*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *peace*, *peace, y'all*

A hip-hop-era goodbye that peaked in the 1990s and now sounds pleasantly retro, though bare *peace* is still current. It is friendly, casual, and completely out of place at work.

1. All right, I'm out. *Peace*.
2. "*Peace out*," he said, throwing up two fingers as he left.
3. *Peace out*, everybody — see you at practice on Thursday.
4. She ended her voicemail with "*Peace*," which I found oddly soothing.
5. My teenager says *peace out* to the dog every morning.

**See also:** [later](#later), [take it easy](#take-it-easy), [shalom](#shalom)

### sayonara

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsaɪəˈnɑrə/ &middot; *sy-uh-NAH-ruh*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** informal

Japanese for goodbye, borrowed into casual American English with a note of finality — it often carries the sense *and good riddance*. In Japanese itself it is a fairly heavy farewell, not a daily one.

1. *Sayonara!* Have fun in Tokyo.
2. One bad review and *sayonara* — the show was canceled.
3. "*Sayonara*, suckers," he said, cashing out and leaving the table.
4. He dropped the last box in the truck and said *sayonara* to the old apartment.
5. My little brother says *sayonara* every time he leaves a room, and he still thinks it's hilarious.

**See also:** [adios](#adios), [ciao](#ciao), [goodbye](#goodbye)

### see you

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsi ju/ &middot; *SEE-yoo*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *see ya*, *see you later*, *see ya later*, *see you soon*, *see you later, alligator*

The workhorse American casual goodbye, usually with a time attached: *see you Monday*, *see you at six*. It assumes you will meet again, which makes it a soft everyday parting rather than a real farewell.

1. *See you* tomorrow!
2. "*See ya*," he said, already halfway down the hall.
3. *See you later* — I'll call when I'm on my way.
4. Okay, *see you* at the restaurant at seven.
5. "*See you later, alligator*." "After a while, crocodile."

**See also:** [later](#later), [catch you later](#catch-you-later), [bye](#bye)

### so long

**Pronunciation:** /ˌsoʊ ˈlɔŋ/ &middot; *soh-LAWNG*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *so long now*

An old-fashioned goodbye with a wistful edge, common in mid-century American speech and song and now heard mostly from older speakers or used for period flavor.

1. *So long*, and thanks for all your help.
2. "*So long*, kid," the old man said, and shuffled off.
3. *So long*, New York — I'm going to miss you.
4. He tipped his hat and said *so long* like a cowboy in a matinee.
5. *So long* for now; I'll see you at the reunion.

**See also:** [farewell](#farewell), [goodbye](#goodbye), [cheerio](#cheerio)

### ta-ta

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtɑˈtɑ/ &middot; *tah-TAH*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *ta ta*, *ta-ta for now*, *TTFN*

A light, sing-song goodbye of British origin. Americans use it playfully or to sound affectedly posh; *ta-ta for now* is remembered chiefly as Tigger's line in *Winnie-the-Pooh*.

1. *Ta-ta!* Don't wait up.
2. She fluttered her fingers at us. "*Ta-ta*, darlings."
3. "*Ta-ta for now*," he said, and logged off.
4. The hostess waved us out the door with a *ta-ta* and a little curtsy.
5. *Ta-ta*, and mind the puddle on your way out.

**See also:** [toodle-oo](#toodle-oo), [cheerio](#cheerio), [bye-bye](#bye-bye)

### toodle-oo

**Pronunciation:** /ˌtudəlˈu/ &middot; *too-dul-OO*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** dated  
**Variants:** *toodles*, *toodle-loo*, *toodaloo*

A frivolous, jokey goodbye that nobody says with a straight face anymore. The shortened *toodles* survives in texts as a wink.

1. *Toodle-oo!* I'm off to the dentist.
2. "*Toodles*," she wrote, and closed the chat window.
3. He waved a napkin like a handkerchief. "*Toodle-oo!*"
4. *Toodle-oo*, folks — the bus leaves in five minutes.
5. My grandmother said *toodle-oo* at the end of every single visit.

**See also:** [ta-ta](#ta-ta), [cheerio](#cheerio), [bye-bye](#bye-bye)

### ttyl

**Pronunciation:** /ˌti ti waɪ ˈɛl/ &middot; *tee-tee-why-EL*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** internet/text  
**Variants:** *TTYL*, *ttys*

Short for *talk to you later*, a written sign-off out of the instant-messaging era. It is typed rather than spoken — if it is read aloud at all, the letters are spelled out — and it now carries a faint whiff of the early 2000s.

1. Gotta go, my mom's calling. *ttyl*
2. "*TTYL!*" she typed, and her status went gray.
3. He always ends with *ttyl* even when he means he'll text again in five minutes.
4. *ttyl* — good luck on the exam tomorrow.
5. My sister signs off with *ttyl*; my dad signs off with "Sincerely, Dad."

**See also:** [over and out](#over-and-out), [later](#later), [bye](#bye)

## Parting wishes and sign-offs

### be well

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbi ˈwɛl/ &middot; *bee-WELL*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *stay well*, *be safe*

A gentle, slightly formal parting wish, common in email sign-offs, in health-care settings, and among people who want to sound warm without sounding casual.

1. *Be well*, and let me know how the appointment goes.
2. She ended the letter with two words: "*Be well*."
3. "*Be well*, Mr. Duncan. We'll see you in six months."
4. *Be well*, everyone. Take care of each other.
5. He hugged her at the gate. "*Be well*, okay?"

**See also:** [take care](#take-care), [safe travels](#safe-travels), [until next time](#until-next-time)

### bon voyage

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɑn vwɑˈjɑʒ/, also /ˌbɑn vɔɪˈɑʒ/ &middot; *bahn-vwah-YAHZH, also bahn-voy-AHZH*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral

French for *good journey*, said to someone leaving on a trip — especially a long or glamorous one. It is written on cards and frosted onto cakes at least as often as it is spoken.

1. *Bon voyage!* Send pictures from Lisbon.
2. The banner over the office door read "*Bon Voyage*, Angela."
3. *Bon voyage* — try not to think about work for two entire weeks.
4. "*Bon voyage*," he said, sliding the passport across the table.
5. Her coworkers signed a card and wrote *bon voyage* in six different languages.

**See also:** [safe travels](#safe-travels), [godspeed](#godspeed), [au revoir](#au-revoir)

### don't be a stranger

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdoʊnt bi ə ˈstreɪndʒɚ/ &middot; *dohnt-bee-uh-STRAYN-jer*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *don't be a stranger now*

A warm American parting formula meaning *stay in touch* — an invitation to call or come by sooner than you did last time. It is affectionate rather than scolding, though it can carry a gentle nudge when the gap really has been too long.

1. *Don't be a stranger!* Come by anytime.
2. "*Don't be a stranger*, now," my aunt said, pressing a bag of tomatoes into my hands.
3. He shook my hand at the curb. "Good seeing you. *Don't be a stranger*."
4. *Don't be a stranger* — the door's always open and the coffee's always terrible.
5. She wrote "Congratulations on the new place, and *don't be a stranger*" inside the card.

**See also:** [take care](#take-care), [be well](#be-well), [see you](#see-you)

### drive safe

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdraɪv ˈseɪf/ &middot; *drive-SAFE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *drive safely*, *drive careful*

A very American parting formula, said to anyone getting into a car — especially at night or in bad weather. Grammar says it should be *drive safely*, and plenty of people say that, but *drive safe* is what most Americans actually say.

1. *Drive safe!* Text me when you get home.
2. "It's really coming down out there — *drive safely*."
3. *Drive safe*, and watch out for deer on 9W.
4. She hugged him in the driveway. "Love you. *Drive safe*."
5. *Drive safe*, everybody. See you next Sunday.

**See also:** [safe travels](#safe-travels), [take care](#take-care), [be well](#be-well)

### godspeed

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡɑdˈspid/ &middot; *gahd-SPEED*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *God speed*

A solemn wish for success and safety, said to someone setting out on something difficult or dangerous. Its flavor is archaic, so it is used in earnest only at genuinely serious moments — anywhere else it reads as a joke.

1. *Godspeed*, and come home safe.
2. "*Godspeed*, gentlemen," the flight director said as the countdown reached zero.
3. He shook each of their hands and wished them *godspeed*.
4. You're really going to reorganize the whole shared drive by yourself? *Godspeed*.
5. *Godspeed* to everyone driving through this storm tonight.

**See also:** [farewell](#farewell), [safe travels](#safe-travels), [be well](#be-well), [good luck](#good-luck)

### good night

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈnaɪt/ &middot; *good-NITE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *goodnight*, *g'night*, *night*, *night-night*

A farewell, not a greeting: you say it when leaving in the evening or heading to bed, never when arriving. The clipped *night* is what most Americans actually say to family at bedtime.

1. *Good night!* Thanks for having us.
2. "*Night*, Mom." "*Night*, honey. Love you."
3. *Good night*, everyone, and drive carefully.
4. He turned off the lamp. "*G'night.*"
5. *Good night*, Chicago — you've been wonderful!

**See also:** [nighty-night](#nighty-night), [good evening](#good-evening), [bye](#bye)

### have a good one

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæv ə ˈɡʊd wʌn/ &middot; *hav-uh-GOOD-wun*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *have a good day*, *have a great one*, *have a good night*

An all-purpose American sign-off that dodges naming the time of day — *one* stands in for day, night, weekend, whatever fits. It is the standard friendly close between service workers and customers.

1. "Here's your receipt. *Have a good one!*"
2. *Have a good one*, man. See you next week.
3. She waved from the register. "Thanks — *have a great one!*"
4. *Have a good day*, and thanks again for your patience.
5. All right, I'm heading out. *Have a good one*, folks.

**See also:** [have a nice day](#have-a-nice-day), [take care](#take-care), [bye](#bye), [take it easy](#take-it-easy)

### have a nice day

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæv ə ˌnaɪs ˈdeɪ/ &middot; *hav-uh-nice-DAY*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *have a nice one*, *have a great day*, *you have a nice day now*

The scripted American service goodbye — printed on receipts, required by more than one employee handbook, and said several hundred times a shift. Warmly delivered it is genuinely pleasant; delivered flat it is wallpaper, and aimed at somebody you have just finished arguing with it becomes a polite way of telling them where to go.

1. "Your total is $18.40. *Have a nice day!*"
2. *Have a nice day*, and thanks for shopping with us.
3. The receipt said *Have a nice day* under a little smiley face.
4. She hung up on him with a bright "*Have a nice day*."
5. "*You have a nice day now*," the clerk said, in a tone that suggested I would not.

**See also:** [have a good one](#have-a-good-one), [take care](#take-care), [bye](#bye)

### nighty-night

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnaɪti ˈnaɪt/ &middot; *ny-tee-NITE*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** childish  
**Variants:** *night-night*, *nite-nite*, *nighty-nite*

A cozy, sing-song *good night* used with small children and, teasingly, with adults. Between grown-ups it is either affectionate or gently mocking.

1. *Nighty-night*, sleep tight.
2. She tucked the blanket under his chin. "*Night-night*, buddy."
3. "*Nighty-night*," he said, closing his laptop at 8:45 in the evening.
4. *Night-night!* Don't let the bedbugs bite.
5. He kissed her forehead and whispered *nighty-night*.

**See also:** [good night](#good-night), [bye-bye](#bye-bye), [bye](#bye)

### safe travels

**Pronunciation:** /ˌseɪf ˈtrævəlz/ &middot; *safe-TRAV-uhlz*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *safe flight*, *safe trip*, *travel safe*

A courteous parting wish for anyone about to travel, and the workplace-safe alternative to *drive safe*. It works equally well spoken and at the bottom of an email.

1. *Safe travels!* Let us know when you land.
2. "*Safe travels*, and thanks again for coming all this way."
3. *Safe travels* tomorrow — I hear the airport is a nightmare on Fridays.
4. She wrote *Safe travels* at the bottom of the email and hit send.
5. *Safe flight*, Dad. Call me from the layover.

**See also:** [drive safe](#drive-safe), [bon voyage](#bon-voyage), [take care](#take-care)

### take care

**Pronunciation:** /ˌteɪk ˈkɛr/ &middot; *take-KAIR*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *take care now*, *you take care*

A warm, faintly caring goodbye that works with friends, coworkers, and near-strangers alike, in speech and in writing. Said with weight it means *look after yourself*; said lightly it is simply a pleasant *bye*.

1. *Take care!* It was really good seeing you.
2. "*Take care* now," the pharmacist said, sliding the bag across the counter.
3. *Take care*, and give me a call if anything changes.
4. She squeezed my hand. "*Take care* of yourself, okay?"
5. *Take care*, everyone. Same time next month.

**See also:** [be well](#be-well), [have a good one](#have-a-good-one), [bye](#bye)

### take it easy

**Pronunciation:** /ˌteɪk ɪt ˈizi/ &middot; *take-it-EE-zee*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *take 'er easy*, *easy now*

A relaxed goodbye between friends and acquaintances. Watch the second sense: said sharply to someone who is upset or moving too fast, *take it easy* means *calm down*.

1. Good seeing you, man. *Take it easy*.
2. "*Take it easy*," he said, waving from the truck.
3. *Take it easy* this weekend — you've earned the rest.
4. Whoa, *take it easy*. Nobody is blaming you.
5. *Take it easy* on that ankle for a few days.

**See also:** [catch you later](#catch-you-later), [peace out](#peace-out), [have a good one](#have-a-good-one)

### until next time

**Pronunciation:** /ənˌtɪl ˈnɛkst taɪm/ &middot; *un-til-NEKST-time*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *till next time*

A tidy closing formula for a performance, a podcast, a broadcast, or a newsletter rather than for ordinary conversation. It is not formal so much as staged — it belongs to somebody signing off an audience — and it promises another meeting while rounding the occasion off.

1. That's all we have for today. *Until next time*, I'm Ana Torres.
2. Thanks for listening, and *until next time*, keep asking good questions.
3. *Until next time*, friends — stay curious.
4. She closed the newsletter with "*Until next time*."
5. *Till next time*, take care of yourselves and each other.

**See also:** [be well](#be-well), [farewell](#farewell), [so long](#so-long)

## Thanks and responses to thanks

### anytime

**Pronunciation:** /ˈɛniˌtaɪm/ &middot; *EN-ee-time*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *any time*, *anytime at all*

A friendly answer to thanks that works by offering the favor a second time — it means *ask me again*. It is warm rather than dismissive, and it lands badly only when both of you know perfectly well that you would not do it again.

1. "Thanks for picking me up." "*Anytime*."
2. *Anytime* — seriously, call me whenever.
3. "I owe you one." "*Anytime at all*. That's what neighbors are for."
4. He waved off my thanks with a quick "*anytime*" and went back to his mowing.
5. "*Anytime*," she said, and I could tell she meant it, which is rarer than it ought to be.

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [you bet](#you-bet), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

### don't mention it

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdoʊnt ˈmɛnʃən ɪt/ &middot; *dohnt-MEN-shun-it*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *think nothing of it*, *forget it*

A modest reply to thanks that waves the favor off as too small to be worth counting. It is a shade warmer and a shade more old-fashioned than *no problem*, and it lives in speech — nobody types it.

1. "Thanks for covering my shift." "*Don't mention it*."
2. *Don't mention it* — you'd have done the same for me.
3. He helped me wrestle the couch up two flights and said "*Don't mention it*" before I could get a word out.
4. "I really appreciate the ride." "*Don't mention it*, you were on my way."
5. *Don't mention it*, honestly. It took me five minutes.

**See also:** [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [no problem](#no-problem), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### my pleasure

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈplɛʒɚ/ &middot; *my-PLEZH-er*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** formal  
**Variants:** *it's my pleasure*, *the pleasure is mine*

A gracious reply to thanks, standard in hotels, restaurants, and customer service, where some chains train staff to say it instead of *no problem*. Between friends it sounds slightly courtly, which is occasionally the joke.

1. "Thank you for staying so late." "*My pleasure*."
2. "Thanks for the refill!" "*My pleasure* — holler if you need anything else."
3. *My pleasure*, ma'am. Enjoy the rest of your evening.
4. She held the door and said "*My pleasure*" before I'd finished thanking her.
5. "You really didn't have to drive me." "*It's my pleasure.* Truly."

**See also:** [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it), [thank you](#thank-you)

### no problem

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈprɑbləm/ &middot; *noh-PRAH-blum*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no prob*, *not a problem*, *no problemo*

The default American reply to thanks among speakers under about fifty, and also a way of accepting a request or brushing off an apology. Some older listeners hear an unwelcome implication in it — that the favor *could* have been a problem — and prefer *you're welcome*, so it is worth reading the room at work.

1. "Thanks for grabbing my mail." "*No problem*."
2. "Sorry I'm late." "*No problem*, we haven't started."
3. "Could you send that over before five?" "*No problem*."
4. *No problem* at all — I was driving right past.
5. He says "*no problemo*," which is not Spanish, and which I have asked him repeatedly to stop saying.

**See also:** [no worries](#no-worries), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [don't mention it](#dont-mention-it)

### no worries

**Pronunciation:** /ˌnoʊ ˈwɝiz/ &middot; *noh-WUR-eez*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *no worries at all*, *not to worry*

An Australian import that has settled all the way into American English, used to answer thanks, to wave off an apology, and to tell someone to stop fretting. It is the softest of the replies; its real job is reassurance rather than acknowledgment.

1. "Sorry, I completely forgot to write back." "*No worries*, I figured you were swamped."
2. "Thanks for waiting." "*No worries*."
3. *No worries* — take all the time you need.
4. She texted back "*no worries!!*" with two exclamation points, so I assume she is furious.
5. "I might be ten minutes behind." "*No worries at all*, we'll save you a seat."

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [take it easy](#take-it-easy)

### thank you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌθæŋk ˈju/ &middot; *thank-YOO*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *thank you very much*, *thank you so much*, *thank you kindly*, *many thanks*

The full form, and the one that carries weight: you use it for a real favor, in a formal setting, or anywhere *thanks* would sound too breezy. Said flatly with heavy stress on *you*, it stops being gratitude and becomes *see, I told you so*.

1. *Thank you*, Doctor. We appreciate everything you did for her.
2. "*Thank you* for having us — dinner was incredible."
3. *Thank you so much.* I don't know what we'd have done without you.
4. "The report's already on your desk." "Oh — *thank you*."
5. "You were right, the exit was on the left." "*Thank you.*"

**See also:** [thanks](#thanks), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [my pleasure](#my-pleasure)

### thanks

**Pronunciation:** /θæŋks/ &middot; *THANKS*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *thanks so much*, *thanks a bunch*, *thanks again*, *appreciate it*, *thx* (text only)

The everyday American thank-you: lighter and quicker than *thank you*, and the default in speech, in texts, and above a signature line. One trap — *thanks a lot* said flat, with the stress falling hard on *lot*, usually means the opposite.

1. *Thanks!* That's exactly what I needed.
2. "Here's your change." "*Thanks*."
3. *Thanks* so much for turning this around on a Friday.
4. She waved through the windshield and mouthed *thanks* as I let her merge.
5. He ended the whole email with one word — "*Thanks*" — and no signature at all.

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome), [no problem](#no-problem)

### you bet

**Pronunciation:** /ˌju ˈbɛt/ &middot; *yoo-BET*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *you betcha*, *you bet you*

A hearty American reply to thanks, and equally a cheerful *yes* to a request or a question. It runs strongest in the Midwest and the Mountain West; *you betcha* is the folksier version, and for a lot of listeners it now arrives with a Minnesota accent attached whether the speaker intended one or not.

1. "Thanks for holding the door." "*You bet*."
2. "Can you have it to me by Thursday?" "*You bet*."
3. *You bet* I'm going — I bought the tickets back in March.
4. "*You betcha*," he said, and topped off my coffee without being asked.
5. She thanked him twice, and both times he just said, "*You bet*, hon."

**See also:** [anytime](#anytime), [no problem](#no-problem), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

### you're welcome

**Pronunciation:** /jʊr ˈwɛlkəm/ &middot; *yur-WEL-kum*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *you're very welcome*, *you're quite welcome*, *yer welcome*

The standard reply to *thank you*, and the one that is safe across every age and setting. Do not confuse it with bare *welcome*, which greets an arrival. Said pointedly to somebody who did *not* thank you, it turns into a rebuke.

1. "Thanks for the ride." "*You're welcome*."
2. *You're very welcome* — I'm just glad it worked out.
3. "Thank you for lunch." "*You're welcome.* Next one's on you."
4. She held the elevator, he walked in without a word, and she said "*You're welcome*" to the back of his head.
5. He answers every thank-you with "*You're welcome*," never "no problem" — he is from a generation that hears a difference.

**See also:** [no problem](#no-problem), [thank you](#thank-you), [welcome](#welcome)

## Apologies and courtesies

### excuse me

**Pronunciation:** /ɪkˈskjuz mi/ &middot; *ik-SKYOOZ-mee*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *'scuse me*, *excuse me a second*, *excuse you*

The hardest-working courtesy formula in American English, and it holds down at least five jobs: it clears a path, it apologizes for a small bodily offense, it flags an interruption, it hails a stranger, and — said with the pitch climbing hard on *me* — it registers that the other person has just said or done something out of line. The last one is not a request for anything; it is a warning shot.

1. *Excuse me* — could I squeeze by?
2. "*Excuse me*, do you know if this bus goes to the airport?"
3. He burped, went red, and said, "*Excuse me*."
4. *Excuse me* for one second, I have to take this.
5. "You'll be covering the difference yourself." "*Excuse me?*"

**See also:** [pardon me](#pardon-me), [sorry](#sorry), [please](#please)

### my bad

**Pronunciation:** /maɪ ˈbæd/ &middot; *my-BAD*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** very informal  
**Variants:** *that's my bad*, *my b*

A light, casual admission of a small mistake, out of 1980s pickup-basketball slang and now general American English. It covers spilled coffee and the wrong meeting room; used after anything that actually hurt someone, it sounds flippant to the point of insulting.

1. "That's my charger." "Oh — *my bad*."
2. *My bad*, I sent that to the wrong Rachel.
3. He backed into the cone, shrugged, and said "*My bad*."
4. *My bad* on the meeting time — I read the calendar in the wrong time zone.
5. "You told me the wrong gate." "*My bad*. It was Terminal B."

**See also:** [sorry](#sorry), [no problem](#no-problem), [no worries](#no-worries)

### pardon me

**Pronunciation:** /ˈpɑrdən ˌmi/ &middot; *PAR-dun-mee*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *pardon*, *pardon me?*, *I beg your pardon*, *pardon my French*

A politer, older-sounding *excuse me*: it squeezes past people, apologizes for a small lapse, and — as a bare *pardon?* — asks somebody to say that again. It is a shade more genteel than *excuse me* rather than genuinely formal, and some speakers avoid it for exactly that reason. *I beg your pardon*, with the stress climbing, is indignation; *pardon my French* apologizes in advance for the word you are about to use.

1. *Pardon me* — is this seat taken?
2. "*Pardon?* I didn't catch that."
3. She stepped on my foot getting off the train. "*Pardon me*, I am so sorry."
4. "*I beg your pardon.* I have been coming here for thirty years."
5. The whole thing was, *pardon my French*, a disaster.

**See also:** [excuse me](#excuse-me), [sorry](#sorry), [please](#please)

### please

**Pronunciation:** /pliz/ &middot; *PLEEZ*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *pretty please*, *please and thank you*

The politeness marker that turns an order into a request. Americans expect it in anything asked of a stranger or a service worker, and leaving it out reads as brusque even in a friendly voice. Standing alone with the vowel drawn out it becomes a plea; said flat and falling, especially as *oh, please*, it is pure scorn.

1. Two black coffees, *please*.
2. "*Please*," she said, "just hear me out."
3. "He says he read the whole thing over the weekend." "Oh, *please*."
4. Could you *please* stop clicking that pen?
5. My mother's rule was that nothing got handed to anybody without a *please*.

**See also:** [thank you](#thank-you), [sorry](#sorry), [you're welcome](#youre-welcome)

### sorry

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsɑri/ &middot; *SAH-ree*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *I'm sorry*, *so sorry*, *sorry about that*, *my apologies*

The all-purpose American apology, running from a real one all the way down to the reflex you produce squeezing past someone in a grocery aisle. It does sympathy work as well (*I'm so sorry for your loss*), which means the same two syllables can mean *I was wrong* or *that happened to you and I feel for you* — context does all the sorting.

1. *Sorry!* I didn't see you there.
2. "*Sorry* I'm late — parking was a disaster."
3. *I'm sorry.* That was a genuinely thoughtless thing to say and I shouldn't have said it.
4. She edged past behind his chair. "*Sorry*, *sorry*."
5. "*I'm so sorry* about your mom. She was wonderful to us."

**See also:** [my bad](#my-bad), [excuse me](#excuse-me), [please](#please), [no worries](#no-worries)

## Toasts, blessings, and good wishes

### bless you

**Pronunciation:** /ˌblɛs ˈju/ &middot; *bles-YOO*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *God bless you*, *bless ya*

The automatic American response to somebody's sneeze — supplied by strangers on buses and in checkout lines, and conspicuous when it does not come. The expected reply is *thank you*. Whatever its origins, almost nobody now hears any theology in it.

1. "*Achoo!*" "*Bless you*." "Thanks."
2. He sneezed four times running, and the whole conference room gave up after the second *bless you*.
3. *Bless you* — do you want a tissue?
4. Nobody said *bless you*, and I stood there in the elevator feeling obscurely wronged.
5. "*God bless you*," the cashier said, and handed me a napkin.

**See also:** [gesundheit](#gesundheit), [thank you](#thank-you), [good luck](#good-luck)

### bon appétit

**Pronunciation:** /ˌboʊn ˌæpəˈti/ &middot; *bohn-ap-uh-TEE*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *bon appetit*

French for *good appetite*, said as the food reaches the table. English never settled on a native formula for that moment, so Americans borrowed this one and use it half in earnest and half as a flourish; *dig in* and *enjoy* are the plain alternatives, and the final *t* stays silent.

1. She set the platter down in the middle of the table. "*Bon appétit*."
2. *Bon appétit!* Careful, the plate is hot.
3. The waiter said "*Bon appétit*" and vanished before we could ask about the check.
4. He microwaved a burrito, handed it to me on a paper towel, and said, "*Bon appétit*."
5. *Bon appétit*, everybody — start before it gets cold.

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [bottoms up](#bottoms-up), [thank you](#thank-you)

### bottoms up

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbɑtəmz ˈʌp/ &middot; *bah-tumz-UP*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *bottoms up!*, *down the hatch*, *drink up*

A toast that tells the table to empty the glass rather than sip at it, which is why it belongs to shots and beer far more than to wine. It also gets used half-jokingly over cough syrup and anything else you would rather swallow in one go.

1. He raised his shot glass. "*Bottoms up*."
2. *Bottoms up*, everybody — last round before the kitchen closes.
3. "*Bottoms up*," the nurse said, handing my son the little paper cup.
4. "*Down the hatch*," my uncle announces every Thanksgiving, before anyone has poured anything.
5. *Bottoms up* — and then somebody is going to have to drive me home.

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [bon appétit](#bon-appétit), [congratulations](#congratulations)

### break a leg

**Pronunciation:** /ˌbreɪk ə ˈlɛɡ/ &middot; *brayk-uh-LEG*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *break a leg out there*

The theater's way of wishing someone luck, built on the superstition that saying *good luck* to a performer invites disaster. It has spread well past the stage to auditions, recitals, big presentations, and job interviews.

1. *Break a leg* tonight! We'll be in the third row.
2. "*Break a leg*," the stage manager said, and took the house lights down.
3. She hugged him outside the audition room. "*Break a leg*, kid."
4. *Break a leg* on the interview — call me the second you're out.
5. He texted the whole cast "*break a leg*" twenty minutes before curtain.

**See also:** [good luck](#good-luck), [godspeed](#godspeed), [congratulations](#congratulations)

### cheers

**Pronunciation:** /tʃɪrz/ &middot; *CHEERZ*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** informal  
**Variants:** *cheers to that*, *cheers, everyone*

In American English this is a drinking toast and almost nothing else — you say it as the glasses come together. The British senses meaning *thanks* and *goodbye* are understood here but sound borrowed, and a *Cheers* at the bottom of an American work email strikes a good many readers as an affectation.

1. "*Cheers!*" Six glasses met over the middle of the table.
2. *Cheers*, everybody — to twenty-five years and counting.
3. He lifted his beer an inch off the bar. "*Cheers*."
4. "To surviving the quarter." "*Cheers to that*."
5. She signs her emails *Cheers, Meredith*, and she has never set foot in England.

**See also:** [bottoms up](#bottoms-up), [congratulations](#congratulations), [mazel tov](#mazel-tov), [cheerio](#cheerio)

### congratulations

**Pronunciation:** /kənˌɡrætʃəˈleɪʃənz/ &middot; *kun-gra-chuh-LAY-shunz*  
**Type:** secondary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *congrats*, *congratulations to you both*, *many congratulations*

The standard formula for somebody else's good news — an engagement, a baby, a promotion, a diploma. Clipped *congrats* is the everyday spoken and texted form; the full word belongs in cards, toasts, and moments you want to sound like you mean it. Note the *-s*: there is no singular *congratulation*.

1. *Congratulations!* When's the wedding?
2. "*Congrats* on the new job, man. Well deserved."
3. *Congratulations* to the class of 2026 — you did it.
4. She wrote *Congratulations!* across the entire inside of the card in gold pen.
5. "We closed on the house this morning." "*Congratulations*, that's huge."

**See also:** [cheers](#cheers), [mazel tov](#mazel-tov), [good luck](#good-luck)

### gesundheit

**Pronunciation:** /ɡəˈzʊnthaɪt/ &middot; *guh-ZOONT-hite*  
**Type:** primary &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *gezundheit*

German for *health*, used in American English as the secular alternative to *bless you* after a sneeze. It is common in the Midwest and in Jewish and German American families, and some speakers reach for it precisely because it blesses nobody.

1. "*Achoo!*" "*Gesundheit*."
2. My grandfather said *gesundheit*, my grandmother said *bless you*, and in sixty years neither one budged.
3. *Gesundheit* — are you coming down with something?
4. She sneezed and three people in the waiting room said *gesundheit* at once.
5. He says *gesundheit* because he'd rather not go around blessing strangers.

**See also:** [bless you](#bless-you), [cheers](#cheers), [thank you](#thank-you)

### good luck

**Pronunciation:** /ˌɡʊd ˈlʌk/ &middot; *good-LUK*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *best of luck*, *good luck out there*, *the best of luck to you*

The plain wish for anyone walking into a test, an interview, a game, or a surgery. Watch the sarcastic turn: *good luck with that*, said flat about a hopeless errand, means *you are going to fail and I want no part of it*.

1. *Good luck* tomorrow — you've studied plenty.
2. "*Best of luck*," the recruiter said, shaking my hand at the door.
3. *Good luck*, everybody. Play smart out there.
4. "I'm going to ask them to refund the whole thing." "*Good luck with that*."
5. He wished us *good luck* and then, more quietly, told us we were going to need it.

**See also:** [break a leg](#break-a-leg), [godspeed](#godspeed), [congratulations](#congratulations)

### happy birthday

**Pronunciation:** /ˌhæpi ˈbɝθˌdeɪ/ &middot; *hap-ee-BURTH-day*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *happy birthday to you*, *happy anniversary*, *happy holidays*, *happy New Year*

The formula owed on the day itself — said in person, sung over a cake, and typed a few hundred times a year onto other people's timelines. The whole *happy ___* family behaves the same way: the wish is due on the date whether or not the person saying it has thought about you since last year, and skipping it is noticed far more than saying it is.

1. *Happy birthday!* Make a wish.
2. "*Happy birthday*, Dad. Sorry the card is late."
3. Forty-one people wrote *Happy birthday!* on her page, and two of them called.
4. *Happy birthday* to my favorite sister — and yes, I know you're the only one.
5. He forgot until 11:40 at night and texted "*happy birthday*!!" from a parking lot.

**See also:** [congratulations](#congratulations), [cheers](#cheers), [mazel tov](#mazel-tov)

### mazel tov

**Pronunciation:** /ˈmɑzəl ˌtɔv/ &middot; *MAH-zul-tawv*  
**Type:** phrase &middot; **Register:** neutral  
**Variants:** *mazal tov*, *mazel tov to you both*

Hebrew and Yiddish for *good fortune*, and the standard congratulation at Jewish weddings, bar and bat mitzvahs, and births — well understood outside Jewish American communities too. It marks something that has already happened, so it congratulates rather than wishing luck ahead of time.

1. The glass broke and the whole room shouted "*Mazel tov!*"
2. "We're expecting in June." "*Mazel tov!* That's wonderful news."
3. *Mazel tov* to Sarah and Ben on thirty years.
4. He got the promotion, so of course my mother called to say *mazel tov*.
5. "*Mazel tov*," she said, "and eat something — you're skin and bones."

**See also:** [congratulations](#congratulations), [cheers](#cheers), [shalom](#shalom)
